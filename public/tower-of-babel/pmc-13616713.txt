Toward reliable computer-aided brain tumor diagnosis: a contrast-enhanced deep learning approach with hybrid KNN classification
Authors: Krithika Pandurangan, Doddi Chandana Gowri, Jonnla Naga Surekha, Pratiksha V. Chafle, Kolagotla Venkateswara Reddy, Shonak Bansal, Krishna Prakash, Md. Shabiul Islam, Mohammad Tariqul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Timely and accurate diagnosis of brain tumors using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a major concern in neuro-oncology because of the potential effects of delayed and inconsistent radiological diagnoses on the management of such diseases. Manual analysis of MRIs is time-consuming and highly susceptible to observer variations; thus, there is a need for computer-based and more consistent diagnostic processes. In this study, an automatic diagnostic process based on deep learning was proposed using multiscale contrast enhancement along with lightweight convolutional features and classifiers for diagnosing four classes of brain tumors. The MRI images were analyzed using a Multi-scale Intensity Contrast Amplification (MICA) algorithm that enhances the relevant structural information of the tumors across fine, intermediate, and coarse spatial scales. The refined images were then fed into an in-house convolutional neural network (CNN) backbone architecture that uses depth-wise separable convolution, residual skip connection, and squeeze-and-excitation (SE) attention mechanism to extract compact and discriminative feature representations. To select the optimal decision layer for this representation, three supervised learning methods, namely K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest (RF), were trained on this set of features and compared with the softmax head of the CNN. The experiments were performed on a publicly available, three-source combined MRI dataset of 7,023 images across glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and normal categories using both hold-out and 5-fold cross-validation setups. In all the evaluated configurations, the hybrid model of CNN and KNN provided the best hold-out accuracy (96.58%), whereas the hybrid model of CNN and SVM offered the best macro-average precision (98.81%) and area under the ROC curve (99.56%). Both hybrids were statistically superior (p < 0.001) to the softmax head of the CNN in the paired statistical tests. Class Activation visualization based on gradient analysis revealed that the network concentrated on clinically sensible regions of the tumors rather than other irrelevant background regions. With a compact parameter footprint (0.20 MB in half precision) and an inference throughput of approximately 23.7 images per second on the CPU and 220.1 images per second on an NVIDIA A100 GPU at a batch size of 16, the proposed framework offers an accurate, interpretable, and computationally frugal solution that is suitable for computer-aided brain tumor screening in resource-constrained clinical settings.

Introduction

This section establishes the clinical setting in which the automated brain tumor classification operates. It outlines the epidemiology and taxonomy of the principal primary brain tumors, reviews the imaging modalities on which their diagnosis depends and the reasons magnetic resonance imaging is preferred among them, and identifies the methodological shortcomings of existing automated approaches that motivate the framework developed in the remainder of this paper.

Brain tumors represent a wide range of medical conditions that occur when brain cells or cells in nearby tissues grow uncontrollably. According to cancer data, approximately 1.6% of people worldwide with cancer develop tumors that hinder essential neurological functions. The Global Cancer Observatory GLOBOCAN reports that approximately 308,000 new cases of cancer are detected worldwide every year (Sung et al., 2021).

The classification of brain tumors plays a vital role in understanding their behavior and treatment procedures during the diagnosis process. Brain tumors are typically categorized as primary or secondary. Primary brain tumors originate within the brain, whereas secondary brain tumors are metastatic lesions that originate from cancers such as lung, breast carcinoma, or melanoma. This study mainly focused on primary brain tumors. Gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary adenomas are frequently occurring among the primary brain tumors. Gliomas (Hersh et al., 2023) are the most common cancerous (malignant) brain tumors and include astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, and ependymomas. Meningiomas (Soni et al., 2025) arise from the meninges and are typically non-cancerous (benign) but can become symptomatic due to their size or location. Pituitary adenomas (Daly and Beckers, 2020) develop in the pituitary gland as mostly benign tumors that cause endocrine disorders when they disrupt hormonal processes.

The use of medical imaging technology makes brain tumor diagnosis more accurate and effective. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for brain imaging because it offers an in-depth description of soft tissues and their boundaries. The human brain functions through two sophisticated methods: functional MRI (fMRI) and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), which show the function and structure of the brain. Computed tomography (CT) scans offer faster results than MRIs; however, they fail to provide the same amount of information regarding tumors, hemorrhages, and edema as MRIs. Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging technology is used in combination with other imaging technologies as a source of metabolic activity information, which is used to determine whether a tumor is malignant. The medical field faces a significant challenge in detecting brain tumors using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Different approaches have been used to classify tumors precisely for that purpose through the process of medical image processing and advanced deep learning (DL) techniques, according to some studies (Khan et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2025). Tumor classification is based on the first-, second-, and higher-order statistical measures of texture, which are important features of texture analysis. The classifier requires additional features, including transform-based, histogram-based local, and shape-based descriptors (Sowjanya et al., 2023). Machine learning (ML) algorithms use features with the help of support vector machines (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), and ensemble methods such as boosting and bagging. All these methods help discriminate gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary tumors in MRI images. All these methods require handcrafted features to function. The introduction of advanced deep learning through convolutional neural networks (CNNs) helps overcome the issues in the traditional method. These methods were used for feature extraction and classification. In the last few years, the main research area of scientists has moved towards DL-based approaches.

The development of deep learning systems has made significant advancements; however, essential operational aspects of medical applications remain unaddressed. Existing common CNN models instantiate standard convolution processes, which restrict their ability to detect meningioma tissue through their fixed field-of-view feature extraction methods and broader audiovisual elements. The system encounters it’s most significant challenge when different classes share similar visual traits, and their boundaries remain difficult to detect. Most MRI scans experience quality loss because of magnetic field variations and coil sensitivity artifacts, which create dimmer tumor borders. Only a small number of researchers have used an MRI-based contrast normalization technique that specifically addresses MRI noise types before feature extraction, which causes networks to learn about artifacts during their training process. Hybrid CNN classifier systems demonstrate potential for success; however, researchers have not explored the direct application of compact CNN models with non-parametric classifiers to handle multiple brain tumor classes.

The practical consequences of these shortcomings are most readily appreciated through a concrete diagnostic example. On contrast-enhanced T1-weighted slices, a convexity meningioma presents as a broad-based, dural-attached mass whose signal intensity may exceed that of the adjacent cortex by only a narrow margin, whereas a low-grade glioma presents as an intra-axial lesion whose margins fade gradually into the surrounding white matter. When such a slice is additionally affected by the smooth, low-frequency intensity gradient that arises from coil sensitivity or B1-field inhomogeneity, the already narrow contrast at the lesion boundary is compressed further, and the boundary on which the distinction between the two entities rests becomes visually unreliable. In contrast, a pituitary adenoma occupies a stereotyped sellar location and is therefore separable on positional grounds alone, which is why it is seldom the limiting class in reported results. Three specific deficiencies follow from the foregoing and together constitute the research gap that motivates this study. First, contrast normalization is ordinarily applied at a single spatial scale and is seldom validated quantitatively; therefore, the low-frequency intensity variation described above is not removed in a manner demonstrably faithful to the underlying anatomy. Second, the architectural mechanisms that would compensate for this at the level of the representation, namely receptive-field expansion, channel recalibration, and stable gradient propagation, are ordinarily adopted collectively based on the literature rather than justified individually for the task at hand, so that the contribution of each remains unquantified. Third, the decision layer that consumes the learned representation is adopted by convention rather than selection, leaving open the question of which decision rule is best matched to the convolutional embedding of brain-tumor MRI. Together, these three deficiencies create the requirement for a system that combines adaptive multiscale contrast improvement with deep feature extraction under channel-wise recalibration and a decision layer that is selected rather than assumed to be optimal. This requirement was met in the present study. These gaps are stated formally in Section 2.4, once the relevant literature has been surveyed, and the contributions that answer them are enumerated in Section 2.5.

Traditional and advanced DL models

Several studies have adapted established deep learning backbones to the brain tumor MRI classification problem, differing primarily in the mechanism used to enrich the representational capacity of the base convolutional network. Tang and Teoh (2023) used the ResNet-18 network to detect tumors using brain MRI images; the adoption of the residual architecture enables the use of a relatively shallow feature extractor; however, due to its shallow nature, it cannot capture the fine texture details necessary to differentiate between the glioma subtypes. Woźniak et al. (2023) adopted a correlation learning mechanism (CLM) in the DL model to extract inter-pixel dependencies beyond the traditional convolutional receptive fields; the idea enhances the hierarchical feature learning process, though, as the authors state, it needs a huge amount of data to avoid overfitting. Gürsoy and Kaya (2024) proposed a combination of a convolutional network and a graph neural network (GNN), where the graph captures the relational structure between the different regions of an image, representing the anatomical context, which is usually neglected by conventional convolutional networks. However, the use of this type of relational model leads to a high computational cost and quality of the graph. Zhou et al. (2024) studied an FL version of EfficientNet-B0 in order to respect patient data privacy, driven by the realistic problem of not being able to centralize the MRI data for training purposes; the communication overhead as well as the performance drop under non-independent and identically distributed client data is still an issue for this category of methods. By incorporating an SE attention mechanism inside the DenseNet-121 architecture, Mao et al. (2025) re-weighted the channel-wise importance of features for better feature reutilization among densely connected layers, highlighting the fact that the combination of channel attention and a very deep densely connected architecture is effective in enhancing discriminative feature learning, although at a relatively higher complexity cost compared to that incurred by a small architecture.

Dewage et al. (2024) proposed a customized, purpose-built CNN oriented toward the interpretability of brain-tumor predictions, prioritizing architectural transparency over the representational capacity offered by very deep or pre-trained networks; the resulting model, while easier to deploy and reason about, was tailored to a specific dataset, and its generalization to unseen acquisition protocols was not established. Latif et al. (2025) combined a collaborative federated learning model (CFLM) with gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) to provide institution-level privacy preservation alongside a visual explanation of the network’s decisions, coupling two increasingly important desiderata for clinical deployment, interpretability, and privacy, within a single framework, albeit one whose architectural complexity grows correspondingly. Yin and Wang (2025) paired a three-dimensional CNN (3D-CNN) with a RF decision layer to exploit volumetric spatial context unavailable to slice-based two-dimensional models, at the expense of substantially higher GPU memory and training-time requirements than a 2D formulation demands. Suryawanshi and Patil (2024) proposed a hybrid CNN-SVM pipeline conceptually closest to the classifier-head comparison undertaken in the present study, using a convolutional feature extractor followed by a support-vector decision boundary. However, their design evaluated only a single classifier head rather than systematically benchmarking multiple decision layers on an identical embedding, leaving the question of which decision layer is best matched to a given feature space open. Castelino et al. (2024) combined a VGG-16 feature extractor with an artificial neural network (ANN) classifier, illustrating that a well-established, heavyweight backbone continues to be a competitive baseline even without task-specific architectural adaptation, although its parameter count and consequent overfitting risk on modestly sized medical-imaging datasets remain a persistent concern for this class of transfer-learning approaches.

Collectively, these studies show that architectural augmentation via relational reasoning, channel attention, volumetric context, and decentralized learning can enhance a basic convolutional architecture; however, in doing so, the augmentation either increases model complexity, the amount of training data, and computational resources or uses only one classifier head at a time based on the same representation. This study bridges this gap by combining a small-sized, dilated, SE-enabled convolutional network with rigorous testing of three different classifier heads, including KNN, SVM, and RF, using the same representation for all of them.

Optimization-based DL models

The second line of research involves enhancing pretrained or lightweight backbones using meta-heuristic optimization of hyperparameters without changing their architecture. Kanchanamala et al. (2025) combined SqueezeNet with exponential walrus hunting optimization (EWHO) to tune the network hyperparameters, capitalizing on SqueezeNet’s naturally low parameter count, whereas the optimizer was responsible for balancing the relatively limited representation power of the backbone. In another study, Aluri and Imambi (2024) employed the Golden Teacher Learning Optimization (GTLO) technique in combination with the LeNet backbone, using a metaheuristic approach to improve the performance of an intentionally lightweight network architecture. Geetha et al. (2024) introduced a sine-cosine Archimedes optimization algorithm (SCAOA) using a SegNet backbone to optimize an architecture aimed at segmentation for classification tasks. Overall, in this study, an optimization technique was used to enhance the untuned baseline of the backbone. Nevertheless, the ceiling of performance potential is still subject to the representational power of the shallow network, which means that no amount of fine-tuning will ever replace the feature extractor’s capacity when the tumor classes are visually similar, such as the meningioma class and the normal tissue around it. This gives rise to an entirely different approach that focuses on putting architectural resources into the actual feature extractor using dilation, residuals, and SE attention mechanisms rather than focusing on the post-hoc tuning of the shallow architecture.

Attention-guided, segmentation-informed, and Hyperparameter-optimized hybrid frameworks

Beyond the transfer learning and metaheuristic optimization strategies reviewed above, a further body of recent work has pursued attention-guided architectural refinement, explicit preprocessing, segmentation-informed feature learning, and systematic hyperparameter search, and its design choices are often closely related to those adopted in the present study. Attention-based multi-scale architectures have also proliferated in adjacent medical imaging modalities beyond brain MRI. Maruthai et al. (2025) proposed a multi-axis transformer-based U-Net with a class-balanced ensemble for multi-label chest radiograph classification, integrating a hierarchical transformer into both the encoder and decoder of a U-shaped network to mitigate the localization bias of conventional CNNs. Although this type of transformer-based attention is effective at modelling long-range spatial dependencies, its self-attention computation scales quadratically with the number of image patches, which is one of the considerations that motivated the use of the substantially lighter squeeze-and-excitation (SE) channel-attention mechanism as a more computationally frugal alternative suited to a 256 × 256 MRI slice.

Within brain-tumor MRI classification, Jemina and Thanarajan (2025) proposed a hybrid lightweight deep-learning framework that first applies a Gaussian bilateral network filter to improve image quality and subsequently classifies the filtered images using a lightweight hybrid twin-attentive pyramid convolutional network, which combines a twin-level attention module and a hybrid pyramid convolution block on a ResNet backbone. This design shares with the present study the principle of coupling an explicit preprocessing stage with attention-guided feature extraction, although it relies on a heavier ResNet backbone and a dual attention pathway rather than the single, computationally frugal SE mechanism used in this study. Bhuvaneshwari et al. (2025) examined the identification of brain tumors from computed tomography, rather than magnetic resonance imaging, using several deep convolutional architectures, illustrating that the underlying representation-learning principles generalize across imaging modalities even as MRI remains the preferred modality for soft-tissue contrast in most clinical workflows, including the present study. Rajendran et al. (2023) addressed the closely related task of brain-tumor segmentation with an attention and residual-block-augmented U-Net, combining Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix texture features with deep features to obtain high segmentation accuracy across tumor sub-regions. Jemina et al. (2025) investigated an extended, optimization-tuned multilayer perceptron for brain-tumor detection by applying a metaheuristic search to a classical feedforward classifier rather than a convolutional feature extractor.

Reddy et al. (2024) proposed BrainCDNet, a concatenated deep neural network that sharpens input MRI slices with a nimble filter prior to feature extraction and mitigates overfitting through He-normal weight initialization, batch normalization, and global average pooling, and reported separate binary and multiclass evaluation protocols. The shared use of He-normal initialization and batch normalization in both BrainCDNet and the present backbone reflects a converging consensus on effective regularization practice for compact brain-tumor CNNs, even though the two studies differ in their preprocessing philosophy, contrast-domain amplification here as against edge sharpening there, and in their treatment of multi-scale information. Kamireddy et al. (2025) introduced DBTNet, a dual-stream neural network motivated by the observation that manual segmentation and handcrafted feature engineering struggle to accommodate the variability and complexity of tumor appearance, processing complementary representations of the input MRI slice through two parallel convolutional streams; this dual-stream philosophy is conceptually related to, but structurally distinct from, the multi-scale MICA amplification used here, which fuses fine-, medium-, and coarse-scale contrast information within a single input stream rather than through parallel network branches. Velpula et al. (2025) proposed a hybrid framework that integrates deep-learning feature extraction with classical machine-learning classification and systematic hyperparameter tuning, explicitly motivated by the invasiveness and inter-observer variability of histopathological confirmation.

A parallel line of enquiry has evaluated deep architectures on non-ionizing imaging modalities that are being developed as low-cost alternatives to MRI, and its findings bear directly on the design adopted here. Hossain et al. (2022) embedded a YOLOv5 object-detection model in a portable microwave head-imaging system, localizing tumors with bounding boxes and separating benign from malignant lesions in reconstructed microwave images, and reported that the largest detector variant surpassed several established detection baselines. The same group subsequently proposed a cascaded pair of deliberately lightweight networks, MicrowaveSegNet for tumor delineation and BrainImageNet for three-class image classification, and showed that classification accuracy rose substantially once the classifier operated on segmented rather than raw reconstructed images (Hossain et al., 2023). Taha et al. (2023) likewise trained a purpose-built convolutional network, BrainVisionNet, to categorize brain tumors from microwave images. These studies reinforce two premises of the present work: that a compact, purpose-designed backbone can rival substantially deeper transfer-learned networks on a modestly sized medical-imaging corpus, and that the quality of the representation presented to the decision layer, whether improved by segmentation as in those studies or by multi-scale contrast amplification as here, materially governs classification performance. They differ from the present study in modality, however, since reconstructed microwave images carry markedly lower spatial resolution than the contrast-enhanced T1-weighted slices considered here, and none of them treats the decision layer itself as an experimental variable. Closer to the present setting, Prakash et al. (2026) cascaded a U-Net delineation stage with a ReXNet-150 classifier for MRI brain-tumor analysis and validated the resulting pipeline using patient-wise cross-validation with confidence intervals, an evaluation practice consistent with the statistical validation adopted in Section 4.6; their design nonetheless retains a heavyweight pretrained classification backbone and an explicit segmentation stage, whereas the framework proposed here dispenses with segmentation supervision and instead recovers tumor-relevant structure through contrast-domain amplification ahead of a compact feature extractor. An overview of these state-of-the-art techniques, along with their advantages and disadvantages, is presented in Table 1.

Collectively, the studies reviewed in this subsection confirm that explicit preprocessing, attention mechanisms, and hybrid deep-classical pipelines are each independently beneficial, echoing several of the design choices adopted in the present framework; however, none of them combine all three within a single, systematically ablated, and statistically validated pipeline. This observation directly informs the research gaps identified in Section 2.4, and the significant contributions are summarized in Section 2.5.

Research gaps

By analyzing the approaches reviewed in Sections 2.1–2.3, we made the following observations.

Most authors (Tang and Teoh, 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Mao et al., 2025; Castelino et al., 2024; Kanchanamala et al., 2025; Geetha et al., 2024) utilized pre-trained DL models such as ResNet, DenseNet, EfficientNet, SqueezeNet, VGG 16, and LeNet with transfer learning. However, these methods have high computational requirements, and their effectiveness in medical imaging can be limited by domain adaptation challenges, potentially affecting generalization if they are not fine-tuned properly.

To address these gaps, we propose a compact framework that combines multi-scale contrast amplification, dilated depth-wise separable convolution, squeeze-and-excitation (SE) attention, residual connections, and a systematically benchmarked classifier head. The contribution of each component is evaluated through controlled ablation experiments, while the alternative decision layers are compared on the same learned embedding using identical data partitions and statistical testing. This design directly links the identified research gaps to experimentally verifiable contributions.

Significant contributions and novelty of the proposed framework

Because the individual mechanisms employed in the proposed backbone are well established in the deep learning literature, it is necessary to state at the outset where the novelty of this work resides and where it does not. No claim of originality is advanced for the squeeze-and-excitation recalibration, residual skip connections, or depth-wise separable convolution taken individually, as each has been described previously and is employed here in its standard form. The contribution lies in the particular composition of these mechanisms around a multi-scale contrast-amplification front end, in the empirical demonstration that every one of them is necessary for the present task, and in the systematic treatment of the decision layer that operates on the resulting representation. Based on this, the significant contributions of the proposed methodology are as follows.

We introduced a multi-scale image contrast amplification (MICA) algorithm that enhances brain MRI contrast at three complementary Gaussian scales whose cutoff frequencies are matched to the tumor-margin, lesion-diameter, and bias-field bands measured on the training corpus (Section 3.2.1). Its necessity is established rather than assumed: removing MICA entirely costs 3.06 accuracy points, and no single scale reproduces that contribution (Section 4.8).

The closest antecedents each realize only part of the configuration: Mao et al. Proposed methodology

The proposed model includes preprocessing of MR images, feature extraction, and classification. However, feature extraction is critical for enhancing the classification performance. Hence, we used a customized CNN architecture to extract significant texture details from brain MR images. Contrast enhancement

During the acquisition of MRI images, various artifacts are usually observed owing to coil resistance loss. Therefore, preprocessing is pivotal for enhancing the brain tumor detection process. To meet these criteria, in this study, we proposed MICA (Vuylsteke and Schoeters, 1994) to improve the contrast of brain MRI images by amplifying the contrast at different scales. This technique particularly improves the visibility of both fine and coarse features of brain MRI scans, which may be crucial for accurate diagnosis. Empirical validation of MICA parameters

This subsection places the two free parameters of the MICA stage, namely the set of Gaussian scales and the amplification factor α, on a quantitative footing, complementing the anatomical rationale given above with a frequency-domain analysis and image fidelity measurements.

The three Gaussian scales used in MICA were not chosen arbitrarily; each corresponds to an approximate spatial-frequency cutoff of the Gaussian low-pass filter, given by fc=12πσ. For σ = 15, 30, and 60 pixels, the cutoff frequencies were approximately 0.0106, 0.0053, and 0.0027 cycles/pixel, respectively, spanning the high-, mid-, and low-frequency bands relevant to a 256 × 256 MRI slice. This range was selected to bracket three anatomically motivated spatial scales measured on a representative subset of the training set: (i) the mean tumor margin width and internal texture period, which fell in the 8–20-pixel range across glioma, meningioma, and pituitary cases and motivated σ = 15; (ii) the mean lesion diameter, which fell in the 25–40-pixel range and motivated σ = 30; and (iii) the characteristic wavelength of coil-sensitivity and B1-field intensity gradients, empirically estimated at 50–70 pixels from the low-frequency component of the bias field, motivating σ = 60. This scale selection follows the same multi-resolution rationale used in the original MUSICA algorithm (Vuylsteke and Schoeters, 1994), which is extended here with dataset-specific measurements rather than generic defaults.

To verify that all three scales contribute meaningfully to enhancement quality, we conducted an ablation study over every non-empty subset of {σ = 15, σ = 30, σ = 60}, with α fixed at 1.5. For each configuration, we report the image-fidelity metrics mean square error (MSE), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), and structural similarity index measurement (SSIM), computed between the MICA-enhanced output and the corresponding raw input over the same hold-out MRI set (Figure 1).

As shown in Table 3, the different scale configurations produce distinct MSE, PSNR, and SSIM profiles. The results indicate that each Gaussian scale affects the enhancement differently, with σ = 15 providing stronger preservation of fine structural information and σ = 60 producing greater changes in low-frequency intensity patterns. The three-scale configuration provides a balanced representation across fine-, intermediate-, and coarse-scale information rather than optimizing any single image-fidelity metric in isolation.

Feature extraction

The feature extraction process is fully in line with the construction of reliable systems for tumor classification, as convolutional neural networks can perform this operation owing to their ability to build feature representations based on initial pixel data rather than ready-made feature descriptions. A shallow CNN learns the patterns of filters that detect elementary image features such as oriented edges, local intensity gradients, and repetitive textural patterns. These basic responses become more abstract representations through layer activations, creating complex regional structures, internal tumor organization, and overall shape patterns. The bottom-up composition of the model enables the construction of a combined network that works with both the complex textural patterns of glioma borders and the simple texture patterns of meningiomas without class-specific modifications. We designed our own CNN backbone that works in the medical imaging framework by working with small datasets yet delivering outstanding computational performance owing to its three system components: depth-separable convolutions, channel recalibrations, and residual connections. Summary of model architecture with layer-wise annotations.

The network contains a 3 × 3 standard convolutional layer that uses ‘He initialization’ to set its weights because this method establishes the initial weight distribution variance according to the input unit count, which makes it appropriate for networks that implement ReLU-family activation functions throughout their structure. The entry-point layer leads to feature representations that undergo continuous improvement through a series of composite blocks, which we refer to as improved blocks. Improved blocks implement depth-wise separable convolution through two steps: first, a single filter per input channel is used, and then a 1 × 1 pointwise convolution is applied for channel information mixing. The depth-wise stage uses a 3 × 3 kernel with a dilation rate of 2, which spans a 5 × 5 effective receptive field at the parameter cost of a 3 × 3 kernel, whereas the point-wise mixing convolution and residual projection convolution both use 1 × 1 kernels. This configuration is identical at all four encoding stages, and the 3 × 3 entry convolution is the only undilated spatial convolution in the backbone. The output passes through batch normalization before reaching the ReLU nonlinearity, which leads to the squeeze-and-excitation module. The SE module starts by using global average pooling to transform the spatial dimensions of each feature map into a scalar value, resulting in a channel descriptor vector that represents the average activation levels for every filter. Two fully connected layers form the network, with the first layer reducing the descriptor to one-sixteenth of its original length, subject to a floor of four units so that the two shallowest stages both reduce to four, and the second layer restores it to its complete form. The system uses a nonlinear mapping function to create per-channel scaling weights, which are output through a sigmoid gate from the summary. The network uses scalar weights to enhance the important channels that contain diagnostic information while reducing the strength of the channels that show background tissue or acquisition noise. The enhanced block extends the sequence because it includes a residual branch that uses a 1 × 1 projection convolution to reduce the input channels while creating a shortcut that maintains the gradient flow to the initial layers and prevents degradation of the representation through depth progression. The backbone system includes four encoding stages, which double their feature channel count after each stage because they start with 32 filters and progress to 64, 128, and finally 256 filters. The network achieves better channel depth through this method because it enables the system to use its available processing power for the most critical needs of its tasks, whereas higher-level semantic patterns require advanced filter banks that include multiple filters beyond the basic edge detectors used in the initial phase. The first stage of each process ends when a 2 × 2 max-pooling operation decreases the spatial resolution by half, which results in two benefits: it decreases the upcoming layer operations while providing translation invariance that protects the representation against small changes in the tumor tissue location within the imaging field. The global max-pooling layer transforms the three-dimensional feature tensor into a 256-dimensional vector by selecting the highest activation value from each channel at every spatial location. Max pooling extracts the most powerful proof for every pattern throughout the entire image, which benefits tumor detection because the important diagnostic information in MRI slices occupies only a brief section of the image. The system uses a dropout layer to control the neuron connections during training, which leads to a four-unit softmax output layer that converts the descriptor vector into class posterior probabilities for four diagnostic categories: normal, glioma, meningioma, and pituitary. Taken together, this architecture transforms raw contrast-enhanced MRI slices into a compact, discriminative feature embedding that is subsequently passed to the KNN classifier described in Section 3.4, avoiding dependence on predefined or hand-engineered image descriptors (Figure 8).

For clarity, the architectural design choices summarized qualitatively above are consolidated into four concrete specifications. First, activation placement is applied consistently throughout the backbone: every convolutional operation, standard, depth-wise, or point-wise, is followed by batch normalization and then a ReLU nonlinearity, in that fixed order, across all four encoding stages; the only deviations from this pattern are the sigmoid gating function at the output of the SE excitation branch, which must produce a bounded [0, 1] channel-scaling weight rather than an unbounded activation, and the softmax (or classifier-head-specific) function at the network output. Second, parameter optimization follows a single fixed strategy rather than a searched one, selected empirically for training stability on the 256 × 256 × 3 input rather than by a grid or random search. The settings are specified in Section 4.2 and Table 6, along with the corresponding search spaces used for the three classical classifier heads. Third, regularization is provided entirely by architectural and scheduling choices rather than by explicit weight penalties: He-normal initialization throughout, batch normalization after every convolution, and a single dropout layer (rate 0.4) immediately before the classification head. L2 weight decay and image-level data augmentation were deliberately not used in this study, a design choice discussed further in Section 4.2 and noted as a direction for future work in Section 4.14. Fourth, the computational complexity of the resulting backbone, together with its CPU and GPU inference latency and throughput, is profiled in Section 4.5 and Table 7.

Classification

To identify the most effective decision layer for the 256-dimensional global-max-pooled embedding produced by the proposed backbone (Section 3.3), we evaluated the native softmax output of the network alongside three classical classifiers trained directly on the extracted features: KNN (Peterson, 2009), SVM (Cortés and Vapnik, 1995), and RF (Breiman, 2001). Decoupling representation learning, performed once by the shared CNN backbone, from decision boundary learning, performed independently by each classifier head, allows the relative merit of each classification strategy to be assessed without confounding differences in the underlying feature space.

The KNN classifier involves voting for the class label among the k nearest training examples based on a distance metric. The value of neighbors, k, was obtained from a cross-validated grid search using k ∈ {3, 5, 7, 9, 11}, which yielded an optimal k value of 5. The chosen dissimilarity metric is Euclidean (L2), because the 256-dimensional GlobalMaxPooling features have a well-clustered distribution such that the L2 metric reliably measures the semantic similarity of the tumor representation. Cosine similarity, which disregards magnitude information, and Manhattan distance, which is less affected by feature dimension correlation, performed slightly worse in the initial trials for the same validation split than the other metrics. Because KNN is a non-parametric model, it makes no assumptions regarding the distribution of the feature space, which works well in the case of highly variable tumor morphologies, as in this study.

The SVM classifier constructs a maximum-margin decision boundary by mapping the CNN embeddings into a higher-dimensional space via a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel and solving for the hyperplane that maximizes the separation between classes under a one-vs-rest multiclass scheme. Because SVM optimization is sensitive to feature scale, embeddings were standardized to zero mean and unit variance prior to fitting. The regularization parameter C and kernel coefficient γ were tuned jointly via a grid search. The RBF kernel was preferred over the linear kernel because the class boundaries observed in the embedding space, particularly between meningioma and normal tissue representations, were not linearly separable.

The RF classifier is an ensemble of decorrelated decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap-sampled subset of the training embeddings with a random subset of features considered at every split. Predictions are aggregated by majority vote across trees, which reduces the variance relative to a single decision tree and provides an implicit measure of feature importance. Unlike KNN and SVM, RF partitions the feature space using axis-aligned splits, which is a structurally different inductive bias whose suitability for this embedding is evaluated empirically alongside the other classifiers below.

These three classifiers, along with the softmax output layer of the CNN, were trained and validated using the same training-validation-testing procedure, ensuring that any difference in performance results mentioned below was due to the classifier only, and not because of any other reason, such as differences in data splitting or feature extraction. The performance of the classifier was analyzed using metrics such as Recall, Specificity, Precision, F1-Score, Accuracy, AUC, and Average Precision (AP) (Omeiza et al., 2019).

Results and discussion

This section offers a systematic, multi-layer evaluation of the suggested CNN backbone architecture combined with four other decision-making layers: CNN backbone with its default softmax head (CNN) and KNN, SVM, and RF classifiers, as discussed in Section 3.4. Two complementary resampling procedures were used to estimate performance: the hold-out protocol and stratified 5-fold cross-validation, both applied to all four configurations so that the two estimates could be compared directly against one another. This section also provides information about the complexity of the CNN backbone, the significance of the differences in the observed performance, ablation studies, feature visualization and interpretability analysis, and state-of-the-art comparison and implications from clinical and algorithmic perspectives.

Evaluation criteria

This subsection defines the quantitative measures used throughout the evaluation and specifies the two validation protocols under which those measures were computed, together with the reasoning for the adoption of each.

All models were assessed using seven complementary metrics— recall (sensitivity), Specificity, Precision, F1-score, Accuracy, AUC, and AP—computed per class and macro-averaged across the four diagnostic categories (normal, glioma, meningioma, pituitary) to avoid class-size bias, since the dataset, while reasonably balanced, is not perfectly uniform across classes. Two complementary validation regimes were adopted in this study. The hold-out protocol reserved 20% of the dataset (1,405 images) as an untouched, class-stratified test set and was used to report detailed class-wise diagnostic behavior, confusion matrices, and threshold-dependent curves (ROC, PR). The 5-fold cross-validation (5-FCV) protocol partitions the full dataset into five stratified folds, trains on four folds, validates on the held-out fold in rotation, and reports the mean performance across folds. Because every image is used exactly once for validation, 5-FCV yields a less optimistic and more broadly applicable estimate of generalization than a single hold-out split at the cost of five-fold higher training expense. In this study, the 5-FCV protocol was applied to all four classifier configurations rather than to the strongest one alone, so that the ranking established on the hold-out partition could be verified under resampling rather than being assumed to persist. The agreement between the two protocols is examined in Section 4.7. In addition, the statistical reliability of the accuracy improvement contributed by each classifier head was assessed using McNemar’s test and paired bootstrap resampling, rather than relying on point estimates alone.

Training configurations

This subsection specifies the computing environment, optimization settings, and hyperparameter search procedures used to train the CNN backbone and the three classical decision layers, and explains the reasoning behind each configuration choice rather than merely recording it.

The suggested CNN backbone architecture was trained in Google Colab on an NVIDIA A100 GPU, with TensorFlow 2.x and the Keras higher-level API through the Adamax optimizer, mini-batch of 16, and 100 epochs, employing the ReduceLROnPlateau and EarlyStopping methods to perform learning rate annealing and to terminate training when hitting a validation loss plateau, respectively. Initialization with He-normal, Batch Normalization, and dropout regularization before classification served as the primary sources of regularization. The three classifiers were trained on the pretrained 256-dimensional features obtained from this backbone model using only the training partition to perform hyperparameter optimization and to avoid test set leakage. Table 6 shows the final hyperparameter search spaces and selected configurations of the four models used in this study.

The configurations listed in Table 6 reflect specific and motivated design choices rather than arbitrary defaults. The Adamax optimizer was preferred over Adam and plain stochastic gradient descent because its per-parameter learning-rate normalization, based on the infinity norm of past gradients, was empirically more stable than Adam across preliminary trials on the MICA-enhanced input distribution, whose dynamic range differed from that of raw natural images. A mini-batch size of 16 was selected as a compromise between the gradient-estimate stability and the GPU memory required to hold 256 × 256 × 3 activations across the backbone’s four encoding stages. A batch size of 16 also matches the batch size used for the CPU and GPU inference throughput measurements reported in Table 7, maintaining the training and deployment configurations directly comparable. The dropout rate of 0.4, applied only immediately before the classification head, was chosen to be aggressive enough to discourage co-adaptation of the 256-dimensional pooled embedding without materially slowing convergence, consistent with its placement after all convolutional regularization (batch normalization) had already been applied. For the classical classifiers, the grid-search ranges in Table 6 were bounded by standard practice for embeddings of this dimensionality: k ∈ {3, 5, 7, 9, 11} for KNN brackets the point at which the bias-variance trade-off typically stabilizes for a few-hundred-dimensional feature space; C ∈ {0.1, 1, 10, 100} and γ ∈ {‘scale’, 0.01, 0.001} for SVM span three orders of magnitude of regularization strength and kernel width; and the RF ranges were selected to keep the ensemble large enough to reduce variance (up to 500 trees) while bounding tree depth to limit overfitting on a training partition of 5,618 images.

5-fold cross-validation

After characterizing the four configurations on a single stratified partition, this subsection establishes whether the characterization survives repeated resampling of the training and evaluation data. The analysis deliberately mirrors the four-stage structure adopted in Section 4.3: a quantitative analysis of fold-wise and aggregated metrics together with the corresponding confusion matrices, the accuracy and loss trajectories of the CNN backbone, a threshold-independent assessment based on receiver operating characteristic curves, and a precision–recall analysis—so that the two validation protocols may be placed directly alongside one another in Section 4.7.

The complete corpus of 7,023 images was partitioned into five class-stratified folds under a fixed random seed, producing training partitions of 5,618–5,619 images and evaluation partitions of 1,404–1,405 images, with the class proportions of the parent distribution preserved in each fold (approximately 324 glioma, 329 meningioma, 400 normal, and 351 pituitary images per evaluation fold). For each fold, a CNN backbone was instantiated afresh and trained from random initialization, after which the three classical decision layers were re-fitted, including an independent hyperparameter search restricted to that fold’s training partition, on the frozen 256-dimensional embeddings extracted from the corresponding backbone. Because every image is used for evaluation exactly once and no image ever contributes to both the training and evaluation of the same fold, the dispersion reported below reflects the variability in the learned representation and fitted decision boundary. Therefore, it constitutes a considerably stricter test of stability than the repeated refitting of a single fixed embedding.

This dissociation replicates the same phenomenon observed in the hold-out results under a substantially more demanding evaluation regime, and its algorithmic origin is transparent. The pseudo-probabilities emitted by a KNN head are the fractions of retrieved neighbors belonging to each class and are therefore quantized to a small number of discrete levels. With the grid search selecting k = 3 in four of the five folds, only four distinct scores were attainable per class. This resolution is sufficient to assign the correct hard label but is far too coarse to order cases finely across the continuum of decision thresholds that an AUC integrates over. In contrast, the margin-based SVM produces a continuous decision function whose magnitude varies smoothly with the distance of an embedding from the separating hyperplane, yielding a strictly better ranking. The practical implication is that the choice of decision layer should follow the intended clinical use rather than a single headline metric: a distance-based head where a categorical diagnosis is required, and a margin-based head wherever the output will be thresholded, calibrated, or used to prioritize a radiological reading queue. Fold-wise training and validation accuracy curves of the CNN backbone under 5-fold cross-validation.

The optimization trends for the five folds followed an extremely similar pattern. For each of the folds, the trend started close to the 25% probability of a balanced four-class problem, rapidly ascended over the initial 20 epochs, and then became very slow from approximately epoch 40 onwards until it converged at a final validation accuracy level of 93 to 95% with a validation loss of less than 0.20. This consistent spread between the five optimization curves is the key point of this subsection: the variance found in the final performance figures in Table 9 results from the random assignment of studies to different evaluation folds, not from instability during optimization.

There are two aspects of these curves that are worth mentioning. First, the validation curve is always above the training curve for the early epochs across all five folds, and both intersect only at approximately epoch 50. This reversal of roles is a clear indicator of the presence of regularization; indeed, the dropout function and batch normalization statistics are active during the training stage but inactive during inference. Therefore, the training performance of the network was assessed under an artificially hampered stochastic setting, whereas the validation performance was assessed under a full deterministic setting. The gradual closure of the gap indicates that the network progressively reduced its dependence on the co-adaptation that dropout was designed to suppress. Second, and even more significantly for the validity of the reported results, no fold displays a discrepancy between the training and validation losses that is indicative of memorization, with the two loss functions remaining close until epoch 100, with the residual gap never exceeding a fraction of a nat. Because no image augmentation was performed and no weight decay was used explicitly, the only possible explanation for this phenomenon is the inherent architectural regularization from batch normalization, pre-classification dropout, and the small number of parameters of the depth-wise separable backbone architecture. In the absence of the third validation set, the evaluation set was also used for the purpose of learning rate annealing and early stopping; therefore, it is better to understand these loss functions as an indication of convergence. The consequences of this setup for the reported estimates are discussed in Section 4.7 of this paper.

The PR curves largely reproduce the AUC ranking of the classification results for a collection in which the imbalance of classes is only moderate but do so more discriminatively where it matters. Again, CNN-SVM scored the best, achieving an average precision of 97–98% for meningioma and 100% for the normal class in each fold. Normal and pituitary cases have precision that holds constant close to unity up to recall reaching approximately 90%, indicating that these classes may be operated at high sensitivity without paying a price in terms of additional false positives. In contrast, meningioma cases started to show a decline in precision below approximately 75% recall and then dropped dramatically above a 95% recall. This illustrates precisely the price of moving the operating point, as discussed at the end of the previous subsection: increasing the sensitivity of the meningioma class to the high nineties implies a sacrifice of precision, and the cases gained by this trade are mainly gliomas.

A further structural detail visible in the CNN-KNN panels is the near-vertical collapse of precision at maximum recall, which is an artifact of the discrete neighbor-vote score: once the threshold falls below the smallest attainable vote fraction, every remaining case is admitted simultaneously, and precision drops abruptly rather than degrading gracefully. The smooth decay of the CNN-SVM curves over the same interval is a practical manifestation of this distinction. Taken with the ROC evidence, the cross-validated PR analysis therefore converges on the conclusion already reached from the hold-out data, but now supported by five independent partitions: CNN-KNN is the preferred head when a single categorical diagnosis is required, whereas CNN-SVM is the preferred head wherever the prediction score itself has clinical meaning.

Computational complexity

This subsection profiles the computational demands of the shared CNN backbone—its parameter count, arithmetic cost, weight and activation memory, and inference latency on both CPU and GPU hardware—and considers what those figures imply for deployment in resource-constrained clinical environments.

With only 107,076 parameters—occupying a mere 0.20 MB in half precision—the proposed backbone is one to three orders of magnitude smaller than commonly used medical-imaging backbones of comparable depth (VGG16 ≈ 138 M parameters, ResNet-50 ≈ 25.6 M, MobileNetV2 ≈ 3.4 M), which is a direct consequence of the depth-wise separable convolutions used throughout every encoding stage. Despite this compact parameter budget, the arithmetic cost per image (≈0.88 GFLOPs) remains within the range typically associated with lightweight mobile-oriented architectures. Table 7 profiles the inference on both classes of hardware because the two correspond to different deployment scenarios. On CPU alone, the model sustains 23.7 images per second at a batch size of 16, with a median per-image latency of 42.2 ms, which is adequate for near-real-time, slice-by-slice screening at the point of care rather than for large-scale batch reprocessing; the per-image latency at this batch size exceeds that of smaller batches because CPU execution does not realize the parallelism-dependent efficiencies of batched inference. On an NVIDIA A100 GPU, the same network completes an image in 4.54 ms at the identical batch size, corresponding to 220.1 images per second and a 9.3-fold acceleration over the CPU configuration. The manner in which GPU efficiency depends on batch size is instructive: single-image inference is dominated by kernel-launch and host-to-device transfer overhead and therefore realizes only 16.5 images per second, whereas throughput rises to 117.9 images per second at a batch of 8, to 220.1 at 16, and saturates near 295.4 at 32, beyond which no further gain is observed. Batched execution is thus essential if the arithmetic frugality of the architecture is to be converted into wall-clock benefit, and a batch of 16 to 32 represents the point at which the latency and memory occupancy are jointly optimized. Expressed in workflow terms, a complete 25-slice MRI volume is classified in approximately 1.1 s on the CPU and 0.11 s on the GPU; thus, neither configuration imposes a clinically relevant delay. The CPU path establishes that the framework is deployable without dedicated accelerator hardware, whereas the GPU path additionally renders the whole-cohort retrospective analysis tractable. The gap between the largest single activation tensor (128.0 MB) and the actual peak resident memory of the process (≈1.36 GB) indicates that at moderate batch sizes, the practical deployment footprint of the model is governed substantially more by activation storage and framework-level overhead than by the intrinsically small parameters and FLOP budget of the network. The peak GPU memory of 4.93 GB recorded at the same batch size is likewise attributable to the workspace reservations and caching-allocator behavior of the accelerated runtime rather than to the model itself, and remains comfortably within the capacity of any contemporary clinical-grade accelerator. This distinction is relevant for selecting appropriate batch sizes for constrained clinical or edge deployments. This backbone cost is shared by all four classifier heads; however, the incremental inference cost of the decision layer differs qualitatively. KNN requires distance computations against the stored training embeddings, so its inference cost increases with the number of stored training samples. In contrast, SVM prediction depends primarily on the number of support vectors, while RF prediction depends on the number and depth of its trees. Therefore, KNN is particularly attractive for the present compact embedding and dataset size, whereas SVM or RF may become more computationally favorable as the number of stored training samples increases substantially in future deployments Table 11.

Statistical significance

This subsection establishes whether the accuracy differences reported above are large enough to be distinguished from sampling noise using a paired per-sample hypothesis test and a bootstrap confidence interval for the strongest hybrid configuration.

Accuracy points estimate alone do not indicate whether a CNN hybrid classifier is reliably better than the CNN-only baseline or whether the observed gap could plausibly be attributed to chance. Therefore, two complementary tests were applied to the hold-out test set. McNemar’s test, a paired test appropriate for two classifiers evaluated on the same samples, was applied to CNN versus each of CNN-KNN, CNN-SVM, and CNN-RF, comparing how often each pair of models is uniquely correct or incorrect on a per-sample basis. A paired bootstrap confidence interval was then constructed for the accuracy improvement of the strongest classifier, CNN-KNN, over the CNN baseline by resampling the hold-out set with replacement 2,000 times.

McNemar’s test confirms that both CNN-KNN and CNN-SVM correct significantly more CNN errors than they introduce (p < 0.001 in both cases), whereas the CNN-RF difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.461): CNN-RF flips almost as many previously correct predictions to incorrect (26) as it fixes (20), so its small apparent accuracy change is not distinguishable from chance at this sample size, corroborating the pattern already observed in the hold-out and ranking-based results. A complementary bootstrap analysis for the strongest classifier, CNN-KNN, reinforces this picture: resampling the hold-out set 2,000 times yields an observed accuracy improvement of 2.92 pp. over CNN, with a 95% confidence interval of [1.71, 4.13] pp. that excludes zero (Figure 29), confirming that CNN-KNN’s advantage is unlikely to be attributable to sampling noise in this particular hold-out split.

Bootstrap distribution of the hold-out accuracy difference between CNN-KNN and CNN (2,000 resamples).

Because a point estimate conveys no information about its own precision, two-sided 95% confidence intervals were additionally computed for the hold-out accuracy of every configuration using the Wilson score method, which is preferable to the normal approximation at proportions close to unity. On the 1,405-image hold-out partition, these intervals are 93.67% [92.27, 94.82] for the CNN softmax head, 96.58% [95.50, 97.41] for CNN-KNN, 96.23% [95.10, 97.10] for CNN-SVM, and 93.24% [91.80, 94.44] for CNN-RF. Comparison of the hold-out and 5-fold cross-validation protocols

Two independent calculations for the same quantity were provided in the previous subsections using different resampling procedures. This subsection provides a comparison of both results to address a question that cannot be addressed independently using either of the procedures: whether the results obtained in Section 4.3 from the 80:20 split are due to the properties of the framework or the result of a particular advantageous split of the dataset. The comparison was performed numerically using the aggregated data in Table 12 and visually using the plots in Figure 30.

Comparison of the hold-out and 5-fold cross-validation protocols: (a) Hold-out accuracy against the cross-validated mean with standard-deviation error bars; (b) Fold-wise dispersion with the hold-out estimate overlaid.

The two methods yielded almost identical results. The greatest difference between the hold-out accuracy score and the respective fold mean was 0.75 percentage points, while each difference fell well within the range of one standard deviation from the respective fold distribution, and the hold-out score resided in the same range as that of the folds in all four configurations. Because both the hold-out split and the cross-validation splits were selected independently of each other, this similarity is not a matter of design but rather serves as a clear demonstration of the general nature of the observed performances.

A graphical depiction of the connection between the hold-out split and fold means is shown in panel (b) of Figure 30. In each of the four configurations, the point of the hold-out diamond lies within the range of the five-fold points and within half a standard deviation from the fold mean in three of the four cases. Moreover, this figure highlights a consistent and significant asymmetry in the direction of the discrepancy. The hold-out split shows a slight tendency to be overly optimistic when it comes to both hybrid heads (−0.48 pp. for CNN-KNN and −0.56 pp. for CNN-SVM) and is slightly pessimistic for the other two configurations (+0.28 pp. for the softmax head and +0.75 pp. for CNN-RF). The result was an increase in the substitution gain owing to the single-split assessment. Thus, the difference between the CNN-KNN method and the softmax head is 2.91 pp. using the hold-out approach and 2.15 points in cross-validation, and the corresponding CNN-SVM advantage decreases from 2.56 to 1.72 points. The RF comparison underwent an outright sign reversal, from −0.43 points on the hold-out set to +0.04 points under cross-validation, which is precisely the behavior expected of a difference that is genuinely null and reinforces the non-significant McNemar result of Section 4.6 from an entirely independent perspective.

Two further points of agreement deserve emphasis because they concern the qualitative conclusions of this study rather than its headline numbers. First, the ranking of the four configurations is identical under both protocols, with CNN-KNN first, CNN-SVM second, and CNN-RF and the softmax head statistically inseparable. Second, the dissociation between hard-label accuracy and threshold-independent ranking quality—CNN-KNN leading on accuracy while CNN-SVM leads on AUC—was reproduced without attenuation, with CNN-SVM recording a macro-AUC of 99.56% on the hold-out set and 99.59 ± 0.11% across folds. The recommendation that the decision layer be selected according to whether the downstream task consumes a label or score, therefore, rests on two independent evaluations rather than one.

Two limitations of this comparison should be stated. The cross-validated estimates carry a mildly optimistic bias because, in the absence of a third partition, each fold’s evaluation split also supplied the monitoring signal for learning-rate annealing and early stopping. The bias is small since only the stopping epoch rather than any model parameter was selected on that signal, and it was applied identically to all four heads; therefore, the between-configuration comparisons that carry the study’s conclusions are unaffected. Conversely, the cross-validated estimates are also conservative in one respect, since each backbone is trained on 80% of the corpus rather than the whole of it, and the learning curves in Section 4.4.2 suggest that the architecture had not yet saturated its data budget. The two effects act in opposite directions, and the close agreement documented in Table 12 indicates that neither dominates corpus size. Where a single figure must be quoted, the cross-validated mean is the more defensible estimate of expected performance on unseen data, whereas the holdout results retain their value as the setting in which the per-sample paired statistical tests of Section 4.6 and the component ablations of Section 4.8 were conducted on a common, untouched partition.

Feature interpretability

Interpretability is examined here at two complementary levels: the geometry of the feature space upon which every decision layer operates and the spatial evidence within the image from which the feature space is derived.

Feature-space visualization

The comparison of decision layers undertaken in Sections 4.3 and 4.4 rests on the premise that the 256-dimensional embedding produced by the global max-pooling layer is already organized by diagnostic category, so that the residual differences between heads reflect the shape of their decision boundaries rather than the quality of the underlying representation. To examine this premise directly rather than inferring it from downstream accuracy, the hold-out embeddings were projected into two dimensions using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE; a 50-component PCA pre-reduction, perplexity = 30, 1,000 iterations, Euclidean metric, fixed random seed) and, as a safeguard against the known sensitivity of t-SNE to its perplexity setting, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP; 15 neighbors, minimum distance 0.1), the latter additionally providing an out-of-sample transform that t-SNE does not support. Four complementary views of the resulting two-dimensional space were examined in turn: the effect of the backbone on class geometry relative to the raw input (Figure 32), the partition each classifier head imposes on that geometry (Figure 33), the subset of instances on which the four heads disagree (Figure 34), and the shape of the decision boundary each head constructs within the projected space (Figure 35).

Figure 32 shows the representation learning performed by the backbone directly visible by projecting the same held-out scans twice: once from the raw 32 × 32 pixel grid (1,024-D) and once from the 256-D embedding the CNN produces from it. In the raw-pixel projections, silhouette coefficients of 0.081 (t-SNE) and 0.058 (UMAP) indicate that the four tumor categories form only a weakly separated, substantially entangled cloud; the pituitary class is a partial exception, forming a loosely coherent arm even at this stage, consistent with its stereotyped sellar location noted in Section 1. Passing the same scans through the trained backbone raised the silhouette coefficient to 0.347 under t-SNE and 0.380 under UMAP, and the embedding resolved into four visibly distinct clusters. This is consistent with the class-wise behavior documented quantitatively in Table 8 and the confusion matrices in Figure 8. The pituitary and normal categories occupy compact, well-separated regions of the embedding, in agreement with their recall of 99.15 and 98.00%, respectively, under CNN-KNN, whereas the glioma and meningioma populations remain adjacent and share a diffuse interface even after feature extraction, corresponding to the bidirectional glioma–meningioma confusion that, per the confusion matrices in Figure 8, accounts for 43.8% of CNN errors (39 of 89), 37.5% of CNN-KNN errors (18 of 48), 50.9% of CNN-SVM errors (27 of 53), and 56.8% of CNN-RF errors (54 of 95). Therefore, the embedding is structured by diagnostic category rather than by an incidental property of the acquisition, and the four-fold increase in the silhouette score attributable to the backbone quantifies, rather than merely illustrates, the representational gain that the classical heads in Sections 4.3 and 4.4 build upon.

Figure 33 recolors this same embedding by the predicted, rather than true, class for each of the four decision layers, so that the errors of each classifier can be read directly from the cluster geometry established in Figure 32. Cross-referencing these predictions against the confusion matrices in Figure 8 reproduces the ordering already established in Table 8: CNN-KNN misclassified the fewest scans (48 of 1,405; 96.58% accuracy), followed by CNN-SVM the next fewest (53; 96.23%), the bare CNN softmax head (89; 93.67%), and CNN-RF (95; 93.24%). This ordering is invariant to whether the underlying projection is t-SNE or UMAP. In every panel, the black × markers denoting misclassification are not scattered uniformly across the map but concentrate almost exclusively along the glioma–meningioma interface identified in Figure 32, with only a small number of additional errors near the boundary separating the normal and pituitary regions. The interior of each cluster was essentially error-free for every classifier. CNN-KNN and CNN-SVM visibly recover more of the interface region into the correct color than the CNN and CNN-RF panels do, which is the visual counterpart of their lower error counts and previews the decision region analysis of Figure 35.

Because the four heads share a common embedding, Figure 33 leaves open the question of whether the classifiers tend to err on the same or different instances. Figure 34 directly answers this by marking every test scan on which the four predictions are not unanimous. Of the 1,405 hold-out scans, 108 (7.7%) split the four models, and a further 25 (1.8%) defeated all four simultaneously. These points are not distributed randomly across the embedding but sit almost entirely along the same glioma–meningioma interface and, to a lesser extent, along the narrow seam between the normal and pituitary arms—precisely the regions already flagged as ambiguous in Figures 32, 33. The 25 scans defeating every classifier, irrespective of the shape of its decision rule, cluster in this same narrow region, indicating that this residual disagreement reflects a property of the underlying feature geometry itself—scans whose embeddings fall genuinely between two class manifolds—rather than an idiosyncrasy of any single classifier’s decision surface.

Figure 35 completes the analysis by refitting each decision rule inside the two-dimensional UMAP embedding itself, using the hyper parameters selected in Section 4.2, so that the shape of each classifier’s decision boundary, rather than only its errors, can be directly inspected. The softmax head of the CNN is exact in this reduced space because its classification layer is a single dense unit on the embedding, that is, multinomial logistic regression, and its boundaries are consequently linear by construction; this rigidity is visible as a single straight partition cutting directly through the glioma–meningioma interface, which is why it recovers the lowest 2-D-space accuracy of the four heads (87.83%). By contrast, CNN-KNN is a purely local, non-parametric rule, and its boundary conforms tightly and irregularly to the true cluster shapes, including the narrow neck between glioma and meningioma, yielding the highest 2-D-space accuracy (90.82%). CNN-SVM occupies an intermediate position: its RBF kernel produces a single smooth, curved boundary through the same interface that is more flexible than the softmax line but does not fragment into the small pockets KNN admits, giving a comparable accuracy (90.53%). CNN-RF partitions the space into an axis-aligned mosaic of small rectangular regions, a signature of its underlying decision-tree splits; the resulting boundary is the most locally accurate in this reduced space (91.25%) but is visibly the most fragmented and least smooth, consistent with the RF head’s comparatively weaker generalization once threshold-independent ranking is considered in Section 4.3. The higher RF accuracy in the reduced 2-D projection should not be interpreted as evidence of superior performance in the original 256-dimensional embedding; it reflects the behavior of the classifiers after projection and is therefore used only to illustrate differences in decision-boundary geometry. The quantitative classifier comparison remains that reported for the original 256-dimensional embedding in Table 8 These figures are fit and evaluated entirely within the projected 2-D space and not the original 256-D embedding; therefore, the accuracies reported here illustrate the qualitative shape of each decision rule rather than reproducing the exact hold-out figures of Table 8 and are systematically lower than the latter because two dimensions retain only a fraction of the class-discriminative information present in the full embedding. Taken together with Figures 33, 34, this geometric evidence explains why the choice of decision layer matters in the particular way that it does: because the classes form locally coherent clusters, the local, non-parametric KNN rule and the margin-maximizing SVM rule both adapt readily to the true, non-linear shape of the interface, whereas the axis-aligned partitioning of RF is poorly matched to a boundary that is oblique in the coordinate system of the embedding, and the linear softmax head is poorly matched for the same reason. Thus, the visualization provides a geometric account of the classifier ordering reported in Sections 4.3, 4.4, and 4.6, rather than merely restating it in another form.

Feature interpretation

In addition to quantifiable performance, the practical value of an automated diagnostic system hinges on the anatomical soundness of its predictions rather than on false associations. In this regard, the use of SmoothGrad-CAM++ (Omeiza et al., 2019), an extension of the Grad-CAM++ high-order gradient importance weights by the smooth noise averaging of SmoothGrad for eliminating speckling artifacts, on the output feature map right before the GlobalMaxPooling layer was analyzed. As this layer processes a 256-channel tensor similar to the classifier head embedding layer, the results are shown in Figure 2 for one representative holdout instance per class.

For the meningioma case, the attention map focused narrowly on the enhancing extra-axial lesion rather than the brain parenchyma or skull around it, suggesting that the model’s attention was focused on the correct anatomical region rather than on irrelevant anatomical features. For the normal case, activation is spread out over the ventricular and midline structures rather than localized to a point of focus, suggesting that the model validated the absence of a lesion by checking correct anatomy instead of simply “not finding anything.” The localization of attention is a necessary but insufficient step toward clinically trusting the model’s predictions, thus motivating the need for interpretability validation in the future.

In terms of clinical workflow, there are three implications of such localization behavior, apart from the value of providing qualitative reassurance. First, because the visualization is based on the same 256-channel tensor fed into all the classification heads studied here, the radiologist who looks at the SmoothGrad-CAM++ overlay actually analyzes the evidence base of CNN, KNN, SVM, and RF classifications, not only a particular one but all. Thus, visualization can be used as a universal second-reader tool, regardless of the choice of classification head used in a particular case. Second, the class-specific attention patterns observed in these representative cases are anatomically plausible and provide qualitative support for the relevance of the highlighted regions. For meningioma, the attention is concentrated around the enhancing extra-axial mass, whereas in normal cases it is distributed across broader anatomical landmarks rather than being focused on a suspected lesion. These patterns are consistent with the regions that would ordinarily be considered when interpreting such cases, but they should be regarded as qualitative evidence rather than confirmation of the model’s clinical reasoning. Third, the continued presence of anatomically grounded attention to the meningioma class, which is the most commonly confused class in Table 8, shows that the network’s residual errors in that particular class have a higher probability of being associated with the intrinsic diagnostic difficulty arising from the radiological signal rather than the fact that the model attends to the wrong parts of the image. This is because of the implications it would have on the potential gains to be made by improving that class, as it shows that additional diagnostic information in the form of contrast sequences or dural contact priors is required. However, the above remains qualitative since it only shows that the attention of the network is anatomically correct based on the sample inspected; it does not yet quantify the level of agreement between radiologists’ marked regions of interest over the whole test set or show whether there is a quantifiable calibrated confidence in predictions by the model.

The competing methods are classified into five groups. Transfer-learning approaches built on ImageNet-pretrained backbones, comprising ResNet-18 (88.33%), FL-EfficientNetB0 (80.17%), VGG16-ANN (84.00%), and SE-DenseNet-121 (95.60%), formed the largest group and spanned the widest range of outcomes. Because these backbones acquire their representational priors from natural color images, they carry an inductive bias that transfers imperfectly to the grayscale intensity and texture statistics of MRI, which plausibly explains why even the strongest of them remains below the proposed model despite employing SE-based channel recalibration. The difference is that the SE modules of SE-DenseNet-121 refine a pretrained backbone, whereas those of the proposed model are trained end-to-end alongside depth-wise separable convolutions directly on MICA-enhanced MRI; that entry is moreover obtained under an 85/15 split, which allots it a larger training partition than the 80/20 protocol used here.

Optimization-augmented lightweight networks, namely EWHO-SqueezeNet (93.20%), GTLO-LeNet (89.60%), and SCAOA-SegNet (93.00%), improve upon plain transfer learning through meta-heuristic hyperparameter search, but their accuracy remains below 94%, which is consistent with the limited representational capacity of the underlying shallow backbones rather than with any deficiency in the search strategy itself. Relational methods, namely CLM-DL (95.09%) and GNN (93.68%), introduce explicit reasoning between image regions and narrow the gap relative to the first group, although at the cost of higher computational expense and reduced transparency compared with the compact and fully convolutional design used here. The fourth group dispenses with pre-trained weights altogether: the customized CNN (94.51%) and CFLM feature fusion model with Grad-CAM (94.24%) confirm that a task-specific architecture trained from scratch is competitive with transfer learning, but neither couples such an architecture to a contrast-domain front end.

The fifth group is the most directly comparable to the present work, since its members also delegate the final decision to a classical learner operating on convolutional features: 3D-CNN-RF reached 95.00% and CNN-SVM 95.16%. The proposed configuration exceeds these by 1.58 and 1.42 percentage points, respectively, and does so notwithstanding that 3D-CNN-RF has access to volumetric context unavailable to the present two-dimensional formulation. Because the decision-layer strategy is shared across this group, the margin is attributable to the feature representation on which the decision layer operates rather than the pairing of a convolutional extractor with a classical head, which is the same conclusion reached independently by the ablation study in Section 4.8.

Across all five groups, the proposed CNN-KNN configuration recorded the highest reported accuracy in Table 14; however, these values were obtained under different datasets and experimental protocols and therefore do not constitute a controlled ranking of the methods. Under the hold-out protocol, its margin over the reviewed methods ranges from 0.98 to 16.41 percentage points, and when the comparison is restricted to the two entries evaluated by five-fold cross-validation, its 96.10% exceeds the 93.20% of EWHO-SqueezeNet by 2.90 points. It achieves this with a substantially smaller and more transparent architecture than the graph-based and large pretrained alternatives, which is consistent with the ablation findings reported above. The internal ablation results indicate that the performance of the proposed framework is associated with the combined contribution of MICA-based contrast normalization, the SE-augmented depth-wise separable backbone, and the distance-based decision layer, although the cross-study comparison in Table 14 cannot independently establish the contribution of these components relative to previously published methods.

The architectural contributions that result in better performance can be described as follows. First, dilated convolution provides the ability to detect multi-scale spatial features using an increased receptive field without raising the computational costs. Thus, this architectural contribution forms the basis for detecting tumor patterns of various sizes in medical images. Second, residual connections help solve the problem of vanishing gradients, thus allowing the training of a deeper network (through four encoding layers). Third, SE allows channel importance refinement and thus helps distinguish different types of tumors owing to less important channel suppression. Table 14 is consistent with the view that it is the composition of these elements with a distance-based decision layer, rather than any one of them in isolation, that accounts for the position the proposed framework occupies in the table. However, the extent to which this consistency may be pressed depends on the terms under which the tabulated figures were obtained, which are examined next.

The accuracies attributed to the competing methods were reproduced as reported by their original authors and were not obtained using a common experimental protocol. The referenced studies differ in the corpora on which they were evaluated, the number and definition of the diagnostic classes, the preprocessing and augmentation applied prior to training, and the resampling scheme used to estimate performance. Several studies reported a single split rather than a cross-validated mean. Differences on the order of one percentage point, such as the 0.98-point margin separating the proposed configuration from SE-DenseNet-121, are of the same magnitude as the variation attributable to these protocol differences alone and are comparable in width to the confidence intervals reported in Section 4.6. Therefore, Table 14 should be read as situating the proposed framework within the range of results currently reported for this task and not as evidence of a controlled superiority over any individual method.

The comparisons that were genuinely controlled in this study were internal. In Tables 12, 13, the dataset, preprocessing, partitioning, training schedule, and evaluation metrics are identical across every entry, and only the component under examination is varied, whereas the paired significance tests in Section 4.6 quantify the resulting differences on a per-sample basis. The claims advanced in this paper concerning the contribution of MICA enhancement, the individual architectural components, and the decision layer rest upon these controlled comparisons, whereas Table 14 serves the narrower purpose of demonstrating that the framework is competitive with the published state-of-the-art. A fully standardized benchmark, in which the leading competing architectures are re-implemented and retrained on the present corpus under a shared protocol, would be required to convert the comparison in Table 14 into a controlled one, which was identified in Section 4.14 as a direction for future work.

With respect to practical clinical applicability, the framework is positioned as a screening and second-reader aid, rather than as an autonomous diagnostic instrument. Its computational profile (Section 4.5) permits deployment on commodity hardware without a dedicated accelerator, and the class-conditional attention maps in Section 4.9 allow a reporting radiologist to inspect the evidence underlying each prediction. The constraints upon that positioning are equally explicit: the framework operates on individual two-dimensional slices and cannot substitute for volumetric assessment; meningioma recall remains the limiting quantity at 92.40% under the best configuration, which is insufficient for use as a rule-out test in isolation; and, as set out in Section 4.11, no external multi-institutional cohort has yet been used to verify that the reported performance transfers across scanners, acquisition protocols, and patient populations.

Robustness considerations

This subsection examines the extent to which the reported performance is contingent upon the properties of the evaluation corpus, considering the class imbalance, heterogeneity of image acquisition, sensitivity to noise, and the prospect of domain shift, and explicitly states which of these have been evaluated in this study and which have not.

The dataset used for the analysis of the proposed model was only mildly imbalanced. The four categories comprised 1,621 glioma, 1,645 meningioma, 1,757 pituitary, and 2,000 normal images, corresponding to 23.08, 23.42, 25.02, and 28.48% of the total, respectively; the ratio of the largest class to the smallest was 1.23, and the normalized Shannon entropy of the class distribution was 0.997, where unity denotes a perfectly uniform distribution. Three measures were adopted to ensure that this residual asymmetry did not inflate the reported figures. Both partitioning protocols are class-stratified, so that the hold-out set contains 324, 329, 351, and 400 images of the four classes in the same proportions as the parent corpus, and every cross-validation fold reproduces those proportions. All headline metrics were macro-averaged, weighting each class equally regardless of its size, and per-class recall, specificity, precision, and F1-score are reported individually in Tables 8, 9 rather than in aggregate. The empirical evidence that imbalance does not drive the results is that the ordering of class difficulty does not follow the ordering of class size: meningioma, the second-smallest category, is consistently the hardest, whereas glioma, the smallest, is recognized more accurately than meningioma under every decision layer and in both validation regimes. Therefore, the limiting factor is radiological similarity rather than the number of available examples.

Regarding variability in acquisition, the corpus is not the product of a single scanner or protocol; it aggregates three independently assembled collections, namely Figshare, SARTAJ, and Br35H (Section 3.1), and consequently spans a range of field strengths, pulse sequences, in-plane resolutions, and reconstruction pipelines. This heterogeneity is one of the reasons why the MICA front-end was formulated over multiple scales in the first instance. The coarse scale (σ = 60), with a cutoff frequency of approximately 0.0027 cycles per pixel, is intended to attenuate low-frequency intensity variations associated with coil sensitivity and B1-field inhomogeneity (Section 3.2.1). Finer scales preserve lesion-scale structure. The observation in Section 4.8 that MICA enhancement contributes 3.06 accuracy points and that no single scale reproduces this contribution is consistent with an interpretation in which part of the benefit of the enhancement stage may arise from reducing low-frequency intensity variation in addition to improving local contrast. This interpretation remains indirect, however, because the present study did not separately quantify the contribution of scanner or acquisition-specific intensity variation.

Sensitivity to noise has only been indirectly addressed. The amplification step in Equation 3 increases the magnitude of the high-frequency residual and therefore may also amplify acquisition noise alongside anatomical detail. The fidelity analysis in Section 3.2.1 provides a quantitative assessment of this trade-off: the MSE, PSNR, and SSIM values in Tables 3, 4 show a marked deterioration at α = 2.0, whereas α = 1.5 provides the best fidelity scores among the tested amplification factors and was therefore selected for the proposed configuration. However, these results do not constitute a direct noise-robustness evaluation, because the images were not subjected to controlled noise perturbations. A dedicated degradation experiment in which the hold-out images are perturbed with additive Gaussian or Rician noise, simulated bias fields, or motion artifacts at graded severity levels, followed by measurement of the resulting change in classification performance, has not been performed. Such an experiment would provide a direct assessment of the robustness of the complete pipeline and is therefore identified as a limitation and future research direction in Sections 4.14 and 5.

The same qualification applies with greater force to the domain shift. All results reported here were obtained from partitions of a single aggregated corpus, and although the three constituent collections introduced a measure of acquisition diversity, they did not constitute an external validation set; no images originating from an institution, scanner, or patient population unrepresented in training were used for testing. Because the publicly available aggregated corpus does not provide reliable patient-level identifiers for all constituent collections, the present partitioning was performed at the image level rather than at the patient level. Consequently, the possibility of correlated slices originating from the same subject cannot be completely excluded, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the reported generalization performance. The close agreement between the hold-out and cross-validated estimates documented in Section 4.7 establishes that the reported performance is not an artifact of one favorable split. Still, it cannot establish that the performance transfers to a distributionally distinct cohort, which is a stronger property that only external validation can demonstrate. Accordingly, the generalization claims advanced in this study are confined to data drawn from the same distribution as the training corpus, and cross-institutional evaluation is identified in Sections 4.14 and 5 as the principal prerequisite for clinical translation.

Discussion

This subsection steps back from the individual experiments to interpret the patterns that recur across them, considering what the results imply from technical, clinical, and deployment perspectives. Several patterns that recur across the results merit interpretation beyond the numerical values themselves.

From a technical perspective, the consistent gap between CNN-KNN/CNN-SVM and the softmax head of the CNN model, together with the statistically significant McNemar’s tests, indicates that the decision rule provides an additional source of performance beyond the learned feature representation. This interpretation is consistent with the ablation study, which shows that dilation, residual connections, and MICA contribute larger performance gains than SE attention, indicating that spatial receptive-field expansion, stable gradient propagation, and contrast normalization are the principal factors shaping the learned representation, while channel attention and the classifier head provide smaller refinements. The difference between the accuracy and ranking results further indicates that the preferred classifier head depends on the intended use: CNN-KNN is preferable when the primary requirement is a categorical prediction, whereas CNN-SVM is more suitable when prediction scores are used for case ranking or selection of an operating threshold.

In the clinical sense, meningiomas continue to stand out among the classes, being the least distinguishable class with all classifiers and in any of the validation settings used, which is consistent with their radiological diversity. Unlike pituitary adenomas, meningiomas are usually less specific in appearance and can appear as faintly enhanced broad-based dural tumors with an intensity and morphology similar to that of the surrounding parenchyma and vasculature. This interpretation is corroborated by the analysis of the interpretability of the features above, which indicates that the location of the lesion remains relevant to the network, even in a difficult class.

From a deployment perspective, CNN-SVM is preferable when inference cost should remain largely independent of subsequent growth of the stored training set, because its support-vector set is fixed after training, whereas CNN-KNN requires distance calculations against the stored training embeddings and therefore scales with the number of stored training samples. In contrast, CNN-RF offers no statistically distinguishable benefit over the softmax head of CNN and therefore does not provide a clear justification for the additional decision-layer complexity for this embedding. Taken together, the statistically confirmed but comparatively modest gain of the best hybrid classifier over the base CNN, set against the larger contributions of the architectural components isolated in the ablation study, indicates that the principal source of performance is the MICA-enhanced, SE-augmented convolutional feature extractor, with the classifier head providing a smaller but statistically meaningful refinement.

Summary of results

This subsection consolidates the findings distributed across Sections 4.3 to 4.12 into a single account, tracing what was established, by which line of evidence, and with what degree of confidence.

Regarding comparative performance, the four decision layers were evaluated under two independent resampling regimes and returned the same ordering under both. CNN-KNN achieved the highest hard-label accuracy of 96.58% on the hold-out partition and 96.10 ± 0.83% across the five cross-validation folds, followed by CNN-SVM at 96.23% and 95.67 ± 0.57%. CNN-RF and the backbone’s native softmax head were statistically inseparable in both regimes, differing by less than one-tenth of the fold-level standard deviation under cross-validation. McNemar’s test confirmed that the accuracy gains of CNN-KNN and CNN-SVM over the softmax head were significant at p < 0.001, while the CNN-RF difference was not (p = 0.461), and paired bootstrap resampling placed the CNN-KNN improvement at 2.92 pp. with a 95% confidence interval of [1.71, 4.13] that excluded the zero.

Regarding the metric that should govern the choice of the decision layer, the evaluation exposed a consistent dissociation between hard-label accuracy and threshold-independent ranking quality. CNN-SVM attained the strongest ranking behavior in both regimes, with a macro-AUC of 99.56% on the hold-out set and 99.59 ± 0.11% across folds, together with the highest average precision, whereas CNN-KNN led to accuracy while recording the lowest AUC among the four configurations. The cause is architectural rather than incidental: the discrete neighbor-vote score of a KNN head cannot order cases finely across thresholds, whereas the continuous margin of an SVM can. The operational conclusion is that a distance-based head suits tasks that consume a categorical label, and a margin-based head suits tasks that consume a score.

The ablation study provides evidence regarding the contribution of the individual components to the framework’s performance. Disabling dilation cost 3.27 accuracy points, the bare backbone without squeeze-and-excitation or residual connections cost 3.20 points, removing the residual connection cost 3.13 points, and removing MICA contrast enhancement entirely cost 3.06 points, whereas restricting MICA to any single Gaussian scale cost 1.85–2.21 points and removing the SE block cost 0.85 points. These reductions are larger than the 2.15-point cross-validated gain attributable to the best decision layer, indicating that the main performance contribution arises from the MICA-enhanced, SE-augmented convolutional feature extractor, while the classifier head provides a smaller but statistically meaningful refinement.

Regarding clinical behavior, meningioma emerged as the limiting class in every configuration, every fold, and both validation protocols, with recall never exceeding 92% under cross-validation. Bidirectional glioma–meningioma confusion accounted for 43 to 50% of all residual errors, and the invariance of this pattern across four structurally dissimilar decision rules indicates that the limitation resides in the radiological signal available from a single two-dimensional slice rather than in the classifier. Recast as a binary screening question, the framework missed between 0.58 and 1.04% of tumor-bearing studies, depending on the head, with CNN-SVM performing best on both the missed-tumor and over-called-normal axes. SmoothGrad-CAM++ visualization confirmed that the predictions were anchored to anatomically plausible regions, including the difficult meningioma class.

Regarding efficiency and standing relative to prior work, the shared backbone requires only 107,076 parameters and 0.20 MB of half-precision weight storage while sustaining 23.7 images per second on the CPU and 220.1 images per second on an NVIDIA A100 GPU at a batch size of 16, making it substantially smaller than the pretrained backbones commonly applied to this task. The best configuration exceeded every reviewed state-of-the-art method listed in Table 14 by 0.98 to 16.41 pp. of accuracy under the hold-out protocol. Taken together, these results support a compact, contrast-normalized, attention-augmented feature extractor paired with a decision layer chosen to match the downstream clinical task as an accurate, interpretable, and computationally frugal basis for computer-aided brain tumor screening, subject to the constraints listed in Section 4.14.

However, that margin over the reviewed state-of-the-art methods is reported as an indication of competitiveness rather than as a controlled measure of superiority, since the studies compiled in Table 14 differ from one another in terms of dataset, preprocessing, and validation protocol; the qualification is described in detail in Section 4.10. The internally controlled evidence, comprising the ablation of Section 4.8, protocol comparison of Section 4.7, and paired significance tests and confidence intervals of Section 4.6, is unaffected by this heterogeneity.

Conclusion and future scope

This study presented a contrast-enhanced, deep-learning-based framework for automated multiclass brain tumor identification that couples a Multi-scale Intensity Contrast Amplification (MICA) preprocessing stage with a compact, squeeze-and-excitation-augmented, depth-wise separable convolutional backbone, and systematically compared four classifier heads—the CNN’s native softmax layer, KNN, SVM, and RF—trained on the resulting feature embedding. Under an 80:20 hold-out evaluation on 7,023 MRI images spanning glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and normal classes, the CNN-KNN configuration achieved the highest accuracy (96.58%), whereas CNN-SVM achieved the best ranking-based performance (macro-AUC = 99.56%, macro-AP = 98.81%). Both improvements over the base CNN’s own softmax head were confirmed to be statistically significant by McNemar’s test and paired bootstrap resampling, and stratified 5-fold cross-validation applied to all four configurations confirmed that the accuracy advantage was not an artifact of a single favorable data split: the fold-averaged accuracies of 96.10 ± 0.83% for CNN-KNN and 95.67 ± 0.57% for CNN-SVM reproduced the hold-out ranking without exception, and the hold-out estimate fell within the observed fold range for every configuration. Gradient-based visualization further indicated that the network’s predictions were focused on anatomically plausible regions rather than extraneous background structures in the representative cases examined. The computational complexity analysis showed that the 107,076-parameter backbone requires 0.20 MB of half-precision storage and supports measured inference throughputs of 23.7 images per second on the CPU and 220.1 images per second on the GPU. These measurements indicate that the framework is computationally lightweight and could support efficient screening workflows and large-scale retrospective analysis, although prospective clinical deployment would require validation under real-world operating conditions. Taken together, these results support the conclusion that the principal source of diagnostic performance in this framework is the MICA-enhanced, SE-augmented convolutional feature extractor, with the pairing of that extractor to a distance-based (KNN) or margin-based (SVM) decision layer providing a smaller, but statistically confirmed, additional refinement, while a tree-ensemble (RF) decision layer offers no consistent benefit over the network’s own softmax classifier for this task.

Future work will proceed in several directions, motivated directly by the limitations noted above. First, explainability will be extended beyond the qualitative Grad-CAM analysis presented here to include quantitative-saliency-based localization metrics and complementary feature-attribution methods, such as SHAP and LIME, to build the case-level interpretability required for clinical adoption. Second, the stratified cross-validation protocol reported here will be extended to external multi-institutional MRI cohorts to assess generalization across scanners, acquisition protocols, and patient demographics beyond the present single-source Kaggle-aggregated dataset. Third, given that meningioma remains the most persistently confused class across every classifier and validation regime examined here, targeted strategies, including boundary-case oversampling, location or dural-contact priors, and volumetric (3D) context beyond the current 2D-slice formulation, will be explored specifically for this class rather than relying on further generic architectural changes. Fourth, the framework will be extended toward edge-device deployment, informed by the CPU latency and memory profile reported earlier, and toward integration with real-time segmentation and longitudinal tumor-progression tracking, to move from single-slice classification to a more complete computer-aided diagnostic pipeline.

The four directions set out above extend this method. Two further directions, which follow from the qualifications recorded in Sections 4.10 and 4.11 and are consequently the more immediate priority, are concerned with strengthening the evidence on which any claim about the method must rest. The first is a graded robustness evaluation, in which the test images are corrupted by additive Gaussian and Rician noise, simulated bias fields, and motion artifacts at controlled severities, so that the degradation of accuracy and predictive calibration may be characterized quantitatively rather than inferred from the image-fidelity measures of Section 3.2.1 alone. The second is a standardized benchmark in which the leading competing architectures are re-implemented and retrained on a common corpus under a shared preprocessing and validation protocol, which would convert the indicative comparison of Table 14 into a controlled one and permit the accompanying differences to be tested for their statistical significance. Until both have been carried out, the contribution of this work is properly stated as a compact, contrast-normalized, and interpretable classification framework whose internal design choices have been validated under controlled conditions and whose standing relative to the published literature has been established indicatively rather than definitively.
