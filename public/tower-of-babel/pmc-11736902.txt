Complex p53 dynamics regulated by miR-125b in cellular responses to reactive oxidative stress and DNA damage
Authors: Md Zubbair Malik, Mohammed Dashti, Amit Jangid, Arshad Channanath, Sumi Elsa John, R K Brojen Singh, Fahd Al-Mulla, Thangavel Alphonse Thanaraj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn response to distinct cellular stresses, the p53 exhibits distinct dynamics. These p53 dynamics subsequently control cell fate. However, different stresses can generate the same p53 dynamics with different cell fate outcomes, suggesting that the integration of dynamic information from other pathways is important for cell fate regulation. The interactions between miRNA-125b, p53, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are significant in the context of cellular stress responses and apoptosis. However, the regulating mechanism of miR-125b with p53 is not fully studied. The dynamics of p53 and its response to the miR-125b regulation are still open questions. In the present study, we try to answer some of these fundamental questions based on basic model built from available experimental reports. The miR-125b-p53 regulatory network is modeled using a set of 11 molecular species variables. The biochemical network of miR-125b-p53, described by 22 reaction channels, is represented by coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs) using the mass action law of chemical kinetics. These ODEs are solved numerically using the standard fourth-order Runge–Kutta method to analyze the dynamical behavior of the system. The biochemical network model we designed is based on both experimental and theoretical reported data. The p53 dynamics driven by miR-125b exhibit five distinct dynamical states: first and second stable states, first and second dynamical states, and a sustained oscillation state. These different p53 dynamical states may correspond to various cellular conditions. If the stress induced by miR-125b is weak, the system will be weakly activated, favoring a return to normal functioning. However, if the stress is significantly strong, the system will move to an active state. To sustain this active state, which is far from equilibrium with little scope for returning to normal conditions, the system may transition to an apoptotic state by crossing through other intermediate states, as it is unlikely to regain normal functioning. The p53 dynamical states show a multifractal nature, contributed by both short- and long-range correlations. The networks illustrated from these dynamical states follow hierarchical scale-free features, exhibiting an assortative nature with an absence of the centrality-lethality rule. Furthermore, the active dynamical state is generally closer to hierarchical characteristics and is self-organized. Our research study reveals that significant activity of miR-125b on the p53 regulatory network and its dynamics can only be observed when the system is slightly activated by ROS. However, this process does not necessarily require the direct study of ROS activity. These findings elucidate the mechanisms by which cells integrate signaling pathways with distinct temporal activity patterns to encode stress specificity and direct diverse cell fate decisions.Keywords: cell stress responses, dynamics, miR-125b, p53, ROS, cellular state

Introduction

The p53 gained the attention of researchers because of its essential role in preventing the cancerization of cells [1, 2]. It acts as a key regulator in the cellular network and response to a variety of cellular stress, including DNA damage, hypoxia, nucleotide depletion, nitric oxide, and aberrant proliferative signals such as oncogene activation [1, 3]. But in most cases of human cancer cell, p53 tumor suppressor signaling pathway is found in inactive states [1]. Its activation results in the fulfilment of key cellular processes such as cell-cycle arrest, senescence, and most importantly tumor clearance to prevent cancer cell formation [4]. Further, the activated p53 protein safeguards the organism against the propagation of cells that carry damaged DNA with potentially oncogenic mutations [3]. It has been reported that activation of p53 functions via the inhibition of Mdm2 protein, thus Mdm2 can be regarded as an effective approach in cancer therapy [5–7]. Mdm2 acts as a negative feedback regulator (inhibitor) to p53 by binding itself to p53, and then physically blocking its ability to transactivate gene expression, and stimulating its degradation [8–10]. Further, the interaction of N-terminal domain of Mdm2 with transactivation domain of p53 (p53-TAD) performs a significant role in the regulation of the G1 checkpoint of the cell cycle and cell function [11, 12]. The various studies on complex p53 regulatory network, by taking into account the feedback mechanism in the regulatory network [13–16], incorporating morphogenesis with Axin, Nutlin, and Wnt [14], embodying signaling molecules such as calcium, SMAR1, HDAC1, etc [14, 15, 17]. Some models capture various possible dynamical states of p53 which associate to different cell state. It is well known that p53 is coupled with Mdm2 via a negative feedback loop [6, 18–21]. When the negative feedback loop of p53 is activated, DNA repair takes place, whereas activation of the positive feedback loop pushes p53 activation into an irreversible apoptotic phase. Other p53 regulators can modulate p53 pulses; for instance, introducing MDMX into the model system suppresses p53 oscillatory amplitude, while knocking out MDMX significantly increases this amplitude [21]. During the recovery phase after DNA damage, repetitive p53 pulses occur as a result of continuous efforts to repair the damage. However, under excessive stress leading to apoptosis, the amplitude of p53 pulses rises sharply and shifts to an irreversible stable state. Similarly, regulators of p53 linked to the positive feedback loop (such as ATM, PTEN, and Akt) can sometimes induce a switch in p53 dynamical states. While these models successfully capture various p53 dynamic states, such as activation, recovery, and apoptosis that qualitatively align with experimental findings, they fail to accurately represent the dynamics of p53 during cancer progression [13, 21].

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are chemically reactive molecules containing oxygen ions and peroxides [22]. These ROS species have identified to be synthesized as a by-product of natural oxygen metabolism. They have seen to be regulating pivotal roles in cell signaling and homeostasis [23, 24]. At low levels of damage, few p53 pulses promote cell survival by inducing p21 and halting the cell cycle, while high levels of damage lead to sustained p53 pulses that trigger apoptosis via p53AIP1 [25]. p53 governs cell fate through a two-phase response, where pulsatile activation leads to cell cycle arrest and, if DNA damage is irreparable, a switch-like activation triggers apoptosis, mediated by distinct feedback loops [20]. However, an elevated level of ROS caused by UV irradiation or heat exposure can drive the cell into different stress states, [23]. These soaring levels could lead to an aggressive promote DNA damage, further leading to mutagenesis, carcinogenesis, and aging [22, 24, 26]. However, there’s no clear cut evidence suggesting a primary response of ROS on cellular machinery, it all depends upon the varying different states, namely, normal, stress, cancerous and apoptosis is still not fully studied.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNA molecules of size 20–24 nucleotides, and are powerful regulators of transcriptional and post transcriptional gene expression which regulate both physiological and pathological processes such as cellular development and cancer [27–29]. miR-125b is a brain-enriched miRNA which acts as a negative regulator of p53 both in zebrafish and human [30–32]. Over-expression of miR-125b suppresses the endogenous level of p53 protein and represses to apoptosis in human neuroblastoma cells and human lung fibroblast cells [30]. Decrease miR-125b level leads to enhance the level of p53, and thus induces apoptosis in human neuroblastoma and human lung fibroblast cells [30, 31]. There are few p53-Mdm2 regulatory models in which stochastic approach has been used for study of stress responses to the pathway, specially in the p53 and Mdm2 dynamics [33]. The study showed the variability of the pathway dynamics driven by stress induced to the system. Further, there are important pathways, e.g. TP53INP1 pathway, which interact with miR-125b and p53-Mdm2 regulatory network and induce various cellular states specially in neuronal dynamics [34]. However, the regulating mechanism of miR-125b with p53 is not fully studied. The dynamics of p53 and its response to the miR-125b regulation are still open questions. In the present study, we try to answer some of these fundamental questions based on a basic model built from available experimental reports.

The p53 regulation by miR-125b can be explained from network theoretical approach. If the stress signal imparted by miR-125b is small (normal and first damped states), the corresponding network to the time series of the p53 dynamics exhibits weak hierarchical network topology (, , and are quite small as in Fig. 7H), where the emergent inter and intra-modules in the networks are loosely wired. At this state the signal processing is fast because of DNA repairing is being processed (the centrality measurements are comparatively large). When the stress is significantly large, ps53 dynamics show sustain oscillations, where DNA repairing process is quite active and hence the corresponding network shows strong hierarchical features (, , and ) Fig. 7H). At this state the emergent modules are tightly bound optimizing the signal processing such that the state is far from equilibrium. Further, at this state the signal processing is also comparatively quite fast (large values of , and of the centrality measurement parameters). Excess stress, which becomes toxic to the cellular system, triggers larger values of , smaller values of moving toward random behavior leading to vanishing of network features. This situation could be the way toward apoptotic state where the network organization is going to lost.

This means that the constructed networks, modules/sub-modules are tightly bound such that every components of the networks are active. The positive value of indicates the assortivity nature of the network [50], which means that there is a possibility of rich-club formation of leading hubs in the networks. However, this rich-club of leading hubs can’t control the network behavior, which means that removing of these leading hubs in the rich-club does not cause network break down. Hence, these networks do not follow centrality-lethality rule which is true for scale-free networks [51]. Therefore, there is no central control system which is one of the most important properties of self-organized systems [52]. This idea of self-organization in each five p53 states, which may correspond to corresponding cellular states, is studied from the networks constructed from the corresponding time series data. We found that stronger the self-organizing behavior in each state better the cellular functioning. We further predicted that active state corresponding to sustain oscillation state has strong hierarchical self-organizing topological behavior, and is a preferred state. Similar behavior is obtained in the constructed networks from the dynamical states of p53 induced by ROS.

Discussion

DNA undergoes continuous damage from both biological processes and environmental agents, with estimates suggesting that tens of thousands of lesions occur daily in each cell [53, 54]. To preserve genomic integrity, these lesions must be resolved. Although the repair of specific DNA lesions often necessitates distinct molecular machinery [55–57], the tumor suppressor p53 serves as a crucial regulator of cell fate in response to various cellular stresses [58]. These observations prompt the question of how cells differentiate between various cellular stresses to execute distinct cell fate decisions and transcriptional responses. Single-cell studies have shed light on this issue, revealing that p53 dynamics can vary depending on the stress, diversify gene expression patterns, and regulate specific cell fates. Single-cell studies have provided insight into this question, showing that p53 dynamics can vary depending on the type of stress [16], diversify gene expression patterns [59, 60], and regulate specific cell fates [61, 62]. However, studies increasingly reveal that p53 dynamics are complex, existing across a spectrum [63] and exhibiting changes in behavior over time and with varying levels of DNA damage [64, 65]. In our present study, we stumbled upon the fact that p53 protein’s natural versatile in expression profile was found to be quite high and seemed to be associated with several genes which were involved in various biochemical pathways. The network of positive and negative feedback loops that regulate p53 stability results in distinct temporal patterns of p53 expression in response to various forms of cellular stress. There are also several factors which can induce cellular stress, such as environmental factors (UV, IR, etc.), stress inducing molecules (ROS, miRNAs, nitric oxide, etc.), and signal propagation from various pathways. The variation in level of ROS in cellular system leads to the changes in the p53 dynamics (various stress states) with overall enhancement in its concentration level in the cellular system. Furthermore, the dynamic expression patterns of p53 include undamped oscillations in response to DNA double-strand breaks [6, 7, 19], a single graded pulse in response to UV radiation [16], and monotonically increasing accumulation during continuous exposure to chemotherapeutic agents [61, 64]. The dynamics of p53 have been implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, including diabetes [66, 67]. Several studies have shown that various signaling pathways target p53 to regulate cell function. For instance, free fatty acids (FFA) increase apoptosis of pancreatic -cells by reducing the phosphorylation and activation of AKT, which normally protects cells from p53-mediated apoptosis [68–70]. In FFA-treated -cells, p53 activation induces the miR-34a, sensitizing -cells to apoptosis and impairing insulin secretion [71, 72]. The study presented in the paper mainly focuses on the response of the p53 dynamics regulated by mir-125b. We expect that the dynamics of active p53 changes when mutant p53 is available. In an earlier study [21], we investigated in detail how active p53 dynamics changes in the presence of mutant p53 depending on different types of stress (Supplementary file 1).

Furthermore, pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN- and TNF- synergistically increase ROS production, p53 induction, sand apoptosis in pancreatic cells [73]. Importantly, ROS are also involved in FFA-mediated apoptosis of -cells, with both ROS generation and p53 activation being downstream effects of NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) [74]. Collectively, these findings indicate that FFAs, inflammatory cytokines, and ROS act upstream of p53 to induce p53-mediated apoptosis in pancreatic -cells [66]. Our study aimed to predict the range of ROS (range of ) population within which the pathway can work (defined by different dynamical states which may correspond to various cellular state) [74]. Further, the introduction of to the system showed inhibitory effect on p53 production and switching of dynamical states by varying concentration in the system [30, 31]. The stress management in the system is quite intricate. If the stress is weak, then the system will start active but come back to its normal functioning. However, if the stress is significantly strong, then the system becomes strongly active and stays in the active state for significantly long time, and the state neither goes to apoptotic state nor come back to its normal functioning, but has a little chance of coming back to its normal situation [66]. This state of the system is quite favorable because all working mechanism of all biological system such as p53 pathways are always far from equilibrium which is somehow always in active state. However, the choice of the parameter values in the model simulation are taken in such a way that the simulation results mimic with experimentally obtained results qualitatively or quantitatively. It is quite true to calculate the probability of happening of any one of the states. However, since the model is multi-dimensional system, it is quite difficult to calculate them. What we show in this work is that depending on the strength of the stress given to the system dynamics there is always a possibility of existence of these states in the complex cellular dynamics. This claim of existence of p53 states is proven by the topological properties of the network constructed from the time series corresponding to the active state which shows hierarchical scale free network. In this network there is no centrality-lethality rule, which means absence of central control system in it. The active dynamical state also shows multifractal behavior. These properties indicate that active state in biological systems are most favorable and are generally self-organized. Various experimental studies reported that concentration miR-125b increases in different cancer cell lines especially in breast cancer, leukemia, and uterus cancer cell lines. Therefore this necessitates the imminent need to further explore and the miRNA in depth in order to illustrate and enlighten copious facets regulating key role in cancerous cells.

These findings unveiled that significant activity of miR-125b on p53 regulatory network and its dynamics can be seen only when the system is slightly activated by ROS however this process does not necessitate to study ROS activity. Further, there is always a competition between several ROS species and miR-125b activities which are needed to be investigated extensively for understanding the roles of these stress inducers in regulating p53 dynamics. Moreover, the impact of the miR-125b on p53 regulatory pathway should be further studied in the stochastic system in order to capture the state switching mechanism quantitatively and to understand the role of noise in the cellular process. Another important and interesting feature to study could be the effect of molecular crowding environment to the pathway dynamics, especially on the variability of the cellular states which could mimic real cellular processes and mechanisms. We have not incorporated the impact of cellular environment which may affect the traffic rules and dynamical behaviors of the molecular species in the model pathway. In the crowded cellular environment, due to the diffusion of other molecular species from cellular environment to the model pathway might increase the interaction of molecular species, molecular traffic, and their own respective molecular conformations. This could lead to the change in the dynamics of the molecular species in the model pathway and may lead to various cellular states. Study in this direction could be a real taste of built model to the real cellular dynamics and we will be working in this direction in future soon.

Regulatory pathway model of p53-miR-125b

The model we considered (Fig. 1) is integration of p53-Mdm2 regulatory network [33] with stress inducing signaling molecules ROS via DNA damage [24] and miR-125b which interact with p53-Mdm2 [32]. In our study, we focussed on the simpler and important model circuit ARF-p53 which is a well studied model with theoretical and experimental evidences. Besides, it has been experimentally reported that ARF binds to Mdm2 which allows fast degradation of Mdm2 [75]. This degradation of Mdm2 flourish the stabilization of p53. This established ARF-Mdm2-p53 regulatory network is a key to regulate various cellular functions [76], namely, protection of cells from several types of damages by establishing oncogenic stress [76], act as a mediator of tumor suppressor activity [77], protection from aging by providing antiaging activity [78], etc. It has been shown that DNA damage signals are complemented by oncogene-mediated activation of ARF to promote senescence and suppress tumorigenesis, through p53 activation and/or p53-independent functions of ARF [79]. Further, another experimental results have shown that DNA damage disrupts the interaction of ARF with the nucleolar protein B23 (nucleophosmin) releasing ARF into the nucleoplasm so that it can bind to Mdm2 [80]. This presence of damaged DNA activates ARF and ARF binds to Mdm2 with a higher affinity than p53 and hence levels of unbound p53 are increased [33]. In this work [33], Proctor and Gray clearly showed that DNA damage activates ARF and then ARF binds to Mdm2 [33]. Another mechanism for the increase in p53 levels is the activation of ARF (known as p14ARF in humans), a nucleolar protein that senses DNA damage [81].
