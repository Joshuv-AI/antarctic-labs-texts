Cervical length and maternal and neonatal outcomes in placenta accreta spectrum: a single-center cohort study from Shiraz, Iran (2020–2024)
Authors: Maryam Kasraeian, Shaghayegh Moradi Alamdarloo, Nasrin Asadi, Homeira Vafaei Cisakht, Hamide Barzegar, Azam Faraji, Fatemeh Shariati nia, Naeimehossadat Asmarian, Khadije Bazrafshan, Atefe Hashemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA short cervical length (CL) in placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) may be associated with adverse outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of CL on maternal and neonatal outcomes in PAS.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences from January 2020 to December 2024. Transvaginal sonography was used to measure in women with PAS. Collected data included demographics, obstetric history, and maternal outcomes (e.g., bleeding volume, complications, ICU admission). Neonatal outcomes included gestational age, birth weight, Apgar scores, NICU admission, and mortality. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, employing chi-squared tests, logistic regression, and comparative analyses, with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsAmong 140 women, 44 had a CL < 25 mm and 96 had a CL ≥ 25 mm. A shorter CL was associated with a longer preoperative hospital stay (median 10.5 vs. 4 days, p = 0.034). There were no significant differences in massive hemorrhage (> 2000 mL, 40.9% vs. 38.5%, p = 0.790), emergency surgery (36.1% vs. 48.8%, p = 0.203), or intraoperative complications (25.6% vs. 29.2%, p = 0.664). Cervical length was not significantly associated with massive hemorrhage in univariate logistic regression analysis (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.92–1.03, p = 0.378). Neonatal mortality and fetal demise were not significantly different(p = 0.672,0.372 respectively). However, neonates of mothers with CL < 25 mm had a longer median NICU stay (19 vs. 13 days, p = 0.012).ConclusionIn PAS, a short cervical length was not independently associated with massive hemorrhage but was linked to prolonged antepartum hospitalization and longer neonatal NICU stays. Adverse neonatal outcomes appear mediated by prematurity. CL assessment, combined with bleeding history, may aid in risk stratification and timing of delivery to improve outcomes.Keywords: Placenta Accreta Spectrum, Cervical length, Intraoperative complications, Surgical blood loss, Neonatal outcome

Introduction

Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) is a pathologic condition of pregnancy in which the placenta adheres abnormally, thereby predisposing affected women and their newborns to serious morbidity and mortality [1–3]. PAS is classified as abnormal placental adherence (creta), invasion into the myometrium (increta), or deep invasion beyond the uterus (percreta, with subtypes involving serosa, bladder, or other pelvic organs) [1]. A recent systematic review reported that placenta accreta accounts for over 60% of PAS cases, whereas increta and percreta represent approximately 15% and 20%, respectively [4] and estimated the overall prevalence of PAS at 0.17% (range 0.01–1.1%) [4], while an Iranian study reported a prevalence of 0.37% [5].

Optimal timing of delivery in PAS requires balancing maternal and neonatal outcomes. Although 34 weeks is often suggested, the precise timing remains debated. Current guidelines recommend planned delivery between 34⁺⁰–35⁺⁶ weeks (ACOG) or 35⁺⁰–36⁺⁶ weeks (RCOG) following antenatal corticosteroids to enhance fetal lung maturity [6, 7].

Cervical length (CL) is not a diagnostic criterion for PAS but complements imaging findings (e.g., placental lacunae, loss of clear zone, bladder wall interruption) and clinical history (prior cesarean, placenta previa) to guide risk stratification and multidisciplinary planning [8, 9]. Evidence shows that shorter CL correlates with increased intraoperative blood loss, with one cohort reporting r = − 0.613 and an AUC of 0.89 when combined with cervical area for predicting massive hemorrhage [10]. MRI-based models also confirm short CL as an independent predictor of PAS and severe hemorrhage [8]. Clinically, reduced CL supports escalation of care, consideration for delivery at PAS referral centers [9, 11], adjustment of delivery timing within guideline-recommended preterm windows [9, 12], and advance scheduling of resources such as blood products and senior surgical teams [8, 11, 13].

Research by Fambro et al. indicates that in pregnancies complicated by placenta accreta, a short cervical length is associated with preterm birth before 32 weeks of gestation, which subsequently worsens maternal and neonatal prognosis [14]. Consistent evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis links a shortened cervical length in PAS to a higher incidence of adverse obstetric outcomes, specifically preterm delivery, antepartum bleeding, and postpartum hemorrhage [15].

Despite evidence linking shorter cervical length to increased intraoperative hemorrhage, its relationship with overall maternal and neonatal outcomes in PAS has not been comprehensively studied. This study therefore aimed to assess the predictive value of cervical length for maternal and neonatal outcomes in women with PAS at a tertiary care center in Shiraz, Iran, between 2020 and 2024, to inform risk stratification and delivery planning.

Method

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS), in collaboration with the Perinatology Research Center, and covered the period from January 2020 to December 2024. The study population included all pregnant women with a definitive diagnosis of PAS, including accreta, increta, and percreta, confirmed by histopathological examination of specimens obtained after cesarean section or cesarean hysterectomy. Women with unavailable or incomplete medical records were excluded. Eligible cases consisted of two categories: women admitted for indications other than PAS who were subsequently diagnosed intraoperatively, and women who were prenatally diagnosed with PAS by ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging and referred for delivery in SUMS-affiliated hospitals. A census sampling method was applied, and all eligible patients during the study period were enrolled.

Data were collected from hospital records using a standardized form and included demographic and clinical variables such as maternal age, obstetric and gynecologic history, social history, medical and surgical history, gravidity, parity, and prior uterine surgery. Prenatal information encompassed screening test results, maternal risk factors, and ultrasonographic findings, including loss of retroplacental clear zone, myometrial thinning, multiple placental lacunae, subplacental hypervascularity, turbulent flow within lacunae, bladder wall interruption, maximum placental thickness, placenta previa, and cervical length. Operative and intrapartum characteristics were recorded, including gestational age at delivery, indication for termination, type and duration of surgery, estimated blood loss, blood product transfusion, need for intensive care unit admission, and perioperative complications such as bladder, bowel, or ureter injury, wound infection, ileus, and maternal mortality. Neonatal outcomes included gestational age at delivery, birth weight, Apgar scores, neonatal intensive care unit admission, use of antenatal corticosteroids or magnesium sulfate, and neonatal mortality.

Cervical length was measured by experienced perinatologists as part of the institutional protocol for women suspected of PAS based on ultrasonographic criteria. Measurements were performed using transvaginal ultrasound with a multi-frequency probe, following the standardized technique described by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, Maternal-Fetal Medicine Units Network. Women were asked to empty their bladder prior to examination, and the probe was covered with a sterile sheath. The functional cervical length, defined as the distance between the internal and external os in the sagittal view, was measured three times, and the shortest value was recorded. In cases of funneling, the measurement was obtained from the funnel tip to the external os. Cervical length was re-evaluated every two weeks in both inpatient and outpatient settings, and additionally whenever patients presented with symptoms such as contractions or vaginal bleeding.

All women diagnosed with PAS were managed by a multidisciplinary team that included perinatologists, anesthesiologists, critical care specialists, neonatologists, and urologists. Additional subspecialty consultations, such as gynecologic oncology and vascular surgery, were obtained when clinically required.

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 23.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables, and associations between cervical length and maternal or neonatal outcomes were assessed. Multiple logistic regression was used to assess factors associated with massive hemorrhage among women with placenta accreta spectrum. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1403.319), and informed consent was obtained from all participants whose data were included. The study protocol was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

During the study period, 140 women diagnosed with placenta accreta spectrum were enrolled. The mean maternal age was 35.05 ± 4.56 years (range: 23–48 years). The mean gestational age at diagnosis was 28.04 ± 6.54 weeks (range: 10–40 weeks), while the mean gestational age at the time of surgery was 31.33 ± 5.62 weeks (range: 11–40 weeks).

There were no statistically significant differences in sonographic features between women with a cervical length ≥ 25 mm and those with a cervical length < 25 mm. A loss of clear zone was observed in 74.1% of patients with a cervical length ≥ 25 mm and 72.6% of those with a cervical length < 25 mm (p = 0.971). Thin myometrium was identified in 69.4% and 56.8% of patients, respectively (p = 0.155). Bridging vessels were detected in 27.1% of the ≥ 25 mm group and 31.8% of the < 25 mm group (p = 0.571). Hypervascularity was present in 82.4% and 88.6% of patients in the two groups, respectively (p = 0.350). Lacunae were observed in 49.4% of women with a cervical length ≥ 25 mm compared with 43.2% of those with a cervical length < 25 mm (p = 0.502). Bladder wall interruption was identified in 7.1% versus 9.1% of patients (p = 0.734). Placental bulge was noted in 7.1% of cases in the ≥ 25 mm group and in none of the patients with a cervical length < 25 mm, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.094). An exophytic mass was observed in one patient (1.2%) in the ≥ 25 mm group and in none of the patients with a cervical length < 25 mm (p > 0.999).

Significant differences were observed in the histopathological severity of PAS when stratified by cervical length (p = 0.001). Women in the short cervical length group (< 25 mm) demonstrated a substantially higher prevalence of placenta percreta compared to those with a cervical length ≥ 25 mm (34.1% vs. 12.5%, respectively; p = 0.001). Conversely, the least invasive form, placenta accreta, was more frequently identified in the group with a longer cervix (50.0% vs. 31.8%, p = 0.001). These findings indicate that while cervical shortening did not directly correlate with the volume of intraoperative hemorrhage in this cohort, it served as a significant marker for the most invasive phenotypes of the placenta accreta spectrum.

The mean intraoperative blood loss was 1,800.85 ± 1,421.91 mL (range: 200–7,000 mL). Based on estimated blood loss, patients were categorized into two groups: those with massive hemorrhage (> 2,000 mL) and those without massive hemorrhage. Baseline demographic characteristics, including maternal age (36 [33–39] vs. 35 [32–38] years, p = 0.295), gravidity (4 [3–5] vs. 4 [3–4.5], p = 0.615), prior cesarean Sect. (2 [1–3] vs. 2 [1–2], p = 0.377), cervical length, and gestational age at diagnosis, were comparable between women with and without massive hemorrhage. Prenatal vaginal bleeding was significantly more frequent in the massive hemorrhage group (43.6% vs. 21.2%, p = 0.005). Postoperative histopathology differed significantly between groups, with placenta percreta more common among women with massive hemorrhage (32.7% vs. 10.6%, p = 0.001) and accreta more frequent in those without massive bleeding (56.5% vs. 29.1%, p = 0.001). As expected, median blood loss was markedly higher in patients with massive hemorrhage (3000 [2500–4000] mL vs. 800 [400–1150] mL, p < 0.001). These patients required more frequent blood transfusion (96.4% vs. 62.4%, p < 0.001) and higher median units of packed red cells (4 [3–8] vs. 1 [0–2], p < 0.001), fresh frozen plasma (3 [0–4] vs. 0 [0–0], p < 0.001), and platelets (3 [0–4] vs. 0 [0–0], p < 0.001). Emergency surgery was significantly more common in the massive hemorrhage group (61.2% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.003). In addition, these patients had higher rates of ICU admission (57.4% vs. 25.9%, p < 0.001), intraoperative complications (50.0% vs. 14.1%, p < 0.001), and second hospital admission (38.2% vs. 11.9%, p < 0.001).

In univariate logistic regression analysis, emergency surgery and postoperative histopathological diagnosis were significantly associated with massive hemorrhage. Patients undergoing emergency surgery had more than a threefold increased odds of massive hemorrhage compared with elective cases. Additionally, compared with placenta percreta, patients with postoperative accreta demonstrated significantly lower odds of massive bleeding.

After multivariable adjustment, emergency surgery remained an independent risk factor, conferring approximately 2.6-fold increased odds of massive hemorrhage. Postoperative diagnosis of accreta also remained independently associated with a substantially reduced risk compared with percreta. Other evaluated variables, including maternal age, gravidity, number of previous cesarean sections, gestational age at surgery, hemoglobin level, and cervical length, were not independently associated with massive hemorrhage (Table 2).

Neonatal outcome

Twenty neonates (14.5%) died. The mean birth weight was 2125.89 ± 560.02 g. The mean Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were 6.45 ± 1.84 and 8.26 ± 1.18, respectively. The mean umbilical cord pH was 7.34 ± 0.08. Overall, 92 neonates (65.7%) required NICU admission, with a mean length of stay of 17.83 ± 14.46 days (range: 1–70 days). Sixty-six neonates (47.1%) were male.

Neonatal outcomes were compared between patients with a cervical length > 25 mm and those with a cervical length < 25 mm. The median gestational age at delivery was similar between the two groups (33 [IQR: 31–34] vs. 33 [IQR: 30.25–34] weeks, p = 0.403). Median neonatal birth weight did not differ significantly (2170 [IQR: 1831–2450] g vs. 2130 [IQR: 1897.5–2400] g, p = 0.965). There were no significant differences in neonatal mortality (7.0% vs. 2.8%, p = 0.672) or the proportion of intrauterine fetal demise (12.5% vs. 18.2%, p = 0.372). Median Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were comparable between groups (7 [IQR: 6–8] vs. 6 [IQR: 5–8], p = 0.385; and 9 [IQR: 8–9] vs. 8 [IQR: 7–9], p = 0.093, respectively). NICU admission rates were also similar (64.5% vs. 65.9%, p = 0.526). However, neonates born to mothers with a cervical length < 25 mm had a significantly longer NICU stay (19 [IQR: 10–36] vs. 13 [IQR: 5.5–20.5] days, p = 0.012).

Neonatal outcomes were compared between cases with and without massive hemorrhage. The median gestational age at delivery was significantly lower in the massive hemorrhage group compared with those without massive hemorrhage (33 [IQR: 29–34] vs. 34 [IQR: 32–34] weeks, p = 0.013). Similarly, neonates in the massive hemorrhage group had a significantly lower median birth weight (2085 [IQR: 1536.25–2367.5] g vs. 2200 [IQR: 1941.25–2487.5] g, p = 0.021). Neonatal mortality rates did not differ significantly between the two groups (7.3% vs. 3.5%, p = 0.433), nor did the rate of intrauterine fetal demise (18.2% vs. 11.8%, p = 0.289). Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were comparable between groups (median Apgar at 1 min: 7 [IQR: 5–8] vs. 7 [IQR: 6–8], p = 0.669; at 5 min: 8 [IQR: 7–9] vs. 9 [IQR: 8–9], p = 0.324). Umbilical cord pH values were also similar (7.30 [IQR: 7.28–7.37] vs. 7.33 [IQR: 7.29–7.38], p = 0.165). However, neonates born in the setting of massive hemorrhage required a significantly longer NICU stay (20 [IQR: 12–30] vs. 11 [IQR: 6–19.5] days, p = 0.010).

When neonatal outcomes were stratified according to histopathological subtype of placenta accreta spectrum, increasing depth of invasion was associated with earlier gestational age at surgery, lower birth weight, higher NICU admission rates, and prolonged NICU stay.

Discussion

In this cohort of women with PAS, cervical length was not independently associated with massive hemorrhage, indicating that cervical shortening alone does not reflect intraoperative bleeding risk. Instead, hemorrhagic severity was primarily driven by placental invasion depth and emergency surgical context, underscoring the dominant role of disease extent and clinical instability in determining maternal outcomes. Although shorter cervical length was associated with longer antepartum hospitalization, this likely reflects greater obstetric complexity rather than intrinsic hemorrhagic potential. Importantly, while cervical length demonstrated a gradient across PAS severity subtypes, its lack of independent association with massive hemorrhage suggests that it may function more as a marker of invasive placentation than as a predictor of surgical bleeding. Neonatal outcomes were largely mediated by prematurity, reinforcing that timing and urgency of delivery—rather than cervical shortening per se—are the principal determinants of perinatal morbidity in PAS.

In the present study, cervical length was not significantly associated with massive hemorrhage in women with placenta accreta spectrum. However, women with a shorter cervical length were more frequently admitted and required pregnancy termination due to vaginal bleeding, suggesting that cervical shortening may be related to antepartum clinical instability rather than intraoperative hemorrhagic severity. This finding partly contrasts with previous reports that proposed a role for cervical shortening in predicting bleeding risk in placental disorders. Mimura et al. demonstrated that a shorter cervical length in women with placenta previa was associated with increased intraoperative blood loss, suggesting that cervical shortening may reflect lower uterine segment involvement and greater surgical complexity [16]. In contrast to findings reported by Mimura et al., we did not observe a significant association between cervical length and massive intraoperative hemorrhage. Several pathophysiological and methodological differences may account for this discrepancy. First, Mimura et al. primarily evaluated women with placenta previa, whereas our cohort consisted exclusively of PAS cases. In placenta previa, cervical shortening may reflect progressive lower uterine segment remodeling and increased exposure of placental vasculature near the internal os, thereby predisposing to heavier bleeding during cesarean delivery. In contrast, in PAS, the depth and topography of placental invasion into the myometrium and surrounding tissues represent the dominant determinants of hemorrhagic severity. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, compared with placenta percreta, placenta accreta was independently associated with significantly lower odds of massive hemorrhage, whereas placenta increta did not show a statistically significant association, while cervical length did not. This suggests that once abnormal trophoblastic invasion is established, bleeding risk is primarily governed by the extent of myometrial disruption rather than cervical morphology. Furthermore, after adjustment for pathology type, gestational age at surgery, prior cesarean deliveries, and intraoperative complications, cervical length showed no independent predictive value, indicating that its apparent influence may be confounded by disease severity. Additionally, our study was conducted in a high-volume tertiary referral center with a standardized multidisciplinary PAS management protocol. Planned surgical timing, preoperative preparation, and experienced surgical teams may mitigate the potential impact of cervical shortening on intraoperative blood loss. Differences in hemorrhage definition thresholds across studies may also contribute to variability in reported associations. Collectively, these findings suggest that while cervical shortening in PAS is associated with antepartum vaginal bleeding and earlier delivery, it does not independently predict the magnitude of intraoperative hemorrhage. In PAS, placental invasion depth rather than cervical anatomy appears to be the primary determinant of surgical bleeding risk. The results of the current study find significant support in the work of Rac et al. [17], who also specifically evaluated cervical length in cases of pathologically proven placenta accreta. 1 Rac and colleagues found that although women with placenta accreta tended to have shorter cervical lengths than those with non-adherent previa, the cervical length measurements did not correlate with the degree of invasion, the rate of vaginal bleeding, or the occurrence of massive hemorrhage. 1 Specifically, they noted that short cervix findings did not increase the likelihood of hemorrhage necessitating urgent cesarean delivery (p = 0.24).

Similarly, Zaitoun et al. reported a higher incidence of antepartum bleeding among women with cervical length ≤ 30 mm, with most severe bleeding episodes necessitating emergency cesarean delivery before 36 weeks of gestation compared with those with longer cervical length [18].

A biologically plausible explanation for the association between a shortened cervix and deeper placental invasion is that progressive trophoblastic extension toward the internal os and cervico-isthmic region produces local structural remodeling of the lower uterine segment and cervical stroma. Invasive placentation is characterized by decidual deficiency and enhanced trophoblast proteolytic activity (including increased matrix metalloproteinase expression) that degrades extracellular matrix and disrupts normal myometrial–decidual architecture, changes that can manifest as measurable cervical shortening on imaging [19]. Clinical series have observed shorter cervical lengths in more invasive phenotypes and in operations that are technically more difficult (e.g., percreta), supporting the concept that cervical shortening is a marker of caudal disease topography rather than a primary causal factor for hemorrhage [18, 20]. Mechanistically, matrix metalloproteinases and other proteases that facilitate trophoblast invasion also participate in cervical extracellular-matrix remodeling and cervical shortening, which provides a molecular link between invasion depth and cervical morphology [21]. Importantly, however, the presence of a shorter cervix appears to identify disease topography and antepartum instability (for example, increased rates of vaginal bleeding and preterm delivery) rather than to be an independent determinant of intraoperative hemorrhage; in PAS the magnitude of bleeding is more directly related to the extent of myometrial and parametrial vascular disruption and to operative factors (timing, planned vs. emergency surgery, multidisciplinary preparedness). This interpretation is consistent with series that show an association between short CL and antepartum bleeding / emergency delivery (including placenta previa cohorts) but demonstrate that depth of invasion and surgical factors better predict intraoperative blood loss in PAS [16].

In our study, antepartum vaginal bleeding was more frequently observed among women with a cervical length < 25 mm, supporting the concept that cervical shortening may reflect increased antepartum clinical instability in PAS. Consistent with this observation, a 145-case management algorithm focusing on women with PAS in whom cervical length was measured using magnetic resonance imaging, stratified patients according to cervical length and the number of antepartum bleeding episodes [22]. This algorithmic approach demonstrated a dose–response relationship between bleeding frequency and earlier gestational age at delivery and supported individualized timing of delivery within 34–35+ 6 weeks of gestation. Specifically, patients with cervical length ≥ 25 mm and no bleeding episodes delivered at a median gestational age of 35+ 5 weeks, whereas those with cervical length < 25 mm and two or more bleeding episodes delivered substantially earlier, with a median gestational age of 33+ 1 weeks [22]. Importantly, urgent deliveries triggered by bleeding events were associated with worse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including higher rates of NICU and ICU admission and increased transfusion requirements (p < 0.05) [8, 22, 23]. Collectively, these findings align with our results and highlight the clinical value of integrating cervical length assessment with bleeding history to optimize delivery planning and reduce unplanned morbidity in PAS.

Adverse neonatal outcomes in PAS are largely driven by preterm birth, which may be precipitated by antepartum bleeding and obstetric instability, particularly in the presence of a short cervical length. In our study, neonates born to mothers with a shorter cervical length required longer NICU admission, an association that was largely mediated by earlier gestational age at delivery, reflecting the indirect impact of cervical shortening on neonatal morbidity through earlier delivery rather than a direct effect on hemorrhagic severity. This observation is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that women with shorter cervical length and bleeding episodes tend to deliver at earlier gestational ages. In a cohort evaluating cervical length and bleeding status, patients with cervical length ≥ 25 mm and no bleeding delivered at later gestational ages, whereas those with cervical length < 25 mm experienced earlier deliveries, often following recurrent bleeding episodes [17]. Earlier and urgent deliveries in these populations have been consistently associated with increased NICU admission and higher neonatal morbidity [17]. Moreover, emergency delivery itself may independently contribute to adverse neonatal outcomes, as urgent operative intervention often reflects acute maternal instability and limited opportunity for optimal antenatal preparation, including corticosteroid administration and planned neonatal support. In addition, as a tertiary referral center, we frequently receive patients transferred under urgent conditions due to active bleeding or clinical deterioration. Such emergency presentations may contribute not only to earlier delivery but also to prolonged neonatal hospitalization, as infants born in unstable maternal settings often require closer monitoring and extended NICU support. In our study, massive maternal hemorrhage was similarly associated with lower gestational age at delivery, reduced birth weight, and prolonged neonatal hospitalization, further supporting that prematurity—rather than cervical length per se—is the principal determinant of adverse neonatal outcomes. Supporting these findings, Polat et al. reported significantly lower neonatal birth weights among cases complicated by massive hemorrhage and shorter cervical length in an MRI-based series [24]. Collectively, these data suggest that cervical length and bleeding status influence neonatal outcomes primarily by determining the timing and urgency of delivery, with emergency surgical context further amplifying neonatal risk, rather than through direct fetal compromise or by independently increasing surgical hemorrhagic severity.

Strength and limitation

This study was conducted at a high-volume tertiary referral center in southern Iran with substantial expertise in the diagnosis and multidisciplinary management of placenta accreta spectrum disorders [25–27]. The standardized clinical approach and experienced surgical and neonatal teams enhance the reliability of maternal and neonatal outcome assessment. In addition, stratifying patients based on a clinically relevant cervical length threshold (< 25 mm versus ≥ 25 mm) allowed for meaningful risk differentiation and facilitated comparison with previously published algorithms. Importantly, whereas most previous studies have evaluated cervical length primarily in the context of placenta previa, our study specifically examined cervical length in patients with placenta accreta spectrum, thereby addressing a relative gap in the literature and extending current knowledge to a higher-risk and surgically complex population. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The single-center design may limit the generalizability of our findings to other settings with different patient populations or levels of expertise in PAS management. The observational nature of the study precludes causal inference, and cervical length was recorded at a single or two times, which may not fully capture dynamic cervical changes during pregnancy. Additionally, we did not include a history of spontaneous preterm birth in the multivariable logistic regression model, which may represent a potential confounding factor in the association between cervical length and preterm delivery. Furthermore, we did not evaluate cervical volume or cervical consistency (tissue characteristics), parameters that may provide complementary information regarding cervical remodeling and mechanical integrity. Furthermore, although our overall sample size was adequate for primary analyses, the study may have been underpowered to detect modest differences in hemorrhagic outcomes within PAS severity subgroups (accreta, increta, and percreta), raising the possibility of a type II error. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings across diverse clinical environments and to allow adequately powered subgroup analyses according to invasion depth and surgical outcomes. Prospective studies incorporating detailed obstetric history, including prior preterm birth, as well as quantitative assessment of cervical volume and biomechanical properties, may further refine risk stratification models in placenta accreta spectrum. Serial cervical length measurements may ensure better risk stratification and clarify the temporal relationship between cervical shortening, bleeding episodes, and adverse perinatal outcomes. Incorporating cervical length into standardized, prospective management algorithms may further optimize individualized delivery timing and improve both maternal and neonatal outcomes in placenta accreta spectrum.

Conclusion

In women with placenta accreta spectrum, a short cervical length was not independently associated with massive intraoperative hemorrhage. Nevertheless, shorter cervical length was significantly associated with increased antepartum vaginal bleeding, earlier gestational age at delivery, and prolonged neonatal intensive care unit admission. The observed adverse neonatal outcomes were largely mediated by prematurity, particularly in pregnancies complicated by recurrent bleeding and urgent delivery.

Taken together, these findings suggest that cervical length alone may be an insufficient parameter for determining the optimal timing of delivery in placenta accreta spectrum. Greater clinical emphasis may need to be placed on other anatomical and disease-severity markers—such as loss of clear zone, thinning of myometrium, and placental invasion characteristics—along with bleeding history, when planning delivery. A comprehensive, multidimensional risk assessment approach may therefore be more appropriate for individualized delivery planning, with the goal of reducing unplanned emergency procedures and optimizing both maternal and neonatal outcomes. Prospective studies are warranted to validate integrated risk-based strategies for delivery timing in this high-risk population.
