War trauma and psychomotor developmental disorders in children aged 6–12 in Lubumbashi: a comparative study
Authors: Joëlle Ngoie Musasa, Malko Ibrahim, Philippe Kaganda, Adélaïde Blavier, Jennifer Foucart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Persistent armed conflict in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo has resulted in large-scale population displacement, exposing many children to traumatic experiences. Although the psychological consequences of such exposure are well documented, its impact on psychomotor development remains insufficiently investigated, particularly in African settings affected by chronic violence.Objective: This study aimed to compare the psychomotor development of displaced children living in Lubumbashi according to their level of exposure to war-related trauma (direct, indirect, or none) and to examine the potential moderating role of school attendance and parental support.Method: The sample comprised 300 children aged 6–12 years (M = 9.55, SD = 2.06) living in Lubumbashi, Democratic Republic of the Congo. Participants were classified into direct exposure, indirect exposure, or no exposure groups based on structured interviews, caregiver reports, and contextual assessments consistent with DSM-5 trauma-exposure criteria. Post-traumatic symptoms were assessed using the French version of the Child Post-Traumatic Stress Reaction Index (CPTS-RI), and psychomotor development was evaluated using the Movement Assessment Battery for Children–Second Edition (MABC-2). Group differences were examined using robust ANOVA and Yuen’s post hoc tests (Yuen, K. K. [1974]. The two-sample trimmed t for unequal population variances. Biometrika, 61(1), 165–170).Results: Trauma exposure significantly affected psychomotor performance, F(2, 111.89) = 458.90, p < .001, η² = .93. Children exposed to trauma, whether directly or indirectly, exhibited significantly lower psychomotor scores than unexposed children. No significant difference was observed between the direct and indirect exposure groups. Trauma exposure emerged as the strongest predictor of psychomotor outcomes, whereas age, school attendance, and parental support were not significant predictors (adjusted R² = .77).Conclusion: Exposure to war-related trauma was strongly associated with impaired psychomotor development in displaced children. Direct and indirect exposure were associated with comparable psychomotor vulnerabilities, underscoring the need for integrated psychosocial and developmental interventions in conflict-affected settings.KEYWORDS: War trauma, psychomotor development, children, trauma exposure, parental support, school attendance

Introduction

For several decades, recurrent armed conflicts in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), particularly in the North Kivu and South Kivu provinces, have generated repeated waves of population displacement affecting large numbers of children. Forced displacement exposes children to unstable environments characterised by the loss of secure reference points, including stable caregivers, attachment figures, family routines, educational continuity, community support systems, and access to essential resources. Such conditions increase the risk of developmental disturbances, particularly in the psychomotor domain, which encompasses the integration of motor coordination, balance, body awareness, spatiotemporal organisation, and the cognitive-emotional processes that support movement and adaptive functioning (Beaulieu, 2021; Bernhardt et al., 2024).

The continuing nature of this humanitarian crisis is reflected in recent reports indicating that, between January and April 2025, renewed violence in eastern DRC led to large-scale population displacement, including an estimated 2.6 million children seeking refuge in Lubumbashi (UNICEF, 2025). Although these figures postdate the present study, they underscore the ongoing relevance of investigating the developmental consequences of forced displacement among Congolese children.

Recent findings in developmental neuroscience indicate that these early adversity contexts can affect brain maturation, particularly in fronto-limbic circuits involved in emotional regulation, motor planning, and sensory integration. In this context, toxic developmental stress refers to prolonged and intense activation of the stress response system in the absence of adequate protective relationships, leading to disruptions in brain development and regulatory processes (Da Silva & Akian, 2021) These effects may vary according to developmental timing, particularly during sensitive periods extending from early childhood to preadolescence, when neural plasticity is especially pronounced. Structurally, such exposures are associated with alterations in key brain regions, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex, which are involved in memory, emotional regulation, and executive functions (Yu et al., 2023). Neuroimaging studies further confirm that exposure to chronic stress or early trauma alters neural connectivity and brain structure, leading to lasting effects on behaviour and cognitive functioning (Buenrostro-Jáuregui et al., 2025). Recent large-scale neurodevelopmental studies have further demonstrated that early-life stress is associated with alterations in cortical development and brain maturation trajectories, which in turn shape cognitive and psychiatric outcomes across development (Kuang et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023).

Child psychomotor development constitutes a fundamental aspect of overall child development, resulting from the dynamic interaction among motor, cognitive, affective and social dimensions (Beaulieu, 2021). From the earliest years, experiences within the immediate environment play a crucial role in shaping body schema, motor organisation, and the emergence of adaptive functioning (Ajuriaguerra, 1980; Boutinaud, 2017). This adaptive functioning encompasses the set of skills that enable the child to respond appropriately to environmental stimuli, regulate emotions, solve problems, and maintain effective social interactions (Tsuchigahata, 2023). However, these developmental processes can be profoundly disrupted when a child is exposed to traumatic events, particularly in contexts of war and armed violence (Romano, 2022).

Furthermore, recent research in cognitive and developmental neuroscience shows that physical activity and motor experiences play a critical role in structuring neural networks, influencing neurogenesis, myelination, and functional connectivity (Yu et al., 2023). These mechanisms are directly involved in cognitive and psychiatric trajectories and may be disrupted in contexts of chronic insecurity characterised by ongoing exposure to armed violence, forced displacement, uncertainty regarding personal safety, disruption of daily routines, and limited access to basic resources, as well as material deprivation (Wu et al., 2025). Early neurodevelopmental alterations are also associated with an increased risk of later psychiatric disorders, including anxiety, depression, and stress-related disorders, particularly when exposure occurs during sensitive developmental periods (Kuang et al., 2023). In this regard, physical activity and motor experiences influence brain plasticity and the organisation of neural networks underlying cognition and emotional regulation; consequently, psychomotor alterations observed in children exposed to adversity may contribute to later cognitive and psychiatric difficulties, highlighting psychomotor rehabilitation as a key preventive and therapeutic intervention for supporting neurodevelopment in vulnerable populations (Wu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025).

Armed conflicts expose children to extreme experiences, such as the loss of loved ones, forced displacement, destruction of their living environment, and disruption of attachment bonds (Giromini, 2024). When these experiences occur during sensitive periods of development, they can have lasting consequences not only for mental health but also for the child’s bodily and psychomotor development (Mukosa, 2023). The importance of environmental stability and continuity of care has been highlighted in several studies of children exposed to war, showing that family and community support can mitigate adverse effects (Shazeen Suleman et al., 2025).

Furthermore, traumatic exposure experienced by parents or parental figures can produce a transgenerational effect, whereby the traumatic experiences of one generation influence the psychological and motor development of children through familial, emotional, and environmental mechanisms (Daskalakis et al., 2021). During development, repeated exposure to an environment of insecurity, persistent parental distress, or dysregulated parental responses can shape the child’s emotional regulation systems, world representations, and adaptive strategies, even in the absence of direct exposure to a potentially traumatic event (Bouvier, 2019; Even et al., 2024). Epigenetic and developmental psychology research indicates that chronic parental stress can alter stress-related gene expression in children, influencing both neurodevelopmental processes and emotional development (Soussignan, 2023).

Even et al. (2024) also distinguish between direct trauma, which corresponds to personal exposure to a violent or threatening event, and indirect trauma, which is related to violence experienced by close others or observed within the familial and social environment. This distinction is consistent with DSM-5 conceptualizations of traumatic exposure in children (Crocq & Guelfi, 2015), which differentiate direct exposure, witnessing traumatic events, learning that traumatic events occurred to close relatives, and repeated exposure to aversive details of traumatic events.

Several studies (Abi-Habib et al., 2025; May & Wisco, 2016; Rubinstein et al., 2025) emphasise that exposure to a traumatic event can lead to significant psychological consequences, whether direct (i.e. personal interaction with the stressor) or indirect (through observation, testimony, or media). However, the underlying psychological mechanisms and clinical effects differ depending on the nature of the exposure (Even et al., 2024). Additional research confirms that indirect exposure, including through observation or narrative, can result in significant distress and behavioural alterations in children (Daskalakis et al., 2021).

On the one hand, direct exposure to traumatic events is classically associated with greater intensity of post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) and more pronounced trauma-related cognitive patterns (Kube et al., 2023). Among populations exposed to armed conflicts, a substantial proportion of directly exposed individuals exhibit high levels of PTSS and dissociation, reflecting a symptom profile characterised by personal experience of danger and imminent threat. However, a notable percentage of individuals indirectly exposed to conflict through media exposure, narratives or social proximity can also reach clinical thresholds of significant psychological distress in the absence of direct contact with the stressor (Rubinstein et al., 2025).

Despite the abundance of research on the psychological consequences of armed conflicts such as anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, or behavioural disorders, bodily and developmental manifestations are often overlooked (Moro et al., 2024). Yet, alterations in muscle tone, coordination, laterality, or spatiotemporal organisation constitute early indicators of bodily distress related to traumatic experiences (Bonneil & Meunier, 2020; Ngoie Musasa et al., 2025; Pireyre, 2021). Early psychomotor manifestations are reliable indicators of children’s stress and well-being, as supported by research on motor development and psychological health (Scialom et al., 2015). In this context, psychomotor development, which relies on the continuous interactions between the child and their environment, appears particularly vulnerable when safe reference points collapse and environmental stimuli become chaotic (Mestre & Veyret-Morau, 2024; Paggetti, 2020).

Accordingly, this study aims to compare psychomotor development among three groups of children aged 6–12 years living in Lubumbashi according to their level of war-related trauma exposure (direct exposure, indirect exposure, or no reported exposure). Based on previous literature, we hypothesised that children exposed to war-related trauma would demonstrate lower psychomotor performance than non-exposed children. We further explored whether school attendance and parental support moderated the relationship between trauma exposure and psychomotor development.

Research methodology

Population and sample composition

The present study focused on a population of children originating from the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). For the exposed groups, participants primarily came from the North Kivu and South Kivu provinces, regions chronically affected by armed conflicts. Following these violent events, the children were displaced to the city of Lubumbashi, located in the Haut-Katanga province (UNICEF, 2025). Data collection was conducted between July 2023 and October 2024.

The sample was divided into three groups according to the type of war-related traumatic exposure: direct exposure, indirect exposure, and no exposure (control group). Each group consisted of 100 children, resulting in a total sample of 300 participants, including 110 girls and 190 boys. The mean age of the sample was 9.55 years (SD = 2.06).

Participants were classified into one of three exposure groups prior to the administration of psychological and psychomotor assessments. Group allocation was based exclusively on documented exposure status and was established through structured clinical interviews, caregiver reports, developmental history, and contextual assessment of displacement trajectories and war-related experiences. Exposure classification followed predefined operational criteria derived from DSM-5 definitions of trauma exposure (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Crocq & Guelfi, 2015).

Post-traumatic stress symptoms and psychomotor functioning were assessed after group classification and were considered study outcome variables. Neither psychological symptoms nor psychomotor performance were used as inclusion or exclusion criteria. This procedure was adopted to avoid circularity bias and to ensure a clear distinction between trauma exposure and trauma-related symptomatology.

Although all participants in the exposed groups were recruited from community settings, all children were assessed using identical procedures, standardised instruments, and testing conditions administered by the principal investigator. Nevertheless, the differential recruitment strategy may have introduced a selection bias, as children receiving psychological support may have been more vulnerable than children recruited from community settings.

Group 1: Direct Exposure

Group 1: Direct Exposure

The first group consisted of children who had been directly exposed to war-related traumatic events. Participants were between 6 and 12 years of age and originated from North Kivu or South Kivu provinces. All had lived for at least six months in areas affected by armed conflict before being forcibly displaced to Lubumbashi.

Direct traumatic exposure was operationalised according to DSM-5 criteria for trauma exposure (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Crocq & Guelfi, 2015). Direct exposure referred to first-hand experience of actual or threatened death, serious injury, armed attacks, bombardments, physical violence, forced displacement, or other life-threatening war-related events. Only direct personal exposure was considered for classification in this group.

Following their arrival in Lubumbashi, children were identified and referred by humanitarian organisations, including UNICEF and the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), to the Bakanja centre, a facility specialising in psychosocial support for vulnerable children affected by armed conflict.

At the time of data collection, all children in this group had resided continuously in Lubumbashi for a period ranging from 9 to 12 months. Eligibility was based exclusively on documented direct exposure to war related traumatic events. Exposure status was established through structured clinical interviews, caregiver reports, developmental history, and contextual review of displacement experiences. Trauma- related symptoms were assessed subsequently and were not used to determine eligibility.

The role of the Bakanja centre was limited to identified potentially eligible participants, facilitating contact with parents or legal guardians, and providing an appropriate setting for assessments. The centre was not involved in data collection, administration of instruments, statistical analyses, or interpretation of findings.

Children were excluded if no evidence of direct war-related traumatic exposure could be established, if they resided in Lubumbashi for less than nine months, if they presented severe neurological disorders likely to interfere with assessment procedures, or if informed consent could not be obtained.

Group 2: Indirect Exposure

Group 2: Indirect Exposure

The second group consisted of children who had not been directly exposed to armed violence but had been indirectly exposed to war-related trauma through the experiences of parents, siblings, or other close relatives directly affected by armed conflict. Participants were between 6 and 12 years of age and originated from North Kivu and South Kivu provinces. Their parents had experienced direct exposure to armed violence and had subsequently fled to internally displaced persons camps, refugee camps, or other temporary settlements. Some children were born in displacement settings, whereas others spent part of their early childhood in such environments without directly experiencing armed violence themselves.

Families subsequently relocated through different regions before settling in Lubumbashi. Children in this group were identified through the Bumi Center, a facility providing psychological services to displaced and vulnerable children. Indirect traumatic exposure was defined according to DSM-5 informed criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Crocq & Guelfi, 2015) and referred to exposure occurring through close relatives, family disruption, forced displacement, chronic conflict-related insecurity, or repeated exposure to traumatic narratives concerning war-related events. Children in this group had not directly experienced armed attacks, bombardments, physical violence, or other forms of direct victimisation.

The absence of direct exposure was systematically verified through structured clinical interviews, caregiver reports, developmental history, and contextual assessment of conflict-related experiences and displacement trajectories. Eligibility was based exclusively on documented indirect exposure. Psychological symptoms, including post-traumatic stress reactions, anxiety, sleep disturbances, hypervigilance, and emotional or behavioural difficulties, were assessed after group classification and were considered study variables rather than inclusion criteria.

Children were excluded if evidence of direct exposure to armed violence was identified, if they did not meet predefined criteria for indirect exposure, if severe cognitive impairments interfered with assessment procedures, or if informed consent could not be obtained.

Group 3: Control Group (No traumatic exposure)

Group 3: Control Group (No traumatic exposure)

The control group consisted of children aged 6–12 years who had never been exposed, either directly or indirectly, to war-related traumatic events. All participants had continuously resided in Lubumbashi and had no history of residence in conflict-affected areas. The absence of direct or indirect exposure was established through caregiver reports, developmental history, and structured screening interviews documenting the absence of conflict-related experiences, forced displacement, exposure to armed violence, or traumatic events affecting close relatives. Children in this group were recruited from churches and community organisations in Lubumbashi following authorisation from community leaders and written informed consent from parents or legal guardians. Eligibility was based exclusively on the absence of documented direct or indirect war-related traumatic exposure was identified, if they had previously lived in conflict-affected areas, if severe neurological disorders interfered with assessment procedures, or if informed consent could not be obtained. To ensure comparability across groups, all participants were assessed using identical procedures, measurements, and testing conditions administered by the principal investigator. The absence of exposure to armed violence, whether direct or indirect, was verified through caregiver reports, developmental history, and structured screening interviews documenting the absence of conflict-related experiences.

Children in the control group were recruited from various churches in the city of Lubumbashi, with the approval of religious leaders and following the acquisition of written informed consent from parents or legal guardians. These children had no documented history of direct or indirect exposure to armed violence. Only children for whom written informed consent had been obtained were included in the study.

Children were excluded from the control group if they had lived, even temporarily, in a conflict- affected area, if they presented any form of direct or indirect traumatic exposure regardless of its nature, or if they exhibited severe psychological disorders. The absence of severe psychological disorders was established through caregiver reports and structured clinical screening interviews conducted during participant recruitment.

The absence of written informed consent also constituted an exclusion criterion. To ensure the comparability of results across groups, children in the control group were assessed using the same procedure as that applied to the other study groups, employing identical measurement instruments and under similar testing conditions. The absence of traumatic exposure in the control group was established through structured screening interviews, caregiver reports, developmental history, and contextual assessment of conflict-related experiences. The CPTS-RI was administered only to assess post-traumatic symptom severity and was not used to determine exposure status or eligibility (Table 1).

Sample construction method and post hoc comparability

Group formation followed a post hoc comparability approach designed to ensure that participants were comparable on key sociodemographic and contextual variables potentially associated with psychomotor development. Children were initially recruited according to their documented level of war-related trauma exposure (direct exposure, indirect exposure, or no exposure). Following recruitment, comparability analyses were conducted to evaluate whether groups differed significantly on baseline characteristics likely to influence psychomotor outcomes. Variables examined included age, sex, school attendance, and parental support.

Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, whereas continuous variables were examined using robust statistical procedures when assumptions of normality were not met. This approach allowed the identification of potential confounding factors and provided a basis for subsequent multivariate analyses. Although participants were not randomly assigned to groups, efforts were made to ensure that observed differences in psychomotor functioning could reasonably be attributed to differences in trauma exposure rather than to major sociodemographic disparities. Variables showing potential associations with psychomotor outcomes were subsequently included as covariates in regression analyses.

Methodological instruments

Child post-traumatic stress reaction index (CPTS_RI)

Exposure to war-related potentially traumatic events was assessed independently from symptom measurement using a structured clinical interview conducted by the principal investigator. The interview systematically documented children’s experiences of conflict-related events, including armed attacks, bombardments, physical violence, psychological violence, forced displacement, injuries, family separation, loss of relatives, exposure through close family members, and displacement history. The interview followed a standardised assessment protocol developed for the study. The interview included standardised questions regarding direct exposure to armed attacks, bombardments, witnessing violence, physical injuries, forced displacement, family separation, loss of relatives, displacement history, exposure through close family members, and perceived threats to life.

Information obtained from children was cross-validated whenever possible through caregiver reports and contextual information concerning displacement trajectories and war-related experiences. Based on these data, participants were classified into three mutually exclusive categories:

Indirect exposure, defined as exposure occurring through close relatives, family disruption, chronic conflict-related insecurity, forced displacement, or repeated exposure to traumatic narratives without direct personal victimisation.

No exposure, defined as the absence of documented direct or indirect war-related traumatic experiences. When multiple forms of exposure were reported, classification was based on the highest level of exposure identified, with direct exposure taking precedence over indirect exposure because direct exposure is generally associated with greater proximity to the traumatic event and higher levels of trauma severity documented in the psychotrauma literature.

The structured clinical interview described above constituted an independent assessment procedure developed specifically to determine participants’ war-related trauma exposure and to classify children into the predefined exposure groups. This interview was conducted prior to the administration of the CPTS-RI and was conceptually and methodologically distinct from the CPTS-RI, which was used exclusively to assess post-traumatic stress symptom severity after group allocation.

Post-traumatic stress symptomatology was subsequently assessed using the child Post-traumatic Stress Reaction Index (CPTS-RI), a standardised 20-item instrument designed to evaluate trauma-related symptoms in children aged 6–16 years. The French-language version validation by (Olliac et al., 2014) was used in the present study because French is the principal language of formal education and professional communication within the study setting.

The CPTS-RI assesses symptom clusters commonly associated with post-traumatic stress reactions, including intrusive recollections, avoidance behaviours, emotional distress, hyperarousal, and trauma-related cognitive responses. Items are rated on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (most of the time), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 80. Reverse scoring was applied to items 7,12, and 15. Higher scores indicate greater severity of post-traumatic stress symptoms.

The CPTS-RI was used exclusively to assess post-traumatic symptom severity and was not used to determine trauma exposure status, participant eligibility, or group allocation. A score of 24 or higher was considered indicative of clinically significant post-traumatic symptomatology in accordance with previous validation studies (Olliac et al., 2014). All assessments were administered individually by the principal investigator using identical standardised procedures across study groups. For younger children, items were read aloud to minimise response bias and preserve response accuracy.

Traumatic events identified through structured clinical interviews, caregiver reports, and developmental histories were systematically coded according to exposure type and exposure level. Coding procedures were completed independently from CPTS-RI scores to ensure conceptual separation between trauma exposure and post-traumatic symptomatology. Exposure was classified into three mutually exclusive categories:

Direct exposure: children were classified as directly exposed when they personally experienced or directly witnessed war-related traumatic events involving actual or threatened death, serious injury, physical violence, armed attacks, bombardments, forced displacement under life-threatening circumstances, or comparable events.

No exposure: children were classified as non-exposed when no evidence of direct or indirect war-related traumatic exposure could be established through interviews, caregiver reports, developmental history, or contextual assessment. When multiple exposure modalities were identified, classification was based on the highest level of exposure documented. Exposure status (direct, indirect, or none) constituted the primary independent variable in all statistical analyses. This coding procedure ensured standardised, reproducible, and mutually exclusive classification across all participants.

Movement assessment battery for children (M-ABC-2)

Children’s psychomotor development was assessed using the Movement Assessment Battery for Children, Second Edition (M-ABC-2), an internationally standardised instrument for evaluating motor skills (Albaret et al., 2018; Henderson et al., 2015). The M-ABC-2 has demonstrated strong reliability and validity across diverse populations and is widely used in both clinical and research settings.

The assessment was conducted individually by the principal investigator in a calm, structured, and standardised environment. Each session lasted approximately 20–40 min, depending on the child’s age and level of cooperation. Assessments were conducted in the absence of parents or legal guardians to minimise external influence and ensure standardised testing conditions.(Henderson et al., 2015)

Raw scores were converted into standardised age-adjusted scores using normative tables provided in the M-ABC-2 manual, allowing comparison with international reference values. Higher scores indicate better motor performance and psychomotor functioning (Henderson et al., 2015; Verbecque et al., 2025).

Although the M-ABC-2 is internationally validated, it does not have normative data specific to the Congolese population. Therefore, interpretation of motor performance scores should be made with caution, as normative references may not fully reflect developmental trajectories and environmental conditions specific to Congolese children. This lack of population-specific norms constitutes a methodological limitation of the present study. Cultural and environmental differences, including variations in physical activity patterns, schooling conditions, and access to motor stimulation, may influence baseline motor performance. Additionally, displacement and trauma-related environmental instability may further affect motor development through reduced opportunities for structured or unstructured physical activity.

Contextual variables

Parental support and school attendance were assessed as dichotomous variables (yes/no). However, this operationalisation simplifies complex dimensions, such as the quality of schooling, regularity of attendance, or the nature of parental support (emotional, educational, material). This simplification constitutes a methodological limitation that may attenuate or obscure certain associations.

Parental support was assessed through caregiver interviews and coded as a dichotomous variable (0 = absence of regular parental support; 1 = presence of regular parental support). Regular parental support referred to the child’s access to consistent caregiving and emotional availability from at least one parent or primary caregiver. School attendance was also coded as a dichotomous variable (0 = not attending school regularly; 1 = attending school regularly) Regular attendance referred to enrolment and active participation in formal educational activities at the time of assessment.

These variables were included because previous research suggests that supportive caregiving environments and educational engagement may mitigate the negative developmental consequences of traumatic experiences. However, this operationalisation necessarily simplifies complex multidimensional constructs. The assessment did not capture qualitative aspects such as the emotional quality of parental support, parenting practices, educational achievement, school quality, or continuity of school attendance. Consequently, findings related to these variables should be interpreted cautiously.

Furthermore, the study did not assess several potentially relevant factors known to influence psychomotor development, including nutritional status, physical health, socioeconomic conditions, housing stability, and access to recreational activities. These variables may represent residual confounding factors and should be examined in future research.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted by the principal investigator between July 2023 and October 2024. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from parents, legal guardians, or institutional representatives when applicable. Children also provided verbal assent appropriate to their age and developmental level.

The data collection procedure followed the same sequence for all participants. It began with the verification of eligibility criteria and the acquisition of informed consent. Subsequently, a structured clinical interview was conducted to assess the participants’ history of trauma exposure. Caregiver-reported developmental and contextual information was then collected. Following this, the CPTS-RI was administered to evaluate post-traumatic stress symptoms, and the M-ABC-2 was subsequently administered to assess motor competence.

All assessments were administered using identical standardised procedures regardless of group membership. Exposure classification was completed before the administration of psychological and psychomotor measures to ensure methodological independence between exposure status and outcome variables. Data were initially entered into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets and subsequently exported to R statistical software (version 4.2.3) for data cleaning, verification, and statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Software (version 4.2.3). Prior to hypothesis testing, descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Continuous variables were summarised using means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Group comparability on baseline characteristics was assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables, examining potential differences in age category, sex distribution, parental support, and school attendance across the study groups.

Psychomotor performance scores obtained from the M-ABC-2 were analysed using robust statistical procedures because preliminary diagnostic analyses indicated violations of the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, as well as the presence of outliers. To compare psychomotor performance across the three trauma exposure groups, a one-way robust analysis of variance (ANOVA) based on 20% trimmed means was performed (Field & Wilcox, 2017; Mair & Wilcox, 2020). This approach is more resistant to non-normal distributions, heteroscedasticity, and extreme observations than conventional ANOVA methos.

When significant overall group differences were identified, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Yuen’s robust test for independent samples based on trimmed means (Mair & Wilcox, 2020; Yuen, 1974). The magnitude of group differences was assessed using robust eta squared (η²) with 95% confidence intervals, providing an estimate of effect size independent of statistical significance.

To further investigate the association between trauma exposure and psychomotor development, a multivariate linear regression model was fitted with psychomotor performance as the dependent variable. Trauma exposure category (direct exposure, indirect exposure or no exposure) was entered as the primary, while age, parental support, and school attendance were included as covariates to control for potential confounding effects. Because residual heteroscedasticity was detected, heteroscedasticity- consistent robust standard errors were applied to obtain more reliable parameter estimates and confidence intervals. For all analyses, statistical significance was established at p < .05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences at the University of Lubumbashi (FPSE/024/2024). Written informed consent was obtained from parents er legal guardians, along with verbal assent from the children. Anonymity and data confidentiality were strictly maintained.

Results

Sociodemographic distribution of the sample

The sample consisted of 300 children aged 6–12 years. The sex distribution showed a male predominance, with boys representing 63.3% of the sample (n = 190) and girls 36.7% (n = 110). Regarding education, 116 children were not attending school, while 184 were enrolled in school. Concerning the family context, 175 children received regular parental support, whereas 125 did not.

As seen in Table 2, significant differences in education (χ2(2) = 111.09, p < .001) and parental support (χ2(2) = 78.69, p < .001) were present between groups. The proportions of children who studied and benefited from parental support were markedly superior in the non-traumatized group. Individuals in the ‘No trauma’ group were also significantly younger (p = .001). However, there was no significant gender difference between groups (χ2(2) = 0.20, p = .90).

This exceptionally large effect size should be interpreted in light of the market differences observed between exposed and non- exposed groups.

The persistence of the association after adjustment suggests that the observed differences in psychomotor functioning were not fully explained by age, school attendance, or parental support.

Discussion

This study provides evidence of a strong association between war-related trauma exposure category and psychomotor functioning among displaced children living in Lubumbashi. Children classified as directly or indirectly exposed to war-related traumatic experiences demonstrated substantially lower psychomotor performance than non- exposed children. The exceptionally large effect size observed suggests that psychomotor functioning differed markedly across trauma exposure categories within this sample. However, these differences may not be attributable solely to exposure to war-related traumatic events. The experience of forced displacement itself may also contribute to poorer psychomotor functioning. Displacement is often associated with disruptions in daily routines, schooling, social networks, and access to healthcare and recreational opportunities, as well as increased socioeconomic hardship and chronic stress, all of which may adversely affect child development and psychomotor performance. The exceptionally large size should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Although exposure classification was established independently from psychomotor assessment and differentiation, the overlap between trauma exposure and displacement-related adversities makes it difficult to disentangle their respective contributions to psychomotor outcomes in the present study. Replication in independent samples is therefore necessary to confirm the magnitude of association and to further clarify the relative impact of war-related trauma and displacement experiences on psychomotor development.

Because of the cross-sectional nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal relationships. Nevertheless, the results are consistent with previous literature suggesting that exposure to armed conflict and forced displacement may be associated with developmental vulnerabilities affecting motor and psychomotor functioning. The observed psychomotor difficulties may be interpreted in light of developmental toxic stress models, which propose that prolonged exposure to threatening and unstable environments can interfere with multiple aspects of child development, including sensorimotor integration, motor coordination, and adaptive functioning (Heying & Witherington, 2024; Romano, 2022; Tsuchigahata, 2023).

These explanatory frameworks should be regarded as theoretical interpretations consistent with the observed findings rather than mechanisms directly demonstrated by the present study. Importantly, no physiological, neurobiological, or neuroendocrine measures were collected. Consequently, it is not possible to determine the specific biological pathways through which trauma exposure may be associated with psychomotor functioning. Furthermore, the present study was not designed to evaluate developmental trajectories over time. Consequently, factors such as duration of displacement, cumulative adversity, and timing of trauma exposure could not be examined directly. Existing developmental literature suggests that prolonged displacement may expose children to multiple environmental stressors, including instability, disrupted routines, educational interruptions, and reduced access to developmentally enriching experiences (McLaughlin et al., 2019).

Similarly, developmental timing may represent an important factor in understanding psychomotor outcomes. Early childhood constitutes a sensitive developmental period during which foundational sensorimotor abilities are acquired and refined. Exposure to severe adversity during this stage may therefore be associated with disruptions in postural control, motor coordination, and fine motor integration (Schaefer et al., 2022). By contrast, exposure occurring later in childhood may be more strongly associated with difficulties involving motor planning, sequencing, and executive control processes (Gee, 2022). Because developmental timing was not specifically assessed in the present study, these interpretations remain speculative and should be investigated through longitudinal research designs capable of examining age-specific developmental trajectories. Interesting, no significant differences in psychomotor functioning were observed between children classified as directly exposed and those classified as indirectly exposed to war-related trauma.

This finding suggests that indirect exposure should not necessarily be conceptualised as a less consequential form of exposure. Instead, both direct and indirect exposure may be associated with comparable developmental vulnerabilities within contexts characterised by chronic insecurity, displacement, and family disruption. Several explanations may account for this pattern. Children indirectly exposed to trauma often experience persistent environmental stressors, including parental distress, family instability, uncertainty regarding safety, and repeated exposure to traumatic narratives. These experiences may contribute to developmental difficulties even in the absence of direct victimisation (Lindenmuth et al., 2025).

The similarity between direct and indirect exposure groups may also be interpreted within frameworks of intergenerational and family-based transmission of stress (Bouvier, 2019; Even et al., 2024). Previous studies suggest that parental trauma may influence child development through emotional, behavioural, relational, and environmental pathways. However, because parental post-traumatic stress symptoms and family functioning were not directly assessed in the present study, the relative contribution of these mechanisms cannot be determined. Contrary to expectations, parental support and school attendance were not significantly associated with psychomotor functioning in the adjusted analyses (Faure, 2022).

Several explanations may account for this finding. First, both variables were assessed using dichotomous indicators that did not capture important qualitative dimensions such as consistency, emotional quality, educational engagement, or school environment. Second, the developmental consequences associated with war-related trauma exposure may have reduced the observed influence of these potentially protective factors. Finally, substantial heterogeneity within groups may have limited the ability to detect more subtle contextual effects. Similarly, age was not significantly associated with psychomotor functioning after adjustment for trauma exposure and contextual variables. Although psychomotor abilities generally improve with maturation, the relatively restricted age range and the strong association observed between trauma exposure category and psychomotor functioning may have reduced the detection of age-related differences within this sample (Cruz et al., 2022; Smith & Pollak, 2020).

The observed psychomotor difficulties may also reflect broader developmental processes. Contemporary developmental neuroscience increasingly recognises motor activity as an important contributor to the organisation of neural systems involved in cognition, emotional regulation, and mental health (Soussignan, 2023). However, because no direct measures of neurodevelopmental functioning, cognitive performance, or neural activity were included, these interpretations should be considered theoretical rather than empirically demonstrated within the present study. Recent literature additionally highlights the potential contribution of physical activity to child development. Regular physical activity has been associated with structural and functional brain adaptations, including improvements in neural connectivity, executive functioning, and emotional regulation (Bekhechi, 2019; Diamond & Ling, 2019).

Some authors have proposed that physical activity may exert neuroprotective effects in stressful environments through mechanisms involving stress regulation, neuroplasticity, and adaptive functioning (Bekhechi, 2019). Nevertheless, these mechanisms were not directly examined in the present study and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. From a developmental cognitive neuroscience perspective, psychomotor function may represent a useful indicator of broader developmental adaptation. Motor experiences contribute to the development of neural networks involved in executive functioning, attention regulation, and emotional processing (Wu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). Consequently, psychomotor difficulties observed among trauma-exposed children may reflect broader developmental vulnerability rather than isolated motor deficits alone. This interpretation remains consistent with contemporary developmental models but requires direct empirical investigation. In practical terms, these findings support the potential relevance of psychomotor assessment within psychosocial care programmes for children affected by armed conflict and forced displacement.

Psychomotor assessment may contribute to the early identification of developmental difficulties and may help guide multidisciplinary interventions that address motor, emotional, cognitive, and social dimensions of child development. Comparisons between direct and indirect exposure further highlight the complexity of trauma-related developmental processes. Previous research suggests that direct and indirect trauma may differ with regard to threat appraisal, attentional biases, intrusive memories, and cognitive representations of safety and vulnerability (Imbriano et al., 2022). Likewise, subjective well-being and family resources may moderate the developmental consequences of indirect exposure (Omar, 2015).

Furthermore, trauma-related difficulties associated with indirect exposure may be less readily identified by caregivers and professionals, potentially reducing access to appropriate support services (Holt et al., 2022). These findings reinforce the importance of recognising indirect trauma exposure as a clinically meaningful experience rather than viewing it solely as a milder variant of direct trauma. Finally, the present findings highlight psychomotor functioning as a potentially sensitive indicator of developmental difficulties among children living in contexts of armed conflict and displacement.

At the same time, interpretation should cautious given the absence of several potentially important variables, including nutritional status, physical health, living conditions, and direct measures of family functioning. Future studies incorporating these factors may help clarify the mechanisms linking trauma exposure and psychomotor development. Overall, the findings suggest that war-related trauma exposure, whether direct or indirect, is strongly associated with psychomotor functioning among displaced children. Although the underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood, the findings support the inclusion of psychomotor functioning as part of comprehensive assessments and interventions for children affected by armed conflict.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causality and developmental change. Although trauma exposure category was associated with psychomotor functioning, the temporal ordering of these associations cannot be established. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine developmental trajectories, cumulative effects of adversity, and potential recovery processes over time.

Second, trauma exposure was assessed using predefined exposure categories (direct exposure, indirect exposure, and no exposure). While this approach allowed standardised group classification, it did not capture the frequency, duration, chronicity, or cumulative severity of traumatic experiences. More comprehensive assessments of trauma exposure would allow a more precise examination of dose-response relationships. In addition, although the Children’s Revised Impact of Event Scale (CRIES-13) could have provided a more detailed assessment of post-traumatic stress symptoms, it was not included because the primary aim of the study was to investigate psychomotor functioning. Considerations related to assessment burden and the absence of locally validated norms also informed this decision.

Third, participants were recruited from different settings based on exposure status. Children in the exposed groups were recruited through psychosocial support centres, whereas children in the non-exposed group were recruited from community settings. This recruitment strategy may have introduced selection bias, as children receiving psychological services may represent a subgroup with greater psychosocial difficulties or more severe trauma-related consequences. Furthermore, children recruited through psychosocial support services may have presented higher levels of vulnerability than children recruited from community settings.

In addition, substantial baseline differences were observed across groups with respect to school attendance and parental support. Although these variables were included as covariates in multivariate analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Consequently, part of the observed association between trauma exposure and psychomotor functioning may reflect broader contextual differences between groups, as well as differences in recruitment pathways and service utilisation.

Fourth, several contextual factors known to influence child development were not assessed, including nutritional status, physical health, living conditions, socioeconomic circumstances, and access to developmental stimulation. The absence of these measures limits the ability to attribute group differences exclusively to trauma exposure or displacement-related adversity. Given the close overlap between war-related trauma and forced displacement in the present sample, disentangling their respective contributions to psychomotor outcomes remains challenging. Moreover, because these variables were not systematically assessed, the comparability of the exposure groups on these characteristics could not be established.

Fifth, parental support and school attendance were measured using dichotomous indicators that did not capture qualitative or contextual aspects of these resources. The absence of significant associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously. It is possible that the measures were insufficiently sensitive to detect protective effects or that the impact of trauma exposure reduced the influence of these factors. Likewise, the absence of an age effect may reflect limited variability within the sample or the pervasive influence of adversity across developmental stages.

Finally, no direct measures of neurocognitive functioning, physiological stress responses, parental mental health, or family functioning were included. Consequently, the mechanisms underlying the association between trauma exposure and psychomotor functioning remain unclear.

Future studies should adopt longitudinal and multidimensional designs integrating developmental, psychological, family, social, and biological factors to better understand the pathways linking war-related trauma and child development.

Conclusion

This study underscores the substantial burden of armed conflict–related trauma on the psychomotor development of children aged 6–12 years residing in the most severely conflict-affected areas of eastern the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

The findings indicate that children classified as directly exposed or indirectly exposed to war-related traumatic experiences demonstrated substantially lower psychomotor performance than children with no documented exposure to armed conflict. These differences were observed across both unadjusted and adjusted analyses and were associated with an exceptionally large effect size. Importantly, the present study assessed trauma exposure through predefined exposure categories established using structured clinical interviews, caregiver reports, and contextual assessment of conflict-related experiences. Post-traumatic stress symptoms were assessed separately using the CPTS-RI and were not used to define trauma exposure categories. This distinction strengthens the interpretation of the observed associations and reduces the risk of conceptual overlap between trauma exposure and post-traumatic symptom severity.

The absence of significant differences between direct and indirect exposure groups suggests that developmental vulnerability may extend beyond children who have personally experienced armed violence. Exposure to the consequences of conflict through family experiences, displacement-related adversity, and chronic insecurity may also be associated with reduced psychomotor functioning. Because of the cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of association rather than causation. Moreover, the close overlap between war-related trauma and displacement-related adversity in the present sample limits the ability to determine the specific contribution of each factor to psychomotor outcomes. Nevertheless, the results underscore the importance of considering psychomotor functioning as a relevant dimension of child development in conflict-affected populations.

From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings support the integration of psychomotor assessment into multidisciplinary services for children affected by armed conflict and forced displacement. Early identification of developmental difficulties may facilitate timely intervention and contribute to more comprehensive support strategies addressing motor, emotional, cognitive, and psychosocial needs. Future longitudinal and multidimensional studies incorporating developmental, family, contextual, and biological indicators are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these associations and to better understand developmental recovery among conflict-affected children.

Overall, the study highlights the importance of adopting a developmental perspective when examining the consequences of war-related adversity and contributes new evidence from a region where empirical research on child psychomotor functioning remains limited.
