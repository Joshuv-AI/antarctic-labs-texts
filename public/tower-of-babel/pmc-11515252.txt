Molecular insights into Solanum sisymbriifolium’s resistance against Globodera pallida via RNA-seq
Authors: Raquel Varandas, Cristina Barroso, Isabel Luci Conceição, Conceição Egas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe presence of potato cyst nematodes (PCN) causes a significant risk to potato crops globally, leading to reduced yields and economic losses. While the plant Solanum sisymbriifolium is known for its resistance to PCN and can be used as a trap crop, the molecular mechanisms behind this resistance remain poorly understood. In this study, genes differentially expressed were identified in control and infected plants during the early stages of the S. sisymbriifolium - G. pallida interaction.ResultsGene expression profiles were characterized for two S. sisymbriifolium cultivars, Melody and Sis6001, uninfected and infected by G. pallida. The comparative transcriptome analysis revealed a total of 4,087 and 2,043 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in response to nematode infection in the cultivars Melody and Sis6001, respectively. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis provided insights into the response of the plant to nematode infection, indicating an activation of the plant metabolism, oxidative stress leading to defence mechanism activation, and modification of the plant cell wall. Genes associated with the jasmonic and salicylic acid pathways were also found to be differentially expressed, suggesting their involvement in the plant’s defence response. In addition, the analysis of NBS-LRR domain-containing transcripts that play an important role in hypersensitive response and programmed cell death led to the identification of ten transcripts that had no annotations from the databases, with emphasis on TRINITY_DN52667_C1_G1, found to be upregulated in both cultivars.ConclusionsThese findings represent an important step towards understanding the molecular basis underlying plant resistance to nematodes and facilitating the development of more effective control strategies against PCN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05694-1.

Background

Potato cyst nematodes (PCN), Globodera pallida and G. rostochiensis, are soil-borne, microscopic, phytoparasitic nematodes that pose a significant threat to potato crops worldwide [1–3]. They are classified by the European and Mediterranean Plant Protection Organization (EPPO) as A2 pests [4], being regulated species in the EU since PCN presence is a considerable threat to potato (Solanum tuberosum) production and results in reduced yields and economic losses. In Portugal, both species are present in all potato-producing regions, with an increase in the presence of G. pallida [5] compared with earlier observations, where G. rostochiensis was prevalent [6–9].

Currently, several methods are used for PCN control. These include the use of resistant potato cultivars, such as G. rostochiensis-resistant cultivars. However, the exclusive reliance on G. rostochiensis-resistant cultivars has led to a prevalence of G. pallida in the fields. Crop rotation is another method for PCN control, but is not cost-effective due to the high turnaround time. Chemical nematicides are also employed to kill or inhibit nematodes, yet their use raises environmental and health concerns due to potential toxic effects on non-target organisms and soil ecosystems. The long survival period of the cysts in the soil and the lack of effective control methods make PCN a persistent challenge for potato growers. It is important to develop sustainable, effective, and non-chemical approaches. Some alternative methods have shown promising results, particularly when used as an integrative approach, like biological control, the use of genetically modified potato cultivars and trap crops [10].

A successful trap crop for PCN is a plant native to South America, from the Solanaceae family, S. sisymbriifolium. This plant can induce nematode hatching with the release of root exudates and attract the juveniles. PCN are obligatory sedentary endoparasites, and a crucial stage during their life cycle is the formation of the specialized feeding structure known as the syncytium [11, 12]. However, when attempting to establish the feeding site in this plant, the nematodes are unsuccessful, inhibiting further development [13, 14].

Despite its success as a trap crop, the direct use of a non-native plant in the fields should be limited to avoid becoming invasive or transmitting other pathogens to which the native plants lack resistance. Currently, little is known about the molecular basis of S. sisymbriifolium’s resistance to PCN. Identifying the genes involved will provide valuable data for the development of new PCN-resistant potato cultivars.

The recent advances in molecular biology have permitted the use of RNA-Seq (RNA sequencing), and particularly de novo sequencing, to uncover novel genes and to characterize plant defence and resistance mechanisms for the control pathogen infection. A study on the interaction between the soil-borne fungus Verticillium dahlia and S. sisymbriifolium utilized RNA-Seq to identify important genes, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) genes, WRKY genes and cytochrome P450 genes [15]. Leucine-rich repeat (LRR)-containing genes, that are important in the disease response, were identified in the plant Arachis duranensis as a response to Meloidogyne arenaria infection, using the same molecular technique [16]. Another study on S. torvum - M. incognita interaction used de novo sequencing and RNA-Seq to provide insights into the genetic basis of disease resistance mechanisms of this plant [17]. The use of RNA-seq is invaluable in identifying relevant resistant and defence genes for application in significant crops.

In this work, a transcriptome-wide overview of gene expression during the first stages of the G. pallida - S. sisymbriifolium interaction was obtained using RNA-seq, to identify genes differentially expressed in infected plants and provide insights into the innate resistance of S. sisymbriifolium to Globodera spp. infection.

Results

To better understand the resistance of Solanum sisymbriifolium to Globodera spp. infection, roots from two S. sisymbriifolium cultivars, cvs. Melody (Melody) and Sis6001 (Sis6001), non-inoculated and inoculated with G. pallida juveniles (3 biological replicates each), were collected three days after inoculation. This timing was chosen to capture early-stage transcriptome changes upon nematode attack, as previous studies reported that nematodes were dead after only four days post-inoculation [13, 14]. Total RNA extraction was performed on these samples, followed by sequencing.

The sequencing of the twelve samples yielded 1,034,702,020 raw sequences. Quality filtering resulted in 1,004,753,670 high-quality reads (Table 1).

The de novo transcriptome assembly resulted in 175,064 transcripts in the 12 samples, with an average of 37.8 million reads (SD ± 1.6) per condition. Transcripts had an average size of 911.07 bases, with a contig N50 of 1,568. The G. pallida-inoculated samples displayed more transcripts than their respective control samples; 95,951 (SD ± 8484) transcripts for the Melody-G. pallida-infected samples compared to 92,893 (SD ± 5769) transcripts of Melody-control and Sis6001-infected with 111,383 (SD ± 3138) transcripts compared to 95,809 (SD ± 4929) transcripts of Sis6001-control. The Sis6001-infected samples exhibited the highest number of transcripts on average. Transcripts were translated into amino acid sequences and annotated against the UniProt knowledgebase (UniProtKB) and the S. lycopersicum reference genome (Solanum DB). Only 2.83% of genes were annotated against the UniProtKB and 4.10% against the Solanum DB. The gene with the highest expression was common across all samples, TRINITY_DN72057_c5_g1, which encodes a Cytochrome P450-like protein.

Removal of low-abundance transcripts before DEG analysis resulted in 47,720 transcripts (Additional file 1), with 10.39% having annotations from the UniProtKB and 15.04% from the Solanum DB. For a selection of transcripts with no annotation (TRINITY_DN69758_C2_G1, TRINITY_DN26341_C0_G1, TRINITY_DN56000_C0_G1, TRINITY_DN60063_C0_G1, TRINITY_DN61037_C5_G1 and TRINITY_DN61080_C1_G2), their presence was validated through PCR amplification from cDNA (data not shown). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the gene-expression profiles confirmed that biological samples were grouped according to their source and highlighted the difference between the samples, revealing that infection by G. pallida affected both S. sisymbriifolium cultivars transcriptome (Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

Comparing each S. sisymbriifolium cultivar infected with G. pallida with its respective uninfected control, 4,087 significant DEGs were identified for Melody, 3,248 upregulated and 839 downregulated, and 2,043 for Sis6001, 1,344 upregulated and 699 downregulated (Fig. 1A, Additional file 3). Represented on the heatmap are all the significant DEGs with their expression level pattern supporting a differential response of the two cultivars to G. pallida infection (Fig. 2). Comparison of the DEGs present in both cultivars showed that 17% (876) of the genes were commonly upregulated and 0.8% (41) were commonly downregulated. Thirty genes, 0.6%, were upregulated in Melody and downregulated in Sis6001, and 0.4% (22) were downregulated in Melody and upregulated in Sis6001 (Fig. 1B, Additional file 4). The complete list of DEGs, with their expression ratios, adjusted p-values and annotations from Solanum database and UniProt knowledgebase is reported in Additional file 3.

Gene expression between inoculated and uninoculated plants is represented in the volcano plot (Fig. 1C/D). Highlighted are the ten most statistically significant genes with the highest fold changes. For both cultivars, many of these genes are unknown, with no annotation and/or predicted function. For Melody (Fig. 1C), the transcripts TRINITY_DN65542_C4_G4 (Solyc10g076405) and TRINITY_DN54901_C0_G2 (Solyc10g076405) matched the described genes in the Sol Genomics Network, but no further information about their function is known. TRINITY_DN60544_C0_G2 (Solyc06g059820) gene encodes an F-box protein and TRINITY_DN65444_C2_G1 (Solyc02g086600) is a Polyribonucleotide nucleotidyltransferase. For the remaining genes, a BLAST analysis against the nonredundant GenBank database indicated similarity with other submitted sequences: TRINITY_DN69830_C3_G1 to a predicted long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 8, TRINITY_DN71338_C2_G1 to a DEK domain-containing chromatin-associated protein and TRINITY_DN60544_C0_G1 to an F-box family protein. For Sis6001 (Fig. 1D), TRINITY_DN56000_C0_G1 was similar to a TMV resistance protein N-like and TRINITY_DN61430_C1_G2 to a Zinc-finger protein in a BLAST analysis.

For each cultivar, the top 10 upregulated (Table 2) and downregulated (Table 3) transcripts were listed, based on the calculated fold change and statistical significance (adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05). There were only two common DEGs between infected Melody and Sis6001 cultivars. The transcript TRINITY_DN69682_C3_G3 was upregulated in both cultivars, and TRINITY_DN61080_C1_G2 was downregulated in both cultivars. No annotations were found for these two genes, however, the comparison of the TRINITY_DN69682_C3_G3 amino acid sequence against the nonredundant GenBank database through BLASTx indicated a 100% identity with a G. pallida hypothetical protein GPALN_006057, suggesting this transcript is of nematode origin. The BLASTx search of the transcript TRINITY_DN61080_C1_G2 did not provide any hits, hindering the identification of the probable function, but the protein structure prediction with Phyre2 indicated an RNA binding protein, daz-associated protein 1 domain (confidence of 83.6% and coverage of 37%).

In Melody, besides the transcripts already mentioned in the previous volcano plot analysis, the most upregulated transcripts were Conserved oligomeric Golgi complex subunit 6 (TRINITY_DN68605_C4_G1) and an elongation factor 1-alpha protein (TRINITY_DN70285_C3_G1). Transcript TRINITY_DN58655_C3_G2 was identified as Solyc06g066447, but no function is described for this SolGenomics gene. The Phyre2 analysis predicted a structure similar to ribonuclease h (confidence of 99.4% and coverage of 70%). A BLAST analysis identified a similarity between TRINITY_DN70142_C1_G1 and a 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase. No function was found for the transcripts TRINITY_DN66843_C2_G3, TRINITY_DN61812_C1_G1, TRINITY_DN67496_C2_G1 and TRINITY_DN60828_C2_G1.

The most downregulated transcripts in Melody included F-box proteins and a polyribonucleotide nucleotidyltransferase, as mentioned previously, and a protein containing an AIG2-like domain (TRINITY_DN33962_C0_G2). For the transcript TRINITY_DN61080_C1_G1, the Phyre2 analysis indicated a similar result to the transcript TRINITY_DN61080_C1_G2, an RNA binding protein daz-associated protein 1. No function was found for TRINITY_DN61080_C1_G3 or TRINITY_DN60063_C0_G1.

In Sis6001, the most upregulated transcripts included a resistance protein Cc-nbs-lrr (TRINITY_DN56972_C0_G1), a Poly(A) RNA binding protein (TRINITY_DN63969_C0_G2) and an Argonaute protein (TRINITY_DN62156_C0_G2). TRINITY_DN71450_C1_G1 and TRINITY_DN65542_C3_G2 were annotated also with Solanum DB, but no function was described. The Phyre2 analysis indicated that the protein encoded by TRINITY_DN71450_C1_G1 might have a recombination function, corresponding to an iscth4 transposase (confidence of 100% and 40% coverage). No functions were detected for TRINITY_DN66325_C2_G1, the transcript with the highest fold-change value, for TRINITY_DN64899_C3_G5, and TRINITY_DN60306_C6_G3.

The most downregulated transcripts in Sis6001 included an Os07g0175100 protein (TRINITY_DN36695_C0_G2), a defensin-like protein (TRINITY_DN96251_C0_G1) and an F-box protein (TRINITY_DN58855_C7_G2). A BLASTx analysis TRINITY_DN65609_C1_G3 showed similarity to an ABC transporter G member 11 and of TRINITY_DN69850_C1_G2 to a BURP domain-containing protein. No function was identified for TRINITY_DN69758_C2_G1, the transcript with the lowest fold-change value, TRINITY_DN26341_C0_G1 and TRINITY_DN43053_C0_G1.

Expressed transcripts were analyzed for gene enrichment to better understand the physiological and signalling processes affected by the nematode infection, using the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Over-representation analysis (ORA) approaches.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed with WebGestalt tools [18] to evaluate all expressed transcripts ranked according to their score (calculated using signal-to-noise ratio, real values). A total of 12 ontologies were identified as significantly enriched (p < 0.05) for the Melody cultivar, three associated with biological processes (BP), 3 with cellular components (CC) and 6 with molecular function (MF). For the cultivar Sis6001, 14 ontologies were significantly enriched, three associated with BP, 4 with CC and 7 with MF (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Solanum sisymbriifolium is a successful trap crop for potato cyst nematodes (PCN), resistant to both Globodera pallida and G. rostochiensis. After invasion the development of the nematodes is quickly inhibited, however, the molecular mechanisms responsible for this resistance remain largely unknown. Identifying the genes involved in these mechanisms will help in the development of resistant S. tuberosum cultivars. In this study, an RNA sequencing analysis was conducted on the roots of two S. sisymbriifolium cultivars (cvs. Melody and Sis6001), both resistant to PCN [22, 23], to investigate the resistance mechanism through transcriptional responses to G. pallida infection.

The de novo transcriptome assembly resulted in 175,064 transcripts, which were then reduced to 47,720 transcripts after filtering out low-abundance transcripts. Similar transcript counts were reported in another study on S. sisymbriifolium-G. pallida interactions [24]. These transcripts were subsequently annotated against the UniProt knowledgebase and the S. lycopersicum reference genome from the Sol Genomics Network database, with matches of 10.39% and 15.04%, respectively. Although relatively low, the number of annotated transcripts is comparable to that obtained against the same reference genome in another study on S. sisymbriifolium response to a fungus (17%) [15]. Among the annotated transcripts, some had uninformative functional annotation, e.g., unknown, predicted, or uncharacterized proteins, providing little cues on their role in the plant due to the limited information on the S. sisymbriifolium genome or gene sequences in public databases. Additionally, non-annotated transcripts were experimentally validated by PCR and rt-PCR. Some of these non-annotated transcripts were among the top 10 differentially expressed transcripts, indicating the need for further studies on the characterization of these transcripts in S. sisymbriifolium.

The comparative transcriptome analysis between infected and non-infected roots identified 4,087 and 2,043 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in response to nematode infection, in the cultivars Melody and Sis6001, respectively. The higher number of identified DEGs, particularly evident in the cv. Melody, suggests an extensive transcriptional reprogramming occurring in response to nematode infection.

The total of DEGs for cv. Melody included 3,248 upregulated, with a mean log2 FC of 3.57, and 839, downregulated, mean log2 FC of -3.12. In the case of cv. Sis6001, there were 1,344 upregulated genes, with a mean log2 FC of 5.61, and 699 downregulated genes, with a mean log2 FC of -4.39. The more pronounced upregulation of genes in both cultivars is consistent with the plant defence against other pathogens at this infection stage [13, 15, 24]. Furthermore, cv. Melody exhibits a higher number of DEGs compared to cv. Sis6001, although in the latter there are transcripts with high statistical significance and fold change value, like transcripts TRINITY_DN69850_C1_G2 and TRINITY_DN66325_C2_G1. Earlier studies with both these cultivars have shown very similar phenotypical resistance responses to both Globodera species [22], and in this study, there were no significant differences in the number of second-stage juveniles inside the roots three days after inoculation between cultivars (Additional file 2: Fig. S2). Despite the phenotypic similarity, a distinct transcriptional response seems to exist between the studied S. sisymbriifolium cultivars. Only 17% of the genes were commonly upregulated between cultivars, with less than 1% being commonly downregulated. This variation in transcriptional response appears to support the activation of different defence pathways most probably caused by temporal responses, suggesting a delayed resistance response to the nematode in the cultivar Sis6001.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis provided valuable insights into the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions that were prominently affected by nematode infection, through two different approaches, the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), with a ranked expressed transcripts list, and Over-representation analysis (ORA), with the DEG list.

Analyzing the enriched GO terms associated with the complete expressed transcript list through GSEA, both cultivars showed similarities, namely with the enriched terms “proteolysis involved in protein catabolic process” and “translation”, suggesting a significant protein turnover and biosynthesis expected in the activation of the plant’s defence mechanisms against nematodes. Similar observations have been reported in other studies on plant-nematode interactions [15, 25]. In cv. Melody the GO terms associated with oxidative stress, “response to oxidative stress” and “peroxidase activity”, were also enriched, consistent with the findings of other studies of plant-pathogen interactions [17, 26–30]. Although these terms were not enriched in the cv. Sis6001, 11 genes encoding for peroxidases were also upregulated, as well as genes encoding for chaperones, also associated with stress responses [31].

The resistance response through oxidative stress in cv. Sis6001 was further validated with the ORA results for the upregulated genes, with an enrichment of “hydroquinone: oxygen oxidoreductase activity”. Several enriched GO terms were also associated with oxidative stress for the cv. Melody.

A relevant enriched GO term in cv. Melody was “syncytium formation” and other terms associated with the cell wall formation, also enriched in the cv. Sis6001 with the GO term “lignin catabolic process”. The enrichment of GO terms related to the metabolism of pectin, cellulose and lignin (“pectin catabolic process”, “pectinesterase activity”, “cellulose catabolic process” and “lignin catabolic process”), along with the upregulation of corresponding genes, suggests a reinforcement of the plant cell walls as a defence strategy against nematode penetration and has been also observed in other plant-nematode interactions, like potato and Globodera spp [26, 27]. and cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) and M. incognita [28] and even between plants and other pathogens like rapeseed (Brassica napus) and Plasmodiophora brassicae [32]. The syncytium formation is a crucial part of PCN development and the destruction of this feeding structure after the nematode has become sedentary leads to its death [10, 33]. The enrichment of terms associated with the formation and modification of this structure in the cv. Melody, such as “plant-type cell wall loosening” and “syncytium formation”, indicates that at the chosen time point for this study (3 days post inoculation), the nematode may still be inducing the syncytium, while the plant starts its resistance response through hypersensitive response. This involves the upregulating of genes associated with oxidative stress, such as “hydrogen peroxide catabolic process”, “peroxidase activity” and “oxidoreductase activity”, among others, that will lead to the death of the cells associated with the nematode feeding structure. Previous studies have reported localized cell death, where the syncytium was developing, at 4 days post-infection in S. sisymbriifolium roots [13, 14, 34].

Additionally, enriched GO terms and DEG associated with common defence and resistance pathways were also identified in one or both cultivars. The plant hormones salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) play important roles in the activation of plant defence mechanisms [35]. The gene NPR1, associated with the SA pathway, was found to be upregulated in cv. Melody, like the response of this plant to the fungus Verticillium dahlia infection [30]. From the JA pathway, the gene JAR1 was upregulated in cv. Melody and the gene LOX was downregulated in both cultivars.

Several of the top differentially expressed genes identified in this study, with higher log2 FC, have been identified in other plant-pathogen responses. Genes encoding 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenases, a key enzyme in the reaction involving the oxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), were found to be upregulated in both cultivars. This enzyme may play an important role in plant resistance by modulating JA, ethylene, and H2O2 pathways [36, 37]. Argonaute proteins, genes also upregulated in both cultivars, act in RNA interference, playing a role in the regulation of gene expression [38, 39]. In cv. Sis6001, one of the most significantly downregulated genes encodes a defensin-like protein (TRINITY_DN96251_C0_G1). Plant defensins have an important role on the defense against pathogens [40, 41] and while the expression of a defensin-like gene was found to be downregulation in syncytia induced by the cyst nematode Heterodera schachtii, the overexpression of this gene increased resistance to the nematode in Arabidopsis [42]. The same gene was found to be upregulated in cv. Melody, further indicating a difference in the temporal responses between cultivars.

Analysis of NBS-LRR (nucleotide-binding site–leucine-rich repeat) domain-containing transcripts, known for their involvement in hypersensitive response (HR) and programmed cell death [43–45], led to the identification of 166 transcripts, which were differentially expressed, including ten transcripts that had no annotations from the databases, with emphasis to TRINITY_DN52667_C1_G1, found to be upregulated in both cultivars (Additional file 8). The LRR (Leucine-rich repeat) proteins play a pivotal role in plant defence by recognizing pathogen-derived molecules, and effectors, subsequently activating defence responses. These responses include the initiation of HR, which leads to localized cell death at the site of invasion [46–48]. In S. sisymbriifolium, necrotic cells were observed around the syncytium [14] effectively restricting the development of the nematode and a similar defence pathway is activated by known PCN-resistant genes, like Gpa2 [49]. Another LRR-protein encoding R-gene is the tomato’s Mi-1 gene, which provides resistance against Meloidogyne spp. and other pathogens [50, 51]. In this study, genes annotated as Mi-1.1 were found to be upregulated in cv. Sis6001 (TRINITY_DN61853_C3_G1, TRINITY_DN67883_C2_G5, TRINITY_DN63768_C2_G1 and TRINITY_DN65019_C2_G1).

The identification of the novel NBS-LRR domain-containing transcripts is therefore important, as it provides valuable insights into how differently S. sisymbriifolium detects and responds to PCN invasion, on a molecular level, compared to other plants. These findings will contribute to future research aimed at developing effective strategies to enhance plant resistance against these nematodes.

Conclusions

The comprehensive transcriptomic analysis conducted on S. sisymbriifolium cultivars Melody and Sis6001 provided new insights into the plant’s resistance to Globodera spp. infection. These findings validated the involvement of known plant-pathogen-resistant mechanisms, indicating the effective utilization of the plant’s innate defense resources. On the other hand, the high differential expression of transcripts with no known function suggests the activation of other mechanisms against nematode infection that may drive or complement S. sisymbriifolium response, highlighting the importance of further studying this plant resistance. The deep understanding of S. sisymbriifolium resistance may be the key to developing resilient crop varieties against PCN, improving crop resistance and sustainable agricultural practices.

Methods

Plant material and inoculation

Solanum sisymbriifolium (cvs. Sis6001 and Melody) plants were grown from seeds placed in polystyrene plates with sterile peat in a glasshouse (20–25 ºC, 70–75% relative humidity and 12 h photoperiod). After germination, the plants were removed from the plates, transferred to plastic pots filled with sterile sandy soil and maintained under the same conditions already referred until inoculation.

Globodera pallida were isolated from a field in Bolho (Aveiro District, Portugal), where soil samples were collected. Cysts were extracted using a modified Fenwick can [52], isolated under a microscope and maintained in the laboratory on susceptible potato, S. tuberosum cv. Désirée.

Cysts of G. pallida were soaked in distilled water for 5–7 days to hydrate. After, the water was replaced by potato root exudate, collected from one-month old potato plants by leaching the soil with 50 mL distilled water [53]. Hatched second-stage juveniles (J2) were collected and counted. Roots of 21-days-old S. sisymbriifolium plants were inoculated with 200 J2. Three plants for each cultivar and condition, control (uninoculated plants) and inoculated, were selected after three days post inoculation (DPI). The roots were washed free of soil, separated from the aerial part, and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The 12 samples (two conditions x three biological replicates x two cultivars) were maintained at -80ºC until further processing.

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

Each root sample was powdered in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted and purified using the QIAGEN RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (RNeasy® Mini Handbook) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA quality (RNA integrity number, RIN > = 7) was monitored with the Agilent RNA 6000 Pico in the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) and quantified with Qubit RNA HS Assay kit in the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA). Twelve poly(A) enriched strand-specific libraries (a control and an infected sample, in triplicate, for each S. sisymbriifolium cultivar) were generated with the TruSeq Stranded mRNA sample preparation kit (Illumina) from approximately 0.5 mg of high-quality total RNA of each sample. Transcriptome sequencing was performed on a NextSeq 550 Illumina sequencer with the NextSeq High Output v2.5 Reagent Kit (150 cycles), according to manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Genoinseq (Cantanhede, Portugal).

De novo transcriptome assembly and functional gene annotation

Raw reads were extracted from Illumina Nextseq in fastq format with bcl2fasta version 2.20.0.422 (Illumina) and quality-filtered with fastp version 0.20.0 [54] to trim bases with an average quality lower than Q25 in a window of 5 bases, remove polyadenylated tails above 10 bases and reads with less than 50 bases. The ribosomal RNA content was estimated with SortMeRNA version 2.1 [55]. High-quality reads were de novo assembled into transcripts using Trinity version 2.2.0 [56]. Reads from each sample were aligned to the de novo transcriptome assembly with Trinity’s Bowtie module. Transcript abundance and count matrix were estimated using Trinity’s Transcript Quantification module with RSEM. Transcripts were translated into amino acid sequences with Transdecoder version 5.5.0 [57] and annotated with Diamond version 2.0.8 [58] against the UniProt knowledgebase (December 2020) and the version ITAG3.2 of the Solanum lycopersicum reference genome, downloaded from the Sol Genomics Network ftp site (https://solgenomics.net). Sequences were also mapped against the Globodera spp. genome, however, the number of reads obtained was low and not enough to identify and annotate transcripts.

For selected transcripts, manual BLAST (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/) and Phyre2 (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/~phyre2/) searches were done to improve annotation.

Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between group samples were analyzed from the count matrix in the iDEP.96 web tool [59] (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/idep96). During preprocessing, the genes with less than 0.5 counts per million (CPM) in at least one sample were discarded from further processing, and counts were normalized using the regularized log option (rlog) (Additional file 1). The distribution of the transformed data and density plots are provided in Additional file 2: Fig. S3. A PCA analysis successfully checked sample inter-and intra-group variability (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Differentially expressed genes were identified with DESeq2 with a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.05 and a minimum fold change of 2 (Additional file 3). Venn diagrams were created with Venny [60]. Volcano plots were created with VolcaNoseR [61].

Significant DEG were divided into upregulated and down-regulated lists for each cultivar to identify the enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) Bioinformatics Resource [19, 20], where an over-representation analysis (ORA) was performed on each gene list. The statistical significance of the enriched terms and pathways was determined based on a p-value threshold of 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing.

A gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify biological pathways that were significantly associated with the infection processes. A list of ranked genes was generated using the GenePattern platform [62], and selecting the GSEA module, with the signal-to-noise metric for ranking genes and real values. GSEA was carried out in WebGestalt [18, 63]. The enrichment analysis was performed with the default settings, using as the Functional Database the gene sets created based on the GO terms and KEGG pathways annotation for S. lycopersicum downloaded from the Sol Genomics Network ftp site (version ITAG4.1, https://solgenomics.net). The enriched pathways were identified based on an FDR threshold of 0.05.

Identification of potential NBS-LRR proteins encoded by the de novo assembled transcripts

Amino acid sequences were scanned and selected for the presence of two or more consensus sequences LxxLxLxxNxL/CxL (where x can be any amino acid), an essential structural element of LRR domain and the NBS–LRR proteins [64, 65]. The selected sequences were validated with a Pfam scan, through “hmmscan”, on the HMMER web server [66]. Only the sequences with detected relevant domains, LRR, NB-ARC and F-box, were chosen and added to a list of sequences with relevant annotations (Leucine-rich repeat-like protein, LRR receptor-like serine, Receptor like kinase, F-box, Cc-nbs-lrr, resistance protein and Disease resistance protein), and filtered to the DEG.

RT-qPCR validation

RT-qPCR of eleven genes (Additional file 9) validated differential expression patterns observed in the RNA-seq data. For each sample, 1 µg of extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed with iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and used in qPCR with iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA), according to standard protocols. qPCR was performed in the CFX 96 Touch Real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA) with a program consisting of 95 °C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 35 s. To ensure accurate and reliable results, initial efficiency optimization experiments were conducted. qPCR amplification efficiency was assessed by analyzing the slope of a standard curve, with values between 90% and 110%. All RT-qPCRs for each gene were performed with three biological replicates, and each biological replicate included three technical replicates. The genes SAND, RPN7 and MST2 were chosen as endogenous controls. The level of gene expression in the control sample was set to 1, and the relative repression of gene expression was determined using the normalized gene expression (ΔΔCq) analysis [67].
