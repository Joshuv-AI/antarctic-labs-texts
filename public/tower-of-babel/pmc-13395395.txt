Temporal Trends in the Incidence and Prevalence of Multiple Sclerosis in Sweden, 2001–2021
Authors: Astrid Pedersen, Fredrik Sandesjö, Peter Alping, Thomas Frisell, Fredrik Piehl, Katharina Fink, Kyla A. Mckay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnderstanding secular trends in demographic patterns of multiple sclerosis (MS) may help elucidate its etiopathogenesis and is also important for future resource allocation and planning healthcare strategies. Though several incidence and prevalence studies exist, nationwide studies utilizing contemporary data collected over decades remain rare. The objective of this study was to determine changes in incidence and prevalence of MS in Sweden from 2001 to 2021.MethodsLeveraging data from multiple linked population-based registries, we utilized two case definitions: one administrative (based on International Classification of Disease codes from inpatient and outpatient visits, 2006–2021) and one clinical (based on the Swedish MS registry, 2001–2021). We estimated the overall and annual incidence and point prevalence using both definitions and age-standardized to the world standard population. Temporal trends in incidence and prevalence were analyzed using joinpoint and binomial regression, respectively.ResultsIn total, 13,055 incident and 28,107 prevalent MS cases were identified in the administrative data. The overall age-standardized incidence rate was 8.37 (95% confidence interval [CI] 8.22–8.52) cases per 100,000 person-years. Incidence rates were stable until 2012, then decreased until 2021 (annual percent change −2.51%; 95% CI: −5.04% to −1.69%), which translated into a decrease in number of new MS cases from 796 in 2006 to 701 in 2021, or –12.0%. In the clinical data, the overall age-standardized incidence was 7.87 (95% CI: 7.74–7.99) cases per 100,000 person-years. Incidence increased between 2001 and 2014 with an annual percent change of 2.00% (95% CI: 1.40%–2.84%) and thereafter decreased through 2021 with an annual percent change of −2.84% (95% CI: −5.06 to −1.49%). The age-standardized prevalence of MS in the administrative cohort increased from 118 cases per 100,000 (95% CI: 116 to 120) in 2006 to 167 cases per 100,000 (95% CI: 164 to 169) in 2021, i.e., an increase of 41.7%. Similarly, in the clinical cohort, prevalence increased from 71 (95% CI: 69 to 72) to 154 (95% CI: 151 to 156) cases per 100,000 persons from 2001 to 2021, representing an increase of 117.9%ConclusionOur findings demonstrate a decreasing incidence of MS in the last decade, while prevalence continuously increased. Although confounding by changes in data density cannot be excluded, this may reflect changes in environmental exposures such as smoking, as well as contemporary treatment practices and a secular trend toward milder disease with reduced mortality.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, incurable neurological disease, which typically presents in early adulthood [1]. Though the cause of MS remains unknown, several innate and acquired factors have been established to contribute to the risk of developing MS [1]. There are a number of genetic variants that have been found to be associated with MS, largely related to the immune system. Environmental risk factors include Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, active or passive smoking, low sunlight exposure, and low vitamin D levels [1]. The impact at both the individual and societal level is substantial. In Sweden, the excess cost of MS per person per year is estimated to be over EUR 20,000 [2]. The first disease modifying therapy for MS was approved in 1995 [3]. Since then, several highly effective treatments have emerged. With improved treatment options, long-term outcomes for MS patients have demonstrably improved over time [4].

Several studies have reported stable incidence rates of MS in recent years [5–7], counter to findings drawn from the late 20th century which suggested increasing rates of MS over time [8, 9]. Despite stable incidence rates, studies have consistently found the prevalence of MS to be increasing over time [5–7]. Sweden is considered an area of high incidence and prevalence of MS; however, the last published study only contained data until 2008, highlighting the need for more contemporary estimates [10, 11].

Many incidence and prevalence studies focus on smaller, local populations, often centered around a single healthcare institution [12]. Larger national incidence and prevalence studies are rare, and the need for more national and standardized studies was underscored by a recent systematic review [12]. Understanding trends in incidence and prevalence is vital to identify future research directions, including generating hypotheses regarding population-level or environmental risk factors, as well as helping to inform future resource allocation and healthcare strategy planning. Our study aimed to estimate the incidence and prevalence of MS in Sweden from 2001 to 2021, leveraging nationwide data from multiple linked population-based registries.

Methods

Study Setting

Sweden is a northern European country with a population of circa 10.6 million people (2025) [13]. In 2001, approximately 11% of residents were foreign-born, which increased to approximately 20% in 2021. Immigration levels peaked in 2016, then re-stabilized to approximately 100,000 persons per year [14]. Healthcare in Sweden is subsidized by the government, providing equal and affordable access to residents. The healthcare system can be roughly divided into primary and specialized care. Specialized care typically requires a referral from primary care or other healthcare institutions [15].

Diagnostic workup and ongoing follow-up during active phases of the disease are almost invariably managed by neurologists at specialized neurology or internal medicine clinics, rather than in primary care [16]. According to the National Board of Health and Welfare guidelines, all MS patients should be offered annual checkups with a neurologist with experience with MS [17].

Data Sources

Information regarding individuals with MS was obtained from two registries: the National Patient Register and the Swedish MS Registry. The National Patient Register contains all inpatient visits nationwide since 1987 and all specialized outpatient visits to a physician since 2001 [18]. Since the establishment of the registry, coverage has improved, especially pertaining to specialized outpatient care [19]. It does not contain information from primary care, and information is collected for administrative purposes, meaning inclusion into the register is automatic, not voluntary. Data including birthdate, sex, dates of hospital or outpatient admission and discharge, and International Classification of Diseases-9/10 (ICD-9/10) diagnostic codes were extracted. The Swedish MS registry was established in 2001 and currently captures approximately 85% of the Swedish MS population nationwide. Inclusion into the registry is voluntary for both patients and healthcare practitioners. Individuals registered have a confirmed MS diagnosis, made by a neurologist [20]. The MS registry contains information regarding demographic and disease characteristics. Birthdate, sex, diagnosis date, symptom onset date, and inclusion date in the registry were extracted.

Information regarding the general population of Sweden, including age- and sex-specific population counts, was obtained from Statistics Sweden [21]. Individual-level migration, death, and country of birth data for MS cases were also sourced from this registry. The world standard population was obtained from the US National Cancer Institute [22].

Study Population

Two cohorts were created using administrative and clinical registries. The administrative cohort was identified from the National Patient Register, in which MS was defined using a validated algorithm with a positive predictive value of 98.8% requiring 3 or more MS-specific ICD codes (340 or G35) as a primary or secondary diagnosis code on 3 separate dates [23]. The first health encounter with an MS code recorded represented the index date. Individuals were included in the administrative cohort if they met the algorithm criteria. Additionally, for the incidence analysis, cases had to be resident in Sweden at the index date and a minimum of 5 years prior, with no records of MS codes during that time or earlier. Data were available through April 30, 2025. Allowing individuals over 3 years of follow-up to satisfy the algorithm criteria resulted in a study period from January 01, 2006, to December 31, 2021.

The clinical cohort was drawn from the Swedish MS registry. The diagnosis date, recorded by a neurologist, represented the index date. If missing, the onset date or date of inclusion into the registry was used. Cases were required to be resident in Sweden at the index date, but the 5-year run-in period was not required; therefore, the study period for the clinical cohort was January 01, 2001, to December 31, 2021.

Cases could be included in both cohorts, but index dates were calculated according to the respective data source. Incidence and prevalence were estimated for each cohort separately.

Analysis

Incidence

Incidence was estimated by dividing the number of new MS cases by the person-years at risk in each calendar year and over the full period. Each individual contributed person-time to the denominator from the 1st of January of each year of analysis until diagnosis, migration, death, or December 31st of that year. Crude incidence rates were estimated with confidence intervals assuming a Poisson distribution, and age-standardized rates were estimated with confidence intervals assuming a gamma distribution. Standardization was performed using the direct method, age-standardized to the world standard population. Crude and standardized incidence rates were calculated for the full population and stratified by age group (<20; 20–29; 30–39; 40–49; 50–59, 60+) and sex. Female:male incidence ratios were calculated annually and over the full period. All incidence rates were reported per 100,000 person-years.

Temporal trends in incidence rates were evaluated using joinpoint regression [24], with year as the independent variable and crude and age-adjusted rates as the dependent variable. Overall and sex-stratified models were run, with the fit of each model assessed using BIC and weighted BIC. Temporal trends in the female:male ratio were analyzed using negative binomial regression, adjusting for age group. In order to attempt to disentangle the effects of immigration on MS incidence, we conducted a sensitivity analysis where we analyzed the incidence of MS in the Swedish-born population and foreign-born population separately.

Prevalence

Point prevalence was calculated by dividing the number of MS cases alive and resident in Sweden on December 31st of each year by the total resident population estimate on the same date. Crude point prevalence was calculated with confidence intervals assuming a binomial distribution, and age-standardized point prevalence was calculated with confidence intervals assuming a gamma distribution. Age standardization was calculated using the direct method, standardized to the world standard population. Crude and standardized point prevalence was estimated annually and stratified by sex and age group. Female:male prevalence ratios were calculated annually and over the full study period. All prevalence estimates are reported per 100,000 persons.

Temporal trends in prevalence were analyzed using log binomial regression. Calendar year was included as a continuous variable. Unadjusted and age- and sex-adjusted models were run. Temporal trends in the female:male ratio were analyzed using log binomial regression, adjusting for age group. Data preparation and statistical analysis were performed in R version 2025.01.1 + 513, Python 3.13.5 (v3.13.5:6cb20a219a8, June 11, 2025, 12:23:45), and the NIH Joinpoint Regression Software, version 5.4.0 – April 2025.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Administrative Cohort

Between 2006 and 2021, 13,035 persons met the criteria for incident MS, and 28,107 for prevalent MS. Women comprised 68% (8,897) of the incident cohort and 69% (19,337) of the prevalent cohort. The median age of the incident population at the index date was 40.36 years (interquartile range [IQR] 30.62–51.48). The median age of the prevalent population in 2001 was 52.63 years (IQR 41.87–61.87) and increased to 54.13 years (IQR 42.40–65.63) in 2021.

Clinical Cohort

In total, 15,218 people had an index date between 2001 and 2021. Women comprised 69% (10,464) of the incident cohort. Of the prevalent population, 70% (15,650) were female. The median age of the incident population at the index date was 38.23 years (IQR 29.59–48.31). The median age of the prevalent population in 2001 was 48.36 years (IQR 38.87–56.46) and increased to 52.49 years (IQR 41.21–63.43) in 2021.

Overlap between Cohorts

Most individuals, 10,949 persons, were included in both incidence cohorts. For these individuals, the median difference between the index date in the administrative definition (first ICD code for MS) and the clinical registry (diagnosis date) was 0 days (IQR -10 to 0 days).

Incidence

Administrative Cohort

The overall age-standardized incidence rate during the study period of 2006–2021 was 8.37 (95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 8.22–8.52) (Table 1, Fig. 1a). There were significant temporal trends in incidence rates, with stable age-standardized rates observed until 2012, followed by decreasing incidence rates between 2012 and 2021 with an annual percent change of −2.51% (95% CI: −5.04% to −1.69%) (Fig. 1b, online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552732). Crude incidence followed similar trends (Table 1, Fig. 1a, online suppl. Table S1). Incidence was significantly higher in women across all years, with an overall age-standardized incidence of 11.72 (95% CI: 11.47–11.97) (Fig. 2a, online suppl. Table S2a). Age-standardized rates for women were stable until 2012 and thereafter were decreasing with an annual percent change of −2.43% (CI: 95% –8.96% to −1.64%) (Fig. 2b, online suppl. Table S1b). For men, the overall age-standardized incidence was 5.16 (95% CI: 5.00–5.32) (Fig. 2a, online suppl. Table S2a). For men, age-standardized rates were stable until 2011, and thereafter were decreasing with an annual percent change of −2.21% (95% CI: −11.11% to −0.857%) (Fig. 2c, online suppl. Table S1b). Crude rates followed similar trends (online suppl. Table S2a, b, Table S1b). The overall female:male ratio was 2.15 and displayed no significant temporal trend. The highest incidence rates were observed in the 30–39 year age group (online suppl. Table S3a). Incidence appeared to decrease predominantly in the older age groups, while remaining stable in the younger age groups (Fig. 3a).

Clinical Cohort

The overall age-standardized incidence rate between 2001 and 2021 was 7.87 (95% CI: 7.74–7.99) (Table 1, Fig. 1a). There were significant temporal trends in incidence rates, with incidence seemingly increasing between 2001 and 2014 (annual percent change: 2.00%; CI: 95% 1.40%–2.84%), followed by a decrease between 2014 and 2021 (annual percent change: −2.84%; CI: 95% –5.06% to −1.49%) (Fig. 1c, online suppl. Table S1). Crude incidence followed similar trends (Table 1, Fig. 1a, online suppl. Table S1). The incidence was significantly higher in women, with an overall age-standardized incidence rate of 11.09 (95% CI: 10.87–11.31) (Fig. 2a, online suppl. Table S2b). Age-standardized incidence rates significantly increased between 2001 and 2014 with annual percent change of 1.44% (95% CI: 0.82%–2.45%) and thereafter decreased with an annual percent change of −2.68% (95% CI: –5.36% to −1.26%) (Fig. 2d, online suppl. Table S1c). The overall age-standardized incidence rate for men was 4.78 (95% CI: 4.64–4.92) (Fig. 2a, online suppl. Table S2b). Age-standardized incidence rates increased between 2001 and 2004 with an annual percent change of 11.67% (95% CI: 3.05%–30.64%), stabilized from 2006 to 2016, then decreased from 2016 to 2021 with an annual percent change of −4.33% (95% CI: –14.49% to 1.35%) (Fig. 2e, online suppl. Table S1c). Crude rates followed similar trends (online suppl. Table S1b, Table S1c). The overall female:male ratio was 2.20 and decreased slightly between 2001 and 2021 with an IRR of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98–0.99). The highest incidence rates were observed in the 30–39 year age group (online suppl. Table S3b). Incidence rates decreased in the older age groups, while remaining stable or increasing in the younger age groups (Fig. 3b).

Sensitivity Analysis

Incidence rates were significantly lower in the foreign-born population compared to the Swedish-born population (online suppl. Table S4). In the administrative cohort, the temporal trend in incidence in the Swedish-born population followed the trend in the overall population, while the incidence rate remained stable in the foreign-born population (online suppl. Figure S1a). In the clinical cohort, the temporal trend in incidence in the Swedish-born and foreign-born population followed the overall temporal trend in incidence (online suppl. Figure S1b).

Prevalence

Administrative Cohort

There were 21,817 cases of MS in Sweden on December 31, 2021, in the administrative cohort (age-standardized prevalence of 167; 95% CI: 164–169), compared to 13,328 cases in 2006 (age-standardized prevalence of 118; 95% CI: 116–120) (Fig. 4a, online suppl. Table S5a). Crude prevalence was slightly higher but followed the same temporal trend (online suppl. Table S5). Crude prevalence increased by 2% each year (relative risk: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.02–1.02), and this increase remained following adjustment for sex and age (relative risk: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03). Prevalence was higher in the female population, but the female:male ratio did not significantly change over time (Fig. 4b, online suppl. Table S6a). The highest prevalence was observed in the 50–59 year age group (online suppl. Table S7a), and the proportion of persons with MS over 60 years of age increased over time (online suppl. Figure S2a).

Clinical Cohort

There were 19,428 cases of MS in Sweden on December 31, 2021, in the clinical cohort, resulting in an age-standardized prevalence of 154 (95% CI: 151–156), compared to 7,181 cases in 2001 (age-standardized prevalence of 71 [95% CI: 69–72]) (Fig. 4a, online suppl. Table S5b). Crude prevalence was slightly higher but followed the same temporal trend (online suppl. Table S5b). Crude prevalence increased by 4% each year (relative risk: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.04–1.04), and this increase remained following adjustment for age and sex. Prevalence was higher in the female population, and the female:male ratio was stable throughout the study period (Fig. 4b, online suppl. Table S6b). The highest prevalence was observed in the 50–59 year age group (online suppl. Table S7b). The proportion of persons with MS over 60 years of age increased over time (online suppl. Figure S2b).

Discussion

This study provides insight into the evolution of the incidence and prevalence of MS in Sweden using two disease definitions drawn from population-based clinical and administrative registries. We identified stable incidence rates in the administrative data and increasing rates in the clinical MS registry in the first decade of the 21st century, most likely reflecting an increasing coverage of the Swedish MS registry. In both definitions, incidence peaked between 2012 and 2014 and then declined to the end of the study period in 2021. In contrast, prevalence consistently increased in both the clinical and administrative data across the full study period.

Several recent studies from Europe and North America have reported decreasing MS incidence rates in the 21st century [25–27], while others have reported stable trends [5–7]. A study from the UK found steadily decreasing incidence rates of MS from 1990 to 2010 [25]. Additionally, studies from Poland and Hungary reported decreasing incidence rates, utilizing administrative healthcare data [26, 27]. A Danish study from 2018 reported increasing incidence rates from 1950 until 2009, with a peak of 9.43 cases per 100,000 person-years between 2000 and 2009 [9], similar to our rates. A more recent Danish study reported higher incidence rates during the same time period (11.37 cases per 100,000 person-years in the year 2000), followed by stabilization until 2022 [7]. Other studies from Germany (2015–2022) [28], France (2011–2021) [6], and Ontario, Canada (1996–2013) [5] reported stable incidence rates. Similar to our results, both the French and German studies also reported stable female:male incidence rate ratios throughout their study periods [6, 28]. The aforementioned studies all utilized either administrative or clinical registries for case identification, while we used both, thereby providing insights into advantages and challenges of either method.

Consistent with our findings, other studies have reported an increasing prevalence of MS [5–7, 29]. Prevalence is a function of incidence and disease duration. Because we found stable and decreasing incidence rates and MS is a chronic and incurable disease, the increase in the prevalence of MS in Sweden may be attributed to improvements in capture of the national healthcare registries or a decrease in mortality. Examining the mortality of MS in Sweden is beyond the scope of this study, but studies from Europe and North American have found decreasing mortality rates [5, 30]. This is further supported by the median age of the MS population increasing throughout our study, which could reflect both reduced mortality and improved identification of older MS cases in the later period of the study.

Importantly, there has been a shift in treatment strategy from so-called escalation therapy, favoring lower efficacy but safer drugs and escalating to higher efficacy but possibly higher risk treatments in the event of treatment failure, to induction therapy, favoring higher efficacy treatments early in the disease course [31]. Long-term studies have found a significantly lower risk of conversion to secondary progressive MS as well as slower disability worsening [31]. Additionally, exposure to disease modifying treatment has been associated with lower mortality rates [4]. In relation to prevalence, it is likely that the improved short- and long-term outcomes that have been observed for MS patients over time have contributed to improved survival and therefore increasing prevalence.

The most recent estimates of MS incidence and prevalence in Sweden included data until 2008 [10, 11]. Compared to these earlier studies, our incidence/prevalence estimates were markedly lower [10, 11]. This can largely be explained by the differing definitions of MS. In our study, we employed a stringent, validated definition of MS, requiring 3 or more MS ICD codes, as well as 5 years of run-in time to ensure incidence. In the previous study, only 1 MS ICD code was required for inclusion, and no run-in period was considered. Additionally, we separated our data sources to employ consistent inclusion and exclusion criteria and allow for comparisons with the wider literature. In the previous incidence study, the clinical and administrative registries were combined, resulting in higher estimates.

Throughout the study period, several diagnostic criteria have been utilized in the identification of MS cases. The first version of the McDonald criteria was introduced in 2001, incorporating MRI metrics in MS diagnoses. These criteria were updated in 2005 and 2010 to further specify MRI findings as well as to define primary progressive MS. The latest update utilized during our study period was in 2017, where changes were made to enhance diagnostic sensitivity [32]. The peak incidence rates identified from 2012 to 2014 could represent a plateau, in which incidence increased due to these improved diagnostic metrics over the period before which “artificially” increased rates of MS. The subsequent decline in rates following this period could relate to a saturation in diagnoses. Incidence decreased most clearly in older age groups in our study, while remaining stable or increasing in the younger age groups. It is possible that this was driven by prompter diagnoses, such that individuals were diagnosed at younger ages in more recent years. Further, more specific diagnostic guidelines and the awareness of other demyelinating diseases which mimic MS may have contributed to the decline in MS diagnoses. Specific antibodies for neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease were first discovered in 2004 and 2007, respectively, but no diagnostic criteria were established until 2006 and 2023 [33]. The incidence of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders in Sweden has previously been estimated to be 0.79 cases per 1,000,000 person-years [34]. Though no nationwide incidence study of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease in Sweden has been conducted, estimates in Europe range from 0.16 cases to 0.48 cases per 100,000 person-years [35]. While some of these patients were likely diagnosed with MS in earlier years, due to the rarity of both disorders, it is unlikely that these misdiagnoses substantially impacted our results. Improvements in MS diagnostics can also contribute to a reduced diagnostic delay.

Possible Explanations for Decreasing Incidence Rates

Decreasing incidence rates must be the result of one or both of two conditions, either fewer people are diagnosed and captured by the case definition, or the general population grows. In our case, both conditions are true. We saw a decrease in the absolute number of new cases per year from 2011 to 2021, and an increasing population at risk over the study period. Because the absolute number of cases per year decreased during the study period, this could indicate a change in the true risk of MS in Sweden, as opposed to being a result of the rapid growth of the population. Here, we speculate as to why we have observed declining rates of MS in Sweden in the last decade.

Smoking

One possible explanation for decreasing incidence rates of MS is the decrease in smoking rates in Sweden. Smoking is a well-known risk factor for MS, with cumulative smoking (active or passive) associated with an increased risk [36]. Since the 1970s, Sweden has implemented several public health measures to decrease smoking rates. Notably, smoke-free environments including childcare and public transport were implemented in 1993, and indoor smoking was banned in 2005 [37]. Since 1980, the proportion of the Swedish population between 16 and 84 years old who were smoking daily decreased from 30 to 35% in 1980 to 5.4% in 2024. For women, the proportion of daily smokers decreased from 25 to 30% in 1980 to 5.8% in 2024 [37]. Oral nicotine use, on the other hand, which is a popular alternative to smoking in Sweden, has increased throughout the study period, from 9% of adults in the late 1980s to 16% in 2024 [38]. Oral tobacco products have been observed to have a mild protective effect against MS [39]. This substantial decrease in smoking could contribute to the observed decrease in incidence rates in the 21st century.

Vitamin D

Another possible explanation for the decreasing incidence rates is the introduction of vitamin D supplementation in Sweden. Sun exposure and vitamin D supplementation have consistently been identified as protective against MS development, especially before the age of 20 years [40, 41]. Due to Sweden’s geographical location, vitamin D deficiency is a common phenomenon. To counteract this, the Swedish Food Agency introduced food-fortification in 1983 which included milk and margarine, which was later updated in 2018 with a higher vitamin D requirement, as well as to include more food products such as lactose-free alternatives [42]. Additionally, vitamin D supplementation has been recommended by the National Board of Social Affairs and Health for all children between the ages of 0–5 years (0–2 years since 2020) in the form of AD drops since 1978 (D-drops since 2009) [43]. Some regions in Sweden also supply D-drops free of charge at Children’s Health Centers [44]. Additionally, vitamin D supplementation has been recommended for all pregnant and breastfeeding individuals since 2012 by the general Nordic Nutrition Recommendations [45]. It has been reported that children supplemented with oral vitamin D in the Swedish population exhibit higher levels of 25(OH)D [43], which further supports this hypothesis.

Immigration

It is plausible that changing immigration patterns have affected the incidence of MS in Sweden, both by contributing MS-free persons to the population at risk (healthy migrant effect), and by affecting the number of new MS cases per year. Immigration to Sweden is largely consisting of persons from low-MS-risk countries, both as a result of refugee migration and labor migration. In our data, we saw lower incidence rates of MS in the foreign-born population as compared to the Swedish-born population, reflecting this difference in risk-profile. Nevertheless, the temporal trend in incidence in the Swedish-born population was consistent with the observed trend in the overall population, indicating that immigration did not significantly influence the observed temporal trend. However, the risk of MS in first- and second-generation immigrants is complex and seems to be dependent on country of origin as well as age at immigration [46, 47]. Therefore, immigration can be regarded as a contributor to decreasing incidence but is unlikely to account for the entirety of the changes observed.

Registry Characteristics

We estimated incidence and prevalence of MS in two separate but overlapping cohorts. In 2006, the incidence estimated in the clinical cohort was considerably lower than in the administrative cohort. This can be explained by changing completeness of the Swedish MS registry. A recent study reported that the completeness of the MS registry increased from 48% of the prevalent MS population in 2005 to 85% in 2020 [20]. Notably, the incidence estimated in both cohorts in more recent years (ca 2015–2021) was more similar, both crude and age-standardized. This reflects ongoing efforts to increase the completeness of the Swedish MS registry, primarily in the context of newly diagnosed individuals. In contrast, the prevalence of MS estimated in the MS registry remained significantly lower than that estimated in the National Patient Register throughout the entire study period but followed the same temporal trend. This could suggest that the MS registry in recent years captures incident MS cases well but does not capture previously existing cases to the same extent. This is further supported by the fact that prevalent cases in the Swedish MS registry are generally younger than prevalent cases captured in the National Patient Register [20].

The quality and completeness of the National Patient Register has changed over the study period as well. When the register was first established, it initially only included inpatient healthcare data and was later expanded to include specialized outpatient clinics [19]. Though all specialized healthcare providers are obliged to report to the register, the National Board of Health and Welfare are not able to ensure reporting. It is thought that non-response is more common with private specialized outpatient clinics [19]. It has been confirmed through comparative studies that the coverage of specialized outpatient care has significantly increased over time, with the most substantial improvement occurring in 2011–2012 [19]. Taking this into account, it is likely that the quality of the National Patient Register has also influenced our estimates. The early years of our study period likely reflect improvements in registry coverage, while the later years may be more representative of the true incidence rates. Last, it is also possible that incidence was overestimated in the earlier years due to misclassification of prevalent cases as incident. To minimize this effect, we employed a run-in period requiring 5 years devoid of demyelinating ICD codes.

Last, the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to a decline in the recognition of MS as persons were less likely to seek healthcare during this period. Though, a prior study has explored this and found no evidence of a decline in the diagnoses of MS within the Swedish MS Registry during the first 2 years of the pandemic [48].

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of stringent, validated definitions of MS from two sources. In this study, we use nationwide registries, allowing for truly population-based estimates. Additionally, we age-standardized our incidence and prevalence rates to the world standard population, increasing external validity, and enabling easier comparisons across countries, as well as temporal comparisons in Sweden while accounting for changing age demographics of the underlying population. By utilizing case definitions based on both clinical and administrative data, we were able to highlight advantages and challenges of the two, supporting future epidemiological research.

Limitations of this study are those inherent to clinical and administrative registry research. While our clinical population has the value of a high confidence in the MS diagnosis, it does not have full coverage of the MS population in Sweden, meaning the incidence and prevalence estimates are undoubtedly lower than the true values. In contrast, the administrative definition offers more complete coverage but carries more uncertainty in the diagnosis of MS. In both, we may have missed individuals with MS who did not seek healthcare for their disease or who were seen exclusively in primary care, thought this is unlikely as MS is predominantly managed in specialized care [16].

Conclusions

We estimated the incidence and prevalence of MS in Sweden over a 20-year period, using two case verification sources for MS. We found a declining incidence together with an increasing prevalence. The decrease in not only incidence rates but in the absolute number of new cases per year could suggest a change in the risk of developing MS but likely also reflects changes in MS diagnostic criteria and coverage of nationwide registries. The increasing prevalence is likely due to treatment improvements and subsequent reductions in mortality, as well as increasing data coverage.
