The association between mobility and a cognitive measure of attention and processing speed in middle-aged adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Uriah Turkel, Roee Hayek, Yaniv Nudelman, Itai Gutman, Shmuel Springer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mobility and cognitive functions are closely linked in old age. Although a relationship between mobility and cognitive function has also been demonstrated in midlife, it has not been tested under conditions that might reflect real-life locomotion. In this study, the association between attention and processing speed, as measured by choice reaction time (CRT), and mobility in midlife was examined using spatiotemporal gait indices during outdoor walking and a self-perceived mobility questionnaire. Fifty middle-aged adults (53.98 ± 5.32 years) completed a 2-minute walk in an urban environment with and without a dual task of texting on a smartphone. Gait speed, gait variability (stride time variability), and gait similarity (dynamic time warping) in the anteroposterior and medio-lateral axes were measured, and self-perceived mobility was assessed using the Mobility in Middle Age Questionnaire (MMQ). Attention and processing speed were measured with the Deary-Liewald CRT. Slower CRT was associated with reduced medio-lateral similarity during dual-task walking (r = 0.316, p = 0.032) and with lower self-perceived mobility (MMQ, r = -0.422, p = 0.007). Hierarchical regression analysis showed that medio-lateral gait similarity explained 10.0% of the variance in CRT, while self-perceived mobility explained 14.4%. The relationship between mobility and cognition in ecologically valid contexts may indicate a common age-related mechanism that emerges in midlife and can be exploited by interventions and monitoring programs aimed at promoting healthy aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32121-8.

Introduction

Middle age, defined as the period between 45 and 64 years, represents a critical phase in the aging healthspan trajectory, particularly in terms of mobility and cognitive function1–3.While aging research has traditionally focused on older adults, midlife is increasingly recognized as a key period for detecting early signs of functional decline. Up to 20% of the middle-aged population report some degree of mobility difficulty3,4, alongside a notably high prevalence of falls, rising from 9% among those aged 40–44 to nearly 28% in those aged 60–645. These mobility challenges are not only due to balance problems, but are also related to cognitive decline, which is associated with an elevated risk of falls in this age group6,7. These findings underscore the importance of early detection of cognitive and mobility impairments to facilitate timely, targeted interventions to support healthy aging8.

Walking is a widely used indicator of mobility and overall functional capacity. Its performance is highly context-dependent and can be influenced by environmental conditions (e.g., uneven terrain, narrow pathways)9, external obstacles10, walking speed11 and concurrent cognitive demands12. Declines in processing speed13,14 and attention14, which often emerge in midlife, may further impair gait as these domains are linked to gait regulation, particularly under cognitive load. Choice Reaction Time (CRT) is a valid measure of attention and processing speed14. It quantifies the time required to recognize a visual stimulus and select the correct response among several options, with shorter times indicating more efficient cognitive processing. CRT is particularly sensitive to subtle cognitive changes in midlife, as it involves rapid decision-making and motor response execution14–17. Slow CRT has been associated with reduced walking speed and impaired gait performance in midlife16, particularly during fast walking12,18.

Although previous studies have shown a relationship between mobility and cognitive function in middle-aged adults11,12,16,18, most were conducted in controlled indoor environments that may not reflect the cognitive and environmental demands of real-world locomotion19. Moreover, while some studies have used dual-task paradigms to examine cognitive-motor interference and strengthen the observed link between cognition and mobility, they often rely on laboratory-based tasks (e.g., serial subtraction, word generation) that lack ecological validity12,18.

Alongside mobility performance assessments, self-perceived locomotion ability is an important tool for characterizing mobility which provides valuable insight into functional status20,21. Self-report questionnaires are widely used to assess perceived functional changes, particularly in early stages of decline22–24. These tools have demonstrated utility in detecting early impairments in daily function, sometimes preceding measurable deficits on performance tests25–27. The Mobility in Middle Age Questionnaire (MMQ) is a recently developed instrument that assesses self-perceived mobility through 16 items evaluating both current mobility ability and one-year mobility change, offering a comprehensive perspective on functional mobility in midlife. The MMQ has demonstrated excellent reliability, validity, and sensitivity for detecting early mobility decline28. Therefore, integrating objective gait measures with self-perceived mobility assessments may provide a more comprehensive understanding of mobility and its cognitive correlates in midlife.

The present study examines the association between attention and processing speed measured by CRT and mobility in middle-aged adults by assessing spatiotemporal gait parameters during outdoor walking while texting on a smartphone in urban environments, which better reflects real-world mobility demands and increases ecological validity19,29.

Additionally, we also explore how self-perceived mobility relates to cognitive function to provide a complementary perspective on midlife functional status.

Methods

Participants

Fifty adults aged 45 to 64 years (44.0% female) were recruited for this cross-sectional study via social media platforms and university bulletin boards.

Individuals were included if they were able to walk independently outdoors without a walking aid, had sufficient skills to understand verbal and written instructions, had no physical limitations restricting moderate to vigorous activity, and had at least six months of texting experience on a smartphone with fluent language proficiency to perform the texting task. Those with musculoskeletal, neurological or visual impairments affecting mobility or balance, significant visual impairments, medical conditions preventing vigorous physical activity, or medications affecting balance were excluded. The sample size was determined using the G*POWER software. Based on Demnitz et al.16, who reported a relationship of β = -0.106 between walking speed and CRT in middle-aged adults, we assumed a moderate association in a more demanding dual-task condition (walking while texting). This analysis (α = 0.05 and 80% power) indicated that a minimum sample size of 49 participants would be required to detect an association between CRT and gait performance under dual-task. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ariel University Ethics Committee approved the study, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Procedure

Data were collected between September 2024 and February 2025 during a single 60-minute session. Participants first completed background questionnaires, the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)30 to assess their physical activity level, and self-perceived mobility was measured using the Mobility in Middle Age Questionnaire (MMQ)28.

Subsequently, each participant completed the cognitive CRT test in a quiet, distraction-free room and the gait assessments in an outdoor urban environment to reflect real-life walking condition.

Attention and processing speed were measured using the Deary-Liewald reaction time task, a validated computer-based tool for measuring simple and choice reaction times17. Participants responded to visual stimuli by pressing the corresponding key on a standard keyboard as quickly and accurately as possible. All participants were tested using the same computer model to ensure consistency.

Gait was assessed during daytime in a typical urban setting on a sidewalk adjacent to a main road, as illustrated in Fig. 1Fig. 1Illustration of the real-world urban environment used for gait assessment.. Participants completed a 2-minute walking trial twice with and without a dual-task condition (texting while walking) at a comfortable, self-selected pace. A smartphone (Galaxy S21 FE 5G; Android 12) was attached to their lumbar spine to collect acceleration and angular velocity data31–33. Data were recorded via the phyphox app (RWTH Aachen, Germany)34, and stored locally for later analysis to avoid interruptions in testing35. Gait performance outcomes included gait speed (m/sec), gait variability assessed by stride time variability (STV%) calculated as the standard deviation of stridetime relative to average stride duration, gait similarity was quantified using the dynamic time warping (DTW) method, which computes the minimal cumulative distance between consecutive gait cycles based on acceleration data in the AP and ML axes. Smaller distances indicate more consistent gait patterns36–39. In addition, to evaluate the magnitude and direction of the of the texting task impact on gait performance, the dual-task cost (DTC) was also calculated, defined as the difference in the gait indices between the single and dual task according to the following formula15:

A positive DTC of gait speed indicates slower walking under dual-task conditions, reflecting a negative impact of the additional task. In contrast, a negative DTC of stride time variability or DTW indicates increased variability or decreased similarity, suggesting a negative influence of the dual-task.

Signal processing and gait parameter extraction were conducted using custom MATLAB scripts (MathWorks, Inc.; version 9.12, Natick, MA, USA), following the same procedure described by Hayek et al.40 Raw accelerometer data were resampled to 100 Hz and filtered using a 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 20 Hz.

During the dual-task condition, participants used a custom mobile application installed on their smartphones. This application presented a series of diverse questions in Hebrew (e.g., personal information recall like ‘Type your first name’; general knowledge queries such as ‘Type the names of two cities that start with the letter R’; or more complex questions like ‘Explain the connection between an airplane and a bicycle’), to which participants were required to type their answers. The application measured reaction time texting (RTT), the latency between question presentation and initiating the first keystroke. Additionally, the correct answer rate was calculated as the number of responses accurately submitted within a 10-second limit. Prior to data collection, participants completed a brief practice session and were instructed to hold the phone naturally, with no explicit prioritization directives for either walking or texting.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS, version 27.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). For descriptive statistics, quantitative variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on the distribution type, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Outliers > 2.5×IQR were excluded to ensure data reliability. Paired t-tests or Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests assessed differences in gait parameters between single and dual-task conditions, with Cohen’s d and r, respectively, serving as effect sizes. Next, Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlations, as appropriate, examined the associations between attention and processing speed (i.e., CRT) with the spatiotemporal gait parameters, and self-perceived mobility (MMQ). Variables showing significant correlations were further analyzed using two separate hierarchical regression models, one testing the link with spatiotemporal gait parameters, and the other to self-perceived mobility. Both models were adjusted for relevant covariates and residuals were checked to confirm model assumptions. Specifically, age was included due to its link to cognitive decline13,14,41, physical activity level (i.e., IPAQ) due to its relationship with cognitive function and mobility42, and the percentage of correct answers during dual-task walking reflected cognitive load, with a lower percentage indicating greater attentional allocation to walking43,44. Finally, to evaluate the ability of the custom mobile application used for the dual task texting to measure attention and processing speed while walking, relationships between CRT and reaction time to texting (RTT) and rate of correct answers were examined.

The effect of the dual-task on the spatiotemporal gait parameters

Figure 2 illustrates the spatiotemporal gait parameters under single- and dual-task conditions. Dual-task walking led to a significant decrease in gait velocity (p < 0.001, d = 0.93; DTC = 6.01%) and a significant increase in gait variability (p < 0.001, r = 0.78; DTC = -45.03%). ML gait similarity declined significantly (p = 0.021, d = 0.36; DTC = 5.14%), whereas AP gait similarity showed no significant change (p = 0.169, r = 0.20; DTC = 1.17%).

The regression model explaining the link between spatiotemporal gait parameters and cognitive function

To further explore the association between ML gait similarity during dual-task and cognitive function, a hierarchical regression model was conducted, as presented in Table 2. Additional covariates, including the percentage of correct answers and physical activity level, were incorporated into the models based on their theoretical relevance and established CRT associations, as summarized in Table 1. ML gait similarity during dual-task walking alone explained 10% of the variance in CRT (R² = 0.100, β = 0.316, p = 0.032). Adding the rate of correct answers during dual-task walking significantly improved the model (ΔR² = 0.200, p < 0.001) and increased the explained variance to 30%. However, the contribution of ML gait similarity under dual-task decreased (β = 0.182). The addition of IPAQ resulted in a modest and non-significant increase in explained variance (ΔR² = 0.055, p = 0.075), and the contribution of ML gait similarity further decreased (β = 0.102). The addition of age as a covariate yielded a modest improvement in the model (ΔR² = 0.064, p = 0.112), with age emerging as the strongest covariate (β = 0.225). When examined independently, age accounted for 16.5% of the variance in CRT (R² = 0.165, p = 0.008), with a higher β = 0.407. The contribution of ML gait similarity under dual-task conditions declined to β = 0.073. However, its non-significant correlation with age (r = 0.253, p = 0.090) suggests that age alone is unlikely to explain the association between CRT and ML gait similarity, as summarized in Table 2.

Self-perceived mobility alone explained 14.4% of the variance in cognitive function (R² = 0.144, β = -0.379, p = 0.027). Adding physical activity (IPAQ) did not significantly enhance the model (ΔR² = 0.005, p = 0.678). However, including age significantly increased the explained variance to 30.2% (R² = 0.302), with age emerging as the strongest predictor (β = 0.412, p = 0.015). Notably, the non-significant correlation between age and MMQ (r = -0.175, p = 0.288) suggests that age does not fully explain the relationship between perceived mobility and cognitive function.

The relationships between CRT and the texting task outcomes

Significant associations were found between CRT and cognitive performance during the dual-task texting condition. Specifically, slower CRT was correlated with longer texting reaction time while walking (r = 0.352, p = 0.015), and with alower rate of correct responses (r = -0.520, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between mobility and cognitive functions related to attention and processing speed, measured by CRT, in middle-aged adults. Our findings demonstrate that both objective gait characteristics and self-perceived mobility are significantly associated with cognitive function. ML gait similarity during dual task walking and self-reported mobility each explained a substantial portion of the variance in CRT, highlighting the association between mobility to cognitive function in midlife.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies11,12,16,18 that showed an association between CRT and spatiotemporal gait indices. However, while those studies reported associations with gait speed, our study found an association only between CRT and ML gait similarity. This divergence could likely stem from methodological differences. Unlike prior studies that collected data in controlled indoor environments, we conducted gait assessments in an outdoor urban setting. Earlier protocols often relied on short walking distances (e.g., 4–10 m)12,16,18 with repeated mat crossings18, or included up to 50 m of walking11. These short, frequently interrupted walks may disrupt natural steady-state gait and alter gait variability45, limiting their ability to accurately reflect continuous gait dynamics in the real world. Furthermore, some protocols instructed participants to “walk as fast as possible”11,12,18 or included turning phases11, both of which may add cognitive demands related to processing speed46. Finally, there were significant differences in the dual task used by the different protocols. These were laboratory-based cognitive dual tasks, such as arithmetic or fluency12,18, whereas we used texting on a smartphone as a dual task, which better reflects real-world mobility demands and increases ecological validity. Collectively, these methodological distinctions may account for the differing patterns of association with cognitive function.

Yet, despite heterogeneity in which spatiotemporal parameters are associated with cognition, the cumulative evidence suggests that gait metrics are sensitive to cognitive processes in midlife. This supports the idea that spatiotemporal gait characteristics may reflect underlying cognitive function even in healthy middle-aged adults. These associations may arise from shared age-related neural mechanisms that govern both locomotion and cognitive control, which begin to exhibit subtle changes during midlife.

Despite the cognitive demands of texting, our participants exhibited more consistent medio-lateral gait patterns during dual-task compared to single-task walking. This pattern likely reflects a compensatory strategy to preserve stability under cognitive load, as previously observed in young adults47. Notably, ML control has been shown to play a more pivotal role in maintaining postural stability during walking than anterior-posterior dynamics, particularly in middle age48–50. Unlike gait variability, which captures only temporal fluctuations through linear measures, gait similarity encompasses both the amplitude and shape of the gait signal using a non-linear approach51,52. This makes it especially sensitive to subtle disruptions in gait control under cognitive demands, even when gait velocity remains unchanged40, and may further explain why only ML gait similarity was associated with CRT in our study.

A key finding of our study is the association between self perceived mobility and cognitive function, explaining approximately 14% of the variance in CRT. This contribution is meaningful, considering that variables such as sex, education, and physical activity account for less than 4% of the variance, and even age, which is regarded as one of the strongest predictors of cognitive function, explains no more than 28% of the variance in CRT14,53,54. Our findings may be aligned with longitudinal and cross-sectional analyses demonstrating that self-reported or subjectively assessed mobility in midlife is associated with an increased risk of later cognitive decline. For example, the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging have shown that cognitive performance is significantly associated with self-reported measures of mobility in adults as young as 45, with the strength of this association increasing with age16. Analyses from the Health and Retirement Study found that self-reported difficulties with mobility in adults aged 50–64 were predictive of cognitive impairment up to 18 years later, even after adjusting for covariates55. Another longitudinal study of adults aged 51–59 showed that self-reported mobility limitations in late midlife were associated with incident Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias over follow-up, with a dose-response relationship between the number of mobility difficulties and dementia risk56. Collectively, these findings indicate that self-perceived mobility in midlife is a clinically relevant marker of cognitive health, supporting the use of its assessments in this population.

Integrating a custom mobile application for the texting dual task in this study provided a practical tool for assessing cognitive-motor interference in real-world conditions. While such interference is well-established40,57,58, the lack of tools for evaluating the cognitive dual task complicates interpretation, making it difficult to determine whether preserved gait reflects intact motor control or a cognitive trade-off58. Attention and processing speed measured by CRT significantly associated with the response time and the rate of correct answers during the texting dual task, highlighting the application potential to assess cognitive-motor interference in ecologically relevant contexts.

This study has several limitations. First, participants were healthy and physically active, excluding individuals with physical or cognitive impairments. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader midlife population. Second, the walking conditions were not randomized, and a single-task cognitive baseline was not included, which limits interpretation of dual-task effects and prevents calculation of cognitive dual-task cost. However, the consistent protocol and use of a cognitively demanding, ecologically valid task strengthen the internal validity of our findings. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and limits insights into changes over time. Future studies with more diverse, larger samples and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to confirm and extend these results.

Conclusions

Gait similarity and self-perceived mobility appear to be meaningful correlates of attention and processing speed in midlife. These findings suggest that these measures may serve as early markers of cognitive function during this transitional life stage, due to shared age-related mechanisms linking mobility and cognition.
