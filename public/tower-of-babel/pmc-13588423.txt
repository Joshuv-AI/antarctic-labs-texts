Temperature-dependent Persistence of African Swine Fever Virus
Authors: Da-Young Kim, Yong-Myung Kang, Yeon-Hee Kim, Hae-Eun Kang, Dae-Sung Yoo, Ki-Hyun Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:African swine fever (ASF) is a highly fatal swine disease caused by the ASF virus (ASFV), resulting in substantial socio-economic impacts in affected regions worldwide. The remarkable environmental stability and tenacity of ASFV play a critical role in indirect transmission through contaminated carcasses and fomites. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the survival of ASFV in multiple sample types relevant to potential indirect transmission routes.Materials and Methods:The stability and tenacity of ASFV in whole blood, oral and rectal swabs, and tissues, including the spleen, liver, kidney, and muscle, were evaluated under four temperature conditions (−20°C, 4°C, 21°C, and 37°C) over defined storage periods using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and virus titration with statistical analysis.Results:ASFV stability and tenacity were significantly influenced by sample type, storage temperature, and incubation time, exhibiting a clear inverse relationship with temperature. Whole blood showed the highest tenacity, maintained for up to 168 days at −20°C and 4°C. Viral genomes and infectivity in tissue and swab samples were better preserved at lower temperatures, whereas infectivity rapidly declined at higher temperatures.Conclusions:ASFV shows strong temperature-dependent environmental resistance, with increased risk of indirect transmission under cold conditions, which supports the establishment of effective quarantine strategies for ASF control.

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) is an infectious swine disease primarily presenting as an acute condition characterized by pyrexia, widespread hemorrhagic lesions, and subsequent death. Mortality can reach up to 100%, resulting in substantial economic losses in affected regions.[1] The causative agent, ASF virus (ASFV), is a large, complex, double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the genus Asfivirus within the family Asfarviridae.[2] ASF was first described in Kenya in 1921 and has since remained endemic in many African countries. The disease has evolved into an unprecedented global epidemic, posing a serious, ongoing threat to the swine industry. The current epidemic began in 2007 following the introduction of ASFV into Georgia from Eastern Africa.[3] Subsequently, the virus rapidly disseminated to the Caucasus region and the Russian Federation, reaching the European Union in 2014, Asia in 2018, Oceania in 2020, and the Americas in 2021. As of December 2025, ASF has been reported in 85 countries across five continents, according to the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH).[4]

ASFV can be transmitted through two principal pathways. Direct transmission occurs through oronasal contact between infected and susceptible pigs, whereas indirect transmission involves exposure to virus-contaminated pork products, fomites, and biological vectors.[5] In wild-boar populations, direct contact is considered the primary driver of virus spread within sounders; however, indirect exposure to infected carcasses and contaminated environments may play a significant role in transmission between sounders.[6] Viral tenacity, a critical determinant of indirect transmission, is influenced by environmental and matrix-related factors, including temperature, pH, relative humidity, surface materials, and the surrounding biological matrix. ASFV possesses a multi-layered virion architecture consisting of a nucleoprotein core enclosed by an internal envelope, an icosahedral capsid, and an outer envelope.[789] This complex structural organization confers exceptional environmental stability. In particular, ASFV exhibits marked resistance to low temperatures and a broad range of pH conditions.[5] Moreover, the presence of organic materials, such as blood, serum, or uncooked pork products, further enhances viral stability and prolongs viral survival in the environment.[510]

In South Korea, ASFV infection was confirmed in 55 pig farms and 4321 wild boars from the first outbreak detected on September 16, 2019, to December 31, 2025. It is presumed that ASFV from nearby infected wild boars intermittently spills over into pig farms through breaches in biosecurity. Of the 4321 wild-boar infections, 1699 cases (39.3%) were reported from December to February, according to the Wild Animal Disease Information System.[11] A comparable seasonal trend, characterized by increased ASF occurrence in wild boars during winter months, has also been documented in Europe.[1213] South Korea is in the temperate zone of the Northern Hemisphere, between latitudes 33.12–43.01°N and longitudes 126.27–129.93°E. Four distinct seasons are observed. Based on climatological data collected between 1991 and 2020, the annual mean temperature ranges from 7°C to 15°C with relative humidity between 59% and 79%. Monthly mean temperatures vary from −6.9°C to 3.6°C in January and from 19.7°C to 26.7°C in August, while regional extremes range from −20 to −30°C in winter and from 37°C to 40°C in summer.[14] Among the factors influencing seasonal ASF dynamics in wild-boar populations, the tenacity of ASFV under temperature fluctuations is considered a major contributor to the higher occurrence of ASF during colder seasons.

This study addresses a critical knowledge gap in understanding the environmental persistence of ASFV by systematically assessing both viral stability and infectivity across multiple matrices, including blood, oral and rectal excretions, and carcass-derived tissues. Unlike previous work that often focused on single sample types or short observation periods, our study employed a multi-matrix design and extended monitoring to 24 weeks for blood and swabs and up to 364 days for tissues, thereby capturing both medium-and long-term dynamics of viral survival. By evaluating ASFV under four distinct temperature conditions (−20°C, 4°C, 21°C, and 37°C), we provide a comprehensive view of how temperature influences viral persistence. Importantly, the integration of advanced statistical modeling approaches – including linear mixed-effects models, nonlinear regression, and survival analysis – offers robust quantitative support for matrix-and temperature-specific differences in ASFV resistance. Together, these findings yield novel insights into ASFV stability and infectivity, explain seasonal transmission patterns under cold conditions, and establish an evidence-based foundation for refining biosecurity, carcass management, and quarantine strategies aimed at reducing indirect transmission risks.

Materials and Methods

Cells and viruses

Pulmonary alveolar macrophages (PAMs) were used for the virus isolation, propagation, and titration. PAMs were collected through pulmonary lavage from a 6-week-old female specific-pathogen-free White Yucatan pig by Optipharm (South Korea). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) at 37°C in a CO2 incubator. Two ASFV isolates, Korea/Pig/Hongcheon/2022 and Korea/Pig/Pocheon1/2023, were used in animal experiments to evaluate viral virulence. The Korea/Pig/Hongcheon/2022 isolate was obtained from the spleen of a dead pig at a farm in Hongcheon County reported on May 26, 2022, whereas the Korea/Pig/Pocheon1/2023 isolate originated from ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-treated whole blood collected from an infected pig at a farm reported on January 5, 2023. Comparative genome sequence analysis revealed nucleotide identities of 99.95% and 99.85%, respectively, relative to the Georgia 2007/1 reference strain.[15] Both viruses were isolated and propagated according to the standard protocols recommended by the European Union Reference Laboratory (EURL) for ASF.[16]

Animal experiment and sample collection

An animal experiment was conducted in the ABSL-3 facility at the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA), South Korea. All procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee (approval number 2023-709 on January 12, 2023) and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the institutional biosafety committee (approval number 2022-29 on December 27, 2022). Ten 8-week-old Landrace pigs were intramuscularly inoculated with 1 mL of 103 50% hemadsorbing doses (HAD50) of two ASFV isolates. The control group of three pigs was mock-inoculated with 1 mL of RPMI 1640 medium. The control group showed no clinical signs with no viremia and antibody until the death of challenged pigs. Ten animals inoculated with two isolates developed acute febrile disease characterized by fever (>40°C), anorexia, depression, and recumbency, and succumbed to death within 10 days post-inoculation. Post-mortem examination consistently revealed splenomegaly and hemorrhagic lesions in the spleen and lymph nodes, as previously described.[15] During the experimental period, oral and rectal swabs in viral transport medium (Noble Bioscience, South Korea) and whole blood in an EDTA tube (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) were obtained from three pigs inoculated with Korea/Pig/Hongcheon/2022 and two pigs injected with Korea/Pig/Pocheon1/2023. Tissue samples, including spleen, liver, kidney, and forelimb muscle, were collected at necropsy from five dead pigs. Until further analysis, whole blood samples were kept at 4°C, whereas all other specimens were stored at − 80°C.

Design of sample preparation and storage

All experimental procedures, including sample storage, were performed in a laboratory within the ABSL-3 facility. The environmental conditions were maintained at a constant temperature of 21 ± 2°C, with a negative pressure of − 130 ± 13 Pa and relative humidity of 60 ± 20%. For each specimen type, five replicates were collected. The storage duration and testing intervals were determined based on previous studies as well as the available sample volume. The stability and persistence of ASFV in oral swabs, rectal swabs, and whole blood were evaluated under four different temperature conditions (−20°C, 4°C, 21°C, and 37°C) over a period of 168 days (24 weeks). Although whole blood obtained from infected pigs is capable of preserving both viral genome and infectious particles for extended periods, the storage period was inevitably limited by the total sample volume collected (approximately 20 mL). Whole blood samples were aliquoted into 0.5 mL volumes in 1.5 mL tubes, and oral and rectal swab specimens in viral transport medium were aliquoted into 0.5 mL volumes in 1.5 mL tubes. Aliquots were incubated for 3, 7, 14, 28, 56, 84, 112, 140, and 168 days at the designated temperatures and subsequently transferred to −80°C until real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and virus titration were performed. For each tissue sample type, five samples were prepared to assess viral-genome quantities and titers at four temperatures over an extended period of up to 364 days (52 weeks). Tissue samples were sectioned into 0.15-g portions and placed in 2.0-mL microcentrifuge tubes. Tissue aliquots were stored for 3, 7, 14, 28, 56, 84, 112, 140, 168, 196, 224, 252, 280, 308, 336, and 364 days and afterward stored at −80°C until further analysis [Figure 1].

Detection of African swine fever virus genome and virus titration

Tissue homogenates were prepared using a TissueLyser II system (Qiagen, the Netherlands) with the addition of 1.5 mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline. Following homogenization, samples were centrifuged at 6,000×g for 5 min, and the supernatants were passed through a 0.45 μm pore-size filter. Viral DNAs were extracted from all specimens using the Maxwell® RSC 48 instrument (Promega, Madison, WI) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed using a CFX96 Touch system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) following the diagnostic protocol recommended by the WOAH.[17] Viral-genome quantities were determined by correlating cycle-threshold (Ct) values with a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of a pUC54 plasmid containing a partial sequence of the B646 L gene of the BA71V strain.

Virus titers were determined following the standard protocol established by the EURL for ASF.[16] Virus titers were assessed using a hemadsorption-based endpoint dilution assay in PAM cells. PAMs were seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in 96-well culture plates with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Each sample was serially diluted 10-fold, and 50 μL of each dilution was inoculated into the corresponding well. A 1% swine erythrocyte suspension was added to each well to enable visualization of hemadsorption. Plates were incubated at 37°C in a CO2 incubator for 6 days, after which hemadsorption was examined. Virus titers were calculated using the Reed and Muench method.

Statistical modeling and analysis

Comparison of different conditions on African swine fever virus stability

A linear mixed-effects model (LMM) was used to compare Ct values of ASFV across different sample types, storage temperatures, and incubation times, accounting for repeated measurements within individual pigs.

The model was specified as:

εijk ~ N(0,σ2)

In this equation, represents the Ctijk value for the i-th pig, subjected to the j-th sample type and f-th temperature at the k-th time point (day). Model fitting was performed using the lme4 package (version 1.1-35.5[18]) in R software (version 4.4.1[19]). Fixed-effect estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were obtained using the Wald approximation. The LMM approach accounted for repeated measurements within individual animals and estimated fixed effects for sample type, storage temperature, and incubation time.

Viral decay and half-life estimation

ASFV infectivity was quantified as HAD50 per mL, determined through endpoint titration assays in primary porcine alveolar macrophages. Titer values were expressed as log10 HAD50/mL for statistical modeling and interpretation. To estimate the rate of viral decay and calculate half-life values under each sample type and temperature condition, nonlinear least squares (NLS) regression was conducted using the following exponential decay model:

V(t) = V0 · e−kt

where V(t) represents the mean viral titer at time t, V0 is the estimated initial titer, and k is the decay constant. This exponential decay model estimates the rate constant (k) and half-life for each sample type and temperature combination. Half-life values and 95% CIs were calculated using standard methods. Survival analysis of African swine fever virus tenacity

To evaluate the duration of viral infectivity, survival analysis was conducted based on time to loss of infectivity, defined as the time point when HAD50 decreased to zero or below the detection limit. Samples that remained infectious until the final observation day were treated as right-censored, particularly whole blood samples.

A Cox proportional hazards frailty model was used to assess the impact of sample type and temperature on time to loss of infectivity, accounting for clustering within individual animals. A Cox proportional hazards frailty model was employed to account for clustering by individual pigs. A shared frailty term with a gamma-distributed random effect for pig ID was included, yielding the following hazard function:

h(t│X,z) = h0(t) * exp (Xβ + zpig), zpig ~ gamma(θ)

This approach accounted for unobserved heterogeneity among individual pigs while estimating the fixed effects of sample type and temperature on time to infectivity loss. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were estimated using the survival package (version 3.7-0[20]). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, standard error, and 95% CIs) were computed by sample type, temperature, and day. Bar plots with error bars (±95% CI), Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and line plots with confidence ribbons were generated using the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1[21]) and the survminer package (version 0.4.9[22]). Model assumptions were assessed using standard diagnostics, and HRs with 95% CIs were estimated.

Relationship between viral stability and tenacity

Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the linear associations among Ct values, log10-transformed viral genome copy numbers, and log10 HAD50/mL infectious titers. Binary logistic regression was performed to determine whether Ct values could predict viable virus status. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and optimal cutoff thresholds (via Youden’s J index) were derived to evaluate model performance. Pairwise correlation matrices were visualized using heatmaps and correlation plots, and the strength and direction of correlations were interpreted according to standard thresholds. Binary logistic regression was performed to assess whether Ct values could predict the presence of viable viruses (defined as HAD50>0). Virus-viability status was used as the binary outcome variable (1 = viable, 0 = non-viable). Predicted probabilities from each model were used to construct ROC curves using the pROC package (version 1.18.5[23]) in R, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess model performance. The optimal cutoff threshold was determined using Youden’s J index,[24] and corresponding sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were reported. The 95% CIs for AUCs were computed using DeLong’s method.[25]

Results

African swine fever virus stability and tenacity in whole blood and oral and rectal swabs

Viral-genome copy numbers and infectious titers were quantified in intact samples. Whole blood contained the highest viral loads, with mean genome copy numbers 6.84 ± 0.10 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL and virus titers 7.63 ± 0.33 log10 HAD50/mL. Oral swab samples exhibited lower viral levels (3.63 ± 0.48 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL and 3.15 ± 0.11 log10 HAD50/mL), whereas rectal swabs (4.39 ± 0.25 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL and 4.10 ± 0.68 log10 HAD50/mL) showed slightly higher values than those of oral swabs [Table 1 and Figure 2].

Across all temperatures, genome-copy quantities in whole blood remained relatively stable, fluctuating around 6.95 ± 0.20 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL over the 168-day incubation period, with no significant decline regardless of temperature. Conversely, infectious titers gradually decreased over time, with the reduction magnitude increasing at higher temperatures. At −20°C and 4°C, ASFV remained infectious throughout the 168 days, although virus titers declined from the initial level (7.63 ± 0.33 log10 HAD50/mL) to 5.33 ± 0.38 and 6.03 ± 0.62 log10 HAD50/mL at − 20°C and 4°C, respectively. At 21°C, infectious virus was detected for 156.8 ± 15.3 days, with titers decreasing to 2.05 ± 2.00 log10 HAD50/mL by day 168. At 37°C, viral infectivity persisted for a shorter duration, remaining detectable for up to 106.4 ± 30.7 days [Table 1 and Figure 2].

In both oral and rectal swab samples, viral-genome quantities and infectious titers declined in an inversely temperature-dependent manner, showing broadly similar patterns. Genome stability in oral swabs was greater than that observed in rectal swabs under elevated temperatures. ASFV genomes in oral swabs were detectable for the entire 168-day period at −20°C, 4°C, and 21°C, whereas at 37°C, genome detection was limited to 95.2 ± 70.3 days. Viral infectivity in oral swabs only persisted in intact samples, during which initial titers were largely maintained. In rectal swabs, viral genomes remained detectable for 168 days at − 20°C, but the detection period shortened to 145.0 ± 30.7 days at 4°C and 106.0 ± 60.7 days at 21°C; at 37°C, genome detection was limited to 25.2 ± 28.7 days. Infectious ASFV in rectal swabs persisted for 36.4 ± 4.35 days at − 20°C, followed by 18.8 ± 21.3 days at 4°C and 1.4 ± 3.1 days at 21°C, whereas at 37°C infectivity was only detected in intact samples [Table 1 and Figure 2].

African swine fever virus stability and tenacity in tissue samples

The spleen harbored the highest viral-genome loads and infectious titers, with mean values 5.94 ± 0.42 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL and 5.83 ± 0.59 log10 HAD50/mL, respectively. Liver samples exhibited comparable genome copy quantities (5.56 ± 0.45 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL), but slightly lower virus titers (4.73 ± 0.77 log10 HAD50/mL). Kidney specimens contained lower viral loads (4.68 ± 0.56 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL) while maintaining relatively high virus titers (5.40 ± 0.48 log10 HAD50/mL). Forelimb muscle samples showed the lowest viral-genome numbers (3.49 log10 ASFV genome copies/μL) and titers (3.43 ± 0.28 log10 HAD50/mL).

ASFV genomes in spleen, liver, and kidney tissues remained detectable at all temperatures throughout the 364 days. Genome copy numbers in the spleen and liver progressively declined with increasing incubation temperature, whereas no marked change in viral loads was observed in kidney samples over time. In muscle tissues, viral genomes were detected for 336–364 days at −20°C, 4°C, and 21°C, while genome detection at 37°C was considerably shortened compared with the lower temperature conditions.

Viral-infectivity duration in tissue samples demonstrated a clear inverse relationship with storage temperature. In spleen samples, infectious ASFV persisted for 179.2 ± 25.0 days at −20°C and for 106.4 ± 36.5 days at 4°C, whereas tenacity rapidly declined at 21°C and 37°C. In both liver and kidney tissues, viral infectivity was maintained for more than 224 days at −20°C but was reduced to 47.6 ± 27.3 days at 4°C and was lost within 7 days at 21°C and 37°C. The minimum virus titers detected at the endpoint in tissues were 2.05 log10 HAD50/mL. In muscle samples, viable ASFV remained detectable for 50.4 ± 12.5 days at −20°C and for 23.8 ± 19.5 days at 4°C, whereas infectivity was eliminated within 7 days at 21°C and 37°C. The endpoint virus titers in muscle specimens were 1.93 log10 HAD50/mL [Table 1 and Figure 3].

Statistical modeling and analysis

Effects of sample type, storage temperature, and incubation time on viral genome detection

Linear mixed-effect modeling revealed significant effects of sample type, storage temperature, and incubation time on Ct values. Compared to whole blood (reference), Ct values were significantly higher in oral swabs (β = 13.10, 95%CI: 12.40–13.80, P < 0.001) and rectal swabs (β = 13.60, 95%CI: 12.90–14.30, P < 0.001), indicating lower viral-genome numbers in swab samples [Table 2]. In addition, each 1°C increase in storage temperature was associated with a 0.08 unit increase in Ct value (95%CI: 0.06–0.09, P < 0.001), while each additional day of storage increased Ct value by 0.02 (95%CI: 0.02–0.03, P < 0.001). When the spleen was used as reference, kidney samples showed slightly higher Ct values (β = 2.92, 95%CI: 2.61–3.23, P < 0.001), while liver (β = 1.76, 95%CI: 1.45–2.07, P < 0.001) had a smaller increase. Muscle tissues exhibited the highest Ct-value increase relative to spleen (β = 7.36, 95%CI: 7.04–7.67, P < 0.001), suggesting significantly lower viral-genome quantities [Table 2]. Similarly, Ct values increased by 0.04 per °C rise (95%CI: 0.03–0.04, P < 0.001) and by 0.01 per storage day (95%CI: 0.01–0.01, P < 0.001), consistent with the trends observed in swab samples.

Among all sample types, whole blood exhibited the longest viral stability across all temperatures. At − 20°C, the estimated half-life in whole blood was 18.27 days (95%CI: 15.36-22.55), which further increased to 30.75 days (95%CI: 22.85–46.99) at 4°C. Conversely, oral swabs showed a sharp half-life reduction with increasing temperature from 22.89 days (95%CI: 17.42–33.37) at − 20°C to 0.29 days (95%CI: 0.23–0.39) at 37°C. Rectal swabs demonstrated the shortest half-lives, with only 0.21 days (95%CI: 0.17–0.26) at 37°C. In tissue samples, the kidney and muscle preserved infectivity significantly longer than the spleen or liver at lower temperatures. At − 20°C, kidney samples had an estimated half-life of 22.56 days (95%CI: 15.56–41.04), whereas the spleen and liver had much shorter half-lives (1.94 days and 0.91 days, respectively). Notably, the liver sample at 4°C maintained infectivity with a half-life of 12.75 days (95%CI: 9.91–17.86), sharply dropping to <0.25 days at 37°C. Across all tissue types, temperature was a key determinant of viral survival. A consistent inverse relationship was observed between storage temperature and viral half-life.

Survival time of virus infectivity by sample type and temperature

Survival analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazards frailty model to assess the impact of sample type and temperature on viral viability over time. Infectivity loss was defined as the point when HAD50 titer declined to zero or below the detection limit (1.5 log10 HAD50/mL). Shared frailty terms accounted for random effects by individual animals (pig ID). Compared to oral swabs (reference category), rectal swabs exhibited a lower hazard of infectivity loss (HR = 0.51, 95%CI: 0.26–1.01, P = 0.052), although the association was marginally non-significant. Temperature was positively associated with hazard (HR = 1.07, 95%CI: 1.04–1.09, P < 0.001), indicating a higher risk of virus inactivation at elevated temperatures. The muscle sample showed the highest hazard for loss of infectivity (HR = 8.74, 95%CI: 4.02–18.99, P < 0.001), followed by the liver (HR = 3.27, 95%CI: 1.55–6.86) and kidney (HR = 2.94, 95%CI: 1.33–6.47), compared to the spleen. Again, temperature was significantly associated with increased viral-decay hazard (HR = 1.11, 95%CI: 1.08–1.13, P < 0.001). Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by temperature and sample type visually confirmed the trends [Figure 4]. No violation of the proportional-hazard assumption was observed based on Schoenfeld residuals.

Diagnostic accuracy of the cycle-threshold value for predicting viable African swine fever virus

Logistic regression demonstrated that Ct values were significant predictors of viral viability (P < 0.001). ROC curve analysis yielded an AUC of 0.92 (95%CI: 0.90–0.94) for whole blood and both swabs, indicating discriminative ability [Figure 5c]. The optimal Ct cutoff value, determined using Youden’s J index, was 24.2. At this threshold, sensitivity and specificity were 82.7% and 91.5%, respectively. In tissue samples, ROC curve analysis yielded an AUC of 0.76 (95%CI: 0.73–0.79), indicating excellent discriminative ability [Figure 5d]. The optimal Ct cutoff value, determined using Youden’s J index, was 22.0. At this threshold, the sensitivity and specificity were 61.5% and 80.1%, respectively.

Discussion and Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the persistence of ASFV is markedly influenced by sample type, storage temperature, and incubation period. Among the tested matrices, whole blood exhibited the greatest stability, retaining viral infectivity for up to 168 days when stored at −20 °C and 4 °C. In contrast, tissue samples such as spleen, liver, and kidney maintained detectable viral genomes for more than 300 days, although the degree of decrease in infectivity varied across tissues and temperatures. Muscle and swab specimens showed comparatively shorter survival periods under low temperature conditions. Across all sample types, viral stability and infectivity displayed a consistent inverse correlation with temperature. Statistical modeling further confirmed significant differences in viral decay kinetics and survival times depending on both the matrix and the storage temperature. These findings highlight the critical role of sample type and temperature in determining ASFV persistence.

The present findings align with earlier studies that have emphasized the remarkable environmental persistence of ASFV, particularly in blood and tissue specimens stored under low-temperature conditions.[2627282930] Previous investigations reported that preserved blood can maintain infectivity for an extended period for up to 24 months at 4°C.[30] Recent works have also demonstrated the inverse temperature-dependent decline in infectivity in various matrices such as feces, feed, straw, and water, further supporting the tendency observed in this study.[3132] Our study supports these observations and further expands upon them by systematically evaluating multiple sample matrices across four distinct temperature settings, thereby offering a more comprehensive perspective on ASFV persistence. Differences in reported persistence across studies may also reflect variations in experimental design, sample handling, and detection methods, underscoring that ASFV stability and tenacity are influenced not only by matrix and temperature but also by methodological factors.

The pronounced stability of the ASFV genome in whole blood is likely associated with the preservative action of EDTA, which inhibits DNA degradation by chelating divalent cations essential for nuclease activity.[33] In addition, the complex virion structure of ASFV – comprising a nucleoprotein core surrounded by double envelopes – provides further protection under diverse environmental conditions.[789] Enhanced persistence observed in tissues such as spleen and kidney may be explained by the protective role of organic matrices, whereas the relatively rapid decay noted in muscle and swab samples suggests limited structural support for viral stability. Interestingly, the data presented in Table 3 revealed that the estimated half-life of ASFV in liver tissue was longer at 4°C compared with − 20°C. This phenomenon may be attributed to the presence of degradative enzymes, including nucleases and proteases, which remain active during freeze–thaw cycles and accelerate viral inactivation. Similar observations have been reported in hepatic tissue, where repeated freeze–thaw cycles trigger the activity of degradative enzymes.[3435] At 4°C, enzymatic activity decreased, allowing viral particles to remain structurally intact for longer durations.

The results of this study have direct implications for ASF control strategies. The prolonged survival of ASFV in blood and tissue samples under cold conditions provides a plausible explanation for the seasonal increase in ASF cases among wild boar populations during the winter period.[111213] These findings emphasize the importance of effective carcass disposal, strict biosecurity practices, and intensified surveillance during colder seasons to reduce the risk of indirect transmission. In addition, whole blood was confirmed as the most reliable diagnostic specimen, consistently yielding stable genome detection and maintaining infectivity. Statistical modeling further demonstrated that Ct values serve as strong predictors of viral viability, with an AUC of 0.92. For blood samples, the optimal Ct cutoff value for successful virus isolation was determined to be 24.2. This highlights that whole blood should be prioritized for both PCR-based diagnosis and virus isolation, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency in ASFV detection.

Several limitations should be considered in interpreting the present findings. First, the detection thresholds of real-time PCR and virus titration may have underestimated the actual duration of ASFV persistence. Second, the restricted sample volumes limited long-term monitoring of blood beyond 24 weeks, although predictive modeling suggested survival periods exceeding 1 year under low-temperature conditions. Third, the experiments were performed under controlled laboratory settings, which cannot fully replicate the complexity of natural environments. Important environmental factors such as ultraviolet exposure, humidity, and soil interactions were not assessed, yet these variables may substantially influence viral stability in the field. Fourth, the study relied on a relatively small number of animals, which may constrain the generalizability of the results. Fifth, the Ct values used to predict viral infectivity were derived solely from this dataset; validation using independent datasets and diverse viral isolates will be necessary before broader application. Finally, while the results provide strong experimental and statistical evidence, clinical validation under field conditions remains essential. Taken together, these results should be interpreted as highlighting potential risks associated with prolonged ASFV persistence in specific matrices and environments, rather than confirming direct transmission pathways. Despite these limitations, the integration of a multi-matrix design, extended observation periods, and robust statistical analyses offers novel insights into ASFV persistence and strengthens the evidence base for improved control measures.

In conclusion, the analysis of the experimental data provides insights into the environmental survival characteristics of ASFV in various sample types under different temperature conditions. The findings highlight differences in viral stability in blood, oral and rectal excretions and tissues, with temperature influencing both ASFV stability and tenacity. Lower temperatures markedly prolong virus survival, potentially extending the duration of environmental contamination. These results should therefore be interpreted as indicating potential risks of indirect ASFV exposure through contaminated environments and carcasses, rather than confirming direct transmission pathways. Such insights contribute to improved understanding of viral persistence across sample types and temperatures, supporting quarantine and mitigation measures against ASF.
