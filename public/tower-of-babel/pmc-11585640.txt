Ibetazol, a novel inhibitor of importin β1-mediated nuclear import
Authors: Thomas Vercruysse, Els Vanstreels, Maarten Jacquemyn, Sandro Boland, Amuri Kilonda, Sara Allasia, Ilse Vandecaetsbeek, Hugo Klaassen, Matthias Versele, Patrick Chaltin, Arnaud Marchand, Dirk Daelemans
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nucleocytoplasmatic transport plays an essential role in eukaryotic cell homeostasis and is mediated by karyopherins. Importin β1 (KPNB1) and its adaptor protein importin α1 (KPNA2) are the best-characterized karyopherins that effect nuclear import. Here, we identify a novel small-molecule inhibitor of the importin β1-mediated nuclear import. We design a reporter cell line by stably tagging endogenous importin α1 with a fluorescent protein to screen for compounds affecting its subcellular localization. We identify a series of compounds that trigger cytoplasmatic accumulation of importin α1. The lead compound, ibetazol, is further characterized in a broad sequence of cellular nuclear transport assays. Ibetazol is shown to inhibit all importin β1-mediated nuclear import quickly and specifically, without affecting transport mediated by other karyopherins. Detailed molecular mechanism of action studies demonstrate that ibetazol inhibits importin β1 by covalently targeting Cys585. In summary, ibetazol is a novel small molecule inhibitor of importin β1 enabling pharmacological inhibition of the importin β1-mediated nuclear import process with wide applicability in different fields.

Introduction

Nucleocytoplasmic transport, the controlled exchange of macromolecules between the nucleus and cytoplasm, is essential to eukaryotic cellular homeostasis. It is mediated by soluble carrier proteins called karyopherins, which can be classified as importins, exportins, or biportins, depending on the directionality of transport. Recognition of cargo by the karyopherin requires a specific modular targeting signal present in the cargo: a nuclear localization signal (NLS) or a nuclear export signal (NES). The driving force behind the transport is the RanGTP gradient across the nuclear membrane which is maintained by the subcellular localization of guanine exchange factor RCC1 (RanGEF) and the GTPase activating protein RanGAP. RanGEF is bound to chromatin and therefore nuclear while RanGAP is localized in the cytoplasm. Therefore, the small GTPase Ran is GTP-bound in the nucleus while in the cytoplasm the RanGAP activates Ran to hydrolyze GTP into GDP, assisted by RanBP1 (Ran binding protein 1). Importins bind their cargo in the cytoplasm, translocate through the nuclear pores and release their cargo upon RanGTP binding in the nucleus. Conversely, exportins bind cargo and RanGTP coordinately in the nucleus to form a ternary complex which transfers through nuclear pores and is subsequently disassembled in the cytosol upon GTP hydrolysis (reviewed in Cautain et al.1, Christie et al.2, Wing et al.3).

Karyopherins fulfill critical roles in a variety of essential eukaryotic cellular processes such as signal transduction, response to changes in the environment and cell cycle progression. Dysregulation or malfunctioning of nucleocytoplasmatic transport has been implicated in various health issues such as cancer and inflammatory, cardiovascular, or neurodegenerative diseases4–6. Hence, the process and regulation of nucleocytoplasmatic transport have triggered broad research interest and efforts.

Study of the nuclear export process has been facilitated by the availability of potent and selective inhibitors of exportin-1 (XPO1), which is the main exportin responsible for the export of a large number of cargo’s with broad functionalities. The long-known antifungal leptomycin B is a natural product that covalently binds to cysteine 528 in the hydrophobic NES-binding groove of exportin-1, thereby blocking interaction with cargo and hence inhibiting its nuclear export7. More recently, potent drug-like small molecule exportin-1 inhibitors with similar mechanism of action were developed8,9. These were further developed into medicines with favorable pharmacological properties of which selinexor (KPT-330, XPOVIO®) has been approved as an anti-cancer drug for the treatment of multiple myeloma (MM) and relapsed or refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (RR DLBCL), becoming the first clinically applied nucleocytoplasmatic transport inhibitor10,11.

However, inhibitors of importins are far less developed and are mostly directed against importin β1 (KPNB1) and/or its adaptor importin α1 (KPNA2), which are the most-studied and best-characterized members of the importin family12. While importin β1 can mediate nuclear import of cargo protein on its own, most of its cargos bind with their NLS to an adaptor protein of the importin-α family (importin α1 to importin α8). These interact via their importin-β binding domain (IBB) with importin β1 followed by import of the ternary importin β1/importin α/cargo complex into the nucleus, where the complex is then released after binding of RanGTP to importin β1. Several inhibitors described as of today are interfering with the importin β1-RanGTP interaction to impede the importin β1-mediated nuclear import process13–16.

We here present a novel low molecular weight compound, ibetazol, with a distinct mechanism of action. Ibetazol interacts with cysteine 585 in importin β1, and thereby effectively prohibits both direct and adaptor (importin α) protein-dependent importin β1-mediated nuclear import. Import mediated by other karyopherins remains unaffected. Ibetazol has favorable properties as compared to previously described inhibitors13–16, such as a higher margin of activity vs cytotoxicity, lack of intrinsic fluorescence, and a rapid effect on nuclear transport. This novel compound and its analogs are therefore of value for the study of importin β1-mediated nuclear import mechanisms, regulation, and cargos; both fundamentally as well as in the context of life-threatening diseases.

Results

High content screening, hit identification and exploration

In search for novel compounds that inhibit nuclear import mediated by importin β1, we set up a phenotypic high-content screening strategy. Importin α1 acts as an adaptor protein between the importin β1 import receptor and its cargos. Therefore, blocking the importin β1- importin α1 interaction in the cell would shift the localization of importin α1 from predominantly nuclear to cytoplasmic, resulting in inhibition of the nuclear import of cargo proteins (Fig. 1A). We thus set up an assay based on the re-localization of importin α1 to screen for importin β1 inhibitors. Using CRIPSR-based genome engineering17, we generated a Hela Kyoto cell line in which the endogenous importin α1 is tagged with mNeongreen. This importin α1-mNeongreen fusion protein localizes predominantly in the nucleus under normal conditions but shifts completely to the cytoplasm when KPNB1 is knocked down (Fig. 1B). This stable importin α1-mNeongreen expressing cell line was then used in a phenotypic screen with a diverse drug-like small-molecule library consisting of ~200,000 molecules (Fig. 1C). Cells were incubated for 2 h with 20 µM of each compound and then fixed with PFA. The localization of importin α1 was quantified by measuring mNeongreen intensities in the nuclear and cytosolic compartments. For each compound the percentage of cells with predominant nuclear localization was quantified and compared to untreated control. The 640 compounds that showed the strongest deviation from untreated control were selected and subsequently re-evaluated in an 8-point dose-response confirmation experiment. Among the 27 compounds surviving this selection, compound CDD-01 showed the strongest effect on importin α1-mNeongreen localization. CDD-01 also affects localization of wild-type endogenous importin α1 that was not fused to the mNeonGreen label, as was demonstrated by immunostaining of importin α1 in wild-type cells (Fig. 1B). In addition, the activity of CDD-01 could be chemically validated in a dose-response experiment using a resynthesized batch of the compound (EC50 = 10.0 ± 2.8 µM) (Fig. 1E). In this assay, the importin β1 inhibitor importazole showed no cytoplasmic relocalization of importin α1 (Fig. 1E). Importazole blocks the interaction between importin β1 and RanGTP. As a result, importin α1 dissociation from the ternary import complex in the nucleus cannot occur resulting in a nuclear localization of importin α1. Structure-activity relationship (SAR) exploration was initiated around CDD-01 and data on representative compounds CDD-02 to CDD-04 are shown (Fig. 1D and Table 1). The minimal cyclopropyl group from CDD-01 could be replaced by other ring structures, e.g., phenyl, while maintaining activity. Nonetheless, several compounds, including CDD-01, displayed various degrees of cytotoxicity. The combined presence of the sulfone, pyrimidine and electron-withdrawing substituents may result in significant reactivity, which can explain the observed cytotoxicity. Replacement of the sulfone by the less reactive thioether resulted in a completely inactive CDD-02. Further variations around CDD-01 led to the sulfoxide derivative CDD-03, which retains good activity in the importin α1 relocalization assay (EC50 = 6.1 ± 1.9 µM) (Fig. 1E) while displaying no apparent toxicity at the highest tested concentration of 60 µM. Alternative attempts to indirectly modulate the reactivity of the sulfone group confirmed that some degree of electrophilic reactivity was required for activity. For instance, the replacement of the CF3 group by a simple methyl led to the inactive compound CDD-04. Ibetazol specifically blocks all importin β1-mediated nuclear transport

Retention of importin α1 in the cytoplasm should in principle inhibit the import of its NLS bearing cargos. To evaluate this effect on import of NLS-cargo, a stable cell line expressing a NLScMyc-AcGFPx2-NESIKB2 reporter construct was generated (Supplementary Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1B). This fusion protein shuttles continuously between nucleus and cytoplasm but in steady state it localizes predominantly in the nucleus due to the importin β1-importin α dependent import signal (NLScMyc) that is relatively stronger than the exportin-1 dependent export signal (NESIKB2). Incubation of these reporter cells with CDD-01 or ibetazol inhibited the import of the reporter in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A, B). A similar dosing effect was also found on the PMA induced importin β1 dependent import of endogenous RelA/p65 cargo (NFκB) (Fig. 2C, D, Supplementary Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1D). As a comparison, the established importin β1 import inhibitor importazole was also evaluated in these two importin β1-importin α dependent cargo assays. While most cargos of importin β1 are imported in complex with the importin α adaptor protein family, a smaller subset of proteins is imported through direct interaction with importin β1, such as for example Snail or SREBP1/2. To be able to discriminate between inhibitors of importin β1-importin α dependent import and direct importin β1 cargos we developed a stable cell line that visualizes the import of SREBP1. A N-terminal truncated version of SREBP1 lacking part of the transcriptional activation domain, was fused to two mNeongreen molecules and over-expressed in Hela Kyoto cells (Supplementary Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 2B). Based on the observations made by Cheng et al.20, that import of SREBP1 into the nucleus depends on glucose mediated N-glycosylation of SCAP, an assay was constructed in which importin β1 dependent accumulation of SREBP1 in the nucleus is induced by adding glucose to the culture medium for 3 h (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Incubation of the mNeongreenx2-SREBP1 reporter cell line with CDD-01 and ibetazol resulted in an accumulation of SREBP1 in structures around the nucleus, which supports the idea that SREBP1 is still able to traffic with SCAP from the ER to the Golgi but cannot be imported in the nucleus anymore (Fig. 2E, F). Importazole did not show an inhibitory effect in our SREBP1 import assay at the concentrations and incubation time tested.

We next determined the specificity of ibetazol and initial hit compound CDD-01 towards importin β1-mediated import by testing their activity in two counterscreen assays. In the first assay the reporter construct NLSSV40-AcGFP-NESPKI was stably expressed in Hela Kyoto cells. This reporter protein also shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm but resides mainly in the cytoplasm because of its strong export signal (NESPKI) (Supplementary Fig 2C and Supplementary Fig 2D). This stable cell line allows evaluation of inhibitory effects on exportin-1-dependent nuclear export21. In the second counter screen assay, a shuttling reporter construct NESPKI*-mNeongreenx2-NLSFUS was stably expressed in HeLa Kyoto cells. This reporter protein localizes to the nucleus in steady state, but its nuclear import via the nuclear import signal of FUS (NLSFUS) is mediated by karyopherins transportin-1 and transportin-2 (Supplementary Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 2F). No relocalization of the reporter constructs was observed upon treatment of these counterscreens with ibetazol and CDD-01 (Fig. 2G–J). This suggests that the inhibitory effect of these compounds is specific for importin β1-dependent import. Taken together our data thus suggest that ibetazol and related compound CDD-01 selectively block importin β1-mediated nuclear import.

To further characterize our CDD compounds, we analyzed their effect on the cellular localization of importin β1 itself as well as on alternative importin β1 dependent functions such as mitotic spindle formation (Supplementary Fig. 3). We visualized importin β1 localization using immunofluorescence staining in wild-type HeLa Kyoto cells. Untreated cells show a clear accumulation of importin β1 at the nuclear membrane as well as a diffuse staining pattern in both cytoplasm and nucleus. Upon compound treatment, importin β1 accumulation at the nuclear membrane diminished and a more pronounced nuclear localization was observed (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Other importin β1 inhibitors that target the RanBP1 binding site in importin β1 have been reported to induce a more cytoplasmic relocalization of importin β113, again pointing to a distinctive mechanism of action of this novel class of compounds including ibetazol. Besides its role in nuclear import, importin β1 also plays a role in mitosis, where it regulates the localization and activity of factors implicated in mitotic spindle organization22,23. Therefore, we investigated whether ibetazol treatment could induce spindle defects. For this purpose, we visualized alpha-tubulin and chromosomes in synchronized HeLa Kyoto cells by immunofluorescence and DAPI staining respectively. Treatment with 50 µM ibetazol for 2 h resulted in multiple spindle malformations and chromosome misalignment in the majority of metaphasic cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B). A similar effect of importazole has been reported earlier15.

Ibetazol binds to cysteine 585 of importin β1

Because reactivity of the sulfoxide group is mandatory for the activity of ibetazol, as evidenced from the SAR study, we hypothesized that this compound class interacts covalently with importin β1. Therefore, we set up an experiment in which we incubated recombinant importin β1 protein with 150 µM of compound for 2 h and subjected it to LC-MS/MS analysis after digestion with trypsin, chymotrypsin and GluC (Supplementary Fig. 4). Covalent binding of ibetazol to one or more residues of importin β1 will change the mass peak(s) of the corresponding peptide(s), resulting in the appearance of additional peaks with increased mass corresponding to the bound ibetazol and/or in decreased peak intensity for the parent (unmodified) peptide. We identified two modified cysteine residues (Cys543 and Cys392), albeit with low intensities compared to the unbound fraction. For a third cysteine (Cys585), the peak of the parent peptide completely disappeared from the mass spectrum in the compound treated condition. This suggested that Cys585 is the main binding site of ibetazol.

To further confirm that our compound covalently binds to Cys585, we introduced alanine mutations on the three cysteine residues (Cys392, Cys543, Cys585) identified as potential binding sites in the mass spectrometry. We edited the KPNB1 gene of the near-haploid HAP1 cell line using CRISPR/Cas9 to create mutant cell lines importin β1C392A, importin β1C543A, and importin β1C585A (Supplementary Fig. 5A). The same editing protocol was applied to create control cell lines with silent mutations importin β1C392C, importin β1C543C, and importin β1C585C. These control cell lines thus still express wild-type importin β1 protein but underwent a similar gene-editing protocol as the alanine mutant cell lines. Mutant and control cell lines were then treated with different concentrations of ibetazol and their cell growth monitored over 48 h. Since importin β1 is a common essential gene (DepMap, https://depmap.org), its inhibition is expected to impair cell proliferation. Indeed, ibetazol incubation of the control HAP1 cell lines, expressing wild-type importin β1, inhibited their growth (Fig. 3A, B). An IC50 of approximately 6 µM was found for all three control cell lines. A similar IC50 was obtained for cell lines expressing alanine mutants importin β1C392A and importin β1C543A (respectively 6.7 ± 0.8 µM and 5.1 ± 1.0 µM), indicating that Cys392 or Cys543 are not required for the activity of ibetazol. However, a relatively small but significant shift in IC50 (11.8 ± 1.2 µM) was measured for the importin β1C585A cell line (Fig. 3B). To further confirm that Cys585 is important for the anti-importin β1 activity of ibetazol, we also applied CRISPR Base Editing to engineer mutant importin β1C585Y/S and control importin β1C585C HAP1 cell lines. We employed these importin β1 mutant HAP1 cell lines to study the effect of our compound on the localization of importin α1. Therefore, wild type, importin β1C585Y/S mutant, and importin β1C585C control HAP1 cells were transfected with an importin α1-mNeonGreen fusion construct (Fig. 3C, D). In untreated cells importin α1-mNeonGreen was localized in the nucleus in all three cell lines. Treatment with ibetazol or CDD-01 resulted in relocalization to the cytoplasm in wild-type and control importin β1C585C cells, while no relocalization could be observed in mutant importin β1C585Y/S cells. These results again demonstrate that the Cys585 residue is the interaction site of our compounds with importin β1.

Finally, an in vitro thermal shift experiment (TSA) was performed on purified wild-type (WT) importin β1 and mutant importin β1C585A protein. Treatment of WT importin β1 with 30 µM ibetazol for 2 h induces a change in the melting profile: on the one hand, ibetazol stabilizes importin β1 (ΔTm 2.2 °C compared to the negative control (CCD-04) and non-treated WT importin β1), and on the other hand, ibetazol also partially destabilizes the protein (Fig. 3E). Interestingly, ibetazol did not stabilize the mutant importin β1C585A protein (Fig. 3F). Altogether, these observations indicate that ibetazol interacts with Cys585 of importin β1.

To provide some insight into how ibetazol binds to importin β1, and more specifically to Cys585, we submitted the compound to a covalent docking simulation (Fig. 4). Multiple crystal structures and cryo-EM structures of importin β1 have been reported and reveal the protein in different conformational states, depending on the bound cargos24–28. In some of those structures, e.g. PDB 1QGK26 (importin β1 + importin α1 IBB), Cys585 is essentially buried in the bulk of the protein structure, and not available for covalent-docking simulations. Structure 2QNA25 features a more accessible Cys585 and, interestingly, a bound cargo (snurportin-1) in its vicinity. After receptor preparation, which included removal of the bound snurportin-1 cargo, ibetazol was docked in its proposed binding site using the covalent docking module from Glide (Schrödinger) (Fig. 4A). A modified version of the “nucleophilic substitution” covalent docking reaction was used for the simulation to treat the non-conventional sulfoxide leaving group, which was not covered by the default reactions. Once bound, the transferred ibetazol fragment (without the sulfoxide leaving group) lies in the inner face of importin β1 and is tied to Cys585 from position 2 of the pyrimidine ring (Fig. 4B). Part of the inhibitor, including the phenyl ring, occupies a small cavity that was used by Arg43 of the initial snurportin-1 cargo (Fig. 4C). None of the two nitrogens from the pyrimidine structure appears involved in a H-bond interaction, suggesting that their essential role was to confer some reactivity to the sulfonyl leaving group. Discussion

Here we describe a new series of nuclear import inhibitors. CDD-01 was identified as an inhibitor of importin β1-importin α mediated nuclear import in a high-throughput phenotypic screen. Further characterization of this compound class using a panel of fluorescent microscopy-based assays revealed that they specifically inhibit importin β1-dependent import (the direct importin β1 dependent cargo SREBP1, importin α1, and importin β1-importin α cargos). Inhibition of importin β1-dependent nuclear import by the most potent compound CDD-03 (ibetazol) is visible within 2 h after treatment demonstrating that these compounds can be used as tool compounds to study specific importin β1-mediated import. An analog of this CDD family has been previously described as BETP, a GLP-1 agonist that binds to the GLP-1R receptor that is expressed on beta cells of the pancreas and neurons in the brain18,19. While we demonstrate that BETP inhibits importin α1 import and importin β1 dependent cargo import, it is significantly more toxic to the cells than CDD-03.

The requirement of a reactive sulfoxide group suggested that the CDD compounds interact covalently with importin β1. Further mechanism of action studies demonstrated that mutation of Cys585 abrogates the interaction of ibetazol with importin β1 protein and confers resistance to the inhibition of importin β1-dependent nuclear import in cell culture. These observations strongly suggest that Cys585 is the covalent binding site for the reactive group. Also, thermal shift experiments confirm interaction of ibetazol with Cys585.

We conducted a covalent docking experiment in order to provide an idea of how and where ibetazol binds to importin β1. Calculations were performed based on PDB structure 2QNA25, which corresponds to a complex between importin β1 and snurportin-1 as cargo. It should however be noted that Cys585 remains buried in a majority of the reported importin β1 structures (e.g. PDB 1QGK or 1UKL29), highlighting some conformational variability in that area. The proposed binding mode should therefore be considered an approximation.

In the 2QNA structure, Cys585 directly interacts with Arg43 of the bound snurportin-1 cargo and seems accessible to an inhibitor. Consequently, the position of the bound ibetazol, as proposed by the covalent docking simulation, overlaps with the initial position of Arg43 from the snurportin-1 cargo. Nonetheless, such a “direct interposition” mechanism would not explain the observed effect of ibetazol on a broad range of importin β1 cargos. Indeed, binding of the importin β1 cargos can occur at areas that are more remote from Cys585 (e.g. the position of SREBP-2 cargo in PDB structure 1UKL) suggesting that an indirect mechanism of action is also involved. Several publications have described flexible conformations of the importin β1 protein that are required to interact with a large set of cargo proteins, nucleoporins and GTPase Ran26,27,30–32. Therefore, ibetazol may alter the conformational freedom of importin β1 by covalent binding to Cys585 thereby indirectly affecting cargo binding.

Several other small molecules interfering with importin β1 function have been described. Importazole (IPZ) was the first importin β1 import inhibitor selected in an in vitro screen to target interaction of RanGTP with importin β1 thereby blocking total importin β1 function15. Although this compound has been widely used as a tool compound to study Ran dependent importin β1 functions and to show the potential of this importin as a target for the treatment of cancer33–38, its selectivity in cell-based assays is very limited. Palma et al. 201939, showed that IPZ blocked nuclear import of NUAK1 in HeLa cells at 75 µM, but not at 50 µM; while being toxic at 100 µM. We observed no effect of IPZ in different importin β1-dependent import assays at concentrations up to 50 µM, while higher concentrations were toxic to the cells. Two other importin β1 import inhibitors INI-43 and INI-60 were identified based on an in silico screen targeting the overlapping binding site on importin β1 of RanGTP and importin α1 (residues 331-361) and demonstrate a similar mechanism of action as IPZ13,16,40. While these compounds can be used at a lower concentration compared to IPZ, they are less amenable to study nuclear import by fluorescence microscopy since INI-43 is highly fluorescent in the green channel. Finally, a class of aminothiazole derivatives was shown to bind directly to importin β1 in vitro (to the pocket surrounding Ser476), but no fluorescent microscopy or comparable technologies showing a direct effect on nuclear import in cells within hours has been demonstrated41,42. In this context, ibetazol represents an interesting tool compound for studies involving importin β1, with a distinct mode of action, higher margin vs cytotoxicity, no fluorescence in the green channel and rapid effects on importin β1-mediated nuclear export.

Inspired by the success of the exportin-1 inhibitor selinexor, several other karyopherins, including importin β1, importin α1, IPO11, and IPO13, have been proposed as potential therapeutic targets for specific cancer types43–46. Importin β1 is one of the most essential proteins in mammalian cells (DepMap, https://depmap.org/portal/), but nevertheless, some of the small molecule inhibitors of importin β1-mediated import mentioned above have been described and proposed as potential treatment for cancers that are sensitive to depletion of importin β113,15,16,41. Besides its role in cancer, nucleocytoplasmic transport deficiencies and importin β1-dependent import specifically, have been associated with several other diseases. Neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS, TFD, Huntington disease and Alzheimer disease (reviewed in Coyne and Rothstein4, Vanneste and Van den Bosch47, Ding and Seprehimanesh48) have been linked with an aberrant import mechanism48. Also, infection and replication of several human disease-causing viruses is dependent on correct cellular nuclear import49–52. In this work, we did not investigate the selective cytotoxicity of ibetazol or analogs towards importin β1-dependent cancer cells nor its potential as a therapy for other importin β1-related health conditions. Given the residual off-target cytotoxicity we observed, we suggest further efforts to structurally optimize ibetazol or its derivatives towards more importin β1-directed selectivity before the onset of therapy-orientated research programs. However, the well-characterized importin β1 inhibitor and transport assays described in this paper can make a valuable contribution in the fundamental study of the role, mechanisms and regulation of nuclear transport processes relevant in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases or the field of virology. In summary, we identified a new series of small molecule importin β1 inhibitors that can be used as a tool compound to study importin β1-mediated nuclear import in a wide field of different disease phenotypes.

Methods

Small molecule screen

A diverse drug-like chemical library (~200,000 small-molecules in total) assembled and provided by the Centre for Drug Discovery and Design (CD3, KU Leuven, Belgium) was screened to look for compounds inhibiting nuclear localization of importin α1 in a Hela Kyoto importin α1-mNeonGreen reporter cell line. The phenotypic high-content screen was performed at Hit Discovery Constance (HDC, Konstanz, Germany). All compounds were dissolved in 100% DMSO. Adenoviral mediated knockdown of KPNB1 employing Ad-U6-h-KPNB1-shRNA (Vector Biolabs), which results in clear cytoplasmic relocalization of the importin α1-mNeonGreen fusion protein, was used as a positive control.

Cell lines

Human parental HAP1 cells were obtained from Horizon Discovery and Hela Kyoto cells were a gift from Prof. Johan Van Lint (KU Leuven). Cells were grown at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and passaged every 2–3 days in IMDM (HAP1) or DMEM (Hela Kyoto) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 20 µg/ml gentamicin. The cells were regularly checked for mycoplasma contamination using the Venor GeM OneStep PCR kit (Minerva Biolabs).

Commercial compounds

Importazole was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and SelleckChem. Selinexor (KPT-330) was provided by Karyopharm Therapeutics (Newton, MA, USA). Phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA) was obtained from Merck. All compounds were dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich). MG-132 was obtained as a 20 mM solution in EtOH from Sigma-Aldrich.

Aqueous solubility assay

Solubility was evaluated as described by Colclough et al.53 Compounds of interest are initially prepared at a concentration of 10 mM in 100% DMSO. Serial dilution of the compounds is then performed using 100% DMSO as the diluent and a 96-well polypropylene V-bottom plate (Greiner Bio One) as the vessel. Negative controls are included in the form of wells containing 100% DMSO to establish the in-solubility threshold. Next, 1.5 µL of each serially diluted compound is transferred into 300 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH = 7-7.2) (Invitrogen) in a flat-bottom, polystyrene 96-well plate, resulting in a starting concentration of 50 µM with 0.5% DMSO. This dilution is repeated in an additional 96-well, flat-bottom, polystyrene plate. The plates are then incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by measurement of the absorbance of each well at 570 nm using an Envision multilabel plate reader (Perkin Elmer). The average background absorbance and standard deviation are calculated from the negative controls located in columns 1 and 12. The first precipitated concentration is identified by determining the concentration at which the absorbance value is more than the average negative control plus five times the standard deviation of the negative control. The aqueous solubility of each compound is then reported as the “soluble at” value, which represents the concentration at which the compound is still soluble and is the concentration before the first precipitated concentration.

Generation of stable CRISPR knock-in cell lines using CRISPaint

For each shuttling construct a different donor plasmid was created with a cytomegalovirus promotor followed by the following shuttling constructs: FLAGx3-NLSSV40-AcGFP-NESPKI (exportin-1 assay), NLScMyc-AcGFPx2-NESIκB2 (cMyc translocation assay) and NESPKI*-mNeonGreenx2-NLSFUS (FUS translocation assay). NESPKI* is a mutant of NESPKI with a K to P mutation (MSLNELALPLAGLDI). These shuttling constructs were followed by a P2A-coupled puromycin resistance gene.

Generation of stable CRISPR knock-in cell lines by microhomology-mediated end-joining

Hela Kyoto mNeonGreenx2-SREBP1 cell line

Names and sequences of the sgRNAs and cDNA used are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The coding sequence of SREBP1 lacking the N-terminal activation domain (29aa) was cloned into an expression cassette behind an EF1A promotor and two mNeongreen molecules in a pcDNA3.1 vector. This plasmid also contains on the 5’ and 3’ side two microhomology sequences compatible with CRISPR/Cas9 based stable integration of this expression cassette into the AAVS1 safe harbor locus. Using the method described by Sakuma et al.54, Hela Kyoto cells stably expressing mNeongreenx2-SREBP1 were generated. A monoclonal cell line was further selected based on the highest expression of the SREBP1 fusion construct.

Generation of stable CRISPR knock-in cell lines by non-homologous end-joining using a knock-in cassette

HAP1 importin β1 C392C/A - C543C/A - C585C/A cell lines

Importin β1 mutants were created by integrating the desired mutations in the corresponding exon of the cysteine that needs to be mutated, using the method described by Geisinger et al.55. For each cysteine an upstream sgRNA was selected using the CRISPOR tool56. The cassettes were amplified starting from the same pcDNA3.1-CMV-KPNB1-P2A-Puro-SV40-polyA plasmid in which the forward primer contained the desired mutation and amplified the missing cDNA of KPNB1 followed by a P2A coupled puromycin resistance gene and the SV40 poly A signal. All the sgRNAs and primers used for cassette amplification can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Phosphorotioate bonds were incorporated into the primers during synthesis by the manufacturer (IDT). HAP1 cells were co-transfected with a plasmid encoding spCas9 and the sgRNA targeting the genome and the knock-in cassette using TurboFectin 8.0 (Origene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Following transfection, cells were incubated for 2 days and then selected over a period of 1 week with 1 µg/mL puromycin. Resistant cells were harvested and plated at a density of 0.5 cells/well in 96-well plates in 20% FBS containing medium to obtain single-cell-derived colonies. Colonies were grown for 2–4 weeks and were regularly screened.

Generation of stable HAP1 importin β1 C585C and C585Y/S cell lines using base editors

pCMV_AncBE4max_P2A_GFP and pCMV_ABEmax_P2A_GFP were a gift from David Liu (Addgene plasmid #112100 and #11210157). HAP1 cells were transfected using TurboFectin 8.0 (Origene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. pCMV_AncBE4max or pCMV_ABEmax were co-transfected with a plasmid encoding the puromycin resistance gene and a sgRNA targeting exon 14 of KPNB1 (Supplementary Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table 1). 24 h after transfection, cells were selected for 2 days with 1 µg/mL puromycin. Colonies were grown for 2–4 weeks and were regularly screened.

DNA extraction and sequencing of importin β1 mutants

When single cell derived colonies were sufficiently grown, cells were washed and then lysed in Bradley lysis buffer at 56 °C (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS, 10 mM NaCl and 1 μg/mL proteinase K). The genomic DNA was extracted from the lysate using ethanol-salt precipitation and centrifugation. For the CRISPR knock in cell lines, the target site of the KPNB1 C585 mutants was amplified by in-out PCR and sequenced by Sanger Sequencing (Macrogen) using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Importin α1, cMyc and FUS import assays and exportin-1-dependent export assay

The Hela Kyoto importin α1-mNeonGreen monoclonal cell line was used to monitor importin α1 translocation to the cytoplasm upon compound treatment. Importin β1-importin α dependent nuclear import of NLScMyc bearing cargo was monitored employing the HeLa Kyoto NLScMyc-AcGFPx2-NESIKB2 shuttling cell line. Transportin-1/2-mediated nuclear import of NLSFUS bearing cargo was monitored employing the Hela Kyoto NESPKI*-mNeonGreenx2-NLSFUS shuttling cell line. All three above mentioned cell lines show predominant nuclear localization of the reporter construct in untreated steady-state condition. HeLa Kyoto cells stably expressing the FLAG3x-NLSSV40-AcGFP-NESPKI shuttling construct were used to evaluate exportin-1-dependent nuclear export. This reporter construct is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm in untreated steady-state condition.

Cells were seeded and the following day incubated with serial dilutions of compound or vehicle control for 2 h. Cells were then fixed and counterstained with DAPI. Positive controls were adenoviral mediated knockdown of KPNB1 employing Ad-U6-h-KPNB1-shRNA (Vector Biolabs) for the importin α1 and cMyc import assays, transfection with M9M peptide expressing plasmid for the FUS import assay58 and treatment with 1 µM selinexor for the exportin-1-mediated export assay. Fluorescence was read on an Cellinsight CX5 High Content Reader (ThermoFisher Scientific). Nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments were segmented and their average pixel intensities in the green channel were quantitated employing the HCS Studio software. Curve fitting and calculation of EC50 values was performed using GraphPad Prism. Compounds were tested in at least two independent experiments.

RelA (p65) import assay

The RelA (p65) translocation assay is based on PMA-induced IκB phosphorylation which leads to its degradation and dissociation from the NFκB p65/p50 heterodimer. This results in importin β1-importin α dependent nuclear import of p65. Hela Kyoto cells were incubated with serial dilutions of CDD compounds for 3 h, the last hour 1 µM PMA was added to induce p65 nuclear import. Adenoviral mediated knockdown of KPNB1 was used as a positive control. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA and immunostained with primary anti-NFkappaB p65 (F-6) antibody (sc-8008, SantaCruz, 1:100) and secondary goat anti-Mouse Alexa Fluor™ 488 antibody (A11029, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:500). Fluorescence readout and quantification and calculation of EC50 was performed as described above. Compounds were tested in at least two independent experiments. For high-resolution images cells were imaged on a Leica TCS SP5 confocal microscope using a HCX PL APO 63x (NA 1.2) water immersion objective.

SREBP1 import assay

Using the monoclonal Hela Kyoto cell line stably expressing mNeongreenx2-SREBP1 and based on the study of Cheng et al.20 on the nuclear import of SREBP1, a SREBP1 nuclear import assay was developed. Briefly, in the absence of glucose SREBP1 is kept in the ER by interaction with SCAP bound to INSIG. In the presence of glucose, N-glycosylation of SCAP leads to its increased instability and dissociation from INSIG, initiating trafficking of the SCAP-SREBP1 heterodimer to the Golgi. In the Golgi, the N-terminal part of SREBP1 is cleavedof by S2P and S1P proteases, and SREBP1 is imported into the nucleus by importin β1 after which the protein is rapidly degraded.

To monitor the import of mNeongreenx2-SREBP1, the stable Hela Kyoto cell line was incubated in DMEM medium without glucose. Stable accumulation of SREBP1 in the nucleus was induced by replacing this medium for 3 h with medium containing 10 g/L glucose and proteasome inhibitor MG132. To measure the influence of the CDD compounds on the importin β1-dependent import of SREBP1, serial compound dilutions in the induction medium where made and incubated for 3 h on the cells. Adenoviral mediated knockdown of KPNB1 was used as a positive control. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA and translocation of SREBP1 was analyzed by high content analysis or confocal microscopy as described above.

Visualization of mitotic spindles in synchronized cells

HeLa Kyoto cells were seeded in 8-well Ibidi microslides and grown overnight. Cells were then synchronized as described in Di Francesco et al.59. Brieflly, cells were treated for ~24 h with 9 µM of CDK1 inhibitor RO-3306 to induce a G2/M arrest. During the last hour of the incubation with RO-3306, ibetazol (50 µM) or vehicle control (DMSO) was added. Then the medium was replaced for medium without RO-3306 but still containing the compound or vehicle control, to allow for synchronous release from the mitotic block. Cells were further incubated for 1 h. They were then fixed and immunostained with primary Anti-α Tubulin Antibody (B-7) (sc-5286, SantaCruz Biotechnology, 1:200) and secondary Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Antibody labeled with Alexa Fluor™ 488 (A-11001, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:500). Cells were counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed on a Leica TCS SP5 confocal microscope using a HCX PL APO 63x (NA 1.2) water immersion objective.

Immunofluorescence of importin α1 and importin β1

HeLa Kyoto cells were seeded in 8-well Ibidi microslides and grown overnight. Upon compound treatment for 2 h, they were fixed and immunostained with primary anti-KPNA2 antibody (#14372, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:50) and secondary Goat anti-Rabbit Alexa Fluor™ 488 antibody (A11008, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:500) or with primary anti-KPNB1 antibody [3E9] (ab2811, Abcam, 1:2000) and secondary Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Antibody labeled with Alexa Fluor™ 488 (A-11001, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:500). Cells were counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed on a Leica TCS SP5 confocal microscope using a HCX PL APO 63x (NA 1.2) water immersion objective.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well microtiter plates (TPP) and treated with a serial dilution of ibetazol. They were placed in an Incucyte S3 live cell analysis device for 48 h and confluence was monitored and quantified using the Incucyte software.

Protein production and purification

KPNB1 wt and KPNB1 mutant (C585A) genes were cloned into a pET29b vector containing a N-terminal HIS-tag. After growing the respective transfected BL21(DE3) competent E. coli cells (69450-4, Novagen) LB cultures containing 100 μg/mL kanamycin and 34 μg/mL chloramphenicol at 37 °C in a shaking incubator to 0.4 to 0.8OD, proteins were expressed by induction with 0.1 mM IPTG and incubated overnight at 18 °C. Cells were harvested (15 min, 5000 g) and cell pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Cells were resuspended in 40 mL lysis buffer (50 mM TrisHCl pH 7.5, 200 mM KCl, 5 mM imidazole, 2.5 mM TCEP (C4706, Merck), 2 mM PMSF, 100 µg/ml Benzonase (70746, Merck), 50 µg/ml lysozyme, cOmpleteTM, Mini, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (11836170001, Roche) and were lysed by sonication and CelLyticTM Express (C1990, Sigma-Aldrich).

The obtained supernatans were subjected to Ni-NTA purification (Ni Sepharose 6 fast flow, Cytiva) in batch purification. After overnight incubation of the cell lysate with the resins, the beads were first washed with 10CV of 50 mM TrisHCl pH7.5, 1 M KCl, 10 mM imidazole pH7.5, 2.5 mM TCEP and then with 10CV of 50 mM TrisHCl pH7.5, 1 M KCl, 25 mM imidazole pH7.5, 2.5 mM TCEP. Proteins were eluted by 100 mM TrisHCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole pH7.5, 2.5 mM TCEP and immediately buffer exchanged to 100 mM TrisHCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM TCEP by PD10 desalting columns to remove the imidazole and stored at −80 °C for later analysis. The protein was further purified with a Superdex 200 increase column (Cytiva) using 100 mM TrisHCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM TCEP. Only peak fractions corresponded to monomeric proteins were pooled and concentrated with a VivaSpin 20 concentrator, molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) 30,000 (Sartorius). 10% of glycerol was added to concentrated proteins and were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Thermal shift assay

The melting temperatures (Tm, temperature at which 50% of the protein is unfolded) of wild-type and mutant (C585A) importin β1 proteins were determined by thermofluor, a fluorescence-based thermal shift assay60. SYPRO Orange dye from the protein thermal shift dye kit (Applied biosystems) was diluted 125× in TSA buffer (100 mM TrisHCl, pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl). 3 µM protein was pre-incubated for 2 h at room temperature with 30 µM of compound in DMSO (final concentration DMSO is 1%) in 30 µL TSA buffer. 10 µl of SYPRO Orange dilution was added to the protein-compound mixture before heating from 4 °C to 95 °C in increments of 1 °C/min on a StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). All measurements were done in duplicate. The fluorescence of SYPRO Orange increases when it binds to hydrophobic parts of proteins that become exposed upon thermal denaturation. Tm values were determined by calculating the first derivative from the melting curve using the Protein thermal shift software (Applied Biosystems).

LC-MS/MS analysis

LC-MS/MS experiments were performed at OmicScouts (Freising, Germany). Trypsin (pilot and main experiment): 35 µM of recombinantly purified importin β1 protein (in 100 mM TrisHCL, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM TCEP, 20% glycerol, pH 7.5) was treated (in singlet or in triplet for respectively the pilot and main experiment) with 70 µM or of CDD-03 (ibetazol) or DMSO O/N at 4 °C and 300 rpm. 5 µg of this reaction was added to a urea buffer (8 M urea, 10 mM TCEP, 40 mM CAA, 40 mM Tris/HCL pH 7.6), incubated for 30 min at 30 °C and diluted to <2 M. urea in 40 mM Tris/HCL pH 7.6. This mixture was digested by trypsin (ratio enzyzme/protein 1:100) at 37 °C O/N. After desalting by C18 SPE using StageTips 60 ng of the digested protein was injected for MS with a 40 min gradient and dynamic exclusion of 10 s. Trypsin/Chymotrypsin/GluC experiment: importin β1 protein (in 100 mM TrisHCL, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM TCEP, 20% glycerol, pH 7.5) was subjected to a buffer exchange in UPLC grade H2O with 1 mM of DTT using ZebaSpin columns. 14.5 µM of importin β1 was incubated with 150 µM of CDD-03 (ibetazol) or DMSO at RT for 2 h. 5 µg of this reaction was digested with 1:50 Trypsin at 35 °C O/N in 8 M urea, 40 mM Tris/HCL pH 7.6, with 1;100 Chymotrypsin at 25 °C O/N in 8 M Urea, 100 mM TrisHCL pH 8, 10 mM CaCl2 and with 1:100 GluC at 35 °C O/N in Ammonium Bicarbonate Buffer 50 mM. The resulting mixtures were desalted by C18 SPE using StageTips and 60 ng of the digested protein was injected for MS with a 40 min gradient and dynamic exclusion of 10 s.

For each experiment, the data is shown for every cysteine-containing residue as a % of the intensities of unmodified/carbamidomethylated or compound-bound peptides of the compound-treated condition versus unmodified/carbamidomethylated peptides of the DMSO-treated control.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software. All analyses were performed at a threshold alpha level of 0.05. Information about the specific statistical test used for each dataset and the number of replicates is mentioned in the respective figure caption. Replicates are defined as wells from one or more independent experiments. All experiments were readily reproducible.
