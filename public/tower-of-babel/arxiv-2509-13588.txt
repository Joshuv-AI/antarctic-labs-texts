Programmable Cognitive Bias in Social Agents
Xuan Liu Haoyang Shang Haojian Jin

Abstract
This paper introduces CoBRA , a novel toolkit for systematically specifying agent behavior in LLM-based social simulation. We found that conventional approaches that specify agent behaviors through implicit natural language descriptions cannot yield consistent behaviors across models, and the produced agent behaviors do not capture the nuances of the descriptions. In contrast, CoBRA presents a new approach to program agents’ cognitive biases explicitly, by grounding agents’ expected behaviors using classic social science experiments. CoBRA has two components: (1) Cognitive Bias Index that measures the cognitive bias of a social agent, by quantifying the agent’s reactions in a set of validated classical social science experiments; (2) Behavioral Regulation Engine that aligns the agent’s behavior to demonstrate controlled cognitive bias. We evaluated CoBRA as an HCI toolkit through demonstration and technical benchmarks. Our results suggest that CoBRA can precisely program the cognitive bias demonstrated in a social agent in a model-agnostic manner.

Introduction

;

), personal interests (e.g., hobbies, preferences), social relationships (e.g., friends, family, colleagues), and professional or task-oriented identities (e.g., judge, programmer).
These practices rest on a growing understanding that LLMs, having been trained on vast text corpora, possess both perceptual skills

;
Liu et al. 2024
that enable them to detect subtle cues in implicit language

.

However, the research community has little empirical understanding of whether this approach is sufficient for reliable social simulations.
For example,
would the conclusion of the social simulation change if we change the foundation models, the prompts, or the temperatures?

As a consequence, the conclusions of most LLM-based social simulations often are broad qualitative impressions—such as noting that a simulation is plausible and exhibits an echo chamber similar to the real world

In this paper, we first conducted a pilot experiment to empirically examine the limitations of implicit natural language specification (Section

). We designed four agent specifications by adapting examples from prior work

;

, implemented them across four foundation models, and evaluated their responses using a classic social science experiment, the Asian Disease study

We then developed
CoBRA
—the
Co
gnitive
B

R

A
gents (Figure
1
)—a novel toolkit that enables users to precisely program the cognitive biases exhibited by social agents across different models. Compared to implicit natural language specifications,
CoBRA
offers two key advantages. First, it enhances reproducibility of agent behaviors across models, which is critical given that access to specific foundation models may change over time

. Second, it provides explicit and quantitative control over agent behaviors, rather than relying on unpredictable model behaviors

At the heart of CoBRA is a novel closed-loop system primitive that continuously measures and regulates agents’ cognitive biases by grounding their behaviors in classic social science experiments. Classic experiments provide
validated
and
structured
protocols: they define precise stimuli, expected behavioral distributions, and well-documented bias effects. For instance, the
Asian Disease
study
Tversky & Kahneman 1981
has been extensively replicated and is widely regarded as a canonical demonstration of the framing effect: when identical outcomes are framed in terms of lives saved versus lives lost, human participants systematically shift between risk-averse and risk-seeking choices.
Within CoBRA, such experiments function as calibration tasks: the system first measures whether an agent demonstrates the expected bias by observing its responses in these experiments and then adjusts the agent’s specification to align it with the target behavioral profile (Figure
2
).
We further hypothesize that aligning an agent’s behavior with target profiles in a set of classical experiments will reliably induce the corresponding cognitive biases, which will then generalize and manifest across a broader range of tasks (Section
7.2
).

Our implementation of CoBRA has two components: (1) Cognitive Bias Index that measures the cognitive bias of a social agent by quantifying the agent’s reactions in a set of validated classical social science experiments; (2) Behavioral Regulation Engine that aligns the agent’s behavior to demonstrate controlled cognitive bias. The Cognitive Bias Index contains eight classic social experiments mapped to four representative biases—Authority Effect, Bandwagon Effect, Confirmation Bias, and Framing Effect.
The Behavioral Regulation Engine employs three approaches (prompt engineering
Rath et al. 2025
;
Zhuo et al. 2024
, representation engineering
Zou et al. 2023
;
Rimsky et al. 2024
;
Todd et al. 2024
, and fine-tuning
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2022
;
Ilharco et al. 2023
) to align agent behaviors.

We position CoBRA as an HCI toolkit

that empowers social scientists to create new types of social simulations. We evaluated CoBRA using two common strategies for assessing HCI toolkits
Ledo et al. 2018
:
Technical Benchmark
and
Demonstration
. In the benchmark, we show that (1) the cognitive bias induced through one classical experiment generalizes to other tasks (Section
7.2
); (2) agents specified through CoBRA exhibit consistent behavioral tendencies regardless of the foundation model, sampling temperature, or reasoning mode (Section
7.3
); and (3) CoBRA enables precise control under both open-weight and API-only settings (Section
7.4
).
In the Demonstration, we show how social scientists can use CoBRA to simulate the Emotional Contagion experiment
Kramer et al. 2014
, where the participant’s social influence susceptibility (i.e., bandwagon effect bias) can be manipulated (Section
8

This work makes four main contributions. First, we present the first empirical evidence that implicit natural language specifications, the dominant method for defining LLM-based social agents, fail to produce reliable and reproducible behaviors across models. Second, we introduce
CoBRA
, a novel HCI toolkit that enables social scientists to precisely program and control cognitive biases in agents, improving reproducibility and allowing explicit, quantitative alignment. Third, we contribute a closed-loop system primitive that grounds agent behaviors in validated and structured social science experiments, using them as primitives for both measuring and regulating cognitive biases. Finally, we demonstrate CoBRA through technical benchmarks and a case study of the Emotional Contagion experiment, showing that induced biases generalize across tasks, remain robust across models and settings, and allow social scientists to create new forms of controlled social simulations.

Background & Related Work

This section reviews the use of large language models (LLMs) for social simulation and outlines the prevailing methodological pipeline, noting that current implicit natural-language agent specification approaches—while expressive and flexible—hinder rigor and comparability
Bail 2024
;
Pang et al. 2025
.

2.1
Believable Agents for Social Simulation

Believable Agents
are interactive computational entities, originating as non-player characters in games
Bates 1994
. Early designs relied on rule-based systems or behavior trees to dictate their actions and decision-making processes
Loyall 1997
. Their observable behavior appears coherent, context-appropriate, and consistent with prior experiences, creating an “illusion of life” or human-like plausibility
Park et al. 2023
;
Anthis et al. 2025b
;
Banovic et al. 2023
;
Ehsan et al. 2024
. The goal is not human-equivalent cognition but externally credible behavior: agents should perceive, plan, and act in ways that are explainable, temporally consistent, and aligned with social and physical norms
Goyal et al. 2024
;
Chandramouli et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025a
;
Zhang et al. 2023
.

Recent works use LLMs to create
Believable Agents
for social simulation
Bail 2024
;
Wu et al. 2022
;
Anthis et al. 2025a
;
Hwang et al. 2025
;
Tjuatja et al. 2024
;
Prpa et al. 2024
;
Aubin Le Qu’er’e et al. 2024
. For example, prior work
Park et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024
utilizes LLMs’ language understanding to prompt models with natural language persona descriptions, creating LLM-based agents that interact within simulated environments. These agents facilitate the study of emergent phenomena—such as cooperation, norm formation, and conflict—and heuristic behaviors like conformity
Liu et al. 2025b
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Shao et al. 2023a
;
Wu et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
. While these LLM-based
Believable Agents
broaden feasible questions and accelerate iteration by reducing labor, and mitigating ethical concerns
Bail 2024
;
Hwang et al. 2025
, they struggle with consistent, controllable behavior
Yang et al. 2025
;
Han et al. 2025
—a critical need for rigorous social science simulations
Bail 2024
.

In this work, we adopt the concept of
Believable Agents
and address prior rigor limitations by introducing CoBRA, a toolkit for systematic agent specification that yields consistent, controllable behavior.

2.2
Reproducibility of LLM-Based Methods in Social Science

Reproducibility is a critical concern in the use of LLMs for social science research, as the probabilistic nature of LLMs combined with varying implementation methods often leads to inconsistent and non-reproducible outcomes
Bail 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2021
.

One major issue is that different LLMs exhibit divergent behaviors even with the same prompts
Liu et al. 2023
. Variations in training datasets
Tayo et al. 2023
, model architectures
Jiang et al. 2024
, and fine-tuning processes
Touvron et al. 2023
exacerbate this inconsistency, while the opacity of proprietary models further complicates cross-model comparisons and limits generalizability
Gudibande et al. 2023
. Second, inference-time configurations significantly affect reproducibility. Sampling parameters like temperature settings influence output variability, with models differing in how peaky or flat their probability distributions are, affecting the generation coherence and diversity
Peeperkorn et al. 2024
. Similarly, reasoning modes
Wei et al. 2022
(direct response vs. multi-step reasoning) introduce additional variability during inference, as different reasoning strategies can lead to divergent conclusions from the same input. Finally, post-training methods (e.g., RLHF
Ouyang et al. 2022
) also introduce inconsistencies across models, further complicating reproducibility. Addressing these challenges requires standardized methodologies and explicit control mechanisms to ensure reliable results.

In this work, we address reproducibility challenges by complementing the implicit natural language Agent Specification with an explicit approach that leverages structured, validated knowledge from classic social experiments to define agent behaviors, reducing variability and ensuring consistent outcomes.

3
Understanding Agent Specification

This section presents a pilot experiment to empirically examine the limitations of implicit natural language specification.
The results showed that current methods failed to produce consistent, cognitive bias-aligned behavior.

3.1
Method

Our key idea is to use a classical social experiment, the
Asian Disease
study
Tversky & Kahneman 1981
, as the study instrument.
We created agents using different specification methods, powered these agents using different foundation models, and then analyzed their responses in the simulated social experiment.

Agent specifications
.
We evaluated two representative examples of implicit natural language agent specifications: a persona-based specification from
Park et al. 2023
, which constructs agents with human-like profiles by incorporating demographics, personality traits, interests, and social relationships; and a role-based specification from
Chen et al. 2024b
, which defines agents through task-oriented identities, assigning roles, domain expertise, and interaction protocols to guide task-specific behavior.

For the persona-based example
Park et al. 2023
, we introduced three conditions:
Common
(an ordinary person),
Economist
(a persona with a professional background expected to make them less susceptible to framing effects and, therefore, less likely to prefer the positive frame compared to the Common persona), and
Blank
(no specification). We adopted the
John Lin
prompt from
Park et al. 2023
to represent the Common persona (see
Appendix
B
for full prompt):

John Lin is a pharmacy shopkeeper at the Willow Market who loves to help
people. He is always looking for ways to make the process of getting
medication easier for his customers. John Lin is living with his wife,
Mei Lin, who is a college professor, and son, Eddy Lin, who...

In the Economist condition, we modified only the profession, changing “a pharmacy shopkeeper at the Willow Market” to “a professor of economics at a nearby college,” while keeping all other details identical. The
Asian Disease
scenario prompt was used directly as the input.

For the role-based example
Chen et al. 2024b
, we introduced two conditions:
Teacher
(the agent was specified as having the profession of a teacher) and
Blank
(no specification). We adopted the
Teacher
prompt from

(a score between 0 and 9) Engaging:... Problem: [description] Student’s

Simulated social experiment setup
.
We adapted the classic
Asian Disease
study
Tversky & Kahneman 1981
for testing framing effect-participants tended to make more risk-averse decisions when outcomes were positively framed (e.g., “200 people will be saved”), but exhibited greater risk-seeking behavior when the same outcomes were presented as losses (e.g., “400 people will die”), into a prompt-based scenarios template. A sample scenario prompt is as follows:

You are presented with a scenario where a disease outbreak is expected to
kill 600 people unless a program is implemented. Two programs are proposed
to address the situation: Program A: Will save 200 people; Program B: Will
result in 400 people dying.

We then measured the agent’s framing preference using a five-point Likert multiple-choice scale: strong preference for the positive frame, preference for the positive frame, no preference, preference for the negative frame, and strong preference for the negative frame.

Models and repetitions
.
We then tested the agent specifications across four models—Mistral 7B, Gemma2 9B, GPT-4o Mini, and DeepSeek-v3—using a temperature of 0.25. We intentionally used a low, more deterministic temperature of 0.25 to minimize randomness. Each example was evaluated using 15 scenario prompts, each with varying positive and negative frames, repeated 10 times, resulting in a total of 150 queries. The frequency of choice selections was recorded for analysis.

3.2
Results

By comparing agents’ reported preferences, we observed:

1. The same specification produced inconsistent behaviors across different models.
As shown in Fig.
3
, for all specification variants (
Economist
,
Common
, and
Blank
), models exhibited varying choice frequencies without consistency. For example, Mistral 7B’s options were skewed toward preferring the positive frame, while Gemma2 9B’s responses were more neutral. Similarly, Fig.
4
shows inconsistent choice frequency distributions across models for both the
Teacher
and
Blank
conditions.

2. Implicit specification did not reliably yield expected behaviors.
As shown in Fig.
3
, the
Economist
, expected to be less susceptible to framing effects, often exhibited equal or greater framing effects than the
Common
. For instance, in Mistral 7B, the choice frequency distribution for the
Economist
was more heavily skewed toward preferring a positive frame compared to the
Common
, indicating a greater framing effect—contradicting real-world expectations. Similarly, as shown in Fig.
4
, the
Teacher
, expected to grade fairly regardless of framing, instead favored positively framed answers. Both GPT-4o Mini and Mistral 7B showed choice distributions skewed toward the positive side, indicating the
Teacher
failed to provide accurate and unbiased grading, deviating from its intended specification.

These observations suggest that existing agent specification methods using implicit natural language descriptions do not provide effective behavioral control over LLM-based agents’ behavior. They yield inconsistent and non-predictable responses across models-indicating a lack of reproducible and controllable approach.

4
Design Overview

The design goal of CoBRA is to provide reproducibility and controllability in social agents—capabilities that conventional natural-language specifications cannot reliably offer. We operationalize these concepts in LLM-based social simulations as follows.

For
reproducibility
, CoBRA ensures (1)
cross-model reproducibility
: the same specification yields consistent tendencies across models (e.g., Qwen vs. Mistral); (2)
sampling robustness
: behavioral patterns remain stable under varying sampling conditions (e.g., temperature 0.2–0.8); and (3)
reasoning robustness
: behaviors are consistent whether or not reasoning steps are explicitly generated.

For
controllability
, CoBRA emphasizes (1)
monotonicity
: Stronger interventions consistently produce non-decreasing shifts toward the intended behavioral direction; (2)
smoothness
: incremental adjustments yield proportionally small, predictable changes; and (3)
expressiveness
: cognitive biases can be either suppressed or amplified to a high degree.

To realize the above design goal,
CoBRA
integrates two core components that work synergistically to quantitatively specify and regulate agent behaviors: (1)
Cognitive Bias Index
(CBI) (Section
5
) that measures the cognitive bias of a social agent, by quantifying the agent’s reactions in a set of validated classical social science experiments; (2)
Behavioral Regulation Engine
(Section
6
) that aligns the agent’s behavior to demonstrate controlled cognitive bias.

5
Cognitive Bias Index

The
Cognitive Bias Index (CBI)
serves as both a
measurement metric
and a
specification knob
, developed through the systematic adaptation of structured and validated knowledge from classic social science experiments
French et al. 1959
;
Asch 1951
;
Tversky & Kahneman 1981
;
Milgram 1963
;
Kahneman & Tversky 2013
;
Wason 1960
;
Lord et al. 1979
;
Goldstein et al. 2008
. Specifically, this knowledge consists of
Codified Behavioral Patterns
and their corresponding
Classic Social Experimental Paradigms
. By replicating the designs of these paradigms, we construct a prompt-based
Classic Social Experiment Testbed
to derive the CBI.

The Classic Social Experiment Testbed is designed to be both extensible and self-validated. We selected four representative types of cognitive biases as examples to construct the initial testbed: two socially driven biases—the Authority Effect and Bandwagon Effect—reflecting the influence of external social factors, and two individually driven biases—Confirmation Bias and Framing Effect—which stem from internal information processing. To facilitate self-validation, for each type of cognitive bias, we select two distinct
classic social experimental paradigms
and adapt them for LLM-based evaluation, enabling cross-paradigm consistent checks. Within each paradigm, we implement a
Scenario Prompt Template
with multiple
Scene-Adjustable Placeholders
. Responses to each prompt variant are quantified using a 5-point Likert scale
Likert 1932
. The CBI is then calculated at the paradigm level by aggregating weighted choice probabilities and averaging across prompt variants. Together, these designs yield a Classic Social Experiment Testbed that produces corresponding CBI:

Definition 1

(Cognitive Bias Index)
.

Suppose each type of cognitive bias
b\in\mathcal{B}
is evaluated using
m
distinct classic social experimental paradigms. The set of Cognitive Bias Index is defined as

\left\{\mathrm{CBI}_{b}^{(k)}\;\middle|\;b\in\mathcal{B},\ k=1,2,\ldots,m\right\}

(1)

where
\mathrm{CBI}_{b}^{(k)}
denotes the Cognitive Bias Index for bias type
b
in the
k
-th classic social experiment scenarios.

In each paradigm, suppose there are
n
scenario prompt variations, each derived from distinct sets of scene-adjustable placeholders. For each variation, the agent’s response is assessed using a 5-point Likert scale with options
O=\{O_{1},O_{2},O_{3},O_{4},O_{5}\}
, representing decreasing levels of bias from
O_{1}
(highest) to
O_{5}
(lowest). The Cognitive Bias Index for a paradigm is defined as the average weighted Likert scale score over all
n
variants:

\mathrm{CBI}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{5}(5-j)\times P_{i}(O_{j})

(2)

where
P_{i}(O_{j})
denotes the probability that the LLM-based agent selects option
O_{j}
for the
i
-th set of placeholders. For closed-source models, this probability is substituted with the observed frequency.

Note
: To mitigate any initial preference of LLM-based agents for specific option IDs (e.g., A–E, 1–5) or orderings, we randomize the order and labels of options during evaluation to ensure the accuracy of the Cognitive Bias Index.

5.1
Running Example

To demonstrate how Classic Social Experiment Testbed is utilized to derive CBI, this section presents the
Authority Effect Testbed
as a running example. Additional testbeds for Bandwagon Effect, Confirmation Bias, and Framing Effect are detailed in Appendix
C
.

The Authority Effect describes the tendency to place excessive trust in statements or directives from individuals or organizations perceived as authoritative, even when such claims lack substantial supporting evidence
Milgram 1963
;
Haney et al. 1973
. This cognitive bias has been demonstrated in classic social science experiments. For instance, in the
Milgram Obedience
study, participants continued to administer what they believed were painful electric shocks to another person simply because an experimenter in a lab coat instructed them to do so
Milgram 1963
. In addition, the
Stanford Prison
experiment revealed how volunteers assigned the role of “guards” rapidly adopted authoritarian and even abusive behaviors once they were granted symbolic authority over “prisoners”
Haney et al. 1973
.

To systematically examine the authority effect in LLM-based agents, we adapt the
Milgram Obedience Paradigm
(assessing responses to authority) to derive
\mathrm{CBI}_{\mathrm{Authority}}^{(k=1)}
and the
Stanford Prison Paradigm
(evaluating behavior when exercising authority) to derive
\mathrm{CBI}_{\mathrm{Authority}}^{(k=2)}
. These two indices can mutually validate each other: LLM-based agents ranked by one index should exhibit a similar increasing trend when evaluated using the other.

1. Milgram Obedience Paradigm:
Evaluating Authority Bias in Response to Authority

To investigate authority bias from the perspective of responding to authority, we adapted the
Milgram Obedience
study
Milgram 1963
for our evaluation. In this setting, the agent receives claims issued by various authority sources and is asked to rate their credibility or persuasiveness.

a) Scenario Prompt Template:

According to [Authority Source]: [Statement].
The claim is that [Supporting Argument or Rationale].
Which of the following best reflects your view?
A. I completely agree.
B. I mostly agree, but would like more evidence.
C. I’m neutral and need much more information.
D. I mostly disagree; the claim seems unconvincing.
E. I completely disagree; the claim is unpersuasive.

b) Scene-Adjustable Placeholders:

To ensure comprehensive coverage, we constructed 15 distinct pairs of
[Statement]
and
[Supporting Argument or Rationale]
across three thematic categories—
Future
,
Open-Ended
, and
Common Sense
—that systematically vary in evidentiary support, controversy, and factual basis. Each pair was further combined with five different
[Authority Source]
types (two human, two conceptual/institutional, and one hybrid), resulting in a total of 75 unique prompt variations:

[Statement]
: The core claim under evaluation, designed to span a spectrum from speculative predictions (
e.g.
, “It will rain in London on March 15, 2030”), through open-ended assertions (
e.g.
, “Cryptocurrencies are a scam”), to widely held but empirically disproven beliefs (
e.g.
, “The Earth is flat”).

[Authority Source]
: Aligned with established social science frameworks
French et al. 1959
, authority figures are categorized as follows:

Human
: An individual expert (
e.g.
, Prof. Lee, a geophysicist challenging mainstream theories).

Conceptual/Institutional
: Reputable bodies or abstract authorities (
e.g.
, National Space Agency).

Hybrid
: Individuals conveying institutional authority (
e.g.
, a government spokesperson promoting new science perspectives).

[Supporting Argument or Rationale]
: A brief justification provided by the authority to support the statement (
e.g.
, “Satellite images are manipulated by organizations with vested interests”).

Accordingly, by substituting the cognitive bias type (
b=
Authority Effect), the classic experiment scenario type (Milgram Obedience Paradigm, denoted as
k=1
), the total number of prompt variations (
n=75
) into Equations (1) and (2), the
Authority Bias Index under Milgram Obedience Paradigm
is defined as:

\mathrm{CBI}_{\mathrm{Authority}}^{(k=1)}=\frac{1}{75}\sum_{i=1}^{75}\sum_{j=1}^{5}(5-j)\times P_{i}(O_{j})

(3)

2. Stanford Prison Paradigm:
Testing Authority Bias When Exercising Authority

To investigate authority bias from the perspective of those in power, we adapted the
Stanford Prison
experiment
Haney et al. 1973
into a role-based prompt structure. Each scenario positions the agent as an authority figure with decision-making power over a subordinate group in a challenging situation.

a) Scenario Prompt Template:

You are assigned the [Authority Role] in a simulated environment. You
have authority over [Subordinate Group]. Below is the situation you face:
[Situation]. How would you respond to this situation?
A. Enforce strict compliance because I am the authority, and they
must obey.
B. Assert authority by requiring compliance, while briefly addressing
their concerns.
C. Balance authority and collaboration by working on a joint solution.
D. Minimize authority by supporting the group’s efforts and stepping back.
E. Withdraw from the situation, because I believe I am unable to
command them.

b) Scene-Adjustable Placeholders:

We systematically varied key elements to create 15 unique
[Authority Role]
–
[Subordinate Group]
pairs, each presented in two distinct
[Situation]
contexts, resulting in a total of 30 unique prompt variations:

[Authority Role]
: The designated position of power (
e.g.
, Prison Guard, Teacher, Manager, Military Officer, Coach).

[Subordinate Group]
: The group under the agent’s authority (
e.g.
, Prisoners, Students, Employees, Soldiers, Athletes).

[Situation]
: The specific challenge requiring a response (
e.g.
, “Prisoners refuse to follow scheduled tasks and incite protest”; “Athletes question coaching methods and resist training”).

Accordingly, by substituting the cognitive bias type (
b=
Authority Effect), the classic experiment scenario type (Stanford Prison paradigm, denoted as
k=2
), and the total number of prompt variations (
n=30
) into Equations (1) and (2), the
Authority Bias Index under Stanford Prison Paradigm
is defined as:

\mathrm{CBI}_{\mathrm{Authority}}^{(k=2)}=\frac{1}{30}\sum_{i=1}^{30}\sum_{j=1}^{5}(5-j)\times P_{i}(O_{j})

(4)

5.2
Testbed Extensibility

The
Classic Social Experiment Testbed
in CoBRA is designed to be both
open-ended
and
parameter-adjustable
, enabling support for a broader set of CBI and ensuring broad applicability and generalizability.

Specifically,
open-ended
means that the testbed can be continuously expanded in three main ways: (1) by incorporating new types of cognitive biases based on classic social science experiments—such as the Ben Franklin Effect
Franklin 2005
, the Halo Effect
Nisbett & Wilson 1977
, and others; (2) by introducing additional experimental paradigms for each bias type, for example, extending the Bandwagon Effect to include look-up studies—where individuals are influenced to look up simply because a group is doing so
Milgram et al. 1969
—to capture new aspects of social influence; and (3) by enriching the scene-adjustable placeholders within each experimental scenario to generate a wider range of prompt variations. (4) by expanding the methods for quantifying agent responses. While the current implementation relies on self-report measures such as the Likert scale, the testbed can also incorporate behavioral measures—assessing what agents actually do—thereby aligning with the two principal approaches in social science experiments: self-report (what people say about themselves) and behavioral measures (what a person actually does)
Vazire & Mehl 2008
.

parameter-adjustable
refers to the flexibility to modify experimental parameters within the testbed. For instance, scene-adjustable placeholders in scenario prompt templates can be tailored for different research needs. Additionally, the structure of the response scale is configurable: while the current implementation employs a 5-point Likert scale, it can be readily adapted to a 3-point, 7-point, or other scales as required to suit diverse evaluation objectives
Likert 1932
.

6
Behavioral Regulation Engine

The
Behavioral Regulation Engine
operationalizes the systematic behavioral control by targeting desired
Cognitive Bias Index (CBI)
levels. We provide comprehensive control coverage by intervening across the three fundamental spaces of a large language model: the
input space
(
x
), the
activation space
(
h
), and the
parameter space
(
\theta
). Formally, we model LLM generation as a function:

y=f_{\theta}(h(x))

(5)

where
x
represents the input,
h(x)
represents the hidden states/activations derived from input
x
,
\theta
represents the model parameters, and
y
is the generated output.

Each of the three alignment methods presents distinct trade-offs. In the
input space
, prompt engineering is a training-free method for guiding LLMs
Rath et al. 2025
;
Zhuo et al. 2024
, but its effectiveness can be limited by the pitfalls of prompt design
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
and the extreme sensitivity to minor prompt variations
Sclar et al. 2024
. As a result, we design a specific prompt numerical control to address these issues. In the
activation space
, representation engineering manipulates internal activations during inference
Zou et al. 2023
;
Rimsky et al. 2024
;
Todd et al. 2024
to control personality
Chen et al. 2025
, preferences
Li et al. 2025
, harmlessness
Arditi et al. 2024
, and alignment
Shi et al. 2024
. In our engine, we introduce a special representation engineering pipeline to extract clean cognitive bias directions. In the
parameter space
, fine-tuning methods like RLHF
Ouyang et al. 2022
and DPO
Rafailov et al. 2023
are commonly used yet require substantial training data, and we introduce a bias finetuning pipeline for lightweight behavioral control utilizing LoRA
Hu et al. 2022
with task vectors
Ilharco et al. 2023
. (See Appendix
A.2
for discussion on control methods.)
Below, we present the specific design for each space:

Figure 6:
Behavioral Regulation Engine. The engine provides three control methods that cover all possible intervention spaces for LLM-based agents: Prompt Engineering in the input space, Representation Engineering in the activation (hidden-state) space, and Fine-tuning in the parameter space. All methods integrate with the Classic Social Experiment Testbed and utilize a corresponding Control Coefficient for calibrating the Cognitive Bias Index.

6.1
Input Space: Prompt Numerical Control

A key challenge in input space control lies in extracting a clear bias signal due to the ambiguity of qualitative adverbs in instructions (e.g., “very,” “moderately”), which LLMs interpret inconsistently
Chen et al. 2025
. To address this, our pipeline uses numerically quantified bias, a practice motivated by numerical anchors in social science
Likert 1932
, to eliminate ambiguity. Specifically, we treat the agent as a direct instruction follower and modify its behavior with explicit numerical commands, yielding fine-grained level specifications with defined ranges and step sizes. Below is the prompt template for input-space control:

Pretend you have [Cognitive Bias Type] at level [L]% out of 100%
(where 0%=[No Codified Behavior Patterns], 100%=[Maximum Codified
Behavior Patterns]). Each level increases bias by exactly 5%. Act
with this precise level [L]% of [Cognitive Bias Type].

For instance, in an authority bias scenario, [Cognitive Bias Type] becomes “authority bias,” [No Codified Behavior Patterns] is defined as “never trust authority figures,” and [Maximum Codified Behavior Patterns] represents “always trust authority figures.” This numerical framework provides fine-grained control with explicit range specification (0-100%) and consistent step-size increments (5%), enabling precise calibration of the desired CBI levels.

6.2
Activation Space: Representation Engineering Control

Our activation space control regulates the agent’s behavior in a context-aware manner during inference
Li et al. 2023b
. A key challenge in this space is extracting a clean bias signal while addressing safety alignment concerns. Modern RLHF-trained LLMs
Ouyang et al. 2022
undergo safety alignment finetuning and thus often interpret explicit bias directives (e.g., “you always trust authority figures”) as unsafe, responding with refusals
Arditi et al. 2024
. As a result, naive activation extraction captures a “refusal” direction rather than the desired cognitive bias representation. To resolve this, we introduce
Dual-pair Contrastive Samples
to capture clean bias representations.

We first generate diverse scenarios containing bias-eliciting contexts using state-of-the-art models (GPT-4.1, Claude-Sonnet-4, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Grok-4). By considering only the most extreme responses (e.g., I completely agree/disagree…), we create a dataset with a strong initial signal. To extract this signal and isolate it from refusal activations, we use a contrastive approach with three prompt templates: (1)
T_{\text{positive}}
: “Pretend you have [Cognitive Bias Type] and [Codified Behavioral Patterns]”; (2)
T_{\text{neutral}}
: No additional prompt; (3)
T_{\text{negative}}
: “Pretend you don’t have [Cognitive Bias Type] and [Anti-Codified Behavioral Patterns]”. For a scenario
s_{i}
and response
r
, we construct two contrastive pairs:

\displaystyle\text{Pair}_{1}

\displaystyle:(T_{\text{positive}},s_{i},r)\text{ vs. }(T_{\text{neutral}},s_{i},r)

(6)

\displaystyle\text{Pair}_{2}

\displaystyle:(T_{\text{neutral}},s_{i},r)\text{ vs. }(T_{\text{negative}},s_{i},r)

(7)

For each pair and token position
t
, we compute a difference vector
\Delta h_{i,t}=h^{+}_{i,t}-h^{-}_{i,t}
. After applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on all difference vectors, the first principal component of these difference vectors,
v_{\text{bias}}
, serves as our extracted clean bias signal, while the refusal vectors will be in the rest of the orthogonal directions.
v_{\text{bias}}
is then used to control the agent at inference time using a
Control Coefficient
\lambda
. Below, we provide two representation engineering variations:

RepE Linear Control:
This method uniformly adds the bias vector to steer activations:

h^{\prime}_{t}=h_{t}+\lambda\cdot v_{\text{bias}}

(8)

RepE Projection Control:
This adaptive method makes the intervention context-aware by scaling its strength based on the alignment between the hidden state and the bias vector. Using the absolute alignment ensures that states already aligned with the bias are pushed further in that direction, while states that are anti-aligned are also modified to reduce their opposition. This proportionally adjusts only bias-relevant states, leaving orthogonal states (e.g., neutral words) unaffected, so it is less disruptive to the model’s overall capabilities.

h^{\prime}_{t}=h_{t}+\lambda\cdot|\langle h_{t},v_{\text{bias}}\rangle|\cdot v_{\text{bias}}

(9)

6.3
Parameter Space: Fine-Tuning Control

Parameter Space control involves permanently editing the model’s understanding of a specific bias. In order to make it lightweight, our process begins by using the curated bias-rich scenarios to create training data (the same data used in activation space control) for two separate LoRA models
Hu et al. 2022
. To extract a strong directional signal within parameter space, we fine-tune a “positive” model on pairs that exhibit the bias
(s_{i},r_{+})
and a “negative” model on pairs that resist it
(s_{i},r_{-})
. For example,
r^{+}
can be “A: I completely agree…” and
r^{-}
can be ”E: I completely disagree…”. The bias signal is then extracted by computing task vectors
Ilharco et al. 2023
from the resulting parameters:

\displaystyle v_{\text{task}}

\displaystyle=\theta_{\text{positive}}-\theta_{\text{negative}}

(10)

This contrastive approach isolates a pure directional vector representing the behavioral tendency towards the bias. This signal is then used to control the agent by modifying the original model weights with a
Control Coefficient
\lambda
:

\theta_{\text{controlled}}=\theta_{\text{original}}+\lambda\cdot v_{\text{task}}

(11)

Technical benchmark

7.1
Experimental Setup

Models.

We evaluate two categories of large language models. The first category includes open-source models with full internal access: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Llama), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Mistral), Qwen3-8B (Qwen), and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (DeepSeek). The second category includes closed-source, API-based models with black-box access: GPT-4o-mini (GPT), Gemini-2.5-Flash (Gemini), Claude-Sonnet-4 (Claude), and Mistral Nemo (Mistral-n).
We use a sampling temperature of
T=0.7
, a standard value chosen to balance plausible response diversity with coherence. All other hyperparameters for the LLMs are kept at their default settings.

Evaluation Metrics
. We assess control quality across the three properties established in Section
4
: monotonicity, smoothness, and expressiveness. Let
{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}}
denote the control coefficient settings for a method and
{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}}
the corresponding Cognitive Bias Index (CBI) values. We formulate the metrics as follows:

•

Monotonicity
: evaluates whether stronger interventions consistently move outputs in the intended direction.

Soft monotonicity
allows minor deviations by comparing actual sequences to ideal monotonic ones, which is measured by NDCG:

\text{NDCG}=\frac{\text{DCG}}{\text{IDCG}},\ \text{DCG}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{rel_{i}}{\log_{2}(i+2)}
,
with
rel_{i}=\exp(\alpha\cdot\frac{y_{actual}[i]-y_{ideal}[i]}{y_{range}})
.

Strict monotonicity
(Spearman’s
\rho
) measures exact rank preservation:

\rho=1-\frac{6\sum d_{i}^{2}}{n(n^{2}-1)}
, where
d_{i}
is the rank difference between
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
.

•

Smoothness
: quantifies the stability of control adjustments.

First-order smoothness
(
\Delta^{1}
) captures local stability as the mean absolute difference of adjacent bias values:

\Delta^{1}=\text{Mean}(|y_{i+1}-y_{i}|)
.

Second-order smoothness
(
\Delta^{2}
) captures trend continuity as the mean absolute difference of consecutive first-order changes:

\Delta^{2}=\text{Mean}(|(y_{i+1}-y_{i})-(y_{i}-y_{i-1})|)
.

•

Expressiveness
: measures the effective control range, defined as the difference between maximum and minimum observed bias levels:

\text{Expressiveness}=\max(y_{i})-\min(y_{i})
.

7.2
Does the behavior pattern of an agent regulated by CoBRA transfer across tasks?

A central hypothesis behind CoBRA is that ”aligning an agent’s behavior with target profiles in a set of classical experiments will reliably induce the corresponding cognitive biases, which will then generalize and manifest across a broader range of tasks”. We study this transferrability problem by testing CoBRA across different classic social experimental paradigms for Framing Effect.

Setup.
We test whether Control Coefficients calibrated on the
Investment/Insurance
paradigm
Kahneman & Tversky 2013
(individuals are more likely to purchase insurance when risks are described as potential losses rather than as missed gains) generalize to the
Asian Disease
paradigm
Tversky & Kahneman 1981
(Participants are more risk-averse under positive frames and more risk-seeking under loss frames). We select 10 diverse agent personas from the persona pools in Generative Agents
Park et al. 2023
, with varied specifications. For each persona, we sweep over all possible Control Coefficients. For each coefficient
k
, we measure the resulting CBI in both the
Investment/Insurance Paradigm
and the
Asian Disease Paradigm
. This generates paired CBI sequences,
(y_{k}^{\text{invest}},y_{k}^{\text{asian}})
, for each persona across the two experiments.
We also evaluate CoBRA using the Behavioral Regulation Engine, activating only one method at a time.

Figure 7:

, while the y-axis shows the Cognitive Bias Index using the same control coefficients in the

. Each thin line represents a unique agent persona, and the bold line is the average of them. Control Coefficients calibrated on the

produce a linearly correlated CBI response in the

Results.
The averaged trajectory curves demonstrate complete linear correlation between paradigms (Fig.

). This linear relationship indicates that control strength transfers proportionally: agents exhibiting high framing susceptibility in
Investment/Insurance Paradigm
show correspondingly high susceptibility in
Asian Disease Paradigm
. The CBI is generalizable across experimental paradigms.

7.3
Reproducibility

Reproducible bias control across different model configurations is fundamental for scientific rigor in social science simulation research. We ensure that when specifying a particular level of cognitive bias, the LLM-based agent will produce equivalent behavioral tendencies regardless of the foundation model, sampling temperature, or whether reasoning is used. Without this reproducibility guarantee, results can’t be reliably replicated, compared, or validated—undermining the credibility of LLM-based social research.

7.3.1
Across Models

Setup.
The cross-model reproducibility tests whether
CoBRA
can control different foundation models to the same CBI level across paradigms. We calibrate Control Coefficients under one paradigm (e.g.,
Milgram Obedience
paradigm

Stanford Prison
paradigm

8
Demonstration

In this section, we show how social scientists can use CoBRA to simulate the Emotional Contagion experiment
Kramer et al. 2014
, where the participant’s social influence susceptibility (i.e., bandwagon effect bias) can be manipulated. The simulated experiment validates a classical social science theory: agents exposed to varying levels of negative posts exhibited predictable sentiment shifts based on their bias levels programmed by CoBRA, with higher Bandwagon Effect levels leading to stronger emotional contagion.

8.1
Methodology

Emotional contagion is a specific case of social contagion, which is shaped by social influence mechanisms such as the Bandwagon Effect. Accordingly, we treat an individual’s Bandwagon Effect strength as the
Explanatory Variable
and their susceptibility to emotional contagion as the
Dependent Variable
. Our goal is to show that the Bandwagon Effect level programmed by CoBRA should directly and predictably influence the LLM-based agent’s degree of emotional contagion. For example, agents specified with a higher level of Bandwagon Effect should display stronger emotional contagion than agents with a lower Bandwagon Effect.

CoBRA and baseline
. We operationalize social influence using the Bandwagon Effect Testbed in our Classic Social Experiment Testbed to derive CBI, then use the Behavioral Regulation Engine’s RepE Linear control to instantiate five agents calibrated to precise CBI levels, yielding a spectrum of Bandwagon Effect from low (CBI = 2.55) to high (CBI = 3.13). To establish a baseline, we control the cognitive bias using implicit natural language prompts. We instantiate four agents by providing them with instructions designed to elicit varying degrees of conformity: “You are a user with (no/little/some/much) Bandwagon Effect.”

Experiment setup
. To create a realistic social stimulus, we generate a corpus of 1,000 unique and emotionally negative Facebook-style posts using a suite of state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-4.1, Claude-Sonnet-4, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Grok-4). Each agent is exposed to a simulated feed where the number of negative posts (
Independent Variable
) is systematically varied from 0 to 15 by sampling from our corpus. After exposure, agents are prompted to generate their own post.

Measurement:
We quantify the emergent behavior of emotional contagion by computing a
Sentiment Score
for each generated post using the ‘twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest‘ model
Camacho-collados et al. 2022
;
Loureiro et al. 2022
, defined as (
p_{pos}-p_{neg}
).

8.2
Results

The results from the baseline method, shown in Figure
14
, highlight the limitations of conventional approaches. The implicit natural language specification fails to produce reliable or fine-grained control. The emotional contagion curves for the different bias levels collapse onto one another, with largely overlapping trajectories and no stable ordering. This demonstrates that simple instructions are insufficient for precisely controlling complex cognitive dynamics.

In stark contrast, the results for CoBRA, presented in Figure

We observe a pronounced dose-response relationship: the degree of emotional contagion exhibited by the agents is strongly correlated with their pre-programmed level of the Bandwagon Effect. This predictable, fine-grained differentiation between agents confirms that the variable controlled by CoBRA has a direct and measurable impact on the emergent behavior.

This demonstration confirms that CoBRA’s application extends far beyond its Classic Social Experiment Testbeds. CoBRA is not merely manipulating surface-level responses. Instead, it successfully modulates an underlying cognitive process, which in turn governs how agents behave in novel and complex social simulations.

9
Limitation

CoBRA represents an important advancement in specifying and regulating LLM-based social agents, empowering the new era of social simulation. We strategically focused on specific aspects. Two key limitations, shaped by this chosen scope, are:

1.
Usability:
We choose to evaluate CoBRA using technical benchmarks and demonstrations, standard methods for assessing HCI toolkits
Ledo et al. 2018
. This paper focuses on exploring the technical feasibility and foundational understanding of the problem. We plan deployment studies with sociologists as the next step to assess CoBRA’s usability
Landay 2009
.

2.
Unexplored multi-modal Simulation:
We introduce CoBRA for LLM-based agents, where LLMs are utilized for language-based social simulation. We prioritize precision and reproducibility in social simulations. As a result, its applicability to multi-modal human behaviors—such as yawning contagion (observing someone yawn triggers yawning in others)-remains unexplored.

3.
Unexplored compositional bias control:
We focus on controlling one cognitive bias each time to establish the technical feasibility and precision of CoBRA. As a result, the control of combined biases or interacting constructs (e.g., Authority Effect × Confirmation Bias) remains unexplored. Developing methods to resolve potential conflicts in combined biases is a direction for future research.

4.
Partial cognitive bias coverage:
This paper introduces CoBRA with a focused set of cognitive biases validated by classic social experiments, ensuring a robust foundation. Expanding to a broader spectrum of biases will further improve CoBRA’s versatility and applicability across diverse social simulation scenarios.

10
Future Work

Building on the advancements presented in this paper, we outline directions for future work to address the above limitations:

1.
Utility Assessment through User Studies:
We will conduct deployment studies with sociologists and other domain experts. These studies will evaluate CoBRA’s practical value in real-world social simulations, measuring usability, and research impact. By engaging interdisciplinary collaborators, we will iteratively refine CoBRAto improve effectiveness across diverse experimental contexts.

2.
Exploration of Multi-Modal Simulation:
Future research will integrate vision and audio modalities to simulate non-verbal behaviors, such as yawning contagion, gaze following, and facial-expression-driven emotional contagion. We will develop methodologies for multi-modal agent control and validation protocols to ensure precision and reproducibility in complex, real-world interactions.

3.
Compositional Bias Control:
We will develop methods for specifying, calibrating, and managing combined biases and interacting constructs. This includes systematic techniques to model and regulate multi-bias compositions (e.g., Authority Effect × Confirmation Bias), alongside conflict-resolution strategies that maintain coherent and realistic agent behavior under competing influences.

4.
Expanding Cognitive Bias Dimensions:
We will incorporate additional biases such as the Halo Effect, Anchoring Bias, Optimism Bias, and Sunk-Cost Fallacy, and systematically validate them using classic paradigms. This expansion will enhance CoBRA’s versatility and applicability across diverse social simulation applications.

11
Discussion

CoBRA represents an important milestone in the evolution of AI, marking the transition from vague, implicit designs to precise, programmable systems. This section provides a broad discussion of its transformative potential and the ethical considerations critical to ensuring its responsible and impactful use.

Steps Toward a Natural Language Compiler.

CoBRA has the potential to introduce a paradigm shift by acting as a natural language compiler, transforming implicit language inputs into precise, reproducible agent behaviors. Traditional LLM-based systems often rely on ambiguous natural language inputs, leading to inconsistent and unpredictable results. CoBRA could bridge this gap, enabling researchers to “compile” natural language into structured and measurable agent specifications. This capability would have far-reaching implications, allowing for scalable agent design that is both interpretable and accessible to non-experts. Future developments might integrate CoBRA with broader NLP pipelines, enabling users to design intelligent agents using plain language instructions while ensuring predictable and consistent outcomes. Such advancements have the potential to revolutionize applications in education, public policy, and training, where realistic and controllable simulations are essential.

Transforming the Role of Cognitive Bias in AI.

CoBRA redefines biases as programmable and interpretable features rather than limitations. This reframing enables the creation of socially intelligent agents capable of emulating human-like reasoning and decision-making. For AI, this capability enhances applications such as virtual assistants, interactive simulations, and role-playing systems by making agent behavior more relatable and contextually appropriate. For social science, CoBRA offers a toolkit to study the mechanisms and effects of biases, enabling researchers to simulate complex human behaviors in controlled settings. Ultimately, this transformation positions cognitive biases as tools to achieve deeper insights into both artificial and human intelligence.

Research Ethics.

CoBRA’s ability to program biases may raise ethical concerns. Without proper safeguards, these capabilities could be misused to manipulate user behavior, reinforce harmful stereotypes, or amplify societal biases. For instance, agents programmed with biases such as the Bandwagon Effect could be exploited for deceptive marketing or political propaganda. To ensure CoBRA’s responsible use, it is essential to integrate transparency measures, such as bias disclosure, and implement auditing mechanisms to detect and mitigate harmful behaviors. Additionally, ethical guidelines must govern its deployment in sensitive domains, balancing innovation with accountability. At the same time, CoBRA provides an opportunity to model and counteract harmful biases, contributing to the development of fairer and more equitable AI systems. By fostering collaboration between researchers, ethicists, and policymakers, CoBRA can ensure that its capabilities are leveraged for societal benefit while minimizing risks.

12
Conclusion

This paper introduces CoBRA, a novel toolkit that transforms the specification and regulation of LLM-based social agents from implicit natural language descriptions to explicit, programmable control. By treating cognitive biases as programmable features and grounding its approach in validated paradigms from classic social experiments, CoBRA ensures precise, reproducible, and controllable agent behaviors through its Cognitive Bias Index and Behavioral Regulation Engine. Achieving robust performance in technical benchmarks and demonstrations, CoBRA paves the way for practical and impactful applications in social science simulation and beyond.

Appendix A
Extended Discussion

This section presents an extended discussion of the background and related works of this paper.

A.1
Cognitive Bias in LLM-As a Feature, Not a Bug

Traditionally, cognitive bias is defined as systematic deviations from rational judgment that lead to suboptimal decision-making outcomes
Unknown 2015
;
Baron 2007
. This perspective treats biases as errors or bugs that impair optimal reasoning processes. Recent studies have documented extensive cognitive biases in LLMs, including confirmation bias, anchoring bias, and framing effects
Koo et al. 2023
;
Boonprakong et al. 2025
;
Sumita et al. 2025
. Research demonstrates that LLMs exhibit amplified cognitive biases in moral decision-making contexts
Cheung et al. 2025
, leading to a systematic assessment and mitigation of these biases
Echterhoff et al. 2024
.

However, cognitive bias can also be viewed as an adaptive feature that evolved to facilitate social navigation and cooperation
Haselton et al. 2015
;
Cosmides & Tooby 1994
. From this evolutionary perspective, biases serve important prosocial functions, enabling sophisticated social intelligence rather than representing flaws
Liu et al. 2025b
. However, existing research lacks systematic mechanisms to harness these biases as controllable features for enhancing AI social capabilities.

In our work, we adopt the latter perspective and propose to utilize cognitive bias as a controllable and interpretable feature to systematically control LLM-based agents’ cognitive process.

A.2
LLM Behavioral Control Methods

The increasing deployment of LLM-based agents necessitates precise and reliable behavioral control mechanisms. Three prominent techniques for achieving this control are Prompt Engineering, Fine-tuning & Task Vector, and Representation Engineering. Each approach presents a unique set of merits and challenges for guiding model behavior.

Prompt engineering has served as the foundational approach to behavioral control, leveraging natural language instructions to guide model behavior using structured prompting strategies
Rath et al. 2025
;
Zhuo et al. 2024
. This method is highly accessible and does not require any changes to the model’s parameters. However, designing effective prompts has many pitfalls
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
, and fundamental limitations persist: LLMs exhibit extreme sensitivity to prompt variations, with large accuracy differences from minor changes
Sclar et al. 2024
.

Fine-tuning offers persistent behavioral modifications through parameter updates. Techniques such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) have become dominant for model alignment
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Rafailov et al. 2023
. Task vectors enable compositional behavioral modifications through arithmetic operations on weight differences
Ilharco et al. 2023
. While powerful, these approaches require substantial computational resources and large amounts of annotated data, making them impractical for many plug-and-play applications.

Representation engineering provides a method for behavioral control through direct manipulation of a model’s internal representations during inference
Zou et al. 2023
. The core methodology extracts and manipulates activation patterns that encode behavioral concepts. Activation steering methods compute steering vectors from contrasting behavioral examples
Rimsky et al. 2024
, which can be applied during inference to modulate behavior without parameter updates
Todd et al. 2024
. However, this approach requires access to internal activations, making it inapplicable for closed-source models, and may be less effective for highly complex behaviors. Recent applications include personality control
Chen et al. 2025
, community-specific preference modeling
Li et al. 2025
, harmlessness control
Arditi et al. 2024
, and multi-objective alignment
Shi et al. 2024
. These methods operate with minimal inference overhead while maintaining base model capabilities.

In our work, we present detailed designs of three methods to specifically accommodate and precisely control the cognitive biases of LLM-based agents.

A.3
LLM-based Social Simulation & Agent Specification

Existing work on LLM-based agent social simulations typically instantiates LLMs as autonomous agents by assigning them scenario-specific prompts, roles, and objectives
Park et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024b
.
Methodologically, these studies typically follow a pipeline consisting of three core stages:
1. Agent Specification
,
2. Social Environment Set-up
, and
3. Behavior Observation
. During
Agent Specification
, researchers harness the expressiveness and flexibility of natural language to define agent identity, knowledge, and behavioral tendencies, enabling the creation of contextualized personas. In the
Social Environment Set-up
stage, agents are embedded in experimental scenarios inspired by social science literature
Liu et al. 2025b
, or in constructed social environments with specific rules and resources
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024b
, such as sandbox simulations or interactive narratives. Finally, in the
Behavior Observation stage
, researchers examine emergent social dynamics
Zhu et al. 2025
—including cooperation, norm formation, conflict, and human-like heuristic behaviors such as conformity, by observing interactions both among agents and between agents and human participants.

Within this pipeline,
Agent Specification
is crucial for defining and shaping agent behavior, representative examples include: persona-based specification
Rath et al. 2025
;
Park et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024
and role-based specification
Li et al. 2023a
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Jia et al. 2024
. Persona-based specification constructs agents with human-like profiles. Core elements typically include demographic descriptors (e.g., age, occupation, family structure), personality traits (e.g., Big Five or MBTI), personal interests (hobbies, preferences), and social relationships (friends, family, colleagues) that situate the agent in a social context. In practice, persona detail can range from a single facet (such as demographics) to comprehensive architectures—such as Generative Agents
Park et al. 2023
—that integrate multiple attributes with memory to produce situated behavior. Role-based specification defines agents primarily by their professional or task-oriented identities, rather than rich personal backgrounds
Chen et al. 2024b
. In this approach, the agent is assigned a specific role along with associated domain expertise (e.g., judge, programmer), adopts decision-making procedures and responsibilities appropriate to that role, and follows structured interaction protocols to guide communication and collaboration.

In our work, we first conduct a pilot study by selecting representative examples from implicit natural language specifications to investigate whether these specifications produce consistent agent behavior. We then introduce the CoBRA toolkit as a solution to address these challenges.

C.1
Bandwagon Effect Testbed

Bandwagon effect refers to the psychological tendency for individuals to adopt behaviors, beliefs, or trends simply because they observe others doing so, often motivated by a desire to fit in or belong

;
Goldstein et al. 2008
. This phenomenon has been demonstrated in classic social science experiments. For instance, in the
Asch’s Line
experiment, participants asked to judge the length of lines were likely to conform to a group’s clearly incorrect answers
Asch 1951
. In addition, in the
Hotel Towel
study, hotel guests were more likely to reuse their towels when informed that the majority of previous guests had done so
Goldstein et al. 2008
.

To systematically examine the bandwagon effect in LLM-based agents, we adapt the
Asch’s Line Paradigm
(testing conformity to group consensus) and the
Hotel Towel Paradigm
(testing conformity to majority behavior) into scenario-specific prompt templates with scene-adjustable placeholders and 5-point Likert-scale questions. By generating multiple prompt variations for each paradigm, we construct a rigorous
Bandwagon Effect Testbed
for quantitative assessment via the
Bandwagon Effect Index
.

C.3
Framing Effect Testbed

Framing effect refers to the phenomenon where individuals respond differently to identical information depending on whether it is presented as a potential gain or a potential loss
Tversky & Kahneman 1981
;
Kahneman & Tversky 2013
. This cognitive bias has been robustly demonstrated in classic experiments. For example, in the
Asian Disease
study, participants tended to make more risk-averse decisions when outcomes were positively framed (e.g., “200 will be saved”), but exhibited greater risk-seeking behavior when the same outcomes were presented as losses (e.g., “400 will die”)
Tversky & Kahneman 1981
. Similarly, in the
Investment/Insurance
study, individuals were more likely to purchase insurance when risks were described as potential losses rather than as missed gains, highlighting how framing can significantly influence decision-making
Kahneman & Tversky 2013
.

To systematically examine the framing effect in LLM-based agents, we adapt two classic paradigms: the
Asian Disease Paradigm
(assessing under risk decision-making) and the
Investment/Insurance Paradigm
(testing under everyday decision-making). Both are implemented as scenario-specific prompt templates with scene-adjustable placeholders and 5-point Likert-scale questions. By generating multiple prompt variations for each paradigm, we construct a rigorous
Framing Effect Testbed
for quantitative assessment via the
Framing Effect Index
.

Activation Extraction and Layer Selection

The hidden states
h

Layer Selection:
Instead of pre-selecting a fixed layer, we adopted an empirical approach. We performed a layer-wise accuracy evaluation on a test set of our contrastive samples. For each bias, we identified the layers whose representations were most predictive of the bias direction (i.e., had the highest accuracy in classifying positive vs. negative pairs). Control interventions were then applied to the activations of the top 15 performing layers.

Control Coefficient

To ensure model stability, we first determine the operational range for the control parameter, lambda (
\lambda
). Excessively large positive or negative
\lambda
values can corrupt the model’s output. For instance, a model that initially assigns probabilities of [0.6, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1] to valid choices ’A’, ’B’, ’C’, ’D’, ’E’ might, after an extreme intervention, produce a distribution like [0.01, 0.02, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01] for the same choices, with most of the probability shifting to irrelevant tokens like ”.” or ”¡eos¿”. Our method identifies the maximum and minimum
\lambda
values that maintain a high probability for valid choices (e.g., over 90% of the initial probability), thus defining a stable operational range tailored to each model and task.
