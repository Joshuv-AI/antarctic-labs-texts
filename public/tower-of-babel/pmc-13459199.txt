Early motor inequities: how birth conditions and social context shape motor development in the first five years of life
Authors: Kety Suelyn Ferreira, Larissa Wagner Zanella, Glauber Carvalho Nobre, Nadia Cristina Valentini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the influence of individual factors (gestational age, birth weight, and body length) and contextual factors (socioeconomic status, school attendance, and region of residence) on motor development in children aged 0 to 5 years. A total of 626 preterm and full-term children were assessed using the Peabody Developmental Motor Scales–Second Edition (PDMS-2). Most children (77.8%) demonstrated average motor performance, while 17.7% scored below average. Full-term children showed higher total motor quotients and fine-motor grasping scores compared with preterm children, and children with low birth weight performed more poorly in object manipulation tasks. Significant socioeconomic disparities were identified: children from higher-income families exhibited better gross motor and stationary balance scores. School attendance was associated with higher total motor quotients and superior performance in stationary balance, grasping, and visuomotor integration, underscoring the developmental value of early educational environments. Children residing in urban areas demonstrated higher scores across most PDMS-2 subscales compared with those living in rural regions. Overall, the findings reveal that both biological vulnerabilities and social environments play important roles in shaping early motor development. Early identification of risk and implementation of targeted interventions across health, education, and community settings are essential for reducing motor development inequities during early childhood.

Introduction

Early childhood (0–5 years) represents a critical period for development, during which rapid changes occur across motor, cognitive, social, and emotional domains [7, 13, 16]. Motor development plays a central and foundational role in this process, as it not only reflects the maturation of neuromuscular systems but also actively drives children’s engagement with their physical and social environments [1]. Importantly, motor development both influences and is influenced by cognitive, social, and emotional domains, emerging from dynamic interactions between the individual and multiple environmental systems, including family, school, and broader sociocultural contexts [9, 11]. Understanding how these factors interact to shape motor development in early childhood is essential for identifying potential risks and promoting optimal development across multiple domains [40].

Building on this perspective, child development results from interactions between individual characteristics and multiple environmental systems [1]. Thus, child development is shaped not only by biological factors but also by the quality and diversity of movement-related opportunities available within children’s immediate contexts (e.g., family, school) and broader socioeconomic and cultural environments [9, 11]. These opportunities are particularly relevant for motor development, as repeated engagement in movement experiences supports skill acquisition and refinement. Further, from a bioecological perspective, proximal processes, such as children’s everyday interactions with people, objects, and spaces in the microsystems where they participate (e.g. at home [30, 40] or in daycare centers [32, 44], represent key mechanisms through witch development occurs [9]. However, despite this theoretical understanding, there is still limited evidence on how these contextual opportunities shape motor development in early childhood.

Conversely, biological and perinatal risk factors, such as prematurity [10, 33] and low birthweight [4, 36], can compromise development. Contextual risks, including poverty [5, 14, 26, 31, 44], limited access to health services [18], lack of school enrollment [26], and restricted access to adequate physical space for movement [8, 35], also negatively influence development, often with long-term consequences [31, 40]. When these constraints co-occur, their effects accumulate across developmental systems, increasing the likelihood of atypical motor development trajectories [26, 30].

According to Brazilian prevalence studies, preterm birth affects approximately 11% of live births in the country, placing Brazil among those with a high burden of prematurity [6, 24]. In addition, the prevalence of Brazilian children born small for gestational age (SGA) remains substantial, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations [42]. Further, national data indicate that 15–45% of young children aged between 0 and 6 years exhibit motor delays [4, 8, 12, 38]. Motor delays are more frequent among children born preterm [40], with low birthweight, or exposed to perinatal risks [28, 36, 40]. The high prevalence of these biological/perinatal risk factors underscores the importance of examining early motor development in the Brazilian context, particularly in relation to the interaction between biological and contextual risk factors.

Importantly, such risks are not evenly distributed across geographic contexts. Preliminary evidence suggests a higher prevalence of motor delays among young children (ages 7–10 years) suggests a higher prevalence of motor delays among those living in rural areas compared to their peers in urban settings [35]. Although this age range differs from that examined in the present study (0–5 years), these findings highlight potential geographic disparities in motor development. Brazilian children living in rural or underserved regions often experience reduced access to health services and early childhood education compared to those in urban areas [21]. Consequently, the risk of motor delays increases substantially when children experience not only multiple perinatal but also contextual risks [28, 30]. Therefore, identifying contextual indicators enables professionals to design targeted interventions and health and education services to prevent or mitigate developmental risks.

Early childhood education, as a strong contextual indicator for children’s development, constitutes the first stage of basic education and is predominantly delivered in Brazil through municipal public systems, alongside private and philanthropic providers. It is structured into two levels: daycare, serving children aged 0–3 years, and preschool for those aged 4–5 years. Attendance in preschool has been mandatory (Brazilian law Nº 12.796/2013) whereas enrollment in daycare remains non-compulsory and comparatively limited, particularly among children under 3 years of age [27]. Consequently, enrollment rates are substantially higher among children aged 4–5 years, while participation in daycare varies considerably according to socioeconomic and regional factors [21, 22]. Such disparities in access are likely to influence opportunities for movement and developmental experiences and should therefore be considered when interpreting motor development outcomes.

Most Brazilian studies have relied on small, regional, or convenience samples and have typically examined biological or contextual factors in isolation. As a result, the literature lacks evidence that simultaneously examines both biological (e.g., prematurity, birth weight) and contextual factors (e.g., socioeconomic status, early childhood school enrolment, area of residence) in explaining early motor development across Brazil’s diverse socioeconomic and geographic contexts. Hence, studies are needed to describe developmental patterns and identify contextual factors that shape children’s development [14, 26].

Therefore, the objective of this study was to examine differences in motor development among children aged 0–5 years according to both individual and contextual factors. Based on this objective, the study addressed the following research questions: (1) Are there differences in motor development among children aged 0 to 5 years according to biological factors (prematurity, birth weight)? (2) Are there differences in motor development according to contextual factors (socioeconomic status, early childhood school enrolment, and area of residence—urban vs. rural)?

Methods

Participants

This descriptive and cross-sectional study was approved by the research ethics committee of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul. The research followed the guidelines of Resolution No. 466/12 of the National Health Council, which regulates ethical standards for research involving human subjects, and it adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013), which establishes ethical principles for research involving human participants. Parents and/or legal guardians of all children signed the informed consent form. When appropriate for age, children provided verbal assent. Thus, every human participant in this study provided their consent.

The present study was conducted within a broader large-scale population-based research project aimed at examining motor development trajectories in children aged 0–5 years, identifying early developmental risk factors, and detecting children in need of intervention to support families in accessing specialized services. Sample size estimation was based on Brazilian national demographic data [20], which indicate a child population of approximately 35.5 million individuals from birth to 12 years of age. Considering a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of 4%, a minimum sample of 604 children was required to adequately represent the target population. To account for potential data loss, an additional 100 participants were invited. Although some families initially agreed to participate, a number were unable to attend the scheduled assessments due to reasons such as parental work constraints, child unavailability, relocation, or lack of follow-up communication. In addition, some children did not complete all assessment tasks, and a small number of families did not provide complete sociodemographic information. Consequently, the study was conducted with a total of 626 children (317 girls −50.6%; 309 boys—49.4%), born preterm or at term at the ages of 0 to 71 months (M = 21.6, SD = 18.5 months) from different cities of Brazil. Eligibility criteria included children aged 0 to 71 months, born preterm or at term. Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of neurological, cardiovascular, hematological, immunological, oncological, or musculoskeletal diseases, as well as children who exhibited signs of discomfort or crying after the second evaluation attempt.

Instruments

Peabody developmental motor scales–second edition

The Peabody Developmental Motor Scales–Second Edition (PDMS-2; Folio & Fewell 2000) validated for Brazilian children [37, 45] was used to assess children motor development in children aged 0 to 71 months. The PDMS-2 comprises four subscales of gross motor domain (Reflexes, Stationary, Locomotion,and Manipulation) and two subscales of fine motor domain (Grasping and Visual-Motor Integration). Scores are provided for each subscale, as well as for the Gross Motor Quotient (GMQ), Fine Motor Quotient (FMQ), and Total Motor Quotient (TMQ). The scale provided raw scores, standard scores (subscales: 1–20; GMQ: 3–60; FMQ: 2–40; and TMQ: 5– > 95), percentiles, Z-scores, and descriptive categories of motor performance (very superior: 131–165; superior: 121–130; above average: 110–120; average: 90–109; below average: 80–89; poor: 70–79; and very poor: 35–69). The assessment is conducted individually and lasts 40 to 60 min.

The Brazilian version of PDMS-2 showed adequate psychometric properties for content validity (concordance among experts was high for relevance (Gwet’s Agreement Coefficients ranged from 0.98 to 0.99) and comprehension (Gwet’s Agreement Coefficients ranged from 0.95 to 0.92). Validity evidence based on internal structure was also observed (adequate index of fit for infants aged from 0 to 11 months (chi2/d.r. ratio = 0.79; RMSEA = 0.000, CI 90% = 0.00 to 0.00; CFI = 1.0; GFI = 1.0) and for children aged from 12 to 71 months (chi2/g.l.ratio = 1.6; RMSEA = 0.000, CI 90% = 0.00 to 0.00; CFI = 1.0; GFI = 1.0) [45]. Further, internal consistency and reliability analysis showed appropriate results for both age bands (0–11 months and 12–71 months (McDonald’s Cronbach’s and Gutmann’s tests varying from 0.68 to 0.96). Unidimensionality of the model was also supported by Rasch analysis, with most items showing adequate fit and correlations. Item removal did not affect reliability,therefore, all items were retained, indicating that the instrument is appropriate for assessing Brazilian children [37].

Brazilian economic classification questionnaire

The Brazilian Economic Classification questionnaire (Brasil & Filiadas, 2015) was used to verify the families’ SES. This instrument classifies households based on a composite score derived from education level, household characteristics, and ownership of durable consumer goods (e.g., car, computer, television, refrigerator), as well number of rooms in the household and presence of domestic workers. Based on these indicators, families are categorized into SES groups ranging from Category A (highest SES: 45–100 points) to Category D-E (lowest SES: 0–16 points).

Procedures

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul. Childcare centers and schools from several Brazilian cities were selected to ensure geographic, socioeconomic, and contextual diversity, including urban and rural settings, central regions, and border municipalities, as well as variation in population size, Human Development Index (HDI), and access to health and early childhood education services. The administrative boards of 15 childcare centers and 21 schools agreed to participate and signed institutional consent forms. The sampled cities represented distinct contextual profiles. The largest city, a state capital, had approximately 1.3–1.4 million inhabitants and a high HDI (≈ 0.80), with broad availability of health and early childhood education services. In contrast, border municipalities had around 120–130 thousand inhabitants and a slightly lower HDI (≈ 0.74), with more limited access to specialized health and educational services, reflecting geographic and structural constraints. Cities located in central regions were mid-sized regional hubs (≈ 200–290 thousand inhabitants; HDI ≈ 0.78–0.79), characterized by the presence of universities and referral health services, representing intermediate levels of service availability and infrastructure. This sampling strategy allowed the inclusion of children from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and varying levels of access to services, capturing heterogeneity in developmental contexts across different regions of Brazil.

Parents were invited to meet the researchers and received explanations about the study goals and procedures. Children were assessed in their homes or at their schools, in the presence of parents and/or guardians, in a quiet room free of distractions, using the recommended toys for the child’s age group. The child’s school and home schedules, rest times, and feeding times were respected.

Data collection was conducted by three evaluators trained in the administration of the PDMS-2, all of whom were specialists in infant and child motor development with extensive experience in motor assessment. Prior to data collection, the evaluators completed a 50-h theoretical and practical training program led by an expert in motor behavior, including standardized instruction on PDMS-2 administration and scoring procedures, as well as simulation assessments with 15 children to ensure consistency and to reduce potential bias.

A pilot study with 93 children aged 0–5 years was conducted to assess reliability. High intra-rater (ICC = 0.96–0.97) and inter-rater agreement (ICC = 0.92–0.94) were observed. Additional reliability analyses were conducted using subsamples: inter-rater reliability was assessed in 64 children, with three independent raters, and intra-rater reliability was examined in 80 children through video-recorded reassessments performed by the same evaluator with a two-month interval. These analyses confirmed high reliability (ICC ≥ 0.86) [45].

The PDMS-2 was administered according to standardized guidelines in quiet and previously organized environments, either in schools or in the children’s homes, depending on family availability and access to educational services. To ensure consistency across settings, the assessment environment, materials, and administration procedures were standardized as much as possible, following the instrument’s guidelines. Most assessments were conducted in school settings (n = 520), while a smaller proportion took place at home (n = 106), particularly in regions with limited access to schools.

Assessments were conducted individually, in the presence of parents or legal guardians, using standardized materials and equipment. Each session lasted approximately 40–60 min, depending on the child’s performance. When necessary, subscales were administered across multiple sessions within a five-day period to accommodate children’s needs.

The principal researcher (R1) administered and scored all assessments for the final sample (n = 626). Additional trained evaluators (R2 and R3) participated in reliability procedures by independently scoring a subset of assessments. A subset of sessions was video recorded for reliability analysis and data verification. All assessments followed standardized PDMS-2 administration procedures, including baseline and ceiling criteria, and were conducted in accordance with ethical principles of confidentiality and participant well-being.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 20.0. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to verify data normality. Variables are presented as mean and standard deviation, and as absolute and relative frequency. Group comparisons were performed using one way ANOVA test controlling for child sex and age. When a group-by-sex interaction was detected, Bonferroni post hoc tests were applied.

Within ANOVA, bootstrapping analyses were conducted to control extreme imbalance groups sizes, mitigating potential bias and enhancing the reliability of the group comparisons. Therefore, results will be reported reflect both assumption-compliant ANOVA outputs and bootstrap-corrected estimates, which together offer a more stable and conservative interpretation of the effects. The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Overall sample characteristics

The sample included an equal distribution of girls and boys, with most children born full term and at adequate birthweight. Those born preterm all were moderate preterm (< 37 weeks to 32 weeks). Most families had lower socioeconomic status, lived in urban areas, and had children enrolled in school. 98 children who were not enrolled in school were observed exclusively among those aged 0 to 11 months. Most children demonstrated average motor development on the Peabody assessment; 4.5% of the children scored above average (very superior, superior, and above average), 77.8% demonstrated average motor performance, and 17.7% scored below average (below average, poor, or very poor).

PDMS-2 scores by SES, school enrollment, and geographic region of residence

Discussion

The aim of this study was to compare the motor development of children aged zero to five years in relation to individual (prematurity, birth weight, and birth length) and contextual (socioeconomic status, attendance at early childhood education, and residence in urban or rural areas) factors. A total of 626 children born preterm and full-term were assessed, representing one of the largest recent Brazilian samples on early motor development, which strengthens the robustness of the findings and enhances their generalizability.

Overall sample characteristics

For the overall sample, 17.7% scored below average (below average, poor, or very poor). This distribution indicates that although most children perform within the expected range, nearly one in five show motor delays, a clinically and public-health–relevant proportion. Previous studies conducted in Australia [41] and Portugal, at similar ages, reported findings like those of the present study. In both cases, approximately 24% of children scored “below average” on the gross motor quotient, and performances in the locomotion, object control, and balance subscales were comparable to those observed in the Brazilian sample, reinforcing the international consistency of early motor development patterns [41]. Similar results, although with a higher prevalence of delays (30% combining below average, poor, or very poor), were also reported for Chinese children. Furthermore, the prevalence observed in the present study is consistent with recent global reviews reporting that 15–30% of children under five present motor delays depending on measurement tools and socioeconomic context [43]. Including this broader epidemiological perspective reinforces the relevance of the findings and situates the Brazilian data within global public-health trends.

The comparison across international [41], and national [4, 8, 40] studies indicates that the present findings align with previous evidence while providing novel data from a large sample that simultaneously considers both individual and contextual factors. This alignment across culturally diverse samples supports the robustness of early motor developmental trajectories and highlights that motor delays remain a relevant issue globally, especially in the Global south countries.

In addition, prior work has emphasized that early motor difficulties can have cascading effects on children’s broader developmental domains, including cognitive, social, and academic outcomes [25, 40]. Integrating this evidence strengthens the interpretation that identifying motor delays early, particularly in populations affected by socioeconomic disparities at the Global South, is essential for preventing longer-term developmental gaps.

Contextual and individual factors by groups

For the overall sample, a greater proportion of families with low SES lived in rural regions, and their children were less likely to be enrolled in school. Specifically, a higher proportion of children with low birthweight also lived in rural areas, and had lower school enrollment, highlighting the disadvantage of being born with low birthweight, residing in rural contexts, and belonging to families with limited socioeconomic resources.

Low birthweight has previously been associated with multiple contextual vulnerabilities, including low SES [23, 29] and residence in rural areas [23], as well as delayed school entry [15], which is consistent with the patterns observed in the present study. In addition, low birthweight is known to increase early-life health and neurodevelopmental risk, such as poorer school readiness and higher morbidity, which may further contribute to lower educational participation [17]. It is also important to note that children in rural areas with low SES face structural barriers that impede access to education and health systems. These include greater distance from school infrastructure, limited availability of maternal and child health services, and fewer household resources to support educational participation [15, 23]. These overlapping disadvantages may therefore compound one another, reinforcing inequalities that begin at birth and persist into the school years.

Motor performance as related to gestational age and birth weight

Full-term children demonstrated higher total motor quotient scores and better performance on the grasping subscale compared to preterm children. The total motor quotient is considered the most comprehensive indicator of PDMS-2 performance because it integrates both gross and fine motor domains [34], highlighting that prematurity continues to exert measurable functional effects on global motor development in early childhood.

Prematurity is widely recognized as a key risk factor for motor delays [31]. Previous studies indicate that 30–50% of preterm infants show motor delays during the first 24 months of life [40], compared with only 1.9–8.3% among full-term infants, emphasizing the magnitude and persistence of this developmental gap. It is noteworthy that none of the preterm children in our study received compensatory therapy, which may have contributed to their lower performance and reflects broader issues of access to early intervention in many regions.

Our findings also identified specific deficits in the grasping subscale, aligning with prior evidence that preterm children are particularly vulnerable to fine motor difficulties. Such deficits may reflect reduced muscle tone and postural control, as well as fewer opportunities for hand–eye coordination and manual exploration due to prolonged hospitalization or early health complications, even when corrected age is considered [40]. These constraints can delay the transition from reflexive to voluntary grasping and later manipulation skills.

Importantly, low birth weight emerged as an additional biological vulnerability, independently associated with lower scores in the manipulation and grasping subscales. This pattern is consistent with evidence showing that children with low or very low birth weight present marked difficulties in manual dexterity [36] and poorer motor outcomes even when gestational age is controlled [40]. These converging findings suggest that both prematurity and intrauterine growth restriction contribute to compromised fine motor precision, bilateral coordination, and neuromotor foundations required for object manipulation.

During gestation, fetal growth accelerates substantially, with weight doubling and length tripling near birth. Deviations from this expected trajectory, such as those resulting in low birth weight (< 2500 g), very low birth weight (< 1500 g), or extremely low birth weight (< 1000 g), signal early neurodevelopmental vulnerability and elevate the risk of motor delays across the first years of life [40]. Although commonly associated with prematurity, low birth weight also occurs among full-term infants [36] and is frequently related to extended neonatal hospitalization, further restricting opportunities for spontaneous motor exploration.

During the first year of life, object manipulation is a critical developmental milestone, yet both prematurity and low birth weight are associated with risk factors—such as prolonged NICU stay, recurrent early-life health complications, and limited exposure to sensorimotor experiences—that compromise this domain. Thus, the reduced manipulation and grasping performance observed in our sample is consistent with a broader developmental trajectory shaped by early biological constraints and restricted experiential input.

From a public-health perspective, identifying prematurity and low birth weight as complementary and partially modifiable risk markers underscores the need for structured motor monitoring, timely rehabilitation referral, and enriched motor experiences beginning in the early months of infancy. Evidence indicates that targeted sensorimotor stimulation can mitigate fine motor delays and reduce longer-term disparities. Integrating such approaches into primary care is especially relevant in settings where access to specialized rehabilitation services remains limited.

Motor performance as related to SES, school enrollment, region of residence

SES, school enrollment, and place of residence are interconnected contextual factors that shape children’s early motor development. In the present study, these three dimensions consistently differentiated motor performance, with children from higher SES, urban regions, and school environments showing clear advantages across multiple PDMS-2 domains. Together, these findings highlight how social, educational, and environmental conditions accumulate to influence children’s opportunities for movement, practice, and exploration. SES is a well-established determinant of motor development [4, 30, 39]. Consistent with this literature, children from higher-SES households in the present study showed higher gross-motor performance and balance scores, reinforcing previous evidence that economic resources shape children’s access to enriched developmental experiences [5, 30].

Higher-SES families typically provide safer play spaces, more toys and materials, participation in structured activities, and better-resourced schools and healthcare [2]. These cumulative advantages serve as protective factors for motor development. In contrast, children from low-income families are disproportionately exposed to risk factors such as perinatal complications, limited stimulation, and restricted access to high-quality early education [31, 39, 44]. By including children from all economic strata, our study provides robust evidence that SES remains a central predictor of motor competence.

Regarding school enrollment during the early years is another powerful predictor of motor development. Children enrolled in school showed higher total, fine-motor, and balance scores, as well as better grasping, manipulation, and visual–motor integration. These findings align with evidence that preschools provide structured instruction, planned motor activities, age-appropriate materials, and peer interaction, all of which foster motor competence [32]. Early education settings expand children’s opportunities to explore new movements, receive guided feedback, and engage in object manipulation; mechanisms which are strongly associated with improved fine and gross motor outcomes [2], [19].

However, access to high-quality early education remains unequal. Children from low-income households often attend under-resourced schools with limited materials and fewer trained professionals [3, 26]. Moreover, children with health vulnerabilities—especially preterm and low birthweight infants—tend to enter school later or attend irregularly, further constraining their developmental opportunities [8, 10].

Together, these findings reinforce that school enrollment operates as both a developmental opportunity and a mechanism through which SES inequalities are amplified or reduced.

Children living in urban regions demonstrated higher overall motor performance across PDMS-2 scores. Urban contexts generally offer closer proximity to healthcare, early-education centers, safe recreational spaces, and diverse movement experiences, which contribute to motor development [5]. Children in rural regions, particularly in low-resource settings, often score lower on motor tests, partly due to lower SES levels, reduced access to early-childhood services, and caregiving arrangements that limit stimulation. Limited access to specialized pediatric services in rural areas also delays the identification and treatment of motor delays. These findings contrast with Sasso et al. [35], who reported better motor outcomes among rural children aged 7–10 years. This discrepancy may reflect developmental differences, as older children typically have greater autonomy and engagement in daily physical activities (e.g., commuting to school, outdoor play, participation in daily chores, active play in natural environments), whereas younger children (0–5 years) rely more on caregiver support, structured stimulation and access to early-childhood services. The result in the present study suggests that rural–urban effects are culturally and contextually dependent, and that structural inequalities in the Global South—including uneven distribution of early-childhood services—may outweigh potential environmental advantages in rural settings.

A key contribution of our study is demonstrating that SES, school enrollment, and geographic region operate as intersecting layers of opportunity or constraint. Children with low SES were more likely to live in rural areas and less likely to be enrolled in school; this overlap reflects a cumulative-risk pattern that disproportionately restricts access to motor-enriching experiences. Thus, the developmental disadvantages observed in low-SES, non-enrolled, and rural children are not independent phenomena. They represent a shared ecological pathway, in which socioeconomic vulnerability limits school access, rurality reduces service availability, and both factors diminish the quantity and quality of motor-learning opportunities. This integrated perspective reinforced the understanding that in the Global South, where structural inequalities shape early developmental trajectories more strongly than in high-income contexts.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of the present study is its examination of multiple contextual determinants of motor development in the same sample (e.g., socioeconomic status, school attendance, and rural/urban residency), each analyzed in separate models, within a large sample of children from birth to 5 years old. Few studies investigate these factors within the same sample, examining their associations with children’s motor performance across separate analyses. This approach, which considers multiple domains without modeling their combined effects, provides a more ecologically valid understanding of motor development in early childhood. The study also benefits from a large and socioeconomically diverse sample, including children from all SES strata and from both rural and urban settings. This diversity enhances the representativeness of the findings and allows more robust comparisons than studies limited to single-income or single-context samples.

Additionally, by including children who do and do not attend school, the study captures variability in early environmental stimulation that is rarely assessed. This enables a more nuanced understanding of how structured educational experiences contribute to motor development. Finally, the study contributes relevant evidence from a Global South context, filling an important gap in literature that is still predominantly based on high-income countries. These results offer insights into inequalities faced by children growing up in socioeconomically vulnerable regions and highlight the need for contextually grounded public policies.

Despite its strengths, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between contextual factors and motor development. Longitudinal studies would provide stronger evidence regarding developmental trajectories and the persistence of early disparities. Second, while we assessed school enrollment as a key variable, we did not evaluate school quality, curriculum characteristics, or teacher training, all of which may moderate the relationship between schooling and motor competence. Third, the study did not include direct measures of home motor affordances or parental practices, which could further clarify the mechanisms through which SES influences motor outcomes. Future studies should incorporate assessments of the home environment, caregiving routines, and opportunities for physical activity. Four, although prematurity and birthweight were considered in supplementary analyses, additional perinatal factors (e.g., neonatal complications, length of hospitalization) were not included, which may partially influence motor performance. Finally, another limitation of this study is the absence of sensitivity and specificity estimates derived from the present sample, as no external criterion measure was available. Although the instrument demonstrates adequate psychometric properties in previous studies, classification accuracy may vary across age groups. This consideration is particularly relevant, even though controlling imbalance group size by using bootstrapping analysis, given that a substantial proportion of the sample consisted of infants, which may influence the interpretation of group comparisons.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight that both individual factors (such as prematurity and low birthweight) and contextual factors (including socioeconomic status, school attendance, and residential environment) exert meaningful and measurable influences on children’s motor development. These disparities underscore that motor competence varies in relation to both biological vulnerabilities and environmental conditions. Importantly, the risks associated with these individual and contextual conditions are not deterministic: they can be mitigated through early developmental monitoring, family guidance, high-quality early childhood education, and access to enriched motor experiences at home, in school, and in the community. Implementing such strategies is essential for fostering protective pathways, reducing inequities, and supporting optimal motor development during the critical years of early childhood, particularly in contexts marked by social and economic vulnerability.
