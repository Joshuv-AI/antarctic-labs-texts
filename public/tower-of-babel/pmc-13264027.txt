Accelerated Turnover of Collagens and Other Extracellular Matrix Proteins Upon Chronic Beta-Adrenergic Stimulation
Authors: Matthew C. Juber, Jacob G. Cox, Thalia Paiz, Timothy A. McKinsey, Maggie P.Y. Lam, Edward Lau
Published: 2026

Cardiac fibrosis accompanies most forms of heart failure and involves the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins in the myocardium.1 In cardiac injury, cardiac fibroblasts become activated and secrete ECM to enable scar formation and prevent rupture. However, prolonged myofibroblast activation shifts from adaptive to maladaptive, resulting in excess collagen deposition which increases myocardial stiffness. Antifibrotic interventions have shown preclinical promise for preserving function and reducing mortality post cardiac injury, but translation into therapeutics remains challenging. A bottleneck in understanding is the continued uncertainty about the half-life of collagens and other major ECM proteins, and how ECM turnover alters in matrix remodeling.

Based on collagen hybridizing peptide staining, a recent report suggested that excessive collagen turnover is a key driver of heart failure pathogenesis.2 Consistent with these findings, a broad body of literature reports elevated circulating markers of (pro)collagen cleavage (PICP, ICTP) in patients with heart failure.3 However, collagen hybridizing peptide and measurement of (pro)collagen cleavage byproducts do not directly quantify ECM protein half-life, and also omit noncollagen components. Conversely, stable isotope labeling mass spectrometry presents a powerful method to measure proteome-wide half-life, but the data often remain inaccessible to nonspecialists, and do not directly reveal whether half-life differences are statistically significant.

Here, we sought to clarify the half-life of collagen and other ECM proteins in the mouse heart. To directly quantify ECM half-life, we applied a new method to analyze data4 involving heavy water (D2O) labeling of normal, hypertrophic (simultaneous isoproterenol deliver (15mg/kg/day) by mini-osmotic pump for 14 days),4 and proteasome-inhibited (epoxomicin 0.5 mg/kg/d) mouse hearts (n = 6) over 14 days. The experimental design allows protein turnover rates to be calculated from subcellular fractions over the treatment time-course. Given mature ECM is extensively crosslinked and resistant to standard lysis, we focused on the insoluble fraction.

Using an analytical method we recently described,5 we calculated the key kinetic parameter from D2O labeling experiments, namely the fraction of newly synthesized protein θ at labeling time t. The method, implemented in the Riana software,5 led to ∼15% gain in peptide coverage. Crucially, this data completeness allows additional ECM proteins such as nidogens to be compared across conditions (Figure 1A). We next introduce a straightforward approach to estimate errors and perform statistical comparisons for in vivo protein turnover data. Because of the quick equilibration of body water and the use of a priming dose, protein kinetics in D2O labeling is well approximated by first-order kinetics, unlike SILAC. This simplicity allows us to calculate the protein clearance parameter as ϕ = log(1-θ)= -kt. As ϕ is linear to rate constant k, half-life and CIs can be estimated via linear interaction models with shared variance (Figure 1B). Statistical comparison of group differences was performed using least-squares means with Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons (Figure 1C). To conclude, we provide a direct reference on the half-life of collagens and major ECM proteins, and their alteration in cardiac hypertrophy. To our knowledge, this is first instance of statistical tests for proteome turnover studies using individual animals across labeling timepoints. Hypertrophy destabilizes (ie, shortens the half-life of) ECM proteins overall, but to various degrees, indicating complex remodeling beyond collagen changes. Although shortened half-life indicates accelerated turnover, further work is needed to untangle the relative contributions of protein synthesis and degradation. Our findings support the notion that therapeutic approaches aimed at blocking excessive cardiac ECM turnover hold promise for the treatment of heart failure.
