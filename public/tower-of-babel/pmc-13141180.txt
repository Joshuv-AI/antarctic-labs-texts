Hypothermia risk factors in patients with burns during emergency presentations: protocol for a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Dhairya Deven Vayada, Maleea D Holbert, Brandon Meikle, Brett Paul Dyer, Carl Lisec, Marc Schnekenburger, Paul Baker, Monique Bertinetti, Andrew J A Holland, Roy Kimble, Anne Darton, Daniel Isacson, Varun Harish, Natalia Adanichkin, Rebecca Schrale, Linda Quinn, Bernard Carney, Bronwyn Griffin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBurn injuries constitute a significant health concern, requiring immediate first aid to mitigate further tissue damage and complications. Most countries worldwide recommend application of 20 min of cool running water (20CRW) within 3 hours of the burn as the cornerstone in burn first aid management. Despite its widespread acceptance and proven benefits in reducing the severity of burns and subsequent interventions, concerns regarding the risk of hypothermia following this intervention persist, representing at least a perceived barrier to the delivery of 20CRW. When it does occur, hypothermia in patients with burns has been associated with higher mortality rates, even after controlling for burn injury severity. Developing an understanding of the incidence of post-burn hypothermia following 20CRW, with a specific focus on potential predictive and/or causative factors, is quintessential.Methods and analysisA retrospective cohort study of all adult and paediatric patients with thermal burn injuries presenting to one of 11 participating Australian or New Zealand hospitals between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2024 will be conducted. The primary outcome is the incidence of hypothermia in patients with burns following their arrival at the emergency department (ED). Secondary outcomes include influence of burn first aid cooling, risk factors influencing hypothermia, impact of hypothermia on clinical patient outcomes and incidence of hypothermia in patients with burns in non-ED settings.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was granted by the Children’s Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (CHQHREC; HREC Ref No: HREC/25/QCHQ/114285) as well as Health and Disability Ethics Committees, New Zealand (HDEC; Ref No: 2026 EXP 23892). The study findings will be formally disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and conference presentations.

Background and rationale

In addition to protecting the body from outside insult and pathogens, the skin plays a crucial role in regulating body temperature, termed thermoregulation. Where skin coverage is disrupted, such as following a burn injury, thermoregulation may be affected, potentially resulting in the development of hypothermia.1 The current recommendation following a burn injury is the application of 20 min of cool running water (20CRW) in most countries.110 Concerns about the risk of hypothermia have been identified as a barrier to the delivery of this recommendation.11 There is an association between hypothermia in patients with burns and poor patient outcomes,1214 including higher rates of mortality.13 At present, hypothermia is poorly defined in the literature, which prevents accurate calculation of the incidence of post-burn hypothermia. For example, when defined as a body temperature below 36°C, the reported incidence is as high as 62.9%,15 however, at a body temperature below 35°C, hypothermia is calculated to occur in only 1.6% of cases.12 Regardless of its definition, there is evidence to suggest that post-burn hypothermia may result in poorer patient outcomes, including increased length of hospital stay and greater mortality,13 although these outcomes are varied. In paediatric patients with burns, age and burn total body surface area (TBSA) percentages have been identified as significant predictors of hypothermia.13 In addition to developing hypothermia as a direct result of skin loss following a burn injury, the application of CRW as burn first-aid has also been questioned as a potential causative factor.

Most burn care and treatment bodies around the world, including the Australian and New Zealand Burn Association, European Burn Association and British Burn Association, recommend applying 20CRW to all thermal burns within 3 hours of the injury.8 9 Despite the widespread adoption and advocacy of this practice, and its proven benefit in lessening burn progression,1 it has been met with caution in some areas due to the concern that it may result in an increased risk of developing hypothermia. Few studies have been conducted investigating the association between CRW application and hypothermia, and these have produced conflicting results. One study found an association between hypothermia and length of cooling with water in adult patients with burns >20% TBSA.15 An earlier study, however, did not find an association between prehospital first aid cooling with water and development of hypothermia in a cohort of adult and paediatric patients with burns.12 This highlights the need for an in-depth review of the incidence of post-burn hypothermia, with a specific focus on potential predictive and/or causative factors, such as treatment with 20CRW. Furthermore, as little is known about the effects that hypothermia may have on patients with burns, understanding the clinical outcomes of patients with burns with hypothermia is important. This study aims to fill this critical gap in the literature. It is anticipated that the results of this investigation can be used to further support the use of 20CRW following burn injuries or suggest caution in its application in certain circumstances and/or climates.

Methods

Aims

The primary aim of this study is to determine the incidence of hypothermia, defined as a recorded body temperature below 36°C, in adult and paediatric patients with thermal burns presenting to emergency departments (EDs) across Australia and New Zealand. The secondary aims of this investigation include: (1) to examine the influence of burn first aid cooling on the development of hypothermia in patients with burns; (2) to assess the influence of hypothermia on outcomes of the patient with burns (such as length of hospital stay, intensive care unit (ICU) admission and mortality) and (3) to determine the potential causal risk factors for development of post-burn hypothermia and develop a prognostic model for these factors in Australian and New Zealand patients.

Project design

A retrospective cohort study will be conducted for adult and paediatric patients with burns who presented to one of 11 participating Australian or New Zealand tertiary hospitals between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2024 with thermal burn injuries. The planned start date of the study is 15 April 2026, and the planned end date is 31 December 2026.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome measure for this retrospective cohort review is the incidence of hypothermia in patients with burns following their arrival at the ED. For the purposes of this investigation, hypothermia will be defined as a recorded temperature of less than 36°C. This temperature threshold has been used in previous studies assessing hypothermia in patients with burns.13 16

Data collection

Data sources

Data sources will include ED databases such as the Emergency Department Information System (EDIS), Cerner FirstNet, TrackED and Epic, as well as local EMRs. Additional potential data sources will include locally stored burns databases. Variables and data sources are presented in online supplemental file 1.

Data collection procedures

Access to ED data will be obtained from all participating sites following required ethics approvals. Patients presenting with complaints of burns or burn-based International Classification of Diseases version 10, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM) codes will be identified from site ED databases (eg, EDIS, Cerner FirstNet, Epic). Health service data (ie, hospital admission, patient management and discharge disposition) will be collected from either EMR or local burn databases to obtain missing values for variables.

Variables

ED data will include time from initial injury to arrival in the ED; admission to hospital; length of stay in the ED; temperature on arrival to the ED; hourly temperature measures; mode of arrival to the ED; first aid received; location of first aid provision (eg, at home with ambulance service, following arrival to the ED). As burn first aid cooling can sometimes be given in stages (eg, 10 min at home, 10 min in the ED), prehospital first aid durations, referral centre/other first aid cooling and first aid cooling durations given in the ED will also be captured. Health services data (inpatient clinical outcome data) will include inpatient length of stay; ICU admission (paediatric and adult); complications; operating theatre requirements and ICD procedure codes. Data sources include EMR, supplemented with hospital-based local burns registry data. Please see online supplemental file 1 for the complete list of variables required for this research.

For the purposes of this investigation, hypothermia will be consistently defined across all participating sites as a recorded body temperature below 36°C, in accordance with the threshold specified in the primary outcome measure. The mode of temperature assessment (eg, oral, tympanic, rectal, axillary, temporal artery) will be extracted and reported where this information is available within the EMR.

Data analysis

Sample size

As a retrospective chart review, a direct sample size calculation will not be completed. This study aims to capture all burn patient presentations to participating EDs. Based on hospital reports of annual acute burn presentations and referrals, this study is likely to include an estimated 5000 patients. It is anticipated that this sample will allow the primary outcome to be explored.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics will be calculated for each of the identified variables. Data will be described as frequencies, percentages and medians (quartiles) for non-parametric data. Crude incidence rates (IRs) and 95% CIs will be calculated to determine rates of hypothermia in the sample. Multivariable regression models will be used to assess the association between potential causal risk factors (eg, CRW application, TBSA%, depth, age, rurality, mode of presentation to ED and other comorbidities) and hypothermia and will also be used in the development of the prognostic model. Model-building strategies will be matched to whether the study question relates to causal inference or prognostic model development.17 For objectives 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7, potential confounders relating to each causal effect estimate of interest will be identified using separate directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), which will be constructed prior to data analysis following a thorough literature review and after consulting subject-matter experts.18 Sensitivity analyses with alternative adjustment sets will be used to test the robustness of results to misspecification of the DAG-implied adjustment sets.19 E values will be used to assess sensitivity to unmeasured confounders.20 Sensitivity analyses will also be conducted to assess the impact of alternative temperature thresholds for hypothermia (eg, 35°C). For objective 6, existing predictors of hypothermia will be identified through a literature review and nominated for inclusion in the model. Following this, LASSO will be used for variable selection and shrinkage.21 Internal-external cross-validation will be performed to assess the site-specific performance of the model, and then random-effects meta-analysis will be used to assess pooled performance.22 Performance of the prognostic model will be assessed using calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope and C statistic.21 Data will be analysed using R.23

Safety considerations

As this research is a retrospective review of patient EMRs, with no active participant recruitment or intervention allocation, there are few safety considerations. The only risk to participants is a potential confidentiality and privacy breach. Governance and other relevant approvals (ie, Public Health Act approval) will be obtained from hospital and health service data custodians to access patient medical records. Patient data will be deidentified and confidentiality of patient information will be preserved at all times. Access to patient data will only be available to necessary members of the research team and stored in accordance with data storage best practices. No identifiable or potentially re-identifiable patient information will be stored or published in manuscripts arising from this research.

Data management and storage

Patient data (including data from the ED, hospital EMR or local burn registry) will be collected from multiple sites and stored securely in a password-protected Microsoft Excel file and/or REDCap database. While the data collected will be from multiple sites across Australia, the database will be located on a Queensland Health server. Permission to extract identifiable data will be obtained from the relevant local ethics committees and/or statewide health services. Data access will be limited to those researchers directly involved in the study. Data analysis will be performed by the researchers with consultation from a statistician.

Patient and public involvement

There is no direct patient and public involvement in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research. Patient EMRs will be accessed to collect relevant information; however no contact will be made between researchers and patients.

Ethics and dissemination

National mutually agreeable human research ethics approval has been granted by the Children’s Health Queensland Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (Australia) (HREC Reference number: HREC/25/QCHQ/114285) as well as through the Health and Disability Ethics Committees (New Zealand), (HDEC; Ref No: 2026 EXP 23892). This study will be performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, ICH GCP for Guidance on Good Clinical Practice and National Health and Medical Research Council National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Research Involving Humans.2426 No part of the study will commence prior to all HREC and governance approvals. Any changes to this protocol will be submitted to the relevant ethics committees as an amendment for approval before being actioned.

Discussion

This study aims to address a critical evidence gap in the delivery of first aid following burn injury, namely, the relationship between the application of 20CRW and the development of hypothermia. Caution surrounding hypothermia development and causing hypothermia is a known barrier adherence to best practice 20CRW delivery.11 This study is the first multicentre retrospective cohort study to examine hypothermia in patients with burns presenting to EDs across Australia and New Zealand. This provides a unique opportunity to encompass various geographic and climate factors, which may assist in enhancing the generalisability of 20CRW first aid guidelines. With an estimated sample size of 5000 burn presentations, the study allows for robust exploratory subgroup analyses and detailed statistical analysis.

As a retrospective cohort review, this study is dependent on the quality of data collected—missing and incomplete data may impact the quality of the study. Furthermore, variability across sites based on local policies and practices may impact how and when the body temperature is measured, which may introduce potential measurement bias.12 As temperature measurement method is not consistently recorded within EMR systems, the authors are unable to fully standardise this variable across sites. The mode of assessment will be reported where available, and this constraint will be explicitly acknowledged as a limitation in the resulting publication. In addition, real-world provision of 20CRW has variations—such as duration, method and water temperature—and depends on resource availability, awareness and the ability to provide first aid, which may introduce potential confounders that impact the analysis.14 However, the sample size of the study enhances the study’s capacity to address these variations and allow for stable estimates across subgroups. Previous large-scale observational studies have demonstrated that large datasets can reduce the influence of site-level and procedural variability.27
