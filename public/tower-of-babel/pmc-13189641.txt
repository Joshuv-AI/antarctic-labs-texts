After referral by a primary care provider, a 39-year-old woman with a history of asthma and Hashimoto thyroiditis presented to the infectious diseases clinic the same day after passing a large, motile worm (approximately 30 cm in length) by rectum (Figure 1A). She reported intermittent nausea and fatigue but denied any other symptoms associated with intestinal parasitic infection, including rash, fever, abdominal pain, or hematochezia. She also noted worsening cough in the preceding year and a half, which she attributed to her asthma.

(A) Live adult Ascaris lumbricoides worm expelled by a 39-year-old woman. (B) Under stereomiscropy, the anterior end of the worm showed the 3 characteristically prominent lips of the Ascaridoidea superfamily, which can be visible to the naked eye. (C) Under microscopy, the anterior buccal cavity showed irregular arrangement of variably sized labial denticles (1 to 4 μm), including larger and more abraded forms previously thought to be characteristic for Ascaris suum. Sodium acetate–acetic acid–formalin washing of the adult worm specimen showed (D) fertilized decorticated and (E) partially corticated ova sized 70 to 75 μm by 35 to 40 μm.

The patient resided in a suburban area of a city in northern Ontario. She was born and raised in Canada, with no travel outside the country in the preceding 2 years. She lived with healthy school-aged children and dogs. She had never travelled outside of North America, nor had her immediate family. Her family vacationed annually at a campground beside a freshwater lake in northern Ontario. The campground provided potable drinking water and sewage services. A swine farm was located within several kilometres of the campsite; however, the patient and family had no direct farm or other animal exposures. They would occasionally purchase produce at nearby family-owned farms. All produce was noted to be cleaned adequately.

The patient’s physical examination was normal and unremarkable. No further investigations were completed at the time of assessment, but a complete blood count 1 month prior showed results within normal limits (leukocytes 7.8 [normal range 4.0 to 11.0] × 109/L, hemoglobin 143 [normal range 120 to 160] g/L, platelets 405 [normal range 150 to 400] × 109/L, neutrophils 4.7 [normal range 2.0 to 7.5] × 109/L, lymphocytes 2.4 [normal range 1.0 to 3.5] × 109/L, monocytes 0.4 [normal range 0.0 to 1.0] × 109/L, eosinophils 0.1 [normal range 0.0 to 0.5] × 109/L, basophils 0.07 [normal range 0.00 to 0.20] × 109/L). A recent chest radiograph — done because of symptoms of shortness of breath, cough, and transient chest pain — was interpreted as normal. The expelled worm was submitted to the provincial public health laboratory and identified macroscopically (Figure 1B) and microscopically (Figure 1C, 1D, and 1E) as an adult female Ascaris lumbricoides worm.

We treated the patient with a single 500-mg dose of mebendazole. She was advised on hygiene practices to prevent reinfection. The patient passed at least 3 more adult Ascaris worms soon after treatment. At 4-week follow-up, she reported improvement in her fatigue and resolution of her nausea. Her chronic cough resolved before the worm passage that prompted the patient’s presentation. We considered her cough to be a result of her underlying asthma, rather than parasitic pulmonary eosinophilia.

Discussion

Ascaris lumbricoides is one of the most common human helminth infections, affecting an estimated 700 million people worldwide.1 Humans and pigs are the definitive hosts, although spurious egg passage has also been rarely documented in other mammals such as dogs, cattle, and sheep.2 Ascaris eggs passed in feces become infective after several weeks under favourable conditions (e.g., warm and humid soil) and may remain viable in the environment for up to 10 years.3 Transmission occurs via the fecal–oral route through ingestion of eggs in contaminated soil or water. After ingestion of infective eggs from the environment, larvae hatch in the small intestine and migrate via blood circulation to the lungs, where they ascend the bronchial tree and are swallowed back into the gastrointestinal tract.4 Final maturation into adult worms occurs in the small intestine. Adult worms can grow as long as 40 cm and live for up to 2 years.4 Given the typical lifespan of adult Ascaris worms and that our patient had not travelled outside of Ontario in the 2 years preceding the worm’s passage, it is most likely that this infection was acquired locally.

Many patients with Ascaris infection are asymptomatic, though specific proportions of symptomatic versus asymptomatic cases are not well documented.2 If present, symptoms may include fatigue, dyspepsia, nausea, or anorexia. Transient respiratory symptoms such as coughing or wheezing may be related to the parasite’s early lung migration phase, termed Löffler syndrome.5 Eosinophilia may be present during larval migration but is usually absent once adult worms have matured in the intestine. Adult worms can move within the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract and can often be spontaneously expelled, usually via the rectum and rarely via the mouth or nose. Severe complications may occur with high worm burden and include chronic malnutrition, growth delays, and intestinal or biliary obstruction. In our case, the patient’s worsening cough could conceivably be attributable to the lung migration phase of ascariasis; however, this phase is typically transient and would be insufficient to explain the months-long history of respiratory symptoms observed in this case. The patient additionally reported fatigue, which was initially attributed to underlying thyroid dysfunction but may have been attributable, at least in part, to the systemic effects of ascariasis.

The laboratory diagnosis of ascariasis is usually made by identifying the expelled adult worm macroscopically, by identifying ova by stool microscopy, or by using molecular diagnostics, although the latter is not currently available in most Canadian jurisdictions (Box 1).6 Human and swine Ascaris infections were historically separated into 2 distinct species, A. lumbricoides and Ascaris suum, but genomic evidence suggests that both organisms belong to the same species.7 Anecdotal morphological differences reported in older studies, such as the shape of the buccal orifice or labial denticles, have not been systematically substantiated and may be normal variations of the same organism.8

Clinicians should consider a noninfectious differential diagnosis that includes passage of undigested food (e.g, sprouts, vegetable skins, seeds, nuts) or mucus casts, incidental finding of environmental critters (e.g., earthworms, horsehair worms, insects), or delusional parasitosis.

Patients with confirmed, uncomplicated ascariasis can be prescribed a single oral dose of mebendazole or albendazole. A routine test-of-cure is not required.

Patients with confirmed, uncomplicated ascariasis can be prescribed a single oral dose of mebendazole or albendazole. A routine test-of-cure is not required.

For patients with uncomplicated ascariasis, treatment with a single dose of albendazole 400 mg or mebendazole 500 mg is highly effective and well tolerated, with cure rates of 88% for albendazole and 95% for mebendazole reported in a 2008 meta-analysis. 2,9,10 Given their comparable efficacy, these agents are considered therapeutically interchangeable. Albendazole can be acquired through Health Canada’s Special Access Program, while mebendazole is readily available at pharmacies. Both are members of the benzimidazole class of antiparasitic medications, which disrupt helminth energy metabolism, ultimately resulting in parasite immobilization and death. Patients should be advised that passage of residual intestinal worms is expected following treatment. A test of cure is not required, nor is treatment of household contacts unless they are symptomatic. Patient education on the prevention of intestinal helminth infection is essential and should emphasize strategies such as avoidance of exposure to contaminated soil, hand hygiene practices, and washing of fresh produce.

The highest prevalence of ascariasis in humans is in tropical regions and in low-resource settings where sanitation and hygiene infrastructure are suboptimal. In temperate, high-resource settings, prevalence has decreased substantially with improved infrastructure. However, sporadic cases still occur in humans, especially people in rural communities, those who use well water, and those with exposure to pigs, such as swine farm workers.2,4,10,11 Pathological surveys of swine markets in Saskatchewan, Quebec, and Prince Edward Island during the 1970s and 1990s reported rates of adult Ascaris infection in pigs ranging from 18% to 39%.12 Wild swine populations have also been increasingly recognized as reservoirs of infection in the United States.3 In the past 15 years, 16 locally acquired cases have been reported in humans in the US, all with swine exposure and no relevant travel risk.3,13,14 Similar sporadic cases have also been recently described in Austria and Japan among people with and without exposure risks.15,16

In Ontario, ascariasis is not a notifiable disease to public health authorities; therefore, its prevalence is not known. Our patient had been camping in proximity to a swine farm but did not visit the farm grounds. The confinement methods and other infection prevention measures used at the farm were not known. Wild pig populations have not been established in the area. The patient had no other known close contacts to individuals with a travel history to endemic regions, although broader ascariasis burden in other campground visitors cannot be fully excluded.

Ascariasis is an uncommon infection in Canada and other regions with robust sanitation and hygiene infrastructure, given that it is most commonly transmitted through ingestion of eggs from contaminated soil. Clinicians should be aware that, although rare, this infection can still occur in Ontario, especially in regions with limited water sanitation and hygiene infrastructure and among people with indirect contact with either infected individuals or infected swine populations. Although most patients presenting with expulsion of worms may in fact have passed undigested material, prompt macroscopic identification by an experienced clinician or by microbiologic evaluation with stool microscopy or polymerase chain reaction at a laboratory is helpful for further management. Treatment of uncomplicated infection is highly effective and should be accompanied by patient education on preventing reinfection, including the importance of hand hygiene and thorough washing of produce that may have been exposed to contaminated soil.
