A class of high-beta, large-aspect-ratio quasiaxisymmetric Palumbo-like configurations
Andrew Brown \aff 1 \aff 2 \corresp, Wrick Sengupta \aff 1, Nikita Nikulsin \aff 3, Amitava Bhattacharjee \aff 1

Abstract
The space of high-beta, approximately quasiaxisymmetric, large-aspect-ratio stellarator configurations is explored using an inverse coordinate approach and a quadratic polynomial ansatz for the flux function, following the method of Palumbo, extended by Hernandes and Clemente. This approach yields a system of nonlinear ODEs that, when solved, give equilibria exhibiting positive or negative triangularity, cusps, and (in an extreme limit) current singularities. It is shown that a cubic ansatz may also be used, but that polynomials of degree four or higher will lead to overdetermination.



1
Introduction

Omnigeneity, zero-average radial drift of the guiding center orbits, is essential for particle confinement in three-dimensional (3D) magnetic fields
(
Helander 2014
)
. One route to omnigeneity is quasisymmetry (QS), in which contours of the magnetic field strength are flat in a special coordinate system known as
Boozer angles
. QS with magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) force balance is known to be an overdetermined system
(
Garren & Boozer 1991
)
. However, recent strides have shown that extremely precise QS can be obtained by numerical optimization
(
Landreman & Paul 2022
)
, reinforcing the confidence that QS is a practical approach to plasma confinement. Because quasisymmetric equilibria have been found only numerically, our understanding of the qualitative behavior of quasisymmetric equilibria is still inchoate. Considerable effort has been applied to the near-axis theory of QS
(
Rodríguez & Bhattacharjee 2021
;
Rodríguez & Bhattacharjee 2021
)
and to weakly nonaxisymmetric equilibria at finite aspect ratio
(
Plunk & Helander 2018
;
Henneberg et al. 2019
;
Plunk 2020
)
. Relatively little work has been done to understand QS analytically far from the axis. One reason for this gap is the lack of a standard Grad-Shafranov equation (of the elliptic type) well known for axisymmetric equilibria. In 3D, even assuming the existence of nested surfaces, the Grad-Shafranov equation is non-standard and has mixed characteristics.

In this work, we consider the limit of large aspect ratio and weak perturbations away from axisymmetry to extract a tractable, semi-analytical model that reproduces many of the qualitative features of numerically optimized QA configurations. The regime explored here is inaccessible by near-axis theory and difficult to analyze using VMEC or similar tools because of sharp features in the plasma boundary. We therefore stress the complementary role that analytical theory must play in enhancing, explaining, and providing new directions for purely numerical optimization.

Our approach is stimulated by a recent 3D quasisymmetric, high-beta stellarator (QS-HBS) model developed in
Sengupta et al. 2024b
and extended in
Nikulsin et al. 2024
, which obtains a Grad-Shafranov equation (of the elliptic type) by imposing asymptotic ordering. In section
2
, we use this model to explore an interesting class of global solutions using a modified form of a method pioneered by Palumbo
(
Palumbo 1968
)
and extended in
Bishop & Taylor 1986
;
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
to construct isodynamic axisymmetric solutions for which magnetic surfaces and constant-
|\bm{B}|
contours coincide. In section
3
, we show that the resulting quasi-axisymmetric configurations exhibit many of the qualitative features observed in finite-aspect-ratio systems such as those found numerically by Landreman and Paul
(
Henneberg et al. 2019
;
Landreman & Paul 2022
)
. These include very low magnetic shear, D-shaped equilibria for small
\iota
, and inboard cusp formation as the maximum plasma volume is approached. In section
4
, we exploit the decoupled axis and plasma shaping to show that non-negligible rotational transform may be generated from weak axis perturbations, and we use VMEC to verify the asymptotic behavior of the QA error. We conclude by considering some possible applications of this work to quasi-axisymmetric stellarator design.

2
Analytic solutions of the Quasisymmetric Grad-Shafranov Equation: An extended Palumbo approach

Throughout this work, we use standard cylindrical coordinates
(R,\phi,Z)
and local Cartesian coordinates
(x,\xi)
centered on the magnetic axis
(R_{0}(\phi),Z_{0}(\phi))

such that
x=R-R_{0}(\phi)
and
\xi=Z-Z_{0}(\phi)
. The quasisymmetric Grad-Shafranov equation (QS-GSE) of the QS-HBS model takes the form (in normalized units)

\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\Psi+2x\frac{d\beta}{d\Psi}+\frac{dH}{d\Psi}=0

(2.1)

and the toroidal current, in terms of the profiles
\beta(\Psi)
and
H(\Psi)
, is

\frac{\mathbf{J_{\phi}}}{B_{0}}=-\epsilon\left(2x\frac{d\beta}{d\Psi}+\frac{dH}{d\Psi}\right).

(2.2)

We shall now employ an alternative approach based on an extension
(
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
;
Bishop & Taylor 1986
)
of Palumbo’s work on isodynamic equilibrium
(
Palumbo 1968
)
to obtain exact solutions to the QS-GSE equation (
2.1
). Our approach uses
(x,\Psi)
as independent variables and solves for
\xi(x,\Psi)
. Therefore, it is an inverse coordinate approach to solve the QS-GSE. The primary advantage of the inverse-coordinate approach stems from the fact that
\xi
enters the QS-GSE only through the
\nabla_{\perp}^{2}
term since the pressure term and the current terms are only functions of
(x,\Psi)
. Moreover, this approach shows that the MHD profile functions cannot be arbitrary if we impose QS.

2.1
An inverse-coordinate approach to solve the QS-GSE

Using an ingenious method, Palumbo
(
Palumbo 1968
)
obtained MHD equilibria with isodynamic magnetic fields, where the magnetic field strength is a constant on a flux surface, i.e.,
|\bm{B}|=B(\psi)
. Extensions of Palumbo’s approach were carried out by
Bishop & Taylor 1986
,
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
and
Hernandes & Nogueira 2013
.

Following the approach outlined in
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
, we now carry out the inverse-coordinate analysis starting with the variable transform from
(x,\xi)
to
(x,\Psi)
using the identities

\displaystyle\and{\partial_{x}}{y}=\and{\partial_{x}}{\Psi}+u\;\partial_{\Psi},\quad\and{\partial_{\xi}}{x}=v\;\partial_{\Psi},\quad u\equiv\Psi_{,x},\quad v\equiv\Psi_{,\xi}.

(2.3)

Since
(u,v)
are components of
\bm{\nabla_{\perp}}\Psi
, they must satisfy the consistency condition
\partial_{\xi}u=\partial_{x}v
, which can be written in the following equivalent forms
(
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
)
:

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x}+u\;\partial_{\Psi}\right)v^{2}=2v^{2}\partial_{\Psi}u\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\partial_{x}\left(\frac{1}{v}\right)+\partial_{\Psi}\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)=0.

(2.4)

The QS-GSE (
2.1
) in terms of
u,v
reads

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u+\partial_{\Psi}\Lambda=0

(2.5)

where, we have defined

\displaystyle\Lambda\equiv\frac{1}{2}\eta+2x\beta(\Psi)+H(\Psi),\quad\eta(x,\Psi)\equiv u^{2}+v^{2}.

(2.6)

Once the QS-GSE equation (
2.5
) is solved subject to the consistency condition (
2.4
), we obtain
\xi
through

\displaystyle\xi=-\int\frac{u}{v}\;dx,\quad\text{or equivalently }\quad\xi=\int\frac{1}{v}\;d\Psi.

(2.7)

The two integrals are equal to each other, as can be seen from the second form of the consistency condition (
2.4
).

The system (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) is a highly nonlinear system of PDEs for
(u,v)
. Fortunately, analytical progress can be made based on the following observation. Integrating (
2.5
) in
x
, we get

\displaystyle u(x,\Psi)=u_{0}(\Psi)-xH^{\prime}(\Psi)-x^{2}\beta^{\prime}(\Psi)-\frac{1}{2}\int dx\;\partial_{\Psi}\eta(x,\Psi).

(2.8)

The form of
u
as given in (
2.8
) suggests that one could seek a solution where
|\bm{\nabla_{\perp}}\Psi|^{2}=\eta(x,\Psi)
is a polynomial in
x
with coefficients that are functions of
\Psi
, as done in
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
. For the cases that
Palumbo 1968
and
Bishop & Taylor 1986
considered, the polynomial nature of
\eta
is a direct consequence of the property of
|\bm{B}|
.

We shall now seek a solution where
|\bm{\nabla_{\perp}}\Psi|^{2}=\eta
is a quadratic polynomial in
x
,

\displaystyle\eta(x,\Psi)=\eta_{0}(\Psi)+x\;\eta_{1}(\Psi)+x^{2}\eta_{2}.

(2.9)

Here
\eta_{i},i=0,1,2
are functions of
\Psi
to be determined by solving the nonlinear system (
2.5
)-(
2.4
). The quadratic nature of
\eta
is suggested by the fact that in (
2.8
) the non-trivial terms that involve
\beta^{\prime},H^{\prime}
are, at most, quadratic in
x
. Substituting (
2.9
) into (
2.8
) we get

\displaystyle u(x,\Psi)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(\Psi)+x\>u_{1}(\Psi)+x^{2}u_{2}(\Psi),\

(2.10)

where,

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\eta^{\prime}_{0}}{2}+H^{\prime}\right),\quad u_{2}=-\left(\frac{\eta^{\prime}_{1}}{4}+\beta^{\prime}\right).

(2.11)

We note that to maintain the quadratic nature of
u
, we have chosen
\eta_{2}
to be a constant.

With
\eta
and
u
available, we can now calculate
\Lambda,v^{2}
. From the definition of
\Lambda
(
2.6
), we find that
\Lambda
is a quadratic in
x
of the form

\displaystyle\Lambda=\left(\frac{\eta_{0}}{2}+H(\Psi)\right)+2x\left(\frac{\eta_{1}}{4}+\beta(\Psi)\right)+\eta_{2}\>x^{2}

(2.12)

Next, from the forms of
\eta,u
as given by (
2.9
), (
2.11
), we have

\displaystyle v^{2}=\eta-u^{2},

(2.13)

which can be expressed in terms of the following quartic in
x

\displaystyle v^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{n=4}V_{n}(\Psi)x^{n},

(2.14a)

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}-u_{0}^{2},\quad V_{1}=\eta_{1}-2u_{0}u_{1},\quad V_{2}=\eta_{2}-2u_{0}u_{2}-u_{1}^{2}

(2.14b)

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle=-2u_{1}u_{2},\quad V_{4}=-u_{2}^{2}.

(2.14c)

We are now in a position to evaluate the consistency condition (
2.4
) that would help us determine the functions of
\Psi
such as
u_{0},u_{1},\eta_{0},\eta_{1}
etc. Using
v^{2}=\eta-u^{2}
, the consistency condition can be written in the following alternative form

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x}+u\partial_{\Psi}-2u_{,\Psi}\right)\eta=\partial_{x}u^{2},

(2.15)

which leads to a fourth-order polynomial in
x
. Equating each of the coefficients of the polynomials to zero, we get five nonlinear coupled ODEs.

Equating the coefficient of
x^{4}
to zero we get the condition

\displaystyle u_{2}=\text{constant}.

(2.16)

The nonlinear ODEs can be simplified by using the following variables inspired by
Bishop & Taylor 1986
:

\displaystyle P=\frac{\eta_{0}}{u_{2}^{2}},\quad Q=\frac{\eta_{1}}{u_{2}^{2}},\quad X=\frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}},\quad\Gamma=\frac{u_{0}}{u_{2}},\quad\gamma=\frac{\eta_{2}}{u_{2}^{2}},\quad\beta_{p}=\frac{\beta}{u_{2}^{2}},\quad H_{p}=\frac{H}{u_{2}^{2}}.

(2.17)

Furthermore, we shall define a variable
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}=u_{2}\frac{d}{d\Psi}\equiv\dot{(\;\;)}.

(2.18)

In these variables, equating to zero the coefficients of
(x^{0},x,x^{2},x^{3})
of the consistency condition (
2.15
), leads to the following coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) for the system
(P,Q,X,\Gamma)

\displaystyle Q-2X\Gamma+\Gamma\dot{P}-2P\dot{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=0

(2.19a)

\displaystyle 2\gamma-2X^{2}+\Gamma(\dot{Q}-4)-2Q\dot{\Gamma}+X\dot{P}-2P\dot{X}

\displaystyle=0

(2.19b)

\displaystyle X(\dot{Q}-6)+\dot{P}-2(Q\dot{X}+\gamma\dot{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=0

(2.19c)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}-4-2\gamma\dot{X}

\displaystyle=0

(2.19d)

Once the system of ODEs (
2.19
) are solved, we can find
H,\beta
from (
2.11
) by integrating

\displaystyle\dot{H_{p}}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{P}+X\right),\quad\dot{\beta_{p}}=-\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\dot{Q}\right).

(2.20)

Though
u_{2}
appears to be a free parameter, if the leading-order magnetic field
B_{0}
is normalized to unity, the poloidal field must be
O(\epsilon)
, which gives some restriction on
u_{2}

viz.

\displaystyle|\nabla\Psi|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta}=\sqrt{\eta_{2}x^{2}+\eta_{2}x+\eta_{0}}

(2.21)

\displaystyle=|u_{2}|\sqrt{\gamma x^{2}+Qx+P},

(2.22)

so if the radicand is
O(1)
, we are forced to choose
u_{2}
to be at most
O(\epsilon)
.

Next, we discuss how we can obtain
\xi
using (
2.7
). The denominator of the integrand is
v
, which is a square root of a fourth order polynomial in
x
. Therefore, the integration with
x
can be carried out exactly in terms of elliptic integrals. It follows that

\displaystyle\xi=\pm X\int_{s_{1}(\Psi)}^{s}ds\frac{\Gamma/X^{2}+s(s-1)}{\sqrt{\left(P/X^{4}-sQ/X^{3}+s^{2}\gamma/X^{2}\right)-(s(s-1)+\Gamma/X^{2})^{2}}},\quad s\equiv-\frac{x}{X},

(2.23)

where
s_{1}(\Psi)
is a value of
s
such that the denominator vanishes.

It is possible to apply the same method assuming a polynomial ansatz of greater degree for
u
. It is shown in Appendix
A
that this procedure may be carried out without issue for a cubic polynomial, but for polynomials of degree
4
or greater, the resulting system of ODEs is overdetermined.

The choice of a quadratic ansatz for
u
is
post hoc
justified by the observation that, to lowest order in
\epsilon
,

\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla B=-\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Psi B_{0}}{(1+\epsilon x)^{2}}+O(\epsilon^{3})

(2.24)

and the field strength satisfies

B=B_{0}(1-\epsilon x)+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(2.25)

so if
v^{2}
is quartic in
x
,
\left(\frac{dB}{dl}\right)^{2}
will be a quartic in
B
(where
\l
is the length along the field line). In
Sengupta et al. 2024a
, convincing evidence has been provided that, in a quasiaxisymmetric field,
\left(\frac{dB}{dl}\right)^{2}
must be either a cubic or a quartic polynomial in
B
. The weakly perturbed tokamaks of the QS-HBS model
(
Sengupta et al. 2024b
;
Nikulsin et al. 2024
)
certainly allow deviation from this form for
\frac{dB}{dl}
, but the Palumbo-like configurations of
(
Bishop & Taylor 1986
;
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
)
do take the
form

\left(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla B^{2}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{P}_{3,4}(B^{2}).

(2.26)

Assuming a quartic form for
\frac{dB}{dl}
, we must have

v^{2}=\eta-u^{2}=\mathbb{P}_{4}(x),

(2.27)

making the simple choice of quadratic polynomials for
\eta
and
u
especially appealing.

This completes the inverse-coordinate approach to obtaining an analytic solution to the QS-GSE. The ODE system (
2.19
) needs to be solved to determine the profiles
H
and
\beta
that appear in the QS-GSE using (
2.20
). Thus, we can clearly see that the two MHD profile functions, and hence the rotational transform profile, cannot be arbitrary if we impose QS and MHD force-balance just like in the isodynamic case
(
Bishop & Taylor 1986
;
Schief 2003
;
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
)
. Finally, we need to address the issue of the closure of the flux surfaces. Nestedness is guaranteed by the consistency condition (
2.4
) but to ensure that flux surfaces are closed we need to impose additional conditions, which we discuss in Appendix
B
.

3
Extended Palumbo Profiles

We now explore the space of Palumbo-like profiles using the quadratic ansatz for
\partial_{x}\Psi
described above. We calculate the plasma profiles in terms of the solution of (
2.19
), using equations (
2.11
) and (
2.2
) to find the pressure and toroidal current (figures
1
and
3
, respectively), and integrating

\iota^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\frac{dx}{v}

(3.1)

to find the rotational transform
\iota
. The integral over a flux surface in (
3.1
) is between two vertical asymptotes of the surface, i.e., two roots of the polynomial
v^{2}
. Thus the integral can be efficiently approximated using Chebyshev-Gauss quadrature
(
Abramowitz & Stegun 1964
)
or written explicitly in terms of elliptic integrals
(
Gradshteyn & Ryzhik 2007
)
.

In the limit of small
\lambda
(equivalently, as we approach the magnetic axis),
v
becomes

v(s)\simeq X_{0}^{2}\sqrt{-s^{2}(s-1)^{2}+\gamma s^{2}/X_{0}^{2}},

(3.2)

with
s=-x/X
. The radicand has a double root at
s=0
and two single roots

s_{\pm}=1\pm\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}}}.

(3.3)

At positive lambda, the degeneracy is split, so the double root becomes a pair
\{\lambda s_{1},\lambda s_{2}\}
, and the other two roots gain an
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
correction. But in the limit of small
\lambda
as we integrate between the roots near zero, these corrections can be neglected:

\displaystyle\iota^{-1}|_{\lambda\to 0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi X}\oint\frac{ds}{\sqrt{-(s-\lambda s_{1})(s-\lambda s_{2})(s-(1+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}}}))(s-(1-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}}}))}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi X_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}}}}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\oint\frac{ds}{\sqrt{-(s-\lambda s_{1})(s-\lambda s_{2})}}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{X_{0}\sqrt{1-\frac{\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}}}}

(3.6)

where the integral can be evaluated for any nonzero
\lambda
and gives
\pm 2\pi
. Thus the on-axis rotational transform is simply

\iota=\sqrt{X_{0}^{2}-\gamma}.

(3.7)

It is possible to evaluate the integral (
3.1
) away from the magnetic axis in terms of elliptic functions, though this representation still depends on the roots of a quartic polynomial whose coefficients are the solutions to a nonlinear set of ODEs. Section 3.145 in Gradshteyn and Rizhik
(
Gradshteyn & Ryzhik 2007
)
gives

\iota^{-1}=\frac{2u_{2}}{\pi X\sqrt{(}pq)}F\left(\pi,\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(p+q)^{2}+(\alpha-\beta)^{2}}{pq}}\right)

(3.8)

where, using their notation,

\displaystyle p^{2}

\displaystyle=(m-\alpha)^{2}+n^{2}

(3.9)

\displaystyle q^{2}

\displaystyle=(m-\beta)^{2}+n^{2},

\alpha
and
\beta
are two real roots of
v^{2}
,
m\pm in
are the complex conjugate pair of roots of
v^{2}
, and
F
is the incomplete elliptic function of the first kind. A similar expression holds when all four roots of
v^{2}
are real.

Further analysis of equation (
3.8
) is difficult. Numerical integration using Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature, as in figure
2
, shows that the
\iota
profile is completely flat.
There is zero shear to order
\epsilon^{2}
in these configurations
. We emphasize that this characteristic is robust to changes in parameters
\gamma
and
X_{0}
and completely absent from the models of Bishop and Taylor
(
Bishop & Taylor 1986
)
or Hernandes and Clemente
(
Hernandes & Clemente 2009
)
. However, it is consistent with the observed low shear seen in optimized QA and the analysis of weakly non-asixymmetric QA of Plunk
(
Plunk 2020
)
, which found that force balance makes the magnetic shear at most second order in the perturbation magnitude.

3.1
Cusps and singularities

We now consider the extreme limits of the equilibria produced by the system (
2.19
). The singularities of this nonlinear set of ODEs correspond to a singular current profile and a corresponding flux surface cusp.

First, the limit of zero rotational transform requires
\gamma\to X_{0}^{2}
, which is a singular limit of the ODE initial conditions (see Appendix
B
). It is apparent from figure
5
that this limit also creates extreme surface elongation near the axis, and D-shaped, positive-triangularity surfaces away from the axis.

At finite rotational transform, the ODEs (
2.19
) are well-behaved near the axis. The profiles eventually develop a singularity at some finite value of
\lambda
, just as two roots of
u
and
v
coincide. The shape of the flux surface is locally determined by the ODE

\frac{d\xi}{dx}=\pm\frac{u}{v},

(3.10)

so for simple roots of
u
and
v^{2}
,
u\sim v^{2}\sim(x-x_{0})
implies

\xi\sim(x-x_{0})^{3/2}.

(3.11)

The collision of roots of
u
and
v^{2}
also occurs at the magnetic axis, but there we can enforce the condition
\partial_{x}v^{2}=0
to ensure local smoothness of
\Psi
. For a simple root of
v^{2}
, however, the flux surfaces lose analyticity, and a current singularity forms, as seen in figure
3
.

Figure
4
shows the
\xi^{2}\sim x^{3}
cusp that forms instead of an X-point, in keeping with the proven non-existence of X-points for perfect QS with nonzero rotational transform
(
Rodríguez & Bhattacharjee 2021
)
. Because the surface shape is independent of
\phi
, the cusp extends to an inboard ridge that moves in tandem with the magnetic axis. Such ridges are common features in optimized QS
(
Jorge et al. 2024
)
, but their behavior is poorly understood.

The formation of an inboard poloidal field null at large pressure is consistent with experimental results at the poloidal beta limit, as observed in TFTR
(
M.E. Mauel et al. 1992
)
. Conversely, in the limit of zero rotational transform, X-points are allowed, which we do observe as flux surfaces push toward a D shape. Though it might appear to be an artifact of our particular choice of ansatz, the index
3/2
in equation (
3.11
) is much more general. Locally, this index is a hallmark of a cusp catastrophe or a saddle-node bifurcation
(
Arnold & Thomas 1984
;
Meiss 2007
)
. The
3/2
cusp is one of only a handful of normal forms for catastrophes.

In figure
6
, we see that the flux label corresponding to the current singularity and cusp formation coincides with the collision of roots of
u
and
v
, which occurs as the denominator of (
2.19
) vanishes. There is an earlier, removable singularity (here, at
\lambda\simeq 0.22
) as the numerator also vanishes. With sufficient resolution, the integration can be continued past the first singularity, whereas the latter singularity represents a breakdown of flux surface analyticity and an infinite (albeit integrable) current density.

As observed above, in the large-aspect-ratio limit, QA configurations must satisfy

\left(\frac{dB}{dl}\right)^{2}\sim\left(\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial y}\right)^{2}\sim\text{quartic or cubic in }x

(3.12)

so
v\sim(x-x_{0})^{1/2}
near any simple root
x_{0}
of
v
. Therefore the local form
y^{2}\sim x^{3}
is the generic behavior for a cusp in small-
\epsilon
, QA equilbria.

Because we are free to choose any nonzero value of
\iota
throughout the plasma volume and thus in particular at the cusp, we emphasize that
\iota
is not necessarily rational at the cusp, further distinguishing the cusp from a standard X-point. This family of solutions may perhaps serve as a prototypical class for the study of nonresonant divertors, which have recently been of considerable interest in the stellarator community.

Away from the cusp, a root of
v^{2}
that does not coincide with a root of
u
gives the principal curvatures
\kappa_{1}\sim-\epsilon(x-x_{0})^{-1/2}
and
\kappa_{2}\sim(x-x_{0})^{1/2}
, so the Gaussian curvature
\mathcal{K}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
is negative and
O(\epsilon)
, but
\mathcal{K}
changes sign as the cusp forms since
\kappa_{1}\sim\epsilon(x-x_{0})^{-1/2}
when
u\sim(x-x_{0})
. By continuity, there is thus some value of
\Psi
at which
\mathcal{K}\to 0
near the cusp, right as the QS-HBS model breaks down. At the same time, the dominant toroidal field decays as
1/R
and the field line is forced to lie on a ridge that has the same
O(\epsilon)
deviation from circularity as the magnetic axis, so the magnetic field strength along the ridge only varies at
O(\epsilon^{2})
. Therefore the field becomes locally isodynamic at the ridge. The connection between ridge formation in the last closed flux surface and zeros of the Gaussian curvature is the subject of ongoing research, to be presented in an upcoming publication. That QS should be unattainable when the Gaussian curvature vanishes is reminiscent of the fact, familiar from near-axis theory, that the axis curvature may not vanish in QA. See appendix
C
for details of calculations of geometric quantities.

4
Three-Dimensional Equilibria

Using the generalized HBS model recently described in
Nikulsin et al. 2024
, it can be shown that the axis perturbations may include some radial component, so long as the perturbation amplitude is
O(\epsilon)
and the axis torsion is everywhere
O(\epsilon^{2})
. Here we show that these conditions may be satisfied, generalizing a similar calculation of the axis torsion in
Sengupta et al. 2024b
. The torsional contribution to the total rotational transform then arises at
O(\epsilon)
, the same order as the current-driven part.

Let the axis be defined by

\displaystyle\bm{r}=R_{0}(\bm{e_{R}}(1+\mathfrak{g}(\phi))+\mathfrak{f}(\phi)\bm{e_{Z}}).

(4.1)

Then the curvature is

\kappa=\left\lvert\frac{d\bm{r}}{d\ell}\times\frac{d^{2}\bm{r}}{d\ell^{2}}\right\rvert,

(4.2)

and the torsion is

\tau=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\frac{d\bm{r}}{d\ell}\times\frac{d^{2}\bm{r}}{d\ell^{2}}\cdot\frac{d^{3}\bm{r}}{d\ell^{3}}.

(4.3)

It is convenient to define the arclength

\displaystyle R_{0}s(\phi)=\frac{d\ell}{d\phi}=\sqrt{\frac{d\bm{r}}{d\phi}\cdot\frac{d\bm{r}}{d\phi}}=R_{0}\sqrt{(1+\mathfrak{g})^{2}+\mathfrak{g}^{\prime 2}+\mathfrak{f}^{\prime 2}},

(4.4)

so that the torsion becomes

\tau=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\frac{1}{R_{0}^{6}s^{6}}\frac{\partial\bm{r}}{\partial\phi}\times\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{r}}{\partial\phi^{2}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{3}\bm{r}}{\partial\phi^{3}},

(4.5)

in which we have excluded those parts of the derivatives of
\bm{r}
that must vanish upon taking the triple product.

Then the torsional part of the rotational transform is

\iota_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\mathrm{d}\phi s(\phi)\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}},

(4.6)

where the integrand is split for legibility into

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathfrak{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\left((1+\mathfrak{g})^{2}-\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime}(1+\mathfrak{g})+2\mathfrak{g}^{\prime 2}\right)-(1+\mathfrak{g})\left(\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime}-\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\mathfrak{f}^{\prime\prime}-\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}(1+\mathfrak{g})\right)

(4.7)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{f}^{\prime\prime}(1+\mathfrak{g})-2\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\right)(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime\prime}-3\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=

\displaystyle\left((1+\mathfrak{g})^{2}-\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime}(1+\mathfrak{g})+2\mathfrak{g}^{\prime 2}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathfrak{f}^{\prime\prime}(1+\mathfrak{g})-2\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\right)^{2}

(4.8)

\displaystyle+\left(\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime}-\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\mathfrak{f}^{\prime\prime}-\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}(1+\mathfrak{g})\right)^{2}

In the work that introduced the HBS model, perturbations were restricted to purely vertical displacements, i.e.,
\mathfrak{g}\equiv 0
, and the integrated axis torsion was
O(\epsilon^{3})

(
Sengupta et al. 2024b
)
. Our more complicated expression (
4.6
) does reduce to the torsion found previously,
viz.
,

\tau_{\mathfrak{g}\to 0}=\frac{1}{R_{0}}\frac{\mathfrak{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}+\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}}{1+\mathfrak{f}^{\prime\prime 2}+\mathfrak{f}^{\prime 2}}.

(4.9)

Expanding in powers of
\epsilon
and setting
\mathfrak{f}=\epsilon\mathfrak{f}_{1}
, this expression gives

\iota_{\tau,\mathfrak{g}\to 0}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint\mathrm{d}\phi\mathfrak{f}_{1}^{\prime 3},

(4.10)

different from the
\mathfrak{f}_{1}^{\prime}\mathfrak{f}_{1}^{\prime\prime 2}
integrand reported in previous work.

Allowing for nonzero
\mathfrak{g}
makes
\iota_{\tau}
formally
O(\epsilon^{2})
, assuming
\mathfrak{f}
and
\mathfrak{g}
are both
O(\epsilon)
quantities. For judiciously chosen
\mathfrak{f}
and
\mathfrak{g}
,
\iota_{\tau}
can be a significant contribution to the total rotational transform. For instance, taking
\mathfrak{f}
and
\mathfrak{g}
to be sums of two harmonics, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(\phi)

\displaystyle=f_{c,m}\cos(m\phi)+f_{c,n}\cos(n\phi)+f_{s,m}\sin(m\phi)+f_{s,n}\sin(n\phi)

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}(\phi)

\displaystyle=g_{s,m}\sin(m\phi)+g_{s,n}\sin(n\phi)+g_{c,m}\cos(m\phi)+g_{c,n}\cos(n\phi)

and choosing a range of aspect ratios
\epsilon^{-1}\in[5,20]
, we numerically maximize
\iota_{\tau}
subject to the (somewhat arbitrary) constraints
|a_{n,m},b_{n,m}|\leq 3\epsilon
and
\max\{\tau(\phi)\}\leq 3\epsilon^{2}
. Keeping only
n=1
and
m=2
, figure

shows that the axis contribution to the rotational transform can be significant, with the caveat that the axis torsion, although formally
O(\epsilon^{2})
, will increase the quasisymmetry error.

Using VMEC, we verify the Palumbo-like equilibria described in section

O(\epsilon^{2})
.

Landreman et al. 2021

\epsilon^{2}
, as shown in figure

5
Conclusion

We have constructed a novel class of solutions to the QS-GSE that arises in the limit of large-aspect-ratio, nearly-axisymmetric quasiaxisymmetry. These solutions exhibit surprising qualitative similarities to QA equilibria at finite aspect ratio, including negligible shear and the formation of surface cusps as the plasma volume approaches its maximum.

We also note that the condition of weak non-axisymmetry means the Palumbo-QS-HBS model is a perturbed tokamak, so many features found in QS-HBS have well-known analogues in the tokamak literature. The QS-HBS ordering has
\beta=O(\epsilon)
and
B_{\theta}/B_{\phi}=O(\epsilon)
, so

\beta_{pol}=O(1/\epsilon)
and
\epsilon\beta_{pol}\sim 1
, a parameter regime that was explored in TFTR, which found that a poloidal field null forms on the inboard side at the poloidal beta limit
(
M.E. Mauel et al. 1992
)
. Furthermore, the slender D-shaped flux surfaces in QS-HBS at
\beta\sim\epsilon
are reminiscent of Cowley’s high-
\beta
equilibria, which exhibit D-shaped surfaces and extreme Shafranov shift at
\epsilon\beta_{pol}\gg 1

(
Cowley et al. 1991
)
. However, vanishing shear and the local breakdown of analyticity of
\Psi
as a cusp (rather than an X-point) forms are not found in the aforementioned tokamak equilibria, providing suggestive, albeit inconclusive, evidence that these features are inherent to quasiaxisymmetry. In addition to these physical insights into QA, we hope that our Palumbo-like equilibria and other semi-analytical solutions to the QS-GSE will prove to be practically useful as hot starts in QA optimization.

Higher-Degree Flux Ansatzes

Here we show that the method of section
2
can be applied to a cubic polynomial ansatz for
\eta
, but for any polynomial of degree
4
or greater the resulting system of ODEs is overdetermined.

First, assuming a cubic ansatz

\eta(x,\Psi)=\eta_{3}x^{3}+\eta_{2}(\Psi)x^{2}+\eta_{1}(\Psi)x+\eta_{0}(\Psi),

(A.1)

where again the coefficient of the highest-degree term is taken to be constant so that
u
is also cubic, we find the following set of ODEs:

\displaystyle 2u_{0}u_{1}-\eta_{1}-u_{0}\eta_{0}^{\prime}+2u_{0}^{\prime}\eta_{0}

\displaystyle=0

(A.2a)

\displaystyle 2u_{1}^{2}+4u_{0}u_{2}-2\eta_{2}-u_{0}\eta_{1}^{\prime}-u_{1}\eta_{0}^{\prime}+2u_{0}^{\prime}\eta_{1}+2u_{1}^{\prime}\eta_{0}

\displaystyle=0

(A.2b)

\displaystyle 6u_{1}u_{2}-\frac{2}{3}\eta_{2}^{\prime}u_{0}-3\eta_{3}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}u_{0}+2\eta_{2}u_{0}^{\prime}-\eta_{1}^{\prime}u_{1}+2\eta_{1}u_{1}^{\prime}-\eta_{0}^{\prime}u_{2}+2\eta_{0}u_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0

(A.2c)

\displaystyle 4u_{2}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}u_{1}+\frac{1}{6}\eta_{0}^{\prime}\eta_{2}^{\prime}+2\eta_{1}u_{2}^{\prime}+2\eta_{3}u_{0}^{\prime}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}u_{1}+2\eta_{2}u_{1}^{\prime}-\eta_{1}^{\prime}u_{2}

\displaystyle=0

(A.2d)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\eta_{1}^{\prime}\eta_{2}^{\prime}-\frac{4}{3}\eta_{2}^{\prime}u_{2}+2\eta_{3}u_{1}^{\prime}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}u_{2}+2\eta_{2}u_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0

(A.2e)

\displaystyle\frac{5}{18}\left(\eta_{2}^{\prime}\right)^{2}+2\eta_{3}u_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0

(A.2f)

As
\eta_{3}
and
u_{3}
are constants, we have six equations in six unknowns. However, if
\eta

\eta_{4}
to be a nonzero constant, the equality

u=u_{0}-xH^{\prime}-x^{2}\beta^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\int dx\left[\eta_{0}^{\prime}+x\eta_{1}^{\prime}+x^{2}\eta_{2}^{\prime}+x^{3}\eta_{3}^{\prime}\right]

Appendix B
Details of the extended Palumbo solution

The ODE system (
2.19
) is highly nonlinear and can have solutions that are not regular or divergent near the axis. Thus, a proper regularity boundary condition must be imposed near the axis. Moreover, as discussed in Appendix A of
Bishop & Taylor 1986
, the closure condition for the flux surfaces holds in the entire volume if it holds near the axis. Therefore, we must carefully look at the near-axis behavior of the system (
2.19
).

The expression for
\xi
given in (
2.23
) suggests the following new variables

\displaystyle\overline{P}=\frac{P}{X^{4}},\quad\overline{Q}=\frac{Q}{X^{3}},\quad\overline{\Gamma}=\frac{\Gamma}{X^{2}},\quad\overline{\gamma}=\frac{\gamma}{X^{2}},\quad\overline{\xi}=\frac{\xi}{X},

\displaystyle\text{such that}\quad\quad\overline{\xi}=\int ds\frac{\overline{\Gamma}+s(s-1)}{\sqrt{\left(\overline{P}-s\overline{Q}+s^{2}\overline{\gamma}\right)-\left(s(s-1)+\overline{\Gamma}\right)^{2}}}.

(B.1)

Near the magnetic axis
(x=0,\Psi=0)
,
\eta=|\bm{\nabla_{\perp}}\Psi|
must vanish. Since
\eta=u^{2}+v^{2}
, we must have both
u=v=0
at
x=0,\Psi=0
. Furthermore, flux surfaces of elliptic or circular cross-sections can exist near the axis provided that
v^{2}
is approximately a quadratic with two real roots, the roots coinciding on the magnetic axis. Therefore,
\partial_{x}v^{2}
must vanish on the axis. Thus, we get three conditions

\displaystyle u=0,\quad v=0,\quad\partial_{x}v^{2}=0\quad\text{at}\quad(x=0,\Psi=0).

(B.2)

From (
2.9
), (
2.10
), and (
2.17
) it follows that (
B.2
) implies

\displaystyle P(0)=0,\quad\Gamma(0)=0,\quad Q(0)=0

(B.3)

The solution to (
2.19
) is very well approximated by its near-axis approximations of the form

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=X_{0}+\lambda X_{1}+\lambda^{2}X_{2}+O(\lambda^{3}),

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\lambda P_{1}+\lambda^{2}P_{2}+O(\lambda^{3}),\quad\overline{P}=\lambda\overline{P}_{1}+\lambda^{2}\overline{P}_{2}+O(\lambda^{3})

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\lambda\Gamma_{1}+\lambda^{2}\Gamma_{2}+O(\lambda^{3}),\quad\overline{\Gamma}=\lambda\overline{\Gamma}_{1}+\lambda^{2}\overline{\Gamma}_{2}+O(\lambda^{3}),

(B.4)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\lambda Q_{1}+\lambda^{2}Q_{2}+O(\lambda^{3}),\quad\overline{Q}=\lambda\overline{Q}_{1}+\lambda^{2}\overline{Q}_{2}+O(\lambda^{3})

where
X_{i},P_{i}...
etc are coefficients given by:

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{X_{0}^{2}}+\frac{3}{X_{0}^{2}-\gamma}\right),\quad X_{2}=-\frac{3\gamma(8X_{0}^{4}-7X_{0}^{2}\gamma+2\gamma^{2})}{16X_{0}^{5}(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma)^{3}}

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=2X_{0}-\frac{2\gamma}{X_{0}},\quad P_{2}=\frac{\left(4X_{0}^{2}-3\gamma\right)\left(\gamma^{2}+10X_{0}^{4}-2\gamma X_{0}^{2}\right)}{4X_{0}^{4}\left(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\gamma+2X_{0}^{2}\right)}{2X_{0}(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma)},\quad\Gamma_{2}=\frac{2\gamma^{3}-8X_{0}^{6}-3\gamma^{2}X_{0}^{2}}{16X_{0}^{4}\left(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma\right)^{3}}

(B.5)

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}}+\frac{3\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}-\gamma}+4\right),\quad Q_{2}=\frac{3\left(\gamma^{2}\left(2\gamma^{2}+8X_{0}^{4}-7\gamma X_{0}^{2}\right)\right)}{8\left(X_{0}^{5}\left(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma\right)^{3}\right)}

The convergence of the series in
\lambda
clearly requires
X_{0}>1,\gamma\ll X_{0}^{2}
. The limit

, in which the rotational transform vanishes, is singular. The quantities
\overline{P},\overline{Q},\overline{\gamma},\overline{\Gamma}
can be similarly expanded as

\displaystyle\overline{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda\left(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma\right)}{X_{0}^{5}}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\left(13\gamma^{3}-24X_{0}^{6}+106\gamma X_{0}^{4}-86\gamma^{2}X_{0}^{2}\right)}{4X_{0}^{8}\left(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma\right)^{2}}+O\left(\lambda^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\overline{Q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\left(\frac{3\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}-\gamma}+\frac{\gamma}{X_{0}^{2}}+4\right)}{X_{0}^{3}}-\frac{3\lambda^{2}\left(-2\gamma^{4}+64X_{0}^{8}-80\gamma X_{0}^{6}+8\gamma^{2}X_{0}^{4}+13\gamma^{3}X_{0}^{2}\right)}{8\left(X_{0}^{8}\left(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma\right)^{3}\right)}+O\left(\lambda^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\overline{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\left(\gamma+2X_{0}^{2}\right)}{X_{0}^{2}\left(2X_{0}^{3}-2\gamma X_{0}\right)}-\frac{3\lambda^{2}\left(2\gamma^{3}+24X_{0}^{6}-16\gamma X_{0}^{4}-7\gamma^{2}X_{0}^{2}\right)}{16\left(X_{0}^{6}\left(X_{0}^{2}-\gamma\right)^{3}\right)}+O\left(\lambda^{3}\right)

(B.6)

\displaystyle\overline{\gamma}

Appendix C
Geometry of the Extended Palumbo Solution

In this section we calculate in detail some geometric properties of axisymmetric extended Palumbo equilibria. For clarity we explicitly include a factor of
\epsilon
in the poloidal field component, rather than subsuming this factor into the definition of
u_{2}
.

The unit vector tangent to magnetic field lines is, in cylindrical coordinates,

\hat{\bm{b}}=\frac{B_{0}}{B(1+\epsilon x)}\hat{\mathbf{\phi}}+\frac{\epsilon}{B}\left(v\hat{\bm{R}}-u\hat{\bm{Z}}\right)

(C.1)

and the surface normal is

\hat{\bm{n}}=\frac{u\hat{\bm{R}}+v\hat{\bm{Z}}}{\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}}.

(C.2)

The geodesic curvature may be written as the triple product

\kappa_{g}=\frac{d\hat{\bm{b}}}{dl}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{n}}\times\hat{\bm{b}}\right).

(C.3)

The field line curvature

\frac{d\hat{\bm{b}}}{dl}=-\epsilon\hat{\bm{R}}+O(\epsilon^{2})

(C.4)

so, inserting into equation (
C.3
),

\kappa_{g}=-\epsilon\frac{v}{|\nabla\Psi|}

(C.5)

A flux surface cusp forms when
u
and
v^{2}
share a common, simple root at
x_{0}
. Inserting local behavior
u\sim x-x_{0}
and
v\sim(x-x_{0})^{1/2}
, we find

\displaystyle\kappa_{g}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(x-x_{0})^{1/2}}{\sqrt{(x-x_{0})^{2}+(x-x_{0})}}\sim\frac{(x-x_{0})^{1/2}}{(x-x_{0})^{1/2}},

(C.6)

so the geodesic curvature stays finite even as the flux surfaces form a cusp. The normal curvature is

\kappa_{n}=\frac{\bm{\kappa}\cdot\nabla\Psi}{|\nabla\Psi|}=-\epsilon\frac{u}{|\nabla\Psi|}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(C.7)

which varies as
\kappa_{n}\sim(x-x_{0})^{1/2}
near the cusp, vanishing while the geodesic curvature stays finite.

To find the principal curvatures, we calculate the first and second fundamental forms of the outermost flux surface, parametrized in Cartesian coordinates as

\bm{r}=(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi,Z)

(C.8)

where
Z=Z(R,\Psi)
is independent of
\phi
in the axisymmetric case. Then the normal vector

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{r}_{R}\times\bm{r}_{\phi}}{|\bm{r}_{R}\times\bm{r}_{\phi}|}

(C.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left(\partial_{R}Z\right)^{2}}}\left(-\partial_{R}Z\cos\phi,-\partial_{R}Z\sin\phi,1\right),

the first fundamental form is

\mathrm{I}_{p}=EdR^{2}+Gd\phi^{2}

(C.10)

where

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\partial_{R}\bm{r}\cdot\partial_{R}\bm{r}=\left(1+\left(\partial_{R}Z\right)^{2}\right)

(C.11)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\partial_{\phi}\bm{r}\cdot\partial_{\phi}\bm{r}=R^{2}

and the second fundamental form is

\mathrm{I\!I}_{p}=LdR^{2}+Nd\phi^{2}

(C.12)

where

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\bm{r}_{RR}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}=\frac{\partial_{R}^{2}Z}{\sqrt{1+\left(\partial_{R}Z\right)^{2}}}

(C.13)

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\bm{r}_{\phi\phi}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}=\frac{R\partial_{R}Z}{\sqrt{1+\left(\partial_{R}Z\right)^{2}}}.

Since the first and second fundamental forms are both diagonal, we can read off the principal curvatures

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{\sqrt{EG}}=\frac{\partial_{R}^{2}Z}{R\left(1+\left(\partial_{R}Z\right)^{2}\right)}

(C.14)

\displaystyle\simeq\epsilon\frac{v\frac{du}{dx}-u\frac{dv}{dx}}{u^{2}+v^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\sqrt{EG}}=\frac{R\partial_{R}Z}{R\left(1+\left(\partial_{R}Z\right)^{2}\right)}

(C.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{uv}{u^{2}+v^{2}}

using
\partial_{R}Z\to\frac{d\xi}{dx}=\pm\frac{u}{v}
.

Appendix D
Analogy to the Heavy Symmetric Top

Here we quote results from Goldstein’s exposition
(
Goldstein et al. 2002
)
(Section 5.7) of the heavy symmetric top with one point fixed, and elucidate parallels between that system and the modified Palumbo equilibria constructed in this work. Following Goldstein’s notation, the time elapsed by an orbit
\mathcal{U}(t)
takes the form of an elliptic integral

t=\int_{\mathcal{U}(0)}^{\mathcal{U}(t)}\frac{d\mathcal{U}}{\sqrt{(1-\mathcal{U}^{2})(\alpha-\beta\mathcal{U})-(b-a\mathcal{U})^{2}}}

(D.1)

and the integrand is the square root of a cubic polynomial

f(\mathcal{U})=\beta\mathcal{U}^{3}-(\alpha+a^{2})\mathcal{U}^{2}+(2ab-\beta)\mathcal{U}+(\alpha-b^{2})

(D.2)

in the variable
\mathcal{U}=\cos\theta
, where
\theta
is the angle between the vertical and the symmetry axis of the top. The comparison to equation (
3.1
) is immediate; in our case,
v
is the square root of a quartic in
x
instead of a cubic in
\mathcal{U}
, but a formal similarity is apparent.

Figure 10
:
Top: Paths traced by a precessing, nutating heavy symmetric top in the cases of (a) smooth (
u^{\prime}>u_{2}
), (b) cusped (
u^{\prime}=u_{2}
), and (c) looped (
u_{1}<u^{\prime}<u_{2}
) nutation. Bottom: Analogous magnetic smooth, cusped, and looped magnetic surfaces as the non-axisymmetric perturbation is increased. Reproduced from
Plunk 2020
with permission.

The behavior of the top may be qualitatively understood from the root of the monomial

g(\mathcal{U})\equiv b-a\mathcal{U}

(D.3)

in relation to the roots
\mathcal{U}_{1,2,3}
of
f(\mathcal{U})
, which are all real. If
\mathcal{U}_{1}<\mathcal{U}^{\prime}<\mathcal{U}_{2}
, the tip of the top traces loops on the unit sphere; if
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}>\mathcal{U}_{2}
, the top smoothly nutates without looping, as shown in figure
10
. Most interestingly, if
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}_{1}
or
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}_{2}
, the loops contract and the locus develops cusps. In other words, the top locus develops cusps when
g(\mathcal{U})
, a polynomial of degree
1
, has a common root with
f(\mathcal{U})
, a polynomial of degree
3
. In the modified Palumbo case,
\partial_{x}\Psi
is a polynomial of degree
2
and
(\partial_{\xi}\Psi)^{2}
is a polynomial of degree
4
, but just as for the top, a cusp forms when these two polynomials share a root. We also note that the sum

f(\mathcal{U})+g(\mathcal{U})^{2}=(1-\mathcal{U}^{2})(\alpha-\beta\mathcal{U})

(D.4)

is analogous to
|\nabla_{\perp}\Psi|^{2}
, which is a quadratic in
x
in the modified Palumbo case. In both models, when
g(\mathcal{U})
or
u
go to zero simultaneously with
f(\mathcal{U})
or
v^{2}
, there is a qualitative change in the local behavior of the integrand in the
t
(or
\iota^{-1}
) integrals.

A crucial difference is that the higher degree in the Palumbo case allows for a pair of complex roots, which may collide outside the domain of the flux surface, creating a removable singularity instead of a physically realized cusp.
