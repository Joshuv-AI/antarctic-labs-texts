Measuring the Vulnerability Disclosure Policies of AI Vendors
Yangheran Piao, Jingjie Li, Daniel W. Woods

Abstract
As AI is increasingly integrated into products and critical systems, researchers are paying greater attention to identifying related vulnerabilities. Effective remediation depends on whether vendors are willing to accept and respond to AI vulnerability reports. In this paper, we examine the disclosure policies of 264 AI vendors. Using a mixed-methods approach, our quantitative analysis finds that 36% of vendors provide no disclosure channel, and only 18% explicitly mention AI-related risks. Vulnerabilities involving data access, authorization, and model extraction are generally considered in-scope, while jailbreaking and hallucination are frequently excluded. Through qualitative analysis, we further identify three vendor postures toward AI vulnerabilities—proactive clarification ( n = 46, include active supporters, AI integrationists, and back channels), silence ( n = 115, include self-hosted and hosted vendors), and restrictive ( n = 103). Finally, by comparing vendor policies against 1,130 AI incidents and 359 academic publications, we show that bug bounty policy evolution has lagged behind both academic research and real-world events.

For RQ1, our analysis shows that 36% of AI vendors do not provide any disclosure channel, while only 18% explicitly mention AI security in their policies, with the earliest reference appearing in 2018. Vulnerabilities described in terms of traditional security objectives are most reliably treated as in-scope (90%). 3.1
Disclosure Policy Collection

Many firms incorporate “AI” into their branding and marketing, even when their offerings use minimal AI techniques
[
46
]
.
Relying exclusively on such self-reported claims would risk including vendors that are not substantively engaged in the development or deployment of artificial intelligence.
Instead, we collected product lists across nine AI-related categories published by an internationally recognized authority in technology market analysis, Gartner
[
27
]
.
These lists are subject to continual review, which allows companies to challenge their exclusion or request reevaluation. Gartner has a commercial incentive to maintain comprehensive and accurate coverage of AI products, given their customers use these lists for procurement decisions.

Some vendors offer multiple AI products, so we performed merging and deduplication to ensure each company was represented only once in our dataset.
This resulted in a list of 264 unique companies,
To assign categories to vendors, we used a priority-based mapping where “core” categories were assigned first.
For example, vendors who offer foundation models are “model providers”, even if they also offer an AI code assistant.
This ensures the classification reflects the most significant products provided by each company, reducing noise from overlapping offerings.

Collecting Disclosure Policies.

To identify whether a structured mechanism for third-party vulnerability reporting, we searched for the disclosure policy across:
a)

security.txt
files

from the Firebounty platform;
b)
examining major bug bounty platforms, including HackerOne, Bugcrowd, and Huntr; and
c)
manually reviewing web search engine results.
We then cross-checked each company’s official website to minimize the risk of missing relevant information.
We found that 96 companies in the sample lacked any kind of vulnerability disclosure mechanism. Two of these firms may have shut down because their website was not accessible.

For the AI companies that allow vulnerability submissions, we collected all available vulnerability disclosure and bug bounty policies through a semi-automated process. For websites that prohibit automated crawling, we used manual collection. In total, we gathered 168 vulnerability-related policy documents.

3.2.1
High-Level Structure

We organized the 168 documents using Walshe
et al.

[
73
]
’s analytical framework that categorizes bug bounty policies into 12 elements.
These include definitions of scope, eligibility of vulnerabilities, rules of engagement, legal & procedural guidelines, restrictions on participants & activities, evaluation of rewards, and official company statements.

After reading the policies in full, we realized it needed to be modified for our research questions.
We added the
Service-Commitments
element to track how many vendors say that AI vulnerability reports will not be responded to within the same time period as traditional reports.
We added the
Contact-Channel
element because many vendors ask for AI vulnerabilities to be submitted via a separate channel.
We also further divided the element
Legal-Clauses
into two different elements:
Compliance-Requirements
and
Safe-Harbor
.
This was not directly motivated by our research questions, but instead because we believe the legal protections for security researchers (safe harbors) require special consideration to respond to their concerns.
[
30
]
.

All collected policy texts were classified according to our expanded 15-element schema. To account for modularity, we segmented the text using section headers and relevant keywords. For policies that did not contain a particular element, the corresponding field was left empty. This process resulted in 1,011 policy elements.
After classification, we manually reviewed all policies to validate the accuracy of elements assignment.

3.2.2
AI-specific Analysis

During manual review, we also identified 48 firms that explicitly mentioned AI-related vulnerabilities in their policies.
For companies without any public vulnerability disclosure channel, we additionally examined their security-related webpages to determine whether they included any relevant statements or implied mechanisms for receiving vulnerability reports.

Snapshot Analysis.

To analyze the most recent policies, we used keyword searches (e.g., AI, LLM, AI safety, and security terminology) to semi-automatically extract all text segments related to AI vulnerabilities or AI products from the reporting policies of the 48 identified vendors. For each extracted segment, we recorded its associated policy element and the corresponding company name.

After reviewing the extracted contents manually, we organized and structured the text into discrete analytical units, each representing a complete and coherent thematic idea, resulting in a total of 422 units.
We grouped these units by both vendor type and the corresponding element they addressed.

We developed a codebook based on an initial review and collaborative discussion among the research team.
To ensure the reliability and consistency of our coding framework, two authors independently applied the codebook to a randomly selected sample of 100 analytical units.
The coded responses achieved a high Cohen’s Kappa (
k
= 0.816), indicating strong agreement between coders.
The discrepancies in coding were clarified and discussed by the research team.

Longitudinal Analysis.

For the 48 companies that mentioned AI, we also collected historical policy data to track changes over time. This used a Python script based on the WaybackPy library
2
2

2

https://pypi.org/project/waybackpy/
and the changelog provided by bounty platforms.
We retrieved archived versions of their vulnerability submission policies from the Internet Archive, and collected the specific sections of text that had changed across versions.
For two companies whose archived pages were not available on the Wayback Machine, we collected public announcements related to their bug bounty programs to identify the approximate time when AI-related vulnerabilities were first incorporated, as well as subsequent modifications and the specific changes made. Finally, we examined the content of each policy update and extracted a total of 205 distinct changes involving AI-related policy elements.

3.3
Incidents and Research Trends (RQ3)

Our final research question asks whether AI security issues in disclosure policies are aligned with actual incidents and academic research.
Comprehensively collecting and analyzing primary data about either would represent a standalone contribution outside the scope of this paper.
Instead we conducted a secondary analysis of of an AI incident repository
[
45
]
, and a database of AI security articles published at the big four security venues.

Incidents.
We analyzed data from the AI Incident Database (AIID)
[
45
]
, a public, community-driven repository of AI failures.
The database collects incidents submitted by journalists, users, and researchers.
The corpus captures a broader notion of security than the CIA triad, covering various harms resulting from AI systems like discrimination, misinformation, and socioeconomic & environmental harms.
Each incident is manually reviewed and enriched with metadata, including tags and categorical labels from different taxonomies. We collected 1,130 incidents as of December 2024.

To structure the analysis, we adopted the AIID’s MIT AI Risk Taxonomy
[
58
]
, a comprehensive framework introduced to systematize risks from AI across multiple dimensions. We also mapped the AI vulnerability types mentioned in the policies to the MIT taxonomy, with the method described in Appendix
A.3
.

After deduplicating the AIID entries and mapping the incidents to taxonomy labels, we linked them to the 47 AI vendors identified in the previous section, resulting in a subset of 320 relevant incidents. We compare these filtered real-world AI incidents against our policy codebook to assess alignment between reported events and AI vulnerability disclosure practices.

Academic Research.

We wanted to understand which AI issues the research community is focused on.
We conducted a comprehensive literature review of academic taxonomies and surveys on AI security to extract a meta-taxonomy of AI vulnerabilities.
Details on the collection criteria and the resulting meta-taxonomy can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

The literature search resulted in 146 papers from computer security conferences.
We applied inclusion criteria focused on offensive security and broad classification, manually screening for relevance.
We excluded research focused on detection, defense, or usability.
To verify the completeness of the resulting meta-taxonomy, we analyzed 260 AI security and safety papers from four security conferences (2020–2024)
3
3

3

https://github.com/gnipping/Awesome-ML-SP-Papers
.
Finally, this resulted in a six-category meta-taxonomy of AI vulnerabilities, as well as the year-by-year thematic distribution of AI security articles over the past four years based on this taxonomy.

4.1
Current Status

How to Disclose an AI Vulnerability.
We first examined the distribution and differences of vulnerability reporting channels.
The adoption of vulnerability disclosure mechanisms among AI companies (64%) is lower than adoption of BBPs among Fortune 100 firms (72%)
[
69
]
.
This is likely because many AI firms are young companies who prioritize growth.

Across 264 AI firms, 36% were found to have no public vulnerability reporting or disclosure channel.
Manual review revealed that a quarter of these firms at least provide security statements or trust centers on their websites.
4
4

4

The trust center is an emerging type of public-facing statement that outlines a company’s internal security, privacy, and compliance practices.

Such firms may rely on internal security research or contracted pen testing.
This still leaves more than a quarter (27%) of AI firms who have no reporting channel or public security information.

Around 16% of AI firms operate VDPs, which accept submissions without offering monetary rewards. However, VDPs at least provide researchers with protection from legal prosecution. Additionally, 21 vendors have not established a formal reporting process but provide a dedicated email address or channel for vulnerability submissions.

Finally, 40% of AI firms run a BBP that explicitly offer rewards for valid vulnerability reports.
Many of these firms rely on a platform to host their public BBP, while 35 independently operate their own bug bounty programs.
Larger companies tend to run independent BBPs.
Additionally, 10 firms operated private programs where the scope was not publicly visible and could only be accessed by invited researchers.

Disclosure Policies by Type of AI Vendor.
Figure
2
shows that just 18% of firms in our sample explicitly mentioned AI issues.
Model providers have the highest focus on AI vulnerabilities. Around half (48%) explicitly mention AI vulnerabilities or include AI products within their defined scope.
Only a small fraction of model providers (8%) had no vulnerability disclosure mechanism.

Many AI firms (24%) focused on traditional vulnerabilities without specifically mentioning AI security.
Generative AI Engineering firms were most likely to be in this category, where 37% of them emphasized traditional vulnerabilities.
The category most likely to lack a vulnerability disclosure channel was Data Science & Machine Learning Platforms, for which the majority (68%) of firms do not have a channel.
Somewhat encouragingly, many of these products are harder for attackers to access compared to an internet-accessible Gen-AI app or model.

The remaining AI firms (22%) could not be categorized because they did not clearly mention any type of vulnerabilities.
These policies are VDPs with minimal description of scope.
It also includes firms who only include
security contact email, lacking any formal disclosure policy.

4.2
Policy Structure

Turning to the high-level structure of documents, Table
2
compares coverage of elements among all documents with coverage in policies that mention AI issues.

Completeness

All policies contained a designated channel for submitting reports (i.e. through the platform, online form or contact the email address), which is a necessary requirement for receiving bug reports.
Beyond that, the most frequent element was a
Company-Statement
, by which vendors articulate a general commitment to security collaboration.
However, many disclosure documents lacked elements.
Nearly 40% of AI companies incorporated fewer than six elements, while only 6% covered the full set of policy elements.
Model providers have the most complete policies among all AI company types, with 42% including more than ten elements and 25% covering all elements comprehensively.

Traditional vs AI-specific Policies.

Intuitively, policies explicitly referencing AI vulnerabilities were more likely to contain all other elements.
For example, 88% of AI policies include a clear
Scope-In
definition that clarifies which systems or vulnerabilities are considered in scope. This rate is 80% higher than the rate for no AI-mentioned policies. Similarly, 79% of AI-mention policies specify
Vuln-Ineligible
conditions, which is higher than the rate of 38% among traditional policies.
Unsurprisingly, companies with more advanced disclosure documents are more likely to mention AI.

Legal Gaps.

Legal or compliance-related elements are scarce.
Only 36% of traditional policies mention relevant
Compliance-Requirement
. Among them, three quarters do not specify any concrete laws or regulations. Instead, they ask researchers to comply with broad obligations like “
all applicable laws and regulations, … governing privacy or the lawful handling of data,
” which offers limited clarity to hackers.
9% of traditional policies mention specific laws like the GDPR, CFAA, CCPA, DMCA and COPPA.
Mentioning compliance requirements is more common (46%) in AI-specific policies and involve laws such as the EU AI Act.

AI-specific policies were more than twice as likely to offer some form of
Safe-Harbor
clause, compared to just 18% across traditional policies.
Only 28% of traditional policies specified
Participant-Restriction
clauses (vs. 38% of AI-specific policies), often noting that individuals from certain countries or under specific legal conditions (e.g., export control restrictions) may be ineligible for participation or rewards.

Interaction Protocols.

The absence of structured interaction protocols remains a concern. Only 31% of traditional policies included a
Service-Commitment
element, which typically outlines expected timelines for vendor responses or resolution. Response windows also varied widely, from fixed timelines like “
within 90 days
” to vague phrasing such as “
we will get back to you as soon as possible.
”
The majority of AI-specific policies (65%) provide a concrete timeline for responding to vulnerability reports.
Moreover, 73% of AI-specific policies include
Engagement
provisions (vs. 39% of traditional policies). These provide requirements for AI testing, such as:
“
Any actions that could potentially harm our LLM systems… are strictly prohibited.
”

Rewards.

The
Reward-Evaluation
element captures pricing of vulnerabilities.
Just 33 bug bounty programs offer publicly accessible AI reward information after excluding private programs, platforms that do not accept AI vulnerabilities, and those that do not offer monetary rewards.
Among the 33 BBPs, 20 vendors apply a unified pricing structure, treating AI vulnerabilities the same as traditional ones in terms of severity tiers and reward brackets.

The remaining 13 vendors offer separate price ranges for AI vulnerabilities.
Four vendors assign lower payouts to AI-related issues, possibly reflecting lower perceived impact or greater ease of finding vulnerabilities. In contrast, nine companies assign higher payouts to AI vulnerabilities than to the majority of traditional issues at comparable severity levels. These companies often classify AI products as “
core assets
”.

We found that the highest reward offered for AI issues does not exceed the top-tier bounty for the most severe traditional vulnerabilities. Even in cases where AI issues are priced higher than the majority of traditional vulnerabilities, they still cap out at the same level as traditional "
Tier-1
" assets.

AI-Specific Vulnerabilities

We classified the 138 AI-specific vulnerabilities extracted from policies into three major categories: AI systems, AI models, and AI features. Table

Among vendors that mention AI vulnerabilities, the largest share belongs to the model providers (60%), followed by Gen-AI App (26%) and Gen-AI Engineering (5%). Model providers also account for the highest proportions of vulnerabilities addressing AI system, AI model, and AI feature vulnerabilities, at 79%, 59%, and 56% respectively.

AI System Vulnerabilities.
The vulnerabilities in this category (21%) do not explicitly name the type of AI attack.
Instead they are defined in terms of the security goals of an AI system. If the goal is violated, the bug is in scope.

AI Features.
Of the remaining “vulnerabilities”, 36% refer to functionality specific to generative AI systems, such as content generation, dialogue capabilities, or contextual personalization. Vulnerabilities in this category mainly include harmful/insecure outputs (

These issues often exploit the interactive interfaces of models, leading the model to produce unsafe content, broadly defined.
For example, Amazon’s policy describes content that is “

These ‘bugs’ are likely better describe as safety problems than security vulnerabilities, a topic we return to in Section

In vs Out of Scope.

We analyzed whether specific types of AI-related vulnerabilities were explicitly acknowledged as eligible within each vendors’ disclosure policies.
At a high-level, AI System vulnerabilities were most likely to be included.
Some AI model vulnerabilities were consistently included, whereas others were excluded relatively often.
Meanwhile, AI feature vulnerabilities were even less consistent.

Consistently Accepted.

Some vulnerabilities demonstrate both high mention frequency and high inclusion rates. Notably, categories under AI system such as authorization and data access, which include issues like unauthorized actions, cross-customer data access, or privilege escalations, are almost universally accepted (100%), and these types also exist in traditional systems. The vulnerabilities that are specific to AI, such as data poisoning, model extraction and inference attacks also show strong eligibility rates (over 80%), which shows that AI system and model vulnerabilities are taken seriously by vendors.
Model extraction was consistently included, likely due to the vendor’s IP being at risk. Google explained that “
AI models often include sensitive intellectual property … such as their architecture or weights.
”

Inconsistent Coverage.
The AI feature category, like harmful output and hallucination, show more fragmented treatment. Harmful output was among the most frequently mentioned, which is defined by Priceline as “
harmful, inappropriate, or misleading responses
”. It had an eligibility rate of only 42%. Similarly, hallucination showed moderate mention frequencies but had the lowest acceptance rate (14%). However, this is likely not due to a lack of importance. As Amazon explained, these issues may only be “
about responsible AI usage
” as there is “
no clear application security impact

4.4
Policy Evolution

As shown in Figure
3
, a total of 205 AI-related policy changes across 46 vendors were identified between 2018 and 2025.
Among them, modifications related to scope and vulnerability eligibility account for the largest share, totaling more than 57%. Specifically,
Scope-In/Vuln-Eligible
changes represent 38%, while
Scope-Out/Vuln-Ineligible
account for 19%.
The earliest recorded policy change involving AI vulnerabilities appeared in December 2018, when Tripadvisor explicitly excluded their AI product from the scope of the bug bounty policy. The next came in June 2020, when Asana included prompt injection in its list of eligible vulnerability categories. Updates remained infrequent in 2021 and 2022, when three vendors made AI-related adjustments, primarily involving scope additions and exclusions.

In 2023, AI-focused policy changes became more frequent, with seven companies updating their policies. Of these, five either began accepting AI vulnerability reports for the first time or expanded their disclosure frameworks to explicitly address AI products. Two others introduced or refined structured evaluation processes, including setting reward ranges and defining severity classifications specifically for AI-related findings. Four vendors also formalized exclusions for certain categories, such as model prompt extraction or off-topic chatbot responses.

At least twenty companies made AI-related updates in 2024, with thirteen explicitly adding AI vulnerabilities to their accepted scope, whether by creating new categories, clarifying applicable AI features, or establishing exclusion criteria. Nine companies refined pre-existing AI policies through expanded scope descriptions, added documentation for testers, or the introduction of new bounty pricing for specific issues such as bias or generative content misuse. Across all updates in 2024, there were fifteen instances of initial AI vulnerability inclusion, eleven cases of scope or procedural refinement, eight changes to reward structures or severity grading, and seven updates defining eligible or ineligible AI vulnerability types.

Until August 2025, more than fifteen companies updated their AI vulnerability policies. Seven of these addressed AI vulnerabilities for the first time, while others focused on adjustments to reward structures, clarification of testing rules, or the introduction of compliance and participation restrictions.

Among vendors, model providers show the most frequent updates. Microsoft had the most AI-specific updates, which primarily focused on
Reward-Evaluation
, continuously refining the evaluation criteria and pricing.
Many AI companies initially did not have a clearly defined acceptance scope for AI vulnerabilities, so their first updates often focused on
Scope-In
(
n
= 41). Even in subsequent iterations,
Scope-In
remained a high-frequency change, indicating that as AI product forms and functionalities evolved, AI companies adjust which parts could be tested and reported. In contrast, reward mechanisms (
n
= 36) and vulnerability type definitions (
n
= 31) also saw many changes, but these typically occurred only after scope had been established.

RQ2: Profiles and Positions

Based on the granular findings in the previous section, we classify AI vendors into three broad approaches.
Section

describes the approach of vendors that affirmatively clarify how AI vulnerabilities should be disclosed.
Section

Finally, Section

5.1
Proactive Clarification

The proactive profile (
n
= 46) encompasses vendors that, to varying degrees, acknowledge AI vulnerabilities in their disclosure programs and take steps to encourage reports. We identify three subtypes:

a)
Active Supporters
: where AI submissions are welcomed as a distinct set of security issues.

b)
Integrationist
: where AI vulnerabilities are listed, but not treated differently.

c)
Back-channel
: where AI issues are not in scope for rewards, but can be reported via a separate channel.

5.1.1
Active Supporters

Vendors in the active supporter category (
n
= 9) articulate a clear structure for the disclosure, evaluation, and remediation of AI vulnerabilities.
These organizations demonstrate not only openness to AI security concerns, but also offer concrete technical and procedural support for external researchers. Their profiles are characterized by both clarity of scope and institutional readiness, even though they may still declare specific AI vulnerabilities as out-of-scope.

Model providers like Google, Microsoft and Meta are over-represented in this category. By contrast, no vendors adopted this approach from the Data Science & ML Platform category, even though it is the largest category.

Defining & Encouraging.
The defining feature of active supporters is explicit encouragement of AI submissions (

= 7). This is often expressed in the

Other vendors extend the scope to unknown or novel vulnerabilities (

To clarify the terrain of acceptable testing, active supporters always specify AI-specific assets to help bug hunters determine scope. The nine vendors clarify that the following products are in-scope: AI-enabled products or services (

5.1.2
AI Integrationists

Many AI vendors (
n
= 29) fold AI vulnerabilities into the existing disclosure policy without providing standalone encouragement, reporting channels, or dedicated testing materials.
These vendors typically clarify which AI products are in scope, or list categories of AI vulnerabilities that are either in scope or excluded.

Reliance on Lists.
One of the most recognizable features of integrationist vendors is reliance on checklist-style enumeration.
This involves providing lists of the scope of AI-related products (
n
= 21) or eligible AI vulnerability types (
n
= 12), without offering further descriptions, reasoning, or attack context. For example, Glean lists the following AI vulnerabilities as in scope: “
LLM related attacks: Data exfiltration; Insecure output handling.
”
These list are the most affirmative approach in this category.

A small subset of these vendors (n = 4) reference AI vulnerabilities using overly broad terminology, such as “
model safety issues
” or “
… issues related to model prompts and responses
”.
Some vendors fail to clearly distinguish AI-related vulnerabilities from traditional security issues (
n
= 8).
In most cases, AI vulnerabilities are subsumed under broader categories of conventional issues, leaving researchers to identify and interpret the relevant scope on their own.

Integrationist vendors also provide little explanation for why certain categories are out-of-scope, offering only lists (
n
= 9). These vendors only accept well-known attack types (
n
= 5), with no acknowledgment of new AI vulnerabilities. This is exemplified by Atlassian who clarify that “
AI issues not mentioned explicitly above are considered ineligible.
”
The lack of additional details or explanation may reflect the vendors’ belief that AI vulnerabilities are a subset of traditional security that does not need further explanation. It is unclear whether this view is shared by security researchers, who may appreciate affirmative clarification.

Lack of Resources
. A handful of integrationist vendors (
n
= 4) add links to the developer documentation or AI product usage pages. Only two vendors offer guides tailored to AI scenarios.
From a structural perspective, there is no separate AI programs, and no price or evaluation mechanism. The result is a lack of signal clarity, researchers have no way of knowing how their reports were evaluated or the range of amounts they might have been paid.

AI Incident and Research Alignment (RQ3)

This section explores the extent to which AI disclosure policies align with actual incidents and also research.
This ambitious research question compares our findings to overarching societal and research trends, which are inherently difficult to quantify (dicussed in Section

6.1
Timing

Vendor disclosure policies were slow to affirmatively address AI vulnerabilities relative to both academia and actual incidents.
The first disclosure policies in industry that mention AI was Tripadvisor’s in late 2018, followed by Asana in 2020, Coveo in 2021, and Sendbird and Dropbox in 2022 (see Figure

By contrast, there were public reports of AI incidents as early as 2012 (see Figure

Academics had already been asking questions like “can machine learning be secure?” since 2006

. A stream of seminal articles suggested the answer was “no”

This raises the question of whether vendors could or should have clarified disclosure policies earlier.
It is useful to focus on AI vulnerabilities that were consistently accepted by vendors, such as model extraction (see Table

The first model extraction attack was published in 2016
[
68
]
. As shown in Figure
4
, model extraction and inversion accounts for a quarter of security articles throughout 2020–2024. Yet, the first vendors to affirmatively clarify that such vulnerabilities were in scope were Google and Microsoft in 2023.
This suggests vendors could have perhaps moved faster.

However, the delay was much shorter in the case of vulnerabilities associated with LLM, such as jail breaking and prompt injection.
Both types of AI vulnerability were widely discussed on social media platforms following the release of ChatGPT in late 2022 and into 2023
[

,
45

.
Meanwhile, vendors added these terms to their disclosure policies as early as March 2020, when Asana published a “

” stating that it would “

”
Academics had studied how adversarial tokens could manipulate model outputs as early as 2019
[

. In the past four years, adversarial attacks and instruction tuning together accounted for more than 64% of AI security papers.
However, the delay addressing prompt injection was much shorter, relative to model stealing
[
68

and model extraction.
Further, the research was less directly analogous to the AI vulnerabilities that were added to disclosure policies.
This suggests vendors may have become more responsive regarding clarifying whether AI vulnerabilities are in scope.

Regardless of when the first data point occurred, all three datasets have experienced hockey rapid growth in the last few years.
However, this growth is sharpest for disclosure policies.
The AI incidents and academic articles in 2023 and 2024 account for, respectively, 45% and 46% of the total datsets.
The equivalent proportion for AI-mentions in disclosure policies is 70%, suggesting that vendors were slower to clarify.

6.2
Types of Vulnerabilities

Across the 319 incidents impacting the vendors in our sample (2011–2024), Figure
5
shows that discrimination and toxicity (26%) and AI system safety, failures, and limitations (25%) together account for just over half of all incidents.
Misinformation accounts for 15%, of which the vast majority (94%) involves false or misleading information.

By contrast, AI disclosure policies focus on technical categories such as system integrity, authentication/authorization, model extraction, data leakage, and prompt injection (see Table

AI system security vulnerabilities and attack
and

Similarly, data leakage or information disclosure vulnerabilities accepted in policies directly correspond to
compromise of privacy
incidents, which account for 11% of the dataset.

Although incident counts are not company-specific, the distribution of mentions highlights how risks are concentrated around particular firms. OpenAI accounts for roughly one-fifth of all company references, and it dominates discussions of misinformation (26%). Notably, OpenAI considers such issues as “
strictly out of scope
,” since “
they are not standalone vulnerabilities…
” and “
Solving these issues usually requires significant research and a broader approach.
”
Meanwhile, Google appears most often in discrimination cases (23%), Meta is the leading actor in safety-related mentions (25%), while Twitter/X dominates the misuse category (28%).

There is potentially some misalignment. Firms like Amazon, Meta and Microsoft dedicate significant attention to model theft and adversarial examples, yet these subdomains account for less than 10% of publicly reported incidents.
Meanwhile, public reporting on AI incidents is dominated by downstream harms such as
toxic content exposure
and
false or misleading information

Only a minority of companies explicitly include content safety or harmful output as reportable vulnerabilities. This shows that the most common incidents are rarely treated as direct disclosure categories, an issue we return to in Section

Much like vendor disclosure policies, academic research has focused primarily on model-level vulnerabilities such as adversarial examples, poisoning, and model extraction. These categories together account for over half of the AI security and safety paper dataset. Such research-driven taxonomies embody an upstream perspective: they emphasize the fundamental weaknesses like fragile architectures, insecure training pipelines, and exploitable decision boundaries, which make AI systems vulnerable in the first place.

7
Discussion

We discuss the implications for academic debates around what constitutes AI security, recommendations regarding what industry should do, and the limitations of our study.

7.1
The Nature of AI Vulnerabilities

The disclosures practices vary across vendors, which belies different perspectives on AI security.

AI Vulnerabilities are just Software Vulnerabilities?

When vendors simply add AI products to a list of products that are in-scope for rewards, they implicitly assume the existing policy covers AI vulnerabilities providing they meet the existing criteria for severity.
This makes sense given firms aim to maintain security properties.
If a vulnerability leaks sensitive user data, the vendor should pay to fix it regardless of whether the bug is a SQL or prompt injection.

The ‘AI security is just security’ view is supported by Table
3
, which shows that AI vulnerabilities are most likely to be in-scope when described in terms of which security policies are violated (e.g. system integrity, data access, or authorization).
By contrast, AI vulnerabilities are more likely to be excluded when defined in terms of buzzwords (e.g.  jail break) or downstream consequences outside traditional security models (e.g. hallucination or harmful output).
In this interpretation, the new aspect of AI is the increased expectations regarding harm prevention.
For comparison, spell-checkers improve spelling and grammar in abusive and factually incorrect content, yet this could not be submitted as a bug.

This is seemingly the majoritarian view, given the vast majority of vendors do not have specific AI disclosure policies.
However, this interpretation is too generous.
It seems more likely that these vendors are yet to consider updating policies to address AI Security.
After all, vendors who actively support AI disclosures are also the vendors who process the most vulnerabilities like Microsoft and Google or BBP platforms like HackerOne and BugCrowd.
The collective experience of this group motivates considering an alternative view, which the industry may or may not be in the process of adopting.

AI Vulnerabilities are Special?

Evidence for the uniqueness of AI security can be seen in policies that direct submissions to an alternative submission channel, often an email address like “
ai-safety@vendor.com
.”
Future work should study which department receives these submissions.
A provocative simplification might be that software engineers receive traditional bug reports, whereas data scientists receive AI bugs.

This raises the question of how a data scientist can “patch” an AI vulnerability by, for example, re-training the model. OpenAI put forward this argument:
“
Model security issues are not well suited for bug bounty programs because they are not standalone vulnerabilities that can be fixed directly.
”
Google’s Abuse VRP make a similar argument with regards to abusive content: “
abuse risks are often inherent to product
”.

This points to an entirely new conception of harm prevention. The traditional model of patch management was widely criticized, with Ross Anderson characterizing vendors strategy of dumping risk on customers with the quote: “ ship it Tuesday and get it right by version three”
[
5
]
.
The AI vendors appear to be shipping products that they acknowledge can never be fixed, or at least not directly. This is a worrying development.

A more optimistic perspective is that safe guards will improve with time and attention, a view that motivated Anthropic to expand their bug bounty to exactly this.
This approach sees academic and industry research focus on improving defences that compensate for the fundamental insecurity of AI models.
However, this sounds worrying familiar to what happened with traditional security.

The AI industry may follow the software industry in not building products that are secure by design, and instead relying on bolt-on security products.
The parallels are uncanny in that foundational research finds modern computers
[
65
,
5
,
55
]
, neural networks
[
28
,
67
]
and LLMs
[
76
,
12
]
are too complex to be made 100% secure.
However, both industry and academia benefits from the constant cycle of proposing defenses, finding attacks that break defenses, and repeating the cycle
[
32
]
.
This brings us back to the idea that AI security is not new.

7.2
Recommendations

Broaden Disclosure Policies.

AI firms with no disclosure channel (36%) should at least publish an email contact for bug reports.
Ideally, these firms would also add a safe harbor to ease the concerns of researchers who often face hostility
[
60
]
.
Finally, there are likely firms running VDPs who should be financially rewarding researchers for their time.
Even though these recommendations apply to non-AI vendors, they are low hanging fruit for AI firms.

Communicate Best Practices.

Vendors are struggling to balance encouraging AI submissions against the volume of reports that have no security implications.
This is clearest when vendors declare jailbreaking and prompt injection as out of scope unless, for example, the vulnerability leads to RCE.
While this is understandable, the current fragmented approach is highly confusing for researchers.
The industry should share best practices and standardize wherever possible in order to set industry-wide norms that researchers can follow to predict which bugs yield pay-out.

Monitor Safety Risks.

BBPs focus on upstream technical issues without recognizing safety issues as in-scope, instead setting up dedicated emails for reporting.
This allows vendors to track issues like abuse and hallucinations without committing to technical prevention, which is impossible given the pervailing approach
[
8
]
.
Best practice involves a response protocol that stops individual users and also chats/features when the abuse becomes intolerable.
OpenAI’s malicious use reports are a step in the right direction
6
6

6

https://openai.com/global-affairs/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-june-2025/
, but data sharing should allow external researchers to empirically study the harm and how to calibrate the threshold.

Future Work.
Future work should revisit our RQs once adoption among AI vendors increases in the long tail. Researchers should conduct work with bug hunters to understand their experiences and challenges with current AI vulnerability disclosure practices. Additionally, comparative studies could examine how different disclosure policy designs affect the real-world reporting and remediation of AI vulnerabilities.

7.3
Limitations

Acknowledging that no study is free of bias, we present the main biases that affect our results.

Focus on Affirmative Discussion.

Our analysis focused on firms that explicitly mention AI-related vulnerabilities.
Studying affirmative policies provides insight into strategies that actively address vulnerabilities in AI systems.
However, this does not mean the other firms do not accept AI vulnerabilities, as they may be accepted under generic terms and conditions not written with AI in mind.

Sampling AI Firms.

Our definition of an AI firm followed Gartner’s product classifications, which
ensured that our results apply to the most significant AI technologies like foundation models, ML platforms, and Gen-AI apps.
However, this definition ignores common ways AI is deployed like offering a web chatbot, or incorporating simple automation features into existing software products. As a result, our analysis omits the ‘long tail’ of AI firms, predominantly smaller firms using off-the-shelf AI components in less sophisticated applications.
Preliminary analysis suggests these kinds of firms have not adopted affirmative AI vulnerability disclosure yet.
Tracking this is an important area for future work.

Public AI Incidents.

The AI Incident Database
[
45
]
has a bias toward incidents that are more visible or newsworthy, as it relies heavily on media reports and public submissions.
This may, for example, over-represent misinformation incidents as they aim to generate attention, which facilitates detection, and the political salience likely drives news coverage.
By contrast, technical security vulnerabilities may not receive public reporting, either because they are quietly fixed or because the attacker remains undetected.

Technical Literature.

It is simply infeasible to conduct a comprehensive literature review into “vulnerabilities” in AI systems.
Sampling from four security conferences biases our sample towards technical security issues, which aligns with the kind of issues that bug bounty programs have covered historically
[
73
]
.
A different approach could have focused on human-centered research into how AI enables discrimination
[
11
]
, perceptions of unfairness
[
10
]
, psychological harm
[
17
]
, and other emerging research topics.

8
Conclusion

Our analysis of 264 AI vendors shows that only 64% provide a disclosure channel, and just 18% explicitly acknowledge AI-specific vulnerabilities, with practices differing substantially across companies. Model providers are the most likely to clearly articulate their stance on AI security, whereas many AI application vendors rely on generic policies that omit such considerations.

AI vulnerabilities were priced higher than traditional vulnerabilities in nine BBPs, lower in four, and no distinction was made in the rest.
Eligibility varied across model vulnerabilities: model extraction (90%), adversarial example (80%) prompt injection (71%), and jailbreaking (27%).
Output issues were least likely to be in scope: harmful output (45%) and hallucination (14%).

We further find that vendors have been slow to revise disclosure policies in response to emerging AI risks, especially when compared with the pace of academic research and real-world incidents. This lag persists even in cases involving intellectual property. Moreover, disclosure policies and academic work tend to emphasize upstream technical flaws in isolation, whereas the majority of public AI incidents center on downstream content safety problems.

A.2
Meta-Taxonomy

Literature Review.
Our goal is to collect papers on taxonomies and reviews concerning AI attacks and vulnerabilities, and to construct a meta-taxonomy using a deductive method. First, we specified the time range, data sources, and keywords. We limited our selection to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference papers. We conducted searches in three academic databases (IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, ScienceDirect) with keywords related to the taxonomy (such as survey, SoK, taxonomy,
etc.
), AI (machine learning, large language models, generative AI,
etc.
), and security & safety (attack, vulnerability, privacy,
etc.
). We focused on literature published after 2020. We identified a total of 458 articles and manually reviewed titles and abstracts for relevance to our theme, resulting in 146 candidate papers during the initial screening phase.

For candidate papers, we established inclusion criteria. We selected only full-length papers that focus on offensive security rather than defensive measures, and the paper should not solely focus on sub-domains (such as only on backdoor attacks) and must provide a high-level classification. The mere mention in the introduction or related work sections was insufficient for inclusion.
One of the authors read the full paper and made a decision about its relevance to our study.
After analyzing 11 papers and checking 2 more, no new items were added to the meta-taxonomy.

Meta-taxonomy Verification.
To ensure the comprehensiveness of our meta-taxonomy, we utilized a dataset of AI security papers published at the four security conferences between 2020–2024 for verification (IEEE S&P, USENIX Sec, NDSS and CCS). Through manual review of 359 papers, we excluded studies focused on AI-based detection methods, AI-driven vulnerability discovery, and usability-related topics. This resulted in a set of 260 papers. We then supplemented this set with any emerging or previously unobserved vulnerability types.
Finally, as shown in Table
4
, we developed a meta-taxonomy comprising six categories of AI vulnerabilities.

To map the vulnerability themes found in vendor disclosure policies with the MIT AI risk categories, we adopted a semantic and functional mapping strategy. The mapping process followed: sub-themes were matched to risk categories based on semantic similarity between the policy descriptions and the taxonomy definitions. In cases of ambiguity, we privileged mappings that reflected the most direct and demonstrable threat surface, while allowed multiple mappings.
