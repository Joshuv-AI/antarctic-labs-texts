Topical local anesthetics for pain control at split-thickness skin graft donor sites in burn patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Wenxia Jin, Lu Zhang, Fengying Dong, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSplit-thickness skin grafting (STSG) is commonly used in the treatment of burn injuries and extensive skin defects. However, significant postoperative pain frequently occurs at donor sites and may impair patient recovery and postoperative rehabilitation. Topical analgesic interventions have been proposed as an effective strategy for localized pain management while minimizing systemic adverse effects.ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of topical analgesic interventions for pain control at donor sites following STSG in burn patients.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to January 2026. Randomized controlled trials comparing topical analgesic interventions with placebo or standard care for STSG donor-site pain were included. The primary outcome was postoperative pain score. Secondary outcomes included incidence of rescue analgesia, time to first rescue analgesia, anesthetic requirement, and adverse events. Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.3.ResultsTen randomized controlled trials were included in the analysis. For the primary outcome, no statistically significant difference was observed in postoperative pain scores between topical analgesic interventions and control treatment (MD = − 1.87, 95% CI: −5.05 to 1.31, P = 0.25). For secondary outcomes, topical analgesic interventions significantly reduced the incidence of rescue analgesia (OR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.01–0.58, P = 0.02). Time to first rescue analgesia and anesthetic requirement were reported by single studies and should therefore be interpreted cautiously; these outcomes suggested prolonged time to first rescue analgesia (MD = 8.56, 95% CI: 7.67–9.45, P < 0.00001) and reduced anesthetic requirements (MD = − 20.61, 95% CI: −31.91 to − 9.31, P = 0.0004). No statistically significant differences were found in nausea (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.36–3.30, P = 0.88) or vomiting (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.16–1.56, P = 0.23).ConclusionTopical analgesic interventions did not significantly reduce postoperative pain scores, which was the primary outcome of this meta-analysis. However, they may reduce the need for rescue analgesia without increasing nausea or vomiting. Because some secondary outcomes were based on single studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm the clinical benefit of topical analgesia for STSG donor-site pain management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03959-w.Keywords: Burn injury, Split-thickness skin graft, Donor-site pain, Topical analgesics, Postoperative pain, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Burn injuries remain a significant global health concern and frequently require surgical treatment to restore skin coverage and function [1, 2]. Split-thickness skin grafting (STSG) is commonly performed in the management of deep burns and extensive soft-tissue defects [3]. Although this procedure is effective in promoting wound closure, the donor site created during graft harvesting is often associated with considerable postoperative pain [4, 5].

Pain at the donor site is frequently reported by patients and may exceed the discomfort experienced at the graft recipient site [4]. Such pain can delay mobilization, interfere with postoperative care, and affect overall recovery. Effective pain control at the donor site is therefore an important component of burn management [6].

Systemic analgesics, particularly opioids, are widely used to control postoperative pain [7, 8]. However, opioid therapy may lead to adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, respiratory depression, and potential dependence [9, 10]. These limitations have prompted increasing interest in alternative strategies that provide effective local analgesia while minimizing systemic exposure.

Topical local anesthetics, including lidocaine [11, 12], bupivacaine [13, 14], and eutectic mixtures of local anesthetics (EMLA) [15], have been investigated as potential approaches for donor-site pain control. These agents inhibit nociceptive transmission by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels in peripheral nerve fibers [16]. Although several randomized trials have evaluated their effectiveness in STSG donor sites, the reported results remain inconsistent. Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of topical local anesthetics for pain control at donor sites following STSG in burn patients through a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17]. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to January 2026. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to burn injuries, split-thickness skin grafting, donor sites, and topical anesthetics. The main search terms included “burn,” “donor site,” “split-thickness skin graft,” “skin transplantation,” “topical anesthetic,” “lidocaine,” “bupivacaine,” and “EMLA.” These terms were combined using Boolean operators to ensure a comprehensive search. In addition, the reference lists of relevant studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible publications (Table S1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) the study population consisted of burn patients undergoing split-thickness skin grafting; (2) the intervention involved topical local anesthetics applied to donor-site wounds. Although different topical analgesic agents were included, these interventions were pooled because they shared the common objective of providing localized analgesia at STSG donor sites; (3) the control group received placebo treatment or conventional wound care; (4) the study design was a randomized controlled trial; and (5) at least one relevant clinical outcome was reported. Studies were excluded if they were review articles, conference abstracts, case reports, or observational studies. In addition, duplicate publications and studies without sufficient data for extraction were excluded from the analysis.

Data extraction

Two investigators independently performed data extraction using a standardized data collection form. The following information was extracted from each eligible study: first author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size, patient characteristics, intervention type, and outcome indicators. The primary outcome of interest was postoperative donor-site pain score. Pain outcomes were extracted as reported in the original studies, including visual analog scale (VAS) and numerical rating scale (NRS) measurements when available. Secondary outcomes included time to first rescue analgesia, incidence of rescue analgesia, anesthetic requirement, and adverse events such as nausea and vomiting. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. This tool assesses potential bias across several domains, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Each domain was categorized as low risk, high risk, or unclear risk of bias. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.3. Continuous outcomes were summarized using mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), whereas dichotomous outcomes were pooled using odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI. Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I² statistic when at least two studies were available for pooled analysis. When I² values were greater than 50%, a random-effects model was applied; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. Subgroup analyses were performed for key outcomes, including time to first rescue analgesia, incidence of rescue analgesia, anesthetic requirement, postoperative pain scores, nausea, and vomiting. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots when sufficient studies were available. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,524 studies were initially identified through database searches. After removal of 352 duplicate records, 1,172 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Among these, 907 studies were excluded due to irrelevance. The full texts of 265 articles were subsequently assessed for eligibility. Six studies lacked accessible full texts and 249 studies did not meet the inclusion criteria. Finally, 10 randomized controlled trials were included in the meta-analysis [18–27](Fig. 1; Table 1).

Study characteristics

The ten included randomized controlled trials were published between 1999 and 2025 and were conducted in several countries including England, Israel, India, Pakistan, Egypt, Sweden, and Thailand. Sample sizes ranged from 6 to 75 participants. The topical analgesic interventions included lidocaine, bupivacaine, and anesthetic-impregnated dressings.

Risk of bias

The risk-of-bias assessment indicated that most studies reported appropriate randomization methods, while several studies reported adequate blinding procedures. However, some studies provided insufficient information regarding allocation concealment and blinding of outcome assessment, resulting in unclear risk in several domains. In addition, the relatively small sample sizes of some included trials may reduce the certainty of the evidence and affect the reliability of pooled estimates. Overall, the methodological quality of the included trials was considered moderate (Fig. 2A-B).

Pain score

Three studies reported postoperative pain scores involving 70 patients in the experimental group and 63 patients in the control group. Significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 83%, P = 0.003). The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain scores between the two groups (MD = − 1.87, 95% CI: −5.05 to 1.31, P = 0.25) (Fig. 3). Subgroup analyses of pain scores are provided in Table S3 and Figure S1A.

Time to first rescue analgesia

One study reported the time to first rescue analgesia. Since this outcome was derived from a single study, between-study heterogeneity was not applicable. The pooled analysis showed that topical analgesic interventions significantly prolonged the time to first rescue analgesia compared with the control group (MD = 8.56, 95% CI: 7.67–9.45, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 4A). Subgroup analyses for this outcome are presented in Table S3 and visually depicted in Figure S2A.

Incidence of rescue analgesia

Five studies reported the incidence of rescue analgesia, including 210 patients in the experimental group and 214 patients in the control group. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 91%, P < 0.00001), and a random-effects model was applied. The pooled results indicated that topical analgesic interventions significantly reduced the incidence of rescue analgesia (OR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.01–0.58, P = 0.02) (Fig. 4B). Detailed subgroup analyses are summarized in Table S3 and represented in Figure S2B.

Anesthetic requirement

One study reported postoperative anesthetic requirements at 6 h, 8 h, and 24 h. Since this outcome was derived from a single study with repeated time-point measurements, between-study heterogeneity was not applicable. The pooled results demonstrated that topical anesthetics significantly reduced anesthetic requirements compared with the control group (MD = − 20.61, 95% CI: −31.91 to − 9.31, P = 0.0004) (Fig. 4C). Subgroup analyses for anesthetic requirement are detailed in Table S3 and illustrated in Figure S2C.

Adverse events

Safety outcomes included nausea and vomiting.

For nausea, heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 57%, P = 0.10). The pooled analysis indicated no statistically significant difference between the two groups (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.36–3.30, P = 0.88) (Fig. 5A). Subgroup analysis for nausea is presented in Figure S3A.

For vomiting, heterogeneity was low (I² = 0%, P = 0.54). The pooled results also showed no statistically significant difference between groups (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.16–1.56, P = 0.23) (Fig. 5B). Subgroup analysis for vomiting is presented in Figure S3B.

Publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots for the included outcomes. Visual inspection of the funnel plots showed an approximately symmetrical distribution, suggesting no obvious publication bias among the included studies (Fig. 6A-F). In addition, the certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to very low according to the GRADE assessment (Table S2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy and safety of topical analgesic interventions for donor-site pain management following split-thickness skin grafting in burn patients. Three main findings were identified. First, topical analgesic interventions significantly reduced the incidence of rescue analgesia. Second, no significant difference was observed in postoperative pain scores between the intervention and control groups. Third, topical analgesic use was not associated with an increased incidence of adverse events such as nausea and vomiting. These findings suggest that topical analgesic strategies may provide beneficial adjunctive effects, particularly in reducing the reliance on systemic analgesics, without necessarily altering overall pain scores. However, some key outcomes, including time to first rescue analgesia and anesthetic requirements, were reported in only a single study, and caution is warranted when interpreting these results.

The findings of the present study are consistent with previous clinical investigations suggesting that localized analgesic approaches may improve postoperative pain management at STSG donor sites [14, 28]. Donor-site wounds often contain exposed nerve endings, which can generate substantial nociceptive signals during the early postoperative period. Topical analgesic agents may exert their effects by directly inhibiting nociceptive transmission in peripheral nerve fibers [29]. Local anesthetics such as lidocaine and bupivacaine block voltage-gated sodium channels in sensory neurons, thereby preventing the initiation and propagation of pain signals [30]. In addition, topical opioid preparations may produce analgesic effects through activation of peripheral opioid receptors in inflamed tissues [31, 32]. These mechanisms may explain why topical analgesic interventions can reduce postoperative analgesic requirements in patients undergoing STSG.

Interestingly, although the use of topical analgesic interventions reduced the incidence of rescue analgesia, no significant difference was observed in pooled pain scores [33–35]. This discrepancy suggests that pain-score outcomes should be interpreted with caution. Pain assessment methods varied across the included studies, with most studies using VAS measurements, whereas some studies used NRS-based evaluations. To improve comparability across studies, continuous pain outcomes were pooled using mean differences according to the reported scales and assessment methods. Nevertheless, variations in pain assessment tools, timing of postoperative evaluation, and patient perception of pain may have contributed to the substantial heterogeneity observed in pain-score analyses. Potential explanations include the subjective nature of pain assessment, variation in assessment time points among included studies, and the mitigating effect of rescue medication on reported pain scores. In addition, pain score was the primary subjective outcome included in this meta-analysis, whereas outcomes such as rescue analgesia requirement may also have been influenced by physician prescribing preferences and institutional analgesic protocols. Variability in opioid administration practices across centers and providers may therefore have contributed to discrepancies between studies and influenced pooled estimates. Nevertheless, the reduction in rescue analgesia is clinically relevant, as it indicates decreased reliance on systemic opioids and potential reduction in opioid-related adverse events, even when overall pain scores remain unchanged.

From a clinical perspective, the reduction in rescue analgesia requirement observed in this meta-analysis may be clinically relevant for postoperative pain management in burn surgery. Reducing the need for systemic analgesics, particularly opioids, may decrease the risk of opioid-related adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, and respiratory depression. Therefore, topical analgesic strategies may serve as an adjunctive component of multimodal analgesia protocols, with the primary benefit being reduced opioid consumption rather than a direct reduction in pain scores. Subgroup analyses suggested that administration route, dose, and assessment time point may influence the efficacy of topical analgesic interventions, which may partly explain the heterogeneity observed across outcomes. In addition, differences in topical analgesic formulations, postoperative analgesic regimens, wound dressing methods, and timing of outcome assessment among individual studies may also represent important sources of clinical heterogeneity. Some included studies had relatively small sample sizes or insufficient reporting of allocation concealment and blinding procedures, which may have increased the risk of bias and affected the stability of pooled estimates. However, these conclusions for some outcomes are based on limited data and should be interpreted cautiously.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the number of included randomized controlled trials was relatively small, and some outcomes were reported by only a limited number of studies. Second, significant heterogeneity was observed in several analyses, likely reflecting differences in intervention type, dosage, application methods, and patient populations. In addition, different classes of topical analgesic interventions, including lidocaine, bupivacaine, opioid-based preparations, and anesthetic-impregnated dressings, were pooled together in the present analysis. Although these agents share the common goal of localized analgesia, differences in pharmacological mechanisms and delivery methods may have contributed to clinical heterogeneity and may limit the interpretability of pooled estimates. Third, although funnel plot inspection suggested no obvious publication bias, the limited number of included studies may reduce the robustness of this assessment. Fourth, variations in follow-up duration and outcome measurement across studies may have influenced the pooled results. Future large-scale randomized controlled trials with standardized analgesic protocols, dosing regimens, and pain assessment methods are warranted to further clarify the clinical effectiveness of topical analgesic interventions for donor-site pain management.

Conclusion

Topical analgesic interventions applied to split-thickness skin graft donor sites may reduce the need for rescue analgesia, although a significant reduction in overall pain scores was not observed. These findings suggest that topical analgesia can serve as a useful adjunctive strategy, primarily by decreasing systemic analgesic requirements and associated adverse effects, while recognizing that some key outcomes are supported by only limited data. Further large-scale randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm these findings and determine optimal analgesic protocols.
