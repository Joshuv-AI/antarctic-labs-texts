Supporting Nursing Efficiency by Understanding Workload: A Critical Need
Authors: Victoria L Tiase, Kensaku Kawamoto, Katherine A Sward
Published: 2023

In this era of intensifying nursing shortages, it has become crucial for health care organizations to value and encourage nursing efficiencies. Limited resources and suboptimal documentation systems contribute to workflow challenges, burden, and burnout. When nurses work efficiently, they are more able to maintain high standards of care, make effective use of resources, complete tasks in a timely manner, and avoid unnecessary workflow steps. This can lead to reduced stress, increased job satisfaction, and improved morale, contributing to staff retention and better team dynamics. Technology can both alleviate and contribute to nurse efficiencies, depending on how it is designed, implemented, and used. By ensuring standardization (to minimize redundant documentation), optimizing user interactions, and involving nurses in the technology adoption process, health care organizations can harness the potential of technology to optimize patient care. However, it is a challenge to simply measure gains in efficiency.

In this issue, Zoghby et al1 describe how they sought to improve documentation efficiency for Mayo Clinic home dialysis program. In the baseline process, nurses tracked referrals and enrollment using standalone documents. The team used an agile approach, incorporating feedback from clinical stakeholders to develop 2 home dialysis applications within the electronic health record (EHR). The study compared the time spent on home dialysis referral and enrollment documentation before and after implementation of the applications using the Nursing Efficiency Assessment Tool. The results found savings of 25 and 20.4 minutes per 8-hour shift for the 2 dialysis nurses, respectively. The authors concluded that the new applications increased documentation efficiency.

Workload (amount of work to be performed) is a component of efficiency (which reflects the effort to do the work). However, measuring nursing workload is an unsolved challenge.2 No single method can fully capture the complexity and subjective experience of nursing care. For example, the Nursing Efficiency Assessment Tool measures time spent on predefined documentation tasks but does not measure all EHR interactions. Combining multiple approaches, such as direct observation, surveys, and workload measurement tools, can provide a more comprehensive understanding of nursing workflows, workload distribution, and resource allocation.

EHR systems store data reflecting aspects of clinical workload, such as the number of patients seen, the frequency of documentation, and the use of specific resources. Analyzing these data can provide insights into care processes that go beyond just the amount of time spent entering data into the system. EHR audit logs—time-stamped records of activities performed in the EHR—afford the opportunity to measure workload in novel and unobtrusive ways. Studies leveraging audit logs have examined interruptions, task switching, cognitive load, and interface navigation as contributors to workload. For example, Lou et al3 described 7 metrics for physician EHR workload that were calculated using EHR audit logs. Similarly, Adler-Milstein et al4 found that 2 measures derived from log files were associated with primary care clinician burnout. Although the roles, responsibilities, and workflow of nurses can be very different from physicians, there are to date no known studies that examine audit logs from a nursing perspective. Based on emerging methods, we offer the following recommendations to build a foundation for audit log-based approaches to measure nursing workload, in contrast to the nursing efficiency for managing home dialysis evaluated in the study by Zoghby et al.1

Create Standardized Measures

To compare across studies and settings, a well-defined set of EHR-based nursing activities must be identified (eg, documentation frequency, time spent documenting, task switching, and task type, such as reading or entering data). Once activities are identified, consistent measures for evaluating the activity must be created. A 2020 American Nursing Informatics Association (ANIA) position paper5 articulated a conceptual framework to understand and address challenges with EHR documentation, identifying 6 domains that impact the EHR experience: cognitive, technical, temporal, emotional, social, and financial. It offers a foundation for associating clinical work activities with correlates of workload. For example, cognitive burden could be estimated based on the number of clinical activities, complexity of the activity, task order, and the number of interruptions during the task.

Optimize User Interactions

A phenotype is a set (or pattern) of observable characteristics resulting from the interaction of inherent characteristics and the environment.6 A nursing workflow phenotype would be a pattern of nursing work behaviors. These behaviors are often reflected in EHR audit logs, which typically document who is logged in to the EHR (including clinician type, eg, nurse); tasks they perform (eg, read and add data) and when; and what part of the record they were in, which can reflect tasks, such as medication administration, communication, or documentation. Audit logs also provide context of the activity, such as the specific patient, encounter, and setting (eg, intensive care unit and outpatient clinic). Through phenotyping, we can infer individual, behavioral, or system characteristics associated with patterns of nursing behavior, thereby supporting generalization that could be used to test interventions broadly. These phenotypes should be explored for key nursing activities, particularly for activities, such as documentation that play a key role in efficiency and burnout. Underscoring the importance of understanding and improving nursing documentation workflows, groups, such as the National Academy of Medicine, the American Medical Informatics Association, and the Office of the Surgeon General, have identified improving documentation as a key focus for addressing clinician stress associated with EHR use.7, 8, 9, 10

Validate With Nurses

Nursing workload is a complex phenomenon. Nursing practice patterns derived from the EHR audit log data should be aligned with and compared with those identified through direct observation of nursing practice. Toward that end, validating interactions between team members and measures such as errors or safety events should be examined alongside EHR-based metrics to provide a systems-oriented view of nursing efficiencies.

Zoghby et al1 address an aspect of nursing efficiency by identifying how integrating documentation into the EHR can support measurable improvements in time savings for specific tasks. It is important to measure the effects of EHR documentation changes on nursing workflow, as illustrated in this study. In addition, care should be taken to ensure that time savings in 1 area do not simply shift documentation to unmeasured aspects of the workflow. Combining time saved with other metrics could lead to a fuller understanding of nursing documentation workload.
