Assessment of the Prevalence and Determinants of Undernutrition Among Adult and Elderly Patients Hospitalized at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital, West Cameroon
Authors: Franklin Stéphano FOTSING, Ghislain MAFFO TAZOHO, Ruth YEMDJE, Joseph Raphael MOYO TACHOUM, Donald Séverin DANGANG BOSSI, Inocent GOUADO, François ZAMBOU NGOUFACK, Hilaire Macaire WOMENI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHospital undernutrition is a major problem affecting the health of adult and elderly patients. The aim of this study was to contribute to the nutritional care of adults and elderly people in hospitals by determining the prevalence of undernutrition and identifying its determinants.MethodsA descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted between April 1 and May 31, 2025, at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital. A sample of 110 hospitalized patients, comprising adults (aged 21–69 years) and elderly individuals (aged ≥ 70 years), was recruited from various departments of the hospital. Data collection was carried out using a questionnaire, and the diagnosis of undernutrition was established based on the recommendations of the GLIM (Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition). Data were analyzed with SPSS software, and the Chi‐square test was used to determine significant associations (p < 0.05).ResultsThe results reveal an overall prevalence of undernutrition of 68.18%, and also 79.4% of overweight or obese patients were undernourished according to their weight history. The medical factors significantly associated with undernutrition include the presence of comorbidities (p < 0.001, OR = 22.37, CI [1.14–437.91]), length of hospital stay (p = 0.014, OR = 4.77, CI [1.19–19.07]), polypharmacy (p < 0.001, OR = 2.48, CI [2.03–7.25]), loss of appetite (p < 0.001, OR = 11, CI [4.17–28.87]), chewing or swallowing disorders (p < 0.001, OR = 7.0, CI [1.61–30.4]), and psychological disorders (p < 0.001, OR = 11.9, CI [4.65–30.62]). Regarding eating habits, meal frequency (p < 0.001, OR = 51.76, CI [4.2–79.1]), the ability to finish one's plate (p < 0.001, OR = 10.13, CI [3.28–11.82]), adherence to a restrictive diet (p = 0.034, OR = 0.55, CI [0.07–0.98]), low consumption of dairy products and animal proteins (p < 0.001, OR = 2.4, CI [0.34–10.32]), as well as adherence to food preference (p < 0.001, OR = 2.23, CI [0.11–6.21]) were strongly correlated with the patients' nutritional status.ConclusionThe prevalence of undernutrition at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital is alarming and is explained by a complex interaction between severe medical determinants, socio‐economic vulnerabilities, and inadequate hospital dietary practices.

Introduction

Undernutrition, defined as a prolonged imbalance between nutritional intake (energy, protein) and physiological needs, leads to impairment of physical and metabolic functions, making it a major problem in hospital settings [1]. The diagnosis, according to Cruz‐Jentoft [2], is based on the GLIM (Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition) criteria, which combine phenotypic criteria (weight loss, low body mass index, reduced muscle mass) and etiological criteria (insufficient food intake, inflammation, with a particular interaction between undernutrition and sarcopenia in the elderly). The causes of hospital undernutrition are multifactorial because on the clinical level, there are acute or chronic diseases, side effects of drug treatments, digestive disorders, severe or persistent pain, impairment of chewing or swallowing function. On the socio‐economic level, there are food insecurity, low level of education, unemployment or retirement, inadequate health coverage; and finally on the structural level, there are limited access to clinical nutrition services, insufficient hospital resources such as staff or adapted infrastructures [3, 4]. Among the most frequently identified risk factors are advanced age, the presence of comorbidities such as cardiovascular diseases or diabetes, social isolation or living alone, cultural or traditional dietary habits influencing intake, severe pain, as well as changes related to hospitalization (e.g., vomiting, surgical interventions, fasting) [4, 5, 6].

The consequences of hospital undernutrition are multiple and affect several areas. They include an increase in morbidity, for example a higher risk of nosocomial infections, postoperative complications, increased mortality (the risk of death is often doubled or more in undernourished patients), prolonged length of hospital stay ( + a few days on average), and significant additional costs for health systems, particularly in sub‐Saharan Africa; added to these are functional impacts, such as loss of autonomy in the elderly [1, 7]. Globally, the prevalence of undernutrition among hospitalized patients is generally between 20% and 25%, with strong variations according to regions and criteria used. In sub‐Saharan Africa, this prevalence is often higher, influenced by food insecurity, the high frequency of infectious diseases, and the weakness of health systems [8, 9]. In some African countries, studies have illustrated these figures. A study conducted in Kenya, Ghana, and South Africa reported that at admission, between 60% and 66% of adult patients are at risk of malnutrition, and during hospitalization a significant proportion lose weight substantially or experience deterioration in their nutritional status [9]. In Cameroon, a study at the Douala General Hospital found a prevalence of malnutrition of 19.3% among patients admitted to internal medicine departments [10]. Furthermore, in the medical–surgical intensive care unit of the same hospital, the prevalence of undernutrition reached 82.7% among hospitalized patients, with high mortality in undernourished individuals [11]. However, despite these data, information remains fragmented. To date, no published study has specifically addressed the prevalence and precise determinants of undernutrition at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital. This gap is particularly critical since in this region, conditions are conducive to risks because food insecurity affects a significant proportion of households, and certain cultural practices limiting protein consumption remain deeply rooted.

The socio‐economic impact of hospital undernutrition is reflected in a vicious cycle where health expenditures increase, patients' productivity decreases, and caregivers' burden becomes heavier, especially in contexts where social safety nets are weak [12]. In Cameroon, this issue is exacerbated by shortcomings in the implementation of national clinical nutrition policies, particularly limited access to nutritional supplements and insufficient training of health personnel in screening and management of undernutrition cases [10]. Moreover, in the majority of previous studies, body mass index (BMI) is often used alone as the main indicator. Yet, there is little overlap with other parameters such as recent weight loss, clinical signs (loss of appetite, digestive disorders), or psychological factors, which may lead to underestimation or misclassification of undernutrition cases [13]. In addition, the assessment of the quality of dietary intake in term of food variety and frequency is rarely taken into account, which limits the scope of dietary recommendations. It is within this context that the research question posed in this study was to know what the prevalence of undernutrition is, as well as its socio‐economic‐demographic, clinical (medical), and dietary determinants, among adult and elderly patients hospitalized at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital. The objective of this study is therefore to contribute to improving nutritional care for adult and elderly hospital patients by determining the prevalence of undernutrition and identifying its determinants.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

The study population included all adult patients (21–69 years) and elderly patients (≥ 70 years) admitted and hospitalized at the Regional Hospital of Bafoussam. These patients had to have been assessed for undernutrition using standardized screening tools and had provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Study Area

The Bafoussam Regional Hospital (BRH) is located in the city of Bafoussam, capital of Cameroon's West Region. BRH covers a population catchment area estimated at more than 2 million inhabitants, including surrounding urban and rural areas. Founded in 1953, BRH has been progressively modernized to meet the growing needs of the region, marked by rapid urbanization and a mixed epidemiological transition (infectious and chronic diseases) [14]. BRH has a bed capacity of 300, distributed across key departments such as internal medicine, general surgery, pediatrics, gynecology‐obstetrics, and emergency care. Although it does not have a dedicated geriatrics unit, elderly patients are mainly treated in internal medicine and chronic disease units. In 2021, BRH recorded about 25,000 annual admissions, with an average occupancy rate of 85%. As the main referral center of the region, BRH receives complex cases requiring prolonged hospitalizations, which is a major risk factor for undernutrition. Its patient catchment area, including rural populations with low food security, makes it a strategic site for studying the determinants of undernutrition [15].

Study Design and Study Period

This was a cross‐sectional descriptive and analytical study whose objective was to contribute to the nutritional management of patients with or at risk of undernutrition, hospitalized at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital. The study was conducted over a 2‐month period from April 1 to May 31, 2025.

Sample Size

A non‐probabilistic sampling method was used. Thus, the sample size depended on the number of hospitalized patients encountered in certain departments of BRH. In total, 110 patients were included: 58 aged between 21 and 69 years and 52 aged over 70 years.

Data Collection Tools

Data were collected using a height rod and scales to measure height in centimeters and weight in kilograms, respectively; a scientific calculator to calculate BMI; and a patient interview guide (structured survey questionnaire) to gather information from patients. Patients' medical records were also consulted to assess changes in their weight over the previous months.

Study Implementation

The study was carried out at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital mainly in the departments of diabetology, cardiology, neurosurgery, surgery, nephrology, oncology, and ENT (Ear, Nose, and Throat). Data were collected using a questionnaire administered during an interview approximately 10–15 min, depending on the patient's level of understanding and/or curiosity. Before concluding the interview, verification of the completeness of the questionnaires and thanks to the participant were carried out.

Data Collection Procedure

The procedure used in this study was guided interviews based on the questionnaire and review of patients' medical records. The interview was conducted as a face‐to‐face conversation between patients and the investigator to collect precise data and gather individual opinions. The questionnaire was pretested with 10 target individuals of the study to ensure its clarity, and it was structured into four sections. The section on sociodemographic data (educational level; income level; marital status; number of caregivers in the hospital); the section on medical data (comorbidities; medication; current treatment; loss of appetite & its causes; psychological problems & their causes); the section on anthropometric parameters (weight, height, BMI); and the section on eating habits (type of diet, frequency of consumption of each food group, meal frequency, estimation of meal portions, source of meals).

The diagnosis of undernutrition and the assessment of current nutritional status were treated as two separate measures. GLIM (Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition) criteria (etiological and phenotypic) were used to diagnose undernutrition while BMI (Body Mass Index) was used to assess the actual nutritional status of participant. Regarding etiological criteria, the presence of reduced food intake was assessed indirectly through several questions while with regard to phenotypic criteria, the diagnosis was based primarily on the phenotypic criteria of unintentional weight loss. For this purpose, undernutrition was considered moderate when 17 < BMI < 18.5 if the patient was < 70 years old; between 20 < BMI < 22 kg/m² if the patient was ≥ 70 years old and when there was a weight loss ≥ 5% in 1 month or ≥ 10% in 6 months. It was considered severe when BMI ≤ 17 kg/m² if the patient was < 70 years old; BMI < 20 kg/m² if the patient was ≥ 70 years old and when there was a weight loss ≥ 10% in 1 month or ≥ 15% in 6 months [16].

To assess the current nutritional status of participants, the BMI of participant was calculated. For weight measurement, the scale was calibrated before each session. Participants were asked to remove heavy objects (watches, phones, jackets, shoes, etc.) and to stand upright with arms relaxed at the sides, looking straight ahead during weighing. For height measurement, a wooden stadiometer with a horizontal slider was used. It was placed on a flat surface against a wall. Participants, barefoot and without hats or headscarves, stood upright in the middle of the stadiometer, and the horizontal slider was brought into contact with the top of the head to obtain the measurement. Finally, the review of the patient's medical record to trace weight history over the last 1 or 6 months was carried out in order to draw a conclusion about the evolution of nutritional status.

Ethical Considerations

To conduct this study, ethical clearance No.572/28/05/2025/CE/CRERSH‐OU/VP was granted by the Regional Committee for Ethic in Human Health Research of the West Region (CRERSH‐West) following research authorization No.411/L/MINSANTE/SG/DRSPO/HRD/D granted by the management of the Bafoussam Regional Hospital. Participants were fully informed about the objective and purpose of the study and provided verbal or written consent. Confidentiality of the information collected from each participant was guaranteed by the anonymity of the questionnaire.

Statistical Data Analysis

Data were collected using a smartphone via the Kobocollect application where the questionnaire had been pre‐designed, then coded and analyzed using IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) software version 25. Qualitative variables were described as effective and/or frequencies. Microsoft Excel version 2019 was used to represent frequencies and associations in charts and tables. The Chi‐square test with a probability threshold of 5% was used to determine the dependency relationships between variables, and associations were considered significant at the 5% probability level. Multivariate analysis was employed to highlight the characteristics of the nutrition status of our target population and therefore an odd ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) and a p value ≤ 0.05 were considered as significant predictors of the occurrence of undernutrition.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of Participants

Table 1 depicts the sociodemographic and economic characteristics of the study population. The results show that patients were almost evenly divided into two age groups means that 58 of them (52.73%) were aged between 21 and 69, corresponding to the adult population, while 52 patients (47.27%) were aged 70 and over, representing the elderly population. In terms of gender, a slight majority of patients were female, with 60 women (54.54%) compared to 50 men (45.45%). In terms of educational level, more than half of the patients (52.73%) had completed secondary school, followed by 29.09% with higher education, while 18.18% had only completed primary school. In terms of marital status, the majority of patients were married (57.27%). Widowed individuals accounted for 30.91% of the sample, while divorced and single individuals were in the minority, representing 6.36% and 5.45% of cases, respectively. In terms of food support, most patients (38.18%) received assistance from two people to ensure they were fed. However, 36.36% were cared for by three or more people, while 25.45% had only one caregiver at their bedside. Finally, from an economic perspective, a large majority of patients (64.54%) lived on a monthly income of less than 150,000 CFA francs. Among them, 9.09% earned less than the minimum wage (44,000 CFA francs), and 27.27% reported incomes between 44,000 and 100,000 CFA francs. In contrast, only 26.36% of patients reported a monthly income above 150,000 CFA francs.

Prevalence of Undernutrition in the Study Population

Figure 1 shows the prevalence of undernutrition among hospitalized patients according to age groups. The results indicate that when combining the two age groups, the prevalence of moderate undernutrition was 38.18% and severe undernutrition 30%, resulting in an overall prevalence of 68.18% in the entire study population. When analyzing undernutrition according to age groups, it was observed that the group of adults aged 21 to 69 years had an overall prevalence of undernutrition of 70.7% (41 out of 58 patients), while the group of elderly patients aged 70 years and above showed a prevalence of 65.4% (34 out of 52 patients). It is noteworthy that the proportion of severe undernutrition is relatively similar in both groups: 14.54% in adults compared to 15.45% in elderly patients.

Prevalence of undernutrition among hospitalized patients according to age groups.

Table 2 indicates the prevalence of undernutrition in the study population according to BMI. The results show that 31.82% of patients (35 out of 110) were not considered undernourished according to body mass index (BMI) criteria. Furthermore, among patients classified as overweight or obese (n = 34), a large majority (79.4%) were actually in a state of undernutrition based on weight loss observed during the 6 months preceding this study. This group included 17 patients with moderate undernutrition (15.45%) and 10 with severe undernutrition (9.09%). Similarly, among patients with a “current normal BMI” (n = 47), more than half (57.4%) were also undernourished, of whom 20 moderately and 7 severely. Finally, among the 29 patients classified as undernourished according to BMI, 27 were indeed in a state of clinical undernutrition, including 5 moderately and 16 severely.

Prevalence of Undernutrition by Hospital Departments

Figure 2 shows the distribution of undernutrition according to hospital departments. It appears that in oncology, 100% of patients were undernourished, with 4 moderate cases and 7 severe cases. In nephrology, 90% of patients were undernourished. Sociodemographic Characteristics Associated With Nutritional Status of Patients

Table 3 explores the associations between certain socio‐economic and demographic characteristics and the state of undernutrition among the 110 patients in this study. The results highlight three factors showing a statistically significant association with undernutrition. Monthly income was the most strongly associated determinant (p = 0.004) as patients with incomes below the Minimum Interprofessional Guaranteed Wage (MIGW) were at greater risk of being in a state of undernutrition (OR = 5.50 and CI [2.02–49.5]); Educational attainment was also a key factor with (p = 0.011), and patients with a primary level of education (OR = 4.00 and CI [3.79–10.08]) were at greater risk of being in a state of undernutrition compared to other patients who had pursued higher education. Finally, sex shows a significant association (p = 0.049) as the risk of being undernourished appears higher among women (OR = 2.33 and CI [2.01–2.68]) compared to men.

Clinical Characteristics Associated With the Nutritional Status of Patients

Table 4 shows the relationship between undernutrition and medical factors. The results indicate that the factors most powerfully associated with undernutrition are psychological, behavioral, and physiopathological. Firstly, psychological disorders stand out as the strongest predictor, increasing the risk of undernutrition by almost 12 times (p < 0.001; OR = 11.9; CI [4.65–30.62]). Expectedly, loss of appetite also constituted a major risk factor for undernutrition (p < 0.001; OR = 11.0; CI [4.17–28.87]) as did the presence of chewing and/or swallowing difficulties with food (p < 0.001; OR = 7.0; CI [1.61–30.4]). Beyond these elements, clinical complexity and length of hospitalization also emerged as significant risk factors. The results also reveal a critical interaction among diabetic patients with 3 or more comorbidities, for whom the risk of undernutrition was very high (p = 0.001; OR = 22.37; CI [1.14–437.91]). It must be noted, however, that the very wide confidence interval (CI [1.14–437.91]), probably due to the small sample size of this subgroup (n = 12), indicates low precision in the estimate despite a clinically relevant association. The duration of stay (p = 0.014) showed a clear dose‐response relationship, as a short stay of 1 to 2 weeks appears protective with an OR = 0.21, while a prolonged hospitalization of 8 weeks or more increases the risk of undernutrition by nearly 5 times (OR = 4.77; CI [1.19–19.07]). Finally, polypharmacy (p < 0.001), defined as taking more than 5 medications per day, was associated with a 2.5 times higher risk of undernutrition (OR = 2.48).

Characteristics of Patients' Dietary Habits Associated With Their Nutritional Status

Table 5 shows the relationship between undernutrition and dietary habits in patients. It has been demonstrated significantly (p < 0.001) that factors such as the frequency of daily meals and the capacity of patients to finish the meal served were strongly associated with the state of undernutrition. In fact, consuming only one to two meals per day was linked to a massively elevated risk of undernutrition (p < 0.001; OR = 51.76; CI [4.2–79.1]). Similarly, patients who reported rarely finishing their plate had a tenfold higher probability of being in a state of undernutrition (p < 0.001; OR = 10.13; CI [3.28–11.82]). Beyond quantity, the qualitative analysis of food intake confirms that the quality of the diet plays a fundamental role. Intake of protein and dairy products shows very marked links with nutritional status. Daily consumption of meat products (meat, poultry, fish) acts as a powerful protective factor, reducing the risk of undernutrition by 91% (p < 0.001; OR = 0.09). Conversely, the absence of dairy product consumption constituted a very significant risk factor. This trend was corroborated by the fact that patients following a restrictive diet for therapeutic reasons presented an increased frequency of undernutrition (p = 0.034), with the type of diet also influencing their nutritional status (p = 0.041).

The results in this table also highlight a crucial behavioral dimension. Patients who reported “always” eating what they like were predominantly well‐nourished, while the inability to “never” or “rarely” consume preferred foods constituted a risk factor for undernutrition (p < 0.001). Other factors like hydration played a non‐negligible role, as a daily consumption of more than five glasses of water was positively associated with good nutritional status. In contrast, certain parameters, such as the consumption of starchy foods, legumes, sweets, fruits and vegetables, as well as the origin of the meals (family, hospital, external), did not show a statistically significant association with undernutrition (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The study conducted among 110 adult and elderly patients hospitalized at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital reveals an extremely high prevalence of undernutrition (68.18%), of which 38.18% was moderate and 30% was severe. These figures reflect an underestimated hospital nutritional crisis, but one comparable to that documented in other low‐ and middle‐income countries. This is the case in India, where studies have shown that the prevalence of undernutrition was 62.8% [17], in Brazil where it was 60% [7], and in Ethiopia where it was 55.6% [18]. This level largely exceeds the concerning thresholds established by European and international scientific societies. According to the guidelines of the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN), the prevalence of undernutrition is considered concerning once it exceeds 30% in medical and surgical wards, rises to 40% in geriatric and pulmonology wards, and to 50% in intensive care units [1]. In this study, the distribution of undernutrition cases by department reveals an alarming concentration in high‐morbidity units such as oncology (100%), nephrology (90%), neurosurgery (86.7%), and surgery (67.9%), suggesting priorities for targeted interventions. Undernutrition is often observed in cancer patients, due to the impact of treatments (chemotherapy, radiotherapy) which cause nausea, vomiting, mucositis, and taste disorders, as well as the disease itself (anorexia‐cachexia syndrome) [19]. In nephrology, undernutrition is frequent in patients with chronic kidney disease, especially those on dialysis, due to protein loss during sessions, chronic inflammation, anorexia, and dietary restrictions [20]. In neurosurgery, patients are often in a hypercatabolic state due to head trauma or brain injuries, which increases their energy and protein needs [21]. Finally, in surgery, surgical stress, prolonged preoperative fasting, and postoperative complications contribute to undernutrition [22]. These departments thus admit patients with pathologies and treatments that significantly increase the risk of undernutrition, which explains the high rates observed. The demographic structure of the sample, divided between adults (52.73%) and elderly (47.27%), reflects the progressive aging of the hospitalized population in Cameroon. Elderly individuals and adults are more often hospitalized because they more frequently suffer from chronic diseases (cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer) that require prolonged medical care and often repeated hospitalizations. Moreover, the aging process is accompanied by a reduction in physiological reserves, making elderly patients more vulnerable to infections and postoperative complications. These results corroborate those of Wysokiński [23], who, in their studies conducted in Poland, showed a prevalence of 49.2% in the elderly and 31.8% in adults. The prevalence of severe undernutrition was more pronounced in women (38.3%) than in men (20%), and these results are consistent with the findings of Leij‐Halfwerk [24] in the Netherlands, who reported a significantly higher risk in women, with a rate of 61.4% compared to 42.7% in men. This overrepresentation of women in undernutrition could be explained by economic precariousness, widowhood (30.91% in this study), increased dependency, and greater exposure to chronic diseases in elderly women [25]. This study highlighted the limitations of using BMI alone to diagnose undernutrition, since nearly 79.4% of overweight or obese patients were actually undernourished according to weight loss and intake reduction criteria, thus illustrating the concept of “masked undernutrition” [26, 27]. This justifies the systematic adoption of GLIM criteria, combining phenotypes and etiologies, or the NRS‐2002 score for rigorous nutritional assessment [1]. Undernutrition in the Bafoussam Regional Hospital is thought to be the result of a combination of sociodemographic, medical, and nutritional factors.

It is important to note that regarding sociodemographic factors, low educational level was significantly correlated with patients' nutritional status (p < 0.011). In this study, 80% of patients with only primary education were undernourished compared to 59% among those with higher education, as already observed in the study by Ouaijan [3] in Lebanon. This low educational capital constitutes an obstacle to understanding nutritional recommendations and to adopting appropriate eating behaviors [3]. It thus appears as a fundamental point for prevention. Monthly income was significantly associated with patients' undernutrition status, given that low income appeared as a central factor, with 80% of patients living below the minimum wage threshold (44,000 FCFA ~ 88 USD) being in severe undernutrition. Economic precariousness therefore impacts access to enriched meals, oral nutritional supplements, and quality food in hospital settings, as shown by Luna [10] at the Douala General Hospital. Furthermore, dependence on multiple caregivers (36.36% of patients surveyed having three or more caregivers ensuring their feeding at the hospital), often perceived as family solidarity, is also a reflection of functional loss of autonomy and a fragile nutritional status, as highlighted by Ouaijan [3] in their study on hospital malnutrition in Lebanon.

The medical factors assessed in this study were strongly associated with undernutrition (p < 0.05), particularly chronic diseases, length of hospitalization, polypharmacy, and feeding disorders. Among the most involved pathologies, cancer, kidney failure, and strokes emerged as the most deleterious for nutritional status. These diseases, associated with hypercatabolism and reduced appetite, induce rapid weight loss, as confirmed by certain authors [1, 28]. Moreover, multi‐morbidity (≥ 3 comorbidities), very frequent in patients over 70 years old in this study, exacerbates loss of lean (muscle) mass and functional fatigue, as reported by [29]. Prolonged hospitalization, especially beyond 8 weeks, was a major risk factor observed in this study (16 out of 18 patients were undernourished, of whom 6 moderately and 10 severely). Indeed, Stratton [30] demonstrated that hospitalization without nutritional compensation increases the risk of undernutrition by 10% to 15% per week. Prolonged hospitalization therefore exposes patients to inappropriate meals, stress, disease‐related hypercatabolism, and fasting periods for examinations. Polypharmacy, present in 85.45% of patients, particularly in those over 70 years old (43.64%), was also recognized for its deleterious effects such as taste disorders, anorexia, drug‐nutrient interactions, and digestive impairment, as confirmed by the systematic review conducted by Kok and Bauer [31, 32]. Aggressive treatments (chemotherapy, corticosteroid therapy, dialysis) were also associated with a very high prevalence of undernutrition in this sample, similar to a study conducted by Badrasawi [33], which showed that certain complications (headaches, hypotension, nausea, difficulties accessing the treatment site) related to treatment during hemodialysis are significantly correlated with malnutrition. Mechanical disorders (chewing, swallowing) and psychological disorders (anxiety, depression, chronic stress), affecting 21.82% and 25.45% of patients respectively, were also significantly associated with undernutrition. These disorders, frequent among the elderly, directly hinder food consumption, as shown by Volkert [34].

The analysis of eating habits highlights major protein and energy deficiencies among patients in this study. The majority of patients consumed fewer than three meals per day (51.8%) and more than half did not finish their plate during a meal. This eating behavior is associated with an undernutrition prevalence of 94.5%, corroborating the findings of Barker and Allard [35, 36], who showed that reduced food intake is the main risk factor for undernutrition, with prevalence reaching 70% among hospitalized patients who eat less than half of their meals. This insufficient intake is often linked to anorexia, digestive symptoms, imposed dietary restrictions, and lack of assistance with meals. Restrictive diets (low‐calorie, low‐protein) were followed by 72.7% of patients, often without supplementation (Oral Nutritional Supplements) or adjustment, contributing to iatrogenic undernutrition [1, 16]. Kondrup [28] had highlighted in their work that poorly individualized hospital diets can worsen nutritional status, particularly in acute contexts. Among the 110 hospitalized patients at the Regional Hospital of Bafoussam, despite almost daily consumption of starchy foods (97.3% of respondents), daily consumption of at least one type of dairy product (32.7%), daily consumption of one portion of animal protein at each meal (72.7%), and fruit/vegetable consumption at least twice per day (56.4%), the data remain below requirements. Moreover, the low consumption of high biological value proteins (Poultry/Meat/Fish/Seafood) compromises muscle mass preservation [26, 37], while the deficit in fruits/vegetables increases antioxidant and micronutrient deficiencies [30]. Eating pleasure, still too often neglected, constitutes a proven protective factor since patients enjoying their meals were significantly less affected by undernutrition. Pouyet [38] showed in their study that anorexia related to hospitalization was partly caused by low attractiveness of meals, meals too copious or unsuited to patients' needs. Patients who dislike the taste, smell, texture, or appearance of the food served have a three times higher risk of undernutrition compared to those who enjoy it. Finally, insufficient hydration (less than 5 glasses/day) was observed in 8% of the surveyed patients, contributing to mental confusion, constipation, and loss of appetite, particularly in elderly individuals. Volkert [34] showed that dehydration is an independent risk factor for undernutrition, often underestimated in elderly individuals, and that it can exacerbate weight loss and muscle weakness. Insufficient fluid intake reduces blood volume, leads to decreased renal function, and worsens constipation, directly affecting nutrient absorption and overall health status. Moreover, proper hydration is crucial for adequate metabolic functioning, and chronic dehydration can impair protein synthesis and tissue repair, thus leading to more severe undernutrition.

Although this study is rich in lessons, it has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design of the study allowed the establishment of strong statistical associations but not direct causal links, and the monocentric scope limited the generalization of the results to all hospitals in Cameroon, although they provide an important warning signal. It would therefore be important to conduct a prospective cohort (longitudinal) study to follow undernourished patients after hospital discharge in order to evaluate the long‐term impact on mortality, rehospitalizations, and quality of life, and to conduct an interventional study to measure the clinical and economic effectiveness of a structured nutritional management program (screening + dietary intervention + nutritional counseling) within the Bafoussam Regional Hospital. It would also be important to conduct a medico‐economic analysis to precisely quantify the additional costs related to undernutrition and the cost‐effectiveness ratio of strategies to combat it in the Cameroonian context, and to deepen the understanding of cultural and social barriers to adequate hospital feeding through qualitative studies (interviews with patients, families, and caregivers). To conclude on the limitations of the study, taking into account the fact that participants may inaccurately remember past events or leave out details due to systematic differences in memory accuracy, some determinants of undernutrition in the study population that were self‐reported, can be subject to recall bias for this study.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to contribute to the nutritional care of hospitalized adult and elderly patients at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital through the determination of the prevalence of undernutrition and the identification of its determinants. At the end, an alarming overall prevalence of undernutrition was revealed, affecting more than half of the study population. The study clearly identified the presence of comorbidities, prolonged hospital stay, polypharmacy, loss of appetite, as well as chewing, swallowing, and psychological disorders as the main medical factors associated with undernutrition. At the same time, inadequate dietary habits such as low meal frequency, inability to finish meals, adherence to unbalanced restrictive diets, low consumption of animal protein and dairy products, as well as lack of pleasure in eating were identified as contributing factors to undernutrition.
