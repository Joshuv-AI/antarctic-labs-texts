Synthesis of thienoacenes by electrochemical double C–S cyclization using a halogen mediator
Authors: Koichi Mitsudo, Takuya Nagahara, Nozomi Kataura, Yuka Okamura, Toki Yonezawa, Yuri Tachibana, Nolan Soulié, Keisuke Shigemori, Eisuke Sato, Hiroki Mandai, Seiji Suga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Thienoacenes are significant compounds as organic materials. One of the most efficient ways to synthesize thienoacenes is to form multiple C–S bonds in a single step. Because unprotected S–H bonds are easily oxidized to S–S bonds, S-Me protected substrates are commonly used for the purpose. However, their reactivity is insufficient, and one-step construction of multiple C–S bonds is still challenging. We herein report the electrochemical synthesis of thienoacenes from S-methoxymethyl (MOM)-protected diarylacetylenes. In the presence of Bu4NBr as a halogen mediator, electrochemical double C–S cyclization of diarylacetylenes bearing two MOM groups proceeded to afford [1]benzothieno[3,2-b][1]benzothiophene (BTBT) derivatives. While S-Me or S-p-methoxybenzyl (PMB)-protected diarylacetylenes did not afford BTBT, BTBT was selectively obtained when a substrate protected with S-MOM groups was used. The S-MOM protection strategy is also effective for the electrochemical synthesis of a more π-expanded thienoacene such as dibenzo[d,d′]thieno[3,2-b,4,5-b′]dithiophene (DBTDT).

Introduction

Thienoacenes, sulfur-containing π-conjugated molecules, have been widely used as active compounds for organic materials such as organic field-effect transistors (OFETs) and organic photovoltaics (OPVs)1–3. In particular, thienoacenes bearing thieno[3,2-b]thiophene (TT) moieties, such as [1]benzothieno[3,2-b][1]benzothiophene (BTBT)4–7, dinaphtho[2,3-b:2′,3′-f]thieno[3,2-b]thiophene (DNTT)8–15, and dibenzo[d,d′]thieno[3,2-b;4,5-b′]dithiophene (DBTDT)16–19, exhibit excellent OFET properties (Fig. 1a). While there have been many reports on the synthesis of thienoacenes20–32, one of the most efficient and attractive ways to synthesize thienoacenes is the formation of multiple C–S bonds in a single step. Because unprotected S–H bonds are easily oxidized to S–S bonds, protection of the S atom is often required, especially when compounds with two thiol moieties are used as starting materials. Methyl protection has been widely used for this purpose. For instance, Takimiya reported that bis(naphthalenyl)ethene bearing two methylsulfanyl groups reacted with an excess amount of iodine to afford a highly π-expanded thienoacene such as DNTT (Fig. 1b)8. While this strategy is efficient for constructing a BTBT skeleton, an excess amount of iodine was required, and only highly π-extended substrates could be used32. Recently, Kitamura reported an I2/PhI(OAc)2-mediated cyclization of diphenylacetylenes bearing methylsulfanyl groups (Fig. 1c)27. An iodocyclized product was obtained, and subsequent photocyclization was required to synthesize BTBT derivatives. Meanwhile, electrochemical carbon–heteroatom bond formation is an environmentally benign method that proceeds under mild conditions and is a powerful tool for organic synthesis33–36. Among these reactions, electrochemical C–S bond formation has recently received considerable attention37–52. For instance, Lei reported anodic C–S bond formation between electron-rich arenes and arylthiols37. Wirth and Xu reported the electrochemical intramolecular coupling of thiocarbonyl compounds for the synthesis of benzothiazoles38. We have been interested in the electrochemical synthesis of π-conjugated compounds53–56 and recently reported dehydrogenative C–S coupling for the synthesis of thienoacenes53 and dibenzothiophenes55 using a halogen mediator (Fig. 1d).

Against the background, we turned our attention to the electrochemical synthesis of BTBT derivatives by sequential double C–S coupling in one-pot. In our preliminary study, we assumed that S-Me-protected bis(o-sulfanylphenyl)acetylene could be used as a substrate for the sequential electro-oxidative cyclization, but bromocyclization predominantly proceeded, and the desired product was not obtained (Fig. 1e). After several examinations, we finally found that a methoxymethyl (MOM) group, which is not a common protecting group for S atoms57–59, is an efficient protecting group for halogen-mediated electrochemical C–S bond formation. We herein report a synthesis of thienoacenes from S-MOM-protected bis(o-sulfanylphenyl)acetylene derivatives via electrochemical double-cyclization using a halogen mediator (Fig. 1f). Double C–S cyclization of simple substrates proceeded in a single step.

Results and discussion

Optimization of the electrochemical double C–S cyclization

MOM-protected bis(o-sulfanylphenyl)acetylene (1a) was chosen as a model compound, and the electrochemical cyclization of 1a was examined (Table 1). After constant current electrolysis at 70 °C without a mediator (12 mA, 2 F mol−1), 1a was consumed completely, but the desired double-cyclized product 2a was obtained in only 9% yield (entry 1). In the presence of Bu4NCl (3 equiv) as a mediator, 2a was obtained in 35% yield along with a mixture of mono-cyclized product 3a and chlorinated mono-cyclized product 4a (13% yield, 3a/4a = 1:0.15). Bu4NI was not effective (entry 3). With Bu4NBr, the yield of 2a drastically increased to 50%, but the yields of 3a and brominated mono-cyclized product 4b also increased (entry 4). Lowering the reaction temperature to 50 °C, the yield of 2a decreased to 20% (entry 5). In contrast, the electrochemical reaction at 80 °C gave a similar result to that at 70 °C (entry 6), suggesting that 70 °C was sufficient for the reaction. Solvent effect was next examined. The yield of 2a decreased to 39% in CH3NO2 (entry 7). DMSO was ineffective (entry 8). We next examined the effect of different electrodes on the reaction (entries 9–11). The use of carbon and stainless-steel electrodes was inefficient, whereas Pt electrodes proved to be the effective for the reaction. Optimization of electrochemical parameters was next performed. Reducing the electrical current to 2 mA, the yield of 2a slightly increased (entry 12). With 4 F mol−1 of charge, the desired product 2a was obtained in 83% yield, but 3a and 4b were still obtained (entry 13). Decreasing the amount of Bu4NBr to 1 equiv, the yield of 2a decreased to 66%, but the generation of 3a and 4b was suppressed (entry 14). Scope of the electrochemical double-cyclization

We chose entry 3 in Table 2 as the optimal conditions, and next examined the scope of the reaction (Fig. 2a). First, several symmetrical diphenylacetylenes 1b–1j were examined. The anodic oxidation of 1b, bearing dimethyl groups at the 4,4′-positions, afforded 2,7-dimethyl-BTBT 2b in 68% yield. 3,8-Dimethyl-BTBT 2c was obtained from 1c bearing dimethyl groups at the 5,5′-positions in moderate yield. Halogenated substrates were next examined. 2,7-Dichloro-BTBT 2d was obtained in 29% yield, but 3,8-dichloro-BTBT 2e was not obtained under the reaction conditions. A similar tendency was observed in the synthesis of dibromo-BTBTs 2f and 2g. In contrast, the electrochemical reaction of 1h bearing difluoro groups proceeded smoothly to afford difluoro-BTBT 2h in 71% yield. 4,4′-Dicyanodiphenylacetyelene 1i was not suitable for the reaction, but 1j, diphenylacetylene bearing two trifluoromethyl groups, gave bis(trifluoromethyl)-BTBT 2j in 75% yield. We next studied the electrochemical synthesis of mono-substituted BTBT derivatives (2k–2w). BTBT derivatives bearing electro-donating groups such as methyl, isopropyl, and tert-butyl groups were obtained in moderate to good yields (2k–2n). We next examined the electrochemical reaction of diphenylacetylenes 1o–1w, which have electron-withdrawing groups. BTBT derivatives bearing halogen atoms were obtained in low to high yields (2o–2t). BTBTs bearing cyano, trifluoromethyl, and acetyl groups were readily obtained by this method, and the corresponding products were obtained in 78%, 81%, and 64% yields, respectively (2u–2w). BTBT derivatives 2x and 2y, which have two different functional groups, could also be synthesized by this method with the use of the corresponding substrates 1x and 1y. Next, a gram-scale synthesis was performed (Fig. 2b). The electrochemical oxidation of 1a (1.98 g, 6.0 mmol) proceeded to afford 2a in 71% yield (1.02 g, 4.3 mmol). Although the yield was slightly lower on the larger scale, the reaction still proved to be efficient and practical. This result demonstrates that the electrochemical double-cyclization can be readily scaled up.

Mechanistic studies and plausible mechanism

To get further insight into the reaction, control experiments were examined (Fig. 3a–f). As mentioned above, S-Me-protected substrate 5 could not be used for the reaction. Electro-oxidation of 5 did not give the desired compound 2a and brominated mono-cyclized product 6 was obtained in 19% yield (Fig. 3a). We also tried the reaction of the substrate protected by p-methoxybenzyl (PMB) groups, but 2a was not observed, and mono-cyclized product 8 was obtained as a major product. To explain the reactivities, we performed cyclic voltammetry of 1a, 5, and 6. Interestingly, the onset values of the oxidation potentials of 1a, 5, and 6 were similar (0.97, 1.00, and 0.93 V vs. Fc/Fc+, respectively, Fig. S2), which were much more positive than that of Bu4NBr (0.21 V, Fig. S1). These results cannot explain why only MOM-protection is effective, but suggest that Br− would be oxidized first on the anode in each case. We also examined electrochemical reactions of mono-cyclized by-products 3a and 4b (Fig. 3b). From 4b, only a trace amount of 2a was obtained. In contrast, 2a was obtained in 52% yield from 3a. These results indicate that brominated by-product 4b could not be converted to 2a, and only non-brominated mono-cyclized product 3a, which would be the intermediate of the double-cyclization, could be transformed to 2a. To confirm the intermediate by a simple NMR analysis, we chose dimethyl-substituted 1b as a substrate, and the electrolysis of 1b with 0.5 F mol−1 of electricity was carried out. Electrolysis with less electricity afforded 2b in only 5% yield, and mono-cyclized 3b was obtained as a major product (Fig. 3c), suggesting that this reaction would be a step-wise reaction through the mono-cyclized intermediate. To confirm the proton source for 3b, we next carried out the electrochemical reaction of 1b in CD3CN and found that partially D-labeled 3b was obtained (27%D) (Fig. 3d). In contrast, 82% D-labeled 3b was obtained when the reaction was performed with D2O (30 equiv) (Fig. 3e). These results suggest that acetonitrile would act as a hydrogen source, but H2O should serve as the major proton source. H2O would also act as a trapping agent of the eliminated MOM group. When the electrochemical reaction of 1a was carried out with 1-dodecanol (5 equiv) instead of H2O, 2a was obtained in a yield similar to that with H2O (81% yield), and 1-(methoxymethoxy)dodecane was obtained in 19% yield (Fig. 3f). This result means that the oxocarbenium ion generated in situ was partially trapped by 1-dodecanol. H2O should play a similar role in the electrochemical reaction.

Meanwhile, Nonaka and co-workers reported that Br2 or Br3−, [Br+] equivalents, were generated by anodic oxidation of Br−60. We previously reported, based on DFT calculations, that an equilibrium exists in which Br₃⁻ reacts with Li⁺ to yield LiBr and Br₂55. We considered that the thus-generated low-concentration Br2 would be the active species to promote the reaction. To confirm that, we next examined the reaction of 1a with [Br+] equivalents instead of electrolysis (Fig. 3g). With NBS, the desired product 2a was not obtained, and brominated disulfide 9 was obtained in 22% yield. In contrast, 2a was obtained by treating Br2 or Bu4NBr3 (19% and 11% yields, respectively), although 9 was also obtained in 30% and 14% yields, respectively. When Bu4NBr3 was added dropwise over 30 min, the yield of 9 decreased to 5% and 2a was obtained in 51%, suggesting that Br2 would be an active species promoting the reaction, and the undesired bromination can be suppressed by lowering the concentration of Br2. Therefore, electrochemically generated low-concentration [Br+] would be preferable to the addition of [Br+] reagents (Similar tendency was observed in our previous studies53,55).

Based on the control experiments and previous studies53,55, a plausible mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 4a. First, Br− would be oxidized on the anode to afford Br255,60, which would be attacked by 1 to afford cationic intermediate A. Subsequent elimination of an oxocarbenium ion would form sulfanylbromide B. The oxocarbenium ion would be trapped by H2O or Br−. Homolytic S–Br bond cleavage of B would form radical intermediate C and Br radical. Subsequent radical cyclization of C would afford the benzo[b]thiophene radical D. Thus-generated D would be reduced on the cathode to anionic intermediate E, which would be protonated by H2O to afford intermediate F (path A). Another pathway is that hydrogen atom transfer from acetonitrile to the intermediate D would give F (path B). Control experiments suggest that the reaction proceeded via both pathways, and path A would be predominant. Thus-generated F would react with [Br+] to form sulfanylbromide intermediate G. Finally, nucleophilic intramolecular cyclization would afford BTBT (2) via cationic intermediate H. To validate the proposed reaction mechanism, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted at the SMD(CH3CN)/M06-2X/6-31+G(d,p) level of theory (Figs. 4b and S4). The bond dissociation energy (BDE) of S–Br bond in intermediate B was calculated to be 51.2 kcal/mol, indicating that the homolytic cleavage of this bond would be feasible61. The activation energy of intermediate C for the formation of radical intermediate D was determined to be 18.4 kcal/mol, suggesting that this step proceeds readily under the reaction conditions. Subsequent reaction of intermediate D with acetonitrile to afford intermediate F is exothermic, with the free energy of F lying 16.3 kcal/mol below that of C. The formation of intermediate B and the final nucleophilic cyclization of intermediate F were also calculated (Figs. S4–S6 and Table S1), and the results obtained were consistent with the proposed reaction pathway.

Application of the S-MOM strategy for the synthesis of DBTDT

The S-MOM-protecting strategy could be applicable for other thienoacenes such as DBTDT (Fig. 5). When non-protected substrate 10a was subjected to electrolysis, DBTDT (11) was obtained in only 33% yield because polymerization of 10a competed with the desired reaction. In contrast, electrolysis of MOM-protected substrate 10b suppressed polymerization, and 11 was obtained in 72% yield under similar conditions and in 82% yield under further optimized conditions. Conclusions

We developed a facile method for synthesizing BTBT derivatives by electrochemical double C–S cyclization using Bu4NBr as a halogen mediator. The MOM group was an efficient protecting group for the sequential transformation, which enables the formation of multiple C–S bonds in one step. A variety of symmetric and asymmetric BTBT derivatives were synthesized by the method, and more π-expanded DBTDT can also be synthesized from the appropriate MOM-protected precursor. Further studies on the application of this strategy are ongoing in our laboratory.

Methods

Synthesis of 2a

The electro-oxidation was carried out in a 30 mL Schlenk tube equipped with a Pt anode (1.0 × 1.5 cm2) and a Pt cathode (1.0 × 1.5 cm2). The Schlenk tube was charged with a solution of tetrabutylammonium bromide (65 mg, 0.20 mmol) and lithium perchlorate (106 mg, 1.00 mmol) in CH3CN (10 mL). After the measurement of water content by a Karl Fischer moisture meter, appropriate water was added to adjust total amount of water in the reaction mixture to 30 equivalents. 1,2-Bis{2-[(methoxymethyl)thio]phenyl}ethyne (1a, 66 mg, 0.20 mmol) was added to the mixture, and a constant current (5 mA, 4.0 F mol−1, 4.3 h) was supplied at 70 °C with magnetic stirring. After the resulting mixture was stirred for an additional 15 min, to the mixture were added sat. NaHCO3 aq (5 mL) and sat. Na2S2O3 aq (2 mL). The mixture was extracted with CHCl3 (3 × 20 mL). The combined organic extracts were washed with brine (20 mL), dried over MgSO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by column chromatography on silica gel (hexane) to afford benzo[b]benzo[4,5]thieno[2,3-d]thiophene (2a) as a colorless solid (38 mg, 0.16 mmol, 81%).
