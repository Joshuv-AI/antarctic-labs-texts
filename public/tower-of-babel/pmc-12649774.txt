A proof-of-concept study using a graphene oxide–bovine serum albumin nanocomposite-based electrochemical aptasensor platform for Salmonella typhimurium detection from chicken meat
Authors: Settasart Sonna, Chatchawan Srisawat, Sakda Jampasa, Orawon Chailapakul, David J. Hampson, Nuvee Prapasarakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalmonella Typhimurium (ST) is an important food-borne pathogen, particularly in chicken meat, making its rapid detection essential for food safety. Conventional detection methods are slow and equipment-intensive, creating the need for sensitive and portable alternatives. We developed a rapid, ultrasensitive, and portable electrochemical aptasensor platform for detecting ST in samples extracted from chicken meat. The sensor employed graphene oxide–bovine serum albumin (GO-BSA) nanocomposite to enhance electrode biocompatibility, stability, and aptamer immobilization. A specific NH2-modified DNA aptamer targeting ST allowed direct binding without prior extraction. Fabrication steps included GO-BSA drop-casting, aptamer immobilization, and BSA blocking before ST binding. The sensor operates on a signal-off mechanism, measured via differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), where the aptamer-ST complex formation reduces the redox signal of [Fe(CN)6]³-/4- due to insulation and electrostatic repulsion. The aptasensor demonstrated excellent sensitivity (limit of detection 3 CFU/mL) and high specificity. Performance validation using spiked samples extracted from chicken meat confirmed the effective detection of ST in a complex matrix, aligning with the results obtained through culture-based methods. The sensor also maintained good stability for up to 28 days at 4 °C. These results showed that aptasensors can be a rapid, cost-effective, and field-deployable tool for monitoring foodborne pathogens.

Introduction

Foodborne illnesses caused by bacterial pathogens remain a critical public health challenge worldwide. Among these pathogens, Salmonella enterica is one of the leading causes of foodborne outbreaks, with more than 2600 recognized serovars. Within this diversity, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (ST) is consistently reported as one of the most prevalent serovars associated with poultry products and human salmonellosis across Europe, Asia, North America, and South America (Kramomtong et al. 2000; Fernandes et al. 2022; EFSA 2023; Hurst et al. 2023). ST may cause salmonellosis in poultry, but it can also be carried sub-clinically in the intestinal contents of the birds. At slaughter, leakage of contents may contaminate chicken meat with ST and other bacterial pathogens, and this material then serves as a major source of human infection, with improper handling of contaminated raw meat leading to food poisoning. There is an urgent need for reliable and rapid detection tools to help mitigate this problem to improve food safety (Mahari and Gandhi 2022; Zambry et al. 2022). As ST has frequently been implicated in chicken meat contamination it is regarded as a sentinel organism for food safety monitoring. Thus, we selected ST as a model pathogen for the present study, recognizing its high epidemiological relevance and role as a representative target in proof-of-concept detection studies.

Conventional methods for Salmonella detection, including culture protocols (ISO 6579-1) and real-time PCR, provide high reliability but are time-consuming, require specialized equipment, and may not be suitable for rapid screening in resource-limited settings. These limitations have stimulated the exploration of alternative diagnostic platforms and strategies. Biosensors offer the potential to deliver rapid and reliable detection with high sensitivity and specificity, reduced time-to-result, cost-effectiveness, and excellent portability. Such attributes align closely with the key requirements of point-of-care diagnostic tools, making biosensors a compelling approach for on-site applications (Paniel and Noguer 2019; Zhuang et al. 2023; Oslan et al. 2024). Amongst potential biosensors, electrochemical aptasensors have gained recognition as promising alternatives, offering high sensitivity, specificity, portability, and cost-effectiveness (Awang et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2021).

A fundamental component of any biosensor is the bioreceptor, which plays a critical role in determining the nature of the signal and its subsequent transduction. The currently available classes of bioreceptors include antibodies, antimicrobial peptides, bacteriophages, nucleic acids, and aptamers (Morales and Halpern 2018). Among these, aptamers have gained considerable attention due to their unique advantages, including high affinity, specificity, chemical stability, and ease of synthesis and modification. Although some limitations exist, these are generally manageable compared to other bioreceptor types. When aptamers are employed as the recognition element, the resulting platforms are referred to as aptasensors. Aptamers, synthetic oligonucleotides that can selectively bind to specific targets, are often central to such platforms. The present study did not attempt to discover or validate a novel aptamer, but instead it deliberately employed a previously validated aptamer specific to ST outer membrane proteins (Joshi et al. 2009), allowing us to focus on the development and demonstration of a novel sensing platform.

Electrochemical aptasensors are gaining recognition as a highly effective detection tool with significant potential across various applications. These aptasensors combine advanced electrochemical techniques with aptamers. These synthetic oligonucleotides exhibit strong and specific binding to their target molecules without the need for additional extraction or denaturation, unlike the use of a DNA probe, thereby shortening the measurement process. This integration facilitates a reliable platform specifically designed for pathogen detection. The primary advantages of electrochemical aptasensors are their exceptional sensitivity, specificity, and cost-effectiveness, making them a promising tool for detecting various pathogens (Wang et al. 2023).

Recent research on electrochemical aptasensors has made significant progress in detecting Salmonella spp. Various nanomaterials, including reduced graphene oxide (rGO), graphene oxide (GO), gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), cellulose nanocrystals, and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), have been widely utilized to enhance sensor performance. These nanomaterials help to increase electrical conductivity, enlarge the active surface area, and sometimes offer catalytic-like activity, which together enhance the sensitivity of detection (Mahari and Gandhi 2022; Zambry et al. 2022). To further improve the stability and compatibility of the sensor surface, nanomaterials are often combined with polymers like chitosan, polypyrrole, polyaniline, and bovine serum albumin (BSA) to form nanocomposite (Petrucci et al. 2021; Pérez-Piñeiro et al. 2023; Sood et al. 2024). These combinations not only stabilize the sensor film but also offer many functional groups, especially in materials like GO, which are ideal for chemical bonding with biomolecules, for use in EDC/NHS coupling (Liu et al. 2021; Rabiei et al. 2024).

GO is one of the most popular materials for electrochemical aptasensors because of its large surface area, excellent water dispersibility, and stable interaction with biomolecules (Pourmadadi et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2025). Although GO offers excellent surface properties, its direct application remains limited by cytotoxicity and poor coating stability on transducer surfaces. To address these challenges, Sivaselvam et al. (2020) engineered pre-formed GO-BSA nanocomposite, enhancing both biocompatibility and surface adhesion for electrochemical aptasensor development. This strategy has not previously been reported for developing an ST aptasensor. Studies by Chiu et al. (2018) and Oberhaus et al. (2020) found that combining GO with BSA not only improved biocompatibility but also increased surface roughness and aptamer binding efficiency, resulting in better sensor performance. Consequently, the use of GO/BSA nanocomposite is expected to function effectively due to its unique structure. BSA forms a thin and stable layer on GO through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. This layer protects biological samples from direct contact with the sharp edges of GO, helping to reduce potential toxicity. At the same time, BSA also helps prevent nonspecific binding and adsorption from interfering substances, making it ideal for electrochemical biosensing applications even in complex food samples.

The primary aim of this study was therefore to establish a proof-of-concept electrochemical aptasensor platform for the sensitive and selective detection of ST. To illustrate laboratory applicability, we validated the aptasensor using both pure bacterial cultures and spiked chicken meat juice as a simulated food matrix. Importantly, we benchmarked its performance against culture methods (ISO 6579-1) and real-time PCR, providing an initial comparison with gold-standard techniques. While full-scale field validation using naturally contaminated meat samples will be addressed in future work, the present study provides a strong foundation for advancing aptasensor-based diagnostics toward practical applications in food safety surveillance.

Materials and methods

Chemicals, materials, and reagents

All materials utilized in this study were of analytical grade. Graphene and silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) ink were obtained from Acheson (California, USA) to produce each screen-printed graphene electrode (SPGE). Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) was purchased from Idee-craft Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand) and served as the substrate. Graphene oxide, 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC), N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), and Bovine serum albumin (BSA) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. The phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) used, with a pH of 7.4, consisted of 140 mM NaCl, 2 mM KH2PO4, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 2 mM KCl, all of which were obtained from Merck. A 5 mM ferri/ferrocyanide solution (K3Fe (CN)6/K4Fe (CN)6) was prepared by combining potassium (II) ferrocyanide (K4Fe (CN)6) and potassium (III) ferrocyanide (K3Fe (CN)6) in a 0.1 M KCl solution at a 1:1 ratio. All solutions employed in this study were prepared using Millipore Milli-Q (MQ) purified water (18.2 MΩ). The ST-specific aptamer was synthesized by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea). The sequence of the aptamer was 5′-C6-NH2- CGGCGTCACCCGACGGGGACTTGACATTATGACAG-3′ (Joshi et al. 2009). The aptamer was prepared as a working stock solution at 100 µM and used for the subsequent experiments.

Bacterial strains

Salmonella Typhimurium (ST) reference strain ATCC 13311 and five Thai field isolates (ST01, ST02, ST03, ST04, ST05) were used as the target bacteria. Non-target negative control bacteria which occur in the intestinal tract that were used included Bacillus cereus (BC, ATCC 11778), Campylobacter jejuni (CJ, field strain), Escherichia coli (EC, ATCC 25922), Enterococcus faecalis (EF, NRIC 1142), Staphylococcus aureus (SA, ATCC 25923), Shigella sonnei (SS, field strain), and Listeria monocytogenes (LM, DMST 17303), which were all obtained from the collection at the Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University. Salmonella Corvallis (SC, field strain) was obtained from the Veterinary Research and Development Center (Upper Northern region), Lampang, Thailand. The bacterial strains were cultured on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) and incubated overnight at 37 °C, except for LM and CJ which were cultivated on blood agar and modified charcoal–cefoperazone–deoxycholate agar (mCCDA) (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK), respectively. CJ was incubated under microaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h. Single colonies were carefully selected and identified using Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF/MS) (Bruker, Switzerland). An identical colony of each strain, except for CJ, was subsequently chosen for subculture in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm overnight. CJ was inoculated into Bolton broth supplemented with 5% (v/v) lysed horse blood under microaerobic conditions. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min, repeated three times before use. Bacterial cell suspensions for subsequent use were then prepared in PBS. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University (Protocol Review No. IBC-2431047).

Aptasensor fabrication

Each SPGE was prepared using an in-house screen-printing methodology, as described by Jampasa et al. (2016). Graphene ink was deposited onto a PVC substrate to construct the working electrode (WE), which had a diameter of 3 mm, in conjunction with the counter electrode (CE), as illustrated in Figure 1. Subsequently, Ag/AgCl ink was applied onto the same PVC substrate to establish a conductive section and to create the reference electrode (RE). The assembled electrodes were dried at 55 °C for one hour to eliminate moisture and residual solvents. Following this, the dried electrodes were pre-treated with 40 µL of 1 M H2SO4 and subjected to cyclic voltammetry (CV) scanning for ten cycles, ranging from −0.5 V to +1.0 V with a scan rate of 100 mV/s, or until a consistent pattern was observed. The electrodes then were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried using an air stream. The GO-BSA nanocomposite were applied to the WE via drop-casting, using a volume of 5 µL, and allowed to air-dry at ambient temperature. Subsequently, the coupling agents EDC (0.4 M) and NHS (0.1 M) were introduced in a 1:1 ratio to the dried GO-BSA and incubated at room temperature for thirty minutes. Following this incubation, the mixture was washed with phosphate-buffered saline 1X (PBS 1X) at pH 7.4. In the final step, an NH2-modified aptamer was covalently immobilized onto the WE, enabling the formation of bonds between the -NH2 and -COOH groups through the action of EDC and NHS. The electrodes were then thoroughly rinsed three times with PBS to remove any unbound aptamer. The overall preparation of the GO-BSA-based electrochemical aptasensor is illustrated in Figure 1(A,B).

Electrochemical detection of ST

All electrochemical measurements were conducted using a potentiostat (PalmSens4) (Houten, Netherlands) and were controlled with PSTrace software. Solutions of ST and the control bacterial strains were prepared in PBS at a concentration of 104 CFU/mL, and 5 µL of each were applied to the WE and incubated for 60 min. Subsequently, the WE was washed three times with PBS in a dropping and pipetting technique to remove unbound bacterial cells. The electrochemical analyses were performed in 40 µL of 5 mM [Fe (CN)6]3-/4- using differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) under optimized parameters of 100 mV amplitude and a 10-mV step potential (data not shown). The current peak corresponding to PBS was designated as the blank (IPBS), while the current peak associated with the target analyte (ST) was defined as IST. The difference in current peaks between the blank and the target analyte (ΔI) was calculated using the following equation: ΔI (µA) = IPBS – IST. This detection methodology was based on the principle that ST in the analytes would result in it binding to the aptamer immobilized on the WE. This interaction would impede electron transfer of the redox mediator, resulting in a lower current peak than that of the blank. An increase in ST contamination would correlate with further reductions in the current peak, resulting in a downward trend on the calibration curve. To facilitate interpretation, ΔI was used to construct an upward trend in the calibration curve. The electrochemical detection process and the corresponding sensing mechanism, along with the anticipated results of this study, are illustrated in Figure 1(C).

Physical and electrochemical characterization

The modified electrodes were systematically evaluated to confirm the success of each modification step by characterizing their physical and electrochemical properties. Characterization was conducted using a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA), which provided insight into the elemental composition of each modification. In addition, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyze variations in the surface area of the WE at different modification stages using the JSM-IT700HR InTouchScope Scanning Electron Microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The beam energy was set at 5 kV, and all samples were examined at a magnification of 5000x. Conductivity assessments were performed utilizing cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) on three electrodes for each modification step. Collectively, these methodologies substantiated the effectiveness of the electrode modifications that were made.

Detection performance evaluation of electrochemical aptasensor

To assess the limit of detection (LOD) and quantitation (LOQ) of the designed electrochemical aptasensor to detect ST, the prepared electrodes were incubated at room temperature for 30 min with cultures of ST at a series of log 10 concentrations ranging from 1.5 × 100 to 108 CFU/mL. Following incubation, the electrodes were thoroughly washed and dried. Subsequently, a ferri/ferrocyanide solution was applied and analyzed using DPV. The PBS was utilized as the blank and underwent processing conditions identical to those of the ST samples. The current change (ΔI) was calculated according to the mentioned formula. A calibration curve was generated by plotting the ΔI values corresponding to each ST concentration. The LOD and LOQ were determined using the equations LOD = 3SD/b and LOD = 10SD/b, where SD represents the standard deviation of the blank samples (n = 10), and b denotes the slope of the calibration curve (NATA 2012). Each concentration was tested in triplicate, and the average ΔI values were plotted against bacterial concentration, thereby producing the calibration curve. The same methodology was employed for specificity testing to analyze the non-target bacteria to evaluate potential cross-reactivity with ST. All bacterial samples were maintained at a uniform concentration of 1.5 × 107 CFU/mL, with each bacterium tested in triplicate. The average ΔI for non-target bacteria was compared to that of ST. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized to compare the results across groups, with statistical significance established at a p-value < 0.05. Furthermore, the stability of the prepared aptasensors was evaluated by preparing a total of 90 electrodes. From these, the detection performance of ten electrodes (ST = 5, Blank =5) were examined at multiple time points from day zero up to day 90 (D0, D3, D7, D14, D21, D35, D42, D60, and D90). The normalized value was calculated as: normalized value (%) = valuestorage×100/valueinitial.

Chicken meat sample preparation

The chicken breast meat sample was purchased from a local market. The sample was subjected to two thorough washings with sterile MQ water. Subsequently, a 5% trisodium phosphate solution was applied to eliminate any background microflora, followed by thorough rinsing with sterile MQ water. Finally, the meat was exposed to ultraviolet light for 5 min to ensure the absence of any excess fluid (Vichaibun and Kanchanaphum 2020). The treated meat was cut into 25 g pieces, and each piece was mixed with 225 mL of buffer peptone water (BPW), before homogenizing with a stomacher at 200 rpm for 2 min to produce a sample dubbed “meat juice”. Aliquots of the samples were held in BPW for 20 h at 37 °C, then 0.1 mL was incubated in 10 mL Rappaport-Vassiliadis soya peptone (RVS) broth at 41 °C for 24 h before culturing on XLD agar. The culture plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Any red colonies with or without black centers were tested with MALDI-TOF to ensure that they were not Salmonella. Subsequently, the meat juice from Salmonella-free homogenates was spiked with the ST solution to obtain final concentrations of 1.5 × 101, 1.5 × 103, and 1.5 × 105 CFU/mL of the meat juice. These concentrations were selected to represent low, medium, and high levels of contamination (Dinshaw et al. 2017; Muniandy et al. 2019; Appaturi et al. 2020; Jampasa et al. 2025). The prepared mixtures were tested with the electrochemical aptasensor, with Salmonella-free meat juice serving as a negative control. The samples were also examined using the standard culture method (ISO 6579-1).

Real-time PCR

Spiked samples were prepared at three concentration levels, consistent with those used for testing the developed aptasensor. DNA extraction was performed following the protocol previously described (Naas et al. 2006), with minor modifications. Briefly, samples underwent a heat–freeze cycle consisting of boiling at 100 °C for 10 min, freezing at −80 °C for 10 min, and an additional boiling step for 5 min. The resulting suspensions were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C until further analysis. The quantitative real-time PCR was performed following previously published methods (Gokduman et al. 2016; Saenkankam et al. 2025). A plasmid containing the ttrRSBC sequence was employed to generate a standard curve by 10-fold serial dilution (106–10° copies/µL). Each dilution was amplified in duplicate, and the cycle threshold (Ct) values were plotted against the log10 copy number. Amplification efficiency and linearity (R2) were calculated from the slope of the standard curve. All reactions included no-template and positive controls to ensure assay validity.

Results

Characterization of the modified aptasensor

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM was employed to assess the morphological alterations of the WE surface at each modification step. Figure 2(A) illustrates the surface of the bare SPGE, which was predominantly covered with stacked graphene layers. Upon applying GO-BSA nanocomposite to the SPGE through drop-casting, the resultant surface exhibited a uniform distribution and arrangement of the GO-BSA nanocomposite flakes (Figure 2(A,ii)). The immobilization of the 5′NH2 modified aptamer onto the SPGE/GO-BSA was facilitated by carbodiimide cross-linking chemistry. The SEM image of the modified electrode (SPGE/GO-BSA/EDC-NHS/Apt) demonstrated a notably cloudier and denser surface than those observed in the preceding modification step, indicating successful aptamer immobilization (Figure 2(A,iii)). Additionally, after the final blocking step with BSA, the electrode surface appeared smoother (Figure 2(A,iv)), thereby confirming the efficacy of the blocking process. All these results indicate that the modification of the aptasensor was successfully established.

FTIR characterization

FTIR spectroscopy was used to characterize each modification step of the working electrode (WE) surface. The FTIR spectra of SPGE, GO, GO-BSA, GO-BSA/EDC-NHS and GO-BSA/EDC-NHS/Aptamer were elucidated as shown in Figure 2(B). The GO-BSA band (light green line) spectrum shows additional protein-specific bands (C-H, N-H, and C-N), especially around 1,562 and 2,950 cm−1 compared to GO spectra (dark green line) (Alhazmi 2019). These changes confirm successful modification of WE with GO-BSA nanocomposite. Besides, there were common spectra of GO-BSA nanocomposite and GO (dark green line), with peaks of oxygen-containing functional groups, including O–H stretching at 3,292 cm−1, C=O stretching at 1,636 cm−1, C–OH bending at 1,379 cm−1, and C–O stretching at 1,042 cm−1(Gul and Alrobei 2021). These peaks confirmed the success of GO deposition and the presence of hydrophilic functional groups on the GO surface (Askari and Naghib 2018). After activating with EDC/NHS, a slight increase in the C–H stretching band at 2,850 cm−1 was observed. This may have been due to the presence of intermediate molecules formed during the EDC activation process, some of which may contain CH3 (methyl) groups (Inam et al. 2022). The final modification step involved immobilizing the NH2-modified aptamer onto the activated GO-BSA surface. After this step, enhanced peaks were observed at 1,525 cm−1 (N–H bending), 1,636 cm−1 (C=O stretching), and 3,292 cm−1 (N–H/O–H stretching) (Zou et al. 2021). These changes indicated the successful immobilization of the aptamer, as they corresponded to the primary amine and amide bonds involved in the conjugation. The FTIR spectra clearly demonstrated stepwise functionalization of the electrode surface from GO-BSA deposition, EDC/NHS activation, and aptamer immobilization, which was confirmed by the emergence or enhancement of specific vibrational bands related to the functional groups involved.

Electrochemical characterization

The electrochemical behavior of the fabricated aptasensor was verified at each modification step using CV and EIS techniques. Five mM [Fe (CN)6]3-/4- was used as the redox mediator. In the CV technique, the exchange of electrons between the redox mediator and the electrode surface was recorded and displayed on a voltammogram, as depicted in Figure 2(C). A higher current peak means a higher electron transfer rate. The current peak was gradually decreased following the modification step, which included the deposition of GO-BSA nanocomposite (green line), EDC/NHS activation (blue line), aptamer immobilization (pink line), and incubation with the ST (red line). Each successive surface modification step increased surface resistance, as indicated by a progressive decrease in redox peak current, confirming successful layer-by-layer functionalization.

The EIS was used to evaluate the electron-exchange resistance between the redox mediator and the electrode interface, as illustrated in the Nyquist plot, which displayed a semicircle shape, as shown in Figure 2(D). A wider semicircle shape means a higher resistance rate of electron transfer. After each modification step, the electrode surface gradually increased the electron transfer resistance (Rct), as observed in the semicircle of the Nyquist plots. The increasing diameter of the semicircles correlated with growing resistance to electron transfer, consistent with successful sequential functionalization of the electrode. The agreement of both CV and EIS results verifies that each electrode modification step was successful.

Optimization

The optimization of key elements and conditions was performed following each modification step, as shown in Figure 2(E). To optimize the nanocomposite interface for aptamer immobilization, GO concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 2.0 mg/mL were tested while maintaining BSA at a fixed concentration of 1% (w/v). The highest ΔI (4.61 ± 0.16 µA) was observed at 0.25 mg/mL GO (Figure 3(A)). This concentration was then selected for the aptamer concentration optimization. Aptamer concentration (0.5–10 µM) was optimized, with 5 µM yielding the highest ΔI (4.78 ± 0.20 µA) (Figure 3(B)). After that, aptamer immobilization times from 30 to 120 minutes were evaluated, with 60 minutes yielding the highest ΔI (5.14 ± 0.12 µA) (Figure 3(C)). The incubation time between the aptamer-modified sensor and ST was optimized to ensure maximum target capture. The highest ΔI (5.18 ± 0.13 µA) was observed at 30 minutes (Figure 3(D)).

Detection performance of aptasensor

Under optimal conditions, the detection performance of the aptasensor was evaluated using DPV and the ΔI ratio was calculated (Figure 4(A)). The difference in the current peak gradually increased as the bacterial concentration increased, because the aptamer-bacterial complex acted as an insulator, blocking electron transfer to the electrode. These reduced or prevented the electrochemical reaction between the redox mediator and the electrode. The ΔI and the log10 of bacterial concentration (CFU/mL) were plotted to create a calibration curve, as shown in Figure 4(B). The detection range exhibited a linear correlation from 10° to 108 CFU/mL (R2 = 0.9876) with a calculated LOD of 3 CFU/mL and LOQ of 9 CFU/mL. The estimated LOD was calculated with the three-fold blank standard deviation divided by the slope of the calibration curve. Moreover, the specificity of this aptasensor was evaluated. The developed aptasensor demonstrated high selectivity for ST compared to the other bacterial food-borne pathogens, as shown in Figure 5. However, it showed some cross-reaction with S. Corvallis, another distinct but less commonly encountered serovar of S. enterica that can contaminate foodstuffs.

Testing samples derived from chicken meat

The aptasensor that was developed was used to detect Salmonella in spiked meat juice samples derived from raw chicken meat, serving as a model for a naturally contaminated sample. MALDI-TOF analysis identified two species of contaminating bacteria, Aeromonas veronii and Kurthia gibsonii in some cultured samples, but these components of the background microflora did not interfere with the analysis.

The developed GO–BSA aptasensor successfully detected ST in spiked chicken meat samples, providing quantitative results with recovery rates ranging from 101.67% to 117.19%. Its performance was concordant with those of ISO 6579-1 culture and quantitative real-time PCR (based on a ttrRSBC plasmid standard curve, with an amplification efficiency of 95% and R2 = 0.9933; Supplementary Figure S1), confirming the reliability of the aptasensor in comparison with established reference methods, as shown in Table 1.

Stability test

Supplementary Figure S2 illustrates the detection performance of the developed aptasensor, measured from day 0 (D0) up to day 90 (D90) at various time points. The results indicated that the designed aptasensor could maintain its detection performance (≥ 90% of the initial value) for up to 28 days after preparation. From 35 days onward, a gradual decrease in detection performance was observed, which was significantly lower than that from D0 to D28. From D42 onward, the detection performance declined significantly until D90.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated the successful development of a graphene oxide–bovine serum albumin (GO–BSA)-based electrochemical aptasensor for the ultrasensitive detection of ST. Unlike many previous reports that focused on the discovery of new aptamers, our approach intentionally employed a previously validated aptamer (Joshi et al. 2009) to emphasize the novelty of the sensor fabrication platform and its laboratory applicability. Our approach involved the innovative use of GO/BSA nanocomposite. The detection principle of this platform is illustrated in Figure 1(A,B). The GO-BSA nanocomposite is a hybrid material formed by the functionalization of GO with BSA. The GO-BSA nanocomposite is typically synthesized through a simple mixing process, where GO nanosheets are dispersed in a BSA solution. Interactions between these two materials are mediated by hydrogen bonding or electrostatic forces, resulting in stable and homogeneous nanocomposite (Xia et al. 2016; Zupančič et al. 2021). Moreover, the material presents several advantages, including enhanced immobilization of aptamers on SPGE, prevention of GO detachment from the transducer surface, decreased nonspecific binding (contributing to improved sensitivity), and increased stability and reproducibility achieved by dispersing GO and BSA in solution. Furthermore, it has an excellent distribution on the WE and functions effectively with [Fe (CN)6]3-/4- as a redox mediator (Zhu et al. 2020; Biswas et al. 2022; Abedi et al. 2024; Devi et al. 2024; Rabiei et al. 2024). This modification was crucial for optimizing the aptamer’s binding efficiency, directly affecting the sensor’s sensitivity and accuracy, and enhancing its detection performance (Lan et al. 2024). Besides that, BSA enhances the biocompatibility of GO, which is beneficial when applied to biological analytes (Sivaselvam et al. 2020). Many studies have utilized BSA and GO to fabricate electrochemical aptasensors for various specific purposes (Kunene et al. 2021), but they differed from this study, as they did not mix the BSA and GO to form GO-BSA nanocomposite before dropping them onto the WE. The GO–BSA nanocomposite offered several advantages, including improved aptamer immobilization, enhanced electrode stability, and reduced nonspecific interactions. These properties contributed directly to the high sensitivity (LOD 3 CFU/mL in culture) and reproducibility of the platform, confirming its potential for application in food safety testing.

The EDC/NHS coupling method activated the –COOH groups of the GO/BSA nanocomposite, facilitating the formation of covalent bonds with NH2-modified aptamers. In this process, EDC reacts with the carboxyl groups present on GO and BSA, forming an unstable O-acylisourea intermediate. Subsequently, the NHS interacts with this intermediate, converting it into a stable NHS-ester. Following this conversion, the NH2 group of the aptamer conducts a nucleophilic attack on the NHS-ester, resulting in the formation of a stable amide (-CONH-) bond. This critical step effectively immobilizes the aptamer onto the GO/BSA nanocomposite, which is paramount for optimizing the detection performance of the aptasensor (Mallakpour and Madani 2015; Oberhaus et al. 2020).

Following the immobilization of aptamers, certain surface areas remain unoccupied, including unreacted NHS-esters or –COOH groups on the GO/BSA nanocomposite. Blocking these sites using BSA is essential to mitigate nonspecific binding and minimize background noise. The application of BSA establishes a protective layer that inhibits the adsorption of other molecules (Jeyachandran et al. 2010). For the detection step, the ST interacted with the prepared aptasensor under the optimized conditions. Then, the signal was measured using electrochemical sensing principles to monitor the electron exchange of the redox mediator [Fe(CN)6]3-/4-. When ST was present, it bound to the immobilized aptamer on the WE, blocking redox electron transfer and resulting in a reduced current peak observed in DPV. Conversely, when there was no ST contamination, the DPV signal remained relatively stable, which was higher than that of the ST-contaminated sample, as shown in Figure 1(C,E). The pre-treatment of the electrode is a crucial step in the development of an electrochemical aptasensor by removing contaminants, improving sensitivity and selectivity, and optimizing surface properties (Chetankumar and Swamy 2020; Shergill et al. 2022). In our study, the electrodes were pre-treated with 1 M H2SO4 and subjected to cyclic voltammetry (CV) scanning (Dekleva et al. 2024). This procedure facilitated the removal of contaminants and the hydration of the electrode surface, which could improve the detection performance and reproducibility of our aptasensor.

Key elements and conditions were optimized to reach the maximum detection performance of the designed aptasensor. The highest ΔI at 0.25 mg/mL GO indicates optimal surface area and electron transfer. At low GO concentrations, BSA dominates the nanocomposite matrix, resulting in poor conductivity and a limited electrochemical response. In contrast, higher GO concentrations likely cause aggregation and poor film uniformity due to insufficient BSA stabilization, reducing aptamer accessibility. This confirms that the GO: BSA ratio critically influences the nanocomposite’s stability, aptamer immobilization efficiency, and overall sensor performance (Chiticaru et al. 2019).

An aptamer concentration of 5 µM provided optimal surface density, as lower concentrations resulted in insufficient aptamer immobilization and weak target binding. In comparison, higher concentrations caused steric hindrance, improper orientation, or multilayer formation. Aptamers must not only be immobilized but also maintain proper folding and spatial orientation to recognize and bind the target epitope (Oberhaus et al. 2020). This demonstrates the importance of balancing surface density for optimal binding efficiency (White et al. 2008).

An immobilization time of 60 min provided sufficient time for stable covalent attachment of aptamers to the GO/BSA-modified surface via EDC/NHS chemistry. Shorter immobilization times (30 min) resulted in incomplete immobilization, whereas longer times (90–120 min) led to signal plateau or decline, likely due to multilayer formation, hydrolysis of NHS esters, or disrupted aptamer folding. Optimal immobilization can balance efficient surface coupling and aptamer orientation (Oberhaus et al. 2020).

The optimal incubation time between the aptamer-modified sensor and ST (30 min) indicated that aptamer–target binding reached equilibrium within this period. Shorter times (10–20 min) gave weaker signals due to insufficient binding time. In contrast, longer incubation times did not improve the signal and could cause non-specific binding (Mei et al. 2022). Aptamer–bacteria binding follows Langmuir-like kinetics, meaning it increases with time and then levels off when saturation is reached (Babaie et al. 2024; Léguillier et al. 2024).

The electrochemical aptasensor demonstrated excellent performance under optimized conditions. It achieved an LOD of 3 CFU/mL for ST, which is lower than the LOD of previous graphene-based DPV assays (10 CFU/mL). This 70% improvement in detection sensitivity demonstrates the advantage of our electrode modification strategy, which enhances signal reproducibility and detection efficiency in bacterial culture sample as shown in Table 2.(Dinshaw et al. 2017; Appaturi et al. 2020; Muniandy et al. 2022). This LOD was calculated based on the calibration equation, as previously explained (NATA 2012). Nevertheless, the aptamer used in this platform was specifically selected to target the outer membrane protein of ST (Joshi et al. 2009). This aptasensor may detect fractions of the protein released from the bacterial cell, resulting in very low LOD, such as 3 CFU/mL or 0.015 cells/test (5 µL).

In this study, the detection performance of the developed aptasensor was demonstrated using spiked samples of meat juice extracted from chicken meat samples that simulated realistic conditions. The aptasensor effectively identified ST even at 106.88 CFU/mL in the presence of background microflora, including Aeromonas veronii and Kurthia gibsonii. This indicated that the aptasensor has the potential for practical applications in food safety monitoring. The specificity of the aptasensor was verified, as it exhibited minimal cross-reactivity with other pathogens (Supplementary Figure S3). Nevertheless, the LOD achieved with the test increased from 3 CFU/mL in bacterial culture samples to ∼ 15 CFU/mL in samples derived from chicken meat. This increase likely resulted from matrix interference, such as from proteins, fats, and ions, that hinder aptamer-target interactions or redox mediator access. Implementing pre-concentration steps, such as aptamer-coated magnetic nanoparticles, may mitigate this limitation in future applications. However, this test only used 5 µL of analyte. This means that it can detect ST in a sample with as few as 1 to 2 cells (Ma et al. 2014; Fathi et al. 2021). To address potential interference, it is advisable to implement a suitable sample preparation procedure before conducting the test. For instance, applying magnetic particles in conjunction with the aptamer may facilitate the pre-concentration of target bacteria, thereby enhancing sensitivity in complex sample analyses (Joshi et al. 2009; Rizzotto et al. 2023; Su et al. 2023).

Specificity tests conducted with non-target bacteria substantiated the robustness of the aptasensor in accurately distinguishing ST from other pathogens that are commonly found in contaminated chicken meat (Klaharn et al. 2022). Notably, cross-reactivity with SC was observed, including sharing similar current peaks with some of the five field isolates of ST (Supplementary Figure S3). This is likely to be attributable to the selected aptamers targeting outer membrane proteins of ST, which are a conserved component among Salmonella spp. (Joshi et al. 2009). Previous investigations have documented similar cross-reactivity with other Salmonella serovars (Dinshaw et al. 2017; Appaturi et al. 2020). In fact, this characteristic may facilitate the development of a detection tool for Salmonella spp. where serovar specificity is not a requisite. Nevertheless, this aptasensor exhibits exceptional selectivity for ST while demonstrating some cross-reactivity with SC and no interaction with other bacterial species. This fabricated aptasensor may be particularly useful as a screening tool for on-site testing.

Despite the promising results, some limitations need to be addressed. The shelf life of the prepared aptasensor was relatively short, although it fell within the same range as that reported in a previous study (Muniandy et al. 2019). The designed aptasensor maintained an average ΔI of approximately 90% for 28 days. The observed degradation beyond 28 days may primarily be attributed to suboptimal humidity control during storage. The aptasensors were kept in a zip-lock plastic bag with desiccant in a refrigerator maintained at 4–8 °C. This may have provided a suitable temperature, but not humidity. Refrigeration at 4–8 °C slows temperature-related degradation, but zip-lock storage with desiccants alone might not provide sufficient protection to prevent water molecule adsorption and oxidation of GO. On the other hand, the use of protective coatings or encapsulation methods can further enhance stability by preventing environmental interference (Guo et al. 2021; Behrent et al. 2024). Humidity can impact several components of graphene-based electrodes through adsorption, interlayer expansion, and oxidation. Water molecules absorb onto the graphene surface and silver/silver chloride, altering its electrical performance. This adsorption can lead to unintentional doping effects, which degrade the device’s stability and performance over time. Moreover, it may cause interlayer expansion, leading to electrode swelling and disrupting electrode conductivity. Moreover, graphene is susceptible to oxidation under humid conditions, which can lead to structural degradation (Bae et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2016; 2021; Kawabata et al. 2024) These aspects require further improvements for future use and commercial production. The sensor’s performance under varied storage conditions should be evaluated and optimized. Moreover, the packaging of the prepared aptasensor needs to be designed and optimized to improve its stability over extended periods and support widespread adoption.

This study should be regarded as a proof-of-concept demonstration rather than a fully validated diagnostic method. Several limitations are acknowledged. First, the specificity evaluation included only one ST reference strain and a single non-Typhimurium serovar. Expanding the panel to multiple field isolates is essential for confirming robustness across natural strain diversity. Second, although spiked chicken meat samples provide realistic simulation of contamination scenarios, they do not fully represent naturally contaminated field samples. Comprehensive field validation with larger sample sizes, collected from farms and markets, will be critical to establish diagnostic accuracy, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy under real-world conditions. Third, the shelf life of the aptasensor was limited to 28 days, which may be sufficient for laboratory use but requires improvement for commercial deployment. Optimizing packaging, storage conditions, and surface stabilization strategies should therefore be a future focus.

Despite these limitations, this work provides a strong foundation for the further development of GO–BSA nanocomposite-based aptasensors. The successful modification of the nanocomposites has demonstrated significant potential in enhancing sensor sensing performance, which can be extended to various electrochemical sensing applications. The introduction of carboxylation-like properties on graphene oxide of GO-/BSA nanocomposite represents a novel methodology for developing graphene-based aptasensors, with potential implications in diverse fields, including environmental monitoring and veterinary diagnostics.

By benchmarking against ISO culture and real-time PCR, and by demonstrating applicability in a simulated food matrix, we have positioned this platform as a promising early-stage technology for rapid foodborne pathogen detection. The next phase of research will involve scaling up validation to include multiple Salmonella serovars and field-collected samples, as well as integrating the aptasensor into portable point-of-care devices. Together, these advances will enable the transition of this platform from laboratory proof-of-concept to a practical tool for improving food safety surveillance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we successfully developed and demonstrated a proof-of-concept electrochemical aptasensor based on a graphene oxide–bovine serum albumin (GO–BSA) nanocomposite for detecting Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium. By adopting a previously validated aptamer, our work focused on the novelty of the sensor fabrication process and its laboratory applicability. The GO–BSA nanocomposite enhanced aptamer immobilization, reduced nonspecific binding, and improved electrode stability, resulting in high sensitivity with a detection limit as low as 3 CFU/mL in pure culture.

Importantly, the aptasensor also performed effectively in a simulated chicken meat matrix, detecting contamination levels as low as 15 CFU/mL, and results were consistent with both ISO culture and real-time PCR methods. This highlights the feasibility of the platform for food safety applications, even though large-scale diagnostic validation has not yet been undertaken.

The present study should therefore be regarded as an initial step toward practical biosensor deployment. While limitations remain, including the need for expanded strain panels, naturally contaminated field samples, and improved long-term stability, this work provides a strong foundation for future development. Subsequent studies will focus on comprehensive field validation, comparative benchmarking with gold-standard diagnostic methods, and integration into portable devices. Ultimately, the GO–BSA aptasensor platform described here offers significant potential to evolve into a rapid, cost-effective, and field-deployable tool for foodborne pathogen surveillance.
