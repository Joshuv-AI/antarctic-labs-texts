Human-enhanced tsunami hazard in large lakes
Authors: Muhammad Naveed Zafar, Pierre Sabatier, Denys Dutykh, William Rapuc, Raphaël Paris, Andrea Piccin, Pascal Vuillemin, Flavio S. Anselmetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Worldwide, human-induced erosion enhances sediment delivery to lake deltas, increasing their susceptibility to collapse. In Lake Iseo, northern Italy, two millennia of intensified land use tripled sediment flux, oversteepening the delta and preconditioning it for failure. Here we show from geological records and numerical simulations that earthquakes in 1117 CE (Mw 6.5) and 1222 CE (Mw 6.0) triggered major subaqueous delta collapses, generating massive tsunamis with maximum run-ups of ~50 m and ~13 m, respectively. Historical accounts from the Vatican Library and archeological evidence support these reconstructions. Since the last collapse, eight centuries of human-induced erosion have rebuilt the delta, leaving shoreline populations highly vulnerable to future earthquake-triggered failures. A global lake synthesis showed that human-driven erosion has reached its highest levels since the Industrial Era, progressively overloading deltas worldwide; thus, similar catastrophic failures may become more likely in the future. These findings reveal a persistent, human-amplified mechanism of lacustrine tsunami generation and intensity with major implications for hazard assessment.

Introduction

Worldwide, land use has drastically accelerated erosion1–4, resulting in increased sediment yield and accelerated delta progradation in downstream lakes5–7, which are especially vulnerable because of their steeper slopes, confined basins, and smaller spatial extents. Paleoenvironmental records show that since the Holocene, human activities such as agriculture, pastoralism, urban expansion, deforestation, and burning have been the main drivers of amplified soil erosion globally8–15, increasing sediment fluxes to deltas by up to 100-fold in some regions16. These abrupt and extensive sediment inputs have profoundly reshaped delta morphologies5, overloaded subaquatic slopes, and weakened structural stability, making deltas more prone to collapse under seismic and gravitational stress17,18.

Driven by intensified land use, multiple lacustrine delta collapses have been documented globally17,19–28. However, the role of anthropogenic sedimentation in tsunami hazards remains critically understudied, especially in tectonically active regions, where sustained sediment loading destabilizes deltaic slopes, preconditioning them for catastrophic failure during seismic events that could generate large tsunamis24,29–31.

In this work, we examine the tsunamigenic consequences of long-term human-driven sediment accumulation in Lake Iseo (60.9 km²), one of the largest lakes in northern Italy. By integrating geophysical, sedimentological, historical, and archeological data with advanced numerical modeling, we reconstruct two medieval delta collapses and assess their tsunamigenic potential. This multidisciplinary investigation highlights how sustained anthropogenic pressure can lead to repeated delta collapses, which are capable of generating devastating tsunamis over relatively short timescales. Our findings underscore the urgent need to incorporate human-driven sediment dynamics into tsunami hazard assessments in densely inhabited lacustrine environments, particularly those in tectonically active regions.

Results

The human footprint on Lake Iseo’s Delta

Lake Iseo (Italy) is located at the downstream end of Val Camonica (Fig. 1), one of the largest valleys of the southern European Alps. The Lake Iseo catchment (1777 km2) is characterized by steep topography and is drained by the Oglio River (Fig. 1), which inflows the lake from the north, supplying substantial sediment that forms a large delta. Extensive human activity in the catchment is well documented by archeological, historical, palynological, and paleoenvironmental studies32–37. Since the Roman period, intensified deforestation, agriculture, and pastoralism have significantly increased hillslope erosion, resulting in a threefold rise in sediment flux to the lake compared with earlier Holocene background levels32. The Val Camonica region has experienced moderate to high seismic activity due to the complex collisional and postcollisional phases of the Alpine orogeny38. Several destructive earthquakes have been recorded over the last two millennia39. Notably, two major medieval earthquakes, the 1117 CE Verona earthquake (Mw 6.5)39,40 and the 1222 CE Brescia earthquake (Mw 6.0)39,41, affected the Lake Iseo region, with estimated macroseismic intensities of approximately VI–VII42 and V–VI43, respectively. They triggered massive subaqueous delta collapses in Lake Iseo17 and induced minor slope failure, likely along the escarpment between Monte Isola and the eastern shore44. According to detailed sediment core and seismic reflection analyses, these delta collapses are recorded as the thickest mass-wasting deposits (MWDs, or megaturbidites) in the deep basin of Lake Iseo17. Reconstruction of catastrophic tsunami events

To assess the tsunami hazards associated with both medieval delta collapses, we reconstructed the landslides and resulting tsunamis by quantifying the event deposits and conducting high-resolution numerical modeling. The reconstruction was based on high-resolution LiDAR topographic and multibeam bathymetric datasets acquired in 2002 by Regione Lombardia and the Istituto Nazionale di Oceanografia e di Geofisica Sperimentale (OGS) within the Italian National Geological Mapping Program (CARG)45,46. The present-day high-resolution bathymetry was used as a geomorphological analogue for the pre-failure delta morphology because sustained sedimentation following the medieval failures largely infilled the delta front and obscured the original landslide scars. This was done assuming that continued sediment delivery after the medieval failures rebuilt the delta along a similar depositional pathway.

Based on this framework, the post-failure delta morphology was reconstructed in GIS by removing areas of sediment accumulation and restoring a plausible failure-scar morphology. This reconstruction was guided by large-scale bathymetric trends, erosional features, and the present-day morphology of the Oglio Delta front. Landslide source volumes were then estimated as the difference between the reconstructed post-failure surface and the modern bathymetric surface. The reconstruction was iteratively refined until the estimated source volumes matched the volumes of the mapped mass-wasting deposits identified from seismic reflection profiles and sediment-core data in the deep basin, yielding source volumes of 6.2 × 107 m3 for the 1117 CE event and 1.0 × 107 m3 for the 1222 CE event (Fig. 2a, d). We then simulated landslide dynamics using a viscoplastic rheological model assuming a single-event failure mechanism. This assumption is supported by the sedimentological structure of the deep-basin event deposits, which comprise an erosive sandy base interpreted as a high-energy remobilization phase, followed by a single upward-fining depositional episode without evidence for repeated grading or multiple stacked subevents17. This unit is overlain by thick homogeneous deposits interpreted as suspension settling associated with basin-wide seiche oscillations47, with an upper fine-clay interval representing the final stage of suspension settling.

Details of the modeling approach and parameter selection are provided in the Methods section. The temporal evolution of landslide thickness, from initiation to final deposition in the deep basin, is shown in Fig. 2b, e. Both landslides originated at the Oglio Delta, but their final depositional geometries differ slightly. During the 1222 CE event, a greater portion of the mass propagated farther into the basin compared to the 1117 CE event, likely due to greater accommodation space, as the 1117 CE deposits had already filled the northeastern deep basin with substantial sediment a century earlier. Moreover, the 1222 CE landslide had relatively high water content and a large proportion of coarse sediment (Supplementary Fig. 2), resulting in a relatively low shear strength. This allowed the material to travel more rapidly (Fig. 2e), with most of the sediment reaching farther into the deep basin. The modeled deposit thicknesses and their spatial distributions strongly agree with those inferred from seismic profiles and sediment core data for both events (Fig. 2c, f), thus validating the rheological assumption and parameterization of our landslide modeling approach.

Following this validation, we simulate the resulting tsunamis based on time-dependent changes in water depth induced by landslide motion. We calculated tsunami wave propagation and inundation by using a nonlinear hydrodynamic model for both events at a 10 m spatial resolution. Details of the tsunami models, Froude number estimation, and sensitivity analyses of dispersion and landslide initiation depth are provided in the Methods section. The maximum tsunami wave heights over the arrival times for both events, along with the corresponding run-up heights and inundation distances at the marked locations, are shown in Fig. 3. Our simulations reveal that the medieval delta collapses in Lake Iseo generated some of the largest subaquatic landslide-induced tsunamis ever inferred in a lacustrine setting. The 1117 CE event produced wave heights greater than 50 m in the northern part of the lake (Fig. 3a, c). This massive tsunami wave inundated nearby shores within just one minute and propagated up to ~2.7 km inland (Fig. 3d). The subsequent collapse in 1222 CE also generated a large tsunami (Fig. 3b), with waves peaking at ~13 m (Fig. 3c) and inundation extending up to ~1 km inland (Fig. 3d). Both tsunamis propagated rapidly across Lake Iseo and were strongly influenced by Monte Isola, the central island, which acts as a natural barrier, splitting the waves and partially shielding the cities behind it. Historical and archeological tsunami evidence

Our numerical tsunami reconstructions are strongly supported by historical and archeological evidence. A vivid account from a 12th-century manuscript48 preserved in the Vatican Library describes the catastrophic 1117 CE event, which states on folio 184r: “Lacus quoque adeo excreverunt ut in villis iuxta positis habitantes paucissimi ab hoc periculo evaderent. Multa quoque castella et arces cum interitu habitantium miserabiliter diruerunt.” meaning, “The lake (Iseo) overflowed so much that very few of the inhabitants of the nearby villages escaped this danger. Many castles and fortresses also collapsed miserably, with the destruction of their inhabitants”. Detailed information on individual buildings is limited, making it difficult to validate the exact tsunami run-up and inundation. However, archeological evidence from the Pieve church of Sant’Andrea d’Iseo provides useful constraints46. The church underwent major reconstruction shortly after the 1117 CE event, involving both its lower and upper parts. Whether this damage resulted from seismic shaking, tsunami impact, or both cannot be determined. Our simulations indicate that the 1117 CE tsunami reached the shoreline in front of the church with a maximum wave height of ~3.5 m and an inundation distance of 165 m, sufficient to inundate the church area.

Additionally, archeological investigations have documented extensive structural damage around Lake Iseo’s shore following the 1222 CE earthquake49, supporting the occurrence of another important tsunami event. Together, these historical and archeological pieces of evidence for both events strongly support our comprehensive numerical reconstruction, confirming that delta collapses triggered two exceptionally large catastrophic tsunamis in Lake Iseo over a century apart.

We also investigated the potential preservation of tsunami deposits along Lake Iseo’s shoreline. However, extensive human modification has largely erased natural sedimentary archives. The only remaining undisturbed site, a peatland at the southern tip near Iseo, was cored at multiple locations using a Russian corer. Numerical models, however, indicate minimal wave heights (<1 m) at this site (Fig. 3) due to wave diffraction around Monte Isola, which splits incoming waves, resulting in indirect and attenuated wave propagation toward the southern tip. Consequently, no identifiable tsunami deposits were found at this site, which is consistent with the findings of Zafar et al.50, highlighting the importance of wave height and directivity in controlling the spatial distribution of tsunami deposits along lake shores. Other factors, such as localized sedimentation dynamics, erosion, historical peatland exploitation or undocumented disturbances, may also explain the apparent absence of deposits on land.

Discussion

A persistent legacy of tsunami hazard

Land use, the primary driver of increased delta instability at Lake Iseo during the medieval period, has intensified significantly in modern times. After a brief decline following the collapse of the 1222 CE, notably during the Black Death, land use resumed and escalated sharply from 1661 CE onward, peaking during the Industrial era and continuing today32 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Thus, over the past eight centuries, sustained anthropogenic erosion has progressively amplified sediment delivery to the Oglio Delta17,32, increasing its vulnerability to future collapse under seismic or gravitational forces.

Today, the shores of Lake Iseo are among the most densely developed in the Italian Alps, with numerous cities, marinas, and extensive tourist infrastructure. Additionally, Monte Isola, the largest inhabited lake island in Europe, attracts a significant amount of tourist activity. A tsunami comparable in magnitude to the catastrophic events that struck Lake Iseo during the medieval period would cause severe devastation today, given the region’s dense population and shoreline development. The simulations suggest that wave run-up would inundate large sections of the current shoreline, including urban areas and the inhabited shores of Monte Isola (Fig. 3). Critically, warning times for near-field locations would be negligible: waves generated by a collapse of the Oglio Delta would reach the northern shore in less than one minute (Fig. 3). In more distant areas, an early warning system might offer a brief window for evacuation to higher ground, but it would remain insufficient for an effective response within such a short time frame.

The legacy of intensive agro-pastoral activities in the Italian Alps32,36,51,52 affected not only Lake Iseo during the earthquakes of the 12–13th centuries but also other major alpine lakes in the region. Although the southern European Alps have been less systematically studied than the northern and western Alps are, the presence of very thick megaturbidites in the deep basins of Lakes Garda53 and Como25 suggests that steep, sediment-overloaded slopes in both lakes also collapsed during medieval seismic activity, and historical records confirm catastrophic tsunami activity in the region’s lakes40,48. Today, the large perialpine lakes of northern Italy (Garda, Como, and Iseo) feature some of Europe’s most densely developed freshwater shorelines, home to more than 370,000 permanent residents54 and welcome tens of millions of visitors annually. Ongoing human-driven erosion in the Italian Alps, which peaked in the 18–19th centuries at its highest levels since the early Holocene52, together with active fault systems43, continues to overload steep deltaic slopes, increasing the likelihood of major tsunamigenic collapses.

This human amplified tsunami hazard is not confined to the southern European Alps. Numerous other alpine lakes, including Lake Geneva22,24,29, Lake Sils30, Lake Brienz18,20, and Lake Lucerne21,23, have experienced recurrent tsunamigenic delta collapses during periods of intensified human-induced erosion triggered by seismic shaking, spontaneous slope failure, or external disturbances such as rockfalls. Over the past several centuries, human-driven erosion, primarily from agro-pastoral activities, has reached levels unmatched in the Holocene, driving substantial sediment accumulation in alpine lake deltas13–15 and increasing future tsunami hazards.

Similar mechanisms have also been observed outside Europe. For example, in south-central Alaska, Lakes Eklutna, Skilak, and Kenai host multiple deltas that have undergone repeated collapse, with the most recent large tsunamigenic delta collapses triggered by the 1964 megathrust earthquake19,31. In New Zealand, delta collapses have also been identified as the primary source of repeated lake tsunamis27.

All of these worldwide tsunamigenic delta collapses have been attributed to sediment overloading from catchment erosion, so the interplay of land use, sediment overloading, and recurrent seismic or spontaneous delta failures observed at Lake Iseo is a global issue. Soil erosion from human activities, as evidenced by a global synthesis of 632 lake-watershed records, is now at its highest level since the Industrial Era14, thereby overloading deltaic slopes worldwide. Our multidisciplinary approach supports a holistic hazard assessment of lacustrine environments. Given that the hidden legacy of human land use can lead to large tsunamis, communities around Lake Iseo and in similar lacustrine settings worldwide can better prepare for these low-probability but high-consequence events. The cascading effects observed in Lake Iseo serve as both a cautionary tale and a call to action, underscoring that today’s hazard assessment and land management strategies will shape the disasters of tomorrow.

Methods

Modeling of the Iseo Delta collapse and tsunami

Tsunamis generated by delta collapses in Lake Iseo were modeled in two stages using a one-way coupling approach. First, the dynamics and time evolution of the landslide were simulated using the landslide model. Second, the simulated landslide evolution was converted into time-dependent bathymetric changes that served as inputs to the tsunami model. The tsunami model accounts for these time-varying lake-bed deformations, which are transferred directly to the overlying water column, leading to the generation and propagation of tsunami waves. In this framework, the influence of landslide motion on the water column is explicitly considered, whereas feedback from the generated waves onto the landslide dynamics is neglected. However, hydrodynamic effects such as viscous drag and added-mass forces are incorporated into the landslide modeling stage prior to coupling with the tsunami model.

Landslide modeling

We simulated delta failure using the two-layer, depth-averaged (2HD) viscoplastic debris-flow model BingClaw, which applies a Herschel–Bulkley rheology and is specifically developed for cohesive subaqueous landslides55. BingClaw has been extensively validated against geological observations, including the 8150 BP Storegga landslide56 and subaqueous landslides in French Alpine lakes47,57. The model represents the failed mass as two layers: an upper plug layer, in which the sediment moves en bloc without internal shear deformation, and a lower shear layer, where deformation occurs once the applied shear stress exceeds the yield strength. Mass balance equations are solved over the entire depth (both layers), whereas momentum equations are solved separately for each layer via a Godunov-type finite-volume scheme. For a detailed description of BingClaw, see Kim et al.55.

Landslide dynamics and runout are controlled by several parameters that describe the rheology of the flow. We chose the rheological parameters that gave the best match to the observed runout distance, as well as to the thickness and spatial distribution of the final deposits, guided by the sensitivity analysis of Kim et al.55. Runout distance is most sensitive to yield strength55. In the present study, yield strength values were constrained via observational runout distances from seismic profiles (Fig. 2c, f). The sediment density and water content were obtained directly from the core data. Tsunami modeling

Time-dependent bathymetric changes from the landslide model were used to drive wave generation in the tsunami model. We used the two-dimensional depth-averaged nonlinear shallow-water equation (NSWE) model, GeoClaw58, to simulate tsunami wave propagation and inundation. GeoClaw is an open-source, widely used tsunami model that has been validated against the National Tsunami Hazard Mitigation Program (NTHMP) benchmark problems59 and applied to numerous events with comparisons to tide gauges60 and geological data, including those from lakes47,50,57. GeoClaw employs a shock-capturing Godunov-type finite-volume scheme61,62 on rectangular grids with adaptive mesh refinement and handles wet/dry interfaces effectively during inundation. For a detailed description of GeoClaw, see LeVeque et al.63. Rectangular computational grids with a cell size of ~10 m were used to simulate tsunami wave propagation and inundation, with a Manning roughness coefficient of 0.03. The present-day reference lake level (185.44 m a.s.l.) was used, as there is no evidence of significant lake-level variations during the last ~2 kyr, also considering the nearly constant extent of the marsh area (Torbiere d’Iseo) at the southern edge of Lake Iseo.

Dispersive sensitivity analysis

To examine dispersion effects, we applied the dispersive tsunami model BoussClaw developed in the framework of modified Boussinesq equations, as suggested by Schäffer et al.67 and Schäffer and Madsen68. BoussClaw retains accurate dispersion for shorter-wavelength waves by incorporating dispersion terms in the momentum equations via operator splitting: the shallow-water equations are solved with a high-resolution finite-volume scheme, followed by dispersive terms solved with a finite-difference scheme. Wave breaking is represented by switching to the shallow-water equations when the wave-height–to–water-depth ratio exceeds 0.8. Further details are provided in Kim et al.69.

Landslide initiation depth sensitivity analysis

The tsunami simulations presented in Fig. 3 are based on the most plausible reconstructed source geometries (Fig. 2) given the present-day delta morphology. However, to evaluate the sensitivity of tsunami generation to landslide initiation depth, we also tested two alternative source scenarios, S1 and S2, for the 1117 CE event, in which the failed mass was initiated from depths of 45 m and 75 m along the delta front, respectively. Scenario S1 produced a maximum tsunami height of ~22 m, whereas Scenario S2 produced a maximum height of ~11 m (Supplementary Fig. 6).
