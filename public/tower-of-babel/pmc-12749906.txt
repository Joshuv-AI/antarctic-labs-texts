Intelligent trajectory tracking in autonomous plantation robots using PSO-tuned nonlinear fuzzy PID, fuzzy PID, and conventional PID controllers
Authors: Gashaye Lewtie Hailu, Tefera Terefe Yatayew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comparative analysis of three trajectory optimization controllers, particle swarm optimization (PSO)-tuned Nonlinear Fuzzy tuned proportional-integral-derivative (PID), Fuzzy PID, and Conventional PID, for a planting autonomous robot using a differential drive mobile robot (DDMR). The focus is on optimizing the trajectory tracking performance, minimizing positional and orientation errors, and ensuring stability and accuracy in seed planting applications. The PSO algorithm is used to tune the parameters of the Nonlinear Fuzzy PID controller, thereby enhancing its ability to adapt to the system’s dynamic and nonlinear nature. The performance of the three controllers is evaluated using various reference trajectories, including rectangular pulses, squares, straight lines, and circular paths, simulated in MATLAB/Simulink. Key performance metrics, such as tracking error, Absolute integral error (IAE), and integral square error (ISE), are analyzed to assess the effectiveness of each controller. Results demonstrate that PSO-FPID achieves significantly lower tracking errors and superior orientation control. For straight-line trajectories, tracking errors were 0.525, 1.529, and 2.992 for PSO-FPID, FPID, and PID, respectively, confirming the advantage of the proposed approach. Overall, the PSO-tuned FPID controller delivers more precise and reliable trajectory tracking, leading to effective seed placement and improved agricultural efficiency.Keywords: Agricultural robot, Differential drive wheel, Seed sowing, Trajectory tracking, Fuzzy logic control (FLC), Fuzzy PID (FPID), Obstacle detection, PID, PSO FPIDSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The integration of autonomous robotic systems into agriculture has gained significant attention in recent years due to the increasing demand for efficient, precise, and cost-effective farming solutions. Autonomous agricultural robots are designed to address labor shortages, enhance productivity, and reduce operational costs, particularly in tasks such as seed sowing, weeding, and harvesting. Among these tasks, row seed sowing presents unique challenges, including maintaining accurate trajectory control, adapting to varying field conditions, and ensuring uniform seed distribution.

Traditional control techniques, such as PID controllers, have been widely used in robotics for their simplicity and effectiveness. However, their performance is often limited in nonlinear and dynamically varying environments like agricultural fields. To overcome these limitations, advanced control strategies, including fuzzy logic controllers (FLCs), have been proposed. FLCs provide robust performance by handling system nonlinearities and uncertainties, making them suitable for autonomous agricultural applications. However, determining optimal FLC parameters can be computationally intensive and time-consuming.

A differential-drive mobile robot equipped with advanced control systems can significantly enhance trajectory tracking and ensure uniform seed placement. Nonlinear Fuzzy PID controllers, combined with PSO, offer a robust solution to address the complexities of dynamic environments, ensuring optimal control performance under varying conditions.

Several studies1–8, highlight the application of PSO for tuning controllers in mobile robots and autonomous vehicles. PSO has been used to optimize fuzzy PD + I controllers1,9, PID controllers1,4, and fractional-order PID controllers5, significantly enhancing path tracking, trajectory optimization, and dynamic performance under varying conditions. While PSO improves system stability and robustness, it also presents challenges, such as computational complexity and the need for careful parameter tuning. The use of derivative filters and adaptive fuzzy PID controllers6,7, further enhances dynamic performance, but their real-time application is limited by the high computational demands of PSO. Moreover, model-free and fractional-order PID controllers offer greater flexibility but require more complex tuning strategies to achieve efficient and accurate control.

Other studies10–15, , focus on advanced control strategies for autonomous mobile robots. Approaches such as backstepping10 and adaptive fuzzy PID11 improve trajectory tracking, while CS-algorithm-optimized fuzzy PID controllers12,13 refine the process further. PSO-based navigation14, fuzzy PD + I control1, sliding mode15, and MPC16,17 methods contribute to robust trajectory tracking and optimization. However, these strategies are limited by computational complexity and real-time performance demands.

Research on path planning for mobile robots18–21, , emphasizes PSO-based strategies for navigation and obstacle avoidance18, multi-objective optimization for collaborative control19, and hybrid control methods for differential-drive robots20. Nonlinear disturbance observer-based PID controllers with PSO tuning21 offer robust control in dynamic environments, yet challenges like computational load and real-time implementation remain.

Comparative studies22–26, assess various control strategies, including NMPC and fuzzy PID controllers, which offer high precision but are computationally intensive22. Modified supervisory (SUP) controllers improve stability but struggle in dynamic environments23. Kinematic and dynamic modeling enhances navigation but lacks flexibility in complex scenarios24. Decentralized fuzzy PID control with PSO tuning boosts autonomy but faces scalability and real-time application challenges25,27.

This study investigates the application of PSO-tuned Nonlinear Fuzzy PID control for trajectory optimization in an autonomous agricultural robot. By integrating the adaptive capabilities of fuzzy logic with the optimization power of PSO, the proposed approach addresses nonlinearities and external disturbances, enhancing stability and accuracy in seed sowing operations. The research aims to demonstrate the effectiveness of this hybrid control strategy in improving agricultural efficiency while reducing operational costs and environmental impact.

Controller design of differential drive mobile robot

The controller design for a Differential Drive Mobile Robot (DDMR) must meet several key requirements and specifications to ensure effective performance. These include accurate trajectory tracking to follow predefined paths, system stability under varying conditions, real-time responsiveness to reference changes and disturbances, and robustness against uncertainties such as wheel slip, load variations, or unstructured environments. Additionally, the controller must adhere to the robot’s non-holonomic constraints and, optionally, incorporate obstacle avoidance for autonomous navigation. The control system takes desired linear and angular velocities as input variables and generates control signals (such as angular velocities or PWM signals) for the right and left wheels as outputs. The primary control objectives are to minimize tracking error, maintain smooth velocity and orientation transitions, and ensure convergence and overall system stability. Performance is evaluated based on key metrics such as settling time, overshoot, steady-state error, and control effort.

For the trajectory control of the DDMR, two identical FLC is considered to control two series DC motors which are distance and angle FLC, in right and left side of the DDMR respectively. The PID controller is used to control the rotational speed of the right and left DC motors. Indirectly, it controls the angular speed of the DDMR wheel with the help of the values of the constants Kp, Ki, and Kd. Kp depends on present errors, Ki depends on the accumulation of past errors, and Kd is a prediction of future errors.

PID tuning method using fuzzy logic controller for DDMR

For the trajectory control of the DDMR, two identical FLC is considered to control two series DC motors which are distance and angle FLC, in right and left side of the DDMR respectively. The fuzzy PID controller integrates fuzzy logic into a conventional PID framework to enhance nonlinear system control and adaptability.

The fuzzy PID controller operates through four main stages: fuzzification, rule base, inference mechanism, and defuzzification. In fuzzification, crisp inputs like error and change in error are mapped into fuzzy sets using predefined membership functions. These fuzzy inputs are then evaluated using a set of linguistic IF-THEN rules that define the controller’s response. While fuzzy controllers can handle MIMO systems. The inference mechanism determines which rules are activated (fired) based on input membership values and combines the outputs of these rules into a single fuzzy set using aggregation. Finally, in the defuzzification stage, this aggregated fuzzy output is transformed into a crisp control signal, often using the center of gravity method, to be applied to the physical system.

To improve stability and accuracy over classical PID, a self-tuned FPID controller, illustrated in Fig. 3, is designed for the DDMR. It combines fuzzy auto-tuning and PID control to nonlinearly map input error e(t) and error rate Δe(t) to output gains ΔKp, ΔKi, and ΔKd via a fuzzy inference system. These correction parameters adjust the initial PID gains (Kp, Ki, Kd), which are then applied to control the DDMR’s linear and angular velocities (v and ω).

PSO-tuned FPID controller for a DDMR

The initial FPID design for DDMR trajectory control is time-consuming and imprecise. To enhance stability and accuracy, a PSO-tuned FPID controller is proposed, as shown in Fig. 4. PSO optimizes fuzzy tuning rules, PID gains, and Gaussian Mamdani rule parameters offline. It determines optimal centers, widths, fuzzy rules, and PID gains for the two FPID controllers’ distance and theta ensuring precise motion control.

Structure of PSO tuning fuzzy PID trajectory control of mobile robot.

Both FPID controllers use two input errors (distance and angle) and their error changes, with three PID gains (Kp, Ki, Kd) as outputs. Each FPID has 7 fuzzy sets, 21 membership functions, and 49 rules totaling 42 membership functions and 98 rules. Each fuzzy set uses Gaussian functions with 3 tunable parameters (left, center, right), totaling 126 parameters plus 6 PID gains, making 132 parameters. PSO optimizes these 132 parameters (membership functions and PID gains) in a 132-dimensional search space, where each particle represents a solution. Optimizing 132 parameters simultaneously in a nonlinear fuzzy PID controller using Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) presents a significant challenge due to the curse of dimensionality and the risk of getting trapped in local optima. To address these issues, this study employs Diversity Preservation Mechanisms, specifically Velocity Clamping, to maintain population diversity and enhance global search capability.

PSO begins with a group of randomly initialized particles in the solution space. Each particle updates its velocity and position based on its own experience (Pbest) and the best solution found by the swarm (Gbest). In each iteration, particles adjust their movements using both self-cognition and social learning in the N-dimensional space29–31.

PSO configuration

To ensure effective tuning of the nonlinear fuzzy PID controller and support reproducibility, the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm was configured with a population size of 100 particles to promote global exploration and minimize premature convergence. The maximum number of iterations was set to 500, allowing sufficient time for the swarm to converge toward an optimal solution. The weight coefficients used in this study was used from 0.9 to 0.4 to balance exploration in early iterations and exploitation in later ones. The acceleration coefficients followed an asynchronous learning strategy: the cognitive component c1​ decreased from 2.5 to 0.5, while the social component c2​ increased from 0.5 to 2.5, enhancing exploration initially and promoting convergence toward the global best in later stages. Velocity clamping was applied at 20% of each parameter’s dynamic range to ensure stable convergence and prevent particles from diverging too rapidly. Finally, the fitness function was formulated by combining position error, orientation error, and control effort, with carefully selected weights to align with the trajectory tracking priorities of the autonomous system.

The computational complexity of the PSO-FPID, FPID, and PID controllers varies significantly. The PSO-FPID controller incurs the highest computational load due to the iterative optimization of multiple fuzzy parameters using the Particle Swarm Optimization algorithm. Its simulation time grows with the number of particles, iterations, and tuning parameters, making it the most intensive method. The FPID controller has a moderate computational burden, as it involves fuzzy inference operations fuzzification, rule evaluation, and defuzzification without real-time optimization. In contrast, the conventional PID controller is the most computationally efficient, relying on fixed gains and simple arithmetic operations, which makes it ideal for real-time applications. Overall, the computational demand ranks as PID < FPID < PSO-FPID.

Conclusion

This paper presents the successful implementation of an autonomous mobile robot for planting in agricultural applications, using a Differential Drive Mobile Robot (DDMR) model with MATLAB/Simulink. A Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)-based algorithm for auto-tuning the parameters of a nonlinear fuzzy PID (FPID) controller is proposed. The PSO algorithm optimizes the fuzzy PID controller for the motion model of a nonholonomic differential drive seed-sowing robot. Various reference trajectories, including rectangular pulses, squares, straight lines, and circular paths, were used in the MATLAB simulations to assess the tracking performance. The proposed PSO-tuned FPID controller showed superior performance in trajectory tracking, with smaller tracking errors, reduced orientation errors, faster response, higher accuracy, and improved stability compared to both the fuzzy PID and conventional PID controllers. The simulation results demonstrate that the PSO FPID controller delivers smoother velocity control (UL and UR), produces lower trajectory tracking errors, and is more effective in obstacle avoidance. The results also highlight the robustness and adaptability of the PSO FPID controller in maintaining minimal position tracking errors, even under dynamic disturbances, making it an ideal solution for precise and reliable trajectory tracking in row planting applications.
