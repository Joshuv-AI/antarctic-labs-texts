Cell-free adipose tissue-derived stem cell extracts mediate immunosuppression of lymphocyte via cell cycle arrest
Authors: Witchayapon Kamprom, Patcharapa Tragoonlugkana, Rattanawan Tangporncharoen, Narong Chitchongyingcharoen, Piyada Permmee, Chatchai Pruksapong, Aungkura Supokawej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cell-free mesenchymal stem cell products have shown promise in reducing inflammation, alleviating tissue degeneration, and regulating immune responses. A recent study has reported that adipose tissue-derived stem cell (ADSC) extract possesses immunomodulatory capacity. However, its comparative efficacy against other cell-free products is not well documented. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism of ADSC extract is under intense investigation. We determined the immunosuppressive efficiency of ADSC extract and ADSC-concentrated conditioned medium (CCM) on T cell proliferation, T regulatory cell expansion, and T cell cycle progression. In addition, the comparative cytokine profiles of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM were investigated by cytokine array analysis. The ADSC extracts were superior in inhibiting T cell proliferation, promoting T regulatory cell expansion, and inducing activated T cell arrest at G0/G1 phase compared with ADSC-CCM. The inhibitory effect of ADSC extract on T cell proliferation was partly associated with control of cell cycle progression but not apoptosis induction. A halt in cell cycle progression of activated T lymphocyte after ADSC extract exposure was mediated via p21 and p27 activation. The cytokine profiling demonstrated that there were distinct patterns of immunoregulatory components in ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM, likely associated with their differing in immunoregulatory actions. The marked expression of TGF-β1 and IL-10 in the composition of ADSC extract implied their key roles in mediating immunosuppressive actions. IL-10 and TGF-β1 neutralization partly abrogated immunosuppressive capacities of ADSC extract. Together, ADSC extract may serve as an alternative source of immunomodulatory mediators with potential for development as cell-free products for therapeutic use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53336-3.

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) obtain trilineage differentiation potential and immunomodulation properties. Therefore, they have been regarded as a source for cell-based therapy in tissue regeneration and immunological-associated disorders1,2. Despite the evidence supporting immunosuppressive effects of MSCs, therapeutic actions are not always achieved. Several studies have demonstrated the plasticity of immunoregulation by transplanted MSCs3. The factors that possibly influence the immunoregulatory properties of MSCs include the rate of cell engraftment, cell viability, the capacity to migrate to the site of tissue injury, and the strength and type of inflammatory stimuli in vivo4–6. These factors add complexity to the use of MSCs as a cell-based therapeutic. Thus, several approaches are being investigated to prevent or overcome these limitations.

MSCs can produce plentiful immunomodulatory molecules, cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors which may alleviate degenerative and immune-associated diseases7. Cell-free MSC products are interesting and can be prepared into several product types such as conditioned medium8, cell extracts9,10, and extracellular vesicles11,12. However, many points of concern must be investigated prior to the future application of these cell-free products, including preparation techniques, biological roles, stability, storage, and transportation. Stem cell-derived extract, which contains a heterogeneous mixture of bioactive molecules, is an alternative for developing MSC-based cell-free products13. Intracellular extract is as effective as intact cells in alleviating myocardial infraction14, periodontal disease15 and other tissue injury16,17. Nowadays, approaches for extraction of intracellular components from various cells have been successfully developed. Candidate cell types for intracellular extraction include bone marrow cells, embryonic stem cells, adipose stem cells, and mesenchymal stem cells which exert their therapeutic effect on various conditions10,13. Therefore, it raises the possibility that cell-free extracts from MSCs may possess immune modulation capacity. Interestingly, our recent study has shown immunosuppressive functions of intracellular extracts from adipose tissue-derived stem cells (ADSCs) spheroids and ADSC monolayers. Both ADSC spheroid extract and ADSC monolayer extract could inhibit T cell proliferation and induce T regulatory cell expansion18. However, it is unclear whether intracellular ADSC extracts gain immunosuppressive effects equivalent to those of other ADSC-derived cell-free products. In addition, the underlying mechanisms of ADSC extracts in regulating T cell functions are not fully elucidated.

MSC conditioned medium is one of the cell-free products that contain various secretory factors for regulating immune cell activities19. Although the immunomodulatory roles of several MSC-derived cell-free products have been actively discussed, the underlying mechanisms may be different due to the distinct bioactive components. Accordingly, to consider MSC extract and other cell-free products as immunomodulatory therapeutics, the precise mechanisms need to be clarified. It is well established that MSCs mediate their therapeutic activity at least partially through the paracrine mechanism. Currently, several studies are focusing on MSC-based cell-free therapy; therefore, comparative analysis in therapeutic potential of cell-free products is essential for therapeutic uses20. Although intracellular extract contains mixtures of growth factors, chemokine, cytokine, and other bioactive molecules, certain intracellular proteins may not be efficiently secreted. In addition, some molecules in intracellular extract may play a distinct role compared to its conditioned medium which affects their therapeutic actions. Taken together, the current study investigated the comparative efficacy of ADSC extract and ADSC-concentrated conditioned medium (ADSC-CCM) on immune modulation including T cell proliferation, T regulatory cell induction, and T cell cycle progression. Additionally, the underlying effects of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM on regulating T lymphocyte functions were determined. We also analyzed 80 bioactive molecules that are associated with immune response and immunomodulation in the contents of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM. The study provides important insights into the immunomodulatory potential of ADSC-derived cell-free products and the related candidate bioactive factors. Knowledge gained from this study will help to better understand the immunologic mechanisms of ADSC extracts and its conditioned medium. Moreover, the successful generation of ADSC-derived cell-free extracts with high immunosuppressive potential might be developed as products for use in tissue regeneration and immunological-associated disorders in the future.

Materials and methods

Samples

The experimental procedures of this study were approved by the Ethical Committee for Human Research of the Mahidol University Central Institutional Review Board, and were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, The Belmont Report, CIOMS Guidelines, and the International Conference on Harmonization in Good Clinical Practice (MU-CIRB 2021/074.2603). Human adipose tissue samples and peripheral blood samples were obtained from healthy volunteers after receiving written informed consent. All experiments were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Isolation and culture of adipose tissue-derived stem cells

Adipose tissue samples were collected from seven healthy donors aged between 28 and 49 years by lipoaspiration technique. Adipose tissue-derived stem cells (ADSCs) were extracted from the stromal vascular fraction (SVF) of subcutaneous adipose tissue from abdomen and flank using enzymatic digestion following previously published procedure21. Briefly, lipoaspirate was washed with pre-warm phosphate-buffered saline, followed by digestion in 0.025% type I collagenase (Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, the cell pellet was harvested by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min and resuspended in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM-LG; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 1X glutamax (Gibco, USA), and 1X penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The cells were plated into 25 cm2 flasks at a density of 2⋅105/cm2. The cells are then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Non-adherent cells were removed after a 48-hour incubation and replaced with fresh complete DMEM medium. Thereafter, culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days throughout the entire culture period; cell morphology was observed by inverted phase-contrast microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

Immunophenotype of all ADSCs in the experiments was determined by flow cytometry. ADSCs were harvested by trypsinization, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in PBS at the density of 1⋅105 cells per 50 µl of PBS. Then, cells were incubated with use of the following antibodies: anti-CD34, anti-CD45, anti-CD90, anti-CD73, and anti-CD105 antibody (The antibodies are listed in Supplementary data, Table S1). The non-stained serve as isotype control. The expression profiles of cell surface molecules were determined by flow cytometry (BD Bioscience, USA). Percentage of positive cells was determined by setting the gate at fluorescence intensity greater than the 95th percentile of the fluorescence intensity from the isotype control.

To determine the trilineage differentiation potential, ADSCs were cultured in adipogenic induction medium, osteogenic induction medium, or chondrogenesis differentiation kit (Gibco, USA) at a density of 1⋅104/cm2. ADSCs that were cultured in complete DMEM medium served as undifferentiated ADSCs (negative control). After 2–3 weeks of culture to allow differentiation, the adipocytes derived from cultured ADSCs were determined by Oil red O (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) staining and osteogenic differentiation was assessed by production of bone matrix containing calcium crystals using Alizarin Red S (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) staining. After 3–4 weeks, chondrogenesis was detected by production of cartilage matrix stained with Alcian blue dye (Himedia, USA)22,23.

Preparation of intracellular ADSC extract

Cell disruption was achieved with a freeze-thawing method to extract biological products using a modification of the procedure previously described18. Briefly, 5 × 105 ADSCs were plated into 75 cm2 flasks containing 10 ml complete DMEM medium. The cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 h, followed by culturing in serum free medium for further 48 h. The confluent ADSC cultures at passage 3–6 were harvested and examined for cell viability using trypan blue exclusion assay. The viable cells were resuspended in normal saline at a density of 2⋅107 cells/ml. The cell suspensions were frozen in a -80 °C refrigerator for 16–18 h and thawed at 37 °C for 10 min. The freeze-thaw cycle was repeated three times. Total cell disruption was assessed by trypan blue positivity. Finally, clear supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 14,000 g, 4 °C for 15 min. Protein concentrations of ADSC extracts (n = 8) were assessed by Bradford protein assay. The extracts were stored at -80 °C and further experiments were conducted within no more than a month.

Preparation of ADSC-concentrated conditioned medium

To prepare ADSC concentrated conditioned medium (ADSC-CCM), 5 × 105 ADSCs were plated into 75 cm2 flasks containing 10 ml complete DMEM medium. The cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow cell attachment. At 80% confluency, cells were washed twice with 1X phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and cultured in serum-free DMEM medium for a further 48 h. Then ADSC conditioned medium was collected and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was harvested and filtered through 0.45 μm syringe filters. Finally, ADSC conditioned medium was concentrated by passing through 3 K ultra-spin columns (Merck Millipore, Germany) and centrifuged at 4500 g, 4 °C for 30 min. The retention was collected, while the flow-through was discarded. The centrifugation step was repeated 3 times. The concentrated conditioned medium was kept at -80 °C until required. Protein concentrations of ADSC-CCM (n = 8) were measured by Bradford protein assay.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation

Peripheral blood was freshly drawn from healthy donors who had given written informed consent. Subsequently, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using density gradient centrifugation. Peripheral blood was overlayed on lymphocyte separation medium (Biowest, France) at ratio 1:1. The suspensions were centrifuged at 500 g for 30 min. Then mononuclear cell layers were collected and washed twice with 1X PBS. The PBMCs were resuspended with complete Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 1X penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), and 1X glutamax (Gibco). The cells were cryopreserved in 95% fetal bovine serum and 5% dimethyl sulfoxide at a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/ml and stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

Lymphoproliferation assay

To determine the efficiency of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM on suppression of activated lymphocyte proliferation, the in vitro lymphocyte proliferation assay was performed. Numbers of live lymphocytes were monitored using a cell counting kit (CCK8; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The optimal concentration of a T-cell mitogen used in the experiment was determined following a previous report24. A final concentration of 1% phytohemagglutinin (PHA; Gibco, USA) was used for lymphocyte activation in all subsequent experiments (Supplementary data, Fig. S1). First, 1 × 104 PBMCs were stimulated with 1% PHA, a T-cell mitogen, in the presence of various concentrations of ADSC extract (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, or 800 µg/ml) or ADSC-CCM (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, or 800 µg/ml). PBMCs cultured without PHA treatment served as unstimulated controls, while PHA-stimulated PBMCs cultured alone served as positive controls. After 72 h of culture, changes in the number of living lymphocytes were determined by cell counting (CCK8; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Absorbance was measured at wavelength 450 nm. The data were presented as optical density and lymphocyte proliferation relative to that of positive controls.

Lymphocyte subpopulation analysis

To address whether ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM changed the immunophenotype of activated lymphocytes, 1 × 106 PBMCs were stimulated with 1% PHA (Gibco, USA) and cultured in the presence of 200 µg/ml ADSC extract or 200 µg/ml ADSC-CCM for 72 h. The cells were harvested and labeled with the following T cell antibodies: anti-CD3, anti-CD4, anti-CD8, anti-CD25, and anti-FOXP3 (The antibodies are listed in Supplementary data, Table S1). The percentages of T lymphocytes (CD3+ cells), CD4+T cells (CD3+CD4+ cells), CD8+T cells (CD3+CD8+ cells), and regulatory T cells (CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ cells) were determined by flow cytometry (BD Bioscience, USA). PHA-stimulated PBMCs cultured alone were used as controls.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle was assessed by identifying DNA contents with propidium iodine (PI) staining. 1 × 106 PBMCs were stimulated with 1% PHA (Gibco) and cultured with either 200 µg/ml ADSC extract or 200 µg/ml ADSC-CCM for 72 h. PHA-stimulated PBMCs cultured alone served as controls. To denature proteins, 1⋅106 lymphocytes were suspended in 70% ethanol for 12–24 h at 4 °C. The cells were centrifuged at 5000 g, 4 °C for 5 min to remove ethanol and washed once with 1X PBS. The cell pellet was resuspended with 1 ml PI staining buffer which consisted of 10 ng/ml PI, 100 µg/mL RNase A in PBS, and 0.1% Triton-X-100 in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were incubated in PI staining buffer for 30 min at 4 °C and analyzed using a FACS Canto II (BD Bioscience, USA) flow cytometer.

Apoptosis assay

Lymphocyte apoptosis was quantitated by FITC-annexin V/PI staining (BD Bioscience, USA) followed by flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, 1 × 106 PHA-stimulated PBMCs were treated with 200 µg/ml ADSC extract for 72 h. PHA-stimulated PBMCs that cultured alone served as controls. Cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 1X binding buffer at a concentration of 1⋅106 cells/ml. The cell suspensions (100 µl) were stained with 5 µl FITC-annexin V solution and 5 µl PI, then incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, 400 µl binding buffer was added to the cell suspensions which were then analyzed by flow cytometry using a FACS Canto II and FACS Diva software (BD Bioscience, USA). The amounts of early and late apoptosis were determined as the percentage of annexin V+PI− and annexin V+PI+ cells, respectively.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The cDNA synthesis used 1 µg RNA and iScript™ RT supermix (Bio-Rad, USA). For quantitative real-time PCR, the PCR reactions were performed in duplicate for each target gene. The PCR master mix consisted of 2 µl cDNA template, 0.5 µl of forward and reverse primers (10 µM each) of the gene of interest (The sequences of primers are listed in Supplementary data, Table S2), 2 µl nuclease free water, and 5 µl SYBR green PCR master mix (Applied Biosystem, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using CFX96™ Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA). The gene expression level was normalized with an internal reference gene, GAPDH, and calculated as the fold change of control.

Western blot analysis

To analyze cell cycle-associated proteins, the cells in experiments which included ADSC extract-treated PBMCs and untreated PBMC controls were harvested and lysed in 350 µl of 1X RIPA buffer (Sigma), supplemented with 1X protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The equal amount of protein (15 µg) was resolved by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, USA). These membranes were incubated in blocking solution (5% BSA in 0.1% TBST) at room temperature for 1 h to block non-specific binding sites followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C using the following primary antibodies (all from Cell Signaling Technology, USA): CKD2 (#2546; 1:1000, 33 kDa), p21 Waf1/Cip1 (#2947; 1:1000, 21 kDa), p27 Kip1 (#2552; 1:1000, 27 kDa), cyclin E2 (#4132; 1:1000, 48 kDa), or GAPDH (#2118; 1:5000, 37 kDa). After washing, blots were incubated with HRP-labeled mouse anti-rabbit antibody (#7076; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. To visualize specific proteins, blots were exposed using a ChemiDoc™ MP imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Band intensities were quantified by Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) and presented as intensity relative to GAPDH.

Determination of IL-10 and TGF-β1

The expression levels of human TGF-β1 in ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM were determined using human TGF-β1 ELISA detection kit (R&D System, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. ADSC extract, ADSC-CCM, and standards were incubated on TGF-β1-coated plate for 2 h at room temperature. The plates were washed with Wash Buffer (R&D system, USA), followed by incubation with the detection antibody and streptavidin-HRP solution. The samples were run in duplicate. Signal absorbance was determined at 450 nm and 540 nm with a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The expression levels of human IL-10 were determined by indirect human IL-10 ELISA. Briefly, ADSC extract, ADSC-CCM, and standard recombinant human IL-10 (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) were coated overnight on 96-well plates at 4 °C. The antigen-coated plates were washed with PBS-Tween, then blocked against non-specific binding with 1% BSA (Invitrogen, USA). The plates were then incubated with a rabbit anti-human IL-10 monoclonal antibody (Abcam, USA), followed by a goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP-conjugated antibody (Abcam, USA). Signals were developed by the addition of TMB solution (Merck, Germany). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Cytokine array

Multiple immunomodulatory cytokine-expression profiles of both ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM were determined using the human cytokine antibody array C5 (RayBiotech, USA). The array was treated with 200 µg/ml of either ADSC extract or ADSC-CCM and processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing, the membranes were incubated with biotinylated antibody cocktail, streptavidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase, and chemiluminescent detection reagents. Chemiluminescence signals were obtained using a ChemiDoc™ MP imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). The expressions of 80 proteins were analyzed by densitometry for integral optical density using ImageJ software (Positions of 80 proteins are shown in Supplementary data, Table S3). The optical density of each chemokine or cytokine spot was normalized using the positive control spots.

IL-10 and TGF-β1 neutralization assay

IL-10 and TGF-β1 functions in ADSC extract were neutralized by addition of either 10 µg/ml anti IL-10 antibody, 2 µg/ml anti TGF-β1 antibody, or combination. The PHA-stimulated PBMCs culture in ADSC extract without neutralizing antibody were used as control. The effects of IL-10 and TGF-β1 neutralization on lymphocyte proliferation and cell cycle progression were assessed by CCK8 and flow cytometry analysis, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least triplicate and presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism software version 8.4.3 (GraphPad, USA). The Mann-Whitney U test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to analyze the obtained results. The ANOVA post hos test was Kruskal-Wallis test. P-values less than 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Adipose tissue-derived stem cell characteristics

The characteristics of ADSCs were investigated including their phenotypic characteristics and differentiation potential (Supplementary data, Table S4 and Fig. S2A). The ADSCs which attached to the plastic surfaces of tissue culture vessels exhibited spindle-shaped morphology (Fig. 1A). Culturing with adipogenic induction medium, ADSCs could differentiate to adipocytes as demonstrated by the change of cell morphology to cuboidal shaped cells with fat droplets that were positive for Oil-red-O staining (Fig. 1B, Supplementary data, Fig. S2B). ADSCs could differentiate to osteocytes when cultured with osteogenic induction medium for 21 days. The osteocyte-like cells were demonstrated by the production of bone matrix containing calcium crystals which were positive for Alizarin red S staining (Fig. 1C, Supplementary data, Fig. S2C). For chondrogenesis, the aggregates which produced cartilage matrix were positive for Alcian blue staining (Fig. 1D, Supplementary data, Fig. S2D). Immunophenotyping of ADSCs showed that the ADSC population was more than 95% positive for CD73, CD90, and CD105 surface markers, and approximately 98% negative for hematopoietic makers, such as CD34 and CD45 (Fig. 1E). Cell-free ADSC products, including cell extract and concentrated conditioned medium, were prepared after characterization (Fig. 1F). The cell viability was assessed prior to ADSC extract preparation (Supplementary data, Fig. S3). The protein yields gained from ADSC extracts were approximately 2-fold higher than those obtained from ADSC-CCM (Fig. 1G and Supplementary data, Table S5).

ADSC extract restricted activated T cell proliferation and induced T regulatory cell expansion

To assess the immunosuppression capacity of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM, various concentrations of those cell-free derived ADSC products were added to cultures of activated lymphocytes. The results demonstrated that 1% PHA significantly increased PBMC numbers in PHA-activated groups compared with unstimulated control (Fig. 2A). The numbers of PHA-stimulated PBMCs were significantly reduced when treated with 100, 200, 400, or 800 µg/ml ADSC extract in comparison with PHA-activated but untreated PBMC controls (Fig. 2Ai). ADSC extract inhibited activated T lymphocyte proliferation in dose-dependent manner. In contrast, ADSC-CCM failed to inhibit proliferation of activated lymphocytes even at its highest concentration (Fig. 2Aii). In comparison of immunosuppressive efficiency, ADSC extract treatments at concentrations of more than 100 µg/ml had inhibitory capacity superior to that of ADSC-CCM (Fig. 2Aiii). These results were further confirmed by investigations with T cell subpopulations. PHA-activated PBMCs were cultured in the presence of 200 µg/ml of either ADSC extract or ADSC-CCM and this concentration was used subsequently for this series of experiments. After a 72 h-incubation, CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cell subpopulations were determined. The percentages of CD3+ cells were slightly decreased in PHA-stimulated PBMCs treated with ADSC extract but not in those cultured with ADSC-CCM (Fig. 2B). The ADSC extract treatment decreased the frequencies of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells to 0.64 ± 0.08 and 0.58 ± 0.09-fold of control, respectively. In contrast to ADSC extract, ADSC-CCM treatment did not significantly reduce the frequencies of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in comparison with PHA-activated but untreated PBMC controls (Fig. 2C). Thus, ADSC-CCM treatment had no effect on CD4+ T cell nor CD8+ T cell proliferation.

The capacities of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM to induce T regulatory populations were investigated based on expression of CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ T cells. When treated with ADSC extract, T regulatory cells increased to 1.59 ± 0.32-fold of control. There was a slight, but not significant, rise in T regulatory cells treated with ADSC-CCM (Fig. 2D). Taken together, ADSC extract could inhibit lymphocyte proliferation and shape T cell population by reducing both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells while inducing T regulatory cell expansion. In contrast, the immunosuppression effects of ADSC-CCM were limited. Thus, ADSC extract outperformed ADSC-CCM in modulation of activated T lymphocyte functions.

ADSC extract induced activated T lymphocytes to undergo cell cycle arrest at G0/G1 phase

To understand if the inhibition of activated T cell proliferation results from the induction of cell cycle arrest, cell cycle analysis was performed following exposure of these T cells to ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM. Baseline cell cycle analysis showed no proliferation in unstimulated lymphocytes. In contrast to PHA-activated lymphocytes, unstimulated lymphocytes were quiescent (G0/G1 phase). The cell-cycle peaks for S and G2/M phase were undetectable (Supplementary data, Fig. S4). Meanwhile, PHA-activated PBMCs were able to enter S phase and proliferated after 72 h of activation. Following the addition of ADSC extract, the results showed that ADSC extract significantly increased the percentage of activated T cells in G0/G1 phase (from 55.38 ± 5.69 to 68.08 ± 2.86%), while the percentage at S phase was reduced. Whereas ADSC extract induced cell cycle arrest, there was no significant difference in cell cycle phase of activated T lymphocytes treated with ADSC-CCM (Fig. 3A). Cell cycle regulatory gene analysis revealed the downregulation of CCNE2, whereas there was upregulation of p21, a cell cycle inhibitor, after ADSC extract treatment. In addition, the transcript levels of p53, p21, and CCNE2 after ADSC-CCM treatment were unaltered compared with untreated controls (Fig. 3B). Thus, ADSC-CCM had no effect on the cell cycle progression of activated T lymphocytes. Western blot analysis further confirmed that activated T lymphocytes markedly decreased the expression of CDK2 levels and were likely to reduce cyclin E2 protein expression when exposed to ADSC extract (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the protein levels of cell cycle inhibitors, particularly p27, were significantly increased, while there was only a slight rise in p21 protein. These results indicated that ADSC extracts could possibly impact T lymphocyte activation by blocking the cell cycle in the G0/G1 phase. To conclude, ADSC extract induces negative regulation of G0/G1 phase in activated T lymphocytes through p21 and p27 pathways. The increases in the cell cycle inhibitory proteins may prevent CDK2 and cyclin E activation and halt cell cycle progression.

ADSC extract failed to induce apoptosis of activated T lymphocytes

Given that ADSC extract could arrest the T cell cycle, we further investigated whether its inhibitory effects also led to lymphocyte apoptosis. PHA-stimulated lymphocytes were cultured in the presence of ADSC extract for 72 h. The apoptotic cells were assessed by Annexin-V/PI staining. The percentages of early apoptotic cells (Annexin-V+PI− cells), late apoptotic cells (Annexin-V+PI+ cells), and total apoptotic cells (Annexin-V+ cells) were determined via flow cytometric analysis. Approximately 42.4 ± 9.8% of activated T lymphocytes were apoptotic in the absence of exposure to ADSC extract. After exposure to ADSC extract, the activated T lymphocyte population included 30.4 ± 11.4% of cells positive for early apoptosis, 12.7 ± 2.1% positive for late apoptosis, and 43.2 ± 13.0% positive for total apoptosis (Fig. 3D). Exposure to the extract resulted in no significant differences in these percentages. Together, these results showed that ADSC extracts failed to induce apoptosis of activated T lymphocytes. Taken together, the inhibitory effects of ADSC extract on the proliferation of activated lymphocytes appear to be mediated through G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, independent of apoptosis induction.

ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM exhibited distinct cytokine profiling

To define the responsible cytokines, proteome profiles of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM were determined using a cytokine array. Although donor variation was seen among the four donors of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM samples, shared cytokines and high expressing factors were further evaluated. The results indicated that expression of bioactive proteins in ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM were distinct (Fig. 4A). It is noteworthy that intracellular extract and concentrated conditioned medium derived from the same ADSC donor exhibited differing cytokine profiles. In addition, most of the cytokines and growth factors had expression levels considered low or undetectable (Supplementary data, Fig. S6). The shared proteins expressed in the ADSC extracts consisted of immunomodulatory cytokines, such as TGF-β1 and TGF-β2. The chemokines and growth factors significantly identified in ADSC extracts were SDF-1, EGF, OSM, VEGF, FGF4, FGF6, and IGFBP-3 which play role in regulation of cell proliferation (Fig. 4B). In ADSC-CCM, the abundant proteins were pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-7, and IL-8, while in ADSC extract, IL-7 expression was rarely detected. IL-6 and IL-8 were present in ADSC extract at minimal values compared with those in ADSC-CCM. TIMP1 and TIMP2 were identified in both ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM. The chemokines and growth factors significantly found in ADSC-CCM were GRO, GRO-α, MCP-1, angiogenin, and OPG. In addition, the differing concentrations of immunoregulatory factors were further confirmed in that ADSC extracts contained higher IL-10 and TGF-β1 levels than did ADSC-CCM. IL-10 was undetectable in ADSC-CCM while in ADSC extract it was at high concentration (range in 250–2000 ng/ml) (Fig. 4C). Moreover, TGF-β1 was prominent in ADSC extract and low in ADSC- CCM (Fig. 4D). Together, the distinct proteome profiling was consistent with the different effects of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM on regulating T cell functions. Given the superior role of ADSC extract in regulating T lymphocyte functions and the predominant expression of the key factors IL-10 and TGF-β, these cytokines may act as candidate mediators of ADSC extract-induced suppression of T cell responses.

IL-10 and TGF-β1 mediate ADSC extract-induced T cell cycle arrest

To clarify whether IL-10 and TGF-β1 in ADSC extract are responsible for suppression of T cell proliferation, those cytokines were inhibited by specific monoclonal antibodies. The amount of activated T lymphocyte dramatically reduced when treated with ADSC extract. Inhibition of IL-10 in ADSC extract was likely to restore the proliferation capacity of activated T lymphocytes, while TGF-β1 neutralization had no effect on recovering T cell proliferation (Fig. 5A). In addition, there were no greater effects observed in both IL-10 and TGF-β1 neutralization. Cell cycle analysis revealed that neutralization of either IL-10 alone or both IL-10 and TGF-β1 significantly reduced the effect of ADSC extract on inducing accumulation of activated T cells in G0/G1 phase, while the percentage at S phase and G2/M phase increased (Fig. 5B). Addition of TGF-β1 neutralizing antibody alone failed to recover the immunosuppressive effects of ADSC extract. Furthermore, the expression levels of cell cycle regulatory genes were altered in IL-10 and TGF-β1 neutralization. The results showed the downregulation of p27 in IL-10 neutralization compared with ADSC extract alone (Fig. 5C). The transcript levels of p21 slightly decreased in IL-10 neutralization and significant effect was observed in both IL-10 and TGF-β1 neutralization. However, their protein levels should be further investigated. Together, both IL-10 and TGF-β1 are the key components in ADSC extract responsible for controlling cell cycle progression of activated T lymphocytes.

Discussion

Mesenchymal stem cells are potent immunoregulators with potential for transfer to therapeutic applications, especially immune-mediated diseases and tissue regeneration. A large and growing set of studies have emphasized the use of cell-free MSC products due to the production of multiple therapeutic factors involving in cytoprotective functions and immunomodulation8,25. This study aimed to assess the comparative efficacies of ADSC extract and ADSC-concentrated conditioned medium on regulating immune responses. Previous studies have reported beneficial effects of MSC-conditioned medium on alleviating immunological diseases and regenerating tissue7. Surprisingly, we found that ADSC-CCM did not inhibit activated T lymphocyte proliferation in vitro, even at the highest concentration tested. Additionally, ADSC-CCM did not induce T regulatory cell expansion nor promote cell cycle arrest. Inconsistent results in the immunosuppressive ability of MSC-conditioned medium were revealed. These findings are in agreement with a previous study that demonstrated limited immunomodulation efficiency of MSC-conditioned medium when cultured as a monolayer26. Moreover, the variation of preparation procedures, the sources of stem cells, and the culture conditions may influence the immunomodulatory properties of MSC-conditioned medium27. The lack of a standard protocol for preparation of cell-free MSC products is the major challenge that may affect the variability of their potential20. In contrast, we found that ADSC extract dose-dependently inhibited the proliferation of activated T cells. Both activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells significantly decreased in number when exposed to ADSC extract. Thus, the effects of ADSC extract were not specific to a particular T lymphocyte subpopulation. The extract also induced activated T lymphocytes to undergo cell cycle arrest and stimulated T regulatory cell expansion. Therefore, ADSC extract was more potent than ADSC-CCM in regulating activated T lymphocyte functions. Due to the limited effects on immunoregulation of ADSC-conditioned medium, cell-free ADSC extract is potentially a better MSC-derived product for therapeutic use.

Although ADSC extract possesses immune modulation capacity, the underlying mechanisms are unclear. To address this, we first demonstrated that the suppression of activated T cell proliferation by ADSC extract results from the induction of cell cycle arrest at G0/G1 phase, and that this is via p21 and p27 activation. This is in agreement with prior investigations of other MSC-derived cell-free products, such as exosomes, that they are capable of inducing T cell accumulation at G1 phase28. We found that expression of CDK2 protein and cyclin E2 transcripts were profoundly inhibited in activated T lymphocytes treated with ADSC extract, whereas p21 and p27 (cyclin dependent kinase inhibitors) were elevated. Previous investigations demonstrated that p21 and p27 target the cyclin E-CDK2 complex and control cell cycle transition from G1 to S phase29,30. The increases of p21 and p27 in the ADSC extract treated group likely prevented CDK2 and cyclin E activation and halted cell cycle progression. A former study has shown that TGF-β is essential for the suppression of T cell proliferation and induction G1 arrest. The synergistic function of p21 and p27 on cell cycle arrest is associated with TGF-β31. Our cytokine analyses showed that ADSC extract prominently contains TGF-β1 and IL-10. This suggests that TGF-β1 and IL-10 present in ADSC extract may be a key immunomodulator mediating the halt of T cell cycle progression by activation of p21 and p27.

We further investigated whether the reduction of activated T lymphocytes in ADSC extract treatment resulted from T cell apoptosis. Our study demonstrated that ADSC extract fails to induce activated T cell apoptosis. Although apoptotic cells have been seen following ADSC extract treatment, the number of apoptotic cells were not significantly different compared to untreated controls. This was possibly due to the requirement for direct cell-cell contact and activation of apoptotic signaling through FASL-FAS pathway. A previous study demonstrated that bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells, which expressed FASL, could mediate CD3+ T cell apoptosis by binding with FAS on activated T cells32. Additionally, FASL knock down MSCs fail to induce CD3+ T cell apoptosis, indicating the important role of FAS-FASL interaction in inducing T cell apoptosis. Apart from cell-cell interactions, soluble factors secreted by MSCs (such as IDO and PD-L1) also play a role in directing T cells to arrest in G0/G1 phase and undergo apoptosis33,34. The IDO protein was not assessed in the current study, however our group previously showed that IDO transcripts are barely expressed in ADSC monolayer culture without priming18.

It is noteworthy that ADSC extract and its conditioned medium expressed distinct cytokine profiles. ADSC extract contained the contents of lysed cells that could be a mixture of intracellular proteins and extracellular vesicles. By comparative cytokine array analyses, ADSC extract contained immunomodulatory molecules while ADSC-CCM predominantly contained inflammatory cytokines, chemoattractants, and angiogenic factors. Consistent with previous reports, pleiotropic bioactive molecules are found in MSC culture medium and are responsible for cytoprotective action and tissue regeneration35–37. However, standardized procedures are required in the preparation of cell-free products with consistent composition of therapeutic molecules38,39. Among the factors increased in ADSC extract are TGF-β1 and IL-10, among the most potent anti-inflammatory agents and immunomodulators known40. Apart from regulation of T cell proliferation, TGF-β shapes T lymphocytes toward a T regulatory cell subpopulation41. Our results revealed that IL-10 was undetectable in ADSC-CCM, however it was present in ADSC extract at low concentrations. Recent studies show that IL-10 suppresses T cell proliferation and activation by reducing IL-2 secretion42. Thus, TGF-β1 and IL-10 possibly mediated the regulatory effects resulting in the upregulation of T regulatory cells and suppression of T cell activation. Our study indicated that IL-10 and TGF-β1 presented in ADSC extract are one of the key factors mediated cell cycle arrest in activated T lymphocyte. Inhibition of IL-10 abrogated immunosuppressive capacity of ADSC extract. The blockade of IL-10 could restore the cell proliferation and cell cycle progression of activated T lymphocyte. This is in agreement with prior study which demonstrated that IL-10 is the component of MSC secretome responsible for inhibiting T cell proliferation42,43. TGF-β1 neutralization alone was unable to reverse the inhibitory effect of ADSC extract on T cell proliferation. However, blockade of both IL-10 and TGF-β1 could reduce the expression of p21 and decrease the proportion of activated T lymphocyte in G0/G1 phase. Although the alteration in transcripts levels of p21, p27, and p53 were observed in either IL-10 or TGF-β1 neutralization, the protein levels of p21, p27, p53 and phosphorylation p53 should be further clarified. Therefore, the wider mixture of pleiotropic molecules in ADSC extract are required to exert its highest immunomodulatory capacity.

In addition, other bioactive molecules predominantly found in ADSC extract including SDF-1, FGF4, and IGFBP-3. SDF-1 is a chemokine that plays a role in regulating lymphocyte trafficking. In addition, there is evidence that SDF-1 is crucial for T lymphocyte survival and expansion44. FGF-4 exerts broad mitogenic and cell survival activities in trophoblast stem cells45. IGFBP-3 plays dual functions as a suppressor and a stimulator for cell proliferation. Interestingly, binding of IGFBP-3 and TGF-β receptor could control the growth of breast cancer cell lines46. However, the roles of FGF-4 and IGFBP-3 on regulating lymphocyte functions remain poorly understood and require further investigation.

The current study highlighted the immune modulation efficacy of ADSC extract compared to its conditioned medium. Although this conditioned medium was characterized in terms of immunomodulation efficacy and its pro-inflammatory cytokine content, other potential beneficial aspects should be explored such as regulation of macrophage polarization27. In addition, these observations help explain the underlying mechanisms of ADSC extract actions on regulating T lymphocyte functions. Both understanding of ADSC extract efficacy and its mechanisms will benefit the designing of therapeutic applications. However, in vivo studies of ADSC extract with focus on reproducibility, immunomodulatory efficiency, stability, dosage and safety will be necessary on the path to clinical application. The anatomical sites of adipose tissue and donor age are among the factors that may influence ADSC functionality and cytokine profiles, leading to variability in therapeutic efficacy. Previous studies revealed that some ADSC characteristics: phenotype, differentiation ability, and angiogenic potential had not been affected by the age of the donor. However, aged ADSC exhibited a decline in proliferation capacity and an increase in cellular senescence47,48. Therefore, the need for an exploratory study of donor variation by age and anatomical site in immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic effects remains to be determined. In addition, ADSCs isolated from distinct anatomical sites exhibited different characteristics and biological function49–51. Prior studies demonstrate that the expression of EV-miRNA profiles52 and secretory factors53 was impacted by the anatomical site of adipose tissue. Herein, the effects of ADSCs on regulating lymphocyte functions were limited to ADSC derived from abdominal and flank adipose tissue of donors aged 28–49 years. The significant limitations of this study are unspecified anatomical sites of adipose tissue and broad range of donor age that might influence variability in ADSC efficacy. Indeed, the influence of donor age and source of adipose tissue on immunomodulatory efficacy of ADSC extract or ADSC-CCM remains to be clarified.

The current study partially identified a set of cytokine and chemokine and did not include all potential factors contained within intracellular components, such as EVs, DNA, RNA, and other intracellular proteins. In contrast to conditioned medium38,54, there is limited information about the full composition of cellular extract. Therefore, comparative of proteomic profiling, investigation on molecular mechanism as well as examining its action in vivo is required to clarify the mechanism of ADSC extract. Apart from cellular extract and conditioned medium, immunoregulatory capacity has been reported in other MSC-derived products including intact cells55 and extracellular vesicles56,57. Therefore, a further comparison of the activity of the cell-free MSC products with that of viable cells or purified extracellular vesicles would enhance our understanding of their mechanism of actions. These insights could help guide the selection of optimal MSC-derived products for therapeutic uses in the future.

Conclusion

Collectively, the current findings demonstrate that ADSC extract effects T lymphocyte activation by enhancing lymphocyte accumulation in the G0/G1 phase, in a manner independent of apoptosis induction. This negative regulation of cell cycle progression in activated T lymphocyte appears mediated through p21 and p27 activation. Our results revealed that ADSC extract outperforms its culture medium on regulating T lymphocyte function. Their distinct actions may be associated with the differential expression of immunomodulatory molecules in the composition of ADSC extract and ADSC-CCM. In ADSC extract, IL-10 and TGF-β1 are among the key immunoregulators that mediate cell cycle arrest by activation of p21 and p27. Thus, ADSC extract is a promising cell-free MSC product for modulating T cell functions and thus may contribute to the improved therapeutics.
