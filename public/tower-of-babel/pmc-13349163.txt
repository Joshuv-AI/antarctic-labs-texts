Compressive bracing for pectus carinatum following congenital heart disease surgery in children: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: De-rong He, Bai-ye Chen, Wei-wen Zhu, Chao-fan Lin, Qing-cheng Liu, Xue-ming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPectus carinatum (PC) can develop as a secondary deformity following median sternotomy for congenital heart disease repair in children. While compressive bracing is an established first-line treatment for primary pectus carinatum, its safety and effectiveness in post-cardiac surgery patients remain uninvestigated.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 54 pediatric patients with PC secondary to congenital heart disease surgery, treated with custom-fitted compressive bracing at a single center (March 2019 to December 2020). The primary outcome was anteroposterior (AP) protrusion measurement at baseline, 3, 6, and 9 months. Satisfactory correction was defined as 50% or greater reduction in AP protrusion. Changes across time points were analyzed using the Friedman test with post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.ResultsThe cohort comprised 40 males and 14 females (median age 60.0 months). The median interval between cardiac surgery and bracing initiation was 36.0 months (IQR 24.0–54.0). Median AP protrusion decreased significantly from 12.2 cm at baseline to 6.8 cm at 9 months (Friedman test: p < 0.001). Satisfactory correction was achieved in 47 patients (87.0%). Adverse effects occurred in 7 patients (13.0%), were mild and self-limiting, with no cardiac complications.ConclusionIn this short-term follow-up study, compressive bracing appears to be a safe and effective non-operative approach for treating PC in pediatric patients following CHD surgery. These findings represent immediate outcomes and warrant confirmation in larger, controlled studies with longer follow-up.

Introduction

Median sternotomy remains the standard surgical approach for repair of congenital heart disease (CHD) in pediatric patients [1]. A recognized but under-studied sequela of this approach is the development of pectus carinatum (PC), a chest wall deformity characterized by anterior protrusion of the sternum and adjacent costal cartilages. PC is the second most common congenital chest wall deformity [2]. In clinical practice, we have encountered pediatric patients who developed secondary PC following CHD surgery [3].

Respiratory function testing in patients with PC generally does not reveal significant pathology, although restrictive ventilatory disturbances may be detected when PC coexists with scoliosis [4, 5]. Children with PC may report symptoms of chest pain and dyspnea on exertion [6]. However, the primary burden of deformity tends to be cosmetic and psychosocial rather than physiological. The prominence of deformity typically increases during puberty due to accelerated skeletal growth. Affected children may experience self-consciousness, embarrassment, anxiety, depression, and social isolation, while their parents often express significant concern regarding the chest wall appearance. Patients may develop negative body image and diminished quality of life [7]. Correction of PC has been shown to improve self-esteem and confidence [7].

Historically, the treatment of PC relied on surgical intervention, including the open or modified Ravitch procedure and minimally invasive techniques [8, 9]. More recently, compressive bracing has emerged as a first-line non-operative therapy for PC, as recommended by the American Pediatric Surgical Association (APSA) guidelines [10, 11]. Non-surgical bracing avoids operative risks, leaves no scars, and incurs lower costs than surgery [12]. Multiple studies have reported highly satisfactory outcomes with bracing in adolescent populations [13–15].

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting bracing for primary PC, no previous study has specifically examined its effectiveness in pediatric patients with secondary PC following CHD surgery. These patients present unique challenges, including the presence of sternal fixation wires, midline surgical scars, and potentially altered chest wall mechanics. The aim of this study was to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of compressive bracing for the correction of PC in pediatric patients who had previously undergone median sternotomy for CHD repair.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study analyzing prospectively collected clinical data on pediatric patients treated with compressive bracing for PC secondary to CHD surgery at Fujian Children’s Hospital (Fuzhou, China), from March 2019 to December 2020. All eligible patients received the same intervention (compressive bracing), and outcomes were compared across sequential time points within each patient. No comparator group was included, as the study was designed to provide preliminary evidence on the safety and feasibility of bracing in this specific population; no concurrent untreated cohort was available because families who declined bracing were not systematically followed.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Fujian Children’s Hospital (approval number: 2020-KY-039). Written informed consent was obtained from the parent or legal guardian of each patient prior to enrollment. All patient data were de-identified for analysis and stored securely in compliance with institutional data protection policies.

Participants

All consecutive patients presenting with PC following prior median sternotomy for CHD repair during the study period were screened for eligibility.

Inclusion criteria

(a) Age < 18 years; (b) PC developing after median sternotomy for CHD repair; (c) Positive compression test; (d) Correction pressure of < 10 pounds per square inch (PSI); (e) Informed consent from parent or legal guardian.

Exclusion criteria

(a) Chronic systemic disease; (b) Complex or mixed-type chest wall deformity; (c) Prior surgical correction of PC or scoliosis; (d) Unwillingness to use the orthosis.

Patients with prior surgical correction of PC were excluded because previous surgical alteration of the costal cartilage and sternum fundamentally changes the biomechanical properties of the chest wall, potentially confounding the assessment of bracing effectiveness. Patients with prior scoliosis correction were excluded because spinal fixation hardware and altered thoracic cage geometry may limit chest wall compliance and introduce additional variables affecting brace response [16].

At the initial clinic visit, patients and families were counseled regarding the options of surgical repair versus bracing therapy; the majority elected conservative treatment.

Cardiac surgical history

The types of prior CHD surgery in this cohort included repair of ventricular septal defect (VSD) in 28 patients (51.9%), atrial septal defect (ASD) repair in 12 patients (22.2%), tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) repair in 8 patients (14.8%), and other procedures in 6 patients (11.1%). The median interval between cardiac surgery and initiation of bracing was 36.0 months (IQR 24.0–54.0; range 12–96 months).

Classification of deformity severity

Deformity severity was classified by the treating clinician based on the baseline AP protrusion measurement as follows: mild (AP protrusion < 15 mm above the normal chest plane), moderate (15–25 mm above the normal chest plane), and severe (> 25 mm above the normal chest plane) [17].

Intervention: compressive bracing protocol

Brace type and fitting

A custom-fabricated anterior compressive orthosis was designed for each patient based on the dimensions of the anterior chest wall. Each brace consisted of an aluminum alloy frame with an adjustable anterior pressure pad lined with silicone cushioning, and posterior counter-pressure plates secured by adjustable straps. The brace was individually molded to conform to the patient’s chest contour. Patients wore a snug-fitting cotton undershirt beneath the brace at all times to prevent skin breakdown (Fig. 1).

Data collected at baseline included age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), chest wall measurements (AP protrusion height and AP chest diameter), deformity characteristics (severity and symmetry), associated symptoms, and cardiac surgical history (Table 1).

Patients wore a snug-fitting cotton undershirt beneath the brace at all times to prevent skin breakdown (Fig. 1). The bracing protocol followed a graduated schedule: during the first week, the brace was worn for a minimum of 2 h per day to allow adaptation; wearing time was increased to 8 h per day during the second week, and further increased to 23 h per day by the end of the first month. During the initial fitting, patients were observed in the clinic for at least 30 min to monitor tolerance.

Initial pressure and adjustments

The initial correction pressure was recorded at the time of fitting. The median initial correction pressure was 3.5 PSI (IQR 2.5–5.0). Pressure adjustments were performed at each 3-monthly follow-up visit by the treating clinician. The target was to gradually increase compression to flatten the protrusion while remaining within the patient’s tolerance. If chest pain, chest tightness, palpitations, or other persistent symptoms developed, the pressure was reduced. Adjustments were guided by clinical assessment of chest wall compliance and patient tolerance rather than a predetermined pressure escalation protocol.

Management of sternal wires and surgical scars

In patients with prominently palpable sternal fixation wires (n = 5), a small protective pad was placed over the wire site beneath the brace to reduce friction and prevent skin lesions. Additionally, five patients had particularly prominent hypertrophic scars along the midline sternotomy incision; extra cotton padding was applied to these areas to reduce discomfort. After approximately one month of adaptation, scar tissue typically softened and patients tolerated continued bracing.

Follow-up

Patients were scheduled for follow-up every 3 months. At each visit, the brace pressure was reassessed and adjusted as described above. If symptoms of cardiac compromise were suspected, echocardiography was performed promptly, and bracing pressure and wearing time were reduced until symptoms resolved. All 54 patients completed the 9-month follow-up period. Patients who achieved satisfactory correction before 9 months continued bracing at a maintenance level through the end of the follow-up period. No formal post-treatment surveillance protocol beyond 9 months was in place during the study period.

Due to travel restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic, patients unable to attend in-person follow-up were contacted via WeChat (a widely used Chinese mobile messaging application) to monitor progress remotely with parental assistance. Caregivers supervised all home bracing sessions.

All 54 patients in this study were regularly followed by outpatient cardiac surgeons in addition to the bracing protocol. Serial echocardiography and electrocardiography performed during follow-up demonstrated preserved cardiac function in all patients, with no evidence of cardiac compromise attributable to brace compression.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the AP protrusion measurement (cm), defined as the anteroposterior distance from the plane of the normal chest wall to the point of maximum sternal protrusion. The normal chest wall plane was defined as the tangential plane connecting the left and right anterior chest wall surfaces lateral to the area of deformity at the level of maximum protrusion, using external calipers. All measurements were obtained clinically using external calipers; no radiological measurements (e.g., CT-based Haller index) were used. Measurements were recorded at baseline (0 months), 3 months, 6 months, and 9 months. All measurements were performed by the same clinician (D.H.) to minimize inter-observer variability.

The secondary outcome was the rate of satisfactory correction, defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in the AP protrusion measurement from baseline to the most recent follow-up. Safety outcomes included all brace-related adverse effects documented during the study period.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median (Q1–Q3); normally distributed variables as mean ± SD. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

The Friedman test was used to evaluate changes in AP protrusion across the four time points (0, 3, 6, and 9 months). Where significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.008). Subgroup comparisons by sex, deformity symmetry, and age group were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Effect sizes for Wilcoxon tests were calculated as r = Z / √N, with 95% confidence intervals computed using bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrapping (1,000 resamples). Effect sizes were interpreted following Cohen’s conventions (small: r = 0.10; medium: r = 0.30; large: r = 0.50).

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) unless Bonferroni correction was applied.

Results

Patient characteristics

Fifty-four patients with PC secondary to CHD surgery were enrolled during the study period (Table 1). The cohort comprised 40 males (74.1%) and 14 females (25.9%), with a median age of 60.0 months (IQR 45.0–78.0; range 35–120 months). The median height was 104.0 cm (IQR 96.0–115.0), median weight was 16.5 kg (IQR 14.5–19.0), and median BMI was 15.2 kg/m² (IQR 14.2–16.3). Regarding deformity severity, 43 patients (79.6%) had mild PC and 11 (20.4%) had moderate PC; no patients had severe deformity. Forty-two patients (77.8%) had symmetric deformity, while 12 (22.2%) had asymmetric PC. The mean daily bracing duration was 19.5 h (range 2–23 h).

The most common prior cardiac procedure was VSD repair (51.9%), followed by ASD repair (22.2%), TOF repair (14.8%), and other procedures (11.1%). The median interval between cardiac surgery and bracing initiation was 36.0 months (IQR 24.0–54.0).

Primary outcome: AP protrusion measurements

AP protrusion measurements were non-normally distributed at all four time points (Shapiro-Wilk test, all p < 0.05). Median AP protrusion decreased progressively from 12.2 cm (IQR 10.5–14.0) at baseline to 8.6 cm (IQR 7.5–10.0) at 3 months, 7.4 cm (IQR 6.5–8.5) at 6 months, and 6.8 cm (IQR 5.8–7.8) at 9 months (Table 2).

Secondary outcome: satisfactory correction

At 9 months, 47 of 54 patients (87.0%) achieved satisfactory correction (≥ 50% reduction in AP protrusion). Among the 7 patients who did not achieve satisfactory correction, 4 had asymmetric deformity and 3 had adherence difficulties related to COVID-19 travel restrictions.

Subgroup analyses

The magnitude of AP improvement did not differ significantly by sex (p = 0.528), deformity symmetry (p = 0.412), or age group (p = 0.087), suggesting broadly consistent treatment effects across subgroups. A non-significant trend toward greater improvement in younger patients (≤ 60 months) was noted (Table 3).

Safety outcomes

Brace-related side effects were observed in 7 patients (13.0%) and were universally mild and self-limiting (Table 1). The most common side effect was petechial bleeding (4 patients, 7.4%), followed by palpitation (2 patients, 3.7%) and thoracalgia (1 patient, 1.9%). No patient experienced shortness of breath, hydropericardium, or cardiac tamponade. All adverse effects resolved with temporary reduction in bracing pressure and wearing time.

Discussion

This study represents, to our knowledge, the first report on the use of compressive bracing for the treatment of PC in pediatric patients who had previously undergone median sternotomy for CHD repair. Our findings suggest that bracing is a safe and effective non-operative approach in this population, with 47 of 54 patients (87.0%) achieving satisfactory correction over a 9-month treatment period, and statistically significant reductions in AP protrusion observed at each consecutive follow-up interval.

The pediatric patients in this study presented several unique clinical characteristics that distinguish them from the general PC population. First, the chest walls of young children following sternotomy tend to be relatively flexible, potentially allowing effective correction with lower compressive pressures than those typically required in adolescents with primary PC. Second, the presence of sternal fixation wires and midline surgical scars in this population necessitated specific adaptations to the bracing protocol (as described in Methods), which should be anticipated by clinicians managing similar patients. While echocardiographic studies have demonstrated right ventricular compression and strain in patients with pectus excavatum [18, 19], the outward protrusion characteristic of PC does not exert similar compressive forces on cardiac structures. Nevertheless, given the cardiac surgical history of our patient population, cardiac monitoring remains an important component of bracing management in this group.

The pattern of diminishing effect sizes over successive intervals (r = 0.79, 0.56, and 0.45) is consistent with the expected clinical trajectory: the greatest correction occurs early in treatment when the protrusion is most prominent and the chest wall is most responsive, with progressively smaller incremental improvements as the deformity approaches normal contour.

Subgroup analyses did not reveal statistically significant differences in the magnitude of AP improvement by sex, deformity symmetry, or age group, although a non-significant trend toward greater improvement in younger patients (≤ 60 months) was observed (p = 0.087). This finding aligns with the clinical expectation that younger patients with more malleable chest walls may respond more favorably to compressive therapy [20, 21]. However, the subgroup sample sizes were modest, and these analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Our findings are consistent with the broader literature on compressive bracing for PC. Kravarusic et al. [22] reported significant subjective improvement in patients wearing compression orthosis for 23 h daily. Colozza et al. [23] found that patients were satisfied with their post-bracing appearance and experienced improved self-esteem. Thaker et al. [24] reported similar gains in patient satisfaction and self-esteem using a non-standardized questionnaire. Sesia et al. [25] emphasized the importance of considering lateral chest expansion during compressive therapy, as AP forces may alter thoracic dimensions in growing children. We recommend incorporating supplementary exercises to optimize deformity correction in conjunction with bracing. Surgical repair remains available as a second-line option should bracing prove insufficient [23]. Lee et al. [26] reported that skeletal maturity influences the degree of correction achievable with bracing, while Emil et al. [27] identified older age, asymmetry, and smaller initial pressure drop as predictors of treatment failure and prolonged orthosis use.

We generally recommend initiating brace therapy during cooler months, when additional clothing layers can serve as natural padding between the skin and the brace. During summer months, we advise patients to wear braces in air-conditioned environments to minimize sweat-related skin complications.

This study has several limitations. First, the study employed a single-arm pre–post design without a control group, precluding causal attribution of observed improvements solely to the bracing intervention; natural growth and maturation may have contributed to changes in chest wall morphology. Second, the study was not blinded; all outcome assessments were performed by the treating clinician, who was aware of the treatment status, introducing potential assessment bias. Third, patient compliance with the prescribed bracing protocol varied; adherence was based on parental report and was not objectively measured (e.g., with pressure sensors or wear-time monitors), introducing potential reporting bias. Similarly, parental and patient satisfaction were not assessed using validated instruments, limiting conclusions about subjective treatment outcomes. Fourth, AP protrusion measurements may be influenced by subcutaneous fat thickness and variations in caliper placement, and all measurements were performed by a single clinician, which reduces inter-observer variability but does not permit assessment of measurement reliability. Fifth, the 9-month follow-up period represents short-term outcomes only and is insufficient to assess long-term durability of correction; longer follow-up is needed to determine recurrence rates. Sixth, the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted in-person follow-up for some patients, necessitating remote monitoring via WeChat, which may have reduced data quality. Finally, as a single-institution study, the generalizability of our findings to other settings requires validation.

Conclusion

In this short-term follow-up study, compressive bracing appears to be a safe and effective non-operative approach for the correction of PC in pediatric patients following surgery for congenital heart disease. In this cohort, the majority of patients achieved satisfactory correction at 9 months with minimal and self-limiting side effects. These findings represent immediate treatment outcomes and support the consideration of bracing as a first-line therapy in this population. Future prospective controlled studies with larger samples, objective compliance monitoring, longer follow-up to assess durability of correction, and validated patient-reported outcome measures are needed to confirm and extend these results.
