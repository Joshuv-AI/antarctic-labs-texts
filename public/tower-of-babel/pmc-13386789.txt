Serological biomarkers of JIA-associated uveitis: ANA titers, ANA AC-30 staining pattern and prefoldin 5 antibodies

Adebiyi, Frank Dressler, Dirk Foell, Ivan Foeldvari, Johannes-Peter Haas, Arnd Heiligenhaus, Gerd Horneff, Anton Hospach, Tilmann Kallinich, Jasmin Kuemmerle-Deschner, Kirsten Moenkemoeller, Prasad T. ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify serum biomarkers associated with the development of uveitis in a German juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) cohort.MethodsA convenience sample, enriched for uveitis cases, was drawn from a prospectively followed inception cohort of newly diagnosed JIA patients (ICON). Baseline serum samples were biobanked, and each was tested for conventional and novel autoantibodies using multi-analyte array technologies. Associations between uveitis occurrence, disease outcomes, and autoantibody profiles were examined.ResultsFifty-two patients with and 141 patients without uveitis were included in the analyses. At first uveitis documentation, 26% of patients presented with uveitis-related complications. Younger age at JIA diagnosis, higher ANA titers (median [IQR]: 960 [240–5120] vs. 160 [0-640]; p < 0.001), and the Anti-Cell (AC-30) immunofluorescence (IIF) staining pattern (63.5% vs. 23.4%; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with the presence of uveitis, but not with uveitis-related complications. In contrast, the AC-4 IIF staining pattern was more often present in children without uveitis. In addition, patients with uveitis showed significantly higher prefoldin subunit 5 (PFDN5) antibody titers compared to non-uveitis JIA patients (mean ± SD; 486.39 ± 495.85 vs. 321.85 ± 330.36; p = 0.010). Other tested autoantibodies, including anti-histone antibodies, were rare in both groups and did not differ significantly.ConclusionsHigh ANA titers, the AC-30 ANA indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) pattern, and younger age at JIA onset are significantly associated with uveitis in JIA. Anti-PFDN5 antibodies show potential as a novel biomarker for JIA-associated uveitis. Combining these serological markers with clinical risk factors may improve early identification of high-risk patients and support the development of better tailored screening strategies to prevent vision-threatening complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03835-5.

Introduction

Uveitis, marked by inflammation of the anterior uveal tract and affecting approximately 8–30% of patients, is the most common and potentially vision-threatening extra-articular manifestation of juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) [1–5]. Early recognition and prompt treatment are essential to avoid rapidly progressive visual complications caused by inflammatory processes. JIA-associated uveitis (JIA-U) represents a major diagnostic challenge, as it often has an asymptomatic and insidious onset that does not necessarily correlate with arthritis activity or flares [6–8]. At initial presentation, 20–45% of affected children already have ocular complications such as cataracts, band keratopathy, glaucoma, posterior synechiae, vision loss, or even blindness [9–11].

Several clinical risk factors for JIA-U have been identified, including young age at arthritis onset (< 7 years), specific JIA categories (25–30% in extended oligoarthritis, 16–18% in persistent oligoarthritis, 4–14% in rheumatoid factor-negative polyarthritis, and 10–12% in psoriatic arthritis), antinuclear antibody (ANA) positivity, and short duration of disease [12–14]. In two prospective studies, the median interval between arthritis and uveitis onset was 4–5 months [15, 16]. Data from the German JIA registry indicate that 73% of uveitis cases occur before or within 12 months after arthritis onset [17]. Arthritis typically precedes uveitis but in less than 10% of JIA patients, uveitis is diagnosed before the onset of arthritis [9, 12, 15].

Biomarkers that predict disease complications and enable stratification of patients into clinically meaningful subsets are increasingly valuable in rheumatology practice [17, 18]. Apart from genetic risk factors mapped to HLA-DRB1, HLA-DPB1, and HLA-A [14], as well as elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and S100A12 (calgranulin C) levels [19], ANA positivity remains the most clinically relevant biomarker associated with an increased risk of JIA-U. However, ANA testing on HEp-2 substrates has limited specificity, as positivity is found in 14–98% of JIA-U cases but also in up to 47% of JIA patients without uveitis [20, 21]. This is not surprising because ANA screening tests are troubled by several confounding variables; most notably it identifies multiple, often unrelated, autoantibody specificities [21–23]. Given the potential for severe, vision-compromising sequelae, the identification of more specific and predictive biomarkers for JIA-U represents a clinical unmet need. Such markers could improve risk stratification, enable personalized monitoring strategies, and help prevent long-term visual morbidity.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to identify biomarkers associated with the development of uveitis in a large, prospectively followed JIA cohort. We aimed to evaluate a panel of autoantibodies known or implicated to be associated with autoimmune diseases [24–26], in addition to assessing ANA titers and their HEp-2 indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) staining patterns.

Methods

Study design and subjects

The ‘Inception cohort of newly diagnosed JIA patients study (ICON) is a multicenter prospective longitudinal cohort study initiated in 2009. Medical history, demographic characteristics, clinical outcome data including uveitis occurrence, treatments, and bio-specimens were prospectively collected according to a defined protocol at enrollment, every 3 months in the first year and every 6 months thereafter. Uveitis-related characteristics, including anterior chamber (AC) cell grade or uveitis-related complications, were documented 3-monthly by an ophthalmologist for patients who developed uveitis after enrollment in ICON [27, 28].

Patients were included in this study if they met the International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification criteria for JIA [2], had a serum sample available at enrollment, a documented enrollment visit, and at least three years of follow-up. A convenience sample of patients with available biospecimens was assembled, prioritizing individuals who had developed uveitis. Demographic information, clinical data, first uveitis documentation, and treatment details of included JIA patients were obtained from the ICON database, and baseline serum samples (stored at -80 °C) were retrieved from the ICON biobank. The ICON study was approved by the ethics committee of the Charité, Universitätsmedizin Berlin (EA1/056/10). Written informed consent was provided by parents and patients ≥ 8 years, according to the Declaration of Helsinki. In this study, the primary analytical objective was to assess whether ANA patterns proscribed by the titers, International Consensus of Autoantibody Patterns (ICAP) and selected autoantibodies previously implicated in autoimmune diseases including anti-PFDN5 antibodies were independently associated with the occurrence of JIA-U. In addition, we aimed to estimate the multivariable association of established uveitis risk factors and biomarkers with uveitis.

Disease activity measurements

At each visit, disease activity was assessed by rheumatologists using a 21-point numeric rating scale (NRS; 0–10, 0 = inactive), the number of active joints, and laboratory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and ESR. In addition, parents provided a global assessment of the child’s overall well-being (NRS 0–10, 0 = best). The clinical Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score (cJADAS)-10 was calculated using the formula described by Consolaro et al. [29]. Disease activity was categorized according to Trincianti et al. [30] as follows: inactive disease (cJADAS-10 ≤ 1.1 for oligoarthritis / ≤2.5 for polyarthritis), minimal disease activity (1.2–4 / 2.6–5), moderate disease activity (4.1–12 / 5.1–16), and high disease activity (> 12 / >16).

Patients underwent slit-lamp examination performed by ophthalmologists in accordance with the screening guidelines by the German study group ‘Uveitis in Childhood’ [15]. Patients diagnosed with uveitis, as defined by the Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN) criteria, were further evaluated by an ophthalmologist for uveitis activity, treatment, and possible uveitis-related complications.

Autoantibody biomarkers measurements

Each serum sample was tested for a spectrum of autoantibodies as detailed below using multi-analyte array diagnostic technologies (Mitogen Diagnostics Corp: https://mitogendx.com). Anti-DFS70 antibodies were measured by a chemiluminescence immunoassay (CIA: Bio Flash, Werfen, San Diego, CA). ANA testing included immunofluorescence patterns (ICAP) [31, 32] and titer determined by IIF on HEp-2 cell substrates (Werfen) at a dilution of 1/80 and processed and read on an automated digital ANA IIF reader (NovaView: Werfen). Anti-cyclic citrullinated protein antibodies were tested by ELISA (Quanta Lite, CCP3, Werfen) and anti-virus citrullinated peptides (VCP)VCP1, anti-VCP2, anti-histone citrullinated peptides (HCP) HCP1 andanti-HCP2 by addressable laser bead immunoassay (ALBIA) (FIDIS®, TheraDiag, Paris, France). Antibodies to extractable nuclear antigens (ENA: histones, ribosomal P, Smith (Sm), U1-RNP, Ro60/TROVE2, Ro52/TRIM21, SS-B/La, Scl-70 (topoisomerase I), Jo-1 [histidyl tRNA synthetase] were tested by ALBIA (FIDIS®, TheraDiag) on a Luminex200® platform (Luminex Inc., Austin, TX) and by line immunoassays (LIA: Euroimmun, Lubeck, Germany) The immunoassay for anti-prefoldin 5 PFDN-5 utilized a purified full-length human recombinant prefoldin subunit 5 protein (ProSpec, East Brunswick NJ USA, PFDN5 Catalogue # PRO-901) in an ALBIA using coupling and assay performance parameters as previously described [33], and detailed in Supplementary File 1. All antibody testing was performed using standardized and validated protocols. Positivity thresholds were defined according to the manufacturers’ recommendations while standard laboratory quality control procedures were applied.

Statistical analysis

Standard descriptive analysis was performed to report the distribution of parameters for the total cohort as well as patients with and without uveitis. Categorical parameters such as ICAP Anti-Cell (AC) IIF patterns were compared between patients with and without uveitis by a Chi² Test. The Youden index was calculated to estimate the associated cut-off for ANA titers ability for classifying patients with and without uveitis. A Poisson regression analyses by using robust error variances was performed to estimate the multivariable association of established uveitis risk factors and biomarkers with uveitis [34]. ANA titers were treated as ordinal variables descriptively and included as continuous variables in regression analyses. A generalized additive model was applied to examine the relationships of ANA titers and uveitis in light of nonlinear dependency structures by splines as preliminary analysis. The area under curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the model performance based on the predicted probabilities. The resulting AUC’s were compared by the test of DeLong et al. [35]. In addition, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) [36] was determined to assess the improvement of the risk difference between patients with and without uveitis. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS® 9.3 [37].

Results

Patients characteristics

A total of 193 JIA patients were enrolled in the study and the median time from diagnosis to enrollment was 1.8 months (IQR 0.5–4.2). Treatment with disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), including conventional synthetic (cs) and biologic (b) DMARDs, had been started prior to enrollment in 95 patients (49.2%), In patients treated with DMARDs prior to enrollment, the median time from JIA diagnosis to enrollment was 2.5 months (IQR 1.0-4.8) and median treatment duration 1.5 months (IQR 0.8–4.1). However, 30 of these patients (31.6%) had been on DMARD treatment for less than four weeks.

Among the 193 JIA patients, 52 (26.9%) had uveitis by the three-year follow-up. Both eyes were affected in 20 of these patients (38%). The median age at uveitis onset was 4.8 years (IQR 3.7–6.6). Median times from JIA diagnosis and ICON enrollment to uveitis diagnosis were 17.9 months (IQR 5.8–26.6) and 1.8 years (IQR 0.7–2.7) respectively, Uveitis preceded the diagnosis of JIA in 4 patients (2.1%) and study enrollment in 8 patients (4.2%). Mean visual acuity in the worse eye was 0.25 logMAR (SD 0.35), and 29 patients (76%) had an anterior chamber cell grade of ≥ 0.5 + at first ophthalmologic assessment. Complications of uveitis were present in 26% (n = 13/49) of affected patients at first uveitis documentation, most commonly posterior synechiae (n = 8/49, 16.3%) and band-keratopathy (n = 2/49, 4.1%).

Patients with uveitis were significantly younger at JIA diagnosis than children without uveitis (median: 3.2 years vs. 9.7 years; p < 0.001). JIA disease activity parameters were comparable between patients with and without uveitis. Detailed patient characteristics are presented in Table 1.

ANA titers and patterns

ANA positivity at titers ≥ 1:80 was detected in 96.2% children with uveitis (50/52) compared to 67.4% children without uveitis (95/141; p < 0.001; Table 2). Only two ANA-negative patients, both with RF-negative polyarthritis, developed uveitis. ANA titers were significantly higher in patients with uveitis than in those without (median [IQR]; 640 [240–5120] vs. 160 [0–640]; p < 0.001). Using a cutoff of ≥ 1:80, ANA demonstrated a sensitivity of 96% and a specificity of 33% for uveitis. A cut-off of ANA titer of ≥ 1:320 estimated by the Youden index, yielded a sensitivity of 75% and a specificity of 62% for uveitis. This threshold corresponded to the maximum difference in the probability of uveitis between patients ANA titers below and above 1:320.

ICAP Anti-Cell (AC) IIF patterns observed in patient groups without and with uveitis

As shown in Table 2 the nuclear fine speckled AC-30 ICAP pattern was significantly more common in children with uveitis than in those without (63.5% vs. 23.4; p < 0.001). In contrast, the nuclear fine speckled AC-4 pattern (distinguished from AC-30 by the absence of mitotic plate chromatin staining) was found more frequent in children without uveitis (28.4.%, n = 40) than in those with uveitis (11.5%, n = 6; p < 0.018). Other AC-patterns or combinations thereof were found only infrequently in both disease groups. Eight children developed uveitis prior to ICON enrollment; however, their ANA titers and patterns did not differ from those who developed uveitis after ICON enrollment. No associations were found between ANA titers and uveitis complications at first uveitis documentation.

The AC-30 IIF pattern occurred most frequently in oligoarthritis patients (46.9%) and less frequent in RF-negative polyarthritis 9.1%, while it was rarely found in other JIA categories. There were no significant differences in ANA titers or patterns between children with persistent versus extended oligoarthritis. Similarly, ANA levels and patterns did not differ significantly between DMARD-naïve patients (DMARD start < 30 days before the first ICON visit = enrollment) and those with longer prior DMARD exposure.

In patients with uveitis, ANA titers associated with the most frequently observed patterns—AC-30, AC-1, and AC-2—were ≥ 1:320 in 80%, 67%, and 100% of cases, respectively. At least two-thirds of ANA-positive uveitis patients with these patterns had titers ≥ 1:640, including 79% with the AC-30 pattern, 67% with AC-1, and 100% with AC-2 as shown in Table 3.

Other autoantibodies

Antibodies against prefoldin subunit 5 (PFDN5) were detected in 44% children with uveitis (23/52) compared with 21% of those without uveitis (30/141; p = 0.009). In addition, patients with uveitis showed significantly higher anti-PFDN5 antibody titers compared to non-uveitis JIA patients (mean ± SD; 486 ± 496 vs. 322 ± 330; p = 0.010). Anti-PFDN5 antibody titers did not significantly differ between JIA categories (mean ± SD; oligoarthritis 325 ± 248; RF-negative polyarthritis 356 ± 362; all other JIA categories 440 ± 554; p = 0.667).

JIA-U patients with positive ANA with AC-30 pattern had significantly higher anti-PFDN5 antibodies (mean ± SD; 557 ± 579) than those without uveitis and AC-30 pattern (mean ± SD; 317 ± 73, p = 0.041).

Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP3) antibodies (> 20 units) were positive in 38.5% of patients with uveitis and 22.7% of patients without uveitis; however, median anti-CCP3 titers did not differ significantly between the groups. Other autoantibodies, including anti-histone, anti-DFS70, anti-Jo-1, anti-ribosomal P70, anti-Sm, anti-Sm/RNP, anti-Scl-70, anti-PM/Scl, anti-CENP-B, anti-PCNA, anti- TRIM21/Ro52 anti- TROVE2/Ro60/, anti-SSB/La, anti-VCP1, anti-VCP2, anti-HCP1, anti-HCP2, and rheumatoid factor were only infrequently detected in both groups.

Multivariable association of uveitis risk factors and autoantibodies/ICAP pattern with uveitis

The multivariable model of established uveitis risk factors (age at JIA onset, ILAR category, female sex) with uveitis resulted in an AUC of 0.77 (see Supplementary File 1.2 for the regression coefficients of the model). The additional inclusion of ANA titer levels, anti-PFDN5 antibodies, ICAP AC-30 and AC-4 IIF patterns resulted in an AUC of 0.86 (Table 4; Fig. 1). The four additional parameters besides the established uveitis risk factors significantly improved the discrimination performance of the model (IDI = 0.06, p = 0.005).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this prospective longitudinal study is the first to broadly examine autoantibodies and their titers in a large JIA cohort with and without uveitis. Among 193 patients, 26.9% developed uveitis, most within two years of JIA diagnosis, with higher rates in younger children and those with oligoarticular JIA. At first documentation, 26% had uveitis complications. Uveitis was strongly linked to ANA positivity, higher ANA titers, and the AC-30 IIF pattern. A titer of 1:320 corresponded to the maximum difference in the probability of uveitis between patients ANA titers below and above 1:320. Conversely, the AC-4 pattern was more frequent in children without uveitis. In addition, Anti-PFDN5 may represent a promising new biomarker for JIA-U.

Risk factors

Younger age at JIA diagnosis (median: 3.2 vs. 9.7 years) was significantly associated with uveitis, a finding consistent with prior studies identifying age < 7 years as a major risk factor [13, 38, 39]. The median time to uveitis diagnosis after JIA diagnosis (17.9 months) and the rate of uveitis-related complications at first uveitis manifestation (26%) aligns with previous reports [9, 11, 15]. These findings highlight the importance of early detection and timely intervention to prevent irreversible ocular damage [38, 40], underscore the need for rigorous ophthalmologic monitoring during the early disease course, particularly in high-risk patients [41].

Role of ANA positivity, titers, and pattern in JIA-associated uveitis

ANA testing remains a cornerstone in the risk assessment for uveitis among children with JIA. In our cohort, ANA positivity was observed in 96.2% of children with uveitis. At titers ≥ 1:160, ANA positivity demonstrated high sensitivity (96%) but low specificity (33%) for uveitis, reaffirming its established role as a screening tool rather than a definitive predictor. A recent meta-analysis reported that the percentage of JIA patients with uveitis who were ANA positive ranged from 14% to 98% [20]. The pooled sensitivity and specificity of ANA positivity across 13 published studies were 75.0% and 66.0% at a 1:40 titer, 63.0% and 60.0% at 1:80, and 52.0% and 68.0% at 1:160, respectively. The wide variation in ANA positivity across studies suggests that defining a specific titer threshold as a reliable inter-laboratory risk factor may not be feasible. The authors called for a standardized, multi-center, systematic, prospective cohort study to confidently determine the sensitivity and specificity of a particular ANA IFA titre in predicting JIA and/or the risk of uveitis [20].

Addressing this gap, to our knowledge, this is the first study to report that ANA titers were significantly higher in children with uveitis compared to those without (median 640 vs. 160, a cut-off of 1:320, respectively), supporting the potential use of not only ANA positivity but also end point titers as a marker of increased JIA-U risk.

Novel and clinically significant findings are the predominance of the nuclear fine speckled ICAP AC-30 ANA pattern among JIA-U patients. Patients with two or more Hep-2 IIF patterns were rarely observed. The AC-30 pattern characterized by fine, evenly distributed speckles in interphase nuclei accompanied by staining of the condensed chromatin plate in metaphase cells, distinguishes it from AC-4 characterized as fine tiny speckles throughout interphase nuclei but no staining of the metaphase chromatin plate and from AC-2 which is similar but the periphery of the nucleus (nuclear envelope region) is irregular rather than smoothly stained [42]. The AC-30 pattern suggests that the target autoantigen is chromatin-related, but neither antibodies to histones nor dsDNA were detected in our JIA-U sera. This suggests that further studies are needed to identify the apparent chromatin-related autoantibody target(s).

The AC-30 pattern was particularly enriched in oligoarticular persistent/extended and RF-negative polyarticular JIA categories, which collectively comprise over 90% of JIA-U cases. This suggests that detailed ANA pattern analysis may enhance risk stratification beyond simple ANA positivity, especially in these high-risk subgroups. Interestingly, the AC-4 pattern was significantly more common in children without uveitis, suggesting it may be a protective factor or associated with another JIA phenotype. The AC-4 pattern, characterized by fine speckles throughout the nucleus but excluding nucleoli, is often associated with extractable nuclear antigens (for example, SSB/La, TROVE2.Ro60, Ku (DNA-dependent protein kinase) and appears to be less frequent in children with uveitis. Interestingly, anti-SSB/La and anti-TROVE2/Ro60 are associated with Sjögren disease and other systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [43].

In contrast to our study, a study of a large JIA cohort by Sener et al. [44] found that uveitis is most associated with the nuclear AC-4/5 (fine or large speckled) and cytoplasmic AC-19 (dense, fine speckled) ANA patterns, and an ANA titer of 1/160. Also, in another small cohort, all 12 patients with JIA-U exhibited the nuclear AC-1 (homogenous) ANA pattern [45]. While our study identified the predominance of nuclear fine speckled AC-30 pattern and higher ANA titers in uveitis patients, the findings of Sener et al. [44] and Koru et al. [45] suggest that other ANA patterns, particularly AC-4/5, AC-19, and AC-1, may be relevant for risk stratification. Since it is not mentioned, it is not clear if that study considered the recently defined AC-30 ‘expert’ pattern included in our study. Taken together, these studies highlight the importance of detailed ANA pattern analysis in identifying JIA patients at increased risk for uveitis, although further multicenter studies are needed to refine their predictive value.

Potential new biomarkers

In our study, PFDN5 antibody was found at significantly elevated levels in children with JIA-U (mean 486.39 vs. 321.85), suggesting it is potentially a biomarker. PFDN5 is a subunit of the prefoldin complex that helps fold newly synthesized proteins such as actin and tubulin, ensuring proper protein assembly and cell homeostasis through proteasome-dependent protein degradation [46]. Our findings are consistent with findings from Kwon et al. [25], who reported that serum levels of PFDN5 antibodies and PFDN5 protein levels were significantly higher in AS patients with uveitis (n = 25) compared to those without (n = 25). In their animal study using curdlan-treated SKG mice that developed both arthritis and uveitis, the serum levels of anti-PFDN5 antibodies increased over time in these mice along with increased expression of PFDN5 and apoptosis in the ocular lesions. Knockdown of PFDN5 in ARPE19 cells resulted in increased apoptosis, suggesting a protective role of PFDN5 against cell death in uveitis. These findings support the potential utility of PFDN5 antibody as a biomarker for uveitis across different autoimmune diseases.

Furthermore, anti-histone antibodies that target the core histones H2a, H2B, H3, or H4 proteins were not confirmed as potential biomarkers for uveitis. A Norwegian study reported that anti-histone antibodies occurred in a significantly higher proportion of children with (JIA-U) (4 of 16; 25%) compared to children with JIA without uveitis (2 of 84; 2.4%) and healthy controls (1 of 58; 1.7%) [26]. It is possible that the differences are related to different histone assays employed in our studies. For example, in their study anti-histone antibodies were detected with a commercial ELISA kit and included IgG and a combined IgM/IgG conjugate, whereas we used a commercial ALBIA that detected IgG antibodies to the core histones H2a, H2b, H3 and H4. In a 1993 seminal study of JIA antibodies directed to chromatin components, including histones and high mobility group proteins, no significant differences in IgG antibody binding to any of these antigens were observed in sera from patients with or without uveitis [47]. Other promising antibodies, such as anti-DFS70, were not confirmed as specific or reliable biomarkers for JIA-U in our study [24].

Clinical implications for consideration

Our study has several important clinical implications for the management of JIA-U. First, risk stratification using IIF on HEp-2 substrates should go beyond simple ANA positivity and include ANA titer levels and specific ANA patterns, particularly the AC-30 pattern. Our data reinforces that the first two years after JIA diagnosis represent the highest risk for uveitis onset, underscoring the need for consistent ophthalmologic screening during this window. Current screening guidelines recommend that high-risk groups, such as ANA-positive patients and those with oligoarthritis or RF-negative polyarthritis, undergo slit-lamp examinations every 3 months during the first four years of disease [15, 48]. However, based on our findings, additional available risk factors, such as ANA titer levels, and specific ANA patterns (for example, AC-30 pattern) may be considered to guide screening frequency. Additionally, our identification of PFDN5 as a potential novel biomarker for uveitis suggests that pending external validation and clinical availability, future screening protocols could be further refined. Collectively, these findings support a more nuanced and individualized approach to screening and monitoring, which may ultimately improve outcomes for children with JIA who are at risk for uveitis.

Limitations

For the first time, we investigated ANA IIF patterns and PFDN5 antibodies in JIA patients with and without uveitis. Therefore, these findings require confirmation in an independent, prospectively recruited cohort. ANA titers were analyzed as a continuous variable in the multivariable model, based on the hypothesis of a linear, monotonic increase in the likelihood of uveitis with increasing ANA titers. The analyses did not indicate a non-linear association in our data; however, this finding should be confirmed in an independent cohort. There was substantial overlap between oligoarthritis and the AC-30 IIF pattern. Whether the AC-30 IIF pattern represents an independent risk factor for uveitis or rather a specific clinical characteristic of oligoarthritis remains to be investigated in future studies. Only uveitis cases occurring within the first three years after JIA diagnosis were considered, which may limit the generalizability of the results to all JIA-U cases.

The potential influence of early DMARD therapy on biomarkers’ expression cannot be excluded, and longitudinal antibody measurements were not done. However, no significant differences in ANA titers, patterns, or other autoantibodies were observed between DMARD-naïve patients and those who had received longer prior to enrollment. Certain monogenic autoinflammatory disorders that can mimic JIA, particularly Blau syndrome (nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 2–associated disease), cannot be completely excluded, as routine genetic screening is not standard of care and is therefore not routinely performed [49, 50]. However, these diseases are very rare and are unlikely to influence our analysis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings indicate that higher ANA titers, the AC-30 ANA IIF pattern, and younger age at JIA onset were independently associated with uveitis in this cohort. The discovery of elevated PFDN5 antibodies in children with uveitis expands the repertoire of potential biomarkers for risk assessment. Combining these serological markers with clinical risk factors may improve early identification of high-risk patients and support the development of better tailored screening strategies to prevent vision-threatening complications. Future research is needed to validate these biomarkers in independent cohorts and to elucidate their mechanistic roles in JIA-associated uveitis.
