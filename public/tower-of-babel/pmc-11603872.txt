Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics adjuvant camouflage therapy for Class III malocclusion: a case report
Authors: Yao Jiao, Yitong Liu, Xiaoyan Li, Song Li, Junji Xu, Yi Liu, Lijia Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthodontic treatment carries potential risks and complications, especially if the path of tooth movement extends beyond the alveolar bone or if the patient has a thin bone plate. This amplifies the patient’s risk of complications such as bone fenestration and bone dehiscence. Hence, orthodontists should consider the patient’s periodontal and dental condition during treatment and opt for a multidisciplinary approach when necessary to achieve optimal results.Case presentationThis case report describes a successfully treated case of Class III malocclusion using an adjuvant therapy known as periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO). A 25-year-old man sought treatment primarily due to an evident anterior negative overjet. The patient was diagnosed with a skeletal Class III relationship and mandibular prognathism. A clinical examination revealed a severe Class III molar relationship and an anterior negative overjet. Moreover, cone-beam computed tomography revealed a thin labial bone plate in relation to the mandibular anterior teeth. The molar relation was corrected through molar distalization using miniscrews, and the mandibular incisors were retracted in conjunction with the PAOO procedure. Post-treatment outcomes demonstrated improved occlusal relationship, thickened labial bone plate of the mandibular anterior teeth, and absence of bone fenestration or dehiscence.ConclusionsThis clinical case shows that orthodontic treatment requires a multidisciplinary approach. The favorable results indicate that PAOO is effective in maintaining alveolar bone thickness and reducing the risk of periodontal complications.Keywords: Class III malocclusion, Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics, Camouflage therapy

Background

Alveolar bone fenestration and bone dehiscence are significant alveolar bone defects. They manifest as absent alveolar bone from the surface of the tooth’s root, thereby either exposing the root in the oral cavity or being only covered by soft tissue [1]. This can lead to symptoms such as gingival recession and tooth mobility, posing a considerable risk during orthodontic treatment [2]. At present, their etiology is not completely clear, as orthodontic treatment may contribute to their occurrence [3, 4]. Therefore, evaluating the patient’s alveolar bone status before orthodontic treatment is crucial. If the position of the expected tooth movement exceeds the alveolar bone thickness, periodontal surgery should be considered to reduce postoperative complications [5].

Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) is a surgical periodontal procedure to increase the alveolar bone thickness, accelerate the speed of tooth movement, and reduce the risk of orthodontic complications by combining corticotomy and bone grafting [6, 7]. PAOO surgery can be performed before orthodontic treatment for patients with thin alveolar bone, insufficient width of keratinized gingiva, or expected tooth movement extending beyond the alveolar bone [8, 9]. This case report describes the successful treatment of Class III malocclusion using adjuvant therapy with PAOO. It discusses the indications for PAOO, the timing of surgery, and the timing and precautions associated with orthodontic treatment post-PAOO.

Case presentation

Diagnosis and aetiology

A 25-year-old man presented to the orthodontics department with an anterior negative overjet. The patient had no relevant medical or family history, and previous clinical examinations and imaging studies had not revealed any temporomandibular joint disorders.

A facial photograph illustrated facial asymmetry of the patient’s right side, featuring a slightly sunken profile on lateral view (Fig. 1). The dentition photograph showed a Class III occlusal relationship, an anterior negative overjet, and a 3 mm negative overjet with the maxillary midline offset to the right by 1 mm and the mandibular midline offset to the left by 0.5 mm. Furthermore, both mandibular and maxillary dentitions displayed mild crowding. The mandible could regress, but not completely, to the end-to-end incisor bite, and a centric occlusion-to-centric relation (CO/CR) discrepancy was evident. In addition, the pretreatment panoramic radiograph confirmed the presence of all the third molars, which were vertically oriented. Notably, the right maxillary third molar exhibited an abnormal tooth morphology (Fig. 2A).

The pretreatment lateral cephalometric assessment showed that the patient had skeletal Class III malocclusion with a slightly protruding mandible (ANB, −2.1°; SNB, 85.1°), a low mandibular plane (FMA, 22.5°), and a mildly compensatory lingual inclination of the mandibular incisors (IMPA, 85.4°) (Fig. 2A; Table 1).

Cephalometric analysis

The preoperative three-dimensional reconstruction of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans showed that the patient’s mandible was deviated, with a 1.4 mm right-sided deviation of the menton (Fig. 2B). The labial alveolar bone of the mandibular anterior teeth had very thin ridge, and there was a risk of bone fenestration and bone dehiscence. Moreover, the height of the alveolar ridge on the lingual side of the mandibular anterior teeth was located at the cervical 1/3 of the root (Fig. 2C–D).

Treatment alternatives

Two treatments were considered for this patient. The first was orthognathic surgery to correct the sagittal differences in the bones wherein, the overdeveloped mandible would be corrected via the sagittal splitting of the mandibular ramus, and the negative overjet of the anterior teeth would be corrected to establish a harmonious skeletal and occlusal relationship. However, the patient was reluctant to accept this treatment option.

The second option involved camouflage therapy employing molar distalization and miniscrew placement. After extraction of all four third molars, a miniscrew can be placed bilaterally in the mandible and maxilla. The molars are then pushed back to adjust the axial inclination of the mandibular anterior teeth, reposition them upright, and correct both overbite and overjet. Subsequently, maxillary molar distalization can be used to correct the dental midline. The patient opted for this treatment regimen; however, it does not significantly improve the facial shape. Moreover, while distalizing the molars, the mandibular anterior teeth could move inwardly, and there is a risk of bone fenestration and bone dehiscence in patients with thin labial bone plates.

Treatment progress

The patient was transferred to the periodontal department for basic periodontal treatment before the orthodontic treatment commenced, and a routine periodontal examination was performed. The preoperative CBCT revealed a thin mandibular anterior labial bone plate (Fig. 2C–D); therefore, to avoid orthodontic treatment associated complications, we performed PAOO with a bone graft (Geistlich Bio-Oss Collagen, Swiss) on the labial side of the mandibular anterior teeth 3 weeks before bonding the fixed appliances (Fig. 3). This can augment the thickness of the bone plate and reduce the incidence of periodontal complications.

Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) procedure; A pre-operative photos; B horizontal incision; C full-thickness flap; D corticotomy, and bone graft material were implanted on the labial side of the operation area; E reduction of gingival flap; F closure without creating excessive tension

After all the third molar extractions, straight wire self-ligating brackets (Damon Q, Ormco, Brea, Calif) were bonded to all the teeth. The initial alignment and leveling of the dentition were achieved by replacing the nickel-titanium wires sequentially (0.014-in, 0.018-in, 0.018 × 0.025-in, and 0.019 × 0.025-in). During this process, a pad was added to the two maxillary first molars to raise the bite and avoid occlusal interference of the anterior teeth. Simultaneously, a long Class III elastic (1/4-in, 3.5 oz) was attached bilaterally to correct the mesial relationship of the molars and anterior negative overjet as well as to adjust the axis of the incisors.

Together with the alignment and leveling of the dentition, a transition was made to a 0.019 × 0.025-in stainless steel archwire, specifically employed for sliding the teeth along the archwire. Following this, one miniscrew (Ormco, Brea, Calif) was placed between the mandibular first and second molars bilaterally and at the maxillary left zygomatic alveolar ridge. After the miniscrew was stabilized, the molar distalization was initiated. During this period, the patient has been utilizing Class III elastics, and achieving good torque control of the anterior teeth necessitates a synergistic approach involving miniscrews in conjunction with Class III elastics [10].

Tooth distalization in all three quadrants involved pulling the elastomeric chain from the miniscrew to the canine, thereby creating a gap between the lateral incisor and the canine. Meanwhile, the maxillary molar distalization involved adjusting the midline; hence, the left maxillary posterior teeth reached the target position faster, and the canines and miniscrew were ligated with a ligation wire to prevent recurrence. At this time, the molars still maintained a mesial relationship, and bilaterally the mandible continued to push back the molars. To expedite the anterior teeth’s adduction and facilitate the long axis adjustment of the teeth, bilateral long Class III elastics (1/4-in, 3.5 oz) were introduced (Fig. 4).

Progress facial and intraoral photos 1 year into treatment

After 1.5 years of treatment, the molar distalization ended when the molars transitioned to a neutral relationship. At this time, the anterior negative overjet had been corrected, but there was still generalized spacing in the dentition. After 2.5 years of treatment, all the spaces were closed, the canines and molars achieved a neutral occlusal relationship, and the overbite and overjet of the anterior teeth reached a normal range. Notably, although the axial inclination of the mandibular incisors changed, due to good torque control, the root apex of the mandibular anterior teeth did not exceed the limit of the alveolar bone. Furthermore, there were no complications observed on the labial and lingual sides of the mandibular anterior teeth, such as fenestration, bone dehiscence, or obvious gingival recession. During the treatment period, owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient was not followed up for about 4 months, and the total treatment time was 30 months.

Treatment results

The expected treatment goals were achieved, as shown in the post-treatment record. A good occlusal relationship and tooth alignment were obtained, and subsequent facial photographs showed no significant changes. The anterior negative overjet was significantly corrected, and the canine and molar relationships were established compared to the pretreatment phase (Fig. 5).

Postreatment facial and intraoral photographs

Panoramic radiographs confirmed that the parallelism of the tooth roots was acceptable (Fig. 6A). The postoperative CBCT 3D reconstruction showed a slight correction of the mandibular deviation (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, images showed increased thickness of the bone plate on the labial aspect of the mandibular anterior teeth, with no apparent signs of alveolar bone resorption or bone dehiscence (Fig. 6C–D). Due to the retraction of the mandibular anterior teeth, the lingual alveolar bone height of the two mandibular central incisors was slightly reduced; however, the lingual alveolar bone height of the lateral incisors and canines was largely maintained. Moreover, intraoral photos indicated the absence of gingival recession after treatment.

The patient’s mandible could retract close to an end-to-end incisor bite before undergoing orthodontic treatment, indicating that the negative overjet had functional components, along with a detected CO/CR shift. This shift in CO/CR was a contributing factor that enabled the patient to undergo camouflage therapy successfully. However, after orthodontic treatment, the patient’s ANB angle improved, and the deviation of the mandibular also changed, as evidenced in Figs. 2B and 6B, indicating that the potential CO/CR discrepancy may have a functional component in the lateral dimension, resulting in a midline shift. To compensate for the bony dissonance, the maxillary anterior teeth were labially inclined, and the mandibular anterior teeth were lingually inclined (U1-SN, 121.0°; L1-MP, 73.6°). Additionally, the pushback of the mandibular molars corrected the molar relationship (Fig. 7).

The patient was satisfied with the final facial esthetics and occlusion achieved. A stable occlusal relationship and a harmonious facial appearance were maintained at the 1.3-year follow-up (Fig. 8A), and the CBCT in the follow-up showed stable thickening of the labial bone of the mandibular anterior teeth (Fig. 8B).

Three-dimensional reconstructions of the CBCT scans at various time points were generated, and the before and after treatment 3D models were superimposed (Fig. 9A), with the depiction in white representing the condition before treatment and that in red, after treatment. Using the mandible as the baseline, it was observed that there was an obvious increment in the labial side of the mandibular anterior teeth, indicating the effect of bone grafting. Additionally, when viewed from the bottom to the top with the skull as the reference, a slight backward movement of the mandible was evident, which suggested that the patient’s mandible was no longer forced to reach forward after the occlusal interference was relieved.

The CBCT images of the labial bone plate of the mandibular anterior teeth at three different time points were observed and measured (Fig. 9B–D). The measurement method involved assessing bone thickness at the cervical, middle, and apical thirds of the tooth root and then calculating their average value to determine the average labial bone thickness at that tooth position. The results showed that the root shape of the mandibular anterior teeth was discernible, and the alveolar bone was thin before treatment. Following the PAOO and orthodontic treatments, the alveolar bone thickness increased. In addition, the effects of retention were still evident and stable even at the 1.3-year follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

Fenestration and dehiscence are common manifestations of malocclusion. Their distribution in the maxilla and mandibular varies, with fenestration being more common in the maxilla and dehiscence being more common in the mandible [11, 12]. The occurrence of fenestrations and dehiscences is also related to the tooth position and volume, and the alveolar bone volume. Bone fenestration usually occurs in the maxillary lateral incisors, canines, and first premolars, while bone dehiscence easily occurs in the mandibular incisors, canines, and maxillary first premolars [13, 14]. In patients undergoing orthodontic treatment, with thin plates, congenital fenestrations, or dehiscences, increase the risk of these issues arising or worsening during treatment [15].

Orthodontic treatment involves application of force to move the teeth within the alveolar bone. This tooth movement is limited, and the anatomical shape and thickness of the alveolar bone are the main factors limiting the stability and range of tooth movement [16]. Studies have shown that the risk of alveolar bone dehiscence increases in adults when the mandibular central incisors are significantly labially inclined during orthodontic treatment [17]. Sheng et al. analyzed the changes in the incidence of fenestration and dehiscence before and after orthodontic treatment [15]. They found that the incidence of dehiscence in the palatal aspect of anterior teeth, the labial side of the mandibular anterior teeth, and the lingual side of the mandibular anterior teeth increased by 19%, 20%, and 30%, respectively, associated with the thickness of the alveolar bone on the corresponding side. Specifically, they noted that if the maxillary incisor and lateral incisor root apex to the labial side have an alveolar bone thickness > 4 mm, the maxillary lateral incisor root apex to the palatal side has an alveolar bone thickness > 3 mm, and the mandibular incisor root apex to the labial and lingual sides has an alveolar bone thickness > 5 mm, the incidence of bone defects is significantly reduced [15].

At the beginning of the 21st century, Wilcko et al. [6] formally proposed the concept of PAOO. They found that corticotomy combined with bone augmentation not only accelerate tooth movement but also increase the thickness of the labial alveolar bone, achieving good treatment results. Their approach combined bone grafting and corticotomy into one procedure for the first time. Numerous clinical trials have since confirmed that PAOO not only accelerates orthodontic treatment but also broadens its clinical applicability and reduces the risk of periodontal tissue damage [7]. To compare the effect of surgery on periodontal conditions during anterior teeth adduction, Wang et al. conducted a study involving skeletal Class I patients. They divided the patients into two groups: an operation group and an orthodontic control group, each consisting of 10 cases, and measured changes in both soft and hard tissues. They found that corticotomy and bone augmentation significantly improved the height and thickness of the alveolar bone and the width of the keratinized gingival tissue following treatment [18]. Compared to traditional orthodontic methods, PAOO can move teeth 2–3 times the distance in 1/3–1/4 of the time while forming new alveolar bone around the teeth, thickening the periodontal phenotype, and enhancing the stability of the periodontal tissue thus expanding the range of tooth movement [19].

For orthodontic patients with skeletal Class III orthognathic surgery, the mandibular anterior teeth usually move labially during the process of decompensation. To avoid the teeth exceeding the alveolar bone limit, bone augmentation can be performed on the labial side of the mandibular anterior teeth to expand the range of tooth movement. For patients with skeletal Class III who need camouflage treatment, PAOO is often performed on the lingual side if there is insufficient bone mass because the mandibular anterior teeth generally move to the lingual side. However, if the torque control is not good during the movement of the mandibular anterior teeth to the lingual side, the crown may tilt to the lingual side and the root may tilt to the labial side. When the patient has thin alveolar bone, gingival recession, thin gingival biotype, or has bone dehiscence and fenestration at the labial side of the mandibular anterior teeth, bone augmentation or soft tissue augmentation can be used to prevent the possible risks. In the present case, the labial bone plate of the patient was thin and some teeth already had bone fenestration; also, the preoperative photographs showed gingival recession (Fig. 3A), so we considered this patient as having a high risk of periodontal complications. Studies have shown that patients with skeletal Class III have a thinner bone thickness on the labial side of mandibular anterior teeth than those with normal occlusion [20], and during Class III orthodontic treatment, patients experience compensatory inclination of mandibular teeth and a higher probability of bone fenestration and bone dehiscence [21]. Therefore, when making treatment plans, the process of tooth movement needs to be fully assessed to avoid periodontal-related complications. And, although this case is a camouflage therapy to control the torque, during the internal retraction of the anterior teeth, if the torque control is unsatisfactory, there is also the risk of the crown rotating lingually and the root labially. This is also one of the reasons to choose labial aspect as the surgical site. Furthermore, studies have analyzed the risk factors of gingival recession after orthodontic treatment and found that thin gingival biotype and previous gingival recession increase the risk of further gingival recession [22], According to the literature review and previous clinical experience, PAOO-assisted orthodontic treatment is a promising method to improve alveolar bone dehiscence and fenestration of the lower anterior teeth for patients with skeletal Class III, and it may also protect the anterior teeth from dehiscence [1]. Besides bone increment, PAOO can also increase the width of keratinized gingiva and change gingival biotype [8]. Moreover, lingual PAOO is limited by technical sensitivity, and the effect of bone augmentation is limited. Therefore, in this case, prophylactic surgery on the labial side was considered. Furthermore, the gingival phenotype was also changed as confirmed by pre- and post-treatment photographs (Figs. 1 and 5). As part of our treatment plan, we decided to perform the PAOO procedure before placing the orthodontic appliance. This approach serves a dual purpose: it expands the limits of tooth movement and expedites the tooth alignment process.

For this patient, we engaged in discussions before orthodontic treatment and did not rule out the possibility of performing PAOO on the upper anterior teeth. However, the pre-treatment CBCT scan revealed an adequate amount of cortical bone on the labial side of the upper anterior teeth, with no indications such as bone fenestration, bone dehiscence, or gingival recession. The pre-treatment photographs further demonstrated that the patient had a thick gingival biotype and ample keratinized gingiva in the region of the maxillary anterior teeth. We also sought the opinion of a periodontist, who advised regular monitoring throughout the orthodontic process, along with the potential for soft tissue augmentation if needed. It is crucial to acknowledge that the labial side of the maxillary anterior teeth constitutes an esthetic zone; consequently, performing periodontal surgery with an incision near the gingival margin carries a risk of gingival recession. As such, we would consider a vestibular sulcus incision if deemed necessary for proper augmentation of the anterior teeth. Ultimately, there were no complications such as bone fenestration, bone dehiscence, or gingival recession, and the CBCT scan confirmed the presence of a bone plate on the labial side of the maxillary anterior teeth (Fig. 10A–B). Therefore, PAOO was not performed on the maxillary anterior teeth.

The timing of orthodontic treatment after PAOO is related to the regional acceleratory phenomenon (RAP). It has been documented that the RAP generally peaks around 1 to 2 months after surgery. This can generally be maintained for about 4 months before gradually disappearing [23]. Amit et al. suggested that orthodontic treatment should be initiated within 2 weeks or even immediately after PAOO surgery [19]. They recommended that patients follow-up with a visit every 2–3 weeks in the first 3 months, followed by monthly visits, to fully leverage the accelerated effects of the RAP. Therefore, brackets should typically be bonded before surgery, and archwires should be placed 2 weeks after surgery. This approach optimizes the utilization of the RAP to accelerate orthodontic tooth movement and promote bone formation while minimizing its impact on graft healing [24]. However, despite these recommendations, the bonding time for our patient was adjusted to 3 weeks after surgery due to scheduling conflicts. Fortunately, because the soft tissue had healed well by this time, no significant soft tissue damage was observed during the bonding and placement of the archwires.

Since our patient opted to undergo non-orthognathic camouflage therapy, they had to sacrifice some teeth to cover up their bony imbalance, and it was necessary to adjust the mandibular anterior teeth to be more lingually inclined and the maxillary anterior teeth to be more labial inclined (Fig. 7). Therefore, the roots of the mandibular anterior teeth may have rotated labially, which is one of the purposes of the PAOO intervention. Subsequent CBCT scans post-treatment revealed a significant increase in the thickness of the labial bone plate adjacent to the mandibular anterior teeth, and the roots of the teeth were all within the confines of the alveolar bone, devoid of any serious complications.

Upon the patient’s initial visit to the orthodontics department for data collection, the black triangle of the gingiva was not visible, although dental calculus was observed in the mandibular anterior teeth (Fig. 1). However, after the initial periodontal treatment, the presence of the black triangle became evident even before the commencement of orthodontic treatment (Fig. 3A), likely due to the alleviation of inflammation and the removal of dental calculus. It is noteworthy that in this case, the PAOO surgery involved the utilization of a gingival papilla preservation incision, contributing to wound healing through primary intention after surgery. Importantly, the black triangle did not expand further during orthodontic treatment, thus mitigating concerns about additional gingival recession.

Overall, the correct application of PAOO surgery requires a detailed treatment plan and a comprehensive assessment of tooth movement under the premise of fully understanding its principles to perfect the timing of the surgery and avoid possible risks during the treatment process.
