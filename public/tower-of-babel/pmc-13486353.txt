Effect of motor competence-based intervention on physical activity and physical fitness in children aged 3–6 years at risk for developmental coordination disorder
Authors: Yu Song, Xi Fei, Shuo Yan, AiMin Liang, Ying Wang, YanJie Chen, HongJuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoor motor competence (MC) is a core feature of young children at risk for developmental coordination disorder (DCD), often limiting participation in physical activity (PA) and negatively affecting physical fitness (PF). Although MC improvement is recognized as effective for promoting PA and PF in typically developing children, its effects in young children at risk for DCD (rDCD) remain unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate the outcomes of MC intervention on MC, PA, and PF in young children with rDCD.MethodsTwenty-six children with rDCD (Mage = 4.64 years) were quasi-randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 12, 10 boys) or a control group (n = 14, 10 boys). The experimental group received an MC intervention that incorporated stability demands within task-specific contexts and progressively integrated locomotor and object control skills. The control group received sensory integration training. Both groups participated in 40-min, one-to-one sessions three times per week for 12 weeks. The primary outcome was overall MC, as assessed using the total score of the Movement Assessment Battery for Children-Second Edition (MABC-2). All outcomes were assessed at baseline and after intervention. Data were analyzed using two-factor repeated measures analysis of variance.ResultsAfter the 12-week intervention, overall MC was significantly higher in the experimental group than in the control group (mean difference = 12.98, P < 0.05). Significant interactions were observed for aiming and catching, balance, and locomotor skills (P < 0.05), whereas no interaction was found for manual dexterity (P > 0.05). Only weekday moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) was higher in the experimental group than in the control group after the intervention (mean difference = 19.11, P < 0.05). Total PF scores were also higher in the experimental group (mean difference = 4.36, P < 0.05), with notable improvements in the double-leg timed hop and balance beam test. No significant associations were observed between changes in total MABC-2 score and changes in total PF score or MVPA (P > 0.05).ConclusionsA tailored motor competence intervention was associated with short-term improvements in overall motor competence, weekday MVPA, and overall PF in young children with rDCD.Keywords: Developmental coordination disorder, Motor competence intervention, Physical activity, Physical fitness, Early childhood

Introduction

Developmental coordination disorder (DCD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder and is characterized by impaired motor coordination that cannot be explained by intellectual disability or other neurological disorders, such as cerebral palsy or muscular dystrophy.1 Globally, the prevalence of DCD among children aged 5-11 years ranges from 5% to 8%, and the ratio of affected males to females is 2:1 to 7:11. Poor motor competence (MC) is a core feature of DCD.2 When compared with typically developing (TD) children, those with DCD show lower fine and gross motor skills and do not acquire the age-appropriate MC that is required for daily life.3 Notably, without timely interventions, the gap in MC between children with DCD and TD does not narrow with age.4 Motor difficulties persist into adolescence and adulthood, seriously affecting the physical and mental health of children with DCD throughout their lives.5

MC is commonly defined as the integration of movement patterns involving two or more body parts.6,7 It often includes stability, locomotor, and object control skills.6,7 Early childhood is a critical period for the development of MC and lays the foundation for later participation in physical activity (PA).8,9 MC is believed to be associated with physical fitness (PF), and higher levels of MC correspond to better PF.9 PA has well-documented health benefits, including improved cardiometabolic health, better executive function, and reduced risk of obesity.10 Thus, regular participation in PA and exercise may have potential benefits for children with DCD in supporting their health and promoting developmental opportunities in various domains.11

However, children with DCD tend to have lower PA than TD children.12,13 This disparity becomes more pronounced in middle and late childhood and may be associated with poorer MC.11 According to the activity deficit hypothesis, the gap in PA between children with DCD and TD is small during early childhood but increases with age as higher MC demands limit participation in PA.14 Early improvement of MC in children with DCD may reduce the risk of premature withdrawal from PA2. Additionally, children with DCD generally exhibit lower levels of PF than TD children, particularly with respect to both aerobic and anaerobic capacity and muscle strength.15,16 Evidence indicates that in early childhood, differences in PF cannot be explained by PA alone and may be more directly associated with underlying motor impairments.15 Therefore, identifying effective early interventions to produce improvements in MC in children with DCD has become an important research priority.

Although MC is fundamental to motor learning, for children with DCD, it does not improve naturally with maturation alone and requires targeted intervention.4,17 Evidence from meta-analyses consistently supports task-oriented MC-based interventions as some of the most effective approaches for improving motor performance for children with DCD18, 19, 20. However, there has been inconsistent evidence regarding whether improvements in MC translate into broader outcomes. Evidence regarding habitual PA is inconsistent, with some studies reporting increases in only moderate-to-vigorous PA following MC interventions,5 while others show no significant changes or even decreases.3,19,21 Evidence regarding PF suggests that MC interventions may have positive effects on cardiorespiratory fitness22 and muscular strength,23 but the impacts on other areas are uncertain, including speed, agility, power, balance, and flexibility.24

These inconsistent findings draw attention to the need to optimize MC interventions to improve motor performance while considering the potential effects on PA and PF. The international guidelines published by the European Academy of Childhood Disability (EACD) emphasize the need to adapt task-oriented MC interventions to ensure task and context specificity25. Given the high prevalence of postural control deficits in children with DCD, stability may be a relevant component to consider in MC interventions.25 Current motor-learning perspectives emphasize the close coupling between stability and specific task demands and indicate that stability should be trained in an integrated and context-dependent manner rather than as an isolated component of MC.25 These perspectives support the design of interventions that integrate stability within task-specific practice conditions and progressively combine locomotor and object control skills.25

Notably, most studies have focused on school-aged children and limited evidence is available for younger age groups. Therefore, the present study focused on younger children at risk for DCD. We designed an MC intervention that incorporates stability demands within task-specific contexts and progressively integrates locomotor and object control skills. The study aimed to evaluate the effects of the intervention on MC and to explore its potential effects on PA and PF in these children. According to the literature, the recommended terminology for children under five years old is “at risk for DCD”.26 The term at risk for DCD (rDCD) is used in this study.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quasi-randomized, single-blind controlled design with two groups (experimental and control) and two assessment points (pre- and post-intervention). The experimental group received a 12-week MC intervention, while the control group received sensory integration (SI) training. MC, PA, and PF were assessed before and after the intervention. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children's Hospital, Capital Medical University (IEC-C-006-A04-V.06).

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Health Care Center of Beijing Children's Hospital of Capital Medical University between July and August 2024. Recruitment was performed by pediatric healthcare professionals who were not involved in the group allocation or outcome assessment.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for DCD according to the DSM-5TR.1 Children scoring below the 16th percentile on the Movement Assessment Battery for Children-Second Edition (MABC-2) were considered at risk for DCD (Criterion A).27 Parents completed an interview with a therapist to confirm that motor skill deficits affected daily activities (Criterion B) and had been present since early childhood (Criterion C). Children completed the Gesell Developmental Schedules, and their developmental quotient (DQ) exceeded 75, which excluded the possibility that the motor skill deficits were due to intellectual disability.28 Parents reported the child's medical history to exclude the possibility that the motor skills deficits could be explained by visual impairment or other neurological conditions (Criterion D). (2) age of 3-6 years, and (3) the ability to regularly participate in the intervention with parental or guardian support. The exclusion criteria included motor-system disorders, other neurological or psychiatric conditions, and previous participation in similar interventions.

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1. A repeated-measures analysis of variance (effect size = 0.3020, α = 0.05, power = 0.80) indicated that the ideal sample size was 24 participants. Considering potential attrition, 36 participants were initially recruited. The participants were stratified based on age and sex into four groups: 3-4-year-old boys, 3-4-year-old girls, 5-6-year-old boys, and 5-6-year-old girls. In each of these groups, participants were allocated to either the experimental or control group using a quasi-random alternating allocation procedure, which was conducted by a researcher who was not involved in outcome assessment. In each stratum, the first enrolled participant was assigned to the experimental group, the second was assigned to the control group, the third was assigned to the experimental group, and so on until all participants in that stratum had been assigned. The allocation sequence was predetermined, and the enrolling researcher was not aware of the upcoming assignment at the time of enrollment. Participants and their parents were not informed of group allocation throughout the study. During the intervention, six participants in the experimental group (4 girls and 2 boys) and four participants in the control group (2 girls and 2 boys) withdrew, which was primarily due to injury, illness, or family-related reasons, leaving 26 participants for the final analysis. Ultimately, there were 12 participants in the experimental group (2 girls and 10 boys) and 14 participants in the control group (4 girls and 10 boys), as shown in Fig. 1.

Interventions

Young children with rDCD typically show poorer MC, which makes it difficult to provide them with adequate attention and individualized support in group-based programs.29 Accordingly, interventions delivered in community or group settings have shown limited effectiveness.29 Therefore, a one-on-one intervention approach was applied. Children in both groups received individualized interventions at the hospital over 12 weeks with three 40-min sessions per week. Each session consisted of three components: a warm-up (10 min), a main training phase (25 min), and a cool-down (5 min). The warm-up included joint mobility exercises, brisk walking, jogging, and dynamic stretching to prepare children for the main training tasks. The cool-down consisted of stretching exercises and relaxation activities to facilitate recovery.

All interventions were delivered on a one-to-one basis by an intervention team composed of three graduate students in sports science and one pediatric rehabilitation therapist. Before the study, all intervention personnel received standardized training to ensure consistency in the intervention delivery. The intervention fidelity was monitored through adherence to the predefined training protocols, which were shown in Table 1, Table 2. Session adherence was documented using a therapist-completed intervention record form, which recorded attendance, session completion, and the delivered intervention content. Any deviations from the protocol were also documented.

Exercise intensity was monitored using an observational checklist (“Performance of Preschool Children with Different Physical Activity Levels”).30 The checklist assessed fatigue responses based on observable indicators, including facial color, sweating, respiratory rate, movement performance, attention, and emotional responses.30 Therapists adjusted the task intensity, difficulty, and rest intervals based on these observations to maintain a light to moderate fatigue level. Training tasks and difficulty levels were individually adjusted according to each child's MC, tolerance, and engagement. Participation was recorded throughout the intervention period, and no additional home-based exercises were assigned to either group.

The experimental group received an MC intervention that incorporated stability demands within task-specific contexts and progressively integrated locomotor and object control skills. The intervention was based on the three fundamental components of MC: stability, locomotor, and object control skills.6,7 Stability demands were considered a component of the intervention due to the common postural control deficits among children with DCD.25 Consistent with contemporary motor-learning perspectives, postural control was considered a task-specific process that was continuously adjusted according to task requirements and environmental constraints rather than a generalized ability.31 Stability demands were embedded in motor tasks rather than trained as isolated components, and locomotor and object control skills were progressively integrated. The selected tasks were designed based on ten fundamental motor skills commonly developed during early childhood,32 comprising stability skills (crawling, supporting, squatting, and tiptoe standing), locomotor skills (walking, running, and jumping), and object control skills (dribbling, catching, and throwing).

The intervention program included three phases: a basic phase (weeks 1-4), an enhancement phase (weeks 5-9), and an integration phase (weeks 10-12). The basic phase focused on foundational motor skill development by incorporating stability demands in simple task contexts to improve postural control and engagement in motor activities. The enhancement phase emphasized progressive integration of locomotor and object control skills, with ongoing stability demands and corrective feedback to improve movement accuracy and overall performance. The integration phase emphasized the comprehensive application of motor skills by combining stability, locomotor, and object control skills in more complex task contexts, which allowed the children to adapt their movement performance according to task demands and environmental constraints. Although different motor domains were emphasized across phases, previously introduced skills were continuously reviewed and incorporated into subsequent tasks. This progression was designed to gradually increase task complexity while facilitating adaptive motor performance. A detailed description of the intervention program is provided in Table 1.

The control group received SI training, which is commonly used as an intervention in pediatric rehabilitation and primarily targets the vestibular, tactile, and proprioceptive systems.26 A detailed description of the control intervention is provided in Table 2. SI training was selected as the control condition because it remains commonly applied in clinical practice, and timely intervention is considered necessary for children with rDCD.26

Outcome measurements

Motor competence

MC was assessed in multiple domains using the MABC-2 and the Test of Gross Motor Development, third edition (TGMD-3). The total MABC-2 score was used as the primary outcome measure of overall MC. It is widely used internationally and has demonstrated good validity and reliability.33,34 In Chinese children, it has also shown excellent content validity (average content validity = 0.985) and good internal consistency.35 For children aged 3-6 years, the MABC-2 consists of eight test items: depositing coins, threading lace, drawing a trail, catching a bean bag, throwing a bean bag, walking along lines, balancing on one leg, and jumping on mats.36 These items are grouped into three motor domains: manual dexterity, aiming and catching, and balance. Raw scores for each test item were converted into standard scores ranging from 1 to 19 according to the standard score conversion table provided in the MABC-2 manual. Standard scores in each domain were totaled to generate domain scores, and the sum of the three domain scores was calculated as the total MABC-2 score. Higher scores indicate better motor performance.

The TGMD-3 was used to assess locomotor skills through its locomotor subscale. The TGMD-3 has demonstrated high reliability and validity in different cultural contexts37,38 and has been successfully used with Chinese children.39 The subscale consists of six items: running, galloping, hopping, skipping, standing long jump, and sliding. Each item is evaluated on three to four performance criteria‬ and is performed twice. One point is awarded when a criterion is performed, and no point is awarded when the criterion is not performed. Scores from the two trials were totaled to obtain the total locomotor score, which ranged from 0 to 46. Higher scores indicate better locomotor proficiency. The test was video recorded, and the researchers rated the video.

Physical activity

PA was investigated using the Questionnaire of Parents of Physical Activities of Young Children.40 The questionnaire assesses total physical activity (TPA), moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), and sedentary behavior (SB) of 3-6 years old children outside school on weekdays and weekends. TPA has 8 levels: above 0, 30, 60, 90, 120,150, 180, and more than 180 min. MVPA has 6 levels: above 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, and more than 60 min. SB has 9 levels: above 0, 30, 60, 120,180, 240, 300, 360, and 420 min. The questionnaire was evaluated through expert consultation and demonstrated good content validity, with a test–retest reliability coefficient of 0.8640. According to the PA guideline for Chinese preschoolers aged 3-6 years, differences in PA are largely related to family activities after school and on weekends.32 Therefore, this study primarily focused on PA outside of school.

Physical fitness

PF in this study defined as a broad construct reflecting overall PF in young children. It was assessed using the Chinese National Physical Fitness Measurement (CNPFM-Pre) battery, which has been widely used with young Chinese children and has shown acceptable reliability and sensitivity.41 It consists of six items: 10 m shuttle run, standing long jump, tennis ball throw, double-leg timed hop, balance beam test, and sit-and-reach. Raw scores from each test were converted into standardized scores ranging from 1 to 5 according to age-and sex-specific normative criteria, which are provided in the manual. The standardized scores of the six tests were summed to obtain the total PF score, with a full score of 3039. Higher scores indicated better performance for both individual items and the total score. The total PF score was used as a composite indicator of overall physical fitness in young children in this study.41

Outcome hierarchy

Given the relatively small sample size and multiple outcome measures, we pre-specified outcomes and organized them according to the study's objectives and the literature. The total MABC-2 score was designated as the primary outcome to represent overall motor competence and was the main endpoint for evaluating intervention effects. Total PF score and MVPA (further stratified into weekday and weekend MVPA) were considered as secondary outcomes to examine potential transfer effects of MC on PF and PA. Other MC domains, PF components, and additional PA indicators were defined as exploratory outcomes to provide a more comprehensive description of the intervention effects. Analyses of these outcomes were considered supplementary and were interpreted with caution.

Data analysis

Data were entered using EPIDATA 3.1 and analyzed with SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare baseline demographic characteristics between groups. Intervention effects on the primary, secondary, and exploratory outcomes were evaluated using a 2 (time: pre-intervention, post-intervention) × 2 (group: experimental, control) repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Before the ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. All assumptions were met for the variables included in the analyses. F-values, P-values, and partial η2 were reported for the main effects of group, time, and their interaction. Between-group mean differences and 95% confidence intervals were also reported. When a significant group × time interaction was observed, simple effect analyses were conducted to examine between-group differences at each time point and within-group changes over time. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons in within-group pre-post analyses. For secondary outcomes, the Holm–Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple testing. Effect sizes were expressed as partial η2 and interpreted based on Cohen's (1988) guidelines, with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively.42 Values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with adjustment for baseline values. Results were compared with those from repeated-measures ANOVA to assess the robustness of the findings. Pearson correlation analyses were also conducted to examine baseline and change-score associations among primary and secondary outcome variables.

Results

The study included a total of 26 participants with 12 participants in the experimental group and 14 participants in the control group. The average age of participants was 4.64 ± 0.79 years (experimental group: 4.55 ± 0.85 years; control group: 4.71 ± 0.77 years). No significant differences were observed between groups in terms of age, height, weight, MC, PA, or PF at baseline (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Changes in motor competence in rDCD children

For the primary outcome, total MABC-2 score, the main effects of time (F(1, 24) = 22.27, P < 0.05, partial η2 = 0.48) and the group × time interaction (F(1,24) = 7.26, P < 0.05, partial η2 = 0.23) were significant, whereas the main effect of group was not significant (F(1, 24) = 1.76, P > 0.05, partial η2 = 0.07). The experimental group improved significantly after 12 weeks (P < 0.05), whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (P > 0.05). The experimental group had higher post-intervention scores than the control group (mean difference = 12.98, 95% CI: 0.90–25.06, P < 0.05). For MC domains, significant group × time interactions were observed for aiming and catching, balance, and locomotor skills (P < 0.05), but not for manual dexterity (P > 0.05). The experimental group showed greater improvements in these domains, with a significant between-group difference in post-intervention balance scores (P < 0.05). (Table 4).

For weekday MVPA, the main effect of time (F(1,24) = 5.79, P < 0.05, partial η2 = 0.19) and the interaction (F(1,24) = 7.28, P < 0.05, partial η2 = 0.23) were significant, whereas the main effect of group was not significant (F(1,24) = 3.15, P > 0.05, partial η2 = 0.12). Higher post-intervention MVPA in the experimental group compared with the control group (mean difference = 19.11, 95% CI: 7.41–30.80, P < 0.05) was observed. For weekend MVPA, only the main effect of time was significant (F(1,24) = 10.46, P<0.05, partial η2 = 0.30) with no significant main effect of group or interaction (P > 0.05). No significant interactions were observed for SB or TPA (P > 0.05). (Table 5).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values yielded results consistent with those of the repeated-measures ANOVA for the primary and secondary outcomes, supporting the robustness of the main findings. For exploratory outcomes, inconsistent results were observed only for locomotor skills and aiming and catching, with ANCOVA showing significantly higher adjusted post-intervention scores in the experimental group than in the control group.

Correlation analyses

Pearson correlation analyses showed that at baseline, the total MABC-2 score was positively correlated with the total PF score (r = 0.68, P < 0.05), while no significant correlations were found with MVPA (P > 0.05). No significant associations were observed between changes in total MABC-2 score and changes in total PF score or MVPA in the whole sample, the experimental group, or the control group (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study examined a motor competence-based intervention for young children at risk for DCD. The intervention incorporated stability demands within task-specific contexts and progressively integrated locomotor and object control skills. The results indicate that the MC-based intervention led to some improvement in overall motor competence in young children with rDCD. This intervention was also associated with increases in weekday MVPA and overall PF. These findings suggest that task-specific interventions may be beneficial for MC in young children with rDCD and may also have potential effects on PA and PF.

Consistent with previous studies,18,19 the present study further supports the potential effectiveness of MC-based interventions in improving overall MC in young children with rDCD. MC-based interventions are task-oriented approaches that emphasize repeated practice in meaningful motor contexts.26 The intervention incorporated stability demands within task-specific contexts and progressively integrated locomotor and object control skills, which allowed the children to repeatedly practice relevant motor skills in contexts that closely resemble actual motor demands. Research has shown that the transfer of learning for children with DCD is influenced by training content and the characteristics of transfer tasks.43 When training tasks and transfer tasks show greater similarity in task demands, contexts, and the motor processes involved, near transfer is more likely to occur.43 Therefore, the task-specific and contextualized practice approach used in the present study may have contributed to improving motor learning and coordination while facilitating the application of acquired skills to tasks and contexts with similar motor demands.19,44

Differential intervention effects were observed in different MC domains. Greater improvements were observed in aiming and catching, balance, and locomotor skills, with the most pronounced improvement observed in balance performance. This finding aligns with meta-analytic evidence suggesting that balance is the most consistently responsive domain following MC interventions for children with DCD, with approximately 83% of included studies demonstrating significant effects.18 This may be due to the inherent requirement of some level of balance control for even basic activities, such as walking and running.25 In addition, stability skills were embedded throughout the program and incorporated into different task contexts, which allowed the children to repeatedly practice and reinforce balance control in similar movement tasks. The similarity between training and assessment tasks may have facilitated near transfer, which may have contributed to improving balance performance.43 Similarly, improvements in locomotor skills and aiming and catching may be related to the similarity between training content and assessment tasks, which may have also contributed to near transfer.43

No significant intervention effects were observed for manual dexterity. This may be attributed to the fact that the intervention primarily focused on gross motor skills and involved limited practice of fine motor skills. In addition, the low similarity between manual dexterity and the trained tasks also suggests that improvements in this domain may rely more on far transfer, which has been shown to be more difficult to achieve than near transfer for children with DCD.43

Regarding PA, only weekday MVPA showed greater improvement in the experimental group than the control group. This may be explained by the emphasis on running, jumping, and throwing activities, which inherently involve movement with moderate-to-vigorous intensity.32 Studies examining the effects of MC interventions on PA outcomes of children with DCD have reported inconsistent findings. Some studies have demonstrated that MC interventions may lead to improvements in MVPA,5 while other studies have reported limited effects. For example, Yu et al. reported no significant changes in PA outcomes following an intervention,21 and similar findings have been reported in a meta-analysis.19 These inconsistent findings suggest that improvements in MC do not necessarily translate to increased PA.

In addition, no significant associations were observed between MC and MVPA at baseline, and changes in MC were not significantly associated with changes in MVPA following the intervention. These findings may be consistent with the relatively weak association between MC and PA during early childhood45 and may also have been influenced by the small sample size. Although there was improvement in MVPA, the absence of significant associations between changes in MC and changes in MVPA suggests that this improvement should not be interpreted as evidence that improvements in MC directly led to increased PA.

It should be noted that the present study used parent-reported measures of PA, and evidence has indicated weak measurement properties of proxy-report questionnaires for children under five years of age.46 Therefore, potential measurement bias should be considered. Parents may have difficulty in accurately estimating children's activity duration and intensity due to recall bias, social desirability bias, and the intermittent and unstructured nature of young children's movement behaviors, which may influence the accuracy of PA assessments.46 Accordingly, the improvement in weekday MVPA should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should consider objective measures such as accelerometers to validate these findings.

It has been suggested that PF may be a byproduct of MC, and improvements in MC during early childhood may contribute to improved PF.39 Validation of the Developmental Mechanisms Influencing Physical Activity Trajectories of Children by Barnett et al. provides support for a proposed developmental pathway in which improvements in MC may be associated with subsequent gains in PF.47 The present study also suggests that improvements in MC may be associated with overall improvements in PF for children with DCD. Similar findings have been reported in TD children.39 Notably, changes in MC were not significantly associated with changes in PF following the intervention, which may have been influenced by the small sample size.

However, the effects of MC interventions may vary between different components of PF. The MC intervention had positive effects on performance in the double-leg timed hop and balance beam test, but improvements in other PF indicators were relatively limited. One possible explanation is that these two tests have greater similarity in motor performance demands to those of the movement tasks incorporated in the MC intervention, which may have facilitated the transfer of acquired motor skills to related PF assessment tasks.43 Farhat et al. reported that a task-oriented intervention involving 8-year-old children with DCD improved performance on the standing long jump and shuttle run.48 However, that study involved an 8-week group-based intervention that was conducted in a school setting,48 and differences in participants’ age and PF assessment protocols between studies may have limited direct comparisons of the findings. Future research is needed to clarify the effects of MC interventions on different PF components among children with DCD.

Notably, changes observed in the control group also warrant consideration. The control group showed slight improvements only in balance and locomotor skills. However, these improvements were not accompanied by changes in PA or PF. SI training primarily emphasizes sensory processing and integration and has less emphasis on direct practice of motor skills.49 These characteristics may partly explain the limited effects on broader outcomes, including PA and PF. However, it should be noted that the SI intervention used in the present study was a commonly applied clinical training approach rather than a standardized Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI) protocol. Therefore, this distinction should be considered when interpreting the findings.

In addition, the attrition rate was relatively high, with approximately one-third of participants not completing the intervention. This may have been partly related to the intervention dosage. MC learning relies on skill consolidation and repetition,50 so sufficient practice exposure may be important for achieving improvements in MC.18 The intervention dosage used in the present study may have provided adequate opportunities for repeated practice and skill acquisition for children with rDCD, but future studies should further explore the optimal balance between intervention dosage, feasibility, and adherence.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Due to the young age of participants, PA was assessed using parental questionnaires rather than accelerometers, which may have introduced recall bias.46 The one-on-one intervention approach provided individualized support but also limited the sample size,29 which may have affected the statistical power and constrained generalizability of the results. Additionally, common neurodevelopmental comorbidities were not systematically screened,18 and the potential influence of unrecognized comorbidities on baseline motor performance and intervention responsiveness cannot be ruled out. Since DCD is more prevalent in boys,1 the small number of girls included in this study precluded further exploration of sex differences. Potential bias may have also arisen from the hospital-based recruitment, the inclusion of only children whose parents provided consent to participate, the relatively high attrition rate, the lack of an intention-to-treat analysis, and the absence of comparisons between completers and dropouts, which may have also affected the robustness of the findings. In addition, the control group received SI training rather than usual care, which may have influenced the magnitude of between-group effect estimates. There may have been some overlap between the intervention content and PF outcomes, which may have affected the interpretation of improvements in PF. Finally, the trial was not prospectively registered, which may have limited transparency in reporting.51 The absence of long-term follow-up assessment also limits inferences regarding the sustainability of the observed effects. Future research should incorporate longer-term follow-up periods, objective measures of PA, and comprehensive assessments of neurodevelopmental characteristics to clarify the intervention effects.

Conclusion

The study examined a 12-week individualized motor competence-based intervention that incorporated stability demands within task-specific contexts and progressively integrated locomotor and object control skills. The intervention was associated with short-term improvements in overall motor competence, weekday MVPA, and overall PF. The findings suggest that interventions for young children with rDCD should target motor competence, a core feature of the disorder, which may be beneficial for PA and PF outcomes. Nevertheless, larger and more rigorous trials should be performed using device-based PA measurements and longer follow-up periods.
