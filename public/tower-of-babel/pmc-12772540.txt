Lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1 (LEF1) immunostaining as a surrogate for β-catenin (CTNNB1) mutations
Authors: Ekkehard Hewer, Pascal David Fischer, Erik Vassella, Laura Knabben, Sara Imboden, Michael D Mueller, Tilman T Rau, Matthias S Dettmer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsMutations affecting exon 3 of the β-catenin (CTNNB1) gene result in constitutive activation of WNT signalling and are a diagnostic hallmark of several tumour entities including desmoid-type fibromatosis. They also define clinically relevant tumour subtypes within certain entities, such as endometrioid carcinoma. In diagnostics, β-catenin immunohistochemistry is widely used as a surrogate for CTNNB1 mutations. Yet, it is often difficult to assess in practice, given that the characteristic nuclear translocation may be focal or hard to distinguish from the spillover of the normal membranous staining.MethodsWe therefore examined lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1 (LEF1) immunostaining, a nuclear marker of WNT activation that serves as a potential surrogate for CTNNB1 mutations.ResultsIn a cohort of endometrial carcinomas with known mutation status (n=130) LEF1 was 85% accurate in predicting CTNNB1 mutation status (64% sensitivity, 90% specificity) while β-catenin was 76% accurate (72% sensitivity; 77% specificity). Across a variety of entities characterised by CTNNB1 mutations as putative drivers, we found diffuse and strong expression of LEF1 in 77% of cases. LEF1 immunostaining proved easier to interpret than β-catenin immunostaining in 54% of cases, more difficult in 1% of cases and comparable in the remaining cases.ConclusionWe conclude that LEF1 immunostaining is a useful surrogate marker for CTNNB1 mutations. It favourably complements β-catenin immunohistochemistry and outperforms the latter as a single marker.Keywords: IMMUNOHISTOCHEMISTRY; ENDOMETRIUM; CARCINOMA; Genes, Neoplasm

Introduction

Mutations in exon 3 of the CTNNB1 gene, which encodes β-catenin, lead to constitutive activation of WNT signalling, contributing to tumorigenesis. They are recognised as the molecular hallmark of various tumour types (including desmoid-type fibromatosis, pilomatricomas and adamantinomatous craniopharyngiomas). Under physiological conditions, β-catenin attaches to adhaerens junctions, resulting in membranous staining, while activating CTNNB1 mutations result in nuclear translocation of β-catenin protein. This nuclear translocation can be visualised by immunohistochemistry, as CTNNB1 mutated tumours show combined membranous and nuclear β-catenin staining.

In routine diagnostic practice, β-catenin immunostaining is widely used as a surrogate marker for CTNNB1 mutations in a variety of lesions because it is less expensive and can be performed more quickly than gene sequencing. For example, the diagnostic utility of β-catenin immunostaining has been demonstrated in solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas, where it can be particularly helpful in the diagnosis of specimens with limited material obtained by endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration.1 Similarly, in desmoid-type fibromatosis, it has been shown that the identification of nuclear β-catenin staining as a surrogate of CTNNB1 mutations can be used diagnostically in difficult lesions.2 3

Sporadic endometrial carcinomas harbour CTNNB1 mutations in about 20%.4 Recently, these mutations were reported to identify patients at increased risk of recurrence, and in follow-up work, the same group reported that β-catenin immunostaining can be used as a screening tool to identify tumours that should undergo CTNNB1 sequencing.4 5 This was confirmed in a large meta-analysis which found that β-catenin immunostaining can indeed be used as a screening tool for CTNNB1 mutations.6

Currently, more than 60 000 new cases of endometrial cancer are diagnosed in the USA each year, according to the SEER database, representing 7% of all new cancer cases diagnosed in women.7 Incidence and mortality rates are increasing, making it urgent to identify patients at risk of tumour recurrence or progression.

Because of the often focal nature of nuclear translocation and nuclear spillover of membranous staining, β-catenin immunohistochemistry is notoriously difficult to evaluate and requires considerable expertise.8 Therefore, a new marker, that is easier to interpret, would be very helpful. This prompted us to investigate the utility of LEF1 (lymphoid enhancer binding factor 1), a downstream mediator of WNT signalling. The LEF1 gene belongs to the TCF/LEF (T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor) gene family. LEF1 physically interacts with β-catenin when the latter is translocated to the nucleus and mediates WNT signalling. LEF1 positivity in terms of immunohistochemistry is characterised by nuclear staining, which is absent in most non-neoplastic epithelial and stromal cells. Furthermore, essentially all tissues contain at least occasional LEF1-positive T cells, so that the interpretation of the immunostaining is usually straightforward. LEF1 is currently used as a diagnostic marker for chronic lymphocytic leukaemia9 and is therefore widely available across diagnostic immunohistochemistry laboratories. Here, we assess its potential as an immunohistochemical surrogate for CTNNB1 mutations.

Material and methods

Case selection and tissue microarray construction

A tissue microarray was constructed as previously described.10 Clinical and histopathological data of the cohort were previously described.11 Specifically, POLE status was defined based on mutation analysis, while DNA mismatch repair (MMR) status, as a surrogate of microsatellite instability and p53 status were defined based on immunohistochemical testing. Clinicopathological features of the cases included in the present study are summarised in table 1. Three representative punches (1 mm cores) were taken per carcinoma to address possible intratumoural heterogeneity, and two additional punches were taken for DNA extraction.

We additionally identified tumour samples across a variety of tumour types in which sequencing within our department’s diagnostic routine for diagnostic or predictive purposes had shown a pathogenic CTNNB1.

Immunohistochemistry

In 130 endometrial carcinomas with known CTNNB1 mutation status, β-catenin and LEF1 were analysed. In addition, these analyses were also performed on 26 different tumours for which the mutation status of CTNNB1 was also known. Immunostainings were performed on a Leica Microsystems Bond Max Stainer. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded slides were stained with β-catenin (CellMarque, clone 14, order nr 224M-15, dilution 1:400) and LEF1 (Abcam, clone EPR2029Y, order nr ab137872, dilution 1:100). Staining was assessed by two experienced pathologists (EH and MSD) who were blinded for clinical data and molecular findings. Any nuclear positivity for β-catenin was considered positive. With regard to LEF1 immunostaining, preliminary analysis of the complete histological slides had shown that the vast majority of tumours were either completely negative or that essentially all tumour cells showed strong nuclear staining. Occasional cases (CTNNB1 wild-type) showed focal nuclear positivity (usually of heterogeneous intensity) at the invasion front. A formal cut-off at 50% tumour cell staining was chosen for LEF1 to be considered positive in order not to overcall cases with such focal staining. In practice, any cut-off between 20% and 80% would have yielded almost identical results. For incongruent cases, consensus was reached in a second session. We also assessed the difficulty in deciding whether a stain was positive or negative. For each sample we considered one immunostaining to be more difficult to interpret than the other if the estimated time required to arrive at a confident conclusion was more than twice as long as for the latter. The same interpretative criteria were used for LEF1 and β-catenin immunostaining on other tumour types.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from frozen tissues using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as described elsewhere.12 Tumour tissue was identified by a molecular pathologist (MSD), and the area of interest was labelled on a slide stained with H&E. DNA was extracted only from samples with more than 50% tumour cell content. The area of interest was identified and punched out of the formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tissue block. In all cases where next-generation sequencing (NGS) was not performed, Sanger sequencing was performed.

Sanger Sequencing

For Sanger sequencing, a fragment encompassing exon 3 of the CTNNB1 gene was amplified by PCR using the primer pair 5’-GCC ATG GAA CCA GAC AG-3’ and 5’-TTC CCA CTC ATA CAG GAC TT-3’ and analysed by Sanger Sequencing using a Genetic Analyzer (GA3500, Thermo Fisher).

Next-generation sequencing

64 endometrial cancers were sequenced using Illumina’s TruSight oncology 500 panel (TSO500), which includes 523 genes and allowed us to examine not only CTNNB1 mutations but also several other genes of the WNT pathway (APC, AXIN1, AXIN2, CSNK1A1, EP300 and PPP2R1A).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (V21.0). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the sample distribution. Statistics were calculated using χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test. Cox regression was used for the multivariate analysis and the Kaplan-Meier method for the survival analysis. A p value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

β-catenin and LEF1 in endometrial carcinomas

CTNNB1 exon 3 mutations were found in 25 out of 130 cases (19%). Among all 130 cases, 42 were positive for β-catenin, 26 of which were also positive for LEF1, while no LEF1-positive case was negative for β-catenin. Mutational status correlated excellently with immunostaining for β-catenin (p<0.001) as well as for LEF1 (p<0.001). β-catenin predicted CTNNB1 correctly in 76% of cases (72% sensitivity; 77% specificity), while LEF1 was correct in 85% (64% sensitivity, 90% specificity). The positive predictive value (PPV) of LEF1 was 61% and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 91%, while for β-catenin PPV was 43% and NPV was 92%.

WNT pathway mutations in genes other than CTNNB1 were identified in 15 cases, only two of which (1 APC mutation, 1 CSNK1A1 mutation) were associated with the positivity of both LEF1 and β-catenin, while in the other 13 cases both markers were negative. Among 16 cases positive for β-catenin, but negative for LEF1, 2 (13%) were CTNNB1 mutated, while no other WNT pathway mutations were identified.

Given that all 26 LEF1-positive cases were also positive for β-catenin, with 16 additional cases being only positive for the latter, combinations of the two markers did not result in higher sensitivity, specificity or accuracy than that of either marker alone.

β-catenin and LEF1 in diagnostic setting of fibromatoses and other tumour types with recurrent CTNNB1 mutations

Fibromatoses of the desmoid type carried CTNNB1 mutations in 63% of cases (5/8). β-catenin and LEF1 were positive in all mutant and two wild-type cases. Superficial fibromatoses were all CTNNB1 wild-type and immunohistochemically, β-catenin and LEF1 again yielded identical results (positive in one, negative in two cases). In addition, we applied LEF1 and β-catenin immunohistochemistry in a variety of other tumour types that we encountered in our routine practice (including solid pseudopapillary tumours of the pancreas, a pancreatoblastoma, pilomatricomas, medulloblastomas and adamantinomatous craniopharyngiomas) known to harbour a CTNNB1 mutation, respectively (data not shown). Again, we found LEF1 to be consistently easier to evaluate, usually the result being evident at low magnification (figure 5D–F).

β-catenin and LEF1 in various neoplasms

CTNNB1 mutations were incidentally detected in the context of NGS performed within diagnostic routine across a variety of tumour types (adenocarcinoma of the lung n=5, colon carcinoma n=5, malignant melanoma n=3, adrenocortical carcinoma, prostate carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, medulloblastoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma of the lung) in a total of 18 cases (online supplemental table 2). Immunohistochemistry for β-catenin was positive in 89% of cases, whereas LEF1 was positive in only 50%. Among samples with another strong molecular driver such as KRAS, BRAF, EGFR or TP53, β-catenin was positive in 87% and LEF1 was positive in 53% (online supplemental table 2).

Discussion

We investigated the utility of LEF1 immunohistochemistry as a surrogate marker for CTNNB1 mutations in various human neoplasms in different diagnostic and prognostic settings. Overexpression of LEF1 has been described in several tumour types harbouring CTNNB1 mutations, such as cribriform-morular thyroid carcinoma15 16 or deep penetrating melanocytic naevi,17 but has not been systematically studied as a surrogate for CTNNB1 mutations across different tumour types.

We found that LEF1 is useful as a surrogate of CTNNB1 mutations across various entities. In a large cohort of endometrial carcinomas, we found LEF1 to be more accurate (85%) than β-catenin (76%), essentially related to a higher specificity. Perhaps more importantly, we found that LEF1 was easier to interpret than β-catenin and that most cases were easy to assess at low magnification. This is due to the simple readout of LEF1 immunostaining (diffuse nuclear overexpression vs its absence), while it is often difficult to distinguish between spillover of membranous β-catenin staining and true nuclear translocation, in particular as the latter may be only very focal. Furthermore, LEF1 benefits from the presence of internal positive controls (in particular T cells) in essentially all tissues of interest. We also corroborated these findings across a number of tumour types.

A possible limitation of the immunohistochemical results gained in the cohort of endometrial carcinomas relates to the use of a tissue microarray which might have impaired recognition of intratumoural heterogeneity. However, our preliminary results obtained with full histological slides and the use of three punches per case, as well as the correlation with the molecular results, suggest that the findings would not have been substantially different if staining had been performed on whole tissue sections.

β-catenin and LEF1 as a diagnostic marker

CTNNB1 mutations can be found in the majority of sporadic fibromatoses.18 They are known to carry this mutation in about 90% of cases which is used diagnostically with β-catenin immunohistochemistry as a surrogate marker. Normally, β-catenin staining patterns are membranous and cytoplasmic. In the case of a CTNNB1 mutation or canonical WNT pathway activation, β-catenin is translocated to the nucleus where it activates downstream transcriptional programmes.19 As previously reported, we could identify a nuclear positivity of both β-catenin in most deep fibromatoses. Interestingly, this also holds true for superficial fibromatoses which do not carry a CTNNB1 mutation but nevertheless show nuclear positivity of β-catenin.20 21 In our hands, LEF1 yielded concordant results in all fibromatosis cases.

A recent study assessed the accuracy of three different β-catenin clones for prediction of CTNNB1 mutation status in suspected desmoid-type fibromatosis and found a variation regarding both sensitivity and specificity.22 The antibody clone 14 we used showed the highest sensitivity (96%) and the lowest specificity (62%) in that study. The authors furthermore assessed LEF1 immunostaining using the same clone used in the present study and found it to be 88% sensitive and 76% specific. A recent study applying the LEF1 clone EP310 in desmoid-type fibromatosis found similar sensitivities and specificities for LEF1 and β-catenin alone, while the combination of both markers improved specificity.23

Of note, both nuclear β-catenin staining nor LEF1 overexpression tended to be absent in occasional cases with CTNNB1 in addition to another strong driver. This finding is in line with published data for β-catenin immunostaining5 and may suggest that the WNT pathway is not actually active in the presence of another potent tumour driver.

Prognostic role of β-catenin and LEF1 in endometrial carcinomas

The role of β-catenin immunohistochemistry has previously been studied. It was demonstrated that approximately 40% of endometrial carcinomas have nuclear β-catenin expression in less than 10% of tumour cells.5 Although the authors concluded that β-catenin can reliably predict the mutational status of CTNNB1, a marker that is somewhat easier to assess and does not require much expertise to obtain a reliable result would obviously be very helpful, which we found to be true for LEF1.

LEF1 was upregulated at the tumour front and in areas of presumable epithelial-mesenchymal transition which fits well with the concept of an activated WNT signalling pathway in these circumstances.24 The pattern of immunostaining differed, however, from CTNNB1 mutation-associated LEF1 overexpression in that it was focal, variably intense and would not be present in the majority of tumour cells.

Ruz-Caracuel et al compared the prediction of CTNNB1 exon 3 mutations by immunohistochemistry for β-catenin and LEF1 in low-grade, early-stage endometrial endometrioid carcinoma and found β-catenin had a higher predictive value than LEF1 for these mutations.25 This difference to our findings might be due to the use of different clones for both LEF1 (EP310) and β-catenin as well as a very low cut-off for LEF1 to be considered overexpressed (Allred score of 3/15). Indeed, the microphotographs provided in this article might suggest technical issues with LEF1 immunostaining compared with both our findings and other studies using the same LEF1 clone EP310.26 27 An adverse effect of the presence of a CTNNB1 mutation in these tumours has also been reported.5 25 A subgroup analysis of early endometrioid carcinomas in our cohort revealed a non-significant trend toward an unfavourable outcome in the presence of a CTNNB1 mutation, confirming these results. However, when we included all carcinomas in the analysis, the presence of a CTNNB1 mutation in our data was a favourable sign, which we also saw in the TCGA data set.28

Nevertheless, the difficulty of β-catenin immunohistochemistry persists. While one of the above articles does not comment on this topic, the other study provides an indirect hint as it reported only a poor correlation between β-catenin and CTNNB1 mutation,5 underscoring the need for a better surrogate marker.

The reliable identification of CTNNB1 mutations by immunohistochemistry is also underscored by the recently published PORTEC-4a trial, in which endometrial carcinomas with MMR deficits were classified into different risk groups based on their mutation status.4

Conclusion

In summary, the present study shows that LEF1 immunohistochemistry can be used as an immunohistochemical surrogate for CTNNB1 mutations in endometrial carcinoma as well as other diagnostically relevant tumour types with a sensitivity and specificity that compare favourably to β-catenin. While β-catenin immunostaining may often be difficult to interpret, we found the assessment of LEF1 immunostaining to be generally straightforward. This is due to the simple distinction between either the absence of staining or diffuse and strong nuclear staining in cells of interest, as well as the presence of an internal positive control in the form of T cells in essentially all relevant tissues. Given that LEF1 is available in many diagnostic immunohistochemistry laboratories as a marker of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma, it may favourably complement β-catenin either alone or in conjunction with the latter.
