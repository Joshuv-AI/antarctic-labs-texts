Differential Modulation of Macrophage Polarization and NF-κB Signaling by Distinct Antibiotics in an in vitro Acute Inflammatory Model
Authors: Chunmin Chen, Jingdi Diao, Yuan Chen, Yi Bo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare the regulatory effects of cefotaxime (CTX), levofloxacin (LVFX), and azithromycin (AZM) on macrophage polarization and NF-κB signaling in an in vitro LPS-induced acute inflammation model.MethodsRAW264.7 murine macrophages were pretreated with CTX (25 μg/mL), LVFX (25 μg/mL), or AZM (10 μg/mL) for 1 h and subsequently stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL). Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 assays. TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-10 levels were quantified by ELISA. M1-associated markers (iNOS and CD86) and M2-associated markers (Arg-1 and CD206) were evaluated using qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence. NF-κB pathway activation was examined by Western blot analysis of p65 nuclear translocation, IκBα expression, and IKKα/β and p65 phosphorylation.ResultsAll three antibiotics attenuated LPS-induced proinflammatory responses, as shown by reduced TNF-α and IL-1β levels and increased IL-10 secretion. CTX, LVFX, and particularly AZM suppressed M1-associated marker expression while increasing M2-associated marker expression. Western blot analysis showed that antibiotic pretreatment inhibited NF-κB activation by reducing IKKα/β and p65 phosphorylation, limiting p65 nuclear translocation, and restoring IκBα expression, with AZM exerting the strongest effect among the three antibiotics.ConclusionCTX, LVFX, and AZM exerted differential immunomodulatory effects in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages. AZM showed the most pronounced ability to attenuate inflammatory activation, promote an M2-like macrophage phenotype, and suppress NF-κB signaling. These findings provide preliminary in vitro evidence that antibiotics may differ in their immunomodulatory properties, although further validation in primary macrophages, pharmacologically relevant exposure ranges, and in vivo inflammatory models is required.

Introduction

Acute inflammation is a rapid and coordinated immune response triggered by microbial invasion, tissue damage, or exposure to harmful stimuli. Although inflammation is fundamentally protective, excessive or uncontrolled acute inflammation can lead to tissue injury, metabolic disruption, and systemic inflammatory responses.1,2 Macrophages are central orchestrators of this process due to their ability to detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and to shape inflammatory progression through their polarization into distinct functional states.3,4

Classically activated M1 macrophages are induced by stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and are characterized by robust secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and high expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). These mediators amplify the inflammatory cascade and are crucial for pathogen elimination.5,6 Conversely, alternatively activated M2 macrophages, stimulated by IL-4 or IL-13, express markers such as arginase-1 (Arg-1) and CD206 and contribute to tissue remodeling, debris clearance, and resolution of inflammation. A balanced transition between M1- and M2-like macrophage states is important for limiting excessive inflammation and promoting tissue repair. When this transition is impaired, inflammatory responses may persist and contribute to conditions such as sepsis, acute lung injury, and autoimmune disease.7

Among the signaling pathways that govern macrophage activation, NF-κB is one of the most pivotal. Under resting conditions, NF-κB dimers are retained in the cytoplasm through their interaction with inhibitory proteins such as IκBα. Upon stimulation by LPS via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), IκBα undergoes phosphorylation and proteasomal degradation, permitting nuclear translocation of the NF-κB p65 subunit and transcription of pro-inflammatory genes.8 Hyperactivation of NF-κB has been implicated in cytokine storms, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), ischemia–reperfusion injury, and endotoxin shock. Therefore, pharmacological modulation of the NF-κB pathway is recognized as a rational strategy to attenuate acute inflammatory responses and restore immune homeostasis.9

In acute inflammatory disorders such as sepsis and acute lung injury, tissue damage is increasingly recognized as a consequence of dysregulated host immune responses rather than pathogen burden alone.10,11 Recent studies have emphasized that innate immune dysfunction in sepsis involves coordinated activation of macrophages, neutrophils, endothelial cells, and inflammatory cytokine networks.12–14 Neutrophils are rapidly recruited during early inflammation and participate in pathogen clearance through degranulation, reactive oxygen species production, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation; however, excessive neutrophil activation can amplify inflammatory injury and contribute to organ dysfunction.15,16 Macrophages further shape this process by regulating cytokine release, neutrophil clearance through efferocytosis, and the transition from inflammatory amplification to resolution.1,17 Therefore, pharmacological modulation of macrophage activation and NF-κB signaling may have broader relevance to innate immune regulation in acute inflammatory diseases.

Growing evidence shows that several widely used antibiotics exert regulatory effects on immune pathways independent of their antimicrobial action. For decades, macrolides such as azithromycin (AZM) have been used clinically for chronic inflammatory airway diseases due to their capacity to suppress inflammatory mediators, inhibit oxidative stress, and promote tissue repair. Mechanistic studies have revealed that AZM can reprogram macrophages toward an M2-like anti-inflammatory phenotype by enhancing STAT6 signaling, modulating autophagy, and attenuating NF-κB activation. Fluoroquinolones, including levofloxacin (LVFX), have been observed to inhibit TLR4-mediated inflammatory pathways, reduce cytokine release, and dampen LPS-induced ROS production.18 β-lactam antibiotics such as cefotaxime (CTX), while primarily antimicrobial agents, also modulate cytokine profiles, influence inflammatory cell recruitment, and may indirectly affect macrophage activation through metabolic and receptor-mediated mechanisms.19

Despite these findings, a critical knowledge gap persists. Very few studies have systematically compared how different classes of antibiotics modulate macrophage polarization and inflammatory signaling under the same experimental conditions. Moreover, the clinical use of antibiotics often occurs in the context of acute inflammation—whether sterile or infection-associated—yet little is known about how these antibiotics reshape macrophage phenotypes in such settings. Understanding these immunomodulatory effects is particularly important because antibiotics may either beneficially mitigate excessive inflammation or, conversely, unintentionally exacerbate immune dysregulation.20

Given that macrophage phenotype is a key determinant of inflammatory outcomes, clarifying the differential influence of antibiotics on M1/M2 polarization may provide important insights for optimizing therapeutic strategies in inflammatory disorders. Furthermore, elucidating whether antibiotics interfere with or suppress NF-κB signaling could help explain their anti-inflammatory actions and identify potential off-target benefits that extend beyond bacterial elimination.21,22

In this study, we established an LPS-induced acute inflammatory model using RAW264.7 macrophages to provide a controlled system for evaluating antibiotic-mediated immune modulation. Three clinically relevant antibiotics representing different pharmacological classes were selected: the β-lactam CTX, the fluoroquinolone LVFX, and the macrolide AZM. Through comprehensive analysis encompassing cytokine profiling, gene expression of polarization markers, immunofluorescence imaging, and Western blot evaluation of NF-κB pathway activation, we sought to delineate the distinct immunoregulatory signatures associated with each antibiotic.

By examining the interplay between antibiotic administration, macrophage polarization, and NF-κB signaling, this study provides mechanistic insight into the immunomodulatory properties of antibiotics. The findings also suggest that beyond their antimicrobial indications, antibiotics possess varying capacities to influence innate immune responses, which may be leveraged to guide individualized treatment strategies in managing acute inflammatory diseases.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

RAW264.7 murine macrophages (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC, SC-6005) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10099141C) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, China; Cat. No. P1400). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 and sub-cultured every 2–3 days. Only cells in logarithmic growth phase were used for experiments.

Reagents and Treatment Protocol

Cefotaxime sodium (CTX; Macklin, China; Cat. No. C804041), levofloxacin (LVFX; Solarbio, China; Cat. No. L8030), and azithromycin (AZM; MedChemExpress, USA; Cat. No. HY-17506) were prepared as stock solutions according to the manufacturers’ instructions and diluted in complete culture medium immediately before use. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma-Aldrich, USA; Cat. No. L2630) was reconstituted in endotoxin-free water and used at a final concentration of 100 ng/mL. All antibiotic stock solutions were prepared according to the manufacturers’ instructions using sterile endotoxin-free reagents and were freshly diluted before use.

To determine appropriate working concentrations, RAW264.7 cells were exposed to a range of antibiotic concentrations and cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 assays. Concentrations that did not significantly affect cell viability were considered non-cytotoxic and selected for subsequent experiments. Based on these preliminary results, CTX (25 μg/mL), LVFX (25 μg/mL), and AZM (10 μg/mL) were used in all mechanistic studies. These concentrations were chosen to minimize direct cytotoxic effects while allowing comparison of potential immunomodulatory activity under standardized LPS-induced inflammatory conditions. They were selected for in vitro mechanistic evaluation and do not necessarily represent clinically achievable plasma concentrations.

For inflammatory stimulation experiments, RAW264.7 cells were divided into the following groups: Control, LPS, CTX + LPS, LVFX + LPS, and AZM + LPS. In the antibiotic-treated groups, cells were pretreated with CTX (25 μg/mL), LVFX (25 μg/mL), or AZM (10 μg/mL) for 1 h before stimulation with LPS (100 ng/mL). The 1 h pretreatment period was selected based on previous studies using LPS-stimulated macrophage models,23 in which test compounds or antibiotics were commonly administered before LPS challenge to evaluate their anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory effects. The duration of LPS exposure was dependent on the experimental endpoint: short-term stimulation was used for NF-κB activation analyses, whereas extended incubation periods were applied for mRNA quantification, cytokine assays, and immunofluorescence imaging. Experimental conditions differed only in the presence or absence of antibiotic pretreatment, LPS stimulation, or their combination. Untreated cells maintained under identical culture conditions served as the baseline control group.

Cell Viability Assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (CCK-8; Guangzhou Yujia Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China; Cat. No. C0037). RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells/well. For dose-selection assays, cells were exposed to CTX, LVFX, or AZM at 0, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL for 24 h. For inflammatory condition assays, cells were pretreated with CTX (25 μg/mL), LVFX (25 μg/mL), or AZM (10 μg/mL) for 1 h and then stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated relative to the untreated control group.

Cytokine Quantification by ELISA

Levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-10 in culture supernatants were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (MultiSciences, China; TNF-α Cat. No. EK0527, IL-1β Cat. No. EK0393, IL-10 Cat. No. EK0417). RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with CTX (25 μg/mL), LVFX (25 μg/mL), or AZM (10 μg/mL) for 1 h, followed by stimulation with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. Culture supernatants were then collected and centrifuged to remove cell debris. The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-10 were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

For qRT-PCR analysis, RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with CTX (25 μg/mL), LVFX (25 μg/mL), or AZM (10 μg/mL) for 1 h, followed by stimulation with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 6 h. As a positive control for M2 polarization, RAW264.7 cells were treated with IL-4 plus IL-13 for 24 h, and the expression of Arg-1 and CD206 was evaluated. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan; Cat. No. 9109). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme, China; Cat. No. R323-01). qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme; China; Cat. No. Q711-02) on a QuantStudio 5 PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10s and 60°C for 30s. Melt curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity, and only reactions showing a single peak were included. No-template controls were included in each run to exclude contamination. β-actin served as the internal reference gene, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used were as follows:

iNOS: forward 5′-CAGCTGGGCTGTACAAACCTT-3′,

reverse 5′-CATTGGAAGTGAAGCGTTTCG-3′

CD86: forward 5′-CCTTGTGATGGCTCGGTTTCT-3′,

reverse 5′-GCGCTTCTGTTTGACCTTCTG-3′

Arg-1: forward 5′-CTCCAAGCCAAAGTCCTTAGAG-3′,

reverse 5′-AGGAGCTGTCATTAGGGACATC-3′

CD206: forward 5′-CTCTGTTCAGCTATTGGACGC-3′,

reverse 5′-CGGAATTTCTGGGATTCAGCTTC-3′

β-actin: forward 5′-AGAGGGAAATCGTGCGTGAC-3′,

reverse 5′-CAATAGTGATGACCTGGCCGT-3′

Immunofluorescence Staining

For immunofluorescence staining, cells were pretreated with antibiotics for 1 h and then stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h before fixation. Cells were seeded on glass coverslips and treated as described. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Boster, China; Cat. No. AR1068) and permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Solarbio; Cat. No. T8200), cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio; Cat. No. A8020) for 1 h.

Primary antibodies used: iNOS (Proteintech, China; Cat. No. 18985-1-AP); CD86 (Abcam, UK; Cat. No. ab239075); Arg-1 (CST, USA; Cat. No. 93668) and CD206 (Abcam; UK; Cat. No. ab64693). After overnight incubation at 4 °C, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, USA; Cat. No. 4412, 8889) for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, China; Cat. No. C1002). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan; Cat. No. IX73) under identical acquisition settings for all groups. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. For each biological replicate, five randomly selected non-overlapping fields were analyzed. Mean fluorescence intensity was calculated after background subtraction and averaged for statistical analysis.

Western Blot and NF-κB Nuclear Translocation Assays

Protein expression and NF-κB nuclear translocation were analyzed using standard Western blot procedures. RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with CTX (25 μg/mL), LVFX (25 μg/mL), or AZM (10 μg/mL) for 1 h and then stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 30 min before protein extraction. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China; Cat. No. P0013B) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China; Cat. No. P1045). For nuclear translocation analysis, cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts were prepared using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Beyotime, China; Cat. No. P0027). GAPDH served as the cytoplasmic loading control, and Lamin B1 (Abcam, UK; Cat. No. ab16048) was used as the nuclear marker. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. No. 23225). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA; Cat. No. IPVH00010). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against p-IKKα/β (AFFINITY; Cat. No. AF3800), IKKα/β (AFFINITY; Cat. No. AF6014), NF-κB p65 (CST, USA; Cat. No. 8242), phospho-p65 (Ser536) (CST, USA; Cat. No. 3033), IκBα (CST, USA; Cat. No. 4814), and GAPDH (Abcam, UK; Cat. No. ab8245), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, USA; Cat. No. 7074; 7076). Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. No. 34580) and quantified with ImageJ. The nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of p65 was calculated to evaluate NF-κB nuclear translocation, while p-IKKα/β and p-p65 Ser536 levels were normalized to total IKKα/β and total p65, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate and repeated independently at least three times. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. When appropriate, data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The effects of the selected antibiotic concentrations on cell viability under inflammatory conditions were then examined. Compared with the control group, LPS stimulation caused a moderate but significant decrease in cell viability (P < 0.01, Figure 1D). Antibiotic pretreatment did not induce additional cytotoxicity during LPS challenge. Moreover, LVFX and AZM pretreatment significantly improved cell viability compared with the LPS-only group (P < 0.05), while CTX showed a similar protective trend without a statistically significant difference. These findings indicate that the selected concentrations were suitable for subsequent immunomodulatory analyses and that the observed anti-inflammatory effects were unlikely to be attributable to overt cytotoxicity.

Consistently, LPS markedly downregulated IκBα expression, whereas antibiotic pretreatment restored IκBα levels, with AZM producing the most pronounced effect (P < 0.001, Figure 4C). To further evaluate upstream NF-κB activation, whole-cell lysates were analyzed for p-IKKα/β and p-p65 Ser536. LPS markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of IKKα/β and p65, while total IKKα/β and total p65 levels remained relatively unchanged. Pretreatment with CTX, LVFX, or AZM reduced LPS-induced p-IKKα/β and p-p65 Ser536 levels, with AZM again exhibiting the strongest inhibitory effect (P < 0.001, Figure 4D–F). Together, these findings indicate that the three antibiotics attenuated LPS-induced NF-κB activation by suppressing IKKα/β and p65 phosphorylation, preventing p65 nuclear translocation, and preserving IκBα expression.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the immunomodulatory properties of three clinically used antibiotics—CTX, LVFX, and AZM—using an LPS-induced acute inflammatory macrophage model. By integrating cytokine profiling, macrophage polarization assessment, and NF-κB signaling analysis, we provide a comprehensive comparison of how different antibiotic classes influence innate immune responses. Several major findings emerged. First, the selected concentrations of CTX, LVFX, and AZM were well tolerated by RAW264.7 macrophages, ensuring that downstream observations reflect immune-regulatory effects rather than cytotoxicity. Second, all three antibiotics effectively mitigated LPS-induced inflammatory activation, suppressing TNF-α and IL-1β while restoring IL-10 secretion. Third, CTX, LVFX, and AZM reshaped macrophage phenotypes by inhibiting M1-associated markers (iNOS, CD86) and inducing M2 markers (Arg-1, CD206), with AZM showing the most pronounced activity. The additional IL-4/IL-13 positive control further confirmed that the Arg-1/CD206 qRT-PCR assay was responsive to classical M2-polarizing stimulation, supporting the reliability of the observed M2-associated marker changes. Finally, NF-κB was identified as a key regulatory axis: all antibiotics reduced nuclear p65 accumulation and preserved cytoplasmic IκBα, again with AZM exerting the strongest inhibitory effect.

These observations align well with prior research demonstrating the immunomodulatory capacity of antibiotics, particularly macrolides. AZM has long been recognized for its anti-inflammatory benefits in airway and chronic inflammatory diseases, where it modulates cytokine release, improves epithelial repair, and promotes M2-like macrophage programming.24 Our data not only confirm these established effects but also extend them by demonstrating that AZM markedly suppresses NF-κB activation in the context of acute LPS stimulation. The additional phosphorylation data further suggest that antibiotic-mediated suppression of NF-κB signaling occurs upstream of p65 nuclear translocation, as evidenced by reduced IKKα/β and p65 phosphorylation. Nevertheless, the present study does not establish whether these effects arise from direct modulation of TLR4 signaling or from indirect regulation of downstream inflammatory pathways. Further studies investigating receptor-level mechanisms, including TLR4- and MD2-related signaling events, would be required to define the precise molecular targets involved. In contrast, LVFX exhibited moderate, yet consistent anti-inflammatory effects, in line with studies reporting that fluoroquinolones interfere with TLR4-mediated inflammatory cascades.25,26 While CTX displayed comparatively weaker immunomodulation, its ability to partially suppress inflammatory cytokines suggests that β-lactams may contribute to immune regulation more than previously appreciated. Importantly, the use of a unified experimental platform allows for direct comparison of antibiotic classes, providing clearer insights into their relative immunoregulatory potency—an aspect rarely addressed in previous studies.

Although NF-κB emerged as a major regulatory pathway in the present study, macrophage polarization is governed by a complex signaling network. Previous studies have shown that MAPK family members, including ERK, JNK, and p38, participate in the regulation of inflammatory cytokine production and macrophage phenotype switching.27,28 Likewise, STAT signaling, particularly STAT1 and STAT6, plays an important role in balancing M1- and M2-associated transcriptional programs.29 It is therefore possible that the immunomodulatory effects observed in response to CTX, LVFX, and AZM are not exclusively mediated through NF-κB inhibition but may also involve coordinated regulation of MAPK- and STAT-dependent pathways. Further mechanistic studies will be required to determine how these signaling networks interact during antibiotic-mediated immune modulation.

Several innovative elements distinguish this work. First, instead of investigating a single drug or pathway, we conducted a multi-endpoint evaluation across three distinct antibiotic classes, encompassing functional cytokine measurements, gene and protein expression of polarization markers, and pathway-level NF-κB analysis.7 This broad assessment provides a more nuanced understanding of antibiotic-induced immune modulation. Second, the parallel examination of M1 and M2 markers offers mechanistic clarity, firmly linking antibiotic treatment to macrophage phenotype switching.30 Third, by demonstrating coherent changes across cytokine release, polarization markers, and NF-κB signaling, we propose a unified mechanistic model describing how antibiotics attenuate inflammation—providing an integrated view of antibiotic-mediated immune modulation. Together, these innovations expand the conceptual understanding of antibiotic–immune interactions and support the potential repurposing of certain antibiotics as adjunct therapies for inflammatory conditions.31

The potential relevance of these findings should be interpreted within the context of an in vitro macrophage model. Antibiotics are frequently administered in clinical conditions accompanied by prominent inflammatory responses, including infectious diseases, sepsis, postoperative inflammation, and tissue injury. Therefore, understanding whether commonly used antibiotics differ in their ability to modulate macrophage activation may provide useful preliminary insight into host immune regulation beyond direct antimicrobial activity. For example, the relatively strong inhibitory effect of AZM on M1-associated markers and NF-κB activation is consistent with previous evidence describing the anti-inflammatory properties of macrolides in airway inflammatory diseases.32 Similarly, the moderate effects observed for LVFX and the weaker but detectable effects of CTX suggest that different antibiotic classes may vary in their capacity to influence inflammatory macrophage responses. However, these findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence for clinical efficacy or as a basis for antibiotic selection in patients, particularly because the concentrations used in this in vitro study were selected based on non-cytotoxicity rather than pharmacokinetic equivalence to clinically achievable plasma levels. Further studies using primary macrophages, animal models, pharmacologically relevant exposure ranges, and clinically relevant inflammatory settings are required before the immunomodulatory implications of these observations can be translated into antibiotic stewardship or personalized therapeutic strategies.33

Although this study provides a comparative assessment of the immunomodulatory effects of CTX, LVFX, and AZM in an LPS-induced macrophage inflammatory model, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, RAW264.7 macrophages were used as the sole cellular model, which cannot fully reproduce the heterogeneity of primary macrophages or the complexity of intercellular interactions in vivo. Second, the antibiotic concentrations were selected based on preliminary viability assays rather than pharmacokinetic equivalence to clinically achievable plasma concentrations; therefore, caution is required when extrapolating the magnitude of these effects to clinical settings. In addition, dose-response analyses of cytokine suppression at lower, more pharmacologically relevant concentrations were not performed, and future studies should address whether similar immunomodulatory effects can be observed across clinically relevant exposure ranges. Third, antibiotic-only control groups were not included, and future studies incorporating such groups will help distinguish basal immunomodulatory activity from effects observed under inflammatory stimulation. Fourth, this study focused primarily on NF-κB signaling, whereas other pathways involved in macrophage polarization and inflammatory regulation, including MAPK-, STAT-, and IRF-related signaling and metabolic reprogramming, require further investigation. Finally, the use of a single inflammatory stimulus, LPS, and the absence of in vivo validation limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies using primary macrophages, sterile injury models, different pathogen-associated molecular patterns, and animal models of acute inflammation are needed to validate and extend these observations.

Conclusion

In summary, CTX, LVFX, and AZM exerted distinct but partially convergent immunomodulatory effects in an LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophage model. All three antibiotics attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine production, increased M2-associated marker expression, and inhibited NF-κB activation, with AZM showing the most pronounced effects. These findings provide preliminary in vitro evidence that commonly used antibiotics may differ in their ability to regulate macrophage inflammatory responses beyond their antimicrobial activity. Further validation using primary macrophages, pharmacologically relevant exposure ranges, and in vivo inflammatory models is required to clarify the translational relevance of these observations.
