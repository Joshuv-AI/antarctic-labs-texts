On an extension problem on the moment curve
Seunghun Lee and Eran Nevo

Abstract
We show that for 2 ≤ d ≤ 4 2\leq d\leq 4 , every finite geometric simplicial complex Δ \Delta in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with vertices on the moment curve can be extended to a triangulation T T of the cyclic polytope C C where Δ , T \Delta,T and C C all have the same vertex set. Further, for d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 we construct for every n ≥ d + 3 n\geq d+3 complexes Δ \Delta on n n vertices for which no such triangulations T T exist. Our result for d = 4 d=4 has the following novel algebraic application, due to a correspondence by Oppermann and Thomas (JEMS, 2012): every maximal rigid object in 𝒪 A n 2 \mathcal{O}_{A_{n}^{2}} is cluster tilting, where 𝒪 A n δ \mathcal{O}_{A_{n}^{\delta}} denotes a higher dimensional cluster category introduced by Oppermann and Thomas for A n δ A_{n}^{\delta} , where A n δ A_{n}^{\delta} denotes a higher Auslander algebra of linearly oriented type A A .

Introduction

The
moment curve
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\gamma_{d}=\{(t,t^{2},\dots,t^{d}):\ t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, has remarkable properties used in applications to combinatorics and discrete geometry, in particular in the study of polytopes: for every finite point set
A\subseteq\gamma_{d}
with
|A|=n\geq d+1
, the convex hull of
A
,
\conv(A)
, is the
cyclic polytope
on
n
vertices of dimension
d
,
C(n,d)
, a simplicial polytope whose face lattice is determined by
n
and
d
, and which maximizes the face numbers among all
d
-dimensional polytopes on
n
vertices by McMullen’s celebrated upper bound theorem
[
18
]
. See e.g. the textbooks
[
8
,
11
,
28
]
for more details.

We consider the following natural extension problem.

Question 1.1
.

Does
every
geometric simplicial complex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on a finite point set
A\subseteq\gamma_{d}
extend to a triangulation of
\conv(A)
without adding new vertices?

This problem is in fact a combinatorial one, as the question of whether a collection of
d
-simplices on the ground set
A\subseteq\gamma_{d}
induces a triangulation of
\conv(A)
can be phrased in combinatorial terms: intersections correspond to interlacing patterns (see Proposition
2.2
) which are forbidden, and boundary faces are identified by the Gale evenness condition (e.g.
[
11
]
) and one checks that exactly these
(d-1)
-faces are contained in a unique
d
-simplex.

As we shall see, the answer to Question
1.1
depends on the dimension
d
. Considering a more general situation first, note that for
d=2
every simplicial complex on a finite point set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
extends to a triangulation of
\conv(A)
, while for
d=3
there are finite point sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
in convex general position, and simplicial complexes on them, which do not extend to a triangulation of
\conv(A)
without adding new vertices. As a simple example consider the Schönhardt’s polyhedron
[
22
]
, see
[
8
, Lemma 3.6.2, Figure 3.43]
: let
A
be the set of six vertices of a triangular prism with its bottom triangle face rotated a little, and let
\Delta
be the union of the three tetrahedra where their vertex sets correspond to the three square sides of the prism, after being rotated. Then
\Delta
does not extend to a triangulation of
\conv(A)
without new vertices.

Perhaps surprisingly, we prove that the answer to Question
1.1
is Yes iff
d\leq 4
. To state it precisely, let us set some terminology first. For an affinely independent set
\sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of size at most
d+1
, we will frequently refer to the geometric simplex
\conv(\sigma)
as
\sigma
when it is appropriate.
We say that two simplices
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

overlap
if the intersection of the simplices is not a face of each of them, namely

\conv(\sigma_{1})\cap\conv(\sigma_{2})\supsetneq\conv(\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}).

In this definition, non-overlapping simplices may intersect, but only in a common face. The non-strict containment above
always holds, and when it holds with equality for every pair of simplices of a given simplicial complex we call it a geometric embedding of the complex.

For convenience in the proof we use a different notation for dimension in our main result:

Theorem 1.2
.

(i) For every
D\leq 4
and every finite collection
\mathcal{F}
of pairwise non-overlapping simplices in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
on
A\subseteq\gamma_{D}
,
\mathcal{F}
can be extended into a triangulation
T
of the cyclic polytope
\conv(A)
such that
A
is exactly the vertex set of
T
.

(ii) For every
D\geq 5
and
n\geq D+3
, there exists a collection of pairwise non-overlapping
D
-simplices in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
exactly on an
n
-vertex set
A\subseteq\gamma_{D}
which cannot be extended into a triangulation of
\conv(A)
without adding new vertices.

The additional vertices required to extend the collection on
A
given by Theorem
1.2
(ii) into a triangulation of
\conv(A)
are not on
\gamma_{D}
as they should be in
\conv(A)
.

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
(i) we will use the known facts that for

the higher Stasheff-Tamari poset
\HST(n,d)
is a lattice; see
[

]
and
[

, Thm.3.6]
respectively and Theorem

. In order to prove Theorem
1.2
(ii) we start with an example of Rambau
[

with

and

.
For

, different

constructed by Oppermann and Thomas

]
.

Theorem

]

-st higher Auslander algebras of linearly oriented

For

[

they showed:

(
[

There is a bijection between internal

In this correspondence, a family of non-overlapping (internal)

by considering its collection of indecomposable direct summands, where each of the direct summands corresponds to a simplex in the family. In

Every maximal rigid object in

Thus, our main result exhibits a combinatorial approach towards a purely algebraic problem. It may be interesting to go in the other direction (in the spirit of

), namely to give a purely algebraic proof of Theorem

Previous studies.
The combinatorics of triangulations of the cyclic polytope
C(n,d)
has been extensively studied.
Edelman and Reiner
[
10
]
defined two partial orders on this set of triangulations; one is given by bistellar flips and the other
by comparing the “heights of the liftings in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
” for those triangulations. These two orders define the so-called
(first and second) higher Stasheff-Tamari poset
. They showed that these two orders are equal for
d=2,3
. Since then there have been active research on this topic
[
21
,
9
,
23
,
20
,
24
,
25
]
. Recently the two higher Stasheff-Tamari orders were proved to be the same for every dimension
d
by Williams
[
26
]
.

The combinatorial criterion for having overlapping simplices on the moment curve (see Proposition
2.2
) is well-known
[
3
]
and is one of the ways of defining the
alternating oriented matroids

[
2
, Section 9.4]
. It is also closely related to recent works on geometric hypergraph colorings and their representations where a similar combinatorial property was introduced and has been studied
[
1
,
15
,
16
,
7
]
: A hypergraph
H
on a totally ordered vertex set is
(AB)^{l/2}
-free
if there is no subsequence of vertices of length
l
following the order such that the odd-numbered vertices are in
A\setminus B
and the even numbered vertices are in
B\setminus A
for some hyperedges
A
and
B
.
For a hypergraph
H
consisting of
d
-simplices on
\gamma_{2d}
with the natural order, we have an equivalence by Proposition
2.2
that
H
is
(AB)^{d+1}
-free if and only if any pair of two
d
-simplices in
H
do not overlap.

2
Ordering simplices and triangulations by height

In this preliminary section, we introduce various relations and partial orders on simplices and triangulations on the moment curve defined by the height of their “liftings” and discuss several properties of them, old and new.
In particular, the partial order on triangulations of
C(n,d)
was originally introduced by Edelman and Reiner
[
10
]
as the
second higher Stasheff-Tamari order
, see Subsection
2.2
for details. We analogously define the “above-below” relation
<_{d+1}
between simplices, and a partial order
\preceq_{d+1}
as the reflexive and transitive closure of
<_{d+1}
. The order
\preceq_{d+1}
is used in the proof of Theorem
1.2
(i).

2.1
Ordering simplices by height

We first discuss properties of the moment curve
\gamma_{d}
which is useful in our discussion.

Every set of at most
d+1
distinct points on
\gamma_{d}
is affinely independent and every
n
points on
\gamma_{d}
are in convex position.
The combinatorial properties, to be discussed below, of finite point sets on
\gamma_{d}
and their convex hulls
depend solely on the order in which the points appear on
\gamma_{d}
rather than the actual coordinates of the points. Hence when we are given
n
points
\gamma_{d}(t_{1}),\gamma_{d}(t_{2}),\dots,\gamma_{d}(t_{n})
with

,
we will frequently abuse notation and refer to the point
\gamma_{d}(t_{i})
by its index

. Also, we may consider
the
cyclic polytope
C(n,d)=\conv\{\gamma_{d}(i):i\in[n]\}
on the vertex set

.
Following the convention from the Introduction, for

a geometric

-simplex
\conv(\sigma)
on a vertex set

of size

will be frequently identified with the subset
\sigma
.
Note that when we consider two simplices
\sigma
and

on
\gamma_{d}
, their sizes may differ.

For positive integers

\mathcal{D}(k,[a,b])=\{\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{2k-1}<i_{2k}\}\subseteq[a,b]:i_{2j}-i_{2j-1}=1\textrm{ for every $j\in[k]$}\},

where

.
The following facts are known (e.g.
[

Proposition 2.1
.

The set of the facets in the upper envelope of

is given by

•

\{\{n\}\}*\mathcal{D}((d-1)/2,[n-1])

is odd, and

•

\{\{1,n\}\}*\mathcal{D}(d/2-1,[2,n-1])
when

is even,

and the set of the facets in the lower envelope of

is given by

•

\{\{1\}\}*\mathcal{D}((d-1)/2,[2,n])

This in particular gives the whole list of facets of

. Here,

\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}:=\{A\cup B:A\in\mathcal{A},\,B\in\mathcal{B}\}
.

For subsets
\sigma
and
\tau
of

, we say that

and

are

-interlacing
if there is a sequence

which satisfies one of the following alternating conditions:

\displaystyle v_{1}\in\sigma,v_{2}\in\tau,v_{3}\in\sigma\dots\textrm{ (beginning with an element of $\sigma$), or}

(
\sigma
)

\displaystyle v_{1}\in\tau,v_{2}\in\sigma,v_{3}\in\tau\dots\textrm{ (beginning with an element of $\tau$)}.

(
\tau
)

The following fact gives a combinatorial criterion for the overlapping condition on the moment curve and
is well-known
[

Proposition 2.2
.

Simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if

(d+2)
-interlacing.

For

and a simplex
\sigma=\{\gamma_{d}(t_{1}),\dots,\gamma_{d}(t_{k})\}

\gamma_{d}
, we define the

of

corresponding to

the

For simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
, we want to compare their heights (or more precisely, the heights of the liftings
\hat{\sigma}
and
\hat{\tau}
) in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
If
\sigma
and

do not overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

and

have the same value at the common domain. Thus we only compare the heights when

and

overlap in

, or equivalently by Proposition

, when they are

-interlacing.

First, we consider the following observation.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that two simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
are
(d+2)
-interlacing by

•

satisfying (

σ

) when

is even, or

•

satisfying (

τ

) when

Then there is a point

and

.

Proof.

When
d
is even, we can find subsets
\sigma_{0}\subseteq\sigma
and
\tau_{0}\subseteq\tau

which are
(d+2)
-interlacing with an element of
\sigma_{0}
comes first. Let
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
and
\hat{\tau}_{0}
be the liftings of
\sigma_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
on
\gamma_{d+1}
. By Proposition

, in the cyclic polytope

, a full simplex in

, we can easily see

(a)

\hat{\sigma}_{0}
is at the lower envelope but not at the upper envelope of

,

(b)

\hat{\tau}_{0}
is at the upper envelope but not at the lower envelope of

, and

(c)

By the general position property of

When

on the set of simplices on

. For two simplices

Proposition 2.4
.

For simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
,
\sigma<_{d+1}\tau
if and only if
\sigma
and
\tau
are
(d+2)
-interlacing by

•

satisfying (

σ

) but not (

τ

) when

is even, and

•

satisfying (

τ

) but not (

) when

is odd.

Proof.

For the if implication, suppose the interlacing condition in the statement holds. Then
\sigma
and
\tau
are
(d+2)
-interlacing but not
(d+3)
-interlacing, thus

(i)

\sigma
and
\tau
do overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but

(ii)

the liftings
\hat{\sigma}
and
\hat{\tau}
do not overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
;

Condition (i) implies that for the intersection

.

Further, Condition (ii) implies that

Corollary 2.5
.

Given a pair of simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
, one of the following four mutually exclusive cases occurs:

(A)

\sigma
and
\tau
do not overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or equivalently,
\sigma
and
\tau
are not
(d+2)
-interlacing.

(B)

\sigma<_{d+1}\tau
, or equivalently,
\sigma
and

(d+2)
-interlacing by satisfying the combinatorial condition of Proposition

.

(C)

\sigma>_{d+1}\tau
, or equivalently,
\sigma
and
\tau
are
(d+2)
-interlacing by satisfying the combinatorial condition of Proposition

with the roles of
\sigma
and
\tau

(D)

the liftings
\hat{\sigma}
and
\hat{\tau}
overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, or equivalently,
\sigma
and

Proof.

It is clear that all four cases (A)-(D) are mutually exclusive by their combinatorial
interpretation in terms of interlacing patterns. To show that (A)-(D) cover all cases, it is enough to show that (D) holds if neither (A), (B), nor (C) holds. Equivalently, we show that when
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if there are points

and

in the common domain of

and

and
\hat{\tau}

. In fact, by again setting

, we can see that

.
As in the proof of Proposition

,

is convex, so along the segment between

and

. This is where the liftings

and
\hat{\tau}

and
\hat{\tau}
overlap in

.

Remark 2.6
.

As conditions (A)-(D) have combinatorial characterizations, a purely combinatorial proof of Corollary
2.5
can be given, left to the reader.

Now, we define another relation
\preceq_{d+1}
from
<_{d+1}
: given simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
,
\sigma\preceq_{d+1}\tau
if

•

\sigma=\tau
, or

•

there is a positive integer
k
and a sequence of simplices
\sigma=\sigma_{0}<_{d+1}\sigma_{1}<_{d+1}\cdots<_{d+1}\sigma_{k}=\tau
on
\gamma_{d}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

The relation
\preceq_{d+1}
is a partial order on the simplices on
\gamma_{d}
.

Proof.

That
\preceq_{d+1}
is
reflexive and transitive is clear from the definition. We show it is also
antisymmetric.
For this, it is enough to show that there is no sequence

\sigma_{0}<_{d+1}\sigma_{1}<_{d+1}\cdots<_{d+1}\sigma_{k-1}<_{d+1}\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{0}

for some positive integer
k
where
\sigma_{0},\dots,\sigma_{k-1}
are all distinct.

Suppose for contradiction that such a sequence exists for some
k\geq 1
.
For every
i\in[k]\cup\{0\}
define the interval
J_{i}=[\min(\sigma_{i}),\max(\sigma_{i})]
.
Now we use induction on the dimension
d
.
For
d=1
, by the condition
\sigma_{i-1}<_{d+1}\sigma_{i}
for every
i\in[k]
and since each
\sigma_{i}
is a point or an edge,
J_{i-1}\subsetneq J_{i}
by Proposition
2.4
. This leads to a contradiction.

For
d=2
, again the condition
\sigma_{i-1}<_{d+1}\sigma_{i}
for every
i\in[k]
implies that
\min(\sigma_{i-1})\leq\min(\sigma_{i})
and
\max(\sigma_{i-1})\leq\max(\sigma_{i})
for every
i\in[k]
by Proposition
2.4
. To have
\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{k}
, we must have
J_{i}=J_{i^{\prime}}
for all
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
. In particular, all
\sigma_{i}
must be triangles, otherwise they do not have the desired interlacing pattern. Let
m_{i}
be the remaining point in the singleton
\sigma_{i}\setminus\{\min(\sigma_{i}),\max(\sigma_{i})\}
. By Proposition
2.4
again, we have
m_{i-1}>m_{i}
for every
i\in[k]
; a contradiction, which proves the base case.

Now suppose
d>2
. By Proposition
2.4
and a similar reasoning, we have that when
d
is even
\min(\sigma_{i-1})\leq\min(\sigma_{i})
and
\max(\sigma_{i-1})\leq\max(\sigma_{i})
for every
i\in[k]
,
and when
d
is odd

\min(\sigma_{i})\leq\min(\sigma_{i-1})\leq\max(\sigma_{i-1})\leq\max(\sigma_{i})
for every
i\in[k]
. By the assumption
\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{k}
, this implies
J_{i}=J_{i^{\prime}}
for every
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
. Let
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=\sigma_{i}\setminus\{\max(\sigma_{i}),\min(\sigma_{i})\}
. The
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}
are non-empty as
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}
and
\sigma_{i-1}^{\prime}
are
d
-interlacing and
d\geq 3
.
We now consider
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}
as a simplex on
\gamma_{d-2}
via projection. By Proposition
2.4
again, we have

\sigma_{k}^{\prime}<_{d-1}\sigma_{k-1}^{\prime}<_{d-1}\cdots<_{d-1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}<_{d-1}\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
, which gives a contradiction by the induction hypothesis.
∎

In particular, we never have
\tau<_{d+1}\sigma
when
\sigma\preceq_{d+1}\tau
.
It is easy to find examples of non-overlapping (in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
\sigma,\tau\subseteq\gamma_{d}
with
\sigma\preceq_{d+1}\tau
. It is also not very difficult to find
\sigma,\tau\subseteq\gamma_{d}
with
\sigma\preceq_{d+1}\tau
and that the liftings
\hat{\sigma}
and
\hat{\tau}
overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
:

Example 2.8
.

Consider the following simplices on
\gamma_{2}
:
\sigma=\{1,4,6\}
,
e=\{2,7\}
and
\tau=\{3,5,8\}
. Then
\sigma<_{3}e<_{3}\tau
(so in particular
\sigma\preceq_{3}\tau
) but
\sigma
and
\tau
are 6-interlacing.

All these suggest that there is subtle difference between the “above-below” relation
<_{d+1}
and the order
\preceq_{d+1}
. While we mostly use
<_{d+1}
to compare heights between two simplices,
\preceq_{d+1}
is helpful when we give a total ordering of given simplices consistent with
<_{d+1}
as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
(i) at Subsection
3.1
and in the proof of Proposition
3.9
at Subsection
3.4
.

2.2
Height of a triangulation and Higher Stasheff-Tamari posets

A
triangulation
of a
d
-polytope
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a geometric simplicial complex whose realization space is exactly the polytope
P
.
Let
S(n,d)
be the set of all triangulations on vertex set
[n]
of the cyclic polytope
C(n,d)
. The
lifting of
T\in S(n,d)
is the family of the liftings of all simplices of
T
, so it is also a geometric simplicial complex whose realization space is a section inside the cyclic polytope
C(n,d+1)
.

For a triangulation
T\in S(n,d)
, we can similarly define the height function
h_{T}:C(n,d)\to\mathbb{R}
as the union of
h_{\sigma}:\conv(\sigma)\to\mathbb{R}
for every
\sigma\in T
, that is, if a point
p\in\conv(\sigma)
, we let
h_{T}(p)=h_{\sigma}(p)
. Then
h_{T}
is well-defined for every
T\in S(n,d)
.

This enables us to compare heights between a triangulation and a simplex, or between two triangulations. For a triangulation
T\in S(n,d)
and a simplex
\sigma\subseteq\gamma_{d}
, we say
T\leq_{d+1}\sigma
(
\sigma\leq_{d+1}T
resp.) if
h_{T}(p)\leq h_{\sigma}(p)
(
h_{\sigma}(p)\leq h_{T}(p)
resp.) for every point
p\in\conv(\sigma)
.

The following lemma follows easily from the definitions and our earlier observations. A similar statement holds for
\sigma\geq_{d+1}T
.

Lemma 2.9
.

For a triangulation
T\in S(n,d)
and a simplex
\sigma\subseteq\gamma_{d}
,
\sigma\leq_{d+1}T
if and only if for every
\tau\in T
either
\sigma
and
\tau
do not overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\sigma<_{d+1}\tau
.

Proof.

By definition,
\sigma\leq_{d+1}T
if and only if for every
\tau\in T
whenever
\sigma
intersects
\tau
we have
h_{\sigma}\leq h_{\tau}
in the common domain.
The latter holds iff either
\sigma
and
\tau
do not overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or they do overlap and additionally
\sigma<_{d+1}\tau
.
∎

The following lemma is a reformulation of Lemma
2.9
which will be useful later. It generalizes
[
10
, Propositions 3.2 and 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.10
.

For every triangulation
T\in S(n,d)
and every simplex
\sigma\subseteq\gamma_{d}
there holds:
\sigma\leq_{d+1}T
(resp.
T\leq_{d+1}\sigma
) if and only if there are no
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma
and
\tau\in T
satisfying
\tau<_{d+1}\sigma^{\prime}
with
|\sigma^{\prime}|=\lceil d/2\rceil+1
and
|\tau|=\lfloor d/2\rfloor+1

(resp. satisfying
\sigma^{\prime}<_{d+1}\tau
with
|\tau|=\lceil d/2\rceil+1
and
|\sigma^{\prime}|=\lfloor d/2\rfloor+1
).

Proof.

We prove the statement for
\sigma\leq_{d+1}T
, the statement for
T\leq_{d+1}\sigma
is proved analogously.
Note first that the conditions in the right-hand side of Lemma
2.9
correspond to Conditions (A) and (B) in Corollary
2.5
with the consistent notation. So Lemma
2.9
implies that
\sigma\leq_{d+1}T
if and only if we never have Cases (C) or (D) in Corollary
2.5
for the simplex
\sigma
and any
\tau\in T
, again with the consistent notation. Concerning interlacing patterns, this condition exactly corresponds to the condition at the "right-hand side" of the lemma.
∎

For two triangulations
T,T^{\prime}\in S(n,d)
, say
T\leq_{d+1}T^{\prime}
if
h_{T}(p)\leq h_{T^{\prime}}(p)
for every point
p\in C(n,d)
. This relation
\leq_{d+1}
is a partial order on
S(n,d)
, and was introduced by Edelman and Reiner
[
10
]
as the
second higher Stasheff-Tamari order
.
This order (which coincides with their
first
higher Stasheff-Tamari order by
[
26
]
)
defines the
higher Stasheff-Tamari poset

\HST(n,d)=(S(n,d),\leq_{d+1})
.

The following result by Rambau
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
will be also useful.

Theorem 2.11
.

There is a surjective map
\Psi
from the set of maximal chains of
\HST(n,d)
to
S(n,d+1)
such that
for a maximal chain
\mathcal{C}:T_{1}\leq_{d+1}T_{2}\leq_{d+1}\cdots\leq_{d+1}T_{k}
in
\HST(n,d)
, the
d
-skeleton of
\Psi(\mathcal{C})
is exactly the union of the liftings of all
T_{i}
(which in turn determines
\Psi(\mathcal{C})
).

We introduced various relations throughout this section: between simplices, between a simplex and a triangulation, and between triangulations respectively;
however, they are easily distinguishable by context.

2.3
Submersion set of a triangulation and lattice property

For a triangulation
T\in S(n,d)
, the
submersion set

\sub(T)
is the set defined as

\sub(T)=\{\sigma\subseteq\gamma_{d}:\sigma\leq_{d+1}T,\,\dim(\sigma)=\lceil d/2\rceil\}.

Submersion sets were used to provide the following encoding result which in particular implies that
\HST(n,d)
is a lattice for
d=2,3
, see
[
10
, Theorems 3.6, 4.9]
. This encoding is equivalent to the one provided in
[
12
]
for
d=2
.

Theorem 2.12
.

For
d=2
or
3
, the poset map
\Phi
from
\HST(n,d)
to the poset
\mathcal{P}=\left(2^{\binom{[n]}{\lceil d/2\rceil+1}},\subseteq\right)

given by
\Phi:T\mapsto\sub(T)
is an injection, and the subposet of
\mathcal{P}
induced by the image of
\Phi
is a lattice where the meet
\sub(T_{1})\wedge\sub(T_{2})
is given by the intersection
\sub(T_{1})\cap\sub(T_{2})
. In particular,
\HST(n,d)
is a lattice.

The join in
\HST(n,d)
is determined by the meet
since
\HST(n,d)
has a maximum.
In order to prove that
\Phi

is injective, one of the key ideas in
[
10
]
is to extract “maximal” elements from a submersion set, or more precisely,
diagonal-closed
or
triangle-closed
families, and (re)construct the triangulation from them.
In a similar manner, we reformulate and prove the following for our purpose without considering the closed families after having a simple observation.

Lemma 2.13
.

For
T\in S(n,d)
and a simplex
\tau\subseteq\gamma_{d}
with
\tau\leq_{d+1}T
,

\tau
is a face of
T
if and only if
there are no
\sigma\in\sub(T)
such that
\tau<_{d+1}\sigma
.

Proof.

The only if implication is obvious since whenever
\sigma\in\sub(T)
and
\tau\in T
overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
\sigma<_{d+1}\tau
by the definition of
\sub(T)
and Lemma
2.9
, which forbids
\tau<_{d+1}\sigma
by Corollary
2.5
.
For the opposite direction, suppose the condition at the right-hand side. For any
\sigma^{\prime}\in T
of size
\lceil d/2\rceil+1
, since
\sigma^{\prime}
belongs to
\sub(T)
, we cannot have
\tau<_{d+1}\sigma^{\prime}
. This implies that
\sigma\in T
and
\tau
never overlap in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
otherwise we have
\tau<_{d+1}\sigma^{\prime}
for some
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma
of size
\lceil d/2\rceil+1
by the assumption
\tau\leq_{d+1}T
, Lemma
2.9
, and Proposition
2.4
.
Therefore, if
\conv(\tau)
and
\conv(\sigma)
intersect in their relative interiors for some
\sigma\in T
,
they should have
\tau=\tau\cap\sigma=\sigma
. This implies
\tau\in T
.
∎

In this section we prove Theorem

(i). There are two ingredients in the proof: the fact that

is a
lattice for

, Lemma
2.12
; and the following theorems showing

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

. Then there is a triangulation

.

We first present the proof of Theorem

(i) assuming these facts in Subsection

. The proofs of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
are given in Subsections
3.2
and

, respectively. The proof of Theorem

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.2
(i) modulo the existence of suitable triangulations

The following proposition enables us to reduce
\mathcal{F}
from Theorem
1.2
(i) to consist only of simplices of dimension two when
D=4
, and of dimensions two or one when

.

Proposition 3.3
.

If a triangulation
T
of a
D
-polytope contains the
\lceil D/2\rceil
-dimensional skeleton of a
k
-simplex
\sigma
for some
k
with
\lceil D/2\rceil\leq k\leq D
, then
T
contains
\sigma
as well.

Proof.

Suppose not. Then there is a facet
\tau\in T
which overlaps
\sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, hence
\sigma
and
\tau
are
(D+2)
-interlacing by Proposition
2.2
. Therefore we can find
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma
of size
\lfloor(D+2)/2\rfloor
or
\lceil(D+2)/2\rceil

which overlaps
\tau
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. But by assumption we have
\sigma^{\prime}\in T
as well as
\tau\in T
, which leads to a contradiction.
∎

We temporarily assume the validity of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
(proved in Subsections
3.2
and
3.3
respectively)
and use them to prove Theorem
1.2
(i).

Proof of Theorem
1.2
(i).

When
D=3
, we may assume
\mathcal{F}
consists only of triangles and edges: indeed, for a 3-dimensional simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}
, we can remove
\sigma
from
\mathcal{F}
and add the four triangles

and

from the boundary of
\sigma
. If a desired triangulation

contains

then

must contain
\sigma
as well by Proposition
3.3
.
When
D=4
, we may assume
\mathcal{F}
consists only of triangles:
a 4-dimensional cyclic polytope contain all possible edges on its vertex set at its boundary, thus we can remove them from
\mathcal{F}
. Hence, by Proposition

again, we only need to consider triangles, as for every
k
-simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}
with

\sigma
with its 2-faces. Obviously, for both

.

Let

and

. We project the simplices in
\mathcal{F}
into

by ignoring the last coordinate.
Abusing notation, we also identify such projections with subsets of

.

We order the projections as

\sigma_{i}<_{D}\sigma_{j}

\sigma_{i}

.

Also, the liftings

, we obtain

.

Now we use the lattice property of

\displaystyle\sub(S_{k})=\sub(\bigwedge_{i=k}^{m}T_{i})=\bigcap_{i=k}^{m}\sub(T_{i})

(1)

3.4
Finding a 2-dimensional triangulation avoiding certain interlacing patterns

This subsection is devoted to the proof of Lemma
3.8
below, a crucial lemma in finishing the proof of Theorem
3.2
. It is a purely combinatorial claim.
Its proof is a bit involved,
however, the proof itself is independent from the rest of this paper.

Let
L
and
R
be two sets of edges on the 2-dimensional moment curve
\gamma_{2}
, and
M
be a set of triangles on
\gamma_{2}
. Again using conventions from Section
2
, following the natural linear order from
\gamma_{2}
we identify an edge or a triangle from

as a subset of

for some positive integer

. Note that the sets

and

might intersect, and any set among

,

and

might be empty.

Regarding these

,

and

, we consider the following forbidden interlacing conditions:

(LR)

No edges
\{l_{1}<l_{2}\}
of
L
and
\{r_{1}<r_{2}\}
of
R
are 4-interlacing starting with
r_{1}
, i.e.,
r_{1}<l_{1}<r_{2}<l_{2}
never holds.

(LMR)

For every edge
\{v_{1}<v_{2}\}
of

and every triangle

of

, they are not 5-interlacing, i.e.,

never holds.

(MM)

For every pair of triangles
\{v_{1}<v_{2}<v_{3}\}
and
\{w_{1}<w_{2}<w_{3}\}
of

, they are not 6-interlacing, i.e., neither
v_{1}<w_{1}<v_{2}<w_{2}<v_{3}<w_{3}
nor

holds.

For an edge
e=\{v_{1}<v_{2}\}\subseteq\gamma_{2}
(as a subset of
[n]
), we define the following
forbidden interlacing
conditions w.r.t. the sets
L
,
M
and
R
. Eventually,
we will need to find a triangulation of the polygon

so that each edge of the triangulation satisfies the following three conditions; achieved in Lemma
3.8
.

(Le)

No edge
\{l_{1}<l_{2}\}
of

is 4-interlacing with

starting with
v_{1}
, i.e.,
v_{1}<l_{1}<v_{2}<l_{2}
never holds.

(Me)

No triangle
\{w_{1}<w_{2}<w_{3}\}\in M
is 5-interlacing with
e
, i.e.,
w_{1}<v_{1}<w_{2}<v_{2}<w_{3}
never holds.

(Re)

No edge
\{r_{1}<r_{2}\}
of

is 4-interlacing with
e
starting with
r_{1}
, i.e.,
r_{1}<v_{1}<r_{2}<v_{2}
never holds.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
L
,

and

be finite families of edges or triangles on
\gamma_{2}
given as above, satisfying (LR), (LMR), and (MM). Suppose there is a vertex subset

on
\gamma_{2}
with

such that each edge of the (convex) polygon

satisfies (Le), (Me) and (Re). Then we can find a triangulation

of

such that each edge of

satisfies (Le), (Me) and (Re).

We may assume that

does not contain any edges of

since any diagonal of

cannot cross such edges.

Proposition 3.9

Lemma

Proof.

We let

,

and

be the union of the set of the edges of

guaranteed by Lemma

to a linear order on their union, namely that an edge of

comes earlier than an edge of

, respectively. Since they do not have intersection in the interior, the list is non-extendable.

Remark 4.6

(Communicated with Francisco Santos)
.

An alternative way to see non-extendability when

can be provided via Gale transform, see Figure

. It is known that when

every triangulation over

points in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
is regular. A given list of

-simplices is extendable to a regular triangulation without adding new vertices if and only if
the intersection of all dual cones (the dual cone of
\sigma
is the cone at the Gale dual over the complement

) has a full-dimensional intersection. Therefore, it is reduced to checking intersection of the dual cones in

Algorithmic aspects.
Theorem
1.2
(i) raises the question of how fast the extension can be computed:

Problem 5.1
.

For
d=3,4
, given a geometric complex
\Delta
on
n
-vertex set
V
on the moment curve
\gamma_{d}
, find the running time of computing a triangulation
T\in S(V,d)
of the cyclic
d
-polytope
\conv(V)
which is an extension of
\Delta
, without adding new vertices; namely in the worst case, as
n
tends to infinity.

Given such extension
T
, by coning the boundary of
T
with a new vertex we obtain a triangulation of
S^{d}
, and thus, by the Dehn-Sommerville relations,
T
has
O(n^{2})
faces, which is tight for some triangulations
T
; see also
[
20
, Thm.2.4]
for
d=4
. Thus the running time is
\Omega(n^{2})
, the size of the output in the worst case.

Proposition 5.2
.

For both
d=3,4
, there exists a greedy algorithm that produces an extension
T\in S(V,d)
of
\Delta
as in Problem
5.1
and runs in
O(n^{5})
time.

Proof.

We may assume that
\Delta
has dimension at most
d-1
, as for any
d
-simplex
\sigma\in\Delta
, every extension
T\in S(V,d)
of
\Delta\setminus\{\sigma\}
must contain
\sigma
by Proposition
3.3
.
We may also assume that the boundary complex
\partial\conv(V)
is a subcomplex of
\Delta
as
\partial\conv(V)
is a subcomplex of any triangulation in
S(V,d)
. All these reductions only require
O_{d}(n^{2})
time.
Consider the following greedy algorithm:

•

Step 0: Let
T_{0}=\Delta
. Make a list
L_{0}
of all
(d-1)
-simplices in
\partial\conv(V)
. Then
L_{0}
is non-empty and
T_{0}
is a geometric complex on
\gamma_{d}
. This step requires
O(n^{2})
time.

•

Step
i
(for
i\geq 1
):

If
L_{i-1}
is empty, stop and declare
T=T_{i-1}
is a desired extension of
\Delta
.

Else, choose a simplex
\tau\in L_{i-1}
. We will have either
\tau
is in
\partial\conv(V)
and is contained in no
d
-face of
T_{i-1}
, or
\tau
is in
T_{i-1}\setminus\partial\conv(V)
and is contained in exactly one
d
-face of
T_{i-1}
.
Order by
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}
the
d
-simplices on
V
containing
\tau

– there are
O(n)
of them – and go through them checking if
\sigma=\sigma_{j}
is
(d+2)
-interlacing
with some facet of
T_{i-1}
.

If it is not, let

T_{i}=T_{i-1}\cup\langle\sigma\rangle
(where
\langle\sigma\rangle
is the simplicial complex with a unique maximal face
\sigma
); by construction
T_{i}
is a geometric simplicial complex on
\gamma_{d}
.
Now change
L_{i-1}
as follows to obtain
L_{i}
: delete from
L_{i-1}
the face
\tau
and all
(d-1)
-faces of
\sigma
which belong to
L_{i-1}
, and add to it all
(d-1)
-faces of
\sigma
which do not belong to
L_{i-1}
.

If
\sigma=\sigma_{j}
does
(d+2)
-interlaces with some facet of
T_{i-1}
, let
\sigma=\sigma_{j+1}
and repeat the check.

The scenario that each face
\sigma_{j}
does
(d+2)
-interlace with some facet of
T_{i-1}
is impossible by Theorem
1.2
(i), hence the algorithm will stop when
L_{i}
is empty, giving
T=T_{i}
as a geometric simplicial complex which is an extension of
\Delta
.
It is left to verify that
T
triangulates
\conv(V)
.
Indeed, the simplices of
T
cover the whole cyclic polytope
\conv(V)
:
if not, then there exists a point
p\in\conv(V)\setminus T
, and
p
sees a
(d-1)
-face
\tau\in T
, hence
\tau\in L_{i}
, contradicting that
L_{i}
is empty.

Running time analysis: a single check in Step
i
whether a given simplex
\sigma
is
(d+2)
-interlacing with any of the facets of
T_{i-1}
or
\sigma\in T_{i-1}
takes
O(n^{2})
time.
If
\sigma
is chosen to be added to
T_{i-1}
, it requires
O_{d}(n^{2})
more time to update
T_{i}
(as repetition of faces are not allowed) and
O(n^{2})
time to update
L_{i}
.
Hence for a single
\sigma
in Step
i
we need
O(n^{2})
time.
This check is repeated in Step
i
at most
O(n)
times. The number of steps is at most
O(n^{2})
– the total number of facets in
T
– hence the total running time is
O_{d}(n^{5})=O(n^{5})
.
∎
