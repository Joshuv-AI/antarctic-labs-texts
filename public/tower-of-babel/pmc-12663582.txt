Effects of diagenetic stage and burial depth on the microstructure and mechanical properties of coal-bearing sandstones
Authors: Jun Wang, Huigui Li, Tingting Mao, Guosheng Xu, Deguo Su, Kai Liu, Liao Zhang, Zhen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The mechanical behavior of sandstones within coal-bearing strata exerts a critical influence on roof stability during coal mining, while their microstructure and porosity fundamentally determine the physical and mechanical properties. This study investigates the diagenetic and mechanical evolution of six medium-grained sandstones from the Bultai Coal Mine, Shendong Mining Area, spanning the Cretaceous (A) to Jurassic (B–F) periods with varying burial depths. Microstructural analysis reveals that the pore system, comprising intergranular and intragranular pores, cement-internal pores, and microfractures, becomes increasingly cemented with depth. The deeply buried Yan’an Formation (D–F) exhibits markedly superior mechanical properties compared to shallower units: porosity is reduced by 45.9–52.1%, while tensile strength, compressive strength, and elastic modulus are enhanced by 582–1134%, 552–656%, and 13.3–19.7 times, respectively, accompanied by a 60.6–69.2% lower peak strain. These results demonstrate that depositional period and burial depth jointly control microstructural and mechanical evolution. This study establishes a quantitative correlation between porosity, strength, and modulus. The results elucidate the coupled influence of diagenesis and burial depth on sandstone mechanics, thereby providing a more reliable framework for assessing geomechanical risks in the Shendong mining area.

Introduction

Coal remains central to China’s energy landscape, where hard roofs—occurring in about one-third of coal mines—pose significant engineering challenges1. These strata, characterized by high strength, great thickness, and strong self-supporting capacity owing to their quartz-feldspar composition and argillaceous cement, often form large hanging areas that resist timely collapse, which complicates roof control and elevates the risks of rockbursts and equipment failure2,3. Consequently, investigating the mechanical properties of these roof rocks is crucial for enabling precise prediction and effective control, thereby ensuring safer and more efficient mining operations4–7.

As a major lithology within coal-bearing strata, sandstone plays a crucial role in transferring, buffering, and dissipating surrounding rock stresses, thereby controlling roof movement, support response, and the development of overburden fractures8–10. Depositional period and burial depth, as key geological factors governing diagenesis and microstructural evolution, jointly determine the microstructure and mechanical behavior of coal sandstones11–14. Therefore, understanding the coupled evolution of their microstructure and mechanical properties is essential for predicting mining-induced hazards, optimizing support design, and ensuring safe coal extraction15,16.

The depositional period defines the tectonic environment, sediment supply, climate, and depositional dynamics that shape the initial texture and composition of sandstones, thereby controlling grain-size distribution, mineralogy, cementation, and bedding structures17,18. Coal-bearing sandstones formed in different depositional periods exhibit distinct diagenetic potentials and microstructural evolutions19,20. Variations in depositional fabric, cement composition, grain sorting, and bedding type among sandstones of different ages lead to differences in pore geometry, fracture development, and mineral filling under comparable burial conditions, ultimately governing their mechanical behavior21,22.

With increasing burial depth, the in-situ stress and temperature of the surrounding environment intensify, leading to enhanced pore closure, mineral recrystallization, and cementation23–26. Progressive burial drives continuous diagenetic evolution and directly alters the sandstone microstructure. Under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions, mineral transformations such as the kaolinite-to-illite conversion further promote structural densification and microfracture closure27–30. Consequently, these processes fundamentally modify the pore–fracture system and significantly strengthen the overall mechanical properties of the sandstone. While previous studies have often focused on the isolated effects of single factors such as porosity or mineral composition on mechanical properties, this study emphasizes the coupled influence of diagenetic stage and burial depth. It is the interplay of these factors, rather than their individual contributions, that comprehensively governs the microstructural and mechanical evolution of medium-grained sandstones.

The mechanical behavior of rocks is not only influenced by macroscopic structures and external conditions but is fundamentally governed by microstructural features such as porosity, pore type, cementation, mineral composition, and microfracture development24–26. With the application of advanced testing techniques such as CT scanning, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and quantitative X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, the characterization capability of rock microstructure has been significantly enhanced. Li et al.27 clarified the relationship between wave velocity and porosity; Su et al.28 analyzed pore structure and fluid distribution in sandstones; Cheng et al.3 evaluated the fractal dimensions and pore distributions of various rocks using micro-CT; and Yang29 and Wang et al.30 revealed that Jurassic sandstones in the Shendong area exhibit distinct micropore structures, with the Yan’an Formation containing higher clay content than the Zhiluo Formation.

The coal-bearing strata of the Shendong Mining Area are dominated by sedimentary rocks, where thick sandstone layers frequently occur in coal seam roofs. The pore structures of these sandstones, characterized by diverse sizes and distributions, strongly influence their mechanical behavior and, consequently, the movement of overlying strata and the load response of support systems17,18. To clarify these relationships, this study investigates six groups of medium-grained sandstones from different depositional periods within the Shendong area. The microstructures were characterized using FEI-SEM, while tensile strength, uniaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, and peak strain were determined via RMT-150 C mechanical testing. MATLAB-based image processing was further applied to quantify the relationship between apparent porosity and mechanical parameters, providing an integrated understanding of the microstructural and mechanical evolution of coal-bearing sandstones across different depositional periods.

Materials

Sample collection

Rock samples were collected from the Buertai Coal Mine in the Shendong Mining Area, Ordos City, Inner Mongolia. Field sampling photographs are shown in Fig. 1, with more than 400 m of continuous lithological cores obtained. The cores were logged from top to bottom according to lithology and depositional period. Six medium-grained sandstones representing different depositional stages were selected for analysis, and their basic characteristics are listed in Table 1. Specimen preparation followed the Specifications for Testing the Physical and Mechanical Properties of Rocks, with samples processed into standard cylindrical and Brazilian disc specimens for mechanical testing. Microstructural samples were cut into cuboids measuring 10 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm for microscopic observation.

Experimental methodology

Microstructural characterization was performed using an FEI field-emission scanning electron microscope (FEI-SEM), with one representative sample analyzed from each sandstone group. Uniaxial compression and Brazilian splitting tests were conducted on all six sandstone types using an RMT-150 C rock mechanics testing system (Institute of Rock and Soil Mechanics, CAS). The tests were displacement-controlled at a loading rate of 0.005 mm/s, and each test type was repeated three times per sandstone group to ensure data reliability.

Analysis of microstructure characteristics

Microstructure characteristics of sandstones

Microstructural characterization of six medium-grained sandstones with different depositional periods and burial depths from the Buertai Coal Mine (Shendong Mining Area) was performed using FEI-SEM at magnifications of 100×, 200×, and 500×. Representative micrographs are shown in Fig. 2. According to established classifications of microscopic pore types21–23, the pores of these sandstones can be categorized as intergranular pores, intragranular pores, intra-cement pores, and microfractures.

Characteristics of sandstone porosity

In recent years, MATLAB has been increasingly applied for processing SEM images, enabling more accurate and convenient acquisition of rock microstructure information31–33. Its Image Processing Toolbox offers robust capabilities for digital image analysis, featuring numerous built-in functions and supporting the development of custom algorithms34–37. To further enhance the systematic characterization of the sandstone microstructure, SEM images at 100× magnification were additionally processed through binarization, complementing the previously presented 200× images. As shown in Fig. 3, the resulting binary image distinguishes the sandstone matrix (white regions) from pores (black regions) at this scale. Porosity was subsequently calculated for six medium-grained sandstone samples from different depositional periods, using SEM images at 100×, 200×, and 500× magnifications. The porosity characteristics of medium-grained sandstones, derived from image analysis (Figs. 3 and 4), reveal significant differences across stratigraphic units. The Cretaceous Zhidan Group (A) and the Jurassic Anding (B) and Zhiluo (C) Formations exhibit high porosity ranges of 12.39–18.09%, 12.74–18.52%, and 14.96–17.29%, with averages of 14.78%, 15.71%, and 16.67%, respectively. In pronounced contrast, the Jurassic Yan’an Formation (D, E, F) shows markedly lower porosity, with a combined average of 7.99% and individual sample averages ranging from 6.76% to 8.91%. This represents a reduction of 6.79% to 8.68% compared to the shallower units. Furthermore, the porosity standard deviation is substantially larger in the shallower (A, B, C) units, indicating greater microstructural heterogeneity. This suggests that sandstones from greater burial depths (Yan’an Formation) possess not only lower porosity but also more homogeneous pore structures.

The physical properties of the sandstone formations, as characterized by ultrasonic wave velocity and density, are summarized as follows. The shallow units—Cretaceous Zhidan Group (A) and Jurassic Anding (B) and Zhiluo (C) Formations—possess low wave velocities (mean: 480–745 m/s) and densities (mean: 1966–2049 kg/m³). In stark contrast, the deeper Jurassic Yan’an Formation subgroups (D, E, F) show markedly higher values, with wave velocities and densities ranging from 1558 to 2587 m/s and 2222–2381 kg/m³, respectively. This represents an increase of 238% to 425% in wave velocity and 12% to 17% in density, which is attributed to substantially greater compaction at depth. The strong positive correlation between these two parameters confirms this trend, highlighting the uniquely superior and distinct petrophysical behavior of the Yan’an Formation.

Tensile strength

As presented in Table 3; Fig. 5, a pronounced disparity in tensile strength is observed. While the Jurassic Yan’an Formation (D, E, F) demonstrates high strength, with averages from 1.061 to 2.918 MPa, the shallower Cretaceous Zhidan Group (A) and Jurassic Anding (B) and Zhiluo (C) Formations are characterized by significantly lower averages, ranging from 0.158 to 0.286 MPa. This stark contrast underscores the superior mechanical integrity of the more deeply buried Yan’an Formation. The Yan’an Formation sandstones demonstrate not only a markedly higher tensile strength (avg. 1.95 MPa)—over 580% greater than the shallower formations—but also a clear strengthening trend with burial depth. Although a minor reversal occurs at 213.07 m, the overarching trend is evident: strength escalates with depth, achieving a 997% increase at the maximum depth of 431.22 m compared to the reference, underscoring burial depth as a dominant controlling factor.

Compressive strength

Figure 6; Table 3 delineate a profound disparity in the compressive strength of medium-grained sandstones. The Jurassic Yan’an Formation (subgroups D, E, F) exhibits significantly higher strength (27.96–43.68 MPa; average: 34.58 MPa) compared to the shallower Cretaceous Zhidan Group and Jurassic Anding/Zhiluo Formations, which are an order of magnitude weaker (4.16–6.48 MPa; averages: 4.57–5.30 MPa). This represents an increase of 552% to 656%. Furthermore, the data reveal distinct depth-dependent trends. In the shallow formations, both compressive strength and its standard deviation decrease with burial depth, indicating a trend towards weaker and more homogeneous rocks. Within the Yan’an Formation itself, despite some variability in standard deviation, the compressive strength demonstrates an overall decreasing trend with increasing depth when sample dispersion is considered.

Elastic modulus

The relationship between the sedimentary period, burial depth and elastic modulus of the six groups of sandstones is shown in Fig. 7. The results show that the Yan’an Formation exhibits greater stiffness (overall average: 7.44 GPa), which is 13.3 times higher than that of the Zhidan Formation, 16.3 times higher than that of the Anding Formation, and 19.7 times higher than that of the Zhiluo Formation. Within the Yan’an Formation, the modulus of elasticity decreases gradually with the increase of burial depth. The significant contrast in mechanical properties between the strata indicates significant diagenetic differences. The inverse depth-modulus relationship in the Yan’an Subgroup may reflect the compaction or cementation process of burial alteration.

Peak strain

As shown in Fig. 8, the average peak strain values for the medium-grained sandstones of the Cretaceous Zhidan Group, Jurassic Anding, and Jurassic Zhiluo Formations are 16.58 × 10− 3, 19.67 × 10− 3, and 21.19 × 10− 3, respectively, indicating a trend of increasing peak strain with burial depth. However, the Jurassic Yan’an Formation exhibits a dramatically different behavior, with a much lower average peak strain of 6.53 × 10− 3. This value is 60.6% to 69.2% lower than that of the shallower formations.

Despite this shared trend of increasing strain with depth between the groups, the absolute peak strain of the Yan’an Formation is substantially suppressed. Comparative analysis reveals that pre-Yan’an Formation sandstones demonstrate both higher strain magnitudes and greater depth sensitivity. In contrast, the Yan’an Formation sandstones show significantly lower strain values while maintaining the positive depth-strain correlation. This distinct mechanical behavior suggests fundamentally different diagenetic controls dominated the sandstone’s mechanical properties.

As illustrated in Fig. 9, the tensile strength generally exhibits a fluctuating downward trend as porosity increases. The relatively low coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.62) suggests that, consistent with prior analyses, porosity is not the sole factor governing tensile strength. The considerable data scatter can be attributed to the influence of other microstructural factors and diagenetic heterogeneities, such as variations in cementation type and distribution, the presence of microcracks, and the specific mineral composition of the grain framework. These factors may disproportionately affect tensile strength compared to other mechanical properties, as tensile failure is highly sensitive to localized defects and the integrity of grain-to-grain contacts, which are not fully captured by porosity alone. In contrast, both uniaxial compressive strength and elastic modulus demonstrate a more consistent and steady decline with increasing porosity, reflecting a strong negative linear correlation. Unlike these parameters, peak strain shows a distinct positive correlation with porosity, gradually increasing as porosity rises.

Scientific significance

The depositional period and burial depth collectively govern the microstructural and mechanical evolution of sandstones, with the former setting the initial diagenetic framework and the latter driving its subsequent transformation. These factors are intrinsically coupled throughout geological history, jointly defining the final petrophysical state of the rock15,27–29. Sandstones from different depositional origins exhibit distinct pore structures, fracture patterns, and cementation characteristics, reflecting divergent diagenetic starting points and evolutionary trajectories. With increasing burial depth, elevated temperature and pressure conditions promote a series of strengthening mechanisms: mechanical compaction reduces pore space, while chemical processes such as pressure dissolution at grain contacts and quartz/calcite cementation enhance intergranular bonding28,29. The transition from early-stage kaolinite to illite further reduces porosity and increases rigidity. These processes collectively lead to microstructural densification and mechanical strengthening. Thus, in assessing the mechanical behavior of coal-bearing sandstones in the Shendong area, it is essential to integrate both depositional age and burial depth. Such a coupled perspective enables a more mechanistic understanding of diagenetic evolution and offers a theoretical foundation for geomechanical risk prediction in underground coal mining.

Conclusion

A systematic investigation of six medium-grained sandstones in the Shendong mining area has elucidated the coupled controls of depositional period and burial depth on microstructure evolution and mechanical properties. This research establishes quantitative porosity-mechanical relationships, demonstrating how these dual factors synergistically govern microstructural development and mechanical behavior.
