Blackhole Near Horizons through the Looking Glass
Arjun Bagchi, Arkachur Bhattacharya, Sharang Rajesh Iyer, and K. Narayan

Abstract
We show that the near horizon of a generic non-extremal black hole (BH) can be understood in terms of a Carrollian geometry with two null directions, also called a String-Carroll (SC) geometry. The base space of this fibre-bundle structure is a sphere (or a plane for a black brane) and the fibre is the two-dimensional Rindler spacetime. We launch a detailed study of probes in this geometry. We study particle geodesics and scalar fields. The first part of the paper constructs geodesics and probe scalar fields directly in the SC geometry. We then look at a wide class of examples, including the Schwarzschild BH and the Kerr BH in asymptotically flat spacetimes, the BTZ BH and the black brane in AdS spacetimes, as well as Lifshitz black holes and construct the explicit maps to the SC geometry to obtain results specific to each case. These results are reproduced by considering the probe particles and fields in the original BH background and taking the near-horizon limit of the solutions. Our encyclopedia of examples establishes the notion of SC geometries as near-horizon geometries of non-extremal black objects, paving the way for a detailed, intricate future analysis of the quantum aspects of this geometry.

Introduction

The spectacular images of M87 by the Event Horizon Telescope has ushered a new era in black hole physics. Now, even more than ever, there is a need for understanding what happens when one approaches the event horizon of a black hole. This is the question we are interested in addressing in this paper, and we do this through the new lens of Carrollian symmetry.

Our primary goal in this paper is to establish that the following statement:

Near horizon geometry of a generic non-extremal black object (black hole or black brane) is a fibre-bundle String Carroll geometry where the 2d fibre is given by 2d Rindler spacetime and the geometry of the black object dictates the base of the fibre.

The other parameters of the black hole determined the fibration of the bundle. We will establish this by looking at specific examples first and then by considering a general solution called the Plebanski-Demianski class. That we can take this geometry seriously is then established by looking at probes in this background. In particular, we will look at point particles and scalar fields. We will first construct the action of these probes in generic String Carroll geometries. We will then look at particular black holes and map the near horizons to specific SC geometries to extract how these probes should behave in the near horizons of these specific from our general formulation. We will then look at geodesics and scalar fields in the background of these particular black holes and then take the near horizon limit on these probes to show agreement. Not only will we find agreement at the leading order, we will show how the subleading order solutions match very non-trivially.

2
Carroll near the horizon

We have earlier emphasised that Carrollian structures show up on generic null surfaces and hence also on the horizon of generic black holes. In this section, we first collect the geometric data on and near the horizon of non-extremal black holes and we will then see how these fit into the fibre bundle structures related to Carrollian symmetry.

2.1
Structures near the horizon

The near-horizon geometry of a
d
-dimensional black hole, which is a solution of the vacuum field equations, can be expressed in terms of the null-Gaussian coordinates
(v,\rho,x^{A})
. Here,
v
is the advanced time coordinate and the horizon
\mathcal{H}
is situated at
\rho=0
. The angular coordinates are denoted by
x^{A}
. The near-horizon metric in the null-Gaussian coordinates takes the form
56
;
29

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-2\kappa\rho~dv^{2}+2dv~d\rho+2\omega_{A}\rho~dv~dx^{A}+(\Omega_{AB}+\rho\lambda_{AB})~dx^{A}dx^{B}+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})\,,

(3)

where
\kappa
,
\omega_{A}
,
\Omega_{AB}
and
\lambda_{AB}
are metric functions which, in general, can depend on
(v,x^{A})
. The induced metric on
\mathcal{H}
is obtained by setting
\rho=0
in (
3
). It is degenerate and is given as

\displaystyle ds^{2}\Big|_{\mathcal{H}}=0~dv^{2}+0~dv~dx^{A}+\Omega_{AB}~dx^{A}dx^{B}\,.

(4)

Instead of directly putting
\rho=0
, let us set up an expansion scheme. We now let
\rho=\varepsilon\rho_{0}
in (
3
), where
\rho_{0}
is a constant and then take
\varepsilon\to 0
limit. This procedure gets us:

\displaystyle ds^{2}\Big|_{\rho=\varepsilon\rho_{0}}=\Omega_{AB}~dx^{A}dx^{B}+\varepsilon(-2\kappa\rho_{0}dv^{2}+2\omega_{A}\rho_{0}~dv~dx^{A}+\rho_{0}\lambda_{AB}~dx^{A}dx^{B})\,.

(5)

The leading order of the metric expansion (
5
) can be identified as the degenerate metric on
\mathcal{H}
. We label the different parts of the expansion as follows:

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{AB}dx^{A}dx^{B}\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}\tau_{\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

\displaystyle=2\kappa\rho_{0}dv^{2}\,,

(6b)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{A}\rho_{0}~dv~dx^{A}+\rho_{0}\lambda_{AB}~dx^{A}dx^{B}\,.

(6c)

We will see in the next subsection how we can related this to a Carroll expansion.

We now look near and not on the horizon. We will achieve this by dialling
\rho
to zero in lieu of setting it to 0. To accomplish this, we will attach a dimensionless parameter
\epsilon
to the
\rho
coordinate and arrange the metric in orders of
\epsilon
and tend
\epsilon\to 0
to get,

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\Omega_{AB}dx^{A}dx^{B}+\epsilon~(-2\kappa\rho~dv^{2}+2dv~d\rho+2\theta_{A}\rho dvdx^{A}+\rho\lambda_{AB}~dx^{A}dx^{B})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,.

(7)

We now make the following redefinitions which would be very important in what follows. We will rewrite the metric as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=h_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}+\epsilon\left(\tau_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}+\Phi_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,.

(8)

where we have made the following identifications:

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{AB}dx^{A}dx^{B}\,,

(9a)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

\displaystyle=-2\kappa\rho~dv^{2}+2dv~d\rho\,,

(9b)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

\displaystyle=2\theta_{A}\rho dvdx^{A}+\rho\lambda_{AB}~dx^{A}dx^{B}\,.

(9c)

We will now do formal Carrollian expansions for generic metrics.

2.2
Carroll symmetries

Historically, Carrollian symmetries were obtained in the vanishing speed of light
c\to 0
limit of relativistic Poincare symmetries. The limit, in terms of group theory, is an Inonu-Wigner contraction. The generators of the underlying symmetry algebra can be written as

\displaystyle\text{Null ``time'' translation:}\quad H=\partial_{u},

(10a)

\displaystyle\text{Carroll boosts:}\quad C_{i}=x_{i}\partial_{u}

(10b)

\displaystyle\text{Spatial rotations:}\quad J_{ij}=-x_{i}\partial_{j}+x_{j}\partial_{i}

(10c)

\displaystyle\text{Spatial translation:}\quad T_{i}=\partial_{i}.

(10d)

These generators close to form the Carroll algebra:

\displaystyle[J_{ij},J_{kl}]=\delta_{ik}J_{jl}-\delta_{jk}J_{il}+\delta_{jl}J_{ik}-\delta_{il}J_{jk}\,,\quad[T_{i},C_{j}]=\delta_{ij}H\,,

\displaystyle[J_{ij},T_{k}]=\delta_{ki}T_{j}-\delta_{kj}T_{i}\,,\quad[J_{ij},C_{k}]=\delta_{ki}C_{j}-\delta_{kj}C_{i}\,.

(11)

We now remind the reader of the definition of a Carroll manifold. We start with the Minkowski spacetime and send
c\to 0
. As mentioned in the intrduction, the metric and its inverse degenerate in the limit and give us (
2
). It is clear from here that
h_{\mu\nu}\theta^{\nu}=0
. A flat Carroll manifold is thus defined with both
(h_{\mu\nu},\theta^{\nu})
make up the structure. We now define a general Carroll structure based on these ideas.

A
d
-dimensional Carrollian manifold is a smooth manifold with a degenerate metric
h_{\mu\nu}
and a nowhere-vanishing vector field
\theta^{\mu}
that generates the kernel of the metric
39
, i.e.

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}\theta^{\nu}=0.

(12)

For flat Carroll manifolds, the isometries of this doublet

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}h_{\mu\nu}=0,\quad\pounds_{\xi}\theta^{\nu}=0,

(13)

generates the Carroll algebra:

\displaystyle\xi^{i}=\omega^{i}_{\,j}x^{j}+a^{i},\quad\xi^{0}=f(x)+a^{0}.

(14)

Notice that this is generically infinite dimensional for all spacetime dimensions. We recover the finite Carrollian algebra when we restrict the function
f(x)
to be linear in
x
.

A flat
(d+1)
-dimensional Carrollian manifold is a fibre-bundle
\mathbb{R}_{u}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{d}
forms a d-dimensional spatial base and the null direction
\mathbb{R}_{u}
forms “temporal” fibres. On top of the finite symmetry transformations mentioned above in (
10
), when we don’t restrict
f(x)
in (
14
), we will have infinite dimensional “supertranslations” which are translations of the null directions dependent on the spatial coordinates. The generator of these supertranslations is

\displaystyle M(f)=f(x^{i})\partial_{u}

(15)

The supertranslations form an abelian subalgebra:

\displaystyle[M(f),M(g)]=0.

(16)

The geometric structure that we have encountered here is a fibre-bundle with a
d
-dimensional spatial base and one dimensional null “temporal” fibres.

Let us contrast Carrollian manifolds with the perhaps more familiar Galilean set-up. In the non-relativistic world, where the speed of light goes to infinity instead of zero, as mentioned in the introduction, it is the upper version of the metric which survives c.f. (
1
). A
(d+1)
dimensional Galilean or a Newton-Cartan manifold is thus given by
(\mathfrak{h}^{\mu\nu},\tau_{\nu})
subject to

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\mu\nu}\tau_{\nu}=0.

(17)

This is a fibre-bundle with 1d base which is the time direction and
d
-dimensional spatial fibres. The well-known Galilean algebra arises as the isometry algebra of flat Newton-Cartan manifolds. As we can now see, Carrollian spacetimes are the base
\leftrightarrow
fibre flipped version of these more familiar Galilean (or Newton-Cartan) structures. Potential dualities between Galilean and Carrollian theories are currently being explored
38
;
40

2.3
Multiple null directions: Carrollian expansions

A natural geometric question is what happens when we have multiple null directions. The geometry is obvious: for a
(n+m)
dimensional manifold with
n
-nonnull and
m
-null directions, we will have a fibre bundle with a
n
-dimensional base made of the non-null directions and
m
-dimensional fibres made of the null directions. In our application to the theory of black holes, as we have stated in the introduction, we will be interested in two null directions and hence will focus on this. The general formulation for arbitrary null directions is straightforward
22
. We will call the null directions longitudinal and the non-null directions transverse.

We first define string-Carroll manifolds generalising our discussion of Carroll manifolds above. A string-Carroll manifold
\mathcal{M}
is defined as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M},h_{\mu\nu},v^{\mu\nu})\,,\quad h_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu\rho}=0\,.

(18)

Here,
h_{\mu\nu}
is a symmetric, degenerate tensor that defines the metric on the transverse space, while
v^{\mu\nu}
is a symmetric, degenerate tensor that defines the metric on the longitudinal space. In the above expansion,
h_{\mu\nu}
has the signature
(0,0,+,\cdots,+)
, while the signature of
\tau_{\mu\nu}
and
v^{\mu\nu}
is
(-,+,0\cdots,0)
.
The generalization to arbitrary number of null directions is obvious.

We will now briefly describe the string-Carroll formalism to methodically expand a d+2-dimensional Lorentzian metric. Some other details of the transformations are collected in Appendix
A
and the generalisations thereof can be found in
8
. Any Lorentzian metric can be decomposed in terms of the vielbeine as follows

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}^{A^{\prime}}_{\mu}\mathcal{E}^{B^{\prime}}_{\nu}\,,\quad g^{\mu\nu}=\eta^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{A^{\prime}}^{\mu}\mathcal{E}_{B^{\prime}}^{\nu}\,,

(19)

where
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}\in\{0,\cdots,d+1\}
are the tangent space indices of the spacetime manifold and
\mu,\nu\in\{0,\cdots,d+1\}
are the spacetime indices. The vielbeine,
\mathcal{E}^{A^{\prime}}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{E}_{A^{\prime}}^{\mu}
can be split into longitudinal and transverse sectors in the following way

\mathcal{E}^{A^{\prime}}_{\mu}=\left(c\,T_{\mu}^{A},E_{\mu}^{\bar{A}}\right)\,,\quad\mathcal{E}_{A^{\prime}}^{\mu}=\left(c^{-1}V^{\mu}_{A},E^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}\right)\,,

(20)

where
A\in\{0,1\}
are the tangent space indices of the longitudinal submanifold and
\bar{A}\in\{2,\cdots,d+1\}
are the tangent space indices of the transverse submanifold. The longitudinal submanifold admits a tangent space metric
\eta_{AB}
while the transverse submanifold admits a tangent space metric
\delta_{\bar{A}\bar{B}}
. The decomposed vielbeine satisfy the following relations

\displaystyle V^{\mu}_{A}E_{\mu}^{\bar{A}}=T_{\mu}^{A}E^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}=0\,,~~T^{A}_{\mu}V^{\mu}_{B}=\delta^{A}_{B}\,,~~E^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}E^{\bar{B}}_{\mu}=\delta^{\bar{B}}_{\bar{A}}\,,~~\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}=T^{A}_{\nu}V^{\mu}_{A}+E^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}E^{\bar{A}}_{\nu}\,.

(21)

The Lorentzian metric in terms of the decomposed vielbeine takes the form

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=c^{2}\,\eta_{AB}T^{A}_{\mu}T^{B}_{\nu}+\delta_{\bar{A}\bar{B}}E^{\bar{A}}_{\mu}E^{\bar{B}}_{\nu}\,,\quad g^{\mu\nu}=c^{-2}\,\eta^{AB}V_{A}^{\mu}V_{B}^{\nu}+\delta^{\bar{A}\bar{B}}E_{\bar{A}}^{\mu}E_{\bar{B}}^{\nu}\,.

(22)

The expansion of the Lorentzian metric is realised when the vielbeine are expanded in powers of
c^{2}
as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &V^{\mu}_{A}=v^{\mu}_{A}+c^{2}m^{\mu}_{A}+\mathcal{O}(c^{4})\,,\quad T_{\mu}^{A}=\tau_{\mu}^{A}+c^{2}m_{\mu}^{A}+\mathcal{O}(c^{4})\\
&E^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}=e^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}+c^{2}\pi^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}+\mathcal{O}(c^{4})\,,\quad E_{\mu}^{\bar{A}}=e_{\mu}^{\bar{A}}+c^{2}\pi_{\mu}^{\bar{A}}+\mathcal{O}(c^{4})\,.\end{aligned}

(23)

The metric expansion in terms of the expanded vielbeine, takes the form

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}\tau_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}\Phi_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{4})\,,~~~g^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}v^{\mu\nu}+\bar{h}^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{2})\,.

(24)

Here, the various terms occurring in the string-Carroll expansion are

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\bar{A}\bar{B}}e^{\bar{A}}_{\mu}e^{\bar{B}}_{\nu}\,,~~\tau_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{AB}\tau_{\mu}^{A}\tau_{\nu}^{B}\,,~~\Phi_{\mu\nu}=2\delta_{\bar{A}\bar{B}}e^{\bar{A}}_{(\mu}\,\pi^{\bar{B}}_{\nu)}

(25a)

\displaystyle v^{\mu\nu}=\eta^{AB}v^{\mu}_{A}v^{\nu}_{B}\,,~~\bar{h}^{\mu\nu}=\delta^{\bar{A}\bar{B}}e^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}e^{\nu}_{\bar{B}}+2\eta^{AB}v^{(\mu}_{A}m^{\nu)}_{B}\,.

\displaystyle v^{\mu}_{A}e_{\mu}^{\bar{A}}=\tau_{\mu}^{A}e^{\mu}_{\bar{A}}=0\,,\quad\tau_{\mu}^{A}v^{\mu}_{B}=\delta^{A}_{B}\,.

Probes in String-Carroll

In the previous section, the near-horizon geometry of a black hole was shown to be string-Carroll. Now we are interested in studying the effects of the string Carroll geometry, when various probes are analysed on the string-Carroll background. These probes serve as useful tools for extracting features of theories near black hole backgrounds. We will study point-particle geodesics and scalar fields on the string-Carroll background. \displaystyle S_{NLO}

Leading Order:
The following geodesic equation is obtained from (

So, in our LO analysis, we notice the emergence of an equivalent form of the geodesic equation.

Sub-leading Order:
In the NLO action (

x^{\mu}(\tau)
and
y^{\mu}(\tau)

Varying
x^{\mu}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mu\rho}(x,y)\ddot{x}^{\rho}+2h_{\mu\rho,\nu}(x)\dot{x}^{(\nu}\dot{y}^{\rho)}+

\displaystyle h_{\mu\rho}(x)\ddot{y}^{\rho}+\partial_{\nu}^{(x)}\Lambda_{\mu\rho}(x,y)\dot{x}^{\nu}\dot{x}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=h_{\nu\rho,\mu}(x)\dot{x}^{(\nu}\dot{y}^{\rho)}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}^{(x)}\Lambda_{\nu\rho}(x,y)\dot{x}^{\nu}\dot{x}^{\rho}\,,

\Gamma^{(\Lambda)}_{\mu\nu\rho}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Lambda_{\rho\mu,\nu}(x,y)+\Lambda_{\mu\nu,\rho}(x,y)-\Lambda_{\nu\rho,\mu}(x,y)\right)

We thus derived the geodesic equations from the quadratic action for a point particle in a string Carroll expanded metric.

Checking our formulation: The algorithm

Our explorations have so far focused on generalising Carrollian structures to more general null structures to multiple null directions. We showed in
section

2
that Carroll expansions with two null directions were directly related to the near horizon of generic non-extremal black holes. We have then focussed on understanding particle trajectories and scalar fields in these so-called string Carroll geometries.

We now move on from this abstract formulation to definite examples to show the validity of our analysis. In the main body of our paper we will address the following cases:

1.

Schwarzschild black holes

2.

Rotating BTZ black holes

3.

AdS black branes

4.

Lifshitz black holes

The
appendix

D
give details of some other black holes, including the very general Plebanski-Demianski class of black holes.

For each example in the coming few sections (Sec
5
–
8
), we will discuss the explicit map of the near horizon geometry to the String Carroll date. We will then consider point-particle geodesics and free scalar field theory in the near-horizon background.

•

The map from NH to SC: For each case, we will specify the String Carroll data
\{h_{\mu\nu},\Phi_{\mu\nu},\tau_{\mu\nu},v^{\mu\nu},{\bar{h}}^{\mu\nu}\}
. This will construct the explicit map between a particular black hole or black brane and a particular SC geometries.

•

Geodesic – Intrinsic SC analysis: We will start with the point particle case and derive the leading and sub-leading order geodesics from the leading and subleading order point particle string-Carroll actions respectively. We will call this the intrinsic string-Carroll analysis.

•

Geodesic – Limiting analysis: To check for consistency, we will take a near-horizon limit on the point-particle geodesics of the complete spacetime, which we will call the limiting analysis.

•

The matching – non-triviality at NLO: We will show that the leading order geodesic from the limiting and intrinsic analysis match exactly. But to match the sub-leading order geodesics, we need to impose the leading order solutions.

•

Field theory: We will follow the same procedure for the free scalar field theory. Again the leading order matching is straightforward. At NLO, while checking for consistency, instead of imposing the leading-order solution, we will show that the sub-leading equations of motion match up only by adding the leading-order equation of motion to the limiting analysis.

We now dive headlong into the examples.

5

Schwarzschild Black hole

We begin with the simplest example, our favourite Schwarzschild black hole in four dimensional asymptotically flat spacetimes. The metric in the Schwarzschild coordinates is given as

ds^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{r_{h}}{r}\right)dt^{2}+\left(1-\frac{r_{h}}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,

5.1

Explicit map

The near-horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole can be reached via a change of the radial coordinate

6

Rotating BTZ Black hole

We now move on beyond asymptotically flat spacetimes. To consider a very different example, let us take the Banados-Tieltelboim-Zaneli (BTZ) black holes in three dimensional Anti de Sitter (AdS
3
) spacetimes. Gravity in three dimensions is of course known to have no propagating degrees of freedom and indeed the presence of non-trivial black hole solutions was a big surprise when these were initially discovered. It was soon understood that these black holes were orbifolds of AdS
3
. We will see below that our formulation of string Carroll geometries encompasses the near horizon of such black holes as well. For making the analysis even more non-trivial, we will consider rotating BTZ black holes.

6.1

Explicit map

As before, we begin with the metric. The metric of a 2+1 dimensional rotating BTZ black hole with mass
M>0
and angular momentum
J\neq 0
in the Boyer-Lindquist-like coordinate system is given as

ds^{2}=-N^{2}(r)\,dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{N^{2}(r)}+r^{2}\left(N^{\phi}(r)dt+d\phi\right)^{2}\,,

(78)

where
N^{2}(r)
and
N^{\phi}(r)
are the lapse and the angular drag functions, respectively, and are given as follows

N^{2}(r)=-M+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}+\frac{J^{2}}{4r^{2}}\,,\quad N^{\phi}(r)=-\frac{J}{2r^{2}}\,.

(79)

The rotating BTZ black hole has two event horizons at
r_{\pm}
.

Throughout the following analysis, we will work with a non-extremal rotating BTZ black hole. In order to write the near-horizon expansion of this metric in a diagonal coordinate system up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, we do the following coordinate transformations

t\to T=\left(1+\frac{J^{2}\alpha_{+}}{2r_{+}^{3}}\right)t-\left(\frac{J\alpha_{+}}{r_{+}}\right)\phi,~~~\phi\to\varphi=\phi-\frac{J}{2r_{+}^{2}}t,

(80)

where

\alpha_{+}=\left(\frac{2r_{+}}{\ell^{2}}-\frac{J^{2}}{2r_{+}^{3}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\ell^{2}r_{+}}{2\left(r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}\right)}\,.

(81)

In this coordinate system, the metric diagonalises as follows

ds^{2}=-\frac{\left(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}\right)\left(r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}\right)}{\ell^{2}r_{+}^{2}}dT^{2}+\frac{\ell^{2}r^{2}}{\left(r^{2}-r_{-}^{2}\right)\left(r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}\right)}dr^{2}+\frac{r_{+}^{2}\left(r^{2}-r_{-}^{2}\right)}{r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}}d\varphi^{2}\,.

(82)

A few remarks are in order regarding the new coordinate system
53
. Firstly, note that we are transitioning to a co-rotating frame, which rotates along with the outer horizon. The new coordinates do not suffer from any causal pathologies outside the outer horizon. In the usual interpretation of the AdS space as a cylinder, the time coordinate chalks out a helix. We can diagonalise the rotating BTZ black hole metric as a consequence of the fact that the spacetime is locally AdS. Next, we do the near-horizon probe

r=r_{+}+\left(\frac{\epsilon}{4\alpha_{+}}\right)\rho^{2}\,.

(83)

The resulting metric becomes

ds^{2}=r_{+}^{2}d\varphi^{2}+\epsilon\left(-\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{4\alpha_{+}^{2}}\right)dT^{2}+d\rho^{2}+\left(\frac{r_{+}^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\right)\rho^{2}d\varphi^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,.

(84)

The near-horizon metric can be mapped to the String Carroll metric (
24
) by identifying
c^{2}=\epsilon
as follows

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=r_{+}^{2}d\varphi^{2}\,,~~\Phi_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=\frac{r_{+}^{2}\rho^{2}}{\ell^{2}}d\varphi^{2},~~\tau_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4\alpha_{+}^{2}}dT^{2}+d\rho^{2}\,.

(85)

The inverse near-horizon metric can be mapped likewise

\displaystyle v^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}=-\frac{4\alpha_{+}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{T}^{2}+\partial_{\rho}^{2}\,,~~~\bar{h}^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}=\frac{1}{r_{+}^{2}}\partial_{\varphi}^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{+}}{2r_{+}}\partial_{T}^{2}+\frac{\rho^{2}(3r_{-}^{2}+r_{+}^{2})}{4\ell^{2}r_{+}^{2}}\partial_{\rho}^{2}\,.

(86)

In the previous sections, we have first laid the foundation of formulating the NH region of non-extremal black holes (and black branes) in terms of a fibre-bundled geometry with two-dimensional null fibres, which we have formalised as a string-Carroll geometry. We have then checked the validity of our formulation by considering various explicit examples of BHs and their NH geometries and probes on these geometries.

A natural question is how one can reach the horizon of the BH from the NH region. We formulate the answer to this important question in this section.

Universality of the NH expansion

In the near-horizon expansion, the metric

arranges in orders of
\epsilon

The leading order (

201

S^{1}

The subleading order (
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
) metric in the string-Carroll expansion is always Lorentzian and is composed of two parts, that is, the longitudinal Rindler and the transverse spatial metric.

A point to note is that in the near horizon metric, the Rindler, unlike the usual lore, does not come at the leading order. In every case, the acceleration parameter of the Rindler geometry determines the thermodynamic temperature associated with the black hole. For example, consider the Schwarzschild black hole, the Rindler geometry at the sub-leading order is given as

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{R}=-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4r_{h}^{2}}dt^{2}+d\rho^{2}=-a^{2}\rho^{2}dt^{2}+d\rho^{2}\,,

The transverse spatial metric depends on the topology of the black object. In case of static asymptotically flat black holes, the spatial metric always takes the form
\Phi\sim\rho^{2}d\Omega^{2}
, whereas, in case of asymptotically AdS or dS static spacetime, the subleading transverse metric also depends on AdS or dS radius
\ell

In the case of non-static black holes, the longitudinal Rindler gets fibred over the transverse manifold. Therefore, a two-dimensional Rindler spacetime is obtained on every point of the transverse manifold. While the metric
g_{\mu\nu}

r_{h}

The leading order-geodesic equations are already present in the sub-leading order. Therefore, to infer the sub-leading order dynamics of the point-particle, the leading order dynamics are automatically imposed. The sub-leading order geodesic equations in every case, contain an essential two-dimensional Rindler, whose horizon serves as the horizon of the black object. Asymptotic observers do not observe the point particle entering the horizon. Rather, for an asymptotic observer, the point particle gets stuck on the horizon.

Scalar fields: What is universal and what is not?

Unlike the leading-order string-Carroll geodesic equations which become spatial, the dynamics of the leading order string-Carroll scalar fields in all the cases is governed by the Rindler. A massive scalar field to begin with becomes massless at the leading-order. This is an indication that the leading order scalar fields propagate along the null directions. To ascertain this, one can look at the orthogonality condition

Conclusions: Towards the horizon with Carroll

11.1

Summary

Black holes are the theoretical laboratory for high energy physicists. In this paper, we have revisited an age-old problem, that of understanding what happens when one nears the horizon of generic black holes. This question has been addressed very carefully for the case of extremal black holes where an exponentially long throat appears and the dynamics of the near horizon decouples from the system. Studies of symmetries and indeed the initial construction of holography in terms of the AdS/CFT correspondence was based on this realisation. A similar de-coupling mechanism is absent for non-extremal black holes which are also the realistic black holes we now observe in the sky. An important question is whether we can find a symmetry based approach to the near horizon of such realistic black holes.

We have answered this question in the affirmative in this paper, and have laid the foundation for a symmetry based formulation for the near horizon of generic non-extremal black holes and black branes. Our main claim is that the near horizon becomes a string Carroll geometry, – a fibre bundle with a spatial base and 2d Rindler as null fibres. The spacetime is doubly degenerate, with a zero appearing in front of the 2d Rindler. This is a generalization of the usual Carrollian structure which had only one null direction, but the ideas and methods applied to Carrollian physics, now used widely in theoretical physics, generalize in a straightforward manner.

We used these intuitions and earlier lessons learnt from Carrollian symmetries to study carefully the NH region of non-extremal black holes. The first part of the paper dealt with providing a general symmetry based analysis of the structure of the near horizon and probe point particles and scalar fields in the string Carroll geometry. Following this, we looked at an almost exhaustive collection of examples where the same results were reproduced in independent ways providing thorough cross-checks of our general symmetry based analyses, firmly establishing the central role doubly null string Carroll geometries would play in black hole physics going forward. We then formulated how to go from the near horizon to the horizon of the black hole geometries in terms of Carrollian expansions and finally summed up the general lessons we have learnt about our analyses in the previous section.

11.2

Comments and future directions

We now end with a list of immediate questions and projects that we would like to address.

•

Spinning fields near BHs: In our analysis of classical fields near BHs, we have only considered scalar fields so far. The natural generalisation is to include fields with spin e.g. fermions, and gauge fields. Carroll fermions come hand in hand with modified Clifford algebras and a lot of interesting structure
4
. The modifications of these structures for string Carroll geometries would perhaps be even more intriguing. When considering gauge fields, the natural setting would be to build this formulation for charged black holes. We will consider these generalisations in upcoming work.

•

Extremal BHs and Carroll: As has been stressed, the NH regions of extremal BHs have been studied in a lot of detail in the existing literature and of late there also has been a lot of work in trying to understand near extremal BHs and their NH symmetries. One of the immediate questions for our framework is figuring out how the structures we have encountered here, viz. the string-Carroll geometry, naturally evolves to a Lorentzian geometry on take an extremal limit.

•

Quantum fields near BHs: Perhaps the most important question in the context of black hole physics is the understanding of the quantization of fields near BHs, now armed with these new structures. We would like to revisit the famous works of Unruh and Hawking, now with the insight of string-Carroll structures. Just as Carrollian quantum field theories are very different from quantum mechanics, even though there may be superficial structural similarities, it is expected that 4d QFTs in a String-Carroll background would have many features very different from a relativistic QFT in 2d Rindler spacetime while retaining some similarities. We will begin our investigations for scalar field theories before generalising to QFTs with spinning fields.

•

Isometries of NH: As we emphasised at the beginning of the paper, Carrollian manifolds come equipped with an infinite dimensional isometry algebra because of the null structure. It is expected that such infinite dimensional isometries are generic features of all higher Carroll structures and in particular would also show up in string Carroll manifolds and hence BH NH geometries.

•

Asymptotic symmetries and connections with Kerr/CFT: For near horizon extreme Kerr (NHEK), the asymptotic symmetries were investigated and a dual description of the extreme Kerr BH was proposed in terms of a 2d relativistic CFT, where a Cardy counting of the states reproduced the entropy of the Kerr BH. This holographic correspondence is known as the Kerr/CFT correspondence
44
;
27
;
31
. In an attempt to build a similar holographic correspondence, now for a non-extremal BH, we will investigate the asymptotic symmetries of the NH region. This would be the asymptotic symmetries of a string Carroll geometry which itself would have infinite dimensional isometries. The structures we encounter and the asymptotic analysis itself would be very rich and rewarding.

•

Hyperscaling violating Lifshitz singularities: As we have seen, nonextremal black holes with Lifshitz/hvLif asymptotics continue to exhibit String Carroll expansions, similar to other cases: this is consistent with other physics aspects that are known to be reasonable at finite temperature
70
;
46
.
A fascinating question pertains to the deep IR singularities that arise in
these theories at zero temperature. In the recasting (
139
),
we see that the transverse space scales nontrivially towards the horizon for
z>1
: this might suggest novel structures beyong the finite temperature Carroll expansion we have seen. It would be very interesting to understand this zero temperature limit here and possible degenerate Carroll-like structures which underly Lifshitz singularities. Note that this is likely to be somewhat different from the zero temperature limit for
charged hvLif theories which give rise to
AdS_{2}
factors and JT gravity
52
.

We hope to report on some of these intriguing points in the near future.

Appendix E

Determinant of the string- Carroll Metric

In this section, we will discuss the expansion of the determinant of the string-Carroll metric. This section has been worked out in collaboration with Pushkar Soni. The determinant of a
d\times d
metric is given as

\displaystyle g=\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{d}}g_{1\mu_{1}}\cdots g_{d\mu_{d}},

(209)

where
\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{d}}
is a Levi-Civita symbol in d-dimensions. Substituting the metric in terms of the decomposed vielbeine (
22
), we get

\displaystyle g=\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{d}}(c^{2}T_{1\mu_{1}}+\Pi^{\perp}_{1\mu_{1}})\cdots(c^{2}T_{d\mu_{d}}+\Pi^{\perp}_{d\mu_{d}})\,,

(210)

such that
T_{\mu\nu}
and
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
are doubly degenerate. Arranging the metric components in orders of
c
and considering four dimensional spacetime we get,

g=\underbrace{\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\Pi^{\perp}_{i\mu_{i}}}_{\det(\Pi^{\perp})}+c^{2}\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4}}\sum_{n=1}^{4}T_{n\mu_{n}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq n\end{subarray}}^{4}\Pi^{\perp}_{i\mu_{i}}\\
+c^{4}\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4}}\sum_{n=1}^{4}\sum_{m>n}^{4}T_{n\mu_{n}}T_{m\mu_{m}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{4}\Pi^{\perp}_{i\mu_{i}}+\mathcal{O}(c^{6}).

(211)

The first term is 0 because it is the determinant of a degenerate metric. The terms of
\mathcal{O}(c^{2})
,
\mathcal{O}(c^{6})
and above are also zero because these terms contain an unequal number of
T_{\mu\nu}
’s and
\Pi^{\perp}_{\mu\nu}
’s. Therefore, we are left with only the
\mathcal{O}(c^{4})
term. Expanding
\Pi^{\perp}_{\mu\nu}
as
\Pi^{\perp}_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}\Phi_{\mu\nu}
and
c^{2}T_{\mu\nu}=c^{2}\tau_{\mu\nu}+c^{4}m_{\mu\nu}
in the
\mathcal{O}(c^{4})
term we get,

g=c^{4}\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4}}\sum_{n=1}^{4}\sum_{m>n}^{4}(\tau_{n\mu_{n}}+c^{2}m_{n\mu_{n}})(\tau_{m\mu_{m}}+c^{2}m_{m\mu_{m}})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{4}(h_{i\mu_{i}}+c^{2}\Phi_{i\mu_{i}}).

(212)

Rearranging this expansion, we get

g=c^{4}\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4}}\sum_{n=1}^{4}\sum_{m>n}^{4}\tau_{n\mu_{n}}\tau_{m\mu_{m}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{4}h_{i\mu_{i}}\\
+c^{6}\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4}}\sum_{n=1}^{4}\sum_{m>n}^{4}(\tau_{n\mu_{n}}m_{m\mu_{m}}+\tau_{m\mu_{m}}m_{n\mu_{n}})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{4}h_{i\mu_{i}}\\

i\neq n\neq m\end{subarray}}\tau_{i\mu_{i}}.

(213)

The
\mathcal{O}(c^{4})

\mathcal{O}(c^{6})

\mathbb{1}+\epsilon A
