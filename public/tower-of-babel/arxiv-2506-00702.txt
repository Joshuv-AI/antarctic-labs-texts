Abstract
Although it is relatively easy to apply, the gradient method often displays a disappointingly slow rate of convergence. Its convergence is specially based on the structure of the matrix of the algebraic linear system, and on the choice of the stepsize defining the new iteration. We propose here a simple and robust stabilization of the gradient method, which no longer assumes a structure on the matrix (neither symmetric, nor positive definite) to converge, and which no longer requires an approach on the choice of the stepsize. We establish the global convergence of the proposed stabilized algorithm under the only assumption of nonsingular matrix. Several numerical examples illustrating its performances are presented, where we have tested small and large scale linear systems, with and not structured matrices, and with well and ill conditioned matrices.



1
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the solving of the linear system

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$b$},

(1)

by means of a stabilized gradient iterative method, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a
n\times n
matrix (which is ill-conditioned or not) and
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a given
n
-vector. The only requirement on this matrix is that it is nonsingular (whatever the structure of the eigenvalues), the exact solution being thus given as follows
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Throughout this work,
\|\cdot\|_{n}
denotes the euclidean vector norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{n}
its associated scalar product, and
\|\cdot\|
is the induced matrix norm to
\|\cdot\|_{n}
.

The linear system (
1
) is the standard formulation of many problems coming from different fields in science and engineering whose economy, chemistry, physics, models of partial differential equations, ect. The solving of this linear system is still challenging, and also represents one of the most time consuming part of the numerical simulation process. We may distinguish two main methods for its solving: Direct and iterative methods. The former methods, which are widely used in many industrial codes, are based on the factorization of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
into easily invertible matrices and require a predictable amount of ressources in time and storage
[
2
,
9
,
37
]
. Unfortunately, these methods are inefficient when the problem size becomes large since the factorization becomes impractical due to lack of time or storage capacity. The use of the latter methods, the
iterative methods
, becomes thus mandatory. Iterative methods require fewer storage, and often, fewer operations than direct methods during the solving process
[
2
,
9
,
37
,
25
,
41
]
. However, these methods may fail to converge in a reasonable amount of time, and sometimes may not have the same reliability than direct methods.

The gradient method which is traced back to A. L. Cauchy
[
10
]
, is one of the most straightforward iterative method to build an approximation solution of the linear system (
1
). A simple way to establish this iterative method is to reformulate equation (
1
) under the linear fixed-point equation
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\alpha(\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$})
, from which the following iterative scheme is built

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}-\alpha_{k}(\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}),\;\;k=0,1,2,\cdots,

(2)

where
\alpha_{k}
is a parameter (a stepsize) to be defined. The gradient method (
2
) can be written under the iterative form (see
[
2
]
- Definition
9.1.1
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha_{k}}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}=\mathbf{N}_{\alpha_{k}}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}+\mbox{\boldmath$b$},

(3)

where the regular decomposition of the matrix
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{N}_{\alpha}
, with
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{N}_{\alpha}=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}\right)
, is made. In the case where the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is symmetric, the gradient method is based on the natural idea of choosing the direction of the greatest rate of change of the quadratic functional

\displaystyle J(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{1}{2}\left<\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\right>_{n}-\left<\mbox{\boldmath$b$},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\right>_{n},

namely, the direction opposite to the gradient
\nabla J(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}
. A process of finding the appropriate stepsize
\alpha_{k}
is also needed. Many iterative algorithms, typically called
line search algorithms
, have been proposed for the choice of this parameter. The most known are the
constant stepsize
, the
exact line search stepsize
and the
backtracking stepsize
(see
[
7
]
- Chap. 4). The main advantage of the constant stepsize strategy is of course its simplicity, but its drawback is how to fixe its value since a large stepsize can cause a non converging gradient method, while a small stepsize can slow the convergence.

As for the exact line search stepsize, even in the simplest case where the marix
\mathbf{A}
is symmetric and positive definite, the gradient method may not be very efficient
[
1
]
. With the exact line search, the gradient method converges linearly and behaves increasingly badly when the condition number of the matrix deteriorates. Attempts to increase the performance of this method have also been investigated
[
19
,
31
,
40
]
. The exact line search method is mainly applied in the special cases of quadratic problems, where the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
is analytically computed. Otherwise, its approximation is used, generating the so called
inexact line search
procedures widely investigated in the litterature
[
4
,
21
,
42
,
35
,
13
,
18
,
36
,
32
,
33
]
. The
backtracking stepsize
is a version of this approximation.

Later on, Barzilai and Borwein
[
5
]
proposed a surprising superlinear convergence result of the steepest descent method, based on a different strategy for choosing the step lenght. For the simplest case of symmetric and positive definite matrix
\mathbf{A}
with
n=2
, Barzilai and Borwein show that the gradient method (
2
), with the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
defined from informations of the previous iteration, converges superlinearly. Their works have also been the origin of many others researches on the steepest descent method. Raydan
[
38
,
39
]
established convergence of the Barzilai and Borwein’s approach in the quadratic case by providing numerical results, whereas Fletcher
[
17
]
analysed its links with the spectrum of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
. The possibility to apply the Barzilai and Borwein’s approach to constrained optimization problems was considered by Friedlander et al.
[
20
]
(in the case of quadratic functionals), and by Birgin et al.
[
8
]
. Others more rescent investigations on the Barzilai and Borwein’s approach have also been made
[
11
,
12
]
, and some explained why it is competitive to the conjugate gradient based method
[
16
]
. More recently, under a quadratic functional, Yuan
[
43
]
proposed a new computational process of the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
, enabling a fast convergence of the steepest descent method. Moreover, its approach also possesses the monotoniticity property that permits a generalization to non-linear functions.

Despite these performances on the gradient method, it is supplanted by the use of the conjugate gradient method or its variants. In the solving of the linear system (
1
), the conjugate gradient method
[
30
]
and some of its preconditionings
[
22
]
remain still the methods of choices. The main feature of these methods is that, they are able to deliver a reasonably accurate approximation solution in few iterations, specially when the matrix has certain eigenvalue structure.

We propose a simple and robust stabilization of the gradient method (
2
), that may be seen as a preconditioned iterative method, defined as follows

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}+\alpha_{k}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$},\;\forall\;k=0,1,2,\cdots,

where
\gamma>0
is a stabilization parameter which can be chosen as large as possible, and independently of the linear system data and the stepsize parameter
\alpha_{k}
. This stabilized iterative method is obtained from (
2
), through the idea that we need to incorporate terms that gradually control the residual of the iterations. One of the nice properties of this iterative scheme is that we no longer need to assume that the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is positive definite, nor that it is symmetric, but only that it is nonsingular in order to the linear system (
1
) to have a unique solution. We also show that the choice of the stepsize parameter
\alpha_{k}
is no longer necessary, since this stabilized method converges whatever
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
(positive or negative) and through the established error estimate

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n}\leq\left(\frac{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{n}}\right)^{k}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\|\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{i}\mathbf{A}\right\|\right)\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[0]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star},

where
\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)=\|\mathbf{V}\|\|\mathbf{V}^{-1}\|
is the condition number of the orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
coming from the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{t}
, and
\sigma_{n}
is the smallest singular value of
\mathbf{A}
. Whereas, taking a constant stepsize
\alpha_{i}=\alpha
this estimate simplifies to

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n}\leq\left(\left(\frac{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{n}}\right)\left\|\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{A}\right\|\right)^{k}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[0]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}.

This work is organized as follows: we introduce in section
2
, a stabilized gradient method built from the gradient method. We then present in section
3
, the convergence analysis of the stabilized method and propose a pseudo-algorithm that underlines the differents steps of its solving. Several numerical examples are presented in section
4
to illustrate the theoretical results. We begin by analysing well-conditioned problems (subsections
4.1
-
4.2
), specially examples in the litterature whose the convergence is clearly proved to be slow or impossible. We specially investigated the cases of rank-deficient and severely ill-conditioned matrix through both small and large scale problems (subsection
4.3
). Through these examples, we also investigate the accuracy and stability of this approach with respect to the stepsize parameter
\alpha_{k}
and to the stabilization parameter
\gamma
. We also note that the stabilized method may behave as a direct method, when the stabilization parameter
\gamma
is chosen greater than the desired accuracy
\varepsilon
(namely
\gamma>\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
).

2
Stabilization of the Gradient Method

2.1
The Stabilized Gradient Method

When using the gradient method in the solving of the linear system (
1
), it is important to properly choose the stepsize parameter
\alpha_{k}
. Although clear recommandations have been made on this choice (see
[
2
]
- Chap.
9
- Theorem
9.1.1
,
[
41
]
- Chap.
1
- Theorem
4.1
), we may also face in some applications non convergence of this iterative method. The aim of this work is, from the formulation (
2
), to define a stable iterative method that not only solves convergence difficulties but also behaves much like a direct method since we may be able to control its iterations. This method will be a very effective technique for the solving of differential and integral equations.

The iterative method (
2
) can also be written under the following minimization problem: Given
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}
, compute
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from the following minimization problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}\right):=\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{F}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),

(4)

where the functional
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
is defined under the following form

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}\right\|^{2}_{n}+\alpha_{k}\left<\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$},\,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}\right>_{n},

(5)

The approach we propose here is formulated from the problem (
4
), with the additional worry to obtain stability. For that, we add the equation (
1
) as a constraint of the minimization problem (
4
) by considering the following affine constrained problem: Given
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}
, compute
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}\in\mbox{\boldmath$K$}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}\right):=\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}\mathcal{F}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),

(6)

where the constraint space is defined as
\mbox{\boldmath$K$}=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\right\}
. Instead of working in this constrained set, we relaxe the constraint by applying the regularization method to the minimization problem (
6
). We thus interest to the regularized problem: Given
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
, compute
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}):=\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),\;\forall\;\gamma>0,

(7)

where the regularized functional
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\cdot)
is defined here as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}):=\mathcal{F}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\right\|^{2}_{n},\;\forall\;\gamma>0,

(8)

and where
\gamma
is a regularisation parameter which controls the weight given to the residual
\left\|\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\right\|^{2}_{n}
, relative to the minimisation of the functional
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
. Taking the first order necessary optimality condition of the problem (
7
) by differentiating the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\cdot)
with respect to
x
and setting it equals to zero at
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}
, we obtain the iterative method: Given
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
, compute
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}+\alpha_{k}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\;\forall\;k=0,1,2,\cdots.

(9)

Comparing the gradient method (
2
) to the stabilized gradient method (
9
), we may see that the latter is obtained from the former by simply adding the terms
\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}
and
\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}
in the left and right hand sides, respectively. These added terms play a stabilization role to the gradient method by controlling the residual, as the iterations go along.

Remark 1
.

1.

The only difference between the gradient method (
2
) and the stabilized iterative method (
9
) is the residual that is taken account as a stabilizer of the iterative problem. This residual plays the role of a stabilizer since its goal is not to ensure the uniqueness of solution, but to bring stability to the whole system by permitting the convergence of the method in few iterations.

2.

What we mean from the formulation (
9
) is, when solving the linear system (
1
) by a gradient iterative method, we also need to control its residual for stability purpose. This thus defines a preconditioned gradient method under the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{N}_{k}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}+\mbox{\boldmath$c$}_{(\gamma,k)},\;\forall\;\gamma>0,\;\forall\;k=0,1,2,\cdots,

(10)

where the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}=\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)
is symmetric and still nonsingular for any
\gamma>0
whatever the properties of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
(even if
\mathbf{A}
is not symmetric), the matrix
\mathbf{N}_{k}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{A}\right)
, and the vector
\mbox{\boldmath$c$}_{(\gamma,k)}=\alpha_{k}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}
.

3.

The regularisation parameter
\gamma
in (
9
) controls the amount of residual we would like to get rid of. The larger this parameter is, the less is the residual and the fewer is the performed iterations.

2.2
Singular Value Expansion of the Iterate
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}

Similarly to the Truncated SVD and the Tikhonov solutions (
[
27
]
, Chap.
4
), the iterate
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
from the stabilized gradient method (
10
) also admits a simple filtered SVD expression. Taking the initial vector
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[0]}_{\gamma}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
, we can express from (
10
) the
k^{\text{th}}
iteration in the following

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{k}\right)^{\ell}\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$c$}_{(\gamma,k)},\;\forall\;\gamma>0,\;\forall\;k=1,2,3,\cdots.

(11)

In order to have some insight into
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
, we use SVD decomposition of the matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,n}(\mathbb{R})

[
9
]

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{t},\,\text{ and }\,\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{V}^{t},

(12)

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,n}(\mathbb{R})
is a diagonal matrix with the singular values
\sigma_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, satisfying

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}>0,\;\text{ and }\,\mathbf{\Sigma}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{n}).

The matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,n}(\mathbb{R})
is orthogonal, that is
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{t}=\mathbf{V}^{t}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}
(see
[
27
]
- Chap 3.2). We thus use the decomposition in (
12
) to rewrite the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{k}
and the vector
\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$c$}_{(\gamma,k)}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{N}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathbf{V}^{t},

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\gamma}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$c$}_{(\gamma,k)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathbf{V}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}.

(14)

The
k^{\text{th}}
iteration is thus express from (
11
), using (
13
)-(
14
) and fixing the stepsize
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
, as follows

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\varphi^{[k]}_{\gamma}\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\alpha\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathbf{V}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$},

(15)

in which
\varphi^{[k]}_{\gamma}=\text{diag}\left(\varphi_{(\gamma,1)}^{[k]},\cdots,\varphi_{(\gamma,n)}^{[k]}\right)
is a diagonal matrix with entries defined by

\displaystyle\varphi_{(\gamma,i)}^{[k]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-\alpha\sigma_{i}}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}\right)^{\ell}=\left(\frac{1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha\sigma_{i}}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}\right)^{k}}{1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha\sigma_{i}}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}\right)}\right),\;i=1,\cdots,n.

Multiplying diagonal matrices
\varphi^{[k]}_{\gamma}
and
\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\alpha\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)
, we obtain the filtered expansion

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left(\phi_{(\gamma,i)}^{[k]}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{t}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{i},

(16)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, are columns of
\mathbf{V}
, and
\phi_{(\gamma,i)}^{[k]}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, are the filter factors defined by

\displaystyle\phi_{(\gamma,i)}^{[k]}=\sigma_{i}\left(\frac{\alpha+\gamma\sigma_{i}}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}\right)\varphi_{(\gamma,i)}^{[k]}=\left(\frac{\alpha\sigma_{i}+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}\right)\left(\frac{1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha\sigma_{i}}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}\right)^{k}}{1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha\sigma_{i}}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{i}}\right)}\right),\,\forall\;k=1,2,3,\cdots.

(17)

The behavior of filter factors in (
17
) is illustrated in Figure
1
(see
1
,
2
,
3
) as a function of the singular values
\sigma_{i}\in[10^{-6},\,10^{0}]
(for simplicity), for each of the stepsize
\alpha=10^{i},i=-3,0,3
and with respect to the stabilization parameter
\gamma=10^{i},i=3,6,8,10
. We see that as
\gamma
increases, the filter factors are shifted toward the unit value. More SVD components are thus effectively included in the iteration
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
in (
16
) as
\gamma
increases, and the limit asymptotic behavior of the filter factors when
\gamma\to\infty
becomes

\displaystyle\phi_{(\gamma,i)}^{[k]}\longrightarrow\phi_{(\infty,i)}=1,\;i=1,\cdots,n,

(18)

provides the the naïve solution
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}_{\text{naïve}}
of the linear system (
1

3
Convergence Analysis of the Stabilized Gradient Method

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a matrix of
\text{rank}(\mathbf{A})=r\leq\min(m,n)
, whose the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{t}
, where
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are orthogonal matrices, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a matrix defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{r})
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}>0
. We obtain the following estimate

\displaystyle\left\|(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\right\|\leq\left(\frac{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{r}}\right),\;\forall\;\gamma>0,

(20)

where
\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)=\|\mathbf{V}\|\|\mathbf{V}^{-1}\|
is the condition number of
\mathbf{V}
.

Proof.

From the SVD of
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{t}
and the orthogonality of the matrices
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\mathbf{V}^{-1},\;\forall\;\gamma>0.

So, the following inverse is obtained

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{-1},\;\forall\;\gamma>0.

We thus establish the following estimate by using the sub-multiplicity of the matrix norm

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}\right\|=\left\|\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\right\|\leq\left\|\mathbf{V}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{V}^{-1}\right\|\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}\right\|,\;\forall\;\gamma>0.

(21)

Since the induced matrix norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
(from the euclidean vector norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) of a diagonal matrix is equal to the absolute value of the largest diagonal element, then the norm of the matrix
\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{-1}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{1}},\frac{1}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{2}},\cdots,\frac{1}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{r}}\right)
is
\frac{1}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{r}}
. Hence, we establish from (
21
) the estimate (
20
), where
\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)=\|\mathbf{V}\|\|\mathbf{V}^{-1}\|
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}
be the exact solution of (
1
). For any
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, the sequence
\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
generated from the stabilized gradient method (
9
) verifies

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n}\leq\left\|(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{A}\right\|\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star},

(22)

Proof.

The gradient iterative method (
2
) is consistent with the linear system (
1
), that is to say the exact solution
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}
satisfies the following equation (see
[
37
]
- Definition
4.1
)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}-\alpha_{k}(\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}),\;k=0,1,2,\cdots,

(23)

Otherwise from the system (
1
), we also obtain the normal equation satisfied by the exact solution

\displaystyle\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}=\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$},\;\forall\;\gamma>0.

(24)

Then adding both equations (
23
) and (
24
), we thus remark that the stabilized gradient method (
9
) is also consistent with the linear system (
1
)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}+\alpha_{k}\mbox{\boldmath$b$}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mbox{\boldmath$b$},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,

(25)

Denoting the error at the
(k+1)^{\text{th}}
iteration by
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}
, we obtain by subtracting equations (
9
) and (
25
) the equality

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$e$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$e$}^{[k]}_{\gamma},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\,\forall\,k=0,1,2,3,\cdots.

The matrix
\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)
being nonsingular for any
\gamma>0
, we then obtain the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$e$}^{[k+1]}_{\gamma}=\left(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A}^{t}\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{A}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$e$}^{[k]}_{\gamma},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\,\forall\,k=0,1,2,3,\cdots,

which established the result (
22
) by using the matrix norm sub-multiplicity, and its compatibility with the euclidean vector norm.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a nonsingular
n\times n
matrix, and
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[0]}_{\gamma}
any initial datum. The sequence
\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
, computed from the stabilized gradient method (
9
), satisfies

1.

If the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
is a non-constant parameter:

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n}\leq\left(\frac{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{n}}\right)^{k}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\|\mathbf{I}-\alpha_{i}\mathbf{A}\right\|\right)\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[0]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}.

(26)

2.

If the stepsize is a constant parameter, namely
\alpha=\alpha_{k}
:

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n}\leq\left(\left(\frac{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{n}}\right)\left\|\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{A}\right\|\right)^{k}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[0]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}\|_{n},\,\forall\,\gamma>0,\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}.

(27)

Proof.

Multiplying term by term the inequality (
22
), for
k=0,\cdots,k-1
, by using the fact that the
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is nonsingular (and thus of rank
n
) in the Lemma
1
, we obtain the estimate (
26
) after simplification. If the stepsize is constant, we thus arrive from (
26
) to the estimate (
27
).
∎

Remark 3
.

1.

We may see from estimates (
26
) and (
27
), how our approach stabilizes the gradient method by damping the smallest singular value
\sigma^{2}_{n}
by the stabilization parameter
\gamma>0
, but also by divising the whole right-hand side term by this parameter (which can be chosen larger).

2.

The convergence of the stabilized gradient method (
9
) is faster than the parameter
\gamma
increases.

3.

We thus obtain, across the stabilization term
\gamma
, a total control of the convergence of the approximate solution
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
independently to the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
and the structure of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
whose the only required criteria is to be nonsingular (for the obtaining of a unique solution).

4.

With respect to the estimate (
27
), the error
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}
converges toward zero if and only if

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{n}}\right)\left\|\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{A}\right\|<1.

(28)

If
\mathbf{A}
is symmetric, then its singular values are equal to the absolute values of its eigenvalues
\sigma_{i}=|\lambda_{i}|
, where
\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, are eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
. We thus obtain from (
28
)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{A}\right\|

\displaystyle<\frac{1+\gamma\sigma^{2}_{n}}{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}

\displaystyle\max_{i=1,\cdots,n}|1-\alpha\lambda_{i}|

\displaystyle<\frac{1+\gamma\lambda^{2}_{n}}{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1+\gamma\lambda^{2}_{n}}{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}<\alpha\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle<1+\frac{1+\gamma\lambda^{2}_{n}}{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)},\;\forall\;\gamma>0,\;\forall\;i=1,\cdots,n.

(29)

We note from (
4
) that, the stepsize
\alpha
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
may be of different signs when

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1+\gamma\lambda^{2}_{n}}{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)}<0\iff\gamma>\frac{\kappa\left(\mathbf{V}\right)-1}{\lambda^{2}_{n}}>0.

(30)

Thus the convergence of the stabilized method (
9
) is no longer based on the idea that the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and the parameter
\alpha
should be of the same sign, as it is the case for the gradient method (
2
) (see
[
2
]
- Chap. 9, Theorem
9.1.1
). Moreover, it is also no longer based on the choice of the stepsize
\alpha
.

5.

The rate of convergence of the stabilized gradient method (
9
) is not governed by the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
(
[
25
]
- Chap. 9, Theorem 9.10). This convergence is guided by the choice of the stabilization parameter
\gamma
, independently of the linear system data
\mathbf{A}
and
b
. If a very large value of the stabilization term is chosen, the stabilized gradient method (
9
) should behave like a direct method by making one iteration.

The convergence criterion of the sequence
\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is determined by the relative criterion

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mbox{\boldmath$b$}-\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}\|_{n}}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$b$}-\mathbf{A}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[0]}_{\gamma}\|_{n}}\leq\varepsilon,\;\forall\;k=1,2,3,\cdots.

(31)

In the building of the algorithm, an initial value
x
, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
, the right hand side
b
, a number of maximum iterations
k_{\text{max}}
, a stepsize parameter
\alpha
and the stabilization parameter
\gamma
are used as inputs. The algorithm returns as output the final value
x
at the end of the
k_{\text{max}}
iterations (when the stopping condition is not verified) or before. We thus present the following general schematic algorithm of the stabilized gradient method (
9
).

4.3
Ill-conditioned linear systems

In this third part, we investigated the ill-conditioned linear systems under both small and large scale problems.

4.3.1
Small-scale inverse problems

In the analyzing the efficiency and the accuracy of the stabilized gradient method (
9
), we also investigated severely ill-conditioned numerical examples. The problems studied throughout this part are standard small-scale inverse problems known as the
shaw
, the
heat
and the
gravity
problems
[
27
,
29
]
. These test problems are classical examples of ill-posed problems, formulated throught the following Fredholm integral equation of the first kind (see
[
24
]
)

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}K(s,t)x^{\star}(t)dt=f(s),\;\,c\leq s\leq d,

(49)

where the kernel
K
and the right hand side
f
are given, and
x^{\star}
is the desired unknown solution.

To apply a quadrature formula in the approximation of the integral equation in (
49
), we use as in
[
29
]
the midpoint quadrature-collocation rule with
n
equidistantly spaced points
s_{i}
and
t_{i}

\displaystyle s_{i}=c+ih,\;\,t_{i}=a+ih,\;\,h=\frac{b-a}{n},\;\,i=1,\cdots,n.

(50)

Rewriting the integral equation (
49
) through the discretizations in (
50
), we obtain the linear system

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}K(s_{i},t^{\star}_{j})x^{\star}(t^{\star}_{j})=f(s_{i}),\;\,i=1,\cdots,n,

(51)

where each midpoint is given by
t^{\star}_{j}=t_{j}-\frac{h}{2}
, and
w_{j}=h
for
j=1,\cdots,n
are the weights of the quadrature formula. We thus compute the
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
, the
n
-vector right hand
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=(b_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, and the
n
-vector exact solution
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}=(x^{\star}_{j})_{1\leq j\leq n}
as follows

\displaystyle a_{ij}=w_{j}K(s_{i},t^{\star}_{j}),\;\,b_{i}=f(s_{i}),\;\,x^{\star}_{j}=x^{\star}(t^{\star}_{j}),\;\,i=1,\cdots,n,\;\,j=1,\cdots,n.

(52)

As we may seen in (
26
) - (
27
) and (
4
) - (
30
), and through the previous numerical examples
4.1
and
4.2
, the value of the stepsize
\alpha
has a limit influence on the convergence of the stabilized gradient method (
9
). What really matter is the value of the stabilization parameter
\gamma
, which should be fixed as large as possible for reducing the residual.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\star}(t)=a_{1}\exp\left(-c_{1}\left(t-t_{1}\right)^{2}\right)+a_{2}\exp\left(-c_{2}\left(t-t_{2}\right)^{2}\right),\;\,-\frac{\pi}{2}\leq t\leq\frac{\pi}{2},

a_{2}

5
General Conclusion

We proposed through this work a stabilization of the gradient method by replacing the gradient algorithm (
2
) by the iterative method (
9
). This stabilization method is based on the idea to build an iterative scheme from the gradient method, by gradually controlling the residual as the iterations go along. We established theoretical convergence results in Corollary
1
and made numerical computations in the Section
4
that illustrated our analysis. These established results are made under the nonsingular matrix property alone, and the performed numerical tests take into account the both symmetric and non-symmetric matrices.

We first analyse the filtered SVD expansion of the stabilized iterative solution
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
in (
16
), and note that as the stabilization parameter
\gamma
increases, the filter factors (
17
) effectively include more and more SVD components in the iterations
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
by shifting toward the unit value. The limit expansion obtained in (
1
) (namely the naïve solution reached when
\gamma\to+\infty
), shows that under ill-conditioning problem we should not take the stabilization parameter as large as we want since it is known that this solution is very inaccurate. This explained the oscillating solution we observe in sub-subsection
4.3.1
under severely ill-conditioned systems (see Table
6
, Table
7
, Table
8
, Figure
5
, Figure
6
, and Figure
7
, when the stabilization parameter is fixed to
\gamma=10^{14},10^{15},10^{16}
). This hence proves that only controlling the residual (as we did in the stabilized gradient method (
9
)), may not provide accurate approximations under severely ill-conditioning context. However, with large stabilization parameter (namely when
\gamma
is around
10^{10}
), we obtain through severely ill-conditioned systems analysed in sub-subsection
4.3.1
, efficient and accurate approximations from the stabilized solution
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{[k]}_{\gamma}
. Through these examples, we only faced errors from the quadrature in the evaluation of the coefficients of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and the right-hand side
\mathbf{b}
, and the rounding errors.

The stabilized gradient method has spared us the intrinsic difficulty associated with determining the stepsize
\alpha
in the gradient method. We first observed in (
26
) and (
27
) that the convergence is speeded up by taking the stabilization parameter
\gamma
as large as possible, and is no longer based on the choix of the stepsize
\alpha
. For well-conditioned linear systems in sub-sections
4.1
and
4.2
whose the matrix is symmetric (or not), the numerical tests have confirmed this result. And for ill-conditioned linear systems, we observe the same behaviour as long as the ill-conditioning is not severe as faced under the
shaw
,
heat
and
gravity
test problems (in sub-subsection
4.3.1
). The convergence of the stabilized gradient method (
9
) is as faster than the stabilization parameter
\gamma
is large (it sometimes behaves as a direct method). Moreover, the convergence of the stabilized method (
9
) no longer depends on the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
(the structure of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
). It is only guided by the choice of the stabilization parameter
\gamma
, independently of the linear system data
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{b}
.

As we have seen through the theoretical results (
26
)-(
27
) and the previous numerical examples, the value of the stepsize
\alpha
has a limit influence on the convergence of the stabilized gradient method (
9
) (meaning that we may fix the stepsize to neutral value
\alpha=1
). What really matter is the value of the stabilization parameter
\gamma
, which should be fixed as large as possible in order to quickly reduce the residual and hence to make less iterations.

In forthcoming works, it will be interesting to remedy to the oscillating solutions observed under severely ill-conditioned systems of the sub-subsection
4.3.1
. The idea which may be tested is, in addition to the stabilization made here, to add some regularization of the solution as in the direct solving method proposed in
[
14
]
.
