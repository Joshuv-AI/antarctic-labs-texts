ReVision : A Post-Hoc, Vision-Based Technique for Replacing Unacceptable Concepts in Image Generation Pipeline
Gurjot Singh, Prabhjot Singh, Aashima Sharma, Maninder Singh, Ryan Ko

Abstract
Image-generative models are widely deployed across industries. Recent studies show that they can be exploited to produce policy-violating content. Existing mitigation strategies primarily operate at the pre- or mid-generation stages through techniques such as prompt filtering and safety-aware training/fine-tuning. Prior work shows that these approaches can be bypassed and often degrade generative quality. In this work, we propose ReVision , a training-free, prompt-based, post-hoc safety framework for image-generation pipeline. ReVision acts as a last-line defense by analyzing generated images and selectively editing unsafe concepts without altering the underlying generator. It uses the Gemini-2.5-Flash model as a generic policy-violating concept detector, avoiding reliance on multiple category-specific detectors, and performs localized semantic editing to replace unsafe content. Prior post-hoc editing methods often rely on imprecise spatial localization, that undermines usability and limits deployability, particularly in multi-concept scenes. To address this limitation, ReVision introduces a VLM-assisted spatial gating mechanism that enforces instance-consistent localization, enabling precise edits while preserving scene integrity. We evaluate ReVision on a 245-image benchmark covering both single- and multi-concept scenarios. Results show that ReVision (i) improves CLIP-based alignment toward safe prompts by + 0.121 0.121 on average; (ii) significantly improves multi-concept background fidelity (LPIPS 0.166 → 0.058 0.166\rightarrow 0.058 ); (iii) achieves near-complete suppression on category-specific detectors (e.g., NudeNet 70.51 → 0 70.51\rightarrow 0 ); and (iv) reduces policy-violating content recognizability in a human moderation study from 95.99 % 95.99\% to 10.16 % 10.16\% .

Introduction

The visual content creation landscape has been fundamentally transformed by recent advances in text-to-image (T2I) and image-to-image (I2I) generative models, such as Imagen
21
, DALL-E
18
, and Stable Diffusion
20
. These large-scale diffusion-based models enable the rapid generation of highly realistic and semantically rich images from user-provided text prompts or image–prompt pairs
17
. However, these capabilities have also been increasingly exploited to generate policy-violating content, including violence, nudity, drugs, and illicit material, raising significant security, privacy, ethical, and societal concerns
17
. As generative image models are increasingly exposed via public APIs and integrated into consumer-facing products, the ability to enforce safety policies reliably and at scale has become a critical systems and security challenge.

To mitigate misuse, model providers deploy a range of built-in safeguards, collectively referred to as
Concept Removal Techniques
(CRTs), which attempt to suppress unsafe content at different stages of the generation pipeline
19
;
15
;
4
;
29
. These interventions include prompt-level filtering, mid-generation constraints, and post-generation suppression. However, previous work has shown that such mechanisms remain incomplete in practice: adversarial prompt perturbations can bypass input filtering while unchanging malicious content
30
, and mid-generation interventions often degrade visual quality or exhibit inconsistent behavior, particularly in complex scenes and I2I settings
8
;
6
. As a result, residual policy violations frequently reach the final output, motivating the use of downstream, post-hoc safety defenses.

Post-hoc safety defenses are particularly appealing in practice because they operate directly on generated images and do not require retraining or modifying the underlying generative model. However, existing post-hoc pipelines face fundamental deployability challenges. Most rely on concept-specific detectors
5
;
6
, which require prior enumeration of unsafe categories, generalize poorly to compositional or unseen violations, and remain vulnerable under adversarial conditions. Beyond detection, post-hoc defenses must localize unsafe content in order to constrain subsequent edits
34
;
6
. In multi-concept scenes, however, imprecise spatial localization frequently leads to two failure modes: incomplete suppression of unsafe regions, or unintended modification of benign content. While the former enables evasion in complex scenes, the latter degrades benign content and, in practice, undermines the usability and continued deployment of post-hoc safety pipelines in real-world systems.

To address these challenges, we propose
ReVision
, a training-free, two-stage post-hoc safety pipeline designed to improve the deployability of image editing–based defenses.
ReVision
performs concept-level analysis of
generic
policy-violating content—such as nudity, violence, drugs, copyrighted entities, and public figures—using a single vision–language detector, and applies targeted suppression in both single- and multi-concept images across T2I and I2I settings. Rather than introducing new safety policies or retraining generative models,
ReVision
focuses on improving spatial precision during post-hoc editing, enabling unsafe regions to be suppressed while preserving benign content.

This work makes the following contributions:

•

We identify mask spilling as a systematic failure mode in attention-based post-hoc image editing pipelines, where imprecise localization in multi-concept scenes leads to unintended modification of benign content; degrading the usability of post-hoc safety pipelines.

•

We propose a VLM-assisted spatial gating mechanism that integrates coarse instance-level localization into attention refinement, enabling instance-consistent edits that reduce mask spilling.

•

We demonstrate that a single, generic vision–language detector can support concept-level post-hoc safety editing across diverse categories of policy-violating content in both T2I and I2I settings.

•

We introduce a curated dataset of 245 single- and multi-concept images to support systematic evaluation of post-hoc safety editing under realistic deployment conditions.

2
Background

2.1
Diffusion Models

In diffusion-based generative models, data generation is formulated as a two-stage stochastic process consisting of a forward noising process and a reverse denoising process.
The forward process gradually corrupts a data sample with noise, while the reverse process iteratively removes noise to produce a clean image conditioned on user-provided inputs.
T2I and I2I generative systems are commonly implemented using these diffusion-based models and deployed as cloud-hosted services. In the T2I setting, the model generates an image conditioned on a user-provided prompt, producing an output that reflects the semantic intent of the input. In the I2I setting, the model additionally conditions on an input image, preserving its structural content while modifying visual attributes or semantics according to the prompt.

2.2
Concept Replacement Techniques

Diffusion-based generative models enable high-quality image synthesis but are trained on large-scale internet data that may contain legally restricted, sensitive, or prohibited content. The ability of these models to reproduce such content raises significant legal, ethical, and regulatory concerns, including privacy violations and reputational harm
3
;
16
. To mitigate these risks, prior work has proposed CRTs, which intervene at different stages of the diffusion pipeline to suppress or modify undesired concepts in generated images.

2.2.1
Pre-Processing CRTs

Pre-processing CRTs operate on user-provided inputs without modifying the underlying model parameters. These approaches intervene on textual prompts, conditioning embeddings, or input images prior to generation. Because both T2I and I2I pipelines rely heavily on conditioning signals, early-stage intervention can, in principle, prevent unsafe semantics from influencing the generative process.

However, pre-processing CRTs are particularly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation. Prior work shows that attackers can craft prompts or input images that bypass filtering mechanisms and inject harmful semantics into the generation pipeline
30
;
31
. In addition, prompt-based sanitization is inherently less effective in I2I settings. Even when a neutral prompt (e.g., “change background”) is provided, unsafe semantics present in the input image can still condition the diffusion process toward policy-violating outputs. Mitigating such cases requires reliable visual concept detection at the input stage, which itself is susceptible to adversarial evasion. As a result, pre-processing CRTs provide limited protection against unsafe content in realistic I2I deployment scenarios.

2.2.2
Mid-Processing CRTs

Mid-processing CRTs
13
;
8
;
26
;
28
intervene during the diffusion generation process by modifying internal model representations rather than input or output. These methods try to make the model
forget
specific unsafe concepts, thereby reducing the likelihood that such concepts are reproduced during generation.
Despite their fine-grained control, mid-processing CRTs face several practical limitations. Complete concept erasure remains infeasible, as adversarial prompts can still induce unsafe content
14
. Retraining or fine-tuning large diffusion models incurs substantial computational cost
5
, and many approaches exhibit poor compositional generalization when benign and unsafe concepts coexist.

Prior work further shows that these methods are particularly ineffective in I2I settings
6
. Moreover, most existing mid-processing CRTs are tailored to U-Net–based architectures, limiting their applicability to newer diffusion models with transformer-based denoisers.

2.2.3
Post-Processing CRTs

Post-processing CRTs operate after image generation, modifying the output image without altering the underlying diffusion model. These approaches function as a corrective stage by applying harmful-content detectors, such as NSFW classifiers, object detectors, or vision–language models, to identify unsafe concepts in the generated image. Once detected, unsafe regions are localized and then edited using techniques such as cropping, blurring, inpainting, or re-diffusion to suppress policy-violating content while preserving surrounding benign regions
32
.
Recent works demonstrate the effectiveness of post-hoc pipelines for mitigating unsafe generations
34
;
6
, but still are underexplored.

3
Threat Model

We define the adversarial setting, attack surface, and defensive scope for
ReVision
in a realistic production deployment.

Deployment Setting.

We consider a standard cloud-based deployment in which a service provider exposes a T2I or I2I generative model via a public API. Users control the input conditioning (text prompts or input images), while all downstream processing, including safety auditing and output delivery, is executed within the provider’s Trusted Computing Base (TCB). The adversary cannot intercept, modify, or replace generated images after delivery as shown in Fig
1
.

Adversary Model.

The adversary is an untrusted API user whose objective is to bypass content policies by inducing the generation of prohibited content (e.g., nudity, copyrighted characters, or identifiable individuals). The adversary may adapt inputs across multiple queries but is restricted to black-box interaction with the system. They have no access to model parameters, gradients, training data, or internal components of either the generator or the downstream safety auditor.

Threat Surface.

The attack surface includes the generator’s input interface and the downstream vision-based safety auditor. Attacks target the semantic content of generated images through adversarial prompts or manipulated input images. We explicitly exclude attacks on system infrastructure, model weights, inter-service communication, or post-audit output.

Defender Objectives and Scope.

The defender’s goal is to detect and suppress unsafe content in generated images prior to delivery, without retraining or modifying the underlying generative model. In addition to suppressing unsafe content, a deployable defense must preserve benign content to avoid service degradation in production systems.
ReVision
operates as a post-hoc, last-line defense to mitigate residual policy violations that evade upstream filters. This threat model reflects realistic deployments in which safety auditors are provided as black-box services and adversaries lack direct optimization access to downstream safety mechanisms.

4
Localization Failures in Post-Hoc Safety Editing

Post-hoc safety editing suppresses unsafe visual content by modifying generated images prior to delivery. Unlike blocking or regeneration, such pipelines must localize unsafe content in order to constrain edits and preserve benign regions. Localization errors, therefore, directly affect both safety effectiveness and system deployability.

4.1
Explicit Localization in Prior Work

Several post-hoc safety systems rely on
explicit localization
to constrain edits using externally generated spatial masks or structured representations. USD
34
performs entity-level segmentation and scene graph reasoning to identify unsafe objects and relationships before applying region-level image repair. USD claims to remove only narrowly defined harmful semantics, including sexual content, violence, disturbing or threatening material, and illegal or self-harm activities, without addressing legal or intellectual property constraints. As noted by the authors, the effectiveness of this pipeline depends on accurate intermediate segmentation and reasoning, and errors in these stages can propagate to downstream editing. In their evaluation, USD reports approximately 76% preservation of benign content, illustrating the practical difficulty of achieving precise localization in complex scenes.

ANTIMIRROR
6
adopts a similar strategy in a more specialized setting, using face parsing and identity-specific masks to remove celebrity likeness. While effective for targeted identity manipulation, this approach relies on concept-specific localization and does not generalize to arbitrary unsafe concepts or complex multi-concept scenes.
These approaches demonstrate the importance of generic detectors and spatial constraints in post-hoc safety editing while highlighting limitations, including segmentation sensitivity, limited generality, and increased pipeline complexity.

4.2
Implicit Localization via Prompt-Based Editing

Implicit localization offers an alternative to explicit spatial constraints by inferring where edits should apply during image editing rather than relying on externally generated masks. In prompt-based editing methods, localization emerges from the interaction between the input image, the editing prompt, and internal attention mechanisms, allowing edits to be guided by semantic relevance rather than predefined regions. While such approaches have been explored primarily in creative editing contexts, they remain underexplored for post-hoc safety enforcement, where this paradigm is attractive because it avoids auxiliary segmentation models, reduces pipeline complexity, and naturally supports generic, policy-driven editing across diverse unsafe concepts without retraining the generative model. In this work, we instantiate implicit localization using a recent prompt-based editing method, LOCATEdit
25
, which provides a practical and representative mechanism for localized post-hoc editing without external masks.

4.2.1
Localized Editing Using LOCATEdit
25

In this work, we adopt LOCATEdit
25
as an off-the-shelf text-guided editing method and do not modify its architecture or training procedure. LOCATEdit requires three inputs to perform localized editing. (i) The
source prompt
describes the original visual content and is used to reconstruct the source branch during diffusion. (ii) The
target prompt
specifies a safe replacement and guides the target branch. (iii) The
blend words
identify the semantic concepts to be modified and serve as anchors for localizing edits within the image.
To determine edit locations, LOCATEdit extracts and aggregates cross-attention maps associated with the blend words during diffusion denoising, producing an initial spatial mask that highlights regions linked to the target concept. This mask is subsequently refined using self-attention–based smoothing to improve spatial coherence and reduce fragmented activations, as discussed in Section
4.2.2
.

Mask-guided denoising for localized editing.

Localized editing is realized through latent blending of the source and target diffusion branches. Let
\mathbf{z}^{\text{src}}_{t}
and
\mathbf{z}^{\text{tgt}}_{t}
denote latent representations from the source and target branches, respectively. A refined spatial mask
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}}
is used to selectively merge these representations during denoising:

\mathbf{z}_{t-1}=\mathbf{M}\odot\mathbf{z}^{\text{tgt}}_{t-1}+(1-\mathbf{M})\odot\mathbf{z}^{\text{src}}_{t-1},

(1)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication. This operation constrains semantic edits to localized regions while preserving background and unrelated content from the source branch.

As illustrated in Table
11
(See Appendix), implicit localization via LOCATEdit effectively constrains edits to target regions in single-concept scenarios, enabling the removal or replacement of unsafe content while largely preserving surrounding benign regions. These results demonstrate that prompt-based implicit localization can serve as a practical mechanism for post-hoc safety editing without relying on external segmentation masks.
However, when extending this approach to multi-concept scenes where unsafe and benign concepts coexist, we observe failures that degrade benign content preservation, which we analyze in the following section.

4.2.2
Mask Spilling in Laplacian Attention

LOCATEdit performs localized editing by constructing spatial masks from cross-attention maps associated with a pair of
blend words
: one token extracted from the source prompt, indicating the concept to be removed, and one token extracted from the target prompt, indicating the replacement concept. While this design enables targeted semantic editing, we observe a systematic failure mode—referred to as
mask spilling
—in scenes containing multiple related or interacting objects. We define mask spilling as the failure mode in which an editing mask, intended to localize a single target instance, unintentionally expands to semantically related but non-target regions, resulting in unintended modifications outside the desired concept scope.

Let
b_{s}
and
b_{t}
denote the source and target blend words, respectively (e.g.,
‘‘naked’’
and
‘‘clothed’’
). LOCATEdit extracts cross-attention maps corresponding to these tokens from the source and target diffusion branches, yielding attention-derived localization signals
\mathbf{A}_{b_{s}}
and
\mathbf{A}_{b_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
. These signals are combined through an attention aggregation procedure to form an initial localization map:

\mathbf{M}_{\text{cross}}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{A}_{b_{s}},\mathbf{A}_{b_{t}}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
denotes the aggregation and normalization mechanism used to integrate per-branch attention responses. To refine this initial localization map during diffusion denoising LOCATEdit uses Laplacian smoothing guided by self-attention affinities, producing a refined mask
\mathbf{M}^{*}
by solving:

(D_{w}+\lambda L)\,\mathbf{M}^{*}=D_{w}\,\mathbf{M}_{\text{cross}},

(3)

where
L=D-A
is the graph Laplacian derived from the self-attention affinity matrix
A
,
D_{w}
is a diagonal confidence-weighting matrix across spatial regions, and
\lambda
controls the smoothness regularization.

A critical limitation arises when the target blend word
b_{t}
is
instance-ambiguous
, i.e., semantically applicable to multiple objects within the same image. In such cases, the target-branch attention map
\mathbf{A}_{b_{t}}
assigns non-zero activation to all regions that satisfy the replacement concept, regardless of whether they correspond to the intended editing target. When these regions are strongly connected to the true target object through the self-attention graph—due to shared object category, spatial proximity, pose similarity, or global scene context—the Laplacian refinement propagates activation across these connections.

As a result, activation mass from
\mathbf{M}_{\text{cross}}
spreads to semantically related but unintended regions, yielding mask coverage beyond the desired concept instance. This effect is illustrated in Table

example, although the edit is intended to replace a nude woman with a clothed woman, the target blend word
‘‘clothed’’

‘‘brad-pitt’’
activates the celebrity instance, but refinement propagates activation to another nearby man due to strong contextual and structural self-attention affinities.

In both cases, mask spilling arises from the interaction between instance-ambiguous target blend words and self-attention-guided Laplacian propagation, rather than from incorrect prompts or semantic misunderstandings. This limitation underscores the challenge of achieving precise instance-level localization using attention-based refinement alone, particularly in multi-object scenes and I2I settings, where preserving non-target content is crucial. To address this limitation, we propose VLM-Assisted Instance-Consistent Spatial Gating, described in Section
5.2.2
.

Methodology

In this section, the data generation process used to construct a benchmark of single- and multi-concept unsafe images is described first. Then the proposed post-hoc safety pipeline is presented. Finally, we address limitations of attention-based semantic editing identified in Section
4.2.2
by introducing a VLM-assisted spatial gating mechanism.

5.1
Data Generation

Following prior work
5
;
11
, the Stable Diffusion 3.5 model is prompted to generate high-quality images containing unsafe or policy-violating concepts.
The dataset is designed to represent both isolated and combined occurrences of sensitive concepts, mirroring real-world scenarios where multiple policy-relevant attributes often coexist within a single image.

Concept Categories.

We focus on five security- and safety-relevant concept categories:

(i) nudity
,

(ii) copyrighted content
,

(iii) public figures
,

(iv) smoking and alcohol consumption

For each category, representative prompts are curated to explicitly evoke the corresponding concept. These categories are selected to reflect content that is commonly restricted or subject to moderation in deployed generative image systems

In total, a benchmark dataset of 245 images is generated. All images are generated at a resolution of
1024\times 1024

5.2
Proposed
ReVision
Pipeline

A training-free, systematic two-stage post-hoc pipeline is proposed that operates on output images generated by both T2I and I2I models. The proposed pipeline is generic and content-agnostic, enabling it to handle a broad range of policy-violating visual content (discussed in Section
5.1
). A key strength of the pipeline lies in its ability to identify multiple unsafe concepts and perform targeted replacement, even in complex scenes containing densely populated or co-occurring violations.

5.2.1

In both T2I and I2I settings, Gemini-2.5-Flash

Gemini performs open-vocabulary semantic reasoning by jointly analyzing the visual content of the image and high-level safety policy definitions, allowing it to detect both explicit and context-dependent violations without being restricted to a fixed set of object categories.

\mathcal{T}=\{(y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T},

5.2.2
Stage 2: VLM-Assisted Spatial Gating

Following semantic detection in Stage 1, the pipeline performs localized post-hoc editing to replace detected unsafe concepts while preserving surrounding image content
2
2

2

In this work, target prompts are automatically generated by Gemini; however, this design is flexible and allows model providers to customize replacement descriptions or policies if desired.
.

To mitigate mask spilling (discussed in Section
4.2.2
caused by attention propagation across visually similar but semantically distinct regions, a
VLM-assisted instance-consistent spatial gating
mechanism is introduced. Unlike approaches that directly constrain attention maps during refinement, the proposed method enforces spatial constraints at the
latent blending stage
of the diffusion process, ensuring instance-level edit isolation while preserving the object-aware structure produced by attention refinement.
In addition to extracting unsafe concepts and generating the corresponding source prompt, target prompt, and blend words, the use of Gemini is extended to return an additional structured output: a coarse spatial localization of the detected concept in the form of a bounding box, denoted as
\mathcal{B}=[x_{\min},y_{\min},x_{\max},y_{\max}]
. Here,
(x_{\min},y_{\min})
and
(x_{\max},y_{\max})
represent the top-left and bottom-right coordinates of the region containing the detected concept, respectively. This bounding box provides an
instance-level spatial prior
that disambiguates multiple occurrences of similar concepts within a scene. Importantly, this bounding box does not replace attention-based localization or define the edit region; rather, it serves as a coarse instance-level safety guardrail that constrains where attention-derived edits may be applied.

As described in Section
4.2.2
, LOCATEdit constructs a refined spatial mask by solving a Laplacian smoothing problem over cross-attention maps associated with blend words. Let
\mathbf{M}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
denote the refined attention map obtained after refinement. While this process promotes spatial coherence, it may also propagate activation mass to semantically unrelated regions when strong self-attention affinities exist between multiple instances.

After refinement, LOCATEdit upsamples
\mathbf{M}^{*}
to latent resolution and applies normalization and thresholding to obtain a binary latent-space edit mask

\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}},

where
(H_{\ell},W_{\ell})
correspond to the latent grid resolution. This mask preserves the object-level structure discovered during attention refinement but may still jointly activate multiple similar instances.

An instance consistency is enforced by converting the bounding box
\mathcal{B}
into a latent-space binary gate

\mathbf{G}\in\{0,1\}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}},

obtained by scaling
\mathcal{B}
from image coordinates to latent coordinates. The final edit mask is then computed as:

\mathbf{M}^{\prime}=\mathbf{M}\land\mathbf{G}.

(5)

Unlike naïve box masking, this gating preserves the fine-grained object structure discovered by attention refinement while explicitly breaking cross-instance propagation paths induced by self-attention.
This operation restricts edits to lie within the spatial support of the detected instance, while preserving the fine-grained shape of
\mathbf{M}
inside the bounding box. Unlike directly masking attention maps, this avoids collapsing the edit region into a coarse rectangular shape.
Crucially, the spatial gate
\mathbf{G}
is applied
only to the edited (target) sample
during latent blending. The reference (source) sample remains unconstrained, preventing inadvertent reintroduction of global spatial support from the base image across diffusion steps. This design addresses a subtle but important failure mode in attention-based editing, where merging source and edited representations can implicitly re-enable edits outside the intended region.
By enforcing instance-level constraints at the latent blending stage, the proposed approach blocks cross-instance activation paths induced by self-attention connectivity without interfering with the Laplacian refinement process, thereby suppressing mask spilling even in scenes with strong contextual or structural relationships.

The effectiveness of this mechanism is demonstrated in Table
2
. Compared to baseline LOCATEdit (Table
1
), VLM-assisted spatial gating successfully isolates edits to the intended instance. In the nudity example, only the unclothed woman is edited, while the nearby clothed person remains unchanged. In the example of famous celebrities, edits are restricted to Brad Pitt without affecting the random person.

Overall, VLM-assisted instance-consistent spatial gating improves edit fidelity (formally defined in Section 4.1) by integrating external semantic localization into the diffusion editing loop. Because the method operates entirely at inference time and does not modify the underlying model parameters.

6.1
Fidelity and Unintended Edit Mitigation

To quantify unintended modifications introduced by post-hoc editing, background fidelity is evaluated using three complementary metrics: Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)
33
, Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR)
1
, and Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)
22
. Fidelity measures how well non-target regions of the image are preserved after editing.

LPIPS measures perceptual similarity using deep feature representations and is sensitive to high-level texture and structural changes; lower LPIPS values indicate greater perceptual fidelity. PSNR quantifies pixel-wise reconstruction quality, where higher values correspond to lower distortion. SSIM evaluates structural consistency by comparing luminance, contrast, and spatial structure between images, with higher values indicating stronger structural preservation. In the context of unintended edit mitigation, improved fidelity is therefore characterized by
lower LPIPS
and
higher PSNR and SSIM
values.
Importantly, because the target concept region is intentionally modified during editing, including it in fidelity evaluation would conflate desired edits with unintended distortions; therefore, the edited region is explicitly excluded from all fidelity computations.

Specifically, the bounding box localization provided by Gemini-2.5-Flash is used to identify the spatial extent of the detected unsafe concept and exclude this region from evaluation. All metrics are computed on the complement of this bounding box, isolating background regions that should remain unchanged.

denotes the edited image, and the subscript

6.4
Human Moderation Evaluation

To complement automated detection metrics, a human moderation audit is conducted to assess whether policy violating content remains recognizable to human annotators (in the original and
ReVision
edited images). Following prior work, human judgments were treated as a validation mechanism for recognizability rather than a scalable replacement for automated evaluation, as exhaustive human annotation does not scale to large benchmarks
10
.

A stratified subset of 36 images sampled from the full 245-image benchmark was evaluated. The subset includes both single-concept (26 images) and multi-concept (10 images) scenarios and is balanced across conditions (18 original images generated by the undefended model and 18 images edited using

). To avoid anchoring effects, paired pre-/post-edit versions of the same underlying image are explicitly excluded.
Annotations were collected in a controlled lab setting using a custom web-based interface. Participants were second-year undergraduate computer science students who volunteered and were not otherwise involved in this research. All annotators were presented with an informed consent form and a content warning indicating that images may contain nudity, violence, or other sensitive material. Participation was voluntary, and no personally identifying information was collected. In total, 35 participants took part in the study.

For each image, annotators independently indicated the presence of five moderation-relevant categories aligned with the detector scope: (A) recognizable copyrighted fictional characters, (B) drugs or alcohol, (C) weapons or violence, (D) public figures, and (E) nudity or sexualized content. 7
Possibility of Attacks on
ReVision

The proposed pipeline begins with Gemini-2.5-Flash, which performs concept-level auditing on generated or edited images. Consequently, any successful attack against
ReVision
must first induce failures in the perception capabilities of this VLM, such as causing it to misclassify an unsafe concept or fail to detect its presence altogether. In practice, this would require the image generation model deployed by the model provider to produce images that are adversarial with respect to the downstream VLM.
Prior work
9
has demonstrated that adversarial examples can be constructed against VLM’s using diffusion-based generation or editing techniques, particularly when the attacker has access to model parameters, gradients, or well-matched surrogate models. However, such attacks are typically developed under open-model assumptions and rely on iterative optimization against the target model or a closely aligned proxy. In the proposed setting, the VLM is closed-source and accessed only through its outputs, meaning that adversarial examples would need to arise indirectly through the deployed generative model itself, substantially constraining the attacker’s ability to reliably induce targeted failures.
Furthermore, transferring adversarial attacks from open-source VLMs to closed source models has been shown to be difficult in practice
23
. Although we do not rule out the possibility of such attacks, existing evidence suggests that adversarial examples crafted against open-source VLMs exhibit limited transferability to closed models, making this attack vector challenging under realistic deployment conditions.

8
Deployment Considerations and Future Work

Post-hoc safety mechanisms operate after image generation and introduce additional inference-time latency. In our experiments, Stable Diffusion 3.5 Turbo serves as the baseline user-facing latency, while applying
ReVision
adds overhead due to semantic analysis and localized editing.
In single-concept scenarios, invoking the Gemini API for concept detection and prompt generation adds approximately 1–2 seconds, while the subsequent LOCATEdit-based editing stage requires an additional 1–2 seconds on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. In multi-concept scenarios involving up to four unsafe concepts, Gemini analysis requires approximately 6–7 seconds, and the
ReVision
editing stage takes around 4–5 seconds in total, reflecting the cost of sequentially performing multiple targeted edits within a single image.
This overhead is partly a consequence of a deliberate design choice. While this additional latency may impact interactive use cases, it is acceptable for provider-side safety enforcement, where reliability and policy compliance take precedence over minimal response time.

ReVision
performs post-hoc editing using a lightweight U-Net based diffusion model (Stable Diffusion 1.5), rather than reusing the larger Stable Diffusion 3.5 Turbo generator. This choice significantly reduces memory requirements and enables efficient, modular deployment of safety editing without duplicating the computational cost of large generative models. However, the architectural mismatch may occasionally introduce minor stylistic inconsistencies in edited regions.

Reducing inference overhead and improving edit quality are important directions for future work. Recent multi-edit diffusion frameworks that enable multiple concept edits within a single pass
35
could improve efficiency in multi-concept settings. Additionally, adapting localized editing to transformer-based diffusion architectures may improve stylistic consistency while retaining advantages of post-hoc safety.

9
Conclusion

We presented
ReVision
, a training-free, prompt-driven, post-hoc safety framework for visual generative models that performs concept-level detection and targeted replacement of policy-violating content at inference time. By combining generic VLM-based semantic auditing with instance-consistent spatial gating,
ReVision
addresses a key usability failure in post-hoc editing that arises from imprecise spatial localization in complex scenes.
Through extensive evaluation, we show that
ReVision
reliably suppresses unsafe concepts while preserving non-target visual regions, as measured by background fidelity, semantic alignment, category-specific detectors, and human moderation. Unlike approaches that require retraining, rely on concept-specific masks, or focus on isolated violations,
ReVision
operates modularly as a last-line, provider-side defense and scales across diverse safety categories via policy prompts under realistic black-box auditing assumptions.
Overall, this work highlights that deployable safety enforcement for generative systems requires not only detecting unsafe content, but also preserving benign content to avoid weakening defenses in practice.

Gemini can generate segmentation masks

We note that Gemini-2.5-Flash also supports prompt-based generation of pixel-level segmentation masks for detected objects or concepts
4
4

4

https://developers.googleblog.com/conversational-image-segmentation-gemini-2-5/
. In principle, such masks could enable direct inpainting-based edits using off-the-shelf image editing models. However, in our experiments, we observed that Gemini’s zero-shot segmentation output was inconsistent across images, frequently failing to return accurate or complete masks for policy-violating concepts. By contrast, Gemini reliably produced bounding-box localizations across all images evaluated in our study. For this reason, we adopt bounding boxes as a robust and consistent form of spatial supervision, which integrates naturally with latent-space gating while avoiding reliance on brittle pixel-level segmentation.

This limitation is further evident at the system level, where Gemini’s segmentation responses occasionally yield structurally incomplete outputs, specifically masks without valid confidence scores, which leads to downstream parsing failures. For example, when processing Gemini-generated segmentation outputs via the supervision framework, we encountered runtime errors indicating empty confidence arrays (e.g., “confidence must be a 1D np.ndarray with shape (1,), but got shape (0,)”), despite valid bounding boxes being returned. Such failures prevent reliable construction of segmentation-based detections and reinforce our decision to rely exclusively on bounding-box supervision for stable and reproducible editing behavior.
