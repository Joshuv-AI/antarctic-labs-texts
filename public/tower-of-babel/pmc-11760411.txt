Incidence of Tooth-related Soft Tissue and Intraosseous Pathologies in Pediatric Population: A Retrospective Analysis of 3,398 Cases
Authors: Fazulunnisa Begum, Deepak Pandiar, Mebin G Mathew, Reshma P Krishnan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo assess the prevalence of tooth-related, soft tissue, and intraosseous pathologies in the pediatric population (0–17 years) in a South Indian dental teaching institution and to compare the results with previous literature.Materials and methodsClinical data from the pedodontics department and histopathological records from the oral pathology department were analyzed from the year 2010 to 2022 and grouped into two major categories: group I, abnormalities of teeth, and group II, intraosseous and mucosal/soft tissue lesions. The data were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, and descriptive analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 26.ResultsIn total, 3,398 children under 17 years of age were included. Overall, tooth-associated anomalies were far more common than intraosseous and mucosal lesions. Males were more commonly affected than females. Regarding the laterality of the lesions, the right side was more commonly affected, with the mandible being the most common site. Among tooth-associated anomalies, enamel hypoplasia was the most common. In group II, mucocele, followed by dentigerous cyst, were the most frequently occurring pathologies, with pediatric malignancies being the least common lesions.ConclusionThe pediatric population deserves more attention and care in treatment planning and execution. The presentation of retrospective data from multiple centers across different ethnic groups and geographic areas is warranted for updates.How to cite this articleBegum F, Pandiar D, Mathew MG, et al. Introduction

The pediatric population differs from adults not only in the incidence and prevalence of pathologies but also emotionally and behaviorally.1 This group needs to be managed with great tenderness and care. A variety of oral and maxillofacial pathologies are encountered in children under 17 years of age, and the incidence varies with ethnicity and shows a marked geographic pattern. The oral tissues (teeth, soft tissue, or bone) of children are widely, or sometimes severely, affected by numerous genetic, environmental, vaccination schedules and statuses, biological, and systemic factors, resulting in a range of congenital, inflammatory, or neoplastic conditions.2,3 Disease processes, particularly those affecting the dentition, are also influenced by the mother's nutritional status, systemic illness, and infectious diseases.4

Early diagnosis and prompt management of these oral lesions are thus of utmost importance for pedodontists and pathologists alike, though often challenging. Previous studies from India and other nations have shown contrasting findings. Patil et al., in a study of a West Indian pediatric cohort, reported salivary gland lesions as the most common pathologies in children.5 Similarly, mucoceles were the most common lesions in a multicenter study from Brazil.4 Conversely, inflammatory lesions were frequently reported in a study from Iran and another Brazilian report.6,7 Odontogenic cysts were most frequently reported in Isfahan, illustrating marked variation in the pediatric population based on location and ethnicity.8

As aforementioned, lesions of different etiologies and pathogeneses may be encountered in pediatric clinics. It is therefore essential to present and report the prevalence of pediatric oral pathologies from various geographic locations and ethnicities to gain a thorough understanding of these lesions, aiding in diagnosis. Furthermore, the data may serve as a baseline reference for updating recent literature. This paper presents data from a leading private tertiary healthcare center in South India, comprising 3,398 children aged between 0 and 17 years, emphasizing the varied prevalence based on ethnic groups.

Materials and Methods

Approval from the Institutional Ethics Board was obtained before the commencement of this retrospective study (IHEC/SDC/FACULTY/23/OPATH/239), which included data from the departments of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry and Oral Pathology and Microbiology. Thirteen years of data were included (January 2010 to December 2022) using patients' medical records and the institutional electronic database. All children aged 0–17 years were included. Cases with inadequate details and unconfirmed histopathological diagnoses were excluded.

Clinical and histopathological data were analyzed for age, gender, site laterality, soft tissue lesions, tooth-related developmental abnormalities, and intraosseous pathologies in the pediatric population. According to the diagnoses obtained from histopathology reports, pediatric oral and maxillofacial lesions were grouped into two major categories: group I, abnormalities of teeth, and group II, intraosseous and mucosal/soft tissue lesions. The latter group included odontogenic cysts and tumors, pulpal and periapical diseases, infections, epithelial pathology, soft tissue pathology, salivary gland lesions, hematological diseases, bone lesions, and dermatological diseases.

Data were tabulated and entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (2021), and statistical and descriptive analyses of the obtained data were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 26).

Results

In total, 3,398 children and adolescents aged 17 years or less were recruited for the present study over a period of thirteen years. Tooth-associated anomalies (3,156, 92.88%) were far more common than intraosseous and mucosal lesions (7.12%). Overall, males were more commonly affected than females (1,831 males: 1,566 females). A detailed analysis of the results is explained in the subsequent sections.

Group I (3,156 cases): Dental anomalies constituted 92.88% of the cases. Of these, 1,705 (out of 3,156 cases) were seen in males, constituting 54.02% of the cases, while the remaining 45.98% affected females (1,451/3,156). The cases were further divided into three age-groups: 0–6, 7–12, and 13–17 years. The largest number of children was seen in the 7–12 age-group. The youngest age-group displayed a preponderance of females. The most common pathologies were enamel hypoplasia (1,811, 57.38%), followed by peg-shaped lateral incisors (342/3,156), talon cusps (333/3,156), and mesiodens (308/3,156) in the same decreasing order. Most cases of enamel hypoplasia were the result of fluorosis, followed by localized hypoplastic defects secondary to trauma. Ten cases of amelogenesis imperfecta were seen. Transposition of teeth (5/3,156) and macrodontia (15/3,156) were the least frequently encountered developmental disorders affecting the teeth. Group II (242 cases of 7,098 biopsies, 3.41%): Similar to tooth-related anomalies, intraosseous and other mucosal and soft tissue lesions were more commonly seen in males (M:F ratio: 1.104:1). There were 27 children in the 0–6 years age-group, 93 children in the 7–12 age-group, and the remaining 122 were in the 13–17 years age-group. With respect to laterality, the majority of the cases affected the right side (127 cases/242), followed by the left side (83/242) and the midline (29/242). Three cases had bilateral presentations (one case each of lichen planus, candidiasis, and herpes simplex ulceration). Intraosseous lesions of the mandible were most commonly seen (95/242, 39.26%), followed by the labial mucosa (44/242) and equal predilection for the maxilla and gingiva (37 cases each). The chin region and incisive papilla region were the least commonly affected (one case each). The detailed figures are shown in Table 2.

The soft tissue lesions were the most frequently affected pathologies (23.31%, n = 54), followed by salivary gland pathologies (20.25%), odontogenic tumors (17.78%), odontogenic cysts (16.12%), and periapical pathologies (12.8%). Other uncommon lesions included those of epithelial origin (2.07%), bone pathologies (4.13%), infectious diseases (1.65%), dermatological manifestations (0.41%), hematological malignancies (acute leukemia, 0.41%), and false intrabony cysts (2.07%). There were 44 cases of mucoceles (mucous extravasation phenomenon, including 2 ranulas of the floor of the mouth), followed by dentigerous cysts (21 cases), 20 radicular cysts, and 19 ameloblastomas. Pediatric malignancies were uncommon, with 2 cases each of osteosarcoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma, followed by 1 case each of Ewing sarcoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, and acute leukemia. Discussion

As mentioned previously, pediatric and geriatric patients constitute the most vulnerable groups, requiring greater care, tenderness, and a gentle approach during diagnosis and the development of a treatment strategy. Previous studies have shown a wide variation in the prevalence of oral lesions, most likely due to geographic regions, ranging from 5.5 to 24.8%.6 There is limited information regarding the prevalence of developmental disturbances of teeth in the literature. This study was designed to present the clinicopathological profile of 3,398 children younger than 17 years of age, including the prevalence of developmental anomalies of teeth, intraosseous lesions, and mucosal/soft tissue lesions of the head and neck region.

We found that the prevalence of enamel hypoplasia was highest among the children, with dental fluorosis being the most common subtype. Saravanan et al. in a previous study from Tamil Nadu found a prevalence of 31.4% for dental fluorosis in children aged 5–12 years, which was esthetically objectionable in 2.1% of cases.9 Another study group from Salem district of Tamil Nadu found a comparatively higher value of 56.9%.10 Dental fluorosis was also the most common dental anomaly in neighboring states of Southern India.11 The high prevalence of fluorosis could be attributed to the increased levels of fluoride in drinking water, with many endemic areas in the region. We found that males were more commonly affected than females, which corroborates previous studies. Apart from enamel hypoplasia, a higher prevalence was noted for peg-shaped maxillary lateral incisors (highest in the 7–12 years age-group), talon cusps, and mesiodens in our study. In a previous study from our institute comprising an adult population, macrodontia was the most common anomaly noted.12 In contrast, hyperdontia, dilaceration, and taurodontism were the most common anomalies reported from Dharwad, Karnataka.13 Stressing the effect of geographic areas on the prevalence of developmental abnormalities of teeth, contrasting results were yielded by a study conducted by Patil et al., in the northern part of India, where congenitally missing teeth (16.7%) were most commonly found.5 Hypodontia and microdontia were also the most frequent defects in a Brazilian cohort of patients with Down syndrome.14

A literature review indicated that the prevalence of oral and maxillofacial pathologies was as low as 2.6% in a study group from Southern India.15 A higher prevalence has been reported globally.3,5,6,8,16,17 This variation could be attributed to two major factors: first, the different genetic makeup among various ethnic groups, and second, differences in methodology for obtaining the data, particularly the cutoff age value. In many studies, the data were presented up to 18 years of age,3,6,8 while, similar to the present study, 17 years was set as the cutoff value.5 Other studies had cutoff values of 16 years or less,16 and 19 years.17 This warrants a universal cutoff value to avoid heterogeneity in global data. Regardless, these studies provide a robust baseline for devising treatment needs for this specialized group, which differs from the adult population.

With regard to oral lesions, Torabi-Parizi et al. found that inflammatory/reactive lesions were the most common in a cohort of the Iranian population from Kerman; pyogenic granuloma and peripheral giant cell granuloma (PGCG) were the most prevalent.7 Similarly, PGCG, along with peripheral ossifying fibroma (POF), constituted the most common reactive pathoses in another Iranian study; however, they reported odontogenic cysts (radicular cyst and dentigerous cyst) as more frequent lesions.3,8 Aghbali et al. reported inflammatory lesions most frequently in East Azerbaijan (Iran), highlighting variation in different geographic regions of the same country.18 We reported fibromas, pyogenic granulomas, vascular abnormalities, myofibromatosis, lipomas, peripheral ossifying fibromas, and giant cell fibromas as the most common soft tissue lesions in the same decreasing trend of occurrence. The youngest child affected by giant cell fibroma was three years of age.19 Reactive lesions of the gingiva arise secondary to local irritants such as poor oral hygiene; thus, the children (and their parents) attending pediatric clinics should be trained and sensitized regarding oral hygiene measures to prevent and reduce the incidence of reactive pathologies. Similar to Iranian reports, we found overall soft tissue/reactive lesions to be most prevalent (54/242); however, considering the individual lesions, mucoceles were the predominant lesions. Except for a few cross-sectional studies, the mucous extravasation phenomenon was ubiquitous, particularly in young boys.1,2,5,6,17 A higher prevalence of mucoceles in the younger age-group could be ascribed to their frequent engagement in sports and outdoor activities. Lip biting secondary to psychological stress and anxiety has also been hypothesized.20 Owczarek-Drabińska et al. commonly reported recurrent aphthous ulceration in boys.21

Odontogenic tumors, cysts, and associated lesions are a heterogeneous group of lesions seen intraosseously or extra-osseously; they can literally affect any age-group and are derived from the odontogenic apparatus.22 A slight variation was noted in the preponderance of odontogenic cysts and tumors among different ethnic groups. We found that ameloblastoma was the most common odontogenic neoplasm, followed by odontoma; out of 19 cases, 9 were unicystic. These findings match the results of Nithya et al. and Ajayi et al.23,24 Contrary to this, most studies report odontoma as the most common odontogenic pathology. Although it is considered a benign mixed odontogenic lesion, many authorities still regard odontoma as a hamartomatous lesion alongside adenomatoid odontogenic tumor and ameloblastic fibroma. It is wise to consider these as benign odontogenic tumors, as also updated recently by the WHO 2022 classification of odontogenic tumors, until the controversy subsides with adequate proof. The lower incidence of odontoma could also be attributed to its asymptomatic behavior, and once excised, not all lesions are submitted for histopathological evaluation. One case previously reported from our institute involved a 7-year-old boy who presented with a large mass that yielded 526 denticles, questioning their hamartomatous nature.25 Similar to the literature, where radicular cysts and dentigerous cysts have undisputedly been reported as the most common inflammatory and developmental cysts, we found the same results. Epithelial lesions, infectious diseases, and lichen planus were the least prevalent lesions. In a retrospective analysis, Sklavounou-Andrikopoulou et al. reported only two cases of lichen planus in Greek children.2

Malignancies of childhood deserve a brief discussion. The malignancies seen in our cohort included osteosarcoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, Ewing sarcoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, and acute leukemia. In corroboration with the existing literature, a recent systematic review on salivary gland tumors also found mucoepidermoid carcinoma to be the most common malignant neoplasm in children.26 Patil et al. reported five malignant neoplasms, among which two were epithelial in origin (one case each of squamous cell carcinoma and verrucous carcinoma), a very rare incidence.5 In contrast, Burkitt lymphoma was most common in the Brazilian population17,27 and rhabdomyosarcoma was the most common malignancy in North Indian children.23 Ten cases of lymphoma were reported by Aly et al. in Egyptian children.28 Thus, it may be believed that the malignancies of children show marked variation according to the reporting ethnic groups.

Although we presented one of the largest datasets comprising 3,398 children, there are some inherent limitations to hospital-based studies. First, many cases are lost to private practitioners, and second, not all biopsied or excised lesions are submitted for histopathological examination, suggesting that the actual prevalence could be higher. However, these findings could be considered as baseline data for devising further intervention programs and management of diseases in this most vulnerable age-group.

Conclusion

The pediatric population is one of the most common groups of patients we see in our daily practice. This group deserves more care and tenderness in treatment planning, which also requires adequate teaching and training of the parents or guardians. In a large dataset, we found that enamel hypoplasia was the most common dental defect, and mucoceles constituted the most frequent lesion of soft tissue. Presentation of retrospective data from multicenters among different ethnic groups and geographic areas is warranted for updating the literature.
