Radiotherapy Utilization and Treatment Continuity of Pediatric Patients With Brain Tumor in Egypt: A Decade of Data
Authors: Breana Wayne, Mohamed Saad Zaghloul, Mohamed El-Beltagy, Ahmed Ibrahim, Amal Refaat, Hala Taha, Rana Sameh, Chloe Teasdale, Amr S Soliman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEWe assessed radiotherapy (RT) utilization and completion among pediatric patients with brain tumor treated at Children's Cancer Hospital Egypt (CCHE).METHODSA retrospective analysis was conducted among pediatric patients with brain tumor treated at CCHE between 2009 and 2020. Patients were categorized on the basis of demographics, tumor types, RT utilization status (prescribed, not prescribed, and did not complete), and geographic place of residence (urban v rural). RT utilization was stratified by tumor type and geographic region and compared using chi-square tests.RESULTSAmong 3,977 pediatric patients with brain tumor, 2,327 (58.5%) were prescribed RT. The highest RT utilization was observed among patients with diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG; 98.2%) and germinoma (98.0%), while nonoptic low-grade glioma (LGG; 11.2%) and optic pathway glioma (OPG; 2.0%) had the lowest among patients prescribed RT, 2,264 (97.2%) completed treatment. The highest noncompletion was observed in patients with atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor (ATRT; 6.7%) and nonoptic LGG (9.7%). Urban patients had a higher rate of RT completion (98.3%) compared with rural patients (95.9%; P < .001).CONCLUSIONRT utilization and completion varied significantly by tumor type, reflecting evidence-based treatment protocols and tumor-specific challenges. Geographic disparities in RT completion underscore systemic health care inequities in Egypt. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions, including decentralized radiotherapy delivery models and public health strategies, to improve access to and adherence to radiotherapy among underserved populations.

INTRODUCTION

Brain tumors are among the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in children, with notable disparities in treatment access and outcomes between high-income countries (HICs) and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)1. In HICs, the advent of precision radiotherapy (RT) techniques, such as proton therapy and stereotactic radiosurgery, combined with advances in molecular diagnostics, has significantly improved survival rates and minimized late-stage toxicities in pediatric patients with brain cancer.2-4 However, in LMICs, like Egypt, access to timely and adequate radiotherapy remains a significant challenge.2,4,5 Health care infrastructure limitations, economic constraints, and the lack of specialized pediatric oncology centers are significant barriers that disproportionately affect rural and low-income populations.6,7

In Egypt, as in other parts of the world, brain tumors represent the most common solid tumors in children, accounting for approximately 25% of all pediatric cancers8. Recent advances in pediatric oncology care, such as the establishment of Children's Cancer Hospital Egypt 57357 (CCHE), have made substantial progress in expanding access to cancer treatment.9-11 CCHE's Radiation Oncology Department staff includes 12 qualified radiation oncologists. Six in training, seven qualified physicists, four in training, 11 radiation therapy technologists (RTTs), five in training, and 12 dedicated radiotherapy nurses. These are supported by dedicated pathologist staff, who provide diagnostic services and integration with digital pathology workflows. Since 2018, CCHE has offered a Pediatric Radiation Oncology fellowship in collaboration with Dana-Farber and Massachusetts General Hospital (Harvard University, Boston, MA).

The department is equipped with two advanced linear accelerators, one Cyberknife, two computed tomography (CT) simulators, one magnetic resonance (MR) simulator, and seven treatment planning systems (TPS). Proton beam therapy is currently under installation, with the first patient expected to be treated in October 2025. CCHE integrates advanced diagnostic technologies, including automated digital pathology systems, which reduce diagnostic turnaround time from an average of 14 days to approximately 24 hours. This also minimizes errors by applying digital imaging to secure archiving and research purposes.12,13 Additionally, CCHE employs precision-based radiotherapy techniques, including the CyberKnife system for stereotactic radiosurgery and Versa HD linear accelerators using Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy technology to optimize radiation delivery.12,13 CT and MR simulators, along with tools such as Catalyst and Clarity, are integrated into treatment planning workflows to enhance target accuracy and maximize patient outcomes.12,13

However, gaps in radiotherapy access and utilization persist, with available facilities centralized mainly in major urban centers.14 Delays in treatment initiation, combined with socioeconomic barriers, contribute to suboptimal outcomes for children with cancer, particularly for rural and underserved populations in Egypt.15

Globally, radiotherapy remains a cornerstone of pediatric brain tumor treatment, often used in combination with surgery and chemotherapy to improve treatment outcomes.16 The integration of modern radiotherapy with novel systemic therapies and precision imaging has further enhanced its role, offering the potential for improved efficacy with fewer side effects.16,17 Despite these advancements, the application of radiotherapy and its outcomes in LMICs, especially in Egypt, have remained understudied. Moreover, the impact of socioeconomic and health care infrastructure challenges on treatment delivery and completion is not well documented in this patient population.18-20

Understanding the current state of radiotherapy utilization is essential for addressing the gaps in care delivery and improving survival outcomes for children with brain tumors.21 This study evaluates the utilization of RT among pediatric brain tumor patients in Egypt and the completion rates among children receiving radiotherapy.

METHODS

Data Source and Study Population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on all pediatric patients with brain tumor treated at CCHE 57357 between 2009 and 2020. CCHE serves as the primary referral center for pediatric oncology in Egypt and nearby regions, providing free care funded entirely by charitable donations.13,22 The hospital follows clearly defined admission criteria: patients must be age 0-18 years, have not received prior cancer treatment, and be referred as suspected or confirmed cases of cancer.22,23 As a nonemergency hospital, CCHE operates on a first-come, first-served basis, with priority given to newly diagnosed cases. The treatment is considered free for patients, as the hospital relies heavily on donations to cover the cost of treatment, meaning most families do not pay directly for their child's care.22,23

The analysis included all pediatric patients <18 years at diagnosis with CNS tumors, specifically brain tumors. Data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical records system and written documents. Demographic variables included age at diagnosis, sex, and place of residence (classified by governorates). Clinical and treatment-related data included tumor type, year of diagnosis, treatment intent (curative, palliative, or salvage), and RT utilization. RT data encompassed utilization status (prescribed v not prescribed), dosage records, and completion status.

The place of residence was categorized as urban or rural on the basis of the patient's governorate of permanent residence.24 The classification followed Egypt's national definition, where urban governorates are those where agriculture is not the primary occupation. Additionally, approximate distances from each governorate to CCHE were calculated, ranging from 75 km to over 800 km, to contextualize travel burdens. Urban governorates included Cairo, Alexandria, Port Said, Giza, and Suez. Rural governorates included Sharkya, Dakahlya, Qalyoubia, Menia, Gharbya, Menoufya, Behera, Fayoum, Beny Swef, Sohag, Kafr El- Sheikh, Qena, Assyout, Damietta, Aswan, Ismaelya, Luxor, North Sinani, Red Sea, Matrouh, New Valley, South Sinai, and outside of Egypt. A standardized mapping of governorates to their respective urban and rural classifications was created for consistency in geographic variation analyses.

Tumor diagnoses were categorized using the CCHE Central Protocol and Research Review and Monitoring Office CNS tumor classification. Subtypes were grouped into clinically relevant categories, including low-grade glioma (LGG), optic pathway glioma (OPG), embryonal tumors (medulloblastoma, pineloblastoma and formerly named peripheral neuroectodermal tumor [PNET]), high-grade glioma (HGG), diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG), atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor (ATRT), ependymoma, craniopharyngioma, choroid plexus tumors, meningioma and schwannoma, non–germ cell germinoma, germinoma, pineal body tumors, and a category labeled others for rare or unclassified subtypes. Tumor grouping was manually performed according to established protocol classifications to ensure consistency and accuracy.2

RT utilization was categorized into three groups on the basis of treatment records. The first group, not prescribed RT, included patients for whom RT was not part of the treatment protocol on the basis of clinical guidelines. The second group, completed prescribed RT, included patients who received the full RT regimen as prescribed. The third group, did not complete prescribed RT, included patients for whom RT was indicated but who did not complete the prescribed course because of treatment refusal, complications, disease progression, or loss of follow-up. RT status was cross-referenced with the hospital's radiotherapy dosage records to ensure accuracy in classification. Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics, including demographics, tumor types, and RT utilization, were summarized as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Differences in RT utilization across tumor types were assessed using chi-square tests. The geographic distribution of RT, chemotherapy, and surgical treatment utilization is described in terms of frequencies and percentages of children receiving each treatment modality in each Egyptian Governorate. The proportion of patients prescribed RT who completed or did not complete treatment was also assessed by tumor category, with chi-square tests conducted to determine statistical significance for each tumor type. To investigate disparities in RT completion between urban and rural patients, all patients prescribed RT were categorized based on place of residence (urban v rural) according to Egypt's national classification and compared using a chi-square test. For statistical tests, P values < .05 were considered significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python software (version 3.8).

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at Washington University in St Louis, MO, and the CCHE in Egypt. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study, and all patients were deidentified to maintain confidentiality.

The proportion of patients prescribed RT varied significantly by tumor type (Table 2). Patients with tumors known to be highly sensitive to radiotherapy were most likely to have been prescribed RT. Specifically, 98.0% of children with germinoma, 98.2% with DIPG, and 94.5% of patients with PNET received RT as part of their treatment plan. Conversely, tumors, where RT is typically avoided, had markedly lower rates of RT use—among patients with LGG and OPG, 11.2% and 2.1%, respectively, received RT, consistent with clinical protocols that prioritize alternative therapies to preserve neurocognitive function. Among the 2,327 pediatric patients with brain tumor prescribed RT, completion rates differed by geographic background. Using the hospital registry data, urban patients achieved a completion rate of 98.3%, compared with 95.9% among rural patients (P < .001). Although Table 3 summarizes treatment distribution (RT, chemotherapy, and surgery) by governorate, these completion rates were calculated directly from the RT data set to capture overall disparities between urban and rural patients. The primary reasons for noncompletion (Table 4) included treatment-related complications (35.1%) and disease progression or loss to follow-up (35.2%), which were the predominant barriers, followed by death (17.8%). These data were extracted from hospital records and validated through cross-references with patient treatment plans. ATRT had the highest proportion of patients with noncompletion (6.7%, P = .170), whereas, among children with some other types of tumors, including germinoma and pineal body tumors, no children were reported to have noncompletion. By contrast, patients with LGG demonstrated a significantly higher noncompletion (9.7%, P < .001), which may have resulted from challenges in RT adherence.

DISCUSSION

This study describes the utilization of RT for pediatric patients with brain tumor over more than a decade in an LMIC setting. The variability in RT utilization highlights the intricate interplay between tumor-specific protocols, patient demographics, and systemic health care challenges. Previous estimates of RT utilization for pediatric CNS tumors were previously reported as 58%,28 which is almost identical to our finding of 59%. This complexity underscores the necessity for tailored interventions to enhance equitable access to RT and improve adherence to treatment protocols.

The use of RT for aggressive tumors and/or those with no effective treatment options except RT, including germinoma (98.0%) and DIPG (98.2%), reflects their reliance on RT as a critical therapeutic modality. These figures underscore CCHE's adherence to evidence-based protocols consistent with international standards, ensuring optimal care for high-risk cases. However, the markedly low RURs for nonoptic LGG (11%) and OPG (2%) reveal an equally important focus on limiting radiation-related long-term toxicities. These findings align with global trends in pediatric oncology, where reducing the late effects of therapy is paramount.21,28,29

The overall very low proportion (3%) of noncompletion among pediatric patients with brain tumor prescribed RT at this Egyptian health center indicates a relatively comprehensive system for delivering care. However, the higher noncompletion observed in tumors such as ATRT (7%), LGG (advanced cases, as the hospital protocol entailed different lines of chemotherapy until progression that endangers brain function; 9.7%), and DIPG (3.0%) highlights specific vulnerabilities among these patients. These gaps are often exacerbated by the highly aggressive nature of the disease, which may deteriorate the patient's general condition, as well as logistical barriers, including travel distances and financial constraints, particularly for rural patients, who constituted over two thirds of the cohort. Such disparities underscore the need for targeted interventions to address health care inequities. Furthermore, urban patients had significantly higher RT completion rates compared with rural patients (98.3% v 95.9%, P < .001). These findings suggest that travel burden centers and socioeconomic barriers likely contribute to lower completion rates among rural patients.

Geographic challenges continue to be a significant factor influencing RT access. Patients from rural and remote regions, such as South Sinai and the Red Sea, experience disproportionately lower RT utilization, reflecting broader systemic inequities prevalent in LMICs. Despite CCHE's remarkable achievements in centralizing pediatric oncology care, these findings highlight the limitations of a single-center model in meeting the diverse needs of a geographically dispersed population. Across the governorates included in this study, the number of megavoltage machines (MVMs) ranged from 1 to 15, with the highest concentrations in Cairo and Alexandria. Many governorates had only one or two MVMs serving large populations, often without pediatric-specific capacity. Distances from the patient's home governorate to CCHE ranged from 75 km to over 800 km (Table 4). These findings highlight how physical distance and limited radiotherapy infrastructure exacerbate access challenges, particularly in rural and lower-resourced areas, even when centralized care is available in the capital.

This issue contradicts the idea of establishing a single well-equipped neuro-oncology center in LMIC and emphasizes the need for more than one to serve the far-distant locations away from the capital city.30 This decentralization policy is of utmost importance in large population countries like Egypt.30

This study is subject to several limitations inherent in its retrospective design. The analysis relied on hospital records, which may not fully capture reasons for treatment discontinuation, especially in cases of loss to follow-up. Furthermore, the study's single-center setting limits the generalizability of its findings to other regions or institutions within Egypt or other LMICs. Finally, the lack of comprehensive long-term follow-up data restricts our ability to assess late outcomes for patients who discontinued treatment or received alternative care. Although the study hospital serves as a referral center for pediatric cancers in Egypt, universities in most major cities in Egypt also provide pediatric cancer management. Therefore, patients who sought care at the study hospitals do not represent all patients of their respective governorates. Finally, there may be reasons for not completing radiotherapy treatment that are not captured after patients leave the hospital and do not complete the treatment.

In conclusion, this study offers critical insights into the utilization and completion rates of RT among pediatric patients with brain tumor treated at CCHE. The findings highlight the hospital's adherence to evidence-based protocols while addressing the challenges specific to a LMIC setting. However, RT utilization for aggressive tumors was found to align with international standards, and disparities in access and completion persist, disproportionately affecting rural patients. These inequities underscore the urgent need for systemic reforms, including the decentralization of care, strengthening supportive services, and leveraging telemedicine. By building on its strengths and addressing these challenges, CCHE has the potential to further its mission of delivering equitable, high-quality oncology care and improving outcomes for pediatric patients across its building satellites, serving children in diverse socioeconomic and geographic contexts.

Building on these findings, there is a critical need to address the barriers that limit access to and adherence to radiotherapy. Expanding decentralized care delivery through establishing regional neuro-oncology centers could significantly reduce the geographic and logistical burdens faced by rural patients. Such centers could bring advanced treatment closer to underserved populations, improving equity in access and outcomes. Additionally, robust supportive care programs, including transportation subsidies and intensifying housing, should be implemented to mitigate the financial strain on families traveling from remote areas. Although no parent housing is dedicated to CCHE, many charities offer suitable houses for CCHE patients and their parents free of charge. Developing and maintaining a national database of all pediatric cancer centers can also improve understanding of pediatric cancer management and treatment patterns in Egypt. The introduction of treatment with proton beam radiotherapy (PBT; that is already installed in CCHE and expected to treat the first patient on October-November 2025) will improve much the pediatric cancer care, decrease their side effect, and increase their compliance of radiotherapy schedules. The start of PBT in Egypt is expected to escalate the cancer care particularly for children.

Integrating telemedicine into follow-up protocols presents another promising avenue for addressing gaps in continuity of care, particularly for patients at risk of losing follow-up. Telehealth platforms can facilitate regular check-ins, provide patient education, and support remote monitoring, especially for those with limited access to specialized care. Furthermore, prospective multicenter studies conducted across Egypt and similar LMICs could provide more comprehensive insights into systemic barriers and inform national strategies to optimize care delivery. International collaborations also help establish RT utilization and outcomes benchmarks, fostering evidence-based policy reforms to promote equitable access.

By addressing these limitations and implementing strategic interventions, CCHE, and similar institutions can continue to lead the way in pediatric neuro-oncology and other pediatric oncology specialties, ensuring that life-saving treatments reach all children, regardless of their geographic or socioeconomic circumstances. Addressing the critical shortage of trained professionals in radiotherapy—including radiation oncologists, medical physicists, and technicians—is essential to scaling up safe and effective treatment delivery across Egypt and Africa. Workforce limitations not only constrain existing facilities but also hinder the expansion of decentralized radiotherapy services needed to meet regional demand. CCHE, in collaboration with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), acts and collaborates with most African countries, where 29 countries of the 54 countries are deficient in any radiotherapy facilities, and many others have few radiation machines that serve millions of populations.28 CCHE training programs are available for radiation oncologists, physicists, RTTs, and radiation nurses from Egypt and Africa to raise the standard of radiotherapy services.
