Psychological wellbeing of eunuchs and other individuals with interests in genital altering procedures
Authors: Erik Wibowo, Thomas W Johnson, Jamie Agapoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Eunuch Archive (EA) is an online community where individuals with interests in genital ablation share information, personal experiences, and social support. This study compared psychological wellbeing among individuals with different genital ablation interests and examined patterns and motivations for engaging with the EA.MethodsAn anonymous online survey was posted on the EA website. Participants were categorized into 4 groups: individuals with genital ablation fantasies without a desire for surgery (Fantasy), individuals desiring orchiectomy but not having undergone the procedure (Aspiring), individuals who had undergone orchiectomy and were receiving androgen replacement therapy (Androgen Eunuchs), and individuals who had undergone orchiectomy without androgen replacement therapy (Eunuchs). Psychological wellbeing was assessed using validated measures of anxiety and depression (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale), insomnia (Insomnia Severity Index), and loneliness (Three-Item Loneliness Scale). Group differences were analyzed using ANOVA and chi-square tests, and regression analyses examined associations between reasons for visiting the EA and psychological outcomes.ResultsThe final sample included 338 participants (mean age 51.1 ± 15.7 years). Significant group differences were observed for loneliness and anxiety, but not for depression or insomnia. Individuals in the Eunuchs group reported lower loneliness and anxiety than participants in the Fantasy and Aspiring groups. Frequency of EA use was similar across groups, although motivations differed. Participants in the Fantasy group more frequently reported reading stories, whereas Aspiring individuals and Eunuchs more often sought medical information and social connection. Posting stories was associated with depressive symptoms (OR = 2.3, P = .007), while seeking connection with others was associated with anxiety (OR = 2.0, P = .007), depressive symptoms (OR = 2.0, P = .010), and insomnia symptoms (OR = 2.0, P = .004).DiscussionPsychological wellbeing and motivations for engaging with the EA differed according to embodiment goals and surgical status. Individuals who had undergone orchiectomy without androgen replacement reported lower loneliness and anxiety than other groups. These findings highlight the importance of understanding online community engagement and psychosocial factors among individuals with genital ablation interests and may help clinicians provide more informed and non-stigmatizing care. Limitations include reliance on self-reported data from a single online community and the inability to verify participant responses.Keywords: eunuch, orchiectomy, psychological wellbeing, genital ablation, Eunuch Archive

Introduction

Some individuals have strong interests in genital ablation. Research suggests they comprise several diverse groups, including those who fantasize about genital ablation but do not necessarily want it (fantasizers), those who aspire to be eunuchs (Aspiring) or have become eunuchs by orchiectomy or other genital ablation procedures (eg, physical injuries, banding, and chemical injection into the testes).1 Eunuchs may be further differentiated by those who chose to be on physiological doses of androgens after surgery (Androgen Eunuchs) and those who do not (Eunuchs).2 Prior studies have described male-to-eunuch (MtE) gender dysphoria and body integrity dysphoria (BID) as 2 clinical frameworks that may be relevant to some eunuchs and aspiring eunuchs seeking genital ablation, although these categories are not exhaustive and do not encompass all individuals who identify as eunuchs.2–4

Individuals with MtE typically identify as eunuchs or an alternate gender (eg, genderqueer), seek testosterone suppression, and typically do not choose to be on a replacement dose of hormones.1 Explicit motivators for MtE individuals include freedom from sexual thoughts, lower reactive aggression, and what has been described as the “eunuch calm.”2,3

Individuals who have genital ablation but take replacement doses of androgens often identify as men and experience dysphoria related to the physical appearance or feel of their genitals. They typically do not desire to be free of the physical and/or emotional effects of testosterone. Diagnostically, they may be classified as having BID, which is characterized by a persistent discomfort or sense of inappropriateness of a non-disabled body part.2,5 Because of the differences between individuals with BID and MtE gender dysphoria, studies have often separated eunuchs on androgen replacement therapy (ART) and those who are not.2,6,7 These frameworks are not intended to be rigid, and not all individuals who identify as eunuchs or seek genital ablation necessarily meet criteria for either condition.

Fantasizers are distinct from eunuchs and aspiring eunuchs in that their interest in genital ablation is solely a sexual fantasy, and not related to an incongruence of gender identity.3 When their sexual fantasies impair daily function, they may meet criteria for a paraphilic disorder.8 Rarely, some may seek orchiectomy or other treatments as a means of controlling clinically significant symptoms.3

Among gender minorities, modern eunuchs in the Western world are a largely underrecognized group. Those who aspire to be eunuchs are particularly invisible as they may fear stigmatization if their desire for orchiectomy or genital ablation is known to others. Due to their heterogeneous embodiment goals, which are defined as the physical characteristics or bodily states individuals seek to achieve to align their bodies with their identity and personal preferences, and diverse gender expressions, they have remained largely outside the purview of health care professionals.9–11

While efforts have been made to characterize the dysphoria of modern eunuchs, little is known about their psychological wellbeing pre- and post-orchiectomy. It is generally known that some possible side effects of androgen deprivation are depression, anxiety, and insomnia.12–14 Interestingly, major depression did not worsen in a sample of eunuchs after surgery. In fact, minor depression and obsessive compulsive disorder improved in postsurgical eunuchs.15 This is in line with the research done in other sex or gender diverse groups that shows that mental health symptoms tend to improve with access to surgical interventions.16

Loneliness is an important negative predictor of psychological wellbeing. It is defined as a negative feeling that arises when someone’s social network does not match their expectations.17 Loneliness has been linked to other psychiatric conditions such as depression, anxiety, and insomnia.17,18 It can also impact physical health.17 Many individuals, who are geographically dispersed and have minority identities built around specific sex and gender interests, have used the internet for social networking.19 Individuals who fantasize about genital ablation and those who aspire to or have obtained genital altering procedures have similarly formed communities online.2,3

One online meeting place for eunuchs and others with exceptional interests in genital ablation was the Eunuch Archive (EA, www.eunuch.org), founded in 1996.20 This large peer-support community had discussion forums where registered users could post questions about genital ablation and respond to threads. EA also had an archive where fictional stories about “castration” were shared and posted for others to read. The website was moderated to remove potentially dangerous content such as solicitations for surgical procedures from unlicensed practitioners. After it closed in December 2024, following loss of several of its key moderators due to medical circumstances, its files were archived at the Kinsey Institute. A successor website, also titled Eunuch Archive (www.eunuch.co), organized by some of its active members was then founded to carry on the tradition of active moderation for accuracy.

Previous research suggests the EA attracted several distinct groups of individuals with interests in genital ablation including fantasizers, aspiring eunuchs, and eunuchs on and off ART.2,3,21 Earlier research referred to aspiring eunuchs as “wannabes.”7 Much work has been done to characterize the motivations, needs, and desires of these particular groups.2,3,21 What has not been explored much is the psychological wellbeing (ie, depression, anxiety, loneliness, and insomnia) of the different groups of individuals who visited the EA.

In this study, we hypothesize that there are differences in the psychological wellbeing between individuals with various interests in genital ablation. Based on a previous study, depressive symptoms may be lower among those who have elected for orchiectomy.15 Since the symptoms of depression often overlap and include other mental health variables like anxiety and insomnia, we anticipate these factors may be lower in individuals who have gone through surgery. We also hypothesize that the groups will differ in their reasons for visiting the EA in line with their interests, motivations, and embodiment goals. We expect the Fantasy group will be more likely to read stories than the other groups and the Aspiring group will be more likely to seek information about orchiectomy. Lastly, we hypothesize that the Eunuch groups will be more likely to post stories as this represents a form of community engagement.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Between March 2022 and July 2023, we posted an online survey on the EA website. This research study had been approved by both the Eunuch Archive Steering Committee and the Institutional Review Board of the California State University, Chico. We built the survey on the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) and the survey’s URL generated from REDCap was posted on the EA website. The landing page of the URL showed the study information. People could then decide to join or decline the study. The survey could only be accessed by those who consented to the study. The survey required approximately 30-45 min to complete. No compensation was offered or provided to participants.

Eight hundred and fifty individuals consented to the study. From these, we excluded some due to various reasons: (1) 6 were not biologically male (4 female, 2 intersex), (2) 84 did not indicate biological sex, (3) 88 did not indicate orchiectomy status, (4) 256 did not reach the end of the survey so their year of birth was blank and we could not confirm their age, (5) 27 answered their year of birth but it was more than ± 2 years different from their stated age at the beginning of the survey, (6) 16 were previously on androgen blockade but no longer on it, (7) 14 only had a desire for penectomy, (8) 13 were only interested in genital ablation for “academic reasons,” (9) 7 did not have clear castration status, and (10) 1 was penectomized only.

The total sample size for the current analyses was 338. Of these, 42 (12.4%) had received an orchiectomy and were on ART (Androgen Eunuchs), and 64 (18.9%) did not take ART (Eunuchs) after surgery, 166 (49.1%) had a desire for an orchiectomy but had not proceeded with the procedure (Aspiring), and 66 (19.5%) had only the fantasy of receiving an orchiectomy but had no desire to act on their fantasy (Fantasy).

Measures

Demographics

Participants completed standard demographic questions on age, education, income, gender, geographic location, and sexual attraction based on the Kinsey Scale (1948).22 Participants could choose one of the following options for their sexual attraction: “X-Asexual or nonsexual (no interest in sexual activity with others),” “0-Exclusively heterosexual with no homosexual attraction,” “1-Predominantly heterosexual, only incidentally homosexual,” “2-Predominantly heterosexual, but more than incidentally homosexual,” “3-Bisexual (Equal heterosexual and homosexual attraction),” “4-Predominantly homosexual, but more than incidentally heterosexual,” “5-Predominantly homosexual, only incidentally heterosexual,” and “6-Exclusively homosexual.” They were also asked about their use of hormone replacement therapy, recreational drugs, as well as whether they had chronic medical conditions.

EA use

Participants were asked various questions on their activities on the EA website. They were asked about their frequency of visiting EA: “How often do you visit the Eunuch Archive?” and the answer options were “more than once a day,” “once a day,” “several times a week,” “once a week,” and “rarely.” They were also asked “What are the reasons you access the Eunuch Archive?”. They could choose one or more of the following options: “read castration stories,” “post castration stories,” “finding more medical-related information about castration,” “connect with other people who have interest in castration topics,” and “other reasons.” Those who selected “other reasons” could elaborate their reason(s).

Psychological wellbeing

In this study, we included 4 scales related to psychological wellbeing. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).23 The scale consists of 7 items related to anxiety and another 7 related to depression in the past week. Each of these items can be rated on a scale from 0 to 3. Each subscale can have a score ranging from 0 to 21, with a higher score indicating worse anxiety or depressive symptoms.23 The internal consistency in our sample was 0.85 for the anxiety subscale and 0.83 for the depression subscale.

Insomnia symptoms in the past 2 weeks were assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index.24 Each item can be rated on a scale from 0 to 4, with a higher score indicating more severe insomnia symptoms. The proportions of participants with insomnia symptoms were categorized as done by Bastien et al.24 The internal consistency in our sample was 0.90.

Loneliness was measured using a short 3-item scale.25 The scale assessed the degree of social isolation and lack of companionship perceived by the participants, with a higher score indicating more loneliness. Each question was rated on a 3-point scale (1 = Hardly ever, 2 = Some of the time, and 3 = Often), where participants indicated how often they related to the stated question. Here, total scores of 1-3, 4-6, and 7-9 were categorized as low, medium, and high loneliness levels, respectively. The internal consistency of the loneliness scale in our sample was 0.90.

Data analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS (IBM, version 29). Data were compared based on surgical status: those with fantasy to receive an orchiectomy (Fantasy group), those wishing to receive an orchiectomy (the Aspiring group), and eunuchs (on ART and those not on ART). Continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey post hoc test. The proportions of participants with anxiety, depressive, and insomnia symptoms as well as loneliness levels were described.

We conducted 2 regression analyses. First, using linear regression, we determined how reasons of visiting EA were associated with the frequency for visiting EA. Second, we performed logistic regression to determine how the reasons for visiting EA were associated with the risk of having medium-high loneliness levels, anxiety, depressive, and insomnia symptoms, after controlling for age and groups. P < .05 was considered significant.

Psychological wellbeing

Figure 1 shows that there were significant differences in the levels of loneliness (F(3,329) = 3.7, P = .011, η2 = 0.033) and anxiety (F(3,320) = 5.4, P < .001, η2 = 0.048), but not in the levels of depression and insomnia, among the 4 groups. More specifically, the Eunuch group reported a lower level of loneliness than the Fantasy (P < .05) and Aspiring groups (P < .01). In addition, the Eunuch group reported lower anxiety than the Fantasy (P < .05) and Aspiring (P < .001) groups. There were similar proportions of participants with depressive (22.6%-29.6%) and insomnia (46.0%-58.5%) symptoms across groups. However, the Aspiring group had a higher proportion (43.9%) of participants with anxiety symptoms than the other 3 groups (22.2%-38.1%). In addition, 46.8% of the Castrated group reported medium-high level of loneliness, as compared to 78.6%-83.1% in the other 3 groups.

Activities on EA

As shown on Figure 2A, we did not find a group difference in the frequency of visiting EA. However, we found that the reasons of visiting EA (Figure 2B); ie, there were different proportions who read stories (χ2(3) = 9.5, P = .023), post stories (χ2(3) = 7.9, P = .048), find orchiectomy information (χ2(3) = 29.8, P < .001), and connect with other people (χ2(3) = 10.8, P = .013) on EA between groups. Specifically, the Fantasy group (71.2%) was more likely to read stories than the Aspiring (51.8%) and Eunuchs (46.9%) groups. The Eunuchs group (29.7%) was also more likely to post stories than the Androgen Eunuchs group (7.1%). Lastly, the Fantasy group (31.8%) was less likely to connect with other people on EA than the Eunuchs (57.8%) group. The average number of stories each group had written were comparable.

Discussion

Demographics

Similar to past samples,2,3 survey participants in this study were predominantly middle-aged, university educated, and of medium to high income. Nearly 20% of participants in this survey identified as eunuchs, although the majority identified as men. Nearly 6 in 10 identified as either bisexual or same-sex attracted. This finding is consistent with past studies on individuals with genital ablation interests.21

Many eunuchs choose not to go on ART after orchiectomy. In our sample, out of 106 eunuchs, 64 reported not being on ART. Past studies have suggested they may desire to be free from intrusive sexual thoughts and/or reactive aggression and achieve what has been described as the “eunuch calm.”2,3

For those who choose to be on ART, the reasons may be medical (eg, to prevent bone loss), emotional (eg, low mood without testosterone), or sexual (eg, to restore sex drive).15 Decisions regarding androgen replacement may also be influenced by gender identity, with some eunuchs who identify as men choosing testosterone therapy to maintain characteristics congruent with their gender.

Nearly one-third of participants reported bisexual attraction. Although one might assume that attraction to more than one sex would broaden opportunities for relationships and social connection, previous research suggests that bisexual individuals often experience greater loneliness and psychological distress than both heterosexual and gay/lesbian individuals.26 Proposed explanations include bisexual-specific minority stress, identity invalidation, and reduced perceived social support from both heterosexual and LGBTQ+ communities.26,27 These findings suggest that the relatively high proportion of bisexual individuals within our sample may represent an additional source of vulnerability to loneliness and other adverse mental health outcomes.

Psychological wellbeing

In this sample, older age was associated with lower risk of having anxiety symptoms. Some data suggest that the frequency of anxiety disorders is lower in older adults.28 Previous samples have indicated that eunuchs tend to receive their orchiectomies in the fourth or fifth decade of their lives,3 which may also explain the age related effect on anxiety observed.

The Eunuchs group had the highest proportion who visited the EA to connect with people (Figure 2B). This group was also more likely to post stories, reflecting a high level of online community engagement. Greater community engagement and social networking with other people may be associated with lower levels of loneliness. This theory is supported by the minority stress model, which identifies community connectedness as a mediating factor between distal/proximal stressors and negative mental health outcomes.29 Differences in androgen exposure may also have a mitigating effect on lonely thoughts, which may be tied into the experience of the “eunuch calm,” although this study was not designed to evaluate such relationships.2,3 There may also be an age effect at play. In previous studies, the average age of orchiectomy in eunuchs was in the mid-forties.30 Although loneliness is often assumed to increase with age, evidence suggests that loneliness may be equally or even more prevalent among younger adults.31

Another significant finding from our group comparisons was the higher levels of anxiety symptoms in the Aspiring group. Individuals in the Aspiring group desire to be eunuchs. They may experience MtE gender incongruence, dysphoria, or BID.3 As such, their efforts to conceal, mitigate, or manage that incongruence or dysphoria may be associated with elevated levels of anxiety or distress similar to that observed in other gender and sexually diverse groups.4,29

These findings have important clinical implications. As illustrated in the previous paper by Agapoff et al., regardless of the diagnostic differences between aspiring eunuchs and fantasizers, the clinical approach by the mental health professional should be the same.7 After addressing any safety concerns such as risk for genital self-injury, a careful assessment of embodiment goals, psychosocial concerns, and mental health symptoms should be gathered. In cases of diagnostic or clinical complexity, coordination among medical, psychiatric, endocrine, surgical, and psychosocial professionals may be helpful to address the diverse aspects of care that can arise throughout evaluation and treatment.4,7

Activities on EA

Most of the stories posted to the EA eroticize genital ablation.32 Given that the Fantasy group derives sexual arousal from “castration fantasies”, it is reasonable that they would be more likely to read stories on the EA than the Aspiring and Eunuch groups. The Fantasy group’s focus on sexual arousal rather than community connectedness may also explain why they are less likely to connect with other people on the EA. Another reason could be fear of stigmatization and discrimination by disclosing their fantasies to others. Expectedly, since they do not have a desire for orchiectomy, they are less likely to come to the EA specifically in search of information about it. Those who eroticize genital ablation may fear rejection by others given that such desires and interests could be classified as a paraphilia.

In this survey, a majority of both eunuchs and aspiring eunuchs sought information about “castration” on the EA. For eunuchs who have already had genital ablations, this increased engagement in the threads may be interpreted as a means of stewardship, mentorship, shared-experience, and or social networking. For example, eunuchs often alerted EA administrators of misinformation or potentially dangerous posts. For aspiring eunuchs, their higher engagement may reflect interests in information gathering and community connectedness.

The EA was one of very few online eunuch communities, and the only one consistently moderated for accuracy and safety. In this regard, it is not surprising that a desire to connect with people was associated with more frequent visits to the EA (Table 2). In studies of gender and sexually diverse communities, social supports are thought to be protective against adverse mental health outcomes,29,33 while offering a space to share medical resources, share sociopolitical concerns, which in turn strengthen the social networks.34 The successor EA (https://eunuch.co/) seeks to fill the role previously held by the original EA with consistent moderation for accuracy.

Predictors for visiting EA

A significant finding in this study was that posting stories on EA was linked to higher odds of having depressive symptoms. Writing and posting stories may be interpreted as a means of managing difficult emotions and an effort to establish a connection with other EA members as is often seen in other online communities.

In our sample, a desire to connect with people was associated with a higher frequency of visiting the EA (Table 2). A desire to connect with people on EA was also associated with elevated risks of anxiety, depression, and insomnia (Table 3). While this study cannot establish causality or determine the directionality of these associations, the observed relationship between community engagement and higher rates of anxiety, depression, and insomnia may reflect individuals with greater psychological distress seeking support through the EA. In this way, the EA may have functioned not only as a forum for sharing knowledge and ideas but also as a source of social connection and support for its members.

This finding has important implications. Individuals with interests in genital ablation may face stressors that have been associated with adverse mental health outcomes. They may struggle with concealment, an internalized negative self-image, and negative expectations. Some may endure non-affirmation, discrimination, and/or rejection by medical providers, family, and friends similar to other gender and sexual minorities.29,35 While this study is not equipped to evaluate the potential positive role of community connectedness on reducing negative mental health outcomes, the association between higher anxiety, depression, and insomnia in those seeking community may reflect attempts by EA visitors to seek social support and cope with psychological distress.

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations. The study relied on online data collection. As such, the accuracy of responses cannot be verified. Data collection was done from a single website dedicated to eunuchs and individuals interested in genital ablation and, thus, has a selection bias. For this reason, the findings may primarily reflect the characteristics of this specific online community rather than individuals with genital ablation interests more broadly. Psychological variables such as depression, anxiety, and loneliness were based on the completion of self-reported instruments used for screening purposes, rather than diagnosis or evaluation by healthcare professionals. Not all potential confounding variables (eg, psychiatric history, psychopharmacology use, and trauma history) may have been accounted for. The sensitivities of the anxiety and depression subscales of the HADS are approximately 0.8,36 so there is some possibility of false negatives. In addition, the HADS23 asks about feelings in the past week so multiple measurements at different times may give more accurate data on participants’ psychological wellbeing. We also recognize that the effect sizes for group comparisons were moderate. In addition, we did not collect any information on current psychopharmacological treatment, psychotherapy, personality traits, and/or personality disorders.

Conclusions

The EA attracted a diverse array of persons interested in the topic of genital ablation. A significant proportion of visitors experienced symptoms of anxiety, depression, insomnia, and loneliness. A desire to connect with others with similar interests was associated with visiting the EA. Eunuchs not on androgens visited the EA in the highest proportions and also experienced the lowest rates of loneliness. The high proportion of mental health comorbidities in this population is consistent with frameworks such as minority stress, and the lower rates of loneliness in the Eunuchs group may be related to differences in community engagement and other unmeasured factors. Taken together, these results suggest that the EA was an important resource for social support and knowledge for individuals with an interest in genital ablation.
