Learning with Local Search MCMC Layers
Germain Vivier-Ardisson gvivier@google.com, Mathieu Blondel mblondel@google.com, Axel Parmentier axel.parmentier@enpc.fr

Abstract
Integrating combinatorial optimization layers into neural networks has recently attracted significant research interest. However, many existing approaches lack theoretical guarantees or fail to perform adequately when relying on inexact solvers. This is a critical limitation, as many operations research problems are NP-hard, often necessitating the use of neighborhood-based local search heuristics. In this paper, we introduce a principled approach for learning with such inexact solvers. Inspired by the connection between simulated annealing and Metropolis-Hastings, we transform the problem specific neighborhood systems used in local search heuristics into proposal distributions, implementing MCMC on the set of feasible solutions. This allows us to construct differentiable, stochastic combinatorial layers and associated loss functions. Replacing an exact solver with a local search strongly reduces the computational burden of learning on many applications. We demonstrate our approach on a dynamic vehicle routing problem with time windows, binary vector and k k -subset prediction tasks, as well as a multi-dimensional knapsack decision-focused learning problem.

Models that combine neural networks and combinatorial optimization

They enrich combinatorial optimization algorithms with context-dependent features, making decisions more resilient to uncertainty. An important subset of this line of research integrates, within a neural network, an integer linear programming layer of the form:

{\bm{\theta}}

{\bm{x}}

neural combinatorial optimization
, which aims to replace the solver entirely with ML-based heuristics.

Unfortunately, many problems in operations research are NP-hard in nature, making exact oracles impractical.
Instead, applications often rely on
local search heuristics
(e.g., simulated annealing), which iteratively generate and then accept or reject a neighbor of the current solution. We aim to provide a principled approach for learning with such inexact combinatorial solvers. This is crucial for exploiting popular heuristics from the operations research literature as layers in neural networks.

To do so, we open the solver “black box”, by bridging local search heuristics and Markov chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) methods. These lines of research have evolved quite separately, and their links remain unexploited for designing principled combinatorial optimization layers. Specifically, we make the following contributions:

We integrate local search heuristics as differentiable, stochastic layers in neural networks, by converting their neighborhood systems to proposal distributions, turning the local search oracle into a discrete MCMC sampler over the combinatorial set of solutions.

•

We show that the proposed layer yields stochastic gradients of a Fenchel-Young loss

The proposed layer reduces the computational bottleneck, especially with few MCMC iterations, enabling larger training instances and better generalization at scale

Background and Related Work

2.1
Problem setup

In this paper, our goal is to learn models that incorporate optimization layers of the form:

\widehat{{\bm{y}}}:{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto\argmax_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\,\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a finite but combinatorially large set, and
\varphi
encodes problem-specific structural costs or preferences on outputs that do not depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
. For example, in our dynamic vehicle routing application (
Section

5.1
),
{\bm{\theta}}
represents context-dependent predicted “prizes” for serving clients, while
\varphi({\bm{y}})
encodes the fixed, context-independent routing cost of solution
{\bm{y}}
.
This formulation therefore extends the standard linear objective in Problem (
1
) by allowing additional problem-specific structure.

We focus on settings where Problem (
2
) is intractable and only
heuristic algorithms are available to obtain an approximate solution. Our goal
is to integrate NP-hard problems arising in operations research (e.g., routing, scheduling, network design), within a neural network. Unfortunately, many existing approaches lack formal guarantees or simply do not work when used with inexact solvers.

We distinguish between two learning settings.
In the unconditional setting, our goal will be to learn
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
from observations
{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
which we will later model as samples from a generative process governed by some
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
, as detailed in
Section

4.4
. In the conditional setting, we will assume that
{\bm{\theta}}=g_{W}({\bm{x}})
and our goal will be to learn the model parameters
W
from observation pairs
({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1}),\dots,({\bm{x}}_{N},{\bm{y}}_{N})
.

2.2
Combinatorial optimization as a layer

Since the layer defined in
Eq.

1
is piecewise constant, a frequent strategy consists in introducing regularization in the problem so as to obtain a continuous relaxation. In some cases, we may have access to an oracle for directly solving a regularized version of the problem.
For instance, if the original problem admits a dynamic programming solution, one can introduce entropic regularization via algorithmic smoothing with a change of semiring
(
34
;
40
)
. As another example, interior point solvers can be used to compute a logarithmic barrier-regularized solution
(
36
)
. More recently,
39
handle quadratic regularization by leveraging projection oracles.

We focus on settings where only a MAP oracle is available for the original, unregularized optimization problem. While prior work is often limited to the linear form in Problem (
1
) for the latter, our framework also handles the more general Problem (
2
). Frank-Wolfe-like methods can be used to solve the regularized problem using only MAP oracle calls
(
45
;
31
)
. Another strategy consists in injecting noise perturbations

(
6
)
in the oracle, which can be shown to be implicitly using regularization.
In both cases, a Fenchel-Young loss
(
8
)
can be associated, enabling principled supervised learning with such combinatorial problems as output layers.
However, formal guarantees require an exact oracle, which is often called multiple times during the forward pass for smoothing reasons. Our proposal enjoys guarantees even with inexact solvers and a single oracle call.

Regarding differentiation, several strategies are possible. When the approach only needs to differentiate through a (regularized)
\max
, as is the case of Fenchel-Young losses, we can use Danskin’s theorem
(
13
)
. When the approach needs to differentiate a (regularized)
\argmax
, one can use autodiff on the unrolled solver iterations, or implicit differentiation of the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions
(
3
;
1
;
19
;
7
)
.
Alternatively,
62
propose to compute gradients via continuous interpolation of the solver.

2.3
Contrastive divergences

An alternative approach to learning in combinatorial spaces is to use energy-based models (EBMs)
(
33
)
, which define a distribution over outputs via a parameterized energy function
E_{\bm{\theta}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})\coloneqq\frac{\exp(E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}))}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\exp(E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime}))}\propto\exp(E_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})).

Such distributions lead to log-likelihood gradients of the form:

\displaystyle\quad\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\bm{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}E_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\bm{p}_{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}E_{\bm{\theta}}(Y)\right].

Therefore, one can use stochastic gradient methods for maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) by sampling from
\bm{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
. However, this is hard both in continuous and combinatorial settings, due to the intractable normalization constant. Contrastive divergences
(
24
;
11
;
53
)
address this by using MCMC to obtain (biased) stochastic gradients. Originally developed for restricted Boltzmann machines with
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}^{d}
and a Gibbs sampler, they have also been applied in continuous domains via Langevin dynamics
(
16
;
15
)
.

MCMC in discrete spaces.

Contrastive divergences rely on MCMC to sample the model distribution. Unfortunately, designing MCMC samplers is often case-by-case, and discrete domains have received less attention than continuous ones. Recent efforts adapt continuous techniques, such as Langevin dynamics
(
65
;
54
)
or gradient-informed proposals
(
22
;
48
)
, to discrete settings. However, these works often assume simple state spaces (e.g., the hypercube or categorical codebooks), and do not handle complex constraints ubiquitous in operations research.

55
allow structured spaces via relaxed constraints in the energy function, yet ignore these structures in their proposal supports. Notably, we emphasize that all these works focus on sampling, not on designing differentiable MCMC layers.

Local search-based MCMC layers

This section introduces our core contribution. We first connect local search heuristics and MCMC methods, then use this link to define a differentiable, stochastic layer based on a single neighborhood system (
Algorithm

1
), and subsequently generalize it to leverage diverse neighborhood systems (
Algorithm

2
).

3.1
From local search to MCMC

Local search and neighborhood systems.

Local search heuristics
(
21
)
iteratively generate a neighbor
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}^{(k)}})
of the current solution
{\bm{y}^{(k)}}
, and either accept it or reject it based on an acceptance rule, that depends on the objective function,
{\bm{y}^{(k)}}
and
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
.
In this context, a neighborhood system
\mathcal{N}
defines, for each feasible solution
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
, a set of neighbors
\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
.

Neighborhoods are problem-specific, and must respect the structure of the problem, i.e., must maintain solution feasibility. They are typically defined implicitly via a set of allowed
moves
from
{\bm{y}}
. For instance,
Table

2
lists example moves for a vehicle routing problem.

Formally, we denote the neighborhood graph by
G_{\mathcal{N}}\coloneqq(\mathcal{Y},E_{\mathcal{N}})
, where edges are defined by
\mathcal{N}
. We assume the graph is undirected, i.e.,
\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})
if and only if
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}^{\prime})
, and without self-loops – i.e.,
\bm{y}\notin\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})
. A stochastic neighbor generating function is also provided, in the form of a proposal distribution
q(\bm{y}\,,\cdot\,)
with support either equal to
\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})
or
\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})\cup\{\bm{y}\}
.

3.2
Mixing neighborhood systems

Central to local search algorithms in combinatorial optimization is the use of multiple neighborhood systems, or move types, to more effectively explore the solution space
(
42
;
10
)
. In this section, we propose a tractable way to incorporate such diversity of neighborhood systems into the combinatorial MCMC layer, while preserving the correct stationary distribution.

Move types and neighborhood systems.

Let
\left(\mathcal{N}_{s}\right)_{s=1}^{S}
be a set of different neighborhood systems. In practice, each system corresponds to a specific type of
move
(such as those in
Table

2
for our vehicle routing application), so that
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\bm{y})
is the set of solutions reachable from
\bm{y}
by applying move type
s
. Since not all move types are necessarily applicable to every solution
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, we denote by
Q(\bm{y})\subseteq\llbracket 1,S\rrbracket
the subset of moves valid on
\bm{y}
. Let
\left(q_{s}\right)_{s\in Q(\bm{y})}
be the corresponding proposal distributions, such that the support of
q_{s}(\bm{y},\,\cdot\,)
is either
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\bm{y})
or
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\bm{y})\cup\{\bm{y}\}
. Let
\bar{\mathcal{N}}
be the aggregate neighborhood system defined by
\bar{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})\coloneqq\bigcup_{s\in Q(\bm{y})}\mathcal{N}_{s}(\bm{y})
.

Computational challenge of neighborhood mixing.

A standard way to combine these neighborhood systems would be to use
Algorithm

1
by defining an aggregate proposal
q({\bm{y}},\,\cdot\,)
on
\bar{{\mathcal{N}}}({\bm{y}})
as, e.g.:

\displaystyle q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{|Q({\bm{y}})|}\sum_{s\in Q({\bm{y}})}q_{s}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}),\quad\text{giving:}\quad\alpha({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})=\frac{|Q({\bm{y}})|}{|Q({\bm{y}}^{\prime})|}\cdot\frac{\sum_{s\in Q({\bm{y}}^{\prime})}q_{s}({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}{\sum_{s\in Q({\bm{y}})}q_{s}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})}.

However, this leads to intractable updates. Indeed, computing the MH correction ratio
\alpha({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})
is prohibitively expensive as it involves summing the forward proposal probabilities for all move types in
Q({\bm{y}})
and the reverse probabilities for all move types in
Q({\bm{y}}^{\prime})
. The difficulty is that multiple, distinct proposal types can generate the same solution
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
from
{\bm{y}}
. For example, in our vehicle routing application in
Section

5.1
, relocating a pair of clients before the first one in a route of three gives the same solution
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
as relocating the first client at the last position (see the
relocate
and
relocate pair
moves from
Table

2
). Identifying and calculating all these potential forward and reverse pathways for every step is a significant computational hurdle.

for the move type
s
that was actually sampled.

4
Loss functions and theoretical analysis

Building on the differentiable MCMC layer developed in
Section

3
, we construct the corresponding learning framework. We derive principled Fenchel-Young loss functions for our layer, present practical stochastic gradient algorithms for conditional and unconditional learning, and provide theoretical convergence guarantees.

4.1
Negative log-likelihood and associated Fenchel-Young loss

We now show that, similarly to related work on structured prediction and combinatorial optimization layers (see
Table

1
), the proposed layer
{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})
can be viewed as the solution of a regularized optimization problem on
\mathcal{C}=\conv(\mathcal{Y})
.
Let
A_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq t\cdot\log\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\left(\left[\langle\bm{\theta}\,,\,\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y})\right]/t\right)
be the cumulant function
(
63
)
associated to
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
, scaled by
t
.
We define the regularization function
\Omega_{t}
and the corresponding Fenchel-Young loss
(
8
)
as:

\displaystyle\Omega_{t}({\bm{\mu}})\coloneqq A^{*}_{t}(\bm{\mu})=\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\bm{\mu}\,,\,\bm{\theta}\rangle-A_{t}(\bm{\theta}),\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta}\,;\bm{y})\coloneqq A_{t}({\bm{\theta}})+\Omega_{t}(\bm{y})-\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle.

Since
\Omega_{t}
is strictly convex on
\relint(\mathcal{C})
(see
Section

E.4
for a proof) and
{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}A_{t}({\bm{\theta}})
,
the proposed layer is the unique solution of the regularized optimization problem:

{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})=\argmax_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-\Omega_{t}(\bm{\mu})\right\},

the Fenchel-Young loss
\ell_{t}
is differentiable,
satisfies
\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}})=0\Leftrightarrow{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{y}}
,
and has gradient given by
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta}\,;\bm{y})={\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})-\bm{y}

(
8
, Proposition 2)
. It is therefore equivalent, up to a constant, to the negative log-likelihood loss, as we have
-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log\pi_{{\bm{\theta}},t}({\bm{y}})=({\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})-{\bm{y}})/t
. Algorithms
1
and
2
can thus be used to perform MLE, by returning a (biased) stochastic estimate of the gradient of
\ell_{t}
.

Note that the regularizer
\Omega_{t}
extends the influence of the objective term
\varphi
from the vertices
{\mathcal{Y}}
to the interior of the polytope
\mathcal{C}
. This can be seen from the low temperature behavior of the regularized maximizer
{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}
, which recovers the maximizer of the unregularized objective containing
\varphi
(see
Section

3.1
).

4.2
Empirical risk minimization

In the conditional learning setting, we are given observations
\left({\bm{x}}_{i},\,{\bm{y}}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathcal{Y}\right)^{N}
, and want to fit a model
g_{W}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(g_{W}(\bm{x}_{i}))\approx{\bm{y}}_{i}
. This is motivated by a generative model where, for some weights
W_{0}
, the data is generated with
{\bm{y}}_{i}\sim{\pi}_{g_{W_{0}}(\bm{x}_{i}),t}
. We aim at minimizing the empirical risk
L_{N}
:

\displaystyle L_{N}(W)\!\coloneqq\!\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{t}\left(g_{W}(\bm{x}_{i})\,;{\bm{y}}_{i}\right),\;\;\text{with}\quad\nabla_{W}L_{N}(W)\!=\!\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{W}g_{W}(\bm{x}_{i})\left({\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(g_{W}(\bm{x}_{i}))\!-\!{\bm{y}}_{i}\right).

Doubly stochastic gradient estimator.

In practice, we cannot exactly compute the gradient above: evaluating
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}
requires performing exact marginal inference over the combinatorially large set
{\mathcal{Y}}
, which is generally intractable even if an exact MAP oracle were available. Instead, using
Algorithm

1
or
2
to get an MCMC estimate of
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}(g_{W}({\bm{x}}_{i}))
, we propose the following estimator:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}L_{N}(W)\approx J_{W}g_{W}(\bm{x}_{i})\biggl(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{y}_{i}^{(k)}\!\!-{\bm{y}}_{i}\biggr),

where
\bm{y}_{i}^{(k)}
is the
k
-th iterate of the algorithm with maximization direction
\bm{\theta}_{i}=g_{W}(\bm{x}_{i})
and temperature
t
.
This estimator is doubly stochastic, since we sample both data points and Markov iterations, and can be seamlessly used with mini-batches. The term
J_{W}g_{W}(\bm{x}_{i})
is computed via autodiff.

Markov chain initialization.

Following the contrastive divergence literature
(
24
)
, we initialize the Markov chain at the ground-truth solution in the conditional setting, as
{\bm{y}}_{i}^{(0)}={\bm{y}}_{i}
. In the unconditional setting, we use a persistent initialization
(
58
)
instead: see
Section

B.3
for a detailed discussion.

4.3
Associated Fenchel-Young loss with a single MCMC iteration

To obtain an unbiased gradient estimator for the Fenchel-Young loss
\ell_{t}
associated with
{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}
, the MCMC sampler must be run until it reaches its stationary distribution
\pi_{{\bm{\theta}},t}
. This requirement makes any practical estimator with a finite number of steps
K
inherently biased. Although our theoretical analysis in
Section

4.4
shows that this bias does not hinder the convergence of the proposed learning algorithms, we now demonstrate that when a single MCMC iteration is used (
K=1
), there exists
another
target-dependent Fenchel-Young loss such that the gradient estimator is
unbiased
with respect to that loss.

{proposition}
[Existence of a Fenchel-Young loss when
K=1
]

Let
\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}
denote the distribution of the first iterate
{\bm{y}}^{(1)}
of the Markov chain defined by
Eq.

3
, with proposal distribution
q
and initialized at ground-truth
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
. There exists a target-dependent regularization function
\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}
with the following properties:
\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}
is
t/\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{y}},\,\cdot\,)}||Y-{\bm{y}}||_{2}^{2}
-strongly convex, it is such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}\left[Y\right]=\argmax_{\bm{\mu}\in\conv\left(\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}\right)}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\mu})\right\},

and the Fenchel-Young loss
\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}
generated by
\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}
is
\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{y}},\,\cdot\,)}||Y-{\bm{y}}||_{2}^{2}/t
-smooth in its first argument, and such that
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}(\bm{\theta}\,;{\bm{y}})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}\left[Y\right]-{\bm{y}}
.

See
Section

E.5
for the construction of
\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}
and the proof. Thus, the expected first iterate of the MCMC layer, when initialized at the ground-truth solution
{\bm{y}}
, is in fact the solution to an optimization problem regularized by
\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}
on the local polytope
\conv({\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\})
. Moreover,
{\bm{y}}^{(1)}-{\bm{y}}
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}(\bm{\theta}\,;{\bm{y}})
. We give properties of the local regularized maximizer
{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}[Y]
in
Section

B.2
.

A similar result in the unconditional setting with data-based initialization is given in
Section

B.1
. Interestingly, these results contrast with prior work on the expected CD-
1
update. Indeed, when applied with Gibbs sampling to train restricted Boltzmann machines, the latter was shown not to be the gradient of any function, let alone a convex one
(
56
)
.

4.4
Convergence analysis in the unconditional setting

In the unconditional setting, we are given observations
\left({\bm{y}}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N}\!\!\in\!\!\mathcal{Y}^{N}\!
and want to fit a model
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
, motivated by an underlying generative model such that
{\bm{y}}_{i}\sim{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},t}
for some true parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. We assume here that
\mathcal{C}=\conv(\mathcal{Y})
is of full dimension in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(if not, the model is specified only up to vectors
{\bm{\mu}}
orthogonal to the affine subspace spanned by
\mathcal{C}
, as
\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\mu}},t}\!=\!\pi_{{\bm{\theta}},t}
).

We have the corresponding empirical and expected risks
L_{N}
and
L_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}
:

\displaystyle L_{N}(\bm{\theta};{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{t}\left(\bm{\theta};\,{\bm{y}}_{i}\right)\;,\quad L_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\left({\bm{y}}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N}\sim({\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},t})^{\otimes N}}\left[L_{N}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N})\right],

which are minimized for
\bm{\theta}
such that
{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(\bm{\theta})=\bar{Y}_{N}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{y}_{i}
, and for
\bm{\theta}
such that
{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(\bm{\theta})={\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
, respectively. While
L_{N}
is the objective actually minimized in practice, our controlled experimental setting in
Section

5.2
allows us to minimize
L_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}
directly, decoupling the stochasticity of our MCMC estimators from the statistical noise inherent in finite datasets.

Empirical risk minimizer.

Let
\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{N}
as the minimizer of the empirical risk
L_{N}
. For it to be defined, we assume that
\bar{Y}_{N}\in\interior(\mathcal{C})
(which is always the case for
N
large enough, as
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},t}
has dense support on
\mathcal{Y}
).
A slight variation on Proposition 4.1 in
6
gives the following asymptotic normality:

{proposition}
[Convergence of the empirical risk minimizer to the true parameter]

\sqrt{N}(\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{N}-\bm{\theta}_{0})\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\,t^{2}\cov_{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},t}}\left[Y\right]^{-1}\right).

The proof is given in
Section

E.6
. Naturally, this convergence result is theoretical, as it only holds if one is able to compute the exact minimizer of the empirical risk
L_{N}
, which is impossible in our setting, as we only have access to noisy estimates of its gradient.

Stochastic gradient estimate.

We now consider the sample size as fixed to
N
samples,
and define
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
as the
n
-th iterate of the following stochastic gradient algorithm:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n+1}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}+\gamma_{n+1}\left[\bar{Y}_{N}-\frac{1}{K_{n+1}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n+1}}\bm{y}^{(n+1,\,k)}\right]\,,

(6)

where
\bm{y}^{(n+1,k)}
is the
k
-th iterate of
Algorithm

1
with temperature
t
, maximization direction
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
, and initialized at
\bm{y}^{(n+1,1)}=\bm{y}^{(n,K_{n})}
. This initialization method, reminiscent of persistent contrastive divergence (PCD) learning
(
58
)
, is further discussed in
Section

B.3
.

{proposition}
[Convergence of the stochastic gradient estimate]

Suppose the following hold for the step sizes
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
sample sizes
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, and proposal distribution
q
:

(i)

\gamma_{n}=an^{-b}
, with
b\in\left(\tfrac{1}{2},1\right]
and
a>0
.

(ii)

K_{n+1}>\lfloor 1+a^{\prime}\exp(\tfrac{8R_{\mathcal{C}}}{t}\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|)\rfloor
,
with
a^{\prime}>0
and
R_{\mathcal{C}}=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\|\bm{y}\|
.

(iii)

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{K_{n}}}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{K_{n-1}}}\leq a^{\prime\prime}n^{-c}
,
with
a^{\prime\prime}>0
and
c>1-\tfrac{b}{2}
.

(iv)

q(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2d^{*}},&\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}),\\
1-\tfrac{d(\bm{y})}{2d^{*}},&\bm{y}^{\prime}=\bm{y},\\
0,&\text{else},\end{cases}

where
d(\bm{y})\coloneqq|\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})|
and

.

Then the iterates
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
defined by
Eq.

6

converge almost surely:
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\textit{a.s.}}\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{N}
.

See
Section

E.7
for the proof. The assumption on the proposal distribution
q
is used for obtaining a closed-form convergence rate bound for the Markov chain using graph-based geometric bounds
(
25
)
.

Problem setting.

In this DVRPTW, requests arrive continuously throughout a planning horizon, which is partitioned into a series of discrete delivery waves
\mathcal{W}

\omega\in\mathcal{W}

\omega+1

Reduction to supervised learning.

Following
4

g_{W}
predicts a “prize” vector
{\bm{\theta}}^{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}^{\omega}|}

\omega
.

To frame this within our linear objective formulation, we define a mapping
\mathsf{expand}\colon\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}^{\omega}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}^{\omega}|\times|\mathcal{R}^{\omega}|}
that broadcasts the predicted vertex prizes
\bm{\theta}^{\omega}
into an edge-level prize matrix
\bm{\Theta}^{\omega}\coloneqq\mathsf{expand}(\bm{\theta}^{\omega})

i

is traversed in the routing graph, we set
\Theta^{\omega}_{i,j}=\theta^{\omega}_{i}
for all

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}}\theta^{\omega}_{i}y^{\omega}_{i,j}=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}}\Theta^{\omega}_{i,j}y^{\omega}_{i,j}=\langle\bm{\Theta}^{\omega},\bm{y}^{\omega}\rangle.

The resulting PC-VRPTW fits the general form of Problem (
2

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{y}}({\bm{\Theta}}^{\omega})=\argmax_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}(\mathcal{R}^{\omega})}\langle{\bm{\Theta}}^{\omega},\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}),

(7)

where
\varphi(\bm{y})\coloneqq-\langle\bm{c},\bm{y}\rangle

i
to
j

Conclusion, limitations and future work

In this paper, we introduced a principled framework for integrating NP-hard combinatorial optimization layers into neural networks without relying on exact solvers. Our approach adapts neighborhood systems from the metaheuristics community to design structure-aware proposal distributions for combinatorial MCMC.
This leads to significant training speed-ups, making it possible to tackle larger problem instances, which is crucial in operations research, where scaling up leads to substantial value creation by reducing marginal costs.

Limitations.

A central trade-off of our method is that it is less “black-box” than approaches based on solver perturbations. Indeed, our framework requires opening the heuristic solver to access its internal local search moves and computing the associated MCMC correction ratios, rather than simply treating the solver as an opaque optimization oracle. However, this loss of abstraction is precisely what allows us to retrieve theoretical guarantees when relying on inexact solvers.

A second limitation is that our theoretical framework, via its connection to Metropolis-Hastings, currently restricts the optimization layer to single-trajectory local search algorithms. While highly efficient in low-compute regimes, the latter naturally possess lower global exploration capabilities than more involved metaheuristics, such as the hybrid genetic search used as a baseline in our vehicle routing experiment.

Future work.

We frame gradient estimation through combinatorial layers as a sampling problem, and this work is a first step towards revisiting traditional operations research metaheuristics from an MCMC perspective in this context. While we focus on local search heuristics, recent work extends this paradigm to large neighborhood search algorithms, which leverage local MAP oracles
(
60
)
. Exploring connections with population-based metaheuristics remains a promising future direction.

Appendix A
Experiments on empirical convergence of gradients and parameters

In this section, we evaluate the proposed approach on two discrete output spaces: sets and
\kappa
-subsets. These output spaces are for instance useful for multilabel classification. We focus on these output spaces because the exact MAP and marginal inference oracles are available, allowing us to compare our gradient estimators to exact gradients. We set
\varphi\equiv 0
in these experiments.

A.1
Polytopes and corresponding oracles

The vertex set of the first polytope is the set of binary vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we denote
\mathcal{Y}^{d}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{d}
, and
\conv(\mathcal{Y}^{d})=[0,1]^{d}
is the “hypercube”. The vertex set of the second is the set of binary vectors with exactly
\kappa
ones and
d-\kappa
zeros (with
0<\kappa<d
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d}\coloneqq\bigl\{\bm{y}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\,\langle\bm{y},\bm{1}\rangle=\kappa\bigr\},

and
\conv(\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d})
is referred to as “top-
\kappa
polytope” or “hypersimplex”.
Although these polytopes would not provide relevant use cases of the proposed approach in practice, since exact marginal inference oracles are available (see below), they allow us to compare the Fenchel-Young loss value and gradient estimated by our algorithm to their true value.

Marginal inference.

For the hypercube, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}}\left[Y_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{d}}\frac{\exp\left(\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\rangle/t\right)}{\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{d}}\exp\left(\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle/t\right)}y_{i}=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}y_{j}/t\right)}{\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}y^{\prime}_{j}/t\right)}y_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{\bm{y}_{-i}\in\{0,1\}^{d-1}}\frac{\exp\left(\theta_{i}y_{i}/t+\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j}y_{j}/t\right)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}_{-i}\in\{0,1\}^{d-1}}\exp\left(\theta_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}/t+\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j}y^{\prime}_{j}/t\right)}y_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\frac{\exp\left(\theta_{i}y_{i}/t\right)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\exp\left(\theta_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}/t\right)}y_{i}\sum_{\bm{y}_{-i}\in\{0,1\}^{d-1}}\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j}y_{j}/t\right)}{\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}_{-i}\in\{0,1\}^{d-1}}\exp\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j}y^{\prime}_{j}/t\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\frac{\exp\left(\theta_{i}y_{i}/t\right)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\exp\left(\theta_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}/t\right)}y_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(\theta_{i}/t\right)}{1+\exp\left(\theta_{i}/t\right)}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\frac{\theta_{i}}{t}\right),

which gives
\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}}\left[Y\right]=\sigma\left(\frac{\bm{\theta}}{t}\right)
, where the logistic sigmoid function
\sigma
is applied component-wise. The cumulant function is also tractable, as we have

\displaystyle\log\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{d}}\exp\left(\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\rangle/t\right)

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{\bm{y}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}y_{i}/t\right)

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{\bm{y}_{1}=0}^{1}\sum_{\bm{y}_{2}=0}^{1}\cdots\sum_{\bm{y}_{d}=0}^{1}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}y_{i}/t\right)

\displaystyle=\log\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{y_{i}=0}^{1}\exp\left(\theta_{i}y_{i}/t\right)

\displaystyle=\log\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\exp\left(\theta_{i}/t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(1+\exp\left(\theta_{i}/t\right)\right).

Another way to derive this is via the Fenchel conjugate.

For the top-
\kappa
polytope, such closed-form formulas do not exist for the cumulant and its gradient. However, they can be computed efficiently using Shannon entropy-regularized dynamic programming. We rely on the framework of
61
, using their Algorithm 1 to compute the cumulant function and their Algorithm 2 to compute its gradient (marginal inference). This allows us to obtain true Fenchel-Young loss values and their gradients in
\mathcal{O}(d\kappa)
time and space complexity.

Sampling.

For the hypercube, sampling from the Gibbs distribution on
\mathcal{Y}^{d}
has closed form. Indeed, the latter is fully factorized, and we can sample
\bm{y}\sim{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
by sampling independently each component with
y_{i}\sim\textrm{Bern}\left(\sigma(\theta_{i}/t)\right)
. Sampling from
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
is also possible on
\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d}
by sampling coordinates iteratively using the computed dynamic programming table, as per Algorithm 3 in
61
or Algorithm 2 in
2
.

A.2
Neighborhood graphs

Hypercube.

On
\mathcal{Y}^{d}
, we use a family of neighborhood systems
\mathcal{N}_{\leq}^{r}
parameterized by a Hamming distance radius
r\in[d-1]
. The graph is defined by:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{d}:\;\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\leq}^{r}(\bm{y})\Leftrightarrow 1\leq d_{H}\left(\bm{y},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)\leq r.

That is, two vertices are neighbors if their Hamming distance is at most
r
. This graph is regular, with degree
|\mathcal{N}_{\leq}^{r}(\bm{y})|=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\binom{d}{i}
. This graph is naturally connected, as any binary vector
\bm{y}^{\prime}
can be reached from any other binary vector
\bm{y}
in
||\bm{y}^{\prime}-\bm{y}||_{1}
moves, by flipping each bit where
y_{i}^{\prime}\neq y_{i}
, iteratively. Indeed, this trajectory consists in moves between vertices with Hamming distance equal to
1
, and are therefore along edges of the neighborhood graph, regardless of the value of
r
.

We also use a slight variation on this family of neighborhood systems, the graphs
\mathcal{N}_{=}^{r}
, defined by:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{d}:\;\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{=}^{r}(\bm{y})\Leftrightarrow d_{H}\left(\bm{y},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)=r.

These graphs, on the contrary, are not always connected: indeed, if
r
is even, they contain two connected components (binary vectors with an even sum, and binary vectors with an odd sum). We only use such graphs when experimenting with neighborhood mixtures (see
Algorithm

2
), by aggregating them into a connected graph.

Top-
\kappa
polytope.

On
\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d}
, we use a family of neighborhoods systems
\mathcal{N}^{s}
parameterized by a number of “swaps”
s\in\llbracket 1,\min(\kappa,d-\kappa)\rrbracket
. The graph is defined by

\displaystyle\forall\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d}:\;\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{s}(\bm{y})\Leftrightarrow d_{H}\left(\bm{y},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)=2s.

That is, two vertices are neighbors if one can be reached from the other by performing
s
“swaps”, each swap corresponding to flipping a
1
to a
0
and vice-versa. This ensures that the resulting vector is still in
\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d}
. All
s
swaps must be performed on distinct components. The resulting graph is known as the
Generalized Johnson Graph

J(d,\kappa,\kappa-s)
, or
Uniform Subset Graph

[
12
]
. It is a regular graph, with degree
|\mathcal{N}^{s}(\bm{y})|=\binom{\kappa}{s}\binom{d-\kappa}{s}
. It is proved to be connected in
26
, except if
d=2\kappa
and
s=\kappa
(in this case, it consists in
\frac{1}{2}\binom{d}{\kappa}
disjoint edges).

When
s=1
, the neighborhood graph is the Johnson Graph
J(d,\kappa)
, which coincides with the graph associated to the polytope
\conv(\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d})=\Delta_{d,\kappa}

[
49
]
.

A.3
Convergence to exact gradients

In this section, we conduct experiments on the convergence of the MCMC estimators to the exact corresponding expectation (that is, convergence of the stochastic gradient estimator to the true gradient). The estimators are defined as

\displaystyle{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}}\left[Y\right]\approx\frac{1}{K-K_{0}}\sum_{k=K_{0}+1}^{K}\bm{y}^{(k)},

where
\bm{y}^{(k)}
is the
k
-th iterate of
Algorithm

1
with maximization direction
\bm{\theta}
, final temperature
t
, and
K_{0}
is a number of burn-in (or warm-up) iterations. The obtained estimator is compared to the exact expectation by performing marginal inference as described in
Section

A.1
(with a closed-form formula in the case of
\mathcal{Y}^{d}
, and by dynamic programming in the case of
\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d}
).

Setup.

For
T>K_{0}
, let
\tilde{\mathbb{E}}(\bm{\theta},T)\coloneqq\frac{1}{T-K_{0}}\sum_{k=K_{0}+1}^{T}\bm{y}^{(k)}
be the stochastic estimate of the expectation at step
T
. We proceed by first randomly generating
\bm{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
, with
M
being the number of instances, by sampling
\bm{\Theta}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independently. Then, we evaluate the impact of the following hyperparameters on the estimation of
\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}_{\bm{\Theta}_{i}},t}\left[Y\right]
, for
i\in[M]
:

1.

K_{0}
, the number of burn-in iterations,

2.

t
, the temperature parameter,

3.

C
, the number of parallel Markov chains.

Metric.

The metric used is the squared Euclidean distance to the exact expectation, averaged on the
M
instances

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}||\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}_{\bm{\Theta}_{i}},t}\left[Y\right]-\tilde{\mathbb{E}}(\bm{\Theta}_{i},T)||_{2}^{2},

which we measure for
T\in\llbracket K_{0}+1,K\rrbracket
.

Polytopes.

For the hypercube
\mathcal{Y}^{d}
and its neighborhood system
\mathcal{N}_{\leq}^{r}
, we use
d=10
and
r=1
, which gives
|\mathcal{Y}^{d}|=2^{10}
and
|\mathcal{N}_{\leq}^{r}(\bm{y})|=10
. For the top-
\kappa
polytope
\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}^{d}

\mathcal{N}^{s}

d=1000

for the hypercube, which give

For each experiment, we use

A.4
Convergence to exact parameters

In this section, we conduct experiments in the unconditional setting described in
Section

4.4
. As a reminder, the empirical and expected risks
L_{N}
and
L_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}
are given by:

\displaystyle L_{N}(\bm{\theta};{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{t}\left(\bm{\theta};\,{\bm{y}}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=A_{t}(\bm{\theta})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Omega_{t}({\bm{y}}_{i})-\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bar{Y}_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta};\,\bar{Y}_{N})+C_{1}(Y),

(8)

and

\displaystyle L_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\left({\bm{y}}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N}\sim({\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},t})^{\otimes N}}\left[L_{N}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N})\right]

\displaystyle=A_{t}(\bm{\theta})+\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},t}}\left[\Omega_{t}(Y)\right]-\langle\bm{\theta},\,{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\rangle

\displaystyle=\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta};\,{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{0}))+C_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{0}),

(9)

where the constants
C_{1}(Y)\!=\!\frac{1}{N}\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\Omega_{t}({\bm{y}}_{i})\!-\!\Omega_{t}(\bar{Y}_{N})

and
C_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},t}}\left[\Omega_{t}(Y)\right]\!-\!\Omega_{t}\left({\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\right)
do not depend on
\bm{\theta}
. As Jensen gaps, they are non-negative by convexity of
\Omega_{t}
.

General setup.

We proceed by first randomly generating true parameters
\bm{\Theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
, with
M
being a number of problem instances we average on (in order to reduce noise in our observations), by sampling
\bm{\Theta}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independently. The goal is to learn each parameter vector
(\bm{\Theta}_{0})_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i\in[M]
, as
M
independent problems. The model is randomly initialized at
\hat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0}
, and updated with Adam
[
28
]
to minimize the loss. In order to better separate noise due to the optimization process and noise due to the sampling process, we use the expected risk
L_{(\bm{\Theta}_{0})_{i}}
for general experiments, and use the empirical risk
L_{N}
only when focusing on the impact of the dataset size
N
. In this case, we create a dataset
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N\times d}
, with
N
being the number of samples, by sampling independently
Y_{i,j}\sim{\pi}_{(\bm{\Theta}_{0})_{i}},\;\forall i\in[M],\,\forall j\in[N]
.

We study the impact of the following hyperparameters on learning:

1.

K
, the number of Markov chain iterations,

2.

C
, the number of parallel Markov chains,

3.

the initialization method used for the chains (either random, persistent, or data-based),

4.

N
, the number of samples in the dataset.

Metrics.

The first metric used is the objective function actually minimized, i.e., the expected risk, averaged on the
M
instances:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}L_{(\bm{\Theta}_{0})_{i}}((\hat{\bm{\Theta}}_{n})_{i}),

where
(\hat{\bm{\Theta}}_{n})_{i}
is the
n
-th iterate of the optimization process for the problem instance
i\in[M]
. We measure this loss for
n\in[n_{\text{max}}]
, with
n_{\text{max}}
the total number of gradient iterations. For the fourth experiment, where we evaluate the impact of the number of samples
N
, we measure instead the empirical risk:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}L_{N}((\hat{\bm{\Theta}}_{n})_{i}\,;\,Y_{i,1},\dots Y_{i,N})

In both cases, the best loss value that can be reached is positive but cannot be computed: it corresponds to the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in
Eq.

8
and
Eq.

9
. Thus, we also provide “stretched” figures, where we plot the loss minus the best loss found during the optimization process.

The second metric used is the squared euclidean distance of the estimate to the true parameter, also averaged on the
M
instances:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}||(\bm{\Theta}_{0})_{i}-(\hat{\bm{\Theta}}_{n})_{i}||_{2}^{2}.

As the top-
\kappa
polytope is of dimension
d-1
, the model is only specified up to vectors orthogonal to the direction of the smallest affine subspace it spans. Thus, in this case, we measure instead:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}||\Proj_{D}\left((\bm{\Theta}_{0})_{i}\right)-\Proj_{D}\left((\hat{\bm{\Theta}}_{n})_{i}\right)||_{2}^{2},

where
\Proj_{D}
is the orthogonal projector on the hyperplane

, which is the corresponding direction.

B.3
Markov chain initialization

In contrastive divergence (CD) learning, the intractable expectation in the log-likelihood gradient is approximated by short-run MCMC, initialized at the data distribution
[
24
]
(using a Gibbs sampler in the setting of Restricted Boltzmann Machines).

Here, we write the gradient at the
n
-th iteration of gradient descent in the conditional setting as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}L_{N}(\hat{W}_{n})\approx\frac{1}{|B_{n}|}\sum_{i\in B_{n}}J_{W}g_{\hat{W}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{i})\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{y}_{i}^{(n+1,\,k)}\!\!-{\bm{y}}_{i}\right),

with
B_{n}
being the mini-batch (or full batch) used at iteration
n
,
{\bm{y}}_{i}
the ground-truth structure associated to
\bm{x}_{i}
in the dataset, and
\bm{y}_{i}^{(n+1,\,k)}
the
k
-th iterate of
Algorithm

1
, with maximization direction
g_{\hat{W}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{i})
, and initialization point
\bm{y}_{i}^{(n+1,\,0)}
. We also write:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{N}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{y}^{(n+1,\,k)}-\bar{Y}_{N}

for the unconditional setting, with
\bm{y}^{(n+1,\,k)}
being the
k
-th iterate of
Algorithm

1
, with maximization direction
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
, and initialization point
\bm{y}^{(n+1,\,0)}
.

In CD learning of unconditional EBMs (i.e., in our unconditional setting), the Markov Chain is initialized at the empirical data distribution
[
24
,
11
]
, as explained earlier. Persistent Contrastive Divergence (PCD) learning
[
58
]
modifies CD by maintaining a persistent Markov chain. Thus, instead of initializing the chain from the data distribution in each iteration, the chain continues from its last state in the previous iteration, by setting
\bm{y}^{(n+1,\,0)}=\bm{y}^{(n,\,K)}
. This approach aims to provide a better approximation of the model distribution and to reduce the bias introduced by the initialization of the Markov chain in CD. These are two types of informative initialization methods, which aim at reducing the mixing times of the Markov Chains.

However, neither of these can be applied to the conditional setting, as observed in
[
43
]
in the context of conditional Restricted Boltzmann Machines (which are a type of EBMs). Indeed, on the one hand, PCD takes advantage of the fact that the parameter
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
does not change too much from one iteration to the next, so that a Markov Chain that has reached equilibrium on
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
is not far from equilibrium on
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n+1}
. This does not hold in the conditional setting, as each
\bm{x}_{i}
leads to a different
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}=g_{\hat{W}}(\bm{x}_{i})
. On the other hand, the data-based initialization method in CD would amount to initialize the chains at the empirical marginal data distribution on
\mathcal{Y}
, and would be irrelevant in a conditional setting, since the distribution we want each Markov Chain to approximate is conditioned on the input
\bm{x}_{i}
.

An option is to use persistent chains if training for multiple epochs, and to initialize the Markov Chain associated to
(\bm{x}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i})
for epoch
j
at the final state of the one associated to the same data point
(\bm{x}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i})
at epoch
j-1
. However, this method is less relevant than PCD in the unconditional setting, as
\hat{w}
changes a lot more in a full epoch than
\hat{\theta}
in just one gradient step in the unconditional setting. It might be relevant, however, if each epoch consists in a single, full-batch gradient step. Nevertheless, it would require to store a significant number of states
\bm{y}_{i}^{(n,\,K)}
(one for each point in the dataset). The solution we propose, for both full-batch and mini-batch settings, is to initialize the chains at the empirical data distribution conditioned on the input
\bm{x}_{i}
, which amounts to initialize them at the ground-truth
{\bm{y}}_{i}
.

\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{Y})

Appendix C
Experimental and methodological details for the DVRPTW (
Section

5.1
)

C.1
Overview of the challenge

We evaluate the proposed approach on a large-scale, ML-enriched combinatorial optimization problem: the
EURO Meets NeurIPS 2022 Vehicle Routing Competition

[
30
]
. In this dynamic vehicle routing problem with time windows (DVRPTW), requests arrive continuously throughout a planning horizon, which is partitioned into a series of delivery waves
\mathcal{W}=\{\left[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\right],\left[\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\right],\dots,\left[\tau_{|\mathcal{W}|-1},\tau_{|\mathcal{W}|}\right]\}
.

At the start of each wave
\omega
, a dispatching and vehicle routing problem must be solved for the set of requests
\mathcal{R}^{\omega}
specific to that wave (in which we include the depot
D
), encoded into the system state
\bm{x}^{\omega}
. We denote by
\mathcal{Y}(\bm{x}^{\omega})
the set of feasible decisions associated to state
\bm{x}^{\omega}
.

A feasible solution
{\bm{y}}^{\omega}\in{\mathcal{Y}}({\bm{x}}^{\omega})
must contain all requests that must be dispatched before
\tau_{\omega}
(the rest are postponable), allow each of its routes to visit the requests they dispatch within their respective time windows, and be such that the cumulative customer demand on each of its routes does not exceed a given vehicle capacity. It is encoded thanks as a matrix
\left(y^{\omega}_{i,j}\right)_{i,j\,\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}}
, where
y^{\omega}_{i,j}=1
if the solution contains the directed route segment from
i
to
j
, and
y^{\omega}_{i,j}=0
otherwise. The set of requests
\mathcal{R}^{\omega+1}
is obtained by removing all requests dispatched by the chosen solution
\bm{y}^{\omega}
from
\mathcal{R}^{\omega}
and adding all new requests which arrived between
\tau_{\omega}
and
\tau_{\omega+1}
.

The aim of the challenge is to find an optimal policy
f\colon{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
assigning decisions
\bm{y}^{\omega}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{x}^{\omega})
to system states
\bm{x}^{\omega}\in\mathcal{X}
. This can be cast as a reinforcement learning problem:

\min_{f}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{\mathcal{W}}(f)\right],\quad\text{with}\quad c_{\mathcal{W}}(f)\coloneqq\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{W}}c(f(\bm{x}^{\omega})),

where
c:\bm{y}^{\omega}\mapsto\sum_{i,j\,\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}}c_{i,j}\,y^{\omega}_{i,j}
gives the routing cost of
\bm{y}^{\omega}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\omega}
and where
c_{i,j}\geq 0
is the routing cost from
i
to
j
. The expectation is taken over full problem instances.

C.2
Reduction to supervised learning

We follow the method of
[
4
]
, which was the winning approach for the challenge. Central to this approach is the concept of prize-collecting dynamic vehicle routing problem with time windows (PC-VRPTW). In this setting, each request
i\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}
is assigned an artificial
prize

\theta_{i}^{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}
, that reflects the benefit of serving it. The prize of the depot
D
is set to
\theta^{\omega}_{D}=0
. The objective is then to identify a set of routes that maximizes the total prize collected while minimizing the associated travel costs.
The model
g_{W}
predicts the prize vector
\bm{\theta}^{\omega}=g_{W}(\bm{x}^{\omega})
.
Denoting
\varphi(\bm{y})\coloneqq-\langle\bm{c},\bm{y}\rangle
, the corresponding optimization problem can be written as:

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\Theta}}^{\omega})=\argmax_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{x}^{\omega})}\sum_{i,j\,\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}}\theta^{\omega}_{i}\,y_{i,j}-\!\!\sum_{i,j\,\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}}c_{i,j}\,y_{i,j}=\langle\bm{\Theta}^{\omega},\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}),

(10)

where
{\bm{\Theta}}^{\omega}=\mathsf{expand}({\bm{\theta}}^{\omega})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{R}}^{\omega}|\times|{\mathcal{R}}^{\omega}|}
is an expanded, edge-level version of
{\bm{\theta}}^{\omega}
with
\Theta^{\omega}_{i,j}=\theta^{\omega}_{i}
for all
j\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\omega}
.

The overall pipeline is summarized in
Fig.

1
.
Following
[
4
]
, we approximately solve Problem (
10
) using
the prize-collecting HGS heuristic (PC-HGS), a variant of hybrid genetic search (HGS)
[
59
]
. We denote this approximate solver
\widetilde{\bm{y}}\approx\widehat{\bm{y}}
, so that their proposed policy decomposes
as
f_{W}\coloneqq\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}\circ\mathsf{expand}\circ g_{W}
.
The ground-truth routes are created by using an anticipative strategy, i.e., by solving multiple instances where all future information is revealed from the start, and the requests’ arrival times information is translated into time windows (thus removing the dynamic aspect of the problem). This anticipative policy, which we denote by
f^{\star}
(which cannot be attained as it needs unavailable information) is thus the target policy imitated by the model – see
Section

C.8
for more details.

C.3
Perturbation-based baseline

In
[
4
]
, a perturbation-based method
[
6
]
was used. This method is based on injecting noise in the PC-HGS solver
\widetilde{\bm{y}}
. Similarly to our approach, the parameters
W
can then be learned using a Fenchel-Young loss, since the loss is minimized when the perturbed solver correctly predicts the ground truth. However, since
\widetilde{\bm{y}}
is not an exact solver, all theoretical learning guarantees associated with this method (e.g., correctness of the gradients) no longer hold.

C.4
Proposed approach

Our proposed approach instead uses the Fenchel-Young loss associated with the proposed layer,
which is minimized when the proposed layer correctly predicts the ground-truth.
At inference time, however, we use
f_{W}\coloneqq\tilde{{\bm{y}}}\circ\mathsf{expand}\circ g_{W}
. We use a mixture of proposals, as defined in
Algorithm

2
. To design each proposal
q_{s}
, we build randomized versions of moves specifically designed for the prize-collecting dynamic vehicle routing problem with time windows. More precisely, we base our proposals on moves used in the local search part of the PC-HGS algorithm, which are summarized in
Table

2
. The details of turning these moves into proposal distributions with tractable individual correction ratios are given in
Section

C.5
.

We evaluate three different initialization methods: (i) initialize
\bm{y}^{(0)}
by constructing routes dispatching random requests, (ii) initialize
\bm{y}^{(0)}
to the ground-truth solution, (iii) initialize
\bm{y}^{(0)}
by starting from the dataset ground-truth and applying a heuristic initialization algorithm to improve it. This heuristic initialization, akin to a short local search, is also used by the PC-HGS algorithm
\widetilde{\bm{y}}
, and has its runtime limited to half the time allocated to the layer (a limit it does not reach in practice).

Neighborhood graph symmetrization.

Then, we ensure that each individual neighborhood graph
\mathcal{N}_{s}
is undirected. This is already the case for the moves
swap
,
swap pair
and
2-opt
, as they are actually involutions (applying the same move on the same couple
(i,j)
from
\bm{y}^{\prime}
will result in
\bm{y}
). However, this is obviously not the case for
serve request
and
remove request
. Indeed, if solution
\bm{y}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\bm{y}
by removing a dispatched client (respectively serving an non-dispatched one),
\bm{y}
cannot be obtained by removing another one (respectively, serving another one). To fix this, we merge these two moves into a single one. First, it evaluates which of the two moves are allowed (i.e., if they are such that
V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y})\neq\emptyset
). Then, it samples one (the probability of selecting “remove” is chosen to be equal to the number of removable clients divided by the number of removable clients plus the number of servable clients) in the case where both are possible, or else simply performs the only move allowed. Thus, the corresponding neighborhood graph is undirected as it is always possible to perform the reverse operation (as when removing a client, it becomes unserved, thus allowing the
serve request
move from
\bm{y}^{\prime}
, and vice-versa). We also allow the
serve request
move to insert a client after the depot of the first empty route, to allow the creation of new routes. In consequence, we allow the
remove request
move to remove the only client in the last non-empty route if it contains exactly one client (to maintain symmetry of the neighborhood graph).

For the
relocate
and
relocate pair
moves, the non-reversibility comes from the fact that they only relocate client
i
(or clients
i
and
\mathsf{next}(i)
in the pair case) after client
j
, so that if client
i
was the first in its route, relocating it back would be impossible (the depot, which is the start of the route, cannot be selected as
j
). Thus, we allow insertions before clients too, and add a random choice with probability
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
to determine if the relocated client(s) will be inserted before or after
j
. We also add this feature to the
serve request
move.

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(i,j)/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(j,i)/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[j]\setminus\{j\}}\exp\left[-d(j,k)/\beta\right]},

where the first term accounts for the probability of selecting
i
then
j
and the second term accounts for that of selecting
j
then
i
(one can easily check that these two cases are the only way of sampling
\bm{y}^{\prime}
from
\bm{y}
). Then, noticing that we have
|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|=|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y})|
, that these moves are involutions (selecting
(i,j)
or
(j,i)
from
\bm{y}^{\prime}
is also the only way to sample
\bm{y}
), and that we have the equalities
V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[i]=V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[i]
and
V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[j]=V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[j]
, we actually have
q_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})=q_{s}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})
.

•

For
swap pair
, the same arguments hold (leading to the same form for
q_{s}
), except for the equalities
V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[i]=V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[i]
and
V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[j]=V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[j]
. Thus, we have the following form for the correction ratio:

\displaystyle\frac{q_{s}({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}{q_{s}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})}=\frac{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]+\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[j]\setminus\{j\}}\exp\left[-d(j,k)/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]+\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[j]\setminus\{j\}}\exp\left[-d(j,k)/\beta\right]}.

•

In the case of
relocate
, let
j^{\prime}
denote
\mathsf{next}(j)
if the selected insertion type was “after”, and
\mathsf{prev}(j)
if it was “before” – where
\mathsf{next}(j)\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega}
denotes the request following
j
in solution
\bm{y}
, i.e., the only index
k
such that
\bm{y}_{j,k}=1
, and
\mathsf{prev}(j)
is the one preceding it, i.e., the only
k
such that
\bm{y}_{k,j}=1
. We have:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(i,j)/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(i,j^{\prime})/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

Indeed, if
i
was relocated
after

j
, the same solution
\bm{y}^{\prime}
could have been obtained by relocating
i

before

j^{\prime}=\mathsf{next}(j)
. Similarly, if
i
was relocated
before

j
, the same solution
\bm{y}^{\prime}
could have been obtained by relocating
i

after

j^{\prime}=\mathsf{prev}(j)
. For the reverse move probability, the way of obtaining
\bm{y}
from
\bm{y}^{\prime}
is either to select
(i,\mathsf{prev}(i))
in the after-type insertion case, or
(i,\mathsf{next}(i))
in the before-type insertion case (where
\mathsf{prev}
and
\mathsf{next}
are taken w.r.t.
\bm{y}
, i.e., before applying the move). Thus, we have:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{prev}(i))/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{next}(i))/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}.

•

For the
relocate pair
move, the exact same reasoning and proposal probability form hold for the forward move, but we have for the reverse direction:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{prev}(i))/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}\cdot\frac{\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{next}(\mathsf{next}(i)))/\beta\right]}{\sum_{k\in V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime})[i]\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]},

as client
\mathsf{next}(i)
is also relocated.

•

For the
serve request
/
remove request
move, we have the forward probability:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}\times\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}

if the chosen move is
remove request
. The expression corresponds to the composition of move choice sampling and uniform sampling over removable clients.

Still in the same case (
remove request
is chosen) and if the removed request
i
was in
\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})
(i.e., was the only client in the last non-empty route if the latter contained exactly one client), we have the reverse move probability:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]}{\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]}{\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}.

The expression corresponds to the composition of move choice sampling and softmax sampling of the depot of the first empty route (which was the route of the removed client
i
, so that
\mathcal{I}_{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
in this case). We use the average distance to dispatched clients
\bar{d}(i)\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})}d(i,k)
as distance to the depot.

In the case where the removed request
i
was not in
\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})
, we have instead:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{prev}(i))/\beta\right]+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{next}(i))/\beta\right]}{\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathcal{I}_{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\}}\cdot\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{prev}(i))/\beta\right]+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\exp\left[-d(i,\mathsf{next}(i))/\beta\right]}{\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathcal{I}_{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\}}\cdot\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}.

The right term corresponds to softmax sampling of the previous node with “after” insertion type (which has probability
1/2
) and of the next node with “before” insertion type. The non-emptiness of
\mathcal{I}_{D}(\bm{y}^{\prime})
is not guaranteed anymore, as all routes might be non-empty (indeed, we did not create an empty one by removing
i
, as
i\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})
in this case).

Similarly, if the chosen move is
serve request
, we have the forward probability:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\exp\left[-d(i,j)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\exp\left[-d(i,j^{\prime})\right]}{\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathcal{I}_{D}(\bm{y})\neq\emptyset\}}\cdot\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

if the selected insertion node
j
is not in
\mathcal{I}_{D}(\bm{y})
(i.e., is not the depot of the first empty route in
\bm{y}
), where
j^{\prime}=\mathsf{prev}(j)
if the insertion type selected was “before” (which has probability
1/2
), and
j^{\prime}=\mathsf{next}(j)
if it was “after”.

We have instead the forward probability:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]}{\exp\left[-\bar{d}(i)/\beta\right]+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})}\exp\left[-d(i,k)/\beta\right]}

if the selected insertion node
j
is in
\mathcal{I}_{D}(\bm{y})
(i.e., is the depot of the first empty route in
\bm{y}
).

In every case, we have the reverse move probability:

\displaystyle q_{s}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})=\frac{1}{|\bigl\{\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}(\bm{y})\bigr\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{y})|+|\bar{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{y})|}.

In each case, we set
d(i,D)=\infty
to account for the fact that the depot can never be sampled during the process (except in the
serve request
/
remove request
move, where we allow the depot of the first empty route / last non-empty route to be selected, for which we use the average distance to other requests as explained earlier) – in fact, the distance measure from a client to the depot is not even defined in the original HGS implementation.

The second correction factor needed is
\frac{|Q(\bm{y})|}{|Q(\bm{y}^{\prime})|}
(see
Algorithm

2
). We compute it by checking if each move is allowed, i.e., if there exists at least one
i\in V^{1}_{s}(\bm{y})
such that
V^{2}_{s}(\bm{y})[i]\setminus\{i\}\neq\emptyset
. This can be determined in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{R}^{\omega})
for each move.

Appendix D
Experimental and methodological details for the MKP (
Section

5.3
)

First, we recall that the combinatorial optimization layer is defined as:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}y_{i}\quad=\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle\,,

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\;\forall j\in[M],\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i,j}y_{i}\leq C_{j}

where
{\bm{\theta}}=g_{W}({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are the item values,
w_{i,j}\geq 0
is the weight of item
i
in dimension
j
, and
C_{j}
is the capacity of dimension
j
. The feasible set is
{\mathcal{Y}}\coloneqq\{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}\mid\forall j\in[M],\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i,j}y_{i}\leq C_{j}\}
.

D.1
Proposal distribution design

In this experiment, defined in
Section

5.3
, we use
Algorithm

2
with a mixture of three proposal distributions
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
and
q_{3}
(
S=3
).

Let
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
be a current feasible solution, and let
I({\bm{y}})=\{i\mid y_{i}=1\}
and
\bar{I}({\bm{y}})=\{j\mid y_{j}=0\}
denote the indices of selected and unselected items. Given a binary vector
{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and an index
i\in[d]
, we denote by
{\bm{y}}_{y_{i}\to\bar{y_{i}}}
the vector where the
i
-th bit is flipped, i.e.:

\displaystyle({\bm{y}}_{y_{i}\to\bar{y_{i}}})_{k}=\begin{cases}1-y_{i}&\text{if $k=i$,}\\
y_{i}&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Given two indices
i,j\in[d]
, we denote by
{\bm{y}}_{i\leftrightarrow j}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
the vector where the
i
-th and
j
-th bits are swapped, i.e.:

\displaystyle({\bm{y}}_{i\leftrightarrow j})_{k}=\begin{cases}y_{j}&\text{if $k=i$,}\\
y_{i}&\text{if $k=j$,}\\
y_{k}&\text{else.}\end{cases}

We use a sampling temperature
\beta=1.0
and define the following moves:

•

Uniform swap (
q_{1}
).
The neighborhood
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}({\bm{y}})
consists of all feasible solutions obtained by swapping an active item
i\in I({\bm{y}})
with an inactive one
j\in\bar{I}({\bm{y}})
, i.e.:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}({\bm{y}})=\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\mid\exists i\in I({\bm{y}}),j\in\bar{I}({\bm{y}}):{\bm{y}}^{\prime}={\bm{y}}_{i\leftrightarrow j}\}.

The proposal is uniform over this neighborhood:
\forall{\bm{y}}_{i\leftrightarrow j}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1}({\bm{y}}),\;q_{1}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}_{i\leftrightarrow j})=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{N}}_{1}({\bm{y}})|}
.

•

Guided swap (
q_{2}
).
Using the same swap neighborhood
{\mathcal{N}}_{2}({\bm{y}})={\mathcal{N}}_{1}({\bm{y}})
, we bias the selection using the predicted item values
{\bm{\theta}}
. We sample item
i\in I({\bm{y}})
to drop with probability
p_{\text{drop}}(i)\propto e^{-\theta_{i}/\beta}
and item
j\in\bar{I}({\bm{y}})
to add with
p_{\text{add}}(j)\propto e^{\theta_{j}/\beta}
.

The proposal distribution is therefore:
\forall{\bm{y}}_{i\leftrightarrow j}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1}({\bm{y}}),\;q_{2}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}_{i\leftrightarrow j})\propto\exp\left(\frac{\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}}{\beta}\right)
.

•

Guided flip (
q_{3}
).
The neighborhood
{\mathcal{N}}_{3}({\bm{y}})
consists of all feasible solutions obtained by flipping a single bit
i
, i.e.:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{3}({\bm{y}})=\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\mid\exists i\in[d]:{\bm{y}}^{\prime}={\bm{y}}_{y_{i}\to\bar{y_{i}}}\}.

We sample index
i
with probability proportional to
e^{-\theta_{i}/\beta}
if
y_{i}=1
(favoring dropping low-value items) and
e^{\theta_{i}/\beta}
if
y_{i}=0
(favoring adding high-value items).

The proposal distribution is therefore:
\forall{\bm{y}}_{y_{i}\to\bar{y_{i}}}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}({\bm{y}}),\;q_{3}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}_{y_{i}\to\bar{y_{i}}})\propto\exp\left(\frac{(1-2y_{i})\cdot\theta_{i}}{\beta}\right)
.

Appendix E
Proofs

E.1
Proof of convergence to the Gibbs distribution

Proof.

At fixed temperature
t_{k}=t
, the iterates of
Algorithm

1
(MH case) follow a time-homogenous Markov chain, defined by the following transition kernel
P_{\bm{\theta},t}
:

P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}q\left(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)\min\left[1,\frac{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})}{q(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})}\exp\left({\frac{\langle\bm{\theta}\,,\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})-\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle-\varphi(\bm{y})}{t}}\right)\right]&\text{if $\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})$,}\\
1-\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})}P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime})&\text{if $\bm{y}^{\prime}=\bm{y}$,}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Irreducibility.

As we assumed the neighborhood graph
G_{\mathcal{N}}
to be connected and undirected, the Markov Chain is irreducible as we have
\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y},\forall\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}),P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})>0
.

Aperiodicity.

For simplicity, we directly assumed aperiodicity in the main text. Here, we show that this is a mild condition, which is verified for instance if there is a solution
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
q(\bm{y},\bm{y})>0
. Indeed, we then have:

\displaystyle P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=1-\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})}P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})}q\left(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)\min\left[1,\frac{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})}{q(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})}\exp\left({\frac{\langle\bm{\theta}\,,\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})-\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle-\varphi(\bm{y})}{t}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})}q\left(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\geq q(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle>0.

Thus, we have
P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y})>0
, which implies that the chain is aperiodic. As an irreducible and aperiodic Markov Chain on a finite state space, it converges to its stationary distribution and the latter is unique
[
20
]
. Finally, one can easily check that the detailed balance equation is satisfied for
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},\,{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})={\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\prime})P_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}),

giving that
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
is indeed the stationary distribution of the chain, which concludes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow 0^{+}]{}0,

as

by definition of
\bm{y}^{\star}
.

\displaystyle{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\star})=1-\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{\bm{y}^{\star}\}}\!\!\!{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow 0^{+}]{}1.

Thus, the expectation of
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
converges to
\bm{y}^{\star}
. Naturally, if the
\argmax

High temperature limit.

\displaystyle{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y})}{t}\right)}{\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\left(\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})}{t}\right)}

under

[

]

\displaystyle J_{\bm{\theta}}{\widehat{\bm{y}}_{t}}(\bm{\theta})

\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})}\frac{1}{t}q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})\cdot||\bm{y}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}||^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{y}},\,\cdot\,)}||Y-{\bm{y}}||_{2}^{2}/t
-smooth. Finally, as
\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}\coloneqq(F_{{\bm{y}}})^{*}
, Fenchel duality theory gives that
\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}
is
t/\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{y}},\,\cdot\,)}||Y-{\bm{y}}||_{2}^{2}
-strongly convex.

•

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}\left[Y\right]=\argmax_{\bm{\mu}\in\conv\left(\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}\right)}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\mu})\right\}
:

Noticing that
g
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, convex on
\left]-\infty,t\log\frac{q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}+\varphi({\bm{y}})-\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})\right[
and on
\left]t\log\frac{q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}+\varphi({\bm{y}})-\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime}),+\infty\right[
, and with matching derivatives on the junction:

g^{\prime}(t)\xrightarrow[t<t\log\frac{q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}+\varphi({\bm{y}})-\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})]{t\rightarrow t\log\frac{q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}+\varphi({\bm{y}})-\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})}q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime}),\quad g^{\prime}(t)\xrightarrow[t>t\log\frac{q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}+\varphi({\bm{y}})-\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})]{t\rightarrow t\log\frac{q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})}{q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})}+\varphi({\bm{y}})-\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})}q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime}),

gives that
g
is convex on
\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
f_{{\bm{y}}}(\,\cdot\,;\bm{y}^{\prime})
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by composition. Thus,

F_{{\bm{y}}}:\bm{\theta}\mapsto\langle\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}\rangle+\!\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})}f_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta};\bm{y}^{\prime})

is closed proper convex as the sum of such functions. The Fenchel-Moreau theorem then gives that it is equal to its biconjugate. Thus, we have:

F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})=\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-(F_{{\bm{y}}})^{*}(\bm{\mu})\right\}=\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\mu})\right\}.

Nonetheless, the gradient of
F_{{\bm{y}}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle={\bm{y}}+\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{{\bm{y}}}^{-}(\bm{\theta})}q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\exp\left(\left[\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})-\varphi({\bm{y}})\right]/t\right)\cdot\left(\bm{y}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\right)\;+\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{{\bm{y}}}^{+}(\bm{\theta})}q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})\cdot\left(\bm{y}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}\left[Y\right].

Thus, we have
\nabla F_{{\bm{y}}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\conv\left(\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}\right)
, which gives:

\forall\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,||\nabla F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})||\leq R_{\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})}\coloneqq\max_{\bm{\mu}\in\conv\left(\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}\right)}||\bm{\mu}||,

so that we have
\dom(\Omega_{{\bm{y}}})\subset B(\bm{0},R_{\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})})
. Thus we can actually write:

F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})=\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in B(\bm{0},R_{\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})})}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\mu})\right\},

and now apply Danskin’s theorem as
B(\bm{0},R_{\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})})
is compact, which further gives:

\partial F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})=\argmax_{\bm{\mu}\in B(\bm{0},R_{\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})})}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\mu})\right\},

and the fact that
F_{{\bm{y}}}
is differentiable gives that both sides are single-valued. Moreover, as
\nabla F_{{\bm{y}}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\conv\left(\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}\right)
, we know that the right hand side is maximized in
\conv\left(\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}\right)
, and we can actually write:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}\left[Y\right]=\nabla F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})=\argmax_{\bm{\mu}\in\conv\left(\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}\right)}\left\{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}\rangle-\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\mu})\right\}.

•

Smoothness of
\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}(\,\cdot\,;{\bm{y}})
and expression of its gradient:

Based on the above, we have:

\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}(\bm{\theta}\,;{\bm{y}})=F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})+\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{y}})-\langle\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}\rangle.

Thus, the
\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{y}},\,\cdot\,)}||Y-{\bm{y}}||_{2}^{2}/t
-smoothness of
\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}(\,\cdot\,;{\bm{y}})
follows directly from the previously established
\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{y}},\,\cdot\,)}||Y-{\bm{y}}||_{2}^{2}/t
-smoothness of
F_{{\bm{y}}}
. Similarly, the expression of
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}(\bm{\theta}\,;{\bm{y}})
follows from the previously established expression of
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})
, and we have:

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\Omega_{{\bm{y}}}}(\bm{\theta}\,;{\bm{y}})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}F_{{\bm{y}}}(\bm{\theta})-{\bm{y}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}\left[Y\right]-{\bm{y}}.

∎

E.7
Proof of
Section

4.4

Proof.

The proof consists in bounding the convergence rate of the Markov chain
\left(\bm{y}^{(k)}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
(which has transition kernel
P_{\bm{\theta},t}
) for all
\bm{\theta}
, in order to apply Theorem 4.1 in
64
. It is defined as the smallest constant
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}
such that:

\exists A>0\,:\;\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y},\,|\mathbb{P}(\bm{y}^{(k)}=\bm{y})-{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})|\leq A\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}^{k}.

More precisely, we must find a constant
D
such that
\exists B>0\,:\;\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}\leq 1-Be^{-D||\bm{\theta}||}
, in order to impose
K_{n+1}>\left\lfloor 1+a^{\prime}\exp\left(2D||\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}||\right)\right\rfloor
.

A known result gives
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}\leq\rho(\bm{\theta})
with
\rho(\bm{\theta})=\max_{\lambda\in S_{\bm{\theta}}\backslash\{1\}}|\lambda|

[
35
]
, where
S_{\bm{\theta}}
is the spectrum of the transition kernel
P_{\bm{\theta},t}
(in this context,
1-\rho(\bm{\theta})
is known as the
spectral gap
of the Markov chain). To bound
\rho(\bm{\theta})
, we use the results of
25
, which study the Markov chain with transition kernel
P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}
, such that
P_{\bm{\theta},t}=\frac{1}{2}\left(I+P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}\right)
. It corresponds to the same algorithm, but with a proposal distribution
q^{\prime}
defined as:

\displaystyle q^{\prime}\left(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d^{*}}&\text{if $\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{y})$,}\\
1-\frac{d(\bm{y})}{d^{*}}&\text{if $\bm{y}^{\prime}=\bm{y}$,}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

As
P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}
is a row-stochastic matrix, Gershgorin’s circle theorem gives that its spectrum is included in the complex unit disc. Moreover, one can easily check that the associated Markov chain is also reversible with respect to
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
, and the corresponding detailed balance equation gives:

\forall\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},\;{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})={\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\prime})P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}),

which is equivalent to:

\forall\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},\;\sqrt{\frac{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})}{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\prime})}}P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})=\sqrt{\frac{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\prime})}{{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y})}}P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y})

as
{\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t}
has full support on
\mathcal{Y}
, which can be further written in matrix form as:

\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}^{1/2}P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1/2}=\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1/2}P^{\prime\top}_{\bm{\theta},t}\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}^{1/2},

where
\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}=\diag({\pi}_{\bm{\theta},t})
. Thus, the matrix
\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}^{1/2}P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1/2}
is symmetric, and the spectral theorem ensures its eigenvalues are real. As it is similar to the transition kernel
P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}
(with change of basis matrix
\Pi_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1/2}
), they share the same spectrum
S^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta}}
, and we have
S^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta}}\subset\left[-1,1\right]
. Let us order
S^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta}}
as
-1\leq\lambda^{\prime}_{\min}\leq\dots\leq\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\leq\lambda^{\prime}_{1}=1
. As
P_{\bm{\theta},t}=\frac{1}{2}\left(I+P^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta},t}\right)
, we clearly have
\rho(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1+\lambda^{\prime}_{2}}{2}
. Thus, we can use Theorem 4.1 of
25
, which gives
\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\leq 1-G\cdot Z(\bm{\theta})\exp(-m\left(\bm{\theta})\right)
(we keep their notations for
Z
and
m
, and add the dependency in
\bm{\theta}
for clarity), where
G
is a constant depending only on the graph
G_{\mathcal{N}}
, and with:

\displaystyle Z(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\left(\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y})}{t}-\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})}{t}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{Y}|\exp\left(\frac{1}{t}\left[\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle+\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\varphi(\bm{y})-\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle-\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{Y}|\exp\left(-\frac{2R_{\mathcal{C}}}{t}||\bm{\theta}||-\frac{2R_{\varphi}}{t}\right),

and:

\displaystyle m(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\biggl\{\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})}{t}\right]-\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y})}{t}\biggr\}-2\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\biggl\{\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})}{t}\right]-\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y})}{t}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})}{t}\right]-\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y})}{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\left(\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle+\max_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})-\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\langle\bm{\theta},\,\bm{y}\rangle-\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\varphi(\bm{y})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2R_{\mathcal{C}}}{t}||\bm{\theta}||+\frac{2R_{\varphi}}{t},

where
R_{\mathcal{C}}=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}||\bm{y}||
and
R_{\varphi}=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}|\varphi(\bm{y})|
. Thus, we have:

\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\leq 1-G|\mathcal{Y}|\exp\left(-\frac{4R_{\varphi}}{t}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{4R_{\mathcal{C}}}{t}||\bm{\theta}||\right),

and finally:

\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}\leq 1-\frac{G|\mathcal{Y}|\exp\left(-\frac{4R_{\varphi}}{t}\right)}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{4R_{\mathcal{C}}}{t}||\bm{\theta}||\right),

so taking
D=4R_{\mathcal{C}}/t
concludes the proof.

Proof of
Section

B.2

Proof.

The first point is directly given by the fact that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}[Y]
is the expectation of a distribution over
{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})\cup\{{\bm{y}}\}
. For the second and third points, as derived in
Section

E.5
, we have:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}[Y]={\bm{y}}+\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{{\bm{y}}}^{-}(\bm{\theta})}\!\!q(\bm{y}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\exp\left(\left[\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{y}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\rangle+\varphi(\bm{y}^{\prime})-\varphi({\bm{y}})\right]/t\right)\cdot\left(\bm{y}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\right)\;+\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{{\bm{y}}}^{+}(\bm{\theta})}\!\!q({\bm{y}},\bm{y}^{\prime})\cdot\left(\bm{y}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\right).

Define then:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{\text{better}}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})\mid\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi({\bm{y}}^{\prime})>\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle+\varphi({\bm{y}})\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{\text{worse}}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})\mid\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle+\varphi({\bm{y}}^{\prime})<\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle+\varphi({\bm{y}})\right\}

as the sets of improving and worsening neighbors of
{\bm{y}}
respectively (assuming no neighbor of
{\bm{y}}
has exactly equal objective value for simplicity, which is true almost everywhere w.r.t.
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
).

Low temperature limit.

We have:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{{\bm{y}}}^{+}({\bm{\theta}})\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow 0^{+}]{}{\mathcal{N}}_{\text{better}}({\bm{y}}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{N}}_{{\bm{y}}}^{-}({\bm{\theta}})\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow 0^{+}]{}{\mathcal{N}}_{\text{worse}}({\bm{y}}).

Then, as
x<0\implies\exp(x/t)\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow 0^{+}]{}0
, we have effectively

{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}[Y]\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow 0^{+}]{}{\bm{y}}+\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\text{better}}({\bm{y}})}q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\cdot({\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}).

High temperature limit.

As
\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}},\;\exp(x/t)\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}1
, we have:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{{\bm{y}}}^{+}({\bm{\theta}})\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}\left\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})\mid q({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})>q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\right\},\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{N}}_{{\bm{y}}}^{-}({\bm{\theta}})\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}\left\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})\mid q({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\leq q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\right\}.

Thus, we have:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}[Y]\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}{\bm{y}}+\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\mid q({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\leq q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})}q({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\cdot({\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}})+\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\mid q({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})>q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})}q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\cdot({\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}),

which gives effectively:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}^{(1)}_{\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}}}[Y]\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}{\bm{y}}+\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}})}\min\left[q({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}),q({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\right]\cdot({\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}).

∎
