Clinical outcomes following treatment of distal biceps tendon partial tears and tendinopathy: a systematic review
Authors: Romano S Sebastiani, Zachary J Herman, Benjamin B Rothrauff, John R Fowler, Albert Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile operative treatment of complete distal biceps tendon ruptures yields superior outcomes compared to nonoperative treatment, surgical indications and optimal treatment algorithms remain uncertain for distal biceps tendinopathy and partial tears. The purpose of this systematic review was to summarize the current literature on both operative and nonoperative treatment of distal biceps tendinopathy and partial tears.MethodsA systematic review was performed according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines by searching PubMed, MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library. Clinical studies reporting outcomes following operative and/or nonoperative treatment of distal biceps tendinopathy and/or partial tears were included. Extracted data included study and patient characteristics, treatment details including postintervention rehabilitation, patient-reported outcomes, and complications. Where possible, comparisons between treatment strategy (operative vs. nonoperative) and tendon pathology (tendinopathy vs. partial tears) were made, albeit meta-analysis was not appropriate as no level I or II comparative studies exist.ResultsThirty studies were included, totaling 614 patients with 500 partial tears (81.4%) and 114 tendinopathies (18.6%). Operative treatment was performed in 20 studies, nonoperative treatment in 5 studies, and both operative and nonoperative treatment in 5 studies. Diagnosis was made by advanced imaging, most commonly magnetic resonance imaging, in 27 of 30 studies, and further aided by variable physical examination maneuvers. Generally, both operative and nonoperative management were reported to be effective in alleviating elbow pain and restoring function, although there was a propensity to treat tendinopathy nonoperatively and partial tears operatively. When reported, there were similar outcomes for full vs. partial tears and tendinopathy vs. partial tears as well as operative vs. nonoperative treatment strategies; however, low-level evidence and study heterogeneity prevented data pooling and statistical comparisons.ConclusionA systematic review of the current literature on clinical outcomes following treatment of distal biceps tendinopathy and/or partial tears found that both operative and nonoperative strategies can improve elbow pain and restore function. At present, operative treatment is commonly recommended for partial tears, especially in high demand individuals. Nonoperative treatment is the initial strategy for tendinopathy and/or low-demand patients with partial tears. Future studies are needed to define surgical indications and elucidate optimal treatment algorithms.Keywords: Distal biceps, Tendinopathy, Tendinosis, Partial tendon tear, Surgical repair, Elbow MRI

Distal biceps tendon pathology exists on a spectrum, spanning degenerative tendinopathy through complete tendon rupture. Proximal ruptures of the long head of the biceps are more common, with only 3% of complete biceps tendon ruptures occurring distally, representing an incidence of 1.2-5.4 per 100,000 patient-years.24,27,39 The incidences of distal biceps tendinopathy and partial tears are unknown given the often insidious onset and absence of dramatic signs and symptoms found with complete distal biceps tendon rupture, such as a reverse Popeye deformity and antecubital ecchymosis with pronounced weakness in elbow flexion and forearm supination. However, given the likely progressive nature of distal biceps tendon pathology, early diagnosis and treatment of distal biceps tendinopathy and partial tears may prevent progression to complete distal biceps tendon rupture.

Several recent high-level systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported superior elbow function and forearm strength and endurance after treating complete distal biceps tendon ruptures with operative repair, as compared to nonoperative management.10,27 Conversely, few studies on the diagnosis and treatment of distal biceps tendon tendinopathy or partial tears have been performed. The most recent systematic review on clinical outcomes following treatment of partial distal biceps tendon tears included a total of 290 patients from 13 studies, composed of 8 case series, 3 case reports, and 2 retrospective cohort studies.20 No studies with only distal biceps tendinopathy were included. Fifty-five patients underwent nonoperative treatment compared to 256 patients with operative repair. Overall, less complications were observed in the nonoperative group, and the operative group experienced greater strength outcomes, while similar outcomes for pain, range of motion (ROM), return to activities, and patient satisfaction were seen amongst both cohorts. However, there remains a need to assess the outcomes of a higher number of patients in both operative and nonoperative groups, delineate tendinopathy and partial tears, and analyze the impact of various operative methods.

Several studies on the diagnosis and treatment of distal biceps tendinopathy and/or partial tears have also been reported.3,4,13,17,25,33,35,36,40,43,47 Therefore, the purpose of this systematic review was to summarize the current literature on both operative and nonoperative treatment of distal biceps tendinopathy and partial tears while expanding upon the most recent systematic review. Where possible, comparisons between treatment strategy (operative vs. nonoperative) and tendon pathology (tendinopathy vs. partial tears) were made.

Materials and methods

Study selection and search strategy

This systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines using a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses checklist. Three reviewers (R.S.S., Z.J.H, and B.B.R.) independently queried PubMed, MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library using the following terms: distal biceps AND (partial OR incomplete OR tendinosis OR tendinopathy OR tendinitis). There was no time limit for publication date. Titles, abstracts, and full-texts were assessed according to eligibility criteria (Fig. 1). Consensus on study inclusion was reached before proceeding to the subsequent search step, with any disagreements arbitrated by a fourth author (A.L.). Reference lists of all included studies were manually screened for additional articles that met inclusion criteria.

PRISMA flow diagram of literature search. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses; PRO, patient-reported outcome.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) clinical studies of the treatment of distal biceps pathology, including tendinopathy to partial tears; (2) publication in English language; (3) human patients; (4) levels of evidence I-IV, including case reports. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathology of structures other than the distal biceps tendon (eg, proximal biceps tendon tear); (2) complete/full distal biceps tendon tears only; (3) if complete biceps tears were included, the inability to delineate results of complete and partial tears; (4) no patient-reported outcomes (PROs); (5) anatomy studies; (6) technique studies (absent clinical outcomes); (7) cadaveric studies (eg, biomechanics); (8) reviews/editorials; and (9) animal studies.

Methodological quality

Methodological quality of nonrandomized (ie, cohort) or noncomparative (ie, case series) studies was evaluated by the Methodological Index for Nonrandomized Studies (MINORS). No randomized comparative studies were found during the database search.

Data abstraction and analysis

All relevant study information was collected independently by 3 reviewers (R.S.S., Z.J.H, and B.B.R.) using a predetermined spreadsheet. If overlapping patient cohorts or sequential studies of the same patient cohort over time were identified, only the study with the longest follow-up time was included. Earlier reports of the same patient cohort were consulted as needed to obtain the most comprehensive description of surgical technique but were not included as independent studies.

Outcomes assessed included study characteristics, patient demographics, injury pathology (ie, tendinopathy, partial tear, complete tear), and pre-operative diagnostic tests utilized. For studies involving operative treatment, the surgical approach (1- or two-incision), fixation method, center and surgeon number, and post-operative protocol were determined. For studies employing nonoperative treatment, center and surgeon/physiotherapist number, nonoperative intervention, and postintervention protocol were collected. For all studies, complications and all clinical outcomes were recorded.

Where appropriate, data across studies were pooled. Meta-analysis was not appropriate as no level I or II comparative studies exist.19 Statistical comparisons were limited, given the heterogeneity of study design and recorded outcomes. Differences in complication rates following operative vs. nonoperative treatments pooled across studies did not reach statistical significance, as determined by Fisher exact tests, and were therefore not reported in the results. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (range) unless otherwise indicated (eg, median).

Results

Study characteristics and patient demographics

Thirty studies were included, as delineated in Figure 1, of which 6 studies were level of evidence III and 24 studies were level of evidence IV, entailing 17 case series and 7 case reports (Table I). Operative treatment was performed in 20 studies, nonoperative treatment in 5 studies, and both operative and nonoperative treatment in 5 studies. A total of 614 patients were included, with 500 partial tears (81.4%) and 114 tendinopathies (18.6%). The median patient age across studies was 51 years (range: 16-86 years) with a male:female ratio of 537:138 (79.6% male). The right side (99 right: 77 left, 56.3% right) and dominant side (290 dominant: 133 nondominant, 68.6% dominant) were more commonly involved. The median time from injury to intervention was 180 days (range: 2 days-9.6 years). A traumatic injury was reported in 330 cases (59.9%) compared to 221 cases of insidious onset. The median follow-up time was 24 months (range: 6 months-24 years). Study characteristics for each included study are shown in Table I. Methodological quality assessed by the MINORS scale showed a median score of 8 (range 4-12) for noncomparative studies (n = 24) and a median score of 13 (range 11-15) for comparative studies (n = 6). Itemized scores on the MINORS scale are shown in Supplemental Table I.

Diagnostic testing

The diagnostic tests utilized in each study are shown in Supplemental Table II. Namely, 27 of 30 studies used advanced imaging, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) alone (n = 18), MRI or ultrasound (US) (n = 5), US alone (n = 3), and MRI or computed tomography (n = 1). Physical examination findings including antecubital pain on palpation, pain with elbow flexion and/or supination, and antecubital ecchymosis, were reported in 13 (43.3%), 12 (40.0%), and 5 (16.7%) studies, respectively. Elbow range of motion was tested in 16 studies (50.0%) and was noted to be “full” (8/30, 26.7%), “near full” (1/30, 3.3%), or “active ROM limited by pain” (3/30, 10.0%). Elbow strength was tested in 13 of 30 studies, 11 with manual resistance and 2 with dynamometer.

Operative treatment

Twenty-five studies employed surgical treatment, of which 5 studies also included a nonoperative treatment arm (Table I).3,4,12,25,44 Of these studies, 2 included patients with distal biceps tendinopathy,4,25 with the remaining studies including patients with partial tears. A 1-incision surgical approach was reported in 139 cases (65.0%) while a two-incision approach was used in 75 cases (35.0%). Repairs were fixed with 1 of 4 methods, from most to least frequent: (1) suspensory (eg, cortical button, 233/357, 65.2%), (2) suture anchors (60/357, 16.8%), (3) suture button with interference screw (36/357, 10.1%), and (4) transosseous suture (28/357, 7.8%). Surgery was performed at a single institution for 22 studies, with 3 studies involving 2 institutions. Ten studies involved a single surgeon, with the remaining studies involving up to 17 surgeons. The post-operative protocol varied across studies. Four studies permitted immediate mobilization, while other studies immobilized the operative limb for 1-2 weeks (n = 7 studies), 3-4 weeks (n = 3), and 6 weeks (n = 4). Light strength exercises were permitted between 6-12 weeks, with 12 weeks being the most common time point for initiation of strengthening. Return to sport and/or unrestricted activities was less commonly reported, but when explicitly stated included 3 months (n = 4 studies), 4 months (n = 1), and 6 months (n = 3). Five studies did not report the post-operative protocol. Surgical details of individual studies that include operative treatment are shown in Table II.

Nonoperative treatment

Ten studies employed nonoperative treatment, with 5 studies also including an operative treatment arm (Table I). Of these 10 nonoperative studies, 3 included patients with distal biceps tendinopathy alone, 4 with patients with distal biceps partial tears alone, and 3 with patients with distal biceps partial tears or tendinopathy. Eight studies were performed at a single institution, with 1 study performed at 2 sites; 1 study did not report the location. When reported, studies involved 1, 2, 3, and 17 surgeons and/or physiotherapists. The nonoperative intervention varied across studies, with 4 studies involving injections of either platelet-rich plasma or corticosteroid, 1 involving eccentric strengthening, 1 involving extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT), and 1 with different protocols for each patient. Three studies did not describe the nonoperative intervention in detail. Similarly, the postintervention protocol varied across studies, but most commonly involved progressive strengthening until returning to sports and/or unrestricted activities. Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes for all included studies are presented in Table IV. There was considerable heterogeneity of utilized outcome scores across studies, preventing valid data pooling. Generally, there was alleviation of pain and restoration of good to excellent elbow function following treatment, whether operative or nonoperative measures were used.

Operative vs. nonoperative: Berthold et al4 reported similar scores in American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) pain, ASES function, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH), and ROM comparing operative vs. nonoperative groups. Bauer et al3 reported that of the 61 patients that initiated nonoperative treatment of a partial distal biceps tear, 34 (55.7%) eventually underwent surgical repair. In patients with jobs that required heavy lifting (>25 kg), surgery resulted in higher satisfaction than nonoperative treatment. In patients with lower demands, operative and nonoperative management yielded equal satisfaction.

Full vs. partial tears: In a study of operative repair of distal biceps tears in an all-female patient cohort, Rao et al35 reported similar Mayo Elbow Performance Score, DASH, and satisfaction scores comparing full to partial tears. Berthold et al4 also found similar scores for full and partial tears, regardless of operative or nonoperative treatment, for Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, DASH, ASES pain, ASES function, and 36-item Short Form Survey scores. Likewise, Robbrecht et al36 found similar strength and ROM after operative treatment of full and partial tears.

Partial tears vs. tendinopathy: No study explicitly compared outcomes of partial tears vs. tendinopathy; however, in studies in which the distal biceps pathology was tendinopathy, nonoperative treatment was most employed. Specifically, of the 114 patients across all studies with tendinopathy, 94 were treated nonoperatively, with only Faict et al13 reporting on outcomes following operative treatment of 20 patients with distal biceps tendinopathy refractory to conservative treatment. Sanli et al40 found improved pain, strength, and function following platelet-rich plasma injection and eccentric strengthening in a 12-patient cohort including 8 with partial tears and 4 with tendinopathy. In a study of 21 patients (9 partial tears, 12 tendinopathy), Lee et al25 reported sufficient improvement with nonoperative treatment alone in 16 patients, with 5 patients requiring surgery. All 5 of these patients had partial tears initially.

Fixation: As described above, surgical technique varied across studies and included differences in approach (1-vs. two-incision) and fixation method (suspensory, suture anchors, suture button with interference screw, and transosseous suture). No study compared outcomes of different surgical approaches. Only Wörner et al47 compared outcomes across fixation methods, finding a lower rerupture rate following surgical repair with suspensory fixation (n = 1/62) vs. suture anchor (n = 4/62).

Discussion

The most important findings of this systematic review of the literature on the clinical management of distal biceps tendon pathology spanning tendinopathy to partial tears were: (1) distal biceps tendon pathology is diagnosed through a combination of physical examination and advanced imaging modalities, most commonly MRI; (2) both operative and nonoperative treatment can be effective in alleviating elbow pain and restoring function; and (3) low-level evidence limits robust comparisons between distal biceps tendon pathology and treatment approaches, but when reported, there were similar outcomes for full vs. partial tears and tendinopathy vs. partial tears as well as operative vs. nonoperative treatment strategies. At present, operative treatment is commonly recommended for partial tears, especially in high-demand individuals. Nonoperative treatment is the initial strategy for tendinopathy and/or low-demand patients with partial tears.

The present systematic review expanded upon a recent work that was only the second systematic review regarding outcomes following treatment of partial distal biceps tendon tears,20 including study number (30 vs. 13), number of included patients (614 vs. 290), the delineation of tendinopathy and partial tears, and the analysis of various operative methods. The present systematic review included 20 studies with operative interventions, 5 studies with nonoperative interventions, and 5 studies with both operative and nonoperative treatments, thereby permitting a preliminary comparison of the relatively efficacy of operative vs. nonoperative measures. The present systematic review included several studies not found in the previous systematic review,2,5,6,9,11, 12, 13, 14,16,17,21, 22, 23,30,32,34,37,38,40,41,44,45 and excluded 5 previously included studies due to absent PROs,7,18,46 injury at the myotendinous junction,28 or full text being unavailable.1 The present patient population, as compared to the aforementioned systematic review, had a similar median age (51 years vs. 52 years), male predominance (80% vs. 75%), and follow-up time (24 months vs. 26 months).

Across the included studies, diagnosis of distal biceps tendinopathy or partial tears was most commonly achieved by advanced imaging, using MRI and/or US. MRI remains the gold standard for diagnosing distal biceps tendon pathology, with Van Melkebeke et al31 reporting a sensitivity of 97%, specificity of 99%, positive predictive value of 94%, and negative predictive value of 99%. Festa et al15 found a similarly high overall sensitivity (92.4%) and specificity (100%) in detecting distal biceps tendon ruptures, but reduced sensitivity (59.1%) with preserved specificity (100%) in specifically diagnosing partial tears. Similarly, Schenkels et al42 reported that MRI, as compared to intraoperative evaluation, correctly identified 83% of tendinosis, 50% of low grade tears (<25% tendon width), 67% of intermediate-grade tears (25-50% tendon width), and 57% of high-grade tears (>50% tendon width).42

US, used independently or in combination with MRI, was the second most common imaging modality employed in the studies included in this systematic review. While some authors26 have reported that US can distinguish complete vs. partial distal biceps tendon tears with an accuracy exceeding 90%, other groups have reported an accuracy of only 45.5% and 66.7% in diagnosing complete and partial distal biceps tendon tears, respectively, constituting accuracy rates inferior to MRI.29 No included study compared the accuracy of MRI vs. US, likely as the aim of the systematic review was principally to summarize clinical outcomes following treatment. However, this variability could certainly limit the ability to properly classify tendinopathy vs. partial tears. In addition, the studies that used US to detect partial tears and tendinopathy did not adequately define their diagnostic criteria.

Physical examination was additionally used in the diagnosis of distal biceps tendon pathology, but less consistently reported than advanced imaging, with no maneuver reported in over half of all studies. Physical examination findings of antecubital pain on palpation, pain and/or weakness with elbow flexion and/or supination, antecubital ecchymosis, and reduced ROM were described. While physical examination tests have historically been used to indicate further advanced imaging to aid in the diagnosis of distal biceps tendon pathology, several recent studies have found a constellation of exam maneuvers can further aid in delineating distal biceps tendon pathology. Namely, Zwerus et al48 reported that a combination of the Hook test and biceps crease interval had a 94% sensitivity and 100% specificity for diagnosing an acute complete distal biceps tendon rupture, while palpation of the tendon footprint had 100% sensitivity for partial distal biceps tendon ruptures. Similarly, in investigating the independent or combined use of 3 physical examination tests to diagnose distal biceps tendinopathy or partial tearing, Caekebeke et al8 found the biceps provocation test to be the most accurate. Reference to these recent publications and inclusion of the described physical examination maneuvers should be performed in future studies on distal biceps tendon pathology.

As reflected in the included studies, distal biceps tendinopathy or partial tears are most commonly treated with an initial trial of conservative measures, entailing some combination of activity modification, immobilization, and/or strengthening. Studies inconsistently detailed symptom duration and/or preceding interventions before the study intervention was initiated, whether nonoperative or operative. As an exception, Lee et al25 reported that all 21 patients (9 partial tears, 12 tendinopathy) were refractory to 3 weeks of splint immobilization before undergoing an US-guided corticosteroid injection; only patients (n = 5) with continued symptoms then underwent surgery, all of whom had a partial tear. In a similar study of 74 patients with MRI-confirmed distal biceps tendon partial tears, 61 patients initially trialed conservative treatment, including some combination of rest and avoidance of aggravating activities combined in some cases with braces and steroid injections, while 13 patients underwent immediate surgery.3 While the indications for immediate surgery were not stated, of the 61 patients who underwent initial conservative treatment, 34 patients subsequently elected to undergo delayed surgery for refractory symptoms, with a partial tear >50% tendon width being the only characteristic predictive of failed nonoperative management.3 In addition, Berthold et al4 displayed that nonoperative management of partial tears can be more successful in low demand populations, but potentially not for higher demand individuals. These studies would support the previously described concept of distal biceps tendon pathology existing on a spectrum (ie, tendinopathy → partial tears → complete tears), for which severe disease is more likely recalcitrant to nonoperative measures. As further support, systematic reviews and meta-analyses of studies comparing operative and nonoperative treatment of complete distal biceps tendon tears have found superior objective strength and subjective PROs following operative repair.10,27 In addition, the majority of patients with distal biceps tendinopathy included in this systematic review were successfully treated nonoperatively. Nevertheless, the paucity of studies with direct comparison of patients with distal biceps tendinopathy vs. partial tears, the limited number of existing studies with distal biceps tendon partial tears treated nonoperatively, and the difficulty in accurately determining partial tear width on MRI,42 limit conclusions regarding operative indications for partial tears.

When operative repair is performed, improvements in pain and function can be expected. As reported in 3 included studies,4,35,36 outcomes following operative repair are equivalent regardless of whether the distal biceps tendon tear is complete or partial. Intuition supports this finding, as surgical repair of partial tears entails tear completion followed by fixation using the same approach and fixation methods as compete tears. While several surgical approaches and fixation devices were utilized for partial tears, no comparisons were made across methods in the included studies, with the exception of Wörner et al47 who found a lower rerupture rate following surgical repair with suspensory fixation (n = 1/62) vs. suture anchor (n = 4/62). In contrast, larger systematic reviews of clinical outcomes following operative repair of complete distal biceps tendon tears have found no significant differences in outcomes regardless of surgical approach or fixation method.10,27

Herein, transient neuropraxias of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (14.6%) and superficial radial nerve (5.8%), as well as heterotopic ossification (6.3%) represented the 3 most common complications following operative intervention. Complications following nonoperative treatment were exceedingly rare, with a single patient suffering exacerbation of carpal tunnel syndrome following ESWT, but the total patient number across nonoperative studies explicitly reporting complications was small (<30). As with operative repair of complete distal biceps tendon tears, lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve neuropraxia is the most common complication following surgery on partial tears.10

Conclusion

Distal biceps tendon pathology is a continuum spanning tendinopathy to complete tears. This systematic review of outcomes following treatment of distal biceps tendinopathy and partial tears found that both operative and nonoperative management can be effective in alleviating elbow pain and restoring function. While low-level evidence limited statistical comparisons between distal biceps tendon pathology and treatment approaches, there were similar outcomes for full vs. partial tears and tendinopathy vs. partial tears as well as operative vs. nonoperative treatment strategies, albeit the strength of these findings is limited by study quality. At present, operative treatment is commonly recommended for partial tears, especially in high demand individuals. Nonoperative treatment is the initial strategy for tendinopathy and/or low-demand patients with partial tears. Future studies are needed to elucidate operative indications and optimal treatment algorithms for distal biceps tendinopathy and partial tears.
