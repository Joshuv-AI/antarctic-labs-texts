Research Misconduct in Academia: Examining the Views of Academics in a Public University in Ghana
Authors: Fred Yao Gbagbo, Percival Delali Agordoh, Esmeranda Manful, Lisa Kearns, Barbara K Redman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe motivations for research misconduct (RM) among Ghanaian academics are likely multifaceted but poorly studied. In this pilot exploratory descriptive case study, we thematically explored institutional policies and conducted thirty (30) in-depth interviews of purposively selected Ghanaian academics in a public university in Ghana by examining their views on RM causes, enablers, inhibitors and preventive strategies. We found diverse views about RM among the academics interviewed. For instance, the majority of academics perceived RM in Ghanaian universities as a common practice, fueled by the ‘publish-or-perish’ phenomenon whilst a few thought RM may not always be intentional. Two important views of RM were also observed: (a) regulatory definitions of fabrication, falsification, manipulation and plagiarism (FFMP) and (b) professional definitions of not meeting scientific standards but not falling into FFMP. We also observed that the common practice of RM among Ghanaian academics may slow scientific progress, dent institutional reputation, harm researchers and participants. The university’s policies provided guidelines on conducting research responsibly but no explicit references were made to what constitutes RM and its related consequences. Our study thus highlights the need for increased awareness and education on ethical research conduct in the university and recommend a policy decision from the University management to publicize research integrity and build the Institutional capacity to prevent and manage research misconduct and the damaging effects on the reputation of academics and the university that can follow. A nationwide study is also recommended for broader empirical evidence for policy impact.Keywords: Academia, Ghana, Public university, Research ethics, Research misconduct

Introduction

The protection of human rights in research and the quality of research outputs depend heavily on research integrity and sound ethical standards (Bain et al., 2022). In recent times, research misconduct and integrity have gained international attention as policy concerns (Davies, 2019). According to the results of a study conducted in Denmark by Davies in 2019, international scientists do not know about the Danish Code of Conduct for Responsible Conduct of Research and use abstracted codes to portray good science as complicated and challenging to handle. Davies (2019) also attribute research misconduct to systemic flaws and portrayed research integrity as a scientific issue. There have also been various calls to promote research integrity in Sub-Saharan Africa and other low- and middle-income countries at all levels due to the increasing documentation of the incidences of research misconduct in these locations (Van Zyl et al., 2019; Laar et al., 2020; Owusu et al., 2022).

Redman (2023) discussed Research Integrity (RI) as a process of conducting research in a responsible manner. This responsible manner comprises the moral and professional norms and guidelines that govern scientific investigation. RI thus includes a variety of procedures, aimed at ensuring that research is carried out in accordance with ethical standards, respecting the rights of research participants, preventing conflicts of interest, ensuring data confidentiality, truthfully disclosing findings and encouraging the proper use of research findings in a manner that it is not abused for economic or political benefit.

Research misconduct (RM), on the other hand, refers to a variety of ethical violations, including purposeful data fabrication (making up data), manipulation, falsification and plagiarism (Stern et al., 2014). RM ought to be contrasted with questionable research practices (QRPs) that refer to less severe deviations from accepted research norms, such as selective reporting or failure to disclose conflicts of interest, which may not necessarily result in invalid research findings but can still undermine the integrity of the research process (Gopalakrishna et al., 2022).

Various studies have indicated that the pressure on academics to publish, perverse incentives and financial interest are some commonly reported risk factors for scientific research misconduct (Olesen et al., 2018; Davis et al., 2007; Fanelli et al., 2022). These have been a concern to the scientific community since the late nineteenth century in the quest to ensure credible research and prevent academic dishonesty (Poduthase et al., 2018). Despite the attention, research misconduct appears to be on the rise in recent time, igniting public uproar as well as professional discussions for redress. Buljan et al. (2018) also identified some other factors influencing research misconduct, and concluded that a number of social and psychological factors contribute to research misconduct in small scientific communities, hence suggesting possible improvements within institutions through research integrity education, forming research integrity bodies, quality control, and external supervisions.

Ravn and Sørensen (2021) explored the similarities and differences in questionable research practices (QRPs) across main areas of research and found that there are distinctions between QRPs research ethics and research misconducts such as plagiarism, fabrication, and falsification. The work of Ravn and Sørensen (2021) offered the first comprehensive explanation of the differences and parallels in QRPs among many fields of study. Their study determined QRPs and their frequency in each location using a five-phase analytical model. While some QRPs were found in one area and others span different practices, they were strongly related to specific research practices within various research topics.

There is a plethora of reasons for research 29, yet the motivations for engaging in such unethical behavior in academia are multifaceted and poorly understood (Stern et al., 2014). According to the ‘Bad Apple Theory’ propounded by Resnik (2010), the individual’s character has a major influence on decisions that might lead to research misconduct. This tendency is particularly potentiated when the individual has poor moral standards and possibly experiences financial or psychological difficulties (Griffiths, 1983; Resnik & Shamoo, 2011).

A meta-analysis by Xie et al. (2021) estimated the prevalence of all forms of misconduct and QRPs, providing a better understanding of irresponsible research behaviors. Xie et al. (2021) This study observed that research misconduct and questionable research practices are prevalent among researchers, with the prevalence of misconduct of 2.9% and 12.5%, respectively. Factors such as response proportion, limited recall period, career level, disciplinary background, and locations were repoted to significantly affect the prevalence of these issues.

Olesen et al. (2018) investigated research misconduct among Malaysian researchers through in-depth interviews with 21 lecturers and found that plagiarism and authorship disputes are common in Malaysian universities but that researchers often avoid reporting due to time and fear of repercussion. Structural and individual factors, along with organizational, situational, and cultural factors, were also found to be influential in avoiding reporting. Their study also highlighted concerns about ‘publish or perish’ pressure, which could lead to a hostile working environment and compromised interpersonal relationships, potentially compromising teaching and supervision of junior researchers and students.

Li and Cornelis (2021), in a study comparing perspectives on research misconduct between Chinese and Flemish researchers, found that while both groups shared elements of scientific culture, they also had distinct dissimilarities. Chinese researchers often mentioned plagiarism and inappropriate authorship as examples of misconduct, while Flemish respondents cited fabrication and falsification. Although the study findings suggest that cultural traits contribute to academics’ perceptions of research misconduct, both groups proposed education and training as key solutions.

Research misconduct in Africa is a growing concern, with little documented evidence of its prevalence (Kombe et al., 2014) yet, public attention has shifted to the developed world, where credible research integrity systems are already in place. With the aim of limiting research misconduct and promoting research integrity in Africa, Kombe et al. (2014) discussed the shared experiences of researchers from nine African countries and propose two approaches to promote credible research (i.e. capacity building to instill responsible research conduct in scientists and development of deterrent and corrective policies to minimize misconduct).

Simiyu et al. (2021) however observed that, for the past two decades, institutions of higher education in Africa have been focusing on knowledge synthesis for social-economic development. Yet concerns remain about Responsible Research Conduct (RCR) and adequate guidelines for Research Integrity (RI). In an attempt to strengthen policymakers’ knowledge base of RCR and RI to make the desired impact on Africa’s development, the strategies used by the institutions of higher education in African, to ensure RCR and RI were examined thereby highlighting the need for effective policies and structures to promote RCR and RI.

In a survey conducted using the Scientific Misconduct Questionnaire-Revised to examine the attitudes and perceptions of Nigerian researchers regarding research misconduct Okonta et al. (2014) found that 50.4% of respondents were aware of a colleague who had committed misconduct, over 88% were concerned about the prevalence of misconduct in their institution, and 96.2% believed that one or more forms of misconduct had occurred. Their study suggested that while researchers perceive scientific misconduct as common in Nigeria, they are also concerned about its negative effect on research credibility.

In Africa, Were et al. (2023) explored the perceptions of Kenyan research regulators on the occurrence of research misconduct as well as the institutional capacity to prevent or manage it. Findings from their study indicated that, although research misconduct is common among students writing their theses, there are no formal norms on research misconduct or institutional or national capacities to prevent or manage them.

Concerns about research misconduct involving authors are attracting much awareness and prominence even in Ghana in recent time because the pervasiveness of research misconduct in today’s scientific community has the potential to hurt not just researchers and scientists, but also society at large (Laar et al., 2020; Owusu et al., 2022). Although Ghana has not yet been in the public limelight for research misconduct, anecdotal evidence from students’ theses and research publications of academics from some universities suggest that the practice may be common among both students and faculty members in various forms within universities in Ghana. Despite the anecdotal evidences the prevalence and variety of research misconduct in Ghana have not been studied in depth to identify root causes. This is a research gap that the current study sought to address.

Theoretical Underpinnings

The study was driven by two theories [the Problem Behavior Theory (PBT) (Jessor, 1987) and the Social Learning Theory (SLT) (Bandura, 1962). PBT is a social-psychological framework that helps explain the development and nature of problem behavior. (Jessor, 1987) used this theory to explain the nature and development of alcohol abuse, drug misuse, and other problem behavior, describing problem behavior as any behavior that deviates from social and legal norms.

SLT on the other hand suggests that social behavior is learned by observing and imitating the behavior of others (Bandura, 1962). Rumjaun and Narod (2020) described SLT as an intermediate between behaviorism (traditional learning theory) and cognitive theory (Rumjaun & Narod, 2020). Behaviorism focuses on a particular view of learning: a change in external behavior achieved through the use of reinforcement and repetition to shape behavior which relates to rote learning. Cognitive learning theory advocates that different processes related to learning can be explained by analyzing mental processes. Thus, SLT is a bridge between behaviorism and cognitive approach. In this regard, Bandura (1962) proposed five essential steps in order for the learning to take place (observation, attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation).

The use of these theories in our current study was to guide the development of the research instruments, identifying the enabling conditions for research misconduct. We believed that a combination of these theories was a best fit for the study because they align very well with the research topic and the study objectives, which may be driven by human behavior that deviates from expected ethical norms in scientific research.

Methods

Study Design

We adopted an exploratory-descriptive case study design with a qualitative approach for data collection and analysis. The choice of the exploratory aspects of this study was because the researchers were investigating a phenomenon which requires an in-depth examination of the research topic within a real-world context of a selected academic institution in Ghana so as to provide vivid answers to the research questions. This integrated design aligns with the objective to examine the views of the university teaching faculty on research misconduct in the selected public university in Ghana. The research policies and selected handbooks of the university in which the study was conducted were also reviewed to examine the provisions made therein for handling research misconduct on the campuses. The COREQ checklist (Tong et al., 2007) was used to provide additional guidance for the study design as well as inform the reporting format of the findings.

Study Setting

This was a rapid pilot study conducted among a group of academics (selected faculty members and research fellows) from a public university in Ghana. We purposely identified the public university [name withheld for reasons of anonymity] that met the following five criteria: (1) is at least ten (10) years old, (2) runs multiple postgraduate programs in various professional areas, (3) has a distinguishable Institutional Review Board (IRB), (4) has written research guidance document(s) or policies retrievable from the institution’s website and (5) whose management were willing to grant formal approval for this pilot study to be conducted among the faculty. We perceived that for a pilot study of this kind, it was best to collect data from one institution, which would require only one IRB approval (as multiple IRB approvals would likely require significant time and other resources amidst the resource constraints available for this study). We deliberately omitted from the write up the year in which the institution was established to protect the anonymity of the institution since all public universities in Ghana are established by Acts of the Ghanaian Parliament at identifiable times in history and disclosing the year of establishment might reveal the identity of the university, thereby compromising the ethics of confidentiality.

Population

The target population for this pilot study comprised academics (faculty members and research fellows) from the selected public university in Ghana. This university was purposefully selected as per the inclusion criteria. Study participants from this university were also purposively selected from the various faculties and institutes involved in research where issues of research integrity were expected to be of high importance.

Selection of Participants

Academics from the selected public university were the focus of this study because available evidence indicates that this public university has well defined structures relating to research and publication of scholarly works in reputable peer reviewed journals, thereby being one of the top universities noted for scholarly works in Ghana. The public university from which the participants were selected for this pilot study is among the universities established before 2015, which marked the end of the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs). This cutoff year point was chosen to align the study with key UN milestones of quality and equitable global education as evident by the MDG 3 which includes a target on achieving quality tertiary education (Burnett & Felsman, 2012; Unterhalter, 2017).

We reached out to one hundred and ten (110) academics on the most popular social media platform (Local UTAG WhatsApp platform) in the university to ask for their consent for participation in the study. Fifty-five (55) declined participation. The rest who consented and those who did not respond to participating in the study were followed up on a one-one basis to seek further consent on an assumption that they were either not comfortable with participating on a public social media page or did not see the call for participation. Since the emphasis of qualitative research is not on large sample sizes, we embarked on recruitment of participants until saturation was reached where we no longer received any new perspectives about research misconduct from the participants at thirty (30) purposively selected participants across the various faculties, departments and institutes of the university.

Inclusion Criteria

Participants in the study comprised faculty members and research fellows from the various faculties and institutes of the identified university who have completed their probations, confirmed as full-time employees of the selected university, and who have consented to participate in the study. We based the study criteria on the assumption that the selected participants have in-depth teaching or research experiences to effectively explain the concepts being investigated.

Exclusion Criteria

Faculty members and research fellows from the selected university who have not completed serving their probations and were not confirmed as full-time employees in the selected university were excluded from the study. This is based on the assumption that these groups of people may not have in-depth teaching or research experiences to effectively explain the concepts being investigated.

Instruments

An author-developed Interview Guide was used for in-depth interviews during data collection. In order to retain the appropriate language, sequencing of questions, and cultural flavor, the research instrument was pre-tested among different groups of participants with similar characteristics as that of the intended participants of the university selected for the study. The lessons learned from the pretest were used to finalize the research instruments for the main study.

Recruitment and Training of Field Assistants

Three Research Assistants (MPhil. students), experienced in qualitative research, were identified and recruited from the identified institution where the pilot study was conducted. To ensure consistency in data collection, a half day orientation meeting was held with the Research Assistants to review the research instruments and discuss the modalities for data collection. Subsequent follow-up zoom meetings were also held weekly to discuss field challenges being encountered by any of the Research Assistants as well as their field experiences in order to strategize for resolving them together as the data collection progressed.

Data Collection

Data were collected at participants’ respective offices on campus via audio recordings, field notes, and desktop review of documents such as research manuals, policies, and guidelines available in the selected university that may be relevant to meeting the research objectives. Data collection was strictly guided by the in-depth interview guide that was developed purposely for the study. Data collection began after ethical clearance and formal approvals were obtained from the identified university. All interviews were conducted in English, and each interview session lasted approximately 20 to 40 min. The data collection period was determined by the availability of the participants and this took two months to finalise (April-May 2021).

Data Analysis

The data analysis was in two parts. First we conducted analysis of research policies of the university by following these seven processes: (1) identification and definition of issues in the policy; (2) rationale for the policymaking; (3) policy formulation process; (4) stakeholder consultation; (5) policy adoption; (6) policy implementation; (7) policy monitoring and evaluation. The second part of the data analysis was based on the raw data obtained from the field work. The data collected and field notes were first transcribed immediately after each interview by our field assistants before analysis to ensure that no relevant information was omitted. We then adopted the Soratto et al. (2020) approach of thematic content analysis where we coded the data and used ATLAS.ti 8 software (Berlin, Germany; Friese, 2017) to identify the main themes and subthemes of the content which we summarized in Table 1.

Permissions and Approvals

We obtained ethical approval/clearance for this study from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the university (name withheld for anonymity) where the study was conducted. Formal permission was also obtained from the office of the Registrar of the participating university to conduct the study. All interviews were conducted after each participant had voluntarily consented to participate in the study. Only consenting faculty members were interviewed. The interview sessions were strictly private and confidential. Names, physical contact addresses and telephone numbers of participants were not reported to ensure strict confidentiality and anonymity of participants and the university from which data were collected. In this regard, data are presented as either aggregated counts, observations, field experiences, and verbal statements or expressions by participants. As much as possible, we strictly adhered to all the required protocols of the university during the entire field work. There was no financial or material remuneration for participants to avoid any bias.

Results

We reviewed the research policies and selected handbooks of the participating university and found that although the university did not explicitly mention what constitutes research misconduct and the consequences, there were some general guidelines on conducting research in a responsible manner, which suggested that some systems were in place for guarding against aspects of research misconduct. Key among these was the recommendation that all research protocols that involve human subjects should be approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the university for ethical clearance. We however noted that the IRB of this university usually reviews research protocols prospectively and thus fails to apprehend or prevent research misconduct that may occur during the field work and subsequent development of manuscripts for publications.

Perception of Research Misconduct

RM Definition / Types of RM (Plagiarism, Fabrication, and Falsification)

We observed that, participants’ knowledge about the concept of “research misconduct defined as comprising the acts of falsification, fabrication and plagiarism (FFP)” was generally low with fewer than half of the participants unambiguously mentioning the three terms while one participants attempted to delineate the distinctions among the three terms. All participants however readily spoke of plagiarism when they were asked the question “what is research misconduct?” We therefore inferred that the main thought of most academics when they hear RM is “plagiarism” rather than falsification or fabrication. Further probes into the descriptions of the other forms of RM yielded the following responses:

…I think [fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism] are all forms of research misconduct, but plagiarism is more common and damaging. Now there are ways that people can beat the plagiarism checker … [Lecturer, PhD; 11 years in academia]

Another participant was more specific by stating:

research misconduct is the act of stealing other peoples’ ideas without proper reference, or the manipulation of research results in line with ones’ expectations… [Lecturer, PhD; 7 years in academia]

Understanding of Research Misconduct

Lecturers in the study shared the notion that research misconducts is an improper behavior which could be categorized into 2 themes: (i) intentional or systematic deviations from ethical norms and (ii) honest mistakes.

Intentional and/or Systematic Deviations from Ethical Norms

Some participants suggested that research misconduct was an intentional and a systematic deviation that are offences which have serious ramifications when exposed. One participant stated:

I will say that research misconduct can be defined as intentional or unintentional fabrication, falsification of information or data and or plagiarizing information … [Senior Lecturer, PhD/Postdoc; 6 years in academia]

Other participants suggested that research misconduct is a deviation from the norm, and one participants summed it up by saying:

research misconduct is a systematic deviation from the truth, and this is intentional e.g. When you cook data [Associate Professor, PhD; 6 years in academia]

Other participants were also of the view that research misconduct is a deviation (either subtly or glaringly) from known and established ethical principles. In essence, most participants were of the view that research misconduct is any behavior or method that violates the ethical principles of research. One participant stated:

…Any action or process during research which falls short of ethical standards… [Senior Lecturer, PhD/Fellowship; 8 years in academia]

Honest Mistake

Some lecturers posit that some research misconducts may be due to honest mistakes, since it is inherent in human nature to make mistakes. A case is made specifically in favor of self-plagiarism as an “honest mistake” and another concept “accidental plagiarism.”. One participant argued by focusing on likely flaws of being human which was clearly stated as:

… you cannot blame [the] researcher(s) for certain misconducts because this is a human institution and mistakes are bound to happen, but aside that the contributing factors could be so many. [Professor, PhD/Fellowship; 9 years in academia]

Other participants identified self-plagiarism as research misconduct that could be described as ‘honest mistake’, one participants argued that:

Self-Plagiarism is possible in research, but there are other forms of plagiarism besides self-plagiarism e.g., accidental plagiarism…. which can equally be considered as honest mistake [Lecturer, PhD; 4 years in academia]

Perceptions about RM in Academic Institutions

RM in Academic Institutions

All participants opined that research misconduct occurs in academic institutions, albeit subtly. The responses obtained from the interviews indicated that the “publish or perish” phenomenon coupled with a lack of adequate funding for research in academia,’ the desire for promotion and fame were the major drivers of an academic climate that contributes to the rising prevalence of RM. This is especially so since success in academia is hinged on one’s research output rather than on teaching skills and community service as indicated in the job descriptions of academics in Ghana. One academic argued that:

In Ghana, you can’t fault researcher for certain misconducts because this is [a] human institution…but aside that, the contributing factors could be so many. For example, in this institution, you are required to have a number of publications before you are promoted. So regardless of your field experience, teaching skills and community services, you won’t be promoted if you don’t have the required numbers of publications. This practice is what we usually call ‘publish or perish’ syndrome [Professor, PhD; 9 years in academia]

Responses from other participants suggested that the quest for fame could be one of the driving factors for RM in academia. A participant’s shared an opinion as follows:

In academia, people always want fantastic publication results, since this will always make others to follow their work so as to become popular…So the more publications that you have the likelihood it for you to become popular in academia to attract certain recognitions for consultancy contracts and/or positions [Lecturer, PhD; 4 years in academia]

RM in Participants’ Institution

In general, many of the participants believed that research misconduct may occur within their institution of work. However, they emphasized that they do not have the evidence of any such occurrence of research misconduct in their institution. One stated that:

For all these years, I have not heard much about research misconduct in this university ……, but I am sure there are such things happening… [Senior Lecturer, PhD/Postdoc; 6 years in academia]

All participants indicated that their institution is concerned about research misconduct. Yet, nearly all participants reported they had no idea of the procedure/processes their institution uses to handle suspected scientific misconduct allegations although they all indicated their institution had a written Research Policy.

Perceived Causes of Research Misconduct

The factors that the participants identified as the causes of research misconduct in the academic institutions were varied but interconnected. Figure 1 visually illustrates the semantic linkages that exist among the various elements that academics in our sample indicated reflects the causes of RM in academia. Taken together, academics suggest that the institutional environment which places a strong emphasis on the phenomenon of ‘publish or perish’ coupled with other institutional enablers (i.e. poor funding, lack of punitive measures and inadequate training / screening among others) might significantly be some of the causes of academics’ motivation to indulge in unethical behavior so that they may mainly be promoted sooner or achieve other personal aspirations within the context of weak supervisory structures.

“Publish or Perish” Phenomenon

Participants generally observed that the current institutional environment, which strongly emphasizes the phenomenon of ‘public or perish’, is the principal cause of research misconduct in academic institutions. This motivates individual researchers to act unethically so they will publish enough papers to get promoted. The “publish or perish” phenomenon refers to the idea of one’s publication output being directly associated with their promotion. One participants put it this way:

In this institution, promotions follow the principle of ‘publish or perish” principle. Thus, your contract is likely not to be renewed if you don’t meet the required number of publications in specified journals by the university [Lecturer, PhD; 4 years in academia]

Another further attributed the lack of support as an additional cause by stating that:

….in academia the publish or perish mentality is causing more harm than good and most of the time there is little support, be it funding or mentorship for academic writing and publishing…. [Lecturer, PhD; 4 years in academia]

Institutional Environment Influencing RM

The information gathered here indicate that the institutional environment influences the prevalence of research misconduct in three broad ways: (1) institutional enablers, (2) institutional inhibitors and (3) ethical climate of the institution.

Institutional Enablers of RM

The participants indicated that individuals commit acts of research misconduct because they know that they can get away with it since the existing formulated policies are often not enforced. In addition, advances in technology today offer easier ways to engage in unethical research behaviors if institutional research mechanisms are not robust enough to detect RM practices. The lack of punitive measures thus serves to empower unethical persons to commit research misconducts in diverse ways and forms. One participant argued that:

Some people think they can get away with it [research misconduct] … as I said earlier people are interested in producing impressive results and also to get certain numbers of publications for promotion… [Senior Lecturer, 8 years in academia]

Institutional Inhibitors of RM

Similarly, it was noted from the interview that participants perceived institutions’ ability to detect and punish culprits of academic misconduct as one of the key institutional inhibitors of research misconduct. One participants summed up the views on how it can be inhibited as follows:

I sincerely believe that…once people know that such forms of research misconduct can be detected, it will demotivate them and its occurrence will reduce… [Associate Professor; 12 years in academia]

Ethical Climate of Institution

Generally, the participants translated the concept of “ethical climate” to the existence of an ethics review board in the institution. While some were of the view that the ethics board had nothing to do with research misconduct, others posited that it contributed to ensuring that researchers meet all ethical requirements before they grant permission to conduct research. Therefore, in their view the ethics board should not be held responsible for research misconduct. One had this to say:

… this issue of research misconduct is purely selfishness of some individuals. The ethics board has nothing to do with research misconduct. [Senior Lecturer, PhD; 8 years in academia]

On the contrary, other participants argued that the Institutional Review Board (IRB) for research ethics must be equally held responsible for research misconduct in academia, some respondents argued that:

What is the use of an IRB if protocols reviewed and approved have major ethical flaws? [Senior Lecturer, PhD; 6 years in academia]

----I think where the study methodology is unethical, the IRB that approved the study should be equally sanctioned -----my concern however is, which institution(s) has/have the power(s) in this country to sanction an IRB involved in any form of misconduct? [Professor; 3 years in academia]

Some participants were of the view that IRBs are established to be independent so as to ensure that things are done correctly without being influenced. One participant therefore provided a clarifying statement as follows:

… I have heard complaints about how long it takes for people to get clearance for their research, but that doesn’t mean you should do the wrong things; this ethics review board is to ensure things are done the right way. So, I don’t see the negative influence here… [Professor, PhD; 9 years in academia]

Processes to Address Identified Research Misconduct

The interviews indicated that none of the participants knew what procedures are to be followed to address research misconduct allegations in the university. Some participants were however able to identify two units that receive alleged research misconduct reports from whistleblowers, as the Quality Assurance Unit and the Research Directorate of the university.

One participants stated that:

I’m told the Quality Assurance Unit of the university has a special way of dealing with such issues, but if one has never been in trouble with research misconduct, you wouldn’t know what processes are involved. [Laboratory Technician, MSc/MPhil; 20 years in academia]

Other participants indicated that:

I know of the Research Directorate of the university and what the Directorate does. But I’ve never seen nor heard about any communication or report from the directorate on research misconduct all these years that I’ve been in this university.[Associate Professor; 12 years in academia]

I am not aware of any situation where a faculty member has been publicly punished/reprimanded for research misconduct in this university. Rather, students whose project works and thesis are suspicious of research misconduct during academic defense are either cautioned, repeated or failed [Associate Professor; 12 years in academia]

Suggestions of Participants to Prevent Research Misconduct

Possible Institutional Strategies to Prevent RM

Some participants posit that capacity building training for all persons at all levels of the academic ladder will be a necessary strategy to prevent research misconduct in academic circles. One academic stated that:

…institutions must organize training sections for lecturers and research fellows, also the institutions should make funds readily available for scientific research…”[Professor, PhD; 9 years in academia]

In addition, others were of the view that strict and firm enforcement of prevailing institutional research misconduct policies and their prescriptive punitive sanctions is another strategy that may be useful. One academic suggested that:

…the offices in charge of this should take this matter to heart and do proper investigations, after which harsh punitive measures be taken. [Senior Lecturer, PhD; 6 years in academia]

Possible Policy Actions must Prevent RM

The interview indicated that most lecturers would appreciate a review of the faculty promotion policy that places a great emphasis on the number of publications and in which journals the papers are published. Also, they made a strong suggestion for institutional policy to make provisions for dedicated funding for research. Some lecturers stated that:

As a matter of fact, I think the university should relax its policies on academic publications for promotions/recognitions especially the over emphasis on sole authorship of published papers which is another major cause of research misconduct……Rather, the focus should be on collaborative research and authors contributions to a publication…. [Senior Lecturer, PhD; 6 years in academia]

… they have to fund research and reduce the burden of multiple publications for promotions… Lecturers also have to be paid well so that even when the university, government and donors fund their work, they will not use the money for something else but solely on the research … [Senior Lecturer, PhD; 3 years in academia]

Possible National Strategies to Prevent RM

At the national level, one key suggestion that stands out prominently is the idea to set up a national research agency using a database to monitor all researches and researchers in the country for research misconduct.

…I think there is a need for a national agency specifically for supervising IRBs for research ethics in Ghana including the individual ones in the various institutions…this in my opinion will serve as a control to minimize RM [Senior Lecturer, PhD; 6 years in academia]

Discussion

Research Misconduct (RM), Responsible Research Conduct (RCR) and Research Integrity are key concepts that are speedily gaining the interest of the public interest and of academia within Africa in recent times thereby necessitating research in this area (Kombe et al., 2014; Okonta et al., 2014; Laar et al., 2020; Simiyu et al., 2021; Owusu et al., 2022; Were et al. (2023). In our current study, we examined the views of 30 academics about RM in a public university in Ghana to provide some baseline information for future studies that may inform policy and programme interventions in that country. We particularly focused on institutional structures and climate that enable or inhibit research misconduct in the study setting and linked some theoretical underpinnings to operationalized the development of research instrument used for data collection. The concept of QRPs was however not explored in this paper.

We adopted the Problem Behavior Theory (PBT) (Jessor, 1987) and Social Learning Theory (SLT) (Bandura, 1962) to unearth the key issues relating to the research topic. The observation that the SLT posits social behavior to be learned through observation and imitation supports our argument that research misconduct is a social behavior that can be learned in an enabling environment through reinforcement and repetition without consequences. Thus, in line with Bandura’s proposal (1962) of the five essential steps for learning (i.e., observation, attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation), the practice of research misconduct in academia could likely follow these five essential steps for learning and practicing research misconduct. The adoption of the Problem Behavior Theory (Jessor, 1987) in this study also stems from its social-psychological framework that helps to explain the development and nature of research misconduct as one of the problem behaviors in academia. For example, a problem behavior such as research misconduct may be influenced by experience, age, rank and status in academia as well as institutional policies and societal norms regarding academic publications where promotions are tied to the number of publications one has regardless of age, years of service and quality of teaching in the university in most cases. In this regard, academics who are prolific and publish more are generally considered hardworking whilst those who publish less are generally regarded as not hard working.

The participants in this study were aware of research misconduct in academia and what research misconduct entails, and some cited some motivators for research misconduct in academia. These findings were in line with findings of other previous studies (Li & Cornelis, 2021; Olesen et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2021). However, it appeared the gravity of the offence of research misconduct was not appreciated for all the different forms of RM enumerated. The labeling of ‘honest mistake’ for self-plagiarism suggests that participants in this study do not regard it as an action that requires punitive measures. Furthermore, participants argued that the ‘publish or perish’ culture in the university is the cause of RM; which is another indication that it is likely that the individual committing RM will receive a ‘free pass’. This finding buttresses the views of Buljan et al. (2018) on how researchers perceive research misconduct.

Findings from our study also indicate that many of the participants believed that their institution is concerned about research misconduct. However, the observation that nearly all participants reported they had no idea of the procedure/processes that the institution employed to handle suspected scientific misconduct allegations is very contradictory, hence a need for an in-depth orientation of all researchers in the university on the administrative policies and procedures for preventing and addressing research misconduct in academia. Education on the research integrity policy implementation strategies is required to ensure that relevant information about the policy is well disseminated to all researchers.

The incentives for research misconduct in some jurisdictions may be contextual and influenced by geography (Van Zyl et al., 2019), academic culture (Li & Cornelis, 2021) and flaws in organizational frameworks (Olesen et al., 2022). Yet, research organizations are supposed to build an atmosphere that promotes research integrity by promoting clear regulations, appropriate advancement and education standards, and a work climate that supports research integrity (Resnik & Shamoo, 2011). Findings from our study suggests that the university had a research policy that was available for download from its website. It also had two bodies that had the mandate to provide research support to the university community to ensure optimal research integrity among its faculty and students. However, evidence from this study indicates that study participants were generally unaware of the procedures these institutional bodies used to address research misconduct allegations. This suggests two scenarios: research conducted adheres to all ethical guidelines or there is no effective mechanism to monitor research activities. Redman and Caplan (2021) concur with the assertions that research misconduct can occur at any stage of the research process and further posit that research misconduct harms the academics and the reputation of their research organizations. Hence, researchers’ behavior must be evaluated in order to maintain the integrity of research findings. To achieve this, institutions may among other things require researchers to submit a written commitment to ethical principles for each study protocol approved, register the study in an institutional repository prior to commencing the research, and use data monitoring committees as well as subscribe to open science practices such as data sharing and open access publication. These may help to promote transparency, accountability, and collaboration in research.

In reality, the true prevalence and magnitude of research misconduct in Africa is unknown, since most institutions do not disclose examples of misconduct that have occurred within their jurisdictions (Anderson et al., 2013; Davies, 2019; Xie et al., 2021). Holtfreter et al. (2020), for example, observed that even in countries in the developed world such as in the US, the pressure placed on professors to seek funding and publish in high-impact journals were contributors to research misconduct. Li and Cornelis (2021) examined the cultural differences between Chinese and Flemish researchers and described how they perceived research misconduct. They reported that Chinese researchers noted plagiarism and improper authorship as significant research misconduct concerns, compared to Flemish researchers who instead considered fabrication and falsification as the most significant form of research misconduct. Other studies have noted that many investigations into research misconduct have primarily been undertaken in the US and Europe, with the low-and middle-income countries (LMIC) or the developing countries contributing far fewer (Hofmann et al., 2020; Holtfreter et al., 2020; Li & Cornelis, 2021). Because not many studies have been conducted on research misconduct within LMIC, it is challenging to determine the extent of research misconduct and the motivating factors, and to devise a solution to the challenge. The responses of the participants in our study reflect two different views of research misconduct: (1) the regulatory definition of FFP (which we assumed is the case in the study university) and (2) a professional definition of not meeting scientific standards but not falling into FFP, which are both important.

In a systematic review that summarized the available evidence of research misconduct between 2009 and 2018, Awasthi (2019) suggested that there is enough evidence to argue that the majority of academic researchers are aware of issues relating to research integrity and the implications for academic institutions. The Horbach and Halffman (2019) study that examined “self-plagiarism,” or text recycling, in four academic fields (psychology, economics, history, and biochemistry and molecular biology) across 922 journal articles using manual interpretation and Turnitin plagiarism detection tools found significant levels of problematic text recycling, especially in the fields of psychology and economics. Though research fields may differ in how much text recycling occurs, their observation that writers who produce more work were more likely to recycle their articles could be associated with some findings from our current study in Ghana where academics who have more publications are generally considered as the most the hardworking staff who deserved promotion compared to the less prolific writers. Both observations shed more light on how institutional policies on publishing may influence problematic text recycling.

Bouter (2020) on the other hand was of the view that combating plagiarism and other types of academic misconduct requires vigilance on the part of researchers and the academic institutions. Thus, the different factors that underlie research misconduct, coupled with the challenges, ramifications, and consequences of failed prevention strategies of research misconduct affect global prevention efforts (Altman et al., 1997; Davies, 2019). Academic institutions would therefore prefer to deal with incidents of research misconduct internally to keep their reputation and credibility (Smith, 2006; Olesen et al., 2018). In other instances, and depending on the legal framework of their host country of origin, some institutions may be legally mandated to keep such RM incidents quiet since any public action may be infringing on the rights of their employees (Olesen et al., 2018). This practice although not an institutional policy in Ghana, was observed as one of the “invisible policies” in academia where some sensitive information is handled internally and sometimes at the micro (departmental) levels. Such quiet resolution arrangements may be part of a settlement agreement which enjoin institutions to act confidentiality about such circumstances of research misconduct involving academic.

One may argue that the internal management of issues of research misconduct at the institutional levels could be preferable to avoid becoming more exposed to public ridicule. There are however counterarguments to this because it is rather shameful when research misconduct is concealed at the institutional levels only for such misconduct to be detected by other academics from other countries, possibly through submission of research reports during publication (Ali et al., 2021; Smith, 2006). Therefore, various academic institutions have implemented a number of procedures to ensure institutional accountability for avoiding research misconduct even though these procedures are very divergent in scope. Bouter (2020), for example, strongly encourages universities to develop a Research Integrity Promotion Plan to assist them in avoiding problematic research practices (RIPP).

Ghana is still developing its national capacity for research integrity. Unlike the developed nations that have well established systems, in Ghana this is a grey area that does not have a national policy on how the country addresses issues of research misconduct. There is a need for a clear national research misconduct framework that guides and regulates all research activities of public institutions in Ghana. We also recommend that this framework, preferably administered by a legally empowered National Research Council, should initiate key stakeholder engagements that encourage a re-acculturation of the publish-or-perish mantra in academic institutions. It is also important to effectively address challenges associated with the national funding for research at the tertiary level, redefine the promotional prerequisites of academic faculty as well as thoroughly enforce strict sanctions against individuals and institutions who engage in unethical conduct of research.

At the institutional level, the Hong Kong Principles (HKP) for assessing researchers provide a framework that encourages research institutions to foster a culture that promotes research integrity and ethical research conduct (Moher et al., 2020). The HKP were developed in response to concerns about the rise of academic misconduct and research integrity issues in the research community. It promotes research transparency and accountability, and supports responsible research practices such as providing resources and infrastructure for research and establishing systems for data management and sharing. Further, the HKP encourage research institutions to use fair and objective processes to evaluate researchers, including merit-based evaluation of research outputs, and transparent and consistent promotion and tenure processes as well as fostering open science and inclusive practices (like open access to research data, collaboration and sharing of research outputs, and promoting diversity and inclusion in research). If Ghanaian institutions adopted the HKP, it could become an important tool for changing the ‘publish or perish’ culture stated by the study participants.

To address the issue of the lack of awareness of the existence of an institutional responsible conduct of research policy, we keenly recommend that institutional authorities ought to develop structured policy awareness and implementation plans with a complementary robust institutional policy for the promotion of research integrity and the investigation of allegations of breech of research norms and ideals. This will be to ensure that all research and academic faculty of the institution are adequately educated about the RM policies and processes in their institution.

Educational Implications

Research misconduct within academic institutions can have deleterious consequences for both individual academics and their institutions of affiliation. Therefore, we suggest that managers of academic institutions in Ghana should actively disseminate all research misconduct policies, procedures and processes among the entirety of its faculty through regular training, workshops and seminars. We also advocate for academic institutions in Ghana to regularly disseminate information on their institutional policies and make it mandatory for all faculty and students to demonstrate proof of reviewing the contents. An indigenous institutionally developed responsible conduct of research course modelled after the American example (a best practice) should be initiated and deployed within the institution. Similar training could be developed and deployed across the other universities in Ghana too. Elsewhere in some US universities, all faculty (and even students) involved in research are required to complete and earn certification in a standardize training on the Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) through the independent Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative’s (CITI) RCR course (Phillips et al., 2018). It is our considered suggestion that academic institutions in Ghana may subscribe to the CITI program or alternatively develop a nationally accepted RCR certification short course on research integrity.

Research Agenda

The findings of this study provide the earliest evidence that academics in public universities in Ghana hold a perception that research misconduct may be widespread in the Ghanaian academia space. Since these perceptions have untold implications for the credibility and the quality of academic outputs in institutions in Ghana, it is imperative to conduct more comprehensive studies (with larger sample sizes) to examine the prevalence and scope of the different forms of research misconduct that could not be determined by our pilot study. Furthermore, universities in Ghana may need to re-evaluate their present emphasis on publications for promotion and recognition, and invest more resources in developing institutional policies and technology-assisted frameworks to detect, minimize and prevent all forms of research misconduct. Additionally, research that focuses on ways to strengthen understanding of research integrity and policy development and implementation in Ghana is also equally valuable and is highly recommended for both the institution included in this study and in all institutions in the country.

Our study highlights significant gaps in the institutional environment that may be hindering institutional measures against research misconduct. More comprehensive research is imperative to explore the drivers and (de)motivations of the role of the institutional environment plays in RM among academics and to particularly deconstruct the public-or-perish phenomenon and its effects on RM. In addition, the capacities of Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) to serve as institution-based safeguards to ensuring credible research outcomes at all points in the research cycle ought to be empirically evaluated, regularly. We recommend a policy decision from the University management to publicize research integrity and build the Institutional capacity to prevent and manage research misconduct at all levels so as to prevent RM and the damaging effects on the reputation of academics and the university.

Conclusions

Ghana is enhancing its research integrity capacity, and our study is a crucial step towards this goal. Our study examined research misconduct among academics from a public university in Ghana and has outlined some institutional gaps and potential motivators for research misconduct. The study thus highlights the need for increased awareness and education on ethical research conduct since research misconduct is a significant ethical transgression that can harm the credibility and reputation of scientific information, researchers, organizations, and funders supporting them. Our findings thus have implications for policy and program interventions, highlighting the need for national coverage and broader empirical evidence for policy impact.
