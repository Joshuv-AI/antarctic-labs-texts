Agreement Between Artificial Intelligence Platforms and the UpToDate Lexidrug Database in Identifying Antihypertensive Drug–drug Interactions in Older Adults
Authors: Januarius Tomi Kiik, Agung Endro Nugroho, Soni Siswanto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to compare the agreement and consistency of classifications generated by ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI (Llama 4 Scout) with those of the UpToDate Lexidrug reference standard for identifying potential drug interactions involving antihypertensive medications in older adults.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using electronic medical record data from January 2024 to December 2024. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated to assess agreement between classifications generated by ChatGPT-4.5 or Meta AI and those of UpToDate Lexidrug as the reference standard. In addition, Cohen’s kappa values were used to assess inter-rater agreement regarding the severity of the identified drug interactions.ResultsChatGPT-4.5 showed greater agreement with the reference standard (UpToDate Lexidrug), with an accuracy of 80.5%, sensitivity of 86.2%, specificity of 78.0%, PPV of 62.8%, and NPV of 92.9%, compared with Meta AI, which had an Should be 70.6%, sensitivity of 75.0%, specificity of 68.7%, PPV of 51.0%, and NPV of 86.3%. Cohen’s kappa analysis showed fair agreement between both platforms and the reference standard (0.367 and 0.359, respectively).ConclusionsChatGPT-4.5 demonstrated greater alignment with the UpToDate Lexidrug classification compared to Meta AI across all evaluated metrics. However, both artificial intelligence platforms showed only fair agreement in severity classification, indicating substantial differences between the classifications generated by the platforms and those of the reference database.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is a major cause of premature mortality worldwide. An estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years have hypertension, two-thirds of whom live in low-income and middle-income countries. Approximately 46% of adults with hypertension remain undiagnosed, and only 42% of those diagnosed receive treatment. One global target for non-communicable diseases is to reduce the prevalence of hypertension by 33% between 2010 and 2030 [1]. In Indonesia, the prevalence of hypertension increased substantially from 25.8% in 2013 to 34.1% in 2018 [2]. The prevalence of hypertension based on physician diagnosis was 32.6%, with the highest prevalence observed among individuals aged ≥80 years (37.4%) and older adults receiving antihypertensive medications (89.2%) [3].

A substantial proportion of individuals aged >80 years have 2 or more chronic conditions, and more than 50% have at least 4 chronic conditions by 85 years of age [4,5]. Advances in disease management have increased polypharmacy among older adults, who commonly use multiple prescription, over-the-counter, and complementary medications simultaneously [6]. Polypharmacy is associated with increased risks of hospitalization, falls, frailty, cognitive impairment, and mortality among older adults [7]. In addition, potentially inappropriate medications increase the risk of adverse effects in older adults. These medications are those for which the risks outweigh the benefits when prescribed to a patient [7,8].

The prevalence of potential drug interactions is high in some settings. A study conducted at a hospital in Palestine involving 513 patients identified 1558 potential drug interactions, with an average of 3 interactions per patient [9]. A study conducted in Ethiopia involving 389 patients identified 641 drug interactions, of which 225 (35.1%) were major, 299 (46.6%) were significant, and 117 (18.3%) were minor [10]. Drug interactions commonly affect older adults and may result from shared metabolic pathways or conflicting mechanisms of action [11]. Negative outcomes associated with drug interactions include increased healthcare costs incurred due to high mortality, prolonged hospital stays, adverse reactions, and treatment non-adherence [12].

Drug interactions can be identified using conventional databases (e.g., UpToDate Lexidrug, Micromedex, and DrugBank) or generative artificial intelligence (AI) platforms (e.g., ChatGPT and Meta AI). Among conventional databases, UpToDate Lexidrug has demonstrated the highest accuracy [13]. Generative AI platforms based on large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI (Llama 4 Scout), can assist pharmacists in detecting drug interactions, although their performance differs across platforms. In 2023, research by Al-Ashwal et al. [14] reported identification accuracies of 85.88% for ChatGPT-3.5, 56.47% for ChatGPT-4.0, 38.04% for Microsoft Bing AI, and 57.65% for Google Bard across 255 clinically relevant drug pairs. Another study evaluating AI for assessing the safety of over-the-counter medicines and supplements in patients with kidney disease found that ChatGPT-3.5 identified 8.9% as potentially harmful, whereas ChatGPT-4.0 identified 23.4% as potentially harmful [15].

The use of AI for drug–drug interaction (DDI) detection has not been systematically evaluated or widely integrated into pharmacists’ workflows. Therefore, validating the accuracy of AI platforms is essential for their safe clinical implementation. With rapid advances in healthcare AI, tools such as ChatGPT are increasingly being used to support clinical decision-making, including drug interaction assessment and clinical pharmacy practice [16–19]. Nonetheless, the use of ChatGPT may raise ethical and data privacy concerns. Because ChatGPT can produce inaccurate and seemingly unfounded responses, further research is needed to determine whether it can reliably provide accurate health-related information [15]. The present study was designed to address this gap by evaluating and comparing the performance of ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI in detecting antihypertensive DDIs in older adults, using UpToDate Lexidrug as the reference standard.

Notably, ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI are not systems specifically designed to detect drug interactions. Unlike reference databases developed through curation, validation, and continuous scientific updating, the outputs of these 2 AI platforms are generated by LLMs based on probabilistic predictions. Therefore, alignment with the reference database in this study should be interpreted as a measure of agreement rather than an indicator of clinical accuracy or evidence that these AI systems are suitable for use as standalone clinical decision support tools.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Sources

This retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed data from older adults receiving antihypertensive therapy between January 2024 and December 2024. Electronic medical records from 1404 eligible patients yielded 819 unique drug pairs involving antihypertensive medications, which were subsequently evaluated. Details of the data source, patient selection, drug-pair generation, and classification procedures are presented in Figure 1.

Terminology Clarification

The study evaluated two conversational AI platforms: ChatGPT-4.5 (OpenAI), accessed through the web interface, and Meta AI (Llama 4 Scout), accessed via WhatsApp. Both platforms were assessed for their ability to identify and classify potential DDIs. Meta AI was selected as a comparator because of its high accessibility and widespread use among healthcare professionals in Indonesia, making it a practical point-of-care tool in low-income and middle-income settings. The exclusion of other LLMs, such as Gemini, Claude, and Copilot, represents a limitation of this study and should be addressed in future comparative evaluations.

Artificial Intelligence Querying Protocol

To ensure methodological transparency, all drug pairs were evaluated once using an English-language prompt adapted from Al-Ashwal et al. [14], with each evaluation conducted in a new conversation to prevent contextual bias. Since both AI platforms were accessed through public interfaces, model parameters (e.g., temperature) could not be controlled, and response variability was considered a limitation of the study. All queries included only drug names; no clinical or patient-identifying information was submitted to the AI platforms.

Study Population

This study included all eligible electronic medical records of patients aged ≥60 years with International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) hypertension codes I10–I15 who received antihypertensive therapy, either alone or in combination with other medications. No formal sample size calculation was performed because all eligible records available during the study period were included as a census-based convenience sample.

Selection of Drug Combinations

Each antihypertensive medication prescribed to an older adult at UGM Academic Hospital was paired with each concurrently prescribed medication, yielding 819 unique drug pairs after deduplication. Each unique pair was evaluated independently using ChatGPT-4.5, Meta AI, and the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database. The complete list of drug pairs is provided in Supplemental Material 1.

Artificial Intelligence Platform Specifications, Prompt Methodology, Comparisons, and Interaction Classification

ChatGPT-4.5 (OpenAI web interface) and Meta AI (Llama 4 Scout; WhatsApp) were evaluated between March 1, 2026, and March 31, 2026. Each unique drug pair was assessed once in a separate session with the same English-language prompt adapted from Al-Ashwal et al. [14]: “Which of the following best describes the drug interaction between (Drug A) and (Drug B)? Classify the severity as A. No Known Interaction, B. Minor, C. Moderate, or D. Major, and provide a brief clinical rationale.” No conversation history or response regeneration was used, and all outputs were generated using the platforms’ default settings. User-adjustable parameters, including temperature, were not accessible. UpToDate Lexidrug was used as the reference standard because it has demonstrated higher agreement than other conventional DDI databases [13]. UpToDate Lexidrug classifies interactions into 5 categories (A–E) [20]. For diagnostic performance analysis, categories C–E were defined as clinically significant (positive) interactions, whereas categories A–B were considered non-significant (negative). The same dichotomization was applied to the AI outputs, and true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) were determined based on agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug classification rather than objectively verified clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed descriptively to evaluate concordance between each AI platform and the reference database. The McNemar test was used to compare paired dichotomous classifications and determine whether ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI differed significantly in classifying drug pairs as clinically significant or non-significant interactions [22]. Agreement between each AI platform and UpToDate Lexidrug was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) values [23]. Agreement between ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI was also assessed using Cohen’s kappa.

For the severity agreement analysis, the 5 UpToDate Lexidrug categories were mapped to 4 levels: A=no known interaction, B=minor, C=moderate, and D–E=major. Kappa values were interpreted according to the criteria proposed by Landis and Koch [24]. Because interaction severity is an ordinal variable, linear-weighted and quadratic-weighted kappa values were also calculated as sensitivity analyses, whereas the unweighted Cohen’s kappa was retained as the primary analysis to facilitate comparison with previous DDI concordance studies.

Ethics Statement

The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Public Health, and Nursing, Gadjah Mada University, Indonesia, approved this study (No. EC/FE/1728/EC/2025). Hospital permission was obtained before data collection, and patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study.

RESULTS

Identification of Antihypertensive Drug Interactions Based on Severity Categories

During the study period, 12 773 visits related to hypertension (ICD-10 codes I10–I15) were identified. Of these, 1404 older adults met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study. Analysis of antihypertensive prescriptions yielded 8386 drug pairs, reflecting a substantial polypharmacy burden in the study population. After deduplication, 819 unique antihypertensive drug pairs remained and were categorized as having no known interaction or a minor, moderate, or major interaction and were assessed for DDI. These unique pairs formed the basis for comparisons between the AI platforms and the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database, whereas the frequency data provided insight into the real-world prescribing burden associated with potential DDIs in older adults with hypertension.

Table 1 presents the numbers of drug interactions between antihypertensive medications and other medications identified by ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI. The identified drug interactions were classified into 4 categories: no known interaction, minor, moderate, and major, according to their level of clinical significance.

As shown in Table 1, among 819 antihypertensive drug pairs, ChatGPT-4.5 classified 327 pairs (39.9%) as having no known interaction, compared with 373 pairs (45.5%) classified by Meta AI. ChatGPT-4.5 classified 152 pairs (18.6%) as minor interactions, compared with 78 (9.5%) for Meta AI. Conversely, ChatGPT-4.5 identified fewer moderate interactions, with 304 cases (37.1%), compared with 344 cases (42.0%) identified by Meta AI.

Evaluation of Agreement for Each Artificial Intelligence Platform

The agreement of each AI platform in identifying antihypertensive drug interactions is presented in Table 2 (Supplemental Material 2). The table presents an evaluation matrix containing the numbers of TP, TN, FP, and FN for each AI platform in detecting antihypertensive drug interactions.

ChatGPT-4.5 produced more classifications concordant with the reference standard (TP+TN) than Meta AI (n=659). At the same time, Meta AI showed a greater number of discordant classifications (FP+FN) than ChatGPT-4.5 (n=241). An ideal system for detecting drug interactions should have 2 key characteristics. First, it should demonstrate high sensitivity for identifying clinically significant interactions. Second, it should maintain high specificity to minimize the identification of irrelevant interactions. Both characteristics are necessary to achieve an appropriate diagnostic balance [25].

As shown in Table 3, ChatGPT-4.5 demonstrated greater agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database than Meta AI across all performance metrics. ChatGPT-4.5 achieved higher sensitivity (86.2%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 81.4 to 90.0), specificity (78.0%; 95% CI, 74.4 to 81.2), PPV (62.8%), NPV (92.9%), and accuracy (80.5%) than Meta AI, which achieved a sensitivity of 75.0% (95% CI, 69.3 to 80.0), specificity of 68.7% (95% CI, 64.7 to 72.3), PPV of 51.0%, NPV of 86.3%, and accuracy of 70.6%. Overall, these findings indicate greater concordance between ChatGPT-4.5 and UpToDate Lexidrug, although the results should be interpreted as agreement with the reference database rather than evidence of diagnostic accuracy. Both AI platforms demonstrated moderate agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database based on weighted kappa analyses. Although the unweighted kappa values were similar for ChatGPT-4.5 (κ=0.367; 95% CI, 0.346 to 0.388) and Meta AI (κ=0.359; 95% CI, 0.339 to 0.379), ChatGPT-4.5 achieved higher linear-weighted (κ=0.499) and quadratic-weighted (κ=0.599) kappa values than Meta AI (κ=0.434 and 0.488, respectively). These findings indicate that, when ChatGPT-4.5 disagreed with UpToDate Lexidrug, its classifications tended to be closer to the reference severity category, suggesting better concordance with the ordinal severity framework. ChatGPT-4.5 also achieved higher overall accuracy than Meta AI (0.804 vs. 0.705), indicating greater agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug classification system. However, these results reflect concordance with a reference database rather than objective clinical accuracy, as agreement with UpToDate Lexidrug does not necessarily confirm the true presence or absence of a DDI. Furthermore, because ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI are general-purpose LLMs without transparent pharmacological knowledge bases or traceable evidence sources, their outputs should be considered AI-generated classifications that complement rather than replace established DDI reference databases.

The McNemar test was used to compare paired dichotomous classifications between ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI using a 2×2 contingency table [22]. The test showed significant asymmetry between the 2 platforms (χ2=11.51, p<0.05), indicating systematic differences in DDI classification. ChatGPT-4.5 more frequently classified drug pairs as interacting when Meta AI classified them as non-interacting than vice versa, suggesting a greater tendency to detect potential interactions. However, because the McNemar test evaluates only discordant pairs, these findings reflect differences in classification behavior rather than overall diagnostic performance (Supplemental Material 3).

As shown in Table 4, both ChatGPT-4.5 (κ=0.367; 95% CI, 0.346 to 0.388) and Meta AI (κ=0.359; 95% CI, 0.339 to 0.379) demonstrated fair agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database. Agreement varied among antihypertensive drugs, with the highest concordance observed for bisoprolol, hydrochlorothiazide, and captopril (moderate agreement), whereas propranolol and telmisartan showed only slight agreement. The remaining drugs demonstrated fair agreement. Agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa values and interpreted according to the Landis and Koch [24] criteria (Table 4, Supplemental Material 4).

Across all 819 drug pairs, exact agreement among UpToDate Lexidrug, ChatGPT-4.5, and Meta AI was observed in 386 cases (47.1%), whereas ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI agreed with each other in 530 cases (64.7%), regardless of the reference classification. These findings indicate that although both AI platforms achieved comparable overall agreement, classification discrepancies remained common, limiting their reliability as standalone tools for DDI severity classification.

Although overall concordance was similar, agreement varied by medication. ChatGPT-4.5 showed greater agreement for amlodipine, diltiazem, bisoprolol, candesartan, and furosemide, whereas Meta AI performed better for nifedipine, irbesartan, lisinopril, ramipril, hydrochlorothiazide, and spironolactone. These findings indicate that agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database was drug-specific. A summary of the 10 most commonly co-prescribed antihypertensive drug combinations and their respective DDI classifications according to UpToDate Lexidrug, ChatGPT-4.5, and Meta AI is provided in Supplemental Material 5. The 2 AI platforms agreed with the reference database for only 3 of the 10 drug combinations. Notably, both platforms misclassified common combinations, including amlodipine–candesartan (n=314) and candesartan–acetylsalicylic acid (n=148), which UpToDate Lexidrug classified as having no known interaction but the AI platforms identified as clinically relevant interactions. Because these discrepancies involved frequently prescribed drug pairs, they may have greater clinical and public health implications than errors involving less common combinations. To further illustrate classification performance, representative examples of underestimated interaction severity, false-positive classifications, and discordant classifications between the 2 AI platforms are presented in Supplemental Material 6.

DISCUSSION

Several studies have compared LLMs, including ChatGPT-4o, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini, with conventional information sources. However, no study has specifically compared ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI with UpToDate Lexidrug, an evidence-based clinical decision support database widely used by healthcare professionals [26]. Although ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI are both based on LLMs, the 2 AI platforms use different models and therefore generate different outputs [14]. ChatGPT-4.5 showed greater concordance with UpToDate Lexidrug than Meta AI, achieving an accuracy of 80.5% in identifying antihypertensive drug interactions among older adults. This finding is consistent with Juhi et al. [27], who reported that ChatGPT correctly identified 19 of 20 drug interaction cases. In an evaluation of ChatGPT-4.0 for medication management services, ChatGPT-4.0 accurately handled 39 of 39 cases (100%), specifically by identifying drug interactions, providing treatment recommendations, and developing medication management plans [28]. Evidence regarding the performance of Meta AI for DDI detection remains limited; therefore, these findings may provide a basis for clinical pharmacists to select an appropriate AI platform for detecting drug interactions.

Previous research comparing several LLMs, ChatGPT-4.5 resulted in less overgeneralization in scientific text summarizing scientific texts compared to Llama 2 and Llama 3.3 [29]. Another study by Li et al. [30] evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of ChatGPT-4o compared to Llama 3–70B. The results showed that ChatGPT-4o achieved diagnostic accuracy in 55 of 287 cases (19.2%) based on medical history, 133 of 287 cases (46.3%) based on imaging findings, and 160 of 287 cases (55.7%) when medical history and imaging findings were combined. In comparison, Llama 3–70B demonstrated diagnostic accuracy in 58 of 287 cases (20.2%) based on medical history, 121 of 287 cases (42.2%) based on imaging findings, and 152 of 287 cases (53.0%) when medical history and imaging findings were combined. Although both AI platforms demonstrated only fair overall agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database, ChatGPT-4.5 consistently achieved greater concordance than Meta AI. This finding is consistent with previous research by Aksoyalp and Erdoğan [25], who reported that ChatGPT-3.5 achieved the highest agreement with UpToDate Lexidrug among the evaluated AI platforms. The higher weighted kappa values suggest that disagreements tended to occur between categories that were relatively close in severity. Overall agreement remained modest, indicating that AI should be used as a complementary DDI screening tool rather than as a replacement for evidence-based databases or clinical judgment. Because no a priori sample size calculation was performed, the findings should be interpreted cautiously when generalized to other populations or settings.

The higher sensitivity of ChatGPT-4.5 may reflect a broader pharmacological training corpus or a greater tendency to over-identify potential interactions rather than superior discriminative performance. This interpretation is consistent with Sicard et al. [31], who found that ChatGPT and Claude achieved approximately 99% sensitivity for known DDIs associated with adverse drug reactions, yet their specificity remained low (0.64–0.68), resulting in frequent misclassification of negative controls as true interactions. Similarly, Radha Krishnan et al. [32] reported that ChatGPT’s sensitivity varied substantially according to prompt wording, increasing when the prompt explicitly referenced “drug interaction,” a pattern consistent with a model that tends to flag an interaction when contextual cues suggestive of risk are present. The higher sensitivity of ChatGPT-4.5 likely reflects its greater tendency to classify drug pairs as interacting, resulting in a lower PPV. The limited specificity of both AI platforms may stem from their reliance on learned language patterns rather than curated evidence-based databases such as UpToDate Lexidrug, thereby increasing the risk of false-positive DDI classifications. A similar pattern was reported by Domián et al. [33], who found that LLMs could identify true interactions while simultaneously generating inaccurate information that may reduce their reliability as independent screening tools.

A related concern is AI hallucination, in which a generative model produces fluent and scientifically plausible outputs that are not grounded in verified evidence [34]. In DDI assessment, such errors may be difficult to recognize because incorrect interaction claims can be presented using legitimate pharmacological terminology. Radha Krishnan et al. [32] likewise noted that ChatGPT may incorporate unverified information from non-traditional sources, increasing the risk of pharmacologically plausible but unsupported classifications. In contrast, reference databases such as UpToDate Lexidrug provide classifications linked to traceable evidence sources.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, a temporal mismatch existed because prescribing data collected in 2024 were evaluated using AI models queried in 2026, meaning that the AI outputs may reflect pharmacological knowledge and database updates that were unavailable when the prescriptions were generated. Second, each drug pair was evaluated using a single standardized prompt; therefore, the reproducibility of AI responses across different prompts or repeated sessions was not assessed. Third, the findings are specific to the evaluated model versions (ChatGPT-4.5 and Meta AI [Llama 4 Scout]) and may not be generalizable to subsequent model updates, which may exhibit different performance characteristics. Fourth, the AI queries were intentionally standardized and did not incorporate patient-specific clinical information, such as drug dosage, renal function, comorbidities, or other contextual factors that may influence the clinical significance of DDIs. Finally, although UpToDate Lexidrug served as the reference standard, it should not be regarded as an absolute gold standard because reference databases differ in their evidence appraisal and updating processes; consequently, discordance between AI outputs and UpToDate Lexidrug does not necessarily imply that the AI classification is incorrect. Some limitations arise from the characteristics of transformer-based LLMs, which generate DDI classifications from statistical language patterns rather than mechanistic pharmacological knowledge [34]. This may contribute to variability between platforms and highlights the need for evidence-grounded approaches, such as retrieval-augmented generation and database-integrated AI systems. Although no identifiable patient data were submitted to the AI platforms, data privacy, governance, and regulatory compliance remain important considerations for clinical implementation.

ChatGPT-4.5 demonstrated a higher level of agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug classification compared to Meta AI (Llama 4 Scout) across all evaluated metrics. However, both platforms achieved only fair agreement in severity classification and produced significant classification discrepancies compared to the reference database. ChatGPT-4.5’s relatively low PPV also indicates a significant false-positive burden, which may limit its clinical utility. Therefore, neither platform should be considered a substitute for established evidence-based drug interaction resources. Future research should evaluate the models’ performance specifically for high-risk and clinically actionable DDIs, as well as assess whether AI-assisted screening can provide added value when integrated into pharmacist-led review processes.

These findings suggest a potential role for AI in medication safety screening, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, the modest agreement with the UpToDate Lexidrug reference database indicates that AI should complement, not replace, established DDI resources. Further external validation and appropriate regulatory oversight are required before AI-based tools can be safely integrated into clinical practice.
