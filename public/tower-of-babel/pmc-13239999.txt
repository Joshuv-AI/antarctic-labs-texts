Paediatric and Adult Solid Tumours Exhibiting NTRK Gene Fusions in Australia, 2020–2044: A Population‐Based Statistical Modelling Study
Authors: Qingwei Luo, Yoon‐Jung Kang, Jeff Cuff, John Zalcberg, Karen Canfell, Julia Steinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTRK inhibitors targeting NTRK fusion‐positive cancers have the potential to improve patient outcomes, but also have high costs. We report the first long‐term projections of cancer prevalence associated with NTRK gene fusions in Australia, to 2044.MethodsWe analysed national Australian Institute of Health and Welfare incidence (1982–2019) and survival (1991–2019), NSW Enduring Cancer Data Linkage incidence (1982–2019) and survival data (1982–2019). We projected 1‐year to 5‐year cancer prevalence using validated statistical methods, for all stages combined, advanced disease at diagnosis (here, distant metastasis/lymph node involvement), and advanced disease after progression post‐diagnosis. We estimated prevalence for all paediatric cancers combined (age < 18 years at diagnosis), all adult solid cancers combined, 18 cancer types/groups, and 5 cancer sub‐types relevant to current Australian government subsidies of TRK inhibitors.ResultsFor all solid cancers combined, we project increasing prevalence of individuals who were diagnosed with tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions, primarily due to population growth and ageing. For example, for 2‐year prevalence (aligning with 2.5 years average treatment assumed in previous economic assessments for a TRK inhibitor subsidy), the projected increases from 2019 to 2044 are: all stages combined, paediatric cancers 80 to 106 (+ 32.5%), adult cancers 645 to 970 (+ 50.4%); advanced disease at diagnosis, paediatric cancers 19 to 23 (+ 21.1%), adult cancers 185 to 261 (+ 41.1%); advanced disease after progression post‐diagnosis, paediatric cancers 22 to 30 (+ 36.4%), adult cancers 110 to 180 (+ 63.6%).ConclusionThe results from this study can support healthcare planning and budget forecasts, including for different potential government treatment subsidy scenarios.

Introduction

The burden of cancer is rising steadily globally and in Australia, and cancer continues to be the primary cause of premature mortality in many countries including Australia [1]. Advances in precision medicine have shown strong potential to improve patient outcomes through targeted cancer treatment based on tumour biomarkers. For example, TRK inhibitors (tropomyosin receptor kinase inhibitors, also known as “NTRK inhibitors”) have been approved or are under consideration for the treatment of advanced/metastatic solid tumours with NTRK (neurotrophic tropomyosin receptor kinase) gene fusions for many different cancer types and in some cases regardless of the originating tumour site, in several countries/jurisdictions including Australia, the European Union, UK, Japan and the USA [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. These approvals are largely based on survival improvements found in analyses of initial small‐scale trials, e. g. longer progression‐free survival for larotrectinib (Bayer Australia Ltd) versus real‐world standard of care (median 36.8 vs. 5.2 months, HR = 0.29 [95% CI: 0.18–0.46]) [9]. Previous case reports and early clinical studies have demonstrated rapid and durable responses to TRK inhibitors in patients whose tumours exhibit NTRK gene fusions, across tumour types [10, 11, 12].

NTRK gene fusions are generally rare, especially in common cancer types. However, high frequencies of NTRK gene fusions are found in paediatric cancers and a small number of adult cancers [5]. As of 01‐January‐2025, the Australian government has approved prescription subsidies for one TRK inhibitor (larotrectinib) for advanced/metastatic solid tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions and meeting certain eligibility criteria, for any paediatric cancers and five selected adult cancer sub‐types: mammary analogue secretory carcinoma of the salivary gland (MASC), secretory breast carcinoma (SBC), non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), soft tissue sarcoma, and glioma/glioblastoma/glioneuronal tumours [13, 14, 15]. To determine treatment eligibility, corresponding subsidies are also in place for tumour NTRK gene fusions tests for these cancer types (noting testing may also be available privately for patients not meeting subsidy criteria) [13].

Notably, original subsidy applications for health technology assessment in Australia included NTRK gene fusion testing and TRK inhibitor treatment subsidies for all cancers regardless of originating site, [16] aligning with preliminary regulatory approvals of the drug itself in Australia and other countries. Thus, to support healthcare planning and budget impact forecasts for TRK inhibitor use, including different scenarios for potential broadening of future subsidy approvals (e. g. more cancer types and/or earlier stage disease), estimates of cancer prevalence for a wide range of cancer types related to NTRK gene fusions are required, beyond those covered by current subsidy approvals. For example, in the revised subsidy application for larotrectinib, adult glioma/glioblastoma/glioneuronal tumours were added during the resubmission process due to already subsided panel testing including NTRK fusions and the low number of cases, which implied minimal budget impact [15]. In general, long‐term prevalence projections are important for strategic planning, anticipating future demand, forecasting long‐term financial implications and resource needs, and informing sustainable funding strategies.

Therefore, this study aims to project prevalence of solid cancers related to NTRK gene fusions in Australia to 2044, separately for paediatric and different adult cancers. This work builds on and advances the approach we previously established for biomarker‐related cancer prevalence projections [17]. Throughout, prevalence is defined as the number of individuals alive who have been diagnosed with cancer, aggregating across the duration of survival (e. g., 2‐year prevalence). In line with current subsidy criteria, analyses for paediatric cancers include everyone diagnosed at < 18 years of age [14]. Using these new prevalence estimates, we also present an illustration of potential costs for a treatment linked to NTRK gene fusions.

Methods

Data Sources and Selection of Cancer Types/Groups

Figure S1 shows the study approach and data sources (see also Appendix p.3). We obtained publicly available tabulated data on cancer incidence (1982–2019) and survival (1991–2019) from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) [18]. Additionally, corresponding Australian Historical Population Statistics and Population Projections (1982–2044; based on medium population growth) were obtained from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) [19, 20].

Individual‐level data for cancer diagnoses and survival were obtained from the Cancer Institute NSW's Enduring Cancer Data Linkage (CanDLe) including NSW Cancer Registry data (NSWCR; year of diagnosis 1982–2019, survival status to end of 2021) [21].

Following the approach established in our previous study, [17] we selected 18 high‐level adult cancer types/groups for detailed prevalence projections related to tumours with NTRK gene fusions, based on NTRK gene fusion prevalence [22, 23] and data availability (Appendix pp.3–4, Table S1). Estimates for all adult solid cancers combined were aggregated estimates for these 18 adult cancer types/groups, 4 additional high‐level adult cancer types/groups with limited data on proportion of tumours with NTRK gene fusions (Appendix p.3) and the remaining group of other solid cancers. As part of a comprehensive approach, our prevalence estimates included cancer types for which only a low proportion of tumours exhibit NTRK gene fusions, to project total numbers of individuals diagnosed with NTRK fusion‐positive tumours. This approach was selected for comprehensiveness and does not assume that testing for NTRK gene fusions will be indicated for all individuals and cancer types in the future.

We further specifically constructed prevalence projections for cancer types/groups or cancer sub‐types with TRK inhibitor treatment currently subsidised in Australia (here and throughout, correct as of 01‐January‐2025). This included paediatric cancers and 5 adult cancer sub‐types (soft tissue sarcoma, NSCLC, glioma/glioblastoma/glioneuronal tumours, MASC, and SBC), with these sub‐types also included in relevant projections for high‐level adult cancer types/groups (Appendix pp.3–4, Table S1).

Detailed Incidence and Prevalence Projections

We adapted previously developed methods, [17] with detailed information on all analyses provided in Appendix (pp.3–10). Briefly, we completed four analysis steps (Figure S1). We used previously published cancer incidence projections [17] for 17 high‐level cancer types/groups for all stages combined (Table S1), with all other incidence and prevalence analyses new or updated in this study using the latest available data.

1) Cancer Incidence Projections

We developed and validated new cancer incidence projections for all paediatric solid cancers combined and adult brain and other central nervous system (CNS) cancer, salivary gland cancer, soft tissue sarcoma, and NSCLC (Appendix pp.4–7, Figure S2). As in our previous study, [17] incidence estimates for advanced disease at diagnosis (here, defined as distant metastasis/lymph node involvement) for each cancer type/group were obtained by multiplying the projected number of new cancer cases by the proportion of cases diagnosed at advanced stage (Appendix p.3) [17]. As part of the analyses to determine the proportion of cases by stage at diagnosis, we applied a previously validated multiple imputation approach (using the Stata ‘ice’ package with multinomial logistic regression) [17] to impute cancer stage at diagnosis where recorded as ‘unknown’ in the NSW Cancer Registry data (Appendix pp.4–5, Figure S3).

2) All‐Cause and Cancer‐Specific Survival

We estimated overall all‐cause survival and cause‐specific cancer survival for 1 to 5 years follow‐up after initial diagnosis, by sex, age groups at diagnosis and 5‐year diagnosis period for individuals diagnosed 1982–2019. Stage‐specific cancer survival estimates were generated after multiple imputation for ‘unknown’ stage at diagnosis. All survival estimates were log–log‐linearly extrapolated (Stata command ipolate) forward to 2024 to account for the impact of recent improvements in treatment on survival. (Appendix p.8; Figure S4).

3) Prevalence Projections

We applied a modified counting method [17, 24] to estimate 1‐year to 5‐year cancer prevalence regardless of biomarker status, as a function of incidence and survival estimates, for all stages combined and advanced disease at diagnosis (Figure S5). As previously, [17] we estimated prevalence of individuals with advanced disease after progression post‐diagnosis by using information on cause‐specific cancer deaths and survival estimates by stage at diagnosis, restricting these estimates to progression ≤ 5 years of initial diagnosis (Appendix pp.8–9).

4) Prevalence of Individuals Previously Diagnosed With Tumours That Exhibit NTRK Gene Fusions

We estimated the prevalence of individuals previously diagnosed with tumours that exhibit NTRK gene fusions by multiplying the projected cancer prevalence by the proportion of tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions in each cancer type/group. The underlying estimates for proportion of tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions by cancer type/group were obtained from recent systematic review/meta‐analyses [22, 23] (Table S1; we note these estimates reflect the proportion of tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions among the tumours tested, not the percentage of people who undergo testing and have a fusion detected). As stage‐specific estimates and data on changes in proportions of tumours with NTRK gene fusions over time were not available, we used constant estimates for all stages combined.

For adult glioma/glioblastoma/glioneuronal tumours, MASC and SBC, we constructed simplified prevalence estimates based on the estimated prevalence for brain/CNS cancers, salivary gland cancers, and breast cancers, respectively, multiplying by the proportion of incident cancers with the specific histologic sub‐type (as the proportion among prevalent cancers was not available). Estimates were obtained separately for all stages combined, advanced disease at diagnosis, and advanced disease after progression post‐diagnosis (Appendix pp. 9–10).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses for prevalence estimates were performed using alternative assumptions for future survival: (1) using the unscaled NSW survival estimates; (2) assuming survival estimates remain constant for 2020–2044; and (3) extrapolating survival to 2029 and then assuming survival remains constant for 2030–2044.

For prevalence estimates for all adult solid cancers combined, we also performed sensitivity analyses with alternative assumptions about the proportion of tumours with NTRK gene fusions for 4 cancer types/groups with limited data (Table S1).

Potential Aggregate Costs for TRK Inhibitor Treatment

As an indicative, exploratory illustration for the potential use of prevalence estimates to support analyses of treatment costs, we estimated the potential costs for larotrectinib treatment based on NTRK gene status, for paediatric cancers and 5 adult sub‐types currently covered by Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) subsidies. This calculation used an average treatment duration of 2.5 years, as per the analysis in the approved subsidy application [14] The cost of larotrectinib was based on the ‘published cost’ in the PBS of AUD$10,062 per 100 mg dose and AUD$2638 per 25 mg dose (Appendix p.10) [25]. We calculated the estimated average 1‐year budget costs over the period 2024–2026, applying the above treatment duration and dosages (as per the analysis in the subsidy application [14]) to relevant estimates of 2.5‐year prevalence (estimated as average of 2‐year and 3‐year prevalence) of individuals diagnosed with tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions (advanced stage at diagnosis or after progression post‐diagnosis only).

We then compared estimates with previously published government announcements related to subsidy approvals, and to aggregate‐level PBS prescription records for larotrectinib ‘initial treatment’ (the first time a patient receives a PBS‐subsidised medicine for a specific condition; as opposed to ‘continuing treatment’, which refers to subsequent prescriptions for the same condition) in 2022, 2023, and 2024 (Table S5).

Across all analyses, estimates from this study are presented with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) as estimated by statistical models based on available data and model‐specific assumptions. We note no estimate can fully capture the inherent uncertainty in future cancer incidence, survival, stage distribution, population dynamics, or the uptake of emerging diagnostic tests. All analyses were performed using Stata (version 18, Stata Corporation, College Station, TX) and R (Version 4.4.1).

Results

Projected Cancer Incidence and Survival for 5 Additional Cancer Types/Groups

For all paediatric solid cancers combined, the age‐standardised incidence rate is expected to remain relatively stable from 2019 to 2044 for all stages combined and for advanced disease at diagnosis (Figure 1). For adult NSCLC, the age‐standardised incidence rate is expected to decrease for all stages combined (33.4 per 100,000 [95% UI: 32.0–34.9] people in 2019, 30.0 [95% UI: 27.3–33.0] in 2044) and for advanced disease at diagnosis (20.1 [95% UI: 19.0–21.3] per 100,000 people in 2019, 16.2 [95% UI: 14.4–18.2] in 2044). For adult brain/other CNS cancers, salivary gland cancers, and soft tissue sarcomas, the age‐standardised incidence rate is expected to remain stable for all stages combined and for advanced disease at diagnosis.

From 2019 to 2044, the 2‐year overall prevalence of individuals previously diagnosed with solid tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions is projected to increase for paediatric (+ 32.5%, from 80 [95% UI: 51–110] in 2019 to 106 [95% UI: 63–150] in 2044) and adult cancers (+ 50.4%, from 645 [95% UI: 407–881] to 970 [95% UI: 589–1355]). The corresponding prevalence is also projected to increase for advanced disease at diagnosis (paediatric: + 21.1%, from 19 [95% UI: 11–26] to 23 [95% UI: 12–34]; adult: + 41.1%, from 185 [95% UI: 113–257] to 261 [95% UI: 151–370]) and advanced disease after progression post‐diagnosis (paediatric: + 36.4%, from 22 [95% UI: 14–31] to 30 [95% UI: 18–43]; adult: + 63.6%, from 110 [95% UI: 62–154] to 180 [95% UI: 94–264]).

Among all cancers regardless of biomarker status, the 5 adult cancer sub‐types for which TRK inhibitor treatment based on NTRK gene fusion status is subsidised by the PBS account for 9.5% [95% UI: 8.5%–10.7%] of the 2‐year prevalence for all stages combined (average 2020–2044), 13.8% [95% UI: 12.0%–16.0%] for advanced disease at diagnosis and 12.3% [95% UI: 9.5%–15.7%] for advanced disease after progression post‐diagnosis (Figure 2A). When restricting to individuals previously diagnosed with tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions, these cancer sub‐types account for substantially higher proportions of the 2‐year prevalence of all adult solid cancers (Figure 2B): 30.1% [95% CI: 19.4%–40.9%] for all stages combined, 25.8% [95% UI: 15.3%–36.6%] for advanced disease at diagnosis, and 33.2% [95% UI: 15.4%–51.0%] for advanced disease after progression.

Potential Aggregate Costs for TRK Inhibitor Treatment

As an illustrative and highly exploratory calculation, based on the published PBS pricing for larotrectinib and prevalence estimates in this study, the total annualised cost of TRK inhibitor treatment for paediatric solid tumours and 5 adult sub‐types currently included in subsidy approvals was estimated to be AUD$46.0 M [95% UI: AUD$29.2 M—AUD$63.9 M] in 2024 (Table 2). We note these estimates are highly approximate and illustrative only, based on prevalence estimates rather than actual treated populations, i.e., do not reflect full eligibility criteria, incomplete uptake, variability in dosing patterns or treatment costs (Appendix pp.10–11).

Estimates included in previous government announcements were generally similar to our 1‐year prevalence estimates and lower than our 2.5‐year prevalence estimates (Table S5), noting direct comparisons cannot be made as our prevalence estimates do not include the detailed eligibility criteria (Appendix pp.10–11).

Discussion

This study presents the first long‐term projections of cancer prevalence associated with NTRK gene fusions in Australia to 2044. We projected 1‐year to 5‐year prevalence of individuals previously diagnosed with solid tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions in Australia, separately for all paediatric cancers combined, all adult cancers combined, and 18 high‐level adult cancer types/groups, as well as for 5 adult cancer sub‐types relevant to current TRK inhibitor subsidies. The prevalence of individuals whose tumours exhibit NTRK gene fusions across all solid cancers is expected to increase for all stages combined, advanced stage at diagnosis, and advanced stage after progression post‐diagnosis, mainly due to population growth (paediatric and adult cancers) and ageing (adult cancers only).

Our prevalence estimates of individuals previously diagnosed with tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions were based on the frequency of NTRK gene fusions among tumours, obtained from recent systematic review/meta‐analyses and derived from different patient cohorts using different assays [22, 23]. Population‐level estimates for frequency of NTRK gene fusions depend on the test technology; as next‐generation sequencing (NGS) is more expensive but also more comprehensive and reliable, [26] we used NGS‐based estimates in this study, noting tests using older assays could have led to an underestimation of the true frequency of NTRK gene fusions [27]. In our study, estimates aggregating prevalence across all adult cancer types/groups and all stages suggest 2‐year prevalence of individuals previously diagnosed with tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions accounts for 0.31% [95% UI: 0.21%–0.40%] of the 2‐year prevalence of individuals with all adult solid cancers. This estimate is compatible with a pooled estimate from a previous systematic review (0.24%, 95% CI: 0.19%–0.29%, based on three studies using DNA/RNA panels, with the published estimate within our 95% UI) [22].

While more comprehensive data on biomarker frequency are expected to emerge through more routine testing, our estimates can help support interim health system planning for NTRK gene fusion testing and TRK inhibitor treatment. These results can also support health economic evaluations for potential future expansion of TRK inhibitor subsidy to additional cancer types/groups or cancer stages. For example, for advanced stage at diagnosis, the 5 adult cancer sub‐types with TRK inhibitors subsidised through the PBS (as of 01‐January‐2025) for tumours with NTRK gene fusions (subject to eligibility criteria) account for 13.8% of the 2‐year prevalence of all solid tumours regardless of biomarker status, but 25.8% when restricting to tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions. As a limitation, we note that not all current or future eligibility criteria (such as different cancer stage criteria or absence of resistance mutations) nor incomplete treatment uptake (e.g, due to general ill health) are reflected in the prevalence estimates. Additionally, we did not incorporate variability in the uptake of NTRK gene fusion testing itself, which may influence the number of patients identified as eligible for treatment, particularly among adults with newly diagnosed solid cancers. Thus, our prevalence estimates are generally higher than estimates of expected TRK inhibitor patient populations from related government announcements. Notably, our estimates focus on the total prevalence related to tumours with NTRK gene fusions (based on proportions among tested tumours in large studies and meta‐analyses), and do not incorporate specific scenarios for incomplete tumour test uptake in practice. Another limitation is that our estimates do not account for potential differences in NTRK gene fusion prevalence by ancestry. For example, a previous study found marginally higher proportions of tumours with NTRK gene fusions in patients with Asian ancestry (though some of the difference may also be explained by differences in cancer types) [28]. Nonetheless, our prevalence estimates can be helpful as robust, independent benchmarks constructed using rigorous statistical methods and with explicit assumptions, and can be adjusted in the future once more detailed test uptake, histology and treatment data become available on population level.

Meanwhile, we provided an exploratory indicative illustration of the potential budget impacts for TRK inhibitor treatment based on NTRK fusion status, focusing on all paediatric solid cancers and 5 adult solid cancers with TRK inhibitors currently subsidised through the PBS. Based on our high‐level prevalence estimates, treating 53 individuals under 18 years and 110 adults could cost AUD$46 M in 2024 based on the PBS cost listings. We note actual costs to the Australian government might differ substantially due to confidential Special Pricing Arrangements with the sponsor [29]. Moreover, as discussed above, the number of patients meeting real‐world treatment eligibility criteria could be substantially lower than relevant estimates of cancer prevalence, due to factors such as drug‐resistant mutations and performance status. Our explicitly exploratory analysis also does not consider real‐world treatment discontinuations and dose adjustments, which could be substantially different from assumptions reflected in original subsidy applications. In the future, generic versions of larotrectinib might also be introduced and reduce treatment costs, with patented use of TRK inhibitors to treat specific cancer types expected to begin expiring as early as 2029 [30].

We note that there are limitations in this study related to the inherent uncertainty of all long‐term projections and potential model misspecification bias in statistical modelling and survival analyses. In particular, we assumed that the most recent trends in cancer incidence and survival will continue into the future. However, the projections cannot account for future changes in population‐level risk or the effects of revised or new screening programs (e g, the new Australian National Lung Cancer Screening Program, which commenced July 2025), new early detection technologies, or new treatments, which may disrupt existing trends [17, 31]. While our sensitivity analyses show that the prevalence estimates are robust to small changes in survival, the introduction of new early detection technologies or new treatments could lead to substantial changes in cancer incidence, stage distribution, progression, and survival, thus impacting estimates in 1 to 5 year prevalence. For example, improved early detection and early‐stage treatment could increase prevalence related to early stage at diagnosis, while decreasing prevalence estimates related to advanced disease at diagnosis and ‐diagnosis. Dedicated efforts beyond the scope of this study would be needed for in‐depth scenario assessments of the impact for different hypothetical changes. As a second limitation, we used NSW data on stage‐specific incidence and survival. However, cancer incidence and mortality rates for most cancer types/groups in NSW are comparable to national figures and NSW accounts for one‐third of the Australian population [18]. Another limitation is that 16.8% of NSW cancer cases had “unknown” stage at diagnosis recorded in the cancer registry. As in our previous study, [17] we used a multiple imputation approach to impute stage where unknown, applying an approach that has been previously validated for prostate cancer [32]. We note the imputation results could be affected if there is a major departure from multiple imputation assumptions. Furthermore, we estimated disease progression using cancer specific survival, which could be an underestimate of cancer prevalence for advanced stage post‐diagnosis because of some misclassifications of cause of deaths. As the quality of the NSWCR data is known to be high, this under‐estimation should be minimal [33, 34]. However, we focused on disease progression within 5 years of initial diagnosis, which could under‐estimate progression due to recurrence outside this timeframe. We applied the same frequency of NTRK gene fusions for all disease stages and across all calendar periods, and did not consider differences in survival by NTRK gene fusion status or different treatment uptake. A key limitation is also that the frequency of NTRK gene fusions was based on international data, which may not reflect testing practices, ancestry distribution, assay use, or population health profiles relevant for Australia. Thus, it would be important to update prevalence estimates once robust Australian data on frequency of NTRK gene fusions become available from sufficiently large‐scale studies. Lastly, we only provided approximate estimates for rarer cancers (MASC, SBC) and glioma/glioblastoma/glioneuronal tumours, which should thus be interpreted with caution. In particular, these prevalence estimates were based on survival in high‐level cancer groups, which may not accurately reflect the prognosis for these sub‐types. For MASC and SBC, there is insufficient robust information on survival to determine potential differences from relevant high‐level cancer groups. For glioma/glioblastoma/glioneuronal tumours, the AIHW reported 5‐year observed survival of 21.7% (95% CI 20.8%–22.6%) in 2017–2021, which was very similar and only very slightly lower than the estimated survival of 22.8% (95% CI 22.0%–23.7%) for brain cancer [18]. Nevertheless, our methodology allows for readily available future updates to projections as more detailed and representative data become available. We also note that there are several limitations to our very approximate estimates of potential treatment costs, as indicated above and discussed in detail in the Appendix (p. 11).

There are many strengths in this study. We used high‐quality long‐term data on cancer, integrating extensive data for stage distribution at diagnosis, stage‐specific survival, and proportion of tumours exhibiting NTRK gene fusions. We used ‐validated statistical methods and applied a systematic approach for long‐term projections of cancer incidence and prevalence, adapted from a previous publication [17] which can be generalisable to other populations. The reliability of our projections was demonstrated through comprehensive validations of the modelling approaches using observed data. We provided detailed prevalence projections for individual high‐level cancer types/groups and for specific cancer sub‐types relevant to current subsidies. All estimates are made publicly available through supplementary data, and can be explored via the interactive Cancer Patient Population Projections (Cancer‐PPP) dashboard [Dashboard URL see https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.28260398] to help support future health policy decisions, budget impact forecasts, and healthcare planning including for potential expanded use of TRK inhibitor treatments.

Conclusion

This is the first study projecting prevalence of solid cancers associated with NTRK gene fusions in Australia, also adding overall projections for all paediatric solid cancers and adult NSCLC, for which there were no existing projections available. These detailed projections can inform national health technology assessments and health policy related to TRK inhibitors. Finally, the easily accessible detailed projections of 1‐year to 5‐year cancer prevalence regardless of biomarker status (based on latest available data) can be used to inform broader health policy and healthcare planning related to cancer.
