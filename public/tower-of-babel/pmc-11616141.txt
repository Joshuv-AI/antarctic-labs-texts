Management of severe traumatic brain injury in a rivaroxaban overdose: illustrative case
Authors: Madeline J Foertsch, Henry T Beckett, Lauren M Dehne, Stephanie Janusz, Simona Ferioli, Laura B Ngwenya, Molly E Droege
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThe management of rivaroxaban overdose in severe traumatic brain injury (sTBI) is undocumented. Reversal with andexanet alfa (AA) and prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs) in cases of supratherapeutic doses remains unproven. Management is further complicated by the absence of real-time serum rivaroxaban concentration assays and drug-specific coagulation assays. Intracranial hemorrhage following traumatic brain injury (TBI) poses a significant challenge in patients on anticoagulation medications. Approximately 9% of patients on anticoagulants who experience mild TBI injuries develop intracranial hemorrhage.1, 2 This is concerning given the increasing rates of oral anticoagulant use, with the most recent population data showing a doubling of Medicare beneficiaries who were prescribed oral anticoagulants between 2011 and 2019.3 An increasing number of these patients are taking direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), which have become mainstays in the management and prevention of thrombosis, replacing vitamin K antagonists (i.e., warfarin).4 DOACs have several advantages, including less routine monitoring, rapid onset of action, shorter half-life, and fewer food and drug interactions. Direct factor Xa inhibitors are a subset of DOACs, with the two most prescribed agents being rivaroxaban and apixaban. Unlike warfarin, controversy remains regarding optimal DOAC reversal, especially in trauma. Additionally, there is no guidance for DOAC reversal of supratherapeutic doses, such as in an overdose.

Direct factor Xa inhibitor reversal can be achieved with andexanet alfa (AA), approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2018.5–7 Concern exists regarding the safety and cost of AA. Specifically, AA can cost up to $27,000 and may carry an increased risk of thrombotic events in the 30 days following administration.8–12 Moreover, the efficacy of AA in the reversal of supratherapeutic doses remains unclear, yet it is paramount to prevent massive hemorrhage and death. Guidance on the management of rivaroxaban overdose in critically ill patients with neurological injury is sparse. The absence of real-time, reliable serum rivaroxaban concentration assays and drug-specific coagulation assays further complicates management.13 Specific treatment modalities, including AA, prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs), and plasma exchange (PLEX), remain unproven.

PLEX, also known as “plasmapheresis,” involves removing blood from the body, separating out plasma from the cellular blood elements, and then discarding the plasma. Plasma is then replaced with fresh frozen plasma (FFP) or albumin. Medications with high protein binding and a low volume of distribution should concentrate in the plasma compartment and, in theory, can be removed by PLEX.14 Rivaroxaban is 92%–95% bound to plasma proteins, which makes PLEX a possible elimination option in an overdose.15 One case demonstrated approximately reduced low-molecular-weight heparin calibrated chromogenic anti-Xa (AXA) by 50% as a surrogate for rivaroxaban removal prior to emergent surgery.16 The case presented herein describes the use of AA, PCC, and PLEX following rivaroxaban overdose in a patient with severe traumatic brain injury (sTBI) requiring hemicraniectomy.

Illustrative Case

Presentation

A 36-year-old female presented to the regional American College of Surgeons–verified level 1 trauma center with a chief complaint of drug overdose and trauma. Her past medical history included Behcet’s disease, epilepsy, stroke, type 2 diabetes mellitus (insulin dependent), venous thromboembolism on anticoagulation, gastric bypass with gastroparesis, and a gastric tube. The initial report from emergency medical services (EMS) included ingestion of an anticoagulant and insulin in unknown amounts. The report indicated that the patient had been found on the scene with several empty insulin vials. The patient originally consented to EMS ground transport to the hospital. However, she jumped out of the ambulance en route to the hospital and was struck by a vehicle, resulting in an sTBI.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, the patient was agitated and combative, requiring sedation. She was hypertensive and tachycardic (blood pressure 157/107 mm Hg, heart rate 110 bpm), with a 3-cm posterior scalp laceration. Her pupils were equal, round, and reactive to light, and her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 13 (eye-opening score 3, verbal response score 4, and motor response score 6). The physical examination was otherwise negative for acute findings. Trauma workup, including head, spine, and abdomen computed tomography (CT), as well as CT angiography with contrast was performed. The patient had a documented severe allergy to contrast, and despite premedication with intravenous methylprednisolone (125 mg) and intravenous diphenhydramine (50 mg), she developed a severe allergic reaction requiring intubation. The head CT scan demonstrated bifrontal hemorrhagic contusions, a 5.6-mm left subdural hematoma (SDH) with 4 mm of midline shift, mild scattered subarachnoid hemorrhage, and diffuse cerebral edema (Fig. 1). Imaging also demonstrated a nondisplaced fracture of the right occipital bone, extending through the condyle and right aspect of the clivus with venous sinus thrombosis of the right transverse sinus adjacent to the fracture. The neurosurgical team was consulted while the patient was in the emergency department after initial imaging was performed.

Initial noncontrast head CT scan obtained upon arrival in the emergency department, demonstrating 4.1 mm of midline shift and a left-sided acute SDH measuring 5.6 mm.

Notable admission coagulation studies included an international normalized ratio (INR) of 4.9, prothrombin time (PT) of 47.4 seconds (normal 12.1–15.1 seconds), thromboelastography (TEG) reaction time of 85 seconds (normal 22–44 seconds), and an undetectable AXA concentration (i.e., < 0.1 units/mL).

Hospital Course

The patient was admitted to the neurosciences intensive care unit due to risk of worsening intracranial hemorrhage due to her coagulopathy. Her medication list through the insurance dispense report linked to the electronic medical record demonstrated a history of both warfarin and rivaroxaban dispensing within the past year. Given the time frame in which the medications had been dispensed, the patient could have had a supply of both medications, although rivaroxaban had been dispensed most recently. Given the low AXA and elevated INR, it was assumed that she had overdosed on warfarin and not rivaroxaban. Four-factor PCC (34 units/kg) and vitamin K (10 mg) were intravenously (IV) administered. Postadministration laboratory values did not improve and remained elevated: INR 3.5; PT 36.2 seconds; activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) 41.1 seconds; and TEG reaction time 100 seconds. Vitamin K (10 mg IV) was repeated, and 4 units of FFP were administered, resulting in minimally changed coagulation parameters: INR of 3.1 and TEG reaction time of 95 seconds (Fig. 2).

The neurological examination worsened, with the left pupil becoming unreactive to light. A repeat head CT scan demonstrated a worsening SDH, now measuring 7 mm with a 9-mm midline shift (Fig. 3). Although emergency surgery was indicated, concerns regarding the patient’s coagulopathy delayed surgical intervention until a strategy for correction was defined. The patient was temporized with hypertonic saline (100 mEq of sodium chloride 14.6%). At this time, the hematology service was consulted, given the recent rivaroxaban dispense history and ongoing coagulopathy despite medical intervention. A repeat AXA assay was performed to re-evaluate the presence of rivaroxaban. Prior to the AXA results, the hematology consult team and neurosurgical team collaboratively decided to empirically administer AA (400-mg bolus) followed by an infusion of 4 mg/min for 120 minutes. The pre-AA administration AXA was supratherapeutic (i.e., > 1.8 units/mL), confirming the presence of rivaroxaban in the bloodstream. Hemicraniectomy continued to be deferred until laboratory parameters were more favorable for intervention.

Second noncontrast head CT (A–C) performed 6 hours after initial CT (preoperatively), demonstrating a worsening 7-mm acute left-sided SDH and multiple bilateral foci of intraparenchymal hemorrhage in the frontal lobes, resulting in a 9-mm midline shift.

Repeat coagulation parameters were obtained 3 hours after AA administration. AXA remained undetectably high at > 1.8 units/mL, PT was 24.6 seconds, aPTT was 36.2 seconds, INR was 2.1, and TEG reaction time was 65 seconds. Multidisciplinary discussion occurred among neurosurgery, clinical pharmacy, hematology, and neurocritical care teams regarding additional urgent strategies for reversal. PLEX was considered since the patient had already received reversal with vitamin K, PCC, FFP, and AA. The renal team was consulted for emergency PLEX for the removal of rivaroxaban from plasma prior to urgent surgery. PLEX effluent was available in real time, which demonstrated an AXA of > 1.8 units/mL, suggesting rivaroxaban removal. The serum rivaroxaban concentration was not available in real time but was ordered pre- and post-PLEX and sent out to the reference laboratory. After PLEX, the TEG reaction time normalized to 40 seconds, and AXA and INR remained high at > 1.8 units/mL and 2.1, respectively.

Given the reassuring TEG reaction time and the AXA level from the PLEX effluent indicating drug removal, the patient was then taken to the operating room for decompressive hemicraniectomy after the completion of PLEX. In case of bleeding complications, the pharmacy was prepared to provide an additional dose of AA; however, intraoperatively, there were no bleeding complications and no additional blood products or pharmacology reversals were administered. Postoperative imaging did not show signs of continued hemorrhage (Fig. 4). Postoperative day (POD) 1 AXA remained > 1.8 units/mL, INR decreased slightly to 1.8, and TEG reaction time was 55 seconds. Over the next 48 hours, the patient’s coagulopathy remained stable and she did not require additional blood products or reversal therapy.

Noncontrast CT (A–C) performed after left-sided decompressive hemicraniectomy, showing successful evacuation of left SDH and improvement of midline shift. Additionally, no progression of hemorrhage appears.

Her immediate postoperative GCS score was 6 (1,T,4), and on POD 6 her GCS score was 7 (2,T,4). Her condition improved with medical management, and she was extubated on POD 8 with a GCS score of 8 (2,1,5); she was also able to follow commands. On POD 12, her GCS score was 11 (4,1,6), but she was aphasic. Finally, on POD 17, she was discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility.

Notably, around 10 days after admission, the pre- and post-PLEX serum rivaroxaban concentrations were 534.6 and 256.8 ng/mL, respectively. The day following PLEX, the rivaroxaban concentration was 167.5 ng/mL. The serum AXA concentrations remained supratherapeutic for a prolonged time and became detectable at 1.2 units/mL on hospital day 11 (i.e., POD 10). Repeat serum rivaroxaban concentration on hospital day 11 was 57.2 ng/mL.

Discussion

Observations

This is the first documented case of rivaroxaban overdose with concomitant sTBI requiring multipronged medical and surgical management with positive outcomes. Moreover, this case is the first documented successful use of PLEX to reduce serum rivaroxaban concentrations by 52% to within a normal therapeutic concentration (reference peak range 249–270 ng/mL; 5th–95th percentile 184–419 ng/mL).17

PLEX has been evaluated to help facilitate drug removal in overdose situations, but there are limited data evaluating rivaroxaban removal.14, 18 Rivaroxaban is highly protein bound (92%–95%) but has a moderate to large volume of distribution. Given the volume of distribution, there is a risk that rivaroxaban could redistribute post-PLEX. The rivaroxaban level immediately post-PLEX was 256.8 ng/mL and remained low, with a 9-hour post-PLEX level of 167.5 ng/mL, indicating minimal redistribution of rivaroxaban. Although this is limited since the second level was assessed less than 24 hours after completion of the PLEX, it did show a continued decrease in the serum concentration.

The deranged admission coagulation studies with unclear pre-admission medications clouded initial management decisions in the setting of severe injuries. The initial coagulation results were more consistent with warfarin ingestion, given the undetectable AXA and elevated INR values. AXA assays can be used to assess the presence of rivaroxaban.19–21 In order to correlate with therapeutic levels or a quantitative assessment, an AXA assay calibrated for the specific suspected agent should be utilized. In our case, the Stago chromogenic AXA assay hybrid calibrated to both unfractionated heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin was utilized.22–24 Although there is a study comparing Stago AXA assay to rivaroxaban concentration, the AXA is still limited as it is not calibrated to DOACs and there is no defined therapeutic AXA level.25 Again, in our case, the initial undetectable AXA delayed the diagnosis of rivaroxaban overdose. It is still unclear why the initial AXA was not detectable given the other coagulation abnormalities noted above. This could have been due to a laboratory error, or the patient’s history of gastric bypass and gastroparesis could have slowed the absorption of rivaroxaban, leading to the delayed increase in AXa levels

This case also demonstrated the expected abnormalities in other standard coagulation studies. Other studies have demonstrated that TEG reaction time is elevated when rivaroxaban concentrations are elevated or on the higher end of the therapeutic range but may be normal when rivaroxaban is in the lower to normal therapeutic range.26–28 Our case also demonstrated that the TEG reaction time remained elevated, while rivaroxaban concentrations remained above the normal therapeutic range. Although not widely available currently, there are point-of-care DOAC-calibrated assays available (i.e., ROTEM EXTEM, ClotPro RVV-test or ECA-test clotting time, and TEG 6s anti-factor Xa or direct thrombin inhibitor channel) that have been shown to reliably detect the coagulation effects of DOACs.27 At therapeutic concentrations, the PT can be normal to slightly elevated, and our case demonstrated elevated PT levels while rivaroxaban concentrations were supratherapeutic.29

Interestingly, in this case, PCC and AA failed to improve the coagulopathy in the setting of rivaroxaban overdose. AA has been shown to be an effective reversal agent during cases of bleeding at therapeutic doses of rivaroxaban.6, 7 Retrospective data have indicated that PCC can have comparable hemostatic efficacy. There are limited data on how to obtain hemostasis in the setting of an overdose. To our knowledge, there is only one report utilizing AA that described successful reversal of an apixaban overdose in sTBI.30 An important factor to consider is that the effect of AA is dose dependent.31 Thus, it is likely that the current recommended dosing of AA is insufficient in the cases of supratherapeutic doses (i.e., overdose) of rivaroxaban. This is consistent with the observations in our case. Thus, we believe that standard AA dosing is potentially insufficient to totally resolve coagulopathy in cases of supratherapeutic doses of rivaroxaban.

It is also telling that it took a significant amount of time for the AXA to normalize. In an overdose, normal pharmacokinetic parameters are altered, and the toxicokinetics of rivaroxaban are not well understood. Rivaroxaban has a half-life of 6–15 hours in patients with normal renal function.31 Therefore, at normal therapeutic concentrations, rivaroxaban concentrations would be expected to be minimal after 3 days of withholding the medication, or 5 half-lives. In our case, it took 11 days to obtain normal therapeutic trough levels of rivaroxaban, with an observed half-life of about 35 hours (reference trough range 26–44 ng/mL; 5th–95th percentile 6–137 ng/mL).17 In an overdose situation, the drug concentration is much higher than therapeutic ranges. Therefore, it will take longer to clear the medication, but that amount of time is unknown as the real-time drug concentrations are not available. It is also difficult to determine alterations in other pharmacokinetic parameters (i.e., excretion, metabolism, and distribution). The patient had normal renal function and liver function; therefore, these factors should not have led to an increase in half-life. Rivaroxaban has a moderate to large volume of distribution of 50 L, which could have also accounted for the prolonged clearance in such a massive overdose.

PLEX is a viable adjunct therapeutic option to help bridge the gap between reversal strategies effect and hemostasis, especially in the setting of hematological stabilization prior to surgery. In our case, PLEX was effective in the removal of rivaroxaban from the serum. The rivaroxaban plasma concentration was reduced by 52% following PLEX therapy, demonstrating its potential usefulness moving forward in the management of rivaroxaban overdose.

Lessons

Understanding the expected abnormalities in coagulation studies is essential when urgently evaluating for the presence of DOACs. This case demonstrated effective stabilization of rivaroxaban overdose prior to neurosurgery. The treatments used included a combination of PCCs, AA, and PLEX. PLEX can be an important adjunct to consider for rivaroxaban removal. Our case demonstrated that the rivaroxaban plasma concentration decreased by 52% following PLEX therapy.
