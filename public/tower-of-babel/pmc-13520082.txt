Peri-treatment ctDNA dynamics in locally advanced cervical cancer: pilot study from a safety-net hospital
Authors: Maya Yasukawa, Adrian Kohut, Diana C Joseph, Jaisa Miller, Nicole Hurtado, Nancy Preston, Chrissy Liu, Lindsey Nguy, Vaagn Andikyan, Thomas Rutherford, Matthew L Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveReal-time kinetics of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) during active concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) for locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC) remain poorly characterized. We evaluated peri-treatment ctDNA dynamics in a real-world safety-net cohort using a commercially available tumor-informed assay.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed serial ctDNA measurements (Signatera) in 17 LACC patients who underwent definitive CCRT, with or without induction chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. Patients were classified by longitudinal ctDNA kinetics as Sustained clearance, Persistent/Re-positive, or Negative.ResultsBaseline ctDNA was detectable in 15 of 17 patients (88%); patients were classified as Sustained clearance (n = 8), Persistent/Re-positive (n = 7), or Negative (n = 2). With mean follow-up of 273 ± 148 days, all 5 clinical recurrences occurred in the Persistent/Re-positive group (5/7 vs 0/8; Fisher's exact P = 0.007), representing a strong but preliminary association. Among 8 non-induction-treated patients with detectable baseline ctDNA and ≥ 2 assessments spanning baseline and intra-CCRT, molecular clearance during active CCRT occurred in 5 (62.5%), first observed between days 27 and 43; none developed recurrence. Trajectories were frequently non-monotonic, with isolated negative results within otherwise-positive courses. ctDNA preceded imaging-based recurrence in three of five recurrent patients by a median of 124 days (range 70–240). Three recurrent patients were considered candidates for total pelvic exenteration.ConclusionsDetectable ctDNA declined rapidly during CCRT but frequently followed a non-monotonic trajectory. Serial rather than isolated measurements appear necessary to interpret molecular response during active treatment. Prospective studies are warranted to determine whether trajectory-based ctDNA monitoring can improve risk stratification and recurrence detection.Keywords: Cervical cancer, CCRT, ctDNA, Recurrence

Introduction

Fragments of tumor-derived DNA are released into the peripheral bloodstream during cellular turnover and cell death (Bettegowda et al., 2014; Stroun et al., 2001; Schwarzenbach et al., 2011). These fragments, known as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), have emerged as a promising, non-invasive biomarker capable of detecting minimal residual disease (MRD), persistence and early recurrence, possibly as early as several months before radiographic or clinical evidence of disease progression (Mayadev et al., 2025; Landon et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2025). In cervical cancer, recent analyses from the phase III CALLA trial have shown that detection of persistent ctDNA following the completion of concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is strongly associated with recurrence risk and survival outcomes in patients with locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC) (Mayadev et al., 2025). These findings established the prognostic value of post-treatment ctDNA assessment and reinforced growing interest in longitudinal ctDNA monitoring (Mayadev et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Diehl et al., 2008). However, the dynamics of ctDNA response to active treatment of cervical cancer remain poorly understood. Most existing studies rely on landmark sampling strategies performed before and after completion of therapy, limiting characterization of the dynamic molecular response occurring during treatment itself (Mayadev et al., 2025). As a result, it is not yet known whether the intra-treatment ctDNA trajectory itself carries clinically relevant information beyond isolated post-treatment time points. Furthermore, it is unclear whether the interpretation of ctDNA kinetics is more complex in patients receiving induction chemotherapy prior to CCRT. Following results of the INTERLACE study, use of induction chemotherapy has gained relevance, especially in safety-net populations (Mayadev et al., 2025; McCormack et al., 2024). However, ctDNA response to induction chemotherapy has not yet been evaluated. Lastly, it remains uncertain whether commercially available tumor-informed assays can capture the kinetics of disease response under circumstances of opportunistic real-world sampling.

To address these deficiencies, we assembled a real-world case series of patients undergoing definitive treatment of LACC for whom levels of ctDNA were serially profiled using a commercially available tumor-informed ctDNA assay. By characterizing longitudinal ctDNA response patterns across treatment phases, we aim to assess the feasibility of integrating ctDNA dynamics into real-time clinical decision-making for women impacted by this disease.

Methods

Study design

After obtaining approval by the local institutional review board (STUDY009759), all adult women (age ≥ 18 years) who underwent definitive treatment for LACC at a safety-net hospital between August 1, 2024, and November 30, 2025, were retrospectively identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes related to cervical cancer (C53.x). Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: 1) initial ctDNA samples obtained more than 30 days after initiation of definitive treatment, 2) initial ctDNA sampling began during maintenance therapy, treatment for recurrent diseases, or surveillance phase, 3) no evaluable ctDNA result was obtained and 4) follow-up duration was less than 3 months. The clinical course of all eligible subjects was reviewed from the time of cohort entry until death, loss to follow-up, or the administrative data cutoff date of March 31, 2026, whichever occurred first.

For all subjects, definitive treatment was defined as CCRT with or without induction chemotherapy, with or without subsequent maintenance, administered at the discretion of gynecology oncologists. Approved investigators reviewed electronic medical records to abstract the following variables including age, 2018 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) cervical cancer stage at diagnosis, histology, PD-L1 status, treatment type, date of treatment initiation, date of recurrence (if applicable), date of death (if applicable), date of last contact, and status at last contact. PD-L1 expression was assessed using the FDA-approved PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx assay for cervical cancer and reported as the Combined Positive Score (CPS). PD-L1 positivity was defined as CPS ≥1. Clinical outcomes were categorized as no evidence of disease (NED), alive with disease (AWD), death of disease (DOD) or death of undetermined cause (DUC). Post-treatment surveillance imaging was performed at the discretion of the treating oncologist rather than on a fixed schedule. Individual-level temporal data are presented as days from treatment initiation without absolute calendar dates, in accordance with HIPAA Safe Harbor provisions.

ctDNA sampling

Data were abstracted for plasma ctDNA measures obtained for each subject as part of routine clinical care. Tumor-informed ctDNA analysis was performed using a commercially available next-generation sequencing assay (Signatera; Natera, Inc.). Decisions regarding the timing of ctDNA assessment were made solely at the discretion of each treating oncologist. As a result, sampling occurred at variable peri-treatment intervals, including during induction chemotherapy and concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), immediately following completion of CCRT, and during post-treatment surveillance or at the time of suspected or confirmed recurrence. Quantitative values and corresponding sample collection dates were extracted for all evaluable ctDNA tests.

ctDNA definitions and kinetic categorization

Binary ctDNA status clinically reported as “Detected” or “Not Detected,” was abstracted for all available time points. Quantitative ctDNA values, reported as mean tumor molecules per milliliter (MTM/mL), were also extracted and analyzed as continuous variables. For longitudinal kinetic classification, “Detected” results were considered ctDNA-positive and “Not Detected” results were considered ctDNA-negative. Sustained clearance was defined as conversion from ctDNA-positive status to at least two consecutive ctDNA negative results with no subsequent ctDNA positive result through the date of last follow up. A single ctDNA negative result after prior positivity was not considered sufficient to define sustained clearance.

Patients were categorized into the longitudinal ctDNA kinetic groups using a hierarchical algorithm: (1) Persistent positive or re-positive: defined as any ctDNA positive trajectory that did not meet criteria for sustained clearance, including persistent ctDNA detection, re-emergent ctDNA positivity after prior negativity, or intermittent negative results without durable clearance. (2) Sustained clearance: defined as an initial ctDNA positive result followed by at least two consecutive ctDNA negative results, with no subsequent ctDNA positive result through last follow up; and (3) Negative: defined as ctDNA negative status at all assessed time points. Lead-time was defined as the interval (days) between the first persistent positive (or first re-positive) ctDNA result obtained after CCRT completion and the date of clinical or radiographic recurrence. In our clinical setting, post-CCRT ctDNA sampling was generally performed at approximately monthly intervals; intervals between molecular and imaging-based detection of recurrence within ±30 days were therefore considered concordant within sampling resolution.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation with range, with median and interquartile range (IQR) reported for non-normally distributed variables; categorical variables as frequency (%). Given small sample sizes and skewed distributions, comparisons of continuous variables across the three ctDNA trajectory cohorts were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Associations between categorical variables (e.g., trajectory cohort and clinical recurrence) were assessed using Fisher's exact test. All inferential statistics and graphics were performed in R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); descriptive tabulations were prepared in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA).

Given the exploratory nature of this study and the small sample size, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; reported p-values are descriptive. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational cohort studies (checklist provided as Supplement).

Results

Subject demographics

Beginning August 1, 2024, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) testing was implemented as part of standard clinical care in our department for all patients with a gynecologic malignancy regardless of treatment phase, including active treatment, maintenance therapy, or surveillance. During this period, a total of 88 patients who underwent treatment for cervical cancer were identified (Fig. 1). After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 17 patients were available for final analysis. Demographics for these subjects are summarized in Table 1. Of 17 evaluable subjects, 14 (82.4%) patients were diagnosed with invasive squamous cell carcinoma while 3 (17.6%) were diagnosed with adenocarcinoma. ctDNA was detected at one or more time points in 12 of 14 (85.7%) patients with invasive squamous cell carcinoma and in all 3 (100%) with adenocarcinoma. PD-L1 expression was identified as positive (CPS ≥1) for 15 subjects (88.2%). Treatment regimens varied across the cohort. Three (17.6%) patients received concurrent chemoradiation (CCRT) alone. Ten (58.8%) patients underwent CCRT concomitantly with immune checkpoint inhibitor followed by maintenance immune checkpoint inhibitor. One (5.9%) patient had induction chemotherapy prior to CCRT, and three (17.6%) patients had immune checkpoint inhibitor along with induction, CCRT, and maintenance immune checkpoint inhibitor.

Longitudinal ctDNA dynamics and clinical outcomes

Mean duration of follow-up for all subjects was 273 ± 148 days (range: 98–613 days). Baseline ctDNA was detectable in 15 of 17 patients (88%), who underwent 6.1 ± 2.4 longitudinal ctDNA assessments (range: 3–13; Supplement Table 1). Mean ctDNA value measured at baseline for subjects (prior to or within 30 days of treatment initiation) was 9.73 ± 11.44 MTM/mL (range: 0.00–33.06). Of note, the Persistent/Re-positive group exhibited a higher mean baseline value (15.03 ± 11.47 MTM/mL; range, 0.64–32.61) than subjects categorized as “sustained clearance” cohort (7.53 ± 11.21 MTM/mL; range, 0.07–33.06), although values were not significantly different between the two cohorts (Mann-Whitney U test, P = 0.152).

Trends for quantitative ctDNA values relative to patient clinical characteristics, including FIGO stage, treatment phase and outcomes are summarized via a swimmer plot (Fig. 2A). ctDNA responses could be classified into three patterns: Sustained clearance (n = 8, 47.1%), Persistent/Re-positive (n = 7, 41.2%), and Negative (n = 2, 11.8%). Clinical recurrence occurred exclusively in the Persistent/Re-positive group. The recurrence rate was significantly higher in this group compared with the Sustained clearance group (71.4% vs. 0%, Fisher's exact test, P = 0.007), representing a strong but preliminary association given the small sample size. ctDNA trajectories within the Persistent/Re-positive group frequently demonstrated non-monotonic behavior. Three of seven patients exhibited isolated negative ctDNA results embedded within otherwise-positive trajectories, and one patient demonstrated rapid short-interval fluctuation between undetectable and strongly positive ctDNA values. These findings indicated that single negative ctDNA measurements obtained during active treatment did not reliably represent durable molecular clearance.

Among the five recurrent cases, ctDNA preceded radiographic or clinical recurrence detection in three patients by 70, 124 and 240 days, respectively (median 124 days). Molecular and imaging-based detection were concordant within sampling resolution in the remaining two cases (8 and 15 days, respectively). Three patients (IDs 3, 6, and 9) were deemed candidates for total pelvic exenteration (TPE); these patients either underwent, were scheduled for, or declined the procedure. Two of three patients had persistent ctDNA detection (Table S2). The remaining two subjects with recurrent disease (IDs 5 and 10) were not eligible for TPE and underwent salvage chemotherapy. A single death was recorded during the observation period; the specific cause of death remained undetermined.

Intra-CCRT ctDNA kinetics

Among the 15 patients with detectable baseline ctDNA, eight (53.3%) patients achieved sustained molecular clearance. Six of these eight (75%) patients became ctDNA negative during active treatment (Fig. 2A). To specifically evaluate intra-CCRT kinetics, we analyzed patients without induction chemotherapy who had detectable baseline ctDNA and at least two ctDNA assessments across the baseline and intra-CCRT window (n = 8; this subset is not identical to the Sustained clearance group). Molecular clearance during CCRT occurred in 5 of 8 patients (62.5%) (Fig. 2B). Notably, clearance occurred rapidly and within a relatively narrow window. Initial sustained ctDNA negativity during active CCRT was first observed between days 27 and 43 after treatment initiation, although additional patients achieved clearance after completion of CCRT (Fig. 2C). Patients who achieved intra-CCRT clearance experienced favorable outcomes, with no subsequent recurrences observed during follow-up. In contrast, recurrence occurred in one of three patients who failed to achieve clearance during active CCRT.

In patients who received induction chemotherapy prior to CCRT (n = 4), only one patient achieved molecular clearance during frontline therapy, and clearance occurred during the radiation phase rather than during induction chemotherapy itself. The remaining three patients remained persistently ctDNA-positive; one subsequently died and two remained alive with disease.

Discussion

Despite enormous promise, the clinical utility of ctDNA measurements in cervical and other gynecologic cancers remains poorly characterized. As a starting point to address this deficiency, we retrospectively reviewed the outcomes of serial ctDNA measurements performed for a real world cohort of 17 LACC patients using a commercially available, tumor-informed assay as they underwent definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT). Heterogeneous treatment regimens were observed including induction chemotherapy with or without immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Consistent with observations reported in the recent CALLA trial, baseline pre-treatment ctDNA was detectable in 15 of 17 patients (88%).

Several key findings were observed from this study. First, the timing of molecular clearance varied substantially across patients in our cohort. Among 8 evaluable intra-CCRT patients, 5 (62.5%) achieved sustained ctDNA negativity during active CCRT, with the first undetectable result observed between days 27 and 43 after treatment initiation; the remaining patients either cleared only after completion of CCRT or did not clear during the observation period. This patient-to-patient heterogeneity suggests that the timing of intra-treatment ctDNA clearance may itself carry biological information potentially reflecting differences in baseline disease burden, tumor radiosensitivity, or treatment responsiveness rather than representing a uniform response shared across patients. Traditionally, treatment response after definitive CCRT is assessed approximately three months following treatment completion using imaging-based evaluation (Autorino et al., 2025; Mbarki et al., 2022). The molecular changes observed during active treatment raise the possibility that ctDNA kinetics may provide earlier and patient-specific insight into treatment response dynamics, potentially informing future tailored treatment and surveilance strategies including selective de-escalation, such as reserving immunotherapy for a later line in patients with rapid molecular clearance although this hypothesis requires prospective validation.

A second important observation was the frequent presence of non-monotonic ctDNA trajectories during active treatment. Several patients demonstrated isolated negative ctDNA measurements within otherwise persistent positive courses, including one patient with rapid short-interval fluctuation between undetectable and strongly positive ctDNA values. These findings suggest that ctDNA dynamics during radiation are biologically and analytically complex and should not be interpreted using isolated measurements alone. Radiation-induced tumor cell death, variable tumor shedding, assay variability near the limit of detection, and treatment timing may all contribute to transient fluctuations in ctDNA detectability. Regardless of mechanism, our data indicate that a single negative ctDNA result obtained during active CCRT should not be interpreted as definitive molecular clearance.

Third, our findings additionally support the potential clinical relevance of longitudinal ctDNA monitoring for recurrence detection. All recurrences in this cohort occurred within the Persistent/Re-positive kinetic group. In three recurrent patients, ctDNA preceded imaging-based recurrence detection by 70–240 days. Furthermore, three of five recurrent patients were considered candidates for TPE, emphasizing the potential importance of earlier molecular identification of recurrence within the curative-intent salvage window. While no clinical decisions in this cohort were made based solely on ctDNA findings, these observations support further investigation of trajectory-based ctDNA monitoring strategies.

Interestingly, only one of four patients treated with induction chemotherapy prior to CCRT achieved molecular clearance during frontline therapy, and clearance occurred during the radiation phase rather than during induction chemotherapy itself. Because induction chemotherapy was selectively administered to clinically higher-risk patients in this cohort, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect underlying disease biology rather than treatment sequencing alone. Given the small sample size, this subgroup analysis should be regarded as exploratory and warrants further evaluation.

Several limitations warrant consideration. This was a small retrospective single-institution study with heterogeneous treatment regimens and opportunistic, non-protocolized ctDNA sampling intervals. Because ctDNA draws were performed at clinically indicated time points rather than on a protocol-defined schedule, the observed bounds of the intra-CCRT clearance window (days 27–43) and of the ctDNA-to-imaging lead-time (range 70–240 days) partly reflect sampling timing and should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as precise biological estimates. Post-treatment surveillance imaging was likewise performed at the discretion of the treating oncologist, and ctDNA-to-imaging lead-time estimates should accordingly be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Use of a single tumor-informed commercial assay without technical replicates precluded formal assessment of assay-level variability near the limit of detection. The intra-CCRT analysis cohort (n = 8) was small and combined patients receiving CCRT alone with patients receiving CCRT plus concurrent immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, so the observed clearance patterns may reflect treatment composition in addition to underlying tumor biology. Given the relatively short follow-up (mean 273 days), the absence of recurrences in the Sustained clearance group may reflect immature follow-up rather than durable disease control, and recurrence rates across kinetic groups should be interpreted as preliminary. A single death occurred during the observation period in a patient with detectable post-CCRT ctDNA and clinical suspicion of recurrence; the patient was lost to follow-up before confirmatory imaging, and the death certificate did not specify a cause of death, reflecting a structural challenge of safety-net follow-up that limited formal recurrence classification. The lead-time advantage of ctDNA over surveillance imaging was also not uniform across patterns of recurrence; in a case of isolated localized pelvic recurrence (e.g., patient ID 6), molecular and imaging-based detection occurred within the same monthly sampling cycle, suggesting reduced ctDNA-based sensitivity for low-volume locoregional disease. Accordingly, our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Despite its limitation, this study provides an initial description of real-time ctDNA kinetics during active CCRT in LACC using a commercially available assay under real-world clinical conditions. Our findings suggest that ctDNA response during treatment may be both temporally clustered and biologically non-monotonic, supporting the need for prospective studies incorporating dense longitudinal sampling during active therapy. Direct comparison of trajectory-based ctDNA monitoring with single-point post-CCRT testing in prospectively designed studies will be needed to determine whether serial sampling provides meaningful incremental prognostic value.

Conclusions

Serial ctDNA measurements during definitive treatment of locally advanced cervical cancer demonstrated rapid but non-monotonic molecular response kinetics during active chemoradiotherapy. Molecular clearance frequently occurred within the first month of treatment, whereas isolated negative ctDNA results were observed within otherwise-persistent positive trajectories. These findings suggest that serial rather than isolated ctDNA measurements may be necessary to accurately interpret molecular response during active radiation treatment and may be potentially useful for subsequently triaging patients to maintenance immunotherapy. Prospective protocol-defined studies are warranted to determine whether trajectory-based ctDNA monitoring can improve risk stratification, optimize surveillance timing, and expand opportunities for curative-intent salvage therapy.
