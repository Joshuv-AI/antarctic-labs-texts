Local Search Improvements for Soft Happy Colouring Journal: Computers & Operations Research
Mohammad H. Shekarriz, Dhananjay Thiruvady, Asef Nazari, Wilfried Imrich

Abstract
For 0 ≤ ρ ≤ 1 0\leq\rho\leq 1 and a coloured graph G G , a vertex v v is ρ \rho -happy if at least ρ ​ deg ⁡ ( v ) \rho\deg(v) of its neighbours have the same colour as v v . Soft happy colouring of a partially coloured graph G G is the problem of finding a vertex colouring σ \sigma that preserves the precolouring and has the maximum number of ρ \rho -happy vertices. It is already known that this problem is NP-hard and directly relates to the community structure of the graphs; under certain condition on the proportion of happiness ρ \rho and for graphs with community structures, the induced colouring by communities can make all the vertices ρ \rho -happy. We show that when 0 ≤ ρ 1 < ρ 2 ≤ 1 0\leq\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}\leq 1 , a complete ρ 2 \rho_{2} -happy colouring has a higher accuracy of community detection than a complete ρ 1 \rho_{1} -happy colouring. Moreover, when ρ \rho is greater than a threshold, it is unlikely for an algorithm to find a complete ρ \rho -happy colouring with colour classes of almost equal sizes. Three local search algorithms for soft happy colouring are proposed, and their performances are compared with one another and other known algorithms. Among them, the linear-time local search is shown to be not only very fast, but also a reliable algorithm that can dramatically improve the number of ρ \rho -happy vertices.

Vertex colouring partitions a graph’s vertex set into disjoint “colour” sets, and solutions to many complex problems are presented in this form.
One type that has recently attracted considerable interest is

-happy

\rho\deg(v)
of their neighbours have the same colour as them
[
17
,
14
]
.

Finding a happy colouring, in a connected graph with some vertices precoloured, is a difficult problem and is mostly concerned with
maximising the number of happy vertices in a partially coloured graph
. Several approaches have been developed for finding happy colourings. For example, in
[
10
]
integer programming based approaches

[

in social networks
[
13
]
, a concept that the

of graphs can fully express. A

2
Theoretical Background

Throughout the paper, we use notation and definitions from graph theory.

By a graph
G
, we mean a finite simple graph. It consists of a vertex set
V(G)
and of a set of edges
E(G)
, whose elements are unordered pairs of distint vertices.
We write
G=(V(G),E(G))
. The numbers of vertices and edges are usually denoted by
n
and
m
. The set of neighbours of a vertex
v
is denoted by
N(v)
. We write
u\sim v
when two vertices
u
and
v
are adjacent, and
u\not\sim v
when they are not.

This study is concerned with random graphs
[
1
]
, more specifically, graphs in the Stochastic Block Model (SBM)
[
7
,
8
]
. The model we use is the simplified version of the SBM, denoted by
\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
, which is the probability space consisting of all graphs on
n
vertices, with an assignment to
k
vertex-disjoint communities. The probability of having an edge between two vertices of the same community is
p
, while two vertices of different communities are adjacent with the probability of
q
. We always suppose that
q<p
to have a meaningful community structure. Moreover, to ease understanding of theoretical results and validate practical ones, we assume that each community is almost equal in size to
\frac{n}{k}
. For more information about the SBM, see
[
9
]
.

For
k\geq 2
, the problem of soft happy colouring is to extend a partial
k
-colouring to a complete vertex
k
-colouring so that the number of
\rho
-happy vertices is maximum among all such extensions of the partial colouring. The uncoloured vertices in the original partial colouring are called
free vertices
. The number of
\rho
-happy vertices of a colouring
\sigma
of the graph
G
is denoted by
H_{\rho}(G,\sigma)
or simply by
H_{\rho}(\sigma)
. The
ratio of
\rho
-happy vertices
in a colouring
\sigma
is
\alpha(\sigma)=\frac{H_{\rho}(\sigma)}{n}
. If
\sigma
is a complete
\rho
-happy colouring, i.e.
\alpha(\sigma)=1
, we write
\sigma\in H_{\rho}
, and if
G
’s communities induce a
\rho
-happy colouring, we write
G\in H_{\rho}
.

Note that in our soft happy colouring problem instances, we always have some precoloured vertices; at least one vertex from each community is assigned the colour of its community number. Therefore, the number of vertices that are assigned colours in accordance with their original community is nonzero.
Accuracy of community detection
of a colouring
\sigma
is the ratio of vertices whose colours agree with their original communities, and it is denoted by
ACD(\sigma)
.

Shekarriz et al in
[
14
]
investigated soft happy colouring for graphs in the SBM. Assuming that
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
,
n
is large enough,
k\geq 2
,
0<q<p<1
,
0<\rho\leq 1
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
, they proved that if

q(k-1)(e^{\rho}-1)+p(e^{\rho}-e)<\frac{k}{n}\mathrm{ln}(\varepsilon),

(2.1)

then the communities of
G
induce a
\rho
-happy colouring on
G
with probability at least
(1-\varepsilon)^{n}
. They defined a threshold

\xi=\max\left\{\min\left\{\ln\left(\frac{\frac{k}{n}\ln(\varepsilon)+pe+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right),\;\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}\right\},\;0\right\},

(2.2)

such that for
\rho\leq\xi
, with high probability, the communities of
G
induce a complete
\rho
-happy colouring.

It was also asserted in
[
14
]
that for
0\leq\rho<\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi
and
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
, when
n\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\operatorname{Pr}(G\in H_{\rho})\rightarrow 1
. Here, we point it out that
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi=\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}
. The reason behind this fact is that we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ln\left(\frac{\frac{k}{n}\ln(\varepsilon)+pe+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right)=\ln\left(\frac{pe+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right).

Let
\tilde{\xi}=\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}
. Then
0<\tilde{\xi}<1
, and since

\frac{pe+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}=1+\frac{p(e-1)}{p+(k-1)q}=1+(e-1)\tilde{\xi},

(2.3)

we have

\ln\left(\frac{pe+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right)=\ln(1+(e-1)\tilde{\xi}).

Now, we have

\ln\left(\frac{pe+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right)\geq\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}

(2.4)

because the function
f(x)=\ln(1+(e-1)x)-x
is positive for
x\in(0,1)
. Thus, we can restate
[
14
, Theorem 3.3]
as follows:

Theorem
2.1
.

[
14
]

Let
0<q<p<1
,
k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
be constants,
0\leq\rho<\tilde{\xi}=\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}
, and
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
. Then,
\operatorname{Pr}(G\in H_{\rho})\to 1
when
n\to\infty
.

As a consequence of this revised statement of the theorem, when
\frac{k}{n}
is very small,
that is, when
the number of vertices is much larger than the number of communities, we can take
\tilde{\xi}
instead of
\xi
and
can
expect that the graph’s communities induce a
\rho
-happy colouring when
\rho\leq\tilde{\xi}
.

3
Community detection via soft happy colouring

For a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
with the communities of
G
are
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
, suppose that
\sigma
is a complete
k
-colouring with colour classes
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
. Recall that the proportion of vertices, whose colours in
\sigma
agree with their original community colouring, is denoted
by
ACD(\sigma)
. Therefore, we have

ACD(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V(G)}\delta_{\sigma}(v),

(3.1)

where

\delta_{\sigma}(v)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&\;\;\text{ if }v\in C_{\sigma(v)}\\
0&\;\;\text{ otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

Suppose that
v\in C_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
and
\mathrm{DEG}
is the random variable giving the vertex degrees. Thus

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{DEG}(v)]=\left(\frac{n}{k}-1\right)p+\frac{k-1}{k}nq\approx\frac{n}{k}\left(p+(k-1)q\right).

(3.2)

Suppose also that
D_{in}^{\sigma}
is the random variable that
denotes
the number of neighbours
of
v
,
whose colours agree with
\sigma(v)
. In other words,

D_{in}^{\sigma}(v)=\sum_{u\in A_{\sigma(v)}\setminus v}\delta(u,v),

where

\delta(u,v)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&\;\;\text{ if }u\sim v\\
0&\;\;\text{ otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

(3.3)

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[D_{in}^{\sigma}(v)]=\sum_{u\in A_{\sigma(v)}\setminus v}\mathrm{Pr}(u\sim v)\approx\frac{n}{k}\left(p\pi_{v}^{\sigma}+q(1-\pi_{v}^{\sigma})\right),

(3.4)

where
\pi_{v}^{\sigma}
is the expected proportion of agreement of the colour class
\sigma(v)
and the community containing
v
. In other words,

\pi_{v}^{\sigma}=\frac{\left|A_{\sigma(v)}\cap C_{i}\right|}{\left|C_{i}\right|}=\frac{k}{n}\left|A_{\sigma(v)}\cap C_{i}\right|.

(3.5)

Therefore,

ACD(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V(G)}\pi_{v}^{\sigma}

(3.6)

Under the condition of almost equal community sizes, it is likely to compare the accuracy of community detection of two
\rho
-happy colourings that preserve a precolouring.

Theorem
3.1
.

Let
G
be a partially coloured graph in
\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
with at least one vertex from each community
i
precoloured by the colour
i
for
1\leq i\leq k
,
n
be sufficiently large,
k>2
be an integer significantly small compared to
n
, and
0\leq\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}\leq 1
. Moreover, suppose that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are extensions of the precolouring to complete
k
-colourings with each of their colour classes of almost the same size as
\frac{n}{k}
, and
\sigma_{1}\in H_{\rho_{1}}
,
\sigma_{2}\in H_{\rho_{2}}
but
\sigma_{1}\notin H_{\rho_{2}}
. Then, with high probability we have

ACD(\sigma_{1})\leq ACD(\sigma_{2}).

Proof.

If
\sigma
is a
\rho
-happy colouring, for a vertex
v
we have

D_{in}^{\sigma}(v)\geq\rho\deg(v).

Therefore, we must have

\mathbb{E}[D_{in}^{\sigma}(v)]\geq\rho\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{DEG}(v)].

Now, by Equations
3.4
and
3.2
we have

\begin{split}&\frac{n}{k}\left(p\pi_{v}^{\sigma}+q(1-\pi_{v}^{\sigma})\right)\geq\rho\frac{n}{k}\left(p+(k-1)q\right)\\
\implies&p\pi_{v}^{\sigma}+q(1-\pi_{v}^{\sigma})\geq\rho\left(p+(k-1)q\right)\\
\implies&(p-q)\pi_{v}^{\sigma}\geq\rho\left(p+(k-1)q\right)-q\\
\implies&\pi_{v}^{\sigma}\geq\frac{\rho\left(p+(k-1)q\right)-q}{p-q}.\end{split}

Hence, greater
\rho
results in greater
\pi_{v}^{\sigma}
. Now, since
\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
, it is inferred with high probability that
\pi_{v}^{\sigma_{1}}\leq\pi_{v}^{\sigma_{2}}
, and therefore by Equation
3.6
, with high probability we have

ACD(\sigma_{1})\leq ACD(\sigma_{2}).

∎

We know from Theorem
2.1
that for
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
that there is high probability that we can find a complete
\rho
-happy colouring for
G
when
\rho\leq\tilde{\xi}
. Based on Theorem
3.1
, we expect to find higher accuracy of communities when
\rho
is larger. An interesting question is how to find the best
\rho
where a complete
\rho
-happy colouring has the highest degree of alignment
with the original communities of
G
.

In practice, a lower bound for
\rho
is desirable so that a complete
\rho
-happy colouring acceptably matches the original community structure. One obvious lower bound is

\rho\geq\mu=\frac{q}{p+(k-1)q}

(3.7)

because when
\rho<\mu
, a vertex
v
can still be
\rho
-happy if assigned to a colour other than its original community.

Another extreme case for the proportion of happiness
\rho
is when
\tilde{\xi}<\rho\leq 1
. Although based on Theorem
3.1
, we expect to have higher accuracy of community detection in this case, but there is a theoretical barrier that prevents this from happening. The following theorem states that for
\rho>\tilde{\xi}
, almost never an algorithm can find a complete
\rho
-happy colouring for a graph in the SBM with a large number of vertices.

Theorem
3.2
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
be a graph where
k\geq 2
is fixed and considered small relative to
n
, and suppose that
0<q\ll p<1
. Assume further that the parameter
\rho
satisfies
1\geq\rho>\tilde{\xi}=\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}
. Let
\sigma
be a
k
-colouring of
G
with colour classes
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
such that
|A_{i}|\approx\frac{n}{k}
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
. Then, as
n\to\infty
, the probability that
\sigma
contains any
\rho
-happy vertex converges to
0
. Moreover, for
n
sufficiently large,
\sigma
is almost never a complete
\rho
-happy colouring.

Proof.

Let the SBM communities of
G
be
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
, and
v\in C_{j}
be a vertex of
G
for
1\leq j\leq k
. Since
0\leq\left|A_{\sigma(v)}\cap C_{i}\right|\leq\frac{n}{k}
, we can deduce from Equation
3.5

that
0<\pi_{v}^{\sigma}\leq 1
. Moreover,
because

q\ll p
, we have

\mathbb{E}[D_{in}^{\sigma}(v)]\leq\frac{np}{k}.

(3.8)

Equality
holds if
\sigma
perfectly aligns with the community structure of
G
, i.e. if

\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\}.

Figure
2
demonstrates this observation for a graph on 120 vertices and 6 communities: for every vertex in the green community, the number of its neighbours inside the green community is less than the number of its neighbours in the original community.

Figure 2
:
A graph on 120 vertices with 6 communities, each community consists of 20 vertices arranged in rectangular forms close to each other. A colour class, green rectangle, which is not aligned with the original community structure, is also shown. Evidently, the number of neighbours of a vertex in this colour class is less than the number of its neighbours in its original community.

Therefore, we observed that

\operatorname{Pr}(D_{in}^{c}(v)>\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v))\geq\operatorname{Pr}(D_{in}^{\sigma}(v)>\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v)),

where
c
is the colouring induced by communities.

To proceed, we need some notations from
[
14
, proof of Theorem 3.1]
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, let
X_{i}
be the random variable which gives the number of adjacent vertices to
v
in
C_{i}
. In other words,

X_{i}=\sum_{u\in C_{i}}\delta(u,v),

(3.9)

where
\delta(u,v)
is introduced in Equation
3.3
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\begin{cases}p\frac{n}{k}&\text{ if }i=j\\
q\frac{n}{k}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For an arbitrary
t>0
, the moment generating function of
X_{i}
is

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX_{i}}\right]=\begin{cases}\left(1-p+pe^{t}\right)^{\frac{n}{k}}&\text{ if }i=j\\
\left(1-q+qe^{t}\right)^{\frac{n}{k}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Now, we have
\mathrm{DEG}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
and
D_{in}(v)=D_{in}^{c}(v)=X_{j}
.

To prove the assertion, we need to show that
\operatorname{Pr}(D_{in}(v)>\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v))
approaches zero when
n\rightarrow\infty
. Suppose that
\rho=\tilde{\xi}+\varepsilon
for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1-\tilde{\xi}
. Then, by the Chernoff bound

12
]
, for
t>0
we
infer

\operatorname{Pr}(D_{in}(v)-\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v)>0)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t(D_{in}(v)-\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v))}\right].

(3.10)

Following an approach that is
similar to
that in

[

,
this result enables us to
find a bound on

. Using the moment generating function, we have

\operatorname{Pr}(D_{in}(v)-\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v)>0)\leq\left(1-p+pe^{t}\right)^{\frac{n}{k}}\left(1-p+pe^{t\rho}\right)^{-\frac{n}{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(1-q+qe^{t\rho}\right)^{-\frac{n}{k}},

Therefore,

\begin{split}\operatorname{Pr}(D_{in}(v)-\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v)>0)&\leq\left(1-p+pe^{t}\right)^{\frac{n}{k}}\left(1-p+pe^{t\rho}\right)^{-\frac{n}{k}}\left(1-q+qe^{t\rho}\right)^{-\frac{k-1}{k}n}\\

By

, we have

p\left(e^{t\rho}-e^{t}\right)+(k-1)q\left(e^{t\rho}-1\right)>0,

(3.12)

In other words, Inequality

\rho>\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{pe^{t}+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right),

which means that Inequality

\varepsilon>\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{pe^{t}+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right)-\tilde{\xi}.

Because

\tilde{\xi}=\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}
, similar to Equation

\frac{pe^{t}+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}=1+(e^{t}-1)\tilde{\xi}

Suppose that

f(x,t)=\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(1+(e^{t}-1)x\right)-x.

We show for each

, proving Inequality

. We expand

1+(e^{t}-1)x=1+xt+\frac{x\,t^{2}}{2}+R_{1}(t^{3},x),\;\text{ and }\;\ln(1+y)=y-\frac{y^{2}}{2}+R_{2}(y^{3}),

and
y=xt+\frac{x\,t^{2}}{2}+R_{1}(t^{3},x)
, we
conclude

f(x,t)=\frac{1}{t}\ln\Bigl(1+(e^{t}-1)x\Bigr)-x=\frac{(x-x^{2})t}{2}+R(t^{2},x),

where
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
, and
R
are Taylor
residuals
that tend to 0 when
t\to 0^{+}
. Thus, for every
x\in[0,1]
,

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}f(x,t)=0,

which proves (
3.13
), and concludes that the probability of
v
being
\rho
-happy by
c
(and therefore by
\sigma
) approaches 0 when
n\to\infty
.

The independence of the events of
an
edge between
a

pair of vertices
and between any other pair of vertices, leads to
the inequality

\operatorname{Pr}(c\in H_{\rho})=\left(\operatorname{Pr}(D_{in}(v)-\rho\mathrm{DEG}(v)>0)\right)^{n}\leq e^{-\frac{n^{2}}{k}\left(p\left(e^{t\rho}-e^{t}\right)+(k-1)q\left(e^{t\rho}-1\right)\right)}.

Therefore,
\operatorname{Pr}(c\in H_{\rho})
should be very small (and
\operatorname{Pr}(\sigma\in H_{\rho})
should be even smaller) if
n
is sufficiently large because of Inequality
3.12
.
This completes the proof.

4
Known algorithms for soft happy colouring

Four heuristic algorithms have already been proposed for soft happy colouring of a graph with some precoloured vertices. In 2015, Zhang and Li
[
17
]
proposed
Greedy-SoftMHV
and
Growth-SoftMHV
. In this paper, because there is no confusion, we denote these two algorithms simply by
Greedy
and
Growth
. The other two heuristic algorithms are
LMC
and
NGC
, which were introduced by Shekarriz et al.
[
14
]
.

In
Greedy
, a partially coloured graph
G
is taken as input, and tries colouring all the uncoloured vertices with one colour to see which colour generates the most
\rho
-happy vertices, which is reported as the algorithm’s output. The time complexity of
Greedy
is
\mathcal{O}(km)
where
m
is the number of edges and
k
is the number of permissible colours, see Carpentier et al.
[
2
]
.

In
Growth
, vertices are partitioned into
several
groups.
The
most notable ones are:

P
:

Already coloured vertices that can become
\rho
-happy, because there are enough uncoloured vertices adjacent to them.

L_{u}
:

Vertices that are not coloured, but will be
\rho
-unhappy, no matter
which
colour they receive.

L_{h}
:

Vertices that are not coloured yet, but can become
\rho
-happy.

The main loop of
Growth
continues as long as the set
L=P\cup L_{u}\cup L_{h}
is not empty. Within this loop, if there
are
P
-verices
, it selects one, appends enough vertices from its neighbours to its colour class to make it
\rho
-happy, and recalculates the sets
P
,
L_{u}
, and
L_{h}
. If there is no
P
-vertex, an
L_{h}
-vertex will be chosen and
appended
to a colour class that makes it
\rho
-happy,
and then
the sets
P
,
L_{u}
, and
L_{h}

are updated
. If there is no
P
or
L_{h}
vertex, an
L_{u}
-vertex is chosen and coloured randomly, then the sets
P
,
L_{u}
, and
L_{h}

are updated again
. The process repeats until all the sets
P
,
L_{u}
, and
L_{h}
become empty, and the algorithm returns the complete colouring of the graph. The time complexity of
Growth
is
\mathcal{O}(mn)
, see Carpentier et al.
[
2
]
.

Shekarriz et al.
[
14
]
introduced two more heuristic algorithms. One of them, namely
Neighbour Greedy Colouring (NGC)
, is an extension of
Greedy
. In its main loop, it tries available colours on uncoloured vertices to find which colour
i
generates the
maximum
number of
\rho
-happy vertices, but only colours neighbours of vertices that are already coloured by
i
. Tests show that
NGC
and
Greedy
almost always give
similar solutions
, although
NGC
usually
takes
much more time, as its time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(dkm)
, where
d=\mathrm{diam}(G)
.

Local Maximal Colouring (LMC)
is also introduced in
[
14
]
and shown to be very fast, as
its time complexity is

\mathcal{O}(m)
. Moreover, its output is highly correlated with the graph’s community structure. In the design of the
LMC
, there is no dependence on the proportion of happiness
\rho
, but some randomness is employed. In its main loop, the algorithm randomly chooses a vertex
v
from the intersection of uncoloured vertices and neighbours of already coloured vertices, and colours
v
with
a colour that appears
the most in
N(v)
.

In order to have an idea
how the improvement
algorithms perform, we can look at their run results when given randomly generated feasible colourings as their inputs. Thus, we also consider
Random
,
i.e.
an algorithm that randomly assigns colours from
\{1,\ldots,k\}
to the uncoloured vertices.

5
Improvement algorithms

An improvement algorithm refines solutions to optimisation problems, typically by iteratively enhancing the quality of the solution. The goal is to arrive at a solution closer to optimal that satisfies a validity criterion. These algorithms are often applied when initial solutions are generated using heuristic, approximation, or other feasible methods, such as
Random
.

Inputs and outputs of our improvement algorithms for soft happy colouring are as follows:

Input:
a connected graph
G
, a (probably partial)

-colouring
\sigma

\rho
, and the set of precoloured vertices

.

Output:

-colouring

We propose three local search improvement algorithms for soft happy colouring, namely

-colouring

so that the number of
\rho
-happy vertices in the latter is greater than or equal to the former.

7
Conclusion

This paper investigates soft happy colouring for graphs in the SBM, contributing to both theory and practice. One of the theoretical results shows that the accuracy of community detection of a complete
\rho
-happy colouring is expected to increase when
\rho
becomes larger. Another result is that an algorithm can almost never find a complete happy coloring
\rho
when the number of vertices is large and
\rho
is greater than the threshold

\tilde{\xi}=\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}.

These theoretical results are experimentally verified for a large set of randomly generated partially coloured graphs in the SBM. Hence, practically, algorithms designed to detect communities of a graph modelled by the SBM should focus on
\rho
being slightly less than
\tilde{\xi}
.

We introduced three new algorithms,
LS
,
RLS
, and
ELS
, that can alter a (probably partial) solution and generate a higher quality solution. We tested these and other existing algorithms on a large set of randomly generated partially coloured graphs in the SBM. The results show that
LS
has low time requirements and is effective as a heuristic or an improvement algorithm, especially when
\rho\leq\tilde{\xi}
or given a random input. Moreover,
RLS
demonstrates improved performance only when it is provided a solution of
Growth
’s as an input. The algorithm
ELS
showed poor performance due to its high time complexity.

In practice,
LS
performs comparatively well considering the other heuristics. It not only generates high number of
\rho
-happy vertices, but is also very fast. The test results, along with short run-time requirements of
LS
mean that it is a good candidate to be used in search-based and population-based metaheuristic algorithms. It can be expected that the ensuing results can be of similar or even better quality solutions, owing to exploring substantially larger areas of the search space.

Since finding a fast and effective heuristic is essential for designing metaheuristics and matheuristics, such heuristics can be used to generate a population of solutions for population-based metaheuristics or to iterate improvements in perturbation-based algorithms. Therefore, we propose using
LS
to design more advanced algorithms for soft happy colouring.
