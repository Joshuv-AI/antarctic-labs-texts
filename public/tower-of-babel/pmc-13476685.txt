Carboxymethyl Chitosan Promotes Osteogenesis Via p38/VEGFR2 and Shows Translational Potential in Sinus Floor Elevation
Authors: Xinda Li, Zhijia Liu, Xinzhu Liu, Jianing Wang, Jinlai Bao, Wuwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore the osteogenic promotion of carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS), clarify the signalling pathways involved and preliminarily evaluate the translational potential of CMCS in transcrestal sinus floor elevation.MethodsOverlapping targets were identified by integrating potential CMCS targets and bone regeneration-related genes, and protein-protein interaction and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted. The GEO dataset GSE50743 was externally validated. Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells from mice were induced to osteogenic differentiation in vitro and treated with CMCS alone or in combination with the p38 inhibitor SB203580 or the VEGFR2 inhibitor SU5416. The osteogenic phenotypes and mechanism-related changes were determined by ALP, Alizarin Red S staining, RUNX2 immunofluorescence, qPCR and Western blotting. A retrospective comparative clinical study further evaluated radiographic, functional and patient-reported outcomes after transcrestal sinus floor elevation.ResultsWe identified 187 common targets of CMCS-bone regeneration. Enrichment analyses revealed that these targets were primarily involved in cell adhesion, inflammatory and immune regulation, extracellular matrix remodelling, and osteogenesis-related pathways, including MAPK and VEGF signalling. Validation with GSE50743 confirmed significant positive enrichment of candidate gene set under osteogenic conditions. In vitro, CMCS increased ALP activity, mineralised nodule formation and the expression of RUNX2, ALP and OPN, whereas inhibition of p38 or VEGFR2 partially reduced these effects. Clinical findings showed that the two groups were generally similar in terms of safety, implant stability and patient-reported outcomes, whereas the CMCS group had better volume maintenance.ConclusionsCMCS promotes osteogenesis and mineralisation, at least partly via p38 MAPK and VEGF–VEGFR2 signalling. Retrospective clinical findings also indicate its potential utility in transcrestal sinus floor elevation.

Introduction

The interplay between osteogenic differentiation, extracellular matrix remodelling and vascular support mediates bone regeneration.1,2 Multiple conserved signalling networks regulate osteogenic differentiation.3 Among them, the MAPK-related pathways have been repeatedly shown to be associated with osteogenic output in various stimulation models of osteogenesis, and the osteogenic phenotype can also be modulated by pharmacological interventions.4 And, at the same time, bone regeneration and angiogenesis are closely related. VEGF-related signalling is not only responsible for angiogenesis, but has also been shown to have an effect on mesenchymal stem cells directly, modulating osteogenic transcription programs and mineralisation capacity.5,6

Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) is a water-soluble derivative of chitosan, which can be easily processed into films or gels and has advantages in manufacturing and cost.7,8 It has been reported that carboxymethylated chitosan materials can promote the expression of osteogenesis-related markers such as Runx2.9 This indicates that CMCS might possess some inherent biological activities other than being a passive carrier.10 But the evidence that exists is still inadequate. Some studies are limited to the phenotypic level and do not clearly identify and validate the key regulatory pathways, and it is often difficult to distinguish the independent contribution of CMCS in composite systems.11,12 Therefore, a chain of evidence from systematic screening to causal validation is required to explore whether CMCS alone can induce osteogenesis and what the key signalling pathways it depends on are.

The transalveolar approach for sinus floor elevation is the most widely employed because of its minimally invasive nature.13 The success of the procedure is primarily dependent on the ability of the implant material to maintain stability in the space created by the elevation and to create a microenvironment for healing.14,15 These properties are in accordance with the properties of carboxymethyl chitosan, which has antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and bone-regenerative properties.7 Therefore, this study screens candidate regulatory modules by bioinformatics and validates their mechanisms experimentally by specific pharmacological inhibition in osteogenic model of mBMSCs. In addition, a retrospective clinical study was performed to preliminarily evaluate the osteogenic efficacy and safety of CMCS in transcrestal sinus floor elevation, extending the evidence chain from biological effects and mechanisms to clinical application.

Methods

Overall research design

First, candidate signalling networks were screened by integrating compound-target predictions with gene sets associated with bone regeneration. Subsequently, protein–protein interaction (PPI) and functional enrichment analyses were performed. Next, pharmacological interventions using p38 and VEGFR2 inhibitors were performed in an osteogenic induction model of murine bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (mBMSCs) to validate the involvement of the candidate pathways in the osteogenic effects of CMCS. Finally, we performed a retrospective clinical study of transcrestal sinus floor elevation to evaluate the translational potential of CMCS.

Bioinformatics screening and validation

Identification of CMCS targets, construction of a bone regeneration gene set, and determination of overlapping targets

The structural information of CMCS was obtained from PubChem and potential targets were predicted by PharmMapper and SwissTargetPrediction. Since CMCS is a polymer rather than a traditional small molecule, a representative carboxymethylated chitosan structural unit available in PubChem was selected as the query structure for target prediction analysis.16 The results were combined, duplicates removed and annotated with standard gene symbols using UniProt. The keyword 'osteogenesis' was used to search for genes associated with bone regeneration from the GeneCards and OMIM databases and then duplicates were removed. These were then crossed with the potential CMCS targets to generate a set of candidate target genes.

PPI network construction and enrichment analysis

The common targets were imported into the STRING database to establish a protein-protein interaction network. The network was then visualised and analysed using Cytoscape 3.10.3. Enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG was performed by clusterProfiler. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. A significance threshold of FDR < 0.05 was used to determine significant differences.

Validation of GEO datasets

To confirm the external consistency of the intersection target gene set between the CMCS and bone regeneration, the GSE50743 dataset was downloaded from the GEO database. Next, the osteogenesis-induced group (OS_D14) was compared with the control group (Ctrl_D14) (n = 4/group). We performed differential analysis using Limma and created a global gene ranking list based on gene t-statistics. Then, the subset of overlapping target genes that could be mapped was subject to pre-ranked GSEA to assess the enrichment trends of this gene set under the condition of osteogenic induction. We also calculated enrichment scores at the sample level with ssGSEA-like method, and compared inter-group differences using Wilcoxon test at the same time.

In vitro experiments: osteogenic differentiation of mBMSCs and mechanistic interventions

Reagents and materials

CMCS was purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co, Ltd (China, catalogue number C304739). The material was a highly substituted water-soluble CMCS, selected to maintain consistency in the principal chemical composition with the CMCS-based membrane used in the clinical study. A sterile stock solution was prepared and filtered through a 0.22 µm filter, then aliquoted for storage. The working concentration was 250 μg/mL. The p38 inhibitor SB203580 and VEGFR2 inhibitor SU5416 were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA; catalogue numbers 559389 and S8442, respectively). Stock solutions were made in DMSO at a final concentration of 10 μmol/L, and solvent control was made at an equal volume. CCK-8 assay kit, ALP assay kit and Alizarin Red S were purchased from Bioyuntian Biotechnology Co, Ltd (China; catalogue no. C0038, P0322S and C0148S, respectively). All reagents were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions unless otherwise noted.

Culture and expansion of mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (mBMSCs)

Mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (mBMSCs) were obtained from Becton, Dickinson & Company (China; catalogue number C6228). Cells were maintained in low glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The following experiments were conducted on cells of passages 3 to 5.

Osteogenesis induction and experimental design

The osteogenic induction medium consisted of high-glucose DMEM, 10% FBS, 50 μM ascorbic acid, 10 mM sodium β-glycerophosphate, 100 nM dexamethasone and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The experiment consisted of a control group and three treatment groups: a CMCS group (250 μg/mL), a CMCS+SB203580 group and a CMCS+SU5416 group. Inhibitors were added 1 h prior to treatment with CMCS. The culture medium with the corresponding reagents was changed every 3 days. All groups were maintained under identical culture conditions, with the exception of inhibitor treatment.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Seed plate mBMSCs at 5 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plate. Treat with the following concentrations of CMCS after the cells have attached: 0, 50, 100, 250, 500 and 1000μg/mL. Each group should have six replicate wells and one blank well. 2. Add 90 μL fresh medium and 10 μL CCK-8 reagent in each well at 1st, 3rd and 7th day after treatment. Incubate 2 hours in the dark at 37 °C and read the absorbance at 450 nm.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and activity assay

On day 7 of osteogenesis induction, cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant. The total protein concentration was determined by the BCA assay and the ALP activity was measured according to the kit instructions. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm and the results are expressed in mU/mg protein.

Alizarin red S (ARS) staining and quantification

Osteogenesis was induced for 14 days and cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with alizarin red S (pH 4.2). Photographs were taken using a microscope to show the mineralised nodules. For quantitative analysis the deposits were dissolved in 10% CPC and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm.

Immunofluorescence staining

RUNX2 immunofluorescence staining was performed on day 5 of osteogenesis induction. Following fixation, permeabilisation and blocking, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit anti-mouse RUNX2 primary antibody (Abcam, UK, catalogue no. ab23981, dilution 1:200). The samples were then incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-labelled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, catalogue no. ab150077, dilution 1:500) and the cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired under standardised exposure conditions and the integrated optical density of RUNX2 was calculated using ImageJ. Each group consisted of three replicate wells, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RUNX2, ALP and OPN were selected as well-established markers of osteogenic differentiation and mineralisation to evaluate the downstream osteogenic effects of CMCS. Total RNA was isolated on day 7 of osteogenesis induction. Reverse transcription was performed to generate cDNA, and then real-time quantitative PCR was performed using the SYBR Green system. Each 20 μL reaction contained 10 μL of 2 × SYBR qPCR Mix, 0.8 μL of forward primer, 0.8 μL of reverse primer, 2.0 μL of cDNA template and nuclease-free water. The amplification programme consisted of 30 sec at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 5 sec at 95 °C and 30 sec at 60 °C. After amplification, melting curve analysis was performed. Runx2, Alp and Opn were the target genes, β-actin was the internal control. The 2^(−ΔΔCt) method was used to calculate the relative expression levels. Sequences of primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Cells from each group were collected at day 7 of osteogenic induction. Cells were washed twice with pre-chilled PBS and lysed on ice for 30 minutes in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. After measuring the protein concentration using the BCA assay, run an SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transfer to a PVDF membrane. Block the membrane with 5% skimmed milk powder or 5% BSA for 1 h and then incubate with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Incubate overnight with an HRP-labelled secondary antibody and visualise the following day by ECL. The detected proteins were p38 MAPK (Proteintech, China, catalogue no. 14064-1-AP), p-p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182) (Proteintech, China, catalogue no. 28796-1-AP), VEGFR2/KDR (Proteintech, China, catalogue no. 26415-1-AP), p-VEGFR2 (Tyr1175) (Cell Signalling Technology, USA, catalogue no. 2478), RUNX2 (Proteintech, China, catalogue no. 20700-1-AP), ALP/ALPL (Proteintech, China, catalogue no. 11187-1-AP) and OPN (Proteintech, China, catalogue no. 22952-1-AP) with β-actin as internal control. The intensity of the bands was quantified using ImageJ after normalising total protein levels to β-actin and phosphorylation levels to the p/total ratio.

Statistical analysis

In vitro experiments were performed at least 3 independent times. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. P of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical study: a retrospective comparative study of transcrestal maxillary sinus floor elevation

Study design and participants

This study was a retrospective controlled study of a single centre, in which patients who underwent maxillary sinus floor elevation through the alveolar ridge and simultaneous implantation at the Dalian Medical University Affiliated Stomatological Hospital were enrolled. We retrospectively analysed the cases from the treatment records. The two materials were randomly assigned to patients in routine clinical practice and all patients signed written informed consents before surgery. The clinical study protocol, including the use of the CMCS membrane and the retrospective analysis of anonymized clinical and radiographic data, was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Dalian Medical University (Approval No. 202604). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to treatment. The inclusion criteria were: age over 18 years, residual bone height of at least 4 mm at the implant site, bone width of at least 6 mm, absence of severe periodontal or periapical lesions in the adjacent teeth, and absence of severe systemic diseases. Exclusion criteria were: uncontrolled systemic diseases, history of radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the head and neck region, poorly controlled oral diseases, acute maxillary sinusitis or intra-sinus fluid levels, bisphosphonate use, pregnancy or lactation, smoking ≥10 cigarettes/day, and significant psychological or psychiatric disorders.

Surgical procedures

All patients underwent a preoperative oral examination and CBCT assessment, and the imaging data were stored in DICOM format. Transcrestal maxillary sinus floor elevation was performed under local anaesthesia by standard procedures and intraoperative check of the integrity of the sinus membrane was performed. Once membrane integrity was confirmed and the subantral space was created, the grafting material used in each case was identified from the operative record. The cases were then compared retrospectively in the CMCS group and the collagen sponge group. In the CMCS group, a piece of CMCS membrane (Qingdao Boyite Biomaterial Co, Ltd, China; registration no. 21163642393) was cut into six to eight pieces under sterile conditions and gradually implanted. The CMCS membrane used clinically was primarily composed of highly substituted CMCS. In the control group, one piece medical collagen sponge (Bonag Technology Co, Ltd, China; registration no. 20153142142) was used to maintain the space stability. Then the placement of the implants was performed according to the standard protocol (Figure S1). Intraoperative data including operation time, sinus membrane perforation, abnormal bleeding and primary implant stability were recorded.

Postoperative management

Post surgery a CBCT scan was immediately performed to verify the position of the implant and to evaluate the maxillary sinus. All patients were given standardised postoperative instructions and a 5-day course of oral antibiotics was routinely prescribed. Amoxicillin was the preferred antibiotic with clarithromycin prescribed as an alternative in patients with a penicillin allergy. Patients were also instructed to use 0.12% chlorhexidine mouthwash twice daily for 14 days. The final restoration was then completed after the second stage surgery was performed approximately 6 months after the initial surgery.

Outcome assessment

CBCT scans were taken at the pre-operative (Tpre), immediate post-operative (T0) and 6-month post-operative (T6) stages. Linear measurements were performed with RadiAnt DICOM Viewer and three-dimensional volumetric analysis was performed with Mimics 21 (Figure S2). Residual bone height, elevation height, intra-sinus implant length, new bone height and bone volume were measured. Each measurement was repeated 3 times and the mean value was calculated. The measurements were carried out independently by two blinded evaluators and the average of the results was used for analysis. Implant stability was evaluated using a resonance frequency analyser measuring the ISQ value right after implantation and six months postoperatively. Also, implant survival rate, post-operative complications, post-operative pain (VAS) and patient satisfaction were recorded.

Statistical analysis of clinical data

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were performed using independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test according to the distribution of data and chi-square test for categorical variables. Values of P < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Systems bioinformatics screening suggests that CMCS may participate in bone regeneration through p38 MAPK and VEGF-related signalling pathways

By intersecting the potential targets of CMCS from the PubChem, PharmMapper and SwissTargetPrediction databases with the genes associated with bone regeneration from the GeneCards and OMIM databases, a total of 187 overlapping candidate targets were acquired (Figure 1A). Importing these into STRING for PPI network construction, we found that the network was tightly connected, suggesting that the overlapping targets were concentrated in several interconnected functional modules instead of discrete distribution (Figure 1B). Further GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these candidate targets are mainly involved in biological processes closely related to bone repair, including cell adhesion, inflammatory and immune responses, extracellular matrix remodelling, and microenvironment regulation (Figures 1E-F). There was significant enrichment of signalling axes closely related with osteogenesis-angiogenesis coupling, such as MAPK and VEGF, at the pathway level. In order to compensate for potential bias in the candidate selection from simple enrichment analysis, we also used the GEO dataset GSE50743 for external validation of signalling trends under osteogenic conditions. The results showed that pre-ranked GSEA showed significant positive enrichment of the candidate gene set (NES = 1.588, FDR = 0.0027; Figure 1C) in the comparison of Day 14 and significant differences in the ssGSEA-like scores between the two groups (Figure 1D). CMCS exhibits good cellular compatibility with mBMSCs at working concentrations and does not affect early-stage cell viability

The results of CCK-8 assay showed that the total cell viability of mBMSCs was stable during the observation period at the screened and determined working concentration of 250 μg/mL. This suggests that this concentration range can be used for the following osteogenic differentiation and mechanism validation experiments without further toxicity corrections (Figure S3).

CMCS significantly enhances the early osteogenic phenotype and promotes terminal mineralization

Compared with the control group, the ALP staining intensity and the ALP activity levels were significantly increased in the standard osteogenic induction conditions with CMCS treatment on day 7 (Figure 2A, D), indicating that CMCS promotes the start and development of early osteogenic differentiation. Further assessment of terminal mineralisation on day 14 showed a greater deposition of calcification nodules in the CMCS group with both ARS staining and the quantitative results consistently showing increased mineralisation levels (Figure 2B, E). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence results showed that RUNX2 fluorescence signals were significantly increased after CMCS treatment (Figures 2C, F), which agrees with the ALP and ARS results. Pharmacological blockade indicates that p38 and VEGFR2 signalling are involved in and partially mediate the osteogenic effects of CMCS

To study the role of p38 and VEGFR2 signalling in CMCS-induced bone formation, the CMCS-treated systems were treated with p38 inhibitor SB203580 and VEGFR2 inhibitor SU5416. Treatment with either inhibitor attenuated the CMCS-induced augmentation of ALP staining and increase in ALP activity to varying degrees compared with the CMCS group. Mineralised nodule formation and ARS quantification on day 14 were also decreased accordingly (Figure 2A, B). Meanwhile, the immunofluorescence intensity of RUNX2 was significantly decreased compared with the CMCS group (Figures 2C), suggesting that both p38 and VEGFR2 signalling are involved in CMCS-induced osteogenic phenotype. At the molecular level, CMCS increased the expression of osteogenesis-related genes including Runx2, ALP and OPN (Figure 3C, D). However, this trend was partially reversed by the addition of SB203580 or SU5416 (Figure 3C, D). Western blot results also confirmed that CMCS treatment increased the expression of RUNX2, ALP and OPN proteins and the levels of p-p38/p38 and p-VEGFR2/VEGFR2 (Figure 3E-H). Then, after intervention with corresponding inhibitors, the expression of relevant osteogenic markers and the corresponding phosphorylation signals were decreased to varying degrees (see Figures 3A, B). Clinical outcomes of the retrospective comparative study in transcrestal maxillary sinus floor elevation

Study completion and safety

The present study included 20 participants (n=10 in the CMCS group, n=10 in the control group; 12 and 14 implants were placed in each group, respectively). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the baseline parameters of age, gender distribution, tooth site distribution, implant diameter, implant length and residual bone height (RBH) (all P > .05), indicating that the two groups were well-matched (Table 1). All subjects completed the 6 month follow-up and received final restorations with no loss to follow-up or withdrawal. All implants were functioning normally in mastication after restoration. No serious adverse events during the perioperative period were observed. Intraoperative perforation of the sinus mucosa was observed in 2 cases in the CMCS group and 1 case in the control group, all cases were successfully performed with implant placement with standard management. Neither group presented with postoperative surgical site infections, sinus-related symptoms, or sinusitis-like manifestations. Functional and patient-reported outcomes

As for functional outcomes, both groups had high levels of implant stability 6 months postoperatively before restoration (Table 3). The ISQ score was 78.40 ± 4.17 in the CMCS group and 77.90 ± 4.12 in the control group. There was no statistically significant difference between the groups (P = .790), indicating good osseointegration in both groups. For patient-reported outcomes, mean pain scores in both groups were in the mild range from day 1 to day 7 postoperatively. There was no statistically significant difference between groups (P = .890) with a pain VAS score of 1.86 ± 0.44 in the CMCS group and 1.83 ± 0.48 in the control group. Six months after surgery, the overall satisfaction was high in both groups after complete healing, with the satisfaction VAS score of 8.60 ± 0.97 in the CMCS group and 8.40 ± 0.84 in the control group, with no statistically significant difference (P = .628). Discussion

Our results demonstrate that CMCS enhances early osteogenic differentiation and subsequent mineralisation of mBMSCs. This effect was greatly reduced by inhibiting p38 or VEGFR2. Bioinformatics analysis shows that targets related to CMCS are predominantly found in cell adhesion, immune regulation, extracellular matrix remodelling and processes and pathways closely related to bone repair, such as the MAPK and VEGF pathways. Together, these results indicate that the function of CMCS is not only restricted to filling or passive support, but it may also be involved in regulating osteogenesis-related biological processes.

Studies at various levels have shown that CMCS is not just a passive carrier.17 Liu et al applied CMCS to the rabbit knee replacement model. The results showed that it can reduce local inflammation and improve the bone formation parameters around the prosthesis.18 Further work by Budiraharjo et al on CMCS three-dimensional scaffolds showed that CMCS scaffolds can support cell adhesion and proliferation and promote osteogenic differentiation in the absence of bioactive factors.19 Fan et al investigated the periodontal ligament stem cell system and found that the treatment with CMCS upregulated the expression of osteogenesis-related markers, such as ALP, Runx2, OPN, and OCN, and stimulated the formation of mineralised nodules, suggesting its osteogenic potential in the context of oral-derived stem cells.9 Sharifi et al reported that in a three-dimensional hydrogel environment, CMCS in the matrix promotes early initiation of the osteogenic program and progression toward the mature stage in mesenchymal stem cells.20 In addition to biological phenotypes, hydroxyapatite deposition has been induced by CMCS in studies, which provided direct evidence of the inherent physicochemical basis of the material for mineralization.12 Based on these results, this study further confirmed the critical roles of p38 and VEGFR2 through an inhibitor approach, which improved the mechanistic understanding of the osteogenic effects of CMCS.21 We also translated these findings to a retrospective clinical comparison, thus providing preliminary translational support for potential clinical applicability of CMCS.

In this study, p38 MAPK and VEGF–VEGFR2 are the major focus from a mechanistic perspective as the two pathways are both closely related to the crucial stages of bone regeneration.22, 23, 24 The p38 MAPK has been long known to be involved in the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) through participating in the initiation and progression of the osteogenic transcriptional programme.25 Although VEGF–VEGFR2 is more commonly associated with angiogenesis, an increasing body of evidence indicates that it also participates in the regulation of MSC osteogenic differentiation and mineralisation.26 The results of enrichment analysis in this study showed that CMCS effects were initially exerted at the level of cell adhesion, matrix and the local microenvironment.27 This results in the facilitation of p38 and VEGFR2-related signalling, leading to the enhancement of ALP activity, increased mineralisation deposition, and upregulation of osteogenesis-related molecular expression.28, 29, 30,4 In other words, CMCS might be useful not for replacing exogenous growth factors, but for making a better early microenvironment for bone formation.

It should be noted that both the in vitro experiments and clinical studies in this research centred on CMCS as the core material. We prioritised the use of highly substituted, water-soluble CMCS for in vitro studies to ensure consistency with the clinical application material in terms of primary chemical composition. Although soluble CMCS was employed in the in vitro experiments whilst CMCS membranes were used in the clinical studies, both rely on CMCS as the primary active ingredient; consequently, the in vitro results reflect the direct influence of CMCS itself on osteogenesis-related signalling pathways and cell phenotypes. Therefore, the signalling pathways identified in vitro are likely to represent the biological response to CMCS itself, whereas the membrane form may additionally provide physical support and space-maintaining functions in vivo. In clinical applications, CMCS membranes may also contribute to the bone regeneration process through physical properties such as local retention, spatial maintenance and degradation behaviour.31 Consequently, the mechanistic evidence obtained in this study primarily reflects the biological activity of CMCS, whilst the clinical results demonstrate the combined effects of CMCS’s biological activity and material properties.

Furthermore, the enrichment analysis in this study suggests that the CMCS-associated network is also linked to biological processes such as immune regulation and angiogenesis. Research by Zhang et al indicates that enhancing the biological functions of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) can promote osseointegration of implants, suggesting that the functional state of BMSCs is a crucial foundation for bone regeneration around implants.32 The study by Liu et al further demonstrates that implant surface topography can influence osteogenesis and angiogenesis by modulating macrophage polarization, suggesting that immune cell-mediated regulation of the local microenvironment may be involved in implant-associated bone repair.33 Similarly, Kazimierczak et al found that chitosan/agarose/nanoHA scaffolds can induce M2 macrophage polarization and promote osteogenic differentiation.34 On the other hand, research by Mayer et al has shown that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) themselves can express VEGF-A and participate in the regulation of osteogenic and endothelial differentiation through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms. In this study, elevated VEGFR2 phosphorylation levels were observed in the mBMSC culture system, which more likely reflects endogenous VEGF-related regulation by MSCs rather than relying solely on the participation of endothelial cells.5 Notably, even in the absence of immune cells and endothelial cells, CMCS enhanced ALP activity, mineralized nodule formation, and the expression of RUNX2, ALP, and OPN, supporting a direct osteogenic effect of CMCS on mBMSCs. Immune regulation and angiogenesis may further influence the bone regenerative effects of CMCS in vivo. However, these potential mechanisms do not diminish the direct pro-osteogenic activity observed in the present study.

This study also confirmed the in vitro results of single CMCS in a clinical situation of maxillary sinus floor elevation by alveolar crest. The short-term follow-up showed that the CMCS group was generally comparable to the control material with respect to safety, implant stability and patient acceptance indicating that it is clinically feasible. The efficient and low-cost features of CMCS, combined with the relatively controllable acquisition and preparation costs of CMCS itself, further underscore the value of CMCS in clinical application, while remaining effective. The results of this study are important in this perspective. However, this study still has some limitations. Firstly, the mechanism of action depends largely on pharmacological inhibition which requires further validation by genetic intervention approaches. Secondly, the in vitro experiments performed so far address mainly two points in time, early osteogenesis and terminal mineralisation; the intermediate continuous process is still poorly characterised. Third, the small sample size and limited follow-up of the clinical study, necessitate further research to address issues related to bone quality, long-term stability and wider applicability. Finally, the retrospective analytical design may have introduced selection bias and limited causal inference despite randomised allocation in the clinical implementation.

Conclusion

Through target prediction and external transcriptomic validation, this study suggests that the CMCS-associated network is consistently linked to the bone regeneration process. In vitro experiments have shown that CMCS increases the activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), mineralisation and the expression of molecules related to osteogenesis in mBMSCs. However, pharmacological inhibition of p38 or VEGFR2 reduces this effect, indicating that the osteogenic promotion involves the p38 MAPK and VEGF–VEGFR2 signalling pathways. A retrospective clinical comparison further provided preliminary translational support for the potential application of CMCS in transcrestal maxillary sinus floor elevation.
