Skyrmions based on optical anisotropy for topological encoding
Authors: Yunqi Zhang, An Aloysius Wang, Runchen Zhang, Zimo Zhao, Yifei Ma, Ruofu Liu, Zhi-Kai Pong, Yuxi Cai, Chao He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The observation of skyrmions across diverse physical domains suggests that they are universal features of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}^{2}$$\end{document}S2-valued fields, reflecting the ubiquity of topology in the study of the natural world. In this paper, we develop an abstract technique of parameter space dimensionality reduction that extends the skyrmion framework to fields taking values in manifolds of dimension greater than 2, thereby broadening the range of systems that can support skyrmions. To prove that this is more than just a mathematical abstraction, we apply our technique to light-matter interactions, directly encoding skyrmionic structures into the optical anisotropy of spatially varying structured matter by selecting a distinguished axis, an approach fundamentally different from the more commonly known skyrmions formed by director fields in liquid crystals. We experimentally realize such skyrmions using a liquid-crystal-based tunable elliptical retarder array as a proof-of-concept platform and demonstrate complex, reconfigurable skyrmionic states exhibiting topological robustness under artificially introduced stochastic perturbations. Exploiting this robustness, we demonstrate a promising application of skyrmions in topologically protected information storage and provide an easily verifiable rule, which we term the 60° rule, that serves as a practical engineering criterion for guaranteeing robustness against noise and measurement errors.

Introduction

Skyrmions are topologically nontrivial field configurations that were originally proposed in particle physics1,2 and have since been realized in a range of naturally \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}^{2}$$\end{document}S2-valued physical systems, most notably in magnetic spin textures3–9. Recently, there has been a growing interest in extending the framework of skyrmions to more unconventional systems that are vector-valued but not necessarily \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}^{2}$$\end{document}S2-valued. This includes recent work in water waves10, acoustic systems11,12, optical and photonic platforms13–20, and more21–25. To interpret such fields as skyrmions, it is often implicitly assumed that the physically relevant field is projected – typically through normalization – onto the 2-sphere, after which standard considerations can be applied. Given this interpretation, we propose an entirely new generalization to higher dimensional fields, whereby different physically relevant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}^{2}$$\end{document}S2-valued subcomponents of the field are used to define skyrmions26.

From a practical perspective, this technique of parameter space dimensionality reduction opens the door to engineering high-dimensional fields that carry topological information, with the potential for a single field to encode multiple topological charges through different projection maps. This perspective is particularly significant for robust information encoding, one of the key applications of skyrmions, and is supported by recent work showing that topological properties can emerge from appropriately constructed mappings of high-dimensional parameter spaces rather than being exclusively tied to real-space field textures27. The added flexibility and information density enabled by parameter space reduction may therefore prove invaluable for real-world applications.

More concretely, various candidates for skyrmion-based information encoding have already been proposed28–30, including magnetic spin textures, optical fields, and the director fields of liquid crystals (LCs). However, each of these strategies presents some limitations. Magnetic skyrmions, while compact, are generally temperature sensitive31–33. Optical skyrmions, although easily generated, are transient in nature and thus more suitable for information transfer than storage34. Lastly, LCs are limited to low orders due to energy considerations35 and the stored topological charge cannot be optically read out easily36. Moreover, the nonpolar nature of LCs37,38 means that it is impossible to distinguish between positive and negative skyrmion numbers, limiting information density.

In this paper, we use the flexibility of parameter space dimensionality reduction to propose a strategy for topological information encoding that overcomes the limitations of magnetic, optical and LC skyrmions mentioned above. This is achieved not by considering matter fields or light fields alone, but rather by focusing on the light-matter interactions of complex, spatially varying structured matter39. Structured matter is an umbrella term for various physical media that manipulate the vectorial properties of light and are often described by a spatially varying Jones or Mueller matrix, depending on whether the material is depolarizing. Since the Jones matrix field is described by a spatially varying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\times 2$$\end{document}2×2 complex matrix, while the Mueller matrix field is described by a spatially varying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4\times 4$$\end{document}4×4 real matrix, both are high dimensional (8 and 16, respectively) and can support many non-trivial \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}^{2}$$\end{document}S2-valued subcomponents. This suggests that multiple, potentially distinct topological numbers can arise via parameter-space dimensionality reduction. In particular, many physically meaningful maps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{P}}$$\end{document}P can be devised to highlight the topology of different aspects of the underlying field. One representative example is provided by the Mueller matrix polar decomposition, in which a Mueller matrix is decomposed into a depolarizer, a diattenuator, and a retarder40. These components provide additional degrees of freedom for engineering skyrmion structures with novel physical properties, as detailed in the Results.

Here, we focus on one such projection map, termed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{P}}}_{\mathrm{aniso}}$$\end{document}Paniso, which picks out a distinguished axis in materials exhibiting optical anisotropy. Not only are such skyrmions directly optically readable, but they can also encode information permanently, making them practical candidates for optical data storage applications. In this work, we use a spatially varying elliptical retarder array39 as a preliminary platform for demonstration, with the construction of the map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{P}}}_{\mathrm{aniso}}$$\end{document}Paniso detailed in Methods 1.

Lastly, to demonstrate the capabilities of our approach, we first use the tunable anisotropy of the retarder array to freely manipulate the effective distinguished axis of the entire cascade41,42 and generate a range of complex skyrmionic field configurations—including higher-order skyrmions, skyrmion bags, skyrmion lattices, and meron lattices. We then assess topological robustness by introducing varying levels of perturbation to a representative skyrmion (Néel-type skyrmion), confirming its stability under noise. This is in line with a newly established sufficient condition for topological protection, referred to as the 60° rule, which serves as a practical and easily verifiable engineering criterion for guaranteeing robustness in the presence of noise and measurement errors. The rule states that the encoded information remains topologically protected provided the physically realized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}^{2}$$\end{document}S2-valued field differs from the designed field everywhere by up to 60°, which is a remarkable margin of error18 (see Methods 2 and Supplementary Note 4). Finally, we demonstrate a proof-of-concept optical encoding scheme by storing ASCII information in perturbed skyrmion bags, highlighting their potential for robust optical data storage. These findings establish a new paradigm for topological data storage, providing a foundation for next-generation programmable information storage and processing that is both high-density and perturbation resistant.

Throughout this work, a spatially varying tunable elliptical retarder array is used to form our proposed optical anisotropy based skyrmions. The array is implemented using cascaded LC spatial light modulators (LC-SLMs) to form a pixel-level controllable and reconfigurable array39 (see Fig. 1b and ref. 39). We emphasize again that this platform is used for demonstration, and our proposed concept is readily applicable to other systems. The skyrmionic structures are decoded using Mueller matrix polarimetry45,46, which provides complete vectorial characterization of the medium.

To validate the feasibility of our framework, we experimentally realized various skyrmionic structures using LC-SLMs. The process involves three key steps: (1) encoding the desired spatially varying patterns into the axis geometry of the synthesized retarder array (see Supplementary Note 2); (2) measuring the corresponding Mueller matrices via polarimetry; and (3) extracting axis information through matrix decomposition47 (see Supplementary Note 1). Using this procedure, we generate six distinct skyrmionic structures, with both theoretical and experimental results shown in Fig. 2a, and two representative Mueller matrix examples illustrated in Fig. 2b. To demonstrate the topological stability of AGB skyrmions and highlight their potential for perturbation-resistant data storage, we conducted experiments under controlled Gaussian noise. While the impact of noise on skyrmion stability has been explored in structured light43,49,50, this problem remains in its infancy. Here, we extend these results to structured matter. The procedure is as follows: (1) we first formed a unit skyrmion, which was characterized via Mueller matrix measurements and decomposition, following the same method described above; (2) external Gaussian noise was artificially added to the retardance of the different layers which make up the structured matter, with the noise standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{\sigma }}$$\end{document}σ) varied from 0 to π in increments of 0.05π. Note that real-world disturbances such as thermal drift and mechanical vibrations—which are inherently non-Gaussian—were also present during the experiments; (3) to quantify the effects of perturbation, we performed 120 independently generated noise samples across all noise levels, with six repeated trials per \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}σ value, and computed the skyrmion number for each case, extracting the mean and standard deviation.

Discussions

In this work, we formalize a technique of parameter space dimensionality reduction that allows higher-dimensional vector fields to be interpreted through the lens of skyrmions. This technique is specialized to the light-matter interactions of structured matter, where we propose a new class of skyrmions constructed based on optical anisotropy. These skyrmions, which we term AGB skyrmions, are then realized using LC retarder platforms, and their robustness and capacity for information storage are demonstrated experimentally.

Conceptually, the work presented here enriches the study of structured matter, which is typically treated as a means to manipulate optical fields, by assigning topological degrees to the matter-field itself. Moreover, compared with conventional LC skyrmions, our approach addresses key limitations in information storage, including the nonpolar nature of LC director fields, which constrains data density, the lack of effective decoding methods, and spatial confinement, which restricts skyrmions to low-order configurations. However, we acknowledge that our current demonstration employs a compound synthetic platform rather than a single, tunable, true elliptical retarder device (see discussion below).

Several promising directions are enabled by our framework: (1) to increase storage density, exploring new LC materials and fabrication strategies—such as tunable LC droplets acting as nanoscale information carriers51 — may prove essential; (2) to enable rewritable and adaptive encoding, photo-responsive dyes such as SD152,53 and other photo-responsive dyes54,55 offer promising integration with LC platforms; (3) to improve modulation speed, fast-response materials such as blue-phase LCs56,57 may open new opportunities for real-time AGB skyrmion manipulation, enabling significantly reduced information writing times; (4) to truly overcome spatial confinement and realize true elliptical retarder devices with pixel-level control, new LC molecules and device architectures can be further investigated; (5) to achieve greater robustness and higher information density, the generalized skyrmion numbers can be adopted43,44.

Importantly, as noted earlier, AGB skyrmions are not restricted to LC systems and can, in principle, be realized in any optical retarder platform with precise axis control. Candidate technologies include laser-written birefringent plates58,59, metasurfaces60, or gradient index lens assemblies61–64. Moreover, we emphasize again that the concept of anisotropy-axis geometry can be extended beyond retarders to include diattenuators39, introducing absorber-based structures into this new skyrmion family with new physical properties yet to be explored. Beyond the encoding strategy demonstrated above, AGB skyrmion lattices can also be employed for image-based data storage (see Supplementary Note 5), offering a rich design space for future developments in topological structured matter.

Overall, we have introduced a new strategy for constructing skyrmions in unconventional, high-dimensional fields. Using light-matter interactions as a case study, we define a new type of skyrmion, the AGB skyrmion, and show that our abstract formalism enables the construction of complex topological states that would otherwise be difficult to observe. Furthermore, we demonstrate a practical use of AGB skyrmions in optically readable, topologically protected information storage. We believe this work opens new avenues for exploring topological phenomena in higher-dimensional systems and for developing robust platforms for high-density information storage and processing.

Topological resilience of AGB skyrmions

Here, we demonstrate the topological stability of AGB skyrmions under external perturbations, where stability here refers to the robustness of the skyrmion number. We identify three distinct stability regimes: (1) in the low-perturbation regime, the skyrmion number remains invariant, indicating strong topological protection; (2) as the perturbation strength increases, the system enters an intermediate regime where the mean skyrmion number begins to decrease due to partial boundary distortions; (3) in the high-perturbation regime, the system undergoes a breakdown, and the skyrmion number collapses, indicating a loss of topological information.
