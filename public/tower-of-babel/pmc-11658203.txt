The mental health of Chinese military personnel: a cross-sectional epidemiological study
Authors: Kaiyuan Jing, Zhengzhi Feng, Jing Xu, Ying He, Quan Tang, Qianhui Zhang, Ni Wang, Yani Xiao, Bicheng Gao, Bingqing Xue, Linkai Wu, Mengxue Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsConsidering the crucial role of military personnel’s mental health in the armed forces, society, and the nation, the present study aims to describe the prevalence and influential factors of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among Chinese military personnel during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsThe present study conducted an epidemiological investigation of mental health among Chinese military personnel between October 25th and November 10th, 2022. Data was collected from military units in thirty provinces across the country. Participants completed a self-reported questionnaire about their demographic characteristics and four validated scales (PHQ-9, GAD-7, ISI-7 and PCL-5), assessing the symptoms of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and PTSD.ResultsA total of 17,423 military personnel participated in the study. The prevalence of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and PTSD were 2.69%, 0.99%, 2.90% and 1.61%, respectively. Certain demographic groups were identified as having a higher prevalence, including older individuals, women, those with longer military service, higher education levels, divorced or widowed individuals, only child, those with one child, and those from urban areas.ConclusionsThe prevalence of mental health problems among Chinese military personnel has remained at a relatively low level, but our data still suggest that a significant number of individuals in the Chinese military population are affected by mental health issues. It is crucial for military units, society, and the nation to continuously prioritize the mental well-being of soldiers and provide them with more mental health care services.Keywords: Mental health, Military personnel, China, Covid-19

Background

Globally, mental health problems are already a major cause of the disease burden [1]. China is no exception, with various types of mental disorders prevalent throughout the country; the 12-month prevalence rate of any mental disorder is 9.3%, and the lifetime prevalence is 16.6% [2]. Mental well-being has received significant attention within the military domain as well. As a critical factor, mental health plays a vital role in the retention and productivity of military personnel [3]. A comprehensive understanding of the mental health conditions of their members ensures efficient planning, recruitment, and decision-making processes in military units. Moreover, the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic has introduced a new factor that further impacts mental health. It has been evidenced that the pandemic has direct and indirect psychological effects and can potentially affect present and future mental health [4]. Therefore, an understanding and assessment of the mental health of military personnel in pandemic environments is necessary.

The present study is conducted to fill two gaps in this research field. First, the disclosure of mental health information concerning military personnel is often kept confidential, previous epidemiological studies normally failed to cover this population, such as the China Mental Health Survey (CMHS) [5].

Second, previous studies on mental health in pandemic environments usually focused on the short-term effects of a pandemic while failed to cover the long-term effects, such as the measurement of depression and anxiety in the Republic of Ireland within one month of the first case of Covid-19 [6], and the mental health study in Australia during the first month of Covid-19 restrictions [7]. The challenges in obtaining comprehensive data on the long-term effects of the pandemic may stem from the gradual easing of restrictive measures by most countries between 2020 and 2022.

In China, however, the Zero-Covid policy remained in place until January 2023, providing an opportunity to study the long-term effects of the consistent, strictly enforced pandemic environment on people’s mental health. Within the condition, the present study collected mental health data from Chinese military personnel from October to November 2022, aiming to report the prevalence of depression, anxiety, insomnia and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), as well as the potential risk factors for those mental health issues. This study not only addresses the lack of epidemiological data on military personnel’s mental health but also provides insights into the long-term psychological impact of a prolonged pandemic environment.

Methods

Study design and participants

The web-based cross-sectional survey study was conducted to examine the mental health status and to explore the prevalence of common mental health issues among Chinese military personnel at the late stage of China’s Zero-Covid Policy. Additionally, the assessment of potential risk factors for those mental health issues was also included in this study. The survey was conducted from October 25 to November 10, 2022, two months before the end of the Zero-Covid Policy.

In this study, we employed cluster sampling to conduct a nationwide survey of the People’s Liberation Army (PLA). The sampling framework was based on the hierarchical structure of the military, where the units were first organized into distinct, non-overlapping clusters according to their geographic locations and organizational functions. Each cluster was defined as one military unit, and all cluster (units) shared the same size, administrative level, and similar number of personnel. From these clusters, a random selection was made, and all selected clusters (units) were included in the sample. This approach allowed us to capture data that was as representative as possible of the overall target population while ensuring practical feasibility in terms of data collection. Main inclusion criteria were: age between 18 and 60; no disability; fair reading and comprehension skills. Key exclusion criteria were: absent from mainland China for most of the past year.

Initially, we reached out to the supervisors of regional units to explain the study’s purpose. Upon their agreement to participate, we provided them with the link to the online questionnaire. Then, the link to the questionnaire was delivered by supervisors of each unit via e-mail and social media (e.g. WeChat) to individuals. The study aimed to collect data from a target population of 18,005 military personnel and successfully gathered responses from 17,423 participants, yielding a response rate of 96.77% (from 92.38 to 98.05%). Permission to conduct the survey was obtained from every military unit involved in the study, ensuring compliance with related regulations. The military personnel consented to this study voluntarily and were not ordered to participate. The military personnel were informed about the purpose of the study, and the present study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures and outcomes

Demographic

Demographic variables included participants’ gender, age, length of military service, education level, marital status, whether they were the only child of their parents, number of children, place of growth and area of residence in the past year.

The sex classification criterion was divided into two categories from a biological perspective, ‘male’ and ‘female’. Age, length of military service, education level, and marital status were divided into three categories. Participants reported whether they were the only child. The number of children the participant had was also one of the demographic variables in this study. The option of growth place: ‘rural’ or ‘urban’, refers to the places where participants grew up before joining the military. Finally, according to the geographic location of China, individuals’ residences in the past year were divided into seven categories: ‘East’, ‘South’, ‘Central’, ‘North’, ‘Northwest’, ‘Southwest’ and ‘Northeast’.

Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9)

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to measure depressive symptoms in this study. The PHQ-9 consists of nine items, and participants indicated the frequency of being bothered in the past two weeks, using a Likert scale from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day) depending on the item. The possible total score range is 0–27, with higher scores indicating higher levels of depression, where PHQ-9 scores of 0–4: absence, 5–9: mild, 10–14: moderate, 15–19: moderately severe, and 20–27: severe depressive symptoms. A total score of ≥ 10 was used in this study to positively screen for depression. This cut-off score was selected based on its established validity in the literature, where a PHQ-9 score ≥ 10 has a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 88% for detecting major depression [8]. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of the PHQ-9 was 0.91 in this study.

General anxiety disorder 7 (GAD-7)

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) was used to measure the severity of self-reported anxiety in the present study. The GAD-7 consists of seven items, and participants indicated the frequency of being bothered in the past two weeks, using a Likert scale from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day) depending on the item. The possible total score range is 0–21, with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxiety, where GAD-7 scores of 0–4: absence, 5–9: mild, 10–14: moderate, and 15–21: severe anxiety symptoms. A total score of ≥ 10 was used in this study to positively screen for anxiety. This threshold is widely accepted in the literature, as a GAD-7 score ≥ 10 has a sensitivity of 89% and a specificity of 82% for detecting Generalized Anxiety Disorder [9]. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of the GAD-7 was 0.94 in the present study.

Insomnia Severity Index 7 (ISI-7)

The Insomnia Severity Index-7 (ISI-7) was used to measure the nature, severity, and impact of insomnia. The ISI-7 consists of seven items, and participants indicated the severity of the problem for each item in the past month, using a Likert scale from 0 (No problem) to 4 (Very severe problem). The possible total score range is 0–28, with higher scores indicating higher levels of insomnia, where ISI-7 scores of 0–7: absence, 8–14: sub-threshold, 15–21: moderate, and 22–28: severe insomnia. A total score of ≥ 15 was used in this study to positively screen for insomnia. This cut-off was chosen based on its established efficacy, with an ISI-7 score ≥ 15 demonstrating a sensitivity of 78% and a specificity of 100% for detecting Insomnia [10]. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of the ISI-7 was 0.90 in this study.

The PTSD checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5)

20-item Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist (PCL-5) was used to measure the severity of PTSD symptoms in this study (regular version for civilian). Participants rated the items on a Likert scale from 0 (Not at all) to 4 (Extremely). The possible total score range is 0–80, with higher scores indicating higher levels of PTSD. A total score of ≥ 33 was used in this study to positively screen for PTSD. This score is commonly used in the literature, with a PCL-5 score ≥ 33 showing a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 69% for detecting PTSD [11]. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of the PCL-5 was 0.97 in this study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics provided an overview of the demographic characteristics and the prevalence of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and PTSD in the sample, including variations across different demographic groups. The sample was also analyzed to uncover the proportions of distinct levels of mental health issues.

We conducted separate binary logistic regression analyses for each mental health outcome—depression, anxiety, insomnia, and PTSD. In these analyses, the nine demographic variables (e.g., age, sex, educational level) were used as independent variables, and each mental health outcome was treated as a binary dependent variable. The mental health outcomes were categorized as: participants were classified as either having depression, anxiety, insomnia, or PTSD (based on a predefined cutoff score on each scale) or not.

Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the association between the demographic variables and each mental health outcome. The logistic regression models were implemented using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29.0, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic

The mean age of the sample was 26.34 years (standard deviation = 5.87), and 97.0% (n = 16906) were male, 3.0% were female (n = 517). The mean length of military service was 7.20 years (standard deviation = 6.11). The vast majority of respondents had completed a college or university degree or higher (n = 13004, 74.7%). Over two-thirds of the respondents had never been married (n = 11743, 67.4%). A larger proportion of respondents were not the only child of their parents (n = 11525, 66.1%). There were more respondents who had no children (n = 13376, 76.8%). Most respondents grew up in rural areas (n = 12707, 72.9%). The largest number of respondents lived in North China (n = 7429, 42.6%) and the smallest number in South China (n = 55, 0.3%) in the past year (Table 1).

Depression

Higher prevalence of depression was observed in military personnel aged 26–35 (odds ratio [OR] 1.46 [95% CI 1.19–1.79]) and 36–60 (OR 2.73 [95% CI 2.09–3.57]) than those aged 18–25, in women (OR 2.66 [95% CI 1.85–3.81]) than men, in those with 7–16 years of military service (OR 1.35 [95% CI 1.10–1.65]) and 17–30 years (OR 2.01 [95% CI 1.51–2.66]) than those with 0–6 years of military service, in those with a college or university degree (OR 1.49 [95% CI 1.17–1.90]) and with a master or higher degree (OR 6.56 [95% CI 4.54–9.49]) than those with up to a high school degree, in those who never married (OR 1.69 [95% CI 1.41–2.04]) and divorced or widowed (OR 7.36 [95% CI 3.73–14.52]) than married ones, in those who were the only child (OR 1.45 [95% CI 1.20–1.75]) than those who were not, in those who grew up in urban area (OR 1.83 [95% CI 1.52–2.21]) than those grew in rural area, and in those with one (OR 2.00 [95% CI 1.62–2.48]) or two children (OR 1.67 [95% CI 1.22–2.29]) than those without any children. Lower prevalence of depression was observed in Central China (OR 0.70 [95% CI 0.49–0.99]) and Northeast China (OR 0.65 [95% CI 0.42–0.99]) compared to East China.

Anxiety

Higher prevalence of anxiety was observed in military personnel aged 26–35 (OR 1.87 [95% CI 1.34–2.61]) and 36–60 (OR 3.51 [95% CI 2.29–5.38]) than those aged 18–25, in women (OR 2.70 [95% CI 1.52–4.78]) than men, in those with 7–16 years of military service (OR 1.69 [95% CI 1.22–2.35]) and 17–30 years (OR 2.57 [95% CI 1.65-4.00]) than those with 0–6 years of military service, in those with a college or university degree (OR 2.12 [95% CI 1.34–3.37]), and a master or higher degree (OR 12.87 [95% CI 7.18–23.08]) than those with up to a high school degree, in those who never married (OR 2.41 [95% CI 1.78–3.27]) and divorced or widowed (OR 13.81 [95% CI 5.81–32.80]) than married ones, in those who were the only child of their parents (OR 1.37 [95% CI 1.01–1.85]) than those who were not, in those who grew up in urban area (OR 2.08 [95% CI 1.54–2.81]) than those grew in rural area and in those who were the only child (OR 1.37 [95% CI 1.01–1.85]) than those who were not, in those who grew up in urban area (OR 2.08 [95% CI 1.54–2.81]) than those grew in rural area, in those with one (OR 2.89 [95% CI 2.07–4.03]) and two children (OR 2.40 [95% CI 1.49–3.87]) than those without any children, and in those living in North China (OR 2.03 [95% CI 1.22–3.40]) than East China.

Insomnia

Higher prevalence of insomnia was observed in military personnel aged 26–35 (OR 1.89 [95% CI 1.55–2.31]) and 36–60 (OR 4.18 [95% CI 3.27–5.35]) than those aged 18–25, in women (OR 2.05 [95% CI 1.39–3.01]) than men, in those with 7–16 years of military service (OR 1.71 [95% CI 1.40–2.08]) and 17–30 years (OR 3.46 [95% CI 2.70–4.43]) than those with 0–6 years of military service, in those with a college or university degree (OR 1.88 [95% CI 1.46–2.42]) and a master or higher degree (OR 7.29 [95% CI 5.01–10.62]) than those with up to a high school degree in those who never married (OR 2.39 [95% CI 2.00-2.86]) and divorced or widowed (OR 6.19 [95% CI 2.93–13.07]) than married ones, in those who were the only child of their parents (OR 1.34 [95% CI 1.12–1.61]) than those who were not, in those who grew up in urban area (OR 1.60 [95% CI 1.33–1.93]) than those grew in rural area, and in those with one (OR 2.69 [95% CI 2.21–3.29]) or two children (OR 2.15 [95% CI 1.61–2.88]) than those without any children.

PTSD

Higher prevalence of PTSD was observed in military personnel who aged 36–60 (OR 2.19 [95% CI 1.56–3.07]) than those aged 18–25, in those with 17–30 years of military service (OR 1.83 [95% CI 1.30–2.59]) than those with 0–6 years of military service, in those with a master or higher degree (OR 6.32 [95% CI 4.03–9.92]) than those with up to a high school degree, in those who never married (OR 1.33 [95% CI 1.04–1.70]) and divorced or widowed (OR 7.54 [95% CI 3.40-16.69]) than married ones, in those who were the only child (OR 1.36 [95% CI 1.07–1.73]) than those who were not, in those who grew up in urban area (OR 1.78 [95% CI 1.40–2.26]) than those grew in rural area, in those with one child (OR 1.84 [95% CI 1.40–2.42]) than those without any children, and in those living in North China (OR 1.53 [95% CI 1.06–2.23]) than East China.

Discussion

Findings from the current study offer initial insights into the prevalence rates of depression, anxiety, insomnia and PTSD within the military population of China during the late period of the COVID-19 quarantine measures. The prevalence rates in this study provide a sample-specific and setting-specific baseline that contributes to a better understanding on the mental health of the Chinese military to the societal and scientific community. By reporting different levels of symptoms, the distribution of the severity of symptoms is revealed, which facilitates effective early intervention for the prevention of mental health issues [12]. By analysing risk factors, this study also suggests a reference for the identification of high-risk groups in the military. The implementation of focused care and interventions for high-risk groups is of great significance for the prevention of mental health issues [13].

The prevalence of depression (2.69%), anxiety (0.99%), insomnia (2.90%) and PTSD (1.61%) observed in the present study remain lower even compared to the prevalence of mental health problems among military personnel prior to the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, with a 4.9% prevalence of PTSD [14], a 9.5% prevalence of depression [15], and a 7.8% prevalence of anxiety [16]. This is an unexpected result, given the extensive research showing that people’s mental health is generally worse during the pandemic [17]. Two phenomena may warrant consideration. Firstly, during the pandemic, due to the implementation of China’s Zero-Covid policy, military personnel experienced a number of changes in their environment. For example, the postponement or cancellation of certain operations due to the pandemic may have contributed to a decrease in occupational stress. Moreover, travel restrictions possibly afforded military personnel more opportunities to spend time with their families than usual. These may have exerted a beneficial effect on their mental health. Secondly, during the pandemic, more physical and psychological medical service was provided to military personnel, thus helping them better cope with stress and adversity.

In an earlier stage after the implementation of Zero-Covid policy in China, the prevalence of depression, anxiety and sleep problems among civilians was 20.1%, 35.1% and 18.2%, respectively [18]; the prevalence of PTSD was 2.7% [19]. These figures are all higher than the results in the current study. Although studies have shown that mental health improves over time as people become more aware of the virus and protective measures [20], the present study suggests the possible role of the difference between civilians and military personnel. On the one hand, the physical and psychological screening of military personnel during the recruitment and enlistment process may help exclude the individuals with potential mental health problems. On the other hand, military training emphasizes on soldiers’ psychological resilience and coping skills, and this reduces the risk of developing mental health problems in the military. In addition, the military’s daily training ensures a better physical health than that of civilians, which may serve as a protective factor for mental health [21].

According to an epidemiological study of military personnel in the United States, the prevalence of depression, anxiety and PTSD was 9.4%, 14.2% and 8.5%, respectively [22]. A similar study in Peru found during the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of depression and anxiety among military personnel was 29.9% and 22.0% [23]. In comparison, the prevalence of mental health problems is lower among the Chinese military personnel in this study. With the challenges of COVID-19, a culture of collectivism paired with a relatively strict Zero-Covid policy has probably offered potential protections to Chinese military personnel in terms of their mental health [24]. The reflection of collectivism and team spirit in Chinese military culture has led to the formation of a tighter social support network for military personnel in challenging times. The social support network provides them with emotional support, affective expression and mutual aid, helping them to relieve the stress and adapt to difficult circumstances. The relatively rigorous Zero-Covid policy likely reduced the likelihood of COVID-19 infection among military personnel and reduced their perception of the virus as a threat through those around them. This heightened sense of safety concerning pandemic may have also played a role in fostering better mental health outcomes within this group. Additionally, in Chinese society, military personnel are widely recognized and respected by society as heroes and protectors of the nation. The higher level of socioeconomic status (SES) and sense of honor may boost their self-esteem, confidence and psychological resilience. These factors, in turn, potentially contribute to better mental health among Chinese military personnel [25].

It is important to note that while the instruments and cut-off points used in this study are common and widely used in epidemiological research, comparisons with other studies still require careful interpretation due to potential discrepancies in measurement tools and standards. Additionally, due to the lack of pre-pandemic data from a sample group with similar characteristics to this study, all speculation and hypotheses regarding the relationship between the pandemic and mental health outcomes (e.g., pressure changes in pandemic environment, or the role of collectivism) in this study should be considered with caution. However, potential interactions that may exist between these factors are suggested, underscoring the need for further research in this area.

In addition, existing studies have shown that the prevalence of mental health problems is higher among military personnel with combat exposure [26]. The results of this study provide an additional reference. As China has been in a state of prolonged peace for many years, the prevalence rates for the results in the present study were lower than those of Ukrainian soldiers who were engaged in war during the same period of the Russian-Ukrainian conflict, with 43.3% prevalence of depression, 44.4% of anxiety and 12.4% of insomnia [27].

In the analysis of risk (protective) factors, the present study reports on the relationship between different demographic characteristics and the prevalence of mental health problems. The study found that participants with longer military service are at higher risk of mental health issues. This may be attributed to the elevated levels of stress encountered in military settings compared to other social environments. Consequently, individuals who live and work under such conditions for prolonged periods are more likely to experience mental health challenges [28]. Another noteworthy finding in this study was that the prevalence of mental health problems was higher among military personnel with higher educational levels, as detailed by the fact that the prevalence in postgraduate group was higher than the college or university group and higher than the high school or lower group. Higher educational levels are usually considered to be a protective factor for mental health problems [29]. However, such a result can be acceptable when the specific nature of the military profession is taken into account: military personnel with higher educational levels are often assigned to more challenging and stressful tasks, and the differences in tasks indirectly lead to differences in prevalence.

Differences in family-related characteristics result in the differences in the prevalence of mental health problems as well. A higher prevalence is observed among military personnel who have one child than those without. This is possibly due to people with children having more concerns about their family than those without, which may potentially affect their mental health. As for the higher prevalence of military personnel with one child than those with multiple children, perhaps an old Chinese proverb can be used to explain it: “The first child is raised according to the book, the second child is raised according to the pig.” It hints at a traditional social belief in China that the first child in a family is given more effort and resources to educate and nurture, while later ones are more likely to be neglected. In other words, military personnel with only one child may instead be under more parenting pressure than those with multiple children.

Military personnel who are the only child have a higher prevalence rate than those who are not. In China, most people born between 1979 and 2015 are the only child in their family. Although there is a lack of relevant research to confirm the relationship between being an only child and the prevalence of mental health issues, some evidence has suggested that the particular upbringing style of the only child can lead to higher levels of loneliness and social isolation than that of non-only children [30].

Military personnel raised in urban environments exhibit poorer mental health compared to those raised in rural areas. Within China, there exists a significant disparity between urban and rural areas in terms of economic conditions, healthcare access, and educational opportunities [31]. Individuals raised in urban settings typically enjoy a higher socioeconomic status, resulting in a lifestyle of privilege. These individuals may face greater challenges when adjusting to the rigid and monotonous military environment compared to those brought up in rural areas.

The present study found no clear trends in the prevalence across seven different subgroups. The working conditions, living arrangements, and medical services provided within the military are roughly consistent regardless of location. This uniformity and fairness across regions may account for the absence of disparities in mental health issues among different areas. Previous studies with Chinese civilian samples have also found that regional differences do not explain the differences in depression prevalence, which is consistent with the results of the current study [32].

The prevalence of mental health problems in the present study was not high by scale standards, but when the huge number of military personnel in the Chinese People’s Liberation Army is taken into consideration, such a prevalence indicates a significant number of Chinese military personnel suffering from mental issues. The critical significance of the military profession necessitates more care to and effective interventions on the mental health of military personnel based on their different types of service, tasks and places of stationing.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, the study is based on cross-sectional data, where only correlations are reported between characteristics and prevalence, no causal relationships can be established. Secondly, self-reported web-based surveys are different from clinical interviews. Although clinical psychiatrists were involved throughout the study, the use of a single criterion score did not allow for a clinical diagnosis of the participants. And self-reported data faces challenges from social desirability and response biases. These biases may lead participants to respond in socially acceptable ways, such as underreporting mental health symptoms, or to answer in patterned ways, like consistently choosing middle options, thus distorting the true distribution of responses.

Thirdly, in some groups classified by demographic characteristics, both the total number and the prevalence were small, which may have led to errors in the estimates of the 95% confidence intervals of the Odd Ratios from the binary logistic regression analysis. Additionally, given that this study was conducted under the unique and temporal context of the pandemic, where factors like travel restrictions had specific societal impacts, the generalizability of the findings may be somewhat limited. Last but not least, surveys in the military are non-anonymous. In non-anonymous situations, people tend to report better mental health [33]. It is possible that the current study is underestimating the prevalence of mental health problems in the military.

Conclusions

The prevalence of mental health problems among Chinese military personnel is not high, but our data still suggest that a significant number of individuals in the Chinese military population are affected by mental health issues. It is essential for military units, society, and the nation to continuously prioritize the mental well-being of military personnel, particularly those with identified risk characteristics.

To strengthen military mental health services, specific actions such as implementing regular mental health screenings and resilience training programs should be considered. These interventions could help early-stage identification and reduce the incidence of mental health issues. Additionally, further research is needed to explore the long-term effects of military service on mental health and to identify causal relationship between specific risk factors within subgroups and mental health outcomes of military personnel.

The findings of this study have important implications for informing mental health policies and practices within the Chinese military, contributing to a more proactive approach to supporting the mental health of military personnel.
