1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

With
\mu
the Möbius function, the statement

\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{1/2+\varepsilon}~~~\text{for all }\varepsilon>0

is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis. This fact motivated the 1944 paper of
[
35
]
, where a probabilistic model for the Möbius function was investigated. Noting that the Möbius function is the unique multiplicative function supported on square-free integers that takes the value
-1
on primes, Wintner took a collection of independent identically distributed random variables
{(f(p))}_{p\text{ prime}}
such that
\mathbb{P}(f(p)=\pm 1)=1/2
, and considered the
random multiplicative function
defined by

f(n)=\prod_{p|n}f(p),

on square-free
n
, and
f(n)=0
otherwise. This function is known in the literature as the Rademacher random multiplicative function. In his paper, Wintner proved that for any
\varepsilon>0
we almost surely have

\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{1/2+\varepsilon},

proving a random analogue of the Riemann hypothesis. An alternative random model is the Steinhaus random multiplicative function: a model for Dirichlet characters and continuous characters. We define a Steinhaus random multiplicative function
f
by taking
{(f(p))}_{p\text{ prime}}
to be independent Steinhaus random variables; that is, random variables uniformly distributed on the unit circle
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon|z|=1\}
, and setting

f(n)=\prod_{p\mid n}{f(p)}^{v_{p}(n)},

where
v_{p}(n)
denotes the
p
-adic valuation of
n
.

Random multiplicative functions have been studied extensively since Wintner. The main result of this paper provides a step towards the longstanding open problem concerning the distribution of the partial sums
\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
, for
f
a Steinhaus random multiplicative function:

Theorem 1
.

Let
P(n)
denote the largest prime factor of
n
, let
\gamma\coloneqq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}-\log n)
be the Euler–Mascheroni constant, and let
C=\frac{e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{2\pi}
. For
f
a Steinhaus random multiplicative function, we have

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}f(n)\xrightarrow{d}\sqrt{CV_{\mathrm{crit}}}\,\mathcal{CN}(0,1)\quad\text{as }x\rightarrow\infty,

where
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
is a standard complex Gaussian random variable and
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
is a random variable that is independent of the Gaussian such that
\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathrm{crit}}^{q}]
exists for each
0<q<1
. We define
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
as a distributional limit of integrals with respect to a critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure. See Theorem
3
for details.

Corollary 1

(Convergence of moments)
.

Let
f
be a Steinhaus random multiplicative function. For any fixed
0<q<2
, there exists a constant
M=M(q)>0
such that

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}{\biggl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\Bigl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}f(n)\Bigr|\biggr)}^{q}=M.

Specifically, we find that
M=C^{\frac{q}{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[V_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\frac{q}{2}}\bigr]\Gamma(\frac{q}{2}+1)
, where
C
and
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
are as in Theorem
1
, and
\Gamma
is the gamma function.

Corollary 2

(Heavy tails)
.

For
f
a Steinhaus random multiplicative function, and for any
\varepsilon>0
,
y\geq 2
, we have

\frac{1}{y^{2+\varepsilon}}\ll_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\biggl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\Bigl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}f(n)\Bigr|>y\biggr)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{y^{2-\varepsilon}},

when
x
is sufficiently large in terms of
y
.

This second corollary tells us that the tails of our partial sums are dominated by the tails of
\sqrt{V_{\mathrm{crit}}}
. Similar tail estimates for the full sum have been proven by
[
15
, Corollary 2]
, but the above gives a more precise lower bound when
y
is bounded.

Remark 1.1
.

Our results handle a positive proportion of the full sum, seeing as the set
\{1\leq n\leq x\colon P(n)>x^{1/2}\}
has positive asymptotic density.

Remark 1.2
.

We do not prove our main theorem in the Rademacher case owing to the covariance structure of
\Re\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}
, which we discuss more in Section
7.1
. This obstruction can likely be overcome but lies outside the scope of this paper. We do, however, prove one of our main results (Theorem
2
) in the Rademacher case, and this result may be of independent interest.

1.2.
Previous work

Since the inception of random multiplicative functions in
[
35
]
, much research has been done to try and understand the almost sure size of the partial sums
\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
, for
f
a Rademacher or Steinhaus random multiplicative function. The upper bound was investigated in
[
4
,
25
,
26
,
35
]
, culminating in the recent work of
[
7
]
, who showed that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we almost surely have

\Bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\Bigr|\ll\sqrt{x}{(\log\log x)}^{3/4+\varepsilon},

for
f
a Rademacher or Steinhaus random multiplicative function. For lower bounds
[
16
]
(improving on earlier work
[
12
]
) has shown that, for any function
V(x)
that diverges to infinity,

\Bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\Bigr|\geq\sqrt{x}\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{V(x)},

for arbitrarily large
x
, almost surely, for
f
a Rademacher or Steinhaus random multiplicative function. Note that
[
26
]
proved a matching upper bound for
\sum_{n\leq x,\,P(n)>\sqrt{x}}f(n)
, so this result is expected to reflect the true size of the fluctuations.

Another central problem has been to understand the expected size of the partial sums,
\mathbb{E}\bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigr|
, for
f
a Rademacher or Steinhaus random multiplicative function. It was conjectured by
[
20
]
that this quantity should enjoy “better than square-root” cancellation, in that
\mathbb{E}\bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigr|=o(\sqrt{x})
. This conjecture was motivated by the relationship

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}n^{it}\Bigr|^{2q}\,dt=\mathbb{E}\Bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\Bigr|^{2q},

that holds for
q>0
. Seeing as the sums
\sum_{n\leq x}n^{it}
are multiplicative analogues of Dirichlet kernels, which possess small
L^{1}
norms, Helson conjectured that the
L^{1}
norm of random multiplicative functions should also be small. The conjecture was the subject of much investigation
[
6
,
19
,
34
]

and, in 2020,
[
15
]
provided a positive answer to Helson’s conjecture, showing that

(1.1)

\mathbb{E}\Bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\Bigr|\asymp\frac{\sqrt{x}}{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}},

using an intricate probabilistic argument relating to the ballot problem. One of the key things noticed in this work was that the partial sums of random multiplicative functions are related to the theory of
Gaussian multiplicative chaos
, an area of probability theory introduced by
[
23
]
in the 1980s.

One of the biggest remaining open problems is to understand the
distribution
of the partial sums
\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
. It was first shown by
[
13
]
that the partial sums
do not
satisfy a central limit theorem, in that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
does not converge in distribution to a (non-degenerate) Gaussian random variable, and, improving on previous work of
[
21
]
, Harper also showed that we do have a central limit theorem if we restrict to integers with few prime factors. Various other central limit theorems have been proven when one restricts to a special set of integers
[
8
,
31
]
, and also for exponential sums
[
5
]
.

In a recent pre-print,
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
use martingale techniques to establish the first non-Gaussian convergence theorem for (appropriately weighted) partial sums of random multiplicative functions. Their result is quite general and applies to sums of the form
{({\mathbb{E}|\sum_{n\leq x}g(n)f(n)|^{2}})}^{-1/2}\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)g(n)
where
f
is a Rademacher or Steinhaus random multiplicative function and
g
is a multiplicative function with sparse support or good decay. These conditions exclude, for example,
g\equiv 1
, where one expects slightly different behaviour more in line with this paper (see the end of Section
1.4
for more on this). Indeed, our result is novel in the sense that our normalised partial sums
are not
of the form
{({\mathbb{E}|\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}f(n)|^{2}})}^{-1/2}\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}f(n)
for some complex coefficients
a_{n}
, as in all previous works. Such sums are sensible to study in most cases, but are not the correct thing to study in the case of the full partial sums
\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
, or some close analogue, as in our case. For example, a corollary of (
1.1
) is that (in the Steinhaus case, say)
{({\mathbb{E}|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)|^{2}})}^{-1/2}\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)=\frac{1}{\lfloor\sqrt{x}\rfloor}\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\xrightarrow{p}0
, and to investigate the distribution of the partial sums one should instead consider
\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
, or
\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{n\leq x,\,P(n)>\sqrt{x}}f(n)
in our case
1
1

1

If one normalises by anything smaller than
\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}
, an application of Markov’s inequality gives convergence in probability to zero. Furthermore, an adapted Payley–Zygmund inequality allows one to show
\mathbb{P}(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\bigl|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\bigr|>c)\gg_{c}1
for

. This motivated
[
10

to conjecture that we should have

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\xrightarrow{d}\sqrt{\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{crit}}}\,\mathcal{CN}(0,1)\quad\text{as }x\rightarrow\infty,

where
\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{crit}}
is a limiting object defined using partial Euler products. Theorem
1
provides evidence towards this conjecture and explicitly relates (our analogue of)
\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{crit}}
to Gaussian multiplicative chaos. We remark that the recent work of
[

]
builds on the previous work of
[
26

on almost sure upper bounds for
\sum_{n\leq x,\,P(n)>\sqrt{x}}f(n)
, so handling this subsum has proved a useful step towards understanding the full sum.

1.3.
Key results

For
f
a Steinhaus random multiplicative function, we begin by noting that

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}f(n)=\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}f(p)\sum_{m\leq x/p}f(m).

Conditioning on the innermost sum, the right-hand side is a sum of independent random variables whose distribution approximates a complex Gaussian with mean
0
and variance
\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\bigl|\sum_{m\leq x/p}f(m)\bigr|^{2}
. This Gaussian approximation is the stage where restricting to integers
n
with
P(n)>\sqrt{x}
is important. One may suspect, by Parseval’s theorem or Perron’s formula, that the variance is roughly

(1.2)

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\frac{F(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt,

where
F(s)\coloneqq\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}{\bigl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\bigr)}^{-1}
is the partial Euler product associated to
f
. An analogous statement should hold in the Rademacher case, and in this case, we define
F(s)\coloneqq\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}{\bigl(1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\bigr)}
instead. Making this statement precise is a key step toward proving Theorem
1
, and constitutes the bulk of the paper. It turns out to be easier to prove a similar statement involving significantly shorter Euler products, showing that the distribution is controlled by the behaviour of
f(p)
on relatively small primes. To state the theorem, we define the truncated Euler products
F_{j}(s)\coloneqq\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{x}^{e^{-j}}}{\bigl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\bigr)}^{-1}
in the Steinhaus case and
F_{j}(s)\coloneqq\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{x}^{e^{-j}}}{\bigl(1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\bigr)}
in the Rademacher case, so that
F_{0}=F
. We prove the following:

Theorem 2
.

Let
f
be a Steinhaus or a Rademacher random multiplicative function, let
C=\frac{e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{2\pi}
,
y=\lfloor 100\log\log\log x\rfloor
, and
B(x)=\sqrt{x}^{e^{-y}}
, so that
F_{y}(s)
is a product over primes below
B(x)
. Then with probability
1-O({(\log\log x)}^{-1/5})
, we have

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\bigl|\sum_{m\leq x/p}f(m)\bigr|^{2}=\frac{C{(\log\log B(x))}^{1/2}}{\log B(x)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\frac{F_{y}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt+E(x),

where
E(x)
satisfies
E(x)\ll{(\log\log x)}^{-1/5}
.

Previously it was only known (using arguments from
[
15
]
) that the low moments of these quantities should be of the same order of magnitude. Now, having shown that our variances concentrate around the mean square of an Euler product, we are in a hopeful position, since previous work of
[
30
, Theorem 1.9]
tells us that, when
t
is restricted to a bounded interval, we have convergence in distribution to an integral with respect to a random measure known as a critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure. In the following theorem, we extend their result to give convergence over the full range of integration. To state our result, we redefine the Euler product over primes
p\leq x
by
F_{(x)}(s)\coloneqq\prod_{p\leq x}{\bigl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\bigr)}^{-1}
in the Steinhaus case. This is a notational convenience that makes the statement of our theorem more natural.

Theorem 3

(Extension of Theorem 1.9 of
[
30
]
)
.

For
g\in C^{l}[a,b]
, denote
\|g\|_{C^{l}[a,b]}=\sum_{j=0}^{l}\|g^{(j)}\|_{L^{\infty}[a,b]}
. For
f
a Steinhaus random multiplicative function, we have

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\frac{F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt\xrightarrow{d}V_{\mathrm{crit}}\quad\text{as }x\rightarrow\infty,

where
\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathrm{crit}}^{q}]
exists for each
0<q<1
.
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
is defined as the limit (in distribution) of
V_{n}=\int_{-n}^{n}\frac{g(t)}{|1/2+it|^{2}}\lambda(dt)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
g(t)
is a positive random continuous function such that, for each
n>0
, all norms
\|g\|_{C^{l}[-n,n]}
and
\|1/g\|_{C^{l}[-n,n]}
possess moments of all orders, and
\lambda(dt)
is a critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure. Note that
g
and
\lambda
may not be independent. The random variable
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
is precisely the one that appears in Theorem
1
.

For details of this weak convergence, see Section
7
, and more specifically Section
7.2
. An analogous theorem is not known in the Rademacher case (and would involve non-trivial adjustments to the argument). For this reason, we do not give a proof of Theorem
1
in the Rademacher case.

1.4.
Connection to Gaussian multiplicative chaos

We now explain the connection between our variance (which we think of as mean squares of random Euler products like (
1.2
)) and the theory of Gaussian multiplicative chaos, restricting ourselves to the Steinhaus case. For an introduction to the theory, see
[
29
]
. A similar discussion can be found in the introduction of
[
15
]
. Here, we put a particular emphasis on understanding the limiting distribution of our variances.

We begin by noting that, for fixed
t
, the quantity
\log|F(1/2+it)|\approx\Re\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}
is distributed like a Gaussian with mean
0
and variance approximately
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{2}\log\log x+O(1)
. Consequently, we think of
X(t)=\log|F(1/2+it)|
as a Gaussian field. The reader is reminded that a key property of Gaussian fields is that their distribution is completely determined by their means,
\mathbb{E}[X(t)]
(which will be zero in the Steinhaus case), and their covariances,
\mathbb{E}[X(t_{1})X(t_{2})]
. With this in mind, we rewrite our variances in (
1.2
) as
\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{0}^{1}e^{2X(t)}\,dt
. For simplicity, we have restricted ourselves to a compact interval and ignored the denominator. It is natural to normalise our integral so that the integrand has expectation one, and, if
X(t)
were actually Gaussian for each
t\in[0,1]
, the correct
2
2

2

This follows from the moment generating function for Gaussian random variables.
renormalisation would be to multiply the integrand by
e^{-2\mathbb{E}{X(t)}^{2}}
. This accounts for the factor of
\frac{1}{\log x}
in (
1.1
), so our variances are roughly

(1.3)

{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\Bigl(2X(t)-2\mathbb{E}\bigl({X(t)}^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dt,

where
X(t)=\log|F(1/2+it)|
. Broadly speaking, Gaussian multiplicative chaos is the study of random measures

\mu_{\gamma}(dt)=\exp\Bigl(\gamma\tilde{X}(t)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl({\tilde{X}(t)}^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dt,

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is some domain, and
{\bigl(\tilde{X}(t)\bigr)}_{t\in\mathcal{D}}
is Gaussian field with a certain type of
logarithmic
covariance structure. Note that these measures are similarly normalised so that
\mathbb{E}\mu_{\gamma}(dt)=dt
. It happens that
X(t)=\log|F(1/2+it)|
does possess an approximately logarithmic covariance structure, in that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[X(t_{1})X(t_{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{\cos\bigl((t_{1}-t_{2})\log p\bigr)}{2p},

which approaches
-\frac{1}{2}\log|t_{1}-t_{2}|
when
x
is large and when
\frac{1}{\log x}\leq|t_{1}-t_{2}|\leq 1
, say. This is (up to scaling) the covariance structure that is central in the study of Gaussian multiplicative chaos, so the theory can be used to analyse (
1.3
). From a number theoretic standpoint, the logarithmic covariance structure is a manifestation of the fact that
p^{it}=e^{it\log p}
rotate at different speeds for different primes
p
. Let
\mathcal{K}=\lfloor\log\log x\rfloor
, and write

\log|F(1/2+it)|\approx\Re\Biggl(\sum_{1\leq k\leq\mathcal{K}}\sum_{\sqrt{x}^{e^{-(k+1)}}<p\leq\sqrt{x}^{e^{-k}}}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\Biggr).

For the largest
k
, the innermost sum is roughly fixed as
t
varies over bounded intervals. However, for the smallest
k
, the innermost sum is roughly fixed over intervals of length
\approx\frac{1}{\log x}
. Thus, there are
\log\log x
“scales”, and on the scale
k
, the innermost sum “takes”
\frac{2^{k}}{\log x}
values as
t
varies over
[-1,1]
, say. An important idea underlying the proof of Theorem
2
is that the values of
f(p)
on small primes dictate the bulk of the distribution of our sum, seeing as the average over
t
has little effect on them. For more about
\log|F(1/2+it)|
and logarithmically correlated fields, see the survey article of
[
3
]
.

Theorem
3
makes explicit the relation between our variances and Gaussian multiplicative chaos and specifically involves a random measure known as the
critical
Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure. We do not explicitly define this object in this paper, but to gain some insight into its behaviour, we consider the more general expression (
1.3
) and describe how the parameter
\gamma
influences the behaviour of

(1.4)

\int_{0}^{1}\exp\Bigl(\gamma X(t)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl({X(t)}^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dt,

where
X(t)=\log|F(1/2+it)|
. When
\gamma=2
this is precisely (
1.3
) without the factor
{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}
. Assuming that
X(t)
is distributed like a Gaussian with mean
0
and variance
\frac{1}{2}\log\log x
, say, one can find that the dominant contribution to the expected value of (
1.4
) comes from points
t
where
X(t)=\frac{\gamma}{2}\log\log x+O(\sqrt{\log\log x})
. Note that
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
have shown that

\max_{t\in[0,1]}\log|F(1/2+it)|=\log\log x-\frac{3}{4}\log\log\log x+o(\log\log\log x),

with probability
1-o(1)
, as
x\rightarrow\infty
. Subsequently, when
\gamma=2
, the dominant contribution comes from the most extreme values, and the analysis of this case inherits the name critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos. Similar ideas suggest that for
\gamma>\gamma_{c}=2
we have convergence in probability to zero, since then we do not expect to find any points in
[0,1]
for which
X(t)>\frac{\gamma}{2}\log\log x
. Thus, in our case, one can think of a critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure as the limiting measure supported on
t
where
\log|F(1/2+it)|
takes its largest values. However, as we have mentioned, to obtain a non-trivial limiting distribution when
\gamma=2
, one needs to multiply (
1.4
) by
{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}
. This factor, which we initially motivated using the work of
[
15
]
, can also be motivated by the
derivative martingale
(see
[
9
]
, for example). Since it can be shown that

M(\gamma)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\exp\Bigl(\gamma\log|F(1/2+it)|-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl(\log|F(1/2+it)|^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dt

almost surely converges to a non-zero quantity for
\gamma<2
, and almost surely converges to zero for
\gamma\geq 2
, we have a (negative) jump in the limiting object at
\gamma=2
. Thus, one may expect that the negative of the derivative at
\gamma=2
may be non-trivial in the limit, leading one to the study of

-M^{\prime}(2)=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(2\mathbb{E}\bigl({X(t)}^{2}\bigr)-X(t)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(2X(t)-2\mathbb{E}\bigl({X(t)}^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dt,

where
X(t)=\log|F(1/2+it)|
. We previously saw that, without this pre-factor, the dominant contribution to the expected size of
M(2)
comes from points
t\in[0,1]
where
X(t)=\log\log x+O({(\log\log x)}^{1/2})
. Assuming these points still give the dominant contribution, and seeing as
2\mathbb{E}[{X(t)}^{2}]=\log\log x+O(1)
, the factor we have introduced increases the probable size of
M(2)
by a factor of
{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}
, suggesting that this is the correct renormalisation. For a survey of critical chaos, see
[
28
]
.

We finish this section by remarking that the recent work of
[
10
]
handles certain subcritical
(\gamma<2)
regimes, and so they do not need to renormalise their partial sums non-trivially to obtain a non-trivial distribution. See also
[
2
]
for subcritical results about random Euler products.

2.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
2
and structure of the paper

In this section, we describe the organisation of the paper and sketch the proof of Theorem
2
. Sections
3
and
4
will collect useful number theoretic and probabilistic tools, respectively. In Section
5
, we perform our conditional Gaussian approximation in the Steinhaus case, which follows from a result of
[
18
]
. As mentioned, this delivers a complex Gaussian with zero mean and variance

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\bigl|\sum_{m\leq x/p}f(m)\bigr|^{2}.

In Section
6
, we prove Theorem
2
, which involves performing numerous approximations to this variance. We outline the key ideas here, restricting ourselves to the Steinhaus case (though the theorem is also proved in the Rademacher case). We note that a similar analysis has previously been conducted by
[
16
, Section 3.2]
. First of all, an application of Perron’s formula allows us to find that our variance is roughly

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-x^{1/3}}^{x^{1/3}}\int_{-x^{1/3}}^{x^{1/3}}\frac{F(1/2+it_{1})\overline{F(1/2+it_{2})}}{(1/2+it_{1})(1/2-it_{2})}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{x^{i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2}.

The contribution to the above integral from points where
t_{1}\not\approx t_{2}
is small with high probability due to the decorrelation of the Euler products and the decay from the prime number sum. This allows us to reduce the above integral to points where
t_{1}=t_{2}+O\bigl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{5}}{\log x}\bigr)
. Over this reduced range, the contribution from the small primes to the Euler products
F(1/2+it_{1})
and
F(1/2+it_{2})
is roughly the same. Specifically, with
y=\lfloor 100\log\log\log x\rfloor
, we have
\frac{F_{y}(1/2+it_{1})}{1/2+it_{1}}\approx\frac{F_{y}(1/2+it_{2})}{1/2+it_{2}}
. Therefore, if we define
I_{y}(t)\coloneqq\prod_{\sqrt{x}^{e^{-y}}<p\leq\sqrt{x}}{\bigl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\bigr)}^{-1}
in the Steinhaus case, we find that, with high probability, our variance is roughly

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t_{1}-t_{2}|\leq\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{5}}{\log x}\\
|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|\leq{(\log\log x)}^{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{|F_{y}(1/2+it_{1})|^{2}I_{y}(t_{1})\overline{I_{y}(t_{2})}}{|(1/2+it_{1})|^{2}}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{x^{i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2},

where we have reduced the range of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
using the decay in the denominator. Getting to this stage is the contents of Propositions
6.1
and
6.2
.

Having obtained the integral of a mean square of an Euler product, we are closer to being able to apply Theorem
3
to deduce the distribution. However, our expression still involves the tails of the Euler products,
I_{y}(t_{1})
and
I_{y}(t_{2})
, which we wish to remove. The key observation that allows us to do this is to notice that, as
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
vary, the larger primes rotate quickly. This suggests that we should typically have
I_{y}(t_{1})\overline{I_{y}(t_{2})}\approx\mathbb{E}I_{y}(t_{1})\overline{I_{y}(t_{2})}
over our domain of integration, and an application of Chebyshev’s inequality allows us to show that this is the case. This is the content of Proposition
6.3
, and, for it to succeed, we make use of barrier events from
[
16
]
(see
[
17
, Section 3]
for an explanation of these). In this step, it is essential for
y
to be relatively small (certainly
o(\log\log x)
), seeing as the behaviour of
f(p)
on small primes
does
influence the distribution of our variance. After replacing
I_{y}(t_{1})\overline{I_{y}(t_{2})}
by
\mathbb{E}I_{y}(t_{1})\overline{I_{y}(t_{2})}
, we integrate over
t_{2}
and find that our variance concentrates around

\frac{e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{2\pi}\frac{{({\log\log B(x)})}^{1/2}}{\log B(x)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\frac{F_{y}(1/2+it_{1})}{1/2+it_{1}}\biggr|^{2}dt_{1},

with high probability, where
B(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{x}^{e^{-y}}
. This is the content of Proposition
6.4
. Combining the propositions in Section
6
delivers Theorem
2
.

In Section
7
, we prove Theorem
3
. As mentioned, this is an extension of the work of
[
30
, Theorem 1.9]
to the weighted integral over the real line. We outline the main ideas of their argument at the beginning of Section
7
. To extend their result, we employ a tightness argument for integrals over compact domains followed by an approximation lemma that gives convergence in distribution for the full integral over the real line.

In Section
8
, we use Proposition
5.1
and Theorems
2
and
3
to prove Theorem
1
. We also prove Corollaries
1
and
2
.

3.
Preliminary number theory results

In this section, we collect some standard number theoretic results that will be used throughout the paper. The first two estimates give mean and pointwise bounds for sums involving prime numbers, respectively:

Lemma 3.1

(Mean value over large primes)
.

Let
\Lambda(n)
denote the von Mangoldt function. Uniformly for any sequence of complex numbers
{(a_{n})}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and any
T\geq 1
, we have

\int_{-T}^{T}\biggl|\sum_{T^{1.01}\leq n\leq x}\frac{a_{n}\Lambda(n)}{n^{1+it}}\biggr|^{2}\,dt\ll\sum_{T^{1.01}\leq n\leq x}\frac{|a_{n}|^{2}\Lambda(n)}{n}.

Proof.

This is Number Theory Result 1 of
[
16
]
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Pointwise bound for sums over primes)
.

For any
100\leq x\leq y
,
t\neq 0
, we have

\biggl|\sum_{x<p\leq y}\frac{1}{p^{1+it}}\biggr|\leq\frac{3}{|t|\log x}+O\Bigl(\bigl(1+|t|\bigr)e^{-c\sqrt{\log x}}\Bigr),

for some positive constant
c>0
.

Proof.

This follows from Number Theory Result 2 of
[
16
]
.
∎

To prove Proposition
6.4
, we will utilise the following lemma:

Lemma 3.3

(Perron integral for rough numbers)
.

Suppose that
B(x)
is a function satisfying
\frac{\log x}{{(\log\log x)}^{1000}}\ll\log B(x)\ll\frac{\log x}{{(\log\log x)}^{2}}
, say. Let
W(x)
be a function satisfying
{(\log\log x)}^{2}\leq W(x)\leq{(\log x)}^{1/3}
when
x
is large. Then

\int_{|u|\leq\frac{W(x)}{\log x}}\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{x^{iu}}{p^{1+iu}}\,du=\frac{2\pi e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{\log B(x)}\biggl(1+O\biggl(\frac{1}{\log\log x}\biggr)\biggr),

where the product over
q
runs over primes, and
\gamma
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Furthermore, an identical estimate holds if

\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}

is replaced by

\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1+\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{q^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr)}.

Proof.

Assuming that the lemma holds with
\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\bigl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\bigr)}^{-1}
, the last statement follows from the elementary estimate

\displaystyle\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1+\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{q^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr)}=\biggl(1+O\biggl(\frac{1}{{B(x)}^{1/2}}\biggr)\biggr)\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}.

To prove the first statement, we notice that the integral in the lemma is approximately (the average of) a count of rough numbers: numbers whose prime factors are all larger than some threshold. To show this, we first introduce a weight
\frac{1}{1+iu}
. By our conditions on
W(x)
, we have
\frac{1}{1+iu}=1+O\bigl(\frac{1}{{(\log x)}^{2/3}}\bigr)
. Hence the left-hand side of the first equation in Lemma
3.3
is

\int_{|u|\leq\frac{W(x)}{\log x}}\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{x^{iu}}{p^{1+iu}}\frac{du}{1+iu}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{{(\log x)}^{4/3}}\exp\biggl(\sum_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{q}\biggr)\biggr).

The error term here is
\ll\frac{1}{{(\log x)}^{1/3}\log B(x)}
.
We now extend the range of integration by showing that the integral over
\frac{W(x)}{\log x}\leq|u|\leq{(\log x)}^{2}
is small. Seeing as we will break up the integral and apply the triangle inequality, we can obtain an upper bound by assuming that
W(x)={(\log\log x)}^{2}
. We split the integral over
|u|\in[\frac{W(x)}{\log x},{(\log x)}^{2}]
into
\ll\log\log x
dyadic ranges, and note that, for
M\in[\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{2}}{\log x},{(\log x)}^{2}]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{M}^{2M}\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{x^{iu}}{p^{1+iu}}\frac{du}{1+iu}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M\log x}\int_{M}^{2M}\biggl|\prod_{B(x)<q\leq x^{1/{(\log\log x)}^{2}}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}\prod_{x^{1/{(\log\log x)}^{2}}\leq q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}\biggr|\,du

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M\log x}\int_{M}^{2M}\frac{\log x}{{(\log\log x)}^{2}\log B(x)}\exp\biggl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{2}}{M\log x}\biggr)\,du\ll\frac{1}{{(\log\log x)}^{2}\log B(x)},

where to obtain the second and third lines we have applied Lemma
3.2
in addition to standard prime number estimates. An identical bound holds over the negative range, so that adding up the dyadic blocks, the contribution from
\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{2}}{\log x}\leq|u|\leq{(\log x)}^{2}
is
\ll\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}
. Therefore, the integral in our lemma is equal to

\int_{|u|\leq{(\log x)}^{2}}\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{x^{iu}}{p^{1+iu}}\frac{du}{1+iu}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}\biggr).

Rearranging, this is

\frac{2\pi}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{1-i{(\log x)}^{2}}^{1+i{(\log x)}^{2}}\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{s}}\biggr)}^{-1}{\biggl(\frac{x}{p}\biggr)}^{s}\frac{ds}{s}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}\biggr).

By
[
27
, Theorem 5.2 and Corollary 5.3]
, we find that, for
\sqrt{x}<p\leq x
, we have

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{1-i{(\log x)}^{2}}^{1+i{(\log x)}^{2}}\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{s}}\biggr)}^{-1}{\biggl(\frac{x}{p}\biggr)}^{s}\frac{ds}{s}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x/p\\
q|n\Rightarrow q>B(x)\end{subarray}}1\\
+O\biggl(1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x/2p<n\leq 2x/p\\
n\neq x/p\end{subarray}}\min\biggl\{1,\frac{x}{p{(\log x)}^{2}|x/p-n|}\biggr\}+\frac{x}{p{(\log x)}^{2}}\sum_{n\colon p|n\Rightarrow p\in(B(x),\sqrt{x})}\frac{1}{n}\biggr).

Using the fact that
\frac{\log x}{{(\log\log x)}^{1000}}\ll\log B(x)
, this error term is

\displaystyle\ll 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|n-x/p|\leq x/p{(\log x)}^{2}\\
n\neq x/p\end{subarray}}1+\frac{x}{p{(\log x)}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|n-x/p|>x/p{(\log x)}^{2}\\
x/2p<n\leq 2x/p\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|x/p-n|}+\frac{x{(\log\log x)}^{1000}}{p{(\log x)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll 1+\frac{x{(\log\log x)}^{1000}}{p{(\log x)}^{2}}.

Summing this over
\sqrt{x}<p\leq x
and dividing through by
x
, this error contributes
\ll\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}
. We deduce that the integral on the left-hand side of Lemma
3.3
is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x/p\\
q|n\Rightarrow q>B(x)\end{subarray}}1+O\biggl(\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\Phi\biggl(\frac{x}{p},B(x)\biggr)+O\biggl(\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}\biggr),

where
\Phi(x,y)\coloneqq\big|\bigl\{n\leq x\colon p|n\Rightarrow p>y\bigr\}\bigr|
is the count of
y
-rough numbers below
x
. We have the standard sieve upper bound (see
[
32
, Corollary I.4.13]
)
\Phi(x,y)\ll\frac{x}{\log y}
whenever
y\leq x
. Applying this bound, the contribution to the main term from large primes
\frac{x}{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}<p\leq x
is

\frac{2\pi}{x}\biggl(\sum_{\frac{x}{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}<p\leq\frac{x}{B(x)}}\Phi\biggl(\frac{x}{p},B(x)\biggr)+\sum_{\frac{x}{B(x)}<p\leq x}1\biggr)\\
\ll\frac{1}{\log B(x)}\sum_{\frac{x}{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}<p\leq\frac{x}{B(x)}}\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{\log x}\ll\frac{\log\log x}{\log x},

which is
\ll\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}
, and so is engulfed into the previous error term. Thus our integral is equal to

(3.1)

\frac{2\pi}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq\frac{x}{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}}\Phi\biggl(\frac{x}{p},B(x)\biggr)+O\biggl(\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}\biggr).

It follows from
[
32
, Theorem III.6.1]
and Mertens’ third theorem that the first term is

\frac{2\pi e^{-\gamma}}{\log B(x)}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq\frac{x}{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}}\frac{1}{p}+O\Biggl(\frac{1}{{\bigl(\log B(x)\bigr)}^{2}}+\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq\frac{x}{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}}\Psi(x/p,B(x))\Biggr),

where
\Psi(x,y)\coloneqq\#\{n\leq x\colon P(n)\leq y\}
is the count of y-smooth numbers below
x
. With
u=\log x/\log y
, we have the classical bound
\Psi(x,y)\ll xe^{-u/2}
(see
[
32
, Theorem III.5.1]
). Applying this bound and Mertens’ second theorem, (
3.1
) is

\frac{2\pi e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{\log B(x)}\biggl(1+O\biggl(\frac{1}{\log\log x}\biggr)\biggr)+O\Biggl(\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq\frac{x}{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}}\frac{1}{p}e^{-\log(x/p)/2\log B(x)}\Biggr).

It also follows from Mertens’ second theorem that the second “big Oh” term is

\ll\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq\frac{x}{{{B(x)}^{2\log\log x}}}}\frac{1}{p\log x}\ll\frac{1}{\log x}\ll\frac{1}{(\log\log x)\log B(x)}.

We conclude that

\int_{|u|\leq\frac{W(x)}{\log x}}\prod_{B(x)<q\leq\sqrt{x}}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{q^{1+iu}}\biggr)}^{-1}\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leq x}\frac{x^{iu}}{p^{1+iu}}\,du=\frac{2\pi e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{\log B(x)}\biggl(1+O\biggl(\frac{1}{\log\log x}\biggr)\biggr),

as required.
∎

4.
Preliminary results on random multiplicative functions

4.1.
Expectation results

We begin this section by collecting some standard results on random multiplicative functions. We also introduce a new result, Lemma
4.4
, which we use to prove one of our key results: Proposition
6.3
.

Lemma 4.1

(Expectation of sums)
.

Let
f
be a Rademacher or Steinhaus random multiplicative function. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Bigl|\sum_{n\leq N}a_{n}f(n)\Bigr|^{2k}\leq{\biggl(\sum_{n\leq N}\tau_{2k-1}(n)|a_{n}|^{2}\biggr)}^{k},

where
\tau_{k}
denotes the
k
-divisor function,
\tau_{k}(n)=\#\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})\colon b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{k}=n,\,b_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Proof.

This is a case of Probability Result 2.3 of
[
14
]
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(Steinhaus Euler product expectation)
.

If
f
is a Steinhaus random multiplicative function, then for any real
300\leq x\leq y
, say, and any
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\biggr|^{-1}\biggl|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it^{\prime}}}\biggr|^{-1}\\
\ll\sqrt{\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggl(1+\min\biggl\{\frac{\log y}{\log x},\frac{1}{|t-t^{\prime}|\log x}+\frac{\log^{2}\bigl(2+|t-t^{\prime}|\bigr)}{\log x}\biggr\}\biggr)},

and

\mathbb{E}\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\biggr|^{-2}\biggl|1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it^{\prime}}}\biggr|^{-2}\\
\ll{\Biggl(\min\biggl\{\frac{\log y}{\log x},1+\frac{{\bigl(|t-t^{\prime}|\bigr)}^{1/100}}{\log x}\biggr\}\Biggr)}^{4}{\biggl(\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggr)}^{2}{\biggl(1+\min\biggl\{\frac{\log y}{\log x},\frac{1}{|t-t^{\prime}|\log x}\biggr\}\biggr)}^{2}.

Proof.

This is Euler Product Result 1 of
[
16
]
followed by a special case of Euler Product Result 2.1 of
[
14
]
, where we replaced the term
e^{O(1+|t-t^{\prime}|/{(\log x)}^{100})}
there by the first term on the right-hand side. This can be done by the same considerations as in Euler Product Result 2.2 of
[
14
]
.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Rademacher Euler product expectation)
.

If
f
is a Rademacher random multiplicative function, then for any real
300\leq x\leq y
, say, and any
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl|1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\biggr|\biggl|1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it^{\prime}}}\biggr|\\
\ll\sqrt{\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggl(1+\min\biggl\{\frac{\log y}{\log x},\frac{1}{|t-t^{\prime}|\log x}+\frac{1}{|t+t^{\prime}|\log x}+\frac{\log^{2}\bigl(2+|t|+|t^{\prime}|\bigr)}{\log x}\biggr\}\biggr)},

and

\mathbb{E}\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl|1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\biggr|^{2}\biggl|1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it^{\prime}}}\biggr|^{2}\ll{\Biggl(\min\biggl\{\frac{\log y}{\log x},1+\frac{{\bigl(|t|+|t^{\prime}|\bigr)}^{1/100}}{\log x}\biggr\}\Biggr)}^{4}\\
\times{\biggl(\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggr)}^{2}{\biggl(1+\min\biggl\{\frac{\log y}{\log x},\frac{1}{|t+t^{\prime}|\log x}\biggr\}\biggr)}^{2}{\biggl(1+\min\biggl\{\frac{\log y}{\log x},\frac{1}{|t-t^{\prime}|\log x}\biggr\}\biggr)}^{2}.

Proof.

This is Euler Product Result 2 of
[
16
]
followed by a special case of Euler Product Result 2.2 of
[
14
]
.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Splitting of random Euler products on large primes)
.

For any real
200\leq x\leq y
, let
I(t)\coloneqq\prod_{x<p\leq y}{\bigl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\bigr)}^{-1}
in the Steinhaus case and
I(t)\coloneqq\prod_{x<p\leq y}\bigl(1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\bigr)
in Rademacher case. In the Steinhaus case, we have

\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})=\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})\\
+O\Biggl({\biggl(\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggr)}^{2}\biggl|\exp\biggl(\sum_{x<p\leq y}\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\biggr)-1\biggr|\Biggr),

and in the Rademacher case, we have

\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})=\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})\\
+O\Biggl({\biggl(\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggr)}^{2}\biggl|\exp\biggl(\sum_{x<p\leq y}\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}+t_{4})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1-i(t_{2}+t_{3})}}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\biggr)-1\biggr|\Biggr),

Furthermore, if
\frac{1}{\log x}\leq|t_{1}-t_{3}|,|t_{2}-t_{4}|,|t_{1}+t_{4}|,|t_{2}+t_{3}|\leq{(\log x)}^{1000}
, then we have

\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})=\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})\\
+O\Biggl({\biggl(\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggr)}^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\log x}\biggl(\frac{1}{|t_{1}-t_{3}|}+\frac{1}{|t_{2}-t_{4}|}+\frac{1}{|t_{1}+t_{4}|}+\frac{1}{|t_{2}+t_{3}|}\biggr)\biggr)\Biggr),

for both Steinhaus and Rademacher random multiplicative functions
f
.

Proof.

The last statement follows from the first two statements by an application of Lemma
3.2
and the fact that
\sum_{x<p\leq y}\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}\log x}
, which is negligible compared to the other terms given our conditions. We then apply the fact that for bounded
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have
|\exp(z)-1|\ll|z|
.

For the first two statements, we begin by calculating the first terms on the right-hand sides,
\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})
. For any
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, in the Steinhaus case, we have

\mathbb{E}I(t)\overline{I(t^{\prime})}=\mathbb{E}\prod_{x<p\leq y}{\biggl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}-\frac{\overline{f(p)}}{p^{1/2-it^{\prime}}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t-t^{\prime})}}\biggr)}^{-1}\\
=\mathbb{E}\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}+\frac{\overline{f(p)}}{p^{1/2-it^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t-t^{\prime})}}+{\biggl(\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}+\frac{\overline{f(p)}}{p^{1/2-it^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t-t^{\prime})}}\biggr)}^{2}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr),

by expanding out the geometric series. Bringing the expectation inside the product, we find

\mathbb{E}I(t)\overline{I(t^{\prime})}=\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t-t^{\prime})}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr).

In the Rademacher case, we obtain
\mathbb{E}I(t)\overline{I(t^{\prime})}=\prod_{x<p\leq y}\bigl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t-t^{\prime})}}\bigr)
by similar techniques. Therefore, for
t_{1},t_{2},t_{4},t_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
, in the Steinhaus case, we have

\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})=\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{3})}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr),

which we note is equal to

\prod_{x<p\leq y}{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}-\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{3})}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr)}^{-1}.

Writing it in this way will aid with the calculations that follow. Note that the same result holds in the Rademacher case. We now calculate the left-hand side,
\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})
, for any
t_{1},t_{2},t_{4},t_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
. In the Steinhaus case, this is

\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})=\mathbb{E}\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it_{1}}}-\frac{\overline{f(p)}}{p^{1/2-it_{2}}}-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it_{4}}}-\frac{\overline{f(p)}}{p^{1/2-it_{3}}}\\
+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{3})}}+\frac{{f(p)}^{2}}{p^{1+i(t_{1}+t_{4})}}+\frac{\overline{f(p)}^{2}}{p^{1-i(t_{2}+t_{3})}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr)^{-1}.

Expanding as a geometric series and bringing the expectation inside the product,

\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})
is equal to

\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{3})}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\biggr)\biggr).

In the Rademacher case, one finds that
\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})
is equal to

\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}+t_{4})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1-i(t_{2}+t_{3})}}+O\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\biggr)\biggr).

We now proceed by calculating
\bigl|\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})-\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})\bigr|
. Seeing as the latter term is nonzero, and by an application of Cauchy–Schwarz, we can write this difference as

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})\bigr|\biggl|\frac{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}-1\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\Bigl(\mathbb{E}|I(t_{1})|^{2}\mathbb{E}|I(t_{2})|^{2}\mathbb{E}|I(t_{4})|^{2}\mathbb{E}|I(t_{3})|^{2}\Bigr)}^{1/2}\biggl|\frac{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}-1\biggr|,

hence

(4.1)

\bigl|\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})-\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})\bigr|\ll{\biggl(\frac{\log y}{\log x}\biggr)}^{2}\biggl|\frac{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}-1\biggr|.

where we have applied Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
to evaluate the mean squares of the Euler products. Making use of our previous calculations, we find that, in the Steinhaus case,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}=

\displaystyle\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{3})}}

\displaystyle+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\biggr)\biggl(1-\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{2})}}-\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{3})}}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\biggl).

In the Rademacher case, one instead finds that

\frac{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}{\mathbb{E}I(t_{1})\overline{I(t_{2})}\mathbb{E}\overline{I(t_{3})}I(t_{4})}=\prod_{x<p\leq y}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}-t_{3})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{4}-t_{2})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1+i(t_{1}+t_{4})}}+\frac{1}{p^{1-i(t_{2}+t_{3})}}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\Bigr)\biggl).

We can then write these products as the exponential of a sum of logarithms. Taylor expanding these logarithms and combining with (
4.1
) gives the desired result.
∎

4.2.
Multiplicative Chaos Results

Recall that
F_{j}(s)=\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{x}^{e^{-j}}}{\bigl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\bigr)}^{-1}

F_{j}(s)=\prod_{p\leq\sqrt{x}^{e^{-j}}}{\bigl(1+\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\bigr)}
in the Rademacher case. We write

in place of

[

for a more in-depth explanation.

To state the first result, we define the net of points

for

. We then have the following result of
[

]

Lemma 4.5

(Expectation with barrier events)
.

Let

be a Steinhaus or a Rademacher random multiplicative function. Suppose that

denote the event that

\displaystyle|F_{j}(1/2+it)|\leq\frac{\log x}{e^{j}{(\log\log x)}^{1000}}\,\,\forall\,y\leq j\leq 0.99\log\log x,\text{and }

\mathbb{E}|F_{y}(1/2+it)|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}(t)}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}(t)\text{ fails}}\ll\frac{\log x}{e^{y}}\frac{{(\log\log\log x)}^{7}}{\log\log x}.

Furthermore, the probability that
\mathcal{D}(t)
holds for all

is

Proof.

The first part of this statement follows from Multiplicative Chaos Result 2 of
[

to those that appear in the statement there. Note that, in order to apply the result as stated there to the shorter Euler product

\displaystyle\sum_{y\leq j\leq\log\log x-\log 2-1}\biggl(\frac{(\log x){(\log\log x)}^{5}}{e^{j}}\biggr)\frac{e^{j}}{(\log x){(\log\log x)}^{10}}\ll\frac{1}{{(\log\log x)}^{4}}.

To obtain the second line, we have used the fact that the number of

∎

In this section, we will prove that our partial sums follow a conditional Gaussian distribution in the Steinhaus case. Note that an analogous statement (with a real Gaussian) holds in the Rademacher case, and is simpler to prove, seeing as one can apply the Berry–Esseen theorem.

.

, thus showing that our variances behave like mean squares of Euler products with high probability. Specifically, we will prove the following two propositions:

Proposition 6.3
.

We have

|\mathcal{V}_{3}(x)-\mathcal{V}_{4}(x)|\ll\frac{1}{{(\log\log x)}^{1/5}},

with probability
1-O\bigl({(\log\log x)}^{-1/5}\bigr)
, in both the Steinhaus and Rademacher cases.

Proposition 6.4
.

We have

|\mathcal{V}_{4}(x)-\mathcal{V}_{5}(x)|\ll\frac{1}{{(\log\log x)}^{1/5}},

with probability
1-O\bigl({(\log\log x)}^{-1/4}\bigr)
, in both the Steinhaus and Rademacher cases.

Remark 6.5
.

One could obtain a similar result where the range of integration in
\mathcal{V}_{5}(x)
is replaced by
[-W(x),W(x)]
, where

Proof of Proposition

This proof is quite involved and constitutes the main innovation of the paper. Beginning with
\mathcal{V}_{3}(x)

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|\leq{(\log\log x)}^{4}\\

, allowing us to insert the barrier that appears in Lemma
4.5

[

. The expected size of the part of the integral where

\mathcal{V}_{3}(x)
is equal to

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|\leq{(\log\log x)}^{4}\\

\ll\frac{1}{{(\log\log x)}^{10}}

.
From Lemma
4.5
, we know that the event

\mathcal{V}_{3}(x)

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t_{1}|,|t_{2}|\leq{(\log\log x)}^{4}\\

\displaystyle\ll

\ll\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{160}}{\log x}\int_{u\geq{(\log x)}^{-1/4}}\frac{du}{u^{3}}\int_{v\geq{(\log x)}^{-1/4}}\frac{dv}{v^{2}}\ll\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{160}}{{(\log x)}^{1/4}}.

Finally, the contribution to (

is

\ll\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{160}}{\log x}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t_{1}|,|t_{3}|\leq{(\log\log x)}^{4}\\
|t_{1}-t_{3}|\geq{(\log x)}^{-1/4}\\
|t_{1}+t_{3}|>1\end{subarray}}{\biggl(1+\frac{1}{|t_{1}-t_{3}|}\biggr)}^{3}\,dt_{1}\,dt_{3},

similar arguments show that this is

. Putting this all together, we have shown that (

∎

.

We begin with

7.
Distribution of the variance: proof of Theorem
3

In this section, we prove Theorem
3
. Throughout this section, we will use the notation
F_{(x)}=\prod_{p\leq x}{\bigl(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\bigr)}^{-1}
. Theorem
3
will be a consequence of
[
30
, Theorem 1.9]
, which we quote below with our notation.

Proposition 7.1

(Theorem 1.9 of
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\pi(x)=\#\{p\leq x\}
. Fix any nonnegative continuous function
h\colon[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
. As
x\rightarrow\infty
, we have

{\bigl(\log\log\pi(x)\bigr)}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\frac{|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}}\,dt\xrightarrow{d}\int_{0}^{1}h(t)g(t)\lambda(dt),

where
g
is a positive random continuous function such that the norms
\|g\|_{C^{l}[0,1]}
and
\|1/g\|_{C^{l}[0,1]}
possess moments of all orders, and
\lambda(dt)
is a critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure.

The function
\pi(x)
appears because their Euler product is taken over the first
x
primes. It can easily be replaced by
x
seeing as the iterated logarithm grows slowly.

7.1.
The work of Saksman and Webb and the Rademacher case

Before proving Theorem
3
, we begin with a brief overview of the proof of
[
30
, Theorem 1.9]
, followed by the difficulties we encounter when modifying the proof to the Rademacher case. We adapt the notation so that it agrees with Theorem
3
, and sketch their proof over the compact domain
[-n,n]
. Beginning with

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{-n}^{n}\frac{e^{2\Re\log F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}}{|1/2+it|^{2}}\,dt,

the goal is to approximate
\Re\log F_{(x)}(1/2+it)
in the exponent by a Gaussian field for which convergence of the integral is known. If the Gaussian field approximates
2\Re\log F_{(x)}(1/2+it)
well, then the (random) error of the approximation should possess nice properties, and will not affect the distributional convergence. This error is captured by the function
g
in our results.
[
30
]
perform many approximation steps to obtain a Gaussian field for which convergence is known. The first step is to apply Taylor expansion and obtain

(7.1)

\exp(2\Re\log F_{(x)}(1/2+it))=\exp\biggl(2\Re\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}+\text{ nice random error term}\biggr),

where the error contributes to the function
g
. They then replace the
f(p)
’s by complex Gaussian random variables,
W_{p}
(defined on the same probability space). The error induced is non-trivial seeing as the individual
f(p)

are not
Gaussian, but they show that the error remains under control (see
[
30
, Theorem 1.7]
). They then work to replace the Gaussian random variables by integrals with respect to Brownian motion, and ultimately end up with an approximation to the original field
2\Re\log F_{(x)}(1/2+it)
which is approximately

G_{x}(t)=2\Re\int_{1}^{\log x}\sqrt{2\widehat{C}(s)}e^{-2\pi its}\,dB_{s}^{\mathbb{C}},

where
C(x)=\max\{-\log|x|,0\}
,
\widehat{C}
denotes its Fourier transform, and
dB_{s}^{\mathbb{C}}
is a standard complex Brownian motion defined on the same probability space as
{(f(p))}_{p\text{ prime}}
. Note that the function
(\widehat{C}(s))^{1/2}
directly corresponds to
\frac{1}{p^{1/2}}
in (
7.1
),
e^{-2\pi its}
corresponds to
p^{-it}
, and the complex Brownian motion to our random variables
f(p)
. We can then write our original integral as

(7.2)

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{-n}^{n}\frac{e^{2\Re\log F_{(x)}(1/2+it)-G_{x}(t)}}{|1/2+it|^{2}}e^{G_{x}(t)}\,dt.

One can then think of
g
as the function
g(t)=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}e^{2\Re\log F_{(x)}(1/2+it)-G_{x}(t)}
which is almost surely defined pointwise. Such a definition only makes sense if one has this underlying probability space, so we cannot give an explicit definition in Theorems
1
,
3
, and Proposition
7.2
. The proof is completed by noting that the covariances of
G_{x}
are
\mathbb{E}G_{x}(t_{1})G_{x}(t_{2})=\max\{-2\log|t_{1}-t_{2}|,0\}+o(1)
for any fixed
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, and convergence of (
7.2
) follows by a covariance comparison result of
[
22
, Theorem 1]
, utilising the fact that convergence is known for other Gaussian fields with the same covariance structure.

The Rademacher case presents some difficulties that we do not overcome here. By symmetry of the integral, we need to understand

(7.3)

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{0}^{n}\frac{e^{2\Re\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}+E_{x}(t)}}{|\zeta(1+2it)||1/2+it|^{2}}\,dt,

where
E_{x}(t)
is a nice random error function and
{(f(p))}_{p\text{ prime}}
are independent Rademacher random variables. The appearance of the zeta function comes from higher order terms in the Taylor expansion of
\Re\log F(1/2+it)
(for example, the second order term is
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p^{1+2it}}
), noting that the Euler product formula for
\zeta(1+2it)
converges conditionally when
t\neq 0
(see
[
33
, Section 3.15.1]
). One can try and proceed as in the Steinhaus case, beginning with
2\Re\bigl(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p^{1/2+it}}\bigr)
and performing a Gaussian field approximation. By identical techniques to the Steinhaus case, one can obtain the analogous approximation

G^{\mathrm{(Rad)}}_{x}=2\Re\int_{0}^{\log x}\sqrt{2\widehat{C}(s)}e^{-2\pi its}\,dB_{s},

where
B_{s}
is a standard real Brownian motion. Note that since the Rademacher random multiplicative function takes only real values, the Brownian motion does too. This is different to the Steinhaus case where we have a standard complex Brownian motion, and a consequence is that we have covariances
\mathbb{E}G^{\mathrm{(Rad)}}_{x}(t_{1})G^{\mathrm{(Rad)}}_{x}(t_{2})=\max\{-2\log|t_{1}-t_{2}|,0\}+\max\{-2\log|t_{1}+t_{2}|,0\}+o(1)
. It is unclear how to obtain convergence of the integrals when the covariances have this additional factor involving
\log|t_{1}+t_{2}|
.

7.2.
Proof of Theorem
3

We begin the proof with a slight generalisation of
[
30
, Theorem 1.9]
.

Proposition 7.2

(Adaptation of Theorem 1.9 of
[
30
]
)
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and take any nonnegative continuous function
h\colon[-n,n]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For
f
a Steinhaus random multiplicative function, as
x\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{-n}^{n}h(t)\biggl|\frac{F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt\xrightarrow{d}\int_{-n}^{n}\frac{h(t)g(t)}{|1/2+it|^{2}}\lambda(dt),

where
\lambda(dt)
is a critical multiplicative chaos measure and
g(t)
is a positive random continuous function such that
\|g\|_{C^{l}[-n,n]}
and
\|1/g\|_{C^{l}[-n,n]}
possess moments of all orders. The random function
g(t)
and the random measure
\lambda(dt)
are independent of

.

Remark 7.3
.

Proposition
7.2
tells us that for each
n
,
\|g\|_{C^{l}[-n,n]}
and
\|1/g\|_{C^{l}[-n,n]}
possess moments of all orders, but does not give dependence on
n
. It can further be shown that, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\|\frac{g(t)}{|1/2+it|^{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
possesses moments of all orders, but the calculations are omitted for brevity.

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

We will describe the necessary adaptations to
[
30
, Theorem 1.9]
. First of all, their result gives convergence of integrals over
[0,1]
, but their proof readily adapts to arbitrary compact intervals, say
[-n,n]
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, as
x\rightarrow\infty
, we have

{\bigl(\log\log\pi(x)\bigr)}^{1/2}\int_{-n}^{n}h(t)\frac{|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}}}\,dt\xrightarrow{d}\int_{-n}^{n}h(t)g(t)\lambda(dt),

for any nonnegative continuous function
h\colon[-n,n]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
g
has the properties stated in the proposition. Looking at the proof of
[
30
, Theorem 1.9]
, we see that
g
and
\lambda
must be independent of
n
, seeing as increasing
n
just extends them to a larger domain. We wish to replace
\mathbb{E}|F_{x}(1/2+it)|^{2}
in the denominator by
\log x
. It follows from Euler Product Result 1 of
[
14
]
and Mertens’ second theorem that
\mathbb{E}|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}=K\log x(1+O(1/\log x))
, for some fixed constant
K>0
. The factor of
K
can be engulfed by the function
g(t)
in Proposition
7.2
. We have

\frac{{\bigl(\log\log\pi(x)\bigr)}^{1/2}}{\log x(1+O(1/\log x))}=\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}+O\biggl(\frac{\log\log x}{{(\log x)}^{2}}\biggr),

and an application of Markov’s inequality shows that the contribution from the error term converges in probability to zero, so
has no impact on convergence in distribution. Relabelling
h\rightarrow\frac{1}{|1/2+it|^{2}}h
gives Proposition
7.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Taking
h(t)=1
in Proposition
7.2
, we know that, for any
n>0
,

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{-n}^{n}\biggl|\frac{F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt\xrightarrow{d}\int_{-n}^{n}\frac{g(t)}{|1/2+it|^{2}}\lambda(dt).

Convergence of the full integral follows in two main steps. First, we will prove that the sequence

V_{n}\coloneqq\int_{-n}^{n}\frac{g(t)}{|1/2+it|^{2}}\lambda(dt),

converges in distribution, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and that the limiting distribution satisfies the desired moment bounds. We will then show convergence in distribution of the full integral to this distribution using an approximation lemma. Beginning with the first step, we note that the collection of random variables

{\Biggl\{{\biggl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{-n}^{n}\biggl|\frac{F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt\biggr)}^{q}\Biggr\}}_{x\geq 10,\,n\in\mathbb{N}},

is uniformly integrable for any fixed
0<q<1
, which follows from Lemma
4.6
. Seeing as we have convergence in distribution for fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows from uniform integrability (see, for example,
[
11
, Theorem 5.5.9]
) that

\mathbb{E}{\biggl(\int_{-n}^{n}\frac{g(t)}{|1/2+it|^{2}}\lambda(dt)\biggr)}^{q}\leq C,

for some fixed constant
C>0
, depending only on
q
, and in particular it is uniformly in
n
. Subsequently, the sequence of random variables
{(V_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight, and by Prokhorov’s theorem has a subsequence that converges in distribution, say
V_{n_{j}}\xrightarrow{d}V_{\mathrm{crit}}
. By monotonicity of
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq t)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(V_{n_{j+1}}\leq t)\leq\mathbb{P}(V_{m}\leq t)\leq\mathbb{P}(V_{n_{j}}\leq t),

whener
n_{j}<m\leq n_{j+1}
. Taking
j\rightarrow\infty
, it follows from the sandwich rule that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(V_{\mathrm{crit}}\leq t\bigr)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(V_{n}\leq t\bigr),

at every continuity point
t
of
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
. By our uniform moment bound, it also follows (for example, by
[
11
, Theorem 5.5.9]
) that
\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathrm{crit}}^{q}]\ll_{q}1
, for all
0<q<1
.

We now perform the second step, showing convergence in distribution of the full integral to
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
. This will be a consequence of the following elementary lemma:

Lemma 7.4

(Approximation result)
.

Suppose that
{(Y_{x})}_{x\geq 0}
is a sequence of real-valued random variables and that we can decompose
Y_{x}=X_{x,n}+E_{x,n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that the following assumptions hold:

(1)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
X_{x,n}\xrightarrow{d}Z_{n}
as
x\rightarrow\infty
, for some random variables
{(Z_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

(2)

Z_{n}\xrightarrow{d}Z
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, for some random variable
Z
.

(3)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{x\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(|E_{x,n}|>\varepsilon)=0
.

Then
Y_{x}\xrightarrow{d}Z
as
x\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof of Lemma
7.4
.

This follows from
[
24
, Theorem 4.2.8]
. We have a slightly weaker third condition which follows immediately upon inspecting the proof there.
∎

In Lemma
7.4
, we take
Y_{x}
to be the full integral

Y_{x}=\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\frac{F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt,

and we write
Y_{x}=X_{x,n}+E_{x,n}
, where

X_{x,n}=\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{-n}^{n}\biggl|\frac{F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt,

and

E_{x,n}=\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{|t|>n}\biggl|\frac{F_{(x)}(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\biggr|^{2}\,dt.

We have seen that
X_{x,n}\xrightarrow{d}V_{n}
as
x\rightarrow\infty
and
V_{n}\xrightarrow{d}V_{\mathrm{crit}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This establishes the first two conditions of Lemma
7.4
. For the last condition, note (similarly to the end of
[
15
, Section 2.4]
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[{|E_{x,n}|}^{2/3}\bigr]

\displaystyle\ll{\Biggl(\sum_{|m|>n-1}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{m-1/2}^{m+1/2}|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}\,dt\Biggr)}^{2/3}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{|m|>n-1}\frac{1}{m^{4/3}}\mathbb{E}{\biggl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{m-1/2}^{m+1/2}|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}\,dt\biggr)}^{2/3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|m|>n-1}\frac{1}{m^{4/3}}\mathbb{E}{\biggl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/2}}{\log x}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|F_{(x)}(1/2+it)|^{2}\,dt\biggr)}^{2/3},

where the last line follows by translation invariance in law. As noted in the proof of Lemma
4.6
, it follows from the main result proved in the section ‘Proof of the upper bound in Theorem 1, assuming Key Propositions 1 and 2’ of
[
15
, Section 4.1]
that the above expectation is bounded uniformly in

. Hence, performing the sum over

, we find that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[{|E_{x,n}|}^{2/3}\bigr]\ll n^{-1/3},

uniformly in

. Applying Markov’s inequality gives

uniformly in

, and the third condition of Lemma
7.4
follows immediately. Since all condition of the lemma are satisfied, we have

as

. This completes the proof of Theorem

.
∎

11
, Theorem 5.10.4]
, we have

\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\Bigl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}f(n)\Bigr|\xrightarrow{d}\sqrt{CV_{\mathrm{crit}}}|Z|,

as
x\rightarrow\infty
, where
C
and
V_{\mathrm{crit}}
are as in Theorem
1
and are independent of
Z\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
. Applying
[
11
, Theorem 5.5.9]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}{\biggl(\frac{{(\log\log x)}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{x}}\Bigl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}f(n)\Bigr|\biggr)}^{q}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}{\Bigl[\sqrt{CV_{\mathrm{crit}}}|Z|\Bigr]}^{q},

\displaystyle=C^{\frac{q}{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[V_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\frac{q}{2}}\bigr]\mathbb{E}{|Z|}^{q},

where the last line follows from independence. It is a standard result that
\mathbb{E}|Z|^{q}=\Gamma(\frac{q}{2}+1)
, where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. This completes the proof of Corollary
1
.
∎

We now prove Corollary
2
. To prove the upper bound, we will make use of the following tail estimate for products of independent random variables (also found in
[
36
, Lemma 2.12]
).

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
U
and
V
be nonnegative independent random variables. Suppose that there exists
C>0
and
q>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(U>t)\leq\frac{C}{t^{q}},

for all
t>0
, and

\mathbb{E}[V^{p}]<\infty

for some
p>q
. Then

\mathbb{P}(UV>t)\leq\frac{C\mathbb{E}[V^{q}]}{t^{q}},

for all
t>0
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{U}
denote the expectation over
U
and
\mathbb{E}_{V}
the expectation over
V
. We have

\mathbb{P}(UV>t)=\mathbb{E}_{V}\mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathbf{1}_{UV>t}]=\mathbb{E}_{V}\mathbb{P}\bigl(U>t/V\bigr)\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\biggl[\frac{CV^{q}}{t^{q}}\biggr]=\frac{C\mathbb{E}[V^{q}]}{t^{q}},

where the inequality follows from the first assumption of the lemma.
∎

Proof of the upper bound in Corollary
2
.

Fix any small
0<\varepsilon<1
. By Theorem
1
, we need to show that
\mathbb{P}\bigl(|XY|>y\bigr)\ll_{\varepsilon}1/y^{2-\varepsilon}
, where

X\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1),\quad Y\sim\sqrt{CV_{\mathrm{crit}}},

where
C=\frac{e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{2\pi}
. From Theorem
3
we know that
\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y^{2-\varepsilon}\bigr]\ll_{\varepsilon}1
, and it follows from Markov’s inequality that

\mathbb{P}(Y>y)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{y^{2-\varepsilon}}.

Since
X
and
Y
are independent, it follows from Lemma
8.1
that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|XY|>y\bigr)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X|^{2-\varepsilon}\bigr]}{y^{2-\varepsilon}}.

Seeing as
\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X|^{2-\varepsilon}\bigr]
is uniformly bounded for all
0<\varepsilon<1
, we obtain the desired bound.
∎

Proof of the lower bound in Corollary
2
.

To prove the lower bound result, we will make use of the following tail estimate for critical chaos measures:

Theorem 4

(Wong
[
36
]
)
.

For any open set
A\subseteq[-2,2]
, say, such that
\mathrm{Leb}(\partial A)=0
, and any nonnegative continuous function
f
on
A
, we have

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\int_{A}f(t)\lambda(dt)>y\biggr)=\frac{1}{y\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{A}f(t)\,dt+o(y^{-1}).

This theorem holds more generally for
A
inside bounded domains. Note that it applies to the specific critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure
\lambda(dt)
appearing in our work. Alternatively, one could use a similar result to Lemma
8.1
for the lower bound (see
[
36
, Lemma 2.12]
for such a result).

Fix any
\varepsilon>0
. We wish to show that
\mathbb{P}\bigl(|XY|>y\bigr)\gg_{\varepsilon}1/y^{2+\varepsilon}
, where

X\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1),\quad Y\sim\sqrt{CV_{\mathrm{crit}}},

are independent random variables given in Theorem
1
, and
C=\frac{e^{-\gamma}\log 2}{2\pi}
. By independence and monotonicity of
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\geq t)
(which are increasing in
n
with limit
\mathbb{P}(V_{\mathrm{crit}}\geq t)
) in Theorem
3
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|XY|>y\bigr)\geq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sqrt{C}|X|>1\bigr)\mathbb{P}\bigl(V_{\mathrm{crit}}>y^{2}\bigr)\gg\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{g(t)}{|1/2+it|^{2}}\lambda(dt)>y^{2}\Biggr).

So it suffices to lower bound the probability

\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\int_{0}^{1}g(t)\lambda(dt)>4y^{2}\Biggr).

We recall the fact that
\|1/g\|_{L^{\infty}[0,1]}
possesses moments of all orders, so we lower bound the above by

\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\frac{1}{\|1/g\|_{L^{\infty}[0,1]}}\int_{0}^{1}\lambda(dt)>4y^{2}\Biggr)\geq\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\frac{1}{\|1/g\|_{L^{\infty}[0,1]}}>4y^{-\varepsilon}\text{ and }\int_{0}^{1}\lambda(dt)>y^{2+\varepsilon}\Biggr).

Seeing as
\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\geq\mathbb{P}(A)-\mathbb{P}(B^{c})
, we find that the right-hand side is bounded below by

\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\int_{0}^{1}\lambda(dt)>y^{2+\varepsilon}\Biggr)-\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\|1/g\|_{L^{\infty}[0,1]}\geq y^{\varepsilon}/4\Biggr).

The first probability is
\gg 1/y^{2+\varepsilon}
by Theorem
4
, while an application of Markov’s inequality allows us to find that the second term is
\ll_{\varepsilon}1/y^{100}
, say. This completes the proof of the lower bound.
∎
