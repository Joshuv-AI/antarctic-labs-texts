A Hitchhiker’s Guide to Poisson Gradient Estimation
Michael Ibrahim, Hanqi Zhao, Eli Sennesh, Zhi Li, Anqi Wu, Jacob L. Yates, Chengrui Li, Hadi Vafaii

Abstract
Poisson-distributed latent variable models are widely used in computational neuroscience, but differentiating through discrete stochastic samples remains challenging. Two approaches address this: Exponential Arrival Time ( Eat ) simulation and Gumbel-SoftMax ( Gsm ) relaxation. We provide the first systematic comparison of these methods, along with practical guidance for practitioners. Our main technical contribution is a modification to the Eat method that theoretically guarantees an unbiased first moment (exactly matching the firing rate), and reduces second-moment bias. We evaluate these methods on their distributional fidelity, gradient quality, and performance on two tasks: (1) variational autoencoders with Poisson latents, and (2) partially observable generalized linear models, where latent neural connectivity must be inferred from observed spike trains. Across all metrics, our modified Eat method exhibits better overall performance (often comparable to exact gradients), and substantially higher robustness to hyperparameter choices. Together, our results clarify the trade-offs between these methods and offer concrete recommendations for practitioners working with Poisson latent variable models.

Introduction

Brains communicate via discrete
action potentials
, or “spikes”
(
18
;
10
)
, that are often noisy
(
11
)
. The
rate coding hypothesis

(
1
;
3
;
29
)
states that information is encoded in
firing rates
(number of spikes per unit time), motivating the use of Poisson distributions to model stochastic spike counts.

The Poisson distribution is a principled choice, for both theoretical and empirical reasons. Theoretically, Poisson can be derived as the maximum entropy limit for sums of independent binary events
(
13
)
, interpretable as spikes arising from many weak synaptic inputs. Empirically, it is supported by evidence showing that cortical spike counts often exhibit approximately Poisson variability
(
33
;
34
)
.

Motivated by this connection,
35
developed the
Poisson Variational Autoencoder
(
\PP
-VAE), a generative model with Poisson-distributed latent variables that encodes inputs as discrete spike counts. Subsequent work
(
36
)
demonstrated that iterative inference in the
\PP
-VAE yields a spiking implementation of
sparse coding

(
26
;
31
)
and captures key psychophysical phenomena in perceptual decision-making
(
17
)
. From a machine learning perspective, the
\PP
-VAE model family demonstrates improved sample efficiency in downstream classification and strong out-of-distribution generalization. These results position the
\PP
-VAE as a strong candidate for NeuroAI applications.

However, gradient-based training of discrete latent variable models is difficult. Unlike Gaussian VAEs
(
20
)
, discrete models generally lack low-variance pathwise estimators, forcing practitioners to rely on high-variance score function estimators
(
27
;
4
;
32
;
23
)
. To overcome this challenge,
35
introduced a differentiable relaxation of Poisson based on point processes, which we call the
Exponential Arrival Time
(
Eat
) method. This enabled end-to-end training of
\PP
-VAE models using standard autograd libraries.

Independently,
22
developed an alternative Poisson relaxation based on the
Gumbel-SoftMax
(
Gsm
) approach
(
15
;
25
)
. They applied this to the
Partially Observable Generalized Linear Model
(POGLM) for neural population data
(
30
)
. POGLMs are important for neural data analysis as they enable inference of network connectivity and latent dynamics from partially observed recordings (a common scenario in neuroscience where not all neurons can be simultaneously measured). While both
Eat
and
Gsm
address the same fundamental challenge—differentiating through stochastic, integer-valued Poisson samples—no systematic comparison between them exists. Consequently, practitioners lack guidance on method selection.

Our contributions.

In this paper, we provide this missing comparison. Crucially, we identify a limitation in the standard
Eat
method: it exhibits high sensitivity to the temperature hyperparameter. We trace this instability to the use of a sigmoid approximation, whose infinite support leads to poor distributional fidelity. Our key technical contribution is a principled correction: we replace the sigmoid in
Eat
with a cubic Hermite interpolant (smoothstep) possessing compact support. We compare our modified algorithm,
Eat
{}_{\textsf{cubic}}
, against the original
Eat
{}_{\textsf{sigmoid}}
,
Gsm
, score-based, and exact gradients (when available), across both
\PP
-VAE and POGLM settings, finding that
Eat
{}_{\textsf{cubic}}
consistently outperforms all alternatives across distributional fidelity, gradient quality, and downstream task performance, while remaining significantly more robust to temperature choices. We synthesize our findings into practical recommendations in
Table

1
.

Background and related work

We use

Probabilistic latent variable models.

Consider a generative model
p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})
, where
\bm{x}
are observed data,
\bm{z}
are unobserved latent variables, and
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}}
are the parameters of the generative model. Since
\bm{z}
is unknown, learning
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}}
via maximum likelihood
\operatorname{argmax}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}}}\int p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}
is challenging due to the often intractable marginalization over
\bm{z}
. This presents two coupled tasks: (i)
learning
the model parameters
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}}
, and (ii)
inferring
the latents
\bm{z}
given observations
\bm{x}
(i.e., finding the optimal posterior
p(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})
).

Variational inference and ELBO.

Variational inference (VI;
5

(
20
;
35
)
and POGLMs
(
22
)
. In VI, we introduce a
variational posterior

q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})
with parameters
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}}
, and address both
inference
and
learning
tasks by optimizing the
E

(ELBO;

improves the marginal likelihood
p(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})
, while maximizing w.r.t.
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}}
minimizes the KL between the approximate posterior
q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})
and the true posterior
p(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})
.

The Exponential Arrival Time (
Eat
) relaxation.

Motivated by the spiking nature of neural computation,
35
introduced the Poisson VAE (
\PP
-VAE) by replacing Gaussian latents with Poisson-distributed spike counts. In a
\PP
-VAE, both the prior and approximate posterior are Poisson:
q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})=\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{x}))
, where
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}
is a vector of firing rates. The encoder network outputs log-rates
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}(\bm{x})=\mathrm{enc}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})
, which are exponentiated to ensure positivity:
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{x})=\exp({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}(\bm{x}))
. As in standard VAEs
(
20
)
, the ELBO (
eq.

1
) further decomposes into a reconstruction term minus a KL regularizer:

\displaystyle\operatorname{ELBO}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})\;=

\displaystyle\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})}[{\ln p(\bm{x}|\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})}]

(2)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{KL}}\!\left({q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})}\,\|\,{p(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})}\right).

The main challenge in training
\PP
-VAE is differentiating through stochastic, integer spike count samples
\bm{z}
. To address this, the authors developed the
Exponential Arrival Time
(
Eat
) relaxation. The key insight is that a Poisson random variable
z\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}})
can be viewed as counting events in a Poisson point process:
z=\sum_{m}\mathbf{1}[t_{m}<1]
, where
t_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta t_{i}
are arrival times generated by cumulating exponential inter-arrival times. The hard indicator
\mathbf{1}[t_{m}<1]
is non-differentiable, but can be relaxed using a smooth approximation
f_{\mathrm{approx}}((1-t_{m})/\tau)
(e.g., sigmoid):

z=\sum_{m=1}^{M}f_{\mathrm{approx}}\!\left(\frac{1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta t_{i}}{\tau}\right),\quad\Delta t_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\operatorname{Exp}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}),

where the number of exponential samples
M
should be large enough and
\tau>0
is a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of the threshold. As
\tau\to 0
, the relaxation recovers the true Poisson distribution. We review the
Eat
method in detail in
appendix

B
, summarize it in
Algorithm

1
, and provide a visual illustration in
fig.

5
.

The choice of
f_{\mathrm{approx}}
and
\tau
are critical modeling decisions.
35
used the sigmoid function without a clear justification, and did not systematically explore the temperature parameter. Their results reveal high sensitivity to
\tau
, with only a narrow range yielding good performance. This sensitivity suggests that at larger
\tau
, the relaxed distribution deviates substantially from the true Poisson, degrading optimization. Understanding and mitigating this distributional mismatch is a central goal of our paper.

The Gumbel-Softmax (
Gsm
) relaxation.

A critical problem in neural recording is that the recorded neurons are only a small part of the entire population in a particular brain region of interest. A partially observable generalized linear model (POGLM)
(
30
;
16
;
24
)
studies the mutual interactions between all neurons underlying the observed neural spike trains
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{T}
, while assuming the existence of latent spike trains
\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{T}
from hidden neurons. Unlike VAEs, POGLM is inherently a sequential model with a time dimension
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
and its generative model cannot be decomposed into a prior-conditional-product explicitly. Specifically,

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{t}\\
\bm{z}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}\left(f\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{t-1}\\
\bm{z}_{t-1}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{t-2}\\
\bm{z}_{t-2}\end{bmatrix},\dots;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}}\right)\right),

where
f(\cdot;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})
can be arbitrary but is usually a linear mapping in neuroscience modeling settings. To learn such a complicated model through VI efficiently, a stable gradient estimation for
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}}
is necessary on the variational distribution
\bm{z}_{t}\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}\left(g\left(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{T};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}}\right)\right)
, where
g(\cdot;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\phi}}})
can be viewed as an encoder.

The
Gumbel-SoftMax
(
Gsm
,
22
) Poisson relaxation method uses the Gumbel-Softmax/Concrete distribution
(
15
;
25
)
that relaxes each
z\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\lambda)
as:

z=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}m\cdot\tilde{z}_{m},\quad\tilde{z}_{0},\dots,\tilde{z}_{M-1}\sim\operatorname{GS}_{\tau}(\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{M-1}),

where
\operatorname{GS}_{\tau}
is the Gumbel-Softmax distribution with temperature
\tau
;
\pi_{m}=\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(m;\lambda)
is the Poisson PMF at
m
.

The difficulty of auto-tuning temperature.

Both
Eat
and
Gsm
relaxations require setting a critical hyperparameter: the temperature

, which controls the smoothness of the continuous relaxation. However, neither
35
nor

and the specific objective function (

a modified
Eat
relaxation that is substantially more robust to temperature selection, reducing the need for careful tuning.

3.3
Gradient analysis: direction, bias, and variance

Distributional fidelity measures how well a relaxation approximates the true Poisson, but optimization ultimately depends on gradient quality. In this section, we develop a principled framework for comparing gradient estimates across relaxation methods.

Linear decoders enable exact gradients.

Evaluating gradient quality requires ground-truth gradients to compare against. How can we obtain these for an intractable ELBO? Linear decoders provide a rare opportunity. When the decoder is linear,

\PP

eq.

64

eq.

69
). We will now use these analytical expressions as our ground truth to evaluate the Poisson relaxation methods.

Decomposing gradient estimates.

In stochastic optimization, we estimate gradients from finite samples. A gradient estimate
\hat{\bm{g}}
can be decomposed as:

\hat{\bm{g}}\;=\;\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b}+\bm{\epsilon},

where
\bm{g}^{*}
is the true gradient,
\bm{\epsilon}
is zero-mean noise with covariance
\bm{\Sigma}=\EE[\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}]
, and
\bm{b}
is a systematic bias:

\bm{b}\;\coloneqq\;\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{\bm{g}}_{i}-\bm{g}^{*}),

where
N
is the number of Monte Carlo samples.

Curvature-aware metrics.

Low bias and variance are desirable, but their impact on optimization depends on the loss landscape. In
appendix

F
, we derive the optimization dynamics (
eq.

78
) via a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss
\mathcal{L}=-\operatorname{ELBO}
, keeping up to the Hessian
\mathbf{H}
(
eq.

69
). This reveals that the relevant quantities are
curvature-weighted
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{BiasEnergy}\;

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;\bm{b}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{b},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{NoiseEnergy}\;

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;\Tr(\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma}),

(8)

These metrics weight errors by local curvature: bias or noise along high-curvature directions harms optimization more than along flat directions.

Cosine similarity metrics.

We also measure directional alignment between estimated and true gradients:

\displaystyle\mathrm{CosMean}\;

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;\cos\!\left(\EE[\hat{\bm{g}}],\,\bm{g}^{*}\right),

(9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{CosSample}\;

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;\EE\!\left[\cos\!\left(\hat{\bm{g}},\,\bm{g}^{*}\right)\right].

(10)

\mathrm{CosMean}
measures alignment of the expected gradient (including bias) with the ground truth, while
\mathrm{CosSample}
measures the average alignment of individual samples.

4
Conclusions and discussion

We systematically investigated two existing approaches to Poisson relaxations—
Eat
{}_{\textsf{sigmoid}}
and
Gsm
—and identified a key limitation of the former: the sigmoid’s infinite support causes substantial divergence between the relaxed and true Poisson distributions. Leveraging the theory of Poisson point processes, we derived closed-form expressions for the moments of the relaxed distribution, revealing that this divergence worsens with temperature. We addressed this issue by replacing the sigmoid with a cubic smoothstep function, whose compact support eliminates spurious contributions from events far outside the counting interval.

Our modified
Eat
{}_{\textsf{cubic}}
consistently outperformed both
Eat
{}_{\textsf{sigmoid}}
and
Gsm
across distributional fidelity, gradient quality, temperature robustness, and downstream task performance, often matching or exceeding results obtained with exact gradients. We distill these findings into practical recommendations in
table

1
.

Distributional fidelity also matters.

Our results highlight a factor often overlooked in stochastic optimization for variational inference:
distributional fidelity matters
. While much attention has focused on reducing gradient variance, the distributional shift introduced by relaxation methods can be equally consequential. We encourage future work
to evaluate distributional fidelity alongside traditional metrics.

Limitations and future directions.

Our experiments focused on variational inference via ELBO optimization. Alternative approaches such as wake-sleep algorithms
(
14
;
6
)
, reweighted wake-sleep
(
2
)
, or score-ascent methods
(
19
)
sidestep the need for custom gradient estimators entirely, and may offer complementary advantages in certain settings. While we demonstrated benefits in linear
\PP
-VAE and POGLM settings, further validation on larger-scale models and diverse application domains would strengthen the generality of our conclusions.

In this work, we focused exclusively on Poisson latents, but the
Gsm
method readily generalizes to arbitrary discrete distributions, including overdispersed alternatives such as the negative binomial (
section

H.3
). This is particularly relevant for cortical data, where spike counts often exhibit overdispersion
(
12
)
; extending our comparative analysis to these settings is a promising direction for future work.

Conversely, cortical neurons sometimes also display sub-Poisson Fano factors
(
8
)
, suggesting an intriguing connection to the underdispersion inherent in our relaxation methods: the mathematical structure enabling gradient-based learning may inadvertently capture aspects of biological neural variability.

More broadly, the tension between discrete neural codes and the differentiability requirements of modern machine learning represents a fertile ground for NeuroAI research, where biological constraints may inspire algorithmic innovations.

Appendix: A hitchhiker’s guide to Poisson gradient estimation

Appendix A
Gradient estimators for variational inference

Variational inference optimizes the evidence lower bound (ELBO) with respect to two sets of parameters: the decoder parameters
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}
of the generative model
p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})
, and the encoder parameters
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}
of the variational distribution
q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})
. While the gradient with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}
is straightforward, the gradient with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}
requires more care since
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}
affects the distribution from which we sample. In this section, we derive the standard gradient estimators used in practice.

Gradient with respect to decoder parameters.

Recall the ELBO definition from
eq.

1
:

\operatorname{ELBO}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\;=\;\int q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big]\ d\bm{z}.

(11)

The gradient with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}
passes directly through the integrand:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{ELBO}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}}\;

\displaystyle=\;\int q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}}\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big]\ d\bm{z}

\displaystyle\approx\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}}\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z}^{(i)};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}^{(i)}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big]

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}}\widehat{\operatorname{ELBO}}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}),

(12)

where
\bm{z}^{(i)}\sim q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})
are samples from the variational distribution. Since the samples do not depend on
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}}
, we can simply evaluate the Monte Carlo estimate
\widehat{\operatorname{ELBO}}
and use automatic differentiation to compute the gradient.

A.1
Score function estimator for encoder parameters

The gradient with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}
is more subtle because
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}
appears both in the sampling distribution and in the integrand. We cannot simply interchange differentiation and integration.

The
score function estimator
(also known as REINFORCE or the likelihood ratio method) handles this by using the identity
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}q=q\,\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\ln q
. Differentiating the ELBO at a fixed point
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{ELBO}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\;

\displaystyle=\;\int\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}_{0})\Big]\frac{\partial q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\ d\bm{z}

\displaystyle\qquad+\;\int q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}_{0})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big]\ d\bm{z}.

(13)

For the first term, we apply the log-derivative trick:
\partial_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}q=q\,\partial_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\ln q
. For the second term, note that
\partial_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})=0
and that
\int\partial_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}q\,d\bm{z}=\partial_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\int q\,d\bm{z}=\partial_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}1=0
. This yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{ELBO}}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\;

\displaystyle=\;\int q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}_{0})\Big]\frac{\partial\ln q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\ d\bm{z}

\displaystyle\approx\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z}^{(i)};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}^{(i)}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}_{0})\Big]\frac{\partial\ln q(\bm{z}^{(i)}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}.

(14)

The term in brackets acts as a
reward signal
that weights the score function
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\ln q
. In implementation, we compute
\ln q(\bm{z}^{(i)}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})
and multiply by the
stop_gradient
(or
detach
) of the reward before backpropagation.

A.2
Pathwise estimator for encoder parameters

When the latent variable can be expressed via the
reparameterization trick
—that is,
\bm{z}=r(\bm{\varepsilon}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})
for some deterministic function
r
and noise
\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{E}(\bm{\varepsilon})
independent of
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}
—we can use the
pathwise estimator
instead:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{ELBO}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\int q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big]\ d\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\int\mathcal{E}(\bm{\varepsilon})\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},r(\bm{\varepsilon}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}});{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(r(\bm{\varepsilon}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big]\ d\bm{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\approx\;\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\ln p(\bm{x},\bm{z}^{(i)};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}})-\ln q(\bm{z}^{(i)}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})\Big]

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}}\widehat{\operatorname{ELBO}}(\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\theta}},{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}),

(15)

where
\bm{z}^{(i)}=r(\bm{\varepsilon}^{(i)}|\bm{x};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}})
with
\bm{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{E}(\bm{\varepsilon})
. The key insight is that we have moved the
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\phi}}
-dependence
inside
the integrand via the reparameterization, so differentiation and integration can be interchanged.

The pathwise estimator typically has much lower variance than the score function estimator, which is why reparameterization-based methods (such as the VAE) have been so successful.

Both the
Exponential Arrival Time
(
Eat
,
35
) and
Gumbel-Softmax
(
Gsm
,
22
) methods extend this approach to Poisson latent variables by constructing differentiable relaxations of the Poisson distribution. We explore these methods next.

Appendix B
The Exponential Arrival Time (
Eat
) relaxation method

Variational inference with discrete latent variables poses a fundamental challenge: we cannot backpropagate gradients through integer-valued samples. The reparameterization trick, which has proven so effective for continuous distributions like Gaussians, does not directly apply. The
Exponential Arrival Time
(
Eat
,
(
35
)
) method circumvents this obstacle by exploiting a beautiful connection between Poisson random variables and continuous-time point processes.

Poisson as event counting.

The key insight is that a Poisson random variable can be viewed as counting events in a stochastic process. Consider events—neural spikes, photon arrivals, customer visits—occurring randomly in time at rate
\lambda
. The number of events
N
falling within the unit interval
[0,1]
follows a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
:

N\;=\;|\{t_{m}:t_{m}\in[0,1]\}|\;\sim\;\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)

(16)

where
\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}
are the arrival times of the events, and
|\cdot|
indicates the cardinality (size) of a set.

The inter-arrival time representation.

Where do these arrival times come from? For a Poisson process, they emerge from a simple generative procedure. Let
\Delta t_{1},\Delta t_{2},\ldots
be independent draws from an
\mathrm{Exponential}(\lambda)
distribution. We interpret each
\Delta t_{i}
as the time duration between consecutive events. The arrival times are then cumulative sums:

t_{m}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta t_{i}.

(17)

This representation is fully differentiable: we can sample
\Delta t_{i}
using the reparameterization
\Delta t_{i}=-\log(u_{i})/\lambda
where
u_{i}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
, and gradients flow through both the rate parameter
\lambda
and the cumulative sum.

The discreteness problem.

The count
N
in (
16
) is computed by summing hard indicator functions:

N\;=\;\sum_{m}\mathbf{1}[t_{m}<1].

(18)

The indicator
\mathbf{1}[t_{m}<1]
is 1 if event
m
arrives before time 1, and 0 otherwise. This is where discreteness enters: the indicator is a step function with zero gradient almost everywhere.

Soft relaxation.

The
Eat
method replaces the hard indicator with a smooth approximation. Let
f_{\mathrm{approx}}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a differentiable function that approximates the step function, and let
\tau>0
be a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of the transition. The relaxed count is:

z\;=\;\sum_{m}f_{\mathrm{approx}}\!\left(\frac{1-t_{m}}{\tau}\right).

(19)

When
t_{m}\ll 1
(event well inside the interval), the argument
(1-t_{m})/\tau
is large and positive, so
f_{\mathrm{approx}}\approx 1
. When
t_{m}\gg 1
(event outside), the argument is large and negative, so
f_{\mathrm{approx}}\approx 0
. Events near the boundary
t_{m}\approx 1
contribute fractional values that interpolate smoothly.

The standard choice, introduced by
35
, is the logistic sigmoid:
f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)\;=\;\sigma(u)\;=\;1/(1+e^{-u}).

Temperature annealing.

As
\tau\to 0
, the soft indicator sharpens toward the hard step function, and
z
converges in distribution to the true Poisson count
N
. In practice, one often anneals the temperature during training: starting with a larger
\tau
for smoother gradients, then decreasing
\tau
to approach the discrete distribution as optimization progresses.

M

Appendix C
The Gumbel-Softmax (
Gsm
) relaxation method

Poisson as infinite categorical distribution.

Rather than thinking of a Poisson variable
z\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\lambda)
from a point process counting perspective, we can expand it explicitly as an infinite categorical distribution, i.e., its probability mass function (PMF), and hence:

z\sim\operatorname{Cat}(\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\dots),\quad\pi_{m}=\mathbb{P}[z=m]=\frac{\lambda^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}}{m!}

(20)

Truncation.

The first step of the relaxation is to truncate the categorical distribution with a large enough upperbound
M
. Now, the approximated sample becomes

z\sim\operatorname{Cat}(\pi_{0}/C,\pi_{1}/C,\dots,\pi_{M-1}/C),

(21)

where
C=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\pi_{m}
is the normalizing factor.

Gumbel-Softmax reparametrization.

The second step is to use the Gumbel-Softmax trick from
(
15
;
25
)
to get a soft and gradient-enabled Gumbel-Softmax sample

\tilde{z}\sim\operatorname{GS}\left(\frac{\pi_{0}}{C},\frac{\pi_{1}}{C},\dots,\frac{\pi_{M-1}}{C}\right).

(22)

Specifically, we sample a
g_{m}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\operatorname{Gumbel}(0,1),\ \forall m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
(via
\tilde{z}_{m}=-\ln(-\ln u_{m}),\ u_{m}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)
). Then, we compute its Softmax

\tilde{z}_{m}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ln\pi_{m}-\ln C+g_{m}}{\tau}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ln\pi_{i}-\ln C+g_{i}}{\tau}}}

(23)

so that
\tilde{z}_{m}
is a soft one-hot representation over the simplex
\Delta^{M-1}
, i.e.,
\tilde{z}_{m}>0
and
\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\tilde{z}_{m}=1
. Finally,

z=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\tilde{z}_{m}.

(24)

Similar to the EAT relaxation method, when
\tau=0
,
\tilde{z}_{m}
becomes a hard one-hot sample from
\operatorname{Cat}(\pi_{0}/C,\pi_{1}/C,\dots,\pi_{M-1}/C)
, so that only one particular
\tilde{z}_{m}=1
out of all
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
.

Numerical stability and the likelihood function.

We now show that we
Gsm
has the property of normalization invariance, which can improve its numerical stability and efficiency. Observing that for each category
m
in the GS distribution,

\frac{\pi_{m}}{C}=\frac{\lambda^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}}{m!C}

(25)

contains the common factor
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}}{C}
for all
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. Hence, we can define

\ln\pi_{m}^{\prime}\coloneqq m\ln\lambda-\ln(m!)=\ln\pi_{m}+\lambda,

(26)

so that

\tilde{z}_{m}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ln\pi_{m}-\ln C+g_{m}}{\tau}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ln\pi_{i}-\ln C+g_{i}}{\tau}}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ln\pi_{m}^{\prime}+g_{m}}{\tau}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ln\pi^{\prime}_{i}+g_{i}}{\tau}}}.

(27)

This implies that we can directly feed the simplified
\pi_{m}^{\prime}
into the Gumbel-Softmax during sampling, and remove the unnecessary factors of the
C
and
\lambda
in its evaluation. Besides, this refactor also simplifies the log-likelihood function of the GS distribution:

\begin{split}&\ln p(\tilde{z}_{0},\dots,\tilde{z}_{M-1};\pi_{0}^{\prime},\dots,\pi_{M-1}^{\prime})\\
&=\ln\Gamma(M)+(M-1)\ln\tau-M\cdot\ln\left(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\frac{\pi_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}}{\tilde{z}_{m}^{\tau}}\right)+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left((\ln\pi_{m}+\lambda)-(\tau+1)\ln\tilde{z}_{m}\right)\\
&=\ln\Gamma(M)+(M-1)\ln\tau-M\cdot\left[\lambda+\ln\left(\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{\pi_{m}}{\tilde{z}_{m}^{\tau}}\right)\right]+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\left(\ln\pi_{m}-(\tau+1)\ln\tilde{z}_{m}\right)+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\lambda\\
&=\ln\Gamma(M)+(M-1)\ln\tau-M\cdot\ln\left(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\frac{\pi_{m}}{\tilde{z}_{m}^{\tau}}\right)\cancel{-M\cdot\lambda}+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\left(\ln\pi_{m}-(\tau+1)\ln\tilde{z}_{m}\right)\cancel{+M\cdot\lambda}\\
&=\ln p(\tilde{z}_{0},\dots,\tilde{z}_{M-1};\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{M-1})\end{split}

(28)

with a large enough truncation
M
and then aggregates to the final relaxed count
z
. All operations are differentiable, enabling gradient-based optimization of the rate parameter
\lambda
and any upstream parameters that determine it.

D.1
Campbell’s theorem

Campbell’s theorem provides a powerful tool for computing expectations of sums over Poisson point processes. The result dates back to
7
, with modern treatments available in standard references on point processes
(
21
;
9
)
.

Intuition.

Consider a Poisson process
\Pi=\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}
with rate
\lambda
on
[0,\infty)
, and suppose we want to compute the expected value of
\sum_{t\in\Pi}f(t)
for some function
f
. The key insight is to think infinitesimally: partition the time axis into tiny intervals of width
dt
. In each interval
[t,t+dt]
:

•

The probability of having exactly one point is
\lambda\,dt
(plus higher-order terms).

•

If there is a point, it contributes
f(t)
to the sum.

Thus, the expected contribution from the interval
[t,t+dt]
is approximately
f(t)\cdot\lambda\,dt
. Summing (integrating) over all intervals gives
\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt
.

Formal statement.

Let
\Pi
be a homogeneous Poisson point process on
[0,\infty)
with rate
\lambda>0
, and let
f:[0,\infty)\to\RR
be a measurable function. Campbell’s theorem states:

\EE\left[\sum_{t\in\Pi}f(t)\right]\;=\;\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt,

(29)

provided the integral on the right-hand side is well-defined (i.e.,
\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|\,dt<\infty
).

For the second moment, we expand the square and use the independence property of Poisson processes to handle cross-terms:

\EE\left[\left(\sum_{t\in\Pi}f(t)\right)^{2}\right]\;=\;\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)^{2}\,dt\;+\;\lambda^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt\right)^{2}.

(30)

The first term arises from “diagonal” contributions (a single point squared), while the second term arises from “off-diagonal” contributions (pairs of distinct points). A direct consequence is the variance formula:

\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{t\in\Pi}f(t)\right)\;=\;\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)^{2}\,dt.

(31)

D.2
Applying Campbell’s theorem to EAT

Recall from
eq.

19
that the relaxed count is:

z\;=\;\sum_{t\in\Pi}f_{\mathrm{approx}}\left(\frac{1-t}{\tau}\right),

where
\Pi
is a Poisson process with rate
\lambda
. This is exactly the form required by Campbell’s theorem, with
f(t)=f_{\mathrm{approx}}\left(\frac{1-t}{\tau}\right)
.

First moment.

Applying
eq.

29
:

\EE[z]\;=\;\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\mathrm{approx}}\left(\frac{1-t}{\tau}\right)dt.

(32)

To evaluate this integral, we substitute
u=(1-t)/\tau
, which gives
t=1-\tau u
and
dt=-\tau\,du
. The limits transform as:
t=0\Rightarrow u=1/\tau
and
t=\infty\Rightarrow u=-\infty
. Thus:

\displaystyle\EE[z]\;

\displaystyle=\;\lambda\int_{1/\tau}^{-\infty}f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)\cdot(-\tau)\,du

\displaystyle=\;\lambda\tau\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)\,du

(33)

We define the
mean factor
:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle c(\tau)\;\coloneqq\;\tau\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)\,du,\qquad\text{so that}\;\;\EE[z]\;=\;\lambda\cdot c(\tau)$}}

(34)

For the relaxation to be unbiased, we would need
c(\tau)=1
.

Variance.

Applying
eq.

31
with the same substitution:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\;\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\mathrm{approx}}\left(\frac{1-t}{\tau}\right)^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\;\lambda\tau\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)^{2}\,du.

(35)

We define the
variance factor
:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle v(\tau)\;\coloneqq\;\tau\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)^{2}\,du,\qquad\text{so that}\;\;\mathrm{Var}(z)\;=\;\lambda\cdot v(\tau)$}}

(36)

For a true Poisson distribution, we have
\mathrm{Var}(N)=\lambda
, so matching Poisson variance requires
v(\tau)=1
.

Underdispersion is unavoidable.

Since
f_{\mathrm{approx}}
takes values in
[0,1]
, we have
f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)^{2}\leqslant f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)
for all
u
, with equality only when
f_{\mathrm{approx}}(u)\in\{0,1\}
. This immediately implies
v(\tau)\leqslant c(\tau)
, with strict inequality whenever
f_{\mathrm{approx}}
takes values strictly between 0 and 1. As we will see, this means the relaxed distribution is always
underdispersed
relative to the true Poisson distribution.

D.3
Explicit formulas: sigmoid

We now evaluate the integrals in
eqs.

34
and
36
for the sigmoid function
\sigma(u)=(1+e^{-u})^{-1}
.

Antiderivatives.

For the first moment, we need
\int\sigma(u)\,du
. Noting that
\sigma(u)=e^{u}/(1+e^{u})
, we recognize this as the derivative of
\ln(1+e^{u})
:

\int\sigma(u)\,du\;=\;\ln(1+e^{u})\;=\;\mathrm{softplus}(u).

(37)

For the variance, we need
\int\sigma(u)^{2}\,du
. We use the identity:

\sigma(u)^{2}\;=\;\sigma(u)\cdot\sigma(u)\;=\;\sigma(u)-\sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))\;=\;\sigma(u)-\sigma^{\prime}(u),

where we used
\sigma^{\prime}(u)=\sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))
. Integrating both sides:

\int\sigma(u)^{2}\,du\;=\;\int\sigma(u)\,du-\int\sigma^{\prime}(u)\,du\;=\;\mathrm{softplus}(u)-\sigma(u).

(38)

Evaluating the definite integrals.

For the mean factor, using
eq.

37
:

\displaystyle c(\tau)\;

\displaystyle=\;\tau\left[\mathrm{softplus}(u)\right]_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}

\displaystyle=\;\tau\left[\mathrm{softplus}(1/\tau)-\lim_{u\to-\infty}\mathrm{softplus}(u)\right]

\displaystyle=\;\tau\cdot\mathrm{softplus}(1/\tau),

(39)

since
\mathrm{softplus}(u)\to 0
as
u\to-\infty
.

For the variance factor, using
eq.

38
:

\displaystyle v(\tau)\;

\displaystyle=\;\tau\left[\mathrm{softplus}(u)-\sigma(u)\right]_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}

\displaystyle=\;\tau\left[\mathrm{softplus}(1/\tau)-\sigma(1/\tau)-(0-0)\right]

\displaystyle=\;\tau\left[\mathrm{softplus}(1/\tau)-\sigma(1/\tau)\right],

(40)

since both
\mathrm{softplus}(u)\to 0
and
\sigma(u)\to 0
as
u\to-\infty
.

Final expressions.

Letting
a=\mathrm{softplus}(1/\tau)=\ln(1+e^{1/\tau})
and
b=\sigma(1/\tau)=1/(1+e^{-1/\tau})
, we have:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{sigmoid}}(\tau)\;=\;\tau\cdot a\;=\;\tau\ln(1+e^{1/\tau})$}}

(41)

{\framebox{$\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{sigmoid}}(\tau)\;=\;\tau(a-b)\;=\;\tau\left[\ln(1+e^{1/\tau})-\frac{1}{1+e^{-1/\tau}}\right]$}}

(42)

Limiting behavior.

As
\tau\to 0
, we have
1/\tau\to\infty
, so
a\to 1/\tau
and
b\to 1
. Thus:

c_{\mathrm{sigmoid}}(\tau)\;\to\;\tau\cdot\frac{1}{\tau}\;=\;1,\qquad v_{\mathrm{sigmoid}}(\tau)\;\to\;\tau\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-1\right)\;\to\;1.

Both factors approach 1 as the temperature decreases, recovering the true Poisson statistics in the limit.

D.4
Explicit formulas: cubic smoothstep

The cubic smoothstep (also known as Hermite interpolation) offers an alternative soft indicator with
compact support
:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)\;=\;\begin{cases}0,&\quad(u<-1)\\[4.0pt]
3w^{2}-2w^{3},\quad w=\dfrac{u+1}{2},&\quad(-1\leqslant u\leqslant 1)\\[8.0pt]
1,&\quad(u>1)\end{cases}$}}

(43)

This function is
C^{1}
continuous (with zero derivatives at
u=\pm 1
) and transitions smoothly from 0 to 1 over the interval
[-1,1]
.
Figure

6
visualizes the cubic smoothstep function alongside sigmoid.

Figure 6:

Eat

The hard indicator
\mathbf{1}[u>0]
(black dashed) is non-differentiable. The sigmoid (blue) provides a

smooth approximation but has infinite support, with tails extending to

. The cubic smoothstep (red) is
C^{1}
smooth with compact support on
[-1,1]
(dotted lines): it equals exactly 0 for

and exactly 1 for
u>1
. This compact support eliminates “ghost spike” contributions from events far outside the counting interval, yielding superior distributional fidelity (
section

3.1
).

The compact support leads to qualitatively different behavior depending on whether the temperature is small (
\tau\leqslant 1
) or large (
\tau>1
).

Case
\tau\leqslant 1
: Full transition captured.

When
\tau\leqslant 1
, the upper integration limit
1/\tau\geqslant 1
lies at or beyond the transition region. We split the integral:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)\,du\;

\displaystyle=\;\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{-1}0\,du}_{=\,0}\;+\;\underbrace{\int_{-1}^{1}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)\,du}_{=\,I_{1}}\;+\;\underbrace{\int_{1}^{1/\tau}1\,du}_{=\,1/\tau-1}.

(44)

To evaluate
I_{1}
, we substitute
w=(u+1)/2
, so
du=2\,dw
, and the limits become
w=0
to
w=1
:

\displaystyle I_{1}\;

\displaystyle=\;\int_{0}^{1}(3w^{2}-2w^{3})\cdot 2\,dw

\displaystyle=\;2\left[w^{3}-\frac{w^{4}}{2}\right]_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle=\;2\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)\;=\;1.

(45)

Therefore, from
eq.

44
:

\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)\,du\;=\;0+1+\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-1\right)\;=\;\frac{1}{\tau},

and the mean factor is:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{cubic}}(\tau)\;=\;\tau\cdot\frac{1}{\tau}\;=\;1\qquad\text{for }\tau\leqslant 1.$}}

(46)

The cubic smoothstep has no mean bias for
\tau\leqslant 1
.

For the variance factor, we need
\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)^{2}\,du
. Using the same decomposition:

\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)^{2}\,du\;=\;0+I_{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-1\right),

(47)

where
I_{2}=\int_{-1}^{1}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)^{2}\,du
. Expanding
(3w^{2}-2w^{3})^{2}=9w^{4}-12w^{5}+4w^{6}
:

\displaystyle I_{2}\;

\displaystyle=\;\int_{0}^{1}(9w^{4}-12w^{5}+4w^{6})\cdot 2\,dw

\displaystyle=\;2\left[\frac{9w^{5}}{5}-2w^{6}+\frac{4w^{7}}{7}\right]_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle=\;2\left(\frac{9}{5}-2+\frac{4}{7}\right)

\displaystyle=\;2\cdot\frac{63-70+20}{35}\;=\;\frac{26}{35}.

(48)

Therefore:

\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)^{2}\,du\;=\;\frac{26}{35}+\frac{1}{\tau}-1\;=\;\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{9}{35},

and the variance factor is:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{cubic}}(\tau)\;=\;\tau\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{9}{35}\right)\;=\;1-\frac{9\tau}{35}\qquad\text{for }\tau\leqslant 1.$}}

(49)

Case
\tau>1
: Partial transition.

When
\tau>1
, the upper limit
1/\tau<1
falls inside the transition region. Define
\beta=(1/\tau+1)/2=(1+\tau)/(2\tau)
, which lies in
(1/2,1)
. The integrals become:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)\,du\;

\displaystyle=\;\int_{0}^{\beta}(3w^{2}-2w^{3})\cdot 2\,dw

\displaystyle=\;2\left[\beta^{3}-\frac{\beta^{4}}{2}\right]\;=\;\beta^{3}(2-\beta).

(50)

Similarly:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{1/\tau}f_{\mathrm{cubic}}(u)^{2}\,du\;

\displaystyle=\;2\left[\frac{9\beta^{5}}{5}-2\beta^{6}+\frac{4\beta^{7}}{7}\right].

(51)

The moment factors for
\tau>1
are:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{cubic}}(\tau)\;=\;\tau\beta^{3}(2-\beta),\qquad v_{\mathrm{cubic}}(\tau)\;=\;\tau\left(\frac{18\beta^{5}}{5}-4\beta^{6}+\frac{8\beta^{7}}{7}\right)\qquad\text{for }\tau>1,$}}

(52)

where
\beta=(1+\tau)/(2\tau)
. These expressions are continuous at
\tau=1
(where
\beta=1
).

D.5
Fano factor analysis

The
Fano factor
is defined as the ratio of variance to mean:

F\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{Var}(z)}{\EE[z]}\;=\;\frac{\lambda\cdot v(\tau)}{\lambda\cdot c(\tau)}\;=\;\frac{v(\tau)}{c(\tau)}.

(53)

For a true Poisson distribution,
F=1
exactly. Distributions with
F<1
are called
sub-Poisson
or underdispersed.

Since the
\lambda
-dependence cancels, the Fano factor depends only on the temperature and the choice of
f_{\mathrm{approx}}
.

Sigmoid.

{\framebox{$\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{sigmoid}}(\tau)\;=\;\frac{\tau(a-b)}{\tau a}\;=\;1-\frac{\sigma(1/\tau)}{\mathrm{softplus}(1/\tau)}$}}

(54)

Cubic (
\tau\leqslant 1
).

Since
c_{\mathrm{cubic}}(\tau)=1
:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{cubic}}(\tau)\;=\;v_{\mathrm{cubic}}(\tau)\;=\;1-\frac{9\tau}{35}\qquad\text{for }\tau\leqslant 1.$}}

(55)

Comparison.

Both relaxations are sub-Poisson (
F<1
) for all
\tau>0
, as predicted by the general argument in
Section

D.2
. However, the cubic preserves the Fano factor substantially better: at
\tau=0.5
, the cubic has
F=0.87
while the sigmoid drops to
F=0.59
.

This difference arises from the compact support of the cubic: events far from the boundary contribute exactly 0 or 1, with only a narrow region of fractional contributions. The sigmoid’s infinite tails create a diffuse “haze” of partial contributions that inflates the mean while reducing variance.

Appendix E
Poisson VAE analytical deviations: loss, gradients, and Hessian

Let the input data be a vector
\bm{x}\in\RR^{M}
. We define the latent log-rates as
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}\in\RR^{K}
and the decoder weights as
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\in\RR^{M\times K}
. The firing rates are given by the element-wise exponential, which we use to construct the approximate posterior:

{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\;=\;\exp({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}})\in\RR^{K}_{+},\qquad q_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}(\bm{z}|\bm{x})\;=\;\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{K}\frac{e^{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}_{i}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}_{i}^{z_{i}}}{z_{i}!}.

(56)

The reconstruction loss is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon.}}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}},{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}})\;=\;\EE_{\bm{z}\sim q_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}(\bm{z}|\bm{x})}\!\Bigg[{\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\Bigg].

(57)

Because the objective is quadratic with respect to the latent variable
\bm{z}
, we can decompose the expected loss into the loss evaluated at the mean plus a trace term accounting for the posterior variance. We utilize the canonical identity for the expectation of a quadratic form of a random variable
\bm{z}
with mean
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}
and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
:

\EE\!\Bigg[{\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\Bigg]\;=\;\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,+\,\Tr\!\left({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{\Sigma}\right).

(58)

Using the fact that the Poisson distribution has equal mean and variance (implying
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})
), we can analytically compute
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon.}}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}},{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}})
. We find that it consists of a Mean Squared Error (MSE) term and a variance penalty term:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon.}}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}},{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}})\;=\;\underbrace{\left\lVert\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{MSE}}\;\;+\;\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{variance}\\
\text{penalty}\end{subarray}}$}}

(59)

Here,
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}}\in\RR^{K}
is the vector containing the squared norms of the dictionary elements:

{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}}\;=\;\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}).

(60)

We now derive the gradient and Hessian of the reconstruction loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon.}}
with respect to the log-rates
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}
. For notational clarity, in the remainder of this section, we drop the explicit dependence on the decoder weights
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}
and use:
\mathcal{L}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}})\equiv\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon.}}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}},{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}})
.

Gradient derivation.

First, we define the Gram matrix of the decoder weights as
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}
. Expanding the MSE term:

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\;(\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})^{\top}(\bm{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})

(61)

\displaystyle=\;\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{x}-2\bm{x}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}

\displaystyle=\;\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{x}-2({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}\bm{x})^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}+{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}.

The variance penalty is simply
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}}\equiv\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}})
.

We first take the derivative with respect to the rates
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}
. Note that
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})=2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}
because
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}
is symmetric.

\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}\mathcal{L}\;=\;-2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}\bm{x}+2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}+{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}}.

(62)

Next, we apply the chain rule to find the gradient with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}
. Since
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}=\exp({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}})
, the Jacobian is diagonal:

\frac{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}\;=\;\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})

(63)

Thus, the gradient
\bm{g}=\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}\mathcal{L}
is:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle\bm{g}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\odot(2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}-2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}\bm{x}+{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}})$}}

(64)

The term
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\odot(\dots)
acts as a multiplicative gate. If a unit is inactive (
\lambda_{k}\approx 0
), its gradient vanishes, leading to the “dead neuron” problem.

Hessian derivation.

Let
\mathbf{v}\;=\;\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}\mathcal{L}\;=\;2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}-2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}\bm{x}+{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{d}}}
. Then the gradient can be written as
\bm{g}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\odot\mathbf{v}
.
To find the Hessian
\mathbf{H}\;=\;\nabla^{2}_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}\mathcal{L}
, we differentiate
\bm{g}
with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}
using the product rule:

\mathbf{H}\;=\;\frac{\partial}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\odot\mathbf{v})\;=\;\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{v})\frac{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}+\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}

(65)

Analyzing the first term:

\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{v})\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})\;=\;\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}\odot\mathbf{v})\;=\;\mathrm{diag}(\bm{g})

(66)

Analyzing the second term:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}\frac{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}}

(67)

\displaystyle=\;(2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}})\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})

Therefore, defining
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\Lambda}}}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})
, the second term becomes:

\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})(2{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}})\mathrm{diag}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}})\;=\;2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\Lambda}}}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\mathbf{G}}}{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\Lambda}}}.

(68)

The exact Hessian is:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\;=\;\mathrm{diag}(\bm{g})\,+\,2{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\Lambda}}}({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}){\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\Lambda}}}$}}

(69)

where the gradient
\bm{g}
is given in
eq.

64
. This expression reveals that the curvature is dominated by the correlation matrix of the features (
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}
), scaled quadratically by the firing rates.

Conclusion.

We have derived the exact analytical gradient and Hessian for the Poisson VAE loss function. These derivations allow us to bypass noisy numerical differentiation.

Appendix F
Statistical analysis of stochastic gradients

In stochastic optimization, we do not have access to the true gradient
\bm{g}^{*}
. Instead, we use an estimator
\hat{\bm{g}}
such that:

\hat{\bm{g}}\;=\;\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b}+\bm{\epsilon},

(70)

where
\bm{b}\;=\;\EE[\hat{\bm{g}}]-\bm{g}^{*}
is the bias, and
\bm{\epsilon}
is zero-mean noise with covariance
\bm{\Sigma}\;=\;\EE[\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}]
.

We wish to define metrics that quantify the quality of this estimator not just by its raw variance, but by its impact on the optimization process. We achieve this by analyzing the expected change in loss after a single gradient step.

F.1
Second-order Taylor expansion

Let the parameter update rule be
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t+1}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t}-\eta\hat{\bm{g}}
. We approximate the reconstruction loss (
eq.

59
) at the new location using a second-order Taylor expansion around
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t}
:

\mathcal{L}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t+1})\;\approx\;\mathcal{L}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t})+({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t+1}-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t+1}-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t})^{\top}\mathbf{H}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t+1}-{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t}).

(71)

Substituting the update rule:

\mathcal{L}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t+1})\;\approx\;\mathcal{L}({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}_{t})-\eta\hat{\bm{g}}^{\top}\bm{g}^{*}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\hat{\bm{g}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\hat{\bm{g}}.

(72)

Taking the expectation over the randomness of the estimator
\hat{\bm{g}}
:

\EE[\mathcal{L}_{t+1}]-\mathcal{L}_{t}\;\approx\;-\eta\EE[\hat{\bm{g}}]^{\top}\bm{g}^{*}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\EE[\hat{\bm{g}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\hat{\bm{g}}].

(73)

The left-hand side,
\EE[\mathcal{L}_{t+1}]-\mathcal{L}_{t}
, quantifies the expected one-step change in loss induced by an update driven by the stochastic gradient
\hat{\bm{g}}
. From the perspective of optimization, we want this quantity to be as negative as possible, which is why it serves as a central object for evaluating the quality of a gradient estimator.

F.2
Decomposing the quadratic penalty

We focus on the quadratic term
\EE[\hat{\bm{g}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\hat{\bm{g}}]
. Substituting
\hat{\bm{g}}\;=\;(\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b})+\bm{\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle\EE[\hat{\bm{g}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\hat{\bm{g}}]

\displaystyle=\;\EE\left[((\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b})+\bm{\epsilon})^{\top}\mathbf{H}((\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b})+\bm{\epsilon})\right]

(74)

\displaystyle=\;(\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b})^{\top}\mathbf{H}(\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b})+2(\bm{g}^{*}+\bm{b})^{\top}\mathbf{H}\underbrace{\EE[\bm{\epsilon}]}_{0}+\EE[\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{\epsilon}]

The term
\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{\epsilon}
is a scalar, so it is equal to its trace. Using the cyclic property of the trace (
\Tr(ABC)=\Tr(BCA)
) and linearity of expectation:

\displaystyle\EE[\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle=\;\EE[\Tr(\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{\epsilon})]

(75)

\displaystyle=\;\EE[\Tr(\mathbf{H}\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}^{\top})]

\displaystyle=\;\Tr(\mathbf{H}\EE[\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}])

\displaystyle=\;\Tr(\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma})

F.3
Definition of energy metrics

Grouping the terms from the expected loss change, we identify two distinct "energy" terms that penalize the optimization based on the local curvature
\mathbf{H}
.

BiasEnergy.

The quadratic cost of the systematic error is:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{BiasEnergy}\;=\;\bm{b}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{b}$}}

(76)

This measures whether the gradient bias points in a direction of high curvature. A biased estimator is acceptable if the bias lies in the null space of the Hessian (flat directions), but catastrophic if it aligns with steep eigenvectors.

NoiseEnergy.

The quadratic cost of the variance is:

{\framebox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{BiasEnergy}\;=\;\Tr(\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma})$}}

(77)

This measures the interaction between noise covariance and curvature. High variance is permissible in flat directions, but noise in stiff directions (high eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
) will cause the optimizer to bounce between valley walls, increasing the expected loss.

F.4
The optimization equation of motion

By combining the linear approximation and the quadratic penalty terms derived above, we arrive at the final expression for the expected change in loss after a single optimization step. This equation serves as the "equation of motion" for the stochastic gradient descent process:

Since our goal is to minimize loss, we desire this total quantity to be as negative as possible. We interpret each term below:

1
The Primary Descent (
-\eta\|\bm{g}^{*}\|^{2}
):

This is the main driver of optimization. It represents the reduction in loss achieved by moving purely in the direction of the steepest descent. It is always negative (beneficial), scaling linearly with the learning rate.

2
Directional Alignment (
-\eta\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{g}^{*}
):

This term measures whether the systematic bias of our estimator works with us or against us. If
\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{g}^{*}>0
(the bias aligns with the true gradient), this term reduces loss further. If
\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{g}^{*}<0
(the bias points uphill), this term fights the descent. If the bias is strong enough, it can completely negate the Primary Descent, causing divergence.

3
The Curvature Speed Limit (
+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\bm{g}^{*\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{g}^{*}
):

This is the penalty for moving too fast in a steep valley. Even with a perfect gradient, if the curvature
\mathbf{H}
is large along the direction of travel, taking a large step
\eta
will overshoot the minimum and land on the opposite wall, increasing the loss. This term dictates the maximum stable learning rate.

4
Drift-Curvature Coupling (
+\eta^{2}\bm{g}^{*\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{b}
):

This is a second-order interaction term. It represents the complexity of moving through a curved landscape while being pushed off-course by bias. It quantifies how the steepness of the terrain amplifies the misalignment errors.

5
BiasEnergy (
+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\bm{b}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\bm{b}
):

This is the “static cost” of being wrong. Even if the true gradient were zero (we are at a stationary point), a non-zero bias would constantly push the parameters up the walls of the loss landscape. In high-curvature directions (large eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
), even a tiny bias incurs a massive penalty here.

6
NoiseEnergy (
+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\Tr(\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma})
):

This is the “thermal cost” of variance. In a convex valley, random jittering does not average out to zero cost; it effectively heats up the system. Because the valley walls curve upward, random steps to the left and right result in a net increase in average height (loss). This term sets the fundamental
noise floor
of the optimization, setting the limit of how close to the optimum we can get with a fixed learning rate.

Conclusion.

By analyzing the second-order Taylor expansion of the expected loss, we identified
\mathrm{BiasEnergy}
(
eq.

76
) and
\mathrm{NoiseEnergy}
(
eq.

77
) as the theoretically justified metrics for evaluating stochastic gradient estimators. These metrics properly weight gradient errors by the geometry of the optimization landscape, offering a more nuanced view than simple Mean Squared Error.

Temperature Annealing.

The temperature

Evaluation protocol.

All models, regardless of their training temperature or annealing schedule, are evaluated at temperature

Evaluating at

\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}),\quad{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}=\exp({\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}\bm{x}).

At
\tau=0

This ensures that our evaluation metrics (ELBO, reconstruction quality, sparsity) reflect the true performance of the learned discrete latent representation rather than artifacts of the training procedure.

Score-based baselines.

Our baseline score function estimator operated only on the reconstruction term of the ELBO objective (

\displaystyle\bar{\ell}_{t}\;

\displaystyle:=\;(1-\alpha)\bar{\ell}_{t-1}\;+\;\alpha\frac{1}{B}\sum^{B}_{b=1}\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\ln p(\bm{x}|\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}}\;

\displaystyle\approx\;\frac{1}{B}\sum^{B}_{b=1}\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left[\ln p(\bm{x}|\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})-\bar{\ell}_{t}\right]\cdot\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}}\ln q(\bm{z}|{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}\bm{x})\;+\;\beta\cdot\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}}D_{\text{KL}}[q(\bm{z}|{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}\bm{x})\|p(\bm{z})].

The above contrasts with the standard score function gradient estimator in variational autoencoders, which takes the form

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}}\;

\displaystyle\approx\;\frac{1}{B}\sum^{B}_{b=1}\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left[\ln\frac{p(\bm{x},\bm{z};{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\theta}}})}{q(\bm{z}|{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}\bm{x};)}\right]\cdot\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}}\ln q(\bm{z}|{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{enc}}\bm{x}).

G.2
Partially observed generalized linear model (POGLM) methods

We evaluate the proposed relaxation methods on a Partially Observable Generalized Linear Model (POGLM) with an adaptive upperbound, following
22
. The POGLM setting provides a controlled benchmark where ground-truth parameters are known, allowing us to directly attribute performance differences to gradient estimation quality rather than confounding factors.

Synthetic dataset.

We generate synthetic spike train data from 10 distinct parameter sets. For each set, the connection weights
w_{n\leftarrow n^{\prime}}
and biases
b_{n}
are sampled i.i.d. from
\mathrm{Uniform}(-2,2)
and
\mathrm{Uniform}(-0.5,0.5)

Model architecture.

The POGLM consists of a variational encoder and a generative decoder:

•

Encoder:
The input (visible neuron spike trains) is passed through a 1D convolutional layer (
C_{\mathrm{in}}=3
,
C_{\mathrm{out}}=2

Eat
{}_{\textsf{sigmoid}}
and
Eat
{}_{\textsf{cubic}}

G.3
Adaptive selection of the upperbound hyperparameter
M

Both
Eat
and
Gsm
methods require providing a hyperparameter
M
, corresponding to the number of exponential samples in
Eat
(
Algorithm

1
), and a truncation upperbound in
Gsm
(
Algorithm

2
).

Rather than setting a globally large enough upperbound for accurate distribution approximation, we can dynamically set the upperbound for each data batch. Specifically, given a significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we seek the upperbound:

M=\lceil\operatorname{invCDF}_{\max(\bm{\lambda})}(1-\alpha)\rceil,

(79)

where
\operatorname{invCDF}_{\max(\bm{\lambda})}
is the inverse cumulative distribution function of the Poisson distribution whose rate parameter
\lambda
is the maximum of the batch, since the largest
\lambda
requires the highest upperbound.

Throughout our experiments, we set
\alpha=10^{-3}
.

G.4
Gradient analysis: additional methodological details

To rigorously quantify the quality of gradient estimators, we isolated the gradient estimation step from the optimization loop. This allowed us to compare estimators against a ground truth without the confounding factors of optimization trajectory or changing model parameters.

Experimental setup.

We extracted the decoder weights
{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.6719,0.8867}{\bm{\Phi}}}
from a fully trained linear
\PP
-VAE model. We selected a fixed batch of
B=90
input samples
\bm{x}
from the validation set (whitened natural image patches). Rather than using the predicted rates from the encoder, we manually fixed the latent firing rates to constant values, sweeping
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}\in\{0.1,0.5,1,2,5,8,10,15,20,30,40,70,100\}
, across all
K=512
dimensions and all batch items. This controlled setting allows us to study estimator performance as a function of the firing rate regime, isolated from encoder variations.

Ground truth computation.

Because the decoder is linear, the reconstruction loss and its derivatives are analytically tractable. For every batch item, we computed the exact gradient
\bm{g}^{*}
and the exact Hessian
\mathbf{H}
with respect to the log-rates
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{u}}}=\ln{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\bm{\lambda}}}
using the closed-form derivations in
appendix

E
(
eqs.

64
and
69
). Note that for a linear decoder, the Hessian is block-diagonal with respect to the batch dimension (samples are independent), allowing efficient per-sample computation.

Gradient Estimation.

For each condition (rate
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}
and temperature
\tau
), we drew
N=100
Monte Carlo samples to form stochastic gradient estimates
\hat{\bm{g}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\bm{g}}_{N}
.

•

Eat
and
Gsm
methods:
Gradients were computed via automatic differentiation.

•

Score function:
We employed a batch-average baseline reduction. The baseline
b_{k}
for latent dimension
k
was calculated as the mean loss across the batch, reducing variance without introducing bias.

Metric Calculation.

All metrics were computed
per batch item

i
and then averaged.

•

Bias and Variance:
The empirical bias
\bm{b}=\bar{\bm{g}}-\bm{g}^{*}
and variance
\bm{\Sigma}
were computed over the
N
samples.

•

Cosine Similarity:
We computed the cosine similarity of the expected gradient (
\mathrm{CosMean}=\cos(\bar{\bm{g}},\bm{g}^{*})
) and the average cosine similarity of individual samples (
\mathrm{CosSample}=\mathbb{E}[\cos(\hat{\bm{g}},\bm{g}^{*})]
).

•

Curvature-Weighted Energy:
As motivated in
appendix

F
, we projected the bias and noise into the geometry of the loss landscape using the exact Hessian
\mathbf{H}_{i}
(per batch item
i
). To make these metrics comparable across different firing rates (where gradients’ magnitudes vary naturally), we normalized them by the
Signal Energy
of the true gradient:

\mathrm{NormalizedBiasEnergy}=\frac{\bm{b}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{b}}{\bm{g}^{*\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{g}^{*}},\quad\mathrm{NormalizedNoiseEnergy}=\frac{\Tr(\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{\Sigma})}{\bm{g}^{*\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{g}^{*}}.

(80)

This normalization provides the interpretable scale used in
fig.

3
, where a value of
\sim\!1.0
implies that the error energy is equivalent to the signal energy driving the optimization.

H.1
Automatic selection of optimal temperature is case-dependent

In the main paper, we saw that
Eat
and
Gsm
methods have different sensitivities to the temperature. While
Eat
{}_{\textsf{cubic}}
was robust, both
Eat
{}_{\textsf{sigmoid}}
and
Gsm
require careful tuning, which is not ideal. Here, we explore whether it is possible to automatically select the temperature hyperparameter for these methods to mitigate this issue.

Existence of the optimal temperature.

Similar to the upperbound (
section

G.3
), we hope to find a clever, adaptive scheme for temperature selection. Therefore, we evaluate the relationship between the optimal
\tau
(that produces the most accurate gradient estimation) and the Poisson rate
\lambda
for different methods under various target functions.

To isolate the performance of gradient estimators from ELBO optimization, we established a benchmark that treats the gradient estimation for the Poisson rate parameter
\lambda
as a regression task. Given a family of test functions
\{f_{k}(z)\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the objective is to estimate the gradient of the expectation with respect to
\lambda

\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}=\nabla_{\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)}[f_{k}(z)]

(81)

Here,
\lambda
is the learnable variable. For an input
\lambda
and temperature
\tau

\hat{g}
using
Gsm
and the EAT Sigmoid/Cubic methods.

We quantify estimation quality by comparing the estimated value
\hat{g}
with the theoretical ground truth
g_{true}
. In the Poisson distribution, the theoretical gradient of any objective function can be expanded as:

\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\sum_{z=0}^{\infty}f(z)\cdot p(z|\lambda)\cdot\left(\frac{z}{\lambda}-1\right)

(82)

To compute this, we use an upperbound for approximation, reducing the infinite to a finite sum
\sum_{z=0}^{K}
. In our experiments, we set this upperbound to
K=\lfloor\lambda\rfloor+20
, which is sufficient to cover most of the Poisson probability mass. The resulting value is treated as the Exact Gradient
g_{true}
. Finally, we adopt the Mean Absolute Error (
\mathrm{MAE}
) as our evaluation metric.

f(z)
exhibiting distinct properties (linear, polynomial, non-linear, trigonometric, and parameter-dependent) to evaluate estimator robustness. The polynomials are
z
,
\sqrt{z}
,
z^{2}
,
z^{1.5}-2z
. Non-linear/trigonometric functions are
\cos^{2}(z)
,
\text{sigmoid}(z)
. The above functions only depend on samples. The parameter-dependent function
z^{2}/\lambda
, this function tests whether the estimator correctly back-propagates gradients when the loss function explicitly depends on the optimization parameter
\lambda
.

Hyperparameters: Rate (
{\color[rgb]{0.9297,0.1094,0.1406}{\lambda}}
) is 0.1 to 20 (total 10). Temperature
\tau
: 0.01 to 10 (total 156). Monte Carlo Samples (
N_{mc}
): 1 to 500 (total 9). Adaptive upperbound precision: 0.01.

We tested the gradient estimation error with temperature
\tau
for each test function. Then we extracted the optimal temperature
\tau^{*}
that minimizes
\mathrm{MAE}
and plotted the relationship between
\tau^{*}
and the input rate
\lambda
.
Figure

8

\tau

\lambda

As shown in the figure, we can see that for each test function, the variation of

\lambda

H.3
Generalizability of
Gsm
in the over/under dispersed regimes

While the main text focuses on the Poisson distribution, the
Gsm
method can be applied to arbitrary discrete distributions supported on
\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 0}
. In this section, we describe the formulation for applying our relaxation methods to the Negative Binomial (over-dispersed) and COM-Poisson (under-dispersed) distributions.

Definition of dispersion.

Dispersion quantifies the variability of a count variable
z
relative to its mean. It is defined as the ratio
F=\mathrm{Var}(z)/{\mathbb{E}[z]}
, i.e., the Fano factor. Distributions are categorized as:

•

Equal-dispersion
(
F=1
): Variance equals the mean (e.g., Poisson).

•

Over-dispersion
(
F>1
): Variance exceeds the mean (e.g., Negative Binomial).

•

Under-dispersion
(
F<1
): Variance is less than the mean (e.g., Binomial, COM-Poisson).

Dispersion in the Poisson distribution.

For
z\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\lambda)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[z]=\lambda
and
\mathrm{Var}(z)=\lambda
, yielding
D=1
. While standard, this strictly equal dispersion is often insufficient for modeling neural data, which frequently exhibits time-varying Fano factors distinct from unity
(
12
)
.

Modeling Over-dispersion: Negative Binomial.

To model over-dispersed data, we utilize the Negative Binomial (NB) distribution, parameterized by shape
r>0
and success probability
\mu\in(0,1)
. The mean is
\mathbb{E}[z]=r(1-\mu)/\mu
and the variance is
\mathrm{Var}(z)=r(1-\mu)/{\mu^{2}}
. The dispersion is
F=1/\mu
. Since
p<1
, we always have
F>1
.

Method 1: Two-step relaxation (Gamma-Poisson Mixture).

The NB distribution can be represented as a Gamma-Poisson mixture
(
39
)
. If
z\sim\operatorname{NB}(r,\mu)
, it is equivalent to the hierarchical sampling procedure:

\lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}\left(r,\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right),\quad z\sim\mathcal{P}\mathrm{ois}(\lambda).

(83)

This representation allows us to apply the
Eat
estimator by differentiating through the Gamma sample (using standard pathwise derivatives for continuous variables) and then applying
Eat
to the conditional Poisson step. We summarize this in
algorithm

3
.

Algorithm 3

Two-step relaxation of the NB distribution (Mixture)

Input:
NB parameters
r,\mu

Output:
Relaxed event count
z\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}

\beta\,\leftarrow\,\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}
{
Calculate rate parameter for Gamma
}

\lambda\,\leftarrow\,\operatorname{Gamma}(r,\beta).\operatorname{rsample}()
{
Sample continuous rate
\lambda
(reparameterized)
}

z\,\leftarrow\,\textsc{Eat}(\lambda)
{
Sample soft Poisson counts given
\lambda
}

Return

z

Method 2: Direct truncation with
Gsm
.

The
Gsm
method does not require the mixture representation. For any discrete distribution (including NB) with support on
\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 0}
, we can truncate the distribution at a sufficiently large upper bound
M
and apply the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation directly to the log-probabilities. This bypasses the intermediate Gamma sampling step.

Modeling under-dispersion: COM-Poisson.

Standard Poisson processes (and thus the standard
Eat
algorithm) cannot model under-dispersion (
F<1
) without modifying the inter-arrival distribution (e.g., to an Erlang distribution), which complicates the inverse-CDF derivation.
In contrast,
Gsm
handles under-dispersion natively by defining the probability mass function directly. We utilize the Conway-Maxwell-Poisson (COM-Poisson) distribution, defined by rate
\lambda
and the inverse dispersion parameter
\nu=\frac{1}{F}
:

P(z;\lambda,\nu)=\frac{\lambda^{z}}{(z!)^{\nu}Z(\lambda,\nu)},

(84)

where
Z(\lambda,\nu)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{(j!)^{\nu}}
is the partition function. Because
Gsm
is invariant to normalization constants (due to the softmax), we can sample from the COM-Poisson without explicitly computing
Z(\lambda,\nu)
, provided we truncate at
M
. The logits are simply:

\ln\pi_{m}=m\ln\lambda-\nu\ln(m!),\quad\forall m\in\{0,\dots,M-1\}.

(85)

These logits are passed directly to the Gumbel-Softmax sampler.
