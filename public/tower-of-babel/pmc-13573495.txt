Spatial ecotype in tumor immune exclusion: from spatial architecture to therapeutic strategies
Authors: Ming Ying, Lei Zhang, Mengwei Jia, Xiaofei Sun, Yibo Su, Chengcheng Liu, Ming Gong, Tao Sun, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tumor microenvironment (TME) exhibits widespread immunophenotypic heterogeneity. Based on the spatial immune contexture, tumor immune profiles can be classified as immune-inflamed, excluded, or desert. Immune-excluded tumors, a distinct tumor immune phenotype, are characterized by the presence of immune cells (especially CD8+ T cells) near tumors but a lack of direct physical contact between immune and tumor cells. Accumulating evidence indicates that tumor immune exclusion is a spatially organized and actively maintained microenvironmental state associated with poor prognosis, impaired T cell infiltration, and resistance to immunotherapy. However, the biological mechanisms and spatial profiling underlying this phenotype remain unclear. Advances in spatial omics technologies and analytical tools have enabled the dissection of the complex spatial architecture of immune-excluded tumors. In this review, we describe recent insights into the core cellular subsets and spatial interaction networks of immune-excluded tumors, incorporating the spatial immune contexture, to provide new theoretical foundations and intervention strategies targeting spatial ecotypes to enhance T cell infiltration and sensitize immune-excluded tumors to immunotherapy. Collectively, these findings support a shift from cell-centric models towards ecotype-centered frameworks in which spatially coordinated cellular alliances govern immune accessibility and therapeutic response. Furthermore, we discuss current challenges in this field, including standardization of spatial multi-omics data integration, real-time monitoring of dynamic spatiotemporal evolution, and optimization of clinical translation pathways. Future investigations should incorporate long-term sampling, organoid models, and basket trial designs to enable precise immune-intervention strategies based on spatial ecotypes.Keywords: Immune exclusion, Spatial ecotype, Tumor microenvironment, Spatial omics, Immunotherapy resistance

Introduction

Tumor immune exclusion is a recognized phenomenon defined by the absence of lymphocytes in the tumor core or stroma, which is associated with poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy [1–5]. Several key drivers contribute to tumor immune exclusion, including (i) physical and biochemical barriers formed by specific stromal cells (especially cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)) and myeloid cells that hinder the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells into the tumor core [6, 7], (ii) chemokine (e.g., C-X-C motif ligand (CXCL)9) gradients that repel or misdirect lymphocytes and immunosuppressive cytokine signaling (e.g., transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)), and (iii) inhibitory interactions (e.g., CD47/signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα)) [1–5]. However, the spatial architecture of immune-excluded tumors remains poorly understood. Importantly, immune exclusion cannot be fully explained by the presence or abundance of individual cell populations alone. Emerging evidence suggests that spatially organized multicellular ecosystems govern immune-cell localization, functional paralysis, and therapeutic resistance [8].

With the rapid development of spatial omics technologies in recent years, spatial profiling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) has gradually entered the era of subcellular resolution [9–11]. The research focus has shifted from simple cell composition analysis to a spatial ecotype-centered functional unit [9–11]. Increasingly diverse spatial technology platforms, such as imaging mass cytometry (IMC) [12], digital spatial profiling (DSP) [13], and Stereo-seq [14], enable the three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of cell types, functional states, and intercellular molecular interaction networks while preserving the in-situ tissue architecture. These technological breakthroughs have compensated for the loss of spatial information in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), allowing the accurate delineation of the spatial distribution of specific cell subsets and their spatial interactions [15, 16].

In cancer, multicellular ecosystems composed of immune, stromal, and/or tumor cells form a dynamic signaling hub, which have a remarkable impact on tumor progression, immune escape, and treatment response [8]. Spatial ecotypes in cancer refer to spatially dependent multicellular ecosystems within the TME, characterized by the co-localization of multiple cell types in specific spatial locations, along with stable transcriptional profiles and functional properties [17]. This concept draws on the “ecotypes” term from ecology, which refers to spatially dependent subpopulations of species, and introduces the term “ecotypes” for correlated transcriptional states in the TME [17]. Advances in spatial omics technologies and algorithmic tools have enabled the systematic decoding of spatial ecotypes [10, 11, 18, 19]. These advances have fundamentally shifted the analytical paradigm of tumor immunology from static compositional profiling towards spatially resolved ecosystem analysis, enabling interrogation of how cellular neighborhoods collectively shape immune accessibility. However, there remains a lack of systematic understanding of spatial ecotypes in immune-excluded tumors.

In this review, we outline tumor immune exclusion and the latest advances in spatial omics technologies for TME analysis. We focus on key cell subsets, their spatial colocalization and interactions that drive tumor immune exclusion, and potential intervention strategies for managing immune-excluded tumors. Additionally, we discuss pan-cancer conserved spatial ecotypes and their clinical implications. Finally, we address the major challenges in this field and potential solutions.

Overview of immune-excluded phenotype in cancer

Definition and key characteristics of immune-inflamed, -desert, and -excluded phenotypes

The spatial architecture of the TME comprises three functionally distinct compartments: tumor core, invasive margin, and tumor stroma [20]. The spatial distribution pattern of immune cells, rather than their overall quantity, is a key determinant of immunotherapy efficacy. Based on the quantity and spatial localization of lymphocytes, tumor immune profiles can be classified as immune-inflamed, -desert, and -excluded phenotypes (Fig. 1) [21, 22]. These distinct spatial immune architectures are not merely histopathological patterns but have profound functional consequences for antitumor immunity and therapeutic responsiveness. The success of cancer immunotherapy is strongly influenced by tumor immune phenotypes [23–25].

Immune-inflamed (also called “hot” or “immune-infiltrated”) tumors account for up to 50% of all human tumors and are characterized by lymphocyte infiltration in both the tumor core and stroma, as well as the proximity of lymphocytes to tumor cells [21, 22]. Patients with immune-inflamed phenotype typically present with favorable responses to immunotherapy [26–29]. The immune-inflamed phenotype is associated with increased genomic instability, enhanced antigenicity, accumulation of proinflammatory cytokines, and activation of the interferon response [22]. The immune-desert (also called “cold” or “ignored”) phenotype is defined by the absence of lymphocytes in the tumor core or stroma [21, 22]. Patients with immune-desert tumors usually respond poorly to immunotherapy [30–32]. This phenotype is associated with defects in the antigen presentation machinery, weakened interferon response, and an expansion in immunosuppressive cells [22].

The immune-excluded phenotype is fundamentally distinct from the above two phenotypes. A consensus definition for tumor immune exclusion was derived using the modified Delphi methodology in 2023 [33]. This phenotype typically refers to the presence of immune cells in tumors but a lack of direct physical contact between tumor and immune cells [33]. Driven by the dual effects of driver mutations and tissue-of-origin microenvironments, distinct cancer types evolve unique tumor immune exclusion [34–36]. CRC shows a 70–75% immune-excluded phenotype and a 10% inflamed phenotype [37]. Conversely, NSCLC comprises a 40% immune-excluded phenotype and a 30–35% immune-inflamed phenotype [37]. Distinct breast cancer subtypes show heterogeneous immune phenotypes: the basal-like subtype is the most prevalent among immune-inflamed breast cancers, followed by HER2 and Luminal-B tumors [38, 39]. About 28% and 26% of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) show immune-desert and -excluded phenotypes, respectively [40]. Furthermore, metastatic TNBC differs from the pattern of primary tumors, showing a higher proportion of immune-excluded (41%) and immune-desert (37%) phenotypes [40]. The immunotherapy efficacy of immune-excluded tumors is generally lower than that of immune-inflamed tumors [36, 41, 42]. However, some studies indicate that the prognosis of immune-excluded tumors is even worse than that of immune-desert tumors [43]. Collectively, as a unique tumor immune phenotype, tumor immune exclusion has significant clinical implications [44–46].

In immune-excluded tumors, immune cells persist and remain in an active transcriptional state. However, because of limited contact with tumor cells, they cannot achieve effective immunosurveillance and tumor clearance [1, 47, 48]. This microenvironment creates a permissive niche in which tumor cells can persist under immune pressure. This immune-excluded niche establishes a permissive and spatially insulated microenvironment in which tumor cells evade effective immune surveillance while remaining protected from immunotherapeutic pressure, eventually resulting in immunotherapy resistance [44, 49, 50]. The determinants of immune exclusion can be broadly classified into three categories: physical, functional, and dynamic barriers [51]. By remodeling extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffold components (e.g., collagen, fibronectin, and chitosan) into a denser, more organized peritumoral structure, physical barriers are established that impede immune cell infiltration and restrict drug penetration and delivery [52–55]. For functional barriers, T cells can reach the tumor, but metabolic barriers, soluble factors, or tumor cell-intrinsic signals prevent them from penetrating deeply and spreading to the tumor core. Dynamic barriers may not exist in the baseline condition but form only after contact between tumor and immune cells [51]. Despite this, the biological mechanisms underlying tumor immune exclusion remain poorly understood.

Tumor-cell intrinsic drivers of immune exclusion

Tumor cells utilize intrinsic genetic and epigenetic mechanisms to spatially exclude T cells from the tumors, contributing to poor immunotherapy efficacy [56] (Fig. 2). Tumor-intrinsic WNT/β-catenin signaling is the first oncogenic pathway demonstrated to drive immune exclusion and resistance to immunotherapy across multiple cancer types (e.g., melanoma, colorectal cancer (CRC), and ovarian cancer) [57–61]. Activation of WNT/β-catenin signaling in melanoma cells inhibits the transcription and subsequent secretion of chemokines (especially C–C motif chemokine ligand (CCL)4), thereby blocking the recruitment of downstream CD103+ dendritic cells (DCs) [57]. The activation of this pathway also induces ATF3 transcription, which in turn suppresses CCL4 transcription as well as other effects. This results in impaired antigen presentation and insufficient infiltration of effector T cells within the tumor parenchyma, eventually forming an immune exclusion barrier [57]. Preclinical research has demonstrated that inhibition of WNT/β-catenin signaling improves anti-PD-1 efficacy and overcomes resistance in multiple cancer types (e.g., CRC [62], glioblastomas [63], and breast cancer [64]). Despite this, clinical trials combining WNT/β-catenin signaling-targeted therapy with immunotherapy are still relatively limited. Interferon γ (IFNγ)-mediated MHC class I (MHC-I) antigen presentation is the core of antitumor immune response [65]. Defects in the IFNγ pathway, often caused by JAK1/2 mutations, disrupt tumor antigen presentation to T cells and restrict immune cell infiltration, leading to resistance to immunotherapy across cancer types [66–68].

Overexpression of oncogenes (e.g., MYC [69–71]), genetic mutations of oncogenes (e.g., KRAS [72], TP53 [73], and isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) [74–76]), and loss of tumor suppressors (e.g., phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) [77–80], STK11 [81, 82], and KEAP1 [83, 84]) promote immune exclusion and immunotherapy resistance via multiple mechanisms: (i) downregulating MHC-I expression to reduce the recognition ability of T cells for tumor cells; (ii) upregulating immune checkpoint ligands (e.g., PD-L1) to inhibit T cell activation and recruitment; (iii)‌ inducing the secretion of immunosuppressive factors to block the infiltration of T cells and DCs and recruit immunosuppressive cells; (iv) inhibiting chemokine secretion to hinder T cell chemotaxis; (v) promoting glycolysis and lactate production to inhibit T cell metabolism and effector function.

Epigenetic silencing, mediated by histone methylation, histone acetylation, and O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promotor methylation, is implicated in tumor immune exclusion. As an epigenetic checkpoint, amplification of H3K9 methyltransferase SETDB1 correlates with immune exclusion and resistance to immunotherapy across human tumors [85, 86]. Furthermore, NSD1 (H3K36 methyltransferase)-deficient squamous cell carcinomas show immune exclusion, impaired interferon response via silencing innate immunity genes (e.g., IFNLR1), and reduced levels of chemokines CXCL9/10, and thus do not respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [87, 88]. Inhibiting KDM2A, a major lysine demethylase for H3K36, can restore T cell infiltration in NSD1-deficient tumors [88]. Increasing H3K27 acetylation via inhibition of histone deacetylase 8 (HDAC8; a H3K27-specific isozyme) can reactivate the production of T cell-trafficking chemokines by hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, ultimately reversing T cell exclusion and eliciting potent and durable response to ICIs [89]. MGMT-silenced pancreatic tumors show immune exclusion [90], but the mechanism remains unclear. Among patients with MGMT-silenced microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC, those with the tumor immune exclusion phenotype usually do not respond to chemo-immunotherapy [91]. Spatial immune architecture is thus a potential predictor of response to chemo-immunotherapy [91]. Collectively, these findings reveal that tumor-intrinsic genetic and epigenetic drivers play a key role in tumor immune exclusion, providing a rationale for precise antitumor treatments.

Spatial omics technologies transform the analytical paradigm of tumor immune exclusion

Due to low spatial resolution, bulk transcriptomics and immunohistochemistry (IHC) cannot distinguish the margin-enriched, core-depleted immune-excluded phenotype from uniformly distributed immune-inflamed or -desert phenotypes (Fig. 3A). This may lead to misclassification of tumor immune phenotypes. When exploring cellular heterogeneity with scRNA-seq, the spatial context of cell populations is lost [16]. Furthermore, scRNA-seq can only indirectly infer intercellular interactions, often neglecting the role of spatial ecotypes in immune-excluded tumors.

To overcome these limitations, spatial omics has emerged as a technology that precisely localizes cells within tissues and quantitatively measures gene expression in situ (Fig. 3B) [92]. Extending beyond single-cell profiling, spatial omics offers a new perspective on spatially resolved interactions among cells within the TME. To date, spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics have been widely applied in cancer research [93–95]. Spatial omics has deepened our understanding of cancer biology, revealing how tumor cells and the TME are organized, interact, and evolve. Because each spatial omics technology captures only a single layer of omics information, spatial multi-omics has been developed to enable simultaneous or sequential analysis of multiple spatial omics datasets [96]. Spatial transcriptomics

Because immune exclusion is fundamentally a spatial phenomenon, technologies capable of preserving tissue architecture while resolving molecular programs are essential for decoding the cellular logic of immune-excluded tumors. In recent years, high-resolution spatial transcriptomics has advanced rapidly, enabling the dissection of the spatial arrangement of cell populations. The TME is one of the research fields for in situ gene expression profiling while preserving spatial information [109]. Spatial transcriptomic techniques can be categorized into sequencing-based or imaging-based methods.

Sequencing-based methods

The mainstream sequencing-based spatial transcriptomic platforms, including Visium [97], Visium HD [98], Slide-seq [99], and Stereo-seq [14], integrate spatial barcoding with whole-transcriptome capture, enabling the localization of gene expression from multicellular to subcellular scales. Visium (55 μm DNA-barcoded spots) captures transcriptomic data from multiple cells per spot, with high scalability but limited spatial resolution [97]. In contrast, Visium HD (10 × Genomics) enables subcellular resolution (2 × 2 μm). Despite this, higher resolution usually leads to reduced capture efficiency and increased technical noise. Therefore, careful optimization and computational correction are crucial. Slide-seq (such as Slide-seqV2) uses DNA-barcoded bead arrays to capture mRNA at 10-μm resolution, enabling precise spatial reconstruction and mapping of complex tissues [110]. The Stereo-seq uses DNA nanoball (DNB) array technology, achieving an ultra-high resolution of 500 nm over a centimeter-scale field of view. Its workflow uses spatial enhanced resolution sequencing and integrates efficient computational frameworks (e.g., SAW and FaST) to address massive data processing challenges, enabling spatial reconstruction at the single-cell and even subcellular level across human tumors [14, 111–114]. Another sequencing-based spatial transcriptomic product is the GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP), which integrates immunofluorescence with optical barcoding technology [100]. This platform uses gene-specific probes linked to unique barcodes on tissue sections. After staining with fluorescent antibodies, regions of interest (ROIs) are selected to collect barcodes, construct libraries, and perform sequencing. Compared to array-based platforms, GeoMx DSP accommodates more samples per section and can be customized using specific probes. Sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics has remarkable advantages, including wide applicability, cost-efficiency, and scalability for high-throughput research. However, the detection sensitivity of sequencing-based methods is relatively low and they cannot reach the resolution of imaging-based methods. The lower resolution indicates that gene expression is usually captured from multicellular “spots”, creating mixed signals that mask potential single‐cell heterogeneity. Therefore, improving resolution and transcript capture efficiency is the key priority for advancing sequencing-based methods.

Imaging-based methods

Imaging-based spatial transcriptomic technologies, e.g., in situ hybridization and in situ sequencing, can directly visualize transcripts in the in-situ tissue, thereby achieving high spatial resolution. Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) uses five fluorescent markers per DNA or RNA molecule to image probe-labeled transcripts. However, spectral overlap limits the number of detectable genes. Multiple rounds of sequential hybridization, such as sequential fluorescence in situ hybridization (seqFISH) with color barcodes [103] and multiplexed error-robust FISH (MERFISH) with binary barcodes [102], can overcome this limitation. The CosMx Spatial Molecular Imager (SMI) can resolve RNA and protein expression at the single-molecule level [101]. The process involves tissue permeabilization, probe hybridization, slide assembly and insertion into the SMI, and the addition of fluorescently labeled secondary probes. CosMx SMI can simultaneously image and quantitatively analyze over 1000 RNAs and more than 64 proteins at the subcellular level. In situ sequencing, an imaging-based method, enables targeted in situ gene expression profiling. This method involves hybridizing padlock probes to transcripts, ligating the probe ends, amplifying the products, hybridizing fluorescent probes to the rolling circle amplification products, and performing iterative imaging. The Xenium in situ platform enables in situ localization analysis of hundreds of genes at subcellular resolution [104]. However, although these imaging-based methods achieve subcellular resolution, they are limited to smaller ROIs and require predefined gene panels.

Recently, several benchmark studies have systematically and quantitatively compared different spatial transcriptomics platforms, providing valuable information for understanding the performance trade-offs. For instance, benchmarking efforts have systematically compared 11 sequencing-based methods, revealing their differences in capture sensitivity, spatial resolution, and molecule lateral diffusion [115]. Furthermore, a systematic benchmarking of subcellular spatial transcriptomic platforms across human tumors has underscored their strengths and limitations in terms of capture sensitivity, specificity, cell segmentation and annotation, spatial clustering, and concordance with adjacent CODEX [116]. A comparation analysis of imaging-based methods has revealed that probe design and panel size are crucial for determining the data quality [117]. By comparing Xenium and CosMx platforms, it has been noted that platform selection should be matched with the research objective, with a priority given to sensitivity and dynamic range, in order to reliably detect gene expression patterns and cell types [118]. These benchmark studies provide practical guidance for selection of appropriate methods in specific research contexts. With high spatial resolution and sensitivity, imaging-based methods are suitable for analyzing subcellular transcript localization, identifying low-abundance genes, and characterizing fine cell subtypes. Given that sequencing-based methods provide whole-transcriptome coverage, they can be extended to large-scale sample cohorts and are suitable for studies that focus on global gene expression patterns and TME characteristics without the need for ultra-high resolution.

Spatial proteomics

Spatial proteomics can achieve single-cell resolution while preserving tissue morphology, and identify cells and structures using canonical protein markers [119, 120]. Representative spatial proteomic platforms include IMC [12], PhenoCycler [105], COMET [107], CosMx [106], and Singular G4X. IMC, a mass spectrometry-based technique, uses antibodies conjugated to metal isotopes and measures proteins via laser ablation and time-of-flight mass spectrometry, avoiding spectral overlap [121]. However, its spatial resolution is slightly lower than that of optical imaging-based platforms, which is approximately 1 μm. The PhenoCycler, CosMx, and Singular G4X platforms combine highly specific oligonucleotide-barcoded antibodies with cyclic fluorophore-targeted imaging, enabling multiplex protein detection with spatial resolution. The COMET platform uses only two label-free primary antibodies per cycle and performs iterative staining and elution, reducing potential cross-reactions and steric hindrance. Despite the rapid development of spatial proteomics technology, it is crucial to improve antibody validation across diverse tissue types, design robust and scalable panels, and adopt more efficient multiplex detection methods.

Spatial metabolomics

Spatial metabolomics can directly map small molecules on tissue sections using surface ionization and laser ablation strategies [122, 123]. MALDI imaging mass spectrometry is one of the most widely used mass spectrometry imaging techniques in spatial metabolomics, supporting both untargeted and targeted metabolite detection [108]. This technique uses a chemical matrix to promote ionization, enabling the acquisition of mass spectrometry data from intact tissue sections with a spatial resolution of 10 ~ 50 μm. Emerging imaging mass spectrometry techniques are advancing rapidly. Desorption electrospray ionization (DESI) uses an electrostatically charged solvent spray for ambient ionization [124]. Because it requires minimal sample pretreatment, it is well-suited for rapid clinical applications. Secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) uses a focused ion beam to sputter and ionize molecules from the tissue surface, achieving submicron spatial resolution [125]. However, the molecular coverage is relatively limited. Improving ionization efficiency, metabolic coverage, and spatial resolution will further facilitate the application of spatial metabolomics.

Integration of spatial multi-omics

Simultaneous or sequential profiling of diverse spatial omics on the same tissue section integrates the advantages of each spatial omics and compensates for the disadvantages, providing a revolutionary strategy for spatially decoding immune-excluded tumors.

Current spatial multi-omics platforms span several major categories. Transcriptome-protein co-detection technologies, such as MERSCOPE, Xenium, and CosMx, quantify RNA and proteins with spatial resolution. In addition to these co-detection platforms, several simultaneous spatial multi-omics platforms have been proposed. Spatial CITE-seq [126], spatial Multi-Omics (SM-Omics) [127], and DBiT-seq [128] enable simultaneous spatial detection of RNA and proteins on the same tissue section. A spatial multimodal analysis integrates histology, mass spectrometry imaging, and spatial transcriptomics to precisely quantify RNA and metabolites within tissues [129]. This workflow is compatible with commercially available Visium glass slides. SpatialMETA, a conditional variational autoencoder-based framework, enables cross-modal and cross-sample integration of spatial transcriptomic and metabolomic data [130]. Multi-omics in situ pairwise sequencing (MiP-seq) simultaneously detects DNA, RNA, proteins, and other biomolecules at subcellular resolution [131]. Compared with other in situ sequencing technologies, MiP-seq improves decoding accuracy and reduces sequencing and imaging costs while maintaining detection effectiveness for gene mutations, allele-specific expression, and RNA modifications. Spatial-Mux-seq enables simultaneous profiling of histone modifications, chromatin accessibility, the whole transcriptome, and protein panels at the tissue scale and cellular level with spatial resolution [132]. In addition to simultaneous detection, several platforms support sequential multi-omics analyses on the same tissue section. For instance, Singular G4X enables spatial analysis of ~ 350 genes, sequential detection of fifteen proteins, and H&E staining. Collectively, spatial multi-omics profiling of the same tissue section not only retains spatial information but also expands molecular-level insights.

Analytical tools for spatially resolved intercellular communications

Inferring intercellular communications from spatial transcriptomics

Traditional experimental techniques, e.g., fluorescence in situ hybridization and immunofluorescence, play a key role in hypothesis-driven studies of intercellular communications via co-localization analyses. Based on spatial transcriptomics, increasing analytical tools have been proposed to infer intercellular communications mainly via ligand-receptor interactions (Table 2). These tools improve the prediction accuracy of ligand-receptor interactions by combining spatial coordinates with gene expression data. They detect local signals that clustering analysis cannot, and they visualize communication hotspots at single-cell resolution, revealing potential intercellular crosstalk within the TME.

According to algorithmic methods: these intercellular communication tools can be generally classified into machine learning-based methods and statistical modeling methods. In the field of machine learning, deep learning (e.g., DeepLinc [146]) and optimal transport models (e.g., SpaOTsc [152]) stand out particularly. As a statistical modeling method, NiCo integrates single-cell and spatial transcriptomics data to assess the influence of the TME on cell states [136]. Spatial localization is crucial for intercellular communication analysis, while spatial transcriptomics data provide the required transcript localization information. Analytical tools, e.g., DeepLinc [146], SpaOTsc [152], and MISTy [140], can analyze both adjacent and distal intercellular communications. Most tools assume that the interaction probability decays with distance, but they do not strictly restrict the interaction by proximity. Several tools, e.g., StereoSiTE [138] and DeepTalk [134], focus on adjacent intercellular communications, which limit their analysis to proximal intercellular communications within 200 μm. Similarly, SPACE identifies intercellular communications only among neighboring cells via constructing a spatial adjacency graph [135]. Paired ligand-receptor expression constitutes the basis of intercellular communication analysis. Most tools depend on curated ligand-receptor interaction databases or infer de novo these interactions. Many tools, e.g., NicheCompass [142] and HoloNet [155], integrate ligand-receptor interaction information from public databases. Analytical tools, e.g., DeepLinc [146], NiCo [136], and MISTy [140], reconstruct intercellular networks without prior ligand-receptor interaction data, providing valuable information for identifying previously uncharacterized interactions. In addition to proximity and ligand-receptor interactions, some analytical tools, e.g., SpaOTsc [152], MISTy [140], and SpaOTsc [152], integrate downstream regulatory signals (including transcription factor activity and target-gene expression), thereby better estimating ligand-receptor interaction strength and functional consequences.

Despite the importance of intercellular communication analysis in interpreting spatial transcriptomics data, no benchmark framework specifically for spatial transcriptomics-based intercellular communication tools has been established. Future research should focus on avoiding false positive results, enhancing adaptability across different spatial transcriptomics resolutions, and combining spatial multi-omics data to improve the detection accuracy and interpretation of intercellular communications.

Spatial 3D technologies

Spatial technologies primarily provide 2D information, limiting a comprehensive understanding of intercellular interactions and neighboring structures. Beyond 2D, 3D spatial analysis is a new research frontier, opening a new avenue for improving cancer diagnosis and treatment [158]. 3D spatial molecular analysis is achieved through serial-section reconstruction [159–161] or non-disruptive volumetric imaging [162–164]. Serial-section reconstruction involves iterative serial sectioning, molecular analysis of each section, and 3D model reconstruction via image co-registration. MERSCOPE and Xenium platforms can achieve 3D reconstruction. Analytical tools, such as Spa3D [165] and So3D [166], reconstruct 3D structures from serial sections, enabling mapping of intercellular interaction networks at the organ scale. These structured communication units indicate that intervention strategies should target cellular alliances at specific anatomical sites rather than generalized molecular targets. In contrast to serial-section reconstruction, non-disruptive volumetric imaging preserves tissue integrity and uses techniques for tissue transparency and permeability to enable deep-tissue staining, confocal or light-sheet microscopy imaging, and label-free phase imaging [162, 164, 167].

Emerging analytical tools for inferring spatial ecotypes in cancer

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated the implications of spatial ecotypes in tumor progression and treatment response [8, 168]. Several analytical tools, especially Spatial EcoTyper, Liquid EcoTyper [8], CANVAS [168], and SSpMosaic [169], have been proposed for inferring spatial ecotypes from spatial omics data mainly via consensus clustering analysis. Recently, a machine-learning framework is established for solid and liquid profiling of spatial ecotypes in the TME, termed Spatial EcoTyper and Liquid EcoTyper [8]. This method integrates data fusion, statistical learning, and deep learning to overcome obstacles encountered when detecting and restoring spatial ecotypes in genomic platforms and bodily compartments. CANVAS is an artificial intelligence (AI) platform that infers spatial ecotype from spatial proteomics and H&E histopathology [168]. Based on spatial proteomics of over 18 million cells, ten spatial ecotypes with unique spatial topologies of tumor, immune, and stromal architectures are characterized in the TME of lung cancer [168]. These spatial ecotypes include tumor core, macrophage-enriched niche, B cell niche, stromal-fibrotic domain, plasma cell cluster, neutrophil-rich zone, tumor-immune interface, T cell compartment, pan-immune activation zone, and vasculature niche. By applying to H&E slides of over 5,000 patients from nine cancer types, CANVAS bridges spatial proteomics and histopathology, with a potential for predicting prognosis and immunotherapy response.

Collectively, these rapidly evolving spatial omics technologies provide powerful tools for dissecting the complex spatial ecotypes in immune-excluded tumors and for revealing spatial organization and intercellular interactions within the TME. These technologies enable immune exclusion to be investigated not as a static phenotype but as a dynamically organized spatial ecosystem governed by multicellular interaction networks.

Decoding the core cellular subsets and their interactions in immune-excluded tumors: insights from spatial profiling

The TME is organized into distinct spatial ecotypes with significant biological and clinical implications. Spatial omics offers a new perspective on the spatial architecture and functional dynamics of immune-excluded tumors. In this section, we discuss the cellular organization and communication networks of immune-excluded tumors using spatial profiling. Immune exclusion emerges from coordinated interactions among stromal, myeloid, endothelial, and lymphoid compartments rather than from isolated dysfunction within a single cellular population.

Cancer-associated fibroblast-mediated tumor immune exclusion

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are a key component of profibrotic spatial ecotypes through physical barrier formation, molecular modulation, and metabolic remodeling [170, 171]. Physically, CAFs secrete many ECM components, e.g., collagen, hyaluronic acid, and fibronectin, forming a highly dense meshwork [172–174]. They also secrete ECM proteins (e.g., periostin (POSTN), fibroblast activation protein (FAP), collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), and actin alpha 2 (ACTA2)) to assemble dense ECM [175]. The dense physical barriers prevent drug penetration and T cell infiltration and promote CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion [176–179]. Molecularly, CAFs produce immunosuppressive molecules that create an immunosuppressive niche [180–182]. Furthermore, CAFs extensively interact with immune cells, eliciting immunosuppressive signaling and forming an immune-excluded niche [183]. Metabolically, hypoxia in myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) induces lactic acid secretion and nutrient competition, hampering T cell access to metabolites and promoting dysfunction [175].

With the advancement of spatial omics technologies, it has been demonstrated that CAF-driven tumor immune exclusion exhibits spatiotemporal heterogeneity. For instance, two distinct CAF subsets contribute to T cell exclusion during lung cancer progression: MYH11+ CAFs appear in early-stage tumors, forming a single-cell layer that lines tumor aggregates, whereas FAP+ CAFs appear in more advanced tumors, forming patches within the stroma or multiple layers surrounding the tumor nest [176]. These two CAF subsets are key factors in primary resistance to ICIs in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC): FAP+ CAFs correlate with an inflammatory response and CD8+ T cell exhaustion, whereas MYH11+ CAFs correlate with CD4+ regulatory T cell (Treg) infiltration and an immunosuppressive TME [184]. Spatial analysis of cervical cancer has demonstrated that MYH11+ CAFs are mainly localized in the normal zone, moderately localized in the junctional zone, and minimally localized in the tumor zone [185]. Furthermore, MYH11+ CAFs facilitate malignant behaviors of tumor cells via soluble SDC1 [185]. Given the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of CAFs, it is crucial to develop new strategies that differentially target distinct CAF subsets to improve clinical responses.

Profibrotic spatial ecotypes: stromal/myeloid cell-driven immune exclusion

In addition to CAF-driven stromal architecture, immune exclusion is reinforced by specialized myeloid populations that metabolically, structurally, and functionally suppress antitumor immunity. Spatial profiling reveals that ECM-related CAF subsets and profibrotic tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) subsets form profibrotic spatial ecotypes at the tumor periphery, which occur in pan-cancer and specific cancer types. The profibrotic spatial ecotype-mediated immune exclusion plays a crucial role in immunotherapy resistance via preventing CD8+ T cells from the tumor core (Fig. 4A).

Collagen triple helix repeat-containing 1 (CTHRC1)+ CAFs and secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI)+ macrophages form a pan-cancer conserved profibrotic spatial ecotype (Fig. 4B) [9]. This profibrotic spatial ecotype is mainly induced by TGF-β and interleukin (IL)−1β signaling via directly activating CTHRC1+ CAFs and inducing profibrotic programs in SLPI+ macrophages [9]. Consequently, the profibrotic spatial ecotype prevents immune cell infiltration via both physical barriers formed by the ECM and immunosuppression due to cell–cell interactions (e.g., interacting with T cells via LGALS9-CD44/CD45 axis to induce CD8+ T cell apoptosis and enhance the stability and functionality of immunosuppressive Tregs). Clinically, the profibrotic spatial ecotype is associated with worse clinical outcomes across various cancer types. These findings demonstrate that beyond acting independently, CTHRC1+ CAFs and SLPI+ macrophages form a highly conserved spatial ecotype across cancer types. Despite their spatial colocalization observed across cancer types, the functional connections remain in-depth exploration.

Several profibrotic spatial ecotypes formed by stromal/myeloid cells have been observed in specific cancer types (Fig. 4B). POSTN⁺ CAFs are characterized by activation of the TGF-β, PI3K-AKT, and ECM pathways [186–188]. POSTN⁺ CAFs serve as a central mediator of a physical barrier that impedes immune cell infiltration [189], leading to tumor immune exclusion and immunotherapy resistance [190]. POSTN secreted by CAFs also suppresses immune checkpoint signaling and induces hypoxia-driven glycolytic reprogramming of T cells [175]. Profibrotic spatial ecotypes formed by POSTN⁺ CAFs and CCL3⁺/secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1)+ macrophages have been observed in HCC [186], clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) [187], and bladder cancer [191]. POSTN secretion by CAFs enhances macrophage chemotaxis via activating the Akt pathway in macrophages [188]. Furthermore, reciprocal TGF-β, SPP1, and IL-6 reinforce the activation of POSTN⁺ CAFs and CCL3⁺/SPP1+ macrophages [187]. The POSTN⁺ CAF-CCL3⁺/SPP1+ macrophage axis has been shown to correlate with unfavorable prognosis and poor response to immunotherapy in HCC [186], ccRCC [187], and bladder cancer [191]. Profibrotic spatial ecotypes composed of FAP+ CAFs and SPP1+/disabled-2 (DAB2)+ macrophages play a key role in tumor immune exclusion in liver cancer and CRC [192, 193]. Both SPP1+ and DAB2+ macrophages enhance the profibrotic function of FAP⁺ CAFs via secreting TGF-β, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and acellular dermal matrix (ADM) [193]. In patients with CRC, high expression of FAP or SPP1 indicates a poor response to anti-PD-L1 treatment [192]. Blockade of FAP+ CAFs [114] or SPP1+ macrophages [194] has been shown to inhibit tumor immune exclusion and improve immunotherapy efficacy in HCC. Laminin gamma2 (LAMC2)+ tumor cells act as a key orchestrator of the profibrotic spatial ecotype composed of collagen type XI alpha 1 (COL11A1)+ CAFs and SPP1+ macrophages in esophageal carcinoma [24]. LAMC2+ tumor cells exhibit abnormal lactate metabolism and increased stemness, driving CAF activation via Semaphorin 3 C secretion. The “LAMC2+ tumor cell-COL11A1+ CAF-SPP1+ macrophage” axis is associated with immunotherapy resistance in esophageal carcinoma [24]. In CRC liver metastases, spatial transcriptomics reveals a spatial ecotype characterized by stereotyped three-layer interface: outer matrix CAF belts, inner SPP1+ TAM zones, and intervening bands of exhausted/stressed T cells [195].

Collectively, these CAFs and macrophages synergistically form unique profibrotic spatial ecotypes that exclude CD8+ T cells, with implications in immunotherapy response. A key characteristic of profibrotic spatial ecotypes is the establishment of an immune-privileged microenvironment by remodeling the ECM, recruiting immunosuppressive cells, and physically hindering the infiltration of CD8+ T cells. These ecotypes are not static entities but are maintained by dynamic interactions between tumor and stromal/immune cells. Future research should prioritize exploring the spatial organization, functional dynamics, and cell–cell interactions of CAFs and neighboring macrophages in tumor immune exclusion.

Tumor-associated macrophage-driven tumor immune exclusion and impaired antitumor immunity

In response to the stiffened fibrotic TME, TAMs reprogram into an ECM-expressing phenotype, with upregulated collagen synthesis-associated genes and activated ECM-associated pathways [196]. In addition to forming profibrotic spatial ecotypes together with CAFs, these TAMs cause immune exclusion via metabolic reprogramming and directly impair the antitumor immunity of CD8+ T cells.

Glucose metabolism is a key determinant of the immunomodulatory function of TAMs [197]. In the TME, TAMs exhibit active metabolism, and their glucose uptake capacity can even exceed that of tumor cells [198]. This inhibits the glycolysis and IFN-γ production of CD8⁺ T cells, while promoting the expression of IL-10 and PD-L1 in TAMs in a lactate-dependent manner [199, 200]. The glucose transporter SLC2A1 is a determinant of TAM-mediated spatial immune exclusion of CD8⁺ T cells in NSCLC, which impairs their effector function and confers resistance to anti-PD-(L)1 therapy [197]. Specific depletion of SLC2A1 in TAMs improves anti-PD-(L)1 efficacy by enhancing infiltration, spatial homogeneity, and effector function of intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells. The spatial proximity between SLC2A1⁺ TAMs and CD8⁺ T cells is a potential predictive biomarker of ICI resistance in NSCLC [197]. Chondroitin-6-sulfate (C-6-S) metabolite secreted by CAFs promotes macrophage M2 polarization via coactivating JAK/STAT3 and Hedgehog pathways and reshapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment via upregulating immunosuppressive factors and immune checkpoints, leading to T cell exclusion in metastatic MSS CRC [201]. The interaction between C-6-S and M2 macrophages mainly occurs at the invasive margin of metastatic MSS CRC [201]. Preclinical findings have demonstrated that blockade of C-6-S enhances the anti-PD-1 response in metastatic MSS CRC mainly via decreasing M2 macrophages and reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME [201]. Despite this, the treatment efficacy of anti-C-6-S metabolism in combination with ICIs warrants further evaluation in preclinical and clinical research.

Rather than spatially hampering T cell infiltration, spatial colocalization of specific TAM subsets with CD8⁺ T cells may affect immunotherapy efficacy [202]. On the one hand, immunotherapy-induced intratumoral CD8+ T cells recruit and skew late-stage activated M1 macrophages via CCR5 signaling, which are critical for effective tumor control [202]. On the other hand, immunosuppressive TAM subsets impair CD8+ T-cell-dependent antitumor immunity. Notably, V-set and immunoglobulin domain-containing 4 (VSIG4)+ TAMs, found in multiple cancer types, e.g., testicular cancer, HCC, lung cancer, and glioblastoma, impair CD8+ T-cell-dependent antitumor immunity via interacting with receptors on CD8+ T cells or inducing IL-11 transcription in themselves (Fig. 5A) [203]. Targeting VSIG4+ TAMs boosts antitumor immunity in CD8+ T cells and thus improves immunotherapy effectiveness [204].

Neutrophil-driven tumor immune exclusion

Neutrophils that infiltrate tumors, also called tumor-associated neutrophils, are an important component of the TME. Tumor-associated neutrophils can be classified as antitumor “N1” phenotype with cytotoxic activity and as immunosuppressive “N2” phenotype that enhances angiogenesis, ECM remodeling, and T cell dysfunction [205, 206]. They originate from myeloid progenitor cells and are shaped by various chemokines: “N1” neutrophils are polarized mainly by IFN-β, IL-1β/−8, and TNF-α, while “N2” neutrophils are polarized mainly by TGF-β, IL-6/−8/−17 [207]. Identifying specific tumor-associated neutrophil subsets that drive tumor immune exclusion represents a key step towards refining neutrophil-targeted treatment.

Moving beyond the oversimplified “N1”/“N2” paradigm, peritumoral and perivascular regions are enriched by different neutrophil subsets. The dcTRAIL-R1⁺ tumor-associated neutrophils are preferentially localized at the hypoxic-glycolytic tumor core, where they exhibit remarkable pro-angiogenic and immunosuppressive characteristics [208]. In HCC, the urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (PLAUR)+ neutrophils with “N2” phenotype mainly occupy at the tumor parenchymal margin of HCC, with high macrophage infiltration and reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration around them [209]. These PLAUR+ neutrophils exclude CD8+ T cells via downregulating chemokines CXCL9/10 and recruit M2 immunosuppressive macrophages via the CCL2-CCR2 axis, leading to anti-PD-1 resistance in HCC (Fig. 5B) [209]. Targeting PLAUR with its inhibitor DAPTA can improve anti-PD-1 efficacy in preclinical models of HCC [209]. Collectively, this study delineates a spatial ecotype in which “N2” neutrophil subsets coordinate with other immunosuppressive cell subsets to exclude T cells from the tumor core. A more refined classification system of tumor-associated neutrophils needs to be established to integrate molecular characteristics, functional states, spatial organization, and cell–cell interactions, thereby comprehensively revealing their role in tumor immune exclusion.

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are web-like chromatin structures released by neutrophils that contribute to tumor immune exclusion via (i) forming dense physical barriers that restrict the migration of CD8+ T cells to the tumor core; (ii) degrading chemokines (e.g., CXCL9/10) via serine proteases to disrupt the chemical gradient that guides T cell directional migration; (iii) serving as a scaffold for PD-L1, binding PD-1 to elicit T cell exhaustion; (iv) promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of tumor cells to prevent T cell infiltration; (v) recruiting immunosuppressive cells (e.g., Tregs, MDSCs, and M2 macrophages) that secrete immunosuppressive factors [210–212]. Multiple factors in the TME affect NET formation and their role in restricting the infiltration of CD8+ T cells within tumors. For instance, tumor-derived cytokine CHI3L1 induces NET formation [213] while DNASE1L3+ DCs can degrade NETs [214].

Despite remarkable progress, many aspects of tumor-associated neutrophils and NETs in tumor immune exclusion remain incompletely understood, especially regarding their spatiotemporal heterogeneity.

Tertiary lymphoid structures

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are ectopic lymphoid tissue structures within or around the tumor, serving as predictors of prognostic outcomes and immunotherapy response across pan-cancer [215, 216]. TLSs are characterized by a B cell-enriched zone, with DCs surrounded by a T cell follicle exhibiting germinal center characteristics, profoundly influencing antitumor immunity [217]. Pan-cancer spatial profiling has demonstrated that IgG+ plasma cells are preferentially enriched in mature TLSs, while CCL19+ perivascular cells promote TLS formation [218]. TLSs exhibit a non-random spatial distribution across various cancers, typically located in the stroma and concentrated at the invasive margin; in contrast, intratumoral TLSs are relatively rare [219]. Spatial analysis has revealed several spatial ecotypes that affect the formation, maturation, and function of TLSs.

In NSCLC, colocalization of APOE+ TAMs and MMP7+ tumor cells forms a spatial ecotype, with their intercellular communication mainly via the MK, SEMA3, and MIF signaling pathways [220]. This spatial ecotype prevents T cell infiltration and TLS formation, contributing to poor immunotherapy response in NSCLC [220]. In HCC, a spatial ecotype formed by colocalization of CCL19+ fibroblasts and plasma cells promotes T cell infiltration and TLS formation via the SPP1 and CCL19-CCR7 signaling pathways, which correlates with better ICI response [221]. However, in immune-excluded tumors, this spatial ecotype is mainly observed in the adjacent normal region [221]. The accumulation of DKK1+ tumor cells within the tumor region, especially at the tumor boundary, hinders the infiltration of CCL19+ fibroblasts and plasma cells into the tumor region [221]. In addition to preventing the formation of TLSs, spatial ecotypes also induce their dysfunction. In immune-excluded tumors, SPP1+ macrophages and lysosome-associated membrane protein 3 (LAMP3)+ dendritic cells (DCs) orchestrate a spatial ecotype around TLSs (Fig. 5C). SPP1⁺ TAMs are recruited at the perivascular region via the CD44-SPP1 axis, which not only physically impede T cell transendothelial migration but also recruit LAMP3⁺ DCs [222]. Consequently, LAMP3⁺ DCs retain an antigen-presenting phenotype but lose the ability to initiate adaptive immunity. LAMP3⁺ DCs and SPP1⁺ TAMs form a positive feedback loop: the former recruit Tregs and suppress CD8+ T cell function [223], while the latter remodel the stroma by secreting MMP9 and TGF-β [224]. Together, this causes TLS dysfunction, thereby preventing effective antitumor immunity.

These findings challenge the traditional linear view that “cell type determines function” and provide a theoretical basis for targeting spatial ecotypes.

Metabolites in the TME that affect tumor immune exclusion

The TME is metabolically characterized by hypoxia, lactate gradients, nutrient restriction and competition, waste product accumulation, and acidity [51]. Dissecting metabolic features is crucial for elucidating tumor immune exclusion and for developing potential strategies to overcome immunotherapy resistance. The evolution of spatial metabolomics has enabled us to gain a deeper understanding of how the distribution of metabolites affects tumor immune exclusion [225, 226].

Hypoxia and acidity are hallmarks of most solid tumors, which facilitate immune exclusion [51]. The G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family members (including GPR4, GPR65, GPR68, and GPR132), as proton sensors, can monitor the extracellular hydrogen ion concentration. Among them, GPR4 is activated by an acidic TME, which promotes tumor immune exclusion by inducing collagen alignment and deposition [54]. Hypoxia‐mediated metabolic reprogramming complicates the role of CAFs in tumor immune exclusion. Hypoxic metabolic myofibroblasts (hmmyCAFs) form an immunosuppressive spatial ecotype: they prevent CD8⁺ T cells by secreting ECM (such as POSTN) as a physical barrier, facilitate CD8⁺ T exhaustion, and establish an acidic, nutrient‐restricted TME that suppresses T cells by HIF‐1α-mediated metabolism [175]. IL-17-induced HIF-1α translation promotes collagen deposition in CAFs, thereby driving tumor immune exclusion and anti-PD-L1 resistance [227].

Tumors contain oxygenated and hypoxic regions. Lactate is a key byproduct of glycolysis in hypoxic tumor cells, creating lactate gradients that mirror the oxygen gradient in the tumor [228]. Lactate produced by tumor cells activates CAFs and immunosuppressive cells, contributing to T cell exclusion [24]. Meanwhile, CAFs enhance glycolysis and secrete lactate to the surrounding niche, driving immunosuppression [229]. A unique tumor-immune-CAF “interface” region has been identified in HCC, characterized by enhanced intercellular interactions and upregulated lactate and long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids [229]. Inhibiting glycolysis enhances CD8+ T-cell infiltration and sensitizes tumors to immunotherapy [230]. Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA)-driven lactate metabolism is a key metabolic checkpoint in tumor immune exclusion [231]. Lactatehigh tumors exhibit elevated infiltration of epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs), while lactatelow tumors are enriched by T/NK cells and monocytes/macrophages [232]. PMN-MDSCs are activated by lactate uptake via monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1), causing immunotherapy resistance [231]. Galectin-4 prevents proteasomal degradation of LDHA to enhance glycolysis and amplify HIF-1α-driven CXCL6 expression, thereby driving metabolic adaptation and tumor immune exclusion [233].

Adenosine-rich TME is also an important barrier that excludes immune cells into the tumors [234]. In normal tissues, extracellular level of adenosine is low due to inosine conversion via adenosine deaminase and cellular uptake via concentrative or equilibrative nucleoside transporters [235]. Apoptotic/necrotic cells in the TME produce ATP, leading to the local accumulation of adenosine via ectonucleotidases CD39 and CD73 [236]. The extracellular adenosine exerts its regulatory effect by binding to adenosine receptors (A1R, A2AR, A2BR, and A3R). Among them, A2AR exerts an immunosuppressive effect via modulating the cAMP signaling. Accumulating evidence suggests that inhibiting CD39, CD73, or A2AR improves antitumor efficacy of ICIs [237–240].

Imbalance in tryptophan metabolism fosters tumor growth and restricts antitumor immunity [241]. Notably, tryptophan metabolism prevents maturation of intratumoral TLSs [242]. Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) is a key rate-limiting enzyme that catabolizes tryptophan to kynurenine, which is an attractive immunotherapy target [243]. Inhibiting IDO1 increases the migration and infiltration of NK and CD4+ T cells towards tumor cells, thereby reducing immune exclusion [244]. However, the failure of IDO1 inhibitors in clinical trials reveals the TME complexity, the compensatory activation of alternative immunosuppressive pathways, and the heterogeneity in tryptophan metabolism [241].

Distinct tumors and distinct regions of the same tumor may have diverse nutritional niches [245]. The activation, proliferation, and effector function of T cells require the involvement of glucose, glutamine, and mitochondrial pathways [245]. Nutrient competition is involved in impaired antitumor immunity. The spatially organized physical barriers also restrict the nutrients available to infiltrating T cells and cause their immunosuppression and exclusion from the tumors [246]. An in-depth exploration of the spatial organization of nutrient availability and immune cells is crucial for a deeper understanding of nutrient limitation and competition in tumor immune exclusion.

Development of intervention strategies against immune-excluded tumors

Because immune exclusion is maintained through spatial ecotypes, effective therapeutic strategies will likely require ecotype-level reprogramming rather than isolated molecular inhibition.

Strategies against physical barriers

Strategies aimed at dismantling spatially organized physical barriers represent a major therapeutic avenue for converting immune-excluded tumors into immune-permissive states [247] (Fig. 6).

CAFs are central architectural organizers of profibrotic spatial ecotypes and serve as major determinants of spatial immune exclusion through coordinated structural, signaling, and metabolic remodeling [272]. Despite many treatment approaches under development, including monoclonal antibodies, CAR-T cells, antibody–drug conjugates, bispecific T cell engagers, and radiolabeled therapy, systemic clearance of specific CAF subpopulations (e.g., FAP+ CAFs) may lead to on-target/off-tumor toxicities [273]. To reduce this risk, several conditional activation or tumor-specific inhibition strategies are proposed, such as RO7122290, a bispecific antibody that targets FAP and CD137/4-1BB [274]. An early clinical trial has demonstrated that RO7122290 plus anti-PD-L1 atezolizumab is effective against advanced solid tumors, with a manageable safety profile [274].

In addition to activating CAFs, TGF-β is a key contributor to ECM remodeling, promoting collagen production and remodeling [53]. TGF-β activation correlates with T cell exclusion and poor ICI response [44]. Targeting TGF-β is a potential strategy to overcome T cell exclusion and boost immunotherapy efficacy [43]. Small-molecule inhibitors (galunisertib and vactosertib), pathway antibodies (PF-03446962 and fresolimumab), and a bifunctional antibody that targets PD-L1 and TGF-β receptor II (TGF-βRII) have been evaluated for the treatment of solid tumors. Notably, Retlirafusp alfa (SHR-1701) was approved in China in January 2026 for the treatment of advanced PD-L1-positive gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma [275]. Given the context-dependent nature of TGF-β, clinical treatment strategies should be designed based on biomarkers and disease staging and combined with appropriate treatments.

The fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signaling maintains angiogenesis, ECM stiffening, and tumor immune exclusion, and compensates for VEGF blockade, positioning this signaling as a prominent treatment target [276]. Many FGFR inhibitors are currently under evaluation in clinical trials, including selective inhibitors (pemigatinib, erdafitinib, futibatinib, AZD4547, HMPL-453, RLY-4008, rogaratinib, and FGF401), non-selective inhibitors (lenvatinib, surufatinib, nintedanib, anlotinib, lucitanib, derazantinib, and tinengotinib), and monoclonal antibodies (vofatamab and bemarituzumab). Of these, three FGFR inhibitors have been approved by the FDA, namely futibatinib, pemigatinib, and erdafitinib, for treating advanced intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and urothelial carcinoma, respectively [277–279]. Given the preferential expression of FGFR on CAFs, blockade of the FGFR pathway (e.g., erdafitinib) suppresses CAFs and thus boosts T cell infiltration by breaking physical and chemical barriers [46].

Collectively, targeting specific CAF subsets holds promise for overcoming tumor immune exclusion and improving immunotherapy effectiveness, warranting further assessment in well-designed clinical trials.

Targeting ECM

The ECM forms a physical barrier that impedes immune cell infiltration and restricts drug penetration and delivery, leading to immunotherapy resistance [280]. Thus, ECM modulation may reverse immune exclusion and overcome immunotherapy resistance. Currently, ECM-targeting strategies include ECM degradation via enzymes, suppression of ECM production by targeting CAFs, and reduction of ECM stiffness and density [281].

Despite a solid theoretical basis and promise in early clinical trials [282], targeting ECM has potential risks, such as recurrence, metastases, and compensatory fibrosis. Notably, the FDA temporarily halted the trial due to hyaluronidase PEGPH20 (a major component of the ECM)-associated thromboembolic events. Moreover, the phase 3 trial (NCT02715804) reported negative results, with no survival benefit, further leading to the discontinuation of PEGPH20 development [283]. Therefore, despite the remarkable short-term effect, ECM interventions remain a “double-edged sword”. Ultimate survival benefit will determine whether such intervention strategies can be successfully applied in the clinic.

Successful modulation of immune exclusion likely requires controlled stromal reprogramming rather than indiscriminate stromal depletion. Given the high plasticity of the TME, transient collagen degradation may activate CAFs, thereby eliciting compensatory ECM synthesis and remodeling. Furthermore, ECM degradation products or released bioactive factors may have long-term effects on the TME, such as intensifying malignant behaviors, recruiting immunosuppressive cells, and inducing tumor angiogenesis. These mechanisms work together to create a tumor-promoting TME. Nanotechnology may offer precise interventions to reduce these risks. By enabling spatiotemporal control of delivery, optimizing degradation, and synergizing with antitumor and antifibrotic agents, nanoplatforms may overcome drug delivery barriers while lowering potential risks. Going forward, these challenges should be comprehensively evaluated in advanced preclinical models. Furthermore, closed-loop ECM regulation systems based on the dynamic responses of biological materials should be investigated to facilitate safe translation in clinical settings.

Targeting abnormal tumor vasculature

Abnormal tumor vasculature is a hallmark of the TME, facilitating tumor immune exclusion and immunotherapy resistance beyond supporting tumor growth [284]. It forms a pathophysiological barrier that prevents T cell infiltration. VEGF/VEGFR signaling is primarily driven by stromal cells: macrophages and CAFs secrete VEGF-A and pro-angiogenic factors (such as angiopoietin-1/2), leading to abnormal tumor vasculature and immune exclusion [285–287]. Destroying tumor vasculature via anti-angiogenic therapy or inducing vascular normalization via vascular-normalizing therapy may boost immunotherapy efficacy by promoting T cell infiltration [288–290]. However, many tumors fail to respond or develop resistance due to alternative vasogenic or immunosuppressive mechanisms [291].

Genetic and metabolic reprogramming of tumor endothelial cells (TECs) reconfigure the immunosuppressive TME and potentially boost immunotherapy efficacy, positioning this approach as a novel vascular-targeting strategy [284]. Genetic reprogramming of TECs enhances lymphocyte infiltration and activation through multiple mechanisms, including upregulating adhesion molecules in TECs and restoring tumor vascular normalization [284]. Targeting the metabolic reprogramming of TECs offers an alternative anti-angiogenic strategy. It restores abnormal tumor vasculature and reconditions the immunosuppressive TME, thereby increasing T cell survival and infiltration [284]. In addition to restoring abnormal tumor vasculature, reprogramming TEC metabolism reduces immunosuppressive metabolite production and metabolic competition [292]. Collectively, targeting TEC metabolism may enhance immunotherapy efficacy.

Targeting TAMs

As mentioned above, TAMs are implicated in tumor immune exclusion and immunotherapy resistance. Several strategies targeting TAMs have been proposed, including (i) inhibiting TAM recruitment by targeting chemokine signals (e.g., CCL2-CCR2/CCR5, CSF1-CSF1R, and CXCL12-CXCR4 axes) [293, 294]; (ii) eliminating TAMs within the TME (e.g., CSF1R and TREM2 blockade) [295, 296]; (iii) enhancing phagocytosis by blocking the “do not eat me” signaling [297]; (iv) reprogramming TAMs (e.g., CD40 receptor activation and PI3Kγ inhibition) [298, 299]; and (v) genetically engineering macrophages using CRISPR-Cas9 technology [300, 301]. Many of these strategies have progressed from preclinical studies to clinical trials (Table 4). TAM-targeted therapies are advancing rapidly, with the goal of improving immunotherapy efficacy and benefiting cancer patients.

Targeting spatial ecotypes formed by TAM/CAF subpopulations

TAMs and CAFs may express complementary ligand-receptor pairs, thereby mutually influencing their gene expression programs [309]. Given the importance of spatial ecotypes formed by TAM and CAF subpopulations in tumor immune exclusion, strategies aimed at disrupting the spatial ecotypes may enhance immunotherapy efficacy and improve clinical outcomes, mainly including disrupting the cell–cell communications between TAM and CAF subpopulations and inhibiting their co-associated cell states. However, studies specifically targeting the spatial ecotypes are relatively rare. Further studies are needed to explore strategies targeting the spatial ecotypes formed by TAM/CAF subpopulations.

Strategies for reprogramming immunosuppressive spatial ecotypes

Reprogramming immunosuppressive spatial ecotypes can be achieved through two main strategies. The first strategy involves directly eliminating or reprogramming immunosuppressive cells, such as using an anti-CCR4 antibody to deplete Tregs [310–312] and all-trans retinoic acid to differentiate MDSCs [313, 314]. The second strategy involves activating antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as using STING and CD40 agonists to induce DC activation and maturation [315, 316]. The core of reprogramming immunosuppressive spatial ecotypes is to transform cold tumors into hot ones. This requires not only reversing immunosuppression but also activating immune-promoting factors to improve antitumor immune responses.

Inducing TLS maturation

TLSs correlate with improved immunotherapy response and better prognosis across pan-cancer, and TLS induction is a potential strategy to enhance antitumor activity and immunotherapy efficacy [242, 317, 318]. Existing anticancer treatments can induce TLS formation and maturation, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, ICIs, and oncolytic virus therapy. Emerging TLS induction strategies primarily include cytokine/chemokine delivery (e.g., CXCL13, CCL19/21, and LTα/LTβ-LIGHT), nanomaterials and tissue-engineering scaffolds (e.g., hydrogels, microporous scaffolds, and covalent organic frameworks), gene delivery and engineered cells (e.g., nanoparticles, viral vectors, and exosomes), and innate immune agonists (e.g., TLRs, cGAS-STING) [319]. Notably, a phase 1 study (NCT04059588), has demonstrated that an Fc-optimized CD40 agonistic antibody can elicit TLS formation and promote systemic and durable antitumor immunity in metastatic cancer [320]. Despite this, these emerging strategies aimed at regulating TLSs remain in preclinical or early clinical stages and have not yet received sufficient clinical validation. To fully exploit the potential of TLSs in cancer immunotherapy, a unified classification system for TLSs based on their maturity and spatial distribution should be refined. Non-invasive imaging and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted histological tools should be developed to detect TLSs. Spatial multi-omics data should be integrated to analyze the immune circuits associated with TLSs. Combination strategies that promote TLS maturation and synergize with ICIs should be designed in the future.

Identification of pan-cancer conserved spatial ecotypes and their clinical significance in immune-excluded tumors

The spatial organization of the TME has a profound impact on biology and immunotherapy response. Therefore, it is crucial to identify pan-cancer conserved spatial ecotypes in immune-excluded tumors.

Development of pan-cancer conserved spatial ecotypes

Emerging spatial profiling technologies have revealed that immune-excluded tumors across solid tumors share conserved architectural principles, suggesting a pan-cancer organizational logic underlying T cell exclusion and immunotherapy resistance [321]. These principles are not merely correlative but mechanistically linked to stromal compartmentalization, ECM patterning, and cell-type-specific spatial zoning.

A central conserved feature is that the tumor nest is surrounded by a physical barrier composed of stromal cells (particularly ECM-related CAFs localized at the tumor boundary), which establishes an immune-excluded barrier through aligned collagen deposition and chemokine gradients [322]. Supporting this view, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analysis has revealed that gene expression profiling of the leading edge is conserved across cancer types, while that of the tumor core is tissue specific [323]. By leveraging over 14 million cells from 10 cancer types across 7 spatial transcriptomic and proteomic platforms, four pan-cancer conserved spatial CAF subsets (s1-CAFs, s2-CAFs, s3-CAFs, and s4-CAFs) have been characterized, which form spatial ecotypes together with other cell components in the TME [324]. Among them, the spatial ecotype composed of s1-CAFs (characterized by myCAFs) and tumor cells fosters T cell exclusion and forms a dense cellular barrier that hinders plasma cell interaction with tumor cells. Another pan-cancer spatial transcriptomic analysis has identified 13 conserved spatial ecotypes across 12 cancer types [325]. Among them, a spatial ecotype formed by macrophages and tumor cells is associated with poor prognosis and immunotherapy resistance, while a spatial ecotype formed by colocalization of macrophages and immune cells predicts better survival and treatment response. A pan-cancer conserved spatial ecotype composed of inflammatory tissue-resident macrophages characterized by CXCL8/IL-1B/IL-6 co-expression, neutrophils, and CAFs, is associated with ICI resistance and poor prognosis [326]. Together, these findings provide a framework for large-scale profiling of conserved spatial ecotypes in immune-excluded tumors.

Analysis of spatial architecture in immune-excluded tumors cannot be limited to a single pathway but should focus on their spatial intercellular interaction networks. Therefore, spatial ecotypes defined by colocalization and cell–cell interactions between different cell populations within the TME are an important prerequisite for understanding clinical relevance and for developing more effective intervention strategies against immune-excluded tumors.

Spatial ecotype as a universal framework for understanding and guiding cancer immunotherapy

The heterogeneous TME limits the utility of single biomarkers, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and tumor mutational burden (TMB), for guiding ICIs. In-depth research on spatial ecotypes will advance precision immuno-oncology, where treatment strategies are customized based on the specific cellular composition of individual tumors [327]. Several clinically relevant spatial ecotypes have been identified, enabling the capture of TME heterogeneity and the prediction of immunotherapy outcomes. Through integrating over 10 million single-cell and spatial transcriptomes from multiple human cancer types, a study has identified nine conserved spatial ecotypes with cellular, spatial, and clinical characteristics (including immunotherapy response) across cancer types [8]. These spatial ecotypes (SE1 ~ SE9) are distributed from the adjacent stroma to the tumor core: SE1 and SE9 are respectively located in the adjacent stroma and the tumor core, and other spatial ecotypes preferentially localize within 250 µm of the tumor-stroma margin. Among them, SE7 and SE8 (two proinflammatory spatial ecotypes with increased interferon signaling) predict ICI benefit, and SE4 (a wound healing-associated spatial ecotype) predicts ICI resistance, with efficacy superior to that of TMB and PD-L1. Notably, these spatial ecotypes can be applied to liquid biopsy in melanoma, indicating the implications for prediction and spatiotemporal detection of treatment response. Despite this, these classifications of spatial ecotypes require to be validated in larger-scale and more diverse single-cell spatial omics cohorts. The biological interpretability of these spatial ecotypes requires experimental validation. Furthermore, the feasibility of spatial ecotype liquid biopsy analysis in other cancer types beyond melanoma warrants further investigation.

In addition to pan-cancer conserved spatial ecotypes, several cancer type-specific spatial ecotypes have also been identified, with the potential for predicting immunotherapy response. For instance, a spatial ecotype-based framework outperforms PD-L1, TMB, and several bulk signatures in predicting ICI outcomes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [328]. By dissecting complex spatial tumor properties, a unique immunosuppressive spatial ecotype, characterized by the spatial co-localization of fibroblasts, tumor cells, and exhausted CD8+ T cells, has been defined in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and predicts poor survival and ICI resistance [329]. Given the prognostic value of spatial ecotypes enriched by CAFs and macrophages for cancer patient prognosis and immunotherapy response, a machine learning framework has been developed to recognize macrophage-fibroblast-tumor co-infiltration patterns for predicting prognosis in gastric cancer [330]. By bridging spatial omics with AI tools, AI-powered models predict NSCLC prognosis with higher accuracy than the traditional staging system [331], facilitating improved risk stratification for treatment selection.

Together, these findings reveal pan-cancer conserved and cancer type-specific spatial ecotypes: the spatial organization and intercellular interactions of immune and stromal cells provide a functional map of tumor immune exclusion and treatment effectiveness. These insights establish spatial ecotype as a universal framework for predicting immunotherapy responsiveness and for assisting precision immuno-oncology.

Challenges and future directions

In this section, we discuss the three major challenges faced by spatial profiling in tumor immune exclusion: the lack of unified standards for integrating spatiotemporal multi-omics technologies, which complicates cross-platform data integration; most studies rely on static samples, making real-time monitoring of the dynamic spatiotemporal evolution of immune-excluded tumors impossible; and preclinical models cannot reproduce the spatial complexity of the human TME, hindering the clinical translation of mechanistic research (Fig. 7). We also propose potential solutions to these challenges. Despite major advances in spatial profiling technologies, several conceptual, technical, and translational barriers continue to limit the clinical implementation of spatial ecotype-guided immunotherapy.

Bottlenecks in technological standardization and cross-platform data integration

Integrating datasets from distinct omics modalities is crucial for unlocking their synergistic effects [332–334]. However, differences in the spatial features of the data make this process challenging. This difficulty makes direct integration of cross-platform data difficult, posing a significant barrier to large-scale validation and clinical translation of the immune-excluded phenotype. Therefore, specialized hardware and software are urgently needed to effectively integrate and visualize these complex spatial omics data.

Recently, the SANTO algorithm, a coarse-to-fine strategy, has been proposed to achieve alignment and stitching for spatial omics data [335]. This algorithm enables cross-platform alignment of spatial transcriptomic and epigenomic data, offering a novel perspective on processing large-scale, image-based spatial omics data. However, cross-cohort multimodal data integration, which links spatial data from different platforms, tissue types, and cohorts, remains challenging. Foundation models trained on large-scale, diverse datasets offer a promising approach to solving this problem. They can learn generalizable features, facilitating the integration of data and the construction of a cancer spatial atlas. Establishing benchmark standards for spatial omics integration algorithms will be highly significant for selecting appropriate integration strategies and designing experiments. Ultimately, multi-omics techniques with spatial single-cell resolution will revolutionize our understanding of the immune-excluded phenotype. A major limitation of current spatial profiling studies is their reliance on static tissue snapshots, despite the inherently dynamic nature of tumor-immune interactions.

Real-time monitoring of dynamic spatiotemporal evolution of the TME

Although immunotherapy has transformed the paradigm of cancer treatment, durable and effective responses remain limited. The heterogeneous TME promotes immune evasion, leading to treatment resistance [336–338]. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct real-time monitoring of the dynamic spatiotemporal evolution of the TME, but this remains challenging. Currently, most methodologies capture only snapshots or rely on longitudinal biopsies, yet they fail to resolve continuous, spatially heterogeneous tumor-immune interactions at the subcellular and regional levels, as well as during cycles of therapy.

Engineering technologies, such as 3D bioprinting, organoids, and organ-on-a-chip systems, can achieve controlled reconstruction of tumor spatial structure and heterogeneity, thereby mimicking in vivo pathophysiology. By integrating with spatial omics, these models can be used to evaluate treatment responses and investigate immunotherapy resistance.

While spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed imaging enable mapping of immune exclusion architectures, the temporal resolution of these platforms is insufficient to track rapid cellular reconfigurations. Recent advances in live imaging and molecular reporter systems are promising for dynamic monitoring. For instance, a nanobody-based immunotracer targeting CD163, a receptor specifically expressed on macrophages, has been developed for dynamic imaging of macrophages during immunotherapy [339]. This immunotracer enables real-time imaging and tracking of CD163+ macrophages in the TME, providing a basis for predicting and monitoring immunotherapy response in cancer patients. Iron oxide nanoplatforms can improve magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/magnetic particle imaging (MPI)-monitored immune cell trafficking and treatment responses and can reprogram the immunosuppressive TME for personalized immunotherapy [340]. Yet imaging techniques and modalities have limited depth penetration, making it difficult to conduct multiplex imaging or resolve metabolic-immune crosstalk at single-cell resolution within intact 3D tissue. Moreover, although microneedle-based sampling of interstitial fluid can be used for longitudinal immune profiling [341], insights into the nature of evolving immune exclusion zones derived from spatial omics have not yet been systematically integrated.

Future studies should focus on the convergence of multimodal sensing, AI-based spatiotemporal models, and closed-loop therapeutic delivery [342]. The SCOIGET framework infers copy number variation (CNV) dynamics and clonal architecture by leveraging spatial omics data and represents a first step towards the computational reconstruction of clonal-immune co-evolution trajectories [343]. Such models could be coupled with real-time biosensors (such as calcium-responsive nanoprobes) that monitor intratumoral T cell activation over 28 days, thereby enabling prediction of the collapse of immune exclusion or its reinforcement under combinatorial regimens. To ensure effective modulation of tumor immune exclusion, it is crucial to develop both advanced techniques and standardized analytical methods to integrate dynamic datasets across scales, from subcellular signaling fluxes to whole-tumor ecosystem remodeling. Through integrative, temporally resolved decoding of the evolving architecture of the TME, effective therapeutic interventions that overcome resistance and induce persistent antitumor immune responses can be achieved [344, 345]. Ultimately, the clinical value of spatial ecotype analysis will depend on whether these insights can be translated into actionable biomarkers and therapeutically targetable ecosystem states.

Bridging the gap: towards clinical translation of spatial ecotypes

Although many spatial ecotypes have been identified as drivers of immune exclusion mechanisms, effective intervention strategies remain limited. This may be due to preclinical models failing to replicate the spatial complexity of the human TME. When patient tumor tissues are implanted into humanized mice, their stroma is largely replaced by host cells, thereby destroying their spatial organization [346, 347].

AI-based integration models that incorporate multi-omics biomarkers can predict ecotypes from routine H&E sections. However, limited interpretability and reliance on tissue-structure-based texture features, rather than actual biological signals, may constrain their clinical applications [348]. To achieve a breakthrough, three strategies should be adopted. First, spatial multi-omics stratification should be incorporated into basket trial designs [349]. Second, generative AI should be used to synthesize virtual patient cohorts to model the efficacy and toxicity of different spatial ecotype-targeted combinations [350]. Third, regulatory agencies should be encouraged to recognize spatial biomarkers as surrogate endpoints to accelerate the translation cycle from mechanism discovery to clinical trials [351]. Successfully developing a navigation map for ecotype decoding will facilitate more personalized immunotherapy.

Conclusion

Spatial profiling has revolutionized the study of the TME, and high-resolution spatial platforms have enabled in situ dissection of the TME at subcellular resolution. Building on this technological framework, this review dissects the core molecular and cellular mechanisms of tumor immune exclusion: immune exclusion is not caused by dysfunction of a single cell type but is the collective outcome of collaborative networks formed by multiple cellular subsets that rely on spatial proximity. These cellular modules establish dual barriers via ECM remodeling and immunosuppression to restrict CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and their functions depend heavily on their spatial distribution. These findings challenge reductionist, cell-centric models of tumor immunity and instead support a systems-level framework in which spatial ecotypes govern immune accessibility, therapeutic responsiveness, and tumor evolution.

Collectively, spatial profiling provides a novel perspective for dissecting immune-excluded tumors. The discovery of spatial ecotypes and core cellular interaction networks opens a new avenue for understanding and guiding tumor immunotherapy. In the future, with the refinement of technological systems and the optimization of translational pathways, spatial ecotype dissection may serve as a navigation tool for personalized immunotherapy, ushering tumor immunotherapy into a new era of precision and individualization.
