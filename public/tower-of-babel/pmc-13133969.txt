Dataset of the changes in sugarbeet taproot cell wall composition, firmness, and cell wall-related gene expression as a function of postharvest storage time and temperature
Authors: Abbas M. Lafta, Fernando L. Finger, Mercedes Morin, Mari B. Natwick, Melvin D. Bolton, Karen K. Fugate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The physical and chemical properties of sugarbeet (Beta vulgaris L.) cell walls influence root susceptibility to physical damage, pathogens, and dehydration during storage, affect factory efficiency and sugar recovery during processing, and generally deteriorate during storage. The specific biochemical and genetic changes that occur after harvest and are responsible for root quality deterioration, however, are largely uncharacterized. Data were collected of the changes in tissue strength, expression of genes that participate in the biosynthesis, degradation or modification of cell walls, and the concentrations of the major classes of chemical compounds that compose cell walls in sugarbeet roots at harvest and following 12, 40, and 120 d storage at 5°C and 95% relative humidity or 12°C and 95% relative humidity. Root tissue strength was measured by penetrometry; gene transcript abundances were determined by RNA sequencing; cell walls isolated from homogenized tissue were fractionated and the concentrations of total cell wall material, cellulose, hemicellulose, total pectin, water-soluble pectin, chelator-soluble pectin, carbonate-soluble pectin, and lignin were determined. Additionally, cell wall-related genes altered in expression during storage were functionally annotated and categorized according to the cell wall component upon which they acted, and their differential expression as a function of storage time and temperature was determined. This dataset describes and quantifies the genetic and biochemical changes occurring in cell walls following harvest which affect sugarbeet root storability and processing quality and is potentially useful for identifying biochemical and genetic targets that can be selected or manipulated for improved storage and processing properties.

Background

Sugarbeets are the source of approximately 20 % of the world’s sugar [1]. The crop is produced predominantly in temperate regions of Europe, North America and Asia, where spring-sown seed develops and grows over the summer production season into plants with a massive, sucrose-rich taproot that typically weighs 0.5 to 1 kg, contains 15 to 22 % sucrose by weight, and is harvested in mid- to late autumn [2,3]. The size of the sugarbeet crop far exceeds sugar factory processing capacity, necessitating the storage of the majority of the crop prior to processing. The roots are stored in large outdoor piles or ventilated sheds which are cooled, actively with large ventilation fans or passively, using the cold ambient air of late autumn and winter for up to 250 d [4]. Stored roots remain metabolically active until they are frozen within storage piles or heat-killed during processing, and massive changes in gene expression and metabolism have been documented in stored roots, presumably in response to the physiological and environmental stresses caused by harvest and storage [5,6]. Roots additionally increase in susceptibility to storage diseases, due in part to the postharvest structural and compositional changes in cell walls that provide the first line of defense against pathogens [7]. Postharvest cell wall changes also alter root tissue strength and composition in ways that reduce the efficiency of root slicing, sucrose extraction, and sucrose recovery during processing [8]. Compositional changes in cell walls during sugarbeet root storage have been the subject of few studies and are limitedly characterized [9,10]. The changes in gene expression that are responsible for postharvest cell wall structural and compositional changes are also largely uncharacterized, although some cell wall modifying genes that differ in expression between sugarbeet varieties that vary in their ability to retain sucrose during storage have been identified [6]. The dataset described here identifies and quantifies compositional and genetic changes that occurred in sugarbeet roots during short (12 and 40 d) and long-term (120 d) storage, under optimal (5°C, 95 % relative humidity) and sub-optimal storage conditions (12°C, 95 % relative humidity) and provides physical measurements of root softening under these same storage conditions. The data are useful for expanding our current understanding of the physical, biochemical, and genetic changes in postharvest sugarbeet roots and can be utilized to identify genetic or biochemical markers or targets to improve sugarbeet root storage and processing traits.

Data Description

Raw data from measurements of root firmness, the concentrations of root cell wall components, and the expression of genes involved in cell wall biosynthesis, degradation, or modification for sugarbeet taproots on the day of harvest and after 12, 40, or 120 d storage at 5°C/95 % relative humidity and 12°C/95 % relative humidity are compiled in Microsoft Excel files Table S1, Table S2, and Table S3, respectively, which are available in the Supplementary Materials of this manuscript. Raw RNA sequencing data used to obtain the cell wall gene expression data in Table S3 are available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession identifier PRJNA938134. Means and statistically relevant changes in root firmness and cell wall composition are presented in Fig. 1 and Table 1, respectively, and were generated by the analysis of data in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Mean and differential expression of genes involved in cell wall metabolism are presented in Table S4 (Supplementary Materials) and were generated by the analysis of data in Table S3. Table 2, Table 3, and Table 4 contain the number of upregulated and downregulated cell wall-related genes as a function of metabolic pathway, a list of genes that were upregulated by 10-fold or more during storage, and a list of genes that were downregulated by 10-fold or more during storage, respectively, and were generated by analysis of Table S4 data. Root firmness data were collected for two repetitions of the experiment. Data for both repetitions of the experiment are available in Table S1. Fig. 1 provides a graphical and statistical analysis of the first repetition of the experiment.

Data of cell wall composition (Table S2, Table 1) includes the water content of roots as a percentage of root fresh weight, content of cell wall material as a fraction of root dry weight, and the concentrations of cellulose, hemicellulose, total pectin, water-soluble pectin, chelator-soluble pectin, sodium carbonate-soluble pectin, and lignin in milligrams per gram of cell wall material. Cell wall compositional data was collected from four replicate samples, each comprised of a pooled sample of equivalent weights of root tissue collected from two separate roots.

Cell wall modifying gene expression (Table S3) includes genes involved in cell wall biosynthesis, modification, or degradation, and cell wall localized proteins that were differentially expressed at one or more timepoint or temperature during storage. Expression is in fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) for each of six replicate roots, with locus identifiers and gene descriptions from GenBank provided for each gene. Genes are organized by functionality based on their contribution to the metabolism of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, lignin and/or phenylpropanoids, expansin and/or cell wall proteins, peroxidases, or miscellaneous proteins and enzymes. Differential expression of cell wall modifying genes (Table S4) were determined utilizing mean expression values for each gene at each timepoint and storage temperature. All changes in expression are relative to gene expression on the day of harvest, with differential expression presented as the fold-change and logbase2(fold-change) in expression. Additional annotation and Enzyme Commission numbers, where available, are also provided in Table S4.

Leaf regrowth during storage (Table S5) was measured as the weight of newly formed leaves on roots following 40 or 120 d storage at 5 or 12°C using eight replicate roots. Data collected at 40 d includes the weight of leaf material produced by regrowth of vegetative buds in the time period between harvest and 40 d storage. Data at 120 d is the weight of leaf material produced by regrowth of vegetative buds in the time period between 40 and 120 d storage. Cumulative leaf weight is the sum of data collected at 40 and 120 d.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Plant material and treatments

One hundred and twelve sugarbeet plants of hybrid VDH66156 (SES VanderHave, Tienen, Belgium) were produced in 15 L pots in a greenhouse as previously described [11]. Plants were manually extracted from pots 18 weeks after planting, and taproots were separated from foliar material with a knife, gently washed under a stream of water, and allowed to dry. Forty-eight random taproots were placed into a Conviron (Winnipeg, MB, Canada) E7/2 growth chamber operating at 5°C and 95 % relative humidity (RH) on the day of harvest; another 48 randomly selected taproots were placed into a similar growth chamber operating at 12°C and 95 % RH on the day of harvest. Of the remaining 16 roots, eight random roots were used for day-of-harvest root firmness determinations (described in Section 4.2); the remaining eight roots were used for the collection of tissue samples for the determination of day-of-harvest cell wall composition and analysis of gene expression (described in Sections 4.3 and 4.4), respectively. After 12, 40, and 120 d in storage, sixteen randomly selected roots were removed from each growth chamber, using eight roots per chamber per timepoint for root firmness determinations and eight roots per chamber per timepoint for tissue samples. Tissue samples were collected by excising a longitudinal root section that was representative of the entire root from each root. Tissue sections were then rapidly diced, frozen in liquid nitrogen, lyophilized, ground to a fine powder, and stored at -80°C prior to analyses.

A second experiment was conducted to repeat determinations of root firmness during storage. In this experiment, sixty-four roots were produced and harvested as described above, and the harvested taproots were evenly divided between the two growth chambers operating at 5 and 12°C as described above. Eight roots were removed from each chamber after 1, 15, 45, and 115 d in storage and used for root firmness determinations.

Firmness determination

Root firmness was determined using a Wagner Instruments FDK 40 penetrometer (Greenwich, CT, USA) fitted with a 6.36 mm probe and mounted to a Wagner Test Stand. Firmness determinations were made according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Firmness measurements were made approximately 5 cm below the taproot apex. Determinations were made with the periderm intact and following periderm removal with a Wagner FT/PLR fruit peeler to determine the strength of root periderm and the underlying tissue, respectively. Four technical replicate measurements were made on each of eight replicate roots collected per temperature x timepoint, with each technical replicate measurement made in a unique quadrant of the root. The four technical replicate values were averaged to generate firmness values for each replicate root.

Cell wall fractionation and analysis

Cell walls were isolated and fractionated using previously described methods [12,13]. Briefly, 0.5 g lyophilized tissue was extracted with 5 mL 80 % (v/v) ethanol, heated to 95°C for 15 min to inactivate enzymes, centrifuged to isolate insoluble material, and the insoluble pellet washed twice with 3 mL 80 % ethanol. Starch and lipids were removed by sequential extraction with 90 % (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and chloroform:methanol (1:1), respectively. The remaining insoluble cell wall material (CWM) was washed with acetone, dried, and weighed with CWM expressed in g-1 dry wt.

Pectin fractions were extracted from CWM. Water-soluble pectin (WSP) was extracted by the addition of 1 mL 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 6.5) to 15 mg CWM and incubation with shaking for 6 h at room temperature. The insoluble residue following WSP extraction was washed with 80 % ethanol, then with acetone, and dried in vacuo. The dried residue was resuspended in 1 mL 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 6.5) containing 50 mM EDTA and extracted with shaking as described above to obtain chelator-soluble pectin (CSP). The insoluble residue remaining after CSP extraction, washing, and drying was resuspended in 1 mL 50 mM sodium carbonate containing 2 mM EDTA, extracted with shaking as described above, and centrifuged. The supernatant of this extraction was used for sodium carbonate-soluble pectin (SSP) quantification. The remaining insoluble material was washed and dried as described above and resuspended in 1 mL 4 mM NaOH containing 100 mM sodium borohydride (NaBH4). The suspension was shaken at room temperature for 6 h, centrifuged, and the supernatant removed for hemicellulose quantification. The insoluble material remaining following hemicellulose extraction was washed twice with water to remove NaOH, washed with acetone, dried under vacuum, and used for cellulose quantification.

The uronic acid content in pectin-containing fractions was determined by the m-hydroxy diphenyl method of Blumenkrantz and Asboe-Hansen [14] using galacturonic acid as standard. Reaction mixtures contained 0.1 mL pectin extract and 0.9 mL concentrated H2SO4 with 75 mM sodium borate and were incubated at 100°C for 10 min. After cooling, 20 µL 0.15 % (w/v) m-hydroxy diphenyl in 0.5 % (w/v) NaOH was added, and the solution absorbance at 520 nm was measured. Hemicellulose and cellulose fractions were quantified by the anthrone method [15] using glucose standards. Prior to quantification, the cellulose-containing pellet was suspended in 67 % (v/v) H2SO4 and incubated for 1 h at 25°C with shaking [16]. Pectin fractions, cellulose, and hemicellulose concentrations were expressed in mg g-1 CWM.

Lignin was extracted and quantified by the method of He et al. [17]. Briefly, 25 % (v/v) acetyl bromide in acetic acid was added to CWM, heated to 50°C for 4 h, and cooled. Following the addition of 2 M NaOH to terminate the reaction, lignin was quantified by solution absorbance at 280 nm relative to lignin (Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) standards and expressed in mg g-1 CWM.

RNA isolation and sequencing

RNA was isolated and sequenced as previously described [5]. Briefly, RNA was isolated from 50 mg of lyophilized tissue using a RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) with on-column DNase digestion according to the kit manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA was enriched in mRNA by oligo dT selection, fragmented, and reverse transcribed into cDNA using random hexanucleotide primers. The resulting cDNAs were end repaired, 3′ adenylated, and ligated to 5′ and 3′ adaptors, then amplified by PCR. PCR products were heat denatured, circularized using a splint oligo, and rolling-circle replicated for the production of DNA nanoballs. DNA nanoballs were single-end sequenced using a 50 bp read length on a BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI Americas, Cambridge, MA, USA). Sequencing yielded 29.5 to 35.0 M raw reads per sample.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Raw sequencing data was analyzed using the bioinformatic procedures described by Fugate et al. [5] to determine gene expression levels and changes in expression with respect to storage time and temperature. Briefly, raw sequencing reads were cleaned of reads with adapters, reads with >10 % unknown bases, and low-quality reads, and the clean reads were mapped to the sugarbeet genome for the identification, annotation, and quantification of genes. Genes were defined as differentially expressed when adjusted P values were ≤ 0.05 and fold-change in expression ≥ 2.00.

Significant differences in tissue firmness and cell wall composition were determined by ANOVA with P ≤ 0.05. Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) was used to identify significant differences between data points. ANOVA and Fisher’s LSD analyses were performed using Minitab Statistical Software, ver. 20.4 (State College, PA, USA).

Limitations

The dataset is limited by the use of a single commercial variety of sugarbeet. However, the dataset remains a valuable source of information regarding cell wall changes in postharvest sugarbeet roots, especially in light of the limited genetic variability in sugarbeet germplasm.
