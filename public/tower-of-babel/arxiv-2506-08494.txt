Multiversion of the Hausdorff–Young inequality
Paata Ivanisvili, Pavlos Kalantzopoulos

Abstract
. We consider a family of jointly Gaussian random vectors ξ j ∈ ℝ k j \xi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}} , each standard normal but possibly correlated, and investigate when 𝔼 ​ F ​ ( B ⁡ ( | T z 1 ​ f 1 ​ ( ξ 1 ) | , … , | T z n ​ f n ​ ( ξ n ) | ) ) ≤ F ⁡ ( 𝔼 ​ B ​ ( | f 1 ​ ( ξ 1 ) | , … , | f n ​ ( ξ n ) | ) ) \mathbb{E}\,F\!\Bigl(B\bigl(|T_{z_{1}}f_{1}(\xi_{1})|,\dots,|T_{z_{n}}f_{n}(\xi_{n})|\bigr)\Bigr)\;\;\leq\;\;F\!\Bigl(\,\mathbb{E}\,B\bigl(|f_{1}(\xi_{1})|,\dots,|f_{n}(\xi_{n})|\bigr)\Bigr) holds, where T z T_{z} is either a Mehler transform ( z ∈ ℂ z\in\mathbb{C} ) or a noise operator ( z ∈ ℝ z\in\mathbb{R} ). This framework unifies and extends real and complex hypercontractivity to multi-function settings, yielding multiversions of the sharp Hausdorff–Young inequality, the log-Sobolev inequality, and a “noisy” Gaussian–Jensen inequality. Applications include a new covariance-based characterization of the Brascamp–Lieb inequality in the presence of noise.

1.
Introduction

A classical result in harmonic analysis is the
Hausdorff–Young inequality with sharp constant
. It states that for functions
g
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and exponents
1<p\leq 2
,
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
, one has

\bigl\|\widehat{g}\bigr\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\;\leq\;C_{p,q}\;\bigl\|g\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where
\widehat{g}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)\,e^{-\,i\,x\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}y
is the Fourier transform, and the constant
C_{p,q}
is best possible. This celebrated theorem was first established by Beckner
[
11
]
. Beckner’s original argument began with an inequality on two points (a discrete
\{-1,1\}
hypercube setting), then used an induction and a central limit theorem to pass to the Gaussian setting, a result that has come to be called
complex hypercontractivity
. Afterward, a suitable change of variables yielded the Hausdorff–Young inequality in its full sharp form.

Over the years, several other remarkable proofs of this sharp inequality have appeared, together with stability results
[
24
]
. Janson
[
41
]
used a Brownian-motion (stochastic-calculus) approach; Lieb
[
44
]
exploited in a very illuminating way the symmetry of the Gaussian extremizers (“Gaussian kernels have only Gaussian maximizers”); and Hu
[
33
]
essentially replaced the stochastic part of Janson’s proof by writing everything in terms of heat flows. Later in
[
37
]
it was noted that the continuous heat-flow argument is, in fact, a limiting version of Beckner’s original discrete inductive scheme. This (continuous) flow is sometimes called the
Beckner–Janson flow

[
37
]
, although it is perhaps less familiar to many experts. It turns out that if one tries to use
standard
heat-flow methods in the usual manner, the monotonic interpolation property for the Hausdorff–Young inequality
fails
, as shown by Bennett, Bez, and Carbery
[
4
]
. In contrast, the subtle Beckner–Janson flow does give a monotonic approach and is the key new ingredient in these proofs.

Multifunction extensions.

In this paper, we carry forward the Beckner–Janson flow ideas to a
multifunction
setting. In doing so, we revisit several classical sharp estimates in harmonic analysis (such as the aforementioned Hausdorff–Young inequality, the Gaussian hypercontractive inequalities, etc.) and obtain their precise sharp analogues for multiple functions. To get a feel for one of our main results on the sharp multiversion of the Hausdorff–Young inequality, we refer the reader to Theorem
7
, i.e., the “baby” two-function case with equal exponents; in that setting, taking
f=g
and
\rho=0
recovers the sharp Beckner–Babenko inequality.

Beyond these specific corollaries, our main technical contribution lies in an
FB theorem
, i.e., Theorem
13
, which provides a fairly general framework for proving sharp multifunction estimates via the Beckner–Janson flow. We show that the classical Brascamp–Lieb inequalities and our new multifunction Hausdorff–Young inequalities can all be viewed as special cases of this single abstract FB theorem. While we mostly focus here on how this theorem extends sharp known inequalities to their natural “multi” analogues, we believe it may well have broader applicability for other families of inequalities, by allowing one to choose different pairs of
(F,B)
.

Theorem 1

(
n
-function complex hypercontractivity)
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
and
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The inequality

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|\prod_{j=1}^{n}|T_{z_{j}}f_{j}(\xi_{j})|^{p_{j}}\|_{\alpha}\leq\|\prod_{j=1}^{n}|f_{j}(\xi_{j})|^{p_{j}}\|_{1}

holds for all polynomials
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
if and only if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(\Re w_{i})\mathrm{cov}(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})(\Re w_{j})^{T}-\alpha(\Re z_{i}w_{i})\mathrm{cov}(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})(\Re z_{j}w_{j})^{T}\geq\Re\sum_{j}\frac{(1-z_{j}^{2})w_{j}w_{j}^{T}}{p_{j}}

holds for all
w_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{k_{j}}
where
j=1,\ldots,n
.

In the theorem
||X||_{p}=(\mathbb{E}|X|^{p})^{1/p}
denotes the
p

. The Mahler transform
T_{z_{j}}
acts on a polynomial

where

is the multiindex,
|\beta|=\beta_{1}+\ldots+\beta_{k_{j}}

and

denotes probabilists’ Hermite polynomial, where for a complex vector

w_{j}^{\beta}=w_{j1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots w_{jk_{j}}^{\beta_{j}}
,
z_{j}w_{j}=(z_{j}w_{j1},\ldots,z_{j}w_{jk_{j}})
, and
\Re w_{j}=(\Re w_{j1},\ldots,\Re w_{jk_{j}})
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
.

Theorem A

(Beckner
[
11
]
)
.

Let
X\sim N(0,\mathrm{Id_{n}})
. The inequality

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|T_{i\sqrt{p-1}}f(X)\|_{q}\leq\|f(X)\|_{p},

holds for all polynomials
f\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1,\quad 1\leq p\leq 2.

The inequality (
2.3
) is equivalent to Hausdorff–Young inequality with a sharp constant, namely

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\widehat{g}\left(x\right)\right|^{q}\mathrm{d}x\right]^{1/q}\leq C\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|g\left(x\right)\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right]^{1/p}

holds for all
g=\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})e^{-|x|^{2}/2p}
with the best possible constant

\displaystyle C=\frac{p^{n/2p}}{q^{n/2q}}(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2q}-\frac{n}{2p}},\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{g}(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)e^{-ix\cdot y}dy.

The connection of
T_{z}f
for imaginary
z
and Fourier transform
\widehat{f}
can be seen through Mehler’s formula

\displaystyle T_{z}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)\frac{1}{(\sqrt{1-z^{2}})^{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{z^{2}}{1-z^{2}}\right)+x\cdot y\frac{z}{1-z^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\gamma(y),

where
d\gamma(y)=\frac{e^{-|y|^{2}/2}}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}dy
is the standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, for
t\geq 1

(2.5)

\displaystyle T_{i\sqrt{t-1}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{|x|^{2}(t-1)}{2t}}}{t^{n/2}}\widehat{fe^{-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2t}}}\left(-\frac{x\sqrt{t-1}}{t}\right).

Using the well-known scaling property of the Fourier transform, one can verify that the function
e_{t}(x):=e^{-|x|^{2}/2t}
has Fourier transform
\widehat{e_{t}}(\omega)=t^{n/2}e^{-|\omega|^{2}t/2}
. Thus formula (
2.5
) rewrites

(2.6)

\displaystyle T_{i\sqrt{t-1}}f(x)=\frac{\widehat{fe_{t}}}{\widehat{e_{t}}}\left(-\frac{x\sqrt{t-1}}{t}\right).

Therefore, Beckner’s inequality (
2.3
) can be seen as another way of rewriting Hausdorff–Young inequality (
2.4
) in a form where the sharp constant becomes to be equal to
1
, and Gaussians can be easily seen to be extremizers

(2.7)

\displaystyle\left\|(\widehat{g}/\widehat{e_{p}})(\eta)\right\|_{q}\leq\left\|(g/e_{p})(X)\right\|_{p}\quad\text{where}\quad\eta=\frac{\sqrt{p-1}}{p}X,\quad X\sim N\left(0,\mathrm{Id}_{n}\right).

To summarize, the connection between (
2.7
) and Hausdorff–Young inequality (
2.4
) is that
g=fe_{p}
. When
z_{j}
’s are purely imaginary numbers in Theorem
1
then the condition (

2.5.3.
Noisy Borell’s theorem

Let
s\in[-1,1]
and let
X
and
X_{s}
be
s

\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the joint distribution
(X,X_{s})
is Gaussian in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Set

\displaystyle M(u,v;s):=\mathbb{P}(X\in H(u),\;X_{s}\in H(v))

for all
u,v\in(0,1)
, where
H(u)
and
H(v)
are any two parrallel halfspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
having Gaussian densities
\gamma(H(u))=u
, and
\gamma(H(v))=v
. For example, we can take
H(u)=(-\infty,\Phi^{-1}(u))\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, where
\Phi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\ell^{2}/2}d\ell
.

It is known that (see
[
47
]
) for
s\in[0,1]
we have

(2.35)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}M_{uu}&sM_{uv}\\
sM_{uv}&M_{vv}\end{pmatrix}\leq 0,

and the reverse inequality holds if
s\in[-1,0]
. Perhaps one of the important properties of
M
is that it satisfies modified Monge–Ampère equation, i.e.,

\displaystyle M_{uu}M_{vv}-s^{2}M_{uv}=0

for all
s\in[-1,1]
and all
u,v\in(0,1)
, see
[
47
,
39
]
for the details. The following corollary of Theorem
15
gives a noisy extension of Borell’s noise stability theorem.

Theorem 16
.

[Noisy Borell’s theorem]
For any
r_{1},r_{2}\in[-1,1]
, and any
s\in[-1,1]
set
\rho:=s\frac{\sqrt{(1-r_{1}^{2})(1-r_{2}^{2})}}{1-r_{1}r_{2}}
. For any Borel measurable sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(2.36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\in A,X_{\rho}\in B)\leq\mathbb{E}M\left(\left|\frac{A-r_{1}X}{\sqrt{1-r_{1}^{2}}}\right|_{\gamma},\left|\frac{B-r_{2}X_{\rho}}{\sqrt{1-r_{2}^{2}}}\right|_{\gamma};s\right)

holds for all
s\in(0,1)
, and the reverse inequality if
s\in(-1,0)
. Here
|C|_{\gamma}
denotes the standard gaussian measure of a measurable set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The inequality is sharp, i.e., the inequality becomes equality when
A
and
B
are parallel halfspaces (see the end of Section
4
).

When
r_{1}=r_{2}=0
the inequality coincides with Borell’s noise stability theorem

Theorem D

(Borell’s noise stability
[
16
]
)
.

For any
s\in[0,1]
and all measurable subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(2.37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\in A,X_{s}\in B)\leq M(|A|_{\gamma},|B|_{\gamma};s),

and the reverse inequality holds if
s\in[-1,0]
.

Borell’s noise stability theorem has found numerous applications across mathematics, including theoretical computer science, where it plays a central role in the Majority is Stablest theorem (see
[
25
,
46
]
). The inequality (
2.37
) also implies the sharp Gaussian isoperimetric inequality; see
[
42
]
.

Multiversion of (
2.37
) was obtained by Neeman
[
48
]
who showed that if the centered Gaussian vector
(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has the property
\mathrm{cov}(\zeta_{i},\zeta_{j})=m_{ij}\geq 0
, and
m_{ii}=1
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. Then

\displaystyle N(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}):=\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{1}\leq\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),\ldots,\zeta_{n}\leq\Phi^{-1}(u_{n}))

defined on

satisfies the inequality

(2.38)

\displaystyle\left\{N_{pq}m_{pq}\right\}_{p,q=1}^{n}\leq 0.

Moreover, the determinant of the matrix above is zero for all

. It follows from (
2.38
) that
\{N_{pq}m_{pq}\}_{p,q}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{k}\leq 0
. Thus Theorem
15
at point
r_{1}=\ldots=r_{n}=0
applied to

gives the following corollary due to Neeman
[
48
]

Theorem E

(Multiversion of Borrel’s noise stability)
.

Let
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{nk}
be Gaussian random vector with
\xi_{j}\sim N(0,\mathrm{Id}_{k})
,
\mathrm{cov}(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})=m_{ij}\mathrm{Id}_{k}
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\in A_{n})\leq N(|A_{1}|_{\gamma},\ldots,|A_{n}|_{\gamma})

for all measuarble sets
A_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
