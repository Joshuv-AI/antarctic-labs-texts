Prepare-and-Magic: Semi-Device Independent Magic Certification in the Prepare-and-Measure Scenario
Santiago Zamora, Rafael A. Macêdo, Tailan S. Sarubi, Moisés Alves, Davide Poderini, Rafael Chaves

Abstract
Non-stabilizerness is an essential resource for quantum computational advantage, as stabilizer states admit efficient classical simulation. We develop a semi-device-independent framework for certifying non-stabilizer states in prepare-and-measure (PAM) scenarios, relying only on assumptions about the system’s dimension. Within this framework, we introduce dimensional witnesses that can distinguish stabilizer from non-stabilizer states, and we provide analytical proofs that threshold violations of these witnesses certify non-stabilizerness. In the simplest setting—three preparations, two measurements, and qubit systems—surpassing a specific threshold guarantees that at least one prepared state lies outside the stabilizer polytope. We extend this approach by linking it to quantum random access codes, also generalizing our results to qutrit systems and introducing a necessary condition for certifying non-stabilizerness based on state overlaps (Gram matrices). These results offer a set of semi-device-independent tools for practically and systematically verifying non-stabilizer states using dimensional witnesses in PAM scenarios.



I
Introduction

Quantum computation offers exponential speed-ups over classical algorithms, yet not all quantum resources are equally powerful. The Gottesman-Knill theorem
Gottesman 1998
illustrates this by showing that stabilizer states, although entangled, are classically simulable
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
. Consequently, non-stabilizer states—those lying outside the stabilizer formalism—are essential for achieving universal fault-tolerant quantum computation
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
;
Howard and Campbell 2017a
. This resource, known as magic
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
(or non-stabilizerness), underpins quantum advantage
Gottesman 1997
.

Non-stabilizerness arises because Clifford operations and stabilizer states alone form a subtheory of quantum mechanics that admits efficient classical simulation
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
. Magic states, such as the T-state or the qutrit strange state
Heinrich and Gross 2019
;
Veitch et al. 2014
, when used as ancillary resources, elevate this subtheory to full universality via state injection and distillation protocols
Souza et al. 2011
;
Campbell and Browne 2009
. Non-stabilizer operations, which cannot be built from Clifford gates, also possess magic and often present implementation bottlenecks in practice.

Recent advances in resource theories have formalized magic through quantifiers such as robustness of magic
Howard and Campbell 2017b
;
Heinrich and Gross 2019
;
Hamaguchi et al. 2024
, mana
Veitch et al. 2014
, trace distance
Junior et al. 2025
, and stabilizer entropies
Leone et al. 2022
, each capturing distinct facets of non-stabilizerness. These measures admit operational interpretations and enable rigorous analysis of how magic evolves under quantum processes. For instance, robustness of magic quantifies the minimum mixture with stabilizer states required to represent a given state, while mana links to the negativity of the discrete Wigner function
Veitch et al. 2014
;
Gross 2006
;
Cormick et al. 2006
, reflecting its quasi-probabilistic character.

Geometrically, stabilizer states form convex polytopes in the state space whose complexity scales exponentially with system size
Heinrich and Gross 2019
. Though this complexity hampers direct analysis in large systems, studies of few-qudit systems have revealed important connections between geometry, entanglement, and magic
Reichardt 2009
;
Junior et al. 2025
;
Warmuz et al. 2024
.

Given the central role of non-stabilizerness, efficient certification methods are critical. Inspired by the use of Bell inequalities to detect entanglement, one might consider device-independent (DI) techniques to certify magic. However, such approaches face obstacles due to the dimension-sensitive nature of stabilizer states. For example, maximally entangled qubit states are stabilizers, whereas qutrit Bell states are not
Macedo et al. 2025
, complicating DI certification.

Nonetheless, specially tailored Bell inequalities have been shown to detect the presence of magic
Macedo et al. 2025
. These methods, however, depend on entanglement and are invariant under local unitaries, rendering them ineffective for certifying magic in single systems. This motivates semi-device-independent (SDI) approaches, which impose operational constraints without full device characterization. In particular, the prepare-and-measure (PAM) scenario
Gallego et al. 2010
emerges as a natural SDI framework for certifying magic without the need for multipartite systems.

The PAM scenario plays a versatile role in quantum information
Tavakoli et al. 2025
;
Svegborn et al. 2025
;
de Gois et al. 2021
. Here, a preparation device encodes data into classical or quantum states of fixed dimension, which are then sent to a measurement device. PAM implementations rely solely on dimensional assumptions, although alternative constraints such as entropy
Chaves et al. 2015a
, distinguishability
Brask et al. 2017
, or photon number
i Carceller et al. 2024
may be considered. PAM scenarios have found applications in quantum communication, QRACs
Li et al. 2012
;
Ambainis et al. 2009
, randomness generation
Rusca et al. 2019
;
Pawłowski and Brunner 2011
, entanglement certification
Moreno et al. 2021
;
Tavakoli et al. 2021
;
Pauwels et al. 2022
, self-testing
Tavakoli et al. 2020
, and foundational tests
Pawłowski et al. 2009
;
Chaves et al. 2015b
;
Roch i Carceller and Tavakoli 2025
;
Diviánszky et al. 2023
;
Chaves et al. 2018
;
Polino et al. 2019
. Their flexibility allows robust certification of quantum advantages using dimension witnesses (DW)
Gallego et al. 2010
.

In this work, we develop an SDI method to certify magic using PAM-based dimension witnesses. By constraining the dimension of transmitted messages, we derive criteria distinguishing stabilizer from non-stabilizer preparations. Extending the approach of
Horodecki et al. 1995
, we generalize the quantum violation criterion introduced in
Poderini et al. 2020
to a broader class of DW inequalities.

Using this framework, we show that violations of DW inequalities can certify the so-called ”set-magic”: a relational definition of the magic contained in the elements of an ensemble of states recently introduced in
Wagner et al. 2024a
. We begin with a minimal PAM setting—three preparations and two measurements—and prove that exceeding a certain threshold guarantees that at least one state lies outside the stabilizer polytope.

We further extend our analysis to scenarios with more preparations. Specifically, we show that dimension witness inequalities used in QRACs allow a finer-grained certification of magic: for instance, with eight prepared qubit states, the observed DW value can determine how many of those must carry magic.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section

, show an explicit analytical witness separating stabilizer from magic states, and demonstrate self-testing of H-type magic states. Section

develops a hierarchy of witnesses based on quantum random access codes: in Subsection
V.1
we treat
N\rightarrow 1
qubit QRACs
Ambainis et al. 2009
, quantifying the gap between classical, stabilizer, and quantum success probabilities as the number of preparations grows; in Subsection
V.2
we extend this framework to
2^{(d)}\rightarrow 1
d-dimensional QRACs
Farkas and Kaniewski 2019
. Section
VI

II
Stabilizer formalism for qudits

In this work, we focus on the detection of magic states in qudit systems. To this end, we begin by introducing and characterizing the set of all stabilizer states, which form a convex structure known as the stabilizer polytope.

Let us begin by defining a pair of operators that act on qudits states described in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In particular, given the computational basis
\{\ket{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{F}_{d}}
with
\mathbb{F}_{d}=\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
, where
d
is a prime number, one can define the Shift and Boost operators
Veitch et al. 2014
;
Macedo et al. 2025
:

X\ket{j}=\ket{j+1 \, \text{mod } d},\hskip 28.45274ptZ\ket{j}=\omega^{j}\ket{j},

(1)

where
\omega=\exp(2\pi i/d)
. These operators satisfy
X^{d}=Z^{d}=I
and
Z^{a}X^{b}=\omega^{ab}X^{b}Z^{a}
and they generate the qudit Pauli group defined as

\mathcal{P}_{d}=\{D_{a,b}=\omega_{d}^{ab}X^{a}Z^{b}\mid(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}_{d}\times\mathbb{F}_{d}\},

(2)

where
\omega_{d}=\omega^{2^{-1}}
if
d>2
, and
\omega_{d}=i
if
d=2
. All operations are taken modulo
d
; in particular,
2^{-1}
denotes the multiplicative inverse of
2
in
\mathbb{F}_{d}
.
The elements
D_{a,b}
are called displacement operators and the group multiplication is defined by

D_{a,b}\cdot D_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}=\omega_{d}^{a^{\prime}b-ab^{\prime}}D_{a+a^{\prime},b+b^{\prime}}.

(3)

The Clifford group
\mathcal{C}\ell_{d}
is defined as the normalizer of
\mathcal{P}_{d}
within the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(d)
. That is, it consists of all unitary operators that preserve
\mathcal{P}_{d}
under conjugation:

\mathcal{C}\ell_{d}=\{C\in\mathcal{U}(d)\mid CPC^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}\;,\;\forall P\in\mathcal{P}_{d}\}.

(4)

In other words, Clifford unitaries map Pauli operators to other Paulis, preserving the group structure. Given a Clifford unitary
C\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{d}
, there is a state
C|0\rangle
which must be
stabilized
by the Pauli
P=CZC^{\dagger}
, that is,
P\;C|0\rangle=+C|0\rangle
. We call such a state a stabilizer state. The group generated by
P
, denoted as
\mathcal{S}=\langle P\rangle
, is called the stabilizer group. We will use
|\mathcal{S}\rangle
to refer to the stabilizer state stabilized by
\mathcal{S}
.

In dimension
d
, there are
d(d+1)
stabilizer states
Veitch et al. 2014
, whose convex hull defines the stabilizer polytope:

\mathrm{STAB}_{d}=\mathrm{conv}\{|\mathcal{S}\rangle\langle\mathcal{S}|\mid\mathcal{S}\mathrm{\;is\;a\;stabilizer\;group}\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\;,

(5)

where
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
refers to the space of density matrices on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. That is, if
\rho\in\mathrm{STAB}_{d}
,
\rho=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\mathcal{S}}|\mathcal{S}\rangle\langle\mathcal{S}|
, with
p_{\mathcal{S}}\geq 0
and
\sum_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\mathcal{S}}=1
. This is the so-called
V
-representation of a polytope
Grünbaum et al. 1967
, where the vertices are specified. Alternatively, the stabilizer polytope can be characterized via its
H
-representation
Junior et al. 2025
:

\mathrm{STAB}_{d}=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\,\middle|\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A^{\vec{q}})\geq 0,\;\vec{q}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{d+1}\right\},

(6)

where the operators
A^{\vec{q}}=-I_{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\Pi_{j}^{q_{j}}
define the facets of the polytope
Howard et al. 2014
. Here,
\Pi_{j}^{q_{j}}
denotes the rank-
1
projector on the eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue
\omega^{q_{j}}
of the
j
-th operator in the set
\{D_{0,1},D_{1,0},D_{1,1},\ldots,D_{1,d-1}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{d}
.

The stabilizer polytope partitions the state space into two disjoint subsets: the polytope itself, which contains all stabilizer states, and its complement, non-stabilizer states
\rho\notin\mathrm{STAB}_{d}
, also referred to as
magic
states. Based on their importance, a quantum resource theory
Chitambar and Gour 2019
of non-stabilizerness can also be formulated
Veitch et al. 2014
, where the sets of free states are
\mathrm{STAB}_{d}
, and free operations are Clifford unitaries and Pauli measurements
1
1

1

This assumes a fixed dimension
d
, corresponding to what is known as the
stabilizer subtheory
. If the dimension is not fixed, then one can also consider tensor products with stabilizer ancillae qudits and partial traces as free operations
.

On the other side, in quantum networks, cryptographic protocols, or prepare-and-measure scenarios, one often deals with collections of independently prepared states. The operational power in such settings often derives from how distinguishable, incompatible, or non-orthogonal these states are with respect to a free set. However, in some cases, the certification of non-classicality in these scenarios can also depend on a relational aspect between the
elements of the set of independent states. As we will show in this paper, this is indeed the case when the task is to certify non-stabilizerness in the PAM scenario in a SDI independent way. We will consider the so-called
set-magic
first defined in Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024a
.
It characterizes, from a relational point of view, the non-stabilizerness of a finite set of states
\underline{\rho}=\{\rho_{i}\}_{i}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. Its definition is as follows: consider an ensemble
\underline{\rho}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
consisting of a finite number of elements, each of dimension
d
. We say that
\underline{\rho}
has set-magic if there is no unitary
U
such that
U\underline{\rho}U^{\dagger}\subseteq\mathrm{STAB}_{d}
. In other words, at least one element in the set must be outside the stabilizer set.

There are various proposals for the quantum verification of stabilizerness, and in particular, there are efficient protocols for magic quantification
Haug and Kim 2023
;
Haug et al. 2024
;
Haug and Tarabunga 2025
. However, these approaches are resource-intensive and rely on several assumptions and requirements. In the next section, we introduce and discuss PAM scenarios, which are semi-device-independent (SDI) protocols in the sense that they require only a single assumption: the quantum dimension
d
of the state. This minimal assumption makes PAM witnesses natural candidates for detecting set-magic.

III
PAM scenario and a hierarchy of three sets of correlations.

The PAM scenario involves two main devices, as seen in Fig.
1
: a preparation device that generates physical systems based on external inputs
x\in\mathbb{X}=\{1,\cdots,|\mathbb{X}|\}
described by a random variable
X
, and a measurement device that performs measurements conditional on another external random variable
Y
with possible values
y\in\mathbb{Y}=\{1,\cdots,|\mathbb{Y}|\}
. The measurement outputs
b\in\mathbb{B}=\{1,\cdots,|\mathbb{B}|\}
are represented by a third random variable
B
. This framework allows for a rigorous analytical and practical approach for examining fundamental distinctions between classical and quantum physical theories, particularly concerning dimensional and causal constraints imposed on the systems under study
Gallego et al. 2010
.

In the quantum description of the PAM scenario, given some fixed dimension
d
, the random variable
X
chooses
\rho_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, while
y\in\mathbb{Y}
fixes the measurement
M_{b}^{y}
to be performed with possible outcome
b\in\mathbb{B}
. By applying Born’s rule, the observed measurement probability is given by

p(b|x,y)=\text{Tr}(\rho_{x}M_{b}^{y}),

(7)

where
\sum_{b\in\mathbb{B}}M_{b}^{y}=1
and
M_{b}^{y}\succeq 0\quad\forall y,b
.

If the distribution admits a classical explanation,

i.e.
if
p(b|x,y)
is such that it can be decomposed as

p(b|x,y)=\sum_{\lambda,m}p(b|m,y,\lambda)p(m|x,\lambda)p(\lambda),

(8)

where
\lambda
is a latent variable that allows for correlations between the preparation and measurement devices, then the quantum message can be replaced instead by a classical random variable
M
taking values
m\in\mathbb{M}=\{1,\dots,|\mathbb{M}|\}
with
|\mathbb{M}|=d
. In Fig.
1
we show the associated causal structure represented by a directed acyclic graph (DAG).

Given the tuple
(d,\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{B})
, we say that a behaviour
\{p(b|x,y)\}_{b\in\mathbb{B},x\in\mathbb{X},y\in\mathbb{Y}}
is in
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}\subseteq[0,1]^{|\mathbb{B}||\mathbb{X}||\mathbb{Y}|}
if it admits the classical explanation written in Eq. (
8
), or in
\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}\subseteq[0,1]^{|\mathbb{B}||\mathbb{X}||\mathbb{Y}|}
if it admits a quantum state-measurement decomposition as in Eq. (
7
). The classical set is known to be a polytope
Gallego et al. 2010
, where the extrema are given by deterministic strategies.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the PAM scenario. A preparation device selects an input
X=x\in\{1,\cdots,|\mathbb{X}|\}
, producing a quantum state represented by the density matrix
\rho_{x}
. This state is subsequently measured by a measurement device configured with input
Y=y\in\{1,\cdots,|\mathbb{Y}|\}
, yielding outcome
B=b\in\{1,\cdots,|\mathbb{B}|\}
. Inside the square, we show the associated causal structure when considering a classical hidden variable
\lambda
between the preparation and measurement device.

Now we introduce a third set
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{STAB},d}\subseteq[0,1]^{|\mathbb{B}||\mathbb{X}||\mathbb{Y}|}
composed of correlations generated only by stabilizer states:

p(b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma_{x}M^{y}_{b}),\quad\quad\sigma_{x}\in\mathrm{STAB}_{d}\;.

(9)

We now show some general relations between the 3 sets.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\underline{\rho}=\{\rho_{x}\}_{x}
be a set of states and
\underline{M}=\{M^{y}_{b}\}_{b,y}
be a set of measurements. The map
(\underline{\rho},\underline{M})\mapsto\{p(b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{y}_{b})\}_{b\in B,x\in X,y\in Y}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}
has the same image for
(\underline{\rho},\underline{M})
and
(U\underline{\rho}U^{\dagger},U\underline{M}U^{\dagger})
for any
U\in\mathcal{U}(d)
.

Proof.

This follows from the cyclic property of the trace:

p(b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{y}_{b})=\mathrm{Tr}(U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}UM^{y}_{b}U^{\dagger})\;.

(10)

∎

This symmetry results in strong constraints for the correlation sets:

Lemma 1
.

(1)
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{STAB},d}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}
and (2) if
|\mathbb{X}|\leq d
, we say that the scenario becomes trivial, meaning that
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{STAB},d}=\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}
.

Proof.

(1) The last inclusion follows from
\mathrm{STAB}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
. We can show the hierarchy by first showing that
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}
. Given a behavior decomposition following Eq. (
8
), construct the following state:

\rho_{x}=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\sum_{m_{\lambda}=1}^{d}p(m_{\lambda}|x)\ket{m_\lambda}\bra{m_\lambda}\;,

(11)

where
\{\ket{m_\lambda}\}_{m_{\lambda}=1}^{d}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
defined by the latent variable
\lambda
. Note that for any choice of
\underline{M}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{y}_{b})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda,m_{\lambda}}p(\lambda)p(m|x,\lambda)\bra{m_\lambda}M^{y}_{b}\ket{m_\lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\lambda,m}p(\lambda)p(m|x,\lambda)p(b|y,m,\lambda)\;,

(12)

where in the last line we defined
p(b|y,m,\lambda)=\bra{m_\lambda}M^{y}_{b}\ket{m_\lambda}
and we relabeled the index
m_{\lambda}
to
m
expressing explicitly the dependence on
\lambda
. Hence, every classical correlation has a quantum description. Note that the state used in the proof is incoherent on the orthonormal basis
\{|m_{\lambda}\rangle\}
. Now let
U
be the unitary that rotates this basis to the computational basis, resulting in
\rho_{x}=U\sigma_{x}U^{\dagger}
where
\sigma_{x}\in\mathrm{STAB}_{d}
is an incoherent state in the computational basis. Then by Proposition
1
,
(\underline{\sigma},U^{\dagger}\underline{M}U)
will generate the same distribution as
(\underline{\rho},\underline{M})
, showing that
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{STAB},d}
.

(2) Consider a scenario where
|\mathbb{X}|\leq d
. Given any orthonormal basis
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
, we consider the set of quantum states

\rho_{x}=\ket{x}\bra{x}\;,

(13)

for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
. Clearly, the corresponding set of correlations
p(b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{y}_{b})=\bra{x}M^{y}_{b}\ket{x}\in\mathcal{C}_{C,d}
for any measurement set
\underline{M}
. Now, let
\{q(b|x,y)\}_{b\in\mathbb{B},\,x\in\mathbb{X},\,y\in\mathbb{Y}}
be any set of behaviors. We construct the measurement operators as

Q^{y}_{b}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{X}}q(b|x,y)\ket{x}\bra{x}+r^{y}_{b}\,\Pi^{\perp},

(14)

where
\Pi^{\perp}
denotes the projector onto the subspace orthogonal to
\mathrm{span}\{\ket{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{X}}
. The coefficients
\{r^{y}_{b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{B}}
define a probability distribution that weights the projector
\Pi^{\perp}
to ensure the completeness relation
\sum_{b}Q^{y}_{b}=\mathbb{I}
holds even when
|\mathbb{X}|<d
. If
|\mathbb{X}|=d
, the vectors
\{\ket{x}\}
form a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, so
\Pi^{\perp}=0
and the extension term is unnecessary. Note that
\bra{x}Q^{y}_{b}\ket{x}=q(b|x,y)
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, so the correlations are preserved. In other words, by considering the measurement
Q^{y}_{b}
, we can reconstruct any given distribution. This is only possible if
|\mathbb{X}|\leq d
, since one can encode all inputs in distinguishable states as done in Eq. (
13
). This shows that
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}=\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}
.
∎

The usual way to characterize the observed PAM correlations is through the so-called
dimensional witnesses
. Given a scenario
S\in\{C,\mathrm{STAB},Q\}
(
S
can represent the classical, stabilizer or quantum scenario), the set of numbers
\{W^{b}_{x,y}\}_{b\in\mathbb{B},x\in\mathbb{X},y\in\mathbb{Y}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
defines the dimensional witness

W^{S}\geq W=\sum_{b,x,y}W^{b}_{x,y}p(b|x,y)

(15)

where

\displaystyle W^{S}

\displaystyle\equiv\max_{p\in\mathcal{C}_{S,d}}\sum_{b,x,y}W^{b}_{x,y}p(b|x,y)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\rho_{x}\}\subseteq S\\
\{M^{y}\}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\end{subarray}}\sum_{b,x,y}W^{b}_{x,y}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{b}_{y})\;,

(16)

denotes the value
S
of
W
.
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
denotes the space of
|\mathbb{B}|
-outcome measurements on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The hierarchy on Lemma
1
guarantees that
W^{C}\leq W^{\mathrm{STAB}}\leq W^{Q}
.

Now, given some set
\{\rho_{x}\}_{x\in X}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
and a witness
W
, considering all possible measurements defines the maximum value achievable by
W
on this set of states as

W(\{\rho_{x}\}_{x})\equiv\max_{M^{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})}\sum_{b,x,y}W^{b}_{x,y}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{y}_{b})\;.

(17)

Consider the violation
W(\{\rho_{x}\}_{x})>W^{S}
. It has the following interpretation.

Lemma 2
.

Let
W(\{\rho_{x}\}_{x})>W^{S}
. Then,
\nexists U\in\mathcal{U}(d)
such that
\{U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}\}_{x}\subset S
.

Proof.

Note that
W(\{U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}\}_{x})=W(\{\rho_{x}\}_{x})
, since the optimization over measurements is invariant under unitaries. Then, if such a unitary exists, we have
W(\{\rho_{x}\})=W(\{U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}\}_{x}\subset S)\leq W^{S}
.
∎

Hence, a violation certifies the non-inclusion of the set
\{\rho_{x}\}_{x\in X}
in
S
. The scenario
S=C
arises when one considers

ensembles of states

;
Wagner et al. 2024a
;
Galvão and Brod 2020
.
Set-coherence
was first introduced in

, and is a basis-independent definition of coherence which measures if a set of states can be written diagonally on the same basis. In the case of
S=\mathrm{STAB}
, it falls exactly into the set-magic definition of the last section.

\mathcal{C}_{C,d}\subset\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{STAB},d}\subset\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}\;.

IV
simplest pam scenario

In this section, we will show that the simplest PAM scenario already separates the classical-stabilizer-quantum correlations. The scenario is defined by the tuple

. Given

S_{3}=E_{11}+E_{12}+E_{21}-E_{22}-E_{31}\leq S^{C}_{3}=3,

\rho_{x}=\frac{\mathbb{I}+\vec{r}_{x}\cdot\vec{X}}{2},\quad M_{b}^{y}=\frac{\mathbb{I}+(-1)^{b}\vec{s}_{y}\cdot\vec{X}}{2},

(20)

with

\vec{s}_{y}=\frac{\vec{r}_{1}+(-1)^{y+1}\vec{r}_{2}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\vec{r}_{3}=\frac{-\vec{r}_{1}-\vec{r}_{2}}{\sqrt{2}},

(21)

and where
\vec{X}=(X,Y,Z)
denotes the vector of Pauli matrices. Remarkably, it turns out that
S_{3}
also witnesses non-stabilizerness.

Lemma 3
.

S^{C}_{3}<S_{3}^{\mathrm{STAB}}=\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{2}<S^{Q}_{3}\;.

(22)

Proof.

Consider the states
\rho_{x}=(1+\vec{r}_{x}\cdot\vec{X})/2
for
x\in\{1,2,3\}
. Ref.
Poderini et al. 2020
showed that optimization over measurements of
S_{3}
yields

\displaystyle S_{3}(\{\vec{r}_{x}\}_{x})=\max_{\{\vec{r}_{x}\}_{x}}\|\vec{r}_{1}+\vec{r}_{2}-\vec{r}_{3}\|_{2}+\|\vec{r}_{1}-\vec{r}_{2}\|_{2}.

(23)

We consider
S=\mathrm{STAB}
, where
\vec{r}_{x}\in\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},\pm e_{3}\}
, with
e_{1}=(1,0,0)
,
e_{2}=(0,1,0)
and
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
. Then, the inequality is optimized by choosing
\vec{r}_{2}\perp\vec{r}_{1}
and
\vec{r}_{3}=-\vec{r}_{1}
, yielding
S^{\mathrm{STAB}}_{3}=\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{2}
.
∎

Upon substituting the numerical values, we have
3<S^{\mathrm{STAB}}\approx 3.65\lesssim 3.82
showing a gap between the stabilizer and quantum value, implying a separation on the sets of correlations
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}\subset\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{STAB},d}\subset\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}
.

IV.1
On two stabilizer preparations

Then, one can ask how much magic is needed in the states in order to saturate the quantum value of the inequality. For the rest of this section, we will explore this question. In fact, define the
2-\mathrm{STAB}
scenario in which two of the three states are restricted to be stabilizers and the third one can be any quantum state. Then the problem reduces to studying

S^{2-\mathrm{STAB}}_{3}(\rho)=\max_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathrm{ext}(\mathrm{STAB}_{2})}\|\vec{r}_{1}+\vec{r}_{2}-\vec{r}\|_{2}+\|\vec{r}_{1}-\vec{r}_{2}\|_{2}\;,

(24)

as a function of the magic of
\rho
. Here we considered
\rho_{x\in\{1,2\}}=(1+\vec{r}_{x}\cdot\vec{X})/2
and
\rho=(1+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{X})/2
. Note also that the optimization is over the extrema of
\mathrm{STAB}_{2}
, where
\mathrm{ext}(\mathrm{STAB}_{2})
correspond to the vertices of the qubit stabilizer polytope:

\mathrm{ext}(\mathrm{STAB}_{2})=\{\frac{1}{2}(1\pm X),\frac{1}{2}(1\pm Y),\frac{1}{2}(1\pm Z)\}\;.

(25)

Without loss of generality, we can always take
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})=(1+\vec{r}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}_{2})/2=1/2
, since orthogonal vectors still maximize the argument of the optimization. One can verify that indeed we can already reach the maximum quantum value if the argument is optimized:

\max_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2})}S^{2-\mathrm{STAB}}_{3}(\rho)=S^{Q}_{3}=1+2\sqrt{2}\;,

(26)

which can be verified to be saturated for example, by
\vec{r}_{1}=(1,0,0)
,
\vec{r}_{2}=(0,1,0)
and
\vec{r}_{3}=(-1/\sqrt{2},-1/\sqrt{2},0)
. In fact, we can show a stronger result: This inequality self-tests
H
-type magic states
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
, defined as the Clifford orbit of
H=[1+(X+Z)/\sqrt{2}]/2
:

Proposition 2
.

S^{2-\mathrm{STAB}}_{3}(\rho)=S^{Q}_{3}
if and only if
\rho=CHC^{\dagger}
, for some
C\in\mathcal{C}\ell_{2}
.

Proof.

We can use Eq. (
21
), where our vector to be optimized is now

\vec{r}=-\frac{\vec{r}_{1}+\vec{r}_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\;,

(27)

where
\vec{r}_{1}\cdot\vec{r}_{2}=0
, corresponding to
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
two orthogonal stabilizer states. From the 6 stabilizer states, there are
6\times 4/2=12
such pairs, which correspond exactly to the
12

H
-type magic states.
∎

This guarantees the self-test of magic states by using a dimensional witness.

One can ask about how much non-stabilizerness is needed to violate this inequality. The natural measure to consider is the non-stabilizerness by trace distance, defined as
Junior et al. 2025

\mathcal{N}\mathcal{S}(\rho)\equiv\min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{STAB}_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}\;,

(28)

plotted on Fig.
2
for visual aid. Geometrically, it represents the distance to the stabilizer polytope as measured by the trace norm, defined as
\|A\|_{1}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}
. It is well-known that it is maximized by
T
-type stabilizer states, defined as the Clifford orbit of
T=\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(X+Y+Z))
.

In Ref.

\mathcal{N}\mathcal{S}(\rho)\geq-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{STAB_{2}}}(\rho)\;,

(29)

where we defined
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{STAB_{2}}}(\rho)\equiv\min[0,\{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A^{\vec{q}})\}_{\vec{q}\in\mathbb{\{}0,1\}^{3}}]
, with
\{A^{\vec{q}}\}_{\vec{q}\in\mathbb{\{}0,1\}^{3}}
the set of operators defining the eight facets of
\mathrm{STAB}_{2}
as shown in Eq. (

). Noting that the set of facets are related by Clifford unitaries, it shows that
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{STAB}}_{2}(\rho)
is a faithful resource measure. In fact, for qubits, the quantity
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{STAB}_{2}}(\rho)
assigns the same magic value to all states on a hyperplane parallel to a stabilizer polytope facet. This leads to an underestimation of magic for states outside the facet’s projection, effectively measuring their distance as if they were in fact parallel to a facet. As such,
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{STAB}_{2}}
acts as a lower bound to the non-stabilizerness measure
\mathcal{NS}
. Within the facet’s projection, however, both measures coincide up to a rescaling by
-\sqrt{3}/3
, since the hyperplane distance matches the minimum distance from the states to the polytope
2
2

2

One can think of it as a coarsed-grained version of the trace distance that neglects the finiteness of the facets of the stabilizer polytope.
. In particular, this is true for the
T
-type stabilizer states
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
, defined as the Clifford orbit of
T(1+(X+Y+Z)/\sqrt{3})/2
, given the maximum of
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{STAB_{2}}}_{\mathrm{max}}\approx 0.366
, or
\mathcal{NS}_{\max}\approx 0.211

\displaystyle s_{3}(w)\equiv

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{STAB_{2}}}(\rho)=w,

which in terms of the Bloch vectors it can be rewritten as

\displaystyle s_{3}(w)\equiv

(31)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\vec{r}_{1}=(1,0,0)\;,\;\vec{r}_{2}=(0,1,0),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\min[0,\{1+{q_{1}}x+q_{2}y+{q_{3}}z\}_{\vec{q}\in\mathbb{\{}\pm 1\}^{3}}]=w,

V
Quantum random access codes

Although it is now clear that a separation already appears even in the minimal PAM scenario, we will present a class of scenarios, with an increasing number of preparations with witnesses corresponding to a physically-motivated quantity.

V.1
Qubit QRACS

The witnesses considered in this section arise in the study of quantum random access codes
Ambainis et al. 2009
;
Li et al. 2012
;
Tavakoli et al. 2015
. In this scenario, there are two parties, Alice and Bob, where Alice encodes a bit-string
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
into a single qubit state and sends it to Bob, who extracts one of the
N
bits of
x
by performing a measurement. The encoding strategy are
2^{N}
assignments of bit-strings into states,
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}\mapsto\rho_{x}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
and the decoding strategy is given by the choice of
N
measurements
(M^{1},\cdots,M^{N})
. The average success probability of Bob correctly measuring the bit-string in such a setup is

\bar{p}_{N}=\frac{1}{2^{N}N}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\sum_{y=1}^{N}p(x_{y}|x,y)\;,

(34)

where
p(b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{y}_{b})
is the probability of obtaining of the outcome
b
of the measurement
M^{y}
in the encoded state
\rho_{x}
. Those behaviors are clearly described by the set
\mathcal{C}_{Q,d}
of a
(d=2,|\mathbb{X}|=2^{N},|\mathbb{Y}|=N,|\mathbb{B}|=2)
PAM scenario, which we will also refer as the
N\to 1
qubit QRAC
Ambainis et al. 2009
. The other two corresponding sets
\mathcal{C}_{C,d}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{STAB},d}
are defined by restricting the encoding to classical and stabilizer states, respectively. Remarkably, as we will discuss below, the average success probability is a separating witness for various values of
N\geq 3
:

\bar{p}^{C}_{N}<\bar{p}^{\mathrm{STAB}}_{N}<\bar{p}^{Q}_{N}.

(35)

First, note that
M^{b=1}_{y}=1-M^{b=0}_{y}
, which allows us to write:

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{N-1}N}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\sum_{y=1}^{N}(-1)^{x_{y}}p(0|x,y)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{T_{N}}{2^{N-1}N}\right)

(36)

where we have defined

T_{N}\equiv\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\sum_{y=1}^{N}(-1)^{x_{y}}p(0|x,y).

(37)

The optimization over
T_{N}
can be written in terms of Bloch vectors as stated in the following Lemma:

Lemma 4
.

Let
S\in\{C,\mathrm{STAB},Q\}
be one of the three scenarios in the
N\to 1
qubit QRAC. Then, the average success probability is given by

\bar{p}^{S}_{N}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{T_{N}^{S}}{2^{N-1}N}\right)\;,

(38)

where

T^{S}_{N}=\max_{\{\vec{r}_{x}\}\in\mathrm{ext}(S)}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y=1}^{N}\|\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}(-1)^{x_{y}}\vec{r}_{x}\|_{2}\;,

(39)

and
\{\vec{r}_{x}\}
are the Bloch vectors of the states with the encoded message. The extremal set
\mathrm{ext}(S)
is defined by

\mathrm{ext}(S)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\{e_{3},-e_{3}\}\;,\mathrm{for\;}S=C\;,\\
&\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},\pm e_{3}\}\;,\mathrm{for\;}S=\mathrm{STAB}\;,\\
&S^{2}\;,\mathrm{for\;}S=Q\;,\end{aligned}\right.

(40)

with
e_{1}=(1,0,0)
,
e_{2}=(0,1,0)
and
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
.

Proof.

We can write both the states and measurements in the Bloch ball as:

\displaystyle\rho_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+\vec{r}_{x}\cdot\vec{X})\;,

(41)

\displaystyle M^{0}_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+\vec{v}_{y}\cdot\vec{X})\;,

(42)

yielding the following form for the optimized witness:

\displaystyle T^{S}_{N}

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\rho_{x}\}\subseteq S\\
\{M_{y}\}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\end{subarray}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\sum_{y=1}^{N}(-1)^{x_{y}}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{x}M^{0}_{y})

(43)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\vec{r}_{x}\}\subseteq\mathrm{ext}(S)\\
\{\vec{v}_{y}\}\subseteq S^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\sum_{y=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{x_{y}}}{2}(1+\vec{v}_{y}\cdot\vec{r}_{x})\;.

(44)

We now proceed to optimize over the measurements. The first term vanishes, due to the fact that every bit string
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
has a corresponding parity-flipped string
\mathrm{XOR}(x)
, canceling in pairs. Defining
\vec{R}_{y}=\sum_{x}(-1)^{x_{y}}\vec{r}_{x}
, we arrive at

T^{S}_{N}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\vec{r}_{x}\}\subseteq\mathrm{ext}(S)\\
\{\vec{v}_{y}\}\subseteq S^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y=1}^{N}\vec{v}_{y}\cdot\vec{R}_{y}\;,

(45)

which is a sum of inner products, maximized when
\vec{v}_{y}=\vec{R}_{y}/\|\vec{R}_{y}\|_{2}
. By substituting in Eq. (
45
), we arrive at the result.
∎

For
N=1
,
|\mathbb{X}|=2^{1}=d
and then due to Lemma
1
the scenario is trivial. Then, our task is to consider the optimization problem only for
N\geq 2
. To do so, we present a helpful proposition.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\{\vec{r}^{*}_{x}\}_{x}\in\mathrm{argmax}(T^{S}_{N})
. Then,
\vec{r}^{*}_{x}=-\vec{r}^{*}_{\mathrm{XOR}(x)}
.

Proof.

Consider a splitting of the set of bit strings of the form
\{0,1\}^{N}=S_{+}\sqcup S_{-}
, where
S_{-}=\mathrm{XOR}(S_{+})
. This is,
S_{-}
are the bitstrings of
S_{+}
under the XOR operation. It follows that

\displaystyle\vec{R}_{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}(-1)^{x_{y}}\vec{r}_{x}

(46)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in S_{+}}(-1)^{x_{y}}\vec{r}_{x}-\sum_{x\in S_{+}}(-1)^{x_{y}}\vec{r}_{\mathrm{XOR}(x)}

(47)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in S_{+}}(-1)^{x_{y}}[\vec{r}_{x}-\vec{r}_{\mathrm{XOR}(x)}]\;.

(48)

This configuration has a witness value of
T_{N}(\{\vec{r}_{x}\}_{x\in S_{+}\sqcup S_{-}})=1/2\sum_{y}\|\vec{R}_{y}\|_{2}
. We express the 2-norm by the optimization

\|\vec{R}_{y}\|_{2}=\max_{\vec{u}_{y}\in S^{2}}\;\vec{u}_{y}\cdot\vec{R}_{y}\;,

(49)

which allows us to write

\displaystyle T^{S}_{N}(\{\vec{r}_{x}\}_{x})

\displaystyle=\max_{\{\vec{u}_{y}\}\in S^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in S_{+}}\left(\sum_{y=1}^{N}(-1)^{x_{y}}\vec{u}_{y}\right)\cdot(\vec{r}_{x}-\vec{r}_{\mathrm{XOR}(x)})

(50)

\displaystyle=\max_{\{\vec{u}_{y}\}\in S^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in S_{+}}\vec{V}_{x}\cdot(\vec{r}_{x}-\vec{r}_{\mathrm{XOR}(x)})\;,

(51)

where we defined
\vec{V}_{x}=\sum_{y}(-1)^{x_{y}}\vec{u}_{y}
. The sum of inner products, upon optimizing over
\{\vec{r}_{x}\}_{x}
, is maximized by:

\vec{r}_{x}^{*}=-\vec{r}^{*}_{x}=\frac{\vec{V}_{x}}{\|\vec{V}_{x}\|}\;.

(52)

∎

For small
N
, we can show the following.

Theorem 1
.

The set of inequalities

•

T^{C}_{2}=2<T^{\mathrm{STAB}}_{2}=T^{Q}_{2}=2\sqrt{2}
,

•

T^{C}_{3}=6<T^{\mathrm{STAB}}_{3}=2\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}
,

are true.

Proof.

The classical values of the witnesses were already derived in
Ambainis et al. 2009

Li et al. 2012

3

\displaystyle\|r_{00}-\vec{r}_{01}\|_{2})\;.

(53)

\{\vec{r}_{00},\vec{r}_{01}\}\in S^{2}

For

(54)

where we optimize over
\{\vec{r}_{000},\vec{r}_{100},\vec{r}_{010},\vec{r}_{001}\}\in\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},\pm e_{3}\}

To complete our analysis, we introduce in the next subsection a formal criterion that shows necessary conditions for the existence of a stabilizer bound for a given witness. The key idea is that, when restricting to stabilizer states, the set of pure stabilizer states is discrete and of measure zero within the set of all quantum states. As a result, the maximum value of any witness is generally unattainable by stabilizer states, unless the optimal quantum strategy happens to align (up to rigid rotations) with one of the polytope’s vertices.

VI
Separating Witnesses and State overlaps

In the previous sections, we introduced separating witnesses for various PAM scenarios, illustrating the hierarchy established in Eq. (
18
). Here, we present a necessary condition to determine whether a given witness
{W_{x,y}^{b}}
may certify magic. Our analysis draws upon recent developments that characterize resource sets through state overlaps
Wagner et al. 2024b
;
Galvão and Brod 2020
.

Due to convexity, the optimization given in Eq. (
16
) can be performed over the extremal sets (of measurements and states, although in the following we will look only at the set of states) defined in the
S
scenario. Denoting
\mathrm{ext}S
the extremal set of states in scenario
S
, Eq. (
16
) is equivalent to

W^{S}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\psi\rangle\in\mathrm{ext}S\\
\{M^{y}\}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\end{subarray}}\sum_{b,x,y}W^{b}_{x,y}\bra{\psi}M^{y}_{b}\ket{\psi}\;,

(69)

In particular, if
S
is the quantum scenario, the extremal set of states are the pure states
\mathrm{ext}S=\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For
S\in\{C,\mathrm{STAB}\}
, the corresponding set of states is not only convex, but is also given by a polytope. This means that
\mathrm{ext}S
is a finite set of extremal elements. In the classical scenario the number of these elements is given by
|\mathrm{ext}C|=d
, where
|\mathrm{ext}C|
denotes the cardinality of a
d
-dimensional orthonormal basis, while in the stabilizer scenario the number of extremal elements is given by
|\mathrm{ext}\mathrm{STAB}|=d(d+1)
,
|\mathrm{ext}\mathrm{STAB}|
denotes the number of vertices considered in Eq. (
5
). We claim that the natural object to look at is the corresponding Gram Matrix of the extremal states

G^{S}_{ij}\equiv\langle\psi_{i}|\psi_{j}\rangle\;;\;i,j\in\{1,\cdots,|\mathrm{ext}S|\}\;,

(70)

which is invariant under unitaries
\ket{\psi_i}\mapsto U\ket{\psi_i}
. Indeed, the following Proposition shows that the Gram matrix completely determines the structure of unitary invariants.

Proposition 4
.

Consider two set of states
\{\ket{\psi_x}\}_{x},\{\ket{\phi_x}\}_{x}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
with same cardinality. Then, it
\exists U\in\mathcal{U}(d)
such that
\ket{\psi_x}=U\ket{\phi_x}
if and only if :

\langle\psi_{x}|\psi_{x^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle\phi_{x}|\phi_{x^{\prime}}\rangle\;,\forall x,x^{\prime}\;.

(71)

The proof can be found in Appendix
B
. Recalling the invariance under unitaries of the witness,
W(\{U|\psi_{x}\rangle\langle|\psi_{x}|U^{\dagger}\}_{x\in\mathbb{X}})=W(\{|\psi_{x}\rangle\langle\psi_{x}|\}_{x\in\mathbb{X}})
, we give a necessary condition for a set of states to violate an inequality: the corresponding Gram matrix must be distinct from those generated in the scenario
S
. We have the following theorem, whose proof is written in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a dimensional witness
W
with classical and stabilizer value
W^{C}
and
W^{\mathrm{STAB}}
respectively. Consider some set of states
\{\ket{\psi_x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
, with the corresponding
|\mathbb{X}|\times|\mathbb{X}|
Gram matrix
G_{x,x^{\prime}}\equiv\langle\psi_{x}|\psi_{x^{\prime}}\rangle
. Given
S\in\{C,\mathrm{STAB}\}
,

1.

Given any subset
\{|\psi^{S}_{x}\rangle\}_{x}\subseteq\mathrm{ext}S
, the corresponding Gram matrices
G^{S}_{x,x^{\prime}}=\langle\psi^{S}_{x}|\psi^{S}_{x^{\prime}}\rangle
is severely constrained in the classical and stabilizer scenarios:
G^{C}_{x,x^{\prime}}\in\{0,1\}
, and
G^{\mathrm{STAB}}_{x,x^{\prime}}\in\{0,1/\sqrt{d},1\}
.

2.

if
W(\{|\psi_{x}\rangle\langle\psi_{x}|\}_{x})>W^{S}
, then, there is no such subset such that
G_{x,x^{\prime}}=G^{S}_{x,x^{\prime}}
.

The theorem shows that state overlaps can be used to efficiently determine whether a set of states respects a given inequality. Consider a witness
W
with a scenario (classical or stabilizer) bound denoted by
W^{\mathrm{S}}
. If one can construct a set of states
{\ket{\psi_x}}_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that their pairwise overlaps match those defined by
\mathrm{ext}S
, then these states cannot exceed the scenario bound, i.e.,
W\leq W^{\mathrm{S}}
.

However, in general, the absence of such matching does not necessarily imply a violation of the dimensional witness value
W^{S}
. This illustrates that, in general, employing a witness offers a stronger form of certification than merely comparing state overlaps. This idea is also reflected in Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024a
, where the authors use overlap inequalities rather than relying solely on the overlaps themselves. A key distinction between our approach and theirs is that dimensional witnesses do not require knowledge of all pairwise overlaps within the considered set of states; it suffices to observe the overall statistics. The drawback, however, is that our method assumes the states are
d
-dimensional, which can be challenging to guarantee in practice.

Finally, we note that dimensional witnesses can be seen trivially as overlap inequalities on the overlaps
O_{xy}^{b}=Tr(\rho_{x}M^{y}_{b})

W=\sum_{b,x,y}W^{b}_{x,y}p(b|x,y)=\sum_{b,x,y}W^{b}_{x,y}O_{xy}^{b}.

(72)

Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024b
demonstrates that inequalities for various quantum resources can be derived by considering polytopes defined by overlaps, such as those between states, or between measurements and states, as in the example above. Although the authors do not explicitly construct dimensional witnesses within this framework, it appears plausible that such witnesses could also be obtained using their overlap-based formalism.
As a less trivial observation, we note that some of the dimensional witnesses studied in this work can be written in terms of state overlaps
r_{ij}=Tr(\rho_{i}\rho_{j})
between state
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}
. For example, considering the form (
23
) of Ineq.
S_{3}
, by expressing the norms in terms of the dot product in the Euclidean space and by noting that for qubits the dot product of their Bloch vectors is related to their overlap as

\vec{r}_{x}\cdot\vec{r}_{y}=2r_{xy}-1
, we can find that
S_{3}
can be written as

\displaystyle\sqrt{-1+2(r_{11}+r_{22}+r_{33})+4(r_{12}-r_{13}-r_{23})}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2(r_{11}+r_{22})-4r_{12}}\leq 3,

(73)

which for pure states reduces to

\sqrt{5+4(r_{12}-r_{13}-r_{23})}+2\sqrt{1-r_{12}}\leq 3.

(74)

Similarly, one can show that the
T_{N}
witnesses take the form

T_{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\sum_{xy}(-1)^{x_{i}+y_{i}}r_{xy}}.

(75)

We note that when expressed through states overlaps, the resulting inequalities cannot represent a polytope facet since they are not linear. It may be worth to contrast with Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024a
where they use linear overlap inequalities for non-stabilizerness detection. As another note, the inequalities they used were designed for detecting coherence, which is compatible with our results, reinforcing the idea that coherence and non-stabilizerness SDI detection may be treated within the same formalism. Regarding this observation and as a final note, we realized that in Ref.
Sarkar and Datta 2025
they constructed a witness to detect coherence, which ends up being able to detect magic as well. We found that the inequality they constructed is nothing else than
S_{3}
, which explains their findings.

VII
FINAL REMARKS

This work introduces a semi-device-independent framework for certifying non-stabilizer (magic) states in prepare-and-measure scenarios. By relying solely on a minimal assumption about the dimension of the quantum system, the proposed dimensional witnesses offer a robust and experimentally feasible approach to detecting non-stabilizerness.

In the simplest setting—three state preparations and two measurement settings—we identify a concrete threshold whose violation certifies the presence of non-stabilizer states. Notably, the maximal violation occurs uniquely for states in the Clifford orbit of the “H-state,” providing a kind self-testing method for the experimental verification of such states. Interestingly, we also observe that in certain cases, states with intermediate levels of non-stabilizerness yield the strongest witness violations, suggesting a nuanced relationship between this resource and operational detectability.

Extensions to scenarios with more preparations reveal further structure. In particular, for eight preparations, the observed witness values allow one to infer a lower bound on the number of non-stabilizer preparations involved. This quantitative feature supports more refined experimental protocols and informs the design of tasks such as quantum random access codes (QRACs), highlighting the operational relevance of non-stabilizer states.

We also propose a criterion based on state overlaps (Gram matrices), offering a necessary condition for non-stabilizerness certification. Specifically, the appearance of matrix elements outside the set
{0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},1}
is shown to be essential in
d
-dimensional systems. This connection points toward a deeper interplay between resource-theoretic concepts and semi-device-independent methods.

Altogether, our results provide a set of analytically grounded and practically applicable tools for the study and certification of non-stabilizer states. They contribute to the broader effort to understand and utilize the resources necessary for achieving quantum computational advantages within a minimal-assumption framework.
