Assessment of Optical Attenuation and Skin Thickness in Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus Patients Using Optical Coherence Tomography
Authors: Minghui Chen, Yue Shen, Jianguo Zhu, Tingwei Su, Yifei Zhang, Weiqing Wang, Chang Chen, Lin Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetes management often involves invasive blood glucose monitoring, which can be uncomfortable for patients. Non‐invasive techniques like multiple μ‐spatially offset Raman spectroscopy (mμSORS) offer a promising alternative. To provide clinical data supporting mμSORS, we conducted a clinical trial with 198 participants to evaluate mμSORS for non‐invasive blood glucose measurement. Using Optical Coherence Tomography, we studied skin thickness and optical attenuation in 172 diabetic and 26 healthy subjects. Results showed thicker stratum corneum and stratum spinosum (SS) in diabetics. Epidermal thickness increased with age and body mass index (BMI), decreased with skin brightness, and varied minimally with gender. Optical attenuation in SS was lower in diabetics, decreased with increasing a*, and was minimally affected by gender and BMI but increased with age in the upper dermis. These findings support mμSORS for accurate non‐invasive glucose monitoring.Keywords: biomedical imaging, optical attenuation, optical coherence tomography, skin thicknesss, type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Blood glucose monitoring is crucial for managing diabetes, especially for the over 500 million patients worldwide [1]. Traditional methods are invasive and uncomfortable. Recently, non‐invasive technologies such as near‐infrared spectroscopy, photoacoustic spectroscopy, transcutaneous impedance spectroscopy, and polarimetry have attracted considerable attention due to their potential to enhance patient comfort and compliance [2]. Raman spectroscopy is particularly promising due to its high specificity for glucose molecules. However, it faces challenges like background signals from the skin surface and the need for individualized calibration, leading to significant variability [3, 4].

Multiple μ‐spatially offset Raman spectroscopy (mμSORS) can bypass the skin surface to directly obtain glucose signals from the epidermis and dermis, showing high precision and accuracy in blood glucose measurements. Despite these advancements, several challenges still exist. One major issue is the variability in skin structure and composition, such as the thickness of the stratum corneum (SC) and stratum spinosum (SS), as well as their optical attenuation properties. The SC, the outermost layer of the epidermis, and the SS, located just beneath it, can vary significantly between individuals due to factors like age, body mass index (BMI), and skin brightness. These variations can affect the penetration depth of the laser and the intensity of the Raman signals, impacting the accuracy and reliability of mμSORS readings. In collaboration with Ruijin Hospital, we used optical coherence tomography (OCT) to investigate skin thickness and optical attenuation, enhancing the mμSORS technology. OCT, a non‐invasive imaging method using low‐intensity infrared light, provides high‐resolution, real‐time images of tissue structures without ionizing radiation. It is widely used in ophthalmology [5], endoscopy [6], dentistry [7], and dermatology [8] due to its safety and efficiency.

We investigated skin thickness and optical attenuation coefficients using existing experiments. Tsugita et al. [9] measured epidermal thickness (ET) at 11 sites in 116 healthy women, finding that ET generally decreases with age until about 30 years, then stabilizes. Gambichler et al. [10] found significant age‐related ET decreases in 83 participants but no gender differences, although older women had thinner forehead skin. Zakharov et al. [11] found a correlation between OCT‐measured skin thickness and BMI and a marginal negative correlation with age. Mogensen et al. [12] observed a negative correlation between ET and age in 20 healthy volunteers, with no gender or skin type differences. Czekalla et al. [13] reported that sun‐exposed areas have thicker epidermis and SC than sun‐protected areas. Varaka et al. [14] showed that optical clearing agents increase ET and reduce optical attenuation. Shlivko et al. [15] found that ET affects skin layer morphology using OCT and Monte Carlo simulations. Lan et al. [16] validated OCT for non‐invasive blood glucose monitoring in diabetes participants. Julia et al. [17] highlighted OCT's value in distinguishing healthy from pathological skin. Ya et al. [18] demonstrated that applied pressure affects optical attenuation at different skin depths. Sanzhar et al. [19] used OCT to study the effect of cosmetics on skin topology, showing that 3D OCT images can quantify wrinkle structures.

In this study, we enhanced the newly proposed mμSORS technology using OCT. We obtained detailed images of the thenar region in 172 type 2 diabetes patients and 26 healthy subjects. Significant differences in skin thickness and optical attenuation coefficients were observed between the groups. We also examined the relationships between skin thickness and optical attenuation with factors such as age, gender, BMI, and chromatic value.

Materials and Methods

OCT and Colorimeter Systems

OCT experiments were performed using a swept‐source OCT (SS‐OCT) device (VEG210, Thorlabs) featuring a 1300 nm wavelength, an 11 mm imaging depth, 102 dB sensitivity, and a 100 kHz A‐scan rate, offering a 14 μm axial resolution. A secondary OCT device (Ganymede HR, Thorlabs) with a 900 nm wavelength, 5.5–36 kHz A‐scan rate, 1.9 mm imaging depth, and 101 dB sensitivity at 5.5 kHz, provided 3 μm axial and 4 μm lateral resolution. Using two OCT devices allows for more comprehensive and precise data acquisition in experiments. The 1300 nm VEG210 device is suitable for deep tissue imaging, offering significant imaging depth. Meanwhile, the 900 nm Ganymede HR device is ideal for superficial tissue imaging, providing high‐resolution detailed images. By combining the strengths of both devices, a more thorough investigation of skin and its optical properties can be achieved, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of the experiments. Skin color (L*, a*, b*) was measured using Colorimeter Max (CHNSpec) with D/8 geometry, a light‐emitting diode source, and USB connectivity, supporting various color spaces with a 400–700 nm range and good repeatability (ΔE ≤ 0.05), completing measurements within 1 s. L* indicates the lightness of a color, ranging from 0 (black) to 100 (white). a* represents red‐green chromaticity, with negative values for green and positive for red. b* represents yellow‐blue chromaticity, with negative values for blue and positive for yellow.

The VEG210 device was set to standard scanning mode with X, Y, and Z sizes of 250, 250, and 459 pixels, respectively, a field of view (FOV) of 3.00 × 3.00 × 3.43 mm, and a pixel size of 12.00 μm. The Ganymede device, also in standard mode, had X, Y, and Z sizes of 250, 250, and 512 pixels, an FOV of 3 × 3 × 1.95 mm, and pixel sizes of 12, 12, and 3.81 μm, with an OCT system refractive index of 1.3.

3D imaging with the OCT device involved the following steps: A series of A‐scans focused on specific points of the sample, collecting reflected light data along the depth. This data was converted into intensity information. The device then moved laterally, performing multiple A‐scans to form B‐scans, which are 2D cross‐sectional images. Multiple B‐scans were performed across the sample surface and combined to create a complete 3D dataset.

Participants

Participants were from a clinical trial registered in the United States. Clinical Trials Registry (NCT05921344) to evaluate multi‐channel micro‐spatial Raman spectroscopy (mμSORS) for non‐invasive glucose detection and skin structure analysis using OCT. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided informed consent.

A total of 198 participants were enrolled in the clinical trial. Diabetes participants were divided into three age groups: youth (under 40 years; 20 participants), middle‐aged (40–59 years; 93 participants), and elderly (60+ years; 59 participants). The average ages and BMIs for these groups were 32.95 ± 5.23 years and 26.09 ± 4.73 kg/m2, 52.17 ± 5.48 years and 25.40 ± 4.06 kg/m2, and 65.83 ± 5.16 years and 23.84 ± 3.13 kg/m2, respectively. There were 102 male and 70 female participants with average ages of 53.31 ± 10.94 and 56.53 ± 11.84 years and average BMIs of 25.17 ± 3.50 and 24.61 ± 4.46 kg/m2, respectively. The 172 type 2 diabetes participants had an average age of 54.62 ± 11.39 years and an average BMI of 24.92 ± 3.92 kg/m2.

To obtain baseline data, 26 healthy volunteers were recruited and categorized into three age groups: youth (under 40 years; 13 participants), middle‐aged (40–59 years; 4 participants), and elderly (60+ years; 9 participants). The average ages for these groups were 25.31 ± 2.98, 53.25 ± 5.91 , and 66.44 ± 3.36 years, respectively. The group included 11 males with an average age of 43.36 ± 20.88 years and 15 females with an average age of 44.2 ± 19.56 years. The overall average age was 43.85 ± 19.72 years (Tables 1 and 2).

Experimental Design

This study was conducted in collaboration with Photonic View Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. To ensure consistency and accuracy, the experiments followed a standardized procedure: (1) Participants sat calmly for 15 min before the experiment to stabilize their physiological state. (2) The skin at the thenar eminence of both hands was cleaned with an alcohol swab and air‐dried. (3) Measurements were alternately taken on the left and right hands to minimize fatigue or bias.

To address potential hand tremors and motion artifacts from keeping the palm upward, the OCT probe was inverted and equipped with a bracket to stabilize the patient's palm. This adjustment significantly reduced image quality degradation and data collection errors. During the initial setup of the experiment, as shown in Figure 1, we designed the bracket with a circular hole that allows the probe's light beam to pass through adequately and target the measurement site. This configuration ensures that the hand remains stable and minimizes tremors.

Data Processing

Data were processed to determine the thickness of the SC and SS and the optical attenuation coefficients of the SS and upper dermis. 3D OCT images were exported as 250 2D images, revealing the skin's hierarchical structure. Each image was preprocessed with grayscale conversion and Gaussian blur, which helps to improve the accuracy of subsequent analyses, such as measuring skin layer thickness and optical properties. This preprocessing step ensures clearer differentiation of structures, making the analysis more reliable and robust. Maximum value indices for each column were calculated, and noise was reduced by eliminating values below a threshold. Images were flattened, corrected, summed, and averaged to calculate and normalize the OCT signal intensity. The thicknesses of the SC and SS were then precisely acquired from the characteristic points on the curve (Figure 2).

Results and Discussion

ET

To control for the impact of age on skin thickness, we selected 66 diabetic patients whose ages closely matched those of the 26 healthy participants for comparative analysis. The ages of the healthy participants ranged from 22 to 70 years, with an average age of 48.7 years. The ages of the diabetic patients ranged from 28 to 70 years, with an average age of 54.8 years. Although the average ages differ, we performed further statistical analyses to ensure the groups were comparable. We conducted a two‐sample t‐test to compare the mean ages of the two groups, and the results showed no statistically significant difference between the groups (p > 0.05).

Table 3 and Figure 3 show SC and SS thickness data. The results confirm experimental stability and accuracy despite significant inter‐individual variability. Diabetes patients had significantly higher average SC and SS thickness than healthy participants (p < 0.001), likely due to increased corneocyte proliferation, higher lipid content, and cell proliferation stimulated by hyperglycemia or metabolic abnormalities. Inflammatory and fibrotic processes may also contribute to these changes.

Multiple Factors in Diabetes Participants

Table 4 shows how demographic parameters like age, gender, height, BMI, and skin color (L*, a*, and b*) correlate with the thickness of the SC and SS in 172 diabetes participants. The bold fonts in table 4 indicate statistically significant values (p < 0.05). Gender had no significant impact, indicating that skin thickness is influenced by local demands and environmental factors rather than sex‐specific hormones. Age positively correlated with SC (p = 0.028) and SS (p < 0.001), likely due to age‐related changes in skin structure. Height negatively correlated with SS thickness (p = 0.018), suggesting taller individuals may have thinner SS layers. BMI positively correlated with SC (p = 0.014) and SS (p = 0.043), indicating higher BMI increases skin thickness due to more fat content. Lightness (L*) negatively correlated with both SC (p = 0.001) and SS (p < 0.001), implying that lighter skin has thinner layers. The a* parameter positively correlated with SS thickness (p = 0.012), suggesting higher redness is associated with thicker SS layers, possibly due to increased blood flow or inflammation.

Figure 4 shows scatter plots of SC and SS thickness against age for 172 diabetes participants, analyzed using linear regression. Results indicate both SC and SS thicken with age. Regression slopes for right and left SC are 0.467 and 0.415, respectively, and for right and left SS are 1.029 and 1.069, respectively, indicating a greater increase in SS thickness with age. This may be due to higher proliferative activity in the SS, containing more actively dividing keratinocytes compared to the inert SC cells. SC responds to physical factors like friction consistently across both hands, resulting in nearly overlapping regression lines. In contrast, SS, sensitive to systemic changes like blood flow and hormones, shows parallel but non‐overlapping lines due to asymmetries in usage and exposure between hands. These findings highlight the complex interplay of intrinsic and extrinsic factors on skin thickness in diabetes participants.

Figure 5 shows scatter plots of SC and SS thickness against BMI for 172 diabetes participants, analyzed using linear regression. Slopes for right and left SC are 1.247 and 1.616, respectively, and for right and left SS are 1.093 and 1.231, respectively, indicating both layers thicken with increasing BMI. SC shows a consistent response across both hands, while SS shows slightly smaller increases and parallel but non‐overlapping lines, suggesting subtle asymmetries in environmental exposure and mechanical stress. This variation reflects differences in cellular composition and function, with SS exhibiting more nuanced responses to BMI changes compared to the inert SC.

Attenuation Coefficient

Changes in blood glucose levels can affect light absorption and scattering in skin tissue, influencing light propagation and attenuation. Due to the correlation between mμSORS Raman bands and venous plasma glucose concentrations, we focus on the attenuation coefficients of the SS layer above the dermal‐epidermal junction and the upper dermis below it, excluding the SC layer. Table 6 and Figure 9 summarize and depict the optical attenuation coefficients (μ t ) of 66 diabetes participants and 26 healthy participants. Significant differences were observed in the SS layer coefficients (p < 0.001) between the groups, likely due to structural changes in diabetes affecting light propagation. No significant differences were found in the upper dermis, suggesting diabetes does not significantly alter its optical properties.

Conclusion

In summary, we used OCT to measure the thenar eminence in 172 diabetes participants and 26 healthy subjects, collecting demographic parameters for the diabetic group. We found that the average SC and SS thickness in diabetes participants were significantly higher than in healthy participants (p < 0.001).

The analysis of 172 diabetes participants showed that ET did not differ by gender but increased with age and BMI and decreased with lighter skin tone. Scatter plots for thickness values against age, BMI, and chroma values were created, and variation parameters were obtained. Significant differences in the SS layer attenuation coefficients (p < 0.001) were found between diabetic and healthy groups. The analysis of 172 diabetes participants showed no significant correlations between the optical attenuation coefficients of the SS and upper dermis with gender, age, or BMI. However, a* negatively correlated with the attenuation coefficients of both layers (p = 0.031, p = 0.009), and age positively correlated with the upper dermis (p = 0.006). Our study is critical for the clinical validation, refinement, and enhancement of non‐invasive blood glucose detection utilizing mμSORS technology.
