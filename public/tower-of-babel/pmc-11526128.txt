Serum neurofilament light chain: a novel biomarker for cardiovascular diseases in individuals without hypertension
Authors: Jing Liu, Ya Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Serum neurofilament light chain (sNFL) is a biomarker for axonal injury. Previous studies have linked sNFL levels to cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension, but its association with cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remains unclear. This study aims to explore the association between sNFL and CVD and evaluates its predictive value. Utilizing NHANES 2013–2014 data, this study included 2,035 participants aged ≥ 20 years with measured sNFL quantified using a Siemens immunoassay. CVD was self-reported and included myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, coronary heart disease, or angina. Logistic regression models assessed the association between sNFL levels and CVD. The predictive value of sNFL for CVD was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC) and DeLong test. Participants with higher sNFL levels were typically older, male, non-Hispanic white, smokers, and had lower socioeconomic status, higher CVD, hypertension, and diabetes prevalence. Higher sNFL levels were significantly associated with increased odds of CVD (adjusted OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.05–1.88). The association was significant in non-hypertensive individuals (OR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.61–4.62) but not in hypertensive individuals (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.81–1.56). sNFL addition to traditional risk models improved predictive accuracy, especially in non-hypertensive individuals (AUC from 0.827 to 0.856). sNFL levels are significantly associated with CVD in the general population, with a strong predictive value in non-hypertensive individuals. Future longitudinal studies should validate sNFL’s efficacy in various populations and explore the underlying mechanisms of its relationship with hypertension and CVD.

Background

Serum neurofilament light chain (sNFL) is a protein present in axons, which is released into the bloodstream following axonal injury due to its high solubility, making it a promising biomarker1,2. The efficacy of sNFL as a biomarker for axonal damage was initially used for prognosis and treatment monitoring in multiple sclerosis3. Currently, accumulating scientific evidence indicates that sNFL is also associated with other neurodegenerative diseases and peripheral neuropathies4,5.

In addition to axonal damage, certain cardiovascular risk factors may influence NFL levels, particularly in blood6. Previous studies have demonstrated significant associations between common cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and smoking with elevated sNFL levels7–9. More importantly, recent studies have reported that blood levels of NfL are significantly higher in individuals with hypertension compared to those with normal blood pressure, even though cerebrospinal fluid NfL levels are similar between the two groups10. This suggests that sNFL may have potential as a biomarker for cardiovascular diseases (CVD). Furthermore, in individuals with atrial fibrillation, lower mean arterial pressure is inversely related to blood NfL concentrations, indicating that sNFL may be more specific in identifying certain low-risk individuals with CVD8.

sNFL is an easily accessible, inexpensive, and reproducible tool with potential applications in the diagnosis and prognosis of CVD11. However, the association between sNFL and CVD has not been investigated. Therefore, the aim of this study is to explore the association between sNFL and CVD and to evaluate the predictive value of sNFL for CVD using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study population

Initiated by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), the NHANES serves as a nationally representative, cross-sectional study aimed at assessing the health and nutrition of American residents12. Since its commencement in 1999, the survey has employed a combination of interviews and physical examinations to collect comprehensive data on demographic, socioeconomic, and health indicators13. Approval for the original survey was obtained from the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. This study utilized data from the 2013–2014 NHANES because only these participants had sNFL data measured. Exclusion criteria were: (1) 4,406 participants younger than 20 years of age; (2) 3,698 participants with missing sNFL data; (3) 36 participants with sNFL levels above or below the detection limit. Ultimately, the study included 2,035 participants for analysis (Fig. 1).

Measurement of sNfL

The measurements were conducted using a highly sensitive NfL immunoassay developed by Siemens Healthineers, which employs acridinium ester chemiluminescence and paramagnetic particles14,15. This assay can be performed on the high-throughput, automated Attelica platform. Initially, the sample is incubated with AE-labeled antibodies that bind to the NfL antigen. Paramagnetic particles coated with capture antibodies are then added, forming complexes of the antigen bound to AE-labeled antibodies and PMPs. Unbound AE-labeled antibodies are separated and removed. Chemiluminescence is initiated by the addition of acid and base, and the emitted light is measured. All assay steps are fully automated on the Attelica immunoassay system.

Outcome ascertainment

CVD diagnoses were determined through self-reported information provided by participants during individual interviews using a standardized medical condition questionnaire16. Participants were asked if a doctor or health expert had ever informed them of having myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, coronary heart disease, or angina. A positive response to any of these conditions classified an individual as having CVD.

Covariates

Covariates for the study were identified with reference to previous studies and included age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), education level, ratio of family income to poverty (PIR), smoking status, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), diabetes status, and hypertension status17–19. Sociodemographic information, lifestyle variables, and disease status information were collected by trained personnel using a standardized procedure in which individuals who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime were considered smokers. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol and total cholesterol were measured enzymatically.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed according to the recommended NHANES analysis guidelines, with less than 8% missing for all variables, and missing data were filled in using random forest interpolation via the “missForest” package in R20.

Baseline characteristics were presented according to quartiles of serum NfL levels, and comparisons of continuous variables were performed using ANOVA and comparisons of categorical variables were performed using chi-square tests. The association between sNfL levels and CVD was assessed using multivariate logistic regression models by transforming sNfL levels by natural logarithms to satisfy normal distribution before further analysis. A total of three models were used: Model 1 was not adjusted for covariates; Model 2 included age, gender, and race; and Model 3 further integrated BMI, education level, HDL-C, TC, PIR, smoking status, diabetes status, and hypertension status. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were used to test the consistency of the association between sNFL and CVD in different groups of the population. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to explore the nonlinear association between sNFL and CVD21. The whole dataset was divided into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the predictive capability of sNFL for CVD. sNFL was added to a base model that included common cardiovascular risk factors (age, sex, race, BMI, smoking status, diabetes status). The DeLong test was employed to assess the difference in predictive accuracy22. Additionally, net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination index (IDI) metrics were calculated to assess the added value of sNfL.

These statistical analyses were executed in R (version 4.3.0), using two-tailed tests, with a significance threshold set at P values < 0.05.

Compared to participants with lower sNFL levels (Q1:1.36–2.13 pg/mL), participants with higher sNFL levels (Q4:2.96–6.21 pg/mL) were typically older, more likely to be male, non-Hispanic white, smokers, and were more likely to have lower socioeconomic status and higher prevalence of CVD, hypertension, and diabetes (P < 0.05). There were no significant differences in BMI and TC levels (Table 1).

Associations between sNFL and CVD

Table 2 demonstrates the association between sNFL and CVD. There was a significant positive association between sNFL and odds of CVD prevalence in all models. In the unadjusted and partially adjusted models, a 1-unit ln transformed sNFL rise was associated with a 197% and 75% increase in odds of CVD prevalence, respectively. This positive association remained significant even after adjusting for all covariates, with a 1-unit rise in ln transformed sNFL associated with a 41% increase in odds of CVD prevalence [OR = 1.41, 95% CI: (1.05, 1.88)]. Participants with higher sNFL levels had a 303% rise in odds of CVD prevalence [OR = 4.03, 95%CI: (1.51, 10.78)] compared to participants with lower sNFL levels (Q1), and there was a significant dose-response relationship (P for trend = 0.005).

Figure 2 demonstrates the association between sNFL and CVD in different subgroups of the population. The association between sNFL and CVD was maintained in the sex, race, age, education, BMI, smoking status, and diabetes subgroups, although the association did not reach statistical significance in some subgroups, and the results of the interaction test still showed that the association between sNFL and CVD was not significantly different in these subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05). Notably, hypertension modified the association between sNFL and CVD (P for interaction = 0.005), and among the 726 participants with hypertension, the association between sNFL and CVD remained nonsignificant even though there were 75% participants with CVD (123/164). In contrast, among participants without hypertension, the positive association between sNFL and CVD was highly significant and had the most prominent effect of all subgroups [OR = 2.72, 95% CI: (1.61, 4.62)].

Considering the differences in the association between sNFL and CVD in hypertension subgroup, the nonlinear association between sNFL and CVD in the total population and those with and without hypertension was investigated using adjusted RCS regression analyses (Fig. 3). In the total population, there was a saturation effect for the positive association between sNFL and CVD (P for nonlinear = 0.009), and the positive association disappeared when sNFL levels reached a certain level (Fig. 3A). The results in those with and without hypertension explained this phenomenon and the differences in the subgroup analysis (Fig. 3B). With a trend toward a consistently positive correlation between sNFL and CVD maintained in participants without hypertension (P for nonlinear = 0.773), whereas there was an inverted U-shaped association between sNFL and CVD in participants with hypertension (P for nonlinear = 0.006), with a positive association to the left of the inflection point and a negative association to the right of the inflection point.

Predictive value of sNFL for CVD

To assess the predictive value of sNFL for CVD, we developed a base model containing common cardiovascular risk factors (age, sex, race, BMI, smoking status, diabetes status) and then compared the predictive accuracy before and after adding sNFL to the base model. In the total population, the AUC increased from 0.815 to 0.845 (P = 0.037) in test set, with NRI of 0.023 and IDI of 0.005. Among participants without hypertension, the AUC increased significantly from 0.827 to 0.856 (P = 0.033) in test set, with NRI of 0.051 and IDI of 0.019. However, in participants with hypertension, the addition of sNFL did not significantly improve AUC (P = 0.067) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The results of this study, based on a representative U.S. population, suggest a positive association between sNFL and odds of CVD prevalence. In addition, we found that hypertension modified this association, with a significant and strong positive association between sNFL and CVD in individuals with hypertension, but not in individuals without hypertension. In addition, sNFL may improve the predictive accuracy of CVD over traditional cardiovascular risk factors, especially in those without hypertension.

Previous studies & Interpretation of findings

Studies of sNFL in the cardiovascular field are still limited. Results from a cross-sectional study based on participants with atrial fibrillation suggest that elevated sNFL levels in individuals with atrial fibrillation are associated with heart failure and peripheral arterial disease10. Our results demonstrate for the first time in the general population that sNFL levels are significantly and positively associated with a composite outcome that includes multiple CVDs, with a dose-response relationship. Past studies have also shown that sNFL levels are associated with multiple cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and smoking7,23. We also examined whether there was a significant effect of multiple traditional risk factors on the association of sNFL with CVD in subgroup analyses, and notably hypertension significantly altered this association, with a significant and robust positive association between sNFL and CVD in participants who did not suffer from hypertension and not in those who did. In addition, sNFL levels could substantially improve predictive accuracy (AUC from 0.827 to 0.856) in participants who did not suffer from hypertension with an already high discriminatory please of the underlying model, whereas the improvement was small in participants who suffered from hypertension. This result is diametrically opposed to most biomarkers of cardiovascular risk, and there are no studies to explain this phenomenon24,25; we suggest that hypertension may alter the relationship between sNFL and CVD through several mechanisms: (1) Hypertension-induced chronic inflammation and vascular damage may mask the predictive ability of sNFL as a marker of nerve damage, as hypertension itself already dominates CVD risk26,27. (2) In people with hypertension, neurologic injury may already be reflected in cardiovascular risk through other mechanisms than through sNFL levels28. (3) Hypertension often coexists with other metabolic syndrome components, and these coexisting risk factors may interfere with the direct association of sNFL with CVD, a hypothesis supported by similar but nonsignificant results in the diabetes subgroup29,30.

Potential clinical applications & future direction

Our findings suggest that sNFL may have significant potential for CVD risk prediction, particularly in specific subgroups such as individuals without hypertension. In clinical practice, sNFL could be used as an additional biomarker to identify high-risk individuals who might appear healthy based on traditional risk factors31. For instance, sNFL could help flag individuals without hypertension who may still be at elevated risk of developing CVD, enabling earlier interventions or more targeted monitoring.

In daily practice, sNFL could be incorporated into existing risk assessment models that typically rely on factors like blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and smoking status32. Adding sNFL to these models could enhance risk stratification, particularly for patients whose traditional metrics might underestimate their true cardiovascular risk. This could inform clinicians’ decisions on whether to initiate preventive therapies or further diagnostic testing, even in individuals without overt hypertension.

Moreover, the clinical utility of sNFL extends to its potential role in personalized risk management. For patients at borderline risk, sNFL measurements could guide the intensification or modification of therapy by providing a more nuanced understanding of their cardiovascular risk profile.

However, before sNFL can be widely adopted in clinical settings, further longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to validate its predictive efficacy across diverse populations. In addition, understanding the physiological and pathological mechanisms linking sNFL with both CVD and hypertension will be crucial in determining how sNFL can be optimally integrated into routine clinical practice. This research could also clarify whether different thresholds of sNFL should be applied to hypertensive and non-hypertensive individuals for more accurate risk predictions.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of a large, nationally representative sample of the U.S. population, which enhances the generalizability of the findings to a broader population. In addition, the investigation of adjustment for multiple traditional risk factors and heterogeneity of associations across populations makes our findings more robust and comprehensive. There are also limitations to this study, starting with the cross-sectional design that limits the ability to infer causal associations between sNFL levels and cardiovascular disease. In addition, reliance on self-reported CVD diagnosis may introduce reporting bias and misclassification, which may affect the observed associations33. Third, data on target organ damage (TOD) were not available in the NHANES, making it impossible to explore correlations between sNFL and TOD in hypertensive individuals. Lastly, although we adjusted for multiple covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significant and positive association between sNFL levels and the CVD in a representative sample of the U.S. population. Notably, this association was strongly modified by hypertension status. In individuals without hypertension, elevated sNFL levels were significantly associated with higher odds of CVD, suggesting that sNFL could serve as a valuable biomarker for identifying high-risk individuals in this subgroup. Conversely, in individuals with hypertension, the association between sNFL and CVD was not significant, indicating that the presence of hypertension may alter the relationship between sNFL and cardiovascular risk. The findings highlight the potential utility of sNFL as a predictive biomarker for CVD, particularly in populations without hypertension, where traditional risk factors might not fully capture the cardiovascular risk.
