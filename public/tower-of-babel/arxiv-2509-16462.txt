Intrinsic Meets Extrinsic Fairness: Assessing the Downstream Impact of Bias Mitigation in Large Language Models
Mina Arzaghi, Alireza Dehghanpour Farashah, Florian Carichon, Golnoosh Farnadi

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit socio-economic biases that can propagate into downstream tasks. While prior studies have questioned whether intrinsic bias in LLMs affects fairness at the downstream task level, this work empirically investigates the connection. We present a unified evaluation framework to compare intrinsic bias mitigation via concept unlearning with extrinsic bias mitigation via counterfactual data augmentation (CDA). We examine this relationship through real-world financial classification tasks, including salary prediction, employment status, and creditworthiness assessment. Using three open-source LLMs, we evaluate models both as frozen embedding extractors and as fine-tuned classifiers. Our results show that intrinsic bias mitigation through unlearning reduces intrinsic gender bias by up to 94.9% , while also improving downstream task fairness metrics, such as demographic parity by up to 82% , without compromising accuracy. Our framework offers practical guidance on where mitigation efforts can be most effective and highlights the importance of applying early-stage mitigation before downstream deployment.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated abilities ranging from complex reasoning to logic and inference predictions, empowering advanced chatbots and agentic compound systems
(
Ke et al. 2025
)
. Concurrently, LLMs have also been shown to exhibit significant social biases, creating harm, particularly against historically marginalized genders, age categories, and racial groups
(
Blodgett et al. 2020
;
Kotek, Dockum, and Sun 2023
)
. Biases in LLMs can be categorized into two distinct categories: (1) intrinsic biases coming from pre-trained model internal representations and (2) extrinsic biases emerging after fine-tuning in downstream applications
(
Delobelle et al. 2022
)
. At the intrinsic level, biases can lead to representational harms such as stereotypes or toxicity. In contrast, extrinsic biases can lead to allocational harms through direct discrimination and disparate treatment due to the membership of these groups
(
Gallegos et al. 2024
)
.

Figure 1:
Impact of unlearning intrinsic bias on downstream fairness for income prediction. The original model (right) favors males in high-income predictions, while the unlearned model (left) produces more balanced outcomes, demonstrating the effect of intrinsic mitigation.

Biases can originate from various sources, including data, algorithms, or broader societal context
(
Ranjan, Gupta, and Singh 2024
)
. Consequently, to reduce unfair treatment or disparate outcomes, mitigation methods address these sources of biases either by modifying datasets with pre-processing methods such as Counterfactual Data Augmentation (CDA)
(
Gallegos et al. 2024
)
or by altering algorithm learning or output with in-processing and post-processing techniques, such as bias unlearning
(
Dige et al. 2024
)
, or prompt adjustment
(
Dai et al. 2024
)
. However, the efficiency of these mitigation techniques, especially in-processing, in addressing either extrinsic or intrinsic biases, is ill-understood and sometimes contested, as their impact is not directly observable in downstream applications
(
Tokpo et al. 2023
;
Goldfarb-Tarrant et al. 2020
)
.

Assessing bias mitigation, especially in the context of free-form text generation, is particularly challenging as most evaluation metrics potentially rely on biased references or algorithms, are sensitive to decoding parameters, or might over-simplify complex phenomena
(
Gallegos et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, in this article, we have chosen to examine bias mitigation approaches and their impact on intrinsic and extrinsic biases through the lens of tasks that reflect real-world power dynamics. In this context, tabular datasets affiliated to financial settings are particularly appealing since they have historically reflected allocational harms and direct discrimination against marginalized groups
(
Coraglia et al. 2024
;
Kozodoi et al. 2025
)
.
Using this type of tabular dataset for evaluation enables the deployment of classic fairness-based metrics, such as demographic parity or equality of odds
(
Hardt, Price, and Srebro 2016
)
, to assess the presence of allocational harms in downstream tasks. Moreover, the financial context associated with this data is all the more interesting since LLMs’ outputs can be highly sensitive to socio-demographic attributes when tasked with predicting financial outcomes for different population groups, demonstrating the presence of strong intrinsic bias in such models
(
Arzaghi, Carichon, and Farnadi 2024
)
.

Financial classification and predictive analyses often rely on data engineering performed by domain specialists
(
Boughaci et al. 2021
)
. More recently, LLMs have been introduced into this classification framework to supplement classic predictive models due to their richness in contextual embedding capacity
(
Huang et al. 2020
;
Borisov et al. 2022
;
Farhangian, Cruz, and Cavalcanti 2024
)
. Finally, other works exploit to explore the generative capabilities of LLMs in prompt-based classification settings
(
Vajjala and Shimangaud 2025
)
. Our pipeline builds on this direction using well-established datasets for financial classification, including Adult Census Income
(
Becker and Kohavi 1996
)
, German Credit Score
(
Yeh 2009
)
, and ACS Employment
(
Ding et al. 2021
)
.
Across all datasets, we apply CDA as a data-level mitigation strategy. For LLM-based classifiers, we further introduce a concept-unlearning method as an in-processing technique. This framework enables a detailed and systematic analysis of how different mitigation strategies influence bias in real-world downstream tasks, bridging intrinsic and extrinsic fairness in ways that have direct social relevance. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a novel framework for evaluating bias in downstream applications using real-world datasets and robust group fairness metrics.

•

We introduce a new unlearning-based mitigation strategy that effectively reduces socioeconomic intrinsic bias in large language models (LLMs).

•

We conduct a systematic comparison of two mitigation methods: unlearning, and Counterfactual Data Augmentation (CDA) analyzing their individual and combined effects on downstream task fairness.

•

We provide an in-depth interpretability analysis, shedding light on the complex interactions between intrinsic and extrinsic bias, mitigation strategies, and downstream harms.

2
Related Works

Fairness in LLMs

Social bias refers to the unfair or prejudicial treatment of individuals or groups based on characteristics such as identity, beliefs, or affiliations
(
Webster et al. 2022
)
. In the context of LLMs, the presence of such biases can lead to a wide range of serious harms, particularly for marginalized or vulnerable populations
(
Blodgett et al. 2020
)
. Social biases in LLMs can be broadly categorized into two main types
(
Delobelle et al. 2022
)
. Intrinsic biases relate to biases present in the internal representations of language models and are evaluated through the study of their embeddings
(
Kaneko and Bollegala 2022
)
. These biases provoke harm, such as stereotyping or increasing toxicity towards marginalized groups
(
Gallegos et al. 2024
)
. In a financial context,
Arzaghi, Carichon, and Farnadi 2024
has shown that intrinsic biases exist and that LLMs associate poverty differently based on gender, race, or religion. However, it seems that there is no reliable correlation between intrinsic biases and a model output
(
Goldfarb-Tarrant et al. 2020
)
. Conversely, extrinsic bias involves directly assessing the fairness of system outputs in downstream tasks and evaluating potential harms as direct or indirect discrimination
(
Gallegos et al. 2024
)
. To promote fairness in downstream applications, one common approach is to look at group fairness, such as demographic parity and equalized odds, which assesses the parity of statistical measures across predefined protected groups
(
Chouldechova and Roth 2018
)
. These can be assessed using three main categories of metrics: Pairwise Comparison Metrics (PCM), Background Comparison Metrics (BCM), and Multi-group Comparison Metrics (MCM)
(
Czarnowska, Vyas, and Shah 2021
)
. Once again, in a financial context, we observe disparities in LLMs between groups due to lower quality of financial advice based on gender
(
Etgar, Oestreicher-Singer, and Yahav 2024
)
. More worrying,
Feng et al. 2023
have noted that LLMs used for tabular data encoding lead to an unfair allocation of credit in tabular datasets, such as Those Related to Fraud or Travel Insurance, even when the datasets were previously balanced. Building on these observations, our paper proposes a double-mitigation approach, based on both intrinsic and extrinsic mitigation techniques, that aims to improve group fairness in credit-scoring tasks.

Bias Mitigation

Effectively addressing bias in machine learning requires understanding its sources, which include data bias, algorithmic bias, and systemic bias
(
Ranjan, Gupta, and Singh 2024
)
. Accordingly, three categories of mitigation techniques have been developed to target these different sources of bias in LLMs. Pre-processing involves modifying the input data before training LLMs
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
and includes oversampling and undersampling methods, such as Counterfactual Data Augmentation
(
Kusner et al. 2017
)
and synthetic data generation
(
Ferrara 2023
)
. In-processing works by modifying the training functions of LLMs to prioritize unbiasedness and fairness
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
. Methods include Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning
(
Agiza, Mostagir, and Reda 2024
)
to align LLMs to intended behaviours, Direct Preference Optimization
(
Ranjan, Gupta, and Singh 2024
)
that favours less biased text completions, or unlearning the relation between semantic concepts and social biases
(
Kadhe et al. 2023
)
. Finally, Post-processing mitigates bias by adjusting the output of LLMs
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
. Recent approaches based on role-playing
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
, debate
(
Raj et al. 2024
)
, or chain-of-thoughts
(
Kaneko et al. 2024
)
impose LLMs to reflect on their generated output to identify and mitigate bias, and have demonstrated interesting performances. In finance, the lack of regulations and ethical safeguards has led to historically embedded data biases, which persist in many datasets and involve complex interactions between sensitive attributes and predictive variables
(
Coraglia et al. 2024
;
Kozodoi et al. 2025
)
. Most efforts to debias have centred on pre-processing methods, such as masking sensitive attributes or counterfactual augmentation. In the context of In-processing, adversarial debiasing is one of the most widely employed methods for debiasing machine learning algorithms due to its robustness to the fairness-performance trade-off
(
Oguntibeju 2024
)
. Post-processing techniques are often criticized for addressing bias too late after the model has already generated potentially skewed predictions
(
Oguntibeju 2024
)
. Our work is the first to address bias in LLMs applied to financial classification tasks through an inclusive approach that combines both pre-processing and in-processing mitigation techniques.

3
Methodology

This section introduces our four-stage evaluation pipeline for analyzing the relationship between
intrinsic
and
extrinsic
bias in large language models (LLMs), as shown in Figure
2
. The stages are: (1) training on the original data without mitigation, (2) applying data-level mitigation via CDA, (3) using unlearned LLMs for intrinsic bias reduction, and (4) combining both strategies. This setup enables systematic comparisons of individual and joint mitigation effects on fairness and performance. We first describe the classification tasks and tabular-to-text serialization, then detail the CDA and unlearning methods.

Figure 2:
Four-stage evaluation pipeline.
Stage 1
: Core setup with three model types—logistic regression, frozen LLM embeddings, and fine-tuned LLMs.
Stage 2
: Applies CDA to balance gender representation.
Stage 3
: Replaces the pretrained LLM with an
unlearned
version fine-tuned to reduce intrinsic socioeconomic gender bias.
Stage 4
: Combines unlearning with CDA across all model configurations.

Stage 1: Downstream task classification

In the first stage, for a given dataset
D
, we perform a binary classification task, where the goal is to predict a target variable
Y_{D}\in\{0,1\}
(e.g., salary classification) based on a set of input features
X_{D}
. The feature set always includes a sensitive attribute, denoted as
x_{D}=sex\in\{male,female\}
. This setup serves as a baseline to assess how bias manifests in models trained on an unmodified, real-world dataset. We evaluate the following three types of models:

•

Classical Logistic Regression:
A baseline machine learning model trained directly on the feature set
X_{D}
extracted from the tabular dataset.

•

LLM Embedding
\rightarrow
Logistic Regression:
A two-step process in which the feature set
X_{D}
is first serialized into text format (see
Serilization
section) and passed through a frozen LLM to generating embedding representation
Z_{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that:
Z_{D}=f_{\text{LLM}}(X_{D})
, where
f_{\text{LLM}}
maps input sequences into an
m
-dimensional embedding space. This new embedding feature set
Z_{D}
is then used as input to a logistic regression classifier.

•

LLM as Classifier:
Once again, here our feature set
X_{D}
is serialized, then a pre-trained language model
f_{\text{LLM}}
is fine-tuned to perform classification by prompting it with an instruction and the serialized textual input. Here is an example of a potential input prompt
pr
”
Predict whether the given person earns a salary
>
50k USD based on their description. Answer only as yes/no”.
Then, the model estimates the probability distribution over the next token
y
given the input
P(y\mid pr;X)
. The final prediction corresponds to the token with the highest probability between “yes” and “no” tokens:
\hat{y}=\arg\max_{y\in\{\texttt{yes},\texttt{no}\}}P(y\mid pr;X)
.

Serialization

To enable language models to process tabular data, we serialize each record into a natural language sentence. Following the recommendations of
Hegselmann et al. 2023
, we use the
Text Template
method for serialization, which their study found to outperform eight other strategies. This method enumerates each feature as a simple statement in the form of “The column name is value.” For example, a row from the Adult dataset is serialized as:

“workclass is Private. hours-per-week is 46. sex is male. age is 27…”

Moreover, we compute feature importance scores based on a logistic regression model, and order them before serialization. This ensures that, if the input is truncated due to the maximum input length of the language model, the most important features are still retained and seen by the model.

Fine-tuning the LLMs as a classifier

Full fine-tuning across multiple datasets would be inefficient and could potentially interfere with the effects of later stages such as unlearning. Thus, we adopt
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, a parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique. LoRA preserves the original model parameters and introduces trainable low-rank matrices into selected components, typically the attention projections.

Stage 2: Counterfactual Data Augmentation (CDA)

In the second stage of the pipeline, we apply a pre-processing mitigation strategy known as
CDA

(
Ma et al. 2020
;
Xie and Lukasiewicz 2023
)
to construct a fairer training set prior to model training. This method duplicates each training instance while flipping the gender attribute, ensuring that each profile appears under both privileged and unprivileged gender groups. Such balanced exposure discourages models from learning spurious gender associations.

CDA is particularly well-suited for our experimental framework due to its
model-agnostic
design, it can be applied consistently across all model types introduced in Stage 1 without requiring any architectural changes. Moreover, since CDA operates entirely at the data level, it does not interfere with in-training mitigation strategies such as unlearning. This separation enables a precise evaluation of both data-level and model-level interventions. Finally, CDA is practical and widely adopted in real-world applications due to its simplicity, scalability, and effectiveness.

Stage 3: Unlearning Socioeconomic Stereotypes

In this stage, we investigate whether mitigating
intrinsic biases
in LLMs leads to improvements in downstream fairness. To this end, we apply an in-processing technique known as
unlearning
. Developed initially to erase memorized content or harmful behaviors in LLMs
(
Blanco-Justicia et al. 2025
)
, unlearning has recently been explored in fairness contexts
(
Dige et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2023
)
. While unlearning has shown promise in reducing intrinsic bias, its effectiveness on downstream tasks has not yet been systematically evaluated.

Unlearning Model

Our method adapts two prior frameworks to the fairness setting. We build on
Yao, Xu, and Liu 2024
, who increase the loss on harmful outputs to make LLMs “forget” specific prompt-output behaviors without requiring positive supervision, while preserving general performance via KL divergence. We also draw from
Wang et al. 2023
, who introduce a knowledge gap constraint to reduce divergence between learned and unlearned predictions.

In this article, we repurpose these unlearning methods to explicitly target and reduce learned associations between gender and financial stereotypes. Unlike safety-focused applications, fairness tasks typically define both undesirable (stereotype) and desirable (anti-stereotype) outputs. Our objective is not to suppress responses entirely, but to equalize the model’s confidence across identity-related terms (e.g., “men” vs. “women”) within socioeconomic contexts.

More precisely
, our approach includes four key components:

•

Gradient ascent
on stereotype answers encourages the model to forget biased associations.

•

Gradient descent
on anti-stereotype answers reinforces fairness-aligned alternatives.

•

KL divergence
regularizes the updated model against the original to preserve general language capabilities and prevent overcorrection.

•

The
Knowledge Gap Loss
is adapted to minimize the squared difference in predicted probabilities between stereotype and antistereotype answers—ensuring bias is reduced rather than reversed.

We apply this unlearning framework to the LLMs used in Stage 1, both as embedding generators and end-to-end classifiers. This integration allows us to systematically assess how in-processing bias mitigation influences intrinsic bias and its downstream effects.

Unlearning Training

To implement our unlearning framework, we adapt the
PoorOrRich
dataset
(
Arzaghi, Carichon, and Farnadi 2024
)
, which contains socio-economic statements involving gender terms. Originally designed for masked token prediction, we convert each example into a question-answering format (e.g., “Who are wealthy?”), paired with:

•

a
stereotype answer
(e.g., “men”) historically favored by the model, and

•

an
antistereotype answer
(e.g., “women”) from the same gender pair.

We augment the dataset with additional questions reflecting generalized financial contexts, such as employment or income. The final dataset is available on our GitHub.

The model is fine-tuned using cross-entropy loss to unlearn stereotypes and reinforce antistereotypes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{unlearn}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{\text{stereo}})}\left[\log P(y_{\text{stereo}}\mid x)\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{learn}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{\text{anti}})}\left[\log P(y_{\text{anti}}\mid x)\right]

(2)

where
x
is a prompt from the
PoorOrRich
dataset,
y_{stereo}
and
y_{anti}
are the stereotype and antistereotype responses, respectively.
To avoid reversing the bias, we include a
gap loss
, minimizing the squared difference in predicted probabilities between stereotype and antistereotype answers:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{gap}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(P(y_{\text{stereo}}\mid x)-P(y_{\text{anti}}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right]

(3)

To preserve the model’s original behavior, we follow
Yao, Xu, and Liu 2024
and apply a KL divergence loss using the TruthfulQA dataset
(
Lin, Hilton, and Evans 2021
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{norm}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left(P_{\theta_{0}}(y\mid x)\,\|\,P_{\theta_{1}}(y\mid x)\right)\right]

(4)

where
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
denote the pre-trained and current model parameters.
The final loss combines all components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\text{unlearn}}+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{learn}}+\lambda_{3}\mathcal{L}_{\text{gap}}+\lambda_{4}\mathcal{L}_{\text{norm}}

(5)

with
\lambda_{i}
denoting the weighting coefficients for each term.

Stage 4: Combined Mitigation

In the final stage of our pipeline, we evaluate the combined effect of applying both CDA and unlearning, to assess whether addressing bias at both the data and model levels leads to improved downstream fairness.

Intrinsic Bias Evaluation

To assess intrinsic bias, we follow a probability-based evaluation approach inspired by
Nadeem, Bethke, and Reddy 2020
, comparing the normalized likelihoods that language models assign to stereotypical versus antistereotypical answers across two socioeconomic contexts:
poverty
and
wealth
. For each prompt, we append a pair of gendered answer options (e.g., “men” vs. “women”) and calculate their respective probabilities based on the model’s next-token predictions. We then normalize these probabilities to obtain a comparable distribution over the two answers. A large gap indicates representational bias, while a smaller gap reflects more equitable treatment.
To ensure that fairness improvements through unlearning do not come at the cost of language quality, we report perplexity on WikiText and WikiText-2
(
Merity et al. 2016
)
.

Unlearning significantly reduces the stereotype–antistereotype probability gap across all models and both socioeconomic contexts (Table
1
). The effect is strongest in the poverty context, where models like Llama-3.1 show a gap reduction from
0.560
to
0.059
. Despite being smaller than in the poverty context, bias gaps in the wealth context are also effectively reduced. Importantly, perplexity remains stable, indicating preserved model fluency. These results demonstrate that unlearning mitigates directional gender bias without harming general performance

Extrinsic Bias Evaluation

In our downstream tasks, we evaluate model performance using accuracy (Acc), and assess fairness using group-based metrics: :
Accuracy Parity
(AccP),
Demographic Parity Difference
(DP), and
Equality of Odds
(EqOdds)
(
Barocas, Hardt, and Narayanan 2019
;
Hardt, Price, and Srebro 2016
)
, all computed with respect to the binary gender attribute (see Appendix B for metric definitions).

We evaluate on three real-world datasets with gender annotations and observable disparities: the ACS Employment dataset
(
Ding et al. 2021
)
for predicting employment status, the Adult Census Income dataset
(
Becker and Kohavi 1996
)
for income classification, and the German Credit dataset
(
Yeh 2009
)
for assessing credit risk.
Detailed dataset descriptions are also provided in Appendix B.
Table
2
presents model performance and fairness metrics across all datasets, with key findings discussed below.

In the
ACS Employment
dataset, LLM-based models outperform logistic regression in both accuracy and fairness in most cases. In the LLM as Feature Extractor setup, unlearning consistently improves fairness across all three LLMs, achieving lower disparities than both the pretrained models and their CDA-enhanced variants. In particular, the combination of the unlearned model with CDA yields the strongest results. Unlearned Llama-3.1 + CDA achieves the best fairness metrics (AccP:
0.096
, DP:
0.014
, EqOdds:
0.167
). In the classifier setup, unlearning again outperforms the pretrained and CDA-only models, notably reducing DP (e.g., Gemma-2: 0.039) and EqOdds (0.244). These results confirm the complementary benefits of unlearning and CDA in enhancing downstream fairness without compromising accuracy.

In the
Adult
dataset, accuracy remains consistently high (0.82-0.84), indicating that fairness interventions do not compromise performance. In LLM as Feature Extractor setup, Unlearned Llama-3.1 + CDA achieves the best fairness overall (DP: 0.288, EqOdds: 0.280), while Unlearned Phi-3 attains the lowest accuracy parity (0.116) with strong accuracy (0.822). In LLM as classifier setup, unlearning alone improves over the pretrained Phi-3 (DP drops from 0.342 to 0.331; EqOdds from 0.399 to 0.362). These trends hold across models: unlearning consistently reduces fairness gaps, and combining it with CDA amplifies the gains. Notably, unlearned Gemma-2 achieves the best EqOdds (
0.280
) and strong DP (
0.303
), confirming unlearning’s effectiveness across architectures.

In the
German Credit
dataset, the
LLM as Feature Extractor
setup outperforms both the logistic regression and LLM as Classifier setup across all metrics. Unlearning consistently improves fairness over pretrained models and often surpasses CDA, which tends to reduce accuracy and AccP. Notably, CDA significantly drops the accuracy of Llama-3.1 and Phi-3, while unlearning maintains or improves both fairness and performance. Although trends are less pronounced due to the dataset’s small size and variability, unlearning remains effective.

Discussion

In this article, we investigate the impact of two mitigation techniques: an adapted unlearning method targeting intrinsic biases, and Counterfactual Data Augmentation addressing extrinsic biases. This section presents the results of our evaluation pipeline and includes an ablation analysis to assess the contribution of each component.

Pipeline Study

The results obtained through the different analyses present in the various stages of the pipeline allow us to make the following observations:

Models Performances

The use of LLMs yields improvements in accuracy for our classification tasks. Specifically, approaches that leverage LLMs, whether as direct classifiers or as sources of embeddings, consistently outperform feature-based logistic regression. This confirms the utility of LLM-derived representations for feature engineering
(
Borisov et al. 2022
)
. However, for fairness the results of using LLMs without mitigation are more contrasted. Although using LLMs as embeddings directly improves fairness compared to logistic regression, LLMs as classifiers can yield to increasing extrinsic biases in downstream applications depending on the dataset.

LLMs Differences

We observe substantial behavioral differences across models depending on the model setup. When used for embedding, Gemma-2 consistently outperforms other models in terms of accuracy, while Llama-3.1, despite its higher theoretical capacity, under-performs. Interestingly, this trend is reversed when considering fairness: Llama-3.1 achieves better fairness metrics, whereas Gemma-2 becomes worse. Phi-3 emerges as a balanced alternative, offering a compromise between accuracy and fairness. Among the LLM-based classifiers, Gemma-2 stands out as the top-performing model, achieving strong results on both accuracy and fairness. In the meantime, Phi-3 exhibits a drop in performance in this configuration, becoming one of the weakest models. Lastly, in the case of the German Credit dataset, Llama-3.1 shows unexpectedly strong performance. This may be attributed to the dataset’s small size, which could lead to overfitting, or the possibility that similar data was included in Llama’s pretraining corpus. This highlights the importance of selecting the appropriate model when performing bias mitigation for downstream applications.

Mitigation Approaches

While the biggest improvements on Fairness mainly comes from the use of LLMs, CDA favorably improve DP and Unlearning impact positively Equality of Odds, which is expected. DP requires equal prediction rates across groups regardless of the true label, and CDA supports this by balancing the sensitive attribute distribution, thereby directly influencing group-level predictions. In contrast, Equality of Odds demands equal error rates conditioned on the true outcome. Unlearning operates directly on the model’s internal representations with respect to the true label, making it well-suited to address prediction conditionned on label disparities. Regarding performance trade-offs, CDA often results in decreases in classification accuracy, highlighting its utility cost. Conversely, unlearning does not seem to exhibit such performance degradation. It even leads to improvements in model accuracy and language modeling perplexity, underscoring the promise of in-processing mitigation techniques for achieving fairness without sacrificing downstream task performance.

Ablation Study

In this article, we also introduced a novel unlearning technique that is essential to understand. This ablation study and the analyses on intrinsic bias mitigation reveal interesting dynamics in this unlearning approach, potentially explaining why these methods have been wrongly considered inefficient for downstream applications until now. The detailed ablation results supporting this analysis are presented in Appendix A (Tables 3, 4, and 5)

KL Divergence Impact

While KL divergence is essential for preserving model coherence, as reflected in perplexity scores, it also preserves the existing intrinsic biases of models. The first factor is that the divergence push model clings to its initial distribution where bias was present. To mitigate this, we attempted to match the distribution of the model on a bias-free dataset as Truthfulqa
(
Lin, Hilton, and Evans 2021
)
. However, Truthfulqa is not designed to address socio-economic biases, leading the model to preserve biased associations during optimization.
Since LLMs used as classifiers rely heavily on coherence constraints during fine-tuning, this reliance may partly explain their comparatively weaker fairness performance in downstream tasks compared to their embedding classifier counterparts.

The GAP Loss

The GAP loss plays a critical role in preserving a balanced probability distribution across sensitive attribute values. Without it, we observe that training can lead to a complete inversion of the original distribution, effectively introducing a new form of bias. Moreover, as we can see, flipping distributions is quite effortless since models have an easier time learning anti-stereotyping answers than unlearning stereotyping ones, and thus demand extensive hyperparameter tuning. In cases where balance is not achieved, since the model becomes as efficient at predicting anti-stereotyped answers as it does at predicting stereotyped ones, models may then exploit both the original and the newly introduced flipped bias in LLM representations, resulting in continued unfair predictions in the downstream application. This phenomenon may partly explain why in-processing methods targeting intrinsic bias have been previously underestimated in terms of their downstream impact. Further investigation is required to better understand this effect and its implications in the broader mitigation literature.

Models and Capacity

Across most models, we observe a consistent pattern in which both learning and unlearning collaboratively reduce the gap between biased and unbiased output distributions; up to a point where one mechanism dominates and flips the bias. However, we notice that Llama-3.1 is the only model where learning and unlearning appear to act in contradictory directions with respect to other models. Based on insights from two other LLMs (See Appendix A for details),
we hypothesize that increased model capacity introduces greater representational complexity, making it more difficult to disentangle and remove bias using our current unlearning method. This opposition between learning and unlearning in Llama-3.1 may also explain its behavior in downstream tasks where it diverges from other models by exhibiting inverse trends (e.g., higher fairness but lower accuracy, or vice versa when used as a classifier).

6
Conclusion and Future Direction

This work presents a unified framework for evaluating and mitigating socioeconomic gender bias in large language models (LLMs), addressing both intrinsic (representational) and extrinsic (allocational) dimensions. We adapt concept unlearning, a gradient-based technique originally used for safety, to reduce representational bias without compromising language fluency or downstream task performance. Combined with Counterfactual Data Augmentation (CDA), our method forms a robust dual mitigation strategy.

Empirical results show that unlearning reduces intrinsic bias and enhances fairness in downstream task. For example, in intrinsic evaluation,
Phi-3
’s gender probability gap dropped from 0.565 to 0.029 (
94.9%
reduction). In the
ACS Employment
dataset,
Unlearned Gemma-2
improved Accuracy Parity from 0.199 to 0.104 (
48%
gain), and
Unlearned Llama-3.1 + CDA
reduced Demographic Parity from 0.080 to 0.014 (
82%
gain). Across four financial datasets, the consistency of these improvements highlights the generalization potential of our pipeline.

Beyond mitigation, this work provides a fine-grained lens into how intrinsic and extrinsic biases interact. For users with access to model weights, our framework offers actionable guidance on where mitigation is most effective; for those relying on frozen models, it underscores the importance of early-stage data-level interventions before downstream deployment.

While our approach shows strong performance, several limitations remain. First, we focus solely on binary gender bias, omitting other sensitive and intersectional attributes. Second, experiments are limited to English due to the lack of annotated fairness datasets in other languages. Third, our unlearning strategy assumes access to model weights, which restricts its applicability to open-source models. Future work will explore extending this pipeline to multi-attribute, multilingual, and black-box LLM settings.

Across all three models, we observe distinct behaviors in response to the unlearning components. Phi-3 demonstrates more moderate shifts, with gap regularization playing a central stabilizing role.

As shown in the main paper, when hyperparameters are carefully optimized, our method can significantly reduce intrinsic bias, for example narrowing the probability gap between stereotype and antistereotype responses from 0.560 to 0.059 in Llama-3.1. These results provide strong evidence that in-processing unlearning methods, when properly tuned, can achieve meaningful and balanced bias mitigation in large language models.

Appendix B
Experimental Details

This section provides additional implementation details on evaluation metrics, datasets, models used, and training configurations for both intrinsic bias mitigation and downstream fairness experiments.

Intrinsic Bias Metrics

To evaluate intrinsic bias, we measure the degree to which the language model assigns unequal probability to stereotype versus antistereotype completions. For each prompt, the model is presented with two candidate answers: one reflecting a stereotypical association, and the other an antistereotypical one.

We compute the total log-probability assigned by the model to each answer, then normalize the scores using a softmax over the two options. The intrinsic bias score is defined as the absolute difference in normalized probabilities:

\Delta=\left|P_{\text{norm}}(\text{stereotype})-P_{\text{norm}}(\text{antistereotype})\right|

(6)

A larger
\Delta
indicates stronger bias — that is, a greater asymmetry in the model’s preference between the two conceptually opposing completions. This metric captures the magnitude of representational bias without assuming either option is more desirable.

Extrinsic Bias Metrics

Our downstream tasks involve classifying individuals based on real-world characteristics, such as salary level, employment status, and creditworthiness. In this setting,
sex
is treated as the sensitive attribute. We define fairness as the requirement that models make predictions equitably, without disproportionately disadvantaging any demographic group.

We report:

•

Accuracy
: Overall classification accuracy across all samples.

•

Accuracy Parity (AccP)
: Measures whether prediction accuracy is equal across groups. For example, if accuracy is 85% for males and 70% for females, the absolute difference is 15%, indicating a fairness gap.

\text{AccP}=\left|P(\hat{Y}=Y\mid A=0)-P(\hat{Y}=Y\mid A=1)\right|

(7)

where
A\in\{0,1\}
denotes gender. For instance, if accuracy is 85% for males and 70% for females, the gap is 15%, indicating a fairness violation.

•

Demographic Parity Difference (DP)
: Measures the difference in positive prediction rates between groups:

\text{DP}=\left|P(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=0)-P(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=1)\right|

(8)

where
A\in\{0,1\}
denotes gender. In the Adult dataset, this captures whether men and women are predicted to earn over $50K at similar rates.

•

Equality of Odds (EqOdds)
: Assesses whether the true positive and false positive rates are equal across groups. It is defined as the average of absolute differences across labels:

\displaystyle\text{EqOdds}=\

\displaystyle|P(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y{=}0,A{=}0)-P(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y{=}0,A{=}1)|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle|P(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y{=}1,A{=}0)-P(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y{=}1,A{=}1)|

(9)

Datasets

In the downstream evaluation, we selected datasets from finance- and employment-related domains that include gender information and exhibit measurable gender disparities.

•

ACS Employment

(
Ding et al. 2021
)
: Derived from the American Community Survey (ACS), this dataset is filtered to include individuals aged 16 to 90. While
Ding et al. 2021
report that the full dataset shows minimal gender disparity, we select a subset from Utah in 2018, which exhibits a clearer imbalance (e.g., 51% of men employed vs. 41% of women). We use this subsample in our experiments. The filtered subsample contains 22,835 observations and 17 features, and it is used for all experiments.

•

Adult (Census Income)

(
Becker and Kohavi 1996
)
: Also known as the Census Income dataset, this dataset contains 48,842 instances and 14 features such as workclass, hours-per-week, education, and age. The task is to predict whether an individual’s annual income exceeds $50K.

•

German Credit

(
Yeh 2009
)
: This dataset includes 1,000 instances and 20 attributes. The task is to classify individuals as having either “good” or “bad” credit risk.
This dataset consists of 1,000 instances and 20 features, including checking account status and credit history. The task is to classify credit risk as either “good” or “bad.”

Datasets (Intrinsic Bias Evaluation)

•

PoorOrRich

(
Arzaghi, Carichon, and Farnadi 2024
)
: Originally designed for masked language modeling, this dataset evaluates associations between demographic groups and socioeconomic descriptors (e.g., poverty, wealth). While the dataset spans multiple demographic axes, we focus exclusively on the gender-related subset. To align it with our unlearning framework, we reformat prompts into a question-answering structure and augment them with more general financial questions, enabling direct comparison with downstream tasks like salary or credit prediction.

•

(
Lin, Hilton, and Evans 2021
)
A benchmark for evaluating the truthfulness and factual grounding of language models. In our setup, we use TruthfulQA as a neutral reference distribution to regularize model outputs via KL-divergence, ensuring that unlearning interventions do not degrade overall language model quality.

transformers
library.

•

Llama-3.1 (8B)
: A 8B parameter model trained on approximately 15 trillion tokens from public sources and optimized for multilingual dialogue tasks through instruction tuning and DPO.
(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)

Implementation Details

Intrinsic Bias Mitigation.

In the unlearning step, we conducted a grid search over learning rates
\{1\text{e}{-5},1\text{e}{-6},1\text{e}{-7}\}
and loss weights for each component—unlearning, learning, GAP, and KL divergence—using values in
\{1.0,0.75,0.5,0.25,0.15\}
. Each configuration was trained using two NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs. Model selection was based on validation fairness performance, as measured by intrinsic metrics (probability gap between stereotype and antistereotype completions). Batch size was adjusted for each model based on GPU memory constraints: 12 for Llama-3.1 , 36 for Gemma-2 and Phi-3.

Downstream Task Evaluation.

For the downstream fairness experiments, we evaluated models on three classification tasks: S. Each model was used in two modes: (1) as a frozen embedding extractor for training a logistic regression classifier, and (2) as a fine-tuned classifier using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) for parameter-efficient adaptation. The LoRA configuration was: rank
r=16
, scaling factor
\alpha=32
, dropout = 0.05, and target modules =
\{\texttt{q\_proj},\texttt{k\_proj},\texttt{v\_proj},\texttt{o\_proj}\}
. The bias setting was ‘none‘, and the task type was set to ‘causal LM‘.

To ensure robustness, each downstream experiment was conducted using three different train/test splits. For each split, the model was fine-tuned over multiple epochs, and the best-performing checkpoint was selected based on validation accuracy. The selected model was then evaluated on the corresponding test set. We aggregated the results across the three splits and reported the average performance (see Table
