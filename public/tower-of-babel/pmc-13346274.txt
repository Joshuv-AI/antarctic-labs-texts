USP43 Inhibits Intestinal Inflammation via TRAF4-mediated NF-κB Signaling
Authors: Li Zhou, Dongliang Li, Yan Lv, Jian Fang, Yue Liu, Zhencheng Zhu, Xin Tang, Bo Zhang, Chenjiang Qu, Qiuhua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory intestinal disorder; however, its exact pathophysiological mechanisms remain elusive. Through integrated RNA-seq and proteome analyses, we revealed that ubiquitin-specific protease 43 (USP43) expression is significantly decreased in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis. USP43 expression was also lower in UC patients than that in healthy controls. Furthermore, USP43 deficiency in mice exacerbated DSS-induced colitis. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated RAW264.7 cells, USP43 overexpression inhibited, whereas USP43 knockdown promoted the phosphorylation of p65 and the production of proinflammatory cytokines; NF-κB-specific inhibitor JSH-23 reversed the facilitatory effect of USP43 knockdown on inflammatory cytokines. Mechanistically, USP43 interacts with and deubiquitinates TRAF4, thereby inhibiting NF-κB signaling and reducing proinflammatory cytokine levels. Importantly, TRAF4 overexpression partially reversed the increase in LPS-mediated NF-κB activation caused by USP43 knockdown. Collectively, our findings highlight a novel role for USP43 in suppressing intestinal inflammation through the inhibition of TRAF4-mediated NF-κB activation. These results reveal a new mechanism involving the USP43/TRAF4 axis in the regulation of intestinal inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02509-3.Keywords: USP43, Ulcerative colitis, Inflammation, TRAF4/NF-κB

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is characterized by chronic, dysregulated inflammation in the intestinal tract, accompanied by diarrhea and intestinal bleeding. In 2023, the global prevalence of UC was estimated to be 5 million cases, with an accelerating incidence worldwide [1, 2]. The etiological basis of UC is multifaceted and influenced by the interplay among genetic predisposition, immunological disorders, microbial dysbiosis, epithelial barrier defects and environmental factors [2–6]. However, the detailed pathogenesis of UC is complicated and not fully understood. Therefore, understanding the underlying mechanisms of UC and developing novel therapeutic targets for UC treatment are critical.

Ubiquitination is implicated in the progression of multiple diseases, such as cancers and inflammatory diseases [7–9]. Ubiquitination modification is catalyzed by E3 ubiquitin ligases, which are counterregulated by deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) [10]. For example, the deubiquitinase OTUD1 removes K63-linked ubiquitination of RIPK1 and suppresses RIPK1-mediated NF-κB activation, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation [11]. Another deubiquitinase, JOSD2, deubiquitinates IMPDH2 and inhibits IMPDH2-NF-κB inflammatory signaling in macrophages to prevent inflammation [12]. Therefore, DUBs are pivotal to the regulation of colonic inflammation. USP43 is a deubiquitinating enzyme that belongs to the ubiquitin-specific protease family. Previous studies have demonstrated that USP43 plays crucial regulatory roles in tumorigenesis and tumor progression [13–15]. In addition, USP43 inhibits the antiviral innate immune response via RNF2-mediated TBK1 ubiquitination [16]. Furthermore, USP43 is aberrantly expressed in colorectal cancer [17]. However, the function and underlying mechanism of USP43 in inflammatory diseases such as UC have not yet been elucidated.

NF-κB signaling is identified as one of the key regulators in colitis. This key signaling process is activated by inflammation, and it contributes to the expression of proteins associated with immune responses. NF-κB activity is inhibited by IκB in cytoplasm, and LPS is a recognized activator of the NF-κB complex. When cells are stimulated, IKK leads to the phosphorylation of IκB, and the subsequent degradation of IκB causes the nuclear translocation of NF-κB [18–20]. Numerous studies have confirmed that some TRAFs are important regulators of NF-κB signaling [21]; however, the regulatory effect of TRAF4 on NF-κB remains largely unknown. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that ubiquitination of TRAFs plays pivotal roles in regulating intestinal inflammatory responses. Nur77 inhibits TRAF6 autoubiquitination, thereby suppressing TLR-IL-1R-triggered inflammatory responses [22]. USP47 deficiency promotes NF-κB signaling by increasing the K63-linked polyubiquitination of TRAF6 [23]. TRAF4, a member of the TRAF family, has been shown to impact the proliferation of ST2+ mTh2 cells during IL-33-mediated type 2 pulmonary inflammation [24]. Moreover, TRAF4 prevents inflammation-induced colorectal cancer through the ubiquitination of LAMTOR1 [25]. Together, these previous studies suggest that TRAF4 plays critical roles in the regulation of inflammation and inflammation-related diseases. Nevertheless, the regulatory function of TRAF4 in colonic inflammation remains largely unknown.

Here, we demonstrate that USP43 relieves intestinal inflammation by suppressing proinflammatory cytokines in vivo. In addition, the role of USP43 in LPS-stimulated macrophages was explored in vitro. We reveal that USP43 prevents NF-κB signaling by removing polyubiquitin chains from TRAF4 to suppress intestinal inflammation. These results indicate the protective role of USP43 in DSS-induced colitis, providing a new approach for colitis prevention and therapy.

Methods

Mice

Usp43−/− male C57BL/6 J mice generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 technique and their wild-type male littermates were obtained from Cyagen (Taicang, Jiangsu, China). All the mice were housed on a 12/12 h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity. The animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care Committee of Soochow University.

Antibodies and Reagents

The antibodies used were anti-USP43 (Invitrogen, PA5-101,945; Abcam, ab196796; Santa Cruz, sc-398436), anti-TRAF4 (Bethyl Laboratories, A302-840A; Proteintech, 66,755–1-Ig), anti-Flag (Proteintech, 20,543–1-AP and 66,008–4-Ig), anti-Myc (Proteintech, 60,003–2-Ig), anti-HA (Roche, 11,867,423,001), anti-Occludin (Proteintech, 27,260–1-AP), anti-ZO-1 (Proteintech, 21,773–1-AP), anti-MUC2 (Santa Cruz, sc-7314), anti-CD8a (Abcam, ab217344), anti-CD11c (CST, 97585S), anti-CD19 (CST, 90176S), anti-F4/80 (CST, 70076S), anti-Ly6C (Abcam, ab314120) anti-Ly6G (CST, 87048S), anti-IκBα (Santa Cruz, sc-1643), anti-p-p65 (Santa Cruz, sc-136548), anti-p65 (Proteintech, 10,745–1-AP) and anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 66,009–1-Ig). Goat anti-mouse (31,430), anti-rabbit (31,460) and anti-rat (31,470) secondary antibodies were obtained from Invitrogen.

DSS (216,011,080) was obtained from MP Biomedicals. Puromycin (A1113803) and G418 (11,811,023) were obtained from Gibco. Lipofectamine 2000 (11,668,019) was obtained from Invitrogen. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; L4391) and N-ethylmaleimide (NEM; E3876) were obtained from Sigma. JSH-23 (HY-13982) and MG132 (HY-13259) were obtained from MCE.

Construction of a DSS-induced Colitis Model

A colitis model was established by DSS as previously described [26]. Briefly, male mice (aged 6 to 8 weeks) were fed with 3% DSS for 7 days. The mice were sacrificed on Day 8 under anesthesia, and the collected colons were used for further analysis.

Histological Analysis

Colon tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin and sectioned. After dewaxing twice with xylene, the sections were hydrated in a gradient alcohol solution. Afterward, slides were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Histological scores of epithelial damage and inflammatory cell infiltration were analyzed as previously described [27].

Immunofluorescence Assay

RAW264.7 cells plated on coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, the cells or cryosections were blocked with normal goat serum, labeled with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation of fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. The coverslips were covered with DAPI-Fluoromount-G. All the images were obtained using a confocal microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

After deparaffinization and rehydration, paraffin sections were heated for antigen retriveal in citrate buffer and endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide. The sections were then rinsed with PBS, blocked with normal goat serum and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃, followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Then sections were stained with diaminobenzidine and counterstained with hematoxylin.

RNA-seq Analysis

Colons were collected from control and DSS-induced mice. Four biological replicates were carried out in each group. RNA-seq analysis was conducted by Shanghai Cloudseq Biotech, Inc. (Shanghai, China). The original data are uploaded to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE308679).

Proteomic Analysis

Colon tissues were collected from control and DSS-treated mice. Differentiated proteins were identified by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) at PTM Biolab (Hangzhou, China). Four biological replicates were conducted for proteomics analysis.

Cell Culture, Infection and Stimulation

The human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T and the mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 were obtained from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. USP43 shRNAs, USP43 overexpression or TRAF4 shRNAs and TRAF4 overexpression lentiviruses were obtained from Genechem (Shanghai, China). The two shRNAs targeting USP43 were as follows: shUSP43-1, CGTTCTTGATTCGGGAAGATA; and shUSP43-2, GCAGAGGGTGACAATGTGTAT. The two shRNAs targeting TRAF4 were shTRAF4-1, GCCCACCCATCTGAAGGATAG; and shTRAF4-2, GCTGGAAGAACTATCCATAGG. RAW264.7 macrophages were infected with the indicated lentiviruses, followed by selection with 6 μg/ml puromycin or 300 μg/ml G418. For LPS stimulation, RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with 50 ng/ml LPS for the indicated times. For NF-κB inhibition, RAW264.7 macrophages were preincubated with 10 μM JSH-23 for 2 h before LPS treatment.

Plasmid Construction and Transfection

USP43 and TRAF4 were generated by PCR and cloned into the pCMV-Flag and pCMV-Myc vectors, respectively. All the constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing. The plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The cells were lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP-40; pH 7.5) supplemented with 1% protease (CWBIO) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Bimake) for 1 h on ice. The supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min. Magnetic beads (Selleck) were coupled to appropriate primary antibodies and rotated for 2 h at RT. Afterward, the supernatant was incubated with the antibody magnetic bead complex and then corotated overnight at 4 °C. The immunocomplexes were subsequently washed with lysis buffer and eluted with SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min.

qPCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from mouse colon tissues and RAW264.7 cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green (Applied Biosystems). We used the 2−△△Ct quantification method with 18S as the internal normalization control. The primers used are listed in Table 1.

Immunoblot Analysis

Colon tissues and cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The protein samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h before they were incubated with primary antibody overnight. The membranes were subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies. The bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL).

Ubiquitination Assay

Flag-USP43, Myc-TRAF4, and HA-Ub were transfected into HEK293T cells, which were subsequently treated with 10 μM MG132 for 6 h before they were harvested. Cells or tissues were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with 10 mM NEM and protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were immunoprecipitated with the indicated antibodies and incubated with magnetic beads on a rotator at 4 °C overnight. Afterward, the immunocomplexes were eluted with SDS buffer and immunoblotted.

Silver Staining and Mass Spectrometry

HEK293T cells transfected with the Flag vector or Flag-USP43 were subjected to immunoprecipitation using anti-FLAG immunoaffinity resin (Sigma). Afterward, the eluted proteins were subjected to SDS‒PAGE, silver-stained, and analyzed by LC‒MS/MS.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

USP43 is Downregulated in Murine Colitis and UC Patients

To identify a pivotal factor in UC pathogenesis, we first established an experimental colitis model in mice treated with 3% DSS (Fig. 1A). Next, we explored RNA transcripts by performing RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in the colons of control and DSS-induced mice, and identified 6359 differentially expressed genes (Fc > 2, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Moreover, we conducted proteomic analysis in control and DSS-induced mice, and identified 869 differentially expressed proteins (Fc > 2, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). Notably, we detected overlap between the results of the transcriptome and proteome sequencing analyses. Given that the altered expression of several DUBs has crucial functions in cancer and immune-related diseases [28–31], we screened for DUBs that respond to DSS-induced mouse colitis. Interestingly, we detected a significant decrease in USP43 expression in UC mice compared with control mice (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, we confirmed decreases in USP43 transcript and protein levels (Fig. 1E–G). Finally, we analyzed the RNA-seq dataset GSE266325 and found that USP43 expression was decreased in samples from patients with UC (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these data indicate a potential role for USP43 in UC pathogenesis.

Usp43−/− Mice are Highly Susceptible to DSS-induced Colitis

To further evaluate the role of USP43 in the systemic inflammatory response to colitis, we generated Usp43−/− mice by using CRISPR/Cas9 to remove all the exons of the USP43 gene (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The knockout mice were further confirmed by PCR analysis of mouse tail DNA (Supplementary Fig. 1B) and western blot analysis of colon tissue (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Next, Usp43−/− and WT mice were treated with 3% DSS. Following DSS administration, Usp43−/− mice exhibited more severe symptoms of colitis, such as greater weight loss and shorter colon length (Fig. 2A–C). In addition, histopathological staining revealed extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in the colons of Usp43−/− mice compared with those of WT mice upon DSS challenge (Fig. 2D, E). UC is often associated with intestinal barrier dysfunction, we further investigated the impact of USP43 on epithelial integrity. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that USP43 deficiency significantly decreased the expression of tight junction proteins Occludin and ZO-1, as well as mucus protein MUC2, in DSS-induced mice (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Collectively, our results demonstrate that the loss of USP43 leads to aggravated colitis.

D H&E-stained colons of DSS-induced WT and Usp43−/− mice. E Semiquantitative histological scores of colon sections from WT and Usp43−/− mice after exposure to DSS. Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001. NS, no significance

USP43 Deficiency Promotes the Production of Inflammatory Cytokines

Considering that colitis induced by DSS involves multiple immune compartments including T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, monocytes and neutrophils, we performed immunohistochemistry to identify which populations were dysregulated in Usp43⁻/⁻ mice. The results demonstrated that Usp43 knockout in DSS-induced colitis mice resulted in an increase in the number of T cells and macrophages, with no significant change in the numbers of dendritic cells, B cells, monocytes and neutrophils. Among these, the increase in macrophages was the most pronounced (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Therefore, we focused on macrophages in vitro. It is well established that proinflammatory cytokines are closely related to intestinal inflammation [32]. To determine whether USP43 could regulate inflammation, we stably transfected macrophages with USP43-knockdown or USP43-overexpressing lentivirus and detected the transcript levels of inflammatory cytokines following LPS stimulation. Immunoblot analysis was performed to evaluate the knockdown and overexpression efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). USP43 knockdown in RAW264.7 macrophages promoted LPS-induced increases in IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels, whereas USP43 overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 3A, B). Consistent with these results, Usp43−/− mice exhibited increased levels of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α in DSS-treated colons (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that USP43 alleviates intestinal inflammation by suppressing proinflammatory cytokine production.

C IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α mRNA levels in colon tissues from WT and Usp43−/− mice upon DSS treatment. Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001

USP43 Inhibits LPS-induced NF-κΒ Activation in Experimental Colitis

Given the crucial role of the NF-κΒ signaling pathway in proinflammatory cytokines [33, 34], we further determined the role of USP43 in LPS-induced NF-κΒ signaling. As expected, USP43 overexpression significantly inhibited NF-κB signaling activation, whereas USP43 knockdown further increased NF-κB signaling activation in LPS-treated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, immunofluorescence experiments demonstrated that USP43 knockdown in RAW264.7 cells enhanced the nuclear translocation of p65 induced by LPS (Fig. 4B, C). Usp43−/− mice exhibited significantly increased degradation of IκΒα and phosphorylation of p65 upon DSS treatment (Fig. 4D, E). To further verify whether the influence of USP43 on inflammatory cytokines was mediated by NF-κΒ, we used the NF-κB-specific inhibitor JSH-23. We observed that JSH-23 robustly reversed the increase in IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels caused by USP43 knockdown (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these data demonstrate that USP43 inhibits LPS-induced NF-κB activation.

USP43 Interacts with TRAF4 and Deubiquitinates TRAF4

To explore the mechanism by which USP43 regulates the inflammatory process, we transfected Flag-USP43 into HEK293T cells to investigate the potential proteins that interact with USP43 by LC‒MS/MS, which led to the identification of TRAF4 (Fig. 5A, B). We first validated the endogenous interactions between USP43 and TRAF4 in RAW264.7 cells by immunoprecipitation analysis (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 6A). We then transfected Flag-tagged USP43 and Myc-tagged TRAF4 into HEK293T cells, and verified that exogenous USP43 interacts with TRAF4 (Fig. 5D, E). Consistently, confocal microscopy analysis also demonstrated the colocalization of USP43 and TRAF4 in RAW264.7 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 6B). We subsequently investigated how USP43 influenced TRAF4 expression. Overexpression of USP43 increased TRAF4 expression; conversely, USP43 knockdown decreased TRAF4 expression in RAW264.7 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 6 C, D). Additionally, TRAF4 expression was significantly downregulated in Usp43−/− mice (Fig. 5F). Next, TRAF4 protein levels were detected in mouse colons from each group. The results indicated that TRAF4 expression was significantly decreased in DSS-induced colitis (Fig. 5G, H). Finally, we evaluated the effect of USP43 on TRAF4 ubiquitylation in HEK293T cells transfected with Flag-USP43, Myc-TRAF4, and HA-Ub. Overexpression of USP43 decreased TRAF4 ubiquitylation (Fig. 5I). Moreover, in DSS-induced colitis model, USP43 deficiency increased the ubiquitination of endogenous TRAF4 in colon tissues (Supplementary Fig. 6E). These findings suggest that USP43 binds to TRAF4 and inhibits its ubiquitylation.

Overexpression of TRAF4 Rescues the Effect of USP43 Inhibition on Macrophage Inflammation

To explore whether USP43 influenced LPS-induced macrophage inflammation through regulating TRAF4 expression, we transfected RAW264.7 cells with shUSP43 lentivirus and TRAF4 overexpression lentivirus. First, we established stable TRAF4-overexpressing or TRAF4-knockdown cell lines and determined the overexpression and knockdown efficiency of TRAF4 by RT‒qPCR and WB (Supplementary Fig. 7A–D). Next, we explored the influence of TRAF4 knockdown on macrophage inflammation. We found that TRAF4 knockdown increased IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α mRNA levels in LPS-challenged RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, TRAF4 overexpression restored inflammatory factor levels in USP43-knockdown cells (Fig. 6B). Immunoblotting assays revealed that TRAF4 partially reversed the ability of USP43 silencing to promote NF-κB activation (Fig. 6C, D). Taken together, these findings revealed that USP43 suppressed inflammation by deubiquitinating TRAF4.

Discussion

Studies have reported that USP43 plays crucial roles in proliferation and metastasis in multiple tumors [13, 17]. USP43 overexpression promotes cell proliferation and migration by suppressing of HDAC2 degradation, thus activating HDAC2-mediated Wnt/beta-catenin signaling and leading to decreased cisplatin sensitivity in epithelial ovarian cancer [35]. Li et al. reported that USP43 stabilizes c-Myc and regulates glycolytic metabolism in bladder cancer [14]. Recently, USP43 was revealed to mediate the deubiquitination of RNF2, thus promoting TBK1 ubiquitination and degradation, which negatively regulate antiviral innate immunity [16]. Here, we reported decreased expression of USP43 in colitis. Knockdown or deletion of USP43 promoted the inflammatory response, whereas overexpression of USP43 had the opposite effect. Compared with WT mice, USP43-deficient mice exhibited increased IκBα degradation and p65 phosphorylation upon DSS treatment. Consistently, the effects of USP43 knockdown on LPS-challenged RAW264.7 cells were abolished by JSH-23. With respect to the underlying mechanism, USP43 interacted with TRAF4 and cleaved polyubiquitin chains from TRAF4, as determined by IP‒MS. Therefore, our data demonstrate that USP43 plays a crucial protective role in colitis.

In recent years, several DUBs have been reported to perform critical regulatory functions in intestinal inflammation [9]. The deubiquitinase OTUD6A in macrophages promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β secretion, leading to the development of colitis and colitis-associated cancer in mice [27]. Intestinal epithelial-specific USP13 attenuates colonic inflammation by selectively removing the K63-linked ubiquitin of GRP78 [36]. Here, we found USP43 expression decreased in DSS-induced colitis and UC patients compared to those in healthy controls. Notably, Usp43−/− mice were more susceptible to DSS-induced colitis, indicating that USP43 plays a novel role in colitis. Accumulating evidence indicates that UC is characterized by dysregulation of the immune response, which leads to intestinal inflammation through the production of high levels of proinflammatory factors [37]. Here, we investigated the effect of USP43 on various immune cell populations and observed that macrophages increased most significantly in Usp43−/− mice upon DSS treatment, indicating that USP43 specifically regulates macrophage function. Consistently, USP43 silencing with shRNA increased IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels in macrophages following LPS stimulation, whereas USP43 overexpression inhibited IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α production. Compared with their WT littermates, Usp43−/− mice exhibited elevated levels of proinflammatory factors upon DSS treatment. Together, these results suggest that USP43 deficiency aggravates colitis, at least in part, by promoting the production of proinflammatory cytokines. We further found USP43 deficiency impaired the integrity of the gut barrier in colitis, manifested as reduced levels of tight junction proteins Occludin and ZO-1, as well as mucus protein MUC2. However, whether the effect of USP43 on intestinal epithelial barrier injury is caused by macrophage inflammation requires further investigation.

NF-κB signaling is involved in multiple biological processes, including inflammation, immunity, cell proliferation and migration [38–40]. The abnormal activation of NF-κB is closely related to the progression of colitis. Once excessive activated, resulting in amounts of proinflammation factors production and intestinal tissue damage [41]. Here, we revealed that reduced USP43 expression in DSS-induced colitis is associated with high levels of inflammatory cytokines. We subsequently demonstrated that USP43 overexpression inhibited, while USP43 knockdown promoted the activation of NF-κB signaling in RAW264.7 cells following LPS stimulation. Immunofluorescence also confirmed that downregulation of USP43 increased LPS-induced p65 nuclear translocation in RAW264.7 macrophages. Similarly, the effects of USP43 knockdown on LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells were abolished by the NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23. Furthermore, our data revealed that USP43-deficient mice exposed to DSS exhibited increased p65 phosphorylation in vivo, suggesting that USP43 regulates NF-κB signaling in intestinal inflammation.

Here, we identified USP43 as a novel DUB that targets TRAF4. As an adaptor protein, TRAF4 has been shown to regulate the inflammatory response. For example, TRAF4 in ST2+ mTh2 cells plays a crucial role in type 2 pulmonary inflammation [24]. Lc-TRAF4 enhances antiviral and inflammatory signaling mediated by Lc-TRIF and Lc-TRAF6 in teleosts [42]. On the other hand, Takeshita et al. reported that TRAF4 physically binds to TRAF6 and TRIF and suppresses TLR-mediated NF-κB and IFN-β promoter activation [43]. TRAF4 can also inhibit NOD2-induced NF-κB activation [44]. Recently, Zhao et al. reported that TRAF4 deficiency in mice accelerates inflammation-induced colon cancer progression [25]. Here, we demonstrated that silencing TRAF4 promoted the production of proinflammatory cytokines in LPS-treated macrophages. In addition, we confirmed that USP43 interacted with TRAF4 in vitro and in vivo. Accumulating studies have reported that the expression and activity of TRAF4 are dependent on ubiquitination [45, 46]. In our study, we found that the polyubiquitination of TRAF4 was suppressed by USP43. Furthermore, TRAF4 overexpression reversed the facilitatory effect of USP43 knockdown on NF-κB signaling and inflammatory factors. These data suggest that USP43 regulates intestinal inflammation in a TRAF4-dependent manner. Future investigations are needed to confirm that the domain of TRAF4 contributes primarily to its interaction with USP43 and to identify the ubiquitination sites in TRAF4 that are regulated by USP43.

In summary, our study reveals that USP43 protects against colitis. The molecular mechanism underlying this phenotype involves USP43 binding to TRAF4 and suppressing its ubiquitination, inhibiting NF-κB signaling and thereby alleviating inflammation (Fig. 7). Thus, our data demonstrate the crucial function of the USP43/TRAF4/NF-κB axis in intestinal inflammation and highlight a promising avenue for colitis treatment.
