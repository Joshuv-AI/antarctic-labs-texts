Approximating functions on ℝ + {\mathbb{R}}^{+} by exponential sums
Alexey Kuznetsov, Armin Mohammadioroojeh

Abstract
We present a new method for approximating real-valued functions on ℝ + {\mathbb{R}}^{+} by linear combinations of exponential functions with complex coefficients. The approach is based on a multi-point Padé approximation of the Laplace transform and employs a highly efficient continued fraction technique to construct the corresponding rational approximant. We demonstrate the accuracy of this method through a variety of examples, including the Gaussian function, probability density functions of the lognormal and Gompertz–Makeham distributions, the hockey stick and unit step functions, as well as a function arising in the approximation of the gamma and Barnes G G -functions.



Keywords
: exponential sum, Padé approximation, Laplace transform, continued fraction

Introduction

Let
f
be a real-valued function defined on an interval
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
. The problem of approximating
f
by exponential sums of the form

\phi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}x},\qquad c_{j},\lambda_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}},

(1)

has a long history. The earliest systematic method is due to Gaspard de Prony, dating back to the late eighteenth century. Prony’s method
[
10
]
samples the function
f
on a regular grid of points inside the interval
I
and transforms the problem into one involving a linear difference equation with constant coefficients. Although Prony’s method is general – it applies to a wide variety of functions – it suffers from numerical instability. Numerous improvements and variants have since been developed; see
[
18
]
and the references therein.

Extensive work has also been done on studying optimal exponential-sum approximations in
L_{p}
spaces and on developing such approximations for specific functions. In this context, a more general class of approximants is often considered, in which the coefficients
c_{j}
in (
1
) may be polynomials in
x
. Rice
[
19
]
studied Chebyshev (i.e.,
L_{\infty}(I,{\textnormal{d}}x)
) approximations on a finite interval. Kammler
[
13
,
14
]
characterized optimal
L_{p}([0,1],{\textnormal{d}}x)
approximations and investigated best
L_{2}((0,\infty),{\textnormal{d}}x)
approximations of completely monotonic functions. Approximations to power functions
x^{-\beta}
were studied in
[
6
,
17
,
11
]
.

Another widely applicable approach was introduced in 2005 by Beylkin and Monzón
[
4
,
5
]
. Their method also samples
f
on a regular grid, but then constructs a Hankel matrix from these values and applies singular value decomposition. This method has become very popular and has been applied in a broad range of contexts
[
8
,
12
,
17
]
.

In this paper we present a new algorithm for approximating functions
f:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
by exponential sums of the form (
1
). Our method is based on a multi-point Padé approximation of the Laplace transform of
f
, and can be viewed as a generalization of the technique introduced in
[
16
]
. Let
F(z)
and
R(z)
denote the Laplace transforms of
f
and
\phi
, respectively:

F(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-zx}{\textnormal{d}}x,\qquad R(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(x)e^{-zx}{\textnormal{d}}x.

(2)

We assume that
f\in L_{1}((0,\infty),{\textnormal{d}}x)
, so that
F
is well defined in the right half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 0
.
If
\phi
has the form (
1
), then
R(z)
is a rational function with simple poles at
-\lambda_{j}
, and the coefficients
c_{j}
come from its partial fraction decomposition:

R(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{c_{j}}{z+\lambda_{j}}.

(3)

The key observation underlying our approach is that if the exponential sum
\phi
is close (in some norm) to
f
, then the rational function
R
is close (in an appropriate sense) to the Laplace transform
F
, and vice versa. For example, if
\|f-\phi\|_{p}
is small for some
p>1
, then by Hölder’s inequality we have

\displaystyle|F(z)-R(z)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{0}^{\infty}(f(x)-\phi(x))e^{-zx}{\textnormal{d}}x\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|f-\phi\|_{p}\times\Bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-q\textnormal{Re}(z)x}{\textnormal{d}}x\Bigg)^{1/q}=\frac{\|f-\phi\|_{p}}{(q\textnormal{Re}(z))^{1/q}},

for all
z
in the right half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)>0
, where
q
is defined by
1/p+1/q=1
. When
p=1
, the same bound holds with the denominator replaced by
1
. Thus, a good exponential-sum approximation to
f
in
L_{p}
norm produces a good rational approximation to
F
in
L_{\infty}
norm. Conversely, writing
f
and
\phi
via the inverse Laplace transform yields, for any
c>0
,

|f(x)-\phi(x)|\leq\frac{e^{cx}}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|F(c+{\textnormal{i}}y)-R(c+{\textnormal{i}}y)|\,{\textnormal{d}}y,

so if
F
is close to
R
in
L_{1}
norm on some vertical line
c+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
, then
e^{-cx}(f(x)-\phi(x))
is small in
L_{\infty}
norm. This shows the close connection between rational approximation of Laplace transforms and exponential-sum approximation of the original function.

Having established this connection, let us examine what information about the original function
f
can be conveniently transferred to its Laplace transform. Suppose that
f
satisfies

f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\infty}-1}\xi_{j}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}+O(x^{n_{\infty}}),\qquad x\to 0^{+}.

(4)

A sufficient condition for this expansion is the boundedness of the derivative
f^{(n_{\infty})}(x)
on some interval
(0,c)
; in that case we necessarily have
\xi_{j}=f^{(j)}(0+)
. Applying Watson’s Lemma
[
21
]
, we obtain

F(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\infty}-1}\xi_{j}z^{-j-1}+O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1}),\qquad z\to\infty,

(5)

uniformly in any sector

S_{\theta}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|\arg(z)|\leq\theta\},\qquad\theta\in(0,\pi/2).

We now require that the approximating rational function
R
satisfies the same asymptotic condition at
z=\infty
. By Watson’s Lemma, this is equivalent to
f(x)-\phi(x)=O(x^{n_{\infty}})
as
x\to 0^{+}
. Imposing this condition ensures that
R(z)
is close to
F(z)
for large values of
z
.

To guarantee closeness for moderate and small values of
z
, we impose additional constraints by requiring that
R
interpolates
F
at selected points
z_{j}
lying in the right half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 0
. The general outline of our method is therefore as follows: we aim to construct a rational function
R
satisfying the following conditions.

Interpolation conditions at finite points:

R(z_{j})=F(z_{j}),\qquad j=1,2,\dots,p,

(6)

where the points
\{z_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
lie in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 0
and satisfy the symmetry condition
z_{p+1-j}=\overline{z}_{j}
for all
j=1,2,\dots,p
.

Expansion at infinity:

as
z\to\infty
,

R(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\infty}-1}\xi_{j}\,z^{-j-1}+O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1}),

(7)

where the coefficients
\xi_{j}
are taken from (
4
).

The above setup defines a multi-point Padé approximation problem with
p+1
points, namely
\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{p},\infty\}
. At each finite interpolation point we match only the value of the rational
function
R
, while at infinity we match the first
n_{\infty}
coefficients of the power series expansion of
R
in
z^{-1}
. This algorithm therefore requires knowledge of the first several coefficients of the Taylor expansion of
f
at zero. These are typically easy to obtain, since in most applications
f
is given by an explicit formula and its derivatives can be computed directly. The algorithm also requires the values of the Laplace transform of
f
at the interpolation points
z_{j}
. When a closed-form expression for
F
is unavailable, these values must be computed numerically (see Section
3
for details).

A rational function
R(z)
of the form (
3
) is determined by
2M
complex parameters
\{c_{j},\lambda_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq M}
. The conditions (
6
) and (
7
) impose
p+n_{\infty}
equations on
R
:
p
equations from interpolation at
\{z_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
and
n_{\infty}
equations ensuring that the coefficients in the expansion of
R
in powers of
z^{-1}
coincide with the prescribed
\xi_{j}
. Thus we obtain
p+n_{\infty}
constraints for
2M
unknowns. It is therefore natural to expect that when
p+n_{\infty}=2M
, there exists at most one rational function
R
satisfying these conditions. The following proposition confirms this expectation.

Proposition 1
.

If
p+n_{\infty}=2M
, then there exists at most one rational function
R
of the form (
3
) satisfying the conditions (
6
) and (
7
). Moreover, if such a rational function
R
exists, it is necessarily real.

Proof.

Suppose there exist two rational functions
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
of the form (
3
) that satisfy (
6
) and (
7
). Each
R_{i}
can be written as
R_{i}(z)=P_{i}(z)/Q_{i}(z)
, where
\deg(P_{i})<\deg(Q_{i})=M
. The fact that both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
satisfy the interpolation and asymptotic conditions implies that their difference

G(z):=R_{1}(z)-R_{2}(z)=\frac{P_{1}(z)Q_{2}(z)-P_{2}(z)Q_{1}(z)}{Q_{1}(z)Q_{2}(z)}

has
p
zeros at the points
\{z_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
and satisfies
G(z)=O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1})
as
z\to\infty
. Consequently, the numerator
P_{1}Q_{2}-P_{2}Q_{1}
is a polynomial with
p
distinct zeros, yet

\deg(P_{1}Q_{2}-P_{2}Q_{1})\leq\deg(Q_{1}Q_{2})-n_{\infty}-1=2M-n_{\infty}-1=p-1.

Thus the polynomial must vanish identically, and we conclude that
R_{1}(z)\equiv R_{2}(z)
.

Next, suppose
R
is a rational function of the form (
3
) satisfying (
6
) and (
7
). Define

R_{3}(z):=\overline{R(\overline{z})},

that is,
R_{3}
is obtained from
R
by conjugating all coefficients. For every
j=1,2,\dots,p
we then have

R_{3}(z_{j})=\overline{R(\overline{z_{j}})}=\overline{F(z_{p+1-j})}=F(z_{j})=R(z_{j}).

Since
\xi_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and the asymptotic condition (
7
) holds, it follows that
R(z)-R_{3}(z)=O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1})
as
z\to\infty
. By the same uniqueness argument as above, we conclude that
R(z)\equiv R_{3}(z)
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. In particular,
R(z)\in{\mathbb{R}}
whenever
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
, which proves that
R
is a real rational function.

The multi-point Padé approximation problem described in (
6
) and (
7
) requires as input two positive integer parameters,
p
and
n_{\infty}
, together with
p
complex numbers
\{z_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
. Even with the symmetry condition
z_{p+1-j}=\bar{z}_{j}
, this still leaves too many degrees of freedom. To simplify the algorithm, we propose the following special choice of interpolation points
z_{j}
. We assume
p\geq 2
, take two numbers
A\geq 0
and
B>0
, and consider the piecewise linear curve
\gamma_{A,B}
consisting of two intervals:

\gamma_{A,B}:=[A-B{\textnormal{i}},0]\cup[0,A+B{\textnormal{i}}].

We then define
z_{1}=A-B{\textnormal{i}}
,
z_{p}=A+B{\textnormal{i}}
, and place the remaining points
\{z_{j}\}_{2\leq j\leq p-1}
equidistantly along the curve
\gamma_{A,B}
. See Figure
1
for an illustration.

The motivation for this choice comes from Watson’s Lemma
[
21
]
, which implies that
F
admits the asymptotic expansion (
5
) for large
z
in any sector

S_{\theta}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|\arg(z)|\leq\theta\},\qquad\theta\in(0,\pi/2).

The farther
z
lies from the origin within such a sector, the more accurately
R(z)
approximates
F(z)
(since
F(z)-R(z)=O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1})
as
z\to\infty
); see Figure
1c
. We therefore enforce
p
interpolation conditions on the boundary
\gamma_{A,B}
of this sector (where
B/A=\tan(\theta)
). This allows us to control the error
F(z)-R(z)
not only for large
z
, but also for moderate and small values of
z\in S_{\theta}
. By the maximum modulus principle, if
F
and
R
are close for large
z
and along the boundary of
S_{\theta}
, then they will also be close throughout the interior of this sector. Consequently, it is unnecessary to place interpolation points
z_{j}
in the interior of
S_{\theta}
.

We can now summarize our algorithm:

(i)

Choose integers
p\geq 2
and
n_{\infty}\geq 1
such that
p+n_{\infty}=2M
is even;

(ii)

Compute the coefficients
\{\xi_{j}\}_{0\leq j<n_{\infty}}
in the expansion (
4
);

(iii)

Choose
A\geq 0
and
B>0

p

z_{j}
, with
z_{1}=A-B{\textnormal{i}}

(vi)

\phi

.

All of the above steps are straightforward, with the exception of step (iv). Several methods exist for solving multi-point Padé approximation problems. We adopt the continued fraction approach due to its speed and simplicity: the algorithm is fully recursive and avoids solving large systems of linear equations. The only drawback is that this approach requires the additional assumption
\xi_{0}\neq 0
. This restriction is not severe, however, and in Section
3.4
we describe a simple workaround.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section we present the continued fraction algorithm for solving the multi-point Padé approximation problem in step (iv). Section
3
discusses the implementation of our algorithm and reports results from numerous numerical experiments that illustrate both the efficiency of the algorithm and the accuracy of the resulting approximations.

2
The continued fraction algorithm

We say that a rational function
R(z)=P(z)/Q(z)
, where
P
and
Q
are polynomials, has degree
[n_{1}/n_{2}]
if
\deg(P)=n_{1}
and

\deg(Q)=n_{2}
.
In this section we present a continued fraction algorithm for constructing a rational function
R(z)
of degree
[M-1/M]
that satisfies
p
interpolation conditions

R(z_{j})=a_{j},\qquad j=1,2,\dots,p,

(8)

together with an asymptotic expansion at infinity of the form

R(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\infty}-1}\xi_{j}z^{-j-1}+O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1}),\qquad z\to\infty.

(9)

The parameters
p,n_{\infty}\in\mathbb{N}
(with
p+n_{\infty}=2M
), the interpolation data
\{a_{j},z_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
, and the coefficients
\{\xi_{j}\}_{0\leq j\leq n_{\infty}-1}
are assumed to be given, and they satisfy the symmetry conditions
z_{p+1-j}=\bar{z}_{j}
,
a_{p+1-j}=\bar{a}_{j}
.

Before describing the general algorithm, we illustrate the idea with a simple example. Let us find a rational function
R
of degree
[1/2]
that satisfies

R(1+{\textnormal{i}})=1-2{\textnormal{i}},\qquad R(1-{\textnormal{i}})=1+2{\textnormal{i}},

(10)

and

R(z)=z^{-1}+2z^{-2}+O(z^{-3}),\qquad z\to\infty.

(11)

As the first step, we define a function
R_{1}(z)
by

R(z)=\frac{R(1+{\textnormal{i}})}{1+(z-(1+{\textnormal{i}}))R_{1}(z)}=\frac{1-2{\textnormal{i}}}{1+(z-1-{\textnormal{i}})R_{1}(z)}.

(12)

Solving for
R_{1}(z)
gives

R_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{z-1-{\textnormal{i}}}\,\bigg[\frac{1-2{\textnormal{i}}}{R(z)}-1\bigg].

(13)

From (
10
) and (
13
) we obtain

R_{1}(1-{\textnormal{i}})=\tfrac{2}{5}-\tfrac{4}{5}{\textnormal{i}},

(14)

while from (
11
) and (
13
), expanding both
1/(z-1-{\textnormal{i}})
and
1/R(z)
in powers of
z^{-1}
, we find

R_{1}(z)=(1-2{\textnormal{i}})+3{\textnormal{i}}z^{-1}+O(z^{-2}),\qquad z\to\infty.

(15)

We now repeat this process. Using (
14
), we define
R_{2}(z)
by

R_{1}(z)=\frac{R_{1}(1-{\textnormal{i}})}{1+(z-(1-{\textnormal{i}}))R_{2}(z)}=\frac{2/5-4{\textnormal{i}}/5}{1+(z-1+{\textnormal{i}})R_{2}(z)}.

(16)

After solving for
R_{2}(z)
and using (
15
), we expand
R_{2}(z)
in powers of
z^{-1}
to obtain

R_{2}(z)=-\tfrac{3}{5}z^{-1}-\big(\tfrac{3}{25}-\tfrac{9}{25}{\textnormal{i}}\big)z^{-2}+O(z^{-3}),\qquad z\to\infty.

(17)

We claim that

R_{2}(z)=-\frac{3/5}{\,z-1/5+3{\textnormal{i}}/5}

(18)

satisfies condition (
17
). Indeed, as
z\to\infty
,

\displaystyle R_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{5z}\times\frac{1}{1-(1/5-3{\textnormal{i}}/5)z^{-1}}=-\frac{3}{5z}\sum_{k\geq 0}(1/5-3{\textnormal{i}}/5)^{k}z^{-k}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{3}{5}z^{-1}-\big(\tfrac{3}{25}-\tfrac{9}{25}{\textnormal{i}}\big)z^{-2}+O(z^{-3}),

which is exactly (
17
).

Finally, we perform back substitution to reconstruct
R
. Substituting
R_{2}
given by (
18
) into (
16
) yields
R_{1}
, which is then substituted into (
12
). The result is the continued fraction representation

\displaystyle R(z)=\cfrac{1-2{\textnormal{i}}}{1+\cfrac{(2/5-4{\textnormal{i}}/5)(z-1-{\textnormal{i}})}{1-\cfrac{(3/5)(z-1+{\textnormal{i}})}{z-1/5+3{\textnormal{i}}/5}}}.

This can be written in the equivalent (standard) form

R(z)=\frac{z+1}{z^{2}-z+1}.

A direct verification confirms that this rational function satisfies both (
10
) and (
11
). By Proposition
1
, we have therefore found the unique solution to the multi-point Padé approximation problem (
10
) and (
11
).

We are now ready to present the general algorithm for finding the interpolating rational function. As a first step, we define a rational function
R_{1}
by

R(z)=\frac{a_{1}}{1+(z-z_{1})R_{1}(z)},

(19)

which is equivalent to

R_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{z-z_{1}}\,\bigg[\frac{a_{1}}{R(z)}-1\bigg].

(20)

By construction, the function
R(z)
defined through (
19
) satisfies
R(z_{1})=a_{1}
, as long as
R_{1}
does not have a pole at
z_{1}
. The remaining
p-1
interpolation conditions for
R
(at the points
\{z_{j}\}_{2\leq j\leq p}
) are satisfied if and only if

R_{1}(z_{j})=a_{j}^{(1)}:=\frac{1}{z_{j}-z_{1}}\bigg[\frac{a_{1}}{a_{j}}-1\bigg],\qquad j=2,3,\dots,p.

(21)

Thus, the linear fractional transformation (
19
) reduces the problem from interpolation at
p
finite points to interpolation at
p-1
points.

Next, we investigate the effect of the transformation
(
19
) on the expansion of
R
as
z\to\infty
. For this purpose it is convenient to assume that
R
has a series expansion in powers of
z^{-1}
(valid for sufficiently large
z
):

R(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}b_{j}z^{-j}.

(22)

We seek the coefficients
b^{(1)}_{j}
of the corresponding expansion of
R_{1}(z)
:

R_{1}(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}b^{(1)}_{j}z^{-j}.

(23)

Rewriting (
19
) gives

R(z)-z_{1}R(z)R_{1}(z)+zR(z)R_{1}(z)=a_{1}.

(24)

We now obtain equations for
b^{(1)}_{j}
by comparing coefficients of powers of
z
on both sides.
Comparing coefficients of
z
in (
24
) yields

b_{0}b^{(1)}_{0}=0.

(25)

Comparing constant terms (coefficients of
z^{0}
) gives

b_{0}-z_{1}b_{0}b_{0}^{(1)}+b_{0}b_{1}^{(1)}+b_{1}b_{0}^{(1)}=a_{1},

(26)

and for coefficients of
z^{-n}
with
n\geq 1
we obtain

b_{n}-z_{1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{n-i}b_{i}^{(1)}+\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}b_{n+1-i}b_{i}^{(1)}=0,\qquad n\geq 1.

(27)

At this stage we must consider two separate cases.

Case 1:

b_{0}=0
. In this case, equation (
25
) is automatically satisfied, and from
(
26
) we find
b_{0}^{(1)}=a_{1}/b_{1}
. The remaining coefficients are obtained iteratively from

b_{n}^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{b_{1}}\Bigg[b_{n}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}b_{i}^{(1)}\big(b_{n+1-i}-z_{1}b_{n-i}\big)\Bigg],\qquad n\geq 1,

which follows from (
27
).

Case 2:

b_{0}\neq 0
.
Then (
25
) implies
b_{0}^{(1)}=0
. From (
26
) and
(
27
) we obtain

b_{1}^{(1)}=\frac{a_{1}}{b_{0}}-1,

and for
n\geq 1
,

b_{n+1}^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{b_{0}}\Bigg[b_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{(1)}\big(b_{n+1-i}-z_{1}b_{n-i}\big)\Bigg].

We observe that in Case 1 (when
b_{0}=0
), if we know the next
k
coefficients
\{b_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq k}
of the expansion of
R(z)
at
z=\infty
, then we can determine
k
coefficients

\{b_{j}^{(1)}\}_{0\leq j\leq k-1}
of the corresponding expansion of
R_{1}(z)
. In this case, the first coefficient
b_{0}^{(1)}
is non-zero. In Case 2 (when
b_{0}\neq 0
), if we know
k
coefficients
\{b_{j}\}_{0\leq j\leq k-1}
of the expansion of
R(z)
at
z=\infty
, then necessarily
b_{0}^{(1)}=0
, and we can compute the next
k
coefficients

\{b_{j}^{(1)}\}_{1\leq j\leq k}
of the expansion of
R_{1}(z)
. This behavior is consistent with the simple example considered at the beginning of this section.

Now we can describe the general algorithm for finding the rational function
R(z)
satisfying (
8
) and (
9
). Define
\gamma_{0}=0
and
\gamma_{j}=\xi_{j-1}
for
j=1,2,\dots,n_{\infty}
, so that
R
has the following asymptotic expansion as
z\to\infty
:

R(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\infty}}\gamma_{j}z^{-j}+O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1}).

(28)

We now define
R_{1}
by

R(z)=\frac{a_{1}}{1+(z-z_{1})R_{1}(z)},

(29)

with interpolation values
a_{j}^{(1)}=R_{1}(z_{j})
for
2\leq j\leq p
, computed using (
21
). Since
\gamma_{0}=0
, the function
R_{1}
has the following expansion for large

:

R_{1}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\infty}-1}\gamma^{(1)}_{j}z^{-j}+O(z^{-n_{\infty}}).

(30)

Here
\gamma_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}^{(1)}
play the same role as
b_{j}
and
b_{j}^{(1)}
in the preceding discussion, and the coefficient
\gamma_{0}^{(1)}
is non-zero. Note that if a rational function
R_{1}
does not have a pole at
z_{1}
, the function
R
constructed via
(
29
) will satisfy the interpolation condition
R(z_{1})=a_{1}
.

We then repeat the procedure, reducing the number of interpolation points at each step. Specifically, we define

R_{1}(z)=\frac{a^{(1)}_{2}}{1+(z-z_{2})R_{2}(z)},

(31)

and compute
a_{j}^{(2)}=R_{2}(z_{j})
for
j=3,4,\dots,p
along with the coefficients of the expansion of
R_{2}(z)
at
z=\infty
. Since
\gamma_{0}^{(1)}\neq 0
, the expansion of
R_{2}
at infinity takes the form

R_{2}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\infty}}\gamma^{(2)}_{j}z^{-j}+O(z^{-n_{\infty}-1}),\qquad z\to\infty,

(32)

with
\gamma_{0}^{(2)}=0
. The condition that
R_{1}
does not have a pole at
z_{1}
is equivalent to
R_{2}(z_{1})\neq 1/(z_{2}-z_{1})
. As long as this condition is satisfied, the function
R
given by
(
29
) will satisfy the interpolation condition
R(z_{1})=a_{1}
. In order for
R_{1}
to satisfy the interpolation condition
R_{1}(z_{2})=a_{2}^{(1)}
, the function
R_{2}
should not have a pole at
z=z_{2}
.

Repeating this procedure
p-2
times, we define successively the functions
R_{3},R_{4},\dots,R_{p}
. Performing backward substitution (as in the illustrative example at the beginning of this section) yields the continued fraction representation of
R
:

R(z)=\cfrac{a_{1}}{1+\cfrac{(z-z_{1})a^{(1)}_{2}}{1+\cfrac{(z-z_{2})a^{(2)}_{3}}{\ddots\cfrac{\;\;\dots\;\;}{1+\cfrac{(z-z_{p-1})a^{(p-1)}_{p}}{1+(z-z_{p})R_{p}(z)}}}}}.

(33)

As long as and
R_{p}
does not have a pole at
z_{p}
and
R_{j}(z_{j-1})\neq 1/(z_{j}-z_{j-1})
for
j=2,3,\dots,p
, the rational function
R
will satisfy the interpolation conditions
R(z_{j})=a_{j}
for
j=1,2,\dots,p
.
Moreover,
R
has the prescribed asymptotics (
28
) if and only if
R_{p}
satisfies

R_{p}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{K}\gamma^{(p)}_{j}z^{-j}+O(z^{-K-1}),\qquad z\to\infty,

(34)

where the coefficients
\gamma_{j}^{(p)}
are computed by the iterative procedure above, and

K=\begin{cases}n_{\infty},&\text{if $p$ is even},\\
n_{\infty}-1,&\text{if $p$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Note that
K
is always an even integer, since
p+n_{\infty}=2M
is even.

The last step of the algorithm is to express
R_{p}(z)
(which must satisfy (
34
)) as a continued fraction (see
[
3
, Section 4.2]
):

R_{p}(z)=\gamma_{0}^{(p)}+\cfrac{d_{1}z^{-1}}{1+\cfrac{d_{2}z^{-1}}{\ddots\cfrac{\dots}{1+\cfrac{d_{K-1}z^{-1}}{1+d_{K}z^{-1}}}}},

(35)

where
d_{1}:=\gamma_{1}^{(p)}
, and the remaining coefficients
\{d_{j}\}_{2\leq j\leq K}
are computed by an iterative scheme similar to (
19
). Specifically, we define
R_{p+1}(z)
by

R_{p}(z)=\gamma_{0}^{(p)}+\frac{d_{1}}{zR_{p+1}(z)},

which is equivalent to

R_{p+1}(z)=\frac{d_{1}}{z\big(R_{p}(z)-\gamma_{0}^{(p)}\big)}.

From (
34
) and this relation, we compute the expansion of
R_{p+1}(z)
as
z\to\infty
:

R_{p+1}(z)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\gamma^{(p+1)}_{j}z^{-j}+O(z^{-K}).

(36)

We then set
d_{2}:=\gamma_{1}^{(p+1)}
and repeat the step. Next, we define

R_{p+1}(z)=1+\frac{d_{2}}{zR_{p+2}(z)},

compute the expansion of
R_{p+2}(z)
at
z=\infty
, and set
d_{3}:=\gamma_{1}^{(p+2)}
. Continuing this procedure for
K
steps yields the coefficients
\{d_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq K}
of the continued fraction (
35
). Substituting
R_{p}
from (
35
) into (
33
) then gives the desired rational function
R(z)
.

There are some exceptional cases in which the algorithm may fail to produce a result, specifically when division by zero occurs. This can happen if, for some
1\leq j\leq p-1
, the function
R_{j}
vanishes at one of the points
z_{i}
with
j+1\leq i\leq p
. Failure can also occur if
\gamma_{0}^{(l)}=\gamma_{1}^{(l)}=0
for some
l
, since in that case the iteration cannot proceed to compute
\gamma_{j}^{(l+1)}
. There are also rare cases where the algorithm produces a rational function
R
, but the result fails to satisfy the interpolation conditions (
8
). As discussed above, this may occur when
R_{j}(z_{j-1})=1/(z_{j}-z_{j-1})
for some
2\leq j\leq p
or if
R_{p}
has a pole at
z_{p}
. In all of our numerical experiments, however, such situations did not arise, and we do not regard them as a major practical concern.

We now claim that whenever the algorithm succeeds, the resulting rational function
R
has degree
[M-1/M]
. When
n_{\infty}=2L
is even, then
p=2M-n_{\infty}
is also even and
\gamma_{0}^{(p)}=0
. By induction on
L
, one can show that in this case
R_{p}
has degree
[L-1/L]
, and hence
R
(constructed via (
33
)) has degree
[M-1/M]
. When
n_{\infty}=2L+1
is odd,
p
is also odd and
\gamma_{0}^{(p)}\neq 0
. A similar argument shows that
R_{p}
has degree
[L/L]
, and therefore
R
again has degree
[M-1/M]
. Thus, once we obtain the rational function
R
and verify that it satisfies the interpolation conditions (
8
), Proposition
1
ensures that
R
is the unique solution to the multi-point Padé approximation problem.

Remark 1
.

As noted earlier, the integer
K
appearing in (
34
) is always even. Writing
K=2L
, we may identify the rational function
R^{*}_{p}(z):=R_{p}(1/z)
as the
[L/L]
Padé approximant at
z=0
to the power series

\sum_{j\geq 0}\gamma^{(p)}_{j}z^{j}
. There are several methods (besides the one described above) for computing the numerator and denominator coefficients of this Padé approximant. For instance, one can determine them by solving a linear system, as explained in
[
3
, Section 2.3]
. An efficient alternative that also uses continued fractions is the Q.D. algorithm (see
[
3
, Section 4.3]
) – this method may be preferable when
n_{\infty}
is large. In our examples
n_{\infty}
is relatively small, so we used the continued fraction approach described above, which is straightforward to implement.

3
Numerical results

We now discuss the implementation and performance of our algorithm.
Implementation requires high precision: we used a working precision of 100 decimal digits to compute the coefficients
c_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
. High precision is necessary due to the iterative nature of the continued fraction algorithm (where precision loss may accumulate) and the need to compute roots of polynomials of relatively high degree (in some of our examples, up to degree 60).

We wrote the code in Fortran and employed D. H. Bailey’s highly efficient MPFUN2020
[
2
]
arbitrary-precision package. Our code is available for download at
https://kuznetsovmath.ca/
. The module
polynomial_module.f90
defines two derived types: a polynomial and a rational function. A polynomial is specified by a sequence of coefficients of type
mp_real
or
mp_complex
(multiple-precision types provided by the MPFUN2020 package). A rational function is represented as a pair of polynomials corresponding to its numerator and denominator. This module includes subroutines for basic operations with polynomials and rational functions (addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division), as well as routines for computing roots of polynomials and partial fraction decompositions of rational functions. Polynomial roots are computed using the Ehrlich–Aberth method
[
1
,
7
]
.

The algorithm requires the values
F(z_{j})
of the Laplace transform of
f
. If
F(z)
is not known in closed form, we evaluate
F(z_{j})
numerically by applying the double-exponential quadrature of Takahasi–Mori
[
20
]
to the integral in (
2
):

F(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-zx}\,{\textnormal{d}}x\approx h\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{n}f(x_{n})e^{-zx_{n}}\big(1+e^{-nh}\big),

(37)

where
h>0
is a small step size and
x_{n}:=\exp(nh-\exp(-nh))
.
Formula (
37
) is obtained by applying the trapezoidal rule to the integral after the change of variables
x=\exp(u-\exp(-u))
. The infinite sum converges very rapidly and is truncated once its terms fall below the working precision.

Our implementation of the exponential sum approximation algorithm is reasonably fast. For example, finding the coefficients
c_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
for the exponential sum approximation to the hockey stick function (see Section
3.5
) with
M=30
(
M=60
) terms took about
0.1
seconds (respectively,
1
second) on a standard laptop (Intel Core i5-1250P processor with 16GB of RAM). This example is relatively simple since the Laplace transform is known explicitly. When
F(z_{j})
must be computed numerically, the runtime increases substantially. In the lognormal example of Section
3.4
, computing the coefficients
c_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
for
M=30
took
1.5
seconds. Fortunately, the computation of
F(z_{j})
for different values of
j
can be fully parallelized. With OpenMP parallelization (compiled using the
-fopenmp
flag), the same example with
M=30
took only
0.4
seconds instead of
1.5
seconds in serial execution.

Our algorithm depends on four parameters: two integers
p
and
n_{\infty}
(with the constraint that
p+n_{\infty}=2M
is even), and two real numbers
A\geq 0
and
B>0
. Based on extensive experiments, we can offer the following empirical guidelines for choosing these parameters:

•

Larger values of
M
generally yield better approximations, corresponding to more terms in the exponential sum (
1
).

•

Increasing
n_{\infty}
improves the accuracy of
f(x)-\phi(x)
for small values of
x
, since
f(x)-\phi(x)=O(x^{n_{\infty}})
as
x\to 0^{+}
.

•

Increasing
A
has a similar effect, as it improves the approximation
F(z)-R(z)
for large
z
, and Watson’s Lemma relates the large-
z
behavior of the Laplace transform to the small-
x
behavior of the original function.

•

In some cases (such as the hockey stick example of Section
3.5
), the algorithm produced exponential sums with very large coefficients
c_{j}
. Increasing
B
alleviated this issue and reduced the values of
c_{j}
to acceptable levels.

We now turn to the results of various numerical experiments. For each example considered in the remainder of this section, the parameters
c_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
of all exponential sum approximations can be downloaded at
https://kuznetsovmath.ca/
.

3.2
Approximations to the gamma function and the Barnes
G
-function

Now we take
f
to be

f(x):=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{3}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\coth\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{x}-\frac{x}{12}\right],\;\;\;x>0.

(38)

The function
f
is smooth, decays exponentially as
x\to+\infty
, and has a removable singularity at
x=0
(in fact, it is analytic in the disk
|x|<4\pi
). Exponential sum approximations to this function
were used in
[
16
]
to construct highly accurate approximations to the gamma function and the Barnes
G
-function.

Let
\phi
be of the form (
1
) and define

\eta_{1}(x):=2x^{2}\big(f(x)-\phi(x)\big),\;\;\;\eta_{2}(x):=6x\big(f(x)-\phi(x)\big)+2x^{2}\big(f^{\prime}(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x)\big),

and set

\epsilon_{i}=\|\eta_{i}\|_{\infty}=\sup\{|\eta_{i}(x)|:x\geq 0\}.

Let
\Phi
be the rational function

\Phi(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zx}x\phi(x)\,{\textnormal{d}}x=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{c_{j}}{(z+\lambda_{j})^{2}}.

(39)

For
\textnormal{Re}(z)>0
, we define approximations to
\ln(\Gamma(z))
and
\ln(G(z))
by

\displaystyle\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))

\displaystyle=\left(z-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln(z)-z+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)+\frac{1}{12z}-\Phi^{\prime}(z-1),

(40)

\displaystyle\ln(\widehat{G}(z))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{z^{2}}{2}-z+\frac{5}{12}\right)\ln(z)-\frac{3}{4}z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)(z-1)+z

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{12}-\ln({\mathcal{A}})-\tfrac{1}{12z}+\Phi(z-1)-(z-1)\Phi^{\prime}(z-1),

(41)

where
{\mathcal{A}}=1.282427\dots
is the Glaisher–Kinkelin constant.

In
[
16
, Proposition 2]
it was shown that

\big|\ln(\Gamma(z))-\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))\big|\leq\epsilon_{1},\;\;\;\big|\ln(G(z))-\ln(\widehat{G}(z))\big|\leq\epsilon_{2},

(42)

for all
z
in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2
.
Thus, if we can find an exponential sum approximation
\phi
to
f
(given by (
38
)) for which

are small, then we obtain easily computable approximations to the logarithms of the gamma function and the Barnes
G
-function in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2
. Using functional equations and reflection formulas, these approximations can then be extended to compute logarithms of
\Gamma(z)
and
G(z)
throughout the cut plane
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]
; see
[
16
]
for details.

3.4
Approximating the density of the lognormal distribution

The probability density function of a lognormal distribution is given by

g_{\sigma}(x):=\frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\ln(x)^{2}\right),\qquad x>0,

where
\sigma>0
is a parameter. Graphs of
g_{\sigma}(x)
for
\sigma\in\{0.5,1,1.5\}

A key property of this density is that all derivatives
g^{(j)}_{\sigma}(x)
vanish as
x\to 0^{+}
. In particular, our continued fraction algorithm cannot be applied directly since
g_{\sigma}(0+)=0
. We address this difficulty by a simple modification: instead of approximating
g_{\sigma}(x)

f(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}g_{\sigma}(y)\,{\textnormal{d}}y,

\phi(x)
of the form (

(43)

g_{\sigma}(x)=-f^{\prime}(x)
.

Since
f(0)=1
and
f^{\prime}(x)=-g_{\sigma}(x)

in (
4

\xi_{0}=1,\qquad\xi_{j}=0\;\;\text{for all $j\geq 1$}.

(44)

where

g_{\sigma}
. Formula (
45
) follows directly by integration by parts.

One possible application of exponential sum approximations to
H(x)
arises in Laplace transform inversion. A relevant example from probability is computing the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of a positive random variable
X
when only its Laplace transform
F_{X}(z)={\mathbb{E}}[\exp(-zX)]
is known. This situation occurs frequently, for instance, when computing the CDF of a sum of independent random variables. The CDF of
X
can be expressed as

{\mathbb{P}}(X\leq u)={\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{1}}_{\{X/u\leq 1\}}]={\mathbb{E}}[H(X/u)]\approx{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}X/u}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{j}F_{X}(\lambda_{j}/u),\qquad u\geq 0.

We leave the investigation of the efficiency of this method, as well as its comparison with other Laplace transform inversion techniques, to future work.
