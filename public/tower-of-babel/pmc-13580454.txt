Anatomy-Driven Aesthetic Design for Facial Contouring in Northeast Asian Females: A Conceptual Framework and Case Report
Authors: Sungjoo Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFacial filler contouring is commonly planned within a volume-centric framework emphasizing product selection and localized augmentation. Although widely used, such approaches may not always clearly separate material selection from aesthetic design intent or fully incorporate population-specific anatomical characteristics, particularly in Northeast Asian faces. This proof-of-concept case report proposes an anatomy-informed, design-oriented conceptual framework that prioritizes whole-face contour continuity and anatomically appropriate pathways over isolated volumetric correction. A population-contextualized Reference Facial Form is introduced as a qualitative design reference from which aesthetic intent is translated into geometric principles and subsequently into predefined vectors. In this framework, vectors are structured cannula-based trajectories defined by specific entry points, advancement directions, withdrawal pathways, and deep anatomical planes. Procedural planning is guided by predefined procedural conditions—including anatomical entry points, vector orientation, and relative filler distribution within deep planes—rather than primarily by fixed injection volumes. The framework is illustrated through a single two-stage clinical case. The initial stage used an off-label diluted polycaprolactone-based admixture to establish broad deep-plane structural support, followed by selective contour refinement with hyaluronic acid fillers after initial stabilization. Early clinical images illustrated the correspondence between the planned vectors and early contour formation but were not suitable for establishing treatment efficacy or durability. The available follow-up was also insufficient to establish long-term safety. Fifteen de-identified supplementary cases constituted a criterion-defined consecutive illustrative series demonstrating recurrent vector and entry-point patterns across similar anatomical contexts; they were not analyzed as a validation or outcome cohort. By framing facial filler contouring as a design-first process, this framework may provide a conceptual language for clinical planning and education. Its clinical efficacy, inter-operator reproducibility, generalizability, and long-term safety require evaluation in larger, systematically designed studies.Keywords: facial contouring, dermal fillers, anatomy-driven aesthetic design, vector-based planning, facial anatomy, northeast asian women

Introduction

Facial filler treatment planning commonly integrates anatomical assessment with product selection, injection technique, and volume determination. These elements are essential for safe and precise procedural execution.1,2 However, when planning is organized primarily around individual sites of deficiency, the relationship between each correction and adjacent facial regions may remain insufficiently defined. Consequently, technically appropriate augmentation of individual areas does not necessarily ensure coherent transitions across the overall facial contour.

Facial shape is determined by the layered relationships among skeletal support, retaining ligaments, soft-tissue spaces, and deep and superficial fat compartments.3–7 These structures influence tissue mobility, projection, and transitions between neighboring facial regions and provide the anatomical basis for selecting injection planes and trajectories. Population-associated differences in craniofacial morphology, soft-tissue distribution, and aesthetic treatment goals may also influence full-face planning. Studies and consensus recommendations addressing Asian and Korean faces therefore support adapting treatment planning to the patient’s individual anatomy and intended facial form rather than directly applying a single population-based template.8–13

Although facial anatomy and region-specific injection techniques have been extensively described,1–7,14 a practical gap remains between identifying anatomical structures and translating a whole-face aesthetic intention into a coordinated procedural plan. To address this gap, we propose “anatomy-driven aesthetic design”, a design-first approach that begins with a patient-specific Reference Facial Form and reverse-translates the discrepancy from baseline into coordinated intervention vectors. The framework introduces contour continuity as a qualitative planning construct for organizing individual interventions according to their relationships across the whole face.

The purpose of this proof-of-concept case report is to define this conceptual framework and illustrate its clinical translation through a representative case. Supplementary cases are included to demonstrate recurrent vector and entry-point patterns in comparable anatomical contexts rather than to serve as an efficacy or validation cohort.

Conceptual Basis of Anatomy-Driven Aesthetic Design

The proposed framework integrates aesthetic judgment and clinical anatomy under a single principle:

“Aesthetic intent defines the target; anatomy determines the pathway.”

Facial contour planning proceeds through two stages. First, the intended contour relationships are established using the qualitative Reference Facial Form described below. These relationships are then translated into patient-specific entry points, trajectories, tissue planes, and relative volume distribution according to skeletal support, retaining ligaments, soft-tissue spaces, fat compartments, and regional anatomy.3–6,14 This conversion of aesthetic intention into an anatomically appropriate procedural plan is defined here as “anatomical translation.”

In the present Northeast Asian context, this planning process is informed by reported morphological tendencies, including relatively broad transverse midfacial proportions and limited anterior projection in certain facial regions.10–13 These population-level observations provide anatomical context for planning, while the intended form remains individualized according to each patient’s baseline anatomy, proportions, and treatment goals.

The framework therefore prioritizes coordinated structural support over isolated superficial volumization and provides the conceptual basis for the vector-based strategy described in the following section.

Vector Definition and Planning

In this framework, a “vector” is defined as a predefined procedural trajectory comprising the cannula entry point, advancement direction, withdrawal pathway, injection plane, and relative filler distribution. It therefore functions as a practical planning unit through which design intent is translated into structured execution. Vector planning is guided by three core considerations:

1)Continuity over projection: Coherent transitions between adjacent facial regions are prioritized over localized projection. Where anatomically appropriate, distributed deep-plane deposition is used to provide structural continuity while limiting focal overprojection.

Continuity over projection: Coherent transitions between adjacent facial regions are prioritized over localized projection. Where anatomically appropriate, distributed deep-plane deposition is used to provide structural continuity while limiting focal overprojection.

2)Morphological transition: Translation of the Reference Facial Form requires differentiation between curvilinear and linear contour segments. Within the proposed reference, a smooth curve extends from the forehead through the temporal and zygomatic regions, whereas the inferior mandibular contour is predominantly linear and converges toward a V-shaped chin. Vector planning is adapted to these differing morphological requirements.

Main Case Presentation

A Northeast Asian woman in her early 30s presented with persistent concerns regarding imbalance in her overall facial contour. Despite previous consultations at multiple institutions, she reported that she had not received a clear explanation of how the individual contour irregularities contributed to the perceived whole-face disharmony or how they might be addressed through a coordinated treatment plan.

Multi-view clinical assessment identified contour discontinuity as the predominant finding, most apparent in the oblique and lateral views. The principal contour-disrupting features included relative volume deficiency in the submalar and anterior cheek regions, a visible palpebromalar groove, and nasolabial shadowing associated with limited malar and deep midfacial support. Together, these findings interrupted the transition from the zygomatic region through the midface to the mandibular contour. Additional findings included hollowing of the temple and lateral forehead. The chin showed mild anterior projection but lacked a clearly defined apex. Mild soft-tissue laxity and limited deep structural support were also observed clinically.

Based on these findings, a comprehensive treatment plan was formulated to restore whole-face contour continuity rather than correct each region in isolation.

Written informed consent was obtained for the procedures and for the publication of the patient’s clinical data and images.

Stage 1 focused on establishing whole-face contour continuity and deep structural support through coordinated vectors across adjacent anatomical regions. A diluted admixture was selected with the clinical intention of facilitating broad distribution within the planned deep-plane framework rather than producing focal volumization.

The admixture consisted of 3 mL of PCL-based filler, 3 mL of normal saline, 3 mL of lidocaine, and 3 mL of non-cross-linked HA. Using a 23G cannula, the admixture was distributed broadly within the deep planes of the submalar, anterior cheek, and jawline regions to establish the foundational framework.

HA filler was subsequently used in regions requiring localized support or precise contour adjustment. Specifically, 1 mL of high-viscoelasticity HA filler was injected into the chin to reinforce the apex, and 0.5 mL was injected into each paranasal (piriform) region to enhance structural support. Lip contour balance was achieved using 1 mL of HA filler delivered with a 30G needle.

The diluted PCL-based admixture represented an off-label application and was administered after specific informed consent had been obtained.

In this case, material selection and treatment staging reflected case-specific clinical judgment rather than a required sequence within the proposed framework. The diluted PCL-based admixture was selected to provide broadly distributed deep-plane support in the setting of diffuse contour insufficiency, whereas HA was used for localized support, projection, and subsequent refinement. The framework could also have been implemented using HA alone. Material selection therefore remains adaptable, whereas the underlying design logic—based on intended contour relationships, anatomically appropriate pathways, and coordinated vectors—remains consistent.

General Principles and Entry Point Strategy

In the main case, a 23G cannula was used to restore disrupted contours through continuous anatomical pathways rather than to augment isolated defects. Entry points were selected according to the intended vector direction, regional anatomy, accessible deep planes, cannula reach, and planned injectate distribution. They were generally positioned near the lateral boundaries of the contour-discontinuity sites described in Recurrent Contour-Discontinuity Patterns in Northeast Asian Faces.

The injectate was distributed along predefined deep-plane vectors to reconnect adjacent regions while limiting focal overprojection. Relative volume distribution was adjusted according to the extent and anatomical characteristics of each contour discontinuity rather than determined by predetermined absolute replacement volumes.

Procedural Integration

These predefined vectors, anatomical planes, and relative volume distributions represent the clinical translation of the design principles described in Design Logic and Vector Definition. Although their regional application was adjusted according to the patient’s anatomy and the extent of each contour discontinuity, the underlying planning sequence remained consistent: identification of the intended contour relationship, selection of an anatomically appropriate entry point and plane, and distribution along the corresponding vector.

Supplementary Illustrative Series

The 15 supplementary cases illustrate recurrent regional vector and entry-point patterns applied in comparable anatomical contexts (Supplementary Figures 1–5). The series comprised all patients aged 20–50 years who underwent facial contouring filler treatment at the study clinic between April and November 2025. Cases were not selected according to treatment outcome.

The restricted age range was intended to illustrate structural contour enhancement with less potential confounding from advanced age-related volume loss or soft-tissue laxity. The series was included solely for procedural illustration and was not analyzed as an efficacy, outcome, or validation cohort.

Procedural Considerations and Peri-Procedural Management

Pre-procedural evaluation included a review of anticoagulant use and previous filler treatments. Procedures were performed under aseptic conditions with topical or local anesthesia. A 23G cannula was used for vector-based injections, whereas a 30G needle was used for lip contouring. Patients were positioned semi-upright or supine according to the treatment region, with repeated multi-view assessment performed during the procedure.

Outcome

Visual review of the available frontal and oblique photographs showed smoother transitions between adjacent facial regions without obvious focal overprojection (Figures 2 and 3). However, differences in lighting and head position, together with the absence of matched lateral pre-treatment photographs, precluded objective comparison. The images therefore illustrate the correspondence between the planned vectors and the observed contour formation rather than establish treatment efficacy.

No adverse events were observed during the 4-month interval between Stage 1 and Stage 2. Following Stage 2, mild bruising was noted at the local anesthesia sites, but follow-up communication 4 hours after the procedure identified no concerning symptoms. No delayed adverse events following Stage 2 had been reported by the time of manuscript preparation. Nevertheless, the available follow-up was insufficient to assess late-onset complications associated with PCL-based filler.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept report presents facial filler contouring as a design-first process in which a patient-specific whole-face form is envisioned before the injection plan is determined. The discrepancy between the existing and intended forms is then translated into coordinated regional vectors, anatomically appropriate pathways, and relative filler distribution. The conceptual contribution of the framework lies in formalizing this planning sequence rather than proposing a new filler product, injection technique, or anatomical plane.

The framework shares with the MD Codes™ and broader consensus approaches an emphasis on structured, anatomy-based, full-face assessment.1,2,16 The principal distinction lies in the organizational starting point. Established approaches, including the MD Codes™, structure treatment through standardized anatomical units and treatment codes selected according to individual facial attributes.1 The present framework instead begins with an intended patient-specific whole-face form and translates the relationships within that form into a coordinated procedural plan. It is therefore proposed as a complementary planning perspective rather than as a replacement for established methodologies.

Regional anatomy—including tissue layers, vascular territories, and established injection planes—determines the anatomically appropriate pathways through which the intended contour may be created.3–6,14 The framework therefore operates within established anatomical safety principles while allowing entry points, trajectories, planes, and relative filler distribution to be adapted to the individual patient.6 In this context, continuity refers to the external three-dimensional contour across adjacent facial regions, rather than to filler placement within a single uninterrupted anatomical plane.

The Reference Facial Form serves as a qualitative working reference that is adapted to the patient’s baseline anatomy, facial proportions, and treatment goals. In the present Northeast Asian context, the zygomatic, mandibular, and chin regions were considered interconnected components of whole-face contour continuity, informed by relevant anatomical, anthropometric, and Asian aesthetic literature.3–6,8–13 This population-specific context supports individualized anatomical translation rather than prescribing a uniform ethnic aesthetic ideal.8,9 The framework may be particularly applicable when contour imbalance extends across multiple adjacent regions and may not be fully represented by a single localized correction.

Considerations Regarding Off-Label Material Use

In the main case, the diluted PCL-based admixture was used off-label during Stage 1 to provide broadly distributed structural support across anatomically appropriate deep planes, followed by selective refinement with HA. Dilution was intended to reduce injection resistance and facilitate broader distribution. However, the rheological and biological properties of the resulting admixture were not experimentally characterized. Its elastic modulus, viscosity, cohesivity, injectability, tissue interactions, and longer-term material behavior therefore remain uncertain.17–19

Distributed deep-plane deposition was used with the intention of limiting focal material accumulation. However, whether this distribution pattern reduces the risk of nodule formation or other PCL-related complications has not been established.20 The absence of adverse events in this single case cannot establish the safety, efficacy, or reproducibility of the admixture, and the available 4-month follow-up was insufficient to exclude late-onset complications.20,21

This material sequence represents a case-specific implementation of the proposed design logic rather than a standardized formulation or a required component of the framework. The same planning principles could be implemented using HA fillers alone or other appropriately selected materials. Accordingly, this case should not be interpreted as a general recommendation for the described admixture. Any off-label application requires careful patient selection, material-specific clinical judgment, explicit informed consent, and appropriate long-term follow-up.

Limitations

The primary clinical evidence in this proof-of-concept report was derived from a single case treated by a single operator, without a comparator or standardized objective outcome measures. The 15 supplementary cases were included solely to illustrate recurrent vector and entry-point patterns and were not analyzed as an efficacy or validation cohort. These features limit conclusions regarding clinical effectiveness, generalizability, reproducibility, and comparative safety.

The Reference Facial Form was not operationalized using numerical landmarks, ratios, or treatment thresholds, and its construct validity and interobserver reliability were not evaluated. The framework should therefore be understood as a qualitative conceptual model rather than a validated or prescriptive treatment protocol.

Clinical photography was not fully standardized, matched lateral pretreatment views were unavailable, and neither morphometric assessment nor validated patient-reported outcomes were used. The available images therefore illustrate early contour formation but do not establish effectiveness, durability, or long-term stability.

Follow-up was also insufficient to assess late-onset complications associated with the off-label diluted PCL-based admixture, whose safety and long-term behavior require separate investigation. Future studies should evaluate the framework using standardized multi-view imaging, predefined morphometric measures, and validated instruments such as the FACE-Q.22

Conclusion

Facial filler contouring may be approached as an anatomy-informed design process that translates an intended patient-specific whole-face form into coordinated vectors, anatomical planes, and relative filler distribution. This proof-of-concept case illustrates a qualitative framework centered on contour continuity rather than isolated volumetric correction. Further prospective studies are required to evaluate its clinical utility, reproducibility, and generalizability.
