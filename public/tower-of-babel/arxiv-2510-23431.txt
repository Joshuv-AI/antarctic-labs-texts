A Newton-Kantorovich Inverse Function Theorem in Quasi-Metric Spaces
Titus Pinta

Abstract
The purpose of this work is to investigate root finding problems defined on (quasi-)metric spaces, and ranging in Euclidean spaces. The motivation for this line of inquiry stems from recent models in biology and phylogenetics, where problems of great practical significance are cast as optimization problems on (quasi-)metric spaces. We investigate a minimal algebraic setup that allows us to study a notion of differentiability suitable for Newton-type methods, called Newton differentiability. This notion of differentiability benefits from calculus rules and is sufficient to prove superlinear convergence of a Newton-type method. Finally, a Newton-Kantorovich-type theorem provides an inverse function result, applicable on (quasi-)metric spaces.



Keywords:
Newton-type Methods, Quasi-Metric Spaces, Newton-Kantorovich
Theorem

Introduction

In the last couple of decades, metric spaces have found themselves at the
forefront of optimization and analysis research. A wide range of problems with
important applications in physics, chemistry, and biology require the generality
of metric spaces as a basis for their formulation. In
[
20
]
,
Lueg et al. provided a geometrical interpretation to the space of phylogenetic
trees, allowing for the problem of interpolating between two such trees,
constructed on different genetic data, to be formulated as a Fréchet mean problem
on a metric space. The space of protein structures has been given a
(pseudo-)metric structure in
[
27

, and protein optimal design
problems can be formulated with respect to this structure. This work fits
into the broader topic of knot theory, where metric-geometric aspects have
blended successfully with optimization notions. In the study of our universe,
computational methods for solving shape optimization problems turned out to be necessary. Such
optimization problems for subsets of Finsler manifolds have been solved
in
[
6

and are natural examples of optimization on metric
spaces.

Another key example is provided by problems defined on trees and graphs, as
presented in the recent compendium
[
12
]
.
In this area, ad-hoc combinatorial algorithms and heuristics have long represented
the state of the art. Integer programming is another area with significant
algorithmic development. The recent work on understanding the metric structure
of trees, latices, and graphs can help develop continuous optimization inspired
algorithms. Cyclic projections in Hadamard spaces have been studied
in
[
22
]
. Lauster et al. developed a fixed point theory for
nonexpansive operators and proximal splitting algorithms in spaces with bounded
curvature in
[

and
[

. This fixed
point approach originates in the work of De Giorgi on minimizing
movements in metric spaces (now called proximity operators in the optimization community), adapted in
[
2

.

The interest in quasi-metric spaces, as opposed to just metric spaces, comes
rather naturally from the fact that all the properties employed in the
analysis of nonlinear optimization algorithms are also satisfied by these
more abstract spaces, thus allowing for more general results. The recent
work
[
8
]
extends the construction of free Lipschitz
spaces to quasi-metric spaces, thus showing that such spaces are fruitful
ground for theoretical analysis results.

On the algorithmic side, Definition

from our work develops a Newton-type, superlinearly
convergent algorithm for root finding problems on quasi-metric spaces.
Quasi-metric spaces do not provide a canonical notion of invertible linear maps,

Riemannian optimization, masterfully presented in the monograph of
Absil

technique giving rise to superlinear convergence of Newton-type methods
consists of fixed point iterations of operators derived from Newton

In parallel with this algorithmic and fixed point theoretical development, the
basis for an analytical framework in metric spaces has been laid down. A

The existence of solutions to optimization problems in metric spaces has

Calculus rules are vital for algebraic manipulation of the objects involved

1.1
Definitions and Basic Properties

Definition 1.1
.

A space
\mathbb{M}
together with a mapping
\dist:\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}\to[0,\infty\char 41\relax
is called
a
quasi metric space
if the following properties hold:

QMS1:

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{M},x\neq y,\quad\dist(x,y)>0,

QMS2:

\forall x\in\mathbb{M},\quad\dist(x,x)=0,

QMS3:

\forall x,y,z\in\mathbb{M},\quad\dist(x,z)\leq\dist(x,y)+\dist(y,z),

We denote the balls in a quasi-metric space by

B_{r}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{M}~|~\dist(y,x)<r\}

and

B_{r}[x]=\{y\in\mathbb{M}~|~\dist(y,x)\leq r\}.

Remark 1.2
.

The difference between metric spaces and quasi-metric spaces lies in the lack
of symmetry for the distance function. As such, any metric space is also a
quasi-metric space.

Definition 1.3

(Distance between Points and Subsets)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}
be a quasi-metric space and
A\subseteq\mathbb{M}
be a subset. The
distance between
a point
x\in\mathbb{M}
and the subset
A
is defined by

\dist(x,A)=\inf_{a\in A}\dist(x,a),

or

\dist(A,x)=\inf_{a\in A}\dist(a,x).

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{M}
. The
distance between
A

and
B
is defined by

\dist(A,B)=\max\{\sup_{a\in A}\dist(a,B),\sup_{b\in B}\dist(A,b)\}.

For the remaining of this work we will consider the topology generated by
the open balls in
\mathbb{M}
. The behavior of such topological spaces has been studied
in
[
17
]
. The topology induces a notion of convergence for sequences.

Definition 1.4
.

A sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{M}
is called
convergent to
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{M}
if

\lim_{k\to\infty}\dist(x^{k},\bar{x})=0.

In order for this work to remain self contained, we recall
the following definition of completeness.

Definition 1.5
.

A sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{M}
is called
Cauchy
if for every

\varepsilon>0
there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
m>N
and
n>N
,

\dist(x^{n},x^{m})<\varepsilon
.
A quasi-metric space
(\mathbb{M},\dist)
is called
complete
if every Cauchy
sequence with elements in
\mathbb{M}
is convergent.

The properties of balls in quasi-metric spaces yield an important topological
property that will be needed in the proof of the Banach Fixed Point Theorem.

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{M}
and
{\{r_{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real numbers. If

\lim_{k\to\infty}r_{k}=0
, then

\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}B_{r_{k}}[\bar{x}]=\{\bar{x}\}.

Proof.

Clearly,
\bar{x}\in B_{r_{k}}[\bar{x}]
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, so

\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}B_{r_{k}}[\bar{x}]\supseteq\{\bar{x}\}.

Next, assume
y\in\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}B_{r_{k}}[\bar{x}]
with
y\neq\bar{x}
, so there exists
\varepsilon>0

with
\dist(y,\bar{x})>\varepsilon
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
r_{k}<\varepsilon
for all
k\geq N
. This
shows that
y\not\in B_{r_{k}}[\bar{x}]
for any
k\geq N
, thus contradicting
the assumption. It follows that the considered intersection is a singleton,
completing the proof.
∎

With quasi-metric spaces as a topological background, we can define the fixed
point iterations of set-valued operators and describe their behavior.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
T:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{M}
, then a point
\bar{x}\in U
is called a
fixed point
if

\bar{x}\in T(\bar{x})
.

The speed of the convergence to a limit point can be quantified, based on the
following definitions.

Definition 1.8

(Convergence Rates)
.

Let
(\mathbb{M},\dist)
be a quasi-metric space.
A convergent sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{M}
with
\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}=\bar{x}
is
called
linearly convergent
if there exists
c<1
such that

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})\leq c\dist(x^{k},\bar{x}).

A convergent sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}=\bar{x}
is called

superlinearly convergent
if there exists a sequence

{\{c^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})\leq c^{k}\dist(x^{k},\bar{x})

with
\lim_{k\to\infty}c^{k}=0
.
A convergent sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}=\bar{x}
is called

convergent with rate
\gamma>1
if there exists
c<1
such that

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})\leq c{\dist(x^{k},\bar{x})}^{\gamma}.

A convergent sequence with rate 2 is called
quadratically convergent
.
A convergent sequence
{x^{k}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}=\bar{x}
is called

convergent with super-rate
\gamma>1
if there exists a sequence

{\{c^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})\leq c^{k}{\dist(x^{k},\bar{x})}^{\gamma}

with
\lim_{k\to\infty}c^{k}=0
.

In order to develop a system capable of analyzing the behavior of fixed point
operators, we need to recall multiple definitions relating to the smoothness
properties of operators.

Definition 1.9
.

A set value mapping
T:U\subset\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{M}
is called
proper
on
U
if

\nexists x\in U
such that
F(x)=\emptyset
.

Definition 1.10
.

A mapping
T:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{M}
is called

1.

Lipschitz continuous on
U

if there exists a constant
L\geq 0
such that

\forall x,\bar{x}\in U,y\in T(x),\bar{y}\in T(\bar{x}),\quad\dist(y,\bar{y})\leq L\dist(x,\bar{x}),

2.

contraction on
U
if
there exists a constant
c<1
such that

\forall x,\bar{x}\in U,y\in T(x),\bar{y}\in T(\bar{x}),\quad\dist(y,\bar{y})\leq c\dist(x,\bar{x}),

3.

Hölder continuous on
U
if
there exist constants
L\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
such that

\forall x,\bar{x}\in U,y\in T(x),\bar{y}\in T(\bar{x}),\quad\dist(y,\bar{y})\leq L{\dist(x,\bar{x})}^{\alpha}.

These definitions have weaker, pointwise analogues that provide an appropriate
environment for the convergence analysis presented in most of this work.

Definition 1.11
.

A mapping
T:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{M}
is called

1.

pointwise Lipschitz continuous at
\bar{x}

if there exists a constant
L\geq 0
such that

\forall x\in U,y\in T(x),\bar{y}\in T(\bar{x}),\quad\dist(y,\bar{y})\leq L\dist(x,\bar{x}),

2.

quasi-contraction at
\bar{x}
if
there exists a constant
c<1
such that

\forall x\in U,y\in T(x),\bar{y}\in T(\bar{x}),\quad\dist(y,\bar{y})\leq c\dist(x,\bar{x}),

3.

pointwise Hölder continuous on
\bar{x}
if
there exist constants
L\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
such that

\forall x\in U,y\in T(x),\bar{y}\in T(\bar{x}),\quad\dist(y,\bar{y})\leq L{\dist(x,\bar{x})}^{\alpha}.

Remark 1.12
.

A mapping is a (quasi-)contraction at
\bar{x}
if and only if it is
(pointwise) Lipschitz continuous at
\bar{x}
with constant
L<1
.

In the study of set-valued mappings it suffices to consider the existence of

Definition 1.13
.

A set-valued mapping
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{M}
has a

1.

, if there exists a (pointwise)

f:U\to\mathcal{M}
with

for all

2.

(pointwise) Hölder selection

f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
f(x)\in F(x)
for all

x\in U
,

3.

(quasi-)contractive selection
, if there exists a
(quasi-)contractive mapping
f:U\to\mathcal{M}
with
f(x)\in F(x)
for all

x\in U
.

Lemma 1.14
.

Let
T:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{M}
be pointwise Lipschitz at
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{M}
, then it is single-valued
at
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{y}_{1},\bar{y}_{2}\in T(\bar{x})
, so

\dist(\bar{y}_{1},\bar{y}_{2})\leq L\dist(\bar{x},\bar{x})\leq 0.

∎

Example 1.15
.

The mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by

F(x,y)=\{(x,0),(y,0)\}

is pointwise Lipschitz continuous at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=(0,0)
, as can be seen from

\|(x,0)-(0,0)\|=x\leq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=\|(x,y)-(0,0)\|

and

\|(y,0)-(0,0)\|=y\leq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=\|(x,y)-(0,0)\|.

We can see that at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=(0,0)
the mapping is single-valued. It is not
single-valued at any other point and as such it is only pointwise Lipschitz
continuous at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=(0,0)
.

The fundamental result of the Banach Contraction Mapping Principle has been
successfully extended to set-valued mappings in
[
24
]
, while the
proof in the setting of quasi-metric spaces has been developed
in
[
28
]
. We provide here a version of this proof adapted to
work for set-valued quasi-contractions on quasi-metric spaces.

Theorem 1.16

(Banach Fixed Point)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}
be a quasi-metric space and
T:V\subseteq\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{M}
be a quasi-contraction
at
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{M}
with
T(\bar{x})=\bar{x}
, then there exists a neighborhood
O
of
\bar{x}
with

U:=O\cap V
such that
T:U\rightrightarrows U
, and any sequence

{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
x^{0}\in U
and
x^{k+1}\in T(x^{k})
converges at least
linearly to the unique fixed point
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

First, we will show that
\bar{x}
is the unique fixed point. Consider
\bar{y}\in\mathbb{M}
a
fixed point, i.e.
T(\bar{y})=\{\bar{y}\}
, and from quasi-contractivity

\dist(\bar{y},\bar{x})\leq c\dist(\bar{y},\bar{x}).

Since
c\in(0,1)
, we can conclude that
\dist(\bar{y},\bar{x})=0
and thus
\bar{x}=\bar{y}
.

The mapping
F
sends balls around
\bar{x}
into themselves because for
x\in\mathbb{M}
with

\dist(x,\bar{x})<r
, quasi-contractivity implies
\sup_{y\in F(x)}\dist(y,\bar{x})<cr
.
Let
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence generated by
x^{k+1}\in F(x^{k})
with

x^{0}\in U
and let
r=\dist(x^{0},\bar{x})
. We can conclude that

x_{k}\in B_{{c^{k}}r}(\bar{x}),

so
\dist(x^{k},\bar{x})\leq c^{k}r
, and the sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is convergent, and

\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}\in\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}B_{{c^{k}}r}(\bar{x})=\{\bar{x}\},

where we used Lemma
1.6

to compute the intersection of the balls.
∎

Remark 1.17
.

When applied to a quasi-contraction, as opposed to a contraction, the
Banach fixed point Theorem cannot guarantee the existence of a fixed point
and because of this, Theorem
1.16
requires the existence of a fixed
point as an assumption. This allows a weakening of the topological
assumptions, namely that we do not require completeness of the space.

2
Algebraic Constructs

The invertible linear mappings that are normally used in the development of
Newton’s method turn out to be more rigid than actually required for
obtaining superlinear convergence rates. Two insights provide the suitable
definition for pseudo-linear maps on quasi-metric spaces. The first insight is
that the only linearity property that is actually employed is the fact that
linear transformations map 0 to 0. The second insight is that we can identify
two points with their difference vector, and as such we can consider linear
mappings equivalently as acting on pairs, or differences, of points. These
observations lead to the following definition of linear mappings on quasi-metric
spaces. For the remaining of this chapter,
(\mathbb{M},\dist)
will be a quasi-metric
space.

Definition 2.1

(Pseudo-Linear Maps)
.

A mapping
H:\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
pseudo-linear
if
\forall x\in\mathbb{M}
,

H(x,x)=0
. We denote the space of such mappings with
S_{n}(\mathbb{M})
.

Likewise, the full range of properties that are associated with inverses of
linear mappings in linear spaces is not required. For Newton-type methods, all
we require is the following notion.

Definition 2.2

(Inversely Compatible Maps)
.

A pseudo-linear mapping
H:\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
inversely compatible

if there exists
H^{-}:\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{M}
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall x,\in\mathbb{M},\quad H^{-}(x,0)=x,

and

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{M},\forall v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\dist(H^{-}(x,v),H^{-}(y,w))\leq m\|v-w-H(x,y)\|.

(1)

The mapping
H^{-}
is called a
quasi inverse
of
H
. We denote

\||H^{-}\||:=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}~|~\mbox{\eqref{eq:def-geodesical-subadditive} holds}\}.

(2)

The set of all such mappings is denoted by
GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})

These notions simplify back to known objects in the case of Euclidean spaces,
by interpreting pseudo-linear mappings as linear maps acting on the difference
vector of two points. Similarly, on Riemannian Manifolds, pseudo-linear maps
act on the vector produced by the Riemannian logarithm of two points. The
next example clarifies both how these notions map back to Euclidean spaces and
where the inspiration for them comes from.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean metric and let
T\in\mathbb{R}^{n{\times}n}
. Then the mapping

H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

H(x,y)=T(y-x)

is pseudo-linear, because clearly

H(x,x)=T(x-x)=0.

Furthermore, if
T
is invertible, then
H
is inversely compatible, with

H^{-}(x,v)=x+T^{-1}v.

This can be seen because

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\dist(H^{-}(x,v),H^{-}(y,w))

\displaystyle=\|H^{-}(y,w)-H^{-}(x,v)\|

\displaystyle=\|y+T^{-1}w-x-T^{-1}v\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T^{-1}\|\|T(y-x)+w-v\|

\displaystyle=\|T^{-1}\|\|v-w-T(y-x)\|.

(3)

Remark 2.4
.

The computation in (
2.3
) justifies the
notation
\||H^{-}\||
from (
2
) because in
the Euclidean metric, considering the pseudo-linear mapping induced by
a linear mapping
T
,
\||H^{-}\||=\|T^{-1}\|
holds.

It is clear that the space
S_{n}(\mathbb{M})
inherits the algebraic structure of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
so for any
H_{1},H_{2}\in S_{n}(\mathbb{M})
, there exists
H_{1}+H_{2}\in S_{n}(\mathbb{M})
and

\langle H_{1},H_{2}\rangle\in S_{1}(\mathbb{M})
defined by
(H_{1}+H_{2})(x,y)=H_{1}(x,y)+H_{2}(x,y)

and
\langle H_{1},H_{2}\rangle(x,y)=\langle H_{1}(x,y),H_{2}(x,y)\rangle
respectively. Similarly,
for
H_{3},H_{4}\in S_{1}(\mathbb{M})
, there exist
H_{3}\cdot H_{4}\in S_{1}(\mathbb{M})
and
H_{3}\cdot H_{1}\in S_{n}(\mathbb{M})

defined by
(H_{3}\cdot H_{4})(x,y)=H_{3}(x,y)H_{4}(x,y)
and

(H_{3}\cdot H_{1})(x,y)=H_{3}(x,y)H_{1}(x,y)
respectively. Finally, the mapping

H_{1}\oplus H_{2}\in S_{2n}(\mathbb{M})
is defined by
(H_{1}\oplus H_{2})(x,y)=H_{1}(x,y)\oplus H_{2}(x,y)
.

3
Newton Differentiability

The work presented in
[
26
]
has introduced Newton’s method for
semismooth functions. The defining property that yields superlinear
convergence can be adapted to functions defined on quasi-metric spaces, using
the previously developed algebraic notions.

Definition 3.1

(Pointwise Newton Differentiability in Quasi-Metric Spaces)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}
be a quasi-metric space. A function
F:\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called

weakly pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}
if there exists a set
valued mapping
\mathcal{H}F:\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows S_{n}(\mathbb{M})
such that

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}<\infty.

(4)

Furthermore, if

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}=0,

(5)

the function
F
is called
pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}
.

When studying Newton differentiability at all the points in a set, we can look
at a stronger smoothness condition, namely that of uniform Newton
differentiability.

Definition 3.2

(Uniform Newton differentiability in Quasi-Metric Spaces)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}
be a quasi-metric space. A function
F:\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called

weakly uniformly Newton differentiable on
V\subseteq\mathbb{M}
if there exists a set
valued mapping
\mathcal{H}F:\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows S_{n}(\mathbb{M})
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
there
exists a
\delta>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{M}
and all
y\in V
with
\dist(x,y)\leq\delta
,

\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(y)-H(x,y)\|}{\dist(x,y)}\in(c+\varepsilon,c-\varepsilon).

(6)

Furthermore, when for every
\varepsilon>0
there
exists a
\delta>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{M}
and all
y\in V
with
\dist(x,y)\leq\delta
,

\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(y)-H(x,y)\|}{\dist(x,y)}<\varepsilon,

(7)

the function
F
is called
uniformly Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}
.

Remark 3.3
.

The equations (
6
)
and (
7
) imply the
convergence of the limits in equations (
4
)
and (
5
) respectively.
Even more, the convergence is uniform in
y
.

Remark 3.4

(Subsets of Newton Differential)
.

It is useful to remark that subsets of a Newton differential are still
Newton differentials. This fact follows clearly by remarking that
if the supremum is taken over a smaller set, its value cannot increase.
As such, the Newton differential of a function is not a unique object.

In order to better understand these notions, we can consider the case of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the standard Euclidean metric. In this setting, all
sufficiently smooth functions are Newton differentiable, with Newton
differentials constructed from the traditional Fréchet differentials.

Proposition 3.5

(Fréchet Differentiability and Newton Differentiability)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
. Then
F
is pointwise Newton differentiable
at any
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with Newton differential
\mathcal{H}F(x)=\{(y,z)->\nabla F(x)^{T}(z-y)\}
.

Proof.

Using Taylor’s expansion for
F
around
\bar{x}
, we see that there exists

h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}h(x)=0
and

F(x)=F(\bar{x})+(\nabla F(\bar{x})^{T}-\nabla F(x)^{T}+\nabla F(x)^{T})(x-\bar{x})+h(x)\|x-\bar{x}\|.

Clearly rearranging, dividing by
\|x-\bar{x}\|
and taking the limit as
x\to\bar{x}

while using the continuity of
\nabla F
at
\bar{x}
shows the desired conclusion.
∎

3.1
Calculus of Newton Differentiability

As Newton differentiability is defined in a manner analogues to Fréchet
differentiability, similar techniques can be used in order to construct
calculus rules for Newton differentiable functions.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
G:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}\in U

with Newton differentials
\mathcal{H}F
and
\mathcal{H}G
. Then
F+G
is Newton
differentiable at
\bar{x}
with Newton differential

x\mapsto\{H_{F}+H_{G}~|~H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x),H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)\}
.

Proof.

From the assumptions, we know that

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H_{F}(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}=0

(8)

and

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|G(x)-G(\bar{x})-H_{G}(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}=0.

(9)

The triangle inequality together with the sub-additivity of the supremum
operator provides the next step in the proof, yielding

\displaystyle\sup_{H_{F}+H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}F(x)+\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|(F+G)(x)-(F+G)(\bar{x})-(H_{F}+H_{G})(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{H_{F}+H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}F(x)+\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H_{F}(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}+\frac{\|G(x)-G(\bar{x})-H_{G}(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H_{F}(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}+\sup_{H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|G(x)-G(\bar{x})-H_{G}(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})},

and taking the limit as
x\to\bar{x}
using (
8
)
and (
9
) finishes the proof.
∎

For the chain rule, we consider
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the standard Euclidean
distance.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
G:F(U)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}

and
F(\bar{x})
respectively with Newton differentials
\mathcal{H}F
and
\mathcal{H}G
. Assume
further that
F
is continuous at
\bar{x}
and that there exists
K>0
such that

\sup_{x\in U}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\sup_{y,z\in U}\|H(x)(y,z)\|\leq K\dist(y,z).

(10)

Then
G\circ F
is Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}
with Newton differential

\mathcal{H}(G\circ F)(x)=\{(y,z)\mapsto H_{G}(F(y),F(z))~|~H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(F(x))\}.

Proof.

We first need to establish a bound on

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}.

From the Newton differentiability of
F
we can conclude that there exists a
neighborhood
V
of
\bar{x}
such that for all
x\in V
,
x\neq\bar{x}

\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}\leq 1.

(11)

With (
11
) and (
10
),
we can use the triangle inequality for the norm to bound

\displaystyle\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})+H(x,\bar{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|+\|H(x,\bar{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\dist(x,\bar{x})+K\dist(x,\bar{x}).

(12)

We can now focus our attention on the key object for the proof at hand and
compute, using (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}

\displaystyle\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}(F\circ G)(x)}\frac{\|G\circ F(x)-G\circ F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}(F\circ G)(x)}\frac{\|G\circ F(x)-G\circ F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}(F\circ G)(x)}(K+1)\frac{\|G\circ F(x)-G\circ F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|}.

(13)

The last step consists in using the continuity of
F
at
\bar{x}
and the
Newton differentiability of
G
at
F(\bar{x})
to calculate

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}G(F(x))}\frac{\|G\circ F(x)-G\circ F(\bar{x})-H_{G}(F(x),F(\bar{x}))\|}{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})\|}=0.

(14)

Combining (
14
)
with (
3.1
) proves the conclusion.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

It is interesting to see how this chain rule behaves in the context of
Example
2.3
. For this, consider
F,G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

of class
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
. From proposition
3.5
, the Newton
differential of
G
at
F(x)
is
(y,z)\mapsto\nabla G(F(x))(z-y)
. Assuming
that
\|\nabla F(x)\|
is bounded on a neighborhood of
\bar{x}
we can compute the
Newton differential of
\mathcal{H}(G\circ F)
as the singleton

\mathcal{H}(G\circ F)(x)=\{(y,z)\mapsto\nabla G(F(x))^{T}(F(z)-F(y))\}
. Looking at the key
object in (
5
) and using the mean value
theorem allows us to relate the chain rule for Fréchet differentiability
with that of Newton differentiability by computing

\|\mathcal{H}(G\circ F)(x)(x,\bar{x})\|=\|\nabla G(F(x))^{T}(F(\bar{x})-F(x))\|\leq\|\nabla G(F(x))^{T}\|\nabla F(\xi)^{T}\|\|\bar{x}-x\|,

where
\xi
is a point in the line segment between
x
and
\bar{x}
.

In order to complete the fundamental calculus rules, we need to describe
the behavior of the direct sum of two Newton differentiable functions.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
G:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}

with Newton differentials
\mathcal{H}F
and
\mathcal{H}G
. Then
F\oplus G:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is Newton
differentiable at
\bar{x}
with Newton differential

x\mapsto\{H_{F}\oplus H_{G}~|~H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x),H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)\}
.

Proof.

The proof simply follows from the fact that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\|x\oplus y\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}.

Applying this to the defining property of Newton differentiability,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}

\displaystyle\sup_{(H_{F}\oplus H_{G})\in\mathcal{H}(F\oplus G)(x)}\frac{\|(F\oplus G)(x)-(F\oplus G)(\bar{x})-(H_{F}\oplus H_{G})(x,\bar{x})\|^{2}}{{\dist(x,\bar{x})}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H_{F}(x,\bar{x})\|^{2}}{{\dist(x,\bar{x})}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|G(x)-G(\bar{x})-H_{G}(x,\bar{x})\|^{2}}{{\dist(x,\bar{x})}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

where we have used the Newton differentiability of
F
and
G
to compute the
two limits.
∎

As the last step necessary in order to provide complete calculus rules,
we will prove the product rule for Newton differentiable functions.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}
and
G:U\to\mathbb{R}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}

with Newton differentials
\mathcal{H}_{F}
and
\mathcal{H}_{G}
. Then
F\cdot G
is Newton
differentiable at
\bar{x}
with Newton differential

\mathcal{H}(F\cdot G)(x)=\{(y,z)\mapsto H_{F}(y,z)G(x)+F(x)H_{G}(y,z)~|~H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x),H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\}.

Proof.

Using a similar argument as in Proposition
3.6
, we can
directly compute

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}

\displaystyle\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}(F\cdot G)(x)}\frac{\|(F\cdot G)(x)-(F\cdot G)(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x),H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|F(x)G(x)-F(\bar{x})G(\bar{x})-H_{F}(x,\bar{x})G(x)-F(x)H_{G}(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x),H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|F(x)(G(x)-G(\bar{x})-H_{G}(x,\bar{x}))-(F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H_{F}(x,\bar{x})G(x))G(\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H_{G}\in\mathcal{H}G(x)}\frac{\|F(x)(G(x)-G(\bar{x})-H_{G}(x,\bar{x}))\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}+\sup_{H_{F}\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|(F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H_{F}(x,\bar{x}))G(\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}

\displaystyle=0,

where the last two limits are zero because of the Newton differentiability of

F
and
G
.
∎

4
Newton-type Methods

The class of Newton differentiable functions provides a large pool of candidates
for Newton-type methods. Under the assumption that a Newton differential of
the function
F
contains
inversely compatible
pseudo-linear maps,
a Newton-type fixed point operator can be defined.
As explained in Example
2.3
, the inverse of
a pseudo-linear map acts as a translation on points in
\mathbb{M}
. This motivates
the adaptation of the classic Newton’s method in our setting.

Definition 4.1

(Newton-type Method in Quasi-metric Spaces)
.

The fixed point iteration of the proper (nowhere empty) set-valued operator

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}F}:M\rightrightarrows M
,

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}F}x=\{{H}^{-}(x,-F(x))~|~H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\cap GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})\},

x^{k+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}F}{x}^{k}

(15)

is called a
Newton-type method
.

In order to analyze the convergence rate of this method, we first need to
establish convergence to a fixed point. For this, we employ the Banach Fixed
Point Theorem.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise weakly Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}

with
F(\bar{x})=0
.
Denote the Newton differential of
F
by
\mathcal{H}F
, and assume that
\forall x\in U
all

H\in\mathcal{H}(x)
are inversely compatible mappings, that is
\mathcal{H}(x)\subseteq GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
.
Furthermore, assume that the set
\bigcup_{x\in U}\{\||{{H}^{-}}\||~|~H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\}

is bounded by
\Omega>0
and let
c>0
be the limit
in (
4
), i.e.

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}=c,

be such that
c\Omega<1
. Then there exists a neighborhood
\bar{x}\in V\subseteq\mathbb{M}
such
that the mapping
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}F}
is a quasi-contraction on
V
.

Proof.

From the Archimedean principle, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

(c+\varepsilon)\Omega<1
. Based on (
4
), there
exists a neighborhood
\bar{x}\in V\subseteq U
such that

\forall x\in V,\quad\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x)(x,\bar{x})\|\leq(c+\varepsilon)\dist(x,\bar{x}).

(16)

Let
y\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}F}(x)
such that
y=H^{-}(x,-F(x))
with
H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)
. Then

\displaystyle\dist(y,\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\dist({H}^{-}(\bar{x},-F(x)),\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\dist({H}^{-}(\bar{x},-F(x)),{H}^{-}(\bar{x},0))

\displaystyle=\dist({H}^{-}(\bar{x},-F(x)),{H}^{-}(\bar{x},-F(\bar{x}))).

Here we use the property from (
1
)
and then (
16
) to
yield

\displaystyle\dist(y,\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\dist({H}^{-}(\bar{x},-F(x)),{H}^{-}(\bar{x},-F(\bar{x})))

\displaystyle\leq\||{H}^{-}\||\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x)(x,\bar{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq(c+\varepsilon)\||{H}^{-}\||\dist(x,\bar{x})

\displaystyle\leq(c+\varepsilon)\||{H}^{-}\||\dist(x,\bar{x})

\displaystyle\leq(c+\varepsilon)\Omega\dist(x,\bar{x}),

with
(c+\varepsilon)\Omega<1
, showing the conclusion that the mapping is a
quasi-contraction on
V
.
∎

Because of the Banach Fixed Point Theorem, quasi-contractivity is sufficient
to guarantee linear convergence of the iterates.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise weakly Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{M}
with

F(\bar{x})=0
.
Denote the Newton differential of
F
by
\mathcal{H}F
and assume that
\forall x\in U
all

H\in\mathcal{H}(x)
are inversely compatible mappings, that is
\mathcal{H}(x)\subseteq GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
.
Furthermore, assume that the set
\bigcup_{x\in U}\{\||{{H}^{-}}\||~|~H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\}

is bounded by
\Omega>0
and let
c>0
be the limit
in (
4
), i.e.

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{\dist(x,\bar{x})}=c

be such that
c\Omega<1
. Then there exists
V\subseteq\mathbb{M}
such that the sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated
by
\mathcal{N}_{F}
is linearly convergent to
\bar{x}
for all
x^{0}\in V
.

Under the stronger assumption of Newton differentiability (as opposed to weak
Newton differentiability) the bound on
c\Omega
can be removed. This is
expected, because under Newton differentiability, the constant
c
is equal to 0
and as such the bound
c\Omega
is satisfied for any
\Omega>0
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}
with
F(\bar{x})=0
.
Denote the Newton differential of
F
by
\mathcal{H}F
and assume that
\forall x\in U
all

H\in\mathcal{H}(x)
are inversely compatible mappings, that is
\mathcal{H}(x)\subseteq GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
.
Assume that the set
\bigcup_{x\in U}\{\||{{H}^{-}}\||~|~H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\}

is bounded by
\Omega>0
. Then there exists a neighborhood
\bar{x}\in V\subseteq\mathbb{M}
such that the
mapping
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}F}
is a quasi-contraction on
V
.

Proof.

From the definition of Newton differentiability there exists a neighborhood

\bar{x}\in V\subseteq U
and a constant
c+\varepsilon
with
(c+\varepsilon)\Omega<1
such that

\forall x\in V,\quad,\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x)(x,\bar{x})\|\leq(c+\varepsilon)\dist(x,\bar{x}).

From this, the rest of the proof proceeds exactly as in the proof of
Proposition
4.2
.
∎

After obtaining convergence of the iterates to a fixed point, we can use
the rate provided by Newton differentiability to yield a convergence
rate for Newton-type methods.

Theorem 4.5

(Superlinear Convergence of Newton-type Methods in Quasi-Metric
Spaces)
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}\in U
with
F(\bar{x})=0
.
Denote the Newton differential of
F
by
\mathcal{H}F
and assume that
\forall x\in U
all

H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)
are inversely compatible mappings, that is
\mathcal{H}(x)\subseteq GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
.
Furthermore, assume that the set
\bigcup_{x\in U}\{\||{{H}^{-}}\||~|~H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\}

is bounded by
\Omega>0
. Then any sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated
by (
15
) converges superlinearly to
\bar{x}
for all

x^{0}
near
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\bar{x}
is a fixed point of
\mathcal{N}_{F}
because

\forall H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\quad{H}^{-}(\bar{x},F(\bar{x}))={H}^{-}(\bar{x},0)=\bar{x}.

Using Proposition
4.4
we can conclude
from Theorem
1.16
that
x^{k}
converges to
\bar{x}
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we can use (
1
), to show that

\displaystyle\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\dist({H(x^{k})}^{-}(\bar{x},F(x^{k})),\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\dist({H(x^{k})}^{-}(\bar{x},F(x^{k})),{H(x^{k})}^{-}(\bar{x},F(\bar{x})))

\displaystyle\leq\||{H(x)}^{-}\||\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x)(x,\bar{x})\|.

(17)

We use the bound on
\||{H(x)}^{-}\||
, to show that

\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})\leq\Omega\|F(x^{k})-F(\bar{x})-H^{k}(x^{k}-\bar{x})\|,

using (
1
) from the definition of inversely
compatible pseudo-linear maps.
Next, we need Newton differentiability to conclude that there exists a sequence,

{\{c_{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, converging to
0
such that

\|F(x^{k})-F(\bar{x})-H^{k}(x^{k},\bar{x})\|\leq c_{k}\dist(x^{k},\bar{x}).

(18)

Together, (
17
) and (
18
) yield

\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})\leq\Omega c_{k}\dist(x^{k},\bar{x}),

with
\Omega c_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, and hence the sequence convergences
superlinearly. Since the sequence
\{x^{k}\}
was arbitrary, this holds for all
sequences, as claimed.
∎

4.1
Higher Convergence Rates

In the classic theory of Newton’s method, quadratic convergence rates are
attained under stronger smoothness assumptions. In order for us to capture
this behavior, we have to expand the considered notion of Newton differentiability.

Definition 4.6

(Rate of Newton Differentiability)
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}
, with Newton
differential
\mathcal{H}F:U\rightrightarrows S_{n}(\mathbb{M})
. The maximal number
\gamma\geq 1
for which one has

\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\sup_{H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)}\quad\frac{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-H(x,\bar{x})\|}{{\dist(x,\bar{x})}^{\gamma}}<\infty

(19)

is called the
rate
of Newton differentiability.
Furthermore, if the limit in (
19
) is
0
,
\gamma
is
called a
super-rate
.

These stronger versions of Newton differentiability will translate directly
into higher convergence rates of the Newton-type method associated to the
Newton differential.

Theorem 4.7

(Faster Convergence)
.

Let
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be pointwise Newton differentiable at
\bar{x}
with
F(\bar{x})=0

with (super-)rate
\gamma>1
.
Denote the Newton differential of
F
by
\mathcal{H}F
and assume that
\forall x\in U
all

H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)
are inversely compatible mappings, that is
\mathcal{H}(x)\subseteq GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
.
Furthermore, assume that the set
\bigcup_{x\in U}\{\||{{H}^{-}}\||~|~H\in\mathcal{H}F(x)\}

is bounded by
\Omega
. Then any sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated
by (
15
) converges with
(super-)rate
\gamma
to
\bar{x}
for all
x^{0}

near
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

Following the exact line of reasoning as in the proof of
Theorem
4.5
, we can use the rate
\gamma

in (
17
), concluding that there exists a convergent
sequence
{\{c_{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\dist(x^{k+1},\bar{x})\leq c_{k}{\dist(x^{k},\bar{x})}^{\gamma}.

Furthermore, if
\gamma
is a super rate, we know that
\lim_{k\to\infty}c_{k}=0
,
proving the desired conclusion.
∎

5
The Kantorovich-type Theorem

In order to obtain convergence rates for Newton-type theorems, the existence of
a zero must be assumed. In this section, we aim to establish sufficient
conditions for the existence of such a point, thus in effect providing an
inverse function theorem. To this effect, a vast body of work has been
undertaken in the past, culminating with Smale’s
\alpha
-Theory
[
29
]
and
the Newton-Kantorovich Theorem. The former provides a test for the solvability
of the equation by using information about all the function’s derivatives at a
given point and as such it is not suitable for our nonsmooth and nonlinear
context. The original Newton-Kantorovich Theorem was first proven by Leonid
Kantorovich in 1948 in
[
16
]
and guarantees the existence of a zero
of a Lipschitz smooth function by requiring only information about the function
and its Jacobian at a given point. This theorem can be easily adapted to our
Newton differentiability context by strengthening the conditions satisfied by
the values of the function and its Newton differential at a point.

Definition 5.1
.

An inversely compatible pseudo-linear mapping
H:\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called

strongly inversely compatible
if

\forall x,\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad v=H(x,H^{-}(x,v)),

and

\forall x,\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad x=H^{-}(x,v)\Rightarrow v=0.

(20)

The set of all such mappings is denoted by
SGS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
.

Remark 5.2
.

This stronger invertibility condition is not required in order to obtain
convergence rate results, but it is required for the Kantorovich-type theorem.

Remark 5.3
.

In the context of Example
2.3
, an invertible
linear map
T
induces a strong-inversely compatible map.

Definition 5.4
.

A mapping
H:\mathbb{M}\to GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
is called
pointwise h-smooth at
x^{0}
if
there exist
\kappa>0
and
\alpha>0
such that

\forall x\in\mathbb{M},\forall y,z\in\mathbb{M}\quad\dist({H(x)}^{-}(x,H(x^{0})(z,y)),x)\leq(1+\kappa{\dist(x,x^{0})}^{\alpha})\dist(y,z).

A mapping
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{M}\rightrightarrows GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
has a
pointwise h-smooth selection at
x^{0}
if
there exists a pointwise h-smooth at
x^{0}
mapping
H:\mathbb{M}\to GS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
with

H(x)\in\mathcal{H}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{M}
.

Remark 5.5
.

In the setting of Example
2.3
,

H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to GS_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
induced
by
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n{\times}n}
is h-smooth at
x^{0}
if
T
is pointwise Hölder
continuous at
x^{0}
, with constant
\alpha>0
, and
T^{-1}
is uniformly bounded,
i.e
\exists M<\infty
such that
for all
x
,
\|{T(x)}^{-1}\|\leq M
. To see
this, we compute

\displaystyle\dist(x,{H(x)}^{-}(x,H(x^{0})(z,y)))

\displaystyle=\|x-{H(x)}^{-}(x,H(x^{0})(z,y))\|

\displaystyle=\|x-x-{T(x)}^{-1}H(x^{0})(z,y)\|

\displaystyle=\|{T(x)}^{-1}T(x^{0})(y-z)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|{T(x)}^{-1}T(x^{0})\|\|y-z\|

\displaystyle=\|{T(x)}^{-1}(T(x^{0})-T(x))+\Id\|\|y-z\|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|{T(x)}^{-1}\|\|T(x^{0})-T(x)\|)\|y-z\|.

(21)

Using the pointwise Hölder continuity of
T
, we know that

\|T(x^{0})-T(x)\|\leq\kappa\|x-x^{0}\|^{\alpha}

and from (
5.5
) we derive the conclusion.

This example justifies the name h-smooth.

The proof of the next theorem uses what Kantorovich called the general theory
of approximate methods, which solves problems by first constructing
an easier to solve instance of the problem and then relating this to the
original instance. The key idea of the proof developed here is that
of analyzing a Newton-type method applied to the auxiliary function defined by
equations (
22
) (
23
) (
24
)
and (
25
).
This idea follows the lines presented by Ortega in
[

]
.

Theorem 5.6

(Kantorovich-type Theorem on Metric Spaces)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}
be a quasi-metric space and
F:U\subseteq\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be uniformly Newton
differentiable on
U
with rate
\gamma
and the Newton differential

\mathcal{H}F:U\rightrightarrows SGS_{n}(\mathbb{M})
having a h-smooth selection
(see Definition
5.4
)
denoted by
H_{F}
. Concretely, let
L>0
and
\gamma\in[1,2]
be such that

\forall x\in\mathbb{M},\forall y,z\in\mathbb{M}\quad\dist(x,{H_{F}(x)}^{-}(x,H_{F}(x^{0})(z,y)))\leq(1+L{\dist(x,x^{0})}^{\gamma-1})\dist(y,z)

and such that

\forall x,y\in U,\quad\|F(x)-F(y)-H_{F}(x)(x,y)\|\leq\frac{L}{2}{\dist(x,y)}^{\gamma}.

Let
x^{0}\in U
and assume there exists
B<\infty
such that

B=\sup_{x\neq y\in\mathbb{M}}\frac{\dist(x,y)}{\|H_{F}(x^{0})(x,y)\|}

and set
\eta:=\dist(x^{0},{H_{F}(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{0},F(x^{0})))
.
Suppose further that there exists a constant
M^{*}>0
such that for all

x\in U

\||{H_{F}(x)}^{-}\||\leq M^{*}.

Assume that for all
x\in U
,

{H_{F}(x)}^{-}(x,-F(x))\in U.

Furthermore assume there exists
\bar{t}\in(0,\infty)
and a function

f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,\bar{t}]
with Lipschitz continuous second derivative such that for all

t<\bar{t}

(a)

\frac{LB}{2}{\left(-\frac{f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)}\right)}^{\gamma}\leq f\left(t-\frac{f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)}\right),

(22)

(b)

f(0)=\eta,\quad f(t)>0,\quad f(\bar{t})=0,

(23)

(c)

f^{\prime}(t)<0,\quad f^{\prime}(t)\geq-{(1+Lt^{\gamma-1})}^{-1},

(24)

(d)

f^{\prime\prime}(t)>0.

(25)

Then, for any
x^{0}
, the sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy and

\lim_{k\to\infty}F(x^{k})=0
.

Proof.

The first step of the proof consists in developing a convergence result
for Newton’s method applied to solving
f(t)=0
. For this we consider
the function
N:[0,\bar{t}]\to[0,\bar{t}]
defined by

N(t)=t-\frac{f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)}.

The case
\eta=0
in (
23
) means that

F(x^{0})=0
, and we are done.
Otherwise (
23
) shows that
\bar{t}
is a fixed
point of
N
i.e.
N(\bar{t})=\bar{t}
. Furthermore, because
f(t)>0
for all

t\in(0,\bar{t})
we can deduce that
\bar{t}
is the unique fixed point.

Next, we have to analyze the behavior of the fixed point iteration of the
mapping
N
. From the definition of
f
, we can easily
conclude that for all
t\in(0,\bar{t})

-\frac{f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)}\geq 0,

(26)

so
n(t)\geq t
.

Using Taylor’s Theorem, we can expand
f
at the unique root
\bar{t}

0=f(\bar{t})=f(t)+f^{\prime}(t)(\bar{t}-t)+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi){(\bar{t}-t)}^{2}.

Simplifying and rearranging gives

\bar{t}-N(t)=-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\xi)}{2f^{\prime}(t)}{(\bar{t}-t)}^{2}\geq 0,

(27)

showing that
N(t)\leq\bar{t}
.

Equations (
26
) and (
27
) together show that for any

t_{0}
the sequence
{(t_{k})}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

generated by

t_{k+1}=N(t_{k})

(28)

is monotonically increasing and bounded, so
convergent. Let
t^{*}
denote the limit of this sequence and as such we can
use the fact that
N(t^{*})=t^{*}
to conclude that
f(t^{*})=0
. Because
of (
23
), we know that
\bar{t}
is the unique zero
of
f
on
[0,\bar{t}]
, yielding
\bar{t}=t^{*}
. This constitutes the part of the proof
in which a different instance of the problem is constructed and studied,
as per the general outline of an approximate method described by Kantorovich.

For the remainder of the proof we consider the sets

\Sigma(t)=\{x\in U~|~\|x-x^{0}\|\leq t,\dist(x,{H_{F}(x^{0})}^{-}(x,F(x)))\leq f(t)\},

for any
t\in(0,\infty)
.
These sets will help us relate the behavior of the Newton-type methods
for
f
and
F
.

Let
{(x^{k})}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be generated by iterating
\mathcal{N}_{F}
, where

x^{k+1}={H_{F}(x^{k})}^{-}(x^{k},-F(x^{k})).

Now consider
{(t_{k})}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by
t_{k+1}=N(t_{k})
with
t_{0}=0
.
We will show using induction that
x_{k}\in\Sigma(t_{k})
. The first step consists
in concluding that
x^{0}\in\Sigma(0)
, i.e.

\eta=\dist(x^{0},{H_{F}(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{0},F(x^{0})))\leq f(0)

and this is exactly the condition from (
23
).

We proceed by assuming that
x^{k}\in\Sigma(t_{k})
and showing that

x^{k+1}\in\Sigma(t_{k+1})
. Indeed, using the
strong inverse compatibility

of
H(x^{0})
from Definition
5.1
, we can derive

\displaystyle\dist(x^{k+1},x^{k})

\displaystyle=\dist({H(x^{k})}^{-}(x^{k},-F(x^{k})),x^{k})

\displaystyle=\dist({H(x^{k})}^{-}(x^{k},H(x^{0})(x^{k},{H(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k},-F(x^{k})))),x^{k}).

(29)

Using the
pointwise h-smoothness
of
H
at
x^{0}
with
x^{k}
and

{H(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k},-F(x^{k}))
as
y
and
z
respectively,
in (
5
) we obtain the bound

\dist(x^{k+1},x^{k})\leq(1+L{\dist(x^{k},x^{0})}^{\gamma-1})\dist(x^{k},{H(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k},-F(x^{k}))).

From the definition of
\Sigma
and the fact that
x^{k}\in\Sigma(t_{k})
we know that

(1+L{\dist(x^{k},x^{0})}^{\gamma-1})\dist(x^{k},{H(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k},-F(x^{k})))\leq(1+L{t_{k}}^{\gamma-1})f(t_{k}),

and from (
24
)
we can conclude the key bound

\dist(x^{k+1},x^{k})\leq-\frac{f(t_{k})}{f^{\prime}(t_{k})}.

(30)

In the next part of the proof, we use
strong-inverse compatibility

of
H(x^{k})
to conclude that

\|F(x^{k+1})\|=\|F(x^{k})-F(x^{k+1})-{H(x^{k})}(x^{k},{H(x^{k})}^{-}(x^{k},-F(x^{k})))\|.

(31)

Using Newton differentiability
in (
31
), we can
bound

\|F(x^{k+1})\|\leq\frac{L}{2}{\dist(x^{k+1},x^{k})}^{\gamma}.

(32)

Combining (
32
)
with (
30
) and
using (
22
) gives

\|F(x^{k+1})\|\leq\frac{L}{2}{\left(-\frac{f(t_{k})}{f^{\prime}(t_{k})}\right)}^{\gamma}\leq\frac{f(t_{k+1})}{B}.

(33)

The definition of
B
then proves that

\dist(x^{k+1},{H_{F}(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k+1},F(x^{k+1})))\leq B\|H_{F}(x^{0})(x^{k+1},{H_{F}(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k+1},F(x^{k+1})))\|,

(34)

while the strong inverse compatibility shows

\|H_{F}(x^{0})(x^{k+1},{H_{F}(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k+1},F(x^{k+1})))\|=\|F(x^{k+1})\|.

(35)

Combining (
33
) with (
34
)
and (
35
) gives

\dist(x^{k+1},{H_{F}(x^{0})}^{-}(x^{k+1},F(x^{k+1})))\leq f(t_{k+1}),

and this is one of the two requirements for
x^{k+1}\in\Sigma(t_{k+1})
.

Clearly, substituting in (
30
)
the definition of
t_{k+1}
from (
28
)

\dist(x^{k+1},x^{k})\leq t_{k+1}-t_{k}.

(36)

It remains to show that
\dist(x^{k+1},x^{0})\leq t_{k+1}
. Indeed, using the
triangle inequality

\dist(x^{k+1},x^{0})\leq\dist(x^{k+1},x^{k})+\dist(x^{k},x^{0})

and from (
36
) and the
induction hypothesis

\dist(x^{k+1},x^{0})\leq t_{k+1}-t_{k}+t_{k},

completing the induction part of the proof and shows that for all

k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x^{k}\in\Sigma(t_{k})
.

The last part of the proof consists in looking at the convergence of the
sequence
{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. A simple telescoping argument shows that

\displaystyle\dist(x^{m},x^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\dist(x^{m},x^{m-1})+\cdots+\dist(x^{n+1},x^{n})

\displaystyle\leq|t_{m}-t_{m-1}|+\cdots+|t_{n+1}-t_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq t_{m}-t_{m-1}+\cdots+t_{n+1}-t_{n}=t_{m}-t_{n}.

Because the sequence
{\{t_{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy we deduce that

{\{x^{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy.

In order to complete the proof, we take the limit as
k\to\infty

in (
33
), using the fact that
f
is continuous
and
f(\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k})=0
to conclude that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|F(x^{k})\|\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{f(t_{k})}{\eta}=0.

∎

Remark 5.7
.

As opposed to the convergence rate proof, the Kantorovich-type theorem
generates a Cauchy sequence, and we need to further impose completeness on the
quasi-metric space in order to obtain a limit point. Furthermore, we also
need to assume continuity of
F
at this limit point in order to be able
to guarantee that
F(\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k})=0
.

A Numerical Example

In this section we will investigate the behavior of our Newton-type algorithm
for a simple toy optimization problem defined on a cubic complex.
For this purpose we
first consider a finite binary tree
B
with root
r
and with its usual
distance
\dist_{B}
, defined as the minimal number of edges of a path between
two nodes.
Such a tree is a uniquely geodesic space, so between any two points
b_{x}

and
b_{y}
there exists a unique path
b_{0},b_{1},\dots,b_{N}
with
b_{0}=b_{x}
and

b_{N}=b_{y}
. This path allows use to introduce the auxiliary functions

\gamma_{b_{x},b_{y}}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
,

\gamma_{b_{x},b_{y}}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x&\text{ if }b_{1}\text{ is a direct descendant of }b_{0}\\
1-x&\text{ if }b_{1}\text{ is the parent of }b_{0}.\end{array}\right.

We define the disjoint union
\tilde{\mathbb{M}}=\dot{\bigcap}_{b\in B}[0,1]
with
the semimetric

\dist((b_{x},x),(b_{y},y))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|x-y|&\text{ if }\dist_{B}(b_{x},b_{y})=0\\
\gamma_{b_{x},b_{y}}(x)+\gamma_{b_{y},b_{x}}(y)+\dist_{B}(b_{x},b_{y})-1&\text{ else. }\end{array}\right.

To construct the metric space, we simply identify points with distance
0

between them, thus setting
\mathbb{M}=\tilde{\mathbb{M}}/\dist
. It is clear that this space is
compact in the topology induced by the distance as it is essentially a closed
subset of the product of finitely many compact spaces.

It is important to note that

7
Conclusions and Limitations

The results of this work are formulated in quite general settings and are
intended primarily to serve as a stepping stone to a more practical optimization
theory in metric spaces. The necessary algebraic constructs for casting an
optimization problem as a root-finding problem are still under active research
and will be the subject of a following article. While the general metric theory
is still under development, the results of this article, in particular the
calculus of Newton differentiability and the Kantorovich theorem, can be applied
to nonsmooth problems on nonsmooth subsets of Euclidean spaces. In this context,
for a practical application of the Kantorovich result, numerical simulation for
the existence of the function
f
can be implemented. Compared to actually
running a Newton-type method, this numerical certificate of existence for a
root has the advantage that its computational complexity does not depend on the
dimension of the ambient space.
