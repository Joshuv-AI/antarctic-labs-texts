Best approximation of a three-variable function by sum of one-variable coordinate functions
Rashid A.Aliev, Vugar A.Guliyev, Amil F.Jabiyev

Abstract
Consider the following approximation problem of a continuous function of three variables by the sum of three continuous functions of one variable: E ⁡ ( f , Ω ) = inf ‖ f ⁡ ( x , y , z ) − ϕ ⁡ ( x ) − ψ ⁡ ( y ) − ω ⁡ ( z ) ‖ ∞ = ? E(f,\Omega)=\inf||f(x,y,z)-\phi(x)-\psi(y)-\omega(z)||_{\infty}=? where f ⁡ ( x , y , z ) f(x,y,z) is a given continuous function defined on Ω = [ 0 , 1 ] 3 \Omega=[0,1]^{3} and the infimum runs over all triplets of continuous functions ϕ ⁡ ( x ) , ψ ⁡ ( y ) , ω ⁡ ( z ) \phi(x),\psi(y),\omega(z) defined on the unit interval [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . In this paper, we will prove a formula to calculate the error E ⁡ ( f , Ω ) E(f,\Omega) under certain conditions.

Introduction

1.1
Problem statement in a general form and previous results

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function in the
n
variables, where
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\dots X_{n}
is the Cartesian product of the compact Hausdorff spaces
n
. Consider the approximation of
f
by means of
g_{i},i=1,\dots,n
, where
g_{i}
is a continuous function on
X_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. In other words, the problem asks for

E(f,X)=\inf_{(g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n})}||f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(x_{i})||_{C(X)}=?

(1.1)

where the infimum runs over all
n
-tuples of functions
(g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n})
satisfying
g_{i}\in C(X_{i}),i=1,\dots,n
.

In general, this problem is related to Hilbert’s 13th problem. Using A.S. Kronrod’s
[
16
]
results, A.N. Kolmogorov
[
14
]
showed that any function
f\in C([0,1]^{n})
is representable as a superposition of continuous functions of three variables. Developing this method, V.I. Arnold
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
proved that each continuous function of three variables can be represented by superposition of continuous functions of two variables. This is a negative answer to Hilbert’s conjecture. Further, A.N. Kolmogorov
[
15
]
proved the following remarkable, stronger theorem in a simple, elementary and elegant way:

Kolmogorov’s Theorem.
There exist increasing functions
\phi_{pq}\in C([0,1])
,
p=1,...,n
,
q=1,...,2n+1
, such that an arbitrary given function

f(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in C([0,1]^{n})

can be represented in the form

f(x_{1},...,x_{n})=\sum_{q=1}^{2n+1}g_{q}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n}\phi_{pq}(x_{p})\right),

where
g_{q}\in C([0,1]
depend on
f
.

Thus, every continuous function on the
n
variables can be represented by superposition of continuous functions of one variable and the simplest function of two variables, namely, the sum of variables.

In addition to the problems of finding a precise expression for a given function in terms of a combination of functions of fewer variables, it is natural to consider the best approximation of functions of several variables by combinations of functions of fewer variables. These problems arise in various areas of mathematics, for example, in solving integral equations (see
[
5
]
), in solving functional equations (see
[
4
]
), in saving information (see
[
6
]
), in scaling matrices (see
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
), etc. In space
C([0,1]^{n})
this problem was first studied by S. P. Diliberto and E. G. Straus
[
6
]
(the problem was proposed by RAND Corporation for the most efficient distribution of data in the small memory of the computers of the day). The same problem was investigated by Yu. P. Ofman
[
20
]
for bounded bivariant functions, and for more general sets by D. E. Marshall and A. G. O’Farrell in
[
17
]
. Diliberto and Straus gave a ’leveling algorithm’ to find the approximation error (1.1) for
n=2
and propose a theorem that will ’analogously’ hold for
n\geq 3
. However, G. Aumann in his work
[
3
]
has shown that while the proof in
[
6
]
was complete for case
n=2
, the generalization of the algorithm suggested for
n\geq 3
was incorrect. A direct counterexample refuting the ”leveling algorithm” in
n\geq 3
was given by V.A. Medvedev
[
18
]
. In the paper
[
3
]
G. Aumann showed a necessary and sufficient condition on the class of approximating functions that the ”leveling algorithm” works.

Another generalization approach for
n\geq 3
to the problem starts with the paper
[
10
]
by M. Golomb, where he gave an important duality formula for the problem (
1.1
). Moreover, the works of G. Aumann and
[
17
]
by D.E. Marshall and A.G. O’Farrell have shown that the proof of the duality given in
[
10
]
also had a gap. Future, based on the results of S.Ya. Khavinson
[
13
]
and K.G. Navada
[
19
]
, V. Ismailov
[
12
]
proved that the formula given by M. Golomb is valid in a stronger form.

Note that for the two-dimensional case, under the condition
f_{xy}\geq 0
T.J. Rivlin and R.J. Sibher
[
21
]
established that

E(f,[0,1]^{2})=\frac{1}{4}[f(1,1)-f(1,0)-f(0,1)+f(0,0)].

L. Flatto
[
7
]
gave a different proof of this formula in his work, where a generalization to the approximation of a
n
variable function by
n-1
variable functions was suggested under similar conditions. In case
n\geq 3
(even with additional conditions), there is no finite algorithm for calculate the error
E(f,X)
. In this paper, under the conditions
\Delta_{x}\Delta_{y}f\geq 0
,
\Delta_{x}\Delta_{z}f\geq 0
,
\Delta_{y}\Delta_{z}f\geq 0
, we will prove a formula to calculate the error (1.1) for the three-variable continuous function on
[0,1]^{3}
.

1.2
Basic definitions and formulas

Let’s give some definitions that will be used throughout this paper. Most definitions in this subsection are taken from the paper
[
12
]
(also can be found in the monograph
[
11
]
).

Definition 1.1
.

A set of points
p=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{m}),x_{i}\in X
where the set
X
is the Cartesian product
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\dots X_{n}
, is called a
projection cycle
if there exist some nonzero real numbers
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},..\lambda_{m}
such that

\sum_{\pi_{j}(x_{i})=\xi}\lambda_{i}=0,\forall j,0\leq j\leq n\text{ and }\forall\xi\in X_{j}

(1.2)

where
\pi_{j}(x)
is the projection of
x
into
X_{j}
and
\xi
is an arbitrary element of the set
X_{j}
.

The definition of a projection cycle goes back to the M.Golomb
[
10
]
. The above-given definition is an equivalent form of Golomb’s definition taken from
[
12
]
.

Remark 1.1
.

For
n=3
, the left-hand side of the equation (
1.2
) becomes the sum over planes parallel to one of the coordinate planes. Thus, if a projection-cycle has a point on a plane parallel to one of the coordinate planes, then it has at least one other point with an opposite sign on the same plane.

Definition 1.2
.

A projection cycle
p=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{m})
together with a vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},..\lambda_{m})
satisfying the equation (
1.2
) is called a projection cycle-vector and denoted by
(p,\lambda)
. The number of points of
p
is called the length of the projection-cycle vector
(p,\lambda)
. And
\lambda_{i}=\lambda(x_{i})
is called the weight at point
x_{i}
.

When we are given a weak projection cycle
p
and a nonzero vector
\lambda
satisfying the equation (
1.3
), by
(p,\lambda)
we mean the projection cycle-vector based on the points of
p
corresponding to nonzero components of
\lambda
.

Definition 1.3
.

A projection cycle is called a minimal projection cycle if it has no proper subset that is a projection cycle. A projection cycle-vector
(p,\lambda)
is called minimal if the set of points of
p
corresponding to nonzero components of
\lambda
is a minimal projection cycle.

Definition 1.4
.

A projection cycle-vector
(p,\lambda)
is called orthonormal if

\sum|\lambda_{i}|=1

Definition 1.5
.

A set of points
p=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{m}),x_{i}\in X
where the set
X
is the Cartesian product
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\dots X_{n}
, is called a
weak projection cycle
if there exist some real numbers
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},..\lambda_{m}
such that, at least one
\lambda_{i}
is nonzero and,

\sum_{\pi_{j}(x_{i})=\xi}\lambda_{i}=0,\forall j,0\leq j\leq n\text{ and }\forall\xi\in X_{j}

(1.3)

where
\pi_{j}(x)
is the projection of
x
into
X_{j}
and
\xi
is an arbitrary element of the set
X_{j}
.

The difference between a projection cycle and a weak projection cycle is that the second may contain points with zero weight. Thus, any projection-cycle is a weak projection cycle, but the converse is not true in general. For example, the set of points
\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1),(2,2)\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a weak projection cycle but not a projection cycle.

The concept of a minimal projection cycle and a weak projection cycle was defined by name
loop
and
weak loop
in
[
19
]
by K.G. Navada.

The following theorem expresses Golomb’s formula (proposed in
[
10
]
), in revisited and improved form, from
[
12
]
(See: Theorem 2.8 in
[
12
]
or Theorem 3.37 in
[
11
]
).

Theorem 1.1

(Golomb’s Formula)
.

The error of approximation in (
1.1
) can be given as

E(f,X)=\sup_{(p,\lambda)}\frac{\sum\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})}{\sum|\lambda_{i}|}.

(1.4)

where the supremum runs over all minimal projection cycles-vectors on the set
X
.

Remark 1.2
.

However, it is not a calculable formula; the formula (
1.4
) can be improved by comparing orthonormal sums over distinct projection-cycles under conditions like
[
21
]
or
[
7
]
used in their works to get a formula. In this paper, we will find a formula for the case
n=3
and
X=[0,1]^{3}
.

Remark 1.3
.

For simplicity, we use the term ”projection cycle-vector” instead of ”projection cycle, vector pair” used in
[
12
]
and
[
11
]
.

Three variable case

Let
\Omega=[0,1]^{3}
denote the unit cube and
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

3

E(f,\Omega)=\inf||f(x,y,z)-\phi(x)-\psi(y)-\omega(z)||_{\infty}=?

\phi(x),\psi(y),\omega(z)

Let,
\Delta_{(x,y,z)}f

\Delta_{(x,y,z)}f(u,v,w)=f(u+x,v+y,w+z)-f(u,v,w)

For simplicity, let’s denote

\Delta_{x}=\Delta_{(x,0,0)},\Delta_{y}=\Delta_{(0,y,0)},\Delta_{z}=\Delta_{(0,0,z)}

Assume

\Omega

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x}\Delta_{y}f\geq 0\\
\Delta_{x}\Delta_{z}f\geq 0\\
\Delta_{y}\Delta_{z}f\geq 0\end{cases}

(2.1)

If
f
has mixed derivatives with respect to any pair of variables at any point of
\Omega

2.1
) are equivalent to

\begin{cases}f_{xy}\geq 0\\
f_{yz}\geq 0\\
f_{xz}\geq 0\end{cases}

(2.2)

Let, for any finite sets
S
, the set
X_{S}
denote the set of all projection cycle-vectors, which, each point belongs to the set
S

X_{S,min}
denote the set of all minimal projection cycle-vectors

.

We obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let,
f\in C(\Omega)
be a function satisfying the relations (
2.1
), then there exist a finite set
U=U_{f}
such that,

E(f,\Omega)=\max_{(p,\lambda)\in X_{U_{x}\times U_{y}\times U_{z},min}}\frac{\sum\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})}{\sum|\lambda_{i}|}

(2.3)

where,
U_{x},U_{y},U_{z}
are the projections of
U
to the coordinate lines
Ox,Oy,Oz
respectively.

Remark 2.1
.

For
n=2
, above-mentioned papers
[
6
]

[
7
]

[
11

Below, we will prove the

We start with applying Golomb’s Formula (

E(f,\Omega)=\sup_{(p,\lambda)}\frac{\sum\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})}{\sum|\lambda_{i}|}.

(2.4)

(p,\lambda)

To proceed further, we will search the supremum (

E(f,\Omega)=\sup_{(p,\lambda)}\frac{\sum\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})}{\sum|\lambda_{i}|}=\sup_{A,B,C}\left({\max_{(p,\lambda)\in X_{A\times B\times C}}\frac{\sum\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})}{\sum|\lambda_{i}|}}\right)

Obviously, for any fixed

Later, we will see that to prove the

\xi_{1}\geq\xi_{2},\gamma_{1}>\gamma_{2},\zeta_{1}<\zeta_{2}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

\lambda_{1}(x)\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)>0\end{subarray}}\left(|\lambda^{1}(x)+\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)|-|\lambda^{1}(x)|\right)

By the triangle inequality, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in p^{1}\cap p^{2}\\
\lambda_{1}(x)\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)>0\end{subarray}}\left(|\lambda^{1}(x)+\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)|-|\lambda^{1}(x)|\right)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in p^{1}\cap p^{2}\\

\lambda_{1}(x)\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)>0\end{subarray}}\varepsilon

\lambda_{1}(x)\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)<0\end{subarray}}|\lambda^{1}(x)|

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in p^{1}\cap p^{2}\\

The next theorem deals with the geometric structure of optimal projection cycles.

Theorem 2.2
