Mapping the humanistic care ecosystem in medical education: development and validation of a novel multidimensional instrument (HCS-ME)
Authors: Yuqun Zhang, Xinyu Liang, Shuoxuan Zhao, Ying Chen, Qiao Zhang, Heng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeficiencies in medical humanistic care contribute to poor therapeutic relationships and clinical conflicts. Existing assessment tools are fragmented, lacking a comprehensive framework to systematically evaluate the multidimensional ecosystem of humanistic education in medicine. Thus, this study developed and validated the Humanistic Care Scale for Medical Education (HCS-ME) to address this gap.MethodsAn initial 68-item pool was generated from a literature review. Following expert review, a 60-item scale assessing five dimensions grounded in an ecosystem framework of medical education was tested, including student literacy, faculty modeling, curriculum, campus environment and professional cognition. 738 medical undergraduates completed the HCS-ME at baseline, while psychometric properties were evaluated using data from 433 after quality control. Among them, 50 students underwent the follow-up evaluation four weeks afterwards.ResultsThe HCS-ME demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.982), split-half reliability (r = 0.956) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.29 ~ 0.54 for each subscale). The factor structure of the HCS-ME fits well with the factor model (χ²/df = 2.245, χ² = 3817.010, df = 1700, P < 0.001; CFI = 0.909; TLI = 0.905; RMSEA = 0.054). A key finding identified students from single-partner households as scoring significantly lower across all dimensions.ConclusionsHCS-ME is a novel tool with good validity and reliability for holistically assessing medical humanistic care education. It provides a practical instrument for identifying educational gaps, informing targeted interventions, and guiding curriculum and policy reform to ultimately foster a more humanistic healthcare workforce.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09631-8.

Background

Medical humanistic care, as an interdisciplinary field applying humanistic principles to healthcare, primarily involves medicine and nursing and engages practitioners, patients, and students in clinical context [1, 2]. Previous researchers have proposed that the ultimate goal of medical humanistic care is to enhance patients’ quality of life and address their complex requirements [1]. Effective implementation of medical humanistic care has been shown to enhance recovery rates, particularly in mental and psychological illnesses, and provide substantial benefits to patients [3]. Furthermore, it fosters essential professional qualities including integrity, honesty, and compassion among healthcare workers, while reducing professional burnout, improving service quality, and strengthening therapeutic and nursing relationships [4, 5].

Based on previous evidence, positive doctor-patient and nurse-patient relationships are a critical factor in ensuring the smooth delivery of healthcare, while medical humanistic care serves as an underlying safeguard for these relationships [2]. Notably poor therapeutic relationships between healthcare providers and patients frequently lead to incidents of medical violence [2]. Empirical evidence from large-scale national surveys confirms the global prevalence of this issue. A 2018 study by the Chinese Medical Doctor Association reported that 62% and 66% of physicians had experienced medical disputes and conflicts, respectively, with verbal abuse constituting, the most common form, at 51% [6]. Similarly, a 2015 survey by the Indian Medical Association revealed that 75% Indian doctors had encountered workplace violence [7]. The lack of medical humanistic care is one of the key contributing factors to such incidents of medical violence.

Although sufficient education in medical humanistic care has been confirmed to lower rates of medical violence, its translation into outcomes varies [8, 9]. A randomized controlled trial showed that structured communication training significantly reduced perceived patient aggression among healthcare workers, demonstrating a large effect size [10]. Conversely, other training focusing on de-escalation, though it greatly improved confidence and reduced burnout, failed to produce a significant short-term decline in violence incidence [11]. This indicates that while the empowerment of healthcare workers through boosted confidence and better mental health is a direct benefit of humanistic education, its effect on tangible violence reduction likely requires a longer period or depends on synergistic advancements with comprehensive measures aimed at patients and the healthcare system. The value of medical humanistic care education is overlooked in many parts of the world, as evidenced by studies in the United States, United Kingdom, and China [12–14]. In mainland China, the deficiency is systemic, characterized not only by a limited range of standalone courses but also by a lack of integration across the educational ecosystem, including the scarcity of dedicated medical humanities institutes, the persistence of didactic pedagogical methods with little innovation, and a broader campus culture and faculty development system that may not consistently reinforce humanistic values [6].

Previous research has disproportionately focused on the nursing field (particularly involving nurses and nursing students). While research on humanistic care in nursing has yielded valuable assessment tools [1], this body of work remains focused on a specific professional domain. Consequently, existing instruments are inherently limited in capturing the broader educational landscape required for all medical professionals. This fragmentation points to a deeper conceptual gap, namely the lack of a systemic framework to guide the evaluation and development of humanistic care education across the medical education continuum. A holistic approach necessitates an ecosystem perspective that acknowledges the interdependent elements shaping learners. We posit that this ecosystem comprises five core and interacting dimensions. These dimensions are the learner’s own literacy and cognition, the role modeling provided by faculty, the design and delivery of the formal curriculum, the pervasive influence of the campus environment, and the development of a professional identity that values humanistic care [15]. Medical and nursing students, as the future workforce, are situated at the center of this ecosystem. Therefore, to move beyond fragmented assessment, this study developed and validated the Humanistic Care Scale for Medical Education (HCS-ME). Grounded in this ecosystem framework, the HCS-ME is designed to provide a comprehensive tool for evaluating the state of medical humanistic care education, with the ultimate aim of identifying leverage points for targeted intervention, curriculum reform, and policy enhancements.

Methods

Participants and procedure

An expert panel from medical universities was recruited to develop the self-rated HCS-ME scale, a 60-item instrument (Table S1) assessing five dimensions on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree, 7 = completely agree). The scale structure is grounded in an ecosystem framework, positing that humanistic education emerges from the interaction of five core dimensions: students’ humanistic cognition and literacy (RW, 10 items), curriculum and instruction (KC, 12 items), campus humanistic environment (XY, 8 items), faculty role modeling (JS, 21 items), and career-development cognition (ZY, 9 items) [16–21]. The abbreviation for each dimension is composed of the initial letters of the pinyin of the corresponding Chinese dimension name. The research team conducted a comprehensive search of the China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang Database to identify core literature pertaining to medical students’ humanistic care competencies. Key elements including curriculum and instruction, as well as faculty role modeling in humanistic care were extracted, resulting in an initial pool of 68 relevant items. Through research group discussions and revisions of two subject matter experts (one in medical education and one in psychometrics), semantically similar items were consolidated, while those demonstrating low relevance to the scale’s core construct were eliminated, ultimately producing a refined 60-item questionnaire. Details of abbreviation for each dimension, developing process and the complete HCS-ME can be found in supplemental methods.

Data were collected offline and online (via Wenjuanxing, WeChat and Tencent QQ platforms) from 28 April 2025 to 16 Jun 2025. Eligible participants were medical-university undergraduates aged 18 years or older with access to a computer or phone. Students in non-medical majors, such as psychology, management, or biomedical engineering were excluded. Of 738 invited participants (609 online, 129 offline), 433 met the quality control criteria, including a completion time of more than 100 s, no extensive missing responses, absence of variant response patterns (e.g., selecting the same option for all times), and a medical academic background. These 433 participants comprised the final sample (Fig. 1). Fifty participants completed a four-week retest, all of them passed quality checks and were retained. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. 202506024-1).

Assessment

Demographic characteristics and potential influencing factors of humanistic care were collected, including high school track selection, personality traits, social relationships, parental education level, and exposure to humanistic curricula in university. Participants completed the HCS-ME either online or offline, and a subset retested after four weeks.

Statistical analyses

The analyses were performed using Predictive analytics software (PASW) Statistics 21 and IBM SPSS Amos 22 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The mean scores of each factor were compared across demographic groups using independent samples t-test or One-way ANOVA (Bonferroni correction). Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and the split-half coefficient. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted, with factorability assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) was used as the extraction method with Promax oblique rotation, considering the potential correlations among dimensions. The number of factors was determined by combining the theoretical expectation of five dimensions and the statistical criterion of eigenvalues greater than 1, ensuring both statistical and theoretical rationality. The factor structure was further validated through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), with model fit evaluated using the following indices: Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean-Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with 90% confidence interval (CI), and Standardized Root Mean Residual (SRMR). Correlation analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.1) using the “corrplot” package. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess test-retest reliability and inter-factor relationships. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and item characteristics

Comparative analyses of factor mean scores were conducted across demographic variables including gender, grade, ethnicity, urban-rural division, only-child status, family structure (single-partner household vs. two-partner household), medical professional kinship, political affiliation, leadership experience, extracurricular participation, personality traits, academic track preference, and parental education levels. Significant differences emerged solely in family structure comparisons (Fig. 2). Students from single-partner households demonstrated significantly lower scores across all measured factors (uncorrected): RW (P < 0.05), KC(P < 0.01), JS (P < 0.01), XY (P < 0.05) and ZY (P < 0.01). No other demographic variables yielded statistically significant differences.

All item characteristics are presented in Table S2. Responses spanned the full range of response options. Item means ranged from 4.92 to 5.90. All item-factor correlations exceeded 0.73 (P < 0.01), except for Item 2 of the JS subscale (r = 0.55, P < 0.01).

Reliability

As shown in Table S2 and Fig. 3A, Pearson correlation analyses revealed strong associations between factor scores and items, as well as among factor scores, with correlation coefficients ranged from 0.55 to 0.85 (all P < 0.01). These robust coefficients indicate excellent internal consistency of the HCS-ME and its subcomponents. Reliability analysis demonstrated outstanding internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.982 and McDonald’s ω = 0.990. The McDonald’s ω for each dimension ranged from 0.935 to 0.968, further supporting the strong reliability of the scale. Additionally, the split-half reliability was 0.956. Item-total redundancy tests showed all items were valid (corrected item-total correlations > 0.52, inter-item correlations < 0.85) with no redundant items retained.

Test-retest reliability was assessed in a subsample of 50 students who completed the HCS-ME at two time points (Fig. 3B). Pearson correlation analysis revealed heterogeneity in test-retest reliability coefficients across different dimensions. Weak test-retest reliability was observed for the RW (r = 0.29, P < 0.05) and KC (r = 0.30, P < 0.05) dimensions. Reliability for the ZY dimension (r = 0.48, P < 0.01), the JS (r = 0.53, P < 0.01) and XY (r = 0.54, P < 0.01) dimensions showed acceptable moderate reliability. These findings suggest that the total scale score has acceptable stability over time, although scores on some dimensions (RW, KC) may exhibit considerable fluctuation.

Construct validity

The dataset demonstrated excellent structural validity (Fig. 4), as evidenced by a KMO measure of 0.982 and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² = 23065.728, df = 1770, P < 0.001), confirming factor analysis appropriateness. EFA yielded six common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, which were 34.156, 2.040, 1.778, 1.306, 1.218 and 1.041 respectively, with a cumulative explained variance of 64.53%. The Scree plot suggested that two factors could be extracted. However, combined with the theoretical framework, literature evidence, and the five predefined dimensions of humanistic care competence in this study, five common factors were ultimately retained to align with the theoretical construct. The relatively strong conceptual correlation and overlap among these dimensions were consistent with the previous result that discriminant validity was not fully satisfied, further supporting the theoretical characteristic that the dimensions of humanistic care competence form a dynamically interactive ecosystem. CFA revealed acceptable model fit for the HCS-ME structure: χ²/df = 2.245 (χ² = 3817.010, df = 1700, P < 0.001); CFI = 0.909; TLI = 0.905; RMSEA = 0.054 (90% CI: 0.051–0.056); SRMR = 0.035. All factor loadings exceeded 0.426 (P < 0.001), providing strong evidence of the scale’s construct validity.

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was assessed using composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). The CR values of each factor ranged from 0.935 to 0.968, while the AVE values of each factor ranged from 0.592 to 0.682. All CR values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, and all AVE values were above 0.50, indicating acceptable convergent validity.

Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity was examined using the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio of correlations. The HTMT values for RW and JS, as well as RW and XY fell below the conservative threshold of 0.85, providing satisfactory evidence of discriminant validity for these relationships. However, several pairs involving the latent construct KS, JS, XY and ZY exhibited HTMT ratios exceeding the more liberal threshold of 0.90.

Discussions

In this study, students from single-partner households have significantly lower scores across all dimensions of the HCS-ME. While previous research has linked family environment to empathy development [22, 23], our assessment reveals that this background factor may be associated with broad, cross-contextual differences in how students engage with and perceive various facets of the educational environment. The role of single-partner household structure, as a distinct contextual factor, has received limited attention in shaping humanistic care competencies. It is possible that single-partner households tend to face greater resource constraints, such as economic limitations, diminished emotional support, and simplified social networks. These may provide fewer care models and learning opportunities. These conditions could potentially hinder children’s acquisition and internalization of humanistic care behaviors during development [24, 25]. Furthermore, children in such contexts may take on adult roles prematurely due to family pressures, which might require them to allocate limited emotional resources primarily to self-regulation and environmental adaptation. This process could persistently suppresses their own emotional needs, potentially weakening their capacity to perceive and respond to others’ emotional states [26, 27]. Additionally, pre-existing conflicts and instability prior to structural changes may undermine the foundation for secure attachment relationships. Such disruption might not only affect self-identity and emotional security, but could also constrain the development of healthy empathy and the capacity to establish trusting relationships [28, 29]. Thus, these findings suggest that medical students from single-partner households may warrant additional attention and support within educational settings.

Additionally, this study developed the HCS-ME, a psychometrically robust tool demonstrating good reliability and validity. EFA extracted two factors according to the Scree plot, while the Kaiser criterion identified six factors, whereas CFA supported the hypothesized five-factor structure. This discrepancy may be attributed to the high inter-correlations among the theoretically related subfactors, which is consistent with the ecosystem framework conceptualizing these constructs as interdependent. Moreover, EFA aims to identify the most parsimonious factor structure in an exploratory manner, whereas CFA is designed to test a theoretically predefined model. Given the strong theoretical grounding of the five-factor structure, we prioritized the CFA results. Although the five-factor structure was empirically supported, it should be noted that the model fit indices exhibited a mixed pattern: RMSEA and SRMR showed excellent performance, while CFI and TLI fell within an acceptable yet not excellent range. This pattern may be associated with the preliminary validation nature of the present study. During the early stages of scale development, subtle aspects of item phrasing and construct operationalization can introduce measurement error, which in turn may impact comparative fit indices such as CFI and TLI [30]. Nevertheless, all key paths were significant and factor loadings were adequate, indicating that the model is fundamentally valid. Thus, these results support the five-factor structure as empirically plausible and theoretically meaningful.

Furthermore, the five dimensions of the HCS-ME are theorized as constituting a dynamically interactive “ecosystem” of humanistic care competence. Conceptually, these dimensions form an organic whole, although the nature and strength of their interactions remain to be empirically tested. In this framework, humanistic cognition serves as an internal foundation; curriculum and instruction as a cultivation mechanism; faculty role modeling and campus environment provide a contextualized field for application; and career-development cognition acts as the system’s integrative outcome and stabilizer. Consequently, strengths or weaknesses in one dimension may influence others. Within this framework, family context has been suggested as foundational to humanistic literacy [31]. Consistent with this perspective, medical students from single-partner households in our study exhibited lower levels of such competence. Nevertheless, university education may help compensate for this deficit. In line with previous research [32, 33], our findings indicate that humanistic knowledge may be a key predictor of care capacity, underscoring that willingness to care alone may not translate into practice without a solid knowledge base [34].

In addition, students’ perception of faculty role modeling is an important indicator of the state of humanistic care education [35, 36], as reflected in the JS dimension of our scale. Wang et al. [37] demonstrated that effective role modeling can cultivate humanistic literacy among medical students, and that interactive or heuristic teaching methods may promote independent thinking and scientific curiosity [38, 39]. A teacher’s respectful demeanor and empathetic communication are also direct expressions of humanistic care [40], which students may internalize through sustained interaction. Conversely, poor role models could undermine students’ caring abilities by setting a negative example [22]. Thus, the JS dimension provides a useful indicator of students’ perceptions, which is a critical component for assessing faculty role modeling in humanistic care education.

The curriculum is widely considered a primary context for teacher modeling and plays a key role in cultivating students’ humanistic care. Curriculum reform has been proposed as one pathway to instilling humanistic values [41], and empirical evidence indicates that humanistic care courses can improve both medical students’ and practitioners’ care capacity [35, 42]. Our findings suggest that students’ perceptions of curriculum and instruction correlate with their overall assessment of humanistic care education. This highlights the potential value of using this tool to identify specific curricular areas that warrant closer attention.

Furthermore, the campus environment can function as a form of hidden curriculum, potentially influencing curriculum implementation and caring behaviors. In the context of medical education, the physical infrastructure, which includes controlled indoor settings, high-fidelity simulation hospitals, and integrated green spaces, serves as a continuous and tacit pedagogical force. This environment consistently models and reinforces the essential values of respect, empathy, and compassionate care for future healthcare practitioners [43, 44]. Simultaneously, the socio-organizational climate of a campus, reflected in supportive peer interactions, an equitable and inclusive atmosphere, and humanistic management practices, has been empirically linked to the demonstration of caring behaviors [45]. Conversely, a high-pressure and predominantly utilitarian climate consumes the psychological resources necessary to nurture such dispositions and can promote values in direct opposition to humanistic ideals [22]. International comparative studies, however, suggest that this erosion of humanistic concern is not an inevitable outcome. Evidence from systems such as those in Japan and South Korea indicates that the determining factors lie within specific environmental and cultural frameworks [23]. Therefore, deliberate environmental redesign presents a viable strategy to transform humanistic education from a matter of formal instruction into a process of pervasive cultural immersion. This shift supports the internalization of humanistic care as a sustained and conscious professional habit.

In our scale, medical students’ perception of humanistic care as integral to career development would be a key indicator of their caring capability. Notably, the humanistic care competencies cultivated in medical students are ultimately fully realized and manifested in their professional practice, critically shaping the dynamics of patient-provider relationships and generating far-reaching implications. Students who regard humanistic care as a core professional competency exhibit stronger care abilities, which in turn are associated with higher patient adherence, fewer clinical disputes, and greater career satisfaction [46, 47]. Conversely, viewing humanistic care as a peripheral skill corresponds to inadequate competency, leading to eroded patient trust, communication failures, increased conflicts, and heightened professional burnout [48]. This dimension thus captures the connection between students’ educational experience and their perception of humanistic care’s to long-term professional growth, which is an important aspect of assessing the current state of medical humanistic care education.

This study also revealed a key and interesting phenomenon: the psychometric properties of the scale’s dimensions are not homogeneous. This pattern of heterogeneity is not merely a methodological shortcoming, but may reflect profound differences in the nature of the psychological constructs measured by each dimension [49]. First, the JS and XY dimensions demonstrated acceptable moderate reliability, suggesting that they likely measure relatively stable individual traits that are less susceptible to situational fluctuations, such as more solidified cognitive tendencies or deep-seated attitudes. However, the lower test-retest reliability of the RW and KC dimensions carries important theoretical implications. This result strongly suggests that RW and KC are more likely measuring state-like variables. In other words, the psychological attributes are inherently prone to considerable change over a period of several weeks [50]. Therefore, the lower correlation coefficients largely reflect legitimate fluctuations in the constructs themselves, rather than mere measurement error. Finally, the reliability of the ZY dimension falls between these two groups (r = 0.48), possibly indicating that it measures a construct comprising a mixture of both trait and state components [51].

Although this study covers multiple aspects of medical humanistic care, it still has several limitations. First, content validity was not formally quantified via expert indices, though the scale expert review. Second, the sample consisted solely of Chinese medical students, limiting cross-cultural generalizability. Third, reliance on self-report data may introduce social desirability and common method bias. Fourth, criterion-related validity was not assessed, and future studies should incorporate external criterion measures. Fifth, some CFA fit indices were marginally below ideal thresholds, suggesting potential for item refinement. Finally, the scale may possess a higher-order structure warranting further investigation. Future research should address these limitations through cross-cultural validation, multi-method designs, and larger split-sample analyses.

This study developed and validated the HCS-ME, a multidimensional instrument with strong psychometric properties suitable for assessing medical students’ humanistic care competencies. It also identified family structure as a significant factor influencing the development of such competencies. The HCS-ME addresses a critical gap in medical humanistic education by providing a cross-professional assessment tool that can assist medical schools in refining curriculum design and faculty development, particularly in offering targeted support to students from specific family backgrounds. Enhancing medical humanistic education using this instrument may help promote more patient-centered clinical practice and professional development, highlighting its practical value in medical education.
