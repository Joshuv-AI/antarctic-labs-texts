Discrete dualities for some algebras from rough sets
Ivo Düntsch, Ewa Orłowska

Abstract
A discrete duality is a relationship between classes of algebras and classes of relational systems (frames) resulting in two representation theorems building on the early work of Jónsson and Tarski, 1951 , Kripke, 1963 , and van Benthem, 1984 . In this section we recall discrete dualities for various types of algebras arising from rough sets. Keywords : Algebras from rough sets, discrete duality, representation theorems, distributive lattice, regular double Stone algebra, Boolean algebra, modal logic S5



1
Introduction

In the present article we present the place of discrete duality in the landscape of knowledge and some of its examples arising in the field of rough sets. Areas of contemporary knowledge can be divided into two major groups, namely, science and humanities. Science includes areas such as mathematics and physics, humanities include literature and psychology, among others. Each of the science fields has its own methodology. Methodology of the fields in the science group can be traced back to the beginning of the age of enlightenment. In his treatise
Discourse de la Methode
(Discourse on Method
[
5
]
, translation from the French by John Veitch
[
6
]
, the mathematician and physicist René Descartes (March 31, 1596, La Haye – February 11, 1650, Stockholm) presented four principles that must be followed by researchers

”The first was never to accept anything for true which I did not clearly know to be such; that is to say, carefully to avoid precipitancy and prejudice, and to comprise nothing more in my judgement than what was presented to my mind so clearly and distinctly as to exclude all ground of doubt.

The second, to divide each of the difficulties under examination into as many parts as possible, and as might be necessary for its adequate solution.

The third, to conduct my thoughts in such order that, by commencing with objects the simplest and easiest to know, I might ascend by little and little, and, as it were, step by step, to the knowledge of the more complex; assigning in thought a certain order even to those objects which in their own nature do not stand in a relation of antecedence and sequence.

And the last, in every case to make enumerations so complete, and reviews so general, that I might be assured that nothing was omitted.”
[
6
, p. 15f]

The attention to rigour and strictness postulated by Descartes was an indication of a trend leading to mathematical logic becoming
metamathematics
. Indeed, since the turn of the 17

Nowadays, a commonly accepted and practiced approach to formalization of mathematical theories is to define a class

and

such that for each frame

•

Representation theorem for algebras

Every

is embeddable into the complex algebra of its canonical structure
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(L)

•

Representation theorem for frames

Every

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X))

We consider two approaches to define algebras from approximation spaces and rough sets following Pawlak’s original ideas on granularity and approximation of information
[
27
]
:

1.

As Boolean algebras with operators induced by the equivalence relation of an approximation space. This approach leads to the variety of monadic algebras, which are the complex algebras of Kripke models for the logic

.

2.

As algebras obtained by defining lattice operations on the collection of rough sets of an approximation space. This leads to, among others, regular double Stone algebras and De Morgan algebras.

2
Monadic algebras

Our first class of algebras obtained from rough sets arises directly from the Pawlak’s concepts of approximation of information. Let us recall the definitions of the approximation operators of an approximation space
\langle X,\theta\rangle
, where
\theta
is an equivalence relation on
X
. If
Y\subseteq X
, then

(Up)

\displaystyle\overline{Y}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{x\in Y:(\exists y\in Y)x\mathrel{\theta}y\},

\displaystyle\text{Upper approximation of $Y$},

(Low)

\displaystyle\underline{Y}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{x\in Y:(\forall y\in Y)[x\mathrel{\theta}y\mathrel{\Rightarrow}y\in Y]\},

\displaystyle\text{Lower approximation of $Y$}.

If
\theta
is understood, we will omit the subscripts. Since
\theta
is an equivalence relation, we may consider
\langle X,\theta\rangle
as a Kripke frame for the modal logic
S5
with accessibility relation
\theta
. The

of

is the structure

, where

is the Boolean powerset algebra of

augmented by the

defined by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\langle\theta\rangle(Y)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{x\in X:\theta(x)\cap Y\neq\emptyset\}.

Its dual
[\theta]

. It is called the

. i.e. for

it is necessary that

. These operators first appeared in the seminal work of

(2.2)

\displaystyle x\text{ is possibly in }Y

\displaystyle\mathrel{\Longleftrightarrow}x\in\langle Y\rangle,

(2.3)

\displaystyle x\text{ is certainly in }Y

\displaystyle\mathrel{\Longleftrightarrow}x\in[Y].

The general situation is described by a class of Boolean algebras with an additional operator. A
possibility algebra
is a Boolean algebra

augmented by a unary operator

(K1)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

Normality,

(K2)

\displaystyle f(a+b)

\displaystyle=f(a)+f(b),

Additivity.

The dual

of

B

f^{\partial}(a)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-f(-a)
.

A possibility algebra
\langle B,f\rangle
is called a

if it satisfies

(T)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq f(a),

(4)

\displaystyle f(f(a))

\displaystyle\leq f(a),

(B)

\displaystyle f(a)

\displaystyle=f^{\partial}(f(a)).

There are various equivalent axiomatizations of monadic algebras. We have chosen the axiomatization above and their names to stay close to the logical nature of the axioms. The following properties of a monadic algebra are well known, see e.g.

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq f^{\partial}(f(a)),

The Euclidean axiom

\displaystyle f(a)\leq b

\displaystyle\mathrel{\Longleftrightarrow}a\leq f^{\partial}(b),

(2.6)

\displaystyle a\cdot f(b)=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\Longleftrightarrow}f(a)\cdot b=\mathbf{0},

(2.7)

\displaystyle f(a\cdot f(b))

\displaystyle=f(a)\cdot f(b),

The cylindric axiom.

The

\displaystyle F\mathrel{\theta_{f}}G\overset{\mathrm{df}}{\Longleftrightarrow}f[G]\subseteq F.

The next decisive result is well known:

For the other direction recall the definition of the complex algebra

We are now ready to exhibit the discrete duality between the class of monadic algebras and the class of approximation spaces. Since monadic algebras are Boolean algebras with operators in the sense of

3
Sufficiency algebras

While upper and lower approximation of sets given an approximation space
\langle X,\theta\rangle
are natural semantic interpretations of monadic algebras, we will introduce another operator which can be naturally obtained from an approximation space. Let
R
be a binary relation on
X
, and define the mapping
[[R]]\colon 2^{X}\to 2^{X}
by

(3.1)

\displaystyle[[R]](Y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{x:(\forall y)[y\in Y\mathrel{\Rightarrow}x\mathrel{R}y]\}=\{x:Y\subseteq R(x)\}.

for
Y\subseteq X
.
The set
[[R]](Y)
collects all those elements of
X
whose
R
-class contains
Y
, in other words, for
x\mathrel{R}y
to hold it is sufficient that
y\in Y
. The operator
[[R]]
has the following decisive properties for all
Y,Z\subseteq X
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle[[R]](\emptyset)

\displaystyle=X,

Co-normality,

(3.3)

\displaystyle[[R]](Y\cup Z)

\displaystyle=[[R]](Y)\cap[[R]](Z),

Co-additivity.

Generalizing
[[R]]
to Boolean algebras we arrive at the following definition: For a Boolean algebra
B
a mapping
g:B\to B
is called a
sufficiency operator
if it is satisfies for all
a,b\in B

(3.4)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1},

(3.5)

\displaystyle g(a+b)

\displaystyle=g(a)\cdot g(b).

The structure
\langle B,g\rangle
is called a
sufficiency algebra
. A sufficiency operator is in some sense a complementary counterpart to the possibility operator. It also is the algebraic counterpart of the window modality, which can be traced back to the works of
Humberstone, 1983
,
Gargov et al., 1987
,
Goranko, 1988
and also
van Benthem, 1979
in the framework of deontic logic. The theory of sufficiency algebras in conjunction with possibility algebras was refined and developed further by
Düntsch and Orłowska, 2000
,
Düntsch et al., 2017
, and
Düntsch et al., 2023
,
Düntsch et al., 2026
.

Suppose that
g\colon B\to B
is a mapping and define
g^{\star}
by
g^{\star}(a)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-{g(a)}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
12
]

g
is a sufficiency operator if and only if
g^{\star}:B\to B
is a possibility operator.

The
canonical structure

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(\mathcal{B})
of a sufficiency algebra
\mathcal{B}=\langle B,g\rangle
is the structure
\langle X,R_{g}\rangle
where
X
is the set of ultrafilters of
B
and
R_{g}
is the relation on
X
defined by

(3.6)

\displaystyle F\mathrel{R_{g}}G\overset{\mathrm{df}}{\Longleftrightarrow}g[G]\cap F\neq\emptyset.

Conversely, a
sufficiency frame
is a structure
\langle X,R\rangle
where
X
is a nonempty set and
R
is a binary relation on
X
. The notation emphasizes that the complex algebra of a sufficiency frame defined below is a sufficiency algebra. Its complex algebra
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X)
is the structure
\langle 2^{X},[[R]]\rangle
, where
2^{X}
is the powerset algebra of
X
, and
[[R]]\colon 2^{X}\to 2^{X}
is the mapping defined by
[[R]](Y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{x\in X:R(x)\subseteq Y\}
.

We now have the duality results for sufficiency structures.

Theorem 3.2
.

1.

[
32
, Proposition 3.3.2]
The complex algebra of a sufficiency frame is a sufficiency algebra.

2.

[
32
, Proposition 3.3.3]
The canonical structure of a sufficiency algebra is a sufficiency frame.

Theorem 3.3
.

[
32
, Proposition 3.3.6)]

1.

Every sufficiency algebra can be embedded into the complex algebra of its canonical structure by the mapping
h
of Theorem
2.4
.

2.

Every sufficiency frame can be embedded into the canonical structure of its complex algebra.

While
\langle R\rangle
and its dual
[R]
talk about properties of the relation
R
, the sufficiency operator can express properties of the complement of
R
, since

(3.7)

\displaystyle[[R]](Y)=[X^{2}\setminus R](-Y).

For example,
[R]
can express reflexivity, while
[[R]]
can express irreflexivity. In the current context we are considering approximations spaces, so the relation
R
in question will be an equivalence relation
\theta
, and
[[\theta]](Y)
is in a sense the lower approximation of
Y
with respect to the complement of
\theta
.

Another construction is worthy of mention in connection with the complement of an equivalence relation. A binary relation
R
on
X
is called

, if for all

,

implies that
x\mathrel{R}z
or

. It is not hard to see that

is co-transitive if and only if its relational complement is transitive. A
diversity relation
on
X
is a binary relation
R
which satisfies

(FDiv1)

\displaystyle R\text{ is irreflexive.}

(FDiv2)

\displaystyle R\text{ is symmetric.}

(FDiv3)

\displaystyle R\text{ is co-transitive.}

The diversity relations on
X
are exactly the complements of equivalence relations, and thus they are of interest for investigating structures arising from rough sets. A
diversity frame
is a structure
\langle X,R\rangle
, where
X
is a nonempty set and
R
is a diversity relation. The algebraic counterpart are

which are sufficiency algebras
\langle B,g\rangle
which satisfy

(Div1)

\displaystyle g(a)

\displaystyle\leq-a.

(Div2)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq g(g(a)).

(Div3)

\displaystyle g(a)

\displaystyle\leq g(-g(a)).

Complex algebras or diversity frames and canonical structures are defined as for sufficiency algebras and sufficiency frames. Following Theorems

and

we obtain the duality results:

.

1.

[

, Proposition 4.6.1]
The complex algebra of a diversity frame is a diversity algebra.

2.

[
32
, Proposition 4.6.2]
The canonical structure of a diversity algebra is a diversity frame.

Theorem 3.5
.

[
32
, Theorem 4.6.3]

1.

Every diversity algebra can be embedded into the complex algebra of its canonical structure by the mapping
h
of Theorem

.

2.

Every diversity frame can be embedded into the canonical structure of its complex algebra.

Since the complement of

is an equivalence relation

is the lower approximation of

4
Algebras of rough sets

A second method to obtain algebraic structures from Pawlak’s approximation spaces is to provide the collection of rough sets itself with an algebraic structure. Let us take again an approximation space
\langle X,\theta\rangle
as a starting point. In Section
2
we have looked at the Boolean power set algebra
2^{X}
and augmented it by a possibility operator. In this section we shall consider the collection
2^{X}_{\theta}
of rough sets of
\langle X,\theta\rangle
and define appropriate operators on
2^{X}_{\theta}
. We start with the lattice operations:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\langle\underline{Z},\overline{Z}\rangle\lor\langle\underline{Y},\overline{Y}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\underline{Z}\cup\underline{Y},\overline{Z}\cup\overline{Y}\rangle,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\langle\underline{Z},\overline{Z}\rangle\land\langle\underline{Y},\overline{Y}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\underline{Z}\cap\underline{Y},\overline{Z}\cup\overline{Y}\rangle,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\emptyset,\emptyset\rangle,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle X,X\rangle.

It is well known, that with these operations
2^{X}_{\theta}
becomes a bounded distributive lattice
[
21
]
. Various forms of negations were defined on
2^{X}_{\theta}
which lead to different classes of algebras among which very strong connections hold. For an in depth investigation of these we invite the reader to consult
[
33
]
. In the present article we will consider two of these, the
regular double Stone algebras
and the
De Morgan
algebras.

All these algebras of rough sets are based on bounded distributive lattices, so we first describe the discrete duality for bounded distributive lattices based on Stone’s construction
[
36
]
. Suppose that
L
is a bounded distributive lattice. The
canonical structure

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(L)
of
L
is the partially ordered set
\langle\operatorname{Prim}(L),\subseteq\rangle
. Conversely, if
\langle X,R\rangle
is a partial order, its
complex algebra

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X)
is the structure
\langle L_{X},\cup,\cap,\emptyset,X\rangle
, where
L_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{Y\subseteq X:\mathop{\uparrow}Y=Y\}
. Note that the universe of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X))
is the set of prime filters of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X)
, and that the universe of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(L))
is the power set lattice of
\operatorname{Prim}(L)
.

We now have the discrete duality for bounded distributive lattices and ordered frames:

Theorem 4.1
.

1.

[
36
]
For each ordered frame
\langle X,\leq\rangle

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X)
is a bounded distributive lattice, and the mapping

defined by

is an embedding of
L
into
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(L))
.

2.

[
32
]
For each bounded distributive lattice
L

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(L)
is a partially ordered set, and the mapping
k:X\to\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(L_{X})
defined by
k(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{A\in\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X):x\in A\}
is a frame embedding of
X
into
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cs}}}(\operatorname{{\mathsf{Cm}}}(X))
. ∎

In the sequel we will assume these embeddings for extensions of bounded distributive lattices and ordered frames.

5
Regular double Stone algebras

Following
Iwiński, 1987
,
Pomykała and Pomykała, 1988
, and
Comer, 1993
we define the
full algebra of rough sets
over the approximation space
\langle X,\theta\rangle
as the structure
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle 2^{X}_{\theta},\lor,\land,{}^{*},{}^{+},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}\rangle
, where
\mathbf{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\emptyset,\emptyset\rangle,\mathbf{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle X,X\rangle
, and

(5.1)

\displaystyle\langle\underline{Y},\overline{Y}\rangle^{*}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle Y\setminus\overline{Y},Y\setminus\overline{Y}\rangle,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\langle\underline{Y},\overline{Y}\rangle^{+}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle Y\setminus\underline{Y},Y\setminus\underline{Y}\rangle,

where the approximations are taken with respect to
\theta
. An
algebra of rough sets
is a subalgebra of some full algebra of rough sets. It turns out that the class of algebras of rough sets has properties well known from lattice theory since the late 1960s
[
42
]
, which we shall now describe. A
double Stone algebra
(DSA)
\langle L,+,\cdot,{}^{*},{}^{+},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}\rangle
is an algebra of type
\langle 2,2,1,1,0,0\rangle
such that

1.

\langle L,+,\cdot,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}\rangle
is a bounded distributive lattice,

2.

a^{*}
is the pseudocomplement of
a
, i.e.

b\leq a^{*}\Leftrightarrow b\cdot a=\mathbf{0},

3.

a^{+}
is the dual pseudocomplement of
a
, i.e.

b\geq a^{+}\Leftrightarrow b+a=\mathbf{1},

4.

a^{*}+a^{**}=\mathbf{1}
,
a^{+}\cdot a^{++}=\mathbf{0}
.

A double Stone algebra
L
is called
regular
(RDSA), if it additionally satisfies

(M)

\displaystyle a^{*}=b^{*}\text{ and }a^{+}=b^{+}\text{ implies }a=b.

It is well known that the class of RDSAs is equational
[
43
]
. Condition (
M
) shows that each
a\in L
is uniquely determined by the pair
\langle a^{++},a^{**}\rangle
just as every rough set is determined by some
\langle\underline{X},\overline{X}\rangle
. RDSAs were studied, among others, by
Varlet, 1968
,
Varlet, 1969
,
Varlet, 1972
and
Katriňák, 1973
,
Katriňák, 1974
.

Moisil, 1940
,
Moisil, 1972
proposed a class of algebras referred to as
3–valued Łukasiewicz algebras
, which are closely related to Łukasiewicz’s 3-valued logic. It can be shown that regular double Stone algebras and Łukasiewicz algebras can be interdefined, and condition (
M
) is named in his honour by
Varlet, 1969
. There is also a close connection to Nelson algebras
[
33
]
. A logic for rough sets based on regular double Stone algebras and their representation developed by
Katriňák, 1974
is presented in
[
8
]
.

There is a close relationship of algebras of rough sets to RDSAs, in particular, each RDSA is representable as an algebra of rough sets::

Theorem 5.1
.

1.

Each algebra of rough sets is an RDSA
[
35
,
3
]
.

2.

Every regular double Stone algebra is isomorphic to an algebra of rough subsets of an approximation space
[
3
]
. ∎

The main task to establish a discrete duality for RDSAs is to find a frame condition on an ordered frame
\langle X,\leq\rangle

8
Summary and outlook

In this paper we have given an overview of discrete dualities for classes of algebras and classes of frames chosen according to a twofold criterion. The chosen classes of algebras provide a characterisation of Pawlak’s approximation spaces, and the chosen classes of frames expose a distinctive nature of relational systems which are the counterparts of those algebras. Together they provide a view for strategies of defining formalised theories for characterisation of various approaches to approximate missing information. In future work we aim to exhibit discrete dualities for deterministic and indeterministic information systems in the sense of
Lipski, 1979
.
