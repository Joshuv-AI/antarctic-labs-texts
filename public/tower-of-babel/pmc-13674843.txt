Response: MicroRNA‐210 level as a biomarker for pre‐eclampsia severity: A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Dávid Márai, Isabel Pinto Amorim das Virgens, Ádám Vincze, Mahmoud Obeidat, Péter Hegyi, Mikolt Bakony, Zsófia Réka Erdélyi, Zsolt Melczer, Sándor Valent
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Zhu for the careful reading of our meta‐analysis and for the thoughtful comments. 1 We are pleased that the central finding, a consistent stepwise elevation of circulating microRNA‐210 (miR‐210) from mild to severe pre‐eclampsia (PE), was convincing. The three issues raised—sample matrix, normalization, and the prognostic versus predictive distinction—are all important for clinical translation. In several respects, our paper and the correspondent are in close agreement. In one respect, we would like to respectfully correct the record.

SAMPLE TYPE: MATRIX IS REPORTED INCONSISTENTLY IN THE SOURCE STUDIES, AND MATRIX DIFFERENCES DO NOT ACCOUNT FOR THE HETEROGENEITY

We appreciate Dr. Zhu's observation that serum and plasma may yield systematically different circulating miRNA profiles because clotting can introduce platelet‐derived contamination, 2 and we agree with the biological rationale. On revisiting the primary sources, however, we found that sample type is documented inconsistently and, in places, contradictorily. Ura et al. used serum. 3 Youssef et al. state in their methods that serum was used, yet in their introduction and discussion, they refer to “plasma” and “serum” interchangeably. The remaining studies used plasma but often with reagents validated for both matrices: Kolkova et al. 4 extracted microRNAs from plasma using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit, and Jairajpuri et al. 5 applied a similar serum/plasma kit. A clean, study‐level classification of plasma versus serum is, therefore, not always possible, underscoring how little the field currently distinguishes between the two.

For the same reason, the matrix cannot be the dominant driver of the heterogeneity Dr. Zhu describes. It is worth distinguishing statistical from clinical heterogeneity here because the high I 2‐values we reported are a statistical measure and primarily reflect statistical heterogeneity rather than a specific clinical or methodological cause, such as sample matrix. Matrix cannot be cleanly assigned, and the pooled effect does not behave as it would if matrix were a confounder. The direction of effect was consistent across all studies, with miR‐210 elevated in PE and severe values exceeding mild, regardless of the matrix or reagent used. A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S4 of our original article 1 ), removing each study in turn and refitting the model, left the pooled effects robust throughout, including when the serum study was omitted. 3 We attribute the heterogeneity instead to several contributors noted in our discussion, 1 including collection tubes, storage conditions, the miR‐210 fraction analyzed, the differing quantification platforms, PE definitions, and sampling times.

Nevertheless, we agree that sample type should be prespecified as a subgroup variable or meta‐regression covariate in future studies and that a fully matrix‐homogeneous evidence base would be preferable. We are grateful to Dr. Zhu for prompting this clarification.

NORMALIZATION CONTROLS: WE SHARE THIS CONCERN AND ADDRESSED IT IN THE PAPER

We fully agree that inconsistent reference RNAs undermine cross‐study comparability, and this was, in fact, one of the limitations we discussed at length. Our included studies used three different normalizers (cel‐miR‐39, U6, and hsa‐miR‐103a), and we explicitly flagged the instability of endogenous controls and “the lack of validated housekeeping genes” as “a major limitation,” noting that measured miR‐210 levels varied within the same research group depending on the normalization tool used (cel‐miR‐39 versus U6). We also cited and endorsed the mean‐expression normalization strategy of Mestdagh et al., 6 which the correspondent recommends (our reference 56, the same work cited as reference 4 in the letter). We could not perform a normalization‐stratified sensitivity analysis because with only seven eligible studies distributed across three normalizers, the subgroups were too small to yield stable estimates. Formal subgroup and publication‐bias testing were constrained by the number of studies (n < 10). We agree that unified exogenous spike‐ins and normalization‐stratified sensitivity analyses should be standard once a larger evidence base exists, and we welcome the reinforcement of this recommendation.

PROGNOSTIC VERSUS PREDICTIVE VALUE: THIS DISTINCTION WAS CENTRAL TO OUR PAPER

We agree that the distinction is essential, and we would point out that our manuscript drew it explicitly rather than omitting it. Our abstract states that miR‐210 is “strongly associated with PE severity at the time of diagnosis” and that “whether miR‐210 holds predictive value earlier in pregnancy remains to be established in prospective studies with first‐trimester sampling.” 1 Our conclusion frames it “as a biomarker of disease severity rather than as an established early predictive marker.” Our limitations section states that the absence of first‐trimester sampling “limits the scope of our findings to severity stratification at clinical presentation rather than early prediction” and that any interpretation of miR‐210 as an antenatal predictive biomarker “remains speculative.” We also discussed the soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase‐1 (sFlt‐1) and placental growth factor (PlGF) ratio as the established predictive comparator, given its high negative predictive value for adverse outcomes, and called for early‐pregnancy cohorts to test miR‐210 against it. We share the correspondent's position and thank Dr. Zhu for the Ghosh et al. reference. 7 We would note, respectfully, that this study would not itself have met our eligibility criteria: it profiled miRNAs within isolated extracellular vesicles by small‐RNA sequencing rather than reporting a poolable total circulating miR‐210‐fold change, and it analyzed PE as a single, predominantly late‐onset group without the mild versus severe stratification our PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparator, and Outcomes) framework required. That does not diminish its value as early‐pregnancy evidence, but it addresses a predictive rather than a severity question.

We thank Dr. Zhu for engaging so constructively with our work. We agree that standardized sampling protocols, unified normalization workflows, and a clear separation of predictive from prognostic endpoints are prerequisites for clinical translation, and these were among the recommendations of our own discussion. We also share the correspondent's view that this work should ultimately serve FIGO's (the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics') goals for the prevention of PE and the reduction of associated maternal and fetal mortality. Realizing that potential will require the well‐powered, first‐trimester prospective cohorts that both we and the correspondent regard as the necessary next step, ideally comparing miR‐210 directly against the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio to quantify any added screening value. We are grateful for the opportunity to clarify these points and for the constructive spirit in which they were raised.
