Projected epidemiological impact of lowering the adult pneumococcal vaccination age from 60 to 50 years in Germany
Authors: Oluwaseun Sharomi, Marion de Lepper, Lisa M. Lang, Thorsten Reuter, Claudia Solleder, Giulio Meleleo, Tufail Malik, Kevin M. Bakker, John C. Lang, Rachel J. Oidtman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe epidemiological impact of lowering the vaccination age of German adults from 60 to 50 y was evaluated. An age- and serotype-specific dynamic transmission model was used to evaluate the impact of vaccination with either the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) or the 20-valent or 21-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV20 and V116, respectively) on pneumococcal disease incidence, including invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) and inpatient and outpatient non-bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia, over 10 y. The base case (PPSV23 of ages ≥60 at a 30% vaccination coverage rate [VCR]) was compared against four alternative scenarios with varying pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV20 vs. V116) and starting age of universal vaccination (60 vs. 50) at the same VCR of 30%. Among all adults (ages ≥18) and compared to PPSV23 vaccination at ≥60 and a VCR of 30%, V116 led to 415,452 fewer cases of total pneumococcal disease, while vaccination at ≥50 led to 520,128 fewer cases; PCV20 with those same settings led to 230,950 and 325,694 fewer cases, respectively. When vaccinating ages ≥60 and ≥50 with V116, the projected incidence of IPD in year 10 declined to 22.1 and 20.5 per 100,000, respectively, and when using PCV20 in the same conditions, incidence was 23.0 and 21.8, respectively. In summary, lowering the adult vaccination age enhanced the epidemiological impact of pneumococcal vaccination in Germany, with V116 showing the greatest overall reductions in disease.

Introduction

Vaccination of children against Streptococcus pneumoniae carriage and pneumococcal disease with pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCvs.) has been implemented in many countries to great effect, reducing invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) incidence in both vaccinated children and unvaccinated children and adults via indirect effects.1 However, there remains a substantial burden of pneumococcal disease in adult populations around the world, including non-bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia (NBPP), community-acquired pneumonia, and IPD, such as meningitis and bacteremic pneumonia.2 In Germany, for example, introduction of childhood PCVs starting in 2006 resulted in a decrease in pediatric and adult IPD caused by vaccine serotypes, but an overall increase in adult IPD related to replacement of vaccine serotypes with non-vaccine serotypes.3–5

Based on its incidence rate, IPD remains in the top third of notifiable diseases in Germany.6 A retrospective analysis of healthcare claims among German individuals aged ≥16 y during 2016–2019 found the incidence of IPD was 8.25 per 100,000 person-years, with 956 of 965 IPD cases resulting in hospitalization.7 The overall fatality rate for hospitalized cases during the study period for all age groups was 17.2/100 cases for all-cause pneumonia and 16.9/100 cases for IPD.7 A claims analysis from 2015 showed that the incidence of community-acquired pneumonia in German adults was 1,054 per 100,000, with a 40% hospitalization rate and post-hospitalization mortality of 44.5% at 1 y.8 Thus, these findings indicate that the burden of pneumococcal disease in German adults is substantial.

For many years, Germany’s Standing Committee on Vaccination (STIKO) recommended vaccination with the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) for all adults aged ≥60, adults aged 18–59 y considered at-risk or high-risk, and adults aged ≥18 y with occupational risk.9,10 In 2023, STIKO updated its recommendation for adult vaccination to replace PPSV23 with the 20-valent PCV (PCV20), based on its higher effectiveness and longer duration of protection.9,10 In March 2025, the European Medicines Agency approved a 21-valent PCV (V116) designed specifically for use in adults11; an evaluation by STIKO is still pending. Polysaccharide and conjugate vaccines differ in the type and duration of immune responses they elicit,12,13 but are both effective in adults. Notably, PPSV23 does not protect against pneumococcal carriage, while adult PCVs do reduce carriage. The pneumococcal vaccines differ in the pneumococcal serotypes they contain (Table 1), which determines the proportion of pneumococcal disease they prevent. Several sources evaluating the age-specific incidence of pneumococcal disease in Germany have noted that incidence rates begin to increase before age 60.6,9,16 Of the 26,189 cases of IPD reported to the Robert Koch Institute in 2023–2025, 18,792 (71.8%) were in adults aged 60 and older, but another 3,082 (11.8%) were in adults aged 50–59.17 In addition, case fatality rates and hospital admission rates associated with IPD from 2016 to 2019 were found to notably increase in German adults starting at age 50–59, not just ages 60 or older, compared to those aged 16–49.7 By offering universal adult vaccination at an earlier age (e.g., 50 y), a noticeable reduction in disease burden and mortality could potentially be achieved. The goal of this study was to estimate the population-level epidemiological impact of reducing the adult vaccination age from 60 to 50 y in Germany using a dynamic transmission model, comparing PCV20 and V116 against a base case of PPSV23. Since the vaccination coverage rate (VCR) among German adults is very low (<30% in those aged ≥60, and even lower among those under age 60),18–22 we also used the model to quantify the additional impact associated with increasing pneumococcal vaccine uptake.

Materials and methods

Model overview

This study employed a previously published dynamic transmission model for S. pneumoniae carriage that was adapted to the German context.23,24 Briefly, the model estimated age- and serotype-specific pneumococcal disease burden (IPD and inpatient and outpatient NBPP) in Germany under various vaccination strategies over a 10-y time horizon (2020 to 2030). Progression to pneumococcal disease was determined by multiplying the number of colonization events by the disease-specific (IPD or NBPP) case-to-carrier ratio. Disease transmission and competition coefficients, case-to-carrier ratios, and vaccine effectiveness against carriage acquisition were estimated through calibration of the model to historical German incidence data for IPD and NBPP, and to regional (UK) pre-vaccine pneumococcal carriage data, as presented in Supplementary Table S1. Calibration involved minimizing the weighted sum of squared errors over annual age- and serotype-specific data (2005–2019).25

An age- and serotype-specific dynamic transmission model was selected for this analysis because it captures the epidemiological and biological dynamics of S. pneumoniae transmission, including age-dependent transmission patterns, indirect (herd) protection, and serotype replacement, all of which are central to evaluating a shift in the age-based pneumococcal vaccination recommendations. Dynamic transmission models are frequently used to describe the epidemiological and biological dynamics of S. pneumoniae transmission.23 Dynamic transmission models capture changes in disease transmission over time and account for both direct and indirect vaccine effects. These models simulate transitions between susceptible, vaccinated, infected, and recovered states while accounting for historical epidemiological trends, demographic characteristics, person-to-person contact patterns, vaccine effects on transmission, and pathogen-specific dynamics. In contrast, static cohort or Markov approaches, while computationally simpler, cannot represent the population-level indirect effects that are expected to drive a substantial component of the impact of expanding adult pneumococcal vaccination.26 As a result, dynamic models provide a more comprehensive representation of infectious disease spread and vaccination impact than static approaches.27 The present model builds on the previously published German adaptation24 and retains the same structural assumptions, which allows consistency with prior analyses.

The current study did not require ethical approval or participant-informed consent as no human participants were involved.

Although the current analysis focused on adult vaccination and epidemiology, the model incorporated vaccination parameters for all ages. Pediatric vaccination at a VCR of 85% was assumed to be split evenly between PCV13 and PCV15 among children under 2 y old under a 2 +1 vaccination schedule. Vaccine effectiveness against IPD and NBPP in children varied by serotype, based on published data as described in Sharomi et al.24 In adults, vaccine effectiveness against IPD for serotypes in PCV20 and V116 was assumed to be the same as the established effectiveness for serotypes in PCV13, and was set to 75% for all vaccine-type serotypes except serotype 3, which was set at 26% effectiveness.28,29 Duration of protection was an average of 10 y for PCV20 and V116 and 7.5 y for PPSV23.30,31 Additional details are available in Sharomi et al.24

Pneumococcal serotypes were grouped into 11 serotype classes, based on their inclusion in current and past vaccines, including PCV7, PCV13, PCV20, V116, and PPSV23 (Supplementary Table S2). The classification is based on whether a serotype is targeted by an individual vaccine so that the assessment of each vaccine can be carried out by grouping some of the classes. For example, serotype classes 1–2 contain serotypes specific to PCV20 (PCV20-nonV116), serotype classes 7 and 10 contain serotypes specific to V116 (V116-nonPCV20), and serotype classes 3–6 and 8–9 include serotypes that PCV20 and V116 have in common. Similarly, serotypes in PPSV23 are represented by serotype classes 1–4 and 6–9. Serotype class 11 consists of serotypes not in any currently licensed vaccines.

Cross-country triangulation of input data

German pre-vaccine carriage data of sufficient granularity to support an 11-serotype-class compartmental model do not exist; UK pre-vaccine carriage was selected because it offers (1) age- and serotype-resolved data from a comparable temperate European, high-income setting, (2) similar pre-PCV vaccination history (PCV7 from 2006), and (3) similar PCV13 pediatric uptake. French mortality inputs were used for adult IPD case fatality rates only when directly comparable German hospital-discharge data were unavailable; both countries are part of the same EU/EEA surveillance framework (TESSy), which standardizes case definitions for pneumococcal disease. All age-stratified outputs were calibrated against German IPD/NBPP incidence (2005–2019), so any residual differences between UK carriage and German carriage were absorbed into the German-specific transmission and competition coefficients during calibration. Mortality inputs were applied as multipliers to projected case counts (not as calibration targets), so they affect deaths/life-years but not transmission dynamics.

The use of UK carriage may introduce structural uncertainty in baseline carriage distribution by serotype, mitigated by the German-specific calibration. French case fatality rates are typical of high-income European settings, and the directionality of the projected death deltas is robust to ±20% perturbation in case fatality rate (consistent with the death averted reported in Table 6).

Model outcomes and scenarios

The model described above was used to estimate cases, incidence (cases per 100,000 individuals), and deaths associated with IPD, inpatient NBPP, and outpatient NBPP in adults of various ages (18–49, 50–59, and ≥60) under vaccination of adults aged ≥60 or ≥50 with PPSV23, PCV20, or V116 at a VCR of 30%. Inpatient NBPP referred to cases requiring hospitalization, while outpatient NBPP referred to cases managed in ambulatory settings, such as by a general practitioner. Across all scenarios, inpatient NBPP, outpatient NBPP, total disease cases, and life-years were also calculated to further evaluate the efficiency of adult vaccination. A total of four scenarios were assessed, as outlined in Table 2. The base case scenario used PPSV23 in adults aged ≥60 at a VCR of 30%. Case counts were analyzed as absolute cumulative numbers and as comparative values, i.e., cases averted by one scenario with respect to another, over the 10y time horizon. Incidence was modeled continuously year over year across the projected 10-y time horizon; discrete values at selected time points (years 0 [2020], 5, and 10) are provided in Supplementary Tables S3–S5. Life-years were calculated by estimating the reduction in pneumococcal disease–related mortality associated with each vaccination scenario and translating deaths averted into years of life saved. Specifically, the model projected deaths due to IPD and inpatient NBPP using age-specific case fatality rates. For each death averted relative to the base case scenario, the remaining life expectancy for the corresponding age group was applied, and life-years were summed across all age groups over the 10-y time horizon. Life-years gained are reported as the cumulative difference between each vaccination scenario and the base case. Results

Overall model performance

The calibration and projected incidence trends for IPD and NBPP in the total German population (all ages) are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. These projections highlight epidemiological trends to be expected under the various vaccination scenarios. Overall, vaccination of adults with either PCV20 or V116 was predicted to lower the incidence of pneumococcal disease compared to vaccination with PPSV23. However, the 30% VCR allowed for rebound effects within the 10-y time horizon. Within the modeled scenarios, vaccination of adults starting at age 50 with V116 offered the largest reductions in IPD and inpatient and outpatient NBPP cases.

Projected cases of pneumococcal disease

Under vaccination of adults aged ≥60 with PPSV23 at a VCR of 30% (the base case), the model projected 43,200 cases of IPD in adults of all ages over the 10-y time horizon (Table 3). At the same VCR, the model projected 40,458 IPD cases with PCV20 vaccination of adults aged ≥60 and 39,338 IPD cases with PCV20 vaccination of adults aged ≥50. Similarly, vaccination of adults aged ≥60 with V116 would result in 38,915 IPD cases, while vaccination of adults aged ≥50 resulted in 37,435 IPD cases. As previously noted, vaccination with V116 in ages ≥60 averted more cases than vaccination with PCV20 in ages ≥50 (Table 5). For example, at a VCR of 30%, using V116 in adults aged ≥60 averted 4,285 cases of IPD, 216,718 cases of inpatient NBPP, and 194,449 cases of outpatient NBPP, whereas use of PCV20 in adults aged ≥50 averted 3,862 cases of IPD, 154,783 cases of inpatient NBPP, and 167,049 cases of outpatient NBPP at a VCR of 30%.

Lowering the age of adult vaccination also averted more deaths from pneumococcal disease (IPD and inpatient NBPP combined) and provided more life-years in adults of all ages over the 10 y time horizon, as shown in Table 6. Compared to the base case scenario, vaccination with PCV20 in ages ≥60 averted 14,021 deaths and increased life-years by 11,771, whereas vaccination in ages ≥50 averted 15,712 deaths and provided an additional 13,137 life-years. Projected incidence of pneumococcal disease

Projected pneumococcal disease incidence over the 10 y time horizon (2020–2030) in adults, overall and by age group, is shown in Figure 1 (results for selected timepoints are shown in Supplementary Tables S2–S4). We observed the same overall findings in adults as in the general population of all ages (Supplementary Figure S1), namely, that both PCVs were projected to reduce pneumococcal disease compared to PPSV23, with some rebound effects observed in ≥60-y-olds. The trends were the same across IPD and NBPP. Across adults aged 18–49, pneumococcal disease incidence decreased similarly across all vaccination scenarios, indicating that these reductions were largely driven by indirect effects rather than direct vaccination. Taking IPD in adults aged 50–59 as an example, with vaccination of adults aged ≥60, incidence at year 10 decreased slightly from the base case, from 8.0 per 100,000 persons with PPSV23 to 7.7 (−3.8%) with PCV20 and 7.6 (−5.0%) with V116; but with vaccination of adults aged ≥50, incidence at year 10 decreased to a greater extent, to 6.6 (−17.5%) and 6.2 (−22.5%) per 100,000 with PCV20 and V116, respectively (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S3).

Looking at adults aged ≥60, the incidence of IPD showed a pronounced rebound effect, with incidence approaching baseline levels at year 10 regardless of age of vaccination (Figure 1). Nevertheless, the incidence of IPD at the 10-y time point was still lower with PCV20 (13.6/100,000) and V116 (12.8/100,000) than with PPSV23 (14.4/100,000) at a VCR of 30% (Supplementary Table S3; decreases ranging from −5.6% to −13.5%).

To better understand the effects of different vaccination strategies on the serotype distribution, incidence was evaluated by serotype class and age grouping (Figure 2; Supplementary Figures S2 and S3). For IPD incidence in adults aged 18–49, the trends across the different vaccination scenarios were very similar across scenarios for serotype classes consisting of PCV20-non-V116 serotypes, V116-non-PCV20 serotypes, serotypes common to both PCVs, and non-vaccine serotypes (Figure 2). In this age group, only incidence due to PCV20-non-V116 serotypes was found to decrease over time under the various vaccination scenarios, whereas disease due to all other serotypes increased over time. The same patterns were observed for inpatient NBPP (Supplementary Figure S2) and outpatient NBPP (Supplementary Figure S3). Incidence of IPD by age group and serotype class.

Incidence of pneumococcal disease in adults aged 50 and older exhibited distinct effects of vaccination scenarios in different serotype classes. Generally, IPD due to specific serotypes responded to vaccination with the PCV containing those serotypes (Figure 2). Among 50- to 59-y-olds, use of PCV20 in adults aged ≥50 offered the only substantive decrease in IPD attributable to PCV20-non-V116 serotypes. Likewise, use of V116 in adults aged ≥50 offered a substantive decrease in IPD attributable to V116-non-PCV20 serotypes, and use of either vaccine in adults aged ≥50 caused a decline in IPD due to the serotypes in common. A slight rebound effect appeared in the V116-nonPCV20 and in common serotype groups. Vaccination of adults aged ≥60 had minimal effects on IPD incidence in 50- to 59-y-olds. Among adults aged ≥60, vaccination of adults aged ≥50 and ≥60 both reduced IPD incidence (Figure 2). The results once again varied by serotype class grouping, where use of the PCV containing the corresponding serotypes reduced incidence while use of the other PCV had minimal effects.

Discussion

This modeling analysis showed that lowering the adult vaccination age in Germany from 60 to 50 y would result in fewer pneumococcal disease cases and lower disease incidence over a 10-y time horizon using either PCV20 or V116 compared to PPSV23. V116 offered additional prevention of pneumococcal disease, as well as additional life-years, compared to PCV20. The largest percentage reductions in incidence were observed in adults aged 50–59.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies. Kühne et al., using a Markov model, found that PCV20 was cost-saving and disease- and death-reducing compared to the then-standard-of-care vaccination regimen for adults in Germany (age- and risk-dependent use of PPSV23 or PCV13+PPSV23 with PPSV23 revaccination).32 Our analysis also found that PCV20 reduced case counts, incidence, and deaths, and increased life-years compared to PPSV23. The model upon which the current study is based, in its previous adaptation to the German population, showed that either increasing the VCR to 60% or revaccination of previously PPSV23-vaccinated adults with PCV20 or V116 substantially reduced the incidence of IPD and NBPP compared to PPSV23 with a VCR of 30%.24 Rebound effects were seen at VCR 30% in this previous study. The current analysis confirmed the rebound effect at a VCR of 30%, highlighting the need to increase the adult VCR in Germany. This may be the case in the German context due to a general increasing trend of IPD in older adults; therefore, although a moderate VCR of 30% allows for an initial decline in disease, a higher VCR is needed to continue to counteract this increasing trend.

Our findings should be interpreted within the context of the current pneumococcal vaccination landscape in Germany. Since January 2024, the German Standing Committee on Vaccination (STIKO) has recommended a single dose of PCV20 for all adults aged ≥60 y and for adults aged ≥18 y with underlying medical conditions that increase the risk of pneumococcal disease.33 Despite these recommendations, vaccine uptake remains relatively low.15 Recent estimates indicate that pneumococcal vaccination coverage among adults aged 60–69 y was approximately 21% in the first quarter of 2025, while coverage among adults aged ≥18 y with risk conditions was approximately 24%.15 These findings suggest that a substantial proportion of adults at risk for pneumococcal disease remain unprotected despite existing recommendations.

The rationale for evaluating vaccination beginning at age 50 y is further supported by epidemiologic evidence demonstrating an increasing burden of pneumococcal disease before individuals reach the current age-based vaccination threshold. German surveillance data have shown that the incidence of pneumococcal pneumonia, IPD, sepsis, and meningitis increases progressively with age, with notable increases beginning in adults aged 50 y and older.34 As a result, delaying routine vaccination until age 60 may leave a sizable population vulnerable during a period of increasing disease risk.

It has been suggested that one reason for low uptake of pneumococcal vaccination in adults is the complicated risk-based recommendations, along with the need for revaccination and supplementation with PCVs when using PPSV23.35 Among other factors, the time required to discuss the various options and decide on a course of action is an added burden in the context of the provision of healthcare for the elderly. In 2022, based on the current epidemiological evidence, the United States (US) Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) removed distinctions among adults with chronic medical conditions and grouped all underlying conditions and other risk factors together, with the expectation that simplification of the recommendations would increase uptake.36 As of 2023, Germany’s STIKO simplified its guidelines by removing the recommendation for use of PPSV23 (in series or alone, with revaccination) and recommending the use of PCV20 (single administration) instead.9 Unfortunately, evidence from one subsequent study has shown that the simplified risk-based recommendation in 2022 did not improve vaccination coverage in the US.37 An assessment of the impact of these changes in Germany awaits forthcoming surveillance data.

Another way to simplify vaccination guidelines would be to use an age threshold rather than risk criteria. This would, in theory, have the dual benefits of decreasing disease burden and, potentially, increasing the VCR. In 2024, the US ACIP expanded its recommendations to include all US adults aged 50 and older, based on (1) the availability of V116, which was developed to target pneumococcal serotypes prevalent in adults; (2) the finding that IPD incidence and mortality were higher in adults aged 50–64 than any other age group except those aged ≥65; (3) the observation that up to 54% of adults aged 50–64 had a risk condition qualifying them for pneumococcal vaccination, and that the vaccination rate in this population was only 37%, compared to 70% in adults aged ≥65; and (4) the differing epidemiological profile of IPD among different racial groups.38 The potential to improve vaccination coverage and the ease of implementing consistent age-based recommendations were cited as factors in the decision, along with the goal of reducing the incidence and mortality associated with IPD. A recent study showed that the serotype distribution in German adults aged 50 and older is the same as that in adults aged 65 and older,39 which would support lowering the vaccination age in Germany. Evidence as to whether the expanded age criteria will improve uptake is not yet available, and future studies will be needed to determine whether age-based criteria are the solution that both lowers disease incidence and increases uptake.

As mentioned above, in addition to the target population, the choice of vaccine is a primary factor that determines the impact of adult vaccination. The results of our model indicate that V116 would reduce the case count and incidence of pneumococcal disease more than PPSV23 and PCV20, even under vaccination of adults aged ≥60, but especially with vaccination starting at age 50. This is because the serotype coverage in the German population is higher for V116 than for the other vaccines.39 A dynamic transmission modeling exercise by Kuhlmann examining both the clinical and economic impact of various adult vaccination regimens in the context of various pediatric vaccination schemes consistently found that V116 was more clinically effective and more economical than PPSV23 and PCV20.40 In addition, Sharomi et al. showed that, at a VCR of 30% and over a 10-y time horizon, V116 reduced IPD cases by 9%, inpatient NBPP cases by 10%, and outpatient NBPP cases by 7% compared to PPSV23; in comparison, PCV20 reduced disease cases by 4–6%.24 Currently, STIKO recommendations mention PCV20,10 but the results of our analysis and the other publications highlighted herein suggest the additional value of V116 for adult pneumococcal vaccination in Germany and could be relevant for future considerations of vaccination strategies.

Limitations in this study are similar to those outlined in the original application of the model to the German population.24 They include the assumption of a static population in terms of age distribution, which may have led to the underestimation of epidemiological impact; grouping of serotypes into classes, which prevented analyses of individual serotypes, such as serotypes 4 and 38, whose prevalence has increased in Germany in recent years16; uncertainty around the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on IPD incidence and the implications thereof for the calibration step; and reliance on non-German source data where such data were not available (e.g., carriage data from the UK, case fatality rates from France, effectiveness assumptions). A further limitation is that our base case scenario assumed a VCR of 30%, consistent with current uptake among the German adult population.15 Higher coverage scenarios (e.g., 60%) were not evaluated in the present analysis, because they were previously examined in Sharomi et al.24 As a result, the projected epidemiological impact reported here may underestimate the benefits that could be realized under higher vaccination coverage.

The model was calibrated to pre-pandemic data (2005–2019) because the COVID-19 pandemic and associated non-pharmaceutical interventions – including lockdowns, masking mandates, and social distancing – substantially disrupted respiratory pathogen transmission dynamics, healthcare-seeking behavior, and pneumococcal disease surveillance across Europe.41,42 Since the model does not incorporate non-pharmaceutical intervention-related transmission reductions, inclusion of 2020–2021 data in the calibration would have risked fitting to a transient, externally imposed perturbation rather than to the underlying biological and epidemiological dynamics of S. pneumoniae, potentially biasing long-term projections. We therefore excluded pandemic period data from calibration and instead used 2020–2024 IPD surveillance data as an out-of-sample validation set. As shown in Supplementary Figure S4, the observed age- and serotype class–specific IPD incidence during 2022–2024 returned to levels consistent with the pre-pandemic model trajectory, and the 5-y centered moving average of the observed data tracked closely with the model predictions across most age groups and serotype classes. This convergence is consistent with published evidence demonstrating that the pandemic-associated decline in IPD was transient and driven primarily by non-pharmaceutical-mediated reductions in respiratory virus co-circulation rather than by changes in pneumococcal carriage dynamics,41 and that IPD incidence rebounded to near pre-pandemic levels following NPI relaxation in multiple European countries.43 In Germany specifically, the immediate reemergence of IPD after lockdown relaxation supports the conclusion that the pandemic disruption represented a temporary perturbation rather than a structural shift in pneumococcal epidemiology. For these reasons, alternative post-pandemic calibration scenarios were not pursued; the approach of calibrating to stable, pre-pandemic dynamics and validating against post-pandemic data provides a more robust basis for forward projections. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that certain serotype-specific or age-specific shifts during the pandemic period – such as the emergence of serotypes not previously dominant – could introduce uncertainty into projections for individual serotype classes, and ongoing post-pandemic surveillance will be important for monitoring such trends.

As in the previous German adaptation,24 UK pre-vaccine carriage data were used as a carriage anchor and French adult case fatality rates were used where comparable German values were not available. UK carriage was chosen because comparable German age- and serotype-resolved pre-vaccine carriage data are not available; the German-specific calibration of transmission and competition coefficients to German IPD/NBPP incidence absorbs residual cross-country differences. French case fatality rates are applied as multipliers and are not calibration targets, so they affect projected deaths/life-years but not transmission dynamics.

Ideally, real-world evidence from Germany could be gathered for use in future studies to improve inputs and ultimately make the model a more accurate representation of the German setting. Additionally, we acknowledge that a 10-y time horizon does not provide a complete picture of long-term clinical impact in the light of waning vaccine effectiveness; however, given that vaccination regimens and serotype prevalence continue to change as new vaccines come to market at a rapid pace, a single-decade time frame seems appropriate. Ongoing surveillance at the population-level is needed to determine which vaccines will be the best match to population-level epidemiology over time.

In conclusion, our analysis suggests that lowering the age-based recommendation from 60 to 50 y could provide meaningful public health benefits by preventing additional cases of pneumococcal disease and associated deaths among adults who are at increasing risk but are not currently eligible for routine age-based vaccination. Our results, along with others, suggest that V116 could avert more cases of NBPP and IPD than either PCV20 or PPSV23, and that efforts to increase adult vaccination are essential for maximizing its benefits.
