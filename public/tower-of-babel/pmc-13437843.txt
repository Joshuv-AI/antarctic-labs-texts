The Spatial Diffusion of Internal Migration Across the Rural-Urban Continuum in Developing Countries
Authors: Mathias Lerch, Wenxiu Du, Dorothee Beckendorff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urbanization constitutes the most important transformation in the population geography of developing countries but evidence on the underlying spatial patterns of internal migration is limited. We investigate in an internationally comparative perspective whether out-migration diffuses over the course of urbanization from larger and denser populated settlements to smaller and sparser ones, and whether migrants head to increasingly smaller urban destinations, as conjectured by the mobility transition hypothesis. Relying on population censuses fielded between 1970 and 2017 and internationally consistent definitions of the spatial extent of urban agglomerations, we estimate different types of in- and out-migration across more than 8000 distinct rural and urban settlements in 41 countries. We use spatial econometrics to estimate migration gradients along the rural-urban continuum and investigate how these spatial patterns evolve during urbanization. Results confirm the conjectured changes in the sending and destination areas of migration. At the onset of urbanization, total out-migration is the highest in large cities. The rural exodus emerges in proximate and dense rural settlements and diffuses into the remote and sparsely populated countryside. While larger cities constitute the main migrant destinations in early stages of urbanization, inter-city and rural-to-urban migrants increasingly head towards intermediate-sized and small cities in later stages. These insights are discussed with reference to the spatial diffusion of development. We conclude that the mobility transition hypothesis remains useful for informing migration trends, urban population projections and regional development policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10680-026-09774-w.

Introduction

As the world completed the first half of its transition from a predominantly rural to a mainly urban society in 2007, future demographic growth is projected to be mainly concentrated in cities of developing countries (United Nations, 2018). This urbanization process (also referred to as the urban transition) supports an efficient provision of public services to a densely settled population, as well as economic growth and innovation. However, it also triggers new social and environmental vulnerabilities related to the faster-rising demand for housing and infrastructure when compared to reasonable speeds of construction, leading to the development of slums. There is significant variation in these opportunities and challenges of urbanization across the distribution of settlements by population size and density (Henderson & Turner, 2020), thereafter also referred to as the rural-urban continuum. To better understand this heterogeneity, improve city population projections and better inform regional development policies, we need geographically more disaggregated evidence on the demographic components of urban population growth (Balk, 2022; Buettner, 2015; Montgomery & Balk, 2011). In this article, we analyze changes in the urban gradients in settlement-specific migration within developing countries, as they urbanize over time.

The analysis of internal migration is crucial for better understanding spatial differences in population growth and age structures, which matter for sustainable development (Du et al., 2025). However, research on migration between cities and rural settlements is hampered by the paucity of data at fine-grained spatial levels as well as by the international differences in the definition of urban areas (both conceptually and in the field). Country case studies dominate the literature (see for example Farrell, 2018; Jiang et al., 2022; Lerch, 2016). Although these provide contextualized assessments, the lack of historical statistics limits the observation to partial sub-sequences of the urban transition. The insights are also difficult to compare across case studies because of the use of different research methodologies, data and definitions. A number of cross-country studies have analyzed internal migration from a dichotomous perspective of space, classifying populations into urban versus rural sectors according to national definitions (Bocquier et al., 2023; Chen et al., 1998; Jiang & O’Neill, 2018; Menashe-Oren & Bocquier, 2021; Preston, 1979). This approach overlooks the heterogeneity of migration patterns between settlements.

In addition, comparative studies seldom account for migration between urban areas (de Carvalho & Charles-Edwards, 2019; Rodriguez-Vignoli, 2017). According to international surveys in developing countries, up to one fourth of all urban in-migrants originate from another city (with the remaining two thirds from rural areas; Montgomery et al., 2016). A recent study on total net migration in 400 cities located in seven developing countries has revealed that population change and replacement in large and intermediate-sized settlements was often driven by migration, rather than by natural increase (Lerch, 2020). Yet the study was unable to document changes in the urban gradients of migration over time, neglected the rural areas, and did not disaggregate net migration into the actual in- and out-flows. We lack a diachronic and internationally comparative assessment of how migration gradients across the full spectrum of rural and urban settlements evolve over the course of the urban transition.

The rural-urban continuum of settlements is an important analytical lens because it shapes the drivers of migration through spatially differentiated opportunities for sustainable livelihoods and access to public services (see next section). The rural-urban continuum is also crucial from a policy perspective. In more than one quarter of all developing countries, the most politically, economically and demographically important city concentrates more than 40% of the total urban population (United Nations, 2018). This high level of urban primacy constrains balanced subnational economic growth and an adequate exploitation of productive resources (World Bank, 2009). In this context, migration may constitute one form of spatial interaction between leading and laggard regions and thereby promote the regional diffusion of development (Lall et al., 2009). Rapid demographic growth in primate cities also leads to industrial congestion effects, pressure on basic infrastructure, environmental degradation and its adverse consequences for population health (Balk, 2022).

The goal of this article is to assess whether the sources of internal migration shift from larger and denser populated settlements to smaller and sparser areas, and whether migrants head towards increasingly smaller cities over the course of urbanization, as conjectured by the mobility transition hypothesis (Zelinsky, 1971; Skeldon, 1990). The study contributes to the literature through its detailed subnational perspective and wide temporal scope. We cover the entire course of the urban transition, relying on more than 8000 distinct rural settlements and urban agglomerations, which were defined consistently in 41 developing countries observed between 1970 and 2017. In addition to total out- and in-migration, we analyze disaggregated types of directional flows that shape the demographic process of urbanization.

In the next section, we review the theoretical foundations and lay down our hypotheses about the spatial diffusion of migration over the urbanization process. We then introduce the data and methods and illustrate the empirical approach. The next section reports how different types of out-migration diffuse across the rural-urban continuum of settlements and how migrant destinations change over the urban transition. In the concluding section, we summarize the main findings and discuss their implications for our understanding of the heterogeneity in city growth and regional development policies.

Background

Migration can be conceived as an integral part of a country’s development process (de Haas 2010a; Massey 1988; Ravenstein 1885, 1889; Skeldon 1990; Zelinsky 1971), which involves different types of social transformations (de Haas et al., 2020). The lag between the decline in mortality and fertility over the demographic transition leads to strong population growth. Zelinsky (1971) expects the bulk of national urbanization to occur precisely during this period, when demographic pressure on agricultural labor markets and social systems motivate people to leave their place of origin (Dyson, 2011). The expansion of higher-level education elevates people’s life goals and consequently fuels their desire to relocate to places where they can achieve these aspirations (de Haas 2010a; Gould 1982). Regional gradients in economic opportunities attract inhabitants to more developed and urbanized areas, where average income is commonly two to three times higher than in the rural counterparts (Dudwick et al., 2011; Lucas, 2016). Yet aspiring migrants face barriers to mobility related to geographic distance, poorly developed transport infrastructure and limited information and financial resources. Improved communication, road and railway infrastructures alongside higher household incomes enable people to actually move from peripheral areas (Beauchemin 2005; de Haas 2010b; Massey 1988). Cross-sectional analyzes confirm the rising intensities and distances of internal migration over the course of economic and human development and urbanization (Bell et al., 2015; Bernard et al., 2017; Stillwell et al., 2016) – three processes that commonly unfold alongside each other (Bloom et al., 2008). Furthermore, technological and economic innovations, as well as new social behaviors, tend to diffuse from the political and economic centers to the periphery of countries (Hägerstrand, 1952; Ravenstein, 1885; Williamson, 1965).

Building on these foundational ideas, the hypothesis of a mobility transition conjectures that the regional diffusion of development leads to patterned changes in the types, trends and geographies of internal and international migration over time (de Haas 2010a; Skeldon 1990, 1997; Zelinsky 1971). The emerging movements from rural to urban areas should substitute pre-existing mobility within the countryside and progressively transform into departures to foreign countries until the rural out-flow levels off. Migration between cities is expected to peak later, when these settlements dominate the population geography and interact with one another in the political, economic and cultural domains (Zelinsky, 1971; Skeldon, 1990). Comparative cross-sectional analyses confirmed these temporal trends in different types of migration over the development and urbanization processes (Lerch et al., 2025a, 2025b; Lucas, 2021). The challenging observations, however, were the initially high levels of internal and international out-migration from the (largest) cities instead of the rural areas. This can be explained by the vanguard role of cities in the regional diffusion of development. The evidence also revealed a rising flow of rural migrants towards the countryside located in the immediate surroundings of urban agglomerations (thereafter referred to as the city hinterlands). This observation suggests a spatially diverted move of aspiring rural-to-urban migrants who may lack the resources for establishing a living base in cities or aim for a multi-sectoral (rural/urban) livelihood (Lerch et al., 2025b).

The present study tests the geographic dimensions of the mobility transition hypothesis (Skeldon, 1990; Zelinsky, 1971). First, the propensity to engage in the rural exodus should diffuse over the urban transition from the densely populated hinterlands of larger cities to progressively sparser surroundings of smaller cities and, ultimately, to the remote countryside. Urban-to-urban out-migration should similarly emerge in larger cities and then extend to smaller settlements. These patterns of spatial propapagation of migration rest on the assumption that the push, aspirational and enabling factors (i.e., population pressure, increased life ambitions and incomes, and improved transport and communication infrastructure) diffuse from central places to national peripheries.

Second, the migrants’ destination focus is expected to shift from the largest urban centers, which overshadow alternative destinations in early stages of urbanization, to intermediate-sized and, ultimately, small cities (Skeldon, 1990). This is because pull factors of migration also spread across space. Strong within-country inequalities in the levels of economic development, the prevalence of poverty and the availability and quality of public services exist between cities of different sizes (Brockerhoff & Brennan, 1998; Ferré et al., 2010; Henderson & Turner, 2020). Contexts of relative deprivation in small cities drive migrations towards larger cities with more diverse labor markets, better-paid and more prestigious jobs, as well as better connections to the global economy (Montgomery et al., 2003; Randell & VanWey, 2015). Over the urbanization process, the regional diffusion of development and the outsourcing of productive activities away from the main centers reduce inter-city disparities in terms of residential attractiveness. Intermediate-sized and small cities may become “intervening opportunities” for rural-to-urban and inter-urban migrants alike (Stouffer, 1940), as observed in developed countries (Bigotte et al., 2014; Plane & Henrie, 2012).

Furthermore, inter-urban migrants may leave large cities and settle in smaller ones because of the rising costs of living and the social and economic congestion effects related to the excessive agglomeration of human activity, as well as because of institutional barriers to basic services (Mu et al., 2022; Rodriguez-Vignoli, 2017; Wang et al., 2023; White et al., 2023). The conjectured change in the geography of destinations of urban and rural migrants implies a polarization reversal, from an early population concentration in the largest urban center to a subsequent demographic dispersion within a more balanced network of cities (Richardson, 1980). This process is beneficial for a more balanced economic and social development within countries.

Beyond inter-rural, rural-to-urban and inter-urban migration, the mobility transition hypothesis ignores a fourth type: international surveys reveal that urban-to-rural migrants represent up to 10% of all movers between the two residence sectors and that one half of those migrants actually spent their childhood in rural areas (Cattaneo & Robinson, 2020; Lucas, 2021). In Sub-Saharan Africa, these flows are attributed to return movements in periods of economic crises among earlier rural-to-urban migrants (Beauchemin, 2011; Potts, 2013). Consequently, there should be no patterned spatial diffusion of this type of migration over the urban transition.

The mobility transition hypothesis is rooted in broad modernization theories from the 1950s, which assume that all countries will experience a transition from a traditional (peasant) to a modern (urban) society alongside the above conjectured changes in the geographies of migration. This development paradigm has been criticized for its assumed universal and linear nature, as well as for the Eurocentric transfer to other world regions of the patterns and capitalist ideologies prevalent in historical Western Europe (de Haas et al., 2020; Skeldon, 1997, 2012; Thornton, 2001). Nevertheless, the world has experienced a common set of multidimensional social transformations, including the rise of industry and the service sector, urbanization and mass consumerism, a diffusion of higher-level education, technological developments, as well as strong population growth and subsequent ageing (de Haas et al., 2020). In sum, the mobility transition hypothesis remains a relevant framework for analyzing the interlinkages between long-term evolutions in the patterns of migration and development processes (Cooke et al. 2018; de Haas 2010a; Skeldon 2012).

Two extensive reviews of country case-studies validated the expected geographic diffusion of the different types of migration (Skeldon, 1990, 1997). However, case-studies only cover partial sub-sequences of the mobility transition (due to the lack of historical statistics), and the insights are not generalizable. Two comparative assessments focused on net internal migration between administrative areas classified by population density in 95 countries. The results confirmed that migrants move from less to more densely populated areas in low urbanized countries, while the opposite occurs at advanced stages of the urban transition (Charles-Edwards et al., 2017; Rees et al., 2017). However, movements between administrative areas may not reflect migration across the rural-urban continuum of settlements because they include residential relocations between administrative units located within the same urban agglomeration (e.g., in periods of urban sprawl; Champion, 2001; Riosmena & Balk, 2023). More generally, net migration can mask different combinations of in- and out-flows and is hard to interpret because the determinants vary according to the direction of the move (Hao & Tang, 2017). It remains unclear whether the negative migration balance in densely populated administrative areas is driven by higher out-migration, as motivated by local congestion effects, or by lower in-migration related to a rising attractiveness of alternative destinations.

A general validation of the mobility transition hypothesis requires an internationally comparative perspective, spanning the full spectrum of urbanization (Zelinsky, 1983). We analyze how the propensity to engage in different types of migration diffuses across the rural-urban continuum of settlements and how migrant destinations change over the urban transition in 41 developing countries. While we acknowledge the international heterogeneity in the paces of urbanization and forms of population mobility, our aim is to uncover long-term changes in the geography of internal migration over a standard urban transition. Data limitations prevent us from examining Zelinsky’s hypotheses on international migration, as well as on short-term migration or circulation (i.e., movements over periods of less than 12 months) (Zelinsky 1971).

Data and Methods

To test the spatial diffusion of different types of long-term migration, we rely on census data and remote-sensing products. In a first step, we relate settlement-specific rates of in- and out-migration to the settlements’ position within the rural-urban continuum to estimate the urban gradients in migration in 85 observed country-specific sub-sequences of the urban transition. In a second step, we investigate how these urban gradients in migration change over the course of urbanization.

Data and Definition of Urban Agglomerations

The data are sourced from the URBDEMO collection, a subset of Integrated Public Use Microdata Samples (IPUMS) of population and housing censuses compiled by the Minnesota Population Center (2020). Maintained and further developed by the Urban Demography Lab of the Ecole Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL), the URBDEMO collection reunifies 85 census rounds in 41 developing countries, covering the period 1970–2017 (see Map 1 and Online-Appendix-Table A1). These countries contain 47% of the population in the world’s less developed regions according to the United Nations. The representativeness of our sample is better in Latin America (with 18 out of a total of 33 countries) when compared to Africa (14 out of 54 countries) and Asia (9 our 49 countries).

The main criteria for including countries in the URBDEMO collection was, first, to cover all stages of the development and urbanization process in the world’s less developed regions and, second, the availability in the IPUMS censuses of information on the individuals’ current and previous place of residence at detailed levels of the administrative geography (which we harmonized over the selected census rounds; see Online-Appendix). This latter criterium is key to identify the census respondents’ distinct settlement of residence at two time-points and accurately estimate local migrant transitions (as proxies for migration flows). Information on the individuals’ previous place of residence refers to either one or five years immediately preceding the enumeration date, or to the time of the last change in residence (this latter information has been converted into 5-year migrant transitions). These differences in the temporal reference of migration do not affect our comparison of the within-country geographic variations in the intensities of migration.

To accurately estimate migration between rural and urban areas, we need to define city boundaries and combine the administrative units that are contained within those boundaries. We spatially matched the administrative census geographies to the spatial location and extent of functional urban areas (FUAs). The European Commission defined FUAs worldwide as clusters of densely populated urban centers (i.e., with at least 1,500 inhabitants per km2) and their commuting areas, using a consistent methodology based on the 2019 version of the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL) of the resident population at a 1 km x 1 km grid resolution of the earth in 2015 (OECD/European Commission, 2020; see the Online-Appendix). In addition, we identified other small cities as those administrative units in which at least one half of the population resides in dense urban areas according to the GHSL Degree of Urbanization classification (DoU; OECD/European Commission, 2020), but which could not be matched to an FUA (because the administrative geography is too coarse). The URBDEMO collection of IPUMS censuses includes 1201 FUAs (with a population between 50 thousand and 22 million), 114 other small cities (which add up to the FUAs) and 7507 remaining (predominantly rural) administrative areas. In the following, FUAs and small cities are referred to as urban agglomerations or simply as cities. The predominantly rural administrative units are referred to as rural.

The use of FUAs is preferable to country-specific definitions of the spatial extent of cities because they are consistently defined across the world and encompass the full extent of the population in each urban agglomeration. This includes the inhabitants of administrative units that are still officially classified as rural but are de-facto urbanized and within commuting distance of a designated city. This spatially inclusive definition of cities allows us to focus on migration flows, while excluding short-distance residential moves from the center into the periurban belts of a given agglomeration or vice-versa. As we applied the FUA boundaries as of 2015 on data for all years, we also avoid confounding effects in the estimation of migration that are attributable to the rural-to-urban reclassification of administrative units over time (see Online-Appendix).

Definition of Migration and the Rural-Urban Continuum of Settlements

We define the rural-urban continuum of settlements in each country by an ascending hierarchical classification of rural areas followed by cities. Rural areas are classified by increasing population density, while cities are ranked by increasing population size. To ensure international comparison across countries, we normalized the rural-urban continuum: the position of each settlement is divided by the total number of distinct places within a given country. Values close to zero indicate bottom tiers within the rural-urban continuum (i.e., sparsely populated rural areas), while values close to unity refer to the top of the settlement distribution (i.e., the largest cities).

Internal migration is defined as a move between any distinct rural settlements and/or urban agglomerations. In addition to total migration, we also distinguish different types of movements: rural-to-urban, urban-to-rural, urban-urban and rural-rural (see Fig. 1). Urban-urban movements are further differentiated by their direction within the size-continuum of cities: migrants move either up when the destination city is larger than the sending city, or down in the inverted case. Rural-to-urban movements are distinguished according to the spatial neighborhood status of the sending and receiving settlements (i.e., neighbor versus non-neighbor), as well as according to the primacy status of the city destination. To investigate the spatially diverted city-ward migration, we also differentiate the rural-rural and urban-to-rural movements according to whether the destinations are adjacent to an FUA or another small city (i.e., in the city hinterlands) or whether they are in the remote countryside. We analyze rates of out- and in-migration, calculated by dividing the number of departures by the sending population and the number of arrivals by the destination population, respectively.

The second-step (global) model reveals that one additional percentage point increase in urbanization significantly decreases by −0.0012 the country- and period-specific slope of the urban gradient in out-migration (Fig. 3). This global regression coefficient confirms that the opportunities to out-migrate diffuse down the rural-urban continuum of settlements over the urban transition, from an initial situation in which rates are higher in cities than in the countryside (e.g., in Botswana 1977–1981 with a positive country- and period-specific slope) to a final situation characterized by a mainly rural out-migration (e.g., in Panama 2006–2010 with a negative country- and period-specific slope). This diffusion of migration down the rural-urban continuum is confirmed in 12 out of the 23 countries with repeated censuses (see dotted lines in Fig. 3). A positive global regression slope would indicate an inverted diffusion of migratory behaviors, from small and sparsely populated places to large and denser ones (i.e., country- and period-specific slopes shift from being negative to positive).

In addition to the study of total out-migration, we have replicated the first and second analytical steps for each disaggregated type of out-flow. We have also conducted the same analysis on in-migration rates to appreciate the shift in migrant destinations over the urbanization process.

Results

Changes in the Sending-Areas of Internal Migration

To evaluate how the sending geography of migration changes over the urbanization process, Fig. 4 presents the second-step (global) regression coefficients (on the horizontal axis) for total out-migration and the disaggregated types of flows (labeled on the vertical axis; e.g., the global regression coefficient of −0.0012* for total out-migration is taken from Fig. 3). Dots situated above zero correspond to types of migration that spread up the rural-urban continuum: from smaller and sparser populated settlements in less urbanized countries to larger and denser ones in more urbanized contexts. Dots situated below zero mean that the respective types of migration diffuse down the rural-urban continuum: from larger and denser places to smaller and sparser ones. Global regression coefficients close to zero refer to the absence of change in the geography of migration. Total out-migration significantly propagates down the rural-urban continuum as countries urbanize (Fig. 4). Similarly, rural-to-urban migration emerges in dense rural areas and then significantly spreads to the sparser populated countryside. This diffusion pattern concerns all types of rural exodus and is congruent to our expectations. The change in the sending regions is most marked for the proximity flow (i.e., rural → urban-neighbor), followed by movements between non-adjacent places (i.e., rural → urban-non-neighbor), and is the least pronounced for rural out-migration towards the largest urban agglomeration. This suggests that the rural sending geography is more generalized (i.e., settlement-specific intensities of out-migration are more homogenous) with increasing distance to, and the primacy status of, urban destinations.

The propensity to move within the countryside is also higher in denser settlements at early stages of urbanization and then diffuses towards sparse areas. This concerns the rural out-flows towards both, remote rural areas and the city hinterlands. However, the change in the geographic sources of migrants is more pronounced in the flow towards city hinterlands. The similarity of this flow in terms of the diffusion down the density-continuum of rural settlements, when compared to the actual rural exodus (into cities), confirms the existence of a spatially diverted rural-to-urban migration.

Inter-urban out-migration diffuses in opposing directions across the size-continuum of sending settlements, depending on the type of destinations. At the onset of urbanization, out-migration towards larger agglomerations is higher among the above-average-sized sending cities. In later stages, however, this upward migration essentially concerns smaller cities. This statistically significant and marked change in the geography of migration indirectly confirms a decreasing relative attractiveness of the largest urban agglomerations over time, except for inhabitants in the smallest cities. Out-migration to smaller agglomerations, by contrast, remains more important in larger sending settlements over the entire urbanization process (i.e., the sending geography does not change, with all country- and period-specific urban gradients in migration being positive; see Online-Appendix-Table A3).

The geography of urban-to-rural out-migration also does not change significantly with progress in the urban transition. This type of migration may thus constitute secondary (return) movements of previous rural-to-urban migrants, instead of being driven by the regional diffusion of development and transportation infrastructure over time. Proximity return movements (i.e., to the neighboring rural areas) tends to be consistently higher in smaller cities over the entire course of urbanization (i.e., virtually all country- and period-specific urban gradients are negative; see Online-Appendix-Table A3).

We now turn to the geography of in-migration to evaluate whether the migrants’ destination focus shifts across the rural-urban continuum of settlements over the course of urbanization. The destination focus of migration significantly shifts from larger cities to smaller and sparser populated settlements over the unfolding urbanization (Fig. 5). This particularly concerns the rural exodus but is statistically significant only for moves between non-adjacent settlements. Inter-rural migration, by contrast, focuses on a generalized set of destinations throughout the urbanization process. Rural movements towards the remote countryside constitute an exception: these head initially to denser and subsequently to sparser populated settlements.

Inter-city migration exhibits among the most marked changes in destinations (Fig. 5) that confirm a process of polarization reversal. Upward migration from smaller to larger cities initially focuses on the top of the city-size distribution and then significantly generalizes to all possible destinations (i.e., the smallest country- and period-specific slope is close to zero; see Online-Appendix-Table A4). Downward migration from larger to smaller cities, by contrast, initially targets a broad range of destinations (i.e., the largest country- and period-specific slope is close to zero; see Online-Appendix-Table A4) and later tends to mainly head towards small cities (although the global regression coefficient is not statistically significant).

The destinations of total urban-to-rural migration, as well as of the sub-flows directed to either another city’s hinterland or the remote countryside, remain diverse over the course of urbanization. However, urban migration towards neighboring or other non-adjacent rural settlements focuses initially on densely populated destinations and, later, on sparse ones. This shift in rural destinations is in line with the spatial diffusion of the rural exodus (see sub-section “Changes in the sending-areas of migration”) and, thus, confirms the existence of return movements among earlier rural-to-urban migrants.

International Heterogeneity in the Geographies of Migration and Their Association with Other Development Indicators

As a robustness test, we have also regressed the country- and period-specific urban gradients in out- and in-migration over the corresponding national levels of the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita and the Human Development Index (HDI), instead of the national percent urban (see Online-Appendix-Tables A3 and A4). When compared to the global regression slopes from the models including the stages of urbanization as the independent variable (in Figs. 4 and 5), the changes in the urban gradients of migration over the development process are in the same direction but generally smaller and, depending on the flow, lose statistical significance. While urbanization is of course highly correlated with economic and human development, it also proxies the spatial concentration of economic activities as well as spatial connectivity between settlements through improved transport infrastructure. Hence, these spatial developments seem to matter on top of human development for the changing geography of out-migration. The only exception is the more important role of human development for the diffusion of inter-urban migration: although the rates of out-migration remain undifferentiated across the size-continuum of sending cities over the course of urbanization, migration opportunities significantly diffuse from larger to smaller cities with rising GDP per capita or HDI (see Online-Appendix-Table A3). The shift from larger to smaller destinations in the inter-urban migration directed up the city-size distribution is also stronger over the process of development than over the course of urbanization (see Online-Appendix-Table A4). In other words, socioeconomic transformations play a more important role than improved spatial connectivity for the rising attractiveness of intermediate-sized and small cities.

Analysis of the international heterogeneity in the urban gradients of migration (see Online-Appendix-Table A2) suggests stronger spatial neighborhood effects in in-migration, when compared to out-migration. The geography of in-migration is more generalized. Moreover, the higher out-migration from cities, when compared to the sparsely populated rural areas in early stages of urbanization, is particularly marked in countries of Africa, where migrants also more systematically focus on large urban destinations.

Discussion

Urbanization is an outcome of the interactions between the processes of demographic transition and socioeconomic development. Yet there is a lack of comparative evidence on the underlying change in spatial patterns of internal migration over time. Our cross-country analysis of different types of directional flows aimed at validating the existing but fragmented insights from case-studies on the geographical aspect of the mobility transition hypothesis. We have assessed migration gradients across the rural-urban continuum in 41 developing countries and evaluated how these spatial patterns evolve over the entire course of the urban transition, using robust census data and spatial econometrics, as well as internationally consistent definitions of the spatial extent of urban agglomerations.

The results are congruent to the mobility transition hypothesis. Urbanization and the accompanying economic and social development are linked to shifts in migration origins and destinations. The opportunities to engage in the rural exodus (or the spatially diverted movements towards the cities’ hinterlands) propagate from more to less densely populated sending areas. This confirms the center-periphery spread of push, aspirational and enabling factors of mobility over the course of urbanization and development. The particularly marked spatial diffusion of the proximity rural-to-urban migration implies local urbanization processes in increasingly more peripheral areas of countries over time. The more generalized sending geography of rural out-migration towards the largest cities, by contrast, is in line with their persistently high political and economic primacy status that continuously attracts migrants from all over a given country (Skeldon, 1997). When compared to these centers with strong economic bases, small cities experience higher out-migration to the countryside over the entire urbanization process. This may be related to their lower resilience to business cycles, motivating crisis-induced return flows of previous rural-to-urban migrants to a larger extent.

Yet the opportunities for out-migration emerge in large cities at the onset of the urban transition and propagate into rural areas only in subsequent stages. This surprising result is in line with survey-based evidence on the importance of inter-urban migration in low urbanized countries (Montgomery et al., 2016). It also aligns with Skeldon’s (1990) expectation that city dwellers play a leading role in initiating a new urban focus in population mobility. This reflects how social transformations typically reach developing countries through their largest cities first, which then act as centers from which changes spread to other regions. The historical and socioeconomic conditions in which some of today’s low-urbanized developing countries experienced their take-off phase of development may also play a role. Building on a historical legacy of colonialism, development was often initiated by foreign direct investments in a context marked by hyper-globalization (Fox & Goodfellow, 2022). This mainly increases the primate city dwellers’ aspirations and capabilities to move, as most prominently observed in Africa.

Although the set of migrant destinations remains more generalized over the course of urbanization, when compared to the patterned changes in the sending geography, the results confirm a process of polarization reversal. Inter-urban migration and the rural exodus to non-adjacent cities focus on increasingly smaller urban destinations over time. This further supports urbanization in the national peripheries. Moreover, the stronger association of this rising attractiveness of smaller cities with economic and human development, rather than with the urbanization process, tends to confirm Richardson’s (1980) conjectured relationship between a geographically more balanced development and the diversification of migrant destinations – two processes that mutually reinforce each other. The lack of significant change in the destination focus of inter-rural migrants, however, suggests that this type of mobility is not pulled by social transformations. The similarly generalized destination geography of the spatially diverted rural exodus may be explained by a negative socioeconomic selection of migrants, who look for the most affordable residential options in the rural surroundings of the target city.

Despite our efforts to accurately and consistently define cities and ensure the comparability of the rural-urban continuum across countries, this study also has limitations. First, we were only able to spatially match one third of all FUAs to the administrative census geographies because the latter are often too coarse for accurately identifying small urban agglomerations. To limit analytical biases in the cross-country comparison of migration patterns, we identified other small cities and classified the predominantly rural units by population density. Second, the commuting belts of urban agglomerations may be heterogeneous in terms of population density, which would imply some degree of bias in the definition of rural-to-urban migration (although this issue is minor when compared to studies relying on administrative city-borders). Third, we have assessed changes in the geography of migration over the entire course of urbanization by chaining together (via regression models) the more advanced sub-sequences of the process observed in Latin America with early and intermediate sub-sequences in Africa and Asia. In other words, we relied on the type of cross-sectional comparison that has been criticized in the development paradigm literature (Thornton, 2001). This strategy implicitly assumes that the geography of migration will evolve similarly in each world region. Although results for the 23 countries with two or more subsequent censuses tend to confirm this assumption, we have also highlighted some regional heterogeneities in migration patterns (see the Online-Appendix). For example, the dominant inter-city migration at the onset of the urban transition – a particularly frequent pattern in today’s low-urbanized African countries – may not apply to the urban transitions in Latin America that have started in earlier historical epochs, which were less impacted by globalization. Future research should rely on longer time-series of migration for today’s highly urbanized countries to better address this international heterogeneity. To make robust inferences for distinct world regions, the sample of countries also needs to be expanded – especially in Africa and Asia. Fourth, we have related the geography of migration to the national-level processes of urbanization and development. Follow-up studies should investigate how local development shapes settlement-specific out- and inflows to identify contextual variations in the relationship between migration and development.

Our robust urban perspective of disaggregated flows of internal migration confirms and extends the insights from earlier comparative analyses (Bell et al., 2015; Bernard et al., 2017; Charles-Edwards et al., 2017; Lerch et al., 2025b; Lucas, 2021; Rees et al., 2017; Stillwell et al., 2016). Not only do the intensities and the distances of migration increase over the processes of urbanization and development. We have shown that the geographies also change in a patterned way: migration originates from increasingly remote areas and heads to increasingly smaller cities over time. Our results further suggest that the negative net migration in densely populated areas at advanced stages of urbanization (as observed by Rees et al., 2017) can be explained by lower in-migration due to the increased attractiveness of smaller cities, rather than by rising out-migration. Moreover, there is significant spatial clustering of in-migration rates that could not be associated with similarities in the destination settlements’ positioning within the rural-urban continuum. Future research may investigate the reasons for these spatial spillover effects in terms of attractiveness between neighboring destinations.

To conclude, we join Bocquier (2005) and Skeldon (2012) in arguing that the mobility transition hypothesis remains relevant for understanding past and future trends in internal migration, city population growth and the process of urbanization. This implies several policy implications. Governments should prepare for (and accommodate) a rising rural exodus and spatial concentration of population in remote regions as countries develop and urbanize. This is key to capitalize on the development potential constituted by young migrants who swell local working age populations in increasingly small cities. Besides governmental initiatives providing infrastructure and public services to sustain economic growth in the national peripheries, migrants may also participate to development projects in their sending areas to speed up the spatial diffusion of social transformations. Finaly, the rising destination attractiveness of intermediate- and small cities confirms the effectiveness of policy measures that promote inter-city interactions to strengthen regional centers and, ultimately, support balanced subnational economic growth.
