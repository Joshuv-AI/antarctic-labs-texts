Case Report of a Giant Mediastinal Neurofibroma Misdiagnosed as Massive Pleural Effusion
Authors: Hongqiang Chen, DanYi Lin, Zhizhong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMediastinal neurogenic tumors are benign neoplasms commonly found along nerves in the posterior mediastinum, with rare malignant transformation. We report a case of a 36‐year‐old woman admitted to the respiratory department with complaints of chest tightness, cough, and sputum production. Chest CT revealed a large hypodense lesion in the left thoracic cavity with scattered streaky hyperdense shadows, initially suspected as massive pleural effusion. Thoracentesis failed to aspirate fluid. Subsequent ultrasound and contrast‐enhanced CT identified a giant mediastinal mass, prompting referral to thoracic surgery for resection. The tumor was successfully removed, and postoperative pathology confirmed neurofibroma. The patient recovered well and was discharged. For unexplained hypodense lesions in the thoracic cavity, ultrasound and contrast‐enhanced CT aid in reducing misdiagnosis. For large intrathoracic tumors, successful resection requires thorough preoperative evaluation, preparation, and optimal surgical access to ensure safe and complete removal.Keywords: mediastinum, nervous system neoplasms, thoracic surgery, video‐assisted

Introduction

Mediastinal neurogenic tumors arise from neural components within the mediastinum. Schwannomas and neurofibromas originate from nerve sheaths, while ganglioneuromas and neuroblastomas derive from autonomic ganglia. Paragangliomas and pheochromocytomas arise from paraganglia. Although rare, these tumors account for 12%–21% of primary mediastinal neoplasms, with 95% located in the posterior mediastinum along intercostal nerves or the sympathetic chain [1]. Most patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally. However, giant tumors presenting with symptoms remain uncommon due to the widespread use of CT and MRI.

Case Presentation

Case History

A 36‐year‐old woman presented with a 1‐year history of recurrent cough and dyspnea, accompanied by a 5 kg weight loss over 6 months. Chest CT revealed a large hypodense lesion (CT value: 22 HU) filling the left thoracic cavity (Figures 1 and 2), initially interpreted as massive pleural effusion with lung collapse. Ultrasound‐guided thoracentesis yielded no fluid, revealing heterogeneous hyperechoic lesions with small anechoic areas. Contrast‐enhanced CT suggested a solitary fibrous tumor. The patient was transferred to thoracic surgery for resection.

Treatment

Preoperative contrast‐enhanced CT identified a small enhancing area near the left chest wall at the sixth intercostal space (Figure 3), suspected as the tumor pedicle. A video‐assisted thoracic surgery (VATS)–assisted approach was employed. An incision was made along the left seventh intercostal space (posterior axillary line) to the anterior chest wall (4 cm) for thoracic exploration. After dissecting the tumor pedicle and ligating nutrient vessels using a 45‐mm white staple (Johnson & Johnson linear stapler), exploration confirmed an intact tumor capsule with no mediastinal and diaphragmatic adhesions. The incision was extended to 16 cm, and the sixth rib was vertically transected at the midaxillary line. The tumor was segmentally resected and removed (Figure 4). Layered closure with hemostasis was performed, followed by rib stabilization using a 45‐mm rib claw for the transected sixth rib (blood loss: 200 mL). To prevent re‐expansion pulmonary edema and hypotension, low tidal volume ventilation was maintained for gradual lung re‐expansion. The thoracic cavity was temporarily filled with 1000 mL saline, and a chest tube was clamped postoperatively. Saline was drained incrementally over 2 days. Postoperative chest CT showed poor lung re‐expansion with pneumothorax. Interventions included cough encouragement, balloon inflation, and closed thoracic drainage, leading to satisfactory recovery.

Results

Postoperative pathology confirmed neurofibroma (29 × 20 × 8 cm, 10 kg). Immunohistochemistry results were positive for S100, SOX10, CD34, Ki67 (3%) and negative for STAT6, SMA, Syn, CK, CD99, CgA, HMB45, PHH3 (Figure 5).

Discussion

Giant mediastinal tumors often compress adjacent structures, causing nonspecific symptoms such as dyspnea and cough. This tumor, originating from an intercostal nerve, occupied nearly the entire hemithorax. Complete resection remains the gold standard [2, 3].

Surgical exposure is critical for tumors with limited operative space. Incision choice (median, anterolateral, or posterolateral) depends on tumor pedicle and vascular supply localization [4]. Preoperative contrast‐enhanced CT is essential for planning. VATS‐assisted pedicle ligation followed by open resection minimizes bleeding. Preoperative embolization may be considered for hypervascular tumors.

Postoperative prevention of acute re‐expansion pulmonary edema, along with volume supplementation and intake management, constitutes crucial elements in patient recovery. When feasible, appropriately prolonging endotracheal intubation time under ICU monitoring postoperatively and supplementing blood volume based on blood pressure parameters until circulatory stability is achieved before extubation is considered a safe and meaningful approach.

However, given the patient's young age and favorable cardiac function with good ejection fraction demonstrated on echocardiography, we opted against this extubation strategy in this case. Following mass resection, we transitioned from anesthesia intubation to normal bilateral lung ventilation, implementing a low tidal volume ventilation strategy to facilitate gradual lung re‐expansion. Concurrently, sputum suction was performed and low‐dose dexamethasone was administered for pulmonary edema prophylaxis. Our team referred to Professor Carlos O Encarnacion's method for preventing postpneumonectomy syndrome [5]. Approximately 1000 mL of normal saline was retained in the thoracic cavity during chest closure to mitigate postoperative mediastinal shift. A drainage tube was placed at the surgical site and initially clamped. The endotracheal tube was removed immediately after the patient regained consciousness.

During the first two postoperative days, strict bed rest was enforced to minimize circulatory disturbances caused by mediastinal movement. The retained thoracic saline was gradually drained over 2 days before completely opening the drainage tube. This management protocol resulted in a stable postoperative heart rate and oxygen saturation without manifestations of cardiopulmonary insufficiency such as chest tightness, dyspnea, palpitations, or orthopnea. The only shortcoming in our management was that while prioritizing pleural fluid drainage, we overlooked the necessity for gas drainage. Postoperative chest CT revealed suboptimal lung re‐expansion. Despite implementing respiratory rehabilitation exercises, follow‐up assessments showed persistently inadequate gas drainage. After thorough communication with the family, we ultimately performed a closed thoracostomy via the second intercostal space to address this issue.

Conclusion

Surgical resection is curative for benign mediastinal neurogenic tumors. Preoperative imaging to localize vascular supply, optimal surgical access, and meticulous intraoperative techniques are pivotal. Gradual decompression prevents re‐expansion pulmonary edema and cardiac overload. Postoperative vigilance for pneumothorax and respiratory rehabilitation ensures optimal recovery.
