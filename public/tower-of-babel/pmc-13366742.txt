Tile: psychosocial well-being of men with infertility: a qualitative study
Authors: Gidson Oppong Kwadwo, Florence Naab, Ernestina Asiedua, Josephine M Kyei, Deborah Armah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfertility is a growing public health concern, profoundly affecting men’s psychological and social well-being. Despite its increasing prevalence in Ghana, research on its multidimensional impact remains limited. This study examined the psychosocial well-being of men with infertility in the Bono Region.MethodsA qualitative exploratory design was employed to provide an in-depth understanding of men’s experiences with infertility and their psychosocial well-being. Using purposive and snowball sampling, eleven married men aged 25 years and older were recruited. Data were collected through in-depth interviews and analysed using thematic content analysis to identify core themes and subthemes.ResultsTwo major themes emerged: (1) Psychological well-being, highlighting emotional distress, triggers, and perceived life quality; (2) Social well-being, encompassing lifestyle-related issues, social support from partners and relatives and disclosure.ConclusionThe findings highlight infertility’s profound impact on men’s well-being and underscore the need for targeted interventions, including psychological counselling and community-based programs, to mitigate stigma and enhance quality of life. Addressing these dimensions is critical for fostering inclusive reproductive health policies and comprehensive support structures for affected individuals and their families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27835-x.Keywords: Psychosocial, Well-being, Infertility, Men

Background

Infertility is a significant global health concern with profound effects on individuals, families, and communities. The World Health Organization classifies infertility as a disease that affects reproductive health, defining male infertility as the inability to achieve conception due to male-related factors after one year of regular, unprotected intercourse [1, 2].

Globally, infertility affects approximately 15% of couples, with a slightly higher burden in sub-Saharan Africa, where pooled estimates indicate a prevalence of about 16.9% [3]. Within this burden, male factor infertility contributes substantially, accounting for approximately 40–50% of all infertility cases globally and up to about 22% of cases in Africa [4, 5].

In the Ghanaian context, nationally representative data on male-specific infertility remain limited; however, existing estimates suggest that approximately 15.8% of men are affected, a rate comparable to and slightly above global averages [6]. This indicates that male infertility in Ghana is neither exceptionally low nor unusually high. Despite men contributing substantially to infertility cases, it remains underdiagnosed, underreported, and underexplored, largely due to gendered norms that attribute infertility primarily to women. Consequently, public discourse and healthcare interventions tend to focus on female infertility, often overlooking male factor infertility [7].

Thus, while both genders experience substantial burdens, the impact on men is uniquely tied to culturally constructed ideals of masculinity, where fertility is closely associated with virility, strength, and social legitimacy, making it particularly critical to examine its implications for men within the framework of masculinity norms.

Male infertility results from diverse causes, including genitourinary infections, chromosomal abnormalities, hormonal imbalances, and unhealthy lifestyle factors [8, 9]. Beyond its biological dimensions, infertility significantly affects the psychosocial well-being of men, with feelings of heightened distress, depression, and anxiety, exacerbated by societal norms that equate masculinity with fatherhood [10, 11]. Cultural expectations reinforce feelings of failure, leading to isolation, economic difficulties, and marital strain [12]. In many parts of the world, particularly in Africa, men experiencing infertility often face profound social consequences, including stigma, public ridicule, derogatory labelling, social exclusion, and diminished social status. Beyond these external pressures, infertility poses a significant threat to men’s sense of masculinity, as it is frequently construed as a “failure of manhood.” This perception can engender deep feelings of shame, enforced silence, and identity disruption, thereby compounding their psychological and emotional burden [13–15].

Within the Ghanaian context, fatherhood is not only a biological expectation but also a critical marker of social status and masculine legitimacy. Childbearing is highly valued, and men are culturally expected to ensure family continuity, reinforcing the significance of fertility to masculine identity. Additionally, emerging evidence from Ghana further demonstrates that male infertility is often met with psychosocial stressors, as infertility contradicts dominant masculine ideals and is also interpreted as weakness or inadequacy [16].

Ghana is a strongly pro-natalist society where childbearing is central to social identity, marital stability, and lineage continuity. In such contexts, infertility carries significant social consequences, including stigma, social exclusion, and reduced social status. Additionally, fatherhood in Ghana and other parts of Africa is often regarded as a key marker of masculine achievement and social recognition. Consequently, infertility can cause considerable distress among men, with serious economic challenges and adverse effects on both psychological and social well-being [11, 17, 18].

The numerous stressors encountered by these men at diverse levels, be it individual, family, and social, eventually alter the well-being and quality of life (QOL) of men with infertility. The negative experiences encountered by men with infertility further worsen the quality of some aspects of their lives, lowering their self-esteem and negatively affecting their mental health [17, 19].

The psychosocial well-being of men experiencing infertility is usually affected by the psychosocial stressors that are usually associated with infertility, coupled with cultural expectations and beliefs about masculinity, which further exacerbate psychosocial distress.

Although infertility significantly affects men’s health, research on its impact in Ghana remains sparse. Cultural beliefs in Ghana often attribute infertility primarily to women, even in cases where male-factor infertility may be present. This gendered attribution contributes to the relative invisibility of male infertility, delays in diagnosis, and limited male engagement in care [20]. Overall, addressing the psychosocial needs of men with infertility is crucial for improving their well-being and promoting healthy relationships and family dynamics, since most studies emphasize medical and epidemiological factors, overlooking the lived experiences and the psychosocial well-being of men with infertility.

Given the psychological, social, and economic burden of infertility, more research is needed to understand how these challenges influence their well-being. These findings underscore that infertility extends beyond a biomedical condition to a deeply gendered experience, where deep-rooted masculine norms intensify its psychosocial burden. Understanding these culturally embedded constructions of masculinity is therefore essential for interpreting the psychosocial well-being of men with infertility and for designing gender-sensitive interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative exploratory design to examine the experiences and psychosocial well-being of men with infertility in the Bono Region of Ghana. The qualitative approach was selected because it was particularly suited to exploring the psychosocial well-being of men experiencing infertility through detailed accounts of their lived experiences. This approach enabled the researchers to gain an in-depth understanding of the emotional, psychological, social, and relational challenges associated with infertility within participants’ specific sociocultural context. Given the sensitive nature of infertility and its close connection to societal expectations of masculinity, the qualitative design allowed participants to express nuanced feelings, personal meanings, and subjective interpretations that could not be adequately captured through standardized quantitative measures. Consequently, the approach provided rich insights into how infertility influences men’s psychosocial well-being and everyday lived realities.

Participants were initially purposively recruited from a hospital setting, where clinicians assisted in identifying men diagnosed with or seeking care for infertility; no flyers were used. Selection was based on key demographic and experiential factors, including marital status, type and duration of infertility, age, education, and occupation, to ensure diverse perspectives. Subsequently, snowball sampling was employed, with participants referring other eligible men within their networks. In total, 7 participants were recruited purposively, while 4 were recruited through snowball sampling.

Data saturation was reached at the 11th interview, as no new codes or insights emerged. It served as the stopping criterion, consistent with qualitative exploratory designs focused on capturing the depth and breadth of participants’ experiences. Saturation was assessed concurrently with data collection and analysis, with transcripts reviewed and coded after each interview until thematic redundancy confirmed that the dataset was sufficient to address the study objectives [21].

Study settings

The study took place in one of the sixteen regions in Southern Ghana. This region, located in Ghana’s mid-western zone, comprises 12 administrative districts and is predominantly Agrarian, with farming as the main occupation [22]. The hospital was selected because it serves as a primary healthcare provider for men experiencing infertility.

Target population

The study population included men diagnosed with infertility who resided in the Bono Region.

Inclusion criteria

Men diagnosed with either primary or secondary infertility, mentally stable, and willing to provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Men experiencing infertility with severe emotional distress, who were critically ill, or unwilling to participate, were excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews using an interview guide (Appendix) developed from the study objectives and existing literature. Interviews were conducted by a single trained qualitative researcher (first author) experienced in sensitive reproductive health research, ensuring consistency and fostering rapport with participants. To enhance the trustworthiness of the study, a pre-test was conducted in a setting similar to, but distinct from, the main study site. This process enabled refinement of the interview guide by improving the clarity, relevance, and sequencing of questions, thereby ensuring consistency and appropriateness in data collection [23]. Conducting the pilot interviews also allowed the researchers to identify and address potential challenges related to probing, question sequencing, and the sensitivity of discussions surrounding infertility, thereby strengthening the credibility and dependability of the qualitative inquiry.

Interviews were conducted in Twi to facilitate effective communication, and subsequently translated into English by the research team, who are fluent in both languages. To ensure accuracy, a forward-backwards translation process was employed, and the translated transcripts were reviewed to preserve the original meanings and participants’ responses. Interviews lasted approximately 35–60 min and were audio-recorded with consent over two months. Probing, active listening, and reflexivity were employed to elicit rich data, while field notes captured non-verbal and contextual details. Rigour was ensured through member checking, peer debriefing, and maintaining an audit trail to support credibility, dependability, and confirmability.

Data processing and analysis

Data was analysed utilising Braun and Clarke [24] a six-phase thematic analysis framework, providing a rigorous and systematic approach to qualitative data analysis. First, interviews were transcribed verbatim and read repeatedly to ensure familiarisation. Second, initial codes were generated manually using an inductive, semantic approach by identifying and labelling relevant data segments aligned with the study objectives, while reflexivity was maintained to critically examine the researcher’s assumptions and potential influence on the coding process. Third, related codes were grouped into broader categories to develop preliminary themes guided by the research questions. Fourth, themes were reviewed and refined by comparing them with coded extracts and the full dataset to ensure coherence and consistency. Fifth, themes were clearly defined and named to reflect participants’ psychological and social experiences, with subthemes developed where necessary. Finally, a narrative report was produced, supported by illustrative verbatim quotes to substantiate and validate the themes.

Theme 1: psychological well-being

The emotional distress experienced by men with infertility ranged from grief to suicidal ideation, which reflects a significant decline in their psychological quality of life. The inability to meet personal and societal expectations of fatherhood exacerbated their stress, undermined their self-esteem, and contributed to emotional dysregulation and the deterioration of their mental health. This main theme generated three subthemes: emotional issues, perceived quality of life, and emotional triggers.

Emotional issues

Expressions such as ‘excessive rumination’, ‘insomnia’, and ‘challenges in daily life’ were employed to depict the subjective experiences of the participants. P1, a married man for ten years with only one child, recounted a distressing incident that profoundly affected his emotional well-being, as evident from his narrative:

Recently, while I was attempting to teach one of my nieces, he misunderstood the instruction I had given him. In response, I tried to discipline him physically. Surprisingly, his mother suggested that I should have my own children and exert such discipline on them. Her comment deeply wounded me, and I wept for the day (P1).

P2 and P3 expressed their deep anguish and immense difficulties related to their inability to conceive within their marriage. They firmly believe that infertility significantly impacts their emotional well-being, as described below.

I experience a profound sense of melancholy and consequently take a moment to reflect. I perceive myself as a source of inconvenience to the woman, as I am unable to fulfil her expectations. My inability to conceive offspring brings me immense anguish (P2).

Being unable to conceive in marriage is a significant challenge for both my wife and me! While we do sleep, we find ourselves contemplating the future, as we have no children to carry on our legacy. At times, I find myself pondering who will inherit the modest wealth I have accumulated (P3).

Tinto expressed his sentiments regarding the impact of their infertility on their emotions and quality of life, as follows:

In fact, I became so lost in my contemplation that I momentarily forgot about my physical presence within the car, only coming to a halt at a random location to continue my pondering (P4).

Perceived quality of life

Perceived quality of life delved into the perception of life and its impact on the psychological well-being of men with infertility. Many participants expressed a belief that life has been unjust to them, as they have been unable to fulfil a desire in life: having children. This sentiment is reflected in the following statements:

I perceive life as inherently unjust, and I view it as indifferent to our aspirations and intentions. While we may have our own meticulously crafted plans, life has an innate ability to operate independently from them (P5).

Sometimes I perceive it as perhaps being the divine will, or perhaps God is subjecting me to a test (P6).

I often feel undeserving of any entitlement in this life, shaped by the cultural belief that without children, one’s value is diminished. Recently, I faced issues concerning one of my properties, and when I sought clarity, my spouse laughed and asked who would inherit the land when I die, emphasizing that without children, all my struggles and sacrifices are in vain. As I have no offspring to succeed me or inherit my possessions, I perceive life as having treated me unfairly (P1).

Emotional triggers

Emotional triggers explored the issues that typically capture the attention of men with infertility and examined how these issues impacted their psychological well-being and quality of life. The majority of participants expressed similar sentiments, noting that encounters with children and pregnant women serve as triggers, prompting contemplation of the challenges associated with infertility:

Even when my wife observes a pregnant woman in public, she promptly notifies me and expresses her longing to experience the same. However, I, on the other hand, do not share the same sentiment (P7).

I frequently come across individuals who are accompanied by their children or pregnant women. During such encounters, I often reflect upon my own circumstances and develop a longing to experience the same situation as these individuals (P6).

According to P7, an elder in his church, the sight of children in his congregation every Sunday serves as a triggering factor for him. Furthermore, this occurrence has been known to impact his appetite for food on those specific days, as he disclosed below:

In fact, even within the church context, when I observe the dynamics of parent-child relationships, it deeply saddens me and adversely impacts my appetite for food (P8).

Theme 2: social well-being

Social well-being focuses on examining the impact of infertility on men’s quality of life in terms of their social well-being. Specifically, it explores the influence of lifestyle factors, support from partners, support from relatives and friends, and the process of disclosing infertility. Three sub-themes emerged as follows: lifestyle-related issues, partners’ support from relatives and friends, and disclosure of infertility.

Lifestyle-related issues

This subtheme delves into the impact of infertility on the daily activities of men. One participant expressed how his inability to reproduce has significantly altered his daily life, as described below:

…even during working hours, when I recall my inability to conceive, I find myself contemplating, thereby diminishing my productivity (P2).

P9 and P3 hold divergent viewpoints, as they exemplify an unwavering determination to prevent diagnoses of infertility from affecting their daily pursuits:

I perceive life as ordinary; my current circumstances are favourable since I am undergoing treatment and diligently adhering to all necessary measures. Consequently, I refuse to let my condition cast a shadow over my existence, recognizing that one day, I too may embark on the journey of parenthood (P9).

My identity remains unchanged. I continue to adhere to my usual routines and do not encounter any existential concerns. As a young individual, I possess aspirations of eventually becoming a parent (P3).

Partners support

Partners’ support delves into the extent to which men experiencing infertility are provided with sufficient support from their partners in relation to their overall social well-being. P9 eloquently recounted the profound level of support he received from his spouse throughout the challenging journey of diagnosing infertility.

When I informed my wife of my inability to impregnate her, she assisted me in scheduling all of my hospital appointments. I noticed no noticeable changes or signs in her attitude, and she did not make any derogatory remarks (P9).

My wife and I engage in conversations, laughter, and activities that are typical of couples, which helps me forget about our infertility issue. It is known that women appreciate attention, and since we are constantly together, we engage in discussions, making us feel more bound together (P6).

P10 also affirms that his wife has consistently been a source of support. Nevertheless, his wife remains apprehensive about his well-being and frequently proposes the exploration of different medications that she believes could alleviate their infertility concerns:

In terms of emotional support, my spouse consistently provides encouragement and assistance in problem-solving, while refraining from participating in complaints or conflicts. When she learns about a particular doctor or medication, she expresses a strong desire for us to pursue the treatment (P10).

Support from relatives and friends

The participants discuss the support they receive from their relatives and friends despite their inability to conceive. They expressed that the support received from significant others enhanced their social well-being and provided them with a sense of acceptance despite their infertility, as indicated by P5 and P3:

My family and friends consistently encourage us and offer advice on seeking medical attention. They also support us in matters of spirituality. My family continues to treat me with the respect deserving of an elder brother (P3).

I receive words of encouragement from my best friend, my mother, and my sisters. They give me the cause to be happy and feel as if I have no issues. At my workplace, some of my colleagues also refrain from discussing these matters in my presence so that I do not feel bad (P5).

Disclosure of infertility

Examining the social well-being of individuals, specifically men, in relation to infertility requires an understanding of the challenges associated with disclosing this condition. Consequently, participants were questioned regarding their disclosure of infertility diagnoses to their partners, family members, and friends. Some participants recounted how they communicated their infertility to their family members to liberate their wives from unjust stereotyping.

I did not conceal any information from her; rather, I truthfully conveyed my inability to conceive in the hope of obtaining her support (P3).

Initially, I refrained from disclosing this information; however, I have since confided in a single family member who not only provided encouragement but also offered guidance on potential courses of action (P11).

Few men have chosen not to disclose their infertility to their relatives or friends due to concerns about the negative reactions they may receive upon learning about their inability to conceive. Consequently, they have only confided in their partners, as outlined below:

None of my family members knew about my condition. I contemplated it privately and refrained from sharing it with others. Instead, I sought medical care and relied on prayer. I am not inclined to divulge all of my personal matters to friends. Fortunately, some elderly individuals can provide guidance and advice on how to address such issues (P9).

Discussion

The results of this current study highlight how infertility is often socially constructed as a predominantly female concern; nonetheless, it exerts significant psychological and social burdens on men, further influencing self-esteem, relationships and coping behaviours. The psychological issues confronted by men experiencing infertility include emotional issues, perceived quality of life and emotional triggers. In Africa, including Ghana, masculinity is a socially constructed concept shaped by cultural norms, expectations, and power relations, often emphasising strength, virility, dominance, and the ability to procreate. Masculinity again is strongly associated with male identity, fertility, sexual potency, and lineage continuation [15]. This, therefore, implies that fatherhood is not only a biological expectation but also a critical marker of social status and masculine legitimacy.

Men with infertility recounted a distressing incident that profoundly affected their emotional well-being. This emotional turmoil occurred when they saw other women’s pregnancies or colleagues’ children; these reminders of their condition saddened them significantly, affecting their psychological and emotional well-being. The findings of this current study reinforce other studies that outlined some psychological turmoil that affected the well-being of men with infertility, including depression, anxiety, worse quality of some aspects of life, and lower self-esteem, with general psychological stress and mental health conditions [10, 17, 19, 25].

Many participants expressed a sense of injustice in life due to their inability to fulfil a deeply desired life goal of having children. In contrast, others perceived life as indifferent to their aspirations and intentions. These perceptions evoked varied emotional responses, particularly during encounters with children and pregnant women, which further heightened reflection on the challenges and emotional burden associated with infertility. These findings align with studies indicating that the sociocultural expectations equating fertility with manhood often intensify emotional triggers, usually associated with disruptions to masculine identity [11, 25].

Psychologically, men with infertility in this study experienced profound emotional distress ranging from grief to suicidal ideation, reflecting a marked decline in psychological quality of life and a significant deterioration in mental health. Numerous studies have shown that men dealing with infertility often experience grief, mental health issues, an induced sense of weakness, and weakness of masculinity, leading to suicidal ideation [11, 12, 17].

The findings of the current study further explored the impact of infertility on men’s quality of life by examining aspects of social well-being, including lifestyle changes, support from partners, support from relatives and friends, and experiences related to the disclosure of infertility. A few participants expressed how their inability to reproduce has significantly altered their daily lives, thereby diminishing their productivity. The above finding corroborates other studies where the researchers revealed that the experience of infertility in men impacted their attendance at work, with a broader influence on their working lives due to their diagnosis [26, 27].

Furthermore, the study indicates that good partner support, encountered by men with infertility, causes them to attain good social well-being. Few men recounted how the profound level of support they received from their spouses throughout their challenging journey has gone a long way in helping them cope. Additionally, significant support received from relatives and friends enhanced their social well-being, whereas it also provided them with a sense of acceptance despite their infertility issues. Few studies have highlighted that men who experienced spousal support and support from significant others enjoyed a better quality of life [28]. These forms of support have a positive influence on men and enhance their experiences throughout their infertility journey. Additionally, partner support plays a critical role in facilitating psychosocial adjustment among men experiencing infertility [29, 30].

An important novelty of this study was the examination of the social well-being of men in relation to the challenges associated with disclosing their condition. Few participants in the current study recounted how they disclosed their infertility diagnoses to their partners, family members, and friends to liberate their wives from unjust stereotyping and criticism, then also with the hope of obtaining their support. Despite their desire to receive support from significant others, most participants in the present study reported a lack of support from relatives and friends. In contrast, existing evidence suggests that men experiencing infertility often suppress their emotions and refrain from expressing distress, despite the significant stress associated with the condition. This reluctance has been attributed to the tendency of men to perceive infertility as a personal issue rather than seeking support by sharing their challenges with others [31]. This challenge could contribute to the cultural pressure exerted by relatives on men to have children.

In Africa, and Ghana to be precise, having biological children is strongly associated with social recognition, inheritance, and security in old age, further intensifying the pressure to conceive. Additionally, the sociocultural norms within pronatalist societies position childbearing as a central marker of adulthood, social status, and fulfilment. Men’s inability to have children is therefore often perceived as a deviation from societal expectations, leading to stigma, social exclusion, and psychological distress. These men are often labelled as incomplete or inadequate, and may experience ridicule, discrimination, and strained family relationships [32].

It’s also worth knowing that sociocultural beliefs and norms strongly shape health-seeking behaviours, whereas cultural interpretations of infertility, including spiritual causation, moral attributions, or reliance on traditional healing, may influence individuals’ pathways to care. Emerging evidence has shown that individuals often pursue multiple treatment options, including biomedical, traditional, and faith-based interventions, sometimes sequentially or concurrently [33, 34].

Although evidence indicates that positive social interactions and socio-emotional support can improve the psychosomatic well-being of couples experiencing infertility by mitigating stress and fostering empathy [35], many men remain reluctant to disclose their infertility. Nonetheless, these findings resonate with the experience of a participant in the present study, who reported receiving encouragement and guidance to pursue both medical and spiritual support following disclosure.

Infertility is not merely a biomedical condition, but a socially constructed experience embedded within cultural expectations of parenthood. This current study, therefore, provided more insights into understanding the multifaceted impact of infertility among men, further addressing a significant gap in reproductive health literature by highlighting the experiences of men, thereby contributing to a more gender-inclusive understanding of infertility.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study provided an in-depth insight into men’s emotional experiences and triggers related to infertility, an area often underreported due to prevailing sociocultural norms of masculinity. The findings also captured culturally embedded expectations of manhood, family roles, and social identity, thereby enhancing the contextual relevance and credibility of the study. There are a few drawbacks to this study. Firstly, a key limitation of this study relates to the contextual specificity of the findings, which may limit transferability where the experiences and perspectives reported may only reflect context-bound realities shaped by local cultural norms, beliefs, and practices surrounding infertility. Consequently, the findings may not be readily generalisable to other populations or settings with differing sociocultural dynamics. Lastly, reliance on self-reported data raises the potential for recall and social desirability biases.

Conclusion

Infertility substantially undermines men’s psychosocial well-being, adversely affecting both psychological and social functioning. It is associated with emotional distress, diminished quality of life, and heightened vulnerability to societal and personal expectations, while also disrupting relationships, social support, lifestyle patterns, and disclosure practices. Overall, these findings underscore infertility as a deeply personal and socially embedded experience for men that warrants attention beyond its biomedical dimensions.

Implications / recommendations

Nursing practice

The findings highlight the need to integrate psychological support, male-inclusive reproductive health interventions, and couple-based care into infertility services to address emotional and relational challenges.

Policy

Evidence from the study supports the development of reproductive health policies that explicitly recognise men’s psychosocial needs and integrate mental health services into infertility care frameworks.

Research

Future studies should employ a mixed-method approach to build on the depth of insight generated by qualitative findings while also providing quantitative evidence on the prevalence, patterns, and determinants of these outcomes.

Education

There is a need to incorporate male infertility and psychosocial well-being into nursing, medical, and allied health curricula to promote gender-sensitive and empathetic care.
