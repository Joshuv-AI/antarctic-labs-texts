Development and validation of a delirium prediction model for hospitalized older medical patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yanli Zhao, Yangyang Jiang, Shen Xu, Quhong Song, Dongmei Xie, Langli Gao, Ning Ge, Peng Lei, Jirong Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelirium is a common complication among older hospitalized adults and associated with poor prognosis, prediction of its occurrence is especially important. Our study aimed to establish and validate a delirium prediction model for hospitalized older medical patients.MethodsWe conducted a prospective study of 1067 hospitalized patients aged ≥ 65 years in the geriatric ward of West China Hospital of Sichuan University from November 2020 to April 2022. A nomogram model was established based on logistic regression. Nomogram performance was internally validated with Bootstrap resampling. The final model was externally validated using 98 patients from the Chengdu Sixth People’s Hospital from June to November 2022. Discrimination, calibration and clinical utility of the prediction model were assessed using the C-statistic, calibration plot and decision curve analysis, respectively.ResultsA total of 1067 patients were in the modelling group, 70 of whom developed delirium. The final model included age, cognitive impairment, polypharmacy, sleep disturbance, activity of daily life, malnutrition, and neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). The area under the curve (AUC) of the model was 0.934, with sensitivity and specificity of 97.1% and 77.3%. The AUC after bootstrap internal validation was 0.925. External validation on 98 patients resulted in an AUC of 0.842.ConclusionWe developed and externally validated a delirium prediction model in hospitalized older medical patients that showed good discrimination and calibration. Its application may be useful to identify patients at high risk of delirium to develop target interventions strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06597-y.Keywords: Delirium, Older patients, Prediction model, Nomogram

Introduction

Delirium is an acute neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by disturbances in consciousness, cognition and attention, resulting in adverse health outcomes such as cognitive impairment, prolonged hospital length of stay, functional disability, and mortality [1–4]. The prevalence of delirium in hospitalized older medical patients has been estimated to range from 11% to 42% depending on the population studied and the measurement tools used [2]. Despite the high rates of delirium, this issue is often missed due to the acute onset and repeated fluctuations of the disease. Previous study has shown that 30%-40% of delirium can be prevented, and the prevention effect is better than the treatment effect [5]. Therefore, the early identification of patients at risk for delirium is essential.

To date, various delirium prediction models for hospitalized older medical patients have been proposed. These models were developed by diverse methodologies, including logistic/Cox regression and machine learning. The sample sizes of these models range from 107 to 518, and most models have only been internally validated. Although these models showed good predictive ability, few were transformed into a simple bedside assessment tool suitable for clinical use. In summary, these prediction models have several limitations, including relatively small sample sizes and lack of external validation. Additionally, no prediction models for hospitalized elderly medical patients are developed as nomogram, which has been widely used as a predictive method in the clinical field in recent years [6, 7]. It meets requirements for an integrated model, plays a role in promoting personalized medicine, and is convenient for clinicians to use in risk prediction. Compared to traditional regression models, nomogram allows clinicians to estimate risk by a visual scale based on patient characteristics, which can improve the risk assessment accuracy for non-specialist clinicians, ensuring long-term utility in clinical practice. Moreover, most existing delirium risk prediction models are developed for Western populations, and models for Chinese hospitalized elderly medical patients are still scarce [8–11].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop a nomogram model for delirium risk among hospitalized older medical patients and to internally and externally validate its predictive performance.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We developed the prediction model of delirium based on a prospective cohort study, in which the consecutive hospitalized older medical patients at the Department of Geriatric, West China Hospital of Sichuan University from November 2020 to April 2022. We externally validated the delirium prediction model with data from the Chengdu Sixth People’s Hospital between June 2022 to November 2022.

Inclusion criteria included the following: (1) patients were age 65 and older and (2) had an anticipated length of stay of at least 2 days. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the presence of delirium on admission; (2) inability to communicate due to severe hearing/vision impairment; (3) psychiatric illness or severe dementia; (4) a terminal condition with life expectancy < 6 months; (5) readmitted patients.

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (2023 − 1585). All patients provided written informed consent. For patients with cognitive impairment, consent was obtained from family members. The present report complies with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement, detailed in Additional file 1.

Sample size calculation

We determined the sample size for validation studies of risk prediction models according to the empirical evidence [12]. Previous studies suggested that incidence of delirium in hospitalized older medical patients was 11%-42% [2]. We hypothesized that the incidence rate in our study was 20%. Our study identified approximately 15 variables that contribute to delirium. Assuming a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 0.05, and taking design into account, we estimated the sample size to be 722. Allowing for 10% attrition, we increased the sample size to 795.

Data collection

The potential risk factors of delirium were identified based on an extensive literature review and geriatric expert consultation. At baseline, all patients were assessed by trained research nurses within 24 h of admission. To minimize error and maximize reliability, the study director (YL Zhao) provided intensive training to the research nurses to ensure high interrater reliability (κ ≥ 0.9) and met with the assessors every week to review procedures and check the quality of the assessment for the delirium-related risk factors and primary outcome. Demographic and general clinical characteristics including age, gender, living situation, education level, marriage status, and alcohol intake were recorded. Previous alcohol abuse was defined as the ingestion of ≥ 140 ml of pure ethanol per week [13]. Polypharmacy was defined as prescribing more than five medications simultaneously during hospitalization [14]. Constipation was defined as a bowel frequency of less than three times per week [15]. Sleep disturbance was defined as having trouble falling asleep or staying asleep in the past month. Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients to measure blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, neutrophil and lymphocyte counts. The neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated by dividing the neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count. The BUN to creatinine ratio was evaluated and more than 18 indicates state of dehydration [16]. Cognitive function was assessed using the SPMSQ (Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire; score counts the number of errors, and all scores are adjusted by educational level: 0–2 errors indicate normal mental functioning; ≥3 errors indicate cognitive impairment) [17]. Baseline functional status was measured by the Barthel Index for activities of living (ADL), in which total scores range from 0 to 100, and and patients were divided into the following three categories according to the following cut-offs: independent/minimally dependent (61–100), partially dependent (41–60), totally dependent (0–40) [18]. Pain was measured using the Numerical Rating Scale (range 0–10) via patient interview [19]. The presence of depressive symptoms was considered when the score of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 item (PHQ-9) was 5 or more [20]. Nutritional status was measured by the short form of the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA-SF), and participants were categorized as malnourished (0–7 points), at risk of malnutrition (8–11 points), and well-nourished (12–14 points) [21]. Vision and hearing function were assessed through self-report at baseline [22].

The study maintained separation of the data management staff and research assessors. All the data management staff were kept strictly blinded to outcome measurements.

Delirium screening

The outcome was the development of delirium during hospitalization. “Patients were screened for delirium by trained research assessors three times a day until discharge or recovery, with only the first episode of delirium included in the final analysis to avoid outcome duplication. Family members or medical staff would immediately inform assessors to screen delirium in the bedside when patients presented with an acute deterioration in behavior, cognition, mental status, or consciousnes. Delirium was assessed using the 3-Minte Diagnostic Interview for Confusion Assessment Method (3D-CAM) based on the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV). The 3D-CAM is a widely used diagnostic tool for delirium with a validated sensitivity and specificity of more than 90% [23]. The 3D-CAM is based on the following four features: (1) acute onset and fluctuating course; (2) inattention; (3) disorganized thinking; and (4) altered level of consciousness. The presence of delirium was defined by the presence of features 1 and 2, with the additional presence of either feature 3 or 4. In case of doubt, an expert panel additionally screened patients according to the DSM-V criteria to determine the final diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were expressed as number and percentage for categorical variables and as medians with the interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Comparisons of difference among groups were tested by Kruskal-Wallis for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Chi-square test for categorical variables.

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to test the association between potential risk factors and delirium in the training set. The variables that were considered clinically relevant or were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were further included in the multiple logistic regression model with the backward method if P less than 0.5. Variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerances were calculated to assess the collinearity assumption, with VIF less than 10 and tolerance greater than 0.1 considered to indicate no significant collinearity. Nomogram performance was internally validated with Bootstrap resampling (500 times) analysis. The final model was externally validated using an independent validation data set. The discrimination of the nomogram model was evaluated by the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis. In addition, the AUC of the model was calculated to evaluate the degree of separability. The AUC greater than 0.7 indicates a better classification capability for the model [24]. The calibration curve was plotted to evaluate the predicted probability and actual outcome. A calibration slope of 1 and an intercept of 0 indicated ideal calibration. Finally, a clinical decision curve analysis was performed to determine the actual validity of the delirium nomogram.

The data were analyzed using SPSS (Version 21.0; IBM) and R 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A significance level of P less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 1214 patients were eligible for the study; 12 patients were excluded for delirium on admission, 27 for terminal condition, 33 for severe dementia, 30 for severe sensorial impairment, 15 for readmission. During the follow-up period, 12 and 18 patients were excluded due to death and transfer to other departments, respectively. Finally, in the development cohort, 1067 subjects were included in the analysis. The median age of the participants was 79 years (interquartile range: 72–88) and 640 (60.0%) were male. In the external validation cohort, we finally included 98 patients. During hospital stay, delirium was diagnosed in 70 (6.6%) patients in the primary cohort and 14 (14.3%) patients in the validation cohort. The duration of delirium in the primary cohort ranged from 1 to 17 days, with a median duration of 3 days (IQR 1–6). Establishment of nomogram

The univariate logistic regression analysis showed that age, hearing impairment, cognitive impairment, depression, polypharmacy, sleep disturbance, ADL impairment, malnutrition, NLR and BUN to creatinine ratio were significantly correlated with delirium. The collinearity diagnostic analysis showed that the VIFs of those risk factors were less than 10, suggesting that there is no strong indication of multicollinearity among variables. Thereafter, above variables were put into multiple logistic regression analysis using the backward method. The results exhibited that age, cognitive impairment, polypharmacy, sleep disturbance, ADL impairment, malnutrition, and NLR were chosen to construct the final model (Table 2).

Assessment of predictive nomogram

The AUC of the nomogram was 0.934 (95% CI: 0.914–0.954) for the training set (Fig. 2A). The sensitivity and specificity of the nomogram were 97.1% and 77.3%, respectively. External validation was performed using external hospital data with the AUC of 0.842 (Fig. 2B), and internal validation used bootstrap validation with the AUC of 0.925, suggesting the model’s good discrimination.

To check the consistency of this model, calibration curves was used. As shown in Fig. 3, the calibration curves of the model in both the development and validation sets were plotted. The calibration curves showed that the probability of delirium predicted by the nomogram is satisfactorily matched.

Construction of a webserver of the nomogram

Online dynamic nomograms based on our study were constructed for delirium prediction (https://deliriumpredictionmodel.shinyapps.io/DynNomapp/). By typing in the variables, predicted delirium rate with 95% CI can be output in the webserver.

Discussion

Delirium, a common complication among hospitalized older adults, carries significant negative implications for patient outcomes. Despite its prevalence and impact, delirium often remains underrecognized in clinical practice due to its complex and fluctuating presentation. This study, encompassing 1067 hospitalized older adults, aimed to identify risk factors associated with delirium incidence during hospitalization. The final sample size exceeded the initially calculated sample size (795 patients) due to the observed incidence of delirium in our cohort (6.6%) was lower than the hypothesized incidence of 20% used in the power analysis. To maintain the statistical power for identifying risk of delirium, we extended the sample size to ensure an adequate number of outcome events. We found that age, cognitive impairment, polypharmacy, sleep disturbance, ADL impairment, malnutrition, and NLR were independently associated with increased delirium risk. Based on these variables, we developed a nomogram model exhibiting good predictive performance. The model achieved an AUC of 0.934, with sensitivity and specificity of 97.1% and 77.3%, respectively. Internal bootstrap validation yielded an AUC of 0.925, while external validation on 98 patients resulted in an AUC of 0.842. These findings suggest the potential utility of our nomogram for clinical risk stratification and delirium prevention efforts.

As we known, automated models based on the growth of healthcare big data enable timely, large-scale delirium risk screening [25]. However, their application relies on well-established electronic health record systems and automated algorithms, which are not yet universally available in many china hospitals, especially in developing regions or primary care settings. Thus, we developed nomogram model, which can be applied in different clinical settings and is visual and intuitive, allowing clinicians to quickly calculate risk probability. Clinicians have enough time to assess delirium risk using the nomogram, because most older patients are admitted through outpatient clinics and these risk factors are routinely assessed by nurses at admission. Compared with previous delirium risk prediction models for hospitalized older medical patients, our prediction model has a relatively higher AUC in internal and external validations, indicating better predictive performance. Importantly, it is the first time to develop nomogram model for delirium risk in Chinese hospitalized older medical patients. Soon, the nomogram model needs to be further validated in multi-center and diverse settings. Meanwhile, it is necessary to integrate the model into electronic health records for automated risk calculation to facilitate the screening of individual with high risk of delirium.

In previous studies, delirium is reported to be present in up to 20% of medical in patients aged 65 years or older [2]. Howerver, our study found a considerably lower incidence of delirium (6.6%). The difference may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, different assessment tools for delirium were used in various studies, resulting in significant differences in delirium incidence rates. Secondly, admissions from outpatient clinics are still higher than those from emergency departments, leading to a relatively lower incidence of delirium. Compared with patients admitted through outpatient clinics, those admitted through emergency departments are more prone to developing delirium due to they were typically present with acute and severe conditions. Moreover, the Geriatrics Department of West China Hospital have established the first delirium prevention and management models based on the Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) strategies in China, which has been proven to reduce the incidence of delirium [26].

Cognitive impairment emerged as an independent risk factor for delirium, with affected individuals being 4.16 times more likely to develop delirium than those with normal cognitive function. This finding is consistent with previous study by Iseli et al. [27] in older patients admitted to a general medical unit. Cognitive impairment or dementia may decrease brain resilience to stress, leading to neuroendocrine dysfunction and inflammation, thereby increasing delirium risk [28]. Preoperative cognitive training, as demonstrated by Humedan et al. [29] has shown effectiveness in preventing postoperative delirium in older non-cardiac surgery patients. Although the clinical settings in previous research differ from those in our study, the findings still suggest that cognitive interventions have potential efficacy in preventing delirium.

Age was identified as another critical risk factor. Our findings reveal that each additional year of age increases the risk of delirium by 1.047 times, consistent with prior studies by Yang et al. [30] Martinez et al. [31], and Douglas et al. [32]. Aging diminishes physiological and cognitive functions and reduces compensatory mechanisms, rendering the older more susceptible to stress and delirium.

We observed that malnutrition was a independent risk factor for dilirium. This finding is consistent with research by Mazzola et al. [33] and Chu et al. [34]. Malnutrition leads to greater risk of poor outcomes such as muscle weakness, impaired immune function, depression, functional impairment, and cognitive impairment, all of which are known as risk factors for delirium [35, 36]. In addition, the brain’s high metabolism demands substantial nutrition. Thus, older patients with malnutrition are more prone to delirium. Furthermore, previous studies have found that nutritional intervention can mitigate the risk of delirium [37, 38].

ADL impairment, which is relatively common among older patients, was also identified as a significant risk factor. Patients with ADL impairment had a significantly higher rate of delirium compared to those capable of self-care, with an 8.136 times increased risk. This finding is supported by studies by Kobayashi et al. [39] and Van et al. [40], as well as meta-analytic results by Ahmed et al. [41]. Early identification and intervention for ADL impairment may prevent its progression and improve quality of life [42, 43].

Sleep disturbance was more prevalent in the delirium group and confirmed as an independent risk factor. This finding is consistent with research by Sveinsson et al. [44] and Helton et al. [46]. Disrupted sleep cycles may impair central nervous system function, potentially causing cognitive impairment or delirium [46, 47]. Improving sleep quality can be an effective preventive strategy, as demonstrated in an RCT involving 646 ICU patients [48].

Polypharmacy was prevalent among older patients with comorbidities, with 53.6% using multiple drugs and having significantly higher rates of delirium. Hein et al. [49] and Best et al. [50] linked polypharmacy to an increased risk of delirium. Clinicians should monitor and adjust inappropriate medication use to minimize adverse reactions, particularly for drugs that increase the risk of liver and kidney function, as well as antipsychotics and sedatives.

NLR, a non-specific inflammatory marker, emerged as an independent risk factor for delirium. Elevated NLR levels have been shown to predict delirium risk in stroke patients [51]. Elevated NLR has emerged as a potential predictor of delirium risk in hospitalized patients. Studies by Kotfis et al. [52] and Reznik et al. [53] demonstrated that higher NLR levels are associated with increased delirium incidence in acute ischemic stroke and brain hemorrhage patients, respectively. This link may be explained by inflammation’s role in destabilizing atherosclerotic plaques, leading to microthrombi formation and cognitive impairment, as well as its direct neurotoxic effects. Therefore, NLR could serve as a valuable screening tool for identifying high-risk delirium patients.

While previous research suggests disease severity as a risk factor for delirium [54], we chose not to include it as a predictor in our model. This decision was informed by findings from our earlier models that showed no significant association between the number of diseases or comorbidity indices and delirium incidence. Moreover, incorporating detailed assessments of disease severity would have increased the questionnaire’s complexity and time required for administration, potentially affecting its practicality for clinical implementation. Despite this exclusion, our predictive model exhibited good discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical applicability, suggesting that other factors included in the model effectively capture the influence of disease severity on delirium risk.

Our study showed that there were significant differences in some characteristics among primary and external validation cohorts. One plausible explanation could be the distinct institutional characteristics of the two hospitals. The West China Hospital, a tertiary referral center, tends to admit patients with more complex geriatric diseases, while the Chengdu Sixth People’s Hospital, serving community-based adult, has a higher proportion of patients with milder chronic diseases. Despite these differences, the model maintained acceptable discriminative performance in the external validation cohort. This suggests that the model has good extrapolateability and clinical applicability.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, we only assessed risk factors within 24 h of admission without dynamic observation of these risk factors, potentially affecting the model’s predictive accuracy over time. Future research should explore dynamic models incorporating real-time data to enhance its predictive accuracy. A prospective cohort study by Fan et al. [55] in ICU patients illustrates a promising approach in this regard. Secondly, we externally validated the prediction model in a single-center. The predictive performance of the model for use in other hospitals needs further external validation. Thirdly, patients with severe dementia, severe sensorial impairment, or psychiatric illness were excluded because these subjects are unable to complete questionnaires. Moreover, the current webserver of model has Chinese-only version due to it has not yet been widely used in clinical practice. Thus, an English version of the webserver needs to be developed for global use.

Conclusions

In this prospective study, we developed a nomogram model for delirium risk among hospitalized older medical patients showing promising calibration and discrimination in internal and external validations. The results suggest that application of the prediction model may facilitate the development of targeted intervention strategies in clinical practice. Future research should prioritize continuous evaluation and adaptation of predictive models, broadening their application to different settings and incorporating dynamic data may improve the model’s predictive precision. Geriatric syndromes share overlapping pathophysiological pathways and the risk factors. Integrating their assessments using dynamic data can capture their interdependencies and provide a holistic risk profile.
