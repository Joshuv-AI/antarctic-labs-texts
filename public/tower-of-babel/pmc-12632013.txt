Correlation between e-cigarette use and salivary flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity: a cross-sectional pilot study
Authors: May Alsenani, Loulwah Alothman, Jawaher Alsenaidi, Alhanouf Alnowaiser, Maha Aldawsari, Norah Alharbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of e-cigarette use has increased rapidly among youth and adults, partly owing to its advertising as a safer smoking cessation aid. In this cross-sectional pilot study, we aimed to explore the effects of e-smoking, including e-cigarette smoking and nonsmoking, on salivary flow rate, pH, buffering capacity, and salivary consistency.MethodsParticipants aged 18–45 years at the Dental University Hospital who were healthy and with no systemic or salivary gland diseases were included. Saliva samples were collected and analysed using the Saliva-Check Buffer test. Twenty e-smokers and nineteen nonsmokers were recruited as the comparison groups.ResultsAll vapers had low resting saliva (χ² = 16.467, p = 0.000), with higher rates of low buffering capacity (36.8% vs. 1.2%, p = 0.000) and acidic pH (92.9% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.000). Vapers also had reduced saliva flow and altered saliva texture (p = 0.000). These findings indicate that vaping is associated with reduced saliva production, decreased buffering capacity, increased oral acidity, and altered saliva texture.ConclusionsThe study results show that e-cigarettes have a direct effect on saliva. Saliva check kits can serve as educational tools in clinics to help dentists advise and educate e-cigarette smokers about the oral health effects of e-smoking.Keywords: (MeSH): electronic nicotine delivery systems, Nicotine, Salivation, Oral health, Salivary glands

Background

Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) are devices designed to vaporize nicotine solutions infused with various liquid flavours. Invented in 2003, the Chinese pharmacist Hon Lik intended to provide a cleaner and safer way to consume nicotine [1]. Over time, e-cigarettes have gained popularity among both younger and older populations, largely owing to their perceived safety as smoking cessation aids. This led to an increase in studies aimed at assessing the safety, health implications, and societal impact of e-cigarettes [2]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention have emphasized the need for caution, highlighting the knowledge gap regarding the long-term health effects of e-cigarette use, despite its potential as a less harmful alternative to traditional smoking [3].

Numerous studies have shown that e-cigarettes pose health risks, primarily owing to the nicotine content and harmful chemicals in e-liquid flavours. Nicotine is well known for its negative effects on the cardiovascular and endocrine systems, including increased heart rate and blood pressure. Moreover, nicotine is classified as a carcinogen and has been linked to the development of conditions such as lung cancer [4, 5].

Moreover, several studies have evaluated the effects of e-cigarette use on oral health. Pushalkar et al. (2020) reported that e-cigarette use alters the oral microbiome, promoting dysbiosis and inflammation [6]. Rouabhia et al. (2017) demonstrated that e-cigarette aerosol exposure impairs oral epithelial cell function and wound healing [7]. Alqahtani et al. (2022) observed increased periodontal inflammation among e-cigarette users compared to nonsmokers [8]. These studies suggest that while e-cigarettes are often marketed as safer alternatives, they pose significant oral health risks, including changes in salivary composition, increased inflammation, and altered microbial communities.

Clinical and epidemiological studies have consistently confirmed the adverse effects of nicotine on oral health, including delayed wound healing, increased susceptibility to periodontitis, and an increased incidence of premalignant and malignant oral lesions [9]. Research has also shown that traditional smoking reduces the salivary flow rate and buffering capacity, which can lead to elevated levels of harmful microorganisms such as Lactobacillus, Candida albicans, and Streptococcus mutans [10–13]. These changes in saliva reduce the ability of the oral cavity to protect against microorganisms, toxins, and oxidants.

Clinically, identifying changes in salivary flow, pH, and buffering capacity among e-cigarette users is critical for guiding dental practice. Understanding these alterations enables clinicians to tailor preventive strategies, such as recommending saliva-stimulating agents, fluoride treatments, dietary counseling, and enhanced oral hygiene measures. Moreover, salivary testing can serve as an educational tool to increase patient awareness about the potential oral health risks associated with vaping, promoting behavior change and smoking cessation efforts. Early detection and management of salivary alterations can help reduce the risk of dental caries, periodontal disease, and mucosal infections, ultimately improving patient outcomes. However, there remains a lack of focused research on how vaping specifically affects salivary flow, pH, buffering capacity, and consistency, areas our study seeks to explore.The aim of the study is to investigate the possible effect of e-cigarettes on salivary flow rate, pH, buffering capacity and saliva consistency.

Methods

Approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB)/Ethical Committee of the Dental College at King Saud University was obtained before the recruitment of participants (IRB Approval No. E-20–5272).

Participants and study design

Thirty-nine individuals participated in this cross-sectional pilot study, including 20 e-cigarette users (e-smokers) and 19 nonsmokers (control group). Participants were aged 18–45 years, free from systemic and salivary gland diseases, and provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included a history of radiotherapy, alcohol consumption, smokeless tobacco use, and any systemic or salivary gland conditions.

All participants underwent a clinical periodontal examination before enrollment. Only individuals without active periodontal disease—defined by the absence of gingival inflammation, bleeding on probing, periodontal pockets, or clinical attachment loss were included to minimize potential confounding effects on salivary parameters.

Saliva collection protocol

Saliva samples were collected between 9:00 a.m. and 12:00 p.m. to control for circadian variations in salivary secretion. Participants were instructed to abstain from eating, drinking, chewing gum, or smoking for at least one hour before the procedure.

Resting (unstimulated) saliva refers to the saliva secreted without any external stimulation, such as chewing or taste.It was collected following the Malmô University Faculty of Odontology protocol [14]. Participants were seated upright in a dental chair and asked to tilt their heads forward, allowing saliva to accumulate naturally in the mouth without stimulation (no chewing or talking). They then expectorated into a sterile collection tube every 60 s for 5 min. The total volume was measured and converted to mL/min to determine the resting salivary flow rate. Reference values used were from Ericson & Hardwick [15] as follow: >0.25 mL/min (normal), 0.1–0.25 mL/min (low), and < 0.1 mL/min (very low). Stimulated salivary flow is produced in response to mechanical or gustatory stimulation and was assessed using mechanical stimulation by chewing wax, following the standardized protocol provided in the Saliva-Check Buffer® kit. Participants were instructed to chew a provided piece of paraffin wax for 30 s and then expectorate the initial saliva into a collection cup. This was followed by continued chewing of the wax for an additional five minutes, during which participants were asked to expectorate every 15–20 s into the same container.Reference values for stimulated salivary flow, as outlined by the manufacturer, were as follows: >5 ml(normal), 3.5 −5 ml (low), < 3.5 ml (very low).

Saliva analysis

Salivary pH and buffering capacity were measured using the Saliva-Check Buffer test (GC America, Illinois, USA), a validated chairside diagnostic tool. A small disposable pipette was used to transfer saliva onto colorimetric test strips. For pH assessment, one drop of saliva was applied to the pH pad, and the resulting color after 10 s was compared to a standardized chart. Buffering capacity was evaluated by applying saliva to three test pads, draining excess fluid, and reading the color change after 2 min based on the manufacturer’s chart.

Salivary consistency was visually assessed during collection and categorized as watery (normal), sticky-frothy, or frothy-bubbly, per the test kit’s guidelines.

Two trained examiners independently recorded the salivary flow rate, pH, buffering capacity, and consistency to minimize observer bias. Inter-examiner reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient [16].

The data were analysed using SPSS version 26.0 statistical software. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to describe categorical outcome variables. Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to assess the associations between the categorical outcome variables. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The key findings were as follows: All vapers (100%) had low resting saliva, whereas the control group had no cases of low resting saliva. Among those with normal resting saliva, only 29.6% were vapers, whereas 70.4% were in the control group. This association was significant (χ² = 16.467, p = 0.000), indicating that vaping was strongly associated with reduced resting saliva (Fig. 1).

Comparison of resting saliva between e-cigarette smokers and nonsmokers

The buffering capacity was lower in vapers, with 36.8% classified as low compared with 1.2% in the control group. Only 12.5% of the vapers had a normal buffering capacity, whereas 87.5% of those with normal buffering capacity were from the control group. The difference was significant (t test, p = 0.000), suggesting that vaping negatively affects the mouth’s ability to neutralize acids (Fig. 2).

Comparison of buffering capacity between e-cigarette smokers and nonsmokers

A significant portion of vapers (92.9%) had a moderately acidic pH, whereas only 7.1% of such cases were in the control group. Moreover, 72% of those with a healthy pH were from the control group, whereas only 28% were from the vaper group. This difference was significant (t-test, p = 0.000), implying that vaping contributes to increased acidity in the mouth (Fig. 3).

Comparison of pH between e-cigarette smokers and nonsmokers

In addition, 57.6% of the control group had a normal saliva flow rate compared to the 42.4% in the vaper group. All cases of “very low” and “low” saliva flow rates were among the vapers (100%). This association was significant (p = 0.000), suggesting that vaping reduces saliva production (Fig. 4).

Among those with normal (watery) saliva, 46.4% were vapers, and 53.6% were from the control group. All cases of ‘sticky frothy saliva’ (100%) were found in the vapers group. The difference was significant (t test, p = 0.000), indicating that vaping was associated with changes in saliva texture.

Discussion

Salivary composition has emerged as a significant biomarker, playing a crucial role in the diagnosis of systemic diseases, tracking treatment responses, and evaluating exposure to various toxic substances.

Saliva offers numerous advantages over serum for biological testing, primarily due to its easy accessibility and non-invasive sample collection. Altered salivary pH and buffering capacity have been linked to the development of dental conditions such as caries, gingivitis, and periodontitis [17–19]. Moreover, saliva plays a critical role in maintaining oral health through its mechanical cleansing action, buffering capacity, antimicrobial components, and remineralization potential. A reduction in salivary flow rate (hyposalivation) diminishes mechanical clearance and lubrication, leading to increased plaque accumulation, dry mouth, and difficulty swallowing. Decreased salivary pH and buffering capacity reduce the oral cavity’s ability to neutralize acids produced by bacterial metabolism, predisposing individuals to enamel demineralization, dental caries, and dental erosion. Furthermore, alterations in salivary composition can disrupt the microbial balance, increasing susceptibility to opportunistic infections such as oral candidiasis and exacerbating periodontal inflammation. Understanding these consequences underscores the clinical importance of investigating salivary changes in e-cigarette users [20, 21].

We observed a marked reduction in buffering capacity among e-cigarette users. This aligns with prior studies showing that nicotine and chemical components in vapor can lower the production of bicarbonate ions, which are central to maintaining salivary buffering [22]. At the cellular level, nicotine disrupts ion exchange channels in salivary acinar cells, reducing bicarbonate secretion. Additionally, oxidative stress induced by vaping compromises the activity of carbonic anhydrase enzymes, which are critical in buffering regulation [23]. This reduction in buffering heightens susceptibility to enamel demineralization and acid attacks, predisposing users to dental caries.

Our study demonstrated a significantly lower salivary pH among individuals who use vapers compared to non-smokers. This finding is consistent with some recent systematic reviews, which report a consistently more acidic salivary environment in vapers [24–26]. However, some studies have observed only a modest decrease in salivary pH, with considerable variability, and have noted that differences between vapers and non-smokers may not always reach statistical significance [27].

This acidic shift can be traced to chemical irritation from e-liquid components and nicotine-induced vasoconstriction, which reduces glandular secretions rich in buffering agents. On a molecular level, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) from e-cigarette aerosol lead to the peroxidation of salivary proteins and a decline in salivary antimicrobial function [6, 7]. A persistently acidic oral environment fosters the growth of acidogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans, increasing caries risk.

E-cigarette users in our study had significantly reduced salivary flow rates. Nicotine’s sympathomimetic effects stimulate adrenergic receptors, leading to vasoconstriction and reduced glandular perfusion [28]. Chronic exposure can also desensitize muscarinic receptors in the salivary glands, impairing the parasympathetic stimulation essential for saliva production [8]. Additionally, propylene glycol, a common carrier in e-liquids, is hygroscopic and can draw moisture from oral tissues, contributing to subjective dry mouth (xerostomia).

Altered salivary consistency, with increased stickiness and frothiness, was prevalent among vapers in our cohort. This change may result from reduced water content and altered mucin production. Nicotine and ROS can disrupt epithelial tight junctions, leading to the leakage of plasma proteins into saliva, which increases viscosity [29]. Moreover, changes in salivary protein composition, including increased pro-inflammatory cytokines, further modify the rheological properties of saliva, impairing its lubricating function [30].

Overall, these interconnected changes have profound implications for oral health, potentially increasing the risk of caries, periodontal disease, mucosal irritation, and altered taste perception. Future mechanistic studies are needed to fully unravel the long-term biological effects of e-cigarette exposure on oral tissues.

The primary limitation of this study is its relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. To address this, we are conducting an ongoing study involving a larger and more diverse cohort of participants. This investigation explores the dynamics between the duration and frequency of smoking, with a specific focus on e-cigarette use and its impact on salivary pH, buffering capacity, and flow rate. Our extended research aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between e-cigarette use and oral health. While the study followed a standardized saliva collection protocol, saliva measurements can be influenced by various external factors such as hydration status, time of day, emotional stress, dietary habits, and recent oral hygiene practices. Despite instructing participants to refrain from eating, drinking, or smoking one hour before collection, complete control over these variables is challenging in clinical settings. Additionally, using passive drool or spit methods can introduce variability. Future studies might employ more precise techniques, such as sialometry with stimulation or collection using absorbent devices, to improve measurement consistency and minimize participant variability.

Future studies should also investigate the reversibility of salivary alterations upon cessation of e-cigarette use.

The second limitation of our study is in the methodology. The conversion of ratio-scale salivary variables (i.e., flow rate, buffering capacity, pH, and consistency) into categorical groupings (e.g., ‘very low,’ ‘normal’). As there are no universally accepted or validated cut-off points for these categories in relation to e-cigarette use, this approach may have limited the sensitivity and statistical power of our analysis and restricts comparability with other studies. Future research should analyze these markers as continuous variables, applying appropriate statistical tests (e.g., Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data or independent t-test for normally distributed data) to enhance precision and reproducibility. Also the use of the Saliva-Check Buffer chairside test, which is validated primarily for measurement of salivary buffering capacity but not for other parameters such as flow rate, pH, and consistency. While this tool offers convenience and utility in clinical settings, its precision and reliability for comprehensive scientific research are limited. Future studies should utilize fully calibrated laboratory instruments for a thorough and standardized evaluation of all saliva parameters.

A limitation of this study is that salivary consistency was assessed visually, which may introduce subjectivity.Future research should consider the use of objective tools such as the Nevameter to more precisely characterize salivary rheology.

The initial findings of our pilot study revealed a significant and direct influence of e-cigarettes on the composition and properties of saliva. These insights hold promise for potential application as educational tools in clinical settings. By using saliva check kits, dentists and healthcare professionals can proactively engage with e-cigarette users, offering personalized guidance and education on the effects of e-smoking on the oral cavity. This approach empowers individuals to make informed decisions regarding their vapour habits, contributing to improved oral health outcomes. Furthermore, we are expanding our research to include a larger and more diverse group of participants to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between e-cigarette use and oral health, allowing for more targeted interventions in the future.

Conclusion

This pilot study provides compelling evidence that e-cigarette use is associated with significant alterations in salivary function, including decreased flow rate, reduced buffering capacity, increased acidity, and altered saliva consistency. These changes compromise the protective properties of saliva and may contribute to a higher risk of dental caries, periodontal disease, and other oral pathologies. Recognizing these associations is clinically relevant for early diagnosis and preventive care in dental practice. The findings underscore the importance of incorporating salivary diagnostics into routine dental assessments for e-cigarette users and highlight the need for continued public health education on the oral risks of vaping. Further research with larger and more demographically diverse populations is essential to validate these results and assess the long-term implications of electronic nicotine delivery systems on oral health.
