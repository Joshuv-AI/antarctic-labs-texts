The quality and reliability of short videos about chronic obstructive pulmonary disease on TikTok and Bilibili: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuyu Dai, Xinze Shi, Jun Gu, Tanao Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) continues to pose a significant global health burden, while social media platforms such as TikTok and Bilibili have become important sources of public health information. However, the quality and reliability of video content on these platforms remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the quality and reliability of COPD-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili in China.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 275 COPD-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili. Video characteristics, uploader types, content themes, and presentation formats were extracted. Video quality and reliability were evaluated using the Global Quality Score (GQS), the modified Decision-making Information Support Criteria for Evaluating the Reliability of Non-randomised Studies (mDISCERN) tool, the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria, and the Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI). Correlation analysis was further performed among video metrics and quality scores.ResultsCompared to Bilibili, TikTok was more popular, although the length of the videos on TikTok was shorter than that of the videos on Bilibili (P<0.001). Videos on Bilibili had significantly higher GQS scores (P<0.001) and mDISCERN scores (P=0.01) than those on TikTok. Videos from medical practitioners and science communicators generally exhibited higher quality and reliability compared to those from general users across most assessment tools. Medical practitioners scored higher on the JAMA criteria compared to science communicators, with no significant differences observed in the other assessment tools. Negative correlations were found between GQS scores and engagement metrics (likes, comments, and shares), while positive correlations were found between VIQI scores and all four engagement metrics.ConclusionsCOPD-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili were deficient in quality and reliability. Enhancing the educational value of health information on both platforms requires greater involvement from medical practitioners and science communicators, alongside improved platform-level content regulation.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), social media, public health, information quality

• Greater involvement of healthcare professionals is needed.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a diverse condition marked by persistent respiratory symptoms and progressive airflow limitation (1). As the disease advances, patients typically face increased symptoms, more frequent exacerbations, and a continuous decline in quality of life (2). In 2019, approximately 212 million people globally were affected by COPD, ranking it as the third leading cause of death. The number of individuals with COPD is expected to rise, potentially reaching 600 million by 2050, thereby imposing a growing burden on health and social systems (3).

In view of the characteristics of COPD, self-management interventions are important in helping patients notice changes in their symptoms and decide how to manage their chronic condition, and have been associated with improved health status and a reduced risk of respiratory-related hospital admissions (4,5). The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) recommends that self-management action plans form a key element of evidence-based COPD care (6). Findings from a Cochrane Review also showed that such interventions can reduce respiratory-related hospital admissions and improve health-related quality of life (HRQoL) (7). Against this background, inaccurate or incomplete information may not only affect the daily disease management of patients, but also affect the understanding of caregivers and their families of symptom monitoring, treatment compliance and disease aggravation identification (8).

Health information has been accessed by the public in markedly different ways in the digital age (9,10). An important shift has occurred as social media is now widely used for information dissemination (11,12). Although social media users, including those engaging with short-video platforms, are predominantly from younger age groups, the number of older adult users has been increasing (13,14). Previous studies have shown that social media use among older adults with multimorbidity has risen in recent years (15), and that social media may serve as an effective way for delivering health-related knowledge to this population (16). TikTok and Bilibili, as representative examples, have become important channels for health communication because of their concise format, visually rich design, and interactive functions support (17,18). However, the credibility of these short videos has been questioned. Since most content can be uploaded with limited pre-review, the informational quality on these services is often uneven, and some videos may contain inaccurate or misleading claims (19,20). The potential presence of inaccurate health information in short videos raises concerns, as it may prompt patients, caregivers, and family members to make inappropriate health decisions, ultimately affecting patient outcomes (8,21,22). Even as short-form video services play an expanding role in public health outreach, the accuracy and trustworthiness of medical material shared on these platforms, including COPD-related content, remain insufficiently evaluated.

Therefore, this cross-sectional study evaluated COPD-related videos on Bilibili and TikTok in China using the Global Quality Score (GQS), the modified Decision-making Information Support Criteria for Evaluating the Reliability of Non-randomised Studies (mDISCERN) instrument, the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria, the Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI), and the video content completeness score. Through systematic analysis, the study aims to offer guidance for improving the quality and reliability of health information on short-video platforms and to provide recommendations for content creators, platforms, and healthcare providers, and ultimately improving health literacy and clinical outcomes among patients with COPD.

Methods

Search strategy and data collection

This cross-sectional study involved searching for COPD-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili in China published before October 28, 2025, using the Chinese keywords: “慢阻肺” (COPD), “慢性阻塞性肺病” (chronic lung disease related to obstructed airflow), and “慢性阻塞性肺疾病” (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease). Video retrieval was conducted using the smartphone applications of both platforms, as content on these platforms is predominantly accessed via mobile devices (23). Although these terms refer to the same medical condition, their frequency of use varies across regions and age groups in China, reflecting differences in everyday language and health communication practices.

To mitigate potential bias from the platform’s algorithmic video recommendations, newly established accounts with no browsing history were used for content retrieval on each platform. The search results were organized based on the default ranking algorithm of each platform. Under each of the three keywords, the top 100 videos were retrieved for each keyword. This selection was informed by prior research, which suggests that users generally view no more than 100 videos related to the same keyword daily (24,25). We excluded videos based on the criteria: (I) duplicates or reuploads of previously analyzed videos (n=145); (II) videos unrelated to COPD or with superficial mentions of COPD without substantive content (n=102); (III) advertisements (n=4); and (IV) videos published within one week prior to data collection to ensure data stability and timeliness (n=74). The data extraction process involved recording the video source (Bilibili or TikTok), upload time, video duration in seconds, and the counts of likes, comments, shares, and collections. All data were documented in Excel (Microsoft) (26).

Classification of videos

Video uploaders were categorized based on their identity into three groups: medical practitioners, science communicators, and general users. The videos addressed a range of topics, including epidemiology, etiology, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis.

Video quality assessment

Two respiratory disease specialists (X.S. and Y.D.) conducted a double-blind evaluation of video quality using validated tools: the GQS (27), the mDISCERN (28,29), the JAMA benchmark criteria (30), and the VIQI (31). Disagreements in scoring were adjudicated by a third senior expert (J.G.). Prior to the formal evaluation, reviewers underwent standardized training and collaboratively assessed 10 pilot videos to align their understanding of the scoring criteria. The GQS, a 5-point Likert scale, evaluated each video’s overall quality by considering dimensions, such as video quality, flow, informational comprehensiveness, and patient education value (Table S1). Evaluators utilized the mDISCERN tool to assess video reliability based on five domains: clarity, relevance, traceability, robustness, and fairness. Each domain was scored as yes (1 point) or no (0 point), and a total score from 0 to 5 was obtained (Table S2). The JAMA benchmark criteria were then applied to appraise source reliability and content accuracy, yielding scores from 0 to 4, with higher values reflecting better reliability and accuracy (Table S3). VIQI evaluates video quality across four dimensions: information flow (VIQI 1), clarity of information (VIQI 2), overall video quality (VIQI 3), and consistency, which refers to the alignment between the video title and content (VIQI 4). Each domain was evaluated using a five-point Likert scale [1–5]. The higher the cumulative VIQI scores (VIQI-sum), the better the overall quality of the video (Table S4). Content completeness was determined based on whether key topics were addressed, including epidemiology, etiology, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. For each topic, coverage was graded on a three-level scale: 0 for no explanation, 1 for partial explanation, and 2 for a comprehensive explanation (32).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile ranges (IQRs), depending on data distribution evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, whereas categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The chi-square test was applied to compare categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test was employed for comparisons between two groups of non-normally distributed continuous variables. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to compare three groups, and when significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s post hoc test. To adjust for multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) (33).

Results

Video characteristics

According to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 147 TikTok videos and 128 Bilibili videos were included (Figure 1). Shapiro-Wilk testing indicated that none of the continuous variables followed a normal distribution. Therefore, continuous variables were summarized as medians with IQRs. As presented in Table 1, Bilibili videos had a substantially longer duration than TikTok videos, with a median duration of 597.5 s (IQR: 194.2–1,741.8 s) versus 86.0 s (IQR: 50.0–158.5 s), respectively (P<0.001). In contrast, TikTok videos showed higher audience interaction metrics, including likes (median: 569.0, IQR: 91.5–3,097.0, P<0.001), comments (median: 51.0, IQR: 9.0–306.0, P<0.001), collections (median: 186.0, IQR: 16.5–1,190.5, P=0.01), and shares (median: 59.0, IQR: 11.0–981.0, P<0.001).

Video content and presentation formats

In terms of video content (Figure 2B), symptoms (74.2%) and treatment (65.1%) were the most frequently addressed topics, while epidemiology was rarely discussed (26.5%). The content distributions on TikTok and Bilibili were largely similar, although Bilibili videos generally covered a broader range of domains than TikTok videos, except for prognosis. Regarding presentation styles (Figure 2C), solo narration (38.5%) and PowerPoint (PPT)-based formats (29.1%) were most common overall, whereas TV show/documentary formats (3.6%) were least frequently used. On TikTok, solo narration was the dominant style (59.2%), with TV show/documentary formats being the least (2.0%). In contrast, PPT-based videos were most prevalent on Bilibili (50.8%), and question-and-answer (Q&A) videos were least common (0.8%). Significant differences were also observed across presentation styles for video length (P<0.001), likes (P<0.001), comments (P<0.001), and shares (P=0.01, Table 2). PPT-based videos had the greatest duration, with a median length of 1,404.5 s (IQR: 364.3–2,339.0 s). Medical scenario videos showed stronger interaction performance, with median likes of 477.0 (IQR: 143.5–1,283.5), comments of 62.0 (IQR: 20.5–154.5), and shares of 124.0 (IQR: 35.0–257.0).

Correlation analysis

Figure 7 presents the results of the correlation analysis. The interaction indicators (likes, comments, collections, and shares) were positively associated with one another (r ranging from 0.78 to 0.93, all P<0.001), and similar positive intercorrelations were observed among the quality-related scores and completeness scores (r ranging from 0.22 to 0.81, all P<0.001). Video length was positively associated with GQS, mDISCERN, and content completeness scores (r ranging from 0.23 to 0.56, all P<0.001) and showed a weaker positive correlation with VIQI-sum score (r=0.13, P=0.03), but was negatively associated with engagement indicators, including likes, comments, and shares (r ranging from −0.37 to −0.21, all P<0.001). GQS score was negatively associated with likes (r=−0.28, P<0.001), comments (r=−0.34, P<0.001), collections (r=−0.19, P=0.002), and shares (r=−0.19, P=0.002). Content completeness scores showed the same direction of association with all engagement indicators, with r ranging from −0.29 to −0.21 (all P<0.001). In contrast, VIQI was positively associated with all engagement (r=0.29 to 0.43, all P<0.001).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined 275 COPD-related videos on TikTok and Bilibili in China and evaluated their content, quality, and reliability using four validated tools: the GQS, mDISCERN, the JAMA benchmark criteria, and VIQI. The completeness of the information presented in each video was also assessed. Compared with previous studies, this study incorporated two major Chinese short-form video platforms and applied multiple complementary evaluation instruments to analyze more recently uploaded videos. In terms of reliability assessment, the study adopted the mDISCERN instrument instead of the original DISCERN scale, as the modified version is derived from the original tool and is generally considered more appropriate for evaluating video-based content (28,29,34). In addition, the use of VIQI allowed the analysis to go beyond textual information and integrate visual characteristics, so as to make a more comprehensive evaluation of the video (35).

Although these videos attracted substantial user interactions, their informational value and reliability remained largely insufficient. TikTok videos displayed greater user engagement but scientific rigor and reliability were lower than those on Bilibili. Symptoms and treatment were the most frequently discussed topics, whereas epidemiology received the least attention. Videos produced by medical professionals and science communicators achieved significantly higher scores in terms of GQS, mDISCERN, JAMA, and VIQI than those uploaded by general users. Correlation analyses further showed that higher GQS and content completeness scores were negatively correlated with user participation indicators, while higher VIQI scores were positively correlated with user participation. Additionally, longer videos may provide more substantive health information, contributing to improved overall video quality and reliability.

Our results show that video duration on Bilibili is markedly longer than that on TikTok, whereas the interaction rate of TikTok content on all indicators is significantly higher than that of Bilibili, including likes, comments, collections and sharing. This pattern aligns with the platform’s specific duration limit and findings reported in previous studies (36,37). A previous research has also reported that TikTok videos exceeding 60 seconds tend to receive fewer likes per day, despite achieving higher content-quality scores (38). Longer videos are generally more capable of conveying complex medical information, while shorter videos are more eye-catching and more conducive to rapid dissemination (39,40). The limitation in video length may diminish the depth and accuracy of health information. Additionally, in order to cater to the audience’s viewing habits, creators may try to further divide the content into shorter segments while simplifying the content to attract more viewers (41). This trade-off reflects a key difficulty in digital health communication: maintaining audience participation and ensuring that the information provided is evidence-based and of high quality. Future interventions should therefore focus on determining video durations that can both maintain the interest of the audience and the educational effect.

Our study revealed that videos uploaded by medical practitioners and science communicators exhibit significantly higher quality and authenticity compared to those uploaded by general users. Medical practitioners and science communicators showed no significant differences, except in the JAMA benchmark criteria. These findings are consistent with results reported in previous studies (37,42). General users typically lack professional medical knowledge, and many videos uploaded by patients primarily focus on personal experiences, which may also be influenced by emotional bias (43,44). At the same time, general users demonstrated relatively higher interaction metrics, which may be attributed to the attractiveness of personal disease narratives, which often resonate with the audience and provide an easy-to-understand and experience-based perspective. By contrast, medical practitioners and science communicators received relatively higher quality scores, likely because professional content creators are more capable of providing accurate, well-structured and educational information. The JAMA criteria highlight the importance of author qualifications, reference usage, timely updates, and conflicts of interest (30). A considerable proportion of authenticated accounts on TikTok and Bilibili may partly explain the phenomenon that medical practitioners have higher scores in JAMA. Considering the professional and specialized nature of medical information, platforms in China should implement stricter rules to limit the medical content generated by patients, and strengthen the supervision of medical practitioners and science communicators to promote the creation of rigorous and evidence-based videos.

Our results showed that symptoms and treatment were the predominant topics of discussion, whereas epidemiology was the least addressed. This observation is consistent with the findings of an early study (29). This pattern suggests that content creators tend to focus on topics that are intuitive and immediately relevant to viewers. Among various forms of presentation, solo narration and PPT-based videos were the most common (45). Solo narration is relatively easy to produce, especially for medical professionals with limited time and technical resources. PPT formats can deliver more comprehensive audiovisual information, but they usually lead to longer video duration and a large amount of information, which may be less suitable for general audiences and therefore yield lower interaction metrics. In contrast, solo narration and medical scenario videos emphasize simplicity and emotional resonance, contributing to their stronger engagement performance. Given the diversity of themes and presentation styles, there is a clear need for platforms and creators on Chinese short-video platforms to adopt a more integrated multimodal approach to improve the ease of use and comprehensibility of medical information.

Additional correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between video duration and content integrity. By contrast, the duration and completeness of the video were negatively related to the interaction indicators. These results suggest that viewers tend to favor shorter videos, and high-quality content usually takes longer to convey messages more comprehensively (40,42). This pattern may partly explain the negative association observed between GQS scores and interaction metrics in our study. Previous studies suggest that viewers often struggle to differentiate between high and low-quality videos (46,47). On short video platforms such as TikTok, the recommendation system is mainly oriented towards user interaction, leading to videos being more likely to be promoted to a broader audience. Low-quality yet popular videos may be excessively promoted, leading to a disparity between information quality and popularity (47). Consequently, enhancing public health education is essential, and platform developers on Chinese short-video platforms also need to refine recommendation systems to prioritize content based on accuracy and reliability instead of merely interactive metrics.

Even though COPD-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili in China generate substantial user interaction, our findings suggest that their informational quality and the breadth of medical content remain insufficient. To improve the quality and communicative value of such medical videos within the regional scope of this study, there are many areas that need to be changed. Greater participation by medical practitioners and science communicators is needed to raise scientific accuracy and strengthen credibility. At the content level, creators should move beyond the current focus on symptoms and treatment and incorporate epidemiological information, since describing disease burden, affected populations, and risk patterns can help viewers understand why prevention and early control matter at the population scale, thereby fostering a more systematic view of disease prevention. In terms of presentation, formats that audiences appear to engage with more readily, such as solo narration and medical scenario-based presentations, could be adopted. Finally, interactivity should not be achieved by compromising accuracy or balance, and evidence-based messages should be maintained rather than overlooked for attention. With professional expertise combined with engaging interaction, short video platforms can more effectively realize their role in public health education.

This study has several limitations. First, despite two independent reviewers and a third adjudicator being involved in the evaluation, there remains the potential for subjective bias. While the assessment tools used focus on specific quality aspects, they cannot fully capture the complexity of health communication. Second, because this study used a cross-sectional design, it reflects only the situation during the research period, not the changes over time. In view of the rapid development of short video platforms, it is necessary to carry out follow-up longitudinal research to monitor the changes in the quality of such content over time. Third, our analysis was limited to Chinese-language videos, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other regions where cultural, linguistic, and regulatory differences may influence content. Future research could broaden the scope to include global platforms such as YouTube, thereby revealing more comprehensive patterns in health information dissemination. Fourth, the study relied on convenience sampling rather than completely random sampling, which may reduce the representativeness of the results. Increasing the sample size in future studies could enhance the reliability and generalizability of the findings. Fifth, COPD primarily affects older adults, whereas platforms such as TikTok tend to attract younger users. This demographic mismatch may limit the relevance of some video content to the core patient population and should be considered when interpreting the public health implications of the findings. Future research will make designs that enable a closer correspondence between target patient populations and platform user demographics, where data availability permits, to improve the interpretation of findings. Sixth, video retrieval was conducted using the smartphone applications of the platforms. Although newly established accounts without prior browsing history were used to reduce potential bias, differences in user behavior across access modes and the reliance of platform algorithms on such signals may still influence the displayed content. Future studies should incorporate multiple access modes for a more comprehensive evaluation.

Conclusions

The study systematically analyzed 275 COPD-related short videos from TikTok and Bilibili within the Chinese context. The quality and reliability of videos on both platforms were generally poor. Videos on Bilibili demonstrated superior quality and reliability compared to those on TikTok. Content shared by medical practitioners and science communicators was notably superior, providing viewers with more informative and trustworthy content. With the continued popularity of short videos, ensuring the availability of high-quality content related to COPD is crucial. Short-video platforms in this context should strengthen their monitoring and auditing to ensure medical information accuracy. Patients should be cautious when seeking medical information on these platforms. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the focus on Chinese short-video platforms.
