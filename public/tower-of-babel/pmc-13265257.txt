Finegoldia magna Infection in the Lumbar Intervertebral Disc Following Acupuncture with Cupping: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Junhong He, Jihui Zhang, Liujun Zhao, Qiong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFinegoldia magna is a common Gram-positive anaerobic coccus that forms part of the normal flora of the skin, oral cavity, and gastrointestinal tract. However, in certain clinical settings, such as disruption of the mucocutaneous barrier or immunosuppression, it can act as an opportunistic pathogen, accounting for approximately 5–12% of anaerobic infections, and may cause a variety of conditions, including skin and soft tissue infections, bone and joint infections, breast abscesses, and diabetic foot infections. Here, we present the first reported case of deep lumbar spine infection caused by Finegoldia magna due to improper acupuncture combined with cupping therapy.Case PresentationWe report the case of a 58-year-old patient with lumbar disk herniation who developed F. magna infection of the lumbar disk following acupuncture with wet cupping therapy. Post-debridement treatment with piperacillin–tazobactam lowered C-reactive protein levels. However, during follow-up, the patient reported persistent pain, and C-reactive protein levels increased again. Changing the antibiotic regimen to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate potassium combined with moxifloxacin improved and stabilized the symptoms.ConclusionOur findings highlight the need for thorough cleaning and disinfection of tools and the patient’s skin during acupuncture treatment. Moreover, as debridement of lumbar spine infections may yield false-positive results regarding antibiotic efficacy, we recommend at least 2 months of follow-up after debridement, as well as prompt adjustment of the antibiotic regimen based on patient symptoms to prevent infection recurrence.

Introduction

Finegoldia magna (F. magna) is a gram-positive anaerobic coccus (GPAC) that commonly colonizes the skin, mucous membranes, and gastrointestinal tract. Although part of the normal human flora, F. magna is a cause of opportunistic infections,1–3 including soft tissue abscesses, diabetic ulcers, infective endocarditis, pneumonia, bone and joint infections, and bacterial vaginosis.4–6 GPAC typically account for approximately 25–30% of anaerobic bacteria isolated from clinical specimens, and F. magna is the GPAC most frequently isolated in pure culture from different clinical specimens.2 In the orthopedic setting, F. magna is a rare cause of periprosthetic joint infections due to its strong adhesion to artificial medical devices, accounting for 5–15% of all anaerobic infections.7,8 However, literature data regarding F. magna causing lumbar disc infections is scarce.7,9 Cases without a prior surgical history are extremely rare. To date, only sporadic cases of F. magna-associated lumbar suppurative myositis have been documented.10

Acupuncture and cupping are traditional but increasingly popular therapies that effectively improve pain symptoms and functional recovery in patients with lumbar disc herniation.11 Despite a high overall safety profile, improper operation can lead to serious deep-seated conditions such as pyogenic arthritis, epidural abscess, and discitis.12,13 A systematic review of adverse events found that infection was the most common complication of acupuncture, accounting for 239 of 284 reported cases, with mycobacteria and staphylococci being the main pathogens.14 To our knowledge, there are no well-documented cases of lumbar disc infection caused by F. magna following acupuncture combined with cupping.

Herein, we report the first known case of deep lumbar spine infection caused by F. magna, a dermal colonizer, due to improper acupuncture combined with cupping therapy. Even in patients with no surgical history, certain procedures can introduce symbiotic anaerobic bacteria from the skin into intervertebral disc tissue.15 Therefore, this case report provides valuable clinical experience for the diagnosis and treatment of similar cases.

Case Presentation

A 58-year-old male patient was admitted to the hospital on August 20, 2025, owing to persistent lower back pain for 3 weeks. The patient had no underlying medical conditions or surgical history but had suffered from lumbar disc herniation for more than 10 years and had a history of multiple acupuncture treatments at the same public community hospital. One month before hospital admission, the patient temporarily received acupuncture treatment at another small private clinic owing to a recurrence of lower back pain, after which the pain worsened and became persistent.

Admission examinations revealed a white blood cell count of 7.9, C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 114.2, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 114, and procalcitonin level of <0.02. Blood and urine cultures were negative. Human leukocyte antigen B27, tuberculosis infection, T-cell detection, and antineutrophil antibodies (ANCA+MPO+PR3) were all negative. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed bone marrow edema around the L1/2 lumbar vertebrae, intervertebral space narrowing, and swelling of the surrounding soft tissues, suggesting a paravertebral abscess (Figure 1). After excluding surgical contraindications, the patient underwent lumbar debridement on August 25. During the operation, a ruptured intervertebral disc was observed, and nucleus pulposus tissue was collected for next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis (Nanjing Practical Medicine Co., Ltd. Nanjing, China) (Supplementary material 1). Complete genomic DNA was extracted from these samples using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (DP316, TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China). Qualified single-end DNA libraries were prepared using the One Shot DNA Library Prep Kit (PDM602, Nanjing Practice Medicine Diagnostics. Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). High-throughput sequencing was performed using the MGISEQ-200 gene sequencer. Pathogen identification was performed using a microbial genome library integrated from multiple public databases, including FDA-ARGOS, BV-BRC (version 3.28.9), EuPathDB (version 52), and NCBI GenBank (version 242).

Postoperatively, the patient’s CRP level decreased significantly to 73.7, and empirical anti-infective therapy was initiated with piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g intravenously three times daily until discharge on September 2. On August 28, the NGS results showed that F. magna accounted for 5047 reads, representing 77.59% of the bacterial reads, and was the only dominant species. Other detected bacteria, including Cutibacterium acnes and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, had read counts less than 4 and were classified as suspected background bacteria by the testing laboratory. No mycobacteria, fungi, mycoplasma/chlamydia/rickettsia, or antibiotic resistance genes were detected. On September 2, the CRP level decreased further to 67.2, and the doctor deemed the antibacterial treatment effective. The patient’s condition was stable, and they requested discharge on the same day, with advice to maintain the current antibiotic treatment regimen. On September 8, the patient returned for a follow-up outpatient visit, complaining of persistent pain, but the CRP level had decreased to 9.8. After evaluation, the doctor decided to maintain the original antibiotic treatment plan.

However, by September 23, the patient reported persistent and worsening pain, and the CRP level had risen to 13.2. The physician consulted a clinical pharmacist to adjust the treatment plan, changing the antibiotic to linezolid 0.6 g twice daily for one week. On September 30, the patient’s pain symptoms persisted, and the CRP level had increased to 26.6. After reviewing the literature, the antibiotic therapy was further adjusted to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate (0.25 g/0.125 g) three times daily combined with oral moxifloxacin 0.4 g once daily. On October 7, the patient returned for a follow-up visit, reporting that the pain symptoms had disappeared and that the CRP level had decreased to 2.4. Considering the patient’s history of recurrent symptoms, the clinical pharmacist recommended continuing antibiotic treatment and monitoring the patient until December 30 (Figure 2). The combination therapy regimen lasted for approximately 3 months. Discussion

F. magna is the most common pathogenic species of Gram-positive anaerobic cocci,16 and it possesses various virulence factors. Superantigen protein L triggers nonspecific B-cell activation, and the resulting powerful immune response, which releases inflammatory mediators, can be fatal. Surface-associated protein F. magna adhesion factor (FAF) establishes initial adhesion to the skin and prevents re-epithelialization of wounds, while binding to fibrin-1 and fibrin-2, thereby causing infection deep within the dermis. During infection, F. magna can adhere to and attach to host cells via sortase-dependent fimbriae, promoting bacterial aggregation and participating in biofilm formation. Biofilm formation can enhance F. magna adaptation to the environment and resistance to antimicrobial agents, leading to persistent chronic infection. Additionally, the polysaccharide capsule confers resistance to phagocytosis and, together with tissue-destroying enzymes, facilitates the spread of infection into deeper tissues. Subtilisin-like extracellular serine protease (SufA) protease can completely degrade collagen IV and hydrolyze human fibrinogen, inhibiting the formation of fibrin networks, allowing F. magna to penetrate deep into dermal tissue, thereby causing delayed wound healing. In addition, SufA counteracts immune defense mechanisms by hydrolyzing antimicrobial peptides involved in human innate immunity, such as LL-37 and MIG/CXCL9. These virulence factors act continuously throughout the infection process, explaining the chronic and insidious clinical course observed in this case.1,17 Immune cells stimulated by these virulence factors release large amounts of inflammatory mediators, thereby affecting infection severity and mortality.1,17–19 Previous cases have involved F. magna causing subcutaneous abscesses, necrotizing fasciitis, or even death.20 Factors such as diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and obesity, which can lead to decreased immune function, can induce opportunistic infections by F. magna.6 Furthermore, F. magna strongly adheres to artificial medical devices.9,21 Therefore, a history of orthopedic surgery with implants and artificial joint replacement is a risk factor for F. magna infection. However, the patient in this case report had never undergone any surgery or trauma and had no underlying medical conditions. Therefore, the physician did not initially consider F. magna infection. After the pathogen was identified by NGS, the route of infection initially appeared obscure, as the patient had no history of surgery, trauma, or underlying comorbidities until the clinical pharmacist discovered the patient’s history of acupuncture treatment during a review of the medication history.

Acupuncture combined with cupping therapy effectively improves pain symptoms and functional recovery in patients with lumbar disc herniation. However, acupuncture-induced spinal infections have been increasingly recognized in the literature. Infectious pathogens—including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Serratia marcescens, and Group C streptococci—can penetrate deep into the spinal tissues through improper acupuncture, causing severe abscesses, osteomyelitis, and even paralysis.22–26 Most infection events associated with acupuncture were related to inadequate disinfection and cleaning, including improper disinfection of skin and equipment, reuse of needles and disinfectants, and failure of disinfectants or aseptic techniques.14,27 In this case report, the patient reported that multiple acupuncture treatments over 10 years effectively relieved recurrent pain. However, the last acupuncture treatment was not performed at the previous public community hospital but at a small private clinic and was combined with cupping therapy. After this treatment, the patient experienced worsening pain symptoms, and a lumbar abscess was discovered. F. magna on the skin surface likely penetrated deep into the lumbar intervertebral disc tissue through acupuncture and gradually progressed.

F. magna infections that form subcutaneous abscesses can be identified by culturing pus and wound secretions. In cases of F. magna infection in the bones and joints, synovial fluid is usually cultured. However, safely and adequately obtaining pus from the lesion would have been difficult because of the deep location of the infection in the lumbar spine. The patient’s acupuncture site had a small skin surface wound with no obvious secretions and had already healed. Blood culture results were negative. This result may be attributed to the following factors: (1) F. magna directly invaded the deep lumbar intervertebral disc via the acupuncture instruments; thus, the infection was limited to the local lumbar intervertebral disc and did not spread to the blood or other bodily fluids. (2) In this case, F. magna caused a chronic infection with small colonies and a slow growth rate, which led to insufficient culture and caused it to be overlooked.28 (3) The patient had been treated with clindamycin before admission, which, although not effective, may have affected the detection results of the blood culture. Despite negative results from conventional microbiological examinations, the patient’s persistent clinical symptoms and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) continued to suggest an infection. Therefore, we opted to use NGS for etiological identification. NGS technology directly extracts microbial nucleic acids from samples for sequencing, improving detection in culture-negative infections.29 However, due to its susceptibility to DNA contamination from environmental and reagent sources, it cannot accurately distinguish among contamination, colonization, and true infection.30 F. magna is part of the normal flora of human skin and mucous membranes. In this patient’s NGS report, F. magna was the only dominant bacterium (5047 reads, accounting for 77.59% of the bacterial reads), whereas all other microorganisms had only 1–4 sequences, and no other potential pathogens were found. Despite the lack of culture and PCR confirmation, clinical and MRI findings, as well as a strong response to antimicrobial treatment for F. magna, support our diagnostic results.

Debridement combined with antibiotic therapy is currently the standard treatment for F. magna infections. Reportedly effective antibiotics include penicillins (piperacillin–tazobactam sodium, amoxicillin, and amoxicillin–clavulanate potassium), lincosamides (clindamycin), quinolones (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, and ciprofloxacin), carbapenems (imipenem–cilastatin and meropenem), vancomycin, linezolid, and tigecycline. Many antibiotics reportedly effective against F. magna may become ineffective. Resistance rates to clindamycin and quinolones are particularly high,17 whereas resistance to penicillins in F. magna has consistently been low (<5%). F. magna is highly sensitive to amoxicillin, piperacillin–tazobactam, and amoxicillin–clavulanate potassium, but there has been one case of intermediate resistance to amoxicillin–clavulanate potassium and one case of piperacillin–tazobactam treatment failure.9,31 Therefore, antibiotic treatment regimens should be promptly adjusted based on assessments of patient symptoms and laboratory indicators to ensure effectiveness. Piperacillin–tazobactam shows good efficacy against most anaerobes, so it was chosen as the starting point for empirical treatment in this case.32 However, the interference of debridement in the evaluation of antibiotic therapy has not been mentioned in the literature. As F. magna invaded the acupuncture channel, the infection in this patient was confined to the nucleus pulposus of the lumbar intervertebral disc. After debridement of the nucleus pulposus, the patient’s inflammatory marker levels and pain improved significantly. However, the slow growth of residual F. magna caused only persistent pain for a period after surgery, without altering blood CRP and ESR levels. This led the physician to mistakenly believe that piperacillin–tazobactam was effective; thus, the antibiotic regimen was not adjusted until the patient experienced recurrence of increased pain and elevated CRP and ESR levels. Notably, the ineffectiveness of piperacillin–tazobactam treatment prompted the clinical pharmacist to suspect penicillin resistance and switch the antibiotic regimen to linezolid. However, linezolid also proved ineffective. It is worth noting that F. magna has a reported near-0% resistance rate to linezolid.32,33 Besides the possibility of resistance, treatment failure may also be related to other factors, such as biofilms, deep infection foci, and insufficient drug penetration. In a previously reported case of piperacillin–tazobactam failure in treating F. magna, a combination of vancomycin and metronidazole was ultimately used.9 However, recent surveillance data showed that 18.2% of clinically isolated F. magna strains exhibited resistance to metronidazole, with 38.9% of these resistant strains displaying multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotypes.34 Furthermore, given the high risk of adverse reactions with long-term vancomycin use, this regimen was not the first choice in this case. All of these factors prolonged the patient’s treatment duration. In a retrospective study of orthopedic infections caused by F. magna, amoxicillin was the most commonly used antibiotic regimen.9 Although F. magna does not typically produce β-lactamase, adding an enzyme inhibitor can effectively reduce the 90% minimum inhibitory concentration.35 Therefore, the antibiotics were ultimately adjusted to amoxicillin–clavulanate potassium with moxifloxacin to prevent potential penicillin resistance. The patient was followed up for 3 months after symptom resolution, and the effectiveness of antibiotic treatment was ultimately confirmed. This is the first reported case of deep lumbar spine infection caused by F. magna due to improper acupuncture and cupping therapy.

This study had several limitations. First, uncertainty in clinical interpretation is an inherent limitation of NGS technology, which cannot reliably distinguish true infection from colonization or contamination. This concern is particularly relevant to the present case because F. magna is a known commensal of the human skin and mucosal flora. Second, the NGS-based identification of F. magna could not be validated by culture or species-specific PCR because routine cultures were negative and the patient declined further sampling after clinical improvement. Consequently, although the clinical evidence and NGS results strongly support the pathogenic role of F. magna in this case, the diagnosis cannot be considered definitively confirmed. Furthermore, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not performed on the F. magna strain in this case; therefore, we cannot determine its resistance profile to specific antibiotics. Despite these limitations, the patient’s improvement in pain symptoms and inflammatory markers following targeted antimicrobial therapy supports our clinical interpretation.

Conclusion

Based on our findings, extra care must be taken to ensure the cleanliness and disinfection of instruments and the patient’s skin during the lumbar spine acupuncture treatment. Many antibiotics reportedly effective against F. magna may become ineffective, and debridement of lumbar spine infections may lead to false-positive results regarding antibiotic efficacy. Therefore, we recommend that such patients be followed up for at least 2 months after debridement and that antibiotics be adjusted promptly based on patient symptoms to prevent recurrent infection.
