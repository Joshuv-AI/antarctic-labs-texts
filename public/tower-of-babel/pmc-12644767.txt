Decoding brain age predictions from sleep electroencephalography across infancy to adolescence
Authors: Kartik K Iyer, Sally Staton, Andrew Collaro, Ajay Kevat, Sampsa Vanhatalo, James A Roberts, Jasneek Chawla, Nathan J Stevenson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildhood sleep electroencephalography (EEG) reveals brain maturation patterns aligned with age, offering a window into development at the bedside. Leveraging this, we used supervised neural networks to predict age from overnight EEG and derive a Functional Brain Age (FBA) across wake, NREM (N1–N3), and REM sleep in 814 children with clinically normal sleep studies. We evaluated how FBA varies across sleep architecture and assessed the accuracy of neural networks, EEG channels, sleep segments, data quality, quantitative EEG features, and explainability methods on prediction performance. Prediction accuracy varied developmentally, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.78 years in infancy (0–2 years), 0.87 years in childhood (2–12 years), and 1.55 years in adolescence (12–18 years), yielding an overall MAE of 0.96 years (95CI 0.90–1.01). FBA fell within ± 25% of chronological age in over 95% of children, with highest accuracy (< 1 year MAE) during N2, N3, and REM stages that reflect well-defined developmental EEG changes such as delta power and spindles. FBA reliability was shaped by signal quality and stable, age-specific patterns across sleep stages. Explainability analyses showed that network activations aligned with quantitative EEG features, supporting the biological validity of FBA. These findings support scalable, non-invasive tools that use sleep EEG to track brain maturation as an objective marker of neurodevelopmental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25482-7.Keywords: Brain age, Children, Sleep, Development, Neural networks, EEG

Introduction

Sleep plays a critical role in the growth and development of children, facilitating various physiological processes essential for their well-being1 such as neurocognitive maturation, memory consolidation, and hormonal regulation 2. The maturation of sleep therefore reflects the underlying maturation of the brain, with changes in a child’s sleep, their genetic predisposition, and the external environment interacting with their overall development2–4. Examining cortical activity during sleep in relation to a child’s developmental age provides valuable neurophysiological insights into age-related variations in sleep architecture and their implications for future development.

An accessible way to gain insights into brain function during sleep is through the analysis of the hypnogram, which is a graphical representation of sleep stages or states over the course of the night. The childhood hypnogram, informed by the electroencephalogram (EEG), reveals a wealth of information about the dynamics of cortical activity as a child ages, such as the presence and decline of slow-wave activity, as well as changes in circadian rhythm1,5,6. During infancy, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is abundant, constituting between 30 and 50% of sleep in the first 12 months, supporting early learning and rapid neurodevelopmental maturation1,6. Over time, as the child grows into adolescence, sleep spindles—key markers of cortical maturation—increase in amplitude, density, and frequency, reflecting the maturation of thalamocortical networks and the ongoing processes of synaptogenesis and synaptic pruning7. These age-dependent changes provide valuable insight into the unique neurophysiological characteristics of sleep architecture that underpin the healthy development of infants, children, and adolescents.

In this study we delve further into age-related relationships across childhood sleep architecture via the prediction of a Functional Brain Age (FBA) from polysomnography EEG using an entire night’s sleep. Given that sleep stages evolve with age, certain aspects of sleep architecture—such as the proportion of slow-wave sleep and REM, the organization of sleep cycles, and the progression of sleep spindles—may serve as robust markers of brain maturation. By integrating these physiological patterns into machine learning models, we aim to refine FBA estimation, enhancing its potential as an age-sensitive biomarker for neurodevelopmental assessment. We used supervised machine learning (ML) via deep neural networks to optimize FBA prediction from EEG and sleep stage labels, to accurately capture an index of cortical development associated with childhood sleep architecture8. To support the utility of an overnight FBA, we explored the relationship between individual brain age predictions with common quantitative EEG features, neural network explainability techniques, and sleep architecture.

Results

Neural networks uncover sleep-state influences on FBA

We trained our neural network models (Res-NN, Res-NN-Seq) to predict a Functional Brain Age (FBA) for every 30-s epoch of overnight EEG data in each child, aligned with their corresponding hypnogram. Following cross-validation procedures, the Res-NN (Fig. 1a) achieved an overall accuracy of MAE = 1.03 years (Fig. 1b; 95CI 0.97 to 1.09 years) and wMAE = 1.20 years (95CI 0.99 to 1.42 years), based on the median FBA across the overnight hypnogram of all 814 subjects. The residual (PAD) was proportional to age (Fig. 1b; R2 = 0.36; 95CI 0.31 to 0.41) suggesting a confounding effect of age on FBA accuracy. FBA prediction improved with the hybrid Res-NN-Seq model, which incorporates the temporal sequence of EEG (see Methods), achieving MAE = 0.96 years (95CI 0.90 to 1.01), wMAE = 1.08 years (95CI 0.87 to 1.28), and a significantly stronger correlation between age and prediction error (95CI_corr: –0.0048 to –0.0008). The relationship between residual and age remained but reduced appreciably with the Res-NN-Seq (R2 = 0.23, 95CI 0.18 to 0.28). The performance of FBAs also varied across different sleep states (Table 1; see also Fig. 1c example), with N2, N3, and REM states offering the most accurate FBAs across all age groups (Fig. 1d), and N1 and wake epochs returning the least accurate overall FBA estimation. Both Res-NN and Res-NN-Seq approaches (Fig. 1e) outperformed age prediction via GPR (MAE = 1.23 years, wMAE = 1.30 years; 95CI 1.15 to 1.30 years). Interestingly, incorporating sleep state information for each subject—specifically, the durations of five sleep stages—alongside their FBA output from the Res-NN, using a multivariable prediction approach (via GPR), improved performance with an MAE = 0.95 years and wMAE = 1.03 years, matching the accuracy of the Res-NN-Seq.

To further tease apart the temporal structure of sleep in FBA prediction, we examined how accuracy varied across the night. Accuracy was highest in the first third (MAE = 1.01 years, wMAE = 1.17 years), followed by a slight decrease in the middle third (MAE = 1.07 years, wMAE = 1.24 years) and the lowest accuracy in the final third of sleep (MAE = 1.13 years, wMAE = 1.29 years). This pattern suggests a potential overnight drift in accuracy or an interaction with age and sleep architecture. We conducted several experiments to probe this by retraining and testing different versions of the Res-NN using cross-validation. We first trained each model on fixed segments of the overnight hypnogram (Fig. 2a). These results reflect the consistency of sleep state proportions across different temporal segments of the hypnogram, as assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). We analyzed fixed-duration segments, starting with the first 30 min capturing the wake-to-sleep transition, followed by the first 30, 60, 90, and 180 min from sleep onset. The accuracy of FBA was highest during the first 180 min of sleep (MAE = 1.05 years, 95CI 0.99 to 1.11 years, wMAE = 1.20 years). We observed that the proportion of wake and sleep states showed increasing consistency, with the ICC improving from 0.4 to 0.8 across the segments (Fig. 2b), except for the initial wake-to-sleep transition.

Our fixed‐duration analysis showed that FBA accuracy increased up to the first 180 min of sleep and then plateaued or declined in later periods, suggesting that sleep architecture interacts with age to influence prediction performance. Here, we opted to test a cross-temporal approach to examine whether later-night patterns could generalize to earlier segments, potentially revealing age-specific differences in sleep architecture that affect prediction. To do this, we trained and tested Res-NN models on different thirds of the night (e.g., last third versus first third; Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, when the Res-NN was trained on the last two-thirds of sleep and tested on the first few hours, accuracy improved across all age groups. This approach significantly improved MAE (CI 0.82 to 0.92 years, Δ: 0.10 years), wMAE (CI 0.75 to 1.12 years, Δ: 0.12 years), and age-based correlations (95CI_corr: − 0.02 to − 0.015). Conversely, training on the last two-thirds and testing on earlier segments improved accuracy across all ages (MAE Δ = –0.10 years; wMAE Δ = –0.12 years; age‐correlation Δ = –0.015). This increase in accuracy in younger children is likely due to two factors: the availability of more training data and the prominence of REM in younger children during the latter half of the night at the expense of NREM sleep. Comparing these findings with our fixed-duration analysis highlights that optimizing FBA models to account for age-specific sleep dynamics could significantly enhance predictive accuracy across different developmental stages.

To examine the relationship between sleep state and FBA predictions, we retrained the final layer of the age-predicting Res-NN to instead classify sleep state (Fig. 2c). This was compared to a dedicated sleep state classifier using the same Res-NN architecture (Fig. 2d). The repurposed FBA model achieved a Cohen’s kappa of 0.65 with expert-labelled sleep stages, slightly lower than the kappa of 0.73 from the dedicated classifier. Conversely, using the sleep state classifier to predict FBA led to higher error (MAE = 1.25 years, wMAE = 1.45 years) and a significantly reduced correlation (95CI_corr: –0.015 to –0.0072). These results suggest that the Res-NN’s early feature extraction stages encode physiologically relevant information for both age and sleep state, regardless of the training label.

Factors affecting FBA accuracy and stability

To understand what underlies individual differences in FBA performance, we examined the influence of demographic variables, sleep quality (available from PSG reports), and EEG characteristics on prediction error. Across our most performant networks, no significant interactions between age and sex, or sex alone, were found with the overnight FBA, despite a significant age interaction (for the Res-NN, PAD ~ sex: β = 0.26, p = 0.13; PAD ~ age*sex: β = -0.004, p = 0.83, PAD ~ age2: β = − 0.007, p = 4.8 × 10–4, n = 814, df = 809). Furthermore, no significant association was found for the overnight FBA with BMI (PAD ~ BMI: β = 0.008, p = 0.65). Similar associations were found for the Res-NN-Seq (Supplementary Table 2). We also found that children reported to have better overnight sleep efficiency (> 70%, n = 735) had lower FBA-age error, whereas those with poorer sleep efficiency (< 70%, n = 79) showed significantly higher PAD (p = 0.024). Older children (> 5 years, n = 69) with poorer sleep efficiency showed significantly lower relative delta power (0.5–2 Hz; pFWE = 5 × 10–5 for 5 to 12 year olds and 3 × 10–4 for 12 to 18 year olds) and higher relative power in the alpha and beta bands (8–12 Hz; pFWE = 3 × 10–8 for 5 to 12 year olds and 1.6 × 10–5 for 12 to 18 year olds), compared to those with good sleep efficiency. This finding prompted us to examine the overall quality of the EEG recording, the underlying features of the EEG (i.e., amplitude and spectral features) and interpretability of the FBA from features extracted from Res-NN and Res-NN-Seq layers.

We also assessed the impact of EEG data quality and montage selection on FBA performance. Without omitting high amplitude epochs (> 500 µV), the Res-NN accuracy declines substantially, with MAE increasing to 1.16 years and wMAE to 1.25 years (95CI_corr: 0.014 to 0.023), underscoring the critical role of EEG artifact rejection in improving predictive performance. The FBA performance also varied across different EEG channels. Using the Res-NN, models trained on individual channels showed poorer accuracy compared to those using all five referential montages (Supplementary Table 1). Among individual channels, F4-M1 yielded the highest accuracy across the hypnogram (MAE = 1.31 years, 95CI 1.24 to 1.39; wMAE = 1.61 years, 95CI 1.38 to 1.84), consistently outperforming other single-channel models by 0.05 to 0.1 years. Combinations of referential channels (e.g., F4-M1, C4-M1, O2-M1) offered better accuracy than single channels or traditional bipolar montages (F4-C4, C4-O2). Overall, these findings reinforce that while specific referential channels can enhance FBA accuracy, the most robust predictions are achieved by leveraging the full five-channel montage.

Having established the influence of EEG quality control and optimal channel configuration for improving FBA accuracy, we next examined the temporal stability of FBA predictions across the night, with a focus performance differences across age. We defined stability as consecutive epoch predictions falling within a ≤ ± 25% threshold of chronological age, equivalent to a 3–6 month MAE for infants (0–2 years), to identify consecutive epoch predictions that were highly accurate or “stable” (Fig. 3a). This threshold revealed that the lack of stable, consecutive predictions most often affects children under 2 years of age, with stability increasing linearly with age and sample size, except in the adolescent group (greater than 10 min of stability) where it remained high despite fewer samples in the upper age bins. In children under 2, stable predictions typically lasted between 1 and 10 min, possibly reflecting the variation of neurodevelopmental changes in this age group. We also analyzed the distribution of run lengths across the hypnogram in 2-year age bins and calculated the skewness of these distributions for each subject. Correlations between run length and skewness (Fig. 3b) indicated that stable consecutive run lengths for children under 4 years of age were normally distributed, while for older children, larger consecutive run lengths were observed in the middle and last thirds of the hypnogram (> 4 years, Spearman’s ≥ − 0.35 to − 0.8, pFWE < 0.05). Stable epoch-to-epoch FBA predictions increase with age, particularly after four years, likely reflecting more consistent sleep patterns and, consequently, more stable EEG features. The median FBA thus provides reliable estimates for older children, but for younger children (< 4 years) network estimates tend to be more variable, likely driven by rapid transitions between sleep states. This suggests that focusing on sleep state-specific features (for example, using only N2 or REM epochs) could improve FBA accuracy in early childhood.

Lastly, the relationships between key EEG features in FBA prediction accuracy were also examined via a Shapley analysis on a multivariable GPR trained with 32 quantitative EEG (QEEG) features (Supplementary Table 3). The top ten QEEG features across the hypnogram (Fig. 3c) included amplitude, spectral, and informational characteristics, which varied across sleep states (Supplementary Table 4). Children with FBAs within ± 25% chronological age differed significantly from outliers on these features (Supplementary Fig. 2a) with more stable FBAs associated with consistent EEG amplitude, spectral power, and sample entropy. In contrast, common hypnogram-based features, such as sleep state latency, wake after sleep onset, and total sleep time, were not significant contributors to FBA prediction (Supplementary Fig. 2b). QEEG feature sets also differed when FBAs were calculated within individual sleep states, aligning with Grad-CAM results from full and per-state hypnograms (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 2c), where spectral and informational features dominated. These findings suggest that improving overnight FBA accuracy may depend on stable EEG acquisition and/or preselection of reliable sleep segments using feature-based methods.

Discussion

In this study we used supervised deep learning with overnight EEG data from children to predict Functional Brain Age (FBA), efficiently modelling the intricate patterns of sleep state architecture from infancy to adolescence. Building on our previous work, we demonstrate that FBA predictions can be optimized using neural network architectures8, such as the ResNet, which effectively account for the diversity of sleep states, transitions between states, and neurodevelopmental signatures of the EEG inherent to childhood sleep9. Findings from this study reveal that FBA accuracy in children is not confined to specific periods of the night, underscoring the value in leveraging the entire night’s EEG to optimize brain age predictions. These findings provide a robust benchmark for refining ML-derived age prediction models from pediatric sleep EEG collected from the overnight PSG.

Our findings show that FBA accuracy is maximized by using a full night’s sleep architecture, high-quality EEG time series data, and a sufficient number of EEG channels, making these key factors for optimizing age prediction via neural networks. Despite the wide chronological age range and temporal variations in sleep state EEG, the lowest MAE (0.96 years) and wMAE (1.08 years) achieved in this study offers improvements over previous models that focused only on N1/N2 states with the limited-channel EEG montages (wMAE = 1.22 years) typically used in pediatric PSG8. The improved performance is likely due to the inclusion of a broader range of sleep states during training (from ~ 20 K to > 700 K data points), allowing the model to train more effectively from a wider range of EEG patterns. Comparatively, resting-state EEG brain age models, have reported MAEs around 0.25 years in infants10 and 1.22 years in children/adolescents11, based on discretized age bins and shorter EEG recordings. Furthermore, our brain age models leverage minimally preprocessed EEG time series for training, bypassing the reliance on building models with handcrafted quantitative features10,12. Instead, we use handcrafted EEG features to provide interpretability to interrogate individual FBA predictions from overnight data. This approach takes full advantage of the passive, minimally intrusive nature of PSG-derived EEG, preserving rich, unaveraged data and capturing variability across the hypnogram more effectively. The abundance of sleep EEG data (average length of 8 h) thus enables end-to-end training with deep learning models, enhancing accuracy and generalizability compared to existing pediatric EEG-based age prediction models that rely on stricter acquisition protocols and subsequently, shorter training sequences10,11,13.

Age bias and heteroscedasticity is evident in our data through the negative correlation between predicted age differences and actual age and an increasing PAD in adolescents, which are well-recognized challenges in age prediction models10. Building on this, future work could leverage the high-dimensional EEG feature space available across sleep, utilizing the temporal context of sleep states and their interaction with chronological age, to implement adversarial tests—such as a parallel classifier or regressor14—that selectively reduce age-related bias in the learned representations. This approach could refine predictions for typical development and extend to larger, mixed typical and atypical cohorts, ensuring robust and generalizable representations across diverse demographic groups. However, in our study, this increasing variance may not simply reflect model error but could instead reflect meaningful variability in brain activity during adolescence, a period marked by highly individualised trajectories in brain maturation shaped by genetic, environmental, and social-emotional factors. On the other hand, some of the observed bias stems from methodological factors common to non-linear models such as regression to the mean (e.g. in our study, higher numbers of children with EEG aged between 4 and 9 years compared to other age groups), a common problem in non-linear models that complicate accurate predictions and is often subject to age-bias correction methods15,16. In our case, the residual in our models is informative, with the Res-NN showing a higher predicted age difference (R2 = 0.36) compared to the Res-NN-Seq (R2 = 0.23). The incorporation of a BiLSTM in the Res-NN-Seq facilitates a hybrid approach by which feature maps from the Res-NN’s final ReLU layer generate a refined age prediction model. We postulate that the reduction in age bias is driven by temporal sequences inherent to age-related EEG patterns and the macrostructure of sleep architecture, which helps minimize biases that typically converge toward the population mean. This ability to capture transitions between stages may be crucial for accurate age prediction. Both models leverage temporal context, and hybrid networks like Res-NN-Seq can be as effective as traditional bias correction methods, as they address age-related bias by learning directly from the data, rather than applying post-hoc corrections to residuals.

While there are small differences in the overall error margins of predicted age across sleep states, supervised deep learning with PSG-derived EEG shows that age prediction is generally effective and widely applicable. We find that N2, N3, and REM states show the highest FBA accuracy, largely because these states are more prevalent during childhood compared to wake and N1 states, which typically appear at the beginning of a recording1,17. The abundance of these states enables more effective training of EEG epochs with chronological age, as their distinct signatures—such as sleep spindles and K-complexes in N2, high-amplitude slow delta waves in N3, and low-amplitude mixed frequencies in REM18—serve as key cortical markers that bridge the EEG from infancy to adolescence. However, the natural variation and progression of sleep state distribution with age3 (e.g., REM being more prominent in younger infants and N3 in older children) poses certain challenges for achieving group-level generalizability of the FBA.

Our cross-validation experiments with temporal segments of EEG provide valuable insights for improving FBA predictions. Notably, training on EEG from the second half of the night yielded more accurate and generalized results, likely due to the prominence of REM sleep during this period. This suggests that the later segments provide a more representative window for age prediction, as compared to the first half, where N3 sleep dominates and may not generalize as effectively19,20. N2 sleep, which is present throughout the overnight periods, offers a consistent basis for prediction. The key takeaway is that while developmental variation is essential for age estimation when analyzing childhood EEG with machine learning, careful selection of temporal segments and model optimization are critical factors to consider in producing developmentally valid age predictions.

The variability of FBA accuracy was also highly interpretable through both handcrafted EEG features, network explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), and sleep efficiency. Lower sleep efficiency—indicated by delayed sleep onset or frequent awakenings—can reduce FBA prediction accuracy, especially in children with frequent disruptions (i.e., not achieving a depth of sleep or stable EEG). The impact of reduced sleep efficiency and subsequent poorer brain age accuracy is most pronounced in adolescents, a group known for greater variations in sleep efficiency maturation and socioemotional adjustment21. We also find that FBA accuracy correlates with sleep depth (as indicated by increases in delta power) and transitions between sleep states as the night progresses. FBA accuracy is also generally more stable during NREM stages, particularly N2 and N3, with exceptions in REM sleep for younger children (< 5 years) and wake stages for older children (> 12 years). As sleep consolidates with age (fewer transitions and more pronounced NREM/REM cycles), prediction stability improves. Thus, while individual transitions can be highly informative for very young children, the emerging consistency of consolidated sleep architecture22,23 further supports reliable age estimation. These findings suggest that individual differences in the microstructure of sleep, including sleep stage distribution, EEG amplitude, and spectral characteristics, are crucial in influencing FBA prediction accuracy. Furthermore, the relationships between FBA accuracy, Grad-CAM, QEEG features, and maturational sleep changes underscore the potential of EEG-based age prediction as a biomarker for tracking maturational variability, reflecting the evolving nature of sleep patterns across development.

This study has certain limitations worthy of consideration. Age-related findings from childhood sleep data derived from a tertiary sleep center are likely skewed due to the overrepresentation of children aged 4 to 9 years, resulting in an imbalance across age groups. Ideally, the training dataset would include a more representative group of children across various developmental stages with established neurodevelopmental profiles, but obtaining such data is challenging. Addressing this imbalance, particularly at the margins of the age spectrum, will be critical for improving model generalizability, potentially through the inclusion of multicentre pediatric datasets or the integration of young adult sleep data to better capture the transition from adolescence to adulthood. While weighted sampling was considered as a strategy to counterbalance the overrepresentation of 4 to 9-year-olds, our dataset of 814 children is relatively modest in size, a reweighting or undersampling of this group risked reducing effective sample diversity often seen in clinic, particularly given the relatively lightweight ResNet architectures employed. Nevertheless, our use of weighted MAE across age bins provides a pragmatic summary of model performance in the presence of the inherent age imbalance.

As sample sizes grow and clinical populations become more diverse, more sophisticated sampling strategies will become increasingly important, particularly for models aimed at accurately capturing developmental variability across typical and atypical populations. Despite the age group imbalance in our study, our results show that over 80% of children in the under-2 years (n = 44) and over-12 years (n = 160) groups have median FBAs within ± 25% of their chronological age. However, the median FBA may not fully capture the variability in younger children, where stable consecutive epoch-to-epoch predictions are typically limited to specific portions of the night. In children under 2, these predictions typically last between 1 and 10 min (Fig. 3a). When the error margin is expanded to ± 50% of chronological age, consecutive epochs within this range can extend up to 20 min of stable epoch-to-epoch prediction. The interaction between stable epoch-to-epoch predictions and the distribution of sleep states could provide more reliable age predictions than focusing on specific sleep periods linked to particular ages. This natural variation of sleep states is therefore an additional variable to consider, as it influences the accuracy and consistency of FBA predictions across different age groups. Beyond these technical considerations, the clinical interpretation of FBA (i.e., younger- or older-appearing predicted brain age) warrants careful evaluation. For example, an FBA substantially younger than expected may highlight children at risk for atypical neurodevelopment24,25, suggesting the potential value of targeted screening or early intervention. Importantly, dysmaturity in EEG patterns may be present across multiple sleep stages, and stage-specific maturational delays or disruptions could interact with underlying pathophysiological processes, such as sleep-disordered breathing or autonomic dysregulation26,27. Such deviations in EEG features, including those associated with depression or other psychopathological states28,29, could contribute to an older-appearing FBA, reflecting a meaningful divergence from typical age-appropriate brain activity rather than a statistical outlier. Examining FBA in a sleep-state–dependent manner may therefore provide additional diagnostic sensitivity, helping to distinguish typical developmental variability from clinically relevant disruptions. These observations highlight the need to further fine-tune the technical capacity of FBA as an informative biomarker, leveraging complementary sleep metrics and EEG features to enhance its sensitivity and interpretability.

The use of hybridized CNNs with BiLSTM in our study essentially applies transfer learning by leveraging the feature maps from the CNN and retraining a BiLSTM model based on these maps. Transfer learning, in this context, contributed to a refined, pretrained age prediction model in our dataset. While ResNets are effective and relatively lightweight, they are outperformed by more advanced architectures and can face limitations when scaling to larger datasets due to computational constraints. As more diverse datasets from broader clinical populations become available, future work could benefit from more advanced architectures. Networks such as ConvNeXt, U-Net, and transformers are already showing promise in sleep stage prediction tasks30–32 due to their capacity for deeper and more complex representations. These models are well suited to large-scale training, although they require high-performance computing resources to be implemented effectively.

Lastly, our data are based on a single night of polysomnography (PSG), and first-night effects (e.g., unfamiliar environment) can introduce noise that lowers FBA accuracy. Our analysis of the factors influencing prediction accuracy (Fig. 3) suggests that the use of consecutive nights of PSG and longer-term monitoring could lead to improved FBA estimates and reduced environmental influences22. Collecting PSG data across multiple nights—while not commonly performed in routine clinical care—would enable assessment of within-subject stability, enhancing the reliability and robustness of FBA measurements over time. This is especially important for clinical groups (such as children with sleep-disordered breathing), whose sleep architecture is already disrupted by events like apnea and arousal2. By capturing multiple nights of data, FBA can leverage these events to provide insights into pathological processes (e.g., SDB severity) while also tracking underlying developmental trajectories. Future studies could explore FBA stability in these clinical cohorts—comparing single-night versus multi-night recordings, evaluating effects of interventions (e.g., CPAP), and assessing developmental versus pathological signals—an endeavor that remains the subject of future work.

Our work provides a summary of age prediction performance in children via the application of supervised deep learning methods to the pediatric EEG across sleep state architecture. This framework not only optimizes age estimation from EEG but also accounts for the unique sleep patterns observed across infants, children, and adolescents, and the factors that drive accurate and unstable age predictions. This knowledge offers potential for exploring the interaction between sleep maturation and brain activity, with promising applications in sleep intervention studies, in either typical or atypically developing populations, and for tracking the evolution of sleep states and functional brain age in relation to behavioral and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study dataset

We studied PSG recordings from 814 typically developing children with normal sleep outcomes. These recordings were sourced from a larger convenience sample of 3800 sleep studies that were performed at the Queensland Children’s Hospital between 2015 and 2021. PSG was collected in these children to diagnose the presence of a pediatric sleep disorder (e.g. central sleep apnea, nocturnal hypoventilation, obstructive sleep apnea). The 814 studies used in this study were those diagnosed as a ‘normal sleep study’ with ‘no evidence of sleep disordered breathing’, and conducted in children with no diagnosed underlying neurological, developmental, genetic, or cardiorespiratory condition. The age range of the cohort was between 3 months and 18 years of age (median age of 8 years, IQR = 5.7 years), with 321 females (39%) and 493 males (61%). Further information extracted from each child’s PSG report included body-mass indices (BMI, n = 723) and sleep efficiency (n = 814), a measure that indicates the total time spent asleep relative to the total time spent in bed. All sleep stages were seen, scored, and labelled by a clinician according to AASM guidelines33. PSG was acquired via the EMBLA N7000 (Natus Neuro, Middleton, WI, USA). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the relevant institutional guidelines and regulations. Ethics approval for data compilation was obtained from Children’s Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (LNR/2021/QCHQ/73,595) and from the QIMR Berghofer Medical Research Institute Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC Nos. P3736 and P3727). Under the Public Health Act 2005 (PHA approval no. 73595), a waiver of individual informed consent was granted by the Children’s Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee for analysis of these retrospective cohorts. Confidentiality and privacy rights of all subjects were maintained in accordance with institutional requirements.

Data processing

The EEG component of PSG recordings was recorded using 10–20 electrode positions at F4, C4 and O2, with reference electrodes attached to the mastoid (A1/M1 and A2/M2). EEG recordings were sampled at either 200 or 500 Hz. Using the EEG channels available, we derived two montages which included: two bipolar derivations (F4-C4, C4-O2) and three referential derivations as per AASM polysomnography (F4-M1, C4-M1, O2-M1). For this study, all EEG data were resampled to 64 Hz and then bandpass filtered in both forward and reverse directions (zero phase) with an infinite impulse response, 4th order Butterworth filter with a lower cutoff of 0.5 Hz and an upper cutoff of 30 Hz. EEG recordings were divided into 30-s non-overlapping segments (epochs) that aligned with sleep state timestamps available from their overnight hypnogram. The overnight hypnogram contained sleep scored annotations of five stages: wake (W), stage 1 (N1 sleep) non-rapid eye movement (NREM) characterized by low-amplitude mixed frequency activity, stage 2 (N2 sleep) characterized by sleep spindles and K-complexes, stage 3 (N3 sleep) which is typically slow-wave EEG activity, and rapid eye-movement (REM) states33. For each subject, EEG data were recorded from five channels and annotated in 30-s epochs across five sleep stages, starting from the time the child went to bed. As a summary the median (± SD) epochs per subject were 1096 ± 110 epochs; with a total number of 880,897 epochs comprising 134,617 wake epochs, 12,923 N1 epochs, 370,328 N2 epochs, 190,828 N3 epochs, and 172,201 REM epochs. Machine learning

For this study we used a residual neural network (Res-NN) architecture previously employed for age prediction from the EEG8 to predict a functional brain age (FBA) across all sleep states. Inputs for training and testing with the Res-NN included EEG time series data from overnight sleep, containing a mixture of sleep states, and the chronological ages of children. The Res-NN was chosen as the candidate architecture for estimating age due to superior performance in age prediction compared to methods such as Support Vector Regression and Gaussian Process Regression. To interrogate the accuracy of the Res-NN, we modified the following neural network parameters: temporal filter width (FW), filter channel depth (FD) and filter number (FN) within the convolutional layers, and mini-batch sizes during training on our new dataset. Initial values for these parameters were chosen for age prediction, with a fixed grid approach used to minimize root mean squared error (RMSE) and MAE also tested to ascertain performance variability (see Supplementary Fig. 3 for a summary performance across these parameters). Based on these experiments and the availability of EEG channels, the Res-NN was configured with inputs FW = 9, FD = 8, FN = 32, a mini-batch size of 256, and a data input window of 1920 (corresponding to a 30-s EEG epoch sampled at 64 Hz), resulting in approximately 600 K learnables.

Additionally, we also tested a hybridized ResNet with a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) back end. Here we used the Res-NN’s feature extraction to obtain globally averaged pooled activations from each 30-s epoch of EEG from the pretrained Res-NN. These activations were extracted specifically from layers prior to the fully connected layer of the Res-NN. We used these features to investigate whether incorporating the temporal sequence of EEG activations could improve age prediction, with the reasoning that the sequence (and transitions) between sleep stages carry age-related information that isolated epochs might miss. The temporal feature sequences were first centered, with the sequences consisting of features, where for a given M sequence length, approximately M/2 epochs backwards in time and forwards in time were selected, capturing either consecutively annotated sleep states (e.g. N2, N2, N2) or changes in sleep state annotation (e.g. N2, N3, N3). These centered feature sequences were used as inputs into a stateful BiLSTM network, with chronological age as the target variable. The intuition here was that by feeding these sequences into a stateful BiLSTM, the model could capture both the temporal order of sleep stages and the dynamics of their transitions. A range of sequence lengths (M = 7, 11, 21) were trained and tested to examine the variation in FBA performance; a sequence length of 7 was chosen for our final Res-NN-Seq model as longer lengths yielded no additional benefit in minimizing MAE and wMAE. The Res-NN and Res-Seq-NN architectures are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4.

For both the Res-NN and Res-NN-Seq networks, we used tenfold cross-validation to assess age prediction performance. Cross-validation entailed a train-test split where 90% of the cohort of EEG recordings were used for training and 10% were held-out for testing, with the process repeated 10 times so that all EEG recordings were excluded from training for at least one iteration (fold). An internal validation set, comprising a subset of training data, was used to internally validate model performance during training and enact a stopping criterion to ensure that the network did not overtrain. Hyperparameters for the Res-NN and Res-NN-Seq were set as follows: an initial learning rate of 0.0001 was chosen for training, and the learning rate was reduced by a factor of 0.1 every 8 epochs. The maximum number of training epochs was limited to 50. Additionally, the squared gradient decay factor for the ADAM solver was set to 0.99. All data extraction, network implementation, and training were conducted on a single PC with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) W-1290P CPU running at 3.70 GHz, 64 GB RAM and a GTX 3080 GPU with 10 GB of VRAM. We used the deep learning toolbox in MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc, Natick, Massachusetts, USA; R2022a) to develop our prediction of FBA.

We also applied Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) as an additional benchmarking tool alongside the Res-NN. GPR models variability in age predictions by first assuming a prior distribution over all possible functions that map EEG features to age. After observing EEG–age data, this prior is updated to a posterior distribution, which provides both a predicted age and its uncertainty for new EEG inputs. This approach provided a feature-based framework for predicting age. Using this approach, we combined 32 quantitative EEG measures34 to predict age. A Shapley analysis on our cross-validated GPR was used to identify which EEG features and combinatorial feature sets (e.g. amplitude, frequency, or informational features), contributed the most towards age prediction across sleep states. Shapley values weight the contribution of each feature to the overall prediction by adding each feature to all possible subsets, and distributing the prediction value based on each feature’s marginal contribution across scenarios35.

FBA estimation during sleep

Using our neural network architectures, we performed a series of iterative computational experiments to assess the performance of age prediction from the EEG across childhood sleep architecture. We first estimated FBA across the overnight hypnogram (median sleep study length of 8 h per subject). To load all data into memory and train on a single computer, resampled EEG data at 64 Hz across 5 channels was converted to 16-bit integers to derive a tensor of size: (1920, 5, 1, 880,897), utilizing 57.6 gigabytes of memory on average per fold. The average computation time for training the Res-NN would vary depending on the parameters chosen, e.g. the average time for a training fold (792,180 epochs) and with our optimal Res-NN parameters was 50 ± 15 min; increasing the approximate number of learnables of the Res-NN from 600 K to 6 M, for example, increased the training time by approximately 450 ± 55 min. The training time of the Res-Seq-NN was 8 ± 2 min, but the use of this network was dependent on successful training and optimization of the Res-NN. Performance of the FBA was assessed across the entire night (median FBA) and further examined within individual sleep states and discretized periods of overnight sleep (i.e., start, middle, and last thirds of sleep). We ensured that the distribution of age was balanced and randomized across the training, testing, and internal validation folds to prevent unwanted effects of age representation in any particular fold. For each subject we also extracted FBA changes with respect to wake, N1, N2, N3, and REM states. Further cross-validation tests varied the length of the hypnogram to fixed durations, where FBAs were derived for the first: i) 30 min of wake and sleep, ii) 30 min of sleep only, ii) 60 min of sleep, iii) 90 min of sleep, and iv) 180 min of sleep. We assessed the consistency of sleep state proportions per subject across the hypnogram using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC was used to evaluate how sleep states were distributed across all subjects over increasing time periods, from discrete segments (e.g., 30-min intervals) to the full overnight period.

Additional variations of the FBA were derived across individual sleep states and EEG channel combinations to further evaluate age prediction performance. Here, training within sleep states was subject to the availability of epochs per subject. Channel-specific FBAs were derived across bipolar montage groups (F4-C4, C4-O2) and referential montage group (F4-M1, C4-M1, O2-M1), and individual/single channel derivations (e.g. F4-M1).

We tested the robustness of age relationships with the temporal structure of the hypnogram, and sleep state prediction. Models were trained on the first 180 min of EEG data and tested on the remaining two-thirds of the overnight recording. We also reversed this test by training on the last two-thirds of the night and testing on the first 180 min of sleep. Other tests involved training on different segments, such as the middle or last portions of sleep, and testing on the held-out hours (e.g., the first two-thirds or the first and last thirds of sleep).

To investigate the relationship between sleep state and FBA predictions, we modified the Res-NN network architecture to independently predict both age and sleep state. Specifically, we repurposed the final layer of globally averaged activations—originally trained to predict age—to predict sleep state. This was compared to a separate sleep state classifier that used the same Res-NN network parameters. For our sleep-state classifier, the limited number of N1 epochs led us to merge N1 with N2, resulting in four classes for training: Wake, N1/N2, N3, and REM. Additionally, we extracted globally averaged activations from the Res-NN sleep state classifier to predict age. By combining these approaches, we were able to assess whether the feature extraction stage of the EEG-derived age predictions inherently capture sleep state information, as well as the mutual information between the two approaches.

Performance metrics

To assess differences between predicted age estimates and chronological age, we derived several metrics. The residual error or predicted age difference (predicted age difference, PAD) was used to assess the difference in years between the FBA and chronological age. The accuracy of prediction was derived via mean absolute error (MAE), which was calculated as the average absolute difference between predicted age and chronological age. A weighted MAE (wMAE) was also computed, which weighted the distribution of age against MAE. The wMAE thus represents an average MAE across age bins (e.g. bin width = 2 years). Using the PAD, we calculated the relative error with respect to age, assessed as the percentage change between predicted age (FBA) and chronological age. We used a threshold of ≤ ± 25% chronological age to track probable error of FBA predictions across all EEG epochs per subject, corresponding roughly to one standard deviation for an age group with a normal distribution36. Although anthropometric growth charts typically allow variations up to ± 3 standard deviations, our ± 25% cutoff is more conservative, providing a basis for future flexibility in brain age prediction models.

Performance of the sleep state classifier was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa, sensitivity, and specificity to quantify agreement with manual annotations and classification accuracy across sleep stages. These metrics were calculated for both the repurposed age-prediction model and the dedicated sleep state classifier to compare classification fidelity and explore the degree of shared representational features between age and sleep state outputs.

To further investigate the stability of the FBA, we extracted the average run lengths (in minutes) of FBA epochs for each subject, focusing on those consistently within ± 25% of the subject’s chronological age over the full night’s recording. Epochs within this range in the hypnogram were marked as ‘1’, while those outside this range were marked as ‘0’. Consecutive epochs marked as ‘1’ (within ± 25% age range) were tallied into a run length until an epoch marked as ‘0’ (outside ± 25% range) occurred, indicating a break in the sequence. The mean run length for each subject was calculated by averaging these values across the entire hypnogram and converting them to minutes. Additionally, we examined the distribution of run lengths across 2-year age bins and calculated the skewness of these distributions for each bin per subject. Spearman correlations between run length and skewness were then computed for each age bin to explore whether the clustering of run lengths was associated with the average run length of epochs.

To enhance the interpretability of the FBA model’s accuracy, we employed explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques; specifically, we used gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)37. Grad-CAM is commonly applied to the final layer of convolutional networks to capture spatial activations of neurons directly related to the model’s output. For the Res-NN, we extracted Grad-CAM from the ReLU layer preceding the fully connected layer, as the gradients of feature activations within this layer were strongly correlated with age. We used Grad-CAM in two ways: (i) for correlating the median Grad-CAM values across all subjects with EEG features to investigate the relationship between the model’s activation patterns and various quantitative EEG features, and (ii) for examining sleep state-specific Grad-CAM values in relation to different EEG feature types, which included amplitude, spectral, and informational features. We also compared the top EEG features identified by Shapley analysis with our median Grad-CAM, which highlighted a subset of the most influential features within these categories.

We generated two types of confidence intervals for our data. For MAE, wMAE, ICC, and Pearson correlations, 95% confidence intervals (referred as 95CI) were generated by resampling (bootstrap) the prediction errors 1000 times with replacement and taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Additionally, we also generated confidence intervals to compare independent and overlapping correlations (referred to as 95CI_corr) between various age prediction models38. Potential interaction effects between age, sex, and BMI, as well as their individual effects, were reported using multivariable linear regression. For all other statistical tests, two-sided tests were used, the level of significance was 0.05 and determined using Mann–Whitney rank sum tests, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons (FWE) where applicable.
