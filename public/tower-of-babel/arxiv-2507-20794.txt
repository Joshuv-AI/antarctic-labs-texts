Existence, uniqueness, and long-time asymptotic behavior of regular solutions in multidimensional thermoelasticity
Piotr Michał Bies, Tomasz Cieślak, Mario Fuest, Johannes Lankeit, Boris Muha, Srdan Trifunović

Abstract
We study a simplified nonlinear thermoelasticity model on two- and three-dimensional tori. A novel functional involving the Fisher information associated with temperature is introduced, extending the previous one-dimensional approach from the first two authors (SIAM J. Math. Anal. 55 (2023), 7024–7038)) to higher dimensions. Using this functional, we prove global/local existence of unique regular solutions for small/large initial data. Furthermore, we analyze the asymptotic behavior as time approaches infinity and show that the temperature stabilizes to a constant state, while the displacement naturally decomposes into two distinct components: a divergence-free part oscillating indefinitely according to a homogeneous wave equation and a curl-free part converging to zero. Analogous results for the Lamé operator are also stated.



Keywords and phrases:
thermoelasticity, strong solutions, long-time dynamics, non-dissipative stability

AMS Mathematical Subject classification (2020):
74A15 (Primary), 74H40, 35M30 (Secondary)

Introduction.

In this paper, we investigate the existence, uniqueness, and long-term asymptotic behavior of solutions to a simplified nonlinear thermoelasticity model in higher-dimensional settings. Indeed, the objects of our interest are displacement
\mathbf{u}:[0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
and positive temperature
\theta:[0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following system of thermoelasticity representing the balance of momentum and heat:

\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{tt}-\Delta\mathbf{u}=-\mu\nabla\theta,&\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\theta_{t}-\Delta\theta=-\mu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t},&\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\mathbf{u}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{u}_{0},~\mathbf{u}_{t}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{v}_{0},~\theta(0,\cdot)=\theta_{0}>0.&\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\mu>0
is the heat expansion constant and
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is the d-dimensional torus. Here, other constants have been set to
1
for simplicity.
We refer the reader to
[
22
,
11
]
(or to
[
16
]
) concerning the physical justification of the model. Let us however briefly mention that (
1.1
) is the simplest possible nonlinearly coupled model of the thermoelasticity phenomenon which is consistent with the laws of thermodynamics, where the Helmholtz free energy is assumed of the
form

\displaystyle H(\theta,\nabla\mathbf{u})=\theta\log\theta-\theta-\mu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}.

The reader is referred to the 1D version of the model in
[
5
,
6
]
which was studied by the first two authors.

Remark 1.1
.

We restrict our analysis to the toroidal domain to isolate and clearly illustrate the essential mathematical difficulties arising from the nonlinear coupling, while avoiding technical complications related to boundary conditions. The analysis of more general bounded domains with appropriate physical boundary conditions remains an interesting and important direction for future work.

Notice that we can formally multiply

(1.2)

Due to expected positivity of the temperature, we can study the evolution of the entropy as well.
Correspondingly, the entropy evolution equation arising from dividing the heat equation (

\displaystyle(\ln\theta+\mu\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})_{t}-\Delta\ln\theta=|\nabla\ln\theta|^{2}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\mathbf{u}_{t}|^{2}(t)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}(t)dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\theta-\ln\theta)(t)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\ln\theta|^{2}dxd\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\theta_{0}-\ln\theta_{0})dx.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that
\theta-\ln\theta>0
, so in the case of global solutions this (in)equality gives control of
\nabla\ln\theta
in
L^{2}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
. It is important to point out that this bound does not come from dissipation. In fact, there is no dissipation in the system and the only stabilizing effect comes from the entropy production. Surprisingly so, this will be enough to ensure the decay of temperature to a steady state and consequently the decay of the curl-free part of displacement to zero.

The following functional will play a key role in the this paper

\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{t}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\Delta\mathbf{u})^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx\right).

It turns out that it has the following attractor set property: there is a constant
D>0
depending on
\mu,d
and
\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}(t),\theta(t)))\leq D\implies\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}(t+\tau),\theta(t+\tau)))\leq D\text{ for all }\tau>0.

This allows us to establish the existence of global-in-time solutions to (
1.1
) for initial data satisfying
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\theta_{0}))\leq D
. For general (possibly large) initial data, we will show the existence of local-in-time solutions. Moreover, the obtained regularity will be sufficient to ensure the uniqueness of solutions. Finally, we analyze the long-time asymptotic dynamics of global solutions. The displacement naturally splits into two distinct components via the Helmholtz decomposition: a divergence-free part, which oscillates indefinitely according to the homogeneous wave equation, and a curl-free part, which converges to zero as time approaches infinity. This asymptotic behavior is fundamentally connected to the structure of the coupling in the heat equation, which depends exclusively on
\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}
. Consequently, the divergence-free component is unaffected by thermal dissipation and retains its oscillatory nature. Interestingly, this phenomenon differs from the one-dimensional setting studied in
[
6
]
, as higher dimensions provide sufficient spatial freedom to sustain permanent oscillations within the divergence-free component, while the curl-free component remains stabilized by thermal effects.

2.1
Discussion of the literature

Thermoelasticity systems have long history, see for instance
[
16
]
. The system we study, (
1.1
), is a particular thermoelasticity system with the Helmholtz free energy given as

\displaystyle H(\theta,\nabla\mathbf{u})=\theta\log\theta-\theta+\mu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}.

The local-in-time unique solutions to the one-dimensional nonlinear thermoelastic system have been constructed by Slemrod in
[
22
]
. Next, the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the one-dimensional nonlinear thermoelastic system in the neighborhood of steady states has also been studied, see for instance
[
22
,
16
,
10
]
. Such results typically follow from energy estimates or fixed-point arguments for the coupled hyperbolic–parabolic system. Further refinements were provided by
R. Racke
and collaborators, who established that in 1D, global solutions are exponentially stable when initial perturbations are small
[
16
]
. Extending these results to higher dimensions proved challenging due to the presence of undamped rotational modes. In
[
16
]
, global solutions in 2D and 3D under symmetry conditions eliminating rotational effects are obtained. A key challenge, however, is the question of
global existence
: whether solutions remain well-defined for all time or develop singularities in finite time. It is well known that for sufficiently large initial data, smooth solutions to the highly nonlinear one-dimensional thermoelasticity system can experience finite-time blow-up due to nonlinear coupling effects, as shown by
C. Dafermos
and
L. Hsiao

[
12
]
.
W. Hrusa
and
S. Messaoudi
extended this analysis to the physically more relevant case, proving that without smallness conditions, global smooth solutions generally do not exist
[
15
]
. Global weak solutions have been constructed in
[
11
]
(see also a construction of solutions to the similar model in
[
8
]
). Finally, recently the global existence of unique solutions to the 1D case of (
1.1
) has been shown in
[
5
]
, introducing a novel functional related to the Fisher information to control the nonlinear coupling.

Another crucial aspect of thermoelastic systems is their
asymptotic behavior
. Since thermal diffusion induces dissipation, one expects that perturbations of equilibrium states decay over time. In 1D,
M. Slemrod
proved exponential decay for small solutions (see
[
22
]
). In
[
6
]
, the full asymptotic behavior of the 1D minimal nonlinear thermoelasticity system, without restriction on initial data, is shown. However, the situation is more subtle in multiple dimensions due to the presence of undamped divergence-free modes.
R. Racke
and others demonstrated that when the displacement’s
curl
is initially zero, the solution exhibits full decay
[
16
]
, but in general, divergence-free components persist as oscillations. This phenomenon is consistent with our findings: We rigorously show that in 2D and 3D, the displacement naturally splits into an oscillatory divergence-free part and a curl-free part that is dampened by thermal effects. See also
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
for the convergence rates in the linear thermoelasticity.

Beyond classical smooth solutions, researchers have also studied
weak solutions
of thermoelastic systems. In three dimensions,
[
11
]
constructed
global weak solutions
via a half-Galerkin approximation and compactness arguments. This result ensures global solvability for large initial data but at the cost of lower regularity. A more generalized framework was considered by
C. Christoforou, M. Galanopoulou
, and
A. Tzavaras

[
8
]
, who studied
measure-valued solutions
to capture fine-scale oscillations and singularities in thermoelastic systems. While such weak solutions are less regular, they provide global existence even in regimes where classical solutions may fail. A related study in
[
10
]
analyzed a simplified one-dimensional thermoelasticity model, providing insights into the interplay between heat diffusion and elastic wave propagation in lower dimensions.

Finally, for a comprehensive background on the mathematical theory of thermoelasticity, we refer the reader to the monograph by
S. Jiang
and
R. Racke

[
16
]
.

Plan of the paper

In Section

3
A priori estimates

The first step towards constructing solutions consists of obtaining suitable a priori estimates. These will initially be derived under the assumption that solutions are sufficiently regular. Later, in sections dealing explicitly with the construction of solutions, we will explain how these a priori estimates can be rigorously justified within our procedures.

Note that the choice of the functional

is motivated by the need to control both displacement and temperature gradients simultaneously, using Fisher information, which naturally captures the smoothing properties of the heat equation. Its monotonicity-like properties in Lemma

will enable us to obtain estimates essential for global-in-time existence. First let us show the following inequality, which is the torus version of

Lemma 3.3
.

There is
C>0
(depending only on

\displaystyle\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

Proof.

Let us denote
\langle v\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}vdx
. We calculate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla v|^{2}dx=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(v-\langle v\rangle)\Delta vdx.

Here, we use the Schwarz and the Poincaré inequality. We get

\displaystyle\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\|v-\langle v\rangle\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

We divide the above inequality by
\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
and get the claim.
∎

Similarly, the below inequality is the torus version of

Lemma 3.4

Let

Lemma 3.5

Let

Proof.

By Lemma

Lemma 3.6

6
Time-asymptotics of solutions – proof of Theorem
2.4

In this section, we analyze the time-asymptotic behavior of the obtained solutions. We begin by recalling that Theorem
2.2
guarantees the existence of global solutions, provided that the initial data satisfy

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\left\|\frac{\nabla\theta_{0}}{\sqrt{\theta_{0}}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq D

(6.1)

as well as upper and lower bounds for the initial temperature.

A key observation is that the displacement naturally decomposes into two components: one that exhibits persistent oscillations and another that flattens out due to heat dissipation. Specifically, the divergence-free part of the displacement remains oscillatory, while the curl-free part is dampened by thermal effects. This decomposition arises naturally from the Helmholtz decomposition, which allows us to separate the displacement equation into two distinct parts: a homogeneous wave equation governing the divergence-free component and a strongly coupled system describing the evolution of the curl-free component under the influence of heat propagation.

Ultimately, the curl-free component behaves analogously to a vibrating string in the one-dimensional thermoelasticity model (see
[
6
]
), while the primary asymptotic distinction in higher dimensions arises from the divergence-free component, which retains its oscillatory nature.

6.1
Decomposition of solutions

In this subsection, we recall the Helmholtz decomposition on the torus. Although both the statement and its proof can be found in the literature, we provide them here for completeness.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let us assume that
\mathbf{v}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=H\mathbf{v}+H^{\perp}\mathbf{v},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi\,dx=0\quad\textrm{ and }\quad H^{\perp}\mathbf{v}=\nabla\varphi.

and

Let

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi=\operatorname{div}\mathbf{v}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi\,dx=0.

Due to elliptic regularity theory (see

We define

\displaystyle H\mathbf{v}:=\mathbf{v}-\nabla\varphi,\quad H^{\perp}\mathbf{v}:=\nabla\varphi.

Then, the defined functions verify the conditions in the claim. In particular,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\mathbf{v}\nabla\varphi dx=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{div}H\mathbf{v}\varphi dx=0.\qed

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\nu}|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}|^{2}dx

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta_{0}\,dx.

Proof.

Let us deal with the wave equation from (
1.1
). We multiply it by
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\nu}
and integrate over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\nu}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\nu})dx=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\theta\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\nu})dx.

We integrate the right-hand side by parts and see that it disappears due to the definition of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. Let us consider the second integral on the left-hand side. We know that
\boldsymbol{\chi}=H^{\perp}\mathbf{u}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\nu})dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H^{\perp}(\mathbf{u}_{tt}-\Delta\mathbf{u})H(\mathbf{u}_{tt}-\Delta\mathbf{u})dx=0.

Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\nu}|^{2}dx=0,

which implies that
\nu
satisfies (
6.2
).

We know that
\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}=\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\chi}+\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\chi}
. It shows that the lower equation from (
6.3
) is satisfied. The upper is attained when we subtract (
6.2
) from (
1.1
). Then the energy decomposition follows.
∎

6.2
Displacement and temperature at infinity

From the previous section, we know that the displacement decomposes into two different vector fields

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\chi}.

As time progresses,
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and
\boldsymbol{\chi}
exhibit fundamentally different behaviors. Specifically, the divergence-free component
\boldsymbol{\nu}
remains oscillatory unless its initial value is identically zero. To formalize this observation, we state the following proposition.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
be a global solution of (
1.1
). Then,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}:=H\mathbf{u}\to 0\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\textrm{ as }t\to\infty

(6.4)

if and only if
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}:=H\mathbf{u}_{0}=0
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}:=H\mathbf{v}_{0}=0
.

Proof.

Due to Theorem
6.2
, the vector field
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is a solution of (
6.2
). This is the wave equation. If
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}=0
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}=0
, then
\boldsymbol{\nu}=0
.

Now, let us assume that (
6.4
) is valid. Let us denote that
\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\}
is basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
consisting of eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, we assume that
\|\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=1
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us also denote the sequence of eigenvalues of
-\Delta
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
as
\{\lambda_{i}\}
. Then, Hence, we can write
\boldsymbol{\nu}(t,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{i}(t)\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}(x)
, where
d_{i}
is a solution of the problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d_{i}^{\prime\prime}+\lambda_{i}d_{i}=0&\textrm{on }[0,\infty),\\
d_{i}(0)=(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},\ d_{i}^{\prime}(0)=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.\end{cases}

Condition (
6.4
) implies that
d_{i}(t)=(\boldsymbol{\nu}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\to 0
, as
t\to\infty
for each
i
. Because
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
, this could happen only if
d_{i}=0
. It means that
\boldsymbol{\nu}=0
and we derive that
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}=0
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}=0
.
∎

We notice that even in the nonhomogeneous case of the wave equation, oscillations prevail almost always, see
[
4
]
for details.

Let us now focus on the asymptotics of the curl-free part of
\mathbf{u}
(i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\chi}
) and the temperature. Our analysis follows morally the one in
[
6
]
, however technical details need to be adjusted. As in
[
6
]
, we start with the stability lemma in appropriate functional spaces.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{0,1},\mathbf{u}_{0,2}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\mathbf{v}_{0,1},\mathbf{v}_{0,2}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\theta_{0,1},\theta_{0,2}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Moreover, we require that there exists
\tilde{\theta}_{1}>0
and
\tilde{\theta}_{2}>0
such that
\tilde{\theta}_{1}\leq\theta_{0,1}
and
\tilde{\theta}_{2}\leq\theta_{0,2}
.
Let us also assume that
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\theta_{1})
and
(\mathbf{u}_{2},\theta_{2})
are global solutions of (
1.1
) (as obtained in Theorem
2.2
) starting from
\mathbf{u}_{0,1},\mathbf{v}_{0,1},\theta_{0,1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{0,2},\mathbf{v}_{0,2},\theta_{0,2}
, respectively. Then, for any
T>0
, there exists
C=C(T,d,|\mu|,\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1,t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))},\|\theta_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})},\|\nabla\theta_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))},\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})})>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}_{1}(t,\cdot)-\mathbf{u}_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\mathbf{u}_{1,t}(t,\cdot)-\mathbf{u}_{2,t}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\theta_{1}(t,\cdot)-\theta_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\left(\|\mathbf{u}_{0,1}-\mathbf{u}_{0,2}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\mathbf{v}_{0,1}-\mathbf{v}_{0,2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\theta_{0,1}-\theta_{0,2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)

for all
t\in[0,T)
.

Proof.

Let us denote
\mathbf{u}:=\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}
and
\theta:=\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
. We subtract the equations for
(\mathbf{u}_{2},\theta_{2})
from the equations for
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\theta_{1})
and
obtain

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{tt}-\Delta\mathbf{u}=-\mu\nabla\theta,\\
\theta_{t}-\Delta\theta=-\mu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}-\mu\theta_{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}.\end{cases}

(6.5)

We multiply by
\mathbf{u}_{t}
the first of the above equations and arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{u}_{t}^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\right)=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{u}_{t}\cdot\nabla\theta dx\leq C\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

(6.6)

Whereas, multiplying the second equation in (
6.5
) by
\theta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\theta|^{2}dx=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta^{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}dx-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta\theta_{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

\displaystyle=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta^{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}dx+\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta\nabla\theta_{2}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}dx+\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta_{2}\nabla\theta\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}dx=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

(6.7)

We bound the integral
I_{1}
using the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality and then the Young inequality.
We get that for each
\varepsilon
, there exists
C=C(|\mu|,\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1,T}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))})>0
such that

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq|\mu|\|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}\|\theta\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+C\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

(6.8)

The integral
I_{3}
we estimate by the Schwarz inequality and the Young inequality. We also utilize Lemma
5.2
here
and for any
\varepsilon>0
find
C=C(|\mu|,\|\theta_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})})
such that

\displaystyle I_{3}\leq|\mu|\|\theta_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+C\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

(6.9)

Let us proceed with
I_{2}
. We use the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality and the Young inequality.

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\mu|\|\theta\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\nabla\theta_{2}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})})(\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\nabla\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})})\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+C(\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}).

(6.10)

We plug (
6.8
), (
6.9
) and (
6.2
) into (
6.2
). The obtained inequality we add to (
6.6
). It leads us to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)+\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+C(\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}).

(6.11)

We take
\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{8}
.

Let us denote
y:=\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
. Then, we rewrite (
6.2
) as

\displaystyle y^{\prime}\leq Cy(1+\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}).

(6.12)

Multiplying (
6.12
) by
\operatorname{exp}\left(-C\int_{0}^{s}\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}d\lambda-Cs\right)
for
s\in(0,t]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left(y\operatorname{exp}\left(-C\int_{0}^{s}\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}d\lambda-Cs\right)\right)\leq 0.

We integrate over
[0,t]
and see that

y(t)\leq y(0)\operatorname{exp}\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}ds+Ct\right).

It gives the thesis.
∎

We denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}:=\left\{\eta\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\colon\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta(x)>0\right\}.

Let us take
t\geq 0
. We define an operator

\displaystyle S(t):H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathcal{M}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathcal{M}

by formula

\displaystyle S(t)(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0},\theta_{0})=(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}_{t}(t),\theta(t)),

where
(\bf u,\theta)
is a solution of (
1.1
) with initial values
\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0}
and
\theta_{0}
. Thanks to the uniqueness of the solutions of the system, we obtain

\displaystyle S(t+h)=S(h)\circ S(t)

for all
t,h\in[0,\infty)
.

Proposition
6.4
implies the following important corollary.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0},\theta_{0}),(\mathbf{u}_{n},\mathbf{v}_{n},\theta_{n})\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathcal{M}
be such that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}_{n},\mathbf{v}_{n},\theta_{n})\to(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0},\theta_{0})\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Moreover, assume that all the quantities occurring in the constant
C
in Proposition
6.4
are controlled along
(\mathbf{u}_{n},v_{n},\theta_{n})
. The latter means that we are restricted to a subset of a phase space. Then, we have

\displaystyle S(t)(\mathbf{u}_{n},\mathbf{v}_{n},\theta_{n})\to S(t)(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0},\theta_{0})\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

for each
t\geq 0
.

The above corollary will be applied to the sequence of values of the solution in times
t_{k}\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, since this is the same solution, the control of quantities appearing in the constant
C
in Proposition
6.4
is ensured.

We need certain global estimates for
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and
\theta
. We will prove them in the below theorem.

Theorem 6.6
.

Suppose that the initial data fulfill (
2.3
) and (
6.1
).
Then the global solution
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
of (
1.1
) given by Theorem
2.2
fulfills

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}:=H^{\perp}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\ \boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\ \boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\textrm{ and }

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\in C([0,\infty);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
, we get

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbf{u}_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\Delta\mathbf{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),

(6.13)

\displaystyle\theta\in L^{2}_{\textnormal{loc}}(0,\infty;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\quad\textrm{and}\quad\theta_{t}\in L^{2}_{\textnormal{loc}}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

Hence,
\theta\in C([0,\infty);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. The Sobolev embeddings in dimensions 2 and 3 yield the existence of a time
t_{0}
such that
u(t_{0},\cdot)
is bounded (because
\theta\in L^{2}_{\textnormal{loc}}(0,\infty;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
).
Therefore, Lemma
5.2
guarantees that
\theta\in L^{\infty}((t_{0},\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Moreover, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\theta|^{2}dx\leq\|\theta\|_{L^{\infty}((t_{0},\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})}4\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx

for
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
.
Consequently, owing to (
6.13
), we observe that
\nabla\theta\in L^{\infty}(t_{0},\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. By (
1.2
), we have that
\theta\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Consequently,
\theta\in L^{\infty}(t_{0},\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. This together with
\theta\in C([0,\infty);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
gives that
\theta\in L^{\infty}(t_{0},\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
.

Because of (
6.13
), we know that

\displaystyle\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\ \Delta\boldsymbol{\chi}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})).

(6.14)

Thanks to Theorem
6.1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\boldsymbol{\chi}dx=0.

(6.15)

Therefore, the Poincaré inequality and Lemma
3.3
give us that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

(6.16)

From the theory of regularity of solutions for elliptic equations (see
[
13
, Section 6.3]
), we have the following inequality

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C(\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\|\boldsymbol{\chi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}).

This with (
6.16
) and (
6.14
) gives us that
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Thanks to the Poincaré inequality (
6.15
) and (

), we also show that
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Due to Theorem
6.1
, we know that the equation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}-\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi}=-\mu\nabla\theta

is satisfied. We already know that
\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi},\nabla\theta\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty,L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. It implies that
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
.
∎

Now, we are in a position to formulate and prove the main theorem of this section and hence also Theorem
2.4
. It follows closely the proof in
[
6
]
.
However,

and

, respectively.

Theorem 6.7
.

Suppose that the initial data fulfill (
2.3
), (
6.1
) and
\theta_{0}^{-1}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Then for the global solution

\displaystyle\to 0\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\textrm{ as }t\to\infty,

\displaystyle\theta(t,\cdot)

,

and

\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot)
. We know by Theorem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\chi}\in C([0,\infty);&H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\ \boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}\in C([0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\\
\theta&\in C([0,\infty);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\end{split}

(6.17)

hence the sequence

) will imply that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\chi}(t,\cdot)=0\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t,\cdot)=0\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t_{n},\cdot)

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{\tilde{v}}_{\infty}\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

(6.18)

\displaystyle\theta(t_{n},\cdot)

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\theta_{\infty}\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

where

,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{v}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

By compact embeddings, we have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot)&\to\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\infty}\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t_{n},\cdot)&\to\boldsymbol{\tilde{v}}_{\infty}\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\\
\theta(t_{n},\cdot)&\to\theta_{\infty}\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).\end{split}

We know that
\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot)\in H^{\perp}(H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and
\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot)\in H^{\perp}(H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for all
n
. Because
H^{\perp}(H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and
H^{\perp}(H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
are convex and closed subsets of
H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, respectively, we obtain that
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\infty}\in H^{\perp}(H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}\in H^{\perp}(H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
.
Next, we define solutions starting from functions, which are obtained as limits of
\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot),\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t_{n},\cdot)
and
\theta(t_{n},\cdot)
. We denote by
\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
and

\bar{\theta}
the weak solution of (
1.1
) with the initial values
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\infty}
,
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
and
\theta_{\infty}
(they can be defined due to the fact
that the limiting objects have enough regularity to define weak solutions, see (
6.2
)) and Theorem
2.2
;
note that the lower bound for
\theta
obtained in Theorem
5.3
implies that bounds for
\theta_{\infty}
carry over to
\ln\theta_{\infty}
.)

Let us denote
\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=H\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
. Then, the function
\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is a solution of the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{tt}-\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=0,\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(0,\cdot)=0,\ \bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}(0,\cdot)=0.\end{cases}

Thus, we have that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=0
and then
\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\in H^{\perp}(H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}\in H^{\perp}(H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Consequently, there exists a function
\varphi
such that
\bar{\chi}=\nabla\varphi
.

Next, we take
h>0
and consider the sequences
\mathbf{u}(t_{n}+h,\cdot)
,
\mathbf{u}_{t}(t_{n}+h,\cdot)
and
\theta(t_{n}+h,\cdot)
. Similarly, as above, there exists

\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
\hat{\mathbf{v}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\hat{\theta}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n}+h,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\textrm{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t_{n}+h,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to\hat{\mathbf{v}}\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

\displaystyle\theta(t_{n}+h,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to\hat{\theta}\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

If necessary, we take a subsequence of
t_{n}
in the above convergences. By Corollary
6.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}},\hat{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n}+h,\cdot),\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n}+h,\cdot),\theta(t_{n}+h,\cdot))=\lim_{n\to\infty}S(h)(\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot),\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t_{n},\cdot),\theta(t_{n},\cdot))

\displaystyle=S(h)(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}},\mathbf{v}_{\infty},\theta_{\infty})=(\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(h,\cdot),\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}(h,\cdot),\bar{\theta}(h,\cdot)).

On the other hand, by (
1.3
), we know that a function

\displaystyle t\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\theta(t,x)dx

is non-decreasing. In view of Lemma
5.2
, it is bounded from above. Hence, it is convergent. Thus, sequences
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\theta(t_{n},x)dx
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\theta(t_{n}+h,x)dx
must be convergent to the same number. Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\theta_{\infty}dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\theta(t_{n},x)dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\theta(t_{n}+h,x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\bar{\theta}(h,x)dx.

(6.19)

Indeed,
\theta(t_{n},x)
is convergent in
L^{2}
, so also a.e. (up to a subsequence). Next, we use Lemma
5.3
to infer that both

\bar{\theta}(h,\cdot)
and
\theta_{\infty}
are bounded away from
0
. Lemma
5.2
ensures that
\log\theta
is also bounded from above. Hence, we conclude (
6.2
) via the Lebesgue theorem.

Next, we notice that (
6.2
) states that

\displaystyle t\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\bar{\theta}(t,x)dx

is constant. The second principle of thermodynamics has just told us that the integral of entropy is constant at the
\omega
-limit set. Further, we
make use of the knowledge of the dissipation rate of the entropy in (
1.3
) to obtain

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\bar{\theta}dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla\bar{\theta}|^{2}}{\bar{\theta}^{2}}dx.

Hence,

\nabla\left(\log{\bar{\theta}}\right)=0\;\;\mbox{in}\;\;\mathbb{T}^{d}

for any
t>0
. So, for each fixed
t>0
,
\bar{\theta}
is constant in space.
But then, applying (
1.3
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\bar{\theta}dx=\frac{d}{dt}\log\bar{\theta}=\frac{\bar{\theta}_{t}}{\bar{\theta}}.

It means that
\bar{\theta}
is also constant in time. So,
\bar{\theta}(t,x)
is constant in space and time.

So far, we identified all the trajectories of (
1.1
) starting from functions
\theta_{\infty}
. We observe now that, actually, the
\omega
-limit set of a solution of (
1.1
) contains only one
temperature
\theta_{\infty}
. To this end, we utilize the second principle of thermodynamics once more. Assume there are two different functions

\theta_{\infty}^{1}
and
\theta_{\infty}^{2}
, which are limits of
\theta(t_{n_{k}},\cdot)
and
\theta(t_{n_{l}},\cdot)
, respectively, for different time sequences

t_{n_{k}},t_{n_{l}}\rightarrow\infty
. Owing to (
1.3
), and following an analogous argument as above, we obtain:

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\left(\theta_{\infty}^{1}\right)dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\left(\theta_{\infty}^{2}\right)dx.

Moreover, we already know that solutions starting from
\theta_{\infty}^{1}
and
\theta_{\infty}^{2}
are constant in space and time. Hence

\log(\theta_{\infty}^{1})=\log(\theta_{\infty}^{2})\Longrightarrow\theta_{\infty}^{1}=\theta_{\infty}^{2}.

Thus, we have just shown that the
\mathbb{T}^{d}
-limit set of a solution to (
1.1
) contains a single function, which is constant. Next, we identify all potential limits of the curl-free part of the displacement.

Since
\bar{\theta}
is constant, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{tt}-\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}=0,\\
0=\mu\bar{\theta}\operatorname{div}\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}.\end{cases}

(6.20)

The second equation in (
6.20
) show that
\Delta\varphi_{t}=0
for all
(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Therefore, the function
\varphi_{t}
is a harmonic function on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Due to the periodicity and the maximum principle, this is only possible if
\varphi_{t}
is a constant. Hence,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}=\nabla\varphi_{t}=0
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and so
\bar{\chi}
is constant
in time for each
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. This, together with the first equation in (
6.20
), states that
\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}=0
, and this implies that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
is constant. However, we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\varphi dx=0,

so
\bar{\boldsymbol{\chi}}=0
.
Finally, the conservation of the energy as in Lemma
3.1
holds for the pair
(\boldsymbol{\chi},\theta)
as well

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta_{0}dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}^{2}(t_{n},x)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},x)|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta(t_{n},x)dx\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta_{\infty}dx=\theta_{\infty}.\qed

7
The Lamé operator

In this section, we shall deal with the following system

\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{tt}-(2\zeta+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}+\zeta\curl\curl\mathbf{u}=-\mu\nabla\theta,&\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\theta_{t}-\Delta\theta=-\mu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t},&\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\mathbf{u}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{u}_{0},~\mathbf{u}_{t}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{v}_{0},~\theta(0,\cdot)=\theta_{0}>0.&\end{cases}

(7.1)

We consider here the operator
Lu:=-(2\zeta+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}+\zeta\curl\curl\mathbf{u}
instead of
-\Delta\mathbf{u}
. The operator is called the Lamé operator (see
[
16
,
21
]
). The constants
\zeta,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the so-called the Lamé moduli, are given. They satisfy

\displaystyle\zeta>0\quad\textrm{ and }\quad 2\zeta+d\lambda>0.

Let us note that in the 2D case the
\curl\curl\mathbf{u}
is understood as
\nabla^{\perp}\curl\mathbf{u}
, where
\curl\mathbf{u}:=u^{2}_{x_{1}}-u^{1}_{x_{2}}
and
\nabla^{\perp}f:=(f_{x_{2}},-f_{x_{1}})
.

Approximate solutions are constructed exactly the same way as in the case of the Laplacian. Basis in the half-Galerkin procedure consists again of the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. Next, the energy and functional
\mathcal{F}
look in the similar way. Below, in the following lemmata, we calculate these quantities.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
with
\theta>0
be a smooth solution of (
7.1
). Then, we have the following total energy balance:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{u}_{t}^{2}(t)dx&+\frac{2\zeta+\lambda}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}(t)dx+\frac{\zeta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}(t)dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta(t)dx\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{2\zeta+\lambda}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u_{0}})^{2}dx+\frac{\zeta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta_{0}dx.\end{split}

(7.2)

Proof.

Let us multiply the first equation in (
7.1
) by
\mathbf{u}_{t}
and integrate over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Integrating by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{u}_{t}^{2}(t)dx+\frac{2\zeta+\lambda}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}(t)dx+\frac{\zeta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}(t)dx\right)

\displaystyle=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\theta\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

(7.3)

Next, we integrate the lower equation in (
7.1
) over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. It yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta dx=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

We add the above equality to (
7
). It gives us the claim.
∎

The functional
\mathcal{F}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{t}|^{2}dx+(2\zeta+\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\zeta\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx\right).

(7.4)

It satisfies a similar formula as in Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
with
\theta>0
be a smooth solution of (
7.1
).
Then, the equality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\theta)=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta|\nabla^{2}\log\theta|^{2}dx-\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

holds.

Proof.

First, we note that the calculations for the Fisher information in the proof of Lemma
3.2
are independent of the wave equation in (
1.1
). Hence, they are also valid for (
7.1
). Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx=-4\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta|\nabla^{2}\log\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx-\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx+\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

(7.5)

Next, we multiply the upper equation in (
7.1
) by
-\Delta\mathbf{u}_{t}
and integrate over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. It results in

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbf{u}_{tt}\cdot(-\Delta\mathbf{u}_{t})-(2\zeta+\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}\cdot(-\Delta\mathbf{u}_{t})dx+\zeta\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\curl\curl\mathbf{u}\cdot(-\Delta\mathbf{u}_{t})dx

\displaystyle\quad=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\theta\cdot(-\Delta\mathbf{u}_{t})dx.

We utilize here the equality
-\Delta\mathbf{w}=-\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{w}+\curl\curl\mathbf{w}
and integrate by parts. It yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{t}|^{2}dx+(2\zeta+\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}+\zeta\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

\displaystyle=-\mu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

We add the obtained equality to (
7.5
) and get the thesis.
∎

We notice that the reasoning making use of the heat equation only, see for instance Lemma
5.1
or the second principle of thermodynamics (
1.3
), is still valid in the Lamé operator case. In the end, a slight modification of the proofs, utilizing (
7.2
) and (
7.4
) lead to the following theorem.

Theorem 7.3

(Existence, uniqueness and asymptotic behavior)
.

Let
d\in\{2,3\}
.

1.

Global solution
:
There is
D>0
depending only on
\mu,d
and
\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that if the initial data satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\mathbf{v}_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\theta_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\ln\theta_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\nabla\sqrt{\theta_{0}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

(7.6)

and

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+(2\zeta+\lambda)\|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\left\|\frac{\nabla\theta_{0}}{\sqrt{\theta_{0}}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq D,

(7.7)

then there exists a unique solution
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
of the following regularity

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\in L_{\textnormal{loc}}^{\infty}([0,\infty);H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap W_{\textnormal{loc}}^{1,\infty}([0,\infty);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap W_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2,\infty}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\in L_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty);H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap H_{\textnormal{loc}}^{1}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),

and

\displaystyle\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\curl\curl\mathbf{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\nabla\mathbf{u}_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\theta}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\ln\theta\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\nabla\ln\theta\in L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})).

of (
7.1
).

2.

Local solution
:
If the initial data satisfy (
7.6
) but not necessarily (
7.7
),
then there exists
T\in(0,\infty)
depending on
\mu,d,\zeta,\lambda
and
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and a unique solution
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
of (
7.1
) on
(0,T)
.

3.

Long-time dynamics:

Let us assume that
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
is a solution of (
7.1
)
starting from initial data
\mathbf{u}_{0}
,
\mathbf{v}_{0}
and
\theta_{0}>0
, satisfying (
7.6
) and
\theta_{0}^{-1}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Then, the following
convergences hold:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to 0

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in }H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\textrm{ as }t\to\infty,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to 0

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\textrm{ as }t\to\infty,

\displaystyle\theta(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to\theta_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in }L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\textrm{ as }t\to\infty,

where
\theta_{\infty}:=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{2\zeta+\lambda}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0})^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\theta_{0}dx\right)
and
\boldsymbol{\chi}
is a curl-free part of
\mathbf{u}
,
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0}
is a curl-free part of
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0}
is a curl-free part of
\mathbf{v}_{0}
.
