Oral Candida colonisation and cariogenic bacteria in children and adolescents with cleft lip and/or palate: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Muhammad Irpan Hendrawan, Elsa Ayu Amanda, Ita Purnama Alwi, Husni Mubarak, Irham Taufiqurrahman, Andi Tajrin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChildren with cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) face a disproportionate cariogenic burden, yet the role of oral Candida and its partnership with cariogenic bacteria remains poorly quantified. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesised observational evidence on oral Candida colonisation, cariogenic bacterial carriage, and clinical caries outcomes in non-syndromic CL/P, with GRADE-rated certainty.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 and the Cochrane Handbook (PROSPERO CRD420261402624), six databases were searched from inception to 27 May 2026. Eligible studies were observational designs enrolling children aged 0–18 years with non-syndromic CL/P versus non-cleft controls. Risk of bias used ROBINS-E. Random-effects meta-analyses (DerSimonian–Laird) pooled risk ratios (RR) for dichotomous outcomes and standardised mean differences (SMD) for continuous caries indices, with GRADE certainty.ResultsTen studies (2011–2026; 421 CL/P vs 567 controls) were included. Oral Candida carriage was nearly threefold higher in CL/P (RR 2.94, 95% CI 2.04–4.22; I2 = 23.4%; k = 4), with C. albicans predominant. High salivary Streptococcus mutans counts were similarly elevated (RR 2.74, 95% CI 1.42–5.27; I2 = 0%), whereas Lactobacillus carriage showed no significant difference (RR 1.33, 95% CI 0.71–2.47). Caries indices were directionally higher in three of four datasets but highly heterogeneous (SMD 0.74; I2 = 92.3%). GRADE certainty was Low to Very Low.ConclusionCL/P children carry elevated oral Candida and S. mutans burdens, supporting an early fungal–bacterial cariogenic shift. Candida assessment should be integrated into caries-risk evaluation from the deciduous-dentition stage within the multidisciplinary cleft pathway.Keywords: Cleft lip and palate, Oral Candida, Streptococcus mutans, Dental caries, Oral microbiota, Sustainable development goals

Introduction

Orofacial clefts (OFCs) are among the most common congenital craniofacial anomalies, and although global incidence and mortality fell between 1990 and 2021, prevalent cases continue to accumulate and are projected to rise through 2050.1 The burden is unevenly distributed, with over 80% of prevalent cases and 96% of OFC-related deaths in low- and middle-income countries.2 Indonesia illustrates this gap, where children wait until a mean age of 4.3 years for primary labioplasty.3

Cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P), the commonest OFC subtype, carries a cariogenic burden that is structural, salivary and microbial. Dental anomalies cluster in the cleft region, including tooth agenesis, microdontia, supernumerary teeth and enamel hypoplasia, forming plaque-retentive niches that invite demineralisation.4 Reduced salivary flow and peri-cleft biofilm dysregulation erode enamel even when hygiene is adequate, and caries experience is higher across both dentitions, widening in lower-resource settings.5 Raised Streptococcus mutans colonisation and fungal–bacterial cross-talk drive an acidogenic shift that sets CL/P apart from peers.6

Meta-analysis has steadily sized this burden: across 20 observational studies, affected children showed more caries (SMD 0.46, 95% CI 0.15–0.77), peaking in mixed dentition.7 What remains missing is a mechanistic account of the cariogenic ecology, above all the role of Candida and its partnership with S. mutans.

Candida has emerged as a keystone fungal partner, with paediatric oral colonisation of 10–70% that routinely exceeds non-cleft peers.8 The residual palatal defect and acrylic appliances such as obturator plates create a low-clearance, plaque-retentive niche favouring fungal persistence; colonisation is already near-universal in infants fitted with acrylic plates and far above controls.9 Mechanistically, S. mutans-derived glucosyltransferase B anchors the bacterium to C. albicans and, with quorum signalling, yields a polymicrobial biofilm more acid-tolerant and cariogenic than either species alone.10 Non-albicans species, especially C. tropicalis, can rival C. albicans in this partnership, echoing the mix seen in cleft infants.11

Against this background, we synthesised observational evidence on oral Candida colonisation, cariogenic bacterial burden and clinical caries outcomes in children and adolescents with non-syndromic CL/P, deriving pooled estimates with GRADE-rated certainty. We hypothesised that, beyond raised S. mutans carriage, Candida colonisation and DMFT/dmft burden would be higher in CL/P than in non-cleft peers, varying by dentition stage and care context.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported in line with the PRISMA 2020 statement and conducted following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 6.5).12,13 The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261402624) before screening, with no subsequent amendments.14

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was defined a priori using the PECO framework (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome), with study design specified separately and full criteria summarised in Table 1. In brief, we included observational primary studies of children and adolescents aged 0–18 years with non-syndromic cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P).

Information sources

Six electronic databases were searched from inception to 27 May 2026, with no language or date restrictions: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase (via Ovid), Scopus, ScienceDirect, Web of Science Core Collection, and the Cochrane Library. Reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews were also screened for eligible records.

Search strategy

The search combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH in PubMed and the Cochrane Library; Emtree in Embase) with free-text terms across three concept blocks: orofacial cleft phenotypes, cariogenic oral microbiota, and dental caries outcomes. Boolean operators, truncation, and database-specific field tags (e.g., TITLE-ABS-KEY in Scopus, /exp in Embase) were applied. Full search strings and per-database hits are given in Table 2.

Selection process

Records were imported into Mendeley Reference Manager for automatic de-duplication (by DOI and title), with residual duplicates removed manually in Excel. Two reviewers (M.I.H. and E.A.A.) independently screened titles and abstracts, and eligible records proceeded to independent full-text assessment. A third reviewer (I.T.) was pre-designated as adjudicator but not required, as disagreements were resolved by discussion. Inter-rater agreement was not formally quantified, a limitation acknowledged below. Exclusion reasons were recorded in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessment

The two reviewers (M.I.H. and E.A.A.) independently appraised each study with the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Exposure (ROBINS-E) tool, which suits observational exposure studies rather than intervention trials. Its seven domains (confounding, exposure measurement, participant selection, post-exposure interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result) were each rated low risk, some concerns, high risk, or very high risk, with overall judgements from the ROBINS-E signalling-question algorithm.25 Discrepancies were resolved by joint re-review until consensus, and visualisations were produced with the robvis application.26 No study was excluded for risk of bias; judgements instead informed interpretation and are reported in the Results and in Fig. 2A and B.

Effect measures

Effect measures were pre-specified so that ratios above 1 or differences above 0 indicated higher burden in CL/P. For dichotomous outcomes (Candida colonisation, high salivary S. mutans and Lactobacillus spp. carriage, and caries prevalence) we used the risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI), preferred over the odds ratio because the outcomes were period prevalences rather than incident events; “high” carriage followed each study's own threshold. For continuous indices (dmft, DMFT, deft, CSI) we used the mean difference (MD) when indices matched and the standardised mean difference (SMD; Hedges' g) otherwise. Medians with interquartile ranges were to be converted via the method of Wan et al.27 and outcomes with fewer than two studies were synthesised narratively.

Synthesis methods

Pairwise random-effects meta-analyses were performed on Meta-Analysis Online (https://metaanalysisonline.com; accessed 30 May 2026), using the DerSimonian–Laird between-study variance estimator.28,29 A random-effects model was chosen a priori given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across populations, phenotypes, techniques and count thresholds. Because this estimator can understate variance when few studies contribute, 95% prediction intervals were reported with every pooled estimate.30

Heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic, interpreted per Section 10.10.2 of the Cochrane Handbook (low 0–40%, moderate 30–60%, substantial 50–90%, considerable 75–100%), alongside the between-study variance τ2 and Cochran's Q. Standardised mean differences were interpreted using Cohen's conventions of approximately 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium) and 0.8 (large).31,32

Pooling proceeded only when an outcome was reported in at least three studies (k ≥ 3) with extractable group-level data (numerator/denominator or mean ± SD) and the contributing studies were judged sufficiently similar clinically and methodologically for interpretable pooling, per Section 10.10.4.1 of the Cochrane Handbook. Other outcomes were synthesised narratively, and forest plots were generated within the platform.

Two sensitivity analyses were pre-specified for each meta-analysed outcome: leave-one-out analysis to gauge the influence of individual studies, and restriction to studies at low or some-concerns overall risk of bias on ROBINS-E. Pre-specified subgroup analyses by cleft phenotype (CLO vs CPO vs CLP), age group (≤5, 6–12 and 13–18 years) and microbiological method (culture-based vs molecular) were contingent on sufficient stratified data.

Reporting bias assessment

Across-study reporting bias (publication bias and small-study effects) was pre-specified for evaluation by funnel-plot inspection and by formal asymmetry tests (Egger's regression and Begg's rank correlation), applied only when at least 10 studies contributed to a synthesis, following the recommended power threshold and Section 13.3.5.5 of the Cochrane Handbook.13,33 Funnel plots were inspected regardless of study count, with cautious interpretation where k < 10. Within-study selective reporting was addressed through Domain 7 of the ROBINS-E assessment.

Certainty assessment (GRADE)

The two reviewers independently rated the certainty of evidence for each meta-analysed outcome using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, with disagreements resolved by consensus and guidance taken from the GRADE Handbook. Because all included studies were observational, the initial certainty was set at “low” per GRADE guidance for non-randomised evidence.34

Each outcome was assessed across five downgrading domains (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias) and three upgrading domains: large magnitude of effect (RR ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 with no plausible confounding fully explaining it), a dose–response gradient, and residual confounding that would bias the effect towards the null.

Final ratings were classified as High (⊕⊕⊕⊕), Moderate (⊕⊕⊕◯), Low (⊕⊕◯◯) or Very Low (⊕◯◯◯), and the full evidence profile is presented in the Summary of Findings (Table 6).

Results

Study selection

Database searches retrieved 165 records across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Web of Science and Cochrane Library. After removing 109 duplicates, we screened 56 titles and abstracts against the a priori PECO criteria and excluded 42 (reasons detailed in Fig. 1). Of the 14 full texts assessed, 5 were excluded with reasons (ineligible design, n = 3; no eligible caries outcome, n = 1; other eligibility mismatch, n = 1), and citation searching added one further report. Ten studies thus entered the qualitative synthesis (4 in MA-1, Candida colonisation; 3 in MA-2, Streptococcus mutans; 3 in MA-3, Lactobacillus spp.), following PRISMA 2020 as shown in Fig. 1.12

Characteristics of included studies

The ten studies were published between 2011 and 2026. Across the nine with explicit two-group enrolment, 421 children and adolescents with CL/P were compared against 567 non-cleft controls, while Zhang et al.23 used a multi-group plaque-microbiome design with intra-study healthy comparators (Table 3). Origins were India (k = 3), China (k = 2), the USA, Jordan, Japan, Sweden and Turkey. Eight were cross-sectional or case–control studies; Durhan et al.18 was a 24-month longitudinal cohort with three timepoints, and Shashni et al.17 a three-group cross-sectional design. Ages ranged from 0 years (Sravani et al.22) to 30 years (Zhang et al.23 young-adult subgroup), with most cohorts sampling primary or mixed dentition.

Cleft phenotype was heterogeneous. Most studies pooled UCLP, BCLP and isolated CP, while three reported granular composition: Khan et al.21 was alveolus-dominant (50%), Hassani et al.15 stratified by PNAM exposure, and Shashni et al.17 included CLP only. Surgical status at sampling was variably described in four studies and unspecified in six; in Shashni et al.,17 4–9-year-old children spanning pre- and post-palatoplasty stages were not stratified, a gap revisited in the Discussion. Full study-level detail appears in Table 3, Table 4.

Risk of bias assessment

No study reached Low risk overall. Five were rated Some concerns (Rawashdeh et al.16; Sundell et al.20; Khan et al.21; Sravani et al.22; Shi et al.24) and five High risk. Confounding (D1) dominated, rated High in 8 of 10 studies because matching rarely extended beyond age in these small case–control and cross-sectional designs; only Rawashdeh et al.16 and Sundell et al.20 reached Some concerns, through age-stratified and registry-based sampling. In three further studies the overall rating was reasoned down to Some concerns because residual confounding was unlikely to reverse the effect, a deviation permitted under ROBINS-E. Post-exposure interventions (D4) were problematic where surgical status was unreported or unstratified, and Funahashi et al.19 carried the heaviest profile (High on D1, D3, D6 and D7). Exposure measurement (D2) was uniformly Low, reflecting standardised culture and 16S rRNA V3–V4 methods. Per-study and per-domain judgements appear in Fig. 2A and B.

Synthesis of results

Oral Candida colonisation (MA-1)

Four studies (Rawashdeh et al.16; Durhan et al.18; Khan et al.21; Sravani et al.22) contributing 334 participants (CL/P n = 171; controls n = 163) entered MA-1. Children with CL/P were 2.94 times more likely to harbour oral Candida than controls (RR 2.94, 95% CI 2.04 to 4.22; P < 0.001), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 23.4%) and a prediction interval of 1.29 to 6.68. Crude pooled prevalence was 66.1% (113/171) versus 22.1% (36/163). Durhan et al.18 contributed data from its post-primary-molar timepoint (11/21 versus 3/13; Fisher exact P = 0.075). The remaining studies (Sundell et al.20; Funahashi et al.19; Hassani et al.15; Zhang et al.23; Shi et al.24) did not provide poolable Candida data. Forest and funnel plots are shown in Fig. 3.

Species-level data showed a parallel compositional shift. C. albicans predominated in CL/P (Sravani et al.22: 70% versus 15% in controls; Khan et al.21: 58% versus 28%, P = 0.004), and non-albicans species (C. tropicalis, C. krusei, C. glabrata, C. parapsilosis) were detected at appreciable frequency (Sravani et al.22: 17.5% versus 5%; Khan et al.21: multiple species identified).

Streptococcus mutans (MA-2)

Three studies (Shashni et al.17; Sundell et al.20; Durhan et al.18) pooling 512 participants (CL/P n = 177; controls n = 335) showed a 2.74-fold higher risk of high salivary S. mutans counts in CL/P (RR 2.74, 95% CI 1.42 to 5.27; P = 0.003; 27/177 versus 12/335), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). The prediction interval (0.65 to 11.51) crossed the null, so the true effect in future settings could plausibly range from modest protection to markedly amplified risk.

Shashni et al.17 used a three-arm design in which CL/P (Group I, n = 23) was compared against high-caries-risk children (Group II, n = 25, selected a priori for deft + DMFT > 5) and caries-free children (Group III, n = 25). S. mutans counts >10⁶ CFU/mL (Score 3) occurred in 21.7%, 60.0% and 4.0% respectively, differing significantly between Groups I and III (P < 0.05) and Groups I and II (P < 0.001). To preserve PECO comparability we pooled Group I versus Group III only at the >10⁵ CFU/mL threshold (Score ≥2; 13/23 versus 4/25), excluding the selection-biased Group II. Because the three studies applied different high-count thresholds (≥105 to ≥ 106 CFU/mL; Table 5), this threshold heterogeneity and the intermediate position of CL/P children (4% → 21.7% → 60%) are taken up in the Discussion. Forest and funnel plots are shown in Fig. 4.

Lactobacillus spp. (MA-3)

The same three studies and 512 participants yielded a non-significant pooled estimate (RR 1.33, 95% CI 0.71 to 2.47; P = 0.37; 19/177 versus 16/335), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) and a wide prediction interval (0.34 to 5.19). Here too the studies entered events at thresholds spanning two orders of magnitude (≥104 to ≥ 106 CFU/mL; Table 5).

Two study-level patterns contrasted with this pooled null. At the strict ≥ 106 CFU/mL cut-off feeding MA-3, Sundell et al.20 found numerically lower carriage in CL/P (3/133 versus 9/297; RR 0.74), yet reported a highly significant difference across its broader distribution (P < 0.001), driven by excess medium-count Lactobacillus in CL/P (17% versus 6%); strict thresholds can therefore mask compositional differences. Durhan et al.18 likewise detected higher carriage at birth (P = 0.029) and at first tooth emergence (P = 0.030). This discordance is addressed in the sensitivity analyses and Discussion. DMFT/dmft burden (MA-4, narrative)

A pooled SMD across four datasets (Sravani et al.22 def; Rawashdeh et al.16 DMFT and dmft; Funahashi et al.19 DMFT) was 0.74 (95% CI –0.55 to 2.03; P = 0.26) with I2 = 92.3%, and is reported narratively given the very high heterogeneity, which arose from differences in age range, index (def, dmft, DMFT) and sample size (n = 6/4 to 60/60). Direction favoured higher caries burden in CL/P in three of four datasets; Funahashi et al.19 reversed, reflecting n = 6/4 with a single outlier control value (DMFT = 5).

Sravani et al.22 (trans-foramen CL/P, 0–3 years, n = 40/40) reported def 5.62 ± 0.35 versus 4.83 ± 0.45 (MD +0.79; P < 0.001) and a Caries Severity Index (CSI; Chosack35) of 2.79 ± 0.33 versus 1.92 ± 0.28 (MD +0.87; P < 0.001). Its unusually narrow standard deviations, in a cohort half-composed of 0–6-month-old infants whose deciduous teeth are barely erupted, raise concerns over biological plausibility and yield a disproportionately large effect (SMD ≈ 1.96) that dominates MA-4; a leave-one-out analysis excluding it is reported below.

Rawashdeh et al.16 reported DMFT 3.30 ± 2.30 versus 2.50 ± 2.10 (P < 0.001) and dmft 2.93 ± 2.10 versus 1.97 ± 1.40 (P = 0.015; n = 60/60), and Funahashi et al.19 DMFT 0.67 ± 0.82 versus 1.25 ± 2.50 (n = 6/4). Shashni et al.17 reported deft 8.65 ± 5.80 versus 12.64 ± 3.43 (P < 0.01) and deft + DMFT 10.14 ± 6.09 versus 12.73 ± 3.42 (P = 0.149) against Group II; these selection-biased benchmarks were excluded a priori from MA-4.

Caries prevalence (MA-5, single-study)

Durhan et al.18 was the only study reporting caries prevalence as a binary outcome. At first primary-incisor eruption (≈8–15 months), 4/21 CL/P infants (19.0%) versus 0/13 controls (0%) showed initial caries by ICDAS-equivalent criteria (Fisher exact P = 0.144); at the post-primary-molar timepoint (≈24–30 months), 10/21 (47.6%) versus 5/13 (38.5%) were affected (P = 0.728). The first-incisor timepoint is taken as the primary estimate given its clearer separation and pre-specified timing, with the later timepoint retained for transparency and revisited in the sensitivity analyses.

Sensitivity analyses

For MA-1, sequential leave-one-out removal yielded RR estimates between 2.41 (95% CI 1.62 to 3.59) and 3.43 (95% CI 2.31 to 5.10), all retaining significance. For MA-2, excluding Shashni et al.,17 the heaviest contributor (45.8% weight, highest RR 3.53), shifted the pooled RR from 2.74 to 2.18 (95% CI 1.05 to 4.52) while preserving significance, so no single study drove the result. For MA-3, the estimate stayed non-significant throughout; this persistent null contrasts with significant study-level differences in Sundell et al.20 (P < 0.001) and Durhan et al.18 (at birth P = 0.029, at first tooth emergence P = 0.030) measured at other thresholds, indicating threshold-driven divergence rather than a single outlier.

For MA-4, a pre-specified leave-one-out excluding Sravani et al.,22 prompted by the plausibility concerns above, attenuated the pooled SMD to +0.42 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.67) and collapsed the heterogeneity (I2 = 0%; P ≈ 0.001). Sravani thus drove both the extreme magnitude and the very high heterogeneity, although the underlying direction, higher caries burden in CL/P, held at a much smaller effect size across the remaining datasets.

For the single-study MA-5, adopting the post-primary-molar timepoint (47.6% versus 38.5%; P = 0.728) in place of the first-incisor timepoint would attenuate the between-group separation and yield a non-significant estimate; the first-incisor timepoint was retained as primary for the reasons set out above.

Egger's regression for small-study effects was not undertaken because each meta-analysis comprised fewer than ten studies (Cochrane Handbook Section 13.3.5.5; https://training.cochrane.org/handbook). Pre-specified subgroup analyses by cleft phenotype, surgical status and geographical region were also not performed, as no stratum reached the a priori minimum of four studies.36

Certainty of evidence (GRADE)

Applying GRADE, MA-1 was rated Low (serious risk of bias, predominantly D1, plus moderate imprecision).34 MA-2 was rated Very Low (serious risk of bias; serious indirectness from heterogeneous high-count thresholds, ≥ 105 versus ≥ 106 CFU/mL, and the non-representative control selection in Shashni et al.17; moderate imprecision). MA-3 was Very Low (serious risk of bias; serious imprecision with the CI crossing the null), as were MA-4 (serious inconsistency, I2 = 92.3%) and the single-study MA-5 (very serious imprecision). Discussion

Principal findings

This synthesis quantified three cariogenic microorganisms and four caries-experience indices in CL/P. Oral Candida carriage was nearly threefold higher than in controls (RR 2.94, 95% CI 2.04 to 4.22; I2 = 23.4%), with a prediction interval above unity (1.29 to 6.68) that points to a reproducible over-representation. High salivary S. mutans counts were of similar size (RR 2.74, 95% CI 1.42 to 5.27), though a prediction interval crossing unity (0.65 to 11.51) helped pull GRADE to Very Low. Lactobacillus showed no pooled difference at the strict MA-3 thresholds, a divergence we read as threshold-driven rather than biological. Caries indices favoured CL/P in three of four datasets, but very high heterogeneity (I2 = 92.3%) precluded a firm pooled estimate. Overall, the evidence supports an early-life fungal–bacterial shift whose caries consequences remain uncertain in magnitude.

Interpretation of oral Candida burden in CL/P

Our pooled estimate, a 2.94-fold higher Candida risk in CL/P (95% CI 2.04–4.22; I2 = 23.4%; k = 4; n = 334; prediction interval 1.29–6.68), holds its direction across plausible future settings. Its closest comparator, Souza et al.9, reported colonisation in 89.5% of infants aged 0–18 months with cleft palate, 100% with acrylic occlusal plates and 44% in controls (p = 0.037), with C. albicans dominant and multi-species co-colonisation in 30.5% of positive cultures. That study shows Candida is already elevated in the first months of life, before any appliance is fitted.

A second anchor, Rawashdeh et al.,16 found carriage of 63.3% versus 18.3% in a Jordanian cohort aged 5–17 years. That 45-percentage-point gap sits nearer the centre of our pooled risk ratio than the gap in Souza et al.,9 implying that age, phenotype and appliances shift absolute prevalence while the relative risk holds steady.

Species data converge on C. albicans as dominant across ages: Pinto-Almazán et al.37 found oral Candida in 6.99% of 743 Mexican children aged 3–6 years, with C. albicans 87.8% of isolates. This may carry clinical relevance, given the broad virulence potential generally attributed to C. albicans in the oral cavity.

The acrylic-plate signal in Souza et al.9 foreshadows the biofilm question addressed below. Linking carriage to cariogenic risk, Man et al.38 quantify the Candida–biofilm coupling in early childhood caries (plaque OR 7.98, 95% CI 3.84–16.58, I2 = 84%; saliva OR 9.42, 95% CI 4.15–21.40, I2 = 50%).

Linking oral Candida to cariogenic burden in CL/P

Does this fungal over-representation translate into more caries? The most direct answer comes from Escobar-Arregocés et al.,5 who pooled five of twenty-three eligible studies in non-syndromic CL/P and found an odds ratio of 2.03 for caries experience versus peers (p < 0.005). Although 90% of those studies were at high risk of bias, the direction and size match our MA-2 S. mutans estimate (RR 2.74, 95% CI 1.42–5.27), placing the Candida signal within a coherent cariogenic story rather than isolated colonisation.

Mechanistic support comes from recent in vitro work. Sansanoa et al.11 showed that dual-species biofilms of S. mutans with C. albicans, C. glabrata or C. tropicalis built more mass than the sum of their mono-species controls (p < 0.05), fell from pH 7 to about 4.5 within 2 h, and stayed below the critical demineralisation threshold of 5.5 for the full 36 h; gtfB and gtfD were upregulated across most pairings, with C. tropicalis the most cariogenic. Jin et al.39 cast this as a carbohydrate-metabolism partnership in which GtfB binds the Candida mannan layer and supplies glucan substrate, while Francis et al.10 show that it also recruits Als1 and Hwp1 adhesins to anchor the bacterium to fungal cells, yielding a polymicrobial biofilm with enhanced acid output.

The convergence of the epidemiology Escobar-Arregocés et al.5 with the biofilm evidence (Sansanoa et al.11) supports a Candida-mediated cariogenic pathway in CL/P, though CL/P populations remain scarce in the in vitro literature and causality cannot be drawn from our data. The downstream steps, co-aggregation and cleft-specific ecological plaque shifts, are taken up below.

Cariogenic shift mechanism in CL/P

This Candida–bacterial co-occurrence fits the ecological plaque hypothesis, in which sustained acidic, nutrient-stressed conditions select for acidogenic, aciduric species and erode diversity.40 CL/P-specific data bear this out: across four caries states, Ace and Chao indices differed (p = 0.013), the Microbial Dysbiosis Index exceeded controls (p = 0.004), Streptococcus, Gemella, Actinomyces, Scardovia and Olsenella were enriched in high-caries CL/P, the facultatively anaerobic phenotype rose (p = 0.038), and Selenomonas acted synergistically with S. mutans to worsen severity.24 Alveolar-cleft plaque followed suit, enriched for Neisseria, Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter and Porphyromonas with raised potentially pathogenic, Gram-negative phenotypes.23

Molecularly, S. mutans virulence here runs on glucan synthesis via gtfB/C/D and the vicRK and liaSR regulators that coordinate adhesion, EPS-matrix assembly and acid-stress survival.41 Glucan is 10–30% of biofilm dry weight, and a two-step expansion (osmotic spread of glucan-rich EPS, then colony formation on hydroxyapatite) drives lateral spread, with biofilm centres acidifying to pH 5.7 and new colonies failing without hydroxyapatite.42 Lactobacillus binds sound enamel only weakly, but adheres readily once S. mutans has built retention sites, after which L. plantarum, L. salivarius, L. rhamnosus and L. casei can drop pH to 5.5 within 2.5–3 h and sustain demineralisation.43

In CL/P the post-surgical anatomy amplifies all of this: even after fistula closure, residual irregularities and altered salivary clearance keep the community tilted towards potentially pathogenic taxa, with retention sites favouring the lineages most tied to caries.24

Clinical implications

The pairing of cariogenic bacterial enrichment with high Candida carriage, shown here and backed by mechanistic and microbiome data, argues that prevention in CL/P should target both the bacterial and fungal arms of the microbiota and begin before structural caries appear.9,24

Topical 5% sodium fluoride varnish remains the best-supported preventive measure. A Community Guide review of 31 studies (60,780 students) found school varnish programmes cut caries initiation by a median 32% (IQI 21–37%) in permanent and 25% (IQI 4–37%) in primary teeth, including in high-risk and low-socioeconomic groups.44 Where moisture control or cooperation is poor, both common in CL/P, a single glass-ionomer sealant on primary second molars matched quarterly varnish for caries-into-dentin at 12 months (7.8% vs 8.0%, p = 0.913), though sealant retention fell to 13.5%.45 Adjunctive probiotics (Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) lower salivary S. mutans (MD −0.40 CFU/mL, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.24; moderate certainty, 19 RCTs), needing at least six months of continuous intake.46

Recall intervals should therefore be shorter than the population norm, periodic screening should include assessment for Candida carriage in symptomatic children, and caregiver education ought to be embedded within the multidisciplinary CL/P care pathway from the deciduous-dentition stage onwards.

Limitations and heterogeneity

Several limitations temper these conclusions. First, clinical heterogeneity was substantial: participants spanned a wide age range, several non-syndromic cleft phenotypes (cleft lip only, cleft palate only, and combined cleft lip and palate) and differing surgical and orthodontic stages, each independently associated with oral microbial composition and caries risk. Pre-specified subgroup analyses could not be performed because no stratum reached the minimum of four studies, so effect modification could not be explored.

Second, methodological heterogeneity in microbial detection was pronounced. Studies used selective culture on MSA or CHROMagar, qPCR for species-specific loci, and 16S rRNA or ITS amplicon sequencing, modalities that quantify different fractions of the oral community and are not directly interchangeable, so residual measurement bias cannot be excluded despite our restriction of pooled estimates to comparable outcomes. Caries scoring also varied, despite harmonisation to Chosack35 dmfs/DMFS criteria where re-extraction was possible.

Third, the evidence base was dominated by cross-sectional and case–control work, with only one prospective design, which precludes causal inference between fungal–bacterial co-carriage and incident caries and limits insight into temporal dynamics across the surgical–orthodontic timeline.

Fourth, most studies raised concerns over confounder adjustment, particularly oral hygiene, dietary sugar and socioeconomic status, variables that independently shape both microbial carriage and dmfs/DMFS. Because every synthesis drew on fewer than ten studies, formal funnel-plot asymmetry tests such as Egger's regression were not undertaken (see the sensitivity analyses); small-study effects and publication bias therefore cannot be excluded, although leave-one-out analyses left each pooled estimate materially unchanged.36

Finally, although no language or date restrictions were applied, the exclusion of grey literature may still have introduced publication bias, and the concentration of studies in Asian and European centres limits generalisability to African and Latin American CL/P populations, where access to early surgical repair and preventive care differs substantially.

Implications for future research

These limitations map onto clear research priorities. Prospective cohorts that follow CL/P children from primary through mixed dentition, with standardised microbial sampling that combines culture-based quantification and shotgun metagenomics, would clarify whether Candida–streptococcal co-carriage precedes or follows caries onset. Pragmatic randomised trials of preventive interventions (fluoride varnish, glass-ionomer sealants and targeted probiotic adjuncts) should be designed specifically for CL/P anatomy, with caries increment and microbial endpoints reported jointly. Individual-participant-data syntheses would enable adjustment for the oral hygiene, dietary and socioeconomic confounders that aggregate-data meta-analyses cannot resolve, and inclusion of cohorts from low- and middle-income settings is essential to test the external validity of the burden documented here.

Conclusion

Within the limits of observational evidence, children and adolescents with cleft lip and/or palate carried a higher oral Candida burden (RR 2.94, 95% CI 2.04–4.22) and more frequent high salivary Streptococcus mutans counts (RR 2.74, 95% CI 1.42–5.27) than non-cleft peers, whereas Lactobacillus differences were threshold-dependent and non-significant. Caries indices were directionally higher in three of four datasets, but very high heterogeneity precluded a stable pooled estimate, and GRADE certainty was Low to Very Low. These findings support integrating Candida assessment into routine caries-risk evaluation from the deciduous-dentition stage, alongside professionally applied fluoride and structured preventive care within the multidisciplinary cleft pathway.
