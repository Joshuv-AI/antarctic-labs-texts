Chronic Recurrent Multifocal Osteomyelitis in a Pediatric Patient: An Uncommon Case With Diagnostic Challenge
Authors: Dosti Regmi, Madhur Bhattarai, Sujata Bhandari, Saurav Jha, Akriti Baral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic Recurrent Multifocal Osteomyelitis (CRMO) is a rare autoinflammatory bone disorder that primarily affects children and adolescents. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of bone pain with sterile inflammatory lesions, often resembling infection or malignancy. We present a case of a 12‐year‐old girl who initially presented with atraumatic right knee pain and was ultimately diagnosed with CRMO after imaging and histopathologic evaluation. Her clinical course was complicated by recurrent episodes, and physeal damage resulted in limb length discrepancy. Early recognition through appropriate imaging and multidisciplinary evaluation helps avoid unnecessary antibiotics and invasive procedures. Long‐term follow‐up is essential, as persistent inflammation can cause irreversible skeletal complications.Keywords: chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis, CRMO, non‐bacterial osteomyelitis, pediatric bone lesions

Key Clinical Message

CRMO is a rare, relapsing autoinflammatory bone disease and may lead to long‐term complications like limb length discrepancy despite being considered self‐limiting. CRMO should be considered in children presenting with persistent or recurrent sterile bone pain and characteristic metaphyseal lesions after exclusion of infection and malignancy.

Introduction

Chronic Recurrent Multifocal Osteomyelitis (CRMO) is an idiopathic, non‐infectious inflammatory bone disorder primarily affecting children and adolescents [1]. The condition was first described by Giedion and colleagues in 1972 as a “subacute” and “symmetrical” aseptic osteomyelitis characterized by multiple, symmetrically distributed bone lesions [2]. Often underrecognized due to its nonspecific symptoms and broad differential diagnoses, CRMO is characterized by recurrent episodes of sterile bone inflammation, typically involving metaphyses of long bones, vertebrae, clavicle, mandible, and pelvis [3].

The exact pathophysiology of CRMO is not fully understood but is believed to involve the dysregulation of the innate immune system due to genetic and environmental influences. Unlike bacterial osteomyelitis, CRMO is characterized as a sterile inflammatory condition and does not respond to antibiotic treatments. Because of its nonspecific clinical presentation, extensive differential diagnosis, and the absence of definitive laboratory tests, the disease is often underrecognized or diagnosed later than it should be [4].

Diagnosis is based on clinical and radiological findings. Histopathological examination is used when needed to rule out infections or tumors. MRI is especially important because it shows bone marrow edema in several areas, while plain X‐rays often look normal early in the disease.

Management is primarily focused on alleviating symptoms, with nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) as the first‐line therapy. Corticosteroids are important for reducing bone inflammation and edema. Parental counseling is necessary due to the relapsing–remitting course of the disease [5]. Early identification is essential to prevent unnecessary biopsies, prolonged antibiotic use, and long‐term complications such as growth disturbances and chronic pain [3, 6].

We present a pediatric case of CRMO that showed recurrent metaphyseal lesions in the distal femur and proximal tibia. This led to premature physeal closure and limb‐length discrepancy. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges, characteristic imaging findings, long‐term skeletal complications, and the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary management of this uncommon autoinflammatory disorder.

Case History/Examination

A 12‐year‐old previously healthy female presented with a two‐month history of persistent right knee pain associated with mild swelling. The pain was insidious in onset without preceding trauma, gradually progressive, and was localized to the distal aspect of the right thigh and knee. There was no history of fever, weight loss, night sweats, recent infections, or constitutional symptoms. There was no history of similar episodes, chronic medical illness, or family history of autoimmune, rheumatologic, or malignant disorders.

On physical examination, she was well‐appearing and afebrile with stable vital signs. Inspection of the right knee revealed mild swelling without overlying erythema or skin changes. Palpation elicited localized tenderness over the distal femur and proximal tibia. There was no joint effusion or increased local warmth or fluctuance. Knee range of motion was mildly restricted due to pain. Neurovascular examination of the affected limb was normal. Examination of the remaining joints and musculoskeletal system was unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Initial laboratory tests revealed mildly elevated inflammatory markers, including ESR and CRP, without significant leukocytosis or other hematological abnormalities. The remainder of the laboratory evaluation was not suggestive of acute bacterial infection. X‐ray of the right knee demonstrated a lucent juxta‐physeal lesion within the distal femoral metaphysis with surrounding sclerotic changes and pseudo‐widening of the distal femoral physis. Subtle sclerotic changes were also noted in the proximal tibial metaphysis (Figure 1). Given these findings, the differential diagnosis included chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis (CRMO), multifocal lymphoma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, bacterial osteomyelitis, and metastatic neuroblastoma.

Plain radiograph of the right knee (anteroposterior view) showing a lucent juxta‐physeal osteolytic lesion in the distal femoral metaphysis with surrounding sclerotic changes and pseudo‐widening of the distal femoral physis. Subtle sclerotic changes are also present in the proximal tibial metaphysis.

To further evaluate, a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the right knee was performed, revealing a peripherally enhancing lesion in the distal femoral metaphysis and a nodular enhancing lesion in the proximal tibial metaphysis. Adjacent bone marrow edema with transphyseal extension into the epiphysis was also observed (Figure 2). A bone biopsy from the femoral lesion showed subacute to chronic inflammatory changes without evidence of neoplastic cells and no microbial growth on culture. Correlation of the clinical presentation, radiological findings, histopathology, and sterile culture results established the diagnosis of chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the right knee (A). Fat‐saturated T2 coronal MRI of the knee shows a hyperintense focus in the distal femoral metaphysis with adjacent marrow edema and soft tissue edema. A similar hyperintense lesion is noted in the proximal tibial metaphysis. (B) The femoral lesion shows peripheral enhancement, and the tibial lesion shows nodular enhancement. Enhancement of the marrow is noted with extension to the epiphyses.

The patient was managed conservatively with anti‐inflammatory therapy including corticosteroids, resulting in symptomatic improvement. During follow‐up, she had several episodes of recurrent right knee pain that responded to medical treatment.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Two years after the remission of the last recurrence, she presented with new‐onset pain involving the left knee and lower back. Follow‐up radiograph of right knee demonstrated persistent widening and hyperostosis of the distal femoral meta‐diaphysis, with no significant progression compared with imaging obtained two years earlier (Figure 3). On physical examination, she exhibited a right inferior pelvic tilt during ambulation. A bone length study confirmed a 1.2 cm shortening of the right femur compared to the left, attributed to physeal injury and premature closure of the distal femoral physis secondary to chronic inflammatory injury. Radiographs of the left knee and spine were unremarkable, suggesting that the newly developed symptoms were most likely related to altered biomechanics and compensatory weight‐bearing resulting from the limb‐length discrepancy.

Follow‐up radiograph obtained two years after the last recurrence. Antero‐posterior view (A) and lateral view (B) of right knee showing persistent widening with hyperostosis in the distal femoral metadiaphysis with premature closure of the distal femoral physis.

Discussion

CRMO is an uncommon idiopathic, autoinflammatory skeletal disease usually occurring in children and adolescents. It is the most common and representative autoinflammatory bone disease and is increasingly referred to as chronic nonbacterial osteomyelitis (CNO), a more inclusive term that encompasses both unifocal and multifocal forms, as not all patients exhibit recurrent episodes or multiple bone lesions [7, 8]. CRMO is characterized by nonbacterial inflammatory bone lesions with a relapsing–remitting course and variable clinical manifestations, ranging from isolated bone pain to multifocal skeletal involvement [3, 9]. However, there is evidence suggesting both an autoinflammatory cause and genetic susceptibility. The disease's response to anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs and corticosteroids) and association with psoriasis, palmo‐plantar pustulosis, sacroileitis, inflammatory bowel disease, acne, and Sweet syndrome supports the hypothesis of autoimmune etiology [10, 11, 12]. It has long been considered to be the pediatric form of the SAPHO syndrome (synovitis, acne, pustulosis, hyperostosis, and osteomyelitis) [13]. The genetic basis of CRMO is supported by mutations in the LPIN2 gene, as observed in Majeed syndrome—a rare autosomal‐recessive condition characterized by CRMO, congenital dyserythropoietic anemia, and inflammatory dermatosis—further supporting a genetic contribution to disease susceptibility [6, 14]. Despite this, currently the diagnosis is based on clinical, radiologic, pathological, and progression patterns [15].

The clinical presentation of CRMO is often nonspecific, making early diagnosis challenging. Patients typically present with gradually progressive localized bone pain, tenderness, swelling, or limited range of motion at one or more sites. The clinical course of CRMO is highly variable; it can have multiple remissions and recurrences with a protracted course. Our patient first showed pain and swelling around the right knee. She did not have a fever or any other systemic symptoms. This closely resembled bacterial osteomyelitis and malignant bone lesions. During follow‐up, she had several recurrences in the same limb. Later, she developed pain in the opposite knee and spine. This demonstrates the chronic relapsing nature of the disease.

Similar to hematogenous bacterial osteomyelitis, CRMO typically affects the metaphysis of long bones. CRMO also involves sites that are atypical for bacterial osteomyelitis, including the clavicle and mandible—locations whose involvement should raise suspicion for CRMO rather than infectious etiology. Other frequent sites include the vertebral bodies, pelvis, ribs, and mandible [3, 6]. Bilateral symmetrical involvement is common.

Diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical presentation, characteristic imaging features, exclusion of infection and malignancy, and, when necessary, histopathological examination. Radiological evaluation plays a central role in the diagnosis and follow‐up of CRMO. Radiographic findings may be unremarkable during the early stages of the disease, which does not exclude the diagnosis. Early manifestations in plain radiographs may include lytic, poorly defined, or well‐defined lesions, followed by progressive sclerosis or cortical thickening of the bone during the reparative phase [4]. Similar findings were observed in our patient, whose initial radiographs demonstrated a juxtaphyseal lucent lesion in the distal femoral metaphysis with surrounding sclerosis and subtle proximal tibial involvement. MRI is much more sensitive than regular X‐rays in detecting early inflammatory lesions and assessing how far the disease has spread [4]. During the active phase of disease, MRI reveals marrow edema, appearing hypointense on T1‐weighted images and hyperintense on T2‐weighted images. It can also show periosteal reaction, surrounding soft tissue edema, and transphyseal disease [6]. In our patient, MRI demonstrated metaphyseal lesions in the distal femur and proximal tibia with associated marrow edema and transphyseal extension into the epiphysis, findings that were highly suggestive of inflammatory osteomyelitis. MRI is invaluable for guiding biopsy site selection and monitoring disease activity over time [6]. Whole‐body MRI or Tc‐99 m bone scintigraphy may identify clinically silent lesions and better define disease extent, thereby reducing the need for unnecessary invasive procedures [16].

The principal differential diagnoses are osteomyelitis, primary bone tumor, lymphoma, hypophosphatasia, and Langerhans cell histiocytosis. Clinical and radiological features such as prolonged clinical course with the patient being healthy between the episodes, atypical locations like clavicle and mandible, bilateral and multifocal involvement, lack of abscess, fistula or sequestrum, lack of response to antibiotics, and comorbid inflammatory cutaneous or bowel disease can help differentiate CRMO from infectious and neoplastic diseases [6].

Uncertainty in diagnosis is frequently encountered among individuals who exhibit solitary lesions, unusual imaging characteristics, or increased inflammatory markers. In this case, the presence of a single lesion and non‐specific radiological findings required differential diagnosis, including bacterial osteomyelitis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, lymphoma, and metastatic disease. Consequently, a bone biopsy was performed to rule out neoplastic and infectious processes. Histopathological examination demonstrated chronic inflammation without malignancy, and microbiological cultures yielded negative results, thereby favoring a diagnosis of chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis (CRMO).

There are currently no universally accepted treatment guidelines for CRMO. Management mainly focuses on controlling inflammation, relieving pain, preventing bone damage, and preserving function. Nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs are usually the first treatment choice. Corticosteroids, disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs, bisphosphonates, and biologic agents like tumor necrosis factor inhibitors are used for severe or refractory cases [4]. Our patient experienced symptomatic improvement following anti‐inflammatory treatment, including corticosteroids. However, she later had recurring episodes that needed ongoing clinical follow‐up.

The long‐term prognosis of CRMO is variable. Although many patients achieve disease remission, persistent or recurrent inflammation may result in irreversible skeletal complications, including vertebral compression fractures, kyphotic deformity, hyperostosis, physeal injury, growth disturbance, and limb‐length discrepancy [6]. Our patient experienced early closure of the distal femoral physis, resulting in a 1.2‐cm shortening of the affected limb and a compensatory pelvic tilt, representing an uncommon but clinically significant complication of chronic inflammatory injury. This case underscores the importance of regular clinical and radiological follow‐up in skeletally immature patients to detect growth disturbances early and facilitate timely orthopedic intervention when required.

This report has some limitations. The lack of whole‐body MRI limited the assessment of clinically silent multifocal lesions. This may have led to an underestimation of the true extent of skeletal involvement. Genetic testing for monogenic autoinflammatory disorders was not done, which limited further evaluation of a possible genetic predisposition.

Conclusion

CRMO should be considered in children and adolescents presenting with persistent or recurrent sterile bone pain, particularly when imaging demonstrates metaphyseal lesions and inflammatory markers are elevated without signs of infection. The diagnosis of CRMO is established through a combination of clinical presentation and radiologic evidence. It is crucial to consider this diagnosis in pediatric patients exhibiting multifocal bone lesions. This can help avoid several invasive biopsies and unnecessary antibiotic treatments. Long‐term follow‐up is essential because recurrent inflammation may result in permanent skeletal sequelae, including premature physeal closure, limb‐length discrepancy, and deformity, as seen in our patient.
