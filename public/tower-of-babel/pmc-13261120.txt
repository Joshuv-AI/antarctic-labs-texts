RETRACTED ARTICLE: An enzymatic BioBattery based on multicopper oxidases with reduced substrate diffusion constraints for sustainable energy harvesting
Authors: Vivek Saxena, Narendra Kumar, Uma Nangia, Saibal Manna, Ankush Gupta, Manoj Kumar Kar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of efficient enzymatic energy conversion systems is often limited by restricted substrate transport to the electrode surface and instability of immobilized biocatalysts. In this work, we report the design and experimental validation of an enzymatic fuel cell, referred to as a BioBattery, aimed at reducing substrate diffusion constraints through the co-immobilization of catalytic components within a structured electrode matrix. The system employs multicopper oxidases as anodic biocatalysts, and three enzymes—multicopper oxidase from Pyrobaculum aerophilum, laccase from Trametes versicolor, and bilirubin oxidase from Myrothecium verrucaria—were systematically evaluated to identify the most effective anodic configuration. In parallel, different cathodic architectures were investigated with emphasis on oxygen reduction as the terminal electron-accepting reaction. The optimal configuration consisted of the multicopper oxidase preparation from Pyrobaculum aerophilum at the anode coupled with an amine-reactive phenazine ethosulfate-modified bovine serum albumin (arPES–BSA) matrix at the cathode, where BSA acts as a structural scaffold for mediator immobilization. The resulting BioBattery was further integrated with a previously reported BioCapacitor system and demonstrated reproducible charging of a 470 μF capacitor. The obtained results highlight the potential of structured enzyme–mediator architectures to improve the operational stability and electrochemical performance of enzymatic energy devices. Overall, the proposed BioBattery configuration provides a promising platform for sustainable energy harvesting in low-power applications such as wearable and implantable electronic systems.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, enzymatic fuel cells have evolved into a significant and rapidly expanding area of scientific investigation. Their development has attracted substantial interest from diverse research communities, encompassing sustainable and renewable energy technologies, nanotechnology-enabled device engineering, electrochemical energy conversion, and advanced functional materials. This multidisciplinary engagement reflects the growing recognition of enzymatic fuel cells as promising platforms for environmentally benign power generation and biointegrated energy systems1–4. Their ability to convert biochemical energy into electrical power has enabled diverse applications, notably in wearable and implantable sensors, smart textiles, and environmental monitoring platforms. A distinguishing feature of enzyme fuel cells is their fuel flexibility, allowing operation with a wide range of substrates, from endogenous biomolecules such as glucose in implantable devices to complex organic matter present in wastewater streams used in treatment systems5.

Despite these advantages, enzyme fuel cells face several critical challenges that limit their broader deployment. Key issues include insufficient long-term operational stability, relatively low power output compared to conventional energy sources, and constraints on miniaturization for compact system integration. Although recent advances have reported notable improvements in power density, enzyme deactivation and performance degradation over extended operation remain persistent concerns6–8.

Nevertheless, enzyme-based energy systems offer distinct advantages over traditional power technologies. These systems can operate efficiently under physiological conditions, catalyze electrochemical reactions at ambient or body temperature and near-neutral pH, and significantly reduce environmental and biological waste associated with conventional power generation. As a result, enzyme fuel cells continue to represent a promising class of bioelectrochemical energy devices, particularly for applications requiring biocompatibility, sustainability, and low environmental impact.

A fundamental limitation of many enzymatic biofuel cells lies in their reliance on the diffusion of fuel from the surrounding bulk medium into the electrochemically active region of the anode. This diffusion-dependent operation represents a major distinction from conventional coin-type batteries widely employed in medical devices, which store reactants internally and therefore do not depend on external mass transport. As a result, enzymatic fuel cells face intrinsic constraints on achievable in vivo power density.

Several studies have demonstrated that the concentrations of physiologically available fuels in the human body are inherently low. Reported values indicate glucose concentrations typically in the range of 4–7 mM, lactate levels of approximately 1–3 mM, and dissolved oxygen concentrations near 0.1 mM under physiological conditions9–11. These limited substrate levels impose strict upper bounds on the reaction rates of enzymatic systems. Moreover, many enzymatic fuel cells operate under mass-transport-limited regimes, where the diffusion rate of substrates from the bulk environment governs the maximum attainable current and power output.

Tissue encapsulation and biofouling can form diffusion barriers around the device, reducing substrate availability at the electrode surface and progressively degrading performance. In addition to mass transport constraints, enzymatic fuel cells typically exhibit relatively low output voltages. Due to internal resistive losses and kinetic overpotentials, the operating voltage of such systems often remains below 700 mV, which is insufficient for direct operation of most modern microscale electronic components that commonly require supply voltages in the range of 0.8–1.2 V12,13.

To address these challenges, various strategies have been proposed, including the use of nanostructured electrodes to increase effective surface area and the deployment of devices in regions with enhanced convective transport, such as blood vessels, to alleviate diffusion limitations. While these approaches can improve power density and operational viability, they often constrain device placement or introduce significant challenges for miniaturization and system integration.

An alternative and potentially more scalable solution involves integrating the fuel source directly within the enzymatic biofuel cell. Such an approach would decouple power generation from external diffusion constraints, facilitate device miniaturization, and enable form factors more closely resembling those of conventional coin-cell batteries. By internalizing the substrate, enzymatic biofuel cells could achieve improved reliability and broader applicability in implantable microsystems.

This work introduces an enzyme-based fuel cell configuration in which the energy source is inherently embedded within the anode matrix, forming a self-contained system referred to as a bio-battery. In this architecture, the fuel substrate is co-immobilized alongside the corresponding biocatalytic enzyme, as depicted schematically in Fig. 1a. This integrated arrangement enables continuous and sustained power generation without dependence on external fuel replenishment or bulk mass transport from the surrounding environment. Moreover, the elimination of a separate membrane obviates the requirement for fuel diffusion across interfacial barriers, thereby reducing structural complexity and enhancing operational stability. The anode surface was modified by immobilizing a hyperthermostable multicopper oxidase onto carbon paper using glutaraldehyde as a covalent cross-linking agent. The enzyme employed in this study originates from the hyperthermophilic archaeon McoP, as reported in earlier investigations. Glutaraldehyde-based cross-linking is known to promote polymerization processes, leading to the formation of structurally complex networks, including polyphenolic-like assemblies14. Experimental observations revealed that McoP can oxidize the cross-linked glutaraldehyde matrix, thereby utilizing it as an intrinsic electron donor. When this anodic reaction is integrated with cathodic systems containing PES, efficient electrochemical energy generation can be achieved.

To complement the optimized anode architecture, the cathode surface was engineered via the immobilization of BSA chemically modified with arPES. This tailored cathodic interface facilitates effective electron mediation while simultaneously preserving mechanical integrity and electrochemical stability under continuous operating conditions. The resulting enzymatic fuel cell was subsequently coupled with a previously established BioCapacitor platform, which has been validated in earlier studies for its capability to reliably power miniaturized electronic components, including glucose sensors, microprocessors, and stepper motors15.

Figure 1b schematically summarizes the design strategy, optimization pathway, and application potential of the proposed BioBattery, from enzymatic configuration screening under non-diffusion-limited operation to BioCapacitor integration and energy delivery for wearable and implantable devices.

While multicopper oxidases are conventionally employed as cathodic biocatalysts for oxygen reduction, their deployment at the anode in this study is based on their intrinsic ability to oxidize polyphenolic substrates. multicopper oxidases catalyze the oxidation of phenolic compounds to the corresponding quinones through a four-electron transfer process coupled with oxygen reduction.

In this BioBattery architecture, the glutaraldehyde cross-linking process creates polyphenolic-like structures within the anode matrix. The McoP enzyme, in particular, has demonstrated activity toward these glutaraldehyde-derived structures, enabling the oxidation of the matrix itself to serve as the electron source.

Methods

Regents and material

In this study, a combination of biological catalysts, electrode materials, chemical reagents, and electronic components was employed to construct and evaluate the proposed bioelectrochemical system. Multiple multicopper oxidases—including a recombinantly produced enzyme derived from Pyrobaculum aerophilum as well as commercially available laccase and bilirubin oxidase—were investigated to assess their electrochemical performance under comparable conditions. The recombinant enzyme was heterologously expressed in Escherichia coli using a standard expression vector system. Electrode fabrication utilized gold disk electrodes and carbon-based substrates, complemented by conductive additives and cross-linking agents to enable stable enzyme immobilization. Redox-active mediators and protein-based modifiers were incorporated to facilitate efficient electron transfer at the cathode interface. Power management and load control were achieved using low-power electronic components, while all electrochemical measurements were conducted using a precision potentiostat. Preparation of McoP

The multicopper oxidase derived from McoP was obtained through recombinant expression followed by a multistep purification protocol designed to enhance enzymatic stability and catalytic activity. The enzyme was heterologously expressed in a specialized Escherichia coli strain using a plasmid-based expression system and cultivated under controlled shake-flask conditions. Following biomass recovery, cell disruption was achieved through ultrasonication, and insoluble debris was removed by centrifugal clarification. To ensure proper metal cofactor incorporation, the clarified enzyme solution was incubated with copper ions prior to a short thermal treatment, which selectively enriched the thermostable enzyme fraction. The purified enzyme was subsequently transferred into a buffered medium suitable for electrochemical applications. Synthesis and processing of KB-based Ink and arPES-modified proteins

The preparation of conductive ink and protein–mediator conjugates followed standardized yet carefully controlled protocols to ensure reproducibility and material stability. A Ketjen Black–based ink was formulated using an aqueous dispersion containing a nonionic surfactant and subjected to extended sonication to achieve uniform particle distribution. To maintain homogeneity, the ink was re-dispersed prior to each application. In parallel, BSA was chemically modified with an amine-reactive phenazine derivative under low-temperature conditions to preserve protein integrity. The conjugation reaction was followed by repeated centrifugal buffer exchange to remove excess reagents and stabilize the final product. Cathodic reaction mechanism

The arPES–BSA conjugate functions as an oxygen-sensitive redox mediator. The formal potential (E°′) of PES is approximately − 210 mV vs. NHE. During operation, the reduced form of arPES (arPESH) donates electrons to molecular oxygen. The primary reduction product is superoxide radical (O₂·⁻), formed through the following reaction:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{arPESH}} + {\mathrm{O}}_{{2}} \to {\mathrm{arPES}}^{{ + }} + {\mathrm{O}}_{{2}} \cdot^{ - } + {\mathrm{H}}^{{ + }}$$\end{document}

This single-electron transfer to oxygen is characteristic of phenazine-mediated oxygen reduction. The superoxide radical can subsequently undergo disproportionation (2O₂·⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂) or further reduction depending on the local environment and available reducing equivalents.

Electrode area determination and biobattery performance evaluation

The experimental evaluation of the BioBattery system comprised electrode surface area estimation, electrochemical performance characterization, and power generation assessment using external circuitry. The effective surface area of enzyme-functionalized TCP electrodes was quantified through image-based analysis of immobilized enzyme regions, enabling accurate normalization of current and power densities. Electrical characterization was performed by subjecting the assembled BioBattery to a broad range of resistive loads while monitoring voltage responses, from which current flow and power output were calculated. To assess practical energy delivery capability, the BioBattery was further integrated with a charge management circuit and capacitive storage elements, and the resulting charging behavior was evaluated under open-circuit conditions. The observed high open-circuit voltage with the arPES–BSA cathode requires consideration of the redox potentials involved. While PES has a formal potential of approximately − 210 mV vs. NHE, which is more negative than the T1 copper sites of laccase (~ 780 mV) and BOD (~ 670 mV), the effective cathode potential is determined by the overall reaction kinetics and the overpotential for oxygen reduction. The arPES–BSA system demonstrates favorable kinetics for mediated electron transfer to oxygen, despite the thermodynamic potential difference. This apparent contradiction is resolved by considering that (i) the rate of electron transfer from reduced arPES to oxygen is rapid, and (ii) the overall cell voltage is determined by the difference between the anodic oxidation potential (glutaraldehyde matrix oxidation by McoP) and the effective cathodic reduction potential, which includes kinetic factors beyond the standard redox potentials of the individual components.

Limitations of the study

Despite the promising performance of the proposed BioBattery system, several limitations should be acknowledged. The electrochemical characterization was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, and system behavior in complex real-world or physiological environments may differ due to variations in temperature, pH, and oxygen availability. The long-term operational stability of the immobilized multi-enzyme system, particularly under continuous or cyclic loading, was not evaluated beyond the experimental timeframe of this study. In addition, while the internal confinement of the fuel reduces dependence on external substrate diffusion, the total energy output remains inherently limited by the finite quantity of immobilized reactants. Finally, further optimization of enzyme loading, electrode architecture, and integration strategies will be necessary to improve power density and extend operational lifetime for practical deployment.

Conclusion

The present work investigates the design, fabrication, and electrochemical characterization of an enzymatic fuel cell that employs a multicopper oxidase as the anodic biocatalyst. In contrast to conventional enzymatic fuel cell architectures that depend on continuous diffusion of substrates from the bulk environment to the electrode interface, the proposed system operates through a configuration in which the electroactive species are confined within the device. By reducing reliance on sustained external substrate transport, this approach seeks to mitigate a key limitation associated with diffusion-controlled enzymatic systems. The resulting architecture represents a step toward more integrated and self-contained enzymatic energy conversion platforms, here referred to as BioBatteries, with potential relevance for low-power and miniaturized electronic applications.

In the BioBattery configuration, both the enzymatic catalyst and its corresponding fuel are immobilized at the anode, providing a battery-like operational mode. The cathodic reaction can utilize ambient electron acceptors, such as dissolved molecular oxygen, which is readily available in many physiological and environmental conditions. This design enhances operational autonomy under suitable conditions and may be advantageous in applications where continuous external fuel supply is impractical. Furthermore, the BioBattery is electrically coupled with a previously developed BioCapacitor to enable voltage amplification, thereby expanding the range of electronic components that can be powered within the limits of the present system.

The primary objective of this study is to demonstrate the fabrication and electrochemical performance of a multi-enzyme system capable of oxidizing phenolic substrates. Enzyme immobilization is achieved via glutaraldehyde vapor deposition, which promotes enzyme confinement and functional stability at the electrode surface, although long-term stability beyond the tested duration remains to be fully evaluated. The resulting BioBattery is shown to power the BioCapacitor, enabling repeated charging of a 470 μF capacitor in under 25 s under the tested laboratory conditions. These results demonstrate the feasibility of the concept and its ability to deliver usable electrical energy to an auxiliary storage component, highlighting its potential as a bioelectrochemical power source while acknowledging that further optimization will be required for practical deployment.

Despite the promising performance of the proposed BioBattery system, several limitations should be acknowledged. The electrochemical characterization was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, and system behavior in complex real-world or physiological environments may differ due to variations in temperature, pH, and oxygen availability.
