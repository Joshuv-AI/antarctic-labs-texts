Patient reported outcome measures (PROMs) and electronic patient reported outcomes measures (ePROMs) are commonly administered by oncology clinics. Their use has been linked to better symptom management, physical function, and health‐related quality of life [1], as well as less healthcare utilization in patients with cancer [2, 3]. Despite these encouraging findings, clinicians' concerns about time burden [4, 5, 6, 7, 8], liability [7, 9], and adequately addressing patients' symptoms [5, 6] persist and may discourage discussion of PROM scores during clinical encounters. Patients may feel more positively about PROMs when their clinician reviews their scores [10], while lack of mention may discourage future reporting. Approaches that automatically direct patients to self‐management materials, in addition to sharing scores with providers, are one way of personalizing responses to ePROMs [11]. Another option for personalizing support while minimizing clinical burden is having remote/centralized, dedicated clinical staff follow up with patients following ePROMs completion as part of a collaborative care model (CCM)‐based approach. CCMs bring together the expertise of professionals from different fields to inform treatment. Collaborative care interventions have successfully addressed pain [12, 13], physical function [12, 13], fatigue [13], and depression [13, 14], among patients who have cancer, including those with poor prognoses [15] and those from minoritized groups [16].

As part of the Enhanced EHR (electronic health record)‐facilitated Cancer Symptom Control (E2C2) pragmatic clinical trial [17], we implemented ePROMs in routine medical oncology care and deployed a remotely delivered, CCM‐based symptom management intervention. The intervention offered patients symptom‐focused support tailored to their needs and preferences and accessible from their homes. The trial was positive and demonstrated significant intervention benefit in overall SPPADE (sleep, pain, physical function, anxiety, depression, energy deficit/fatigue) symptom burden, individual symptoms, quality of life, and health care utilization [18]. The usual care comparator consisted of ePROMs administered to patients at clinical encounters, with scores provided to oncology care teams via EHR interfaces and clinical decision support (CDS) tools. The intervention added automated delivery of self‐management materials to patients reporting moderate or severe symptoms, and the opportunity for those reporting severe symptoms to work with a symptom care manager (SCM). The SCMs were allied health providers who delivered symptom management support to patients and worked collaboratively alongside care teams. SCMs presented more complex patient cases at physician‐led Case Management Conferences (CMC) for consideration of symptom etiology and multidisciplinary management, drawing on palliative care, rehabilitation medicine, pain medicine, psychiatry, and medical oncology expertise.

To overcome some of the known barriers to implementation of PROMs [4] and ePROMs [11], we developed a robust implementation plan [19]. Planned implementation strategies included CDS built into the EHR, recruitment and training of internal practice facilitators known as Symptom Sages, and audit and feedback. We adapted aspects of the intervention and its implementation over time [19] in response to challenges related to implementation in heterogeneous and geographically dispersed oncology clinics. Challenges also related to the diversity of cancer types, stages, treatment‐status, and treatment intent, as well as the fact that clinics were situated in both tertiary academic and community settings. Adaptations were also needed due to the COVID‐19 pandemic occurring during the trial [19].

As our team addressed barriers to implementation with adaptations targeting multi‐level issues, we gained insights that may inform the design and implementation of future CCM interventions. This served as a reminder that the lessons learned from clinical trials extend beyond the stated aims and may benefit health care systems, providers, and investigators. We present recommendations derived from our experience implementing an EHR‐facilitated CCM intervention and conducting a hybrid Type II effectiveness‐implementation study embedded into routine medical oncology care. Our pragmatic clinical trial was conducted in the medical oncology and hematology clinics in a large Upper Midwest healthcare system. The healthcare system included a quaternary medical center and an extensive network of community hospitals and clinics. Most patients included in this trial were over age 55, and over 40% lived in rural or highly rural areas [18].

Initial reflections on lessons learned were developed by the lead author and another study team member (JLR) following their review and consideration of pre‐implementation interviews with clinical stakeholders [20], feedback gathered from care teams throughout the implementation period, post‐implementation interviews with Symptom Sages [21], and tracking of changes made to the intervention and its implementation [19]. Their initial reflections were presented during an hour‐long reflection session with the other co‐authors, who were members of the E2C2 study team and/or clinical stakeholders (four researchers, four physicians, one SCM, one project manager, one supervisor, and one senior program coordinator). During the reflection session, the lead author summarized her and JLR's initial reflections on lessons learned, and study team members were asked to reflect on the challenges faced and what recommendations they would make to those implementing and evaluating similar ePROM systems of symptom management and surveillance. After the session, the lead author revised the initial summary of lessons learned from prior work and circulated it for additional reflection and revision and group approval. The final recommendations related to intervention design, implementation, and pragmatic trial conduct are described below, and listed in Table 1, with their corresponding Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.0 construct [24].

Intervention Design Recommendations

Present existing evidence related to how the intervention improves upon usual care. [Innovation‐ Innovation Evidence Base, Relative Advantage]

Present existing evidence related to how the intervention improves upon usual care. [Innovation‐ Innovation Evidence Base, Relative Advantage]

Some clinical stakeholders shared concerns about the intervention being finalized with little opportunity for practices to provide input, particularly those from community sites, who had concerns about implementing an intervention developed at an academic site [20]. We presented the E2C2 intervention as being designed to supplement the care patients received in oncology clinics by providing symptom education and guided self‐management, as well as using collaborative care to address severe symptoms that oncologists generally lacked time to manage or that they perceived to fall outside their scope of care (e.g., anxiety symptoms exacerbated by cancer). Our communications and presentations to clinical stakeholders included a diagram of the CCM and stated that the intervention used an evidence‐based approach. However, we did not consistently reference specific trials that had validated the CCM‐based approach among patients with cancer. Clinical stakeholders may have benefited from more information about the robust evidence supporting the CCM‐based approach, perhaps increasing providers' acceptance and confidence that the intervention would benefit their patients.

Ensure CDS provides information that is perceived to be clinically relevant and provide clinicians with ongoing CDS support and clear expectations around its use. [Innovation‐ Innovation Design]

Ensure CDS provides information that is perceived to be clinically relevant and provide clinicians with ongoing CDS support and clear expectations around its use. [Innovation‐ Innovation Design]

Epic CDS developed for the trial were intended to keep providers informed about their patients' symptoms (e.g., Synopsis and Snapshot summaries of the patient's symptoms, a dotphrase that could auto‐populate the patient's symptom scores in their note). An Our Practice Advisory (OPA) also displayed on the EHR interfaces of patients reporting severe symptoms. The OPA supported clinicians in responding to the symptom and referring patients to an SCM, using symptom‐specific order SmartSets. It also provided a link to a website with self‐management materials that clinicians could provide patients. OPAs are a common way of alerting clinicians to pertinent info in the EHR, but they may be overused and clinicians experience alert fatigue [25]. To avoid contributing to alert fatigue, we placed the OPA in an unobtrusive location in the EHR and programmed it so clinicians could continue working in the EHR without addressing it. However, we found that only about half of care team members reported using the OPA [26]. It is possible that it was underutilized because clinicians did not perceive enough value [27], which may be remedied by asking clinicians what information they want to be alerted to. Furthermore, despite initial education, we found that clinicians continued to lack awareness of the CDS over the course of the trial and needed ongoing support and reminders [27]. Similarly, other research has noted challenges with staff finding symptom reports and additional training needs [11]. Furthermore, we told clinicians that they could choose how to interact with the CDS. As a result, some clinicians may not have felt compelled to help manage those symptoms or utilize the CDS given the role of the SCM in the intervention [27]. Future ePROMs CCM interventions that utilize CDS may consider early efforts to ensure clinicians find value in the information conveyed via the CDS and provide continued support for and more clear expectations around CDS use.

Implementation Recommendations

Utilize external practice facilitators to lead implementation efforts and recruit clinical champions from within the practice. [Individual domains‐ Opinion leaders, implementation facilitators]

Utilize external practice facilitators to lead implementation efforts and recruit clinical champions from within the practice. [Individual domains‐ Opinion leaders, implementation facilitators]

We recruited members of oncology teams to champion the intervention and serve as internal practice facilitators known as Symptom Sages. We envisioned the Symptom Sage role as combining two Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) implementation strategies: champions and facilitation [28]. The clinical champion role is more centered around encouraging adoption, often utilizing existing relationships and influence [29], while facilitators carry out implementation activities [30]. We thought Symptom Sages could capitalize on their existing relationships and use evidence‐based implementation strategies we taught them to promote intervention adoption. However, many Symptom Sages lacked time to perform ongoing facilitation activities, especially when faced with competing clinical demands [21]. Our expectations for Symptom Sages may have been especially burdensome during the COVID‐19 pandemic [21] due to staff turnover, limited in‐person staffing, and a rapid transition to delivery of care via telehealth. Even outside of the pandemic context, it may be unrealistic to expect busy clinicians to take time away from clinical duties to join additional meetings or support intervention implementation [21]. It may be a better fit for clinicians to act as clinical champions, allowing them to leverage their role in the practice to encourage others to support the intervention, and leave the majority of implementation‐related activities to a small number of external practice facilitators with implementation training [21]. In the absence of funding for external practice facilitators, the intervention proponent may need to take on the implementation facilitation role. Similarly, the study team asked some leaders to pass on specific information about intervention implementation with their staff. This type of information may be better communicated by external practice facilitators or the intervention proponent, who could dedicate time to check in with staff on a weekly or monthly basis to ensure proper processes are followed.

Proactive strategies may be needed to facilitate ePROMs completion, especially in groups most likely to experience health disparities. [Individuals‐ Innovation Recipient Capability]

Proactive strategies may be needed to facilitate ePROMs completion, especially in groups most likely to experience health disparities. [Individuals‐ Innovation Recipient Capability]

Without proactive efforts to reach patient populations that do not typically use the patient portal, remote collaborative care interventions that require electronic reporting of symptoms to prompt symptom management support may unintentionally exacerbate health disparities [31]. We used multiple modes to administer ePROMs to patients who were not portal users. Patients had the option of completing ePROMs on tablets in clinic prior to their appointment, and some clinic staff were proactive about helping patients complete ePROMs during appointments. We also introduced interactive voice response software so that patients could complete ePROMs telephonically. These methods offered scalable means for patients to complete ePROMs in clinic or at home without logging into their patient portal, offering an alternative option for those who lacked broadband access or had lower digital literacy. If we had additional resources, assisting patients with signing up for the patient portal, explaining how to navigate to and complete portal‐based questionnaires, and explaining the importance of completing portal‐based questionnaires could have helped patients actively engage in their symptom management, particularly those with broadband access but who possessed less digital knowledge or confidence completing portal‐based questionnaires. We briefly had a staff member make targeted efforts to increase response rates by sitting in the patient waiting area and offering to help patients complete the portal‐based questionnaires via tablet. However, continuously providing this level of support was not feasible with the available E2C2 funding and staff. Nonetheless, if resources are available to support getting patients signed up for the portal to complete ePROMs, this may be a worthwhile investment to promote portal use and address disparities [31]. Indeed, prior to intervention commencement, patients cared for by participating medical oncology practices who were male, belonged to minoritized groups, resided in rural areas, were older (compared to younger age groups), not working, or had limited access to broadband were less likely to use the portal [31].

Identify and dialog with highly‐engaged individuals whose vantage may suggest intervention and implementation adaptations to promote use and sustainment among clinicians. [Implementation Process‐ Reflecting and Evaluating]

Identify and dialog with highly‐engaged individuals whose vantage may suggest intervention and implementation adaptations to promote use and sustainment among clinicians. [Implementation Process‐ Reflecting and Evaluating]

Patients' symptom severity scores automatically triggered self‐management materials to be sent. Additionally, those with severe symptoms were also offered the opportunity to speak to a SCM. This design was intended to largely bypass clinicians so that they were not responsible for ensuring patients received intervention resources. However, clinicians were aware of the self‐management resources and could send in‐basket messages to SCMs asking them to connect with patients. A small number of oncology clinicians made a disproportionate number of referrals and may offer insights into how the intervention could be adapted to promote greater clinician engagement. For instance, conversations with these highly engaged clinicians could reveal whether additional changes to the Epic build could make it easier for clinicians to refer patients to a SCM. We held periodic meetings with tumor groups (which included these highly engaged clinicians), during which we elicited feedback on ways to improve intervention processes. However, we may have benefited from meeting with highly engaged clinicians individually to obtain richer feedback. Learning from cases that are outside of the norm is a purposeful sampling technique called extreme or deviant sampling and can be useful when the goal is to learn what caused the unexpected outcomes [32, 33, 34]. Future efforts may also consider evaluating whether highly engaged clinicians increase the reach or effectiveness of the intervention, or whether greater clinician involvement is unnecessary with a robust symptom management CCM intervention in place.

Adapt evidence‐based strategies to the real‐world context and track changes over time for potential iteration. [Implementation Process‐ Adapting, Reflecting and Evaluating Implementation]

Adapt evidence‐based strategies to the real‐world context and track changes over time for potential iteration. [Implementation Process‐ Adapting, Reflecting and Evaluating Implementation]

Prior to implementation, we interviewed stakeholders, conducted site visits, and administered validated survey measures to assess implementation readiness. Still, we encountered implementation barriers and unanticipated variations in practice workflow between clinics. The occurrence of the COVID‐19 pandemic also drastically impacted existing workflows and staffing (e.g., nursing leadership turnover), which resulted in varying levels of leadership engagement with the intervention and changed how we implemented our carefully selected evidence‐based implementation strategies. For instance, we transitioned to online training and monthly touchpoints with Symptom Sages (instead of in person), perhaps decreasing engagement [21]. Symptom Sages may also have had fewer opportunities to engage their colleagues as in‐clinic care delivery shifted to telehealth [21].

We had also planned on sharing monthly audit and feedback data with clinicians on their patients' symptom severity compared to other providers' patients based on monthly ePROM response data. However, due to concerns about further stressing providers already stressed by the COVID‐19 pandemic [21] and the inability to account for differences in the complexity of different providers' patients across disease types, we shared practice level monthly audit and feedback data on symptom severity of the practice's patients. Evidence suggests that audit and feedback that is timely and provided at the individual level in a way that is not punitive may better achieve the intended effect [35]. We may have emphasized the evidence‐based rationale for providing individual level feedback to obtain practice buy‐in for doing so, which may have helped providers recognize the need to more proactively refer their patients to symptom management resources.

Evidence‐based implementation strategies may not work as planned when tested in the real‐world environment so it is important to remain flexible to modifying the implementation plan when unexpected events and obstacles arise. As part of a learning consortium with other research centers conducting similar clinical trials using ePROMs and symptom management support, we collaboratively developed and used the longitudinal implementation tracking system (LISTS) to record our planned implementation strategies and document changes to these strategies and the reason for such changes throughout the duration of the trial [19, 22]. Others can use the LISTS infrastructure to systematically track implementation strategies and modifications as part of future research projects, although completing it requires some time and personnel resources [22].

Pragmatic Trial Conduct Recommendations

Tailor interactions between collaborative care providers and oncology care teams according to care team preferences when possible but avoid barriers to scalability. [Inner Setting‐Communications]

Tailor interactions between collaborative care providers and oncology care teams according to care team preferences when possible but avoid barriers to scalability. [Inner Setting‐Communications]

As part of the CCM, we sought to integrate the intervention with oncology practices so that SCMs could work collaboratively alongside patients' oncology care teams. Remote delivery of symptom management support, which was necessary for scalability, was acceptable to some but a cause of concern for others [21]. Some providers felt strongly that SCMs should be embedded within clinics to facilitate informal, in‐person discussions about patients' symptom management needs to promote collaboration [21]. Similarly, some providers were concerned that their patients would discuss their symptoms with an SCM instead of with the care team [20, 21], resulting in delays in addressing treatment‐related side‐effects [21]. However, because we had 2–6 SCMs working at a given time and the study included tens of thousands of patients who received the intervention across multiple sites [18], it would not have been feasible to provide in‐clinic symptom management support without hiring additional SCMs. Doing so would have increased study costs and likely decreased long‐term sustainability. Additionally, space in the oncology clinics was limited and many could not physically accommodate the SCMs. Thus, the most feasible way to address these concerns may have been having more conversations to distinguish symptoms managed by SCMs and symptoms managed by the care team. SCMs did send oncology providers updates via the EHR to keep them informed about working with their patients. However, oncology providers differed in their preferences for receiving information from SCMs. Our team could have more proactively elicited providers' communication preferences by developing a way for providers to indicate their communication preferences in the EHR and then automated EHR‐directed communication to align with providers' indicated preferences. This may have improved receptivity to the intervention and fostered collaborative communication even with SCMs being stationed remotely.

Clearly communicate realistic expectations for opportunities and limitations to adapt the intervention. [Innovation‐ Innovation Adaptability]

Clearly communicate realistic expectations for opportunities and limitations to adapt the intervention. [Innovation‐ Innovation Adaptability]

The inability to customize the intervention for different practices represents a challenge of large‐scale ePROMs implementation. Developing the infrastructure to integrate and deliver ePROMs within the Epic EHR required substantial time and financial investment. As a result, it was not feasible to make modifications in the EHR for individual practices, especially when frequent updates to the EHR required validation to confirm EHR build integrity and, at times, revision of intervention build to harmonize with the updated EHR. Moreover, once a clinical trial is underway, it is important to deliver the intervention with high fidelity, or “per protocol”. Thus, opportunities to refine the intervention itself may be limited to preserve the trial's internal validity. However, lower adoption of intervention components observed for providers at community sites [26] may have been a result of their perceived lack of inclusion in the development of the intervention [20]. As clinicians identify challenges with intervention implementation or offer opportunities for improvement, it may be important to bring awareness to the key intervention components that cannot be modified to preserve intervention integrity. A logic model [19, 23] that shows the components of the intervention and strategies deployed to support their implementation may provide a helpful visual to aid communication. A logic model can be used to introduce the intervention to care teams and explain why certain modifications may not be possible to maintain intervention fidelity so that care teams understand the rationale for such decisions.

Concluding Thoughts

Implementing ePROMs and collaborative care for remote symptom management in patients with cancer at the population level is feasible. However, getting care teams fully onboard and engaged may come with challenges. Explicitly presenting the evidence‐based rationale for decisions may be key to obtaining buy‐in. Early efforts should seek and incorporate care team preferences into intervention design as feasible.

Although implementation and communication efforts may be less complex in a single clinic compared to the multi‐site setting in which this trial was conducted, less‐resourced settings may find it challenging to find a clinician with the enthusiasm and bandwidth to champion the intervention and funds for an external practice facilitator may be lacking. In such cases, the intervention proponent may need to commit to championing the intervention and leading facilitation efforts because early engagement of clinical staff is essential for buy‐in and ultimate integration of processes. Following change management principles and clearly stating goals of improving outcomes while reducing clinical burden are crucial to successful implementation. If the selected implementation strategies are not working as intended, these strategies should be modified and the changes and the reason for such changes rigorously documented. For ePROMs interventions, specifically, the need to adapt strategies to foster ePROMs completion should be monitored to ensure non‐portal users can still benefit from remote symptom monitoring interventions. We found that, consistent with past research, certain patient populations were less likely to use the patient portal. We overcame this by offering opportunities for IVR and tablet‐based completion in clinic.

Further, implementation of ePROMs CCM interventions in complex health care systems as part of pragmatic clinical trials may be more challenging because of the need to uphold intervention fidelity (e.g., standardization of questions asked and timing of ePROMs administration, standardized protocol for SCMs offering symptom management support) despite potential differences in workflows and patient populations across hospital sites. Moreover, despite implementing our intervention with grant funding in a well‐resourced health care enterprise, it was not feasible to make clinic specific EHR updates or hire the number of SCMs that would be required for embedding SCMs within clinics in‐person. ePROMs CCM interventions implemented in a single clinic or hospital site or outside of a clinical trial context may offer the ability for greater individual tailoring (e.g., CDS built with one clinic's workflow in mind, SCM(s) could be embedded within a single clinic in person), but such tailoring may still be costly and limit future scalability. Scalability and sustainability should remain at the forefront when developing and implementing these types of interventions to avoid developing interventions that fail to become meaningfully adopted and integrated into usual practice in a sustainable way.
