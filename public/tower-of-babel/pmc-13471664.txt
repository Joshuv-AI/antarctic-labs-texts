Anti-NRP1 peptide-engineered ROS/pH dual-responsive nanoparticles for Alpelisib delivery regulate Sema3A-NRP1/PI3K-AKT signaling to balance oxidative stress and inhibit angiogenesis in endometriosis
Authors: Jie Zhang, Hong Zhang, Wei Liu, Yi Zhang, Wenjie Hou, Chi Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endometriosis progression is driven by oxidative stress and excessive angiogenesis within an inflammatory microenvironment. To overcome these challenges, we designed ROS/pH dual-responsive Alpelisib-loaded nanoparticles (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs) functionalized with an anti-NRP1 peptide for targeted therapy. The nanoparticles exhibited superior stability, responsive drug release, and selective internalization by NRP1-overexpressing endothelial cells. In vitro results showed efficient inhibition of NRP1 and downstream PI3K/AKT signaling, along with decreased reactive oxygen species (ROS) and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities. In an endometriosis rat model, treatment with Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly reduced ectopic lesion burden and angiogenic markers (VEGF, CD34), while suppressing systemic inflammation and oxidative injury indicators such as IL-6, TNF-α, and MDA. Fluorescence imaging confirmed preferential accumulation of nanoparticles in CD31⁺ vascular regions. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that modulation of the Sema3A-NRP1-PI3K/AKT signaling axis restored redox homeostasis and inhibited pathological angiogenesis. These findings identify Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs as a synergistic nanoplatform that integrates microenvironment responsiveness with NRP1-targeted intervention, providing a promising therapeutic strategy for endometriosis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04560-6.

Introduction

Endometriosis (EMS) is a common benign gynecological disorder with malignant tumor-like characteristics, defined by the ectopic growth of endometrial glands and stroma [1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that EMS affects 10–15% of women of reproductive age and 30–50% of infertile patients, posing a significant threat to reproductive health [2–4]. Clinically, patients often experience chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, and infertility, which markedly impair quality of life and psychological well-being. Current treatments mainly rely on hormonal therapy or surgical excision; however, hormonal side effects limit long-term tolerability, and postoperative recurrence rates remain as high as 40–50% within five years [5, 6]. As no curative therapy is currently available, developing new treatment strategies that enable precise intervention within the pathological microenvironment is urgently needed.

The pathogenesis of EMS is driven by a complex interplay between inflammatory responses, aberrant angiogenesis, and oxidative stress [7]. Ectopic lesions exhibit elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and sustained reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which overwhelm antioxidant defenses like superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione (GSH) [8–10]. This redox imbalance aggravates mitochondrial dysfunction and promotes pathological angiogenesis, a key process supporting lesion survival and expansion [10, 11]. At the molecular level, the semaphorin 3 A (Sema3A)-NRP1/phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating angiogenesis and oxidative stress [12]. Neuropilin-1 (NRP1), highly expressed in endothelial cells of newly formed vessels, mediates Sema3A signaling and cooperates with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), while downstream PI3K/AKT activation helps sustain excessive ROS production and lesion progression [13]. Consequently, targeting the Sema3A-NRP1 interaction or inhibiting PI3K/AKT offers a promising therapeutic avenue, provided that efficient and safe drug delivery can be achieved [14, 15].

Nanoparticle (NP)-based drug delivery systems have shown significant advantages in disease treatment, particularly for lesion-specific targeting and microenvironment-responsive therapy [16, 17]. Intelligent design allows NPs to respond to pathological triggers for precise release. For example, pH-responsive nanoplatforms can selectively eliminate cells in acidic microenvironments [18], while systems combining internal pH sensitivity with external NIR light have shown efficacy in controlled delivery and imaging [19]. Furthermore, functional materials like polydopamine have been employed to modulate ROS levels for tissue regeneration [20]. In recent years, ROS/pH dual-responsive NPs have emerged as a research hotspot, as they can accelerate drug release under inflammatory and oxidative stress conditions, thereby significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy [21, 22]. Meanwhile, the PI3K inhibitor Alpelisib has shown therapeutic potential in multiple diseases, but its clinical application is limited by systemic toxicity and poor pharmacokinetic properties [23, 24]. Therefore, integrating a dual-responsive nanoplatform with anti-NRP1 modification for the targeted delivery of Alpelisib may enhance efficacy while reducing adverse effects, offering a promising strategy for EMS treatment.

Based on the above background, this study aims to construct an anti-NRP1 peptide-modified ROS/pH dual-responsive NP drug delivery system (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs) for precise Alpelisib delivery. This platform is designed to block Sema3A–NRP1 binding and inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby restoring redox homeostasis, suppressing aberrant angiogenesis, and alleviating the progression of EMS. Scientifically, this work may provide new evidence for the interplay between angiogenesis and oxidative stress in EMS, while establishing a nanoplatform capable of both targeted delivery and microenvironment-responsive release. Clinically, this strategy may lay the groundwork for safer and more effective EMS therapies with fewer side effects and lower recurrence, and further highlight the potential of nanotechnology in precision gynecological treatment.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China). Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in standard cages at 23 °C and 55% humidity with a 12 h light/dark cycle. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) (Ethical Approval No. 202508A0091).

Establishment of the EMS model

Unmated female SD rats (6 weeks old, 180–200 g) were randomly assigned to the control group or EMS model group (n = 6 per group). Under anesthesia with ketamine (50 mg/kg) and sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), a midline abdominal incision was made and the left uterine horn was excised and immediately placed in sterile saline. The endometrium was carefully separated and cut into two approximately 5 mm fragments, which were implanted into subcutaneous pockets on both sides of the abdominal wall with the endometrial surface facing the abdominal muscle.

To facilitate ectopic lesion formation, estradiol benzoate (30 µg/kg) was administered subcutaneously immediately after surgery and again on day 10. On day 7, the successful establishment of ectopic lesions was confirmed by visual inspection and palpation of the subcutaneous nodules. Rats were then assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): (1) Sham, (2) EMS, (3) EMS + shNC, and (4) EMS + shNRP1. shNC or shNRP1 was delivered by tail vein injection of adeno-associated virus (1 × 1012 vg/mL, 100 µL/rat). Warm stimulation was applied before and after injection to improve tail vein visibility and injection success.

Ectopic lesion volume was calculated using the formula: V = 1/2×L×W2, where L represents the longest diameter and W the shortest diameter of the cyst. On postoperative day 20, rats were sacrificed and tissue samples were collected for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC; CD34 and VEGF), Western blot (WB) (NRP1 and PI3K/AKT), and assessment of oxidative stress markers (ROS, SOD, CAT, GSH, etc.) [25].

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Ectopic endometrial tissues were collected from the control and EMS groups (n = 3 per group). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Cat#15596026, Invitrogen, USA). RNA purity and concentration were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7 were used for library preparation.

RNA libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Cat#20020595, Illumina, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw data were quality-checked using FastQC, and adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed with Trimmomatic. Clean reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (Rnor_6.0) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 (v1.34.0) using the criteria |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05.

Angiogenesis-related gene screening

To identify angiogenesis-related genes potentially involved in EMS pathogenesis, genes annotated with the keyword “angiogenesis” were retrieved from the GeneCards database, and those with a relevance score > 10 were selected. This gene set was intersected with the DEGs identified by RNA-seq analysis, and the overlap was visualized using the Venny 2.1 online tool. Six overlapping candidate genes were identified, including NRP1.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

Uterine tissues were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using collagenase and DNase digestion. Libraries were prepared with the 10x Genomics Chromium platform and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE150). Raw data were processed with Cell Ranger (v6.1.2) and aligned to the rat reference genome (Rnor_6.0) to generate expression count matrices.

Downstream analyses were performed using Seurat (v4.0.6). Low-quality cells were filtered out (nFeature_RNA < 200 or > 6000; mitochondrial gene percentage > 20%). Data were normalized with NormalizeData, highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. Clustering was conducted on the top 20 principal components using t-SNE. Cell types were annotated based on canonical lineage-specific marker genes and further verified using the CellMarker database.

To assess the cell type-specific expression pattern of NRP1 in the uterine microenvironment, its expression across different cell subsets was extracted and visualized using FeaturePlot and ViolinPlot, with particular focus on stromal and endothelial cell populations.

Primary endothelial cell isolation and culture

Primary uterine artery endothelial cells (UAECs) were isolated from the uterine tissues of Control and EMS rats. Tissues were minced and digested with type I collagenase (1 mg/mL, Cat#LS004197, Worthington, USA) at 37 °C for 45 min. The digested suspension was filtered through a 200 μm cell strainer, centrifuged, and resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium (Cat#11320-033, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Passage 2 (P2) cells were used for subsequent RT-qPCR analyses.

Cell culture and grouping

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; CP-H082, Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS (Cat#10099158, Gibco, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Cat#C0222, Beyotime, China) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

For hypoxia experiments, cells were incubated under normoxic (21% O2) or hypoxic (2% O2) conditions for 0, 24, 48, or 72 h. For estrogen stimulation, cells were treated with different concentrations of 17β-estradiol (0, 10− 8, and 10− 7 mol/L; Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Germany) for 48 h under either normoxic or hypoxic conditions. Protein expression levels were determined by WB [26].

For viral transduction, Sema3A overexpression was achieved using a lentiviral vector (oe-Sema3A; GeneChem, Shanghai, China), with oe-NC as the control. NRP1 knockdown was performed using shRNA lentiviral vectors (sh-NRP1-1 and sh-NRP1-2; GeneChem; sequences listed in Table S1), with sh-NC as the control. HUVECs were seeded in six-well plates and transduced at 60–70% confluence with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 in the presence of polybrene (8 µg/mL). After 24 h, the medium was replaced, and cells were cultured for an additional 48–72 h. Transduction efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR and WB. For combined intervention experiments, cells were first transduced with oe-Sema3A, followed by sh-NRP1 lentivirus 24 h later to generate the oe-Sema3A + sh-NRP1 model. For rescue experiments, the PI3K/AKT agonist IGF-1 (100 nM; 100 − 11, PeproTech, USA), prepared in serum-free DMEM, was added to the oe-Sema3A + sh-NRP1 model and incubated for 2 h, generating the oe-Sema3A + sh-NRP1 + IGF-1 group. All viral transduction experiments were conducted under 2% O2 and 10⁻8 mol/L estrogen to mimic the EMS microenvironment.

Drug Treatments: The PI3K inhibitor Alpelisib (Cat#S2814, Selleck, USA) was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 10 mM stock solution and diluted to a 24 µM working concentration. Treatment groups included Alpelisib, blank NPs, TAT-AT7-modified NPs (TAT-AT7-NPs), Alpelisib-loaded NPs (Alp@NPs), and anti-NRP1 peptide-modified NPs (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs), with equivalent Alpelisib concentrations of 24 µM. Cells were treated in serum-free DMEM for 48 h.

For ROS scavenging experiments, cells were pretreated with GSH (100 µM; Cat#Y0000517, Merck KGaA, Germany) for 2 h before NP exposure.

In NP treatment groups, HUVECs were incubated with fresh serum-free DMEM containing Alpelisib, NPs, Alp@NPs, or Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs (all at an equivalent Alpelisib concentration of 24 µM) under either normoxic or hypoxic conditions for 48 h. High-quality FBS, culture media, and sterile plasticware designed for tissue culture were used to ensure cell culture reliability [27].

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 (Cat#C0037, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well; Corning, USA) with three replicate wells per group. After 24 h of attachment, cells were treated according to the experimental design. At the end of treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA), and relative cell viability was calculated for statistical analysis.

EdU cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed by measuring DNA synthesis using an EdU Apollo567 Staining Kit (Cat#C10310-1, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). After treatment, HUVECs were seeded in 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well. Cells were incubated with 50 µM EdU at 37 °C for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX63, Japan). Five random fields were selected per well, and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified to assess proliferation.

Transwell migration assay

Cell migration was evaluated using Transwell chambers with 8 μm pore-size inserts (Cat#3422, Corning, USA). The lower chambers were filled with 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS. Treated HUVECs were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chambers at 1 × 105 cells/well. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Migrated cells were counted in five randomly selected fields under a microscope (Olympus BX63, Japan).

Wound healing assay

HUVECs were seeded in six-well plates and grown to confluence. A scratch was created using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip (Axygen, USA), followed by PBS washing to remove debris. Cells were then cultured in serum-free medium. Images were captured at 0 h and 24 h using a microscope (Olympus BX63, Japan). Wound closure was quantified with ImageJ software, and the healing rate was calculated as: Healing rate=(Initial wound area-Final wound area)/Initial wound area×100%.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (Cat#356234, Corning, USA) was thawed at 4 °C, added to 96-well plates (50 µL/well), and polymerized at 4 °C for 30 min. HUVECs (1 × 104 cells/well) were suspended in medium containing 1% FBS and seeded onto the Matrigel surface, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 6 h. Images of five random fields were captured using a microscope (Olympus BX63, Japan). The number of nodes and total tube length were quantified using ImageJ software to evaluate angiogenic capacity.

ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, 10 µM; Cat#D6883, Merck KGaA, Germany). Following treatments, HUVECs were incubated with serum-free medium containing DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Excess probe was removed by washing three times with PBS. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX63, Japan) in the DAPI (blue) and DCFH (green) channels, and fluorescence intensity was semi-quantified using ImageJ software. In parallel, intracellular ROS levels were also measured by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX LX, Beckman Coulter, USA) using the FITC channel (excitation 488 nm, emission 525 nm), and fluorescence intensity was compared among groups.

To evaluate the effects of ROS responsiveness and the oxidative microenvironment on NP-mediated drug release, HUVECs were pretreated with GSH (100 µM; Cat#Y0000517, Merck KGaA, Germany) for 2 h to scavenge intracellular and extracellular ROS. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs (equivalent Alpelisib concentration: 24 µM) for 6 h. Following three washes with PBS, intracellular Cy5 fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX63, Japan) to assess NP uptake and drug release efficiency. Quantitative analysis was further performed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX LX, Beckman Coulter, USA) using the red fluorescence channel (excitation 640 nm, emission 675 nm).

To further confirm drug release behavior after GSH pretreatment, culture supernatants were collected for dialysis analysis. Samples were loaded into dialysis bags and immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C with gentle shaking (100 rpm). Dialysates were collected at predetermined time points, and Alpelisib concentrations were measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Agilent 1260 Infinity II, C18 column, detection at 240 nm). Cumulative release curves were plotted to evaluate the effect of GSH on ROS-responsive drug release.

VEGF secretion analysis (Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA))

After experimental treatments, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove debris. The clarified supernatants were used to quantify human VEGF secretion using a human VEGF ELISA kit (Cat#DVE00, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Standards and samples were assayed in duplicate. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA), and VEGF concentrations were calculated from the standard curve and expressed as pg/mL.

Co-Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry (Co-IP/MS) for NRP1-interacting proteins

When HUVECs reached approximately 80% confluence, cells were washed with PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer (Cat#P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Cat#88803, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were incubated with anti-NRP1 antibody (ab81321, Abcam, UK; 1–5 µg) at 4 °C for 2 h with rotation to form antibody–bead complexes, which were then incubated with cell lysates overnight at 4 °C.

The following day, immune complexes were washed, separated by SDS-PAGE, and visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining (Cat#P0017, Beyotime). Protein bands were excised, digested overnight with trypsin (Cat#V5113, Promega, USA), desalted using C18 spin columns (Cat#84840, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), dried in a SpeedVac, and reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid.

Peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Eksigent nanoLC 415 system coupled to a TripleTOF® 5600 + mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA). Raw data were processed with MaxQuant (v1.5.2.8) against the UniProt human database (release 2023_02), and high-confidence NRP1-interacting proteins were identified using a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Co-IP of NRP1-sema3A interaction

HUVECs were cultured under hypoxic conditions (2% O2) and treated with 17β-estradiol (10⁻7 mol/L; Cat#E2758, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 48 h. Cells were then harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime). The lysates were incubated overnight with antibodies against NRP1 (ab81321, Abcam), Sema3A (ab199475, Abcam), or rabbit IgG isotype control (Cat#3900S, CST, USA). On the following day, Protein G agarose beads (Cat#20399, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were added and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h.

The immune complexes were washed and analyzed by WB using primary antibodies diluted 1:1000. To assess the competitive binding effect of TAT-AT7, Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were added during the co-incubation step for 4 h, after which lysates were processed using the same Co-IP procedure.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

A lentiviral vector for NRP1 overexpression (GeneChem, China) was administered to EMS model rats via tail vein injection (1 × 108 TU/rat). After 20 days, ectopic endometrial tissues were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat#P1110, Solarbio, China). Tissue sections were permeabilized with CHAPSO, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide.

Sections were incubated overnight with rabbit anti-NRP1 antibody (ab81321, Abcam; 1:100) and mouse anti-Sema3A antibody (sc-74554, Santa Cruz, USA; 1:100). A Duolink® PLA kit (Cat#DUO92101, Sigma-Aldrich) was used for probe ligation and signal amplification. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescent signals were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX63, Japan). The number of PLA signal dots was quantified using ImageJ software.

WB analysis of PI3K/AKT pathway proteins

Total protein was extracted from tissues or cells using RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF (Cat#P0013C, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat#23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were mixed with 2× SDS loading buffer, boiled at 100 °C for 5 min, and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore), which were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies (Table S2). After three washes with TBST (10 min each), membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (ab97051, 1:2000, Abcam, UK) or rabbit anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (ab6728, 1:2000, Abcam, UK). After additional TBST washes, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Cat#abs920, Absin, Shanghai, China). Images were captured with a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA), and band intensities were quantified using Quantity One software (v4.6.2). Relative protein expression was normalized to GAPDH. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and mean values were used for analysis.

Molecular docking

The three-dimensional (3D) structures of Sema3A (PDB ID: 1Q47) and NRP-1 (PDB ID: 6TKK) were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking was performed using the ZDOCK server (https://zdock.umassmed.edu/) to investigate the binding modes among TAT-AT7, Sema3A, and NRP-1. From the docking results, the top 10 ranked complexes were selected, and the most stable complex was chosen for further analysis. The protein–peptide interactions within the complex were visualized and analyzed using the PyMOL molecular graphics system (https://pymol.org/).

Blocking ELISA

A blocking ELISA assay was established and optimized using a checkerboard titration method to evaluate the binding between NRP-1 and Sema3A, as well as the competitive inhibitory effects of TAT-AT7, TAT-AT7-NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs on this interaction. A 96-well flat-bottom ELISA plate (Cat. No. 3455, Thermo Fisher, USA) was coated overnight at 4 °C with 100 µL NRP-1 antibody (final concentration: 10 µg/mL) in 0.1 M carbonate–bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6; Cat. No. 88975, Merck, USA). The plate was washed three times with PBST (Cat. No. P3563, Merck, USA) and then blocked with 100 µL of 5% skim milk (Cat. No. P0216, Beyotime, China) at 37 °C for 1.5 h. After washing three times with PBST, the following solutions were added: 100 µL of 4 nM Sema3A antibody, 100 µL of 4 nM Sema3A antibody + 50 nM TAT-AT7, 100 µL of 4 nM Sema3A antibody + 50 nM TAT-AT7-NPs, and 100 µL of 4 nM Sema3A antibody + 50 nM Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing three times with PBST, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (diluted 1:4000 in PBS; Cat. No. A0216, Beyotime, China) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The plate was then washed three times with PBST, followed by the addition of 100 µL TMB substrate (Cat. No. ST746, Beyotime, China) and incubation at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 µL TMB stop solution (Cat. No. P0215, Beyotime, China). The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis of protein interaction affinity

The binding affinity between Sema3A and NRP1, as well as the competitive binding effects of TAT-AT7 and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, was evaluated using a Biacore T200 system (GE Healthcare, USA). A CM5 sensor chip (Cat#BR100530, GE Healthcare) was activated using the standard amine coupling method with a mixture of 0.2 M 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and 0.05 M N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS). Recombinant NRP1 protein (Cat#10084-H02H, R&D Systems), diluted in 10 mM acetate buffer (pH 5.0), was immobilized on the chip surface to approximately 1000 resonance units (RU), and remaining active sites were blocked with 1 M ethanolamine (pH 8.5).

In the first set of experiments, PBS-T (0.05% Tween-20) was used as the running buffer. Sema3A protein (Cat#1250-S3-025, R&D Systems) was diluted in HBS-EP buffer (Cat#BR100669, GE Healthcare) to final concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 nM and injected at a flow rate of 30 µL/min. Association and dissociation responses were recorded in real time, and kinetic parameters were analyzed using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software (v3.1). The apparent dissociation constant (KD) was then calculated.

In the second set of experiments, the competitive inhibition of Sema3A–NRP1 binding by TAT-AT7 or Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs was assessed. Under constant NRP1 immobilization and Sema3A concentration, serial dilutions of TAT-AT7 peptide (16, 64, 256, and 1000 nM; GenScript, China) or self-prepared Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were premixed with Sema3A and co-injected under the same conditions. Decreases in RU values were used to evaluate inhibitory effects on Sema3A–NRP1 binding. All assays were performed at 25 °C, and the chip surface was regenerated between runs using 10 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.5). Each experiment was repeated three times.

RT-qPCR analysis of gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent (Cat#15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA concentration and purity were measured at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop LITE spectrophotometer (ND-LITE-PR, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Cat#RR047Q, TaKaRa, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Cat#4351106, Thermo Fisher, USA) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 34 s. GAPDH was used as an internal control. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All RT-qPCR experiments were performed in triplicate (Table S3).

Synthesis of CA-HPAP-αCD and construction of NPs

All chemical reagents used for the synthesis of the CA-HPAP-αCD polymer were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). CA-HPAP-αCD was synthesized according to a previously reported method [28]. Briefly, 3.5821 g of aldehyde-modified 3-carboxybenzaldehyde (3-CA, lot#232793) and 1.6293 g of α-cyclodextrin (α-CD, lot#28705) were dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, lot#472301), followed by the addition of 0.2522 g pyridin-1-ium 4-methylbenzenesulfonate (PPT, lot#82815) as a catalyst. After three cycles of vacuum/argon degassing, the mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 48 h to complete the acetalization reaction. The product was then dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO 3500 Da, PURG35020) for 48 h to remove small-molecule impurities, yielding the CA-αCD intermediate. Next, 0.9438 g of CA-αCD was dissolved in anhydrous DMSO, and 1.1211 g of carbonyldiimidazole (CDI, lot#115533) was added to activate hydroxyethyl phosphate (HPAP, lot#702927). After three cycles of vacuum/argon degassing, the reaction mixture was stirred at 30 °C for 72 h to obtain the crude CA-HPAP-αCD product at a molar ratio of 1:1:1. The crude product was further purified by dialysis against deionized water (MWCO 3500 Da, PURG35020) for 48 h and freeze-dried to yield CA-HPAP-αCD as a white powder for subsequent NP preparation.

Alp@NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were prepared by a nanoprecipitation/self-assembly method [28]. Briefly, lecithin (6.0 mg, Avanti) and DSPE-PEG2000 (6.0 mg, Avanti) were dissolved in 400 µL anhydrous ethanol and 7.0 mL deionized water, followed by ultrasonication to form an emulsion. The dispersion was stirred at 65 °C and 200 rpm for 30 min to obtain a stable surfactant system.

For drug loading, 10.0 mg Alpelisib and 50.0 mg CA-HPAP-αCD were dissolved in a mixture of 400 µL methanol and 200 µL DMSO to form a clear organic solution. This solution was added dropwise to the preheated aqueous dispersion at 1.0 mL/min, followed by vortexing at 800 rpm for 3 min to facilitate interfacial self-assembly and nucleation. The mixture was then maintained at room temperature for 3 h. The resulting NPs were collected by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 10 min, washed three times with ultrapure water to remove residual solvent and unreacted components, and resuspended in deionized water to obtain Alp@NPs.

The TAT-AT7 peptide (RKKRRQRRRCATWLPPR) was synthesized by China Peptides Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PEI-PEG2000-TAT-AT7 was synthesized as previously reported [29]. Briefly, TAT-AT7 and MAL-PEG-NHS (MW = 2 kDa; Cat#A5003-1, Jenkem Technology, Beijing, China) were dissolved in DMSO at a molar ratio of 4:3 and stirred at room temperature for 12 h under nitrogen. Polyethylenimine (PEI, MW = 25 kDa; Cat#408727, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in PBS (0.1 mM, pH 7.0), after which a fourfold molar excess of PEG2000-TAT-AT7 in DMSO was added. The mixture was stirred overnight under nitrogen, dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO 10 kDa; Cat#88404, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and lyophilized to obtain PEI-PEG2000-TAT-AT7. For surface modification, PEI-PEG2000-TAT-AT7 was conjugated to preformed Alp@NPs to generate vascular-targeting Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs. Briefly, EDC/NHS activation was used to couple PEI-PEG2000-TAT-AT7 to surface carboxyl groups under gentle shaking in PBS (pH 7.4) at 4 °C in the dark for 12 h. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove unreacted components, yielding the final Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs.

For intracellular tracking, Cy5 fluorophores were covalently conjugated to the CA-HPAP-αCD copolymer during NP preparation using an active ester method. Briefly, carboxyl groups were activated with EDC/NHS in anhydrous DMSO and then reacted with Cy5-NH2. After 12 h of reaction in the dark, free dye was removed by ultrafiltration (MWCO 10 kDa), and purified Cy5-labeled NPs were obtained. The resulting Cy5@Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were used for subsequent cellular and in vivo imaging experiments.

Particle size and morphology

The morphology of the NPs was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; H-7650, Hitachi, Japan). Particle size distribution, hydrodynamic diameter, and ζ-potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Nano-ZS90, Malvern, UK).

To evaluate colloidal stability, CA-HPAP-αCD NPs were incubated in PBS (pH 7.4), DMEM, or DMEM containing 10% FBS for 6–12 h. Changes in particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were recorded to assess dispersion stability under physiological conditions.

Drug loading (DL) and encapsulation efficiency (EE)

The EE and DL of Alpelisib in Alp@NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were determined by HPLC. Briefly, 20 µL of freshly prepared NP suspension was freeze-dried, and the resulting lyophilized powder was weighed, dissolved in 1 mL anhydrous methanol, and sonicated for 30 min to ensure complete dissolution. The solution was then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before HPLC analysis (Agilent 1260 Infinity II).

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Discovery C18 column (Supelco Inc., PA, USA) with methanol/0.01 M KH2PO4 buffer (45:55, v/v) as the mobile phase, a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, an injection volume of 10 µL, a detection wavelength of 275 nm, and a column temperature of 30 °C. The retention time of Alpelisib was approximately 6–8 min. Quantification was based on a standard calibration curve (R2 > 0.99).

DL and EE are calculated using the following formulas: DL(%)=Amount of Alpelisib in NPs/Weight of NPs×100%, EE(%)=Amount of Alpelisib in NPs/Total Alpelisib used in preparation ×100%.​.

pH/ROS responsiveness

The pH/ROS-responsive behavior of the NPs was evaluated under simulated pathological conditions. HPAP-αCD NPs, CA-αCD NPs, and CA-HPAP-αCD NPs were dispersed in PBS at pH 7.4, 6.0, or 5.0, with or without 1 mM H2O2, and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h. Changes in hydrodynamic size were monitored by DLS (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS) to assess NP stability and disassembly. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments.

For in vitro drug release studies, Alp@NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were loaded into dialysis bags (MWCO 3500 Da) and immersed in 10 mL release buffer under different conditions (pH 7.4 or pH 5.0, with or without 1 mM H2O2) at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm). At predetermined time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h), 1 mL of release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh buffer. Samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and analyzed by HPLC under the same chromatographic conditions described above. Cumulative drug release was calculated and plotted as release profiles.

Hemocompatibility test

The hemocompatibility of the NPs was assessed using rat erythrocytes in accordance with ISO/TR 7406 standards. Fresh anticoagulated whole blood was collected from SD rats and centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min to remove plasma. Red blood cells (RBCs) were washed three times with PBS and resuspended to a final concentration of 2%. Equal volumes of RBC suspension and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs at different concentrations (50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1000 µg/mL) were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Deionized water and PBS were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). The hemolysis ratio was calculated as follows:

Hemolysis ratio (%) = (ODsample − ODnegative control) / (ODpositive control − ODnegative control) × 100%.

In vitro safety evaluation of NPs

The cytotoxicity of Alp@NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs toward HUVECs was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. HUVECs in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at 8,000 cells/well and allowed to attach for 12 h. The culture medium was then replaced with serum-free medium, and cells were treated with Alp@NPs or Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs at concentrations equivalent to 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 µM Alpelisib. After incubation for 24–48 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Cat#CK04, Dojindo, Kyushu, Japan) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA).

For apoptosis analysis, HUVECs were seeded into six-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured until attachment. Cells were assigned to the following groups: control, free Alpelisib (48 µM), blank NPs, Alp@NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs. All treatment groups were exposed to equivalent drug concentrations for 24 h. Cells were then harvested by trypsinization, washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4), centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer. Apoptosis was detected using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining (BD Biosciences), followed by incubation in the dark for 10 min. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II), and apoptosis rates were quantified using FlowJo software (v10.0).

Cellular uptake assay

To investigate the uptake of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs by HUVECs, Cy5-labeled NPs were incubated with cells at concentrations equivalent to 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 µM Alpelisib for 6 h. After three washes with PBS, nuclei were stained with DAPI, and intracellular Cy5 fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope (BX63, Olympus, Japan). Uptake efficiency was further quantified by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX LX, Beckman Coulter, USA) based on red fluorescence intensity, and uptake–concentration curves were generated.

To investigate the selective internalization of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs in NRP-1⁺ endothelial cells, HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight. The following groups were established: Control group (complete medium under normal culture conditions), Model group (2% O2 hypoxia with 10− 7 mol/L 17β-estradiol), Model + TAT-AT7 group (20 µM TAT-AT7), Model + anti-NRP-1 antibody group (anti-NRP-1 antibody, 1:1000), and Model + chloroquine group (100 µM CQ, HY-17589 A, MCE). Except for the Control group, all groups were subjected to modeling treatment for 24 h. Subsequently, the corresponding reagents were added to each group and incubated for 30 min, followed by the addition of Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs (equivalent Alpelisib concentration: 12 µM) for further co-incubation with HUVECs for 1 h. Cellular uptake was quantified by measuring red fluorescence intensity using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX LX, Beckman Coulter).

To determine time-dependent uptake, HUVECs were incubated with Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs (12 µM equivalent Alpelisib) for 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. After washing with PBS, intracellular fluorescence was imaged by fluorescence microscopy and quantified by flow cytometry.

To explore intracellular trafficking, HUVECs incubated with Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs for different time periods were stained with LysoTracker Green DND-26 (1:1000; Cat#C1045, Beyotime) for 30 min in the dark, washed with PBS, and counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (SP8, Leica Microsystems, Germany) in the DAPI (blue), LysoTracker (green), and Cy5 (red) channels. Colocalization of red and green fluorescence was analyzed using ImageJ software with Pearson’s correlation coefficient to assess lysosomal involvement in NP delivery. For comparison, Cy5-labeled HPAP-αCD NPs and CA-αCD NPs were prepared as single-responsive controls and evaluated under the same conditions by confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

In vivo treatment with NPs

On day 7, the successful establishment of ectopic lesions was confirmed by visual inspection and palpation of the subcutaneous nodules, after which the animals were assigned to the following experimental groups (n = 6/group): PBS group: EMS model + PBS (tail vein injection); EMS + TAT-AT7 group: EMS model + TAT-AT7 (28.3 µM/kg, tail vein injection); EMS + Alp group: EMS model + free Alpelisib (Selleck, Cat. No. S2814, 28.3 µM/kg, tail vein injection); EMS + NPs group: EMS model + empty NPs (28.3 µM/kg, tail vein injection); EMS + Alp@NPs group: EMS model + Alp-loaded NPs (Alp@NPs, 28.3 µM/kg, tail vein injection); EMS + Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs group: EMS model + TAT-AT7-modified Alp-loaded NPs (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, 28.3 µM/kg, tail vein injection); and EMS + Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs + NAC group: EMS model + TAT-AT7-modified Alp-loaded NPs plus NAC (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, 28.3 µM/kg, tail vein injection; N-acetylcysteine, MCE, Cat. No. HY-B0215, 150 mg/kg, intragastric administration, once daily), which was included to verify the mechanism through ROS scavenging. The injection dose was selected based on a previous study [21], and the tail vein injection volume was 100 µL per rat. All animals were treated continuously for 2 weeks. Except for NAC, which was administered once daily, all other treatments were given once every 2 days. Warm stimulation was applied before and during injection to facilitate tail vein dilation and improve injection success.

Pharmacokinetics of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs

Healthy male SD rats (180–220 g) were randomly assigned to groups (n = 3 per group) and received a single tail vein injection of free Alpelisib (12.5 mg/kg), Alp@NPs (28.3 µM/kg), or Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs (28.3 µM/kg). Blood samples were collected from the tail vein at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h after injection. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min. Plasma concentrations of free Alpelisib were quantified by LC-MS/MS using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system coupled to a QTRAP 6500 + mass spectrometer (SCIEX), with internal standard calibration.

In vivo safety evaluation of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs

The biosafety of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs was evaluated by histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney. Briefly, organs were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E (Cat#G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Tissue morphology was examined under an upright optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci, Nikon, Japan), with particular attention to edema, necrosis, hemorrhage, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Serum biochemical markers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT, Cat#JN20465), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, Cat#JN20681), blood urea nitrogen (BUN, Cat#JN55961), and creatinine (CREA, Cat#JN7806), were measured using commercial kits (Jining, Shanghai, China) [27].

In vivo and In vitro fluorescence imaging

Small-animal fluorescence imaging in this study was performed to evaluate the biodistribution and lesion-targeting ability of Cy5-labeled nanoparticles, not to visualize unlabeled ectopic endometrial fragments during model establishment. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 8, 24, and 48 h after injection using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system with excitation/emission wavelengths of 640/680 nm under standardized exposure settings. Fluorescence intensity in the lesion regions was analyzed to assess NP accumulation. At 48 h post-injection, animals were sacrificed, and major organs (liver, lung, spleen, kidney, and heart), as well as the uterus, ovary, and ectopic endometriotic lesions (EcEM), were collected for ex vivo imaging. Images were acquired under identical exposure conditions, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using the instrument’s analysis software. Semi-quantitative biodistribution was expressed as fluorescence intensity normalized to tissue wet weight (FI/g), allowing comparison of NP accumulation in target tissues and non-target organs.

Measurement of lesion volume and weight

After anesthesia, rats were sacrificed and ectopic lesions were excised. The longest diameter (L) and shortest diameter (W) of each lesion were measured with calipers, and lesion volume was calculated using the formula:

V = (L × W2)/2 mm3. Lesions were then weighed and photographed. Differences in lesion volume and weight among groups were used to evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

IHC for CD34 and VEGF

Ectopic lesion tissues were collected, rinsed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Samples were then dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness for immunohistochemical analysis.

Paraffin sections were baked at 65 °C for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by natural cooling to room temperature and three washes with PBS. Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD34 (ab81289, Abcam, UK; 1:100) and VEGF (ab52917, Abcam, UK; 1:200). The following day, sections were brought to room temperature, washed with PBS, and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab205718, Abcam, UK; 1:1000) for 30 min. After washing, color development was performed using a DAB substrate kit (Zsbio, Cat#ZLI-9018). Sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin.

Images were captured using an upright optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci, Nikon, Japan), with 3–5 random fields analyzed per section. ImageJ software (NIH, USA) was used for quantitative analysis with a uniform grayscale threshold. Integrated optical density (IOD) and the percentage of positive staining area were calculated for CD34 and VEGF. Microvessel density was determined by manually counting CD34-positive vascular structures per field, and the mean value was used for analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

To assess NRP1 expression in ectopic lesions and the accumulation of TAT-AT7-modified NPs in neovessels, immunofluorescence staining and colocalization analyses were performed. For NRP1 detection, ectopic lesion tissues were collected from EMS rats under isoflurane anesthesia, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT compound (Cat#45345, Merck KGaA, Germany), and sectioned at 30 μm using a cryostat (CM1950, Leica Microsystems, Germany) at − 20 °C.

After PBS washing and blocking, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-NRP1 antibody (ab81321, Abcam, UK; 1:250) at 4 °C for 12 h. Following PBS washes, Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab150080, Abcam, UK; 1:1000) was applied for 2 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime, China) for 10 min. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (SP8, Leica Microsystems, Germany), and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

For NP accumulation analysis, EMS rats were intravenously injected with Cy5-labeled Alp@NPs or Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs. After 48 h, ectopic lesion tissues were harvested, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm. Following deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-CD31 antibody (ab28364, Abcam, UK; 1:100), followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab150081, Abcam, UK; 1:1000) for 2 h in the dark. After DAPI counterstaining, images were captured by confocal microscopy to detect DAPI (blue), CD31 (green), and Cy5 (red) signals. Colocalization of NPs with neovessels was analyzed using ImageJ. CD31 and Cy5 double-labeled images were acquired by sequential channel scanning to avoid fluorophore bleed-through. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) between CD31 and Cy5 signals was calculated using the Coloc2 plugin in ImageJ [30]. Background subtraction was performed using the rolling-ball algorithm with identical parameters for all images, and regions of interest were manually selected for analysis.

For ROS detection, dihydroethidium (DHE) staining was performed on fresh uterine cryosections (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software to evaluate ROS accumulation.

Biochemical assays

Ectopic lesion tissues, aortic blood, and peritoneal lavage fluid were collected and centrifuged at 4 °C to obtain supernatants for biochemical analysis. Commercial assay kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were used on day 20 post-treatment to measure oxidative stress and inflammatory markers, including ROS (Cat#S0033M), SOD (Cat#S0109), CAT (Cat#S0051), GSH (Cat#S0052), MDA (Cat#S0131S), IL-6 (Cat#PI326), and TNF-α (Cat#PT512). Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.1) in the RStudio environment and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA). Data were first tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using independent-samples t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Repeated-measures analysis was used for data collected at multiple time points.

Results

NRP1 knockdown alleviates hypoxia- and estrogen-induced oxidative stress and pro-angiogenic behaviors in endothelial cells

In this study, ectopic lesion tissues were collected from both Control and EMS rat models for bulk transcriptome and scRNA-seq, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. Transcriptomic analysis identified 136 genes that were upregulated in EMS lesions. By intersecting these with the top 1000 angiogenesis-related functional genes retrieved from the GeneCards database, five candidate genes were screened (Figure S1A). The Gene Ontology/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (GO/KEGG) enrichment analysis results (Table S4) revealed that these genes were enriched in biological processes (BP) related to endothelial cell migration, localized in cellular components (CC) such as membrane microdomains, and involved in molecular functions (MF) including adhesion molecule binding and tyrosine kinase activity (Figure S1B). Moreover, KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in the classical PI3K-Akt and VEGF signaling pathways (Figure S1C).

Integration of scRNA-seq data by t-SNE identified six major cell populations, including epithelial, stromal, endothelial, pericyte, lymphoid, and myeloid cells (Fig. 1B), among which stromal, endothelial, and myeloid cells were predominant (Figure S1E). Differential expression analysis showed that endothelial cells specifically expressed genes such as Esam, Emcn, and Fam167b, which are closely related to vascular development and barrier remodeling (Fig. 1C, Figure S1D). Primary UAECs were isolated from sham and EMS model rats. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that NRP1 expression was significantly elevated in UAECs from the EMS group (Figure S1F). Consistently, scRNA-seq results confirmed that NRP1 was predominantly enriched and highly expressed in endothelial cells (Fig. 1D-E), underscoring its pivotal role in vascular remodeling within EMS lesions. Furthermore, immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR analyses validated that NRP1 expression was markedly upregulated in EMS lesions (Fig. 1F-G).

Because hypoxia is closely associated with EMS and contributes to abnormal angiogenesis, HUVECs were exposed to different concentrations of 17β-estradiol (0, 10⁻8, and 10⁻7 mol/L) under hypoxic conditions (2% O2) for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h to examine NRP1 expression. Compared with normoxia, hypoxia significantly increased NRP1 expression at 24 and 48 h, although this effect declined at 72 h (Figure S2A). High-dose estrogen further enhanced NRP1 expression, and the combination of hypoxia and estrogen produced the strongest induction (Figure S2B).

To further define the role of NRP1, lentiviral knockdown was performed in HUVECs (Figure S2C) under EMS-like conditions. Cells were divided into four groups: Control, Model, Model+shNC, and Model+shNRP1. CCK-8 and EdU assays showed that cell proliferation was significantly increased in the Model group compared with the Control group, whereas NRP1 knockdown markedly suppressed this increase (Figure S3A-B). Scratch wound and Transwell assays demonstrated enhanced migration in the Model group, which was substantially reduced after NRP1 knockdown (Figure S3C-D), indicating that NRP1 facilitates endothelial cell migration under hypoxia.

Regarding angiogenic capacity, VEGF levels in culture supernatants were elevated in the Model group but decreased following NRP1 knockdown (Fig. 1H). Matrigel tube formation assays revealed that the Model group formed more extensive capillary-like structures, whereas NRP1 knockdown reduced branching points and impaired network integrity (Fig. 1I). For oxidative stress, DCFH-DA staining demonstrated significantly elevated ROS levels in the Model group, which were markedly decreased after NRP1 knockdown (Fig. 1J). Colorimetric assays further showed reduced GSH/GSSG ratios and decreased SOD and CAT activity in the Model group, whereas NRP1 knockdown partially restored these antioxidant parameters (Fig. 1K-M).

Thus, these findings indicate that hypoxia and estrogen stimulation upregulate NRP1, which promotes HUVEC proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis while aggravating oxidative stress. Knockdown of NRP1 effectively alleviates these pathological processes.

NRP1 knockdown attenuates lesional angiogenesis and oxidative stress while improving the local inflammatory microenvironment in EMS model rats

To further verify the functional role of NRP1 in the pathogenesis of EMS, EMS model rats were established and subjected to local intralesional injection of shNRP1 lentivirus. The overall in vivo experimental workflow, including modeling, intervention, and detection time points, is illustrated in Fig. 2A. Fourteen days after intervention, lesion volume was evaluated. Compared with the sham group, lesion volume was significantly increased in the EMS group, and shNC treatment had no obvious effect on lesion growth. In contrast, lesion volume was markedly reduced in the EMS + shNRP1 group (Fig. 2B), indicating that NRP1 knockdown effectively suppresses ectopic lesion growth in vivo.

At the angiogenesis level, immunohistochemical staining revealed that CD34 and VEGF were highly expressed in lesion tissues from both the EMS and EMS+shNC groups, suggesting active neovascularization. In the shNRP1-treated group, expression of these markers was significantly decreased, accompanied by a marked reduction in vascular density (Fig. 2C). Consistently, WB analysis confirmed that shNRP1 effectively downregulated NRP1 protein expression in lesion tissues, demonstrating efficient knockdown (Fig. 2D).

Oxidative stress-related indices, including ROS, MDA, SOD, GSH, and CAT, were further evaluated in lesion tissues, serum, and peritoneal fluid. ROS staining showed that ROS levels were significantly elevated in lesion tissues from the EMS group compared with the sham group, whereas this increase was clearly attenuated in the EMS + shNRP1 group relative to the EMS + shNC group (Fig. 2E). In parallel, antioxidant enzyme analysis revealed that SOD and CAT levels were reduced in the EMS group but were markedly restored after shNRP1 treatment (Fig. 2F), suggesting improved redox balance within the lesions. Similarly, biochemical assays of serum and peritoneal fluid showed that EMS induced a significant increase in MDA and a decrease in GSH, whereas NRP1 knockdown reversed these changes, as evidenced by reduced MDA and elevated GSH levels (Fig. 2G-H). To further assess inflammatory status, IL-6 and TNF-α levels in serum and peritoneal fluid were measured by ELISA. Both cytokines were markedly increased in the EMS and EMS + shNC groups, whereas shNRP1 treatment significantly reduced their levels (Fig. 2I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NRP1 knockdown suppresses angiogenesis and oxidative stress in EMS lesions, alleviates inflammatory responses, and improves the local pathological microenvironment.

Interaction between sema3A and NRP1 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to promote oxidative stress and angiogenesis in endothelial cells

To investigate the potential mechanisms by which NRP1 regulates angiogenesis and redox homeostasis, we performed Co-IP combined with MS to screen for its downstream interacting molecules (Fig. 3A). Among the candidates, Sema3A showed the strongest interaction with NRP1 (Fig. 3B). Previous studies have identified NRP1 as a membrane receptor for Sema3A and demonstrated its role in angiogenesis regulation [31, 32]. In our study, Co-IP assays showed that combined hypoxia and estrogen stimulation enhanced the interaction between Sema3A and NRP1 in HUVECs (Fig. 3C). PLA further confirmed that this interaction was elevated in EMS lesions and was further amplified following NRP1 overexpression (Fig. 3D).

Transcriptomic analysis of EMS lesions suggested that angiogenesis-related genes were enriched in the PI3K/AKT pathway (Figure S1C), consistent with previous reports showing that NRP1 promotes angiogenesis via PI3K/AKT signaling [13]. We examined PI3K/AKT activity in HUVECs. Lentiviral overexpression of Sema3A significantly increased the levels of phosphorylated PI3K and AKT, and this effect was further enhanced under combined hypoxia and estrogen stimulation (Figure S4A). In contrast, Sema3A knockdown suppressed hypoxia- and estrogen-induced activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway (Figure S4B).

To determine whether this effect depended on NRP1, HUVECs under hypoxia plus estrogen stimulation were subjected to Sema3A overexpression with or without NRP1 knockdown. Compared with the Model group, the levels of NRP1, Sema3A, p-PI3K, and p-AKT were all increased, and Sema3A overexpression further enhanced pathway activation (Model + oe-Sema3A + sh-NC). However, simultaneous NRP1 knockdown (Model + oe-Sema3A + sh-NRP1) markedly reduced p-PI3K and p-AKT levels, nearly restoring them to baseline model levels, indicating that Sema3A-mediated PI3K/AKT activation is largely dependent on NRP1 (Fig. 3E).

Because the PI3Kα inhibitor Alpelisib has been reported to exert anti-angiogenic and anti-proliferative effects in EMS and other diseases [33–36], we further examined whether PI3K signaling mediates Sema3A-induced angiogenesis and oxidative stress. HUVECs were assigned to the oe-NC, oe-Sema3A, oe-Sema3A + sh-NRP1, and oe-Sema3A + Alp groups. Tube formation assays showed that Sema3A overexpression significantly promoted capillary-like network formation, as reflected by increased branching points and tube length (Fig. 3F). These effects were markedly attenuated by either Alpelisib treatment or NRP1 knockdown, supporting the involvement of the NRP1/PI3K axis.

Oxidative stress assays further demonstrated that Sema3A overexpression significantly elevated ROS levels (Fig. 3G), reduced the GSH/GSSG ratio, and decreased SOD and CAT activities (Fig. 3H-J), indicating impaired antioxidant capacity. Both Alpelisib treatment and NRP1 knockdown markedly reduced ROS accumulation, increased the GSH/GSSG ratio, and restored SOD and CAT activities, thereby improving the redox state of endothelial cells (Fig. 3G-J). On the basis of Sema3A overexpression and NRP1 knockdown, IGF-1 treatment reversed the changes in ROS levels, the GSH/GSSG ratio, and antioxidant enzyme activities (Fig. 3G-J).

Taken together, these results indicate that Sema3A activates PI3K/AKT signaling through NRP1, thereby promoting angiogenesis and oxidative stress in endothelial cells. Importantly, Alpelisib effectively reverses these pathological effects, supporting the therapeutic relevance of targeting the NRP1/Sema3A/PI3K/AKT axis in EMS.

Preparation of pH/ROS dual-responsive NPs modified with vascular-targeting peptide AT7 for Alpelisib loading

Given the acidic and oxidative stress microenvironment of endometriotic lesions, intelligent responsive nanomaterials have recently been widely explored for targeted therapy of EMS [37]. In this study, a dual pH/ROS-responsive nanoplatform was constructed using α-cyclodextrin (α-CD) modified with CA and HPAP. Briefly, CA was conjugated to α-CD through acetalization, followed by reaction with CDI-activated HPAP to generate CA-HPAP-αCD NPs (Figure S5A). In this design, HPAP conferred ROS responsiveness, whereas CA provided pH sensitivity. TEM revealed that HPAP NPs, CA-αCD NPs, and CA-HPAP-αCD NPs all exhibited uniform spherical morphology (Figure S5B). Their ζ-potentials were consistently negative (Figure S5C), and the hydrodynamic diameters ranged from 178 ± 2 to 184 ± 4 nm (Figure S5D-E), indicating favorable compatibility and stable morphology.

The hydrolytic behavior of the NPs was further examined in PBS containing 1 mM H2O2 under different pH conditions [38]. CA-αCD NPs underwent rapid hydrolysis under acidic conditions (pH 5.0 or 6.0) but showed minimal responsiveness to ROS (Fig. 4A). In contrast, HPAP NPs displayed accelerated hydrolysis in the presence of H2O2 regardless of pH (Fig. 4B). Notably, CA-HPAP-αCD NPs exhibited typical dual pH/ROS-responsive hydrolysis behavior (Fig. 4C). Moreover, after incubation in PBS, DMEM, or 10% FBS for 6–12 h, their particle sizes remained stable, demonstrating excellent in vitro stability (Figure S5F).

To clarify the vascular-targeting mechanism, SPR analysis demonstrated that TAT-AT7 had the highest binding affinity for NRP1 (KD = 2.14 × 10− 8 M), exceeding that of TAT or AT7 alone (Figure S6A), in agreement with previous findings [39]. Based on this result, Alpelisib was loaded into the dual-responsive nanomaterial by nanoprecipitation/self-assembly, followed by surface modification with PEI-PEG2000-TAT-AT7 to generate vascular-targeted NPs (Fig. 4D). Four NP formulations were prepared: HPAP-αCD NPs (NPs), TAT-AT7-HPAP-αCD NPs (TAT-AT7-NPs), Alpelisib-loaded HPAP-αCD NPs (Alp@NPs), and Alpelisib-loaded TAT-AT7-modified HPAP-αCD NPs (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs). TEM confirmed that all NPs exhibited uniform spherical morphology (Fig. 4E). DLS analysis showed consistent size distribution across groups (Fig. 4F), while ζ-potentials remained stable at approximately − 30 mV, indicating that TAT-AT7 modification and drug encapsulation did not alter the surface charge (Fig. 4G). The DL and EE of Alp@NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were 9.21 ± 0.20% vs. 9.00 ± 0.28% and 30.04 ± 2.54% vs. 27.50 ± 1.96%, respectively, demonstrating satisfactory encapsulation performance (Figure S6B-C).

Drug release studies showed that under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), cumulative release from Alp@NPs reached only about 40% after 500 min, indicating good stability. In contrast, acidic conditions (pH 5.0) or ROS stimulation significantly accelerated drug release (Fig. 4H), with the fastest release observed under combined pH/ROS conditions, leading to nearly complete release within 480 min. Notably, TAT-AT7 modification did not substantially alter the overall release profile but further enhanced ROS responsiveness (Fig. 4I). Thus, these findings demonstrate that the Alpelisib-loaded, TAT-AT7-modified NPs possess favorable structural stability, effective drug encapsulation, and robust pH/ROS dual responsiveness, supporting their suitability for subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies.

Targeted uptake, biocompatibility, and redox regulation of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs in endothelial cells

To assess NP cytotoxicity, CCK-8 assays were performed after treatment with different NP formulations. PEI-PEG2000-TAT-AT7, blank NPs, free Alpelisib, and Alp-loaded formulations at concentrations ranging from 3 to 48 µM showed no significant cytotoxicity in HUVECs. Notably, encapsulation of Alpelisib into NPs reduced the intrinsic cytotoxicity of the free drug, indicating improved safety. Cell viability remained stable after both 24 and 48 h of treatment (Figure S7A-B). Consistently, Annexin V/PI staining showed no significant differences in apoptosis among groups, further supporting the good biocompatibility of these NPs (Figure S7C).

To evaluate targeted cellular uptake, Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs were incubated with HUVECs under hypoxic (2% O2, 48 h) and estrogen-stimulated (10⁻7 mol/L) conditions. Confocal microscopy showed that intracellular Cy5 fluorescence increased progressively with NP concentration (Figure S8A). Flow cytometry analysis quantitatively confirmed that uptake was dose- and time-dependent (Figure S8B-D). Co-localization with LysoTracker demonstrated that the NPs were internalized predominantly through the lysosomal pathway (Figure S8C). Compared with single-responsive NPs (HPAP NPs and CA-αCD NPs), Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs displayed faster cellular uptake and drug release kinetics (Figure S8E-F), suggesting that TAT-AT7 modification enhances targeting efficiency, while dual pH/ROS responsiveness facilitates intracellular release.

To investigate the selective internalization of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs in NRP-1⁺ endothelial cells, HUVECs were treated under the following conditions: normoxic complete medium, hypoxia plus estrogen (2% O2 hypoxia with 10− 7 mol/L 17β-estradiol), Model + TAT-AT7 (20 µM TAT-AT7), and Model + anti-NRP-1 antibody (anti-NRP-1 antibody, 1:1000). Flow cytometric quantitative analysis showed that the hypoxia + estrogen environment significantly promoted the uptake of Cy5-Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs by HUVECs, whereas TAT-AT7 and the anti-NRP-1 antibody inhibited the uptake of Cy5-Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs under hypoxia + estrogen conditions (Figure S8G). To further confirm lysosome-dependent internalization, the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine was applied in the Model group. The results showed that chloroquine markedly suppressed the internalization of Cy5-Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs in HUVECs (Figure S8G).

Elevated ROS levels in the inflammatory microenvironment disrupt cellular functions and are closely associated with the progression of EMS [40, 41]. To further investigate the mechanism by which Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs regulate oxidative stress (Fig. 5A), we first assessed intracellular H2O2 levels. The Control group exhibited low H2O2 concentrations, whereas the Model group showed a significant increase. Both free Alpelisib and blank NPs markedly reduced intracellular H₂O₂, with Alp@NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs showing comparable effects (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that pH/ROS-responsive NPs effectively scavenge intracellular H2O2, thereby facilitating drug release and alleviating oxidative stress. Consistently, measurement of extracellular free Alpelisib showed that hypoxia plus estrogen markedly accelerated drug release from Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, whereas GSH pretreatment significantly suppressed this effect (Fig. 5C), confirming ROS-responsive drug release.

DCFH-DA probe staining showed that hypoxia for 24 h significantly elevated ROS levels (Fig. 5D), whereas Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs effectively reduced ROS accumulation, a result corroborated by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5E). These findings further validated the ROS-responsive behavior of the NPs.

To assess their ability to regulate pH homeostasis, intracellular pH was measured using the BBcellProbe® P01 sensor. Normal cells (pH 7.0–7.5) exhibited strong green fluorescence, whereas fluorescence intensity was reduced in hypoxia-treated cells. Treatment with blank NPs, Alp@NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly restored fluorescence intensity, indicating pH recovery, while free Alp failed to restore fluorescence (Fig. 5F). Notably, Alp@NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs achieved greater restoration due to the synergistic effects of drug release and material responsiveness, with Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs showing the most pronounced effect.

Together, these results demonstrate that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and targeted uptake, and can effectively respond to the acidic and ROS-enriched inflammatory microenvironment. This enables rapid drug release, significant ROS clearance, and restoration of cellular pH balance.

Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs inhibit sema3A-NRP1 binding and modulate downstream PI3K/AKT signaling

NRP1 lacks an intrinsic intracellular catalytic domain and functions as a co-receptor by forming complexes with ligands such as Sema3A and signal-transducing receptors, thereby indirectly mediating angiogenesis [42–45]. Based on this, we hypothesized that TAT-AT7 could competitively bind to NRP1, thereby preventing formation of the Sema3A–NRP1 complex and suppressing downstream PI3K/AKT activation.

To test this mechanism at the structural level, molecular docking was performed to analyze the binding modes of Sema3A, TAT-AT7, and NRP1. The results showed that Sema3A primarily bound to the b1 domain of NRP1 through its C-terminal basic motif, occupying the classical ligand-binding pocket and forming stable electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions with multiple key acidic residues of NRP1 (Figure S9A). TAT-AT7 also exhibited stable binding to the NRP1 b1 domain, and its binding site showed substantial spatial overlap with that of Sema3A. In addition, TAT-AT7 formed dense hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions with residues critical for Sema3A recognition (Figure S9B). Superposition of the two complexes further demonstrated marked overlap between TAT-AT7 and the C-terminal region of Sema3A within the NRP1 b1 pocket, suggesting that TAT-AT7 may competitively occupy the ligand-binding site and thereby block Sema3A–NRP1 interaction (Figure S9C). By contrast, docking analysis did not identify a stable binding conformation between TAT-AT7 and Sema3A, indicating that TAT-AT7 does not directly neutralize Sema3A but instead acts mainly by targeting NRP1 (Figure S9D). Consistently, blocking ELISA results demonstrated that TAT-AT7, TAT-AT7-NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs all competitively inhibited the binding of Sema3A to NRP-1, with TAT-AT7-NPs and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs showing stronger inhibitory effects than free TAT-AT7 (Fig. 6A).

SPR analysis further confirmed this competitive inhibition. Recombinant NRP1 was immobilized on the sensor chip, and increasing concentrations of Sema3A were injected sequentially. As expected, Sema3A bound to NRP1 in a concentration-dependent manner, as reflected by progressively increased RU (Fig. 6B). In contrast, co-incubation with TAT-AT7 or Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly reduced RU values, indicating effective blockade of Sema3A–NRP1 binding. Notably, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs showed stronger inhibition than free TAT-AT7 (Fig. 6C).

To further determine whether TAT-AT7-modified NPs interfered with Sema3A–NRP1 complex formation in cells, Co-IP assays were performed. In the untreated control group, Sema3A was readily detected in NRP1 immunoprecipitates, indicating a stable interaction between the two proteins. In contrast, this interaction was markedly weakened in the Alp@TAT-AT7-NP-treated group, as evidenced by reduced Sema3A signal in the immunoprecipitated complex (Fig. 6D). Importantly, total input levels of Sema3A and NRP1 remained unchanged, suggesting that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs disrupted the interaction itself rather than altering protein expression.

We next investigated the effects of TAT-AT7 and its drug-loaded formulation (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs) on the PI3K/AKT signaling. WB analysis showed that both TAT-AT7 and TAT-AT7-NPs significantly attenuated hypoxia- and estrogen-induced phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, with a stronger effect observed for TAT-AT7-NPs. However, neither treatment significantly affected total PI3K or AKT protein levels (Fig. 6E). Notably, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs produced the most pronounced inhibition of p-PI3K and p-AKT. Although Sema3A and NRP1 expression was markedly increased in the Model group compared with the normal control, their overall expression levels remained relatively stable after treatment with TAT-AT7, TAT-AT7-NPs, or Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs (Fig. 6E). These findings indicate that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs suppress PI3K/AKT activation in association with disruption of the Sema3A–NRP1 interaction, while the potential contribution of other NRP1-associated ligands, such as VEGFA and PDGFβ, cannot be excluded (Fig. 6F).

Cell proliferation assays showed that, compared with the Model group, free Alpelisib exerted only a modest inhibitory effect on cell viability and DNA synthesis. In contrast, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs markedly suppressed HUVEC proliferation, as evidenced by reduced CCK-8 activity and a pronounced decrease in EdU-positive cells, indicating strong anti-proliferative activity. NAC pretreatment partially attenuated this effect, although the overall inhibitory trend remained, suggesting that the antiproliferative activity of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs is at least partly dependent on ROS-responsive drug release (Fig. 7B-C).

This inhibitory effect was further confirmed by migration assays. Both Transwell and wound-healing analyses demonstrated that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly impaired HUVEC migration, as reflected by fewer migrated cells and reduced wound closure. Compared with free Alpelisib or unmodified NPs, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect. Although NAC pretreatment moderately weakened this effect, it did not reverse the overall trend (Fig. 7D-E).

Similarly, Matrigel tube formation assays showed that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs markedly reduced capillary-like structure formation, including both tube number and branching points, indicating potent anti-angiogenic activity. NAC pretreatment slightly diminished this effect, but the overall suppression of angiogenesis remained evident (Fig. 7F).

To determine whether these therapeutic effects were associated with the regulation of redox homeostasis, intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFH-DA. Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly reduced ROS fluorescence intensity. Consistently, biochemical assays showed an increased intracellular GSH/GSSG ratio and enhanced SOD and CAT activities after NP treatment (Fig. 7G-I). These improvements were partially attenuated by NAC pretreatment, suggesting that the anti-angiogenic and antioxidant effects of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs are closely linked to their ROS-responsive properties.

Our findings highlight that the TAT-AT7-modified Alpelisib nanoformulation not only effectively suppresses proliferation, migration, and tube formation of EMS-associated endothelial cells but also restores intracellular redox balance, thereby providing a critical mechanistic basis for its therapeutic potential.

Hemocompatibility and pharmacokinetic evaluation of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs

We further validated the in vivo biosafety and pharmacokinetics of the NPs. Before systemic administration, rat erythrocyte suspensions were used to assess hemocompatibility. According to the hemolysis index, biomaterials are generally classified as non-hemolytic (< 2%), slightly hemolytic (2–5%), or hemolytic (> 5%). Our results showed that even at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, the hemolysis rate of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs remained below 5% (Figure S10A), indicating acceptable blood compatibility and suitability for intravenous administration. To assess pharmacokinetics, rats were intravenously injected with either free Alpelisib or Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, and plasma Alpelisib concentrations were measured at multiple time points. Compared with free Alpelisib, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly prolonged the plasma elimination half-life (T1/2β) (T1/2β) (Figure S10B-C), demonstrating the advantage of NP-mediated delivery in reducing drug clearance and extending circulation time. In addition, the plasma concentration of unbound Alpelisib during the first 4 h after injection was markedly lower in the Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs group than in the free-drug group (Figure S10D). These findings indicate that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs can effectively reduce unnecessary free Alpelisib in peripheral circulation, potentially minimizing off-target pharmacological activity.

Systemic safety was further evaluated by H&E staining of major organs, which revealed no obvious histopathological abnormalities in any treatment group (Figure S11A). Consistently, serum biochemical parameters, including ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA, showed no significant changes (Figure S11B). Collectively, these results indicate that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs possess favorable systemic biosafety.

Taken together, these data suggest that to achieve equivalent Alpelisib concentrations in the brain, NP-mediated delivery may allow a reduction in systemic dosage compared with free drug administration, thereby minimizing peripheral side effects.

TAT-AT7-modified Alpelisib NPs accumulate in endometriotic lesions and synergistically regulate angiogenesis and the oxidative stress microenvironment

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of TAT-AT7-modified pH/ROS dual-responsive NPs in an EMS rat model (Fig. 8A), an experimental EMS model was established (Figure S12A). Cy5-labeled delivery systems were used for fluorescence imaging. At 8 h after injection, fluorescence signals were already detectable in ectopic lesions, and the Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs group exhibited stronger and more sustained accumulation at 24–48 h than the NPs and Alp@NPs groups (Figure S12B), indicating superior lesion enrichment and retention. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging at 48 h further showed that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs preferentially accumulated in lesion tissues, with significantly higher signal intensity than in the ovary and uterus and greater enrichment than the other formulations (Figure S12C). Semi-quantitative analysis revealed that the FI of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs in lesions was approximately twofold higher than that of NPs and 1.5-fold higher than that of Alp@NPs (Figure S12D). Confocal microscopy further localized Cy5-labeled Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs to CD31-positive vascular regions within lesions; Quantitative colocalization analysis showed that, compared with Alp@NPs, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs exhibited a higher PCC with CD31-positive vascular regions (Figure S12F), highlighting their distinct vascular-targeting capability (Figure S12E).

After 14 days of treatment, analysis of body weight and lesion volume showed that TAT-AT7, free Alpelisib, Alp@NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs all significantly inhibited lesion growth, with Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs producing the strongest therapeutic effect (Fig. 8B-C). Co-treatment with NAC partially restored lesion growth, suggesting that the efficacy of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs depends, at least in part, on ROS-responsive mechanisms. Immunohistochemical staining further showed that CD34 and VEGF expression was reduced by TAT-AT7, Alpelisib, Alp@NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, with the most pronounced suppression observed in the Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs group (Fig. 8D). WB analysis further confirmed that TAT-AT7, Alpelisib, Alp@NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs markedly suppressed phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, while NAC pretreatment failed to reverse the inhibitory effect of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs on the levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT (Fig. 8E).

In terms of redox homeostasis, Alpelisib, Alp@NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly reduced ROS levels in lesion tissues, increased SOD and CAT activities in serum and peritoneal fluid, decreased MDA levels, and elevated GSH content compared with the PBS group (Fig. 8F-G). Notably, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs exhibited the most pronounced effects, while TAT-AT7 treatment did not produce the above effect (Fig. 8H-I). Importantly, NAC pretreatment reversed these improvements, indicating that exogenous ROS scavenging attenuated the “high ROS-triggered release” mechanism underlying the therapeutic efficacy of the NPs.

Cytokine analysis further demonstrated that Alpelisib, Alp@NPs, and Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly reduced serum and peritoneal IL-6 and TNF-α levels, with Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs achieving superior anti-inflammatory effects. Again, NAC pretreatment abrogated these effects, suggesting that the anti-inflammatory activity of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs is closely dependent on ROS regulation, while TAT-AT7 treatment did not produce the above effect (Fig. 8J).

Taken together, these findings suggest that TAT-AT7 may exert therapeutic effects in EMS by inhibiting lesion angiogenesis through blockade of PI3K/AKT signaling. More importantly, the TAT-AT7-modified Alpelisib nanoplatform more effectively suppresses neovascularization by targeting the Sema3A–NRP1–PI3K/AKT axis while simultaneously restoring redox homeostasis and improving the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby markedly alleviating local EMS pathology.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an anti-NRP1 peptide-modified ROS/pH dual-responsive nanoparticle delivery system (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs) for the treatment of EMS. This platform showed favorable physicochemical properties and hemocompatibility, and enabled efficient drug release under acidic and ROS-enriched conditions, thereby allowing precise intervention in lesion-associated angiogenesis and redox imbalance. Compared with conventional free-drug administration, this strategy combines microenvironment-responsive release with molecular targeting, helping to overcome the limitations of nonspecific distribution and systemic toxicity. In addition to improving drug delivery efficiency, we systematically validated its therapeutic mechanisms, including disruption of NRP1-mediated signaling, inhibition of PI3K/AKT activation, reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and restoration of antioxidant capacity. Together, these findings support a therapeutic framework that integrates mechanistic validation with efficacy evaluation. More broadly, this work highlights the value of microenvironment-targeted intervention and offers a more comprehensive strategy than previous approaches focused solely on angiogenesis or oxidative stress.

Angiogenesis is a key driver of the persistent growth and invasive behavior of EMS lesions. Previous anti-angiogenic studies have mainly focused on VEGF and its receptors, and VEGF/VEGFR blockade has been widely explored as a therapeutic strategy [46–48]. However, single-pathway targeting of VEGF often leads to resistance and unsustained therapeutic effects [49]. This study focused on the Sema3A–NRP1 signaling pathway and found that Sema3A alone can bind to NRP1 and activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, whereas the combination of Sema3A with estrogen and hypoxic conditions further enhances its binding to NRP1 and the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. In addition, NRP1 is highly expressed in neovasculature within EMS lesions and plays a critical role in angiogenesis. Unlike prior VEGF-centric approaches, NRP1 represents a more specific and promising target. Our results show that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs, by virtue of anti-NRP1 modification, achieve precise vascular targeting of EMS lesions, significantly reduce CD34 and VEGF expression, and thereby suppress pathological angiogenesis. These findings validate NRP1 as a feasible therapeutic target and provide a novel strategy for the treatment of EMS. A clinical study [50] reported that serum NRP-1 levels in women with EMS (n = 120, 29.91 ± 2.87 µg/L) were significantly higher than those in the control group (n = 80, 9.40 ± 3.05 µg/L) (p < 0.001). These findings support the pathological relevance of NRP-1 in human EMS and further corroborate the clinical applicability of our results.

Oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of EMS [9, 51, 52]. Previous studies have primarily aimed to reduce ROS levels using exogenous antioxidants such as NAC, vitamin C, and vitamin E to alleviate microenvironmental imbalance. However, these approaches often suffer from poor targeting and inadequate local drug accumulation, which limits their therapeutic efficacy [53–55]. In the present study, we developed a ROS/pH dual-responsive nanoplatform that couples drug release to the pathological microenvironment, enabling efficient payload release under acidic and oxidative conditions. Our results showed that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs significantly decreased ROS and MDA levels while increasing SOD, CAT, and GSH activity, thereby restoring redox homeostasis. Compared with conventional antioxidant strategies, this system offers greater selectivity and more sustained effects. Importantly, we further demonstrated that the improvement in oxidative stress was accompanied by inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway, providing additional evidence for the close interplay among inflammation, angiogenesis, and oxidative stress in EMS.

Alpelisib, a selective PI3K inhibitor, has shown promising therapeutic effects in malignancies such as breast cancer [23]. Nevertheless, systemic administration of Alpelisib is frequently associated with adverse effects and insufficient drug retention in target tissues, which restricts its use in non-malignant diseases [56]. In this study, Alpelisib was loaded into ROS/pH dual-responsive NPs, which prolonged its plasma elimination half-life in rats and reduced residual Alpelisib levels in plasma after administration, thereby exhibiting improved release kinetics compared with free Alpelisib. In addition, TAT-AT7 modification conferred enhanced accumulation in ectopic lesion sites and vascular-targeting capability. Moreover, compared with free Alpelisib, Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs demonstrated superior efficacy in inhibiting NRP1, p-PI3K, and p-AKT signaling, reducing inflammatory cytokine levels, and decreasing lesion size. TAT-AT7-NPs exerted a stronger inhibitory effect on Akt and PI3K phosphorylation than free TAT-AT7, and encapsulation of Alpelisib within this nanoplatform further enhanced this inhibitory effect. These results suggest that both TAT-AT7 modification and NP encapsulation contribute to the enhanced inhibition, and that a potential synergistic effect may exist between TAT-AT7 and Alpelisib. These findings provide a new direction for the application of Alpelisib in gynecological diseases and highlight the significant clinical translational potential of this nanoplatform.

In recent years, nanodrug delivery systems have been extensively applied in gynecological diseases and cancer research. However, most systems rely solely on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect or a single pH-responsive mechanism, which often leads to insufficient targeting and limited release efficiency [57–59]. To illustrate this limitation, previous studies have shown that cisplatin-loaded lipid/polymer NPs (e.g., DDP-NLC, DDP-PNP) typically achieve cellular uptake rates of only 40% to 70% in cancer cell lines [60]. In stark contrast, our Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs demonstrated a superior uptake rate of approximately 90% in HUVECs, highlighting the efficacy of the TAT-AT7 peptide modification for active targeting. Furthermore, while recent advancements have introduced pH/ROS dual-responsive systems targeting NRP-1/CAIX for tumor therapy [61], our study uniquely tailors this dual-responsive strategy to the specific pathological microenvironment of EMS. By integrating ROS/pH dual-responsiveness with anti-NRP1 targeting, our system not only adapts to the complex inflammatory milieu of EMS lesions but also achieves precise localization to neovasculature—evident in our in vivo data showing stronger vascular co-localization compared to conventional formulations. Mechanistically, the unique “proton sponge effect” of PEI can induce lysosomal osmotic swelling, thereby facilitating the release of NPs from lysosomes [62] and ultimately protecting Alpelisib from degradation. However, PEI is generally considered cytotoxic, whereas anti-NRP1 peptides and PEG modification can shield the positive charges of PEI [29, 63], thereby reducing its cytotoxicity. The results of this study further showed that PEI-PEG2000-TAT-AT7 had minimal effects on the viability of HUVECs. Taken together, this “targeted modification + dual responsiveness” composite design surpasses previous single-mechanism nanoplatforms and provides a novel strategy for precision therapy of complex gynecological diseases.

In previous studies, antioxidants such as NAC have often been combined with conventional agents to improve oxidative stress in EMS. However, these approaches generally lack precise regulation of disease-related signaling pathways, which may limit their therapeutic benefit. In our study, co-administration of Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs with NAC led to more pronounced improvements in redox-related parameters, suggesting a synergistic effect between antioxidant intervention and pathway-targeted inhibition. This finding not only broadens the rationale for combination therapy in EMS but also provides experimental support for multi-target therapeutic strategies, highlighting the potential advantages of integrated interventions over single-agent treatment.

This study further elucidates the coupled mechanisms of angiogenesis and oxidative stress in EMS. By blocking the Sema3A-NRP1 interaction and inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, we achieved dual regulation of angiogenesis and oxidative stress. These findings provide new evidence for understanding the pathophysiology of EMS and highlight a future therapeutic paradigm that emphasizes “pathway-microenvironment dual targeting.” In addition to SEMA3A, NRP1 also functions as a co-receptor for multiple growth factors, including VEGFA and PDGFβ [64, 65]. Both VEGFA and PDGFβ have been implicated in the pathogenesis of EMS [66, 67]. Therefore, NRP1 knockdown may inhibit VEGF signaling and angiogenesis through mechanisms beyond SEMA3A-dependent pathways. The nanoplatform developed in this work integrates both targeting and controlled-release properties, demonstrating translational potential, particularly in reducing side effects and enhancing therapeutic efficacy, thereby offering a promising direction for precision treatment of complex gynecological diseases.

Despite promising potential, the clinical translation of nanomedicine remains constrained by challenges in efficacy validation, long-term safety, manufacturing consistency, and regulatory approval. Preclinical animal models often fail to fully replicate human vascular permeability, tumor microenvironments, and immune system heterogeneity, making it difficult to predict clinical outcomes [68–70]. Furthermore, concerns regarding the potential chronic toxicity and immunogenicity of nanomaterial accumulation, combined with the difficulty of maintaining quality control during large-scale production, pose significant hurdles [68, 71]. Current regulatory frameworks also lack specific standards for complex nanocarriers, increasing uncertainty in the approval process [72].

Reflecting these broader challenges, our study has certain limitations. First, our data is derived exclusively from animal models; thus, the pharmacokinetics, long-term safety, and immune responses in humans require further validation. Second, the inhibitory effect of TAT-AT7 on NRP1-mediated signaling cannot be attributed exclusively to blockade of the Sema3A–NRP1 axis. Although our docking, blocking ELISA, SPR, and Co-IP data support that TAT-AT7-modified NPs interfere with Sema3A–NRP1 complex formation, TAT-AT7 binds to the b1 domain of NRP1, a ligand-binding region that may also participate in interactions between NRP1 and other pro-angiogenic ligands, including VEGFA and PDGFβ. Because VEGFA and PDGFβ are important contributors to angiogenesis and lesion progression in EMS, we cannot exclude the possibility that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs partially exert their therapeutic effects through modulation of VEGFA–NRP1 and/or PDGFβ–NRP1 signaling. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as evidence that Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs regulate NRP1-centered angiogenic signaling with demonstrated inhibition of Sema3A–NRP1 interaction, rather than as proof of selective inhibition of Sema3A–NRP1 alone. Future studies using direct competitive binding assays, such as SPR or blocking ELISA for VEGFA–NRP1 and PDGFβ–NRP1, as well as ligand-specific rescue experiments, will be needed to define the relative contribution of these parallel pathways. Future research should aim to bridge these gaps by initiating clinical trials and expanding the application of this ROS/pH dual-responsive platform. Potential strategies include delivering other therapeutic agents for EMS, such as simvastatin or mifepristone [73, 74], or combining this system with immunotherapies like anti-PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [75]. Overall, despite these limitations, this study provides a novel strategy for the precision treatment of EMS and holds significant scientific and potential clinical value for managing refractory gynecological disorders.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully constructed and validated, for the first time, an anti-NRP1 peptide-modified, ROS/pH dual-responsive nanodrug delivery system (Alp@TAT-AT7-NPs) and applied it to the treatment of EMS. This system enables efficient accumulation and controlled drug release within the lesion microenvironment, and by specifically blocking the Sema3A-NRP1 interaction and downregulating the PI3K/AKT signaling axis, it effectively disrupts the vicious cycle between angiogenesis and oxidative stress, thereby significantly reducing lesion volume. From a scientific perspective, this study reveals the coupling mechanism between signaling pathways and the pathological microenvironment in EMS, and proposes a novel “dual-targeting” therapeutic strategy. From a clinical perspective, this nanoplatform exhibits advantages including high targeting efficiency and reduced side effects. Although multiple challenges remain for clinical translation, the present work provides important experimental evidence and a technical foundation for developing safer and more effective therapeutic strategies for refractory gynecological diseases.
