Empathy as risk and protective factor in the development of adolescent aggression; the role of autism unraveled
Authors: Carolien Rieffe, Charlotte Qiaochu Hu, Evelien Broekhof
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmpathy can strengthen interpersonal relationships, but the question is to what extent empathy also functions this way in autistic adolescents. Despite previous perceptions of lower empathic levels in autistic adolescents, recent studies have stated otherwise. The present study aimed to investigate the developmental trajectories of affective and cognitive empathy, as well as how they predicted the development of proactive and reactive aggression over time in autistic and non-autistic (allistic) adolescents. Eighty-two autistic (Mage = 11.73 years, SD = 1.34) and 105 allistic adolescents (Mage = 11.49 years, SD = 1.36) between 9 and 15 years old were included in this study. Self-reports were administered at 3 time points with 9-month intervals. Longitudinal analyses revealed both affective and cognitive empathy remained stable over time in both groups. Moreover, higher and increasing levels of affective empathy were longitudinally related to increasing levels of reactive aggression for autistic adolescents only, while no relationship was found for allistic participants. Additionally, higher levels of cognitive empathy were longitudinally related to decreasing levels of proactive aggression in both groups. The outcomes support the assumption that, compared to allistic adolescents, autistic adolescents are more easily over-aroused when affected by another person’s emotional states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30360-3.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Risk factors

Introduction

Empathy is an important factor for interpersonal bonding, as it strengthens social relationships. This capacity for perceiving and responding appropriately to others’ emotional states causes people to overcome or disregard—temporarily—their idiosyncratic feelings and needs, whilst prioritizing those of the emotional person1,2. In fact, concern for other people’s emotional states also prevents intentionally hurting that person, and is thus related to less aggression and even more prosocial behaviors in allistic (non-autistic) youth3–5. Autistic individuals are often less visibly responsive to others’ emotional states, for example, when the other is in pain6–8. Does this indicate that they also feel less for the other person, and thus more easily resort to aggressive behaviors in their social interactions? To date, the relationship between empathy and aggression over time in autistic youth has been scarcely studied, which was the focus of this study.

Empathy in autistic youth

The ability to feel for someone else who shows signs of an emotional reaction, to be concerned about that person, and to try to help alleviate their stress has been a controversial topic when it comes to autistic youth. Autistic individuals, by definition, have difficulties in their social communication9, but also in recognizing and understanding emotions in others10–13. It is often argued that these difficulties contribute to lower levels of empathy compared to allistic individuals14. When trying to unravel how empathy affects their social functioning, or interpersonal aggression in particular, it is important to distinguish the two main components of empathy, namely affective and cognitive empathy. Affective empathy refers to the ability for emotion contagion, i.e. “to feel what the other person feels”, which is supposedly innate, whereas cognitive empathy refers to the capacity to understand why the other person feels that way, which depends on children’s social learning (i.e. their so-called Theory of Mind)1. The observed lack of engagement and response towards others’ emotional expressions in autistic individuals is often interpreted as a lack of both affective and cognitive empathy, giving the impression that they may not feel or care for others, but also lack an understanding of the causes for other people’s emotions.

Studies that compared levels of cognitive empathy between autistic and allistic individuals of all ages, applying multiple methods, consistently showed lower levels of understanding of others’ emotions in autistic participants15–17. It is argued that this is related to difficulties in their Theory of Mind development, which requires autistic individuals to take other people’s motives and perspectives into account when explaining or predicting their actions or emotional reactions16,18. However, cognitive empathy in both autistic and allistic children is expected to increase over time as cognitive abilities in both groups develop with age17,19.

Contradictory, when it comes to affective empathy, some researchers argue that autistic individuals might, in fact, be more sensitive and thus be more and not less susceptible to feeling other people’s emotions, compared to allistic individuals20–22. Autistic individuals are known for their oversensitivity to all kinds of sensory input, like sound and light, but emotional arousal could also cause over-arousal23,24. Possibly, when witnessing others in distress, the contagious emotional experience is intensified among autistic youth, leading to an immediate sense of feeling overwhelmed. As a result, they might opt to distract themselves to internally calm down. Thus, redirecting attention elsewhere could serve as an adaptive coping mechanism for autistic individuals to protect both themselves and the person in distress22,23.

This view is supported by studies showing stronger neurological responsiveness in autistic individuals than in allistic individuals when observing people in pain20,25,26. Additionally, studies utilizing self-reports, teacher-reports, or parent-reports measures found similar levels of affective empathy in autistic and allistic youth16,27. Taken together, these findings seem to suggest that autistic youth do experience emotional arousal in the face of others’ distress. However, this arousal might quickly escalate into overarousal, hindering instead of helping them from focusing on the well-being of the other person and engaging in an adaptive empathetic reaction.

Previous outcomes in the longitudinal literature on the development of affective empathy among autistic and allistic adolescents are somewhat ambiguous. Among allistic adolescents, some studies found an increase in affective empathy for both boys and girls28,29, whereas other studies found a stable pattern for girls but a decrease in boys19. There has been limited focus on the development of affective empathy in autistic adolescents. To the best of our knowledge, only one study using observational tasks found that older autistic adolescents are more likely to be affected by others’ emotions than younger autistic adolescents17. However, since the nature of this study was not longitudinal, age differences do not necessarily reflect a change of affective empathy over time.

Empathy and aggression

Empathy motivates a person to focus on the person who is emotional. Inherently to empathy, adolescents and adults who have more empathy are also more willing to help others, and show more prosocial behaviors; furthermore, they are also less aggressive30,31. For example, in a longitudinal study, higher levels of both self-reported affective and cognitive empathy contributed to lower levels of physical and verbal aggression in adolescents one year later3. Two types of aggression are often distinguished in the literature, based on different motives: proactive and reactive aggression, which may or may not have differential links with empathy.

Proactive aggression is an instrumental form of aggression, aimed to obtain certain goals, which can be material goals, but the aggressor might also aim for power, and thus pursue social gain. Proactive aggression is a calculated type of aggression. The aggressor can be emotionally calm and deliberately attack another person for their self-gain32. It remains stable or decreases during adolescence33–35. Empathy serves as a protective factor against proactive aggression, according to the literature on allistic youth. Prior research consistently reported that affective and cognitive empathy are negatively associated with proactive aggression31,36,37. Both components of empathy may trigger a person to switch from being self-focused to becoming other-focused, which can have an inhibiting effect on acting out of self-gain, and thereby reduce proactive aggression. However, causal inferences cannot be made given the nature of these studies, thus it could also be argued that adolescents with a tendency towards proactive aggression become less empathic over time.

Contrary to proactive aggression, reactive aggression refers to a defense mechanism against perceived hostility, provocation, or threat. There is no intrinsically sought goal, but reactive aggression is instead characterized by impulsivity, a lack of self-control, and a so-called ‘hot-tempered’ reaction, that might actually further damage social relationships rather than smoothening or fixing them32. Reactive aggression is thus also accompanied by heightened physiological arousal38. Multiple studies have shown a stable or decreasing pattern in reactive aggression among allistic adolescents 33,35. In line with proactive aggression, empirical studies also showed negative relationships between reactive aggression with both affective and cognitive empathy31,36,37. Reactive aggressive youth, however, seem more easily aroused, but they are not necessarily insensitive to other people’s needs as one might expect from youth with a tendency towards proactive aggression. In fact, youth who are prone to reactive aggressive reactions might be even more susceptible to others’ distress once they overcome or disregard their own issues. Thus, also in the case of reactive aggression, the shift from self-focus to other-focus seems essential, and higher levels of empathy could obtain this4.

Present study

While there is ample research on empathy in autistic individuals, surprisingly, the relationship between empathy and aggression in autistic adolescents has scarcely been studied. Presumably, the atypical development of empathy among autistic adolescents, namely, intact affective empathy whereas reduced cognitive empathy, could potentially influence their aggressive behaviors differently than how it affects aggressive behaviors in allistic adolescents. When an autistic adolescent has the capacity to feel the distress of another person but struggles with analyzing the situation and keeping their own emotional arousal under control, negative feelings could build up and lead to heightened arousal. This, in turn, may lead to the exertion of reactive aggression. This was indeed what we found in an earlier cross-sectional study conducted by our team39.

For the present study, autistic and allistic adolescents’ empathy and aggression were collected at 3 time points using the same self-reports, with 9-month intervals. The first aim of our current study was to investigate the developmental trajectories of affective and cognitive empathy, as well as proactive and reactive aggression, in both autistic and allistic adolescents. Based on prior research, we expected increasing cognitive empathy over time for both groups17,19. Additionally, we expected stable or decreasing levels of proactive and reactive aggression over time for allistic adolescents33–35. Due to inconsistencies and limited research in this area, the hypotheses regarding the developmental trajectories of affective empathy in both groups, along with proactive and reactive aggression in autistic adolescents, were exploratory in nature.

The secondary aim of this study was to investigate how affective and cognitive empathy would contribute to the prediction of the development of proactive and reactive aggression in autistic and allistic adolescents. For allistic adolescents, we expected that affective and cognitive empathy would contribute to a decrease in both proactive and reactive aggression over time31,36,37.

Conversely, for autistic adolescents, we expected that affective empathy would be associated with an increase in proactive and reactive aggression over time. Considering a stronger susceptibility of autistic adolescents to become over-aroused, one might anticipate that higher levels of affective empathy could be related to increased levels of reactive aggression39. Affective empathy may amplify the perception of others’ emotional expressions, making the distress of others overwhelming for autistic adolescents. Moreover, some autistic adolescents might internalize those emotions, viewing them from a self-focused perspective, and encounter difficulties in differentiating that these emotions belong to others rather than themselves. In addition, autistic adolescents often struggle with effectively regulating their emotions (an important indicator of reactive aggression), finding it difficult to calm themselves down. Taken together, higher levels of affective empathy could thus contribute to more aggressive behaviors among autistic adolescents over time, especially in response to the distress of others.

Regarding cognitive empathy in autistic adolescents, we expected that higher levels of cognitive empathy would contribute to a decrease in proactive and reactive aggression over time. In line with their allistic peers, higher levels of cognitive empathy enable autistic adolescents to understand and appreciate what someone else is experiencing, directing their attention towards the other. As such, cognitive empathy could act as a deterrent to aggressive behavior31,36,37, aiding in understanding and anticipation of the negative consequences their actions may have on others.

Method

Participants

A total of 82 autistic and 105 allistic adolescents between 9 and 15 years old participated in this study (see Table 1 for participant characteristics). Autistic adolescents were diagnosed by child and adolescent psychiatrists working in a specialized center for autism, using the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised40. To participate in this study, participants were pre-selected on an IQ > 80; and had no diagnoses besides autism. The allistic participants were matched to the autistic participants on mean age and sex, and came from mainstream schools, which assumes an IQ > 80 and had no additional diagnosis either. Besides, an indication of IQ scores was examined between groups using two subtests of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale (WISC; Block design and Picture Arrangement)41. No significant differences were observed in IQ between allistic and autistic adolescents, t(172) = − 1.28, p = 0.203.

Materials

Socioeconomic status is based on the highest attained level of parental education level: (1) no/primary education, (2) lower general secondary education, (3) higher general secondary education, (4) college/university. A mean score was calculated based on these two indicators.

IQ was assessed using two nonverbal subtests from the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children—Third Edition (WISC-III)41. In the block design subtest, participants arranged red-and-white cubes to match a displayed geometric pattern. In the picture arrangement subtest, participants sequenced cartoon images in a logical order. The raw scores were converted into standard scores, which compare an individual’s performance to the average performance of others in the same age group. The population mean was set to 10. The overall IQ score was calculated by averaging the two standard scores (see Table 1 for mean standard scores for IQ in both the autistic and allistic groups).

Empathy was assessed using two subtests of the Empathy Questionnaire for children and adolescents (EmQue-CA46). Adolescents were asked to indicate to what extent descriptions applied to them on a three-point scale (1 = not true, 2 = somewhat true, and 3 = true). We used two scales of the EmQue-CA to assess affective empathy (e.g., “When a friend is upset, I feel upset too’’) and cognitive empathy (e.g., “When a friend is angry, I tend to know why”). All items were scored such that higher scores reflected a higher level of empathy. Mean scores were calculated per scale.

Aggression was assessed using the Instrument for Reactive and Proactive Aggression (IRPA47). The IRPA instrument incorporates two scales: proactive and reactive aggression, encompassing 6 aggressive behaviors–3 physical (kicking, pushing, hitting) and 3 verbal (name-calling, provoking fights, gossiping). Adolescents were asked to report the frequency of their engagement in these aggressive behaviors over the past four weeks, using a 3-point scale where 1 indicates almost never and 3 indicates often. Subsequently, the motives behind the aggressive behaviors were collected and categorized into proactive aggression (“I wanted to be mean”, “I took pleasure out of it”, “I wanted to be the boss”) and reactive aggression (“I was angry” “I was being bullied”, “I was called names”). For each scale, total scores were calculated. The internal consistency of each scale is good to high (range Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81–0.93 for all timepoints).

Procedure

This study received approval from the Psychology Research Ethics Committee of Leiden University. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Parental consents were secured through signed forms for the participation of their children. Parents and adolescents were informed that their participation was completely voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time.

Self-report questionnaires were administered to adolescents at three-time points, with an interval of approximately 9 months. Adolescents were instructed to independently fill out the questionnaires in a quiet environment, either at home or school. All questionnaires were presented on a computer. Adolescents were informed that their responses would be analyzed anonymously.

Statistical analyses

We used Linear Mixed Models (LMM) to deal with the nested structure of our data (i.e., within-child measures). First, we examined the development over time for both empathy and aggression and whether these developmental trajectories differ between autistic and allistic youth. Using a formal modeling procedure, we fit an unconditional means model with a fixed and random intercept. Subsequently, we added Age (centered around 109 months) and examined three models of change: a linear, quadratic, and cubic model. In addition, a random slope effect for the best age model was added, but this did not improve the model fit of any model. Subsequently, Group (i.e., 0 = allistic adolescents, 1 = autistic adolescents) was added in order to assess general group differences and in a next step the interaction of Age and Group was added to assess differences between the groups in developmental trajectories. Preferred models had significant lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values.

Second, LMM models were used to assess whether affective and cognitive empathy contributed to the development of proactive and reactive aggression. We used baseline levels (i.e., score at T1) and change scores (i.e., T1-T1, T2-T1, and T3-T1) of the empathy variables as the predicting variables. Given the significant overlap between both types of aggression and both forms of empathy, we controlled for these interrelations to better isolate their unique effects. Specifically, proactive and reactive aggression were moderately correlated at each time point: r(164) = 0.47, p < 0.001 at Time 1; r(148) = 0.40, p < 0.001 at Time 2; r(128) = 0.22, p = 0.012 at Time 3), as were affective and cognitive empathy (r(162) = 0.30, p < 0.001 at Time 1; r(141) = 0.16, p = 0.049 at Time 2; r(126) = 0.25, p = 0.004 at Time 3). To assess the distinct contributions of affective and cognitive empathy to proactive and reactive aggression, we therefore controlled for the corresponding form of aggression in each model. The best-fitting age-model and Group were also included in the analyses. In a second step, interactions of the empathy variables and Group were added. Again, a comparison between nested models was made by comparing AIC values (i.e., significant lower values indicate better fit). Given the small number of autistic girls in our sample (n = 9), we conducted additional analyses excluding autistic girls to examine whether their inclusion influenced the main findings. No substantial differences emerged. These analyses with boys-only can be obtained by contacting the first author. All analyses were performed in SPSS version 27.0.

Discussion

Empathy is supposed to be the ‘social glue’ for interpersonal bonding1, and it is argued that focusing on another person’s emotions, feeling for that person, and trying to understand their emotional reactions (i.e. affective and cognitive empathy), may contribute to better social relationships, but also prevent individuals who are empathic from harming others (i.e., interpersonal aggression). Although such a relationship between empathy and aggression is not yet crystallized in childhood, ample empirical studies confirmed associations for affective and cognitive empathy with both reactive and proactive aggression in adolescence and beyond4,36,37,39.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is thus one of the first to examine how empathy develops and affects levels of reactive and proactive aggression in autistic adolescents over time. Based on existing literature, we expected an increase in cognitive empathy in autistic adolescents over time. However, no specific expectations were made for the development of affective empathy in autistic adolescents due to inconsistent literature.

Additionally, the question of whether autistic individuals can ‘feel’ for others (affective empathy), has been examined in various studies, but how this relates to their social functioning, and both forms of aggression in particular, remained unclear. Based on previous literature39, we expected that affective empathy could easily over-arouse autistic adolescents, which would then hamper their social functioning; thus we expected an increase in both reactive and proactive aggression over time.

This and other predictions were partly confirmed. Most importantly, our results showed that affective empathy was related to increased levels of reactive aggression over time in autistic adolescents only, whereas this relationship was unrelated in allistic adolescents. Note that no other group differences appeared in the relationships that were tested between empathy and aggression in autistic and allistic adolescents. Besides the longitudinal relationship for affective empathy with reactive aggression in autistic youth, no other longitudinal relationships were found for affective empathy towards proactive aggression in either group. Regarding cognitive empathy, results showed that higher levels of cognitive empathy were longitudinally related to decreasing levels of proactive aggression, and again, no other relationships appeared. Some of these outcomes will be further discussed below in more detail.

As expected, higher levels of affective empathy were associated with higher levels of reactive aggression in autistic youth over time. However, two other expected outcomes related to affective empathy were not shown. First, the longitudinal relationship between affective empathy and proactive aggression in autistic youth was absent. In the self-report we used, the IRPA47, which is a widely used questionnaire for reactive and proactive aggression, the items for proactive aggression imply the intention to be mean to others (e.g. ‘I wanted to be mean’). Heightened arousal levels in autistic youth might increase the risk for unplanned, impulsive forms of aggression, but there is no reason to assume that this harm to others is actually done intentionally. Other forms of proactive aggression that are more instrumental (aggression in order to obtain material gain for example), might overlook the harm to the other person, which is more in line with what could be expected from autistic individuals due to their frequently noted difficulties in their Theory of Mind development18. Thus, future studies could consider including other measures for proactive aggression to examine these assumptions made here.

Second, the expected negative association between affective empathy and reactive aggression over time in allistic adolescents did not show either, despite a consistent negative association in previous studies with cross-sectional data31,36,37. Note however that 2 items out of 7 on the scale we used for affective empathy were about positive emotions (‘If my mother is happy, I also feel happy’ and ‘If a friend is laughing, I also laugh’)46, whereas the scales in the studies mentioned only included items about negative emotion experiences. It seems plausible to assume that the expected relationship between affective empathy and reactive aggression will apply in allistic youth to negative emotion arousal only, and not when feeling happy. Reactive aggression occurs more often when feeling provoked or perceiving hostility, thus by definition, this does not include positive emotions.

In line with previous studies, the expected negative relationship over time between cognitive empathy and proactive aggression was confirmed in both allistic and autistic youth31,36,37. When individuals can effectively understand others’ emotions, they may be less inclined to strategically use proactive aggression, thus intentionally harming the other person for personal gain. However, unexpectedly, no longitudinal relationship between cognitive empathy and lower reactive aggression emerged in either group, despite the cross-sectional findings in other studies. Yet, reactive aggression is an impulsive, hot-tempered kind of reaction, which is difficult to control for individuals prone to reactive aggression. Subsequently, the relationship over time found in previous studies might be the other way around: possibly adolescents prone to reactive aggression have more difficulties changing from self-focused to other-focused, and in turn, understand the causes for other people’s emotions.

Lastly, and contrary to outcomes reported in a longitudinal study by Van der Graaff et al.19, levels of cognitive empathy in our study remained stable over time. However, the increase in the study by Van der Graaff appeared in males who were somewhat older, i.e. 15 years and onward, whereas the age in our sample ranged from 11 to 14 years old. Possibly, a follow-up study with an older age-range might confirm the expected increase in cognitive empathy.

Limitations

This study aimed to shed light on the distinct impact of affective empathy on the development of reactive aggression in autistic and allistic adolescents. However, it is crucial to acknowledge some limitations in this study. An important limitation is the relatively small sample size, which might have limited the ability to detect small but significant predictions. We also acknowledge the absence of a-priori power analyses as a limitation of the current study, given that such analyses were not conducted prior to data collection. Additionally, the majority of the participants in this study (both autistic and the allistic group) were males. Since autistic females may have different levels and developmental trajectories of affective and empathy, and proactive and reactive aggression, our findings should be interpreted with caution as they cannot be generalized to the entire autistic population. For future studies, a larger and more diverse sample, including female participants, should allow a thorough exploration of sex differences in the development of affective and cognitive empathy and how this affects the development of interpersonal aggression in these populations.

Moreover, reliance on self-report measures only might potentially lead to a response bias and create the possibility of shared method variance. Future studies could apply a multi-informant, multi-method design, for example using in-vivo empathy evoking tasks or observations, and taking multiple informants into account (e.g. parents, siblings, or teachers). Incorporating multiple methods and informants in future studies would provide a more holistic understanding of how autistic adolescents respond empathically.

Conclusion

Importantly, the outcomes of this study confirmed that affective empathy may hinder rather than smoothen interpersonal relationships for autistic adolescents, even when this concerns positive emotions in the emotional person. This further strengthens the idea that autistic adolescents might in fact be more and not less sensitive than their allistic peers to emotional reactions in other people. Understanding these dynamics is important for recognizing and acknowledging the complexities of empathy in autistic youth, and invites a shift away from deficit-based views toward a more nuanced appreciation of the emotional experiences of autistic adolescents.
