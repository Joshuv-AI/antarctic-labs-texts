Modified fourth-generation minimally invasive technique with a modified K-wire fixation technique for hallux valgus: a biomechanical and clinical study
Authors: Pu Chen, Hua Guan, Enhui Feng, Jiachang Liang, Xinying He, Weiming Huang, Yiyin Xu, Jiewei Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMinimally invasive techniques have become prominent in hallux valgus (HV) correction. However, prevalent third and fourth-generation methods using the “in-out-in” screw fixation often involve extensive intraoperative fluoroscopy, a steep learning curve, and higher costs. This study introduces and evaluates a modified fourth-generation technique featuring an extra-articular transverse osteotomy combined with a modified Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation technique and the concept of “fourth-dimensional correction” (dynamic and static assessment).MethodsThe study comprised two parts. First, a finite element analysis (FEA) model was developed to compare the biomechanical performance (bone/implant stress, stability) of the modified K-wire fixation technique versus the standard “in-out-in” K-wire fixation at different lateral translation distances (4, 8, 12 mm). Second, a prospective case series of 108 patients (164 feet) with mild to severe HV who underwent the modified technique was analyzed. Outcomes included radiographic parameters (HVA, IMA, DMAA), the AOFAS score, complications, and intraoperative fluoroscopy usage.ResultsFEA demonstrated that the modified K-wire fixation technique provided stability comparable to the “in-out-in” technique while exhibiting significantly lower stress on the K-wires, suggesting a reduced risk of pin fracture. Clinically, all osteotomy sites achieved bony union. Significant improvements were observed in the entire cohort: mean HVA corrected from 36.93°±9.32° to 9.57°±4.88°, mean IMA from 14.86°±3.82° to 6.08°±3.43°, and mean AOFAS score from 61.26 ± 6.97 to 88.90 ± 4.68 (all P < 0.01). Excellent correction was maintained across all severity subgroups. The mean intraoperative fluoroscopy shots were 4.14 ± 3.04 per foot, showing a distinct learning curve that stabilized after approximately 25 cases, with usage significantly lower than rates reported for traditional “in-out-in” methods.ConclusionThe modified fourth-generation minimally invasive technique with the modified K-wire fixation technique is biomechanically sound and clinically effective. It provides reliable radiographic and functional outcomes for HV of varying severities while substantially reducing intraoperative radiation exposure, offering a valuable alternative in the minimally invasive HV correction arsenal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10151-1.Keywords: Hallux valgus, Minimally invasive surgery, Four-dimensional correction, K-wire, Modified technique, Finite element analysis

Introduction

Minimally invasive hallux valgus (HV) correction surgery has gradually gained favor among foot and ankle surgeons due to its advantages of less pain, quick recovery, minimal trauma, no damage to the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, and satisfactory correction of HV deformity [1–4]. The third-generation minimally invasive HV correction technique has developed from the Chevron osteotomy, which is performed percutaneously using 3 to 4 small incisions under fluoroscopy to complete the osteotomy and fix the osteotomy ends with screws [5]. Compared to the third-generation technique, the fourth-generation technique places greater emphasis on extra-articular transverse osteotomy of the metatarsal, which has advantages in correcting rotational deformities [6, 7]. However, both the third and fourth-generation techniques do not focus on evaluating the inversion and eversion balance of the first MTP joint in dynamic and static conditions, leading to recurrence of deformity or the development of hallux varus postoperatively. Additionally, both third- and fourth-generation techniques utilize “in-out-in” fixation, a procedure involving a guide pin passed through the medial and lateral cortices of the proximal metatarsal to the metatarsal head, followed by cannulated screw placement. This method requires extensive fluoroscopy, has a steep learning curve, and carries disadvantages including skin irritation, need for secondary removal surgery, and high costs [8, 9].

Therefore, we propose a modified fourth-generation technique for HV correction incorporating an extra-articular transverse osteotomy and a modified K-wire fixation technique, where the internal implant passes through the medial cortex of the proximal metatarsal, then through the medial cortex of the distal metatarsal, and finally reaches the lateral cortex of the distal metatarsal—allowing direct visualization and potentially reducing fluoroscopy. This study aims to: (1) compare the biomechanical performance of the modified K-wire fixation versus standard “in-out-in” fixation using finite element analysis; (2) evaluate short-term clinical outcomes and fluoroscopy frequency across different HV grades; and (3) introduce “fourth-dimensional correction”—assessing post-correction hallux position not only statically but also under simulated weight-bearing to identify residual instability requiring adjunctive procedures, thereby minimizing recurrence risk.

Methods

Finite element analysis of “In-Out-In” versus modified K-wire fixation

A finite element model was developed based on CT data from a patient with moderate HV (HVA 37°, IMA 15°), simulating a transverse osteotomy with distal fragment lateral translation of 4, 8, and 12 mm [10]. For each translation distance, two fixation configurations were compared: the conventional “in-out-in” technique and the modified K-wire fixation technique. Under a simulated 250 N weight-bearing load, the following parameters were evaluated: stress distribution at the osteotomy site and on the K-wires, three-dimensional displacement of the osteotomy site, change in IMA, and plantar stress distribution. Detailed modeling parameters are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Clinical efficacy evaluation: patient data

Collected from March 2023 to February 2024, 108 patients with HV treated with modified fourth-generation minimally invasive techniques combined with K-wires, totaling 164 feet, with an average age of 44.40 ± 14.85 (16–85 years), including 12 males and 96 females, with 52 single feet and 56 double feet. Inclusion Criteria: (1) Primary HV correction using the modified fourth-generation technique with K-wire fixation; (2) failure of conservative treatment; (3) all surgeries performed by the same surgeon. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Previous ipsilateral hallux or first metatarsal surgery; (2) active foot infection; (3) severe peripheral vascular/neurological disorders or poorly controlled diabetes compromising outcomes; (4) inability to comply with follow-up due to neuropsychological impairment. Surgical method

First, an approximately 1 cm incision was made at the base of the proximal phalanx. The medial joint capsule of the first MTP joint was dissected using a periosteal elevator. An oscillating saw was then used to excise the medial eminence (bunion) along the longitudinal axis of the first metatarsal. A 1.5 cm longitudinal incision was made at the proximal first metatarsal neck to expose the first metatarsal, followed by a transverse osteotomy performed perpendicular to the second metatarsal shaft using a mini oscillating saw. After osteotomy, a periosteal elevator was inserted into the proximal medullary canal to translate the fragment medially while depressing the metatarsal head to reconstruct the transverse arch. Simultaneously, the hallux manually rotated to neutral to correct pronation. Fixation was achieved with two 1.5 mm K-wires inserted via the modified K-wire fixation technique: from the medial cortex of the proximal fragment, passes through the medullary canal, reaches the medial cortex of the distal fragment, and then advances further to penetrate the lateral cortex of the distal fragment. At this point, the first MTP joint is routinely mobilized to assess joint motion. If the K-wire penetrates the lateral cortex without causing joint impingement or clicking, we retain this position—in which case the wire can safely penetrate the lateral cortex. If joint impingement or clicking occurs, we retract the K-wire appropriately until smooth joint motion is restored. Two K-wires are distributed along the dorsal and plantar aspects of the first metatarsal shaft axis. When inserting the K-wires into the distal metatarsal, it should be placed as distally as possible to avoid first metatarsophalangeal joint incongruence, known as the “seesaw effect” (Fig. 1).

To avoid first metatarsophalangeal joint incongruence (the seesaw effect), the K-wire should be inserted as distally as possible within the distal metatarsal. Preoperative radiograph of moderate hallux valgus is shown in (A). When positioned too proximally (near the osteotomy line), drilling causes the metatarsal head to tilt inward, leading to joint malalignment (B). In contrast, distal placement allows the head to evert properly during drilling, preserving joint congruity (C). Lateral view shows K-wires distributed along the dorsal and plantar aspects of the metatarsal axis (D)

Correction was assessed under both static and dynamic conditions (simulated weight-bearing by applying pressure to the first and fifth metatarsal heads). Dynamic assessment was performed by manually applying simultaneous pressure to the first and fifth metatarsal heads toward the plantar direction to simulate weight-bearing. The hallux position was evaluated: if a residual valgus angle > 5° or obvious deviation from neutral was observed under dynamic testing, it was defined as inadequate correction, triggering adjunctive procedures. This assessment was performed by the same surgeon, and intraoperative reproducibility was confirmed. If malalignment or residual deformity persisted, percutaneous lateral soft tissue release was performed. If residual valgus remained, an Akin osteotomy was added (Fig. 2). For those with a hammer toe deformity of the second toe, a closed wedge osteotomy of the second metatarsal and extension of the extensor tendon of the toe are performed.

The prominent proximal metatarsal was resected using a mini oscillating saw, and the excised bone was packed laterally at the osteotomy site to promote healing. A mixture was prepared by combining 1 ml of Compound Betamethasone Injection (Hangzhou MSD Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), 10 ml of Ropivacaine Hydrochloride Injection (Guangdong Jiabo Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), and 50 mg of Flurbiprofen Injection (Wuhan Yanda Life Sciences Co., Ltd.). Subsequently, an appropriate portion of the mixture was injected into the periosteum, joint capsule, and subcutaneous tissue for analgesia [11]. The incision was closed in layers, followed by compressive bandaging with a toe separator placed in the first intermetatarsal space.

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, oral NSAIDs were administered. K-wires removed at 6 weeks. Patients walked in a forefoot-offloading shoe at 4–6 h post-surgery, transitioned to regular footwear at 8 weeks, and resumed sports at 3 months. Weight-bearing radiographs and clinical assessments were scheduled at 6 weeks, 3, 6, and 12 months.

Observation indicators of clinical efficacy evaluation

Record the number of intraoperative fluoroscopy shots, incision healing status, and osteotomy healing status. Get weight-bearing X-rays before surgery and at 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery, measuring changes in HVA, IMA, and DMAA indicators. The clinical efficacy is evaluated using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society MTP-InterPhalangeal scale (AOFAS MTP-IP) [12].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data are presented as mean±standard deviation. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed prior to group comparisons. Depending on data distribution, either the unpaired t-test (with or without Welch’s correction) or the Mann-Whitney rank-sum test was applied. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Results of finite element analysis

Analysis of Von Mises stress distribution revealed distinct patterns between the two fixation techniques. In the “in-out-in” group, stress concentrated at the metatarsal head, while in the modified K-wire fixation group, stress was primarily distributed around the osteotomy site. No significant differences in stress magnitude were observed at 4 mm and 8 mm translation, though at 12 mm, the modified technique showed higher osteotomy site stress (35.252 MPa vs. 26.355 MPa).

K-wire stress increased with greater translation distances. The modified fixation technique demonstrated significantly lower K-wire stress at both 4 mm (14.440 MPa vs. 42.406 MPa) and 12 mm (115.840 MPa vs. 193.630 MPa) translation, suggesting reduced pin fracture risk. Stress was predominantly concentrated at the osteotomy site in both groups.

No significant differences were found between techniques regarding plantar fascia stress, plantar load-bearing distribution, or IMA correction. Both fixation methods restored physiological plantar loading patterns without abnormal stress concentration. Three-dimensional displacement analysis confirmed comparable initial stability between techniques, with minimal displacement (< 0.1 mm) across all translation distances. Excessive translation resulted in negative IMA values, indicating potential hallux varus risk. Detailed data are presented in Table 2; Fig. 3.

Results of clinical efficacy evaluation

A total of 108 patients (164 feet) with a mean follow-up of 18.90 ± 3.57 (13–25) months were enrolled, including 38 mild, 70 moderate, and 56 severe HV cases. All incisions healed primarily. Two patients developed superficial pin site infections, which resolved with outpatient care, and two reported transient metatarsalgia that improved with arch support. No cases of K-wire breakage or migration, malunion, hallux varus, or deep infection were observed. During K-wire removal at 6 weeks, no wire breakage or retained fragments occurred. Complete bony union was achieved in all patients at one year postoperatively.

Significant postoperative improvements were observed across all radiographic and functional outcomes (all P < 0.01). Overall, HVA improved from 36.93°±9.32° to 9.57°±4.88°, IMA from 14.86°±3.82° to 6.08°±3.43°, and AOFAS score from 61.26 ± 6.97 to 88.90 ± 4.68. Subgroup analysis by severity demonstrated consistent correction across all groups. In severe HV patients, HVA improved from 47.33°±5.85° to 12.59°±4.92°, IMA from 17.32°±3.52° to 5.80°±3.60°, and AOFAS score from 62.54 ± 6.52 to 89.48 ± 4.10 (Table 3).

Discussion

With the introduction of third and fourth-generation techniques, their clinical application has become increasingly widespread in the treatment of HV. Although numerous studies have explored the biomechanical properties of different treatments for HV, comparative research on different K-wire fixation techniques remains relatively scarce [13–16].

Based on the fourth-generation technique, we developed a modified K-wire fixation method, designated as the modified K-wire fixation technique. Compared to the “in-out-in” technique, the K-wire can be seen passing through the proximal metatarsal head in the modified technique, and the first MTP joint can be assessed to determine whether the K-wire has crossed the MTP joint, allowing the entire implantation process to happen with less fluoroscopy. However, the biomechanical differences between these two K-wire fixation techniques were previously unclear.

Our results revealed that at 4 mm and 8 mm of translation, the Von Mises stress on the first metatarsal showed no significant difference between the modified K-wire fixation and “in-out-in” techniques. However, the Von Mises stress on the K-wires themselves was significantly lower in the modified group, suggesting a reduced risk of pin breakage compared to the “in-out-in” technique. Regarding plantar fascia stress, no significant differences were observed between the two groups at any translation distance (4, 8, 12 mm). The displacement data at the osteotomy site further indicated no significant differences between the two fixation techniques across the different translation distances, implying that their fixation strengths are comparable. The comparable stability between the modified K-wire fixation and “in-out-in” techniques can be explained by their similar biomechanical principle. The modified K-wire fixation creates a three-point support construct: two points on the medial cortex (proximal and distal fragments) and one point on the lateral cortex of the distal fragment. This configuration resists shear and rotational forces effectively, analogous to the three-cortical fixation of the “in-out-in” technique, which engages the medial and lateral cortices of the proximal fragment and the lateral cortex of the distal fragment. Thus, the reduced pin stress in the modified technique does not compromise stability.

K-wire fixation is increasingly used in HV correction. Traditionally, K-wires were employed in the Bösch technique [17], where fixation was achieved through the intramedullary canal of the proximal metatarsal. This method fell out of favor due to insufficient fixation strength, poor rotational control, and high complication rates [18]. Subsequently, some scholars applied K-wire fixation in modified Chevron osteotomies, with biomechanical studies suggesting that three-cortical K-wire fixation provides stability comparable to single-screw fixation [19]. Mohammed et al. demonstrated that K-wire fixation serves as a viable strategy in proximal metatarsal osteotomy. This method offered clinical outcomes equivalent to those of plate and screw constructs but was more cost-effective and associated with a lower rate of reoperation [20]. With the clinical adoption of third and fourth-generation transverse metatarsal osteotomies, K-wires have regained popularity for stabilizing the osteotomy site. Kim et al. reported that augmenting single-screw fixation with a K-wire can better correct the DMAA and reduce the recurrence of HV [21]. However, to the best of our knowledge, clinical studies on minimally invasive osteotomy correction utilizing K-wire fixation alone have not yet been reported.

All osteotomy sites healed uneventfully, with K-wires removed at 6 weeks and bony union was achieved at the osteotomy site in all patients at 1 year. A key technical consideration is distal K-wire placement within the metatarsal head: directing the wire away from the osteotomy site prevents the “seesaw effect” (intraoperative head inversion and subsequent MTP joint malalignment), whereas proximal placement predisposes to this complication.

In this study, the mean number of fluoroscopy shots per foot for HV correction was 4.14. During the first 24 correction procedures (cases 1–24), the mean fluoroscopy shots was 9.71 times. As surgical experience increased, intraoperative fluoroscopy usage gradually decreased, with a mean of 3.19 shots for procedures 25–164. The inflection point of the learning curve occurred at approximately 25 cases, which is comparable to the learning curve observed for the MICA technique, where the inflection point has been reported around the 29th–30th case [8]. Notably, the fluoroscopy frequency required for the modified fourth-generation minimally invasive technique was significantly lower than that of the “in-out-in” fixation technique, which reported requires over 60 shots [22]. This suggests that the modified fourth-generation minimally invasive technique can effectively reduce intraoperative fluoroscopy exposure. However, it is important to note that this comparison is indirect, as we did not perform a contemporaneous head-to-head trial with the “in-out-in” or MICA techniques. Direct comparative studies are warranted to confirm the superiority of our method regarding fluoroscopy reduction.

The direction of the osteotomy determines whether there will be shortening of the hallux postoperatively. Both the third and fourth-generation minimally invasive techniques suggest doing the osteotomy vertically on the first metatarsal shaft, slightly favoring the distal side, which helps make up for the 2 mm bone loss from the Shannon drill during the osteotomy and preventing shortening of the hallux [23]. In the modified fourth-generation minimally invasive surgery, since we use a miniature oscillating saw for the osteotomy, it has almost no impact on the length of the first metatarsal. Performing the osteotomy perpendicular to the second metatarsal shaft allows for better parallel alignment of the first and second metatarsals after shifting, improving the IMA angle.

How to prevent recurrence of HV deformity after correction surgery is a key concern for foot and ankle surgeons during the procedure. We propose a new dimension to assess whether the correction of HV deformity has been adequately achieved. In addition to correcting the coronal plane HV, sagittal plane elevation of the first metatarsal head, and transverse plane pronation deformity of the hallux, it is also necessary to consider whether the HV deformity has improved in both dynamic and static aspects, referred to as the fourth dimension of correction. The static aspect refers to assessing whether there is any residual HV deformity after the internal fixation is implanted; the dynamic assessment refers to evaluating whether the HV deformity maintains a neutral position while simulating weight-bearing. If the hallux still shows valgus in the dynamic aspect, the risk of postoperative recurrence goes up, necessitating adequate soft tissue release or additional procedures, such as lateral adductor release, additional Akin surgery, or insufficient metatarsal head shifting. If the hallux shows varus in the dynamic aspect, it may indicate excessive shifting of the metatarsal head or excessive soft tissue release laterally.

This study has several key advantages. First, FEA confirmed that the modified K-wire fixation technique provides comparable stability to conventional “in-out-in” fixation while significantly reducing pin fracture risk, offering a strong biomechanical foundation for clinical application. Second, unlike third- and fourth-generation methods requiring specialized drills, our modified technique utilizes a standard mini-oscillating saw or K-wire drilling, enhancing accessibility in resource-limited settings. Third, direct visualization of K-wire passage through the medial cortex minimizes fluoroscopy dependence, reducing radiation exposure and operative time. Fourth, K-wire fixation eliminates the need for costly implants or secondary removal surgery, lowering healthcare costs and improving patient acceptance.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The finite element model was derived from a single patient’s CT data, limiting generalizability, and simplified soft tissue modeling may not fully replicate in vivo biomechanics. Nevertheless, the selected patient had moderate HV (HVA 37°, IMA 15°) and represented a typical case, with first metatarsal morphology consistent with most clinical presentations. Moreover, the primary purpose of our FEA was to compare the relative biomechanical differences between the two K-wire fixation techniques rather than to predict absolute stress values; therefore, a single-patient model still provides valid comparative insight. Additionally, FEA alone cannot replace experimental validation. No cadaveric or laboratory biomechanical studies were conducted to corroborate the FEA findings, which represents a limitation of this study.

In addition, this study is a retrospective case series (Level IV evidence) without a control group. All patients were treated by a single surgeon using the same technique during the study period, and no contemporaneous comparison with alternative procedures was performed. Therefore, selection bias cannot be fully excluded, and the reported outcomes should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to further validate the efficacy of this modified technique. Furthermore, the AOFAS score, while widely used, is a clinician-administered instrument and has well-documented limitations, including a potential ceiling effect and lack of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) such as the Manchester-Oxford Foot Questionnaire (MOXFQ) or the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS). The absence of validated PROMs in this study is an important limitation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that the modified fourth-generation minimally invasive technique, which incorporates an extra-articular transverse osteotomy, the modified K-wire fixation technique, and the principle of fourth-dimensional correction, is a safe and effective approach for managing HV across a spectrum of deformity severities.
