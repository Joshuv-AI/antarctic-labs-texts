Effects of single‐session transcranial direct current stimulation on response inhibition in stop‐signal task performance: A meta‐analysis and systematic review
Authors: Qiuxuan Yu, Mingyue Zhang, Ximei Zhu, Sanwang Wang, Tingting Wu, Yinjiao Li, Wenrong Wen, Yanping Bao, Jie Shi, Lin Lu, Jiahui Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundResponse inhibition is a fundamental component of executive control and a transdiagnostic mechanism underpinning numerous mental disorders. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has been investigated as a potential neuromodulatory intervention to enhance response inhibition; however, findings remain inconsistent due to methodological heterogeneity in stimulation parameters and outcome measures.AimsTo quantitatively evaluate the effects of tDCS on response inhibition as measured by the stop‐signal task (SST) and to explore potential moderators influencing tDCS efficacy.MethodsThis meta‐analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses. We systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Embase and Scopus up to 1 April 2026. Quality assessment was conducted utilising the Cochrane risk of bias 2 tool, with random‐effects meta‐analysis, moderator and subgroup analyses and sensitivity analysis for statistical evaluation.ResultsThe search yielded 2982 articles, of which 35 were considered eligible for inclusion, including 1768 participants. The risk of bias 2 assessment indicated acceptable methodological quality, with the majority of studies rated as low risk of bias or as having some concerns. The overall effect of tDCS on response inhibition was modest yet statistically significant (Hedges' g = −0.22, 95% confidence interval (CI) −0.34 to −0.11, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis indicated a small‐to‐moderate effect of anodal tDCS (Hedges' g = −0.38, 95% CI −0.51 to −0.26, p < 0.001), especially when targeting the right inferior frontal gyrus, right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex or motor‐related cortex; additionally, online tDCS demonstrated greater efficacy than offline tDCS. Cathodal tDCS demonstrated a small but statistically significant detrimental effect on response inhibition (Hedges' g = 0.19, 95% CI 0.03–0.35, p = 0.023).ConclusionsOur findings support the potential of anodal tDCS as a neuromodulatory approach for enhancing response inhibition. The polarity‐, target‐ and timing‐specific effects highlight key methodological considerations for optimising tDCS protocols in research on response inhibition impairments.PROSPERO Registration NumberCRD42024565038.

INTRODUCTION

Response inhibition is a core component of executive control and is crucial for swift and accurate decision‐making in dynamic environments. 1 In everyday contexts such as education, students freely converse during breaks yet swiftly shift to focused attention upon auditory cues such as class bells. This neurobehavioural flexibility relies on efficient response inhibition supported by coordinated activity across distributed cortical networks. 2 , 3 , 4 Impairments in response inhibition are strongly implicated in various neuropsychiatric disorders, including attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), 5 obsessive‐compulsive disorder (OCD), 6 addiction 7 and eating disorders. 8

In recent decades, noninvasive brain stimulation has gained prominence as a promising approach to modulate cognitive and motor functions. 9 , 10 Among its modalities, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is particularly appealing for its safety, simplicity and portability. 11 , 12 Although recent studies have examined tDCS‐induced enhancement of response inhibition, their findings remain inconsistent, largely due to methodological heterogeneity. Notably, accumulating evidence suggests that anodal and cathodal tDCS exert distinct neural effects, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 potentially leading to divergent behavioural outcomes. Prior reviews seldom addressed these polarity‐specific effects, contributing to conflicting conclusions. This review, therefore, aims to clarify tDCS‐induced modulation of response inhibition by systematically distinguishing anodal and cathodal effects, thereby providing a more precise framework for future research and clinical applications.

Response inhibition

Cognition refers to the neurobiological process by which the brain acquires, encodes and transforms external information into organised mental representations, ultimately guiding behaviour. It involves evaluating, organising and achieving goals, as well as flexibly adapting to novel challenges. 18 Among various cognitive processes, executive functions represent higher‐order abilities that facilitate goal‐directed behaviour, intricate problem‐solving and adaptive decision‐making in dynamic contexts. 19 These functions are typically categorised into three core components: inhibitory control, working memory and cognitive flexibility. 20 Inhibitory control—the ability to suppress automatic or dominant responses—comprises several dimensions, with response inhibition as a fundamental component. 21 Response inhibition specifically refers to the capacity to stop initiated or intended actions in pursuit of higher‐order goals. It is essential for self‐regulation, impulse control and adaptive decision‐making, hence underpinning daily functioning and numerous cognitive and behavioural processes. 22

Stop‐signal task

The stop‐signal task (SST) is one of the most widely used paradigms for assessing response inhibition. 23 Based on the independent race model, it conceptualises inhibition as a competition between a ‘go’ process and a ‘stop’ process, with successful inhibition occurring when the stop process finishes first. 24 This theoretically robust framework has made the SST a cornerstone in both cognitive neuroscience and clinical research across healthy and clinical populations. 25 As illustrated in figure 1A, the SST includes two types of trials: GO trials and STOP trials. In GO trials, participants perform a choice reaction‐time task by responding to directional stimuli (e.g., left‐ or right‐pointing arrows). In STOP trials, a supplementary stop signal (e.g., a visual ‘X’ or an auditory tone) appears after a variable stop‐signal delay, instructing participants to withhold their initiated response. The primary measure, stop‐signal reaction time (SSRT), estimates the latency required for the stopping mechanism to cancel the initiated motor action. A shorter SSRT reflects more efficient inhibition, whereas a longer SSRT indicates impairment. 21 Other indices, such as go reaction time or stop accuracy, may reflect processing speed or attentional control, but do not directly quantify inhibition efficiency. Owing to its strong psychometric validity and cross‐study consistency, SSRT serves as a precise and stable indicator of inhibitory control. 21 Thus, the SST is widely used across both healthy and clinical populations and offers a stable behavioural index (SSRT) of response inhibition, making it an ideal paradigm to examine the neuromodulatory effects of tDCS.

In ‘STOP’ trials, a stop signal (red ‘X’) appears after a variable stop‐signal delay, instructing participants to withhold their response. Stop‐signal reaction time is calculated as an index of response inhibition efficiency. (B) Illustration of tDCS mechanisms. In contrast, cathodal stimulation (bottom) decreases cortical excitability by inducing hyperpolarisation via potassium (K+) efflux. Neural mechanisms of response inhibition

Response inhibition is mediated by a distributed cortical network involving the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and the supplementary motor area (SMA). 26 , 27 , 28 Among these regions, the right inferior frontal gyrus (rIFG) plays a pivotal role as a key hub for inhibitory control. 29 , 30 , 31 Neurological evidence shows that lesions in the rIFG result in a significantly prolonged SSRT compared with that in healthy controls. 32 Moreover, low‐frequency transcranial magnetic stimulation over the rIFG, which transiently suppresses cortical excitability, produces a ‘virtual lesion’ effect, leading to SSRT prolongation. 33 Lesions in the DLPFC and SMA also extend the SSRT, highlighting their contributions to executive and motor control. 4 Neuroimaging studies further support these findings: Children with ADHD exhibit reduced grey matter volumes in the prefrontal cortex, IFG, caudate nucleus and globus pallidus, particularly in the right hemisphere, 34 , 35 , 36 whereas patients with OCD show hypoactivation in the IFG and medial frontal gyrus. 37 Aberrant activation across this network is also observed in other psychiatric disorders marked by compromised inhibitory control, 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 underscoring its central role in the neural basis of impaired response inhibition.

The role of tDCS in response inhibition

tDCS has attracted increasing attention as a cost‐effective and noninvasive neuromodulation technique for modulating response inhibition. 42 , 43 By applying low‐intensity electrical currents (0.50–2.00 mA) to cortical regions, tDCS alters neuronal resting membrane potential, thereby modulating cortical excitability. Its efficacy depends on several parameters, including current intensity, duration, polarity, electrode placement and stimulation timing (online vs. offline stimulation). 44 According to stimulation polarity, tDCS can be categorised as anodal and cathodal, determined by electrode placement over the target region; ‘anodal’ places the anode over the target cortex, whereas ‘cathodal’ places the cathode over the target area. 45 Recent research indicates that anodal and cathodal tDCS involve distinct neurophysiological mechanisms. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Anodal stimulation enhances cortical excitability, whereas cathodal stimulation is generally associated with reduced excitability (figure 1B). Given these polarity‐dependent mechanisms, electrode configuration critically shapes the behavioural effects of tDCS on response inhibition. 46 , 47

Despite its theoretical promise, prior research remains inconsistent, and numerous studies have failed to distinguish between the effects of anodal and cathodal tDCS. Several studies and meta‐analyses have reported a small yet significant effect of tDCS on inhibitory control, particularly when stimulation is applied over the rIFG or DLPFC, whereas others have found negligible effects. 48 , 49 These discrepancies likely stem from methodological heterogeneity and inadequate consideration of polarity‐specific mechanisms. Moreover, prior reviews often combined heterogeneous paradigms—including the go/no‐go, Stroop and Flanker tasks—that index broader inhibitory processes but not the stopping function measured by the SST. Because distinct paradigms recruit different neural circuits, 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 aggregating them may obscure the specific effects of tDCS on response inhibition. Clarifying these task‐ and polarity‐specific effects is, therefore, essential for accurately delineating the neuromodulatory role of tDCS.

Aims of the study

This systematic review and meta‐analysis examine the effects of single‐session tDCS on response inhibition, focusing exclusively on studies employing the SST. By synthesising data from 35 studies, we aim to ascertain polarity‐specific efficacy across cortical targets, with emphasis on anodal and cathodal stimulation protocols. This analysis provides refined insights into tDCS‐induced modulation of response inhibition and guidance for future research and clinical applications.

METHODS

This systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted according to the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement. 54 The PRISMA checklist is reported in Supporting Information S2. The study protocol and search strategy were registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024565038) (Supporting Information S2: PROSPERO).

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com/), PsycINFO (via APA PsycNet, https://psycnet.apa.org/), Embase (https://www.embase.com/) and Scopus (https://www.scopus.com/) for peer‐reviewed studies published up to 1 April 2026. The search strategy used a combination of keywords and MeSH terms to identify studies investigating tDCS effects on response inhibition using the SST. The following terms were used: ‘tDCS’ OR ‘transcranial direct current stimulation’ AND ‘response inhibition’ OR ‘inhibitory control’ OR ‘stop‐signal task’. The final literature search was completed on 1 April 2026.

Study selection

Only peer‐reviewed experimental studies (within‐subject, between‐subject, randomised or crossover designs) written in English were included to ensure methodological rigour and consistency across studies. Eligible studies were required to examine the acute effects of single‐session tDCS on response inhibition, assessed exclusively with the SST.

Eligibility criteria were defined according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison and Outcome (PICO) framework: (1) Population: Participants were either healthy adults (without current or past psychiatric or neurological disorders confirmed by self‐report or clinical interview) or clinical populations diagnosed with psychiatric disorders. Studies involving participants with neurocognitive disorders, epilepsy or a history of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) were excluded to minimize confounding effects. (2) Intervention: Studies applying single‐session tDCS with clearly reported stimulation parameters—current intensity, duration, polarity (anodal or cathodal), electrode montage (target and reference sites), electrode size and stimulation timing (online vs. offline)—were included. Single‐session designs were selected to isolate immediate, causal effects of tDCS while avoiding potential confounds such as practice effects, neuroplastic adaptation or treatment‐related changes. (3) Comparison: a sham‐control or active‐control tDCS condition. (4) Outcome: The SST was used to measure response inhibition, with SSRT as the primary outcome.

Studies were excluded if they (1) used alternative inhibitory control tasks (e.g., go/no‐go, Stroop and Flanker tasks), (2) applied multisession tDCS or combined tDCS with other neuromodulation techniques, (3) lacked a control condition or (4) were non‐human studies, reviews or unpublished works.

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records, followed by full‐text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary.

Data extraction

Two authors (Mingyue Zhang and Qiuxuan Yu) independently extracted data from all eligible studies using a standardised template to ensure accuracy and consistency. The following information was recorded: (1) study characteristics: first author, publication year, sample size, study design (between‐ or within‐subject), control condition, blinding success and population type (healthy or clinical); (2) SST parameters: type of stop signal (visual or auditory) and the mean and variance of SSRT for real tDCS versus control conditions, both preintervention and postintervention; and (3) stimulation parameters: tDCS polarity (anodal or cathodal), target and return electrode locations, current intensity, electrode size, timing (offline or online) and stimulation duration. Stimulation polarity was defined according to the electrode position over the target region—‘anodal’ when the anode was positioned over the target cortex and ‘cathodal’ when the cathode was placed there—consistent with prior meta‐analyses. 48 , 55 When multiple independent comparisons were reported, each was extracted as a separate effect size.

For studies with incomplete or missing data, corresponding authors were contacted twice for clarification. When numerical values were unavailable, data were extracted from figures using WebPlotDigitizer. 56 Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. The complete dataset is available in Supporting Information S1: table S1.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Cochrane risk of bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, 57 which provides a standardised and transparent framework for evaluating potential bias. Given that most studies employed randomised, crossover or within‐subject designs, RoB 2 was appropriate for ensuring methodological rigour. Each study was evaluated across five domains: (1) the randomisation process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of the outcome and (5) selection of the reported result. Two authors independently conducted the assessments, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a third author.

In addition, the certainty of evidence for the primary outcome (SSRT) was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. 58 The overall quality of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low or very low based on the following domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. The GRADE assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion to reach consensus.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. 59 Effect sizes were calculated as Hedges' g, comparing SSRT between the active tDCS and the control (sham or active) conditions before and after stimulation. For studies reporting means and standard deviations (SDs) of SSRT before and after tDCS stimulation, sample sizes and pre‐post between‐group comparisons, effect sizes were computed using the metafor package in R. 60 When only p‐values or test statistics (e.g., t and F) from between‐group comparisons were provided, effect sizes were estimated using the esc package in R, which enables computation based on reported statistics, correlation coefficients or odds ratios. 61

A random‐effects meta‐analysis was conducted to estimate the overall effect of tDCS on response inhibition, as the included studies varied in sample characteristics, stimulation parameters and study design. Given this expected heterogeneity, a random‐effects model was considered more appropriate than a fixed‐effects model, which assumes a common true effect size. 62 , 63 Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q‐statistic (QE test) and quantified by I 2. 64 To explore potential moderators of effect size, subgroup and moderator analyses were first performed by stimulation polarity (anodal vs. cathodal), followed by additional analyses of other factors. Subgroup analyses were conducted only when moderators showed significant influence on pooled outcomes, in accordance with established meta‐analytic recommendations to minimise over‐testing. 62

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess publication bias and the robustness of results. Here, we applied funnel plots, Egger's test, trim‐and‐fill, precision‐effect test–precision‐effect estimate with standard error (PET‐PEESE) and meta‐outlier detection. Funnel plots are scatterplots used in meta‐analysis to visually assess publication bias by displaying the relationship between study size and effect size; asymmetry suggests potential bias or heterogeneity. 65 Egger's test is a statistical method for detecting funnel plot asymmetry in meta‐analysis. A significant result from Egger's test suggests the presence of small‐study effects or publication bias. 66 Trim‐and‐fill adjusts for asymmetry in funnel plots by imputing missing studies and recalculating pooled effects. 67 The trim‐and‐fill procedure was applied only when Egger's test indicated significant asymmetry. 67 PET‐PEESE, regression‐based methods, detect small‐study effects and correct for potential bias in effect size estimation, 68 whereas meta‐outlier analysis identified studies exerting disproportionate influence on overall estimates. 69 The combination of these methods ensured the robustness and reliability of the meta‐analytic results.

RESULTS

Search results

The initial database search yielded 2982 studies. After removing duplicate studies, 1937 potentially relevant studies were retrieved for screening. After title and abstract screening, 59 studies were assessed in full text. After thorough assessment, 35 articles were deemed eligible for inclusion. A PRISMA flowchart of the study search and selection process is shown in figure 2.

PRISMA flowchart. The literature screening process is displayed. Databases were searched using the following keywords: ‘tDCS’ OR ‘transcranial direct current stimulation’ AND ‘response inhibition’ OR ‘inhibitory control’ OR ‘stop‐signal task’ OR ‘stop signal task’. After excluding irrelevant articles, the final sample comprised 35 studies. The cut‐off date for the publication of studies was April 2026.

Characteristics and quality assessment of included studies

A total of 71 independent effect sizes, derived from 35 studies involving 1768 participants, were included in the meta‐analysis. Publication trends indicate a steady increase in research on this topic in recent years (figure 3A). The majority (74.65%) of studies reported implementing blinding procedures, whereas 5.63% did not use blinding and 19.72% failed to specify blinding details (figure 3B). The designs included both within‐subject (45.07%) and between‐subject (54.93%) paradigms (figure 3C). The SST was primarily administered using visual stop signals (76.06%), with a smaller proportion employing auditory signals (23.94%) (figure 3D). Most studies (88.73%) examined healthy individuals, whereas 11.27% focused on clinical populations, such as individuals with ADHD, addiction or other neuropsychiatric conditions (figure 3E). Regarding stimulation characteristics, most studies used offline stimulation (81.69%), with only 18.31% employing online stimulation (figure 3F). Anodal tDCS was applied in 73.24% of effect sizes, whereas cathodal stimulation was used in 26.76% (figure 3G). Electrode size varied across studies; the most common size was 25 cm2, followed by 35 cm2 and 10 cm2 (figure 3I). Stimulation current intensity ranged from 0.50 to 2.50 mA, with 1.50 and 1.00 mA used most frequently (figure 3J). Stimulation duration ranged from 9 to 30 min, with the majority of studies applying 20 min (figure 3K). The most common anodal targets were the right DLPFC (rDLPFC), right inferior frontal cortex (rIFC) and motor‐related areas, whereas cathodal stimulation was primarily applied to the rDLPFC and motor‐related areas (figure 3L, M). The return electrode was most frequently placed over the contralateral supraorbital area (figure 3H).

We conducted a quality assessment of the included studies using the Cochrane risk of bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. Among all included studies, four were judged to have a high risk of bias, seven studies were rated as low risk, and the remaining 24 studies were assessed as having some concerns (moderate risk) (see Supporting Information S1: figure S1).

Overall effect of tDCS on response inhibition

A random‐effects meta‐analysis was conducted to assess the overall effect of tDCS on response inhibition, indexed by SSRT. Across 35 studies and 71 effect sizes, tDCS demonstrated a small but significantly facilitative effect on response inhibition (Hedges' g = −0.22, 95% confidence interval (CI) −0.34 to −0.11, p < 0.001), indicating that, on average, tDCS improves inhibitory control in SST performance. Heterogeneity analysis revealed moderate between‐study variability (Q E(70) = 188.77, p < 0.001; I 2 = 62.32%, 95% CI 46.96%–72.60%; Akaike information criterion [AIC] = 103.07), suggesting that methodological and sample differences contribute to variability in outcomes. These results warranted further moderator analyses to identify factors influencing the efficacy of tDCS on response inhibition.

Effect of anodal versus cathodal tDCS

Polarity significantly moderated the effect of tDCS on response inhibition (Q = 26.68, AIC = 82.86, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis revealed that anodal tDCS had a significant and small‐to‐moderate improvement in SSRT (Hedges' g = −0.38, 95% CI −0.51 to −0.26, p < 0.001), whereas cathodal tDCS showed a small but statistically significant detrimental effect (Hedges' g = 0.19, 95% CI 0.03–0.35, p = 0.023) (figure 4). Incorporating polarity as a moderator into the mixed‐polarity model led to markedly reduced residual heterogeneity (Q E(69) = 130.40, p < 0.001, I 2 = 47.70%, 95% CI 26.77%–62.62%, AIC = 82.86). Notably, heterogeneity for the anodal tDCS model (Q E(51) = 102.74, p < 0.001, I 2 = 50.94%, 95% CI 27.91%–67.12%, AIC = 66.45) was substantially lower than that of the mixed‐polarity model (Q E(70) = 188.77, p < 0.001; I 2 = 62.32%, 95% CI 46.96%–72.60%; AIC = 103.07), supporting the need to analyse anodal and cathodal tDCS effects separately to obtain more precise estimates.

Predefined moderators and subgroup analysis in the anodal tDCS model

Given that the cathodal tDCS exhibited only a weak effect and was based on a limited number of included studies, all subsequent moderator and subgroup analyses were conducted using the anodal tDCS model. Among the predefined moderators, stimulation montage (Q = 10.03, p = 0.040) and stimulation timing (Q = 4.10, p = 0.043) significantly influenced the effects of anodal tDCS on response inhibition. In contrast, study design, control condition, population type, stop‐signal type, return electrode montage, electrode size, stimulation intensity, stimulation duration and electric density showed no significant effects (Supporting Information S1: table S2).

Across stimulation targets, anodal tDCS over the rIFC yielded the strongest facilitative effect (g = −0.70, SE = 0.10, p < 0.001), followed by motor‐related areas (g = −0.45, SE = 0.15, p = 0.004) and the rDLPFC (g = −0.36, SE = 0.11, p < 0.001). Stimulation over the left DLPFC (lDLPFC) and other regions showed no significant influence (table 1).

Stimulation timing was categorised according to the timing of behavioural assessments in relation to tDCS administration: ‘online’ if assessed during stimulation and ‘offline’ if assessed postcompletion. Moderator analysis revealed a substantial impact of stimulation timing, with both ‘online’ (g = −0.67, SE = 0.13, p < 0.001) and ‘offline’ (g = −0.33, SE = 0.07, p < 0.001) stimulation enhancing response inhibition; however, ‘online’ stimulation exhibited a more pronounced effect (table 1). Incorporating these two significant moderators into the anodal tDCS model substantially reduced residual heterogeneity (Q E(46) = 76.98, p = 0.003; I 2 = 41.49%, 95% CI 12.49%–61.19%, AIC = 65.04), indicating improved model precision.

To further evaluate the robustness of these findings, we performed a GRADE assessment of the certainty of evidence for overall, anodal and cathodal tDCS (Supporting Information S1: table S3). The overall evidence for tDCS was rated as low certainty, primarily due to concerns regarding risk of bias and inconsistency. When stratified by polarity, the certainty of evidence for anodal tDCS was moderate, with no serious concerns regarding inconsistency, indirectness or imprecision. In contrast, the evidence for cathodal tDCS was rated as low certainty, largely due to residual heterogeneity and risk of bias.

Sensitivity analysis

The funnel plot was approximately symmetrical (Supporting Information S1: figure S2), and Egger's test indicated no significant publication bias (z = 1.18, p = 0.237); thus, the trim‐and‐fill procedure was not applied. PET‐PEESE analysis further showed no small‐study effects (PET: B = 1.14, p = 0.237; PEESE: B = 1.50, p = 0.282). The corrected effect sizes (g PET = −0.74, p = 0.016; g PEESE = −0.54, p < 0.001) were consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the results. Outlier detection using the altmeta package identified no extreme studies, 70 as all standardised residuals were below the predefined threshold.

DISCUSSION

Summary of our findings

This systematic review and meta‐analysis investigated the effects of tDCS on response inhibition as measured by the SST. Overall, tDCS exerted a modest but significant facilitative impact on response inhibition (g = −0.22). Polarity emerged as a key determinant: anodal tDCS significantly enhanced response inhibition (g = −0.38), whereas cathodal tDCS showed a small detrimental impact (g = 0.19). Moderator and subgroup analyses based on the anodal tDCS model further indicated that target sites and stimulation timing influenced efficacy, with stimulation over the rIFG and online protocols producing the strongest effects. These findings highlight the superior effectiveness of anodal tDCS and emphasise the importance of polarity‐specific and parameter‐optimised protocols in future research.

Polarity‐specific effects

The overall effect observed in the mixed‐polarity model (g = −0.22) aligns with prior meta‐analyses reporting small yet significant improvements in inhibitory control after tDCS. 55 However, these earlier studies did not differentiate polarity‐specific effects, likely because of combining heterogeneous task paradigms such as the go/no‐go (GNG) task and SST. Theoretically, these paradigms rely on distinct neural mechanisms: The SST primarily engages a specialised rIFG‐based stopping network, whereas GNG tasks recruit broader prefrontal regions involved in response selection and general inhibition. 50 , 51 , 71 Lasogga et al. 48 further demonstrated that tDCS effects on SST performance are typically stronger than those on GNG performance, emphasising the importance of task‐specific analysis. 48 By focusing exclusively on the SST, our research isolated the differential effects of anodal and cathodal tDCS, enabling a more precise mechanistic interpretation.

The polarity‐specific pattern observed here aligns with established neurophysiological principles. Anodal tDCS depolarises neuronal membranes, increases cortical excitability and reduces the inhibitory neurotransmitter gamma‐aminobutyric acid, thereby facilitating neural activity in task‐relevant regions. In contrast, cathodal stimulation generally induces hyperpolarisation and decreases excitability and is associated with reduced glutamate levels. 17 Our results provide quantitative support for these mechanisms. Notably, anodal stimulation over the rIFG and pre‐SMA has been shown to improve stopping efficiency by modulating excitability within core inhibitory networks. 72 In contrast, cathodal tDCS may fail to exert measurable inhibitory effects, potentially due to insufficient intensity. Prior work indicates that higher‐intensity cathodal stimulation can paradoxically increase excitability, particularly during cognitive tasks that engage prefrontal areas. 73 The 1–2‐mA range used in most included studies may have been inadequate to elicit robust inhibitory modulation.

Methodological factors may also account for the limited efficacy of cathodal tDCS. Supraorbital return electrode placement in bilateral montages can produce diffuse current flow or unintended stimulation of adjacent prefrontal regions such as the left DLPFC, counteracting inhibitory effects. 74 , 75 Moreover, cathodal tDCS exhibits stronger state dependency—its outcomes depend on task context, baseline activation and individual neurocognitive profiles. Variability in these factors likely contributed to inconsistent results. 76 , 77 Additionally, anodal tDCS was more frequently applied over the rIFG, a critical inhibitory hub, whereas cathodal targeting of this region was rare. The effectiveness of tDCS is significantly influenced by specific neuronal targets, and the limited number of studies applying cathodal stimulation over the rIFG may have constrained the observed efficacy.

Overall, the enhanced inhibitory performance under anodal tDCS underscores its potential for cognitive enhancement, whereas the limited effects of cathodal stimulation highlight the need for further systematic investigation into intensity, electrode placement and individual differences underlying polarity‐specific modulation.

Influence of target stimulation site

This meta‐analysis indicates that the efficacy of anodal tDCS in enhancing response inhibition depends critically on the target site, consistent with neurophysiological models of inhibitory control. In line with prior evidence, our findings demonstrate that anodal tDCS of the rIFC produced the most pronounced improvement in inhibition. The rIFC plays a pivotal role in regulating activity within the pre‐SMA and basal ganglia circuits, which are central to stopping behaviour. 78 Lesion studies confirm that rIFG damage impairs inhibitory capability, whereas its neurostimulation improves inhibitory function.

Anodal tDCS targeting motor‐related areas, including the pre‐SMA and primary motor cortex, also significantly improved response inhibition. The pre‐SMA serves as a critical interface between the rIFC and M1, initiating motor inhibition. 79 Combined stimulation of the rIFC and pre‐SMA has been shown to further strengthen inhibitory control. By contrast, stimulation of the rDLPFC yielded smaller yet significant effects, likely reflecting its role in higher‐order cognitive regulation of inhibitory demands. 55 , 80 Conversely, tDCS applied to other cortical areas (e.g., lDLPFC, orbitofrontal, medial frontal or parietal regions) failed to elicit significant benefits. These findings underscore the functional specificity of the rIFC–motor network and highlight the need for precise or dual‐site targeting (e.g., rIFC–pre‐SMA) to optimise tDCS efficacy for inhibitory control enhancement.

Role of stimulation timing

This meta‐analysis revealed that stimulation timing significantly modulated the effects of anodal tDCS on response inhibition. Both online and offline anodal stimulation improved response inhibition; however, online stimulation had a more pronounced impact, consistent with prior research. Online tDCS is administered concurrently with task performance, allowing real‐time interaction between externally applied currents and ongoing neural activity. Mechanistically, it modulates neuronal membrane potentials—depolarising neurons under the anode and hyperpolarising those under the cathode—thereby enhancing task‐relevant cortical excitability. 17 , 81 This real‐time ‘gating’ effect likely facilitates recruitment of inhibitory control networks, such as the rIFG, during response inhibition tasks such as the SST. 48 , 82 In contrast, offline tDCS primarily induces neuroplastic after‐effects, mediated by long‐term potentiation‐ or long‐term depression‐like mechanisms. Although these effects can persist beyond stimulation, they are more variable due to their dependence on post‐stimulation task engagement, individual brain states and the neurobiological profile of the stimulated region. 83 The weaker yet significant offline effects may therefore reflect inconsistencies in translating tDCS‐induced plasticity into behaviour. Previous studies indicate that offline effects may be influenced by task attributes, individual differences and the interplay between stimulation parameters and cognitive requirements. In certain instances, offline stimulation may fail to adequately engage the intended neuronal circuits, resulting in reduced or inconsistent outcomes. 84 , 85

Future studies should examine whether repeated online or offline tDCS sessions yield cumulative or enduring benefits in inhibitory control. Although single‐session online stimulation acutely enhances performance, its long‐term durability remains uncertain. Longitudinal designs are needed to determine whether repeated interventions induce progressive neuroplastic changes, particularly in populations with impaired inhibitory function.

Clinical application

Deficits in response inhibition are a transdiagnostic hallmark across multiple psychiatric and neurological disorders, including ADHD, 5 OCD, 6 substance use disorders 7 and impulse‐control‐related eating disorders. 8 Accordingly, our findings hold strong translational relevance for clinical applications. This meta‐analysis demonstrates that single‐session anodal tDCS, particularly when applied online and over the rIFG, significantly enhances inhibitory control, as measured by the SST—an effect particularly relevant to conditions characterised by impaired self‐regulation.

In ADHD, impaired inhibitory control is a well‐established cognitive marker, 5 and prefrontal tDCS has been shown to reduce impulsivity. 86 , 87 Similarly, in substance use disorders, impaired inhibition contributes to craving and relapse, 88 and prefrontal tDCS has been reported to attenuate craving‐related neural activation while improving inhibitory responses. 89 , 90 Our findings highlight the importance of stimulation timing (online > offline), polarity (anodal > cathodal) and targeting (rIFG) in optimising clinical protocols. Furthermore, populations with disordered eating or obesity may also benefit from enhanced inhibitory control via tDCS. For example, Schroeder et al. found that anodal stimulation over the rIFG improved SST performance in restrained eaters, indicating the potential for cognitive normalisation even outside psychiatric contexts. However, as To et al. noted, bilateral prefrontal activation may paradoxically impair control, underscoring the need for precise and task‐congruent stimulation strategies. 91

Despite promising evidence, several challenges remain for clinical translation, including parameter optimisation, accounting for interindividual neuroanatomical variability and establishing the durability and generalisability of effects. Although single‐session tDCS provides mechanistic insights, multisession or longitudinal interventions are likely required for sustained therapeutic benefits. In sum, these findings support the development of individualised, cognition‐focused tDCS interventions targeting inhibitory control while emphasising the necessity of systematic translational research to bridge the gap between laboratory efficacy and real‐world clinical utility.

Heterogeneity

This meta‐analysis identified a small but significant overall effect of tDCS on response inhibition, accompanied by moderate between‐study heterogeneity. Such variability suggests that methodological differences across studies substantially influence tDCS efficacy, consistent with prior meta‐analyses reporting variability in cognitive outcomes. 55 Importantly, polarity emerged as a key factor: Incorporating polarity as a moderator significantly reduced residual heterogeneity, supporting distinct effects of anodal and cathodal tDCS on inhibitory control. Moreover, heterogeneity within the anodal model was notably lower than that in the mixed‐polarity model, highlighting the need for polarity‐specific analysis. Beyond polarity, stimulation montage and timing were identified as additional contributors to variability. Including these moderators further reduced heterogeneity, reinforcing their explanatory value. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that tDCS effects on response inhibition are shaped by a complex interplay of polarity, electrode placement and stimulation timing. Future research should systematically account for these parameters to enhance methodological consistency and improve reproducibility across studies.

Limitations

The studies included in this meta‐analysis have several limitations. First, residual heterogeneity, despite moderator analysis, indicates the potential impact of unmeasured variables, such as interindividual differences in cognitive abilities, neuroanatomy and tDCS responsiveness. Prior studies have shown that baseline neural excitability and genetic polymorphisms significantly influence individual responses to tDCS. 92 Future research should incorporate personalised factors to improve the precision of neuromodulatory interventions.

Second, most included studies focused on healthy participants, limiting generalisability to clinical populations. Our aim was to evaluate the generalisable effects of tDCS on SST performance across diverse groups rather than disorder‐specific outcomes. Subgroup analysis by population was not feasible due to (1) the small number of clinical studies, (2) our task‐based focus on core inhibitory mechanisms independent of diagnosis and (3) the finding that population type was not a significant moderator of heterogeneity. Nevertheless, rigorous clinical trials are needed to determine whether tDCS exerts comparable or distinct effects across psychiatric and neurological conditions and to define optimised, disorder‐specific parameters.

Third, interactions among key stimulation parameters (e.g., intensity, polarity and montage) were not systematically explored. Prior work indicates that current intensity can reverse expected polarity effects, with cathodal stimulation potentially enhancing excitability at higher intensities. 73 Similarly, bilateral montages may produce compensatory or opposing effects depending on electrode placement. Future research should employ factorial designs to disentangle these interactions and refine stimulation protocols.

Additionally, this review excluded non‐English publications and grey literature, which, although ensuring methodological rigour, may introduce language or publication bias. Broader inclusion in future meta‐analyses may help mitigate this limitation.

Finally, only single‐session tDCS studies were analysed, leaving the long‐term effects of repeated stimulation unclear. Repeated sessions may induce cumulative neuroplastic changes and more durable improvements in inhibitory control, especially when paired with cognitive training. 93 For example, Jeong et al. showed that multisession bilateral DLPFC stimulation improved response inhibition and modulated frontostriatal connectivity in individuals with problematic alcohol use. 87 Similarly, lasting behavioural and neural effects were reported after multitarget high‐definition tDCS in healthy participants, highlighting the importance of stimulation dose and network targeting. 94 Future longitudinal studies integrating multisession designs and neuroimaging biomarkers are essential to clarify the long‐term efficacy and clinical translation of tDCS for enhancing inhibitory control.

CONCLUSIONS

This meta‐analysis suggests that anodal tDCS is associated with improved response inhibition, as measured by the SST, whereas cathodal tDCS shows a small detrimental effect. Variability in effects across stimulation targets was observed, consistent with the involvement of neurocognitive networks underlying response inhibition. In addition, online stimulation appeared to produce greater effects than offline stimulation, indicating potential state‐dependent modulation of neural circuits. However, given the heterogeneity and methodological variability among the included studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Overall, the results suggest that anodal tDCS may have potential as a neuromodulatory approach for enhancing response inhibition, although further research is needed to establish its clinical applicability. Future studies should investigate the long‐term effects of repeated stimulation, optimise stimulation parameters and evaluate its therapeutic potential in clinical populations.
