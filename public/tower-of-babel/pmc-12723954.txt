A pre-post evaluation of the Family Engagement in Research Course: does it increase perceived knowledge, abilities, and confidence in family engagement in neurodevelopmental disability and child health research?
Authors: Andrea Cross, Alice Kelen Soper, Donna Thomson, Connie Putterman, Dayle McCauley, Myanca Rodrigues, Patricia Solomon, Christine Chambers, Jan Willem Gorter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Family Engagement in Research (FER) Course launched in 2018 for researchers and family members to co-learn the principles and practice of engagement in neurodevelopmental disability and child health research. The first six cohorts of the FER Course (2018–2021) were part of a pre-post evaluation to assess learners’ perceptions and satisfaction of the FER Course, and the impact on their perceived knowledge, abilities, attitudes, and self-confidence in FER. This paper reports on the findings from this pre-post study.MethodsSixty-nine self-selected researchers and family members enrolled in the course completed pre-and post-evaluations; satisfaction was evaluated after the course only (n = 96). Data collection involved surveys with 10-point Likert Scale questions about experiences of the course, perceived knowledge, abilities, attitudes towards, and self-confidence in family engagement in research, and overall satisfaction. We conducted Wilcoxon rank sum to compare differences between researchers and family members’ responses about self-confidence to engage, with the Wilcoxon signed rank tests used to evaluate change in outcomes from before to after the course. Open-ended survey responses were analyzed descriptively.ResultsThere were statistically significant increases in survey respondents’ perceived knowledge, abilities, and self-confidence to engage in research. Positive attitudes towards FER were rated highly at baseline and post-test, with no significant change. Respondents reported high levels of satisfaction with the FER Course.ConclusionsThe FER Course meets the need for training both family members and researchers, so that they are equipped with the perceived knowledge, abilities, and confidence to collaborate in neurodevelopmental disability and child health research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-025-00815-y.

Background

Patient-oriented research (POR) is an approach to health research that actively involves patients, families, and caregivers as partners throughout the research process [4]. Like patient and public involvement (PPI), POR emphasizes collaboration and partnership, ensuring that research is guided by the priorities of those it aims to benefit [2]. These types of approaches (e.g., POR and PPI) can improve the relevance and application of research findings, ultimately enhancing health outcomes [4]​. In the field of neurodevelopmental disability (NDD) and child health, engaging families as partners throughout the research process is particularly important [14, 15]. Family partners provide critical insights from their lived and living experiences, which can inform research to address challenges and foster innovation in NDD and child health research. Informed by lived experience, researchers can gain a deeper understanding of their field and good rapport with knowledge users. For family partners, engaging in research can be empowering through active contribution to research that affects their lives [3].

In 2011, Canada launched the Strategy for Patient Oriented Research (SPOR) to build capacity for POR across the country [4]. Since the inception of SPOR, there has been an increasing emphasis on embedding POR across all health research. To support POR, training programs have emerged, such as the Patient-Oriented Research Curriculum in Child Health (PORCCH) [10], Partners in Research (PiR) [19], and Foundations in Patient Oriented Research [1]. However, at the time, the growing demand for POR was not matched by adequate training opportunities, particularly in child health research. In 2017, researchers and parent leaders from Canadian childhood disability research organizations came together and mutually recognized a lack of training opportunities in POR. Specifically, there was no formal training focused on engagement with families and researchers in NDD research. With a goal to support meaningful engagement of families in NDD research, two parents (DT and CP) and researchers (AC, DM, PS, and JWG) partnered to co-develop the Family Engagement in Research (FER) Course [6]. The first cohort was launched in 2018 by CanChild Centre for Childhood-Onset Disability Research in partnership with and co-funded by Kids Brain Health Network, and McMaster University Centre for Continuing Education.

The FER Course is a 10-week, 30-hour, online McMaster University certificate of completion course that is co-delivered by family partners and researchers and trains family members and researchers together. Every component of the FER Course is done in partnership between researchers and family partners [6]. This is an essential component and strength of our program, which aligns with our core values of authenticity, inclusivity, collaboration, and mutual respect.

Coursework involves four online 2-hour synchronous (in real-time) group discussions, individual review of weekly course materials, participation in weekly small group activities, and an experiential learning component where learners work together in small groups to co-create a tool or resource on family engagement. Through this group project, the FER Course fosters community- and relationship-building, which are essential for successful engagement in health research and is what sets the FER Course apart from other training initiatives. Figure 1 summarizes the course delivery, course format, course topics, group work, and core values. For additional details about the development of the course and details on the content and delivery format please see Cross et al. [6]. The FER Course started with an initial project with six cohorts (2018–2021) and was then scaled. As of April 2025, we have trained >700 researchers and family members in the field of NDD and child health.

We recognized that it was integral to evaluate the FER Course to ensure it was meeting the training needs of learners. Furthermore, we wanted to ensure that the FER Course developed capacity for family engagement in NDD and child health research by equipping learners with the knowledge, abilities, and confidence to partner in research. By assessing course outcomes, we can refine and enhance course content, delivery, and overall design to better meet the needs of learners. The first six cohorts of the FER Course were part of a pre-post evaluation to assess learners’ perceptions of and satisfaction with the FER Course, and the impact on their perceived knowledge, perceived abilities, attitudes, and self-confidence in FER. This paper reports on the findings from this pre-post study.

Study design

A pre-post study design was used to evaluate: (i) learners’ perceptions of and satisfaction with the FER Course; and (ii) the impact on perceived knowledge, perceived abilities, attitudes, and self-confidence in family engagement in research. Data were collected from 2018 to 2021 from the first six cohorts as part of the research study. Ethics approval was received in 2018 by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (project 5360).

This study was conducted as part of AC’s post-doctoral fellowship that was supervised by an integrated team of parents (DT and CP) and researchers (JWG, PS, CC). DT and CP co-developed and co-delivered the FER Course and were active decision-makers throughout all parts of the research process (e.g., planning the study, interpreting the findings, disseminating the findings, etc.) The patient and public involvement (PPI) is reported in the GRIPP2 short form checklist [17] (supplementary file).

Sample and recruitment

Participation in the study was voluntary. Learners (families and researchers) in the first six cohorts of the FER Course (2018–2021) could self-select whether they participated in the study, with no effect on their involvement in the course. FER Course cohort size ranged from 18 to 34 learners per cohort. We increased cohort size over time. At the time of this study, the course was funded by Kids Brain Health Network, and as such the course was offered free of charge to all learners. The course was open to any family caregiver of a child with a NDD or chronic health condition and researchers in the field of NDD and child health. At the time, preference was given to Canadian applicants with a few spaces in each cohort open to international applicants. For researchers, PhD students and post-doctoral fellows were prioritized. Individuals were recruited for the FER Course through sharing information on social media, through Kids Brain Health Network and CanChild’s newsletter and through word of mouth.

One week prior to the start of the course, all FER Course learners were invited to participate in the study via email, which included the letter of information and recruitment flyer with information about the study. Learners who agreed to participate were asked to complete an online informed consent form after reviewing the letter of information and recruitment flyer. Consent was sought again in the same manner for the post-survey since all learners were eligible to participate, regardless of whether they had done the pre-survey.

Data collection instrument and process

The data collection instrument was an online survey including both closed- and open-ended questions. The survey was developed collaboratively by family and research team members (AC, DT, CP, DM, PS, JWG). The survey questions were designed to align closely with the course goals and learning objectives. Demographic information was collected in the pre-survey. The online survey was administered through REDCap from September 2018 to April 2021. The survey was sent to learners enrolled in the FER Course one week prior to the start of the course and immediately post-course completion.

Ten-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 10 = strongly agree) questions were used to rate the extent of agreement with a series of questions (statements) pertaining to perceived knowledge, abilities, attitudes, and self-confidence. To measure perceived knowledge, learners responded to questions about their perceived familiarity, understanding of the meaning and value of FER, and perceived knowledge about FER. Abilities were measured by the extent to which learners felt they achieved the learning objectives. Attitudes towards FER were evaluated through three questions related to beliefs about its importance, how families can meaningfully contribute, and how family engagement can improve research outcomes. Learners reported self-confidence to engage in research as a family member or researcher on a single 10-point Likert scale. In the post-survey, learners rated their overall satisfaction with the course on a scale of one to ten and elaborated on their rating through open-ended questions.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize demographic information and learners’ expectations and satisfaction with the course. Given that data were ordinal and not normally distributed, the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to determine whether there were statistically significant changes between scores at baseline and at post-course completion. The two-sample Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to determine whether there were statistically significant between-group differences across family members and researchers. To account for multiple comparisons across the four primary pooled outcome domains (perceived knowledge and abilities, attitudes, and self-confidence), a Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of 0.0125 (i.e., 0.05/4). All other inferential analyses, including item-level and subscale-specific tests, were considered exploratory and interpreted accordingly. These additional analyses were included to provide descriptive insight into course-specific content areas and to support future refinement of the FER curriculum. Stata SE Version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) was used for all statistical tests. Qualitative data from an open-ended question asking to elaborate on learners’ satisfaction ratings were analyzed using descriptive content analysis [18] by AKS and AC. Both read the open-ended responses and together through conversations identified codes. Learners (both family members and researchers) who chose to participate in the study hereafter will be referred to as survey respondents or respondents.

Results

Survey respondents

Of the 163 FER Course learners from the first six cohorts, 49 (30.1%) completed the pre-survey and 27 (16.6%) completed the post-survey and 69 (42.3%) completed both the pre- and post-survey. Thus, a total of 145 learners completed at least one survey (89.0%). For all outcomes, other than satisfaction, data were analyzed for those who completed both the pre- and post-surveys (n = 69) because the evaluation focused on change in outcomes from before to after course completion. For satisfaction, we analyzed all 96 responses (58.9%).

Demographics

Sixty-nine people completed both the pre- and post- survey across the first six cohorts (2018–2021). Respondents were located across eight Canadian provinces (n = 62), the United States (n = 4), and one from France, New Zealand, and the Netherlands. Most Canadians were located in Ontario (n = 37/62, 59.7%) and Alberta (n = 11/62, 17.7%). Forty-one (59.4%) were researchers, 23 (33.3%) were family members, and five (7.2%) possessed both researcher and family member roles. The majority were women (n = 63/69, 91.3%).

Family member respondents:

All family members who participated in the study were women. Family members had a range of education levels from a high school diploma to a doctoral degree (Table 1). The majority of family members had post-secondary education (n = 22/24, 91.7%) and one did not respond. Six family members (n = 6/25, 24.0%) reported their main occupation was caring for their family, while 19 reported in addition to caring for their family they were working (n = 15/25, 76.0%), going to school (n = 3/25, 12.0%), or retired (n = 1/25, 4.0%).

Survey respondent background in patient/family engagement in research

Respondents were asked about their past experience partnering in research and prior training in family engagement. Half of the researchers had previously engaged family members as partners in their research, and just over half (n = 12/22, 54.6%) of family members had prior experience engaging in research. Most respondents (n = 56/66, 84.8%) did not have prior training in FER before completing the FER Course. Ten reported having prior training which included the PORCCH (Patient-Oriented Research Curriculum in Child Health) e-learning modules [10], Partners in Research (PiR) course [5], the Foundations in Patient Oriented Research Certification Modules offered by CIHR [20], Institute of Health, Policy, Management, and Evaluation Workshop on Patient Participation in Research, and Quebec SPOR Support Unit Module 1 Basis of patient engagement, and conference workshops, such as Clinical Trials Ontario.

Course satisfaction

Survey respondents were asked about their satisfaction with the FER Course by rating it on a scale of 1 (lowest level of satisfaction) to 10 (highest level of satisfaction) and through an open-ended question to elaborate on their rating. The mean satisfaction was 8.5/10 (n = 95; SD = 1.20) with a median of 9/10 (IQR = 1; range 5–10). Through descriptive content analysis [18], the open-ended comments (Table 3) provided further insight into respondents’ satisfaction with various components of the course, including the instructors, content, format, group project, learning environment/community, and overall satisfaction.

The group project to develop a knowledge translation (KT) tool was consistently reported as a highlight and opportunity for applying the theory of FER into practice. A researcher explained that “the way the instructors paired us with the [family partner] was really something that I thought was novel and an awesome opportunity, which I think added a richness to the experience that I wasn’t anticipating and that I enjoyed.” A family member elaborated on their satisfaction with the course, stating that they “thoroughly enjoyed the partner project.”

The workload was described by 14 respondents (7 researchers and 7 family members) as the primary reason for any dissatisfaction with the course, describing it as a “little bit heavy at times” or “overwhelming”. Reflections shared were that the reading requirements could be overwhelming and that the anticipated time commitment of three hours/week was an underestimation. For example, a family member (satisfaction rated 6) explained that “the readings and the homework exceeded the 3 hours that I thought I could devote to the course.” Other dissatisfaction was due to technological challenges within the learning platform, described as “bit clunky and didn’t facilitate easy communication.” There is also an expectation that learners post and respond to peers on a discussion board, and two found it difficult to “keep up with.”

Perceived knowledge and perceived abilities in FER

Perceived knowledge of FER

Survey respondents were asked to rate the extent to which they agreed with four statements pertaining to perceived knowledge, which included familiarity with FER, an understanding of what FER means, the value and benefits of FER, and overall perceived knowledge of FER (Table 4). The four variables pertaining to perceived knowledge were pooled together for a total score on a scale of 1–40. The baseline median score was 27/40 (IQR = 12; range 7–40) and at post-course completion, the median was 36/40 (IQR = 5; range 23–40). There was a statistically significant increase in total perceived knowledge about FER at post-course completion (z = 6.48, p < 0.004).

Learning objectives

To measure whether the learning objectives were met, survey respondents were asked to rate the extent to which they agreed with their abilities to demonstrate or perform each objective (Table 5). The learning objectives included the ability to: (1) define and describe the benefits of FER; (2) identify and understand how to engage throughout each step of the research process; (3) discuss role and responsibilities on integrated team; (4) understand the ethics surrounding FER; (5) recognize barriers and facilitators and the strategies to support FER; (6) understand and utilize tools and resources to implement and evaluate FER; and (7) communicate ideas related to FER verbally and in writing. There was a statistically significant increase in respondents’ perceived ability to achieve each learning objective.

Survey respondents reported that the perceived knowledge and perceived abilities gained from the course prepared them to apply the concepts of FER into practice. For example, a researcher commented that they felt they had the perceived abilities and knowledge to adequately collaborate with families throughout the research process. A family member explained that they “learned a lot more than I expected. I thought FER was limited to some of the phases of a research project. I learned it is possible to have FER throughout every phase, as well as how to engage families in research.” A researcher who felt knowledgeable in FER prior to the course (rated 7/10 at baseline), still felt they learned a lot from the course afterwards (rated 9/10 at post-course completion). They explained that “I am very much an advocate for the value of integrating different expertise and experiences and I was interested in the topic; I had no idea how much I still needed to learn.”

Attitudes and beliefs about FER

There were no significant differences in attitudes towards family engagement for respondents from baseline to post-course completion (Table 6). Attitudes were consistently rated very high (Likert scale of 1–10, where 10 is the highest score), with favourable feelings towards its importance in NDD research, how family partners can contribute meaningfully, and how it can improve research. Given the already high baseline (on a 10-point Likert scale) of positive attitudes among respondents prior to the FER Course the potential for detecting further improvement was limited by a ceiling effect.

Self-confidence in FER

Researchers were asked about their self-confidence in their ability to engage patient/family members in research (Table 7). Similarly, family members were asked about their self-confidence in their ability to partner on research projects. The only significant difference found between family members and researchers was with respect to self-confidence. At baseline, family members were significantly more confident in their abilities to partner in research than researchers were to engage patient/family partners (z-score = 4.08 and p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in self-confidence between family members and researchers at post-course completion. From pre- to post-course completion, there was a significant difference in self-confidence for both researchers and family members, with a median of 8.5 (family members) and 8 (researchers) at post-course completion. A family member commented on their self-confidence from the course, reporting that they “feel so much more confident in participating in research moving forward.” Similarly, a researcher commented how “the framework is already built on collaboration between families and researchers, which makes us more confident in the process of applying this approach and expands our network.”

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of the FER Course on perceived knowledge, perceived abilities, attitudes, and self-confidence in family engagement in NDD and child health research. Our findings indicate that there were statistically significant increases in both perceived knowledge and perceived abilities to practice FER among survey respondents. Additionally, the results demonstrated that family members and researchers experienced a significant increase in their self-confidence to partner in research. It is important to note that the median self-confidence level prior to the course was relatively high and that family members entered the course feeling significantly more confident than researchers. These family members may be considered early adopters of family engagement in research [7], which is also reflected by the fact that over half entered the course with previous experience partnering in research.

Other POR training programs (e.g., PORCCH and PiR) also reported significant increases in similar outcomes in perceived knowledge, perceived abilities, and self-confidence to partner in research [5, 10, 19]. Walsh et al. [19] found significant increases in perceived knowledge and self-efficacy to engage in research after patients, caregivers, and researchers completed the self-paced PORCCH e-learning modules. Likewise, the PiR 2-month online course demonstrated significant increases in perceived knowledge and self-efficacy to engage in POR [5]. The PORCCH modules, PiR Course, and FER Course were launched around the same period (2016–2018), which aligns with the growing attention towards the importance of POR in Canada and the need for training in POR. Education in POR is essential for capacity-building [16] and the growing availability of training opportunities is a testament to the recognition of the importance of these initiatives.

In comparison to the other POR training programs, one unique component of the FER Course is the experiential, community building, and co-learning approach [13]. In the FER Course, learners are placed in small groups and over the 10-weeks they work together to develop a KT tool on family engagement, culminating with a presentation about the process of collaborating throughout the course. The opportunity to apply the theory that they are learning and to practice engagement by working together on a project has been reported to be one of the highlights of the course. Some learners in the course have published their KT tools [9, 11] and many groups have co-presented on their KT tools at conferences.

Overall, there were high levels of satisfaction with the course. Over time, learners have provided suggestions for improvements which we continue to integrate on an ongoing basis. Notably, changes were made to the FER Course after the first six cohorts. For example, we have streamlined the workload by reducing the number of weekly required readings and focusing on one required reading accompanied by a lay summary. Survey respondents reported challenges with the original learning platform, and so we created our own Moodle-based FER Course Learning Platform. The platform is more user friendly and offers accessibility features where users can customize text size, contrast, etc. We continue to review the curriculum after the six cohorts through quality improvement evaluations. Course improvements post the first six cohorts include adding simulations [12, 13] and enhancing the accessibility of the course through ensuring all course materials are screen-reader friendly and adding lay summaries to all course readings and resources [6].

There are a few limitations to the study. First, a limitation of this study is the use of self-developed survey items, which were not formally assessed for psychometric properties, such as validity or reliability. As such, the precision and generalizability of the measures remain uncertain. However, the surveys were developed collaboratively by the team with lived and academic expertise in FER and were designed to align closely with the course objectives. Second, ceiling effects with respect to attitudes made it difficult to examine changes in attitudes towards FER since respondents rated attitudes high at baseline. Third, while we applied a conservative Bonferroni correction to our primary pooled outcomes, the exploratory nature of subscale and item-level comparisons should be noted and considered in the interpretation of the results. Fourth, the generalizability of the findings is limited because many survey respondents had prior experience partnering in research and were highly educated. We recognize that the findings may differ if researchers and family members are entering the course with less education and experience partnering in research. Lastly, there is a potential for self-selection bias in terms of which FER learners participated in the study.

Future directions and conclusions

The FER Course has been shown to be a valuable POR training program with hundreds of people trained worldwide, and emerging evidence that the FER Course has a positive impact on learners’ perceived knowledge, perceived abilities, and self-confidence to partner in research. In this way, we have achieved our goals for the course to serve as a means of building capacity for FER in NDD and child health research. The findings reported in this paper are limited to short-term outcomes. To explore the longevity of the impacts on the increase perceived knowledge, perceived abilities, and self-confidence, we have conducted a qualitative study that follows course graduates over the span of two years after completing the course.

The course’s scalability across Canada and internationally presents exciting opportunities. To date, the FER Course has scaled through both sponsored cohorts, where institutions fund entire cohorts (e.g., Azrieli Accelerator Program at the University of Calgary and the Brighter Path Project at McMaster University) and licensed cohorts (e.g., University Medical Center Utrecht in the Netherlands, Healthy Trajectories Child and Youth Disability Research Hub in Australia) where, after training, institutions independently deliver the course. We have also created two additional training courses including foundational training (Family Engagement Fundamentals) and an advanced-level certificate of completion course (Family Engagement Leadership Academy). Furthermore, members of the FER team along with youth partners have explored training needs of youth and young adults with lived experience of disability [8]. With funding from the CIHR Healthy Youth Catalyst Grant, we are working with youth to pilot a youth version of the FER Course in 2025 and 2026. The continued delivery of the FER Course, coupled with the delivery of additional training opportunities and a growing international Community Network, highlight our commitment to advancing the field of youth and family engagement in health research through capacity building initiatives.
