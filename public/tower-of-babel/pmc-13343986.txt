Piezo1 in the central nervous system: decoding the mechanical signature of neuroinflammation
Authors: Guihong Shi, Rui Tan, Yiyao Cao, Xiujue Zheng, Renya Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroinflammation has historically been viewed through a biochemical lens, governed by cytokines and danger signals. While this paradigm has provided foundational insights, integrating the physical dimension of tissue stiffening, hemodynamic shear stress, and compressive forces offers a more complete understanding of the pathological microenvironment of the central nervous system (CNS). In this Review, we examine Piezo1 as an important mechanosensitive channel that can translate such physical cues into neuroinflammatory responses. We synthesize emerging evidence showing that Piezo1-mediated calcium signaling can regulate activation, migration, and metabolic reprogramming in resident CNS cells and infiltrating immune populations. Importantly, the strength of evidence is not uniform across all cell types: genetic studies provide the strongest support in microglia, oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, and endothelial cells, whereas roles in astrocytes, dendritic cells, and T cells remain more context-dependent and emerging.Furthermore, we outline the distinct mechanical signatures across major CNS pathologies and discuss how Piezo1 may shape context-dependent outcomes—from plaque-associated microglial responses in Alzheimer’s disease to mechanically restricted remyelination in multiple sclerosis, acute vascular or parenchymal injury in stroke and trauma, and emerging links to seizure-associated swelling and hyperexcitability in epilepsy. Finally, we propose an integrated “Mechanical Alterations–Piezo1–Immune Regulation” framework and discuss how mechano-therapeutic strategies might be used to modulate, rather than uniformly enhance or suppress, neuroinflammatory responses.Keywords: Piezo1, Neuroinflammation, Mechanotransduction, Neurovascular unit, Microglia, Blood–brain barrier, CNS disease

Introduction

Neuroinflammation serves as a convergent pathological mechanism underlying a broad spectrum of central nervous system (CNS) disorders, ranging from acute injuries like stroke and trauma to chronic neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease and multiple sclerosis [1–3]. Historically, the initiation and propagation of neuroinflammation have been viewed primarily through a biochemical lens, governed by the release of danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), cytokines, and chemokines [4]. While this “chemical-centric” paradigm has yielded foundational insights, an integrated mechanobiological view of brain function and disease provides a more complete perspective, because the CNS is physically enclosed, mechanically sensitive, and subject to distinct changes in tissue stiffness, pressure, and fluid dynamics during pathology [5].

Accumulating evidence suggests that the neural microenvironment is not a static background but a dynamic mechanical landscape. Pathological states impose distinct physical forces: acute injuries generate rapid tissue deformation and shear stress [6]; cerebrovascular diseases disrupt hemodynamic forces at the blood-brain barrier [7]; and chronic degeneration leads to progressive extracellular matrix remodeling and altered tissue stiffness [8]. Crucially, these mechanical cues are not merely passive byproducts of tissue damage. Instead, they actively modulate the activation thresholds, migratory behaviors, and effector functions of both resident glial cells and infiltrating peripheral immune cells [9].

To understand how physical forces are translated into immune regulation, mechanotransduction provides the critical theoretical framework. Central to this process is Piezo1, a mechanosensitive non-selective cation channel [10]. Unlike its homolog Piezo2, which is specialized for sensory perception, Piezo1 is expressed across multiple non-sensory cell types relevant to neuroinflammation, including microglia, astrocytes, endothelial cells, and peripheral leukocytes, acting as a pivotal transducer of cellular mechanical stress [11–13]. Upon sensing membrane tension or curvature, Piezo1 facilitates rapid calcium influx, thereby coupling extracellular mechanical stimuli to intracellular inflammatory signaling pathways [12].

In this review, we synthesize emerging evidence linking mechanical microenvironmental changes to neuroinflammation across diverse CNS pathologies. We examine the cell-type-specific roles of Piezo1 in resident CNS cells and peripheral immune populations, highlighting how this channel integrates mechanical signals to regulate processes such as phagocytosis, cytokine release, and trans-endothelial migration [14, 15]. By constructing an integrated framework of “Mechanical Alterations–Piezo1–Immune Regulation,” we aim to elucidate the mechanobiological underpinnings of CNS diseases and discuss the potential of targeting Piezo1 to therapeutically reprogram the neuroinflammatory response.

Mechano-immunology as an integrated perspective in neuroinflammation

Historically, neuroinflammation has been conceptualized through a predominantly biochemical lens, portraying the CNS as a privileged immunological niche orchestrated principally by soluble mediators. Within this classical framework, inflammatory cascades are initiated when pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on resident glial cells detect DAMPs released from injured or degenerating neurons [16]. Following detection, microglia and astrocytes—the intrinsic sentinels of the CNS—propagate the signal through the release of cytokines, chemokines, and the assembly of inflammasomes, eventually orchestrating leukocyte recruitment across the BBB [1]. This chemocentric framework has been instrumental in elucidating the molecular drivers of pathology and remains foundational to our understanding of CNS inflammation. At the same time, immune responses unfold within a physical microenvironment whose mechanical properties can modulate how these canonical pathways are engaged [5].

The CNS is also a mechanically dynamic tissue enclosed within rigid anatomical boundaries and characterized by distinctive viscoelastic properties. Emerging evidence from mechanobiology indicates that innate immune cells are not merely chemical sensors but are also mechanosensitive cells capable of probing substrate stiffness, hydrostatic pressure, and fluid forces [17]. Even modest alterations in tissue mechanics can restructure the cytoskeleton of glial cells and thereby modulate activation thresholds, migration, and effector functions in synergy with biochemical cues [9]. In this sense, mechanical forces are best viewed as context-defining or costimulatory inputs rather than as competitors of canonical cytokine-, PRR-, or inflammasome-driven signaling.

Pathological states impose distinct remodeling of this mechanical landscape, creating disease-relevant mechanical microenvironments. In acute conditions such as traumatic brain injury (TBI) and hemorrhagic stroke, neural tissues are subjected to rapid deformation, shear stress, and supraphysiological compression [5]. These forces can act as early co-determinants of glial and endothelial responses, often before inflammatory mediators fully accumulate [6, 18]. Conversely, chronic neurodegenerative disorders involve slower but persistent alterations in matrix architecture. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and multiple sclerosis (MS), for example, amyloid deposition or glial scar formation can create local tissue stiffening and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [8, 19]. At the neurovascular interface, altered hemodynamic forces further influence endothelial behavior, BBB integrity, and immune-cell extravasation [20].

Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of neuroinflammation requires an integrated perspective that couples canonical biochemical signaling with the dynamic mechanical forces of the CNS. Piezo1 signaling does not operate in isolation but can synergize with canonical pathways—including Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), cytokine receptors, and inflammasome-related signaling—to fine-tune the magnitude and duration of immune responses [11, 12]. Realizing this integrated view requires identifying the molecular machinery that converts stiffness, shear stress, and pressure into intelligible biological signals. While cells use several mechanosensors such as integrins and cytoskeletal proteins to sense steady-state mechanics, mechanosensitive ion channels (MSCs) provide a particularly rapid route by which physical deformation can be translated into intracellular electrochemical and inflammatory signaling.

Piezo1 as a context-dependent mechanotransductive integrator in neuroinflammation

To understand how physical forces reshape the immune landscape of the CNS, it is necessary to identify the molecular machinery that translates cues such as stiffness, shear stress, and pressure into biological signals. While the CNS expresses several mechanosensitive channels (e.g., TRP, ASIC, K2P), Piezo1 has emerged as a major candidate mechanotransduction node in neuroinflammation, with the strongest direct evidence in microglia, oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, and endothelial cells [21, 22]. Rather than replacing canonical inflammatory pathways, Piezo1 is better understood as a force-responsive integrator that can modulate how biochemical signals are interpreted. Mechanical stress can evoke Ca²⁺ entry through Piezo1, while inflammatory mediators can increase Piezo1 expression or sensitivity, thereby creating bidirectional coupling between mechanical and biochemical programs [11, 12].

The unique capacity of Piezo1 to function as this high-fidelity hub is dictated by its evolutionarily distinct architecture. It is a massive (~ 900 kDa) homotrimeric channel comprising over 2,500 amino acids per monomer, sharing no sequence homology with other known ion channel families [23]. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies have revealed its distinctive three-bladed, propeller-like structure, where highly curved transmembrane domains create a nano-scale “membrane dome” that locally deforms the lipid bilayer even at rest [24, 25]. This structure supports the “force-from-lipids” gating mechanism: the curved blades act as molecular levers that, upon sensing lateral membrane tension from tissue stiffening or swelling, flatten to transmit torque to the central cap, physically un-gating the pore [26]. This mechanism allows Piezo1 to detect minute changes in biophysical forces with exceptional speed (< 10 ms) and facilitates a rapid, non-selective cation influx with a high preference for Ca²⁺, effectively acting as a mechanochemical converter that translates the physical reality of the microenvironment into a decisive biochemical signal.

Upon activation, Piezo1-mediated Ca²⁺ influx engages transcriptional, cytoskeletal, and metabolic programs relevant to neuroinflammation. Acting as a second messenger, Ca²⁺ can engage Calpain and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), influence nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, and facilitate Rho GTPase-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling required for migration [12, 27]. In parallel, Piezo1 signaling can intersect with immunometabolic pathways by promoting mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) stabilization, thereby biasing cells toward glycolytic adaptation under inflammatory stress [28]. This coupling can create a feed-forward interaction in which inflammatory stimuli increase Piezo1 responsiveness and altered mechanosensing, in turn, modifies the magnitude and duration of downstream inflammatory signaling [11, 29].

The context-dependent nature of Piezo1 signaling. A recurring theme in CNS mechanobiology is that Piezo1 activation is not intrinsically protective or harmful; rather, its outcome is shaped by the spatiotemporal context of the mechanical signal. Three variables appear especially important. First, duration: transient activation, as seen under physiological fluid flow, can support homeostatic programs such as endothelial alignment, whereas chronic activation in stiff scars or tumor tissue is more likely to promote inflammatory locking and functional exhaustion [30–32]. Second, magnitude: low-level mechanical inputs may help maintain surveillance, but supraphysiological forces associated with trauma, acute hypertension, or hematoma compression can trigger Ca²⁺ overload and stress-associated injury pathways [6, 33, 34]. Third, metabolic context: the downstream consequence of Piezo1 opening is strongly modified by high glucose, lipid dysregulation, and mitochondrial redox state, which can bias signaling toward ROS production and glycolytic reprogramming [35, 36]. Recognizing these variables helps explain why Piezo1 can support adaptive homeostasis in some settings yet promote maladaptive inflammation in others. Piezo1 signaling in resident CNS cells

While Piezo1 serves as a ubiquitous molecular entry point for translating mechanical cues into intracellular signaling, the biological consequences of its activation diverge markedly across CNS cell types. Rather than executing a uniform program, Piezo1 operates within distinct cellular architectures, mechanical constraints, and immunological roles, giving rise to qualitatively different inflammatory and functional outputs. In resident immune sentinels like microglia, Piezo1 interfaces with surveillance programs to regulate polarization and phagocytosis in response to tissue stiffness; in support cells like astrocytes, it acts as a mechanical amplifier that propagates inflammatory signals across the glial network; in the regenerative compartment, it functions as a checkpoint for oligodendrocyte maturation; and at the neurovascular unit, it coordinates hemodynamic sensing with barrier integrity. Piezo1 in microglia: surveillance and phagocytosis

Microglia, the resident innate immune cells of the CNS, form a dynamic macrophage network that continuously surveys the neural parenchyma to maintain homeostasis [66–68]. Upon injury, infection, or neurodegenerative stress, these cells undergo distinct morphological and functional remodeling, transitioning from a ramified surveillance state to an amoeboid, phagocytic phenotype. Historically, this transformation was attributed solely to biochemical cues; however, accumulating evidence suggests that microglial activation spatially coincides with regions of marked mechanical alteration, such as the compressive forces of edema, the fibrotic remodeling of the ECM, and the formation of rigid pathological aggregates [8, 69, 70]. Experimental studies utilizing tunable substrates have revealed that microglia exhibit a unique behavior known as durotaxis—the tendency to migrate along stiffness gradients toward more rigid environments [71, 72]. Microglial morphology, spreading area, and cytoskeletal organization are exquisitely sensitive to these mechanical contexts, suggesting that they actively sense the physical microenvironment rather than merely reacting to secondary chemical signals. At the molecular level, Piezo1 has emerged as a principal mechanotransducer enabling microglia to convert these biophysical stimuli into intelligible intracellular signaling, thereby governing migration, phagocytosis, and inflammatory polarization through context-dependent pathways.

In the context of AD, genetic ablation of microglial Piezo1 provides direct evidence that this channel senses plaque-associated rigidity. In 5×FAD mice, loss of Piezo1 impairs microglial clustering, plaque compaction, amyloid-β (Aβ) internalization, and cognitive performance, whereas stiff Aβ fibrils—but not soluble Aβ monomers—trigger Piezo1-dependent Ca²⁺ influx and downstream cytoskeletal remodeling [19]. Complementary studies in human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived microglia and pharmacological activation models further support a role for Piezo1 in lysosomal activation and Aβ clearance through the CaMKII–AMPK–mTOR axis [37, 38]. Together, these data place microglial Piezo1 among the strongest in vivo-supported examples of CNS mechanosensing discussed in this Review.

However, the role of Piezo1 in regulating neuroinflammation appears to be a double-edged sword, with outcomes heavily dependent on the specific pathological context and upstream stimuli. On one hand, evidence indicates that Piezo1 promotes a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Substrate stiffness has been shown to regulate microglial migration and cytokine production through Piezo1-dependent Ca²⁺ influx. In stiffness-driven models, Piezo1 deficiency attenuated durotaxis and reduced lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced production of TNF-α and IL-6, potentially by disrupting crosstalk between Piezo1 and cytoskeletal proteins such as integrins [27, 39]. This aligns with observations in peripheral macrophages, where Piezo1 senses cyclic pressure to trigger inflammatory cascades [12]. On the other hand, paradoxical findings suggest that Piezo1 activation can exert an anti-inflammatory effect. Pharmacological activation of the channel has been observed to inhibit LPS-induced microglial activation by suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway, specifically reducing the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK [40]. This discrepancy implies that Piezo1 may function as a bimodal switch: driving inflammation when triggered by sterile mechanical stiffness (as in fibrosis) while acting as a negative feedback brake during intense pathogen-induced activation to prevent excitotoxicity.

Beyond canonical inflammatory signaling, Piezo1 is also linked to the metabolic state of microglia, a concept termed “immuno-metabolic” regulation. In models of diabetic encephalopathy or acute hyperglycemia, high glucose conditions upregulate Piezo1 expression, disrupt Ca²⁺ homeostasis, and alter JNK1–mTOR signaling, promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and a shift toward aerobic glycolysis [35]. These changes enhance secretion of TNF-α, IL-1β, and MCP-1 while impairing proliferation and migration, and pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 can partially rescue these defects [35]. In addition, membrane lipid composition may tune Piezo1 gating kinetics, raising the possibility that fatty-acid availability modifies how mechanical cues are coupled to inflammatory programs [36, 73]. Collectively, these studies suggest that Piezo1 acts as an important point of integration between mechanical stress, metabolism, and immune signaling in microglia.

Piezo1 in astrocytes: reactivity and glial scarring

Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells in the CNS, are integral components of the tripartite synapse and the neurovascular unit, maintaining ion homeostasis and neurotransmitter clearance [74]. Unlike microglia, astrocytes are traditionally viewed as structural support cells; however, recent advances indicate that they are also mechanosensitive and can adapt their morphology and function to matrix stiffness, shear stress, and hydrostatic pressure [75]. In this context, Piezo1 has emerged as an important mechanosensitive regulator in astrocytes, with proposed roles in both physiological signaling and reactive responses [41].

In the healthy brain, genetic evidence indicates that astrocytic Piezo1 contributes to neural plasticity and stem-cell regulation. Astrocyte-specific Piezo1 deletion impairs long-term potentiation (LTP) and cognitive performance, and Piezo1-mediated Ca²⁺ influx in hippocampal astrocytes promotes ATP release that supports adult neural stem-cell proliferation and neuronal differentiation [41]. In addition, nanoscale surface roughness can modulate astrocyte-neuron interactions via mechanosensitive cation channels, suggesting that Piezo1 may help astrocytes sense neural extracellular topography [42].

However, evidence for pathological astrocytic Piezo1 signaling in neuroinflammatory disease is currently based largely on pharmacological and in vitro studies. These studies indicate that inflammatory mediators, particularly LPS and Aβ, can upregulate Piezo1 expression in astrocytes and sensitize them to subsequent mechanical stimulation [11, 18]. In culture systems, Piezo1 activation has been linked to Ca²⁺ oscillations, altered purinergic responsiveness, and release of cytokines or chemokine-like signals, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and CX3CL1 [14, 18]. Importantly, this Ca²⁺ influx can also promote extracellular ATP release. Once outside the cell, ATP can function as a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that engages purinergic receptors such as P2 × 7 on neighboring microglia and may facilitate inflammasome-related signaling and IL-1β secretion [18, 41, 43].

In vitro mechanical-stimulation models further suggest that forces relevant to traumatic CNS injury—such as shear stress, rapid deformation, ultrasound, or magnetomechanical stimulation—can induce astrocytic reactivity through Piezo1-associated Ca²⁺ signaling [44, 45]. Pharmacological evidence also suggests that excessive Piezo1 activation may slow astrocyte migration through calpain-dependent remodeling of focal adhesions, a process potentially relevant to glial scar organization and the mechanical restriction of axon repair [8, 18]. In related mechanically stressed astrocyte systems, such as the optic nerve head, Piezo1 activation is associated with TGF-β2 induction and extracellular-matrix deposition [46, 47]. However, further in vivo studies using astrocyte-specific conditional knockout models are needed to determine how strongly these mechanisms contribute to neuroinflammatory disease and traumatic scarring in the CNS.

Piezo1 in oligodendrocytes: myelination and repair

Myelination is a fundamental biological process that ensures rapid nerve impulse transmission and provides metabolic support to axons, a function critical for maintaining CNS homeostasis [76, 77]. The regenerative capacity of the CNS relies heavily on the recruitment, proliferation, and differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) into mature, myelinating oligodendrocytes. However, in demyelinating disorders such as progressive MS and during physiological aging, this reparative process frequently fails, leading to axonal degeneration and irreversible neurological disability [78, 79]. While the failure of remyelination has historically been attributed to biochemical inhibitors or a lack of progenitor recruitment, emerging paradigms in mechanobiology propose that the physical properties of the tissue niche—specifically changes in matrix stiffness and topography—act as a decisive checkpoint for oligodendrocyte fate [80, 81]. In the healthy CNS, the parenchymal tissue is soft and compliant, but chronic lesions and the aging brain microenvironment undergo progressive stiffening due to extracellular matrix deposition and gliosis [48]. High-resolution mechanobiological studies have established that Piezo1 is highly expressed in OPCs and serves as the primary sensor of this stiff microenvironment. When activated by high matrix stiffness, Piezo1 mediates a robust influx of Ca²⁺, which triggers downstream intracellular signaling cascades that specifically inhibit OPC proliferation and differentiation. This mechanism provides a biophysical explanation for why remyelination efficiency declines with age: the senescent, stiffened niche tonically activates Piezo1, effectively applying a “mechanical brake” on repair processes [49].

The expression profile of Piezo1 is dynamically regulated during the oligodendrocyte lineage progression, underscoring its developmental importance. Investigations utilizing human oligodendrocyte cell lines (such as MO3.13) and human brain tissue samples have shown that Piezo1 is abundantly expressed in progenitor cells but is naturally downregulated as they mature into differentiated oligodendrocytes [50]. This downregulation appears to be a prerequisite for the wrapping of axons. Experimental activation of Piezo1 using chemical agonists in human OPCs mimics the inhibitory effects of a stiff environment, leading to the retraction of cellular processes, arrest of the cell cycle, and failure of maturation [14, 49, 50]. Conversely, inhibition of the channel promotes a pro-regenerative phenotype characterized by enhanced motility and differentiation. Further supporting this, pharmacological blockade of Piezo1 using the peptide antagonist GsMTx4 has been shown to significantly attenuate demyelination severity and accelerate functional recovery in ex vivo and in vivo toxin-induced demyelination models [14]. These findings validate Piezo1 as a druggable target to overcome the differentiation block in fibrotic CNS tissues.

Mechanistically, the translation of mechanical forces into differentiation arrest involves sophisticated intracellular crosstalk. Converging evidence points to the transcriptional coactivators Yes-associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) as key downstream effectors (Fig. 1). YAP is a canonical mechanotransducer known to regulate oligodendrocyte morphogenesis and maturation [51]. It has been documented that while substrate stiffness can enhance initial differentiation, it paradoxically impedes structural myelination through YAP-dependent regulation [52]. Given that Piezo1 activation drives Ca²⁺-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling, it is hypothesized that Piezo1 acts as an upstream regulator that modulates YAP nuclear translocation and activity. This “Mechanical-Piezo1-YAP” axis likely integrates physical tension from the axon-glia interface to fine-tune the wrapping process, and its sustained activation in stiff lesions locks cells in an immature, non-myelinating state.

Beyond chronic demyelination, Piezo1 also plays a detrimental role in acute white matter injury, particularly under conditions of supraphysiological compression. In the context of intracerebral hemorrhage, the expanding hematoma exerts significant mechanical stress on surrounding tissues. Recent findings indicate that this mechanical compression upregulates Piezo1 expression in oligodendrocytes, leading to lethal intracellular calcium overload. This aberrant Ca²⁺ influx activates the PERK-ATF4-CHOP axis-associated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress pathway, which subsequently triggers apoptotic cell death and exacerbates myelin loss. Pharmacological suppression of Piezo1 was found to alleviate this ER stress cascade, reduce oligodendrocyte apoptosis, and preserve white matter integrity [34]. Collectively, these studies portray Piezo1 as a multifaceted regulator: it acts as a stiffness sensor that halts repair in chronic lesions and as a death switch driving degeneration under acute mechanical stress.

Piezo1 in endothelial cells: vascular tone, barrier function, and inflammatory activation

Genetic and in vivo vascular studies establish Piezo1 as a key mechanosensitive ion channel in endothelial cells, directly converting mechanical forces into intracellular Ca²⁺ signals [30, 53–55]. In endothelial membranes, Piezo1 is gated by changes in membrane tension induced by laminar or disturbed shear stress, circumferential stretch, and dynamic cell–cell or cell–matrix interactions [54]. Channel opening triggers rapid Ca²⁺ influx that functions as an upstream “force-to-inflammatory” switch: under physiological laminar flow, transient Piezo1 activity supports endothelial alignment, cytoskeletal organization, and nitric oxide (NO)/adrenomedullin-dependent homeostasis, thereby maintaining vascular tone and a quiescent anti-inflammatory endothelial state [30, 31, 53]. By contrast, sustained or excessive Piezo1 activation under pathological mechanical stress engages Ca²⁺-dependent kinases and transcriptional regulators (including CaMKII, Src/PYK2, NF-κB, JNK, and YAP/TAZ), pushing endothelial cells toward oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, and dysfunction [56, 57]. Thus, the key determinant is not whether Piezo1 opens, but the intensity and duration of Piezo1–Ca²⁺ signaling and the disease context in which it occurs.

During cerebrovascular development, Piezo1-mediated Ca²⁺ signaling is also essential for angiogenic wiring and vessel morphogenesis, providing an early-life “mechanical set point” that later shapes vulnerability to inflammation and barrier failure. In developing brain vessels, Piezo1 activation in endothelial tip cells generates localized Ca²⁺ activity at branch points that controls extension–retraction behavior during pathfinding. Mechanistically, high mechanical stress drives Piezo1-dependent Ca²⁺ entry and calpain activation to promote cytoskeletal disassembly and branch retraction, whereas lower Piezo1 activity favors extension through NO-dependent programs [55]. Endothelial genetic loss of Piezo1 disrupts this balance, yielding excessive branching and disorganized, hypervascularized architectures [53–55]. These developmental data provide some of the strongest in vivo evidence that Piezo1 couples force sensing to endothelial structural decisions in the CNS vasculature [53, 54].

In the adult brain microcirculation, Piezo1 is functionally expressed in brain capillary endothelial cells and contributes to cerebral blood flow regulation—an axis that intersects strongly with inflammation because hypoperfusion and endothelial activation reinforce each other [58, 59]. Capillary endothelial cells are now recognized as active components of neurovascular coupling rather than passive conduits. Piezo1 activation evokes endothelial Ca²⁺ transients that can influence communication with mural cells and upstream flow control, and recent work indicates that endothelial Piezo1 can act as a brake on hyperemia. Importantly, disease-associated factors can directly sensitize this pathway: pathological amyloid species can shift Piezo1 gating and amplify endothelial Ca²⁺ responses, providing a mechanistic route by which proteinopathy perturbs mechanotransduction and contributes to microvascular dysfunction [58–60]. The downstream consequence is a feed-forward loop in which disturbed flow and impaired coupling increase mechanical stress signals that further bias the endothelium toward inflammatory activation [30].

Piezo1-mediated Ca²⁺ signaling is tightly coupled to BBB integrity and neuroinflammatory amplification. Elevated Ca²⁺ downstream of Piezo1 promotes actomyosin contractility (via Ca²⁺-dependent kinases including CaMKII), increasing cytoskeletal tension and destabilizing junctional complexes [61–63]. In parallel, Ca²⁺-dependent proteolysis and junctional remodeling can reduce effective barrier sealing, while Piezo1-associated mechanotransduction at endothelial junctions (including cooperation with junctional molecules such as PECAM1) provides a structural basis for force-dependent permeability and leukocyte passage [14, 61, 62]. In ischemia/reperfusion and related stresses, Piezo1-driven Ca²⁺ overload has been linked to oxidative stress programs (including suppression of antioxidant defenses such as Nrf2) and inflammatory injury cascades that aggravate BBB disruption [63–65]. Finally, during inflammatory activation, leukocyte adhesion and traction forces generate local membrane tension that can synergize with shear stress to trigger mechanosensitive Ca²⁺ microdomains and signaling (e.g., Src-family and PYK2-linked remodeling), facilitating diapedesis [61, 82]. Collectively, endothelial Piezo1 integrates mechanical stress into coordinated programs of endothelial activation, BBB breakdown, and immune invasion that drive neuroinflammatory progression.

Piezo1 at the CNS borders: neurovascular coupling and immune cell extravasation

The central nervous system is protected not only by anatomical barriers but also by mechanically active interfaces, including the blood–brain barrier (BBB), the meninges, the choroid plexus, and the perivascular space. At these sites, leukocyte entry is shaped by endothelial shear stress, junctional tension, basement-membrane resistance, and spatial confinement. In this context, Piezo1 is best considered as a candidate mechanosensor operating at the neurovascular border, where it may couple force sensing to endothelial activation, barrier opening, and immune-cell transit into CNS tissue [61–65, 82].

Accordingly, this section does not attempt to review the full peripheral biology of monocytes, neutrophils, dendritic cells, or T cells. Instead, we focus specifically on how Piezo1-related mechanotransduction may regulate CNS-relevant steps of infiltration—namely vascular adhesion, intravascular crawling, diapedesis, perivascular retention, and border-zone reactivation—at the interfaces where neuroinflammation is physically initiated [83].

Monocytes and border-associated macrophages: mechanosensing during CNS entry

For monocytes, the most CNS-relevant role of Piezo1 lies in the transition from the circulation to the inflamed neurovascular unit. During adhesion to activated brain endothelium, intraluminal crawling, and trans-endothelial migration, monocytes experience shear stress, membrane stretch, and marked deformation. Available evidence indicates that Piezo1 can translate these forces into Ca²⁺-dependent signaling linked to integrin activation, PYK2-associated remodeling, and chemokine responsiveness, thereby influencing whether circulating monocytes successfully cross the BBB and seed perivascular inflammatory niches [84–87].

Once monocytes enter the perivascular or lesion-adjacent space, the local mechanical environment changes from vascular shear to matrix stiffness and confinement. In that setting, Piezo1 may further bias early monocyte-to-macrophage programming toward inflammatory or reparative states; however, the key point for CNS disease is not generic macrophage biology, but the possibility that the same mechanosensor couples BBB transit efficiency with the initial inflammatory phenotype acquired immediately after entry [27, 28, 88, 89]. Supporting this plausibility, a substantial extra-CNS literature now shows that macrophage Piezo1 can influence inflammatory polarization, TLR4-coupled host defense, foam-cell biology, phagocytosis/efferocytosis, and fibrosis-associated repair across infection, colitis, atherosclerosis, renal fibrosis, liver fibrosis, and irradiated tissue repair models [90–101]. These studies should not be overinterpreted as direct CNS evidence, but they strengthen the rationale for examining post-entry monocyte/macrophage mechanosensing in neurovascular lesions.

Thus, in the context of neuroinflammation, Piezo1 should be viewed primarily as a regulator of monocyte extravasation and early perivascular adaptation rather than as a universal controller of all macrophage effector programs. Defining this step more precisely will require in vivo models that resolve where monocytes first engage Piezo1 during CNS entry and how that mechanical checkpoint shapes subsequent lesion macrophage composition.

Neutrophils: BBB injury, intravascular arrest, and immunothrombosis

Neutrophils are especially relevant to CNS disease because they act at the luminal side of injured vessels before they fully enter the parenchyma. In ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, and intracerebral hemorrhage, neutrophils adhere to activated endothelium, accumulate within narrow microvessels, release proteases and reactive oxygen species, and generate neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), all of which aggravate BBB injury and reduce tissue perfusion [102–109]. More broadly, pathological contributions of peripheral neutrophils are now recognized across Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and ALS, indicating that border-zone neutrophil biology is relevant well beyond acute vascular injury [110–113].

Within this vascular setting, Piezo1-dependent mechanosensing provides a plausible link between abnormal flow and thromboinflammatory amplification. Pathological shear stress can activate Piezo1, trigger Ca²⁺ influx and calpain-dependent remodeling, and lower the threshold for rapid NET formation. Recent mechanobiology work further supports the idea that abnormal shear can function as a trigger for thromboinflammatory NETosis and mechanothrombotic plugging, including Piezo1-linked intravascular thrombosis programs in diabetes and Piezo1-directed NET formation during viral infection [114–117]. Rather than emphasizing neutrophil antibacterial functions, the CNS-relevant consequence is that Piezo1-driven NETosis may stabilize platelet-rich intravascular plugs, worsen no-reflow, and amplify endothelial damage at the neurovascular unit [118–120].

Piezo1 also contributes to the biomechanics of neutrophil trafficking during diapedesis. When neutrophils squeeze through inflamed endothelium and perivascular bottlenecks, they undergo extreme membrane deformation that generates localized Ca²⁺ signals required for efficient migration. Neutrophil-specific Piezo1 deletion impairs this mechanically demanding step, supporting the idea that Piezo1 links vascular confinement to CNS entry behavior [121].

Taken together, the most important implication for neuroinflammation is that Piezo1 may coordinate two coupled vascular events—transmigration and immunothrombosis—at the moment when neutrophils first interact with injured brain vessels. This border-focused view is more relevant to CNS pathology than a broader treatment of systemic neutrophil biology.

Dendritic cells and T cells at CNS borders

For dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells, the strongest CNS-relevant question is not how Piezo1 regulates lymph-node priming in general, but how mechanical cues at CNS borders shape local antigen presentation and immune reactivation. Meningeal, choroid-plexus, and perivascular compartments are spatially confined and mechanically heterogeneous niches in which DC-like antigen-presenting cells and infiltrating T cells encounter adhesion forces, substrate resistance, and tissue confinement [122–126]. This possibility is reinforced by evidence that substrate stiffness can directly reprogram dendritic-cell metabolism and function, and that dendritic-cell Piezo1 can bias T(H)1/T(reg) differentiation in mechanically defined immune contexts, providing a mechanistic bridge between confinement, APC activation, and T-cell priming [127, 128].

In this setting, evidence from mechanically defined antigen-presentation systems suggests that Piezo1 may tune two linked processes. First, in border-associated antigen-presenting cells, mechanosensitive Ca²⁺ signaling could influence local costimulation and the quality of antigen presentation under altered stiffness or confinement. Second, ex vivo analyses and in vitro confinement assays indicate that Piezo1 can modulate actin remodeling, traction generation, confined migration, and the balance between effector and regulatory programs in T cells, features that may become especially relevant during endothelial crawling and perivascular reactivation in disorders such as multiple sclerosis [129–134].

However, compared with microglia, OPCs, or endothelial cells, the evidence in DCs and T cells remains less direct in CNS disease settings. These mechanisms should therefore be interpreted as emerging, context-dependent models of border-zone immune regulation rather than as fully established, universally dominant pathways in all neuroinflammatory contexts.

Decoding the mechanical signatures of CNS pathologies

This section shifts from cell-intrinsic signaling to disease-level interpretation. Rather than repeating the molecular cascades detailed in Sections "Piezo1 signaling in resident CNS cells" and "Piezo1 at the CNS borders: neurovascular coupling and immune cell extravasation", we focus here on how distinct mechanical signatures—such as plaque stiffening, glial scarring, shear stress, compression, or solid stress—shape disease progression and therapeutic windows across major CNS disorders.

Alzheimer’s disease: sensing plaque stiffness

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) presents a mechanically heterogeneous brain environment that evolves over time. At the macroscopic scale, magnetic resonance elastography studies consistently indicate global softening of affected brain regions, whereas at the microscale, fibrillar Aβ deposition creates local islands of stiffness around plaques [135–138]. This temporal and spatial mismatch—global softening but focal plaque-associated stiffening—provides the key mechanical signature of AD and fits with the broader view that AD unfolds as a stage-dependent biological cascade rather than a single static lesion state [139, 140].

Within this heterogeneous landscape, the best-supported role of Piezo1 is in plaque-associated microglial mechanosensing, as detailed in "Piezo1 in microglia: surveillance and phagocytosis" section. At the disease level, the important implication is that local stiffening can initially favor plaque compaction and clearance, whereas persistent exposure to stiff plaque niches may eventually contribute to dysfunctional, exhausted microglial responses and ineffective debris removal [19, 141].

The AD context is further complicated by the biophysical state of Aβ itself. Soluble and aggregated Aβ species do not influence Piezo1 in the same way, and prolonged exposure to stiff plaque-like environments may progressively shift Piezo1 signaling from adaptive surveillance toward maladaptive stress responses [141, 142]. Thus, AD is best viewed not as a static Piezo1-on/Piezo1-off condition, but as a stage-dependent mechanical continuum.

Beyond microglia, astrocytic and vascular Piezo1 pathways are likely to modify this disease trajectory, although the supporting evidence is less mature. As discussed in "Piezo1 in astrocytes: reactivity and glial scarring" section and "Piezo1 in endothelial cells: vascular tone, barrier function, and inflammatory activation" section, astrocytes may respond to plaque-associated inflammatory stiffness, while vascular and meningeal Piezo1 signaling can influence perfusion, lymphatic drainage, and brain–cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) exchange, thereby indirectly affecting amyloid clearance and inflammatory burden [143–145]. A related translational clue comes from blood-cell mechanics: increased Yoda1-evoked Piezo1 activity in red blood cells has been associated with mild cognitive impairment (MCI)/AD, suggesting that systemic biomechanical phenotypes may eventually complement CNS-focused biomarkers [146].

Therapeutically, this framing suggests that Piezo1 modulation in AD will need to be stage-aware: early enhancement of beneficial clearance programs may differ fundamentally from later intervention in chronically stiff, metabolically compromised plaque environments. The central disease-level issue is therefore not simply whether Piezo1 is protective or harmful, but when and where a given mechanical state is being sensed.

Multiple sclerosis: the mechanical brake on remyelination

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is characterized by a mechanically heterogeneous lesion landscape. Although acute inflammatory lesions may soften, chronic demyelinated plaques progressively stiffen because of astrogliosis, extracellular matrix accumulation, and scar maturation [147–151]. This evolution creates a pathological transition from an immune-dominated early lesion to a mechanically restrictive chronic niche in which repair becomes increasingly difficult.

At the level of immune entry, Piezo1 may contribute to MS by facilitating mechanically demanding steps of T-cell adhesion, crawling, and reactivation at CNS borders, as outlined in "Dendritic cells and T cells at CNS borders" section. This border-focused role is particularly relevant in perivascular spaces, where confined geometry and endothelial tension shape the quality of T-cell restimulation and help sustain compartmentalized inflammation [131, 134, 148].

The defining disease-level consequence, however, is the emergence of a mechanical brake on remyelination. Building on the OPC mechanisms described in "Piezo1 in oligodendrocytes: myelination and repair" section, chronic scar stiffening creates a hostile niche in which progenitor cells remain trapped in a repair-incompetent state. In other words, the MS lesion is not only immunologically inflammatory, but also mechanically non-permissive for regeneration [14, 48, 50].

This interpretation helps explain why Piezo1 is attractive yet complex as a therapeutic target in MS. A successful strategy may need to combine suppression of mechanically assisted immune infiltration with release of the stiffness-imposed brake on OPC-mediated repair. The main translational issue is therefore timing and cell specificity: intervention in early inflammatory lesions may require a different Piezo1 strategy than intervention in chronic, scar-dominated plaques.

Stroke: hemodynamic and compressive stress

Stroke is defined by a rapid mechanical collapse of neurovascular homeostasis. In ischemic stroke, abrupt flow cessation and subsequent reperfusion expose the vasculature to extreme changes in shear stress, endothelial tension, and microvascular obstruction, whereas intracerebral hemorrhage adds direct compressive stress from the expanding hematoma [152–154]. These acute forces form the core mechanical signature of stroke.

In ischemia–reperfusion, the most consistent disease-level implication of Piezo1 lies at the neurovascular interface. As outlined in "Piezo1 in endothelial cells: vascular tone, barrier function, and inflammatory activation" section, endothelial Piezo1 is positioned to couple flow-sensitive mechanotransduction to inflammatory endothelial signaling, junctional remodeling, leukocyte diapedesis, and, in ischemic stroke, BBB disruption and oxidative stress. Stroke severity may therefore reflect not only neuronal ischemia, but also a force-dependent endothelial response that amplifies secondary injury [61, 63, 82, 155]. Additional stroke-related studies further link Piezo1 to post-stroke cognitive dysfunction, neuronal oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation injury, and ferroptosis-related ischemia/reperfusion mechanisms, although these data are more heterogeneous and are not limited to endothelial compartments [156–158].

Stroke also illustrates how vascular mechanics and immune mechanics converge. Disturbed hemodynamics can favor thrombosis and platelet activation, while the neutrophil programs summarized in "Neutrophils: BBB injury, intravascular arrest, and immunothrombosis" section suggest that Piezo1-dependent NETosis and intravascular arrest may further aggravate no-reflow and thromboinflammation in the injured microcirculation [159, 160].

In hemorrhagic stroke, tissue compression becomes the dominant mechanical insult. Here, Piezo1-linked responses may extend from the endothelium to white matter cells, including oligodendrocytes exposed to perihematomal stress, thereby worsening demyelination and neurological dysfunction. Thus, the stroke context is best understood as an acute convergence of shear-driven vascular injury and compression-driven parenchymal injury rather than as a repetition of the cell-specific pathways detailed earlier [33, 34, 161].

Traumatic CNS injury: acute mechanical deformation

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) and spinal cord injury (SCI) are archetypal disorders of rapid mechanical deformation. Stretch, strain, compression, and pressure transients occur within milliseconds and immediately perturb cell membranes, the cytoskeleton, and the neurovascular interface [162, 163]. The defining issue in trauma is therefore not chronic stiffness, but the abrupt conversion of physical deformation into prolonged secondary inflammation.

At the disease level, trauma likely recruits Piezo1 at several interfaces simultaneously. As discussed in "Piezo1 in astrocytes: reactivity and glial scarring" section and "Piezo1 in endothelial cells: vascular tone, barrier function, and inflammatory activation" section, astrocytes and endothelial cells are positioned to translate acute deformation into Ca²⁺-dependent inflammatory signaling, BBB remodeling, and altered leukocyte recruitment. In TBI, current evidence remains suggestive rather than definitive, but the broader pathological theme is that early mechanical damage creates the conditions for later glial and vascular amplification [164–166].

SCI provides somewhat stronger support for this framework. Pharmacological inhibition of stretch-activated channels and myeloid-lineage Piezo1 deletion both improve outcome in experimental models, consistent with the idea that mechanically stressed lesion borders use Piezo1-like signaling to intensify innate immune activation within a spatially confined cord environment [167–169].

Accordingly, the central disease question in traumatic CNS injury is not simply whether Piezo1 is present, but how the magnitude, duration, and cellular location of deformation determine the transition from primary biomechanical insult to secondary inflammatory degeneration. Resolving that timeline will be essential for defining when mechanosensitive interventions are most likely to work [170, 171].

Glioma: solid stress and invasive mechanics

Glioma progression occurs within a chronically distorted mechanical microenvironment characterized by solid stress, elevated interstitial pressure, extracellular matrix remodeling, and invasive front heterogeneity [172–177]. Unlike stroke or trauma, where force is abrupt, glioma imposes persistent mechanical remodeling that coexists with BBB disruption, edema, immune infiltration, and long-term inflammatory signaling [178–184].

At the disease level, the strongest clinical message is that high Piezo1 expression tracks with mechanically aggressive tumors. Across patient cohorts and transcriptomic datasets, Piezo1 correlates with tumor stiffness, malignant subtype, peritumoral edema, and poor outcome, suggesting that it marks a mechanically activated tumor state that is also neuroinflammatorily active [32, 185–188].

Mechanistically, glioma exemplifies a feed-forward relationship between tissue mechanics and tumor behavior. Rather than restating generic Ca²⁺ signaling, the key pathological concept is that Piezo1 integrates chronic stiffness with integrin–focal adhesion kinase (FAK)–YAP/TAZ programs that reinforce invasion, extracellular-matrix remodeling, and immunosuppressive niche formation [32, 189–196].

This context also highlights the therapeutic ambiguity of Piezo1. Chronic activation in stiff tumor tissue may support malignancy, whereas acute and spatially controlled activation can be exploited to enhance tumor-cell killing by TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) sensitization or sonodynamic stress. Glioma therefore underscores the broader principle that Piezo1-targeted therapy must be interpreted through the local mechanical state of the disease rather than through a single uniformly activating or inhibitory model [197–199].

Epilepsy and hyperexcitability: the mechanosensitive trigger

Beyond structural lesions, mechanotransduction is increasingly implicated in the functional dysregulation of neural networks, particularly in epilepsy and seizure-associated hyperexcitability. During seizures, neurons and astrocytes undergo cell swelling, ionic redistribution, and marked changes in tissue tension, all of which can generate mechanical stress within the neurovascular unit [5]. In hippocampal astrocytes, Piezo1-mediated mechanotransduction promotes ATP release and can influence purinergic signaling, while recent pharmacological work in the murine neocortex shows that Piezo1 activation enhances astrocyte-neuron communication and NMDA-receptor-dependent slow inward currents, supporting a potential role in regulating neuronal firing thresholds [41, 200]. In parallel, an exploratory study of human mesial temporal lobe epilepsy specimens and experimental seizure conditions found increased Piezo1 expression at the glio-vascular unit, with positive correlations to TNFα, IL-1β, and NF-kB, particularly in astrocytes, microglia, pericytes, and microvascular compartments during peak neuroinflammation [201]. Together, these observations support a working model in which seizure-associated swelling and inflammatory remodeling amplify Piezo1 signaling, which may in turn reinforce excitability and border-zone neuroinflammation. Although direct causal evidence remains limited, this axis identifies Piezo1 as a plausible mechanopharmacological target for drug-resistant epilepsy. Targeting the mechanochemical axis: therapeutic strategies

The growing evidence linking Piezo1 to CNS mechanobiology has made it an attractive target for mechano-therapeutic exploration. Unlike conventional targets defined only by biochemical ligands, Piezo1 offers an opportunity to modulate how CNS cells interpret physical features of their microenvironment. However, the therapeutic landscape is inherently context-dependent: Piezo1 signaling can support clearance or vascular homeostasis in some settings yet exacerbate excitotoxicity, scarring, or autoimmunity in others. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies are better framed as context-specific efforts either to restore insufficient mechanosensing or to dampen pathological overactivation.

Agonist strategies: restoring clearance and repair

In pathologies characterized by accumulation of metabolic waste or suppression of regenerative capacity, pharmacologically lowering the mechanical threshold of Piezo1 represents a promising strategy to “jump-start” cellular function. The prototypical gating modifier, Yoda1, acts as a molecular wedge that stabilizes the channel’s open conformation, effectively sensitizing cells to physiological forces [202]. Recent medicinal-chemistry optimization has yielded higher-potency analogues such as Yoda2 [203], while newly reported brain-targeted Jedi1/Jedi2 derivatives suggest that CNS-directed agonist delivery may become more feasible in the future [204].

The therapeutic utility of these agonists is evident in Alzheimer’s disease, where Yoda1 treatment reinvigorates microglial phagocytosis, promoting the compaction and clearance of amyloid-β plaques against a stiffening matrix background [19]. Beyond parenchymal immunity, Piezo1 activation also improves meningeal lymphatic vessel function and brain–CSF exchange in aging or disease models, supporting the removal of neurotoxic solutes and inflammatory metabolites [144]. Material-based mechanical priming of antigen-presenting cells further illustrates the broader principle that Piezo1-responsive programs can be engaged by defined physical cues; however, most such platforms remain extra-CNS proof-of-concept rather than validated neuroinflammatory therapies [128, 205]. Together, these findings support controlled mechanical sensitization of Piezo1 as a potentially useful strategy to restore clearance or repair, while also highlighting that the strongest CNS-directed evidence still comes from microglial and meningeal-lymphatic studies.

Antagonist strategies: dampening pathological mechanosensing

Conversely, in acute injuries (stroke, trauma) or chronic conditions driven by stiffness-induced inflammation (MS, fibrosis), the therapeutic goal is to silence the maladaptive overactivation of Piezo1. The peptide GsMTx4, derived from tarantula venom, remains the gold standard for this purpose. Its mechanism is unique: rather than blocking the pore directly, it inserts into the lipid bilayer to modify local membrane tension, acting as a “gating modifier” that preferentially targets the high-tension states found in pathologically stressed tissues [206].

Preclinical evidence strongly supports the use of GsMTx4 to mitigate blood-brain barrier breakdown and neuronal excitotoxicity in ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke models [63, 161]. In multiple sclerosis, pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 by GsMTx4 not only dampens the inflammatory drive of infiltrating T cells but also relieves the differentiation block in oligodendrocyte precursor cells caused by lesion stiffness [14]. To expand the chemical toolbox, recent studies have identified small-molecule inhibitors such as Isoquercitrin, which downregulates Piezo1 expression to block the downstream NLRP3 inflammasome [161], and Tubeimoside I, which antagonizes Yoda1-evoked Piezo1 activation [207]. However, truly CNS-selective delivery remains at an early stage, and many nanocarrier concepts discussed in the broader mechanomedicine literature have not yet been validated as Piezo1-specific neuroinflammatory interventions.

Translational challenges: specificity and delivery

Despite the promise of mechano-therapeutics, clinical translation faces major hurdles. First, the broad expression of Piezo1 in the cardiovascular system raises concerns about systemic off-target effects, because Piezo1 also participates in endothelial force sensing, vascular tone regulation, and blood-pressure control [31, 53, 54]. Consistent with this, pharmacological activation of Piezo1 with Yoda1 produces clear vascular effects, including endothelium-dependent aortic relaxation, underscoring that systemically delivered agonists are unlikely to remain CNS-selective [208]. Second, the double-edged nature of Piezo1 signaling means that timing is critical: a strategy that may support early clearance or vascular adaptation could become maladaptive in chronic scarring, persistent stiffness, or excitotoxic injury. Third, the pharmacological toolbox remains limited: currently available agonists and inhibitors are relatively few, selectivity is incomplete, and several compounds act as gating modifiers or indirect modulators rather than lesion-specific channel blockers [209, 210]. For agonist development, Yoda2 optimization and brain-targeted Jedi conjugates are encouraging, but these advances remain preclinical [203, 204]. Future translational strategies will therefore need to prioritize spatiotemporal precision, including regionally restricted delivery, biomaterial-enabled force tuning, and ultrasound-enabled targeting approaches that concentrate Piezo1 modulators within the neuroinflammatory niche while minimizing peripheral exposure [206]. Conclusion and future perspectives

The evidence synthesized in this review supports a more integrated view of CNS pathology: neuroinflammation is shaped not only by biochemical danger signals but also by the mechanical signature of the tissue microenvironment. From plaque-associated stiffening in Alzheimer’s disease to hemodynamic disturbance in stroke and solid stress in glioma, mechanical forces are increasingly recognized as important determinants of immune-cell behavior. Within this framework, Piezo1 emerges as an important mechanotransductive integrator that can couple shear stress, membrane tension, and spatial confinement to Ca²⁺-dependent programs of metabolism, cytoskeletal remodeling, and inflammatory signaling across the neurovascular and immune continuum. At the same time, defining Piezo1 as simply protective or harmful would be misleading, because its effects depend on when, where, and in which cell type the channel is engaged.

Therefore, the future of targeting Piezo1 lies not in blunt pharmacology, but in attaining spatiotemporal precision. A primary objective for future research is to map the specific mechanical checkpoints that tip Piezo1 signaling from homeostatic adaptation to pathological inflammation, including transitions between regulatory and effector immune states. This mechanistic understanding should also be integrated with the concept of mechanical immunosenescence, given that aging alters membrane stiffness and Piezo1 sensitivity, thereby intersecting with inflammaging and dysregulated mechanotransduction. Ultimately, clinical success will likely depend on next-generation mechano-therapeutics capable of delivering modulators selectively to sites of mechanical injury, such as the stiffened glial scar or inflamed endothelium. By viewing Piezo1 not simply as an ion channel but as a dynamic interface between physical cues and immune signaling, future studies may identify more precise therapeutic opportunities in otherwise intractable CNS disorders.
