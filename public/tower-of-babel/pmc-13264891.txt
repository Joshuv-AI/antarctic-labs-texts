Trauma care in Morocco: observations and opportunities from an American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) delegation visit
Authors: Kristy Lynn Hawley, Mary E Fallat, Stephanie Bonne, Katherine Kelley, Ajai K Malhotra, Amy Murphy, Michelle Papathanas, Anne Rizzo, David H Livingston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTrauma is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the Kingdom of Morocco, where rapid socioeconomic development and the 2023 Al Haouz earthquake intensified demands on the health system. The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma was invited to visit the country to assess trauma care capacity and identify opportunities for system strengthening.MethodsA multidisciplinary delegation visited Morocco in November 2024. Meetings were held with the Ministry of Health in Rabat, and the delegation visited the Université Mohammed V de Rabat, Al Ghassani Hospital in Fes, and Mohammed VI University Hospital in Marrakech. Site visits and structured discussions with clinicians, administrators, and policymakers occurred. Findings were organized using a trauma prevention framework.ResultsPrimary prevention challenges included road traffic injuries as the leading cause of injury death, inequities between urban and rural populations, and cultural barriers to blood and organ donation. Secondary prevention gaps centered on prehospital care, which is minimally equipped and relies on the French SAMU model of providing medical care in the field (“stay-and-play” model), fragmented trauma governance without a national authority, variation in hospital infrastructure, and absence of a trauma registry. Tertiary prevention weaknesses included workforce shortages, limited trauma-specific education, scarce rehabilitation and mental health services, and insufficient pediatric capacity. The 2023 earthquake highlighted both resilience—through central coordination and rapid mobilization of care to an exceedingly rural site—and a valuable opportunity to build on this foundation by formalizing a national trauma system response to enhance consistency and equity of care delivery.ConclusionsMorocco is at a pivotal moment in trauma system development. Addressing challenges across prevention levels offers opportunities to reduce preventable mortality and disability, improve equity, and strengthen preparedness. Lessons learned may inform national policy and provide insights for other low- and middle-income countries.

Introduction to AAST delegation activities

The Kingdom of Morocco invited the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) to visit the country to provide a bi-directional exchange and dialog regarding trauma care delivery. This invitation was suggested by an American College of Surgeons delegation assessing surgical care in Morocco in 2022. The AAST delegation visit was rescheduled to November 2–8, 2024, after the September 2023 Al Haouz earthquake near Marrakech. The earthquake—which caused major loss of life and limited access to mountain communities—helped focus the delegation on trauma care organization and response. The visit was a descriptive exchange, documenting on-the-ground perspectives on governance, training, and hospital capacity within Morocco’s recovery and health-system reforms. The institutions visited and the coordination of the visit were arranged by the Moroccan Ministry of Health. The observations are intended to be transferable, offering practical considerations for countries at different stages of trauma-system development—particularly in governance, workforce training, data systems, and preparedness capacity. The delegation met with the Ministry of Health in Rabat and visited Université Mohammed V de Rabat, Al Ghassani Hospital in Fes, and Mohammed VI University Hospital in Marrakech. This report reflects the combined observations of the delegates from these engagements. While the discussions at each location were frank and detailed, given the limited exposure and understanding of the overall healthcare system in the Kingdom, it is not an audit or prescriptive guidance. Nonetheless, we believe this report can serve as a starting point for further discussion with healthcare clinicians in Morocco as well as serve as a general blueprint for similar AAST delegations.

Current status of trauma care in Morocco

Injuries represent a substantial public health burden in Morocco, driving preventable mortality, disability, and added strain on health services. These pressures have grown more acute alongside rapid socioeconomic development, which accelerated urbanization and widened the gap between health system capacity and the population. National indicators show both strengths—expanding universal health insurance, growing medical education, and resilience during the 2023 Al Haouz earthquake—and persistent gaps, including fragmented governance, uneven prehospital services with robust urban but limited rural capacity, and an absence of a trauma registry. With urbanization, rising road traffic, and the 2030 FIFA World Cup ahead, developing a coordinated system that equitably serves urban and rural populations is a national priority (table 2). In this report, we examine the delegation’s understanding of how trauma care is organized across the continuum—from access to recovery—then consider the capacities that shape performance (workforce, information systems, preparedness) and the structural conditions that influence equity, drawing on field perspectives and lessons from the 2023 earthquake to frame reflections rather than prescriptions.

Primary prevention: preventing injuries before they occur

Injury epidemiology and prevention

Injuries account for ~7% of deaths in Morocco, with road traffic injuries as the leading contributor. Moroccan stakeholders described weak enforcement of safety measures and variable prevention campaigns, alongside persisting risks from workplace and domestic injuries. These prevention challenges, together with national trauma indicators, are summarized in table 2. Lack of national data (see below) also hampers prevention strategies.

Equity and regional disparities

As with many developed and developing countries, urban–rural inequities persist in Morocco. These disparities appeared profound when contrasting modern urban centers such as Rabat with villages a mere 50 km away. Urban centers benefit from advanced infrastructure and staffing, while rural populations face long transport times, few providers, limited rehabilitation, and weaker infrastructure. The 2023 Al Haouz earthquake further exposed these disparities: rural High Atlas communities suffered disproportionate mortality and damage, as traditional masonry and adobe structures—many predating Morocco’s seismic code—collapsed at high rates, whereas modern code-compliant urban buildings withstood shaking.1 2 World Bank assessments likewise noted the earthquake disproportionately affected already impoverished mountain provinces, showing how poverty remains excluded from Morocco’s economic transformation.3

These inequities exacerbate trauma-related mortality and disability and underscore how the built environment functions as a determinant of public health, with structural vulnerability shaping survival during disasters. International experience suggests rural populations often remain underserved unless planning tools explicitly account for geography, road infrastructure, and transport times, a lesson highly relevant to Morocco’s mountainous regions, where limited helicopter transport exists and many communities remain difficult to access.4 Moroccan stakeholders pointed to opportunities including targeted workforce deployment, mobile health interventions, telemedicine expansion, and rural transport investment. Equity was consistently framed as a core principle for any sustainable trauma system.

Cultural barriers to blood and organ donation

The delegation was informed about significant cultural and systemic barriers to blood and organ donation. Blood donations have declined since the transition from directed donations to voluntary campaigns, resulting in chronic shortages. Over 90% of families refuse organ donation, citing mistrust in the healthcare system. Moroccan stakeholders suggested that building public trust through transparent governance, visible leadership advocacy, and national media campaigns may help overcome these cultural barriers. Strengthening donation systems was frequently described as important for improving trauma outcomes, as access to blood and organs directly impacts survival and recovery.

Secondary prevention: rapid detection and early intervention

Prehospital care

Moroccan stakeholders described prehospital care as one of the weakest links in Morocco’s trauma system. Given Morocco’s history and connection with France, it is understandable that the country adopted the French SAMU model of emergency medical services (EMS), which focuses on delivery of basic and advanced medical care in the field by advanced medical providers, the classic “stay-and-play” model of EMS. At each medical institution, stakeholders discussed this in detail, and the delegation strongly advocated for a more rapid “scoop-and-run” approach, which has demonstrated improved outcomes by decreasing scene times.5 6 These observations align with broader evidence that developing trauma systems often emphasize hospital capacity, while barriers to reaching care remain under-assessed.7 Interviewees emphasized paramedic training, stronger communication systems, and prehospital notification as feasible near-term priorities. Key features of Morocco’s current system, relevant international evidence, and opportunities for reform are summarized in table 3. Normally, there is no notification from an ambulance bringing in a patient. EMS is directed by patient choice; unconscious victims come first to the trauma center, but patient or family can ultimately choose where to be admitted. Prehospital trauma care in Morocco and international comparisons

Governance and system fragmentation

The Ministry of Health has identified trauma care as a key reform priority. However, Moroccan stakeholders described trauma system governance as fragmented. Regional autonomy has been associated with inconsistent protocols, duplication of effort, and uneven quality of care across Morocco. There is no national trauma authority or formal system of trauma center designation. Trauma care pathways depend on local practices, varying between urban and rural settings. This mirrors findings from a global review showing that, although WHO’s essential trauma care guidelines have been widely disseminated, their systematic incorporation into national policy remains limited across many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Morocco.8 Stakeholders noted centralized governance could unify standards, resources, and monitoring.

Experiences from other developing countries suggest that national investment in prehospital trauma systems can deliver measurable mortality reductions,9 which several Moroccan stakeholders cited when discussing the potential value of strengthened central coordination. Stakeholders noted that centralized governance, such as a national trauma body, could unify standards, resources, and monitoring. Such a structure was described as one that could oversee the creation of a national trauma plan, ensure accountability, and facilitate integration across prehospital, hospital, and rehabilitative services. Use of evidence-based planning tools that incorporate geography, transport times, and patient volume has been reported to improve trauma system design and resource allocation,4 and stakeholders viewed these approaches as potentially informative for Morocco’s context.

Hospital infrastructure and acute care

Site visits highlighted stark contrasts in hospital infrastructure. Al Ghassani Hospital in Fes, built in 1935, is a public hospital with an aging infrastructure and limited operating room (OR) capacity. This facility serves 4.5 million residents but only has two trauma surgeons. The stakeholders at this facility described the need and incentive to work at private hospitals in Fes to supplement income but also for improved access to operating rooms. By contrast, Mohammed VI University Hospital in Marrakech exemplifies modernization, with advanced telehealth and a robust earthquake response. Across facilities, trauma care remains fragmented. The teams are variably defined, composed of various clinicians and often led by junior staff with limited Advance Trauma Life Support (ATLS) exposure. There is difficulty caring for multiple patients simultaneously. Patient care responsibility shifts between services rather than a dedicated trauma model. These patterns contribute to delays, inconsistent triage, and outcome variability. Data systems

Data collection remains a critical weakness with no local, regional, or national trauma registry, leaving gaps in injury epidemiology, outcomes, and system performance. This absence hinders identification of high-risk groups, trend tracking, and system improvement. Most LMIC trauma evaluations also overemphasize facility readiness while neglecting community and prehospital barriers, leaving major drivers of avoidable mortality unmeasured.7 Similar patterns are seen globally, where quality measurement in emergency care focuses on structure and process indicators, with limited outcome metrics.10

An evaluation of Moroccan trauma services likewise noted the lack of quality improvement programs and a registry, representing current challenges.11 Across LMICs, data quality issues, resource shortages, and organizational obstacles often impede registry development despite proven benefits.12 Where implemented, registries paired with provider literacy efforts have improved care quality and funding.13 Morocco’s cancer registry shows such systems are feasible and effective. Moroccan stakeholders viewed a national trauma registry—integrating police, hospital, and EMS data—as essential for evidence-based planning and resource allocation.

Tertiary prevention: reducing long-term disability and improving outcomes

“Trauma center” development

Interest has emerged in developing a dedicated trauma center separate from existing tertiary hospitals, reflecting some variation in how the term “trauma center” is understood. In the USA, level 1 trauma centers are typically integrated within quaternary care institutions to ensure immediate access to subspecialty, operative, and critical care resources—an approach that promotes coordination and efficiency. Plans to build a new trauma center near the 2030 FIFA World Cup complex may expand regional access but risk separation from essential service lines needed for comprehensive trauma care across all service lines.

Workforce challenges and education

Stakeholders cited severe workforce shortages: ICU nurses often cared for three to four patients in extended (12–24 hours) shifts, while physician deficits were worsened by voluntary migration to private hospitals or abroad. Adult and pediatric surgeon stakeholders described government policies that assign physicians to underserved regions following publicly funded training, an approach intended to strengthen rural coverage. Recent reforms that shortened this mandatory service were viewed variably—by some as a needed strategy to attract new graduates, but by others as inadvertently facilitating earlier transitions to private practice or international employment, thereby accelerating attrition in rural areas.

Educational reforms aim to expand medical school enrollment from 20 000 to 60 000 students by 2030, but trauma-specific training remains limited. Junior residents and interns often serve as frontline providers with minimal supervision, while ATLS and team-based trauma training are scarce. Stakeholders expressed interest in adopting international standards, such as those of the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma. Expanding trauma curricula and introducing mid-level providers, including nurse practitioners, physician assistants and pharmacists, were frequently cited as ways to address critical gaps.

Rehabilitation and mental health services

Stakeholders described rehabilitation and mental health services as underdeveloped. Infrastructure was often characterized as limited, and there are reportedly few trained specialists. Patients with long-term disabilities were reported to face limited access to rehabilitation centers and psychosocial support. Pediatric trauma patients are particularly vulnerable, as stakeholders noted few rehabilitation resources tailored to children and no inpatient rehab centers, resulting in long lengths of stay. Stakeholders emphasized integrating rehabilitation into the trauma continuum to restore function and reduce long-term burden.

Mental health services and psychosocial support were generally reported to be limited, and stakeholders cited very few trained providers. The 2023 earthquake displaced more than 100 000 children, many of whom remain at risk of enduring psychological trauma.14 Stakeholders frequently identified expanding mental health services, including training counselors and embedding psychosocial care into hospital settings, as a priority. These needs were characterized as urgent given evidence that earthquakes can generate persistent psychiatric conditions such as post-traumatic stress, depression, and anxiety that may last for years.15

Pediatric trauma care

Pediatric trauma clinical expertise was frequently described by stakeholders as a major unmet need in Morocco. Mohammed VI Hospital maintains a pediatric ward, though stakeholders noted challenges with staffing and operating room availability, with operations often occurring overnight. Children disproportionately suffer motorcycle, fall, and domestic injuries, yet clinicians had limited protocols to expedite care. There are just over 300 pediatric surgeons and surgical subspecialists in Morocco and few available training programs. Pediatric Orthopedics is a specialty, and neurosurgeons care for both children and adults. There are “firega” (traditional medicine) and chiropractor-like providers, contributing to delays in care; for pediatric fractures this may be up to 1–2 months. Pediatric surgeons care for emergency patients, perform elective surgery, see clinic patients, and provide ICU care. Nursing competencies for children are limited, with nurses often caring for a variety of patients, including children. Stakeholders frequently identified expanding pediatric trauma training, strengthening rehabilitation capacity for children, and targeted injury prevention campaigns, such as helmet use for children, as priorities that could support a trauma system that meets the needs of the entire population.

Disaster preparedness

The 2023 Marrakech earthquake served as a natural stress test of Morocco’s trauma system. An emergency intervention plan already existed, and there had been simulation exercises. The military rapidly deployed a field triage hospital and coordinated air and ground transport. As the closest tertiary referral center with advanced trauma and critical care services, Mohammed VI University Hospital in Marrakech suspended elective surgeries, mobilized multiple operating rooms, and coordinated care across city hospitals through load balancing. There are several specialty hospitals, and they were opened for victims during the earthquake. Telemedicine was used to take care of patients with chronic disease. There was a plan to keep families together. Patients who arrived alive consistently received timely, high-quality care reflecting modern disaster-trauma practice. Teams used surgical and interventional radiology strategies for hemorrhage control, alongside fasciotomy and resuscitation in crush and compartment syndromes.

The response underscored vulnerabilities but also strengths: reliance on the military and ad hoc coordination instead of pre-existing plans proved to be a successful strategy during the earthquake, with the government authorizing the central and coordinated response, making Mohammed VI the lead. This avoided the pitfall of fragmented delivery of care. After-action analysis of the earthquake emphasized that effective disaster response depends not only on the rapid clinical and logistical deployment but also on inter-institutional coordination, simulation training, and advance planning.16 Stakeholders repeatedly cited routine disaster drills, permanent helipads at major hospitals, and integration of disaster preparedness into the trauma system as priorities going forward. Public health reports described heightened needs among vulnerable groups, reinforcing the value of psychosocial support and community resilience in planning.17 18

Conclusion

Based on our delegation’s visit, the Kingdom of Morocco is at a pivotal moment in the development of an organized system of trauma care delivery. The nation faces issues similar to those faced by more developed countries, such as the urban/rural divide, workforce shortages, “brain drain,” a lack of regional and national trauma data, and no overarching trauma-system governance. Yet this visit also demonstrated a clear understanding of the need to improve the delivery of trauma care throughout the Kingdom. The 2023 earthquake demonstrated that rapid mobilization and high-quality care are possible when coordination and resources align. This report reflects lessons from site visits and stakeholder perspectives, highlighting barriers and opportunities that could shape Morocco’s path forward. We hope that this delegation will be the first of many interactions between the AAST and the Kingdom of Morocco, and the organization can provide ongoing expertise to help develop and improve the trauma system in Morocco.
