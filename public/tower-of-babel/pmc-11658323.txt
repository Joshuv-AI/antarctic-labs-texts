Mediating effect of social constraints in the association between stigma and depressive symptoms in Chinese breast cancer patients
Authors: ChunYing Cui, Lie Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe present study aimed to investigate the effect of stigma on depressive symptoms and explore the mediating role of social constraints between stigma and depressive symptoms in Chinese breast cancer (BC) patients.MethodsA cross-sectional design was conducted in China from December 2020 to July 2021. Out of 256 participants, 222 BC patients completed the questionnaires composed of demographic and clinical information, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Social Impact Scale (SIS), and 15-item Social Constraints Scale (SCS-15). The hierarchical regression analysis was adopted to explore the associations of stigma and social constraints with depressive symptoms and the mediating role of social constraints.ResultsApproximately 20% of participants reported having depressive symptoms in Chinese BC patients. The indirect effect of stigma on depressive symptoms through social constraints was significant (a*b = 0.24, 95%CI = 0.15–0.33). When the mediator was controlled for, the direct impact of stigma on depressive symptoms was also significant (c’=0.16, p < 0.01).ConclusionThe current study suggested that stigma may contribute to developing depressive symptoms by increasing social constraints in Chinese BC patients. Interventions based on reducing stigma and social constraints should be conducted to improve mental health in BC patients.

Introduction

Female breast cancer (BC) is the second leading cause of global cancer incidence in 2022, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases, accounting for 11.6% of all cancer cases [1]. It is the fourth leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, with 666,000 deaths (6.9% of all cancer deaths) [1]. It is estimated that the five-year prevalence of BC was 168/100,000 in China [2]. Furthermore, there are 357,161 new BC cases and 74,986 deaths in Chinese women in 2022 [2]. BC diagnosis, as the most stressful event in an individual’s life, often causes various physical and psychological challenges. BC survivors suffer physical torment owing to cancer treatment and disease symptoms. Besides, they have more psychological burdens due to a variety of negative emotions, such as fear of recurrence and extreme emotional distress [3]. Depressive symptoms are one of the most frequent psychological distresses in patients with BC [4, 5]. Depressive symptoms were significantly associated with psychosocial factors.

The association between stigma and depressive symptoms has been supported by emerging evidence [6, 7]. Stigma is feelings of isolation, degradation, criticism, and rejection during personal experience and a social process, which affects psychological, physical, and social adjustment [8]. Patients feel devalued or discriminated against due to cancer diagnosis, and cancer-related stigma thereby occurs [9]. In Chinese culture, interdependent values underline the importance of maintaining social standing and one’s face [10]. Several studies reported that some traditional Chinese beliefs thought that cancer is a result of bad karma or is a punishment owing to the individuals’ or their ancestors’ prior wrongdoing [11–13]. Therefore, one’s social standing may be undermined by BC diagnosis, which can result in self-blame and shame. Past studies found that BC patients with high stigma often suffer greater depressive symptoms [13] and poorer sleep quality [14].

Problematically, the psychological mechanism underlying the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms in patients with BC is not yet clear. Tang et al. [15] found that stigma was significantly related to higher social conflicts and lower social support in BC patients, indicating that social interactions were implicated in the stigma-psychological distress relationship [16]. Studies have reported that stigma negatively affects social interactions in BC cancer patients [16]. Regarding this view, stigma about BC could result in social isolation [17] and limit the ability to discuss their diseases within their social network [18], worsening psychological distress. Depressive symptoms were the most frequent psychological distress in cancer patients [19]. Based on the social-cognitive processing theory [20], the social environment affects people’s cognitive processing and shapes individuals’ adjustment to cancer by altering how they talk, think, and feel the effects of cancer and their relationships. Stigma in patients was associated with a negative social environment, such as limited sources of social support and social relationship issues. Thereby, constraints on disclosure within the social network environment could generate, which may cause persistent psychological distress during disease treatment [21]. Social constraints are defined as the objective existence and subjective perceptions of social conditions that hinder individuals from disclosing or modifying how they disclose illness-related feelings or concerns [20]. Wu et al. found that stigma was positively associated with more social constraints in BC patients [14]. In addition, Chambers et al. [22] social constraints mediated the effect of stigma on quality of life and psychological distress in lung cancer patients. Accordingly, our study expected that stigma could limit BC patients’ emotional expression in the face of traumatic events, and patients thereby experience higher levels of depressive symptoms.

Studies have supported the relationship between social constraints and depressive symptoms. According to the social-cognitive processing theory [23], depressive symptoms may be influenced by one’s ability to discuss a traumatic event (e.g., cancer) with a significant other. Specifically, this theory thought that if a person’s attempts to talk about a stressful event are blocked by social constraints or unsupportive responses (e.g., avoidance, denial, or minimization), they may not be able to adequately process the event [24], resulting in depressive symptoms. For BC patients, individuals with higher social constraints had greater depressive symptoms [20]. Previous studies have displayed that those social constraints were associated with greater depression in patients with BC [25–27]. For example, less emotional expression about cancer was associated with greater depression and less well-being [25].

At present, few studies have explored the psychological mechanism between stigma and depressive symptoms in BC patients. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association between stigma, social constraints, and depressive symptoms and their underlying psychological pathways in Chinese patients with BC. Specifically, our study hypothesized that stigma was linked to depressive symptoms in Chinese BC patients. Social constraints were expected to mediate the impact of stigma on depressive symptoms, such that stigma was related to higher social constraints, which in turn affects depressive symptoms.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional design study was conducted in Shenyang, Liaoning province, China, from December 2020 to July 2021. Participants were recruited from the Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University. The inclusion criteria of this study included patients (1) diagnosed with BC, (2) able to understand and communicate with Chinese, and (3) at least 18 years old. The exclusion criteria of this study included that the patients (1) had other severe diseases (such as severe cardiovascular disease), (2) had a history of psychiatry, and (3) had cognitive and intellectual disorders. The study was approved by the Committee on Human Experimentation of China Medical University, and the study procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Each eligible patient will be distributed self-reported questionnaires after writing the informed consent form for this study.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated before breast cancer patients’ recruitment by the formula n=(Zα/2)2P(1-P)/δ2. In the formula, α = 0.05, Zα/2=1.96. P was the prevalence of depressive symptoms in Chinese breast cancer patients, which was estimated according to the results of previous studies. δ was a tolerable error. In our research, P = 0.338 [28], and δ = 0.2P. Therefore, the minimum sample size of this study was 188. Initially, 256 breast cancer patients were recruited as potential participants. However, 20 patients refused this investigation, and 14 questionnaires were excluded due to invalid data (missing data > 30%). Therefore, our study had 222 Chinese BC patients. The effective response rate of this study was 86.7%.

Recruitment procedures

In our study, the second author was responsible for recruitment. He contacted doctors who worked at the Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University. Then, the doctors directly recommended potential participants and stated the study’s purpose for them. Recruitment was in two steps. First, participants who signed the informed consent form were offered the questionnaire to fill out during hospitalization. Second, clinical information about patients was obtained through the medical records. Non-responders were re-contacted if no response was received after discharge. Each participant needed approximately ten minutes to fill out the questionnaire.

Measurement of depressive symptoms

The present study adopted the Chinese version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [29] to evaluate the depressive symptoms of patients with BC cancer over the past two weeks. It consists of nine items and the global score ranges from 0 to 27. A total score of > 9 is regarded as a cut-off value, which indicates the existence of depressive symptoms in BC patients. A higher score of PHQ-9 reports more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s coefficient for the PHQ-9 was 0.868.

Measurement of stigma

The level of stigma was measured by using the Chinese version of the Social Impact Scale (SIS) [30] in BC patients in the present study. Each item of SIS adopts a 4-point Likert type varying from “1 = very disagree” to “4 = very agree”, and higher scores mean a higher level of stigma. The Cronbach’s coefficient for the SIS was 0.949 in the present study.

Measurement of social constraints

The Chinese version of the 15-item Social Constraints Scale (SCS-15) [31] was used to test the level of social constraints in BC patients in the past month. The SCS-15 was translated from English to Chinese and tested by You and Lu [32] and has been reported to have good reliability and validity in BC patients [33, 34]. According to a previous study [35], two items (tell you not to worry so much about your health; tell you to try not to think about cancer) were omitted because of low item-total correlations. Therefore, the scale in the current study includes 13 items that are scored on a four-point Likert type (from 1= “never” to 4= “often”). The total score is from 13 to 52, and a higher score suggests more social constraints in BC patients. In the present study, Cronbach’s coefficient for the scale was 0.892.

Demographic and clinical information

The present study also included several demographic information, including age, marital status, educational background, and monthly income. Clinical information, including period situation, time after diagnosis, and cancer stage, was collected from medical records.

Statistical analysis

T-tests and one-way ANOVAs were used to examine group differences in depressive symptoms. The correlation coefficient (r) in continuous variables was tested by using correlation analysis. Asymptotic and resampling strategies [36] were used to examine the mediating roles of social constraints on the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms. The regression equation modeled stigma as an independent variable, depressive symptoms as the outcome, social constraints as the mediator, and age and marriage status as covariates. In the analysis, the first step was to examine the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms (the c path), and the second was to test the mediating effect (the a*b products) of social constraints. There was the mediating role of social constraints if the c’ path coefficient in step 2 was smaller than the c path coefficient in step 1 or was insignificant. The bootstrap estimate was based on 5000 bootstrap samples. The bias-corrected and accelerated 95% confidence interval (BCa 95% CI) for mediation was calculated, and a BCa 95% CI excluding 0 indicated a significant mediating role. SPSS 22.0 software was used for statistical analysis, and the significance level was p-value < 0.05.

Social constraints as a mediator of the association between stigma and depressive symptoms

Figure 1 presents the result of the simple mediation model. In step 1, stigma was associated with more significant depressive symptoms (c = 0.40, p < 0.001). In step 2, stigma was related to higher depressive symptoms (c’=0.16, p = 0.007), and the c’ path coefficient was smaller than the c path coefficient in step 1. Stigma was associated with more significant social constraints (a = 0.49, p < 0.001). In addition, social constraints were related to more significant depressive symptoms (b = 0.49, p < 0.001). These results suggested that the indirect effect was significant such that social constraints mediated the association between stigma and depressive symptoms (a*b = 0.24, 95%CI = 0.15–0.33). In sum, the result suggested that social constraints partially explained the link between stigma and depressive symptoms, such that individuals with high stigma tend to suffer more significant social constraints, and they, thereby, are more likely to have more significant depressive symptoms.

Discussion

This was the first study to explore the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms and examine its mediator in Chinese BC patients. Our study suggested how stigma is associated with depressive symptoms and how social constraints mediated the link in Chinese BC patients, which contributes to the literature. Although research studying the impacts on depressive symptoms has been conducted in BC patients [37, 38], the psychological pathways underlying the relationship are not well-understudied. Our findings reported that social constraints may be one mechanism by which stigma was related to depressive symptoms. Namely, the dilemma of whether to conceal or express emotions related to stigma may contribute to depressive symptoms. Besides, the proportion of social constraints mediating effect was 60% for the association between stigma and depressive symptoms in the present study. The significant indirect effects in our study more empirically supported the mediation model.

First, our results reported that Chinese BC patients who conflict with stigmatizing beliefs tend to conceal sensitive, emotional events concerning their cancer diagnosis and treatments. Studies on illness stigmatization among chronic disease patients presented that individuals experience stress, anxiety, and significant social isolation owing to internalized negative beliefs related to their disease [39]. Based on the cognitive-affective-behavioral model of burying stigmatization, the stigma experienced when patients talk about their cancer diagnosis and treatments may trigger negative affect and fear of discovery, which thereby contributes to concealing behaviors.

Second, concealing emotions results from cultural beliefs to maintain interpersonal harmony among Chinese [40]. Openly speaking concerns and feelings with family and close friends are few among Chinese cancer patients. Past research on social constraints found that cancer patients’ caregivers tend to avoid discussing cancer-related experiences and hide negative feelings rather than support cancer patients’ emotional disclosure [40, 41], which thereby exacerbates psychological distress. Our findings suggested that social constraints on disclosure were associated with more significant depressive symptoms. Besides, our results reported that stigma beliefs (e.g., cancer is a contagious disease) could contribute to the development of depressive symptoms in Chinese BC patients, which was by previous research [42, 43]. For example, Warmoth et al. found stigma is a frequently reported and experienced stressor in Chinese American BC patients, which can negatively affect patients’ mental health and psychological adjustment [43].

Furthermore, our study found a statistically significant mediation effect of social constraints between stigma and depressive symptoms, which indicates that depressive symptoms could be improved by reducing the contributions of stigma after taking social constraints into account. Consistent with the social cognitive processing theory [23] and with stigma theory [44], shame or guilt and fear of discrimination caused by stigma appeared to constrain BC patients’ perception that they can talk about their treatment and prognosis of diseases. It can be seen that these constraints limit patients’ ability to access support and their opportunities to process their cancer experience to gain a sense of identity, leading to depressive symptoms in BC patients. Chambers et al. [22] also found that social constraints in lung cancer patients mediated the association between stigma and psychological distress. However, our results do not necessarily indicate that social constraints are the only mediator in the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms. Therefore, the following research could investigate other mediators in the relationship.

At last, univariate analysis between demographic and clinical information and depressive symptoms presented that the younger or the Single/divorced/widowed/ separated was associated with more significant depressive symptoms, respectively. Our findings are consistent with past studies [45, 46]. Younger women patients are more afraid of infertility since the production of female hormones is less, and the period is short before menopause due to chemotherapy treatment [47]. Besides, younger women pay more attention to body image [48]. Hence, such women would have more fear of disease recurrence and family and marital problems and suffer from more body afflictions [48]. Married BC women have fewer depressive symptoms owing to high levels of emotional and family support compared with Single/divorced/widowed/ separated.

Clinical implications

Several clinical implications should be emphasized in practice. Firstly, cancer-related outcomes should be taken seriously, especially psychological distress (namely depressive symptoms), during the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. Depression has a detrimental effect on treatment adherence, potential survival, and quality of life. Secondly, past research reported that social constraints on disclosure could be decreased [49, 50]. It is challenging for Chinese BC patients to reduce social constraints because of maintaining interpersonal harmony from cultural expectations [40]. However, another study found that expressive writing interventions effectively contribute to avoiding social constraints and improving the quality of life in Chinese American BC patients. Lastly, clinicians should pay more attention to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depressive symptoms in Chinese BC patients since CBT has been shown to have a positive effect on the treatment of psychological distress in BC patients [51–53].

Limitations

There were some limitations in this study. At first, a cross-sectional survey was conducted in the present study, which had difficulty in drawing casual associations of stigma and social constraints with depressive symptoms in Chinese BC patients. Thus, longitudinal designs should be used to replicate our findings in further study. Second, stigma, social constraints, and depressive symptoms were measured by adopting self-reported scales, which might result in recall bias and response bias. Therefore, some effective process control measures should be used to minimize these biases in further study. Third, our study only included breast cancer patients at stages I and II. In other words, more severe patients were not included in our research, which would be selection bias. Finally, although our study examined the relationships between stigma, social constraints, and depressive symptoms, treatment-related variables were not collected in this study. Hence, the effect of these possible risk factors on depressive symptoms should be explored in further research.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated the detrimental influence of stigma on depressive symptoms, which contributes to the growing research on Chinese BC patients. Our study found that stigma and social constraints were significantly and positively associated with depressive symptoms. Meanwhile, social constraints could mediate the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms in Chinese BC patients. Therefore, interventions based on culturally sensitive behaviors should be applied to address concerns about stigma and depressive symptoms in Chinese BC patients.
