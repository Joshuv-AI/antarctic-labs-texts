A subspace-conjugate gradient method for linear matrix equations Thanks: Version of August 24, 2026
Davide Palitta, Martina Iannacito †, Valeria Simoncini †

Abstract
The efficient solution of large-scale multiterm linear matrix equations is a challenging task in numerical linear algebra, and it is a largely open problem. We propose a new iterative scheme for symmetric and positive definite operators, significantly advancing methods such as truncated matrix-oriented Conjugate Gradients ( cg ). The new algorithm capitalizes on the low-rank matrix format of its iterates by fully exploiting the subspace information of the factors as iterations proceed. The approach implicitly relies on orthogonality conditions imposed over much larger subspaces than in cg , unveiling insightful connections with subspace projection methods. The new method is also equipped with memory-saving strategies. In particular, we show that for a given matrix 𝒀 \bm{Y} , the action ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒀 ) {\cal L}(\bm{Y}) in low rank format may not be evaluated exactly due to memory constraints. This problem is often underestimated, though it will eventually produce Out-of-Memory breakdowns for a sufficiently large number of terms. We propose an ad-hoc randomized range-finding strategy that appears to fully resolve this shortcoming. Experimental results with typical application problems illustrate the potential of our approach over various methods developed in the recent literature.

Multiterm matrix equations, conjugate gradient, Galerkin condition.

Introduction

We are interested in the numerical solution of the problem

\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}\bm{B}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{A}_{\ell}\bm{X}\bm{B}_{\ell}=\bm{C},

(1)

where
\bm{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{A}\times n_{A}}
,
\bm{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{B}\times n_{B}}
,

i=1,\ldots,\ell
, are symmetric matrices,
and
{\bm{{C}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{A}\times n_{B}}
has rank
s_{C}\ll\min\{n_{A},n_{B}\}
so that we can write
\bm{C}=C_{1}C_{2}^{\text{T}}
,
C_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{A}\times s_{C}}
,
C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{B}\times s_{C}}
.
By introducing the linear operator

{\cal L}(\bm{X})=\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}\bm{B}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{A}_{\ell}\bm{X}\bm{B}_{\ell}
,
in the following we will use the more compact notation

{\cal L}(\bm{X})=\bm{C}

for the matrix equation above.
We assume that
\cal L
is positive definite in the matrix inner product, that is
it holds that
\langle\bm{X},{\cal L}(\bm{X})\rangle>0
for any nonzero

\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{A}\times n_{B}}
.
Given two
n\times m
matrices
{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}
, we define the matrix inner product as

\langle{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}\rangle={\rm trace}({\bm{X}}^{\text{T}}{\bm{Y}}).

This inner product defines the Frobenius norm
\|\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}=\langle{\bm{X}},{\bm{X}}\rangle
.

Encountered samples of
\cal L
include
for instance the generalized Lyapunov equation

\mathcal{L}({\bm{{X}}})={\bm{{AXE^{\text{T}}}}}+{\bm{{EXA}}}^{{\text{T}}}

and its multiterm counterpart
\mathcal{L}({\bm{{X}}})=\bm{A}\bm{X}\bm{E}^{\text{T}}+\bm{E}\bm{X}\bm{A}^{{\text{T}}}+{\bm{{MXM}}}^{{\text{T}}}

with
{\bm{{A}}},{\bm{{E}}},{\bm{{M}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, as they occur in control theory

[
5
]
,
[
4
, Ch.6]
;
in our setting we have the additional hypothesis that all coefficient
matrices are symmetric.
In the following we will call a multiterm Lyapunov equation an equation as in (
1
) where
\bm{C}
is symmetric and
\mathcal{L}
is such that
(\mathcal{L}(\bm{X}))^{{\text{T}}}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{X})
for any symmetric
\bm{X}
. This
implies that the solution to a multiterm Lyapunov equation is symmetric.

More general forms typically arise whenever the terms on the left and right of the unknown have different meaning in the original application,
such as geometric space vs time (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
,
[
20
]
),
or geometric space vs
parameter space (e.g.,
[

]
,
[

]
), or
optimization (e.g.,
[

]

[

We will refer to this general latter structure as multiterm Sylvester equation
*

Often
the term “generalized Sylvester” is used for the same equation. The term
“generalized” is also employed for two-term equations, for rectangular problems, and some
multi-variable contexts. To avoid ambiguity we prefer to use the name “multiterm Sylvester”.
.

Many different solid methods for the solution of
equation (
1
) for
\ell=2

past two decades (see, e.g., the survey
[
30
]
).
On the other hand,
having a number of terms

in (
1

treatment of this equation extremely challenging. Fewer options are
available in the literature for medium up to large dimensions of the
coefficient matrices.
In particular, to the best of our knowledge, no decomposition-based method able to simultaneously triangularize
\ell>2
matrices is available in the literature
so that, up to date, recasting the problem in terms of its Kronecker form is the only option to get a direct solution.
However, this strategy suffers from excessive
memory constraints and computational cost even for moderate dimensions of the coefficient matrices,
so that only iterative procedures for the solution of (
1
) are being explored.

Among the classes of contributions in this direction are
matrix-oriented Krylov methods with low-rank
truncations

[
19
]
,
[
32
]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
projection methods tailored to the equation at

[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
fixed-point iterations
[

]
,
[

]
,
Riemannian optimization schemes
[

]
, and
greedy procedures
[

]
.

In the following we focus our attention on short recurrences associated
with matrix-oriented Krylov methods.
These schemes amount to adapting standard Krylov schemes for
linear systems to matrix equations by leveraging
the equivalence between (
1
) and its Kronecker form.
Thanks to our hypotheses on
\mathcal{L}
, the coefficient matrix of
the linear system in Kronecker form is symmetric positive definite so that the
Conjugate Gradient method (
cg
) can be applied;
see, e.g.,
[
19
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
3
]
and
section
2
for more details.
By building upon the low rank structure of the right-hand side,
matrix-oriented
cg
generates matrix recurrences, rather than
vector recurrences, in
factored
form, thus allowing high memory
and computational savings, while retaining the optimality properties
when brought back to the vectorized form.
Unfortunately, as the iterations progress, recurrence factors may quickly
increase their rank, losing the advantages of the whole matrix-oriented procedure.
Rank truncation strategies of the factor iterates are usually enforced
so as to keep memory allocations under control. As a side effect, however,
convergence is often delayed, also possibly leading to stagnation

[
19
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
31
]
.

By taking inspiration from this class of methods, we design a
new iterative scheme for the solution of (
1
) that better
exploits the rich subspace information obtained
with the computed quantities, to define the next factorized iterates.
More precisely, at each iteration the next approximate solution and
direction are obtained by imposing a functional optimality with respect
to the whole range of the low rank factors available in the current iteration.
This should be compared with the approximate solution in matrix-oriented
cg
,
obtained at each iteration by a functional optimality with respect to a single vector.

The idea appears to be new, as it goes far beyond
the algorithmic developments
usually associated with matrix-oriented approaches. While being closer to
optimization procedures based on manifolds, it does not share the same complexity
in the definition of the funding recurrences, as the new method is still fully derived
from the original Conjugate Gradient method for linear systems.
Truncation strategies are devised to maintain the computed matrix iterates low rank.

In designing the new approach we address a memory allocation issue that becomes crucial
when the number
\ell
of terms in (
1
) is significantly larger than two.
More precisely, for a given matrix
\bm{Y}
, the action
{\cal L}(\bm{Y})
in low rank format
may not be evaluated exactly, making it impossible to generate quantities such as
the residual matrix. This problem is often underestimated in the current literature,
though it will eventually produce Out-of-Memory breakdowns in actual computations, for

\ell
large enough. We propose an ad-hoc
randomized range-finding strategy that appears to fully resolve this shortcoming,
keeping the memory allocations under control.

We name the new method the preconditioned
subspace-conjugate gradient method

(
s
s
–
cg
)
to emphasize the role of subspaces in the recurrences.
This novel point of view leads to remarkable computational
gains making
s
s
–
cg
a very competitive option for the solution of
multiterm linear matrix equations of the form (
1
).
Computational experiments with a selection of quite diverse problems illustrate
the potential of the new strategy, when compared with other methods specifically
designed for the considered matrix equations.

2
Truncated matrix-oriented CG

This section is devoted to surveying the well exercised
matrix-oriented version of the Conjugate Gradient method, together
with its truncated variant.

When addressing the solution of (
1
), the
Kronecker formulation of the problem, namely

(\bm{B}_{1}^{\text{T}}\otimes\bm{A}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{B}_{\ell}^{\text{T}}\otimes\bm{A}_{\ell}){\rm vec}(\bm{X})={\rm vec}(\bm{C})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad{\cal A}x=c,

(2)

allows one to directly employ the classical Preconditioned
Conjugate Gradient (
pcg
) method. A careful implementation should avoid the
explicit construction of
\cal A
, so that matrix-vector products
can be carried out in the original matrix form with the

{\cal L}
operator.
Even with this precaution, all vector iterates still
have
n_{A}n_{B}
components, so that whenever

n_{A},n_{B}
are large, memory allocations may become prohibitive.
A particularly convenient way out occurs when
\bm{C}
is very low rank.
In this case, under certain conditions, the
exact
solution
\bm{X}
may also
be well approximated by a low rank matrix

[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
19
]
.
To exploit this characterization, all vector iterates are transformed
back to matrices and kept in low-rank matrix format.
Unfortunately, although during the first few
pcg
iterations
the iterates maintain low rank, the rank itself grows as the method
proceeds.
To control the memory requirements of the procedure after the first
few iterations, truncation of the iterate factors are usually performed.
We refer to, e.g.,

[
19
, Algorithm 2]
,
[
31
]
for the
algorithmic description.

As long as no low-rank truncations are performed,
truncated
pcg

is mathematically equivalent to applying the
standard,
vectorized

cg
method to the linear system
stemming from (
1
) via the Kronecker form in (
2
).
Implementing low-rank truncations may be viewed as a simple computational device
to make the solution process affordable in terms of storage allocation and not
as an algorithmic advance.
The final attainable accuracy when truncation is in place depends on the truncation
tolerance and on the decay of the singular values in the problem solution matrix; we
refer to

[
2
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
31
]

for a detailed discussion on the effect of truncation.

3
The
s
s
–
cg
method for the multiterm Lyapunov equation

In this section we derive our new method for the multiterm Lyapunov equation,
that is we assume that
{\cal L}(\bm{X})={\cal L}(\bm{X})^{\text{T}}
for any symmetric
\bm{X}
, and
that
\bm{C}
is symmetric. In section
5
we will discuss the changes occurring
when generalizing the procedure to the multiterm Sylvester case.

The key idea is to
build a Conjugate Gradient type method remaining in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
while generating quantities based on
subspaces
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, rather than
on
vectors
of
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
, the way the original truncated
pcg
does.

We follow the classical derivation of
cg
as a procedure to approximate
the minimizer of a convex function.
Let the function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined as

\Phi({\bm{{X}}})=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{{X}}},\mathcal{L}\bigl({\bm{{X}}}\bigr)\rangle-\langle{\bm{{X}}},{\bm{{C}}}\rangle.

(3)

We then consider the following minimization problem: Find
{\bm{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

\bm{X}={\rm arg}\min_{{\bm{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\Phi({\bm{{X}}}).

Starting with a zero initial guess
{\bm{{X}}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and

{P}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s_{C}}
, where we recall that
s_{C}
is the
rank of
\bm{C}
, we define the recurrence
\{\bm{X}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}

of approximate solutions by means of the following relation

{\bm{{X}}}_{k+1}={\bm{{X}}}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}},

(4)

where
\bm{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}\times s_{k}}
and
{P}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s_{k}}
,
with corresponding recurrence for the residual

{\bm{{R}}}_{k+1}=\bm{C}-{\cal L}({\bm{{X}}}_{k+1})
, that is

{\bm{{R}}}_{k+1}={\bm{{R}}}_{k}-\mathcal{L}({P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}})
.
We emphasize that
s_{k}
depends on the iteration index
k
, that is the number of columns of
P_{k}
may (and will)
change as the iterations proceed, possibly growing
up to a certain maximum value, corresponding
to the maximum allowed rank of all iterates.
Since we assume that the operator
\cal L
is
symmetric, that is
{\cal L}(\bm{X})=({\cal L}(\bm{X}))^{\text{T}}
for any symmetric matrix
\bm{X}
,
and that
\bm{C}=CC^{\text{T}}
, all iteration matrices are square and symmetric.
In section
5
we will relax these assumptions,
yielding possibly rectangular solution and iterates.

Like in the vector case, we require that the matrix
\bm{P}_{k}=P_{k}P_{k}^{{\text{T}}}

satisfies a descent direction
requirement completely conforming to the vector case, that is

\langle\nabla\Phi({\bm{{X}}}_{k}),{\bm{{P}}}_{k}\rangle<0.

(5)

To determine
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
, we
let
\phi(\bm{\alpha})=\Phi({\bm{{X}}}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}})
.
For given
{\bm{{X}}}_{k},{P}_{k}
,
at the
k
th iteration we construct
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
so that

\phi(\bm{\alpha}_{k})=\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}\times s_{k}}}\phi(\bm{\alpha}).

(6)

The minimizer
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
can be explicitly determined by
solving a linear matrix equation of reduced dimensions,
as
described in the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\bm{A}_{i}
and
\bm{B}_{i}
are symmetric, and
that
{\cal L}
is positive definite.
The minimizer
\bm{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}\times s_{k}}
of (
6
)
is the unique solution of

P_{k}^{T}{\cal L}({\bm{{X}}}_{k}+P_{k}\bm{\alpha}P_{k}^{T})P_{k}=P_{k}^{T}{\bm{{C}}}P_{k},

(7)

or, equivalently, of

P_{k}^{T}{\cal L}(P_{k}\bm{\alpha}P_{k}^{T})P_{k}=P_{k}^{T}{\bm{{R}}}_{k}P_{k}
.

Proof.

We start by explicitly writing the function
\phi
, that is

\phi(\bm{\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{{X}}}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}},{\cal L}({\bm{{X}}}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}})\rangle-\langle{\bm{{X}}}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}},\bm{C}\rangle.

To find the stationary points of
\phi
, we compute the
partial derivatives of
\phi
with respect to
\bm{\alpha}
; this can
be done in matrix compact form; see, e.g.,

[
25
]
.
We carry out this computation term by term,

\frac{\partial\text{tr}({\bm{{X}}}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\cal L}(\bm{X}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}P_{k}^{{\text{T}}}))}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial\text{tr}({\bm{{X}}}_{{k}}^{{\text{T}}}{\cal L}({P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}))}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}={P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\cal L}(\bm{X}_{k}){P}_{k},

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,\text{tr}(\bigl({P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}})^{\text{T}}\mathcal{L}\bigl({\bm{{X}}}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bigr)\bigr)}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\,\text{tr}\,\bigl(\bigl({P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bigr)^{{\text{T}}}\mathcal{L}\bigl({\bm{{X}}}_{k}\bigr)\bigr)}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}+\frac{\partial\text{tr}\,\bigl(\bigl({P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bigr)^{{\text{T}}}{\cal L}({P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}})\bigr)}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\mathcal{L}\bigl({\bm{{X}}}_{k}\bigr){P}_{k}+2{P_{k}}^{{\text{T}}}{\cal L}(P_{k}\bm{\alpha}P_{k}^{\text{T}})P_{k}.

Moreover, it holds that

\frac{\partial\text{tr}\,\bigl(\bigl({P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\bigr)^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{C}}}\bigr)}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}={P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{C}}}{P}_{k}
, see, e.g.,
[
25
, Equations (101), (102), (108), (113)]
.
The final expression of the Jacobian of
\phi
with respect to
\bm{\alpha}
is thus

\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{\alpha})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}=P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(P_{k}\bm{\alpha}P_{k}^{\text{T}})P_{k}-{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{R}}}_{k}{P}_{k}.

Consequently, the solution
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
of (
7
) is a
stationary point of
\phi
.
To ensure that
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
is a minimizer, we show that the
Hessian of
\phi
is positive definite.
To ease the reading, the Jacobian of
\phi
is vectorized, resulting in

\text{vec}\left(\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{\alpha})}{\partial\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bigl(P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\bm{{B}}}_{i}P_{k}\otimes P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\bm{{A}}}_{i}P_{k}\bigr)\text{vec}\bigl(\bm{\alpha}\bigr)-\text{vec}\bigl({P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{R}}}_{k}{P}_{k}\bigr).

The Hessian
{\bm{{H}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}^{2}\times s_{k}^{2}}
is given by

{\bm{{H}}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bigl(P_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{B}}}_{i}P_{k}\otimes P_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}_{i}P_{k}\bigr)
.
Then, using the hypothesis on the operator
\cal L
,
for any nonzero
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}^{2}}
, it holds that

\mathbf{y}^{\text{T}}{\bm{{H}}}_{k}\mathbf{y}>0
, so that
{\bm{{H}}}_{k}
is positive definite,
and
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
is a minimizer of
\phi
.

Remark 2
.

For
P_{k}
full rank, the
quantity
P_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}}
is invariant with respect to the
basis of
{\rm range}(P_{k})
used to compute
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
.
\hfill\square

The minimization problem in (
6
) can also be recast in terms of an orthogonality condition.
Indeed, solving (
6
) is equivalent to imposing
the following
subspace orthogonality condition

\text{vec}({\bm{{R}}}_{k+1})\perp\text{range}({P}_{k}\otimes{P}_{k}).

(8)

More precisely, (
8
) is equivalent to

P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\bm{{R}}}_{k+1}P_{k}=0
with

{\bm{{R}}}_{k+1}={\bm{{C}}}-\mathcal{L}({\bm{{X}}}_{k}+{P}_{k}\bm{\alpha}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}})
.
Hence, the computation of
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
follows from a
local

matrix Galerkin projection of the original problem onto a space of
dimension
s_{k}^{2}
given by the current direction matrix factor
P_{k}
. We will return on
this aspect in section
4
.

Remark 3
.

Thanks to the linearity of the matrix operator
\cal L
, products of
the form
P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(\bm{X}_{k}+\bm{Y}_{k})P_{k}
for some matrix
\bm{Y}_{k}
, become

P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(\bm{X}_{k}+\bm{Y}_{k})P_{k}=P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(\bm{X}_{k})P_{k}+P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(\bm{Y}_{k})P_{k}.

In addition,
using the low rank factor form of the argument matrix, the
left and right products act on the coefficient matrices
as in reduction processes;
see, e.g.,
[
1
,
30
]
.
For instance, for
\bm{Y}_{k}=P_{k}{\bm{\omega}}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}}
and substituting the
general operator
\cal L
in (
1
), we obtain

{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\cal L}({P}_{k}\bm{\omega}_{k}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}){P}_{k}=\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{1}{\bm{\omega}}_{k}\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{1}+\ldots+\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{\ell}{\bm{\omega}}_{k}\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{\ell},

with
\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}={P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{A}}}_{i}{P}_{k}
,
and
\widetilde{\bm{B}}_{i}={P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}{\bm{{B}}}_{i}{P}_{k}
,
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

\hfill\square

We define the recurrence for
the directions
{\bm{{P}}}_{k}
as

{\bm{{P}}}_{k+1}={\bm{{R}}}_{k+1}+{P}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}.

The matrix
\bm{\beta}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{k}\times s_{k}}

is obtained by imposing that the new directions
\bm{P}_{k+1}
are
\mathcal{L}
-orthogonal with respect to the previous ones. In particular, we write

\text{vec}({\bm{{P}}}_{k+1})\perp_{\mathcal{L}}\text{range}({P}_{k}\otimes{P}_{k})
,
that is

({P}_{k}\otimes{P}_{k})^{{\text{T}}}\text{vec}(\mathcal{L}({\bm{{P}}}_{k+1}))=0.

(9)

Inserting the expression for
{\bm{{P}}}_{k+1}
, (
9
) becomes

{P}^{{\text{T}}}_{k}\mathcal{L}({\bm{{R}}}_{k+1}+{P}_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}){P}_{k}=0
,
that is,

{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{{R}}}_{k+1}){P}_{k}+{P}_{k}^{{\text{T}}}\mathcal{L}(P_{k}\bm{\beta}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}}){P}_{k}=0.

This is a linear matrix equation in the unknown
\bm{\beta}_{k}
, with
the same coefficient matrices of the linear matrix equation used to
compute
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
.
Once again, and using the considerations in Remark
3
,

\bm{\beta}_{k}
is obtained
by solving a linear matrix equation of the same type as the original one, but
with very small dimensions, by projecting the problem orthogonally onto
\text{range}(P_{k}\otimes P_{k})
,
in a matrix sense.

Concerning the quality of the computed direction iterates,
we next show that the descent direction property (
5
)
is maintained.

Proposition 4
.

Let

The orthogonality conditions imposed in deriving
the coefficient matrices
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
,
\bm{\beta}_{k}
allow us
to extend orthogonality properties to other iterates,
and to derive optimality results later in the section.
To this end, we introduce
some notation for operations with the generalized Lyapunov operator.

For
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}
we will denote with
\bm{A}_{\star}\bullet R
the matrix

\bm{A}_{\star}\bullet R=[\bm{A}_{1}R,\ldots,\bm{A}_{\ell}R],

and analogously for
\bm{B}_{\star}\bullet R
. Moreover, for
k\geq 0
we define

\bm{A}_{\star}^{k+1}\bullet R=\bm{A}_{\star}\bullet(\bm{A}_{\star}^{k}\bullet R).

We are going to characterize the spaces generated by the
factors
P_{k},R_{k}
of
\bm{P}_{k},\bm{R}_{k}
, respectively, with the next proposition.
To this end, with the new notation we define the approximation space

\mathscr{K}_{k}(\bm{A}_{\star},R_{0})={\rm range}([R_{0},\bm{A}_{\star}\bullet R_{0},\ldots,\bm{A}_{\star}^{k}\bullet R_{0}]);

Note that the spaces are nested, that is

\mathscr{K}_{k}(\bm{A}_{\star},R_{0})\subseteq\mathscr{K}_{k+1}(\bm{A}_{\star},R_{0})
.
The notation above is reminiscent of a block Krylov subspace. However, the space is
in general very different.
Indeed,
it involves all matrices associated
with the operation
\bullet
, that is
\bm{A}_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}_{\ell}
. Although the space dimension grows very quickly,
it can be significantly smaller than the sum of the number of
terms; for instance, if one of the
\bm{A}_{i}
’s is the identity matrix,
then the product
\bm{A}_{\star}\bullet R_{0}
will surely contribute at most

(\ell-1)\cdot s
vectors to the space

{\rm range}([R_{0},\bm{A}_{\star}\bullet R_{0}])
, for
R_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}
,
due to the redundancy of
R_{0}
.
We also observe that for the Lyapunov operator,

\mathscr{K}_{k}(\bm{A}_{\star},R_{0})=\mathscr{K}_{k}(\bm{B}_{\star},R_{0})
.
Note that the fact that the left and right spaces are the
same justifies our use of iterates in the form

P_{k}\bm{\omega}P_{k}^{\text{T}}
for some
\bm{\omega}
.

The iteration for the multiterm Sylvester equation

When the matrix operator
{\cal L}
is nonsymmetric, that is

{\cal L}(\bm{X})\neq({\cal L}(\bm{X}))^{\text{T}}
for symmetric
\bm{X}
, or
\bm{C}
is
indefinite or even nonsymmetric, the iteration
obtained with the new algorithm needs to be revised to address the general
Sylvester problem in (
1

For general multiterm Sylvester equations, we still assume that all coefficient
matrices are symmetric, although the
\bm{A}_{i}
’s and the
\bm{B}_{i}
’s matrices are different.
This is in fact the more common situation
for the problem (
1
), as the coefficient matrices
\bm{A}_{i},\bm{B}_{i}
,

i=1,\ldots,\ell
may even have different dimensions, leading to a
rectangular solution matrix
\bm{X}
.
Fortunately, the algorithmic differences are only technical, mostly affecting the notation.
Given two matrices
P_{k}^{l},P_{k}^{r}
, the iterates are computed by
means of the relation

\bm{X}_{k+1}=\bm{X}_{k}+P_{k}^{l}\bm{\alpha}_{k}(P_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}}
,
with
\bm{X}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{A}\times n_{B}}
,
and
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
is computed by solving the reduced equation

(P_{k}^{l})^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(P_{k}^{l}\bm{\alpha}(P_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}})P_{k}^{r}=(P_{k}^{l})^{\text{T}}{\bm{{R}}}_{k}P_{k}^{r}.

Analogously,

\bm{P}_{k+1}=\bm{R}_{k+1}+P_{k}^{l}\bm{\beta}_{k}(P_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}}
, so that

P_{k+1}^{l}\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}(P_{k+1}^{r})^{\text{T}}=\bm{P}_{k+1}
,
where
\bm{\beta}_{k}
solves

(P_{k}^{l})^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(\bm{R}_{k+1})P_{k}^{r}+(P_{k}^{l})^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(P_{k}^{l}\bm{\beta}(P_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}})P_{k}^{r}=0
.
This construction of
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}
ensures
that the orthogonality conditions discussed in the previous sections
continue to hold, with respect to the left and right factors.

Like for the direction matrix
\bm{P}_{k}
, also the iterates
\bm{X}_{k}
and
\bm{R}_{k}

will be nonsymmetric and possibly rectangular,
and they need to be factorized accordingly,
namely
\bm{X}_{k}=X_{k}^{l}\bm{\tau}_{k}(X_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}}

and
\bm{R}_{k}=R_{k}^{l}\bm{\rho}_{k}(R_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}}
.
All truncation strategies will have to
keep into account the nonsymmetry of the iterate, and in particular, we
will see that all computations need to be performed in a mirrored fashion
for the left and right spaces.
The overall algorithm for the resulting
multiterm Sylvester equation will be given in section

Algorithm

In this section we discuss some advanced
devices to make Algorithm
1
competitive and robust
in terms of memory requirements,
running time, and final attainable accuracy for the given chosen truncation thresholds.
We start by analyzing in detail the low-rank iterate truncation, including
the residual matrix computation,
then we discuss the computation of the (matrix) coefficients

\bm{\alpha}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}

6.1
Low-rank truncations

Solvers for large-scale matrix equations often require a low-rank truncation step to make the overall solution process affordable in terms of storage allocation.
This is usually carried out by performing a thin QR decomposition
and a subsequent truncated singular value decomposition (SVD)
of small dimensional objects; see, e.g.,
[
19
]
.
For the sake of brevity, we will refer to this procedure as a QR-SVD (low-rank) truncation in the following.

In matrix-oriented constructions of
cg
type methods, the truncation step is
a crucial part of the implementation, because it determines the actual success of the whole
procedure. A well designed truncation strategy, balancing the low-rank requirement and the
singular value accuracy, may allow the algorithm to deliver a sufficiently accurate
solution. In the past few years the effects of truncation have been analyzed – both
experimentally and theoretically – in several articles, see, e.g.,

[
19
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
32
]
.
In our setting the space truncation is completely analogous to that encountered
in truncated
cg
, so that a similar sensitivity to truncation is expected;
this was confirmed by our extensive computational experience;
we refer to Example
13
for a sample.

In our setting, if we consider

\bm{X}_{k}=X_{k}^{l}\bm{\tau}_{k}(X_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}}
, this is updated as

\bm{X}_{k+1}=\bm{X}_{k}+P_{k}^{l}\bm{\alpha}_{k}(P_{k}^{r})^{\text{T}}=[X_{k}^{l},P_{k}^{l}]{\rm blkdiag}(\bm{\tau}_{k},\bm{\alpha}_{k})[X_{k}^{r},P_{k}^{r}]^{\text{T}}.

Let
Q^{l}{\bm{r}}^{l}=[X^{l}_{k},P_{k}^{l}]
,
Q^{r}{\bm{r}}^{r}=[X^{r}_{k},P_{k}^{r}]
be
the thin QR decompositions of the two matrices,
and compute the singular value decomposition

{\bm{r}}^{l}{\rm blkdiag}(\bm{\tau}_{k},\bm{\alpha}_{k})({\bm{r}}^{r})^{{\text{T}}}=U\Sigma V^{{\text{T}}}
, with

\Sigma={\rm diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{s})
.
The low-rank truncation then takes place following two different criteria.
The first one uses a threshold
tolrank
and the other one
a maximum rank
maxrank
. In the first case,
the number of columns
\widehat{j}_{k+1}
of the low-rank
terms
X_{k+1}^{l}
and
X_{k+1}^{r}
defining
\bm{X}_{k+1}
will be given by

\widehat{j}_{k+1}={\rm arg}\max_{j}\{\sigma_{j}\,:\,(\sigma_{j}/\sigma_{1})\leq\mathtt{tolrank}\}
, where the
\sigma_{j}
s are the
singular values just computed.
In the second case, for
\Sigma
of size
s\times s
, we have

\widehat{j}_{k+1}=\min\{\mathtt{maxrank},s\}
.
These two selections of

6.2
Inaccurate coefficients

The computation of
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}
requires the solution of an algebraic
equation with a linear operator having the same nature of the
original
\cal L
, but with smaller dimension.
Up to a certain column dimension of
P_{k}
,
the matrices

\bm{\alpha}_{k}
,
\bm{\beta}_{k}
can be computed by solving the related
linear systems in Kronecker form by means of a direct solver.
Notice that
the coefficient matrix of such linear systems has to be assembled
only once to compute both
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}
.
However, it may be the case,
either because of the not-so-small dimension, or because of the complexity of the operator
\cal L
,
the computation of
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}
may have to be
done via an iterative procedure such as the truncated matrix-oriented
cg
.
If this is the case,
these quantities are computed inexactly, that is, the exact matrices are
replaced by
approximate quantities. We denote them as

\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k}=\bm{\alpha}_{k}+\bm{\epsilon}_{k}
,

\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k}=\bm{\beta}_{k}+\bm{\eta}_{k}
.
To ease the connection with the derivations
of section
2
and section
4
,
in the following we use the symmetric notation for the factors.
The inexact solutions
\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k}

no longer grant the orthogonality properties associated with the exact
quantities
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}
.
In particular, by defining
\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{k+1}=\bm{R}_{k}-{\cal L}(P_{k}\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}})
,
the orthogonality property
P_{k}^{\text{T}}\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{k+1}P_{k}=0
is lost.
Nonetheless, loss of local orthogonality can be
tracked at each iteration, and directly related to the accuracy
with which the small problems are solved. This is described in the following proposition.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k}
be the approximate solution to

P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(P_{k}\bm{\alpha}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}})P_{k}=P_{k}^{\text{T}}\bm{R}_{k}P_{k}
, and
let
\bm{\varrho}_{k}
be the associated residual matrix. Then

P_{k}^{\text{T}}\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{k+1}P_{k}=\bm{\varrho}_{k},

where it also holds that
P_{k}^{\text{T}}\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{k+1}P_{k}=P_{k}^{\text{T}}(\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{k+1}-\bm{R}_{k+1})P_{k}
.

Proof.

We notice that
\bm{\varrho}_{k}=P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(P_{k}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}})P_{k}
.
The residual recurrence gives

\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{k+1}=\bm{R}_{k}-{\cal L}(P_{k}\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}})=\bm{R}_{k+1}-{\cal L}(P_{k}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}})
.
Hence,

P_{k}^{\text{T}}\tilde{\bm{R}}_{k+1}P_{k}=P_{k}^{\text{T}}\bm{R}_{k+1}P_{k}-P_{k}^{\text{T}}{\cal L}(P_{k}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}P_{k}^{\text{T}})P_{k}=\bm{\varrho}_{k}
.

A similar relation holds for the residual matrix associated with the
reduced matrix equation yielding the coefficient
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k}
.

Inexactness also implies loss of
orthogonality with respect to the previous iterates.
However, in a context where all iteration
factors are anyway truncated, we do not expect this truncation to have
particularly strong implications.
In most of our numerical experiments we compute

\bm{\alpha}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}

exactly
, by solving the
related linear systems by a direct solver.
Indeed, thanks to the rather moderate caps on the rank of the iterates we
adopt, this operation does not remarkably affect the computational cost
of the overall iterative solver.

Preconditioning

As the number of iterations increases, the iterates rank grows,

Since information may be lost during truncation, strategies

Equipping Algorithm

Indeed, the different computation

and

Consider a preconditioning operator

symmetric matrices, and positive definite with respect to the matrix inner product.
We restrict our attention to invertible operators such that

\displaystyle{\cal P}^{-1}(Y^{l}(Y^{r})^{\text{T}})=[\bm{H}_{1}^{l}Y^{l},\ldots,\bm{H}_{t}^{l}Y^{l}]\bm{\kappa}[\bm{H}_{1}^{r}Y^{r},\ldots,\bm{H}_{t}^{r}Y^{r}]^{\text{T}}=[\bm{H}_{\star}^{l}\bullet Y^{l}]\bm{\kappa}[\bm{H}_{\star}^{r}\bullet Y^{r}]^{\text{T}},

(14)

\bm{\kappa}
of conforming dimensions.
Under these hypotheses, there exists a nonsingular symmetric

§
§

§

In Kronecker form,

The new preconditioned subspace-conjugate gradient method (

Special attention deserves the stopping criterion.
While the randomized procedure is able to remarkably
reduce the memory requirements of the overall solution process,
it does not construct an approximation of
the norm of the residual matrix
\bm{C}-\mathcal{L}(\bm{X}_{k+1})
that can be used

As an alternative common choice (see, e.g.,

\|\bm{X}_{k+1}-\bm{X}_{k}\|_{F}/\|\bm{X}_{k+1}\|_{F}\leq\mathtt{tol}.

(15)

The computation of the Frobenius norms exploits
the fact that the columns

and
X_{j}^{r}
are kept orthonormal for every

\|X_{k+1}^{l}\bm{\tau}_{k+1}(X_{k+1}^{r})^{{\text{T}}}\|_{F}=\|\bm{\tau}_{k+1}\|_{F},

and

\|X_{k+1}^{l}\bm{\tau}_{k+1}(X_{k+1}^{r})^{{\text{T}}}-X_{k}^{l}\bm{\tau}_{k}(X_{k}^{r})^{{\text{T}}}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\bm{\tau}_{k+1}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{\tau}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}-2\text{trace}(\bm{\tau}_{k+1}(X_{k+1}^{r})^{{\text{T}}}X_{k}^{l}\bm{\tau}_{k}(X_{k}^{r})^{{\text{T}}}X_{k+1}^{l}).

Remark 12
.

It is known that when used with iterative methods, the stopping criterion in (
15
) may be
sensitive to the ill-conditioning of the problem, as a small relative difference
does not necessarily correspond to a small residual. In our setting, however, we recall that
stagnation of the approximate solution is more likely to occur as an intrinsic effect of
the truncation step.
The criterion (
15
) was chosen because computing
the true residual norm is expensive, as previously discussed.
Nonetheless, if one is interested in monitoring the residual,
a careful implementation may consider including an estimation of the true residual norm
once the criterion (
15
) is satisfied. In case such estimate is not satisfactorily small,
the iteration will continue a few more steps, until the residual norm either
converges or stagnates, or the
maximum number of allowed iterations is reached. The estimation of the true residual 2-norm
can be obtained – not inexpensively – by running a few iterations of a sparse SVD iterative method
using the factorized version of
{\bm{R}}_{k+1}
.
This variant is not included in the experiments below. However, the true residual norm was
computed at completion for all considered methods.

We consider a multiterm Sylvester equation
arising from the Galerkin approximation of the following two dimensional
elliptic PDE problem with correlated random inputs,

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(x,\omega)\nabla u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\quad\text{in}\,\,D,\quad u(x,\omega)=0,\quad\text{on}\,\,\partial D.

(17)

Here
\omega\in\Omega
, where
\Omega
is a sample space associated with a
proper probability space;
see, e.g.,
[
21
, Chapter 9]
, and
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is the
space domain.
The diffusion coefficient is assumed to be a random field, with
expansion in terms of a finite number of real-valued independent random variables
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\leq\ell-1}

defined in
\Omega
. Following the derivation in
[
27
]
,
we consider a truncated Karhunen-Loève expansion, giving

a(x,\omega)=\mu(x)+\sigma\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\phi_{j}(x)\xi_{j}(\omega)
,
where
\mu
corresponds to the diffusion coefficient expected value,
\sigma
is
the standard deviation, while
(\lambda_{j},\phi_{j})
are the leading eigenpairs of
the associated covariance matrix.
Under proper hypotheses on the coefficients, the
problem is well posed, and its Galerkin finite element discretization on a tensor space
(see
[
27
]
)
gives an algebraic problem of type (
1
) with
\ell
terms:
the matrices
\bm{A}_{i}
account for the spatial discretization terms,
while the matrices
\bm{B}_{i}
contain the discretized weighted moments in the
random basis. The right-hand side is a rank-one matrix
\bm{C}=f_{0}e_{1}^{\text{T}}
, where

f_{0}
is the finite element discretization of the forcing term in (
17
).

corresponding

[

]

\ell=9

Conclusions

We have proposed a new iterative method for solving multiterm matrix equations
with symmetric and positive definite operator. The method generates
a sequence of approximate solutions by locally minimizing a functional
over a subspace that is allowed to grow up to a desired threshold.
The derivation closely follows that of matrix-oriented Conjugate Gradients on the
Kronecker form, without being affected by the same dramatic loss of optimality.
By using particularly convenient randomized range-finding strategies,
the method is able to ensure low memory requirements.
Our numerical experiments have shown that the new method is computationally
robust and competitive with respect to state-of-the-art methods in addition to
tcg
,
on quite diverse application problems.
