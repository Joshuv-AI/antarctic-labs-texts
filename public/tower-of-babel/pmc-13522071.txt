Pre-Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography Cholangitis and Stent Outcomes in Malignant Distal Biliary Obstruction: A Multicenter Propensity Score-Matched Analysis
Authors: Fumitaka Niiya, Tatsunori Satoh, Yosuke Kobayashi, Junichi Kaneko, Kazuma Ishikawa, Naoki Tamai, Masataka Yamawaki, Jun Noda, Tetsushi Azami, Yuichi Takano, Masatsugu Nagahama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical evidence on whether acute cholangitis accelerates early stent failure via rapid bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation remains limited. We assessed the influence of pre-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) cholangitis on time to recurrent biliary obstruction (TRBO) and early post-ERCP infection in patients with distal malignant biliary obstruction (DMBO).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients with DMBO who underwent their first ERCP-guided stenting at five Japanese centers (January 2020–August 2024). Patients were classified by pre-ERCP cholangitis status and matched at a 2:1 ratio of noncholangitis to cholangitis using propensity scores (PSM). The primary outcome was TRBO; secondary outcomes were post-ERCP infection, clinical success, and RBO rates. TRBO was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier, log-rank, and multivariable Cox analyses; sensitivity analyses yielded concordant results.ResultsOf 588 eligible patients, 508 had no cholangitis. PSM yielded 136 and 68 patients in well-balanced groups. The median TRBO was 140 vs. 114 d (p = 0.939). In the Cox models, nonpancreatic etiology lowered RBO risk (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.59, p = 0.04); plastic stents carried a higher risk than metal (HR: 4.71, p < 0.001), and pre-ERCP cholangitis was not associated with RBO (HR: 1.20, p = 0.46). Infections occurred in 9.6 and 4.4% (p = 0.33). Independent infection predictors were dilated bile duct (protective; odds ratio: 0.26, p = 0.02) and prolonged procedures (odds ratio: 3.73, p = 0.03).ConclusionsPre-ERCP cholangitis did not reduce stent patency or increase early infection rates in patients with DMBO. TRBO-depended primarily on stent type; nondilated ducts and prolonged procedures were predictive of infection. These findings support possible internal drainage during index ERCP with contemporary periprocedural management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-09973-9.Keywords: Pre-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, Cholangitis, Time to recurrent biliary obstruction, Infection, Malignant distal biliary obstruction

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the preferred first-line modality for diagnosing and managing biliary obstruction [1, 2]. Early recurrent biliary obstruction (RBO) due to stent dysfunction occurs in 20–30% of patients and can delay chemotherapy or surgery, prolong hospitalization, and precipitate cholangitis [3, 4]. Although stent design and placement techniques affect patency, inflammation may also drive early failure. Identifying factors that predispose patients to early dysfunction is essential in preventing RBO and improving outcomes.

Acute cholangitis has long been implicated in early occlusion. Bacterial loads in bile rise in vitro during cholangitis; organisms such as Enterobacter and Klebsiella adhere to metal (MS) and plastic stents (PS), forming a biofilm rich in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) within 24 h. Furthermore, a conditioning film of nonphysiological bile proteins (including fibronectin, vitronectin, laminin, fibrin, and collagen) augments adhesion and sludge formation [5–10]. However, clinical evidence directly linking acute cholangitis to shortened stent patency remains limited.

Post-ERCP infectious complications, such as bacteremia, cholangitis, and cholecystitis, can delay planned surgery or chemotherapy in patients with distal malignant biliary obstruction (DMBO). Although their overall incidence is approximately 5%, it increases to 18% with underlying obstruction [11–14]. Prevention of post-ERCP infectious complications in patients with DMBO is critical.

Preprocedural cholangitis is a potential risk factor for such infections; however, existing studies vary widely in patient selection and methodology, leaving the association unresolved [15, 16]. To address this uncertainty, we restricted our analysis to DMBO, wherein transpapillary stenting across a single distal stricture is standard. Proximal (hilar) disease often requires multisegment drainage and complex stent configurations or non-ERCP routes, introducing heterogeneous techniques and unmeasured confounders. Within this more homogeneous clinical setting, the burden remains nontrivial: approximately 25% of patients with DMBO present with cholangitis before their first ERCP [17, 18]. However, the effect of pre-ERCP cholangitis on stent patency and post-ERCP infectious complications in this population remains unclear. We compared clinical outcomes (stent patency duration and post-ERCP infectious complications) between patients with DMBO presenting with and without preprocedural cholangitis to identify optimal drainage strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients who underwent initial biliary stent placement for DMBO at five Japanese tertiary care centers (Showa University Hospital, Shizuoka General Hospital, Iwata City Hospital, Sapporo Medical University Hospital, and Seirei Hamamatsu General Hospital). Eligible patients had DMBO lesions located ≥ 2 cm distal to the hepatic hilum and underwent ERCP for stent insertion. To allow reliable evaluation of post-ERCP cholecystitis, patients with prior cholecystectomy or surgically altered upper gastrointestinal anatomy other than Billroth I reconstruction were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions (approval number 2025–0280) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with all data anonymized before analysis.

ERCP Procedure

All ERCP procedures were performed under sedation, using standard duodenoscopes (JF-260 V, TJF-Q260V, and TJF-Q290V; Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan). After a frontal view of the duodenal papilla was obtained, selective bile duct cannulation was performed using a conventional cannula, followed by cholangiography and guidewire placement to delineate the biliary stricture. Endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST) and biliary biopsies were performed at the discretion of the endoscopist. For biliary drainage, an MS or PS was deployed across the papilla based on operator preference; prophylactic pancreatic stenting was employed when indicated. Diclofenac suppositories and ulinastatin were administered without contraindications to reduce the risk of pancreatitis following ERCP.

Study Outcomes and Definitions

The primary outcome was time to recurrent biliary obstruction (TRBO), which was compared between noncholangitis and cholangitis groups; RBO-associated factors were also examined. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of post-ERCP infectious complications (cholangitis and cholecystitis) within 5 d of stent placement [19], clinical success rate, and rate and causes of RBO. Pre- and post-ERCP cholangitis and cholecystitis were defined according to the Tokyo Guidelines [20, 21]. Pre-ERCP cholangitis was defined as acute cholangitis that met the Tokyo Guideline criteria on the day of ERCP or on the previous day. Post-ERCP cholangitis was defined as either relapse (after confirming early improvement within 24–48 h [defervescence with a 20–30% decrease in CRP or total bilirubin {T-Bil}], occurrence within 5 d of fever [≥ 38 °C] or a ≥ 50% rebound in CRP or T-Bil requiring antibiotic escalation or unplanned biliary drainage) or progressive (failure to achieve early improvement, followed within 5 d by antibiotic modification or escalation or unplanned biliary drainage). RBO, adverse events (AEs) and their severity were graded according to the Tokyo Criteria 2024 [22]. Post-ERCP pancreatitis was defined as abdominal pain with a serum amylase level ≥ 3 times the upper normal limit. Clinical success was defined as a ≥ 50% reduction or normalization of serum bilirubin within 14 d after stent placement [22]. Maximum CBD diameter was determined from the most recent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging obtained before stent placement; CBD dilatation was defined as ≥ 11 mm. Duodenal invasion was defined as CT evidence of a tumor abutting or infiltrating the duodenal wall or endoscopic findings of mucosal irregularities (erosions, ulcers, or strictures) at the lesion site.

Statistical Analysis

Patients were classified into noncholangitis and cholangitis groups based on preprocedural status and matched 2:1 using propensity score matching (PSM). Propensity scores were estimated by logistic regression including age, sex, etiology (pancreatic vs. nonpancreatic cancer), pre-stent T-Bil and albumin, duodenal invasion, maximum CBD diameter, EST, biliary biopsy, stent type (MS vs. PS), and receipt of post-stent chemotherapy, with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviation of the logit of the score. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to evaluate covariate balance after PSM.

Categorical variables were compared in the matched cohort using Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. TRBO was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test; patients who underwent surgery, died without RBO, or were lost to follow-up were censored at the date of the last contact or surgery, whichever came first. Factors associated with RBO were examined using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. To assess robustness across adjustment strategies, sensitivity analyses included multivariable Cox regression in the PSM cohort, an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)-weighted Cox model, and an unweighted multivariable Cox model for the entire cohort. We also evaluated post-ERCP infectious complication risk factors using univariable and multivariable analyses. The candidate covariates were sex, stent type (MS vs. PS), EST, preprocedural cholangitis, CBD dilatation, T-Bil, biliary biopsy, and ERCP procedure time. T-Bil and procedure time were dichotomized at their medians, and variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analyses were entered into the multivariable model. All analyses were performed using R v3.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics

The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Between January 2020 and August 2024, 622 patients underwent endoscopic stent placement for DMBO. Thirty-four patients with a history of cholecystectomy were excluded, leaving 588 patients for analysis. Among the remaining cohort, 508 patients (86.4%) had no evidence of preprocedural cholangitis (noncholangitis group), while 80 patients (13.6%) did (cholangitis group) (Table 1). After PSM, two patients without cholangitis were matched to each patient with pre-ERCP cholangitis, yielding 136 noncholangitis and 68 cholangitis patients for subsequent analyses.

Before matching, patients with preprocedural cholangitis were significantly older (median: 81 vs. 74 years, p < 0.001, SMD: 0.507), had lower serum albumin (median: 3.0 vs. 3.6 g/dL, p < 0.001, SMD: 0.774), and had a lower percentage of cases attributable to pancreatic cancer (58.8 vs. 80.5%, p < 0.001, SMD: 0.487) than the noncholangitis group. Duodenal invasion was also less frequent in the cholangitis cohort (1.2 vs. 9.8%, p = 0.009, SMD: 0.382). Significant imbalances were observed in stent type (plastic 68.8 vs. 47.6%, respectively; p < 0.001, SMD: 0.438) and EST performance (77.5 vs. 85.6%, p = 0.068, SMD: 0.211).

No significant differences were noted in age (median: 78 vs. 81 years, p = 0.545, SMD: 0.079), sex (female: 30.1 vs. 32.4%, p = 0.75, SMD: 0.048), etiology (pancreatic cancer: 64.7 vs. 61.8%, p = 0.758, SMD: 0.061), laboratory values, anatomic factors (including duodenal invasion), or ERCP-related variables (EST, biliary biopsy, stent type, and prophylactic pancreatic stenting) in the matched cohort (all SMDs < 0.2). These results confirmed that PSM achieved an adequate balance between groups (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Comparison of TRBO Between Noncholangitis and Cholangitis Groups

Figure 2 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves for TRBO in the PSM cohort. The median TRBO was 140 d (95% confidence interval [CI]: 93–263) in the noncholangitis group and 114 d (95% CI: 75–382) in the cholangitis group; the difference was not statistically significant (log-rank p = 0.939). To determine whether the stent type modified this relationship, we conducted a subgroup analysis (Fig. 3), in which preprocedural cholangitis had no significant effect on TRBO in either the PS or MS cohorts (Fig. 3). Among PS recipients (95 noncholangitis vs. 45 cholangitis), the median TRBO was 93 (95% CI: 74–210) versus 75 d (43–112), respectively (log-rank p = 0.229). Similarly, in the MS cohort (41 noncholangitis vs. 23 cholangitis cases), the median TRBO was 426 (139–568) vs. 382 d (263 not reached) (p = 0.134).

Multivariable Cox Proportional Hazards Analysis in the PSM Cohort

In the PSM-matched cohort, multivariable Cox analysis demonstrated that etiology and stent type were the only independent predictors of RBO. Patients with nonpancreatic cancer had a significantly lower risk of RBO than those with pancreatic malignancies (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.36–0.97; p = 0.004), whereas use of a PS was associated with a higher risk of RBO compared with MS use (HR: 4.71; 95% CI: 2.52–8.80; p < 0.001). Preprocedural cholangitis did not significantly influence RBO (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 0.73–1.97; p = 0.46). Sensitivity Analyses

Across all cohorts (including the IPTW‐weighted and entire cohorts), preprocedural cholangitis consistently showed no significant association with RBO (HR range: 1.20–1.43; p = 0.08–0.46), whereas PS use remained a strong, significant risk factor compared with MS use (HR range: 4.40–4.71; p < 0.001 in all models).

Clinical Success, RBO, and AEs by Cholangitis Status

Table 3 summarizes the clinical success rates, RBO incidence and causes, and ERCP-related AEs in the PS-matched cohort. Clinical success was achieved in 115 of 136 noncholangitis cases (84.6%) and 64 of 68 cholangitis cases (94.1%; p = 0.068). Overall RBO incidence was identical in both groups (52/136 [38.2%] vs. 26/68 [38.2%]; p = 1.00). Sludge accounted for the majority of RBO cases (22.1 vs. 25%), followed by migration (10.3 vs. 7.4%), overgrowth (1.5 vs. 4.4%), ingrowth (< 1% in both groups), and other causes (3.7 vs. 0%).

AEs other than RBO did not differ significantly between groups. Post-ERCP pancreatitis occurred in 6.6% of patients without cholangitis and 8.8% of those with cholangitis (p = 0.083). Post-ERCP Infectious Complication Predictors

Table 4 shows the univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses of the risk factors for post-ERCP infectious complications. In univariable analysis, a dilated bile duct was associated with a lower risk (OR: 0.38; 95% CI: 0.15–0.99; p = 0.034), whereas procedure time > 27 min increased risk (OR: 3.06; 95% CI: 1.02–9.17; p = 0.019). Preprocedural cholangitis showed no significant association (OR: 0.46; 95% CI: 0.14–1.57; p = 0.10), which persisted in the multivariable model (OR: 0.48; 95% CI: 0.12–1.81; p = 0.28). In multivariable analysis, a dilated bile duct remained protective (OR: 0.26; 95% CI: 0.08–0.81; p = 0.02), and prolonged procedure time (> 27 min) was an independent risk factor (OR: 3.73; 95% CI: 1.11–12.6; p = 0.03). No significant associations were observed between sex, stent type, EST, T-Bil level, and biliary biopsy.

Severity of Pre-ERCP Cholangitis and Periprocedural Antibiotic Use

In the PSM cohort, all patients with pre-ERCP cholangitis received antibiotics before ERCP, whereas antibiotics were administered to 90 of 136 patients (66.2%) in the noncholangitis group (Supplementary Table 1). Among patients with pre-ERCP cholangitis, cholangitis severity according to the Tokyo Guidelines was mild in 46 patients, moderate in 20, and severe in 2. The cholangitis group had higher inflammatory markers than the noncholangitis group, with median WBC counts of 7600/µL (IQR, 6208–10575) versus 5965/µL (IQR, 5005–7130), and median CRP levels of 5.7 mg/dL (IQR, 2.9–9.6) versus 0.9 mg/dL (IQR, 0.4–2.7), respectively. The most frequently used antibiotic was sulbactam/cefoperazone, followed by cefmetazole and tazobactam/piperacillin. The median duration of antibiotic therapy was longer in the cholangitis group than in the noncholangitis group (5 d [IQR, 3–7] vs. 2 d [IQR, 1–4]). Among patients with pre-ERCP cholangitis, TRBO did not differ significantly between mild and moderate/severe cholangitis groups (median, 114 d [95% CI, 35–263] vs. 382 d [95% CI, 56–not reached]; log-rank p = 0.41; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

In vitro studies have elucidated the mechanisms by which preprocedural cholangitis can accelerate early stent occlusion and post-ERCP infection; however, clinical confirmation remains scarce. In this multicenter cohort of patients with DMBO, we used PSM to balance baseline characteristics and assess whether pre-ERCP cholangitis influenced TRBO or post-ERCP infectious complications. Multivariable analysis demonstrated that nonpancreatic cancer etiology and MS use were associated with lower RBO risk; however, preprocedural cholangitis showed no significant effect on either TRBO or post-ERCP infectious complication rates. These findings were confirmed in sensitivity analyses using IPTW weighting and in the full cohort, supporting their robustness. Additionally, a nondilated bile duct and prolonged procedure time emerged as independent risk factors for post-ERCP infectious complications. Given the high frequency of cholangitis before initial ERCP in DMBO, our results offer important guidance for optimizing drainage strategies and preprocedural management.

ERCP‐guided drainage is the recommended first‐line approach for DMBO [1, 2]; however, early stent occlusion remains relatively frequent, underscoring the need to identify predictive factors. While MS offers longer patency than PS, PS is often chosen in cases without a confirmed diagnosis or pancreatic duct dilation, making it imperative to explore additional determinants of early dysfunction. In vitro studies have demonstrated that bacterial biofilm formation, comprising LPS and EPS, can rapidly narrow stent lumens [5–10], suggesting that pre-ERCP cholangitis predisposes patients to early occlusion. However, TRBO did not differ between the patients with and without pre-ERCP cholangitis in our PSM-matched analysis. Subgroup analyses stratified by stent type confirmed that neither the PS nor the MS cohorts exhibited a significant difference in TRBO based on cholangitis status, indicating that preprocedural inflammation does not materially influence stent longevity, regardless of material.

One potential explanation for the absence of a difference in TRBO between the cholangitis and noncholangitis groups is that all patients with pre-ERCP cholangitis in our cohort received antibiotics with good biliary penetration before the procedure and the median duration of antibiotic therapy was longer in the cholangitis group than in the noncholangitis group. In vitro studies have shown that timely antibiotic administration can significantly reduce bacterial adhesion to PS surfaces and prevent early occlusion [23]. By decreasing the number of Gram-negative rods, antibiotic therapy lowers LPS and EPS matrices, destabilizes the biofilm, and reduces stent-lumen narrowing. The administration of high-biliary-excretion agents such as ceftriaxone or piperacillin/tazobactam before and after ERCP mitigates Gram-negative-derived biofilm accumulation on PS and prolongs stent patency [24]. In addition, although more severe cholangitis could theoretically increase biliary sludge and inflammatory debris, our exploratory analysis showed no significant difference in TRBO between patients with mild cholangitis and those with moderate/severe cholangitis. Taken together, these findings suggest that appropriate antibiotic treatment and prompt biliary drainage may mitigate the potential adverse effect of pre-ERCP cholangitis on stent patency. However, severe cholangitis was uncommon in our cohort, and this result should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

We performed Cox proportional hazards analyses in a PSM cohort, followed by sensitivity analyses in the IPTW-weighted and entire cohorts. Across all three models, pre‐ERCP cholangitis did not independently predict TRBO (PSM HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 0.73–1.97, p = 0.46; IPTW HR: 1.43, 95% CI: 0.95–2.17, p = 0.08; entire-cohort HR: 1.43, 95% CI: 0.95–2.17, p = 0.08). In contrast, patients with nonpancreatic malignancies had a 41% lower risk of stent occlusion than those with pancreatic cancer (PSM HR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.36–0.97, p = 0.04), with consistent trends in IPTW and full‐cohort analyses. To our knowledge, no previous study has directly compared stent patency between pancreatic and nonpancreatic malignancies. Given the aggressive nature and rapid progression of pancreatic cancer, these tumors may predispose patients to early stent dysfunction. Additionally, stent material emerged as a consistently strong predictor of TRBO across PSM, IPTW, and entire‐cohort analyses, reaffirming the superior durability of MS and demonstrating high concordance with prior reports [1]. This consistency further confirms the reproducibility and robustness of our findings.

Post-ERCP infectious complications, including cholangitis and cholecystitis, are clinically significant. In our DMBO cohort, the incidence of post-ERCP infectious complications was 7.8%, closely matching previously reported rates of 11–14%. Prior studies have identified pre‐ERCP cholangitis as a risk factor for postprocedural cholangitis and bacteremia [16]; however, data specific to DMBO have been lacking. In this analysis, pre-ERCP cholangitis did not influence the development of post-ERCP infectious complications, likely reflecting the impact of periprocedural antibiotic administration. A large Swedish cohort demonstrated that prophylactic antibiotics reduced infectious complications in patients with ERCP presenting with jaundice or malignancy [25]. In addition, a randomized trial by Leem et al. demonstrated a significant decrease in infection rates with antibiotics versus placebo (2.8 vs. 9.8%) [19]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of establishing optimal antibiotic protocols along with stent selection and patency considerations to prevent infection and prolong stent function during DMBO drainage. However, routine prophylactic antibiotic use warrants caution owing to increased healthcare costs, risks of nephrotoxicity and allergic reactions, and potential reduction in the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [26, 27].

In our multivariable model, nondilated bile ducts and prolonged procedure time emerged as independent predictors of post-ERCP infectious complications. Elevated intraductal pressure in high-risk scenarios can facilitate bacterial reflux. For patients with nondilated ducts or anticipated lengthy procedures, targeted antibiotic prophylaxis combined with efforts to shorten the procedure duration or implement predrainage strategies should be considered to mitigate infection risk.

This study had several limitations. First, despite adjusting for known confounders using PSM, the retrospective multicenter cohort could not fully exclude bias from unmeasured variables. In addition, the exclusion of a substantial number of patients during matching may have introduced selection bias. Nonetheless, the consistency of our findings across the three sensitivity analyses (PSM‐matched, IPTW‐weighted, and full cohort models) strengthens the validity of the results. Additionally, we were unable to assess whether antibiotic administration was guided by blood culture results or by adherence to optimal regimens. Although data on antibiotic use, agents, and duration were collected, antibiotic selection, timing, and duration were not standardized across participating centers. Therefore, the independent effect of antibiotic therapy on stent patency and post-ERCP infection could not be directly assessed. Furthermore, the number of patients with severe cholangitis was small, and patients with unstable severe cholangitis who underwent temporary external drainage without initial internal stent placement may have been underrepresented. In addition, the exact interval from cholangitis onset to ERCP was not uniformly available across centers, which limited our ability to evaluate the impact of drainage timing. Thus, our findings should not be directly generalized to patients with severe or unstable cholangitis. Moreover, all participating centers were located in Japan, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other countries or healthcare systems; prospective randomized studies are warranted to confirm these results. Finally, bile culture results for patients with pre-ERCP cholangitis were not systematically collected; therefore, we were unable to perform analyses based on microbiological findings. This limitation stems from the retrospective multicenter design of the current study. Nevertheless, bile cultures are reportedly positive in approximately 90% of cases of acute cholangitis [28], which supports the interpretability and clinical relevance of our conclusions despite this constraint. Despite these limitations, this is the first multicenter clinical study to test the suggested in vitro association between pre-ERCP cholangitis and early stent occlusion, providing valuable insights for optimizing drainage strategies for DMBO.

In conclusion, this multicenter PSM analysis of patients with DMBO undergoing ERCP revealed that preprocedural cholangitis was not associated with shorter stent patency or an increased incidence of post-ERCP infectious complications. Instead, nonpancreatic cancer etiology and MS use emerged as protective and risk factors for RBO, respectively, whereas nondilated bile ducts and prolonged procedure time independently predicted post-ERCP infectious complications. In patients with nondilated ducts or when a lengthy procedure is anticipated, appropriate periprocedural antibiotic prophylaxis should be ensured.
