Assessing the reach and engagement of three anti-vaping messages on Facebook Targeting Australian Youth: a protocol for a randomised trial
Authors: Xiao Li, Lisa Gaye Smithers, Alberto Nettel-Aguirre, Robyn Gillespie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaping among 18-24-year-old Australians has increased from 5.8% in 2019 to 21% in 2023. This protocol describes a trial to investigate the dissemination and engagement achieved by three anti-vaping messages on Facebook.MethodsThis research employs a 3-arm randomised experimental design. Three distinct anti-vaping messages will be disseminated via Facebook using Meta Ads Manager. Each arm has a message that focuses on either health risks, environmental impact, or anti-vape industry sentiment. The campaign will run for three months. The primary outcome is the engagement rate as a measure of the effectiveness of anti-vaping message, and the secondary outcomes include network indicators: size, density, centralisation, and centrality to evaluate the extent to which the messages are disseminated. Participants will be randomly exposed to one of the three messages. Data on reach and engagement will be compared across the groups.DiscussionThis study will provide insights into the dissemination of social media-based anti-vaping campaigns. By evaluating engagement rates and network indicators, the research aims to identify which message themes engage most with young Australians. The findings will contribute to the development of more effective public health strategies for vaping cessation and prevention among youth.Trial RegistrationThe study was registered on July 19th 2024 with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12624000885594).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20178-5.Keywords: Vaping, Social media, Public health, Randomized experimental design, Youth, Australia, Anti-vaping campaign, Facebook engagement, Network analysis

Background

The use of e-cigarettes among Australia’s 18-24-year-olds has increased from 5.8% in 2019 to 21% in 2023 [1]. According to recent data from the National Drug Strategy Household Survey 2022–2023, nearly half of Australians aged 18–24 years have used e-cigarettes (vaping) at least once.

The use of social media is also very common among young Australians. It was estimated that 85% of all young Australians aged 18 to 34 years were active on at least one social media platform in the past six months [2]. Nearly two-thirds of young people engaged with an average of five or more social networking sites or apps (64% in the previous six months to June 2020), compared to 22% of other Australians [2]. The top three social media sites used by 18- to 34-year-olds are Facebook at 91%, YouTube at 83%, and Instagram at 81% [2].

Research has shown that youths are susceptible to the influence of e-cigarette marketing on social media [3]. The e-cigarette industry has used social media to disseminate marketing information about vaping widely [3]. Collaborations between e-cigarette companies and social media influencers to influence consumer attitudes and promote their products are common [4]. This strategic marketing by industry capitalises on the vulnerability of young adults to persuasive advertising, as research has found associations between viewing online content promoting the use of substances, such as tobacco, and subsequent use of that product, especially among young adults [5, 6]. This widespread engagement by the industry with social media provides a channel through which marketing strategies can be disseminated and adopted among young people.

Given that a large portion of Australian youth are connected to at least one social media platform, social media can also be leveraged to raise awareness about the risks of using e-cigarettes among young Australians. However, in our recent systematic review on the use of social media for health promotion, we found limited evidence on the use of social media in improving awareness about vaping or aiding in vaping cessation, and no campaigns were conducted in Australia (The manuscript is under review). A protocol involving a randomised controlled trial to assess vaping cessation interventions on Instagram has been underway, though its findings are still pending [7].

Precisely what information can attract young people to pay attention to vaping campaign messages and actively engage with them remains not well understood. Survey results indicate that young adults are concerned about the environmental impact and misinformation from the e-cigarette industry [8]. However, there is little evidence from intervention studies to assess methods for motivating young Australians to engage in quitting campaigns, moving beyond merely collecting observational data. In addition, our systematic review found that no studies have conducted a head-to-head comparison of anti-vaping messages from these perspectives within the Australian context (The manuscript is under review).

Network analysis can be one approach to tackling these issues. By depicting how participants connect and communicate within networks, health institutions can gain insights into the depth and breadth of health message dissemination. Although one study had used social network analyses to describe the social networks and structures of health campaigns on social media platforms [9], research remains limited [10]. Network analysis can also help identify key communication brokers, allowing health institutions to design tailored messages that specifically target these influencers, thus facilitating the dissemination of messages for future campaigns. Such in-depth analysis of social media networks will help assess the extent of reach and engagement in vaping campaigns. Without these data, our understanding of the interventions’ effectiveness in terms of outcomes remains incomplete.

Objective

This protocol describes a trial investigating the dissemination of anti-vaping messages on Facebook through a public health club account managed by students at the University of Wollongong. The primary goal is to evaluate the reach and engagement of the different message themes (health, environmental and anti-vaping industry). Additionally, the secondary aim involves analysing the distribution of these messages within followers and followers’ networks, assessing each individual’s position, connections, and influence within the network of message spread.

Method

The study protocol is reported according to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) checklist [8]. This intervention trial is prospectively registered with the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ACTRN12624000885594).

Development of the intervention

The content for this intervention was developed in a series of steps, from defining the program objectives, conducting a survey, co-designing social media content (proposed posts for Facebook), and pilot testing through qualitative interviews.

The social media intervention designed for the current study aims to raise awareness of the potential impacts of e-cigarette use among young adults in Australia. The Centres for Disease Control and Prevention template was used to develop a communication plan for social media content to ensure a structured and effective approach to social media communication [11]. A social media channel, Illawarra Public Health Society (IPHS), was selected to run the ads campaign. IPHS is a university student-run organisation that aims to activate, educate, and engage individuals and communities to promote health and well-being. The primary audience of the IPHS Facebook page consists of individuals aged 25–34, followed by those aged 18–24 and 35–44. Consequently, the IPHS is a suitable partner for running a public health-related campaign targeting the appropriate age group.

To better comprehend our target audience’s views and inform the development of the social media intervention, we first conducted a survey. The survey was based on a tool developed by Rath et al. [9] and included an 18-item survey measuring five message themes: social acceptability of vaping, anti-vape industry sentiment, independence from vaping, non-vaping identity, and perceived risk of harm. We added an additional item exploring the impact of vape use on the environment. This item was considered by Rath et al. but excluded from their 18-item survey tool [9]. Consequently, our revised survey tool consisted of 19 items, measuring attitudes to six message themes, each rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree (1–5 scores). The survey was conducted among university students on the main campus of the University of Wollongong, Australia. Students were invited to participate in the online survey by setting up an advocacy stall at the main entrance of campus library and using snowball sampling methods.

A full description of the survey will be reported elsewhere. Briefly, the survey was completed by 236 participants aged 18 to 37 years (131 self-identified as female, 94 male and 11 identified as another gender). Of the survey respondents 46.8% agreed that vape companies make them feel angry. In addition, 48.3% of survey respondents strongly agreed or agreed with the sentiment of “standing up against vape companies”. Furthermore, 35.2% of respondents were aware of the benefits of independence from vaping. 64.4% of participants strongly agreed or agreed that vape companies lie and 78.3% strongly agreed or agreed that vape companies harm the environment.

Based on the foundational work by Rath et al. [9] and insights from the survey among the University of Wollongong students, we focussed the social media intervention message themes on the health and environmental risks associated with vaping, as well as anti-vape industry perspectives. The survey findings highlighted the importance of incorporating a health perspective into our social media intervention. Given that the primary goal of a health promotion campaign is to raise awareness of potential health risks and promote behaviour change, this decision is both reasonable and strategic. In response to the participants’ strong concerns about the marketing tactics of the vape industry and the environmental impact of vape use, our campaign will actively highlight and address these themes through targeted messaging and informative content. This decision was further supported by an informal review of content from government sources, the vape industry, and social media.

Based on the three identified message themes, IPHS student members contributed to the development of consumer-driven health messages aimed at raising awareness of these message themes. For each message theme, three unique messages, along with corresponding posts, were developed. These messages were piloted through individual qualitative interviews with 17 participants, assessing the student’s view of the potential impact and likely engagement with the content. Following analysis of the qualitative data, one message from each theme was chosen based on its perceived impact, involvement and relevance. As a result, a total of three messages, each representing a distinct message theme, were selected for A/B/C testing to be conducted according to the study described in this protocol.

Study design

This 3-arm randomised experimental design involves three ads of anti-vaping messages to be disseminated via Facebook. An advertisement will be launched using Meta Ads Manager, and the goal of the advertisement is to raise post engagement in all three posts with the target audience. We will use the A/B/C testing function embedded in Meta Ads Manager to allocate one of three test messages sent by @Illawarra Public Health Society Facebook account. The potential recipients are all Facebook users in the target demographic and not only IPHS page followers. The A/B/C testing algorithm will be set to ensure the potential recipients are evenly split in numbers by using the same advertisement costs for each and to ensure that only one of the three messages is ‘pushed’ to each participant’s account. Therefore, the A/B/C testing is equivalent to randomisation and allocation concealment in a randomised design. In this design, we cannot control which vape messages participants view, nor can we restrict the audiences from being exposed to a different vape message shared by their peers (i.e. different from the one to which they were originally randomised). Blinding of participants or researchers in this study is not feasible, as the social media message will be viewed. After the campaign, we will collect data to determine the number of reached and engaged recipients and identify whether they received the messages directly or from other users, following the campaign initiated by @Illawarra Public Health Society. A Data Monitoring Committee will not be formed, as this is a social media marketing intervention with no data collection procedures to monitor. The intervention has a low risk of adverse events since it is viewing a social media message about vaping and therefore no plans are in place for reporting adverse events or unintended effects.

Advertisement objective and target audience

Within the Meta Ads Manager platform, the target audience will be set to Facebook users aged 18 to 34, of all genders, residing in Australia. The campaign objective will be set to “Awareness,” one of Meta campaign objectives, to reach the largest number of participants.

Advertisement budget and duration

The overall budget for the advertisements is $1500 Australian dollars, which will be divided and allocated equally to each message theme. Our recent systematic review showed no ‘standard’ duration for a social media campaign, although most campaigns were 2–3 months long (The manuscript is under review). In the absence of other evidence, the campaign will run for three months, which also meets budgetary constraints. Participant recruitment commenced on August 29th, 2024. During the campaign, participants will receive a unique message from one of three ads at least once per day on their Facebook feed. If no engagement is observed on any of the three ads within two weeks after the last action, we will cease data collection, even if the three-month campaign period has not yet been completed.

Advertisement placement

Facebook ad placement refers to the various spots on the platform where ads can appear. Based on the Meta Ads manager delivery system, we will choose the manual placements approach to display the ad campaigns, targeting Facebook only. The approach allocates ads across multiple placements based on where they are expected to perform best. This approach will ensure that the ads achieve the best results across all Facebook default placements, providing confidence that no ad receives preferential treatment and leading to similar exposure for all ads.

Intervention groups

One social media intervention message will address the environmental risks associated with vaping, and another will address anti-vape industry sentiment. From an environmental perspective, the intervention will highlight that disposable vapes are difficult to recycle and, therefore, harmful to the environment. Regarding the anti-vape industry perspective, the intervention will indicate that vape companies profit from the hard-earned money of young adults. (See Appendix 1 for details about each social media message.)

Control group

Given that health-themed messages are the most common perspective chosen for health promotion campaigns, the health risks message will be defined as the reference group though it is not exactly a control group since the health-themed message will be posted.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome is the engagement rate as a measure of the effectiveness of anti-vaping messages. Measures of engagement rate will come from the Facebook backend data. The engagement rate is calculated by dividing the sum of all interactions (shares, comments, reactions, etc.) by the total number reached. The number of reaches is defined as how many unique users are exposed to a message at least once. To assess the reach and engagement of social media-based interventions, the number of reviews, reactions, comments, shares and reaches will be collected.

The secondary outcomes include network indicators: size, density, centralisation, and centrality. Network size is a measure of the effect of spreading anti-vaping messages, reflecting the boundary of the network. At the network level, the overall density is the number of connections in the network reported as a fraction of the total links possible [10], centralisation measures refer to the degree to which network connections are concentrated around one or a few key nodes within the network [10]. At the node level, centrality measures quantify how centrally a node is positioned within a network. We will use secondary outcomes to explore the depth and breadth of the dissemination of health messages and identify key influencers. We will likely encounter a considerable number and percentage of isolates, defined as people with no connections and subnetworks. These indicators will be recorded across networks consisting of the recipients of each of the three themed posts. In these networks, each participant is represented as a node. If a participant interacts with a post from another, a directional link is established between these two nodes. The number of nodes in the network by the end of the campaign determines the network size in this study.

Sample size estimation

The sample size is based on the main outcome of interest which is the comparison of engagement rates (proportions) between each trial arm. Based on a 3-group chi-square test, with expected engagement rates of 4%, 7% and 7% for health, environment and anti-vaping themes, respectively. Based on these, a sample size of 904 per group is required (n = 2,711 in total, alpha 0.05, 80% power).

Data collection and processing

Data extraction will be conducted using the Meta Business Suite, including the campaign details, performance outcomes, costs, and duration. We will collect data from Meta Ads Manager, including the number of reposts, comments, shares, and the relationships between users for the three themed posts. Only publicly available information will be collected.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics for the three themed messages will summarise the reach and engagement metrics, respectively. Chi-square tests will be used to test the relationships between engagement and message themes, allowing for comparison across the three groups. In addition, network visualisations for the three themes will be used to depict diffusion networks according to the observed nodes and ties. The diffusion network will focus on drawing a complete network of participants involved in the campaign. In this way, we will estimate the described statistics for each of the three themes to evaluate the effect of different message contents in terms of dissemination and extent of spread. Moreover, we will make statements about how individuals behave in the network based on the metrics of individuals’ ties.

Furthermore, we will compare the network structures by comparing network densities, and centralities among the dissemination networks of the three theme messages at the end of the campaign. This will be accomplished using UCINET [12] and the Statnet [13] package in R. The comparison of these measurements is based on bootstrapping methods as described by Snjiders and Bogartti [14]. No missing data will be present during the data collection process for the following reasons: only users who provide both their age and gender on their social media profiles will qualify for the study, with the algorithm excluding those who do not meet these criteria, and all reach and engagement metrics will be automatically collected in the backend of the Meta ads campaign.

Ethics approval

This project has been approved by The University of Wollongong Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) with non-individual consent (Ethics Number: 2023/219, Approval Date: 22/08/2023). Any protocol modifications will be submitted as amendments for HREC re-approval and reported in the study findings. Metrics relevant to measuring engagement and sharing of advertisement messages will be accessed via Facebook’s social media metrics, ensuring that individual users are not identified. Although the network will use actual engagement data, each recipient’s Facebook username will be replaced with a unique Facebook profile ID, which remains anonymized. Therefore, individual consent was not required.

Research dissemination

The findings will be disseminated to health promotion groups and researchers through peer-reviewed publications, a student thesis and academic presentations. International Committee of Medical Journal Editors authorship criteria will be applied, and no professional writers will be used. In addition, the findings will be communicated to the University of Wollongong students and lay media via a media release.

Discussion

The primary aim of this research is to evaluate the reach and engagement of a bespoke anti-vaping campaign to determine which message themes result in the most reach and engagement. The delivery of health promotion campaigns on social media is an area of interest, and we are aware of at least one other published protocol evaluating the efficacy of a vaping cessation intervention delivered through Instagram [7]. In our case, our study will assess the reach and engagement of different vaping messages on the Facebook platform, which will provide much-needed complementary information for understanding the effectiveness. Thus, the current trial will advance knowledge about social media messaging by integrating frequently used metrics for social media campaigns (i.e. engagement) with an analysis of how health messages are disseminated with networks.

The proposed network analyses will allow us to identify and explore influential members within the network. A unique aspect of this trial is the scientific question of understanding how youths respond specifically to a health promotion intervention with anti-vaping messages on social media. If social media messages with an environmental or anti-vaping industry focus were shown to have greater engagement than health messages, we would gain new strategies for addressing the harms of vaping. The rise in vaping and the widespread use of social media among youth make this trial an ideal setting for testing social platforms for health promotion.

Typically, social media interventions are developed with a health promotion framework and then evaluate their program from a health promotion perspective. Like many social media interventions we co-designed the campaign with the target age group, but we plan to evaluate the social media campaign more like a health or medical intervention. In part, this was driven by the findings of our systematic review, where we are aiming to deliver the most rigorous research possible. This is reflected in the pre-registration of our trial (The manuscript under review) and in our use of the SPIRIT guideline to define and publish the protocol in advance of the implementation [8].

This study protocol was developed during a period of reforms to the regulation of vapes in Australia, which included restrictions on the importation and selling of vapes. From 1 January 2024, the Australian government has imposed a ban on the importation of all disposable vapes with very few exceptions. From 1 March 2024, only therapeutic vapes with an import licence and a permit from the Office of Drug Control will be permitted for importation. Furthermore, only pharmacies will be permitted to sell vapes that contain nicotine, with a loophole on the sales of non-nicotine vapes expected to be closed in the near future. Additionally, all pharmacies selling vaping products will need to be licensed. Federal legislation is still ongoing in 2024 to prevent domestic manufacturers from advertising, supplying, and commercially possessing non-therapeutic and disposable vapes. By conducting this campaign while these regulatory changes are happening, this study can further enhance the awareness of the potential impacts of e-cigarette/vape use. Our work to develop the vaping messages occurred prior to the recent government policy and we were unaware of the extent of the changes. Although our trial is somewhat complicated by the changing policy landscape it is not the time for to postpone its’ implementation. In fact, across the world, the Australian government policy actions have made vaping such a hot topic in the Australian community, that we now want to capitalise on this extraordinary moment in time to test the social media strategy.

There are several strengths of this trial. The concealment of allocation is entirely algorithm-based; therefore, both the potential participants and the research team are unable to know which of the messages are going to be pushed to which individuals, and this process cannot be manipulated by anyone. As a result, this work can reflect a real-world scenario rather than an ideal scenario in a traditional trial. Moreover, intervention messages were co-developed with consumers, which is empowering and builds autonomy. This project covers a broader population of individuals aged 18 to 34 years across Australia rather than targeting individuals in specific locations, which is typical of other health promotion campaigns. Our choice of campaign duration and metrics are evidence-based (The manuscript under review). However, our campaign is only being delivered on Facebook, which means that the target audience must be active Facebook users. At the present time, we do not have funding or approval to deliver the messaging across multiple platforms. Furthermore, most social media campaigns continue to be delivered on Facebook and it remains one of the largest and most common platforms for delivering social media messages by organisations working in health promotion. Ultimately, we want our study to inform health organisations to reach their target population better, increase engagement, and advise health institutions to optimise their limited resources for maximum effectiveness.
