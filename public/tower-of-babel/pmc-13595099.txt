Genotype–Phenotype‐Environment Associations and Potential Local Adaptation Signals in the Mesquite Lizard Along the Eastern Trans‐Mexican Volcanic Belt
Authors: Óscar Romero‐Báez, Verónica Zepeda, Ella Vázquez‐Domínguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnthropogenic activities modify the composition and configuration of landscapes and fragment habitats, generating environmental gradients and dispersal barriers that alter gene flow and generate selective pressures. Identifying local adaptation, which depends on the balance between selection and gene flow, requires integrative approaches that combine multiple sources of evidence. Notably, signals of local adaptation have been poorly evaluated in Neotropical lizards. By integrating phenotypic, genomic, environmental, and landscape data, we assessed whether the mesquite lizard Sceloporus grammicus exhibits morphological changes associated with landscape variables, and also potential signals of local adaptation along a natural‐anthropogenic gradient across the eastern Trans‐Mexican Volcanic Belt. Phenotypic results showed traits that varied with landscape variables, including a negative effect of elevation on snout‐vent length (SVL) and a positive effect of distance to forested areas (Dfz) on body condition index (BCI). Using genotype‐environment and genotype–phenotype associations, we detected 72 candidate single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with adaptive signals, based on which we identified four adaptive units across the study area. Functional enrichment highlighted four genes (ADCY10, LMOD3, NINJ1, STX16) with overrepresented molecular functions. The frequency of the alternative allele in SNPs annotated to ADCY10, LMOD3, and NINJ1 increased with elevation and in individuals with smaller SVL, while it increased with Dfz and BCI for STX16. Overall, our results support spatially structured, landscape‐linked candidate adaptive variation consistent with previously inferred functional connectivity patterns for the mesquite lizard. They also suggest mechanisms for exploring physiological and metabolic responses under environmental heterogeneity and anthropisation that could favor local adaptation.Keywords: anthropic landscapes, gene flow, landscape genomics, local adaptation, Sceloporus grammicus

Introduction

Anthropogenic activities, like agriculture, land use changes, urbanisation, and road infrastructure, modify the environmental conditions and the composition and configuration of landscapes (Doherty et al. 2020; Supple and Shapiro 2018; Wenner et al. 2022). Such modifications fragment and disturb the habitats of species, creating environmental gradients and barriers to dispersal that limit migration and gene flow while promoting isolation and genetic differentiation among populations (Borja‐Martínez and Vázquez‐Domínguez 2024; Miles et al. 2019; Romero‐Báez et al. 2024; Wang and Bradburd 2014). Varying degrees of landscape modification can create selective pressures, influencing local adaptation and extinction (Ruiz‐Miñano et al. 2022; Slatkin 1973; Tigano and Friesen 2016). The spatial structure of the landscape (e.g., the configuration of habitat patches) plays a crucial role in determining the strength of local adaptation (Forester et al. 2016; Li et al. 2017). The evolution of traits within a population leads to greater fitness in native individuals within their local landscape compared to those from external populations, resulting in adaptive divergence of phenotypes (Li et al. 2017; Richardson et al. 2014; Yeaman and Whitlock 2011).

The extent to which populations diverge genetically along environmental gradients and are able to locally adapt in response to changes in the landscape is tightly linked to the balance between selection and gene flow (Cheptou et al. 2017; Räsänen and Hendry 2008; Tigano and Friesen 2016). Often, an intermediate level of gene flow can inhibit local adaptive divergence, although this depends on population size and the degree of habitat heterogeneity, which influence the intensity and directionality of the gene flow (Forester et al. 2016; Garant et al. 2007). Additionally, in isolated populations with limited gene flow and low genetic variation, a moderate increase in gene flow can increase genetic variation and, consequently, favor local adaptation (Bell and Gonzalez 2011; Richardson and Urban 2013; Richardson et al. 2014). Recent evolutionary processes often leave weak signatures of selection in the genome, as allele frequencies rarely reach fixation within a few generations. Also, false signals of selection in populations can be generated due to demographic factors like bottlenecks and genetic drift (Salmón et al. 2021). Genotype by environment association analyses have been effective in detecting genetic variants under weak selection. Importantly, combining different detection methods and overlapping candidate variants can help reduce spurious associations and false positives (Ahrens et al. 2021; Forester et al. 2018). Furthermore, genotype–phenotype assessments that incorporate the association with the environment have improved our understanding of the genomics of traits that are affected by landscape modification, to assess the adaptive potential of such traits, and to identify traits under selection (Assis et al. 2025; Bourgeois et al. 2025; Gienapp et al. 2017).

Hence, given the complex relationship between environmental and spatial landscape heterogeneity, gene flow, and selection, identifying local adaptations in natural populations requires an integrative analytical approach that includes multiple sources of evidence (Araya‐Donoso et al. 2022; Jones et al. 2013; Meek et al. 2023). Combining physiological, morphological, genomic, environmental, and landscape information certainly aids in this purpose (Cheek et al. 2022; Strickland et al. 2023; Thompson et al. 2025), as shown in studies with reptiles, particularly lizards. For example, Campbell‐Staton et al. (2020) integrated physiological, genomic, environmental, and landscape data and identified repeated adaptation to urban heat islands in Anolis cristatellus , with parallel increases in heat tolerance and convergent changes in gene expression variants and networks. Similarly, genomic, environmental, and landscape data in A. carolinensis showed strongly differentiated genomic regions associated with genes enriched for locomotion, behaviour, and immunity functions along urbanisation gradients, suggesting rapid adaptive responses in cities (Bourgeois et al. 2025). Also, potential local adaptation in Sceloporus undulatus was revealed by morphological and genomic information in a landscape disturbed by the introduction of the fire ant. Individuals exhibited changes in hind leg length and in genomic regions under selection related to musculoskeletal development and immune pathways (Assis et al. 2025). Notably, the detection of signals of local adaptation has been scarcely studied in lizards distributed in the Neotropics (Araya‐Donoso et al. 2022; Campbell‐Staton et al. 2020; Prates et al. 2018; Rodríguez et al. 2017), including Mexico, to the point that information is lacking for most species in this region.

The mesquite lizard, Sceloporus grammicus , is a generalist species with a wide distribution, ranging from southern Texas in the United States to Oaxaca in Mexico, occurring between 100 and 4400 m above sea level (Arévalo et al. 1991; Domínguez‐Godoy, Gómez‐Campos, et al. 2020; Marshall et al. 2006). It inhabits areas from deserts and scrublands to forests and high‐altitude mountainous regions, as well as agricultural zones (Pérez‐Mendoza et al. 2013, 2014). It is an active thermoregulator that uses trees, rocks, and walls as microhabitats (Domínguez‐Godoy et al. 2025; Flores et al. 2023). Significant differences in S. grammicus thermoregulatory efficiency have been documented along elevation gradients and between agriculture, forest, and grassland zones (Díaz de la Vega‐Pérez, Barrios‐Montiel, et al. 2019; Domínguez‐Godoy et al. 2022; Rivera‐Rea et al. 2023). Interestingly, it exhibits morphological changes with altitude, having greater mass, larger body size, and better body condition at lower elevations compared to higher ones (González‐Morales et al. 2021a, 2021b). Little information has been documented about the population genetics of the mesquite lizard in general, and particularly along its distribution in Mexico. Romero‐Báez et al. (2024) found that S. grammicus populations along the eastern Trans‐Mexican Volcanic Belt (TMVB) showed marked genetic differentiation yet maintained functional connectivity for tens of kilometres despite their low vagility. They also documented that forest cover, environmental variables (relative humidity, substrate, and air temperature), and anthropogenic factors (agricultural cover and presence of roads) facilitated and limited its functional connectivity at regional and local scales.

The eastern TMVB includes two protected natural areas and encompasses a gradient of fragmented and modified ecosystems associated with anthropogenic processes (Díaz de la Vega‐Pérez, Jiménez‐Arcos, et al. 2019; Mastretta‐Yanes et al. 2018), rendering it an optimal scenario for studying phenotypic differences and local adaptation in the mesquite lizard. Here, we ask whether S. grammicus (1) exhibits phenotypic variation associated with environmental and landscape variables in the natural‐anthropogenic gradient along the eastern TMVB, and (2) displays distinct potential local adaptation signals to cope with the heterogeneity of the gradient of the anthropised landscape. To this end, we characterise landscape heterogeneity using environmental, vegetation, topographic, and anthropogenic variables. We combine this information with phenotypic (morphological) and genomic (single nucleotide polymorphisms—SNPs) data to identify phenotype‐landscape associations, as well as candidate SNPs potentially under selection via genotype‐environment (GEA) and genotype–phenotype (GPA) associations. Finally, we map potentially adaptive SNPs found by both GEA and GPA and identify their association with biological functions and metabolic pathways. We predict that the geographic areas identified by Romero‐Báez et al. (2024) with higher gene flow will exhibit similar phenotypic traits and adaptation signals and will be associated with landscape variables that facilitated functional connectivity. Conversely, areas with limited gene flow will show unique phenotypic traits and local adaptation signals and will be linked to landscape variables that limited functional connectivity. We also expect that candidate SNPs associated with phenotypic and landscape variables are linked to genes related to physiological mechanisms associated with environmental stress and to components relevant to morphological variation, which could favor the response of individuals to cope with the pressures of the heterogeneous and anthropised landscape.

Materials and Methods

Fieldwork and Sampling

Fieldwork was performed as described in Romero‐Báez et al. (2024); briefly, we worked in the states of Mexico, Puebla, and Tlaxcala between October 2019 and August 2022 in the eastern TMVB. The study area included six regions located at elevations between 2282 and 3393 m: Ostol (OST), San Sebastián (SAS), and San Juan (SAJ) mountains (which together are known as Cerros Grises), as well as Mount Tlaloc (TLA) and two protected natural areas that contain the volcanoes La Malinche (LAM) and Iztaccihuatl (IZT) (Figure 1). We performed a pairwise sampling protocol (forest and agricultural zones) within each region, except in LAM, which is solely agriculture, for a total of 11 sampling sites selected based on our field experience (Table S1).

We captured 171 S. grammicus individuals across the 11 sampling sites, using a sliding rope or by hand; we measured 11 phenotypic traits: snout‐vent length (SVL), tail length (TaL), total length (ToL), forearm length (FoL), humerus length (HuL), tibia length (TiL), femur length (FeL), skull length (SkL), skull width (SkW), and skull height (SkH), using a digital calliper TITAN (±0.1 mm; TITAN Professional Tools, Renton, Washington, USA), and body mass (Ms) with an electronic pocket scale Camry EHA601 (±0.01 g; Camry Electronic Ltd., Zhaoqing, Guangdong, China). We also collected tail tissue samples using non‐lethal sampling techniques and preserved them in labelled 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes with 99% ethanol for subsequent genetic analyses. At each capture site, we took geographic coordinates using a portable GPS (Garmin eTrex‐20), measured relative humidity with a data logger (HOBO UX100‐003), and recorded air and substrate temperatures using a digital thermometer (Fluke 54‐II). We captured and managed lizards with the appropriate scientific collection permit (SGPA/DGVS/07931/21) and in strict adherence to the guides and norms for working with amphibians and reptiles (Beaupre et al. 2004). All individuals were released at their capture location, while two specimens were taken from each sampling site for the Colección de Anfibios y Reptiles of the Facultad de Estudios Superiores Zaragoza, UNAM.

Exploration and Estimation of Phenotypic Traits

Prior to phenotypic analysis, we filtered individuals to only consider sexually mature organisms (SVL range 39–44 mm; Domínguez‐Godoy, Hudson, et al. 2020) and discarded those that had a high number of no‐data (NA ≥ 6) or lacked sex identification or sampling site coordinates. Therefore, phenotypic analyses were performed for 153 individuals (Table S1; 74 males and 79 females).

Following the protocol of Zuur et al. (2010), we explored the phenotypic traits to detect outlier measurements, homogeneity of variance, and normality. We evaluated outliers for each trait using boxplots (1.5 × interquartile range [IQR]), percentiles (1st and 99th), and Hampel filter methods (median ±3 times median absolute deviation [MAD]; Pearson 2005; Zuur et al. 2010) with R (R Core Team 2024). A value was considered an outlier if detected by two of the three methods. We imputed the outliers and NA (2.8% and 7.5%, respectively) with missMDA v.1.20 (Josse and Husson 2016). This method uses a regularised iterative PCA algorithm to impute, which estimates the number of components by means of K‐fold cross‐validation (Husson and Josse 2013; Josse and Husson 2012). Likewise, six traits did not exhibit homogeneity of variance, identified with Stats v.4.2.2 in R; however, their ratio values were < 4 (Table S2), not affecting the coefficients estimation, and thus were included in the linear models (Zuur et al. 2010). We also tested that the most likely distributions for traits were normal or log‐normal using fitdistrplus v.1.2–4 (Delignette‐Muller and Dutang 2015) (Table S3).

For an additional phenotypic trait, we estimated the body condition index (BCI) as a measure of individual health status, following the scaled mass index (SMI) method proposed by Peig and Green (2009, 2010), which outperforms (Green 2001; Peig and Green 2009) the commonly used OLS (residuals from an ordinary least squares regression of mass against SVL; Hayes and Shonkwiler 2001; Jakob et al. 1996). SMI standardises mass at a fixed value of a linear body measurement based on the scaling relationship between mass and length, using the equation M^=MiL0/LibSMA where M i and L i are the body mass and the linear body measure (phenotypic trait) of individual i, respectively; L 0 is the arithmetic mean of the linear body measure of the species; b SMA is the scaling exponent estimated by regression of the standardised major axis (SMA; slope value) between M and L; and M^ is the predicted body mass for individual i when the linear body measure is standardised to L 0. L is chosen based on the correlation of linear body measures with M, so we used SVL due to its high correlation (Peig and Green 2009). We calculated the Pearson correlation using psych v.2.5.6 (Revelle 2025; 2010; males: r = 0.92, females: r = 0.85) and the SMA regression using lmodel2 v.1.7–4 (Legendre 2024). We performed data exploration and BCI estimation by sex, because S. grammicus exhibits sexual dimorphism (Díaz de la Vega‐Pérez, Barrios‐Montiel, et al. 2019; Domínguez‐Godoy, Gómez‐Campos, et al. 2020). Finally, we scaled and centered all the phenotypic traits for further analyses.

Environmental and Landscape Data

We created a dataset of categorical and continuous layers with a 10 m resolution to characterise the landscape heterogeneity with environmental (air temperature [At], substrate temperature [St], relative humidity [Rh], moisture stress index [MSI]), vegetation (normalised difference vegetation index [NDVI], distance to forest zones [Dfz], natural cover [Nc]), topographic (elevation [Ele], slope [Slo], aspect [Asp]), and anthropogenic variables (distance to agricultural zones [Daz], urban zones [Duz], and roads [Dr]; agricultural [Ac], urban [Uc], and road [Rc] covers) (Table S4).

Regarding the categorical layers, we obtained the vegetation classification and land use layer from the monitoring activity data for the Mexican REDD+ program (MADMEX‐CONABIO), which has 31 classifications at 10 m (available at https://monitoreo.conabio.gob.mx/snmb_charts/descarga_datos_madmex.html; accessed on 12 January‐2023). From this, we created three layers: one of vegetation zones that included mainly forests (Nc), agricultural (Ac), and urban zones (Uc) of the study area using terra v.1.6–7 (Hijmans 2022). We rasterised with QGIS v.3.22 (QGIS Development Team 2021) roads and highways (Rc) at the target resolution from a vectorial layer of communication routes for Mexico (available at https://www.inegi.org.mx/temas/viascomunicacion/#Descargas; accessed on 15 January‐2023). The resulting layer was overlaid and visually checked against the vector layer to ensure no road data were lost during processing. From each layer, we calculated the percentage at each capture site using a 30 m buffer (Romero‐Báez et al. 2024) with landscapemetrics v.1.5.4 (Hesselbarth et al. 2019).

For the continuous layers, we estimated the distance from each pixel center to the nearest pixel center of the target variable to create distance layers (Dfz, Daz, Duz, Dr) from the four categorical layers using QGIS. For the environmental variables, we used the Kriging method (Holdaway 1996) to interpolate Rh, At, and St measured at each capture site with SAGA v.7.8.2 (Conrad et al. 2015). We implemented a linear function with 90% of the data and estimated the root mean square error (RMSE) with a cross‐validation of 1000 with the K‐fold method. This geostatistical interpolation accounts for geographic autocorrelation and accurately adjusts for environmental variables (Li and Heap 2014). We used the Mexican elevation continuum surfaces v.3.0 for Estado de Mexico, Puebla, and Tlaxcala (15 m resolution; available at https://www.inegi.org.mx/app/geo2/elevacionesmex; accessed on 22 January‐2023). To obtain the working resolution (10 m), we performed the following with QGIS: restricted the layer to the study area and randomly generated 2,000,000 points (~10% of the data); took the elevation value of every point and performed an interpolation using the triangulated irregular network method that performs best for this variable (Habib et al. 2020); and generated the Slo and Asp layers from the Ele layer using Horn's algorithm, which is useful for more rugged terrain (Horn 1981). We also estimated the NDVI and the MSI from Sentinel 2 satellite images (10 m resolution; IDs: L2A_T14QNG_A022117_20210531T171305 and L2A_T14QPG_A022117_20210531T171305; available at https://browser.dataspace.copernicus.eu/?zoom=5&lat=50.16282&lng=20.78613&themeId=DEFAULT‐THEME&demSource3D=%22MAPZEN%22&cloudCoverage=30&dateMode=SINGLE; accessed on 4 January‐2023). Prior to calculation, we applied an atmospheric correction to the images to eliminate the effects of the atmosphere (Valdivieso‐Ros et al. 2021). For each layer, we estimated the median at each capture site from a 30 m buffer (Romero‐Báez et al. 2024). We used the median given its adequate performance with skewed distributions and outliers (Wenner et al. 2022). Finally, for the following analyses, values of all the variables (categorical and continuous) were scaled and centered.

Phenotypic Traits and Landscape Analyses

We evaluated if the degree of phenotypic variation was explained by regions (6) or by sampling sites (11) (Table S1) using generalised linear mixed‐effects models (GLMM) with lme4 v.1.1–37 (Bates et al. 2015). We compared, for each phenotypic trait (response variable), three models with different structures of random effects: (i) a model that included regions to consider a geographic scale, (ii) a model with sampling sites to consider a local landscape variation, and (iii) a null model with no random effects. We fitted all models to a Gaussian error distribution and included sex as a fixed effect to consider sexual dimorphism of the species and SVL as a covariate to reflect the effect on size‐corrected traits (Li et al. 2024; Strickland et al. 2023), except for the model where this trait was analysed. We tested the sex and SVL interaction, but it did not improve the fit of the models, so it was discarded. We calculated the p‐values for each model using lmerTest v.3.1–3 (Kuznetsova et al. 2017) and selected the best model based on AIC values and ΔAIC < 2 as a cut‐off point (Akaike 1973; Burnham and Anderson 2002).

We assessed the effect of landscape variables on phenotypic traits using GLMM in lme4. We compared, for each phenotypic trait, two models: (i) a model with landscape variables (predictors) and (ii) a null model without landscape variables. We chose to use sampling sites as random effects for all models because they showed the lowest AIC and ΔAIC values that did not exceed the established cut‐off value, as well as the largest variance explained among the random effects tested (see Results). We fitted all models to a Gaussian error distribution and again included sex as a fixed effect and SVL as covariate, except in the model where the latter was analysed. We discarded Uc and Rc because of their high proportion of zeros (99.35% and 84.31%, respectively), as well as At, Duz, Nc, and MSI, given their correlations (> 0.7). The remaining landscape variables (Asp, Ele, Slo, Dfz, NDVI, Rh, Dr., Daz, Ac, St) were used as fixed effects. However, we had to exclude Rh from all models and include Dr. and Daz only in two and eight models, respectively, because they showed evidence of multicollinearity (variance inflation factor > 2; VIF) (Zuur et al. 2010). We obtained the p‐values for each model with lmerTest and selected the landscape variables model when it had a lower AIC value or ΔAIC < 2 with respect to the null model.

DNA Extraction, Sequencing, Assembly and Filtering

The genomic data used in this study were obtained from one of our previous studies (Romero‐Báez et al. 2024). Importantly, we here performed new filtering and bioinformatics protocols appropriate for GEA and genomic selection analyses. Briefly, we used the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) to extract genomic DNA from 95 randomly selected tissue samples across the 11 sampling sites (Romero‐Báez et al. 2024), following the manufacturer's instructions. We quantified DNA with a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and used GelRed (Biotium, Fremont, CA) to visualise quality on a 1% agarose gel. Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the University of Wisconsin Biotechnology Center, using the paired‐end ddRAD protocol (Peterson et al. 2012) and the restriction enzymes PstI and BfaI. Samples were sequenced to generate 151‐bp paired reads using an Illumina NovaSeq line.

We used FASTQC v.0.11.9 (Andrews 2019) to assess data quality and process_radtags from Stacks v.2.6 (Catchen et al. 2011, 2013) for demultiplexing. We eliminated adapters and reads with Phred quality < 20, trimmed reads to length of 126 bp using Trimmomatic v.0.36 (Bolger et al. 2014), and removed reads with kmers using kmer_filter in Stacks (Rochette et al. 2019). We aligned the resulting reads to the Sceloporus undulatus reference genome (NCBI RefSeq assembly: GCF_019175285.1; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_019175285.1/; accessed on 4 April‐2023) using BWA mem in BWA v.0.7.17 (Li 2013) and used SAMtools v.1.22 (Danecek et al. 2021) to sort and index the alignments. Finally, we called SNPs with gstacks and populations in Stacks (Catchen et al. 2011, 2013; Rochette et al. 2019), using the Marukilov model, ‐p 11, ‐r 0.8, and ‐‐write‐random‐snp.

We filtered SNPs that had genotypes with a minimum depth < 5 and a minimum quality < 20. We also removed SNPs with an average depth < 16, a value that was established using two times the mode of the average depth calculated across all SNPs (Willis et al. 2017). Next, following the iterative filtering protocol of O'Leary et al. (2018), we removed individuals and SNPs based on their percentage of missing data and completeness, respectively, to maximise the number of individuals and SNPs retained. For individuals, we started with a percentage of 90% and decreased by 20% with each iteration until reaching 50%. For SNPs, we started with a percentage of 50% and increased by 10% with each iteration until reaching 70%. We then removed heterozygous genotypes that were not within the indicated allelic balance ratio (0.25–0.75) to discard SNPs from paralogous sequences (O'Leary et al. 2018). Again, we filtered out individuals with more than 30% missing data and SNPs with more than 80% completeness. Because removing individuals resulted in invariant SNPs, we remove them with min‐mac = 1. We then filtered SNPs that did not have a minimum distance of 10,000 base pairs to reduce linkage between SNPs. All these filters were done with SNPfiltR v.1.0.2 (DeRaad 2022). Finally, we removed SNPs that had a maximum observed heterozygosity > 0.5 using populations in Stacks and a minor allele frequency < 0.01 with VCFtools v.0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011).

Sequencing resulted in 468,301,806 raw reads from the paired‐end ddRAD library of 95 S. grammicus individuals, with 91.82% of reads with a Phred quality ≥ 30 and a mean quality score of 35.5, indicating accurate sequencing. After the first filters we kept 195,019,171 reads (average per individual: 2,052,833), while with the assembly and SNP calling process using the Sceloporus undulatus genome we obtained 43,766 SNPs. Five individuals were discarded after final filtering due to high missing data; thus, our final database comprised 89 individuals and 1600 SNPs with an average depth per individual of 11X.

Identification of Potential Candidate SNPs

We followed the protocol used by Carvalho et al. (2021) to identify potential candidate SNPs assessing genotype‐environment associations (GEA) and genotype–phenotype associations (GPA). We used multivariate and univariate methods, namely partial redundancy analysis (pRDA) and latent factor mixed models (LFMM), respectively. Both methods assume linear relationships between explanatory variables (landscape or phenotypic) and genotypic variation (response variable) to detect candidate SNPs under selection by controlling for neutral genetic structure. Furthermore, they provide an adequate compromise between detection power and error rates and are robust to sampling design limitations and spatial autocorrelation between populations (Carvalho et al. 2021; de Villemereuil et al. 2014; Forester et al. 2018; Lotterhos and Whitlock 2015; Rellstab et al. 2015). However, these methods require complete datasets (without missing values), so we did imputation of missing genotypes (13.22%) with a Bayesian method in Bimbam v.1.0 (Servin and Stephens 2007), using 100 runs, 500 steps per run, and 20 clusters.

For the GEA assessment, we performed pRDA with vegan v.2.7–2 (Oksanen et al. 2025), excluding, like in the phenotypic analyses, the landscape variables that showed a high proportion of zeros or high correlation. To control for neutral genetic structure (Capblancq and Forester 2021), we used the first three axes of a PCA, obtained from a genetic structure analysis by Romero‐Báez et al. (2024), as covariates. We assessed multicollinearity among predictor variables based on VIF and excluded Dr. (VIF > 8). We estimated the significance of the global model and the constrained axes with vegan using an ANOVA with 9999 permutations; based on these results, only significant axes were used to identify candidate SNPs. We applied a Mahalanobis distance‐based approach for the identification of candidate SNPs (Capblancq et al. 2018; Carvalho et al. 2021), which allows adjusting p‐values using the genomic inflation factor (GIF); GIF was modified until finding a calibrated distribution of p‐values (Figure S1a,b). From the obtained distribution, we estimated the false discovery rate (FDR; Storey and Tibshirani 2003) with Stats to assess significance level and to minimise the number of false positives. Regarding LFMM, we used the same explanatory variables as in the pRDA and applied a model per variable with lfmm v.1.1 (Jumentier 2021), using ridge estimates which minimise regularised least squares with an L2 penalty (Carvalho et al. 2021; Caye et al. 2019). We used 1,000,000 iterations and five genetic clusters (identified by Romero‐Báez et al. (2024)) as latent factors to control for neutral genetic structure. Likewise, we calculated and modified the GIF until a calibrated distribution of p‐values was obtained (Figures S1c–j and S2), followed by an FDR significance estimation.

We followed the same pRDA and LFMM procedures for the GPA evaluation (see Figures S3 and S4 for GIF plots). However, results for the pRDA (global model and constrained axes) were not significant (p > 0.05); therefore, we applied a genome‐wide association (GWA) (see Blanco‐Pastor et al. 2021; Cheek et al. 2022) using Gemma v.0.98.3 (Zhou and Stephens 2012). GWA is a univariate linear method that evaluates the effect of genotypes (fixed effect) on each phenotypic trait (response variable) while correcting for neutral population structure using a kinship matrix (random effect; Sul et al. 2018), reducing the false positive rate even with small sample sizes (Strickland et al. 2023; Zhou and Stephens 2012). We included sex as a fixed effect and SVL as a covariate, except when SVL was analysed. We used Wald tests to calculate coefficients and p‐values and assessed significance with FDR in Stats. Since GWA and LFMM are univariate models, we did a model for each phenotypic trait regardless of its correlation. Likewise, a candidate SNP was considered significant with a p‐value < 0.05.

We identified combined candidate loci, namely, those SNPs detected in at least one GEA‐GPA combination, by pRDA, LFMM, and GWA methods, with a Venn diagram using ggVennDiagram v.1.5.4 (Gao and Dusa 2025). Next, we ran additional RDA models with vegan to visualise the GEA and GPA patterns using only the combined candidate SNPs. For the RDA in GEA, we used the same landscape variables as in the pRDA; for the GPA, we excluded SkL because it showed no significant associations with any SNP (Table S5). Next, using an iterative procedure based on VIF we sequentially removed ToL and Ms. as they exhibited high multicollinearity with the other phenotypic variables included in the RDA (VIF > 10). We evaluated the significance of both RDAs (global models) with vegan using 9999 permutations.

Mapping Combined Candidate SNPs to Identify Adaptive Units

We mapped the combined candidate SNPs with a potential adaptive signal detected by GEA and GPA to identify analogous adaptive units, using the axes of a spatial principal component analysis (sPCA) (Carvalho et al. 2021). sPCA is a spatial multivariate method that summarises genetic variability and spatial structure among individuals using scores (Jombart et al. 2008). The spatial structure is estimated with the Moran's Index, which compares the observed allele frequencies in an individual with those of neighbouring individuals. Neighbouring individuals are established using connection networks, which we define as a distance‐based neighbourhood (recommended for aggregated or patchy distributions; Jombart et al. 2008). The Moran's Index generates two types of spatial structuring, reflecting positive (global structure) and negative (local structure) spatial autocorrelation (Jombart et al. 2008). We performed local and global tests with 9999 permutations with adegenet v.2.1.11 (Jombart and Ahmed 2011) to determine whether the global or local structure should be used in the sPCA analysis. Next, as in Carvalho et al. (2021), we retained the first three axes of the spatial structure that were significant and interpolated them using inverse distance weighted interpolation (IDW; Shepard 1968) across the study area at a 10 m resolution. We merged the resulting raster layers into a Red‐Green‐Blue (RGB) composite raster to highlight spectral patterns and visualise the adaptive units with QGIS.

SNPs Annotation and Functional Enrichment Analyses

We identified the flanking sequences of the combined candidate SNPs and annotated them by homology search using the Sceloporus undulatus (NCBI ID: taxid:8520) and Anolis carolinensis (NCBI ID: taxid:28377) reference genomes, using BLASTn in the NCBI non‐redundant (nr) nucleotide database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?PROGRAM=blastn&PAGE_TYPE=BlastSearch&LINK_LOC=blasthome; accessed on 11 September‐2025). We applied an E‐value threshold ≤ 1 × 10−5 and a percentage identity (PI) ≥ 80% to filter the Blast hits and, for each annotated flanking sequence, selected the best Blast hit as the one with the greatest query cover (QC). We transformed the gene names obtained from the annotation to A. carolinensis specific ENSEMBL identifiers with g:Convert included in g:Profiler v.e113_eg59_p19_6be52918 (Raudvere et al. 2019; https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/convert; accessed on 26 September‐2025). We performed functional enrichment analysis with g:Gost on g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost; accessed on 27 September‐2025) using the ENSEMBL identifiers to identify overrepresented gene ontology (GO) terms (Ashburner et al. 2000) and KEGG pathways (Kanehisa et al. 2017) with the A. carolinensis genome (Alföldi et al. 2011). We applied hypergeometric tests with a p < 0.05 threshold and adjusted significance based on FDR. We calculated the allele frequencies of the combined candidate SNPs annotated to enriched genes using R. Finally, we graphically visualised the relationship between the alternative allele frequencies and the associated landscape and phenotypic variables obtained in the RDAs of GEA and GPA across the sampling sites.

We captured 171 individuals across the 11 sampling sites, of which 18 were discarded because they were not sexually mature, had a high number of NA (≥ 6), or lacked sex or sampling site coordinates data (Table S1). Phenotypic analyses were performed for 153 individuals (74 males and 79 females).

Effect of Landscape Variables on Phenotypic Traits

Results of the generalised linear mixed effects models showed significant effects for six landscape variables (elevation [Ele], slope [Slo], distance to forest [Dfz] and to agricultural zones [Daz], normalised difference vegetation index [NDVI], and agricultural cover [Ac]) on seven phenotypic traits (Table S5). However, we only considered the models for five traits (snout‐vent length [SVL], forearm length [FoL], tibia length [TiL], body mass [Ms], and body condition index [BCI]) because they had a lower AIC value or ΔAIC < 2 compared to the null model (Table S6). Sex had a significant positive relationship with the same traits as in the previous models, except TiL, which was not significant, while SVL showed a positive and significant relationship for all traits except BCI, which was negative (Table S5). In more detail, the association between the landscape variables Ele and Slo with SVL showed a contrasting trend, negative for Ele (β = −0.3460, p < 0.001) in the sampling sites (see Figure 1) La Malinche (LAM‐A) and Cerros Grises (Figure 2a) and positive for Slo (β = 0.1804, p = 0.032) in Ostol (OST‐A, OST‐F) and San Juan (SAJ‐A, and SAJ‐F) (Figure 2b). Therefore, individuals inhabiting lower elevations or on steeper slopes had a larger size. The variables NDVI (β = −0.2597, p = 0.006) in OST‐F, SAS‐A and San Juan (SAJ‐A, SAJ‐F) (Figure 2c), and Ac (β = −0.1824, p = 0.027) in OST‐F, SAS‐A, SAJ‐F, and SAJ‐F (Figure 2d) showed a significant negative effect on FoL; namely, individuals present in open areas or with less agricultural cover had longer FoL. Contrasting trends were also observed in TiL between Slo (positive; β = 0.2289, p = 0.020) and Ac (negative; β = −0.2483, p = 0.002) at several sampling sites. Thus, individuals on steeper slopes or sites with less agricultural cover had longer TiL. This trend was observed for Slo in OST‐A, OST‐F, and SAJ‐A (Figure 2e), while for Ac in OST‐F, SAS‐A, and SAJ‐A (Figure 2f). Finally, Dfz had a positive effect on Ms. (β = 0.1533, p < 0.001) and BCI (β = 0.2479, p = 0.005), where individuals at greater distances from forest zones had higher Ms. and BCI. The Ms. pattern was observed in LAM‐A, OST‐A, SAS‐A, SAS‐F, and Tlaloc (TLA‐A) (Figure 2g), while BCI in LAM‐A, SAS‐A, and TLA‐A (Figure 2h).

The genomic analyses were based on our final database that comprised 89 individuals (Table S1) and 1600 SNPs with an average depth per individual of 11X. Additionally, we detected 411 candidate SNPs with LFMM, of which 89 were associated with more than one variable, resulting in a total of 559 associations (Table S7). Regarding the GPA, we identified 70 and 54 candidate SNPs with GWA and with LFMM, respectively; for the latter, nine were associated with more than one variable, resulting in 71 associations (Table S8). Of the candidate SNPs, 110 and five were shared between methods within GEA and GPA, respectively (Tables S5 and S6). Finally, 72 combined candidate SNPs were identified, that is, those that were detected in at least one GEA‐GPA combination using pRDA, LFMM, and GWA (Figure 3a).

Annotation of Combined Candidate SNPs, Functional Enrichment and Pattern Visualisation

We annotated 16 genes using the flanking sequences of the 72 combined candidate SNPs (E‐value ≤ 1 × 10−5; PI ≥ 80%, and high QC), of which nine had an ENSEMBL identifier specific for A. carolinensis (Table S9). Among these, four genes (ADCY10, LMOD3, NINJ1, STX16) showed significant enrichment (p < 0.05) in GO molecular function terms. ADCY10 was associated with intracellular cAMP signaling processes linked to metabolic regulation (GO:0004016, GO:0009975, GO:0016849); LMOD3 with binding activity to components of the actin–tropomyosin system associated with thin filament organisation in striated muscle (GO:0005523, GO:0003785); NINJ1 with membrane interaction functions associated with adhesion, damage and immunity (GO:0140912, GO:0001530, GO:0098631); and STX16 with endomembrane traffic and autophagy SNARE‐mediated (GO:0005484, GO:0019905) (Tables S10 and S11). Therefore, these genes represent plausible candidates that may contribute to adaptive physiological responses.

Regarding allele frequency patterns, three candidate SNPs annotated to ADCY10, LMOD3, and NINJ1 showed positive associations with Ele and negative associations with SVL (Table S12). Accordingly, the alternative allele frequency increased at higher elevations and in smaller individuals, a pattern observed mainly in IZT‐F, followed by TLA‐A, TLA‐F, and IZT‐A (Figure 5a–f). Conversely, a candidate SNP annotated to STX16 exhibited increasing alternative allele frequency with greater Dfz and BCI, a pattern notable in SAS‐A and SAJ‐A (Figure 5g,h; Table S12).

Discussion

The mesquite lizard, Sceloporus grammicus , has been extensively studied regarding various aspects of its ecology and evolution; however, it remains mostly unexplored in terms of landscape genomics. Here we describe the first findings on potential local adaptation signals for this species along a natural‐anthropogenic gradient in the eastern TMVB, based on an integrative analytical approach and multiple sources of evidence. We found that S. grammicus exhibits both concordant and unique phenotypic patterns associated with the landscape. The identification of 72 shared candidate SNPs between GEA and GPA and of four adaptive units indicates that landscape heterogeneity is promoting specific adaptive responses in S. grammicus . Furthermore, the functional enrichment of four genes to molecular functions is consistent with physiological mechanisms associated with responses to environmental stress and with traits underlying morphological variation, by which the mesquite lizard may be coping with landscape modification in this region. Our results contribute to understanding the effects of the landscape on the evolutionary potential of a widely distributed ectotherm species that can inhabit modified and fragmented habitats.

Phenotypic Variation and Its Association With the Landscape

Species with a wide geographic distribution that inhabit different types of habitats experience a diversity of environmental, ecological, and anthropogenic features and, consequently, spatially heterogeneous selective pressures (Lattanzio et al. 2020; Leyte‐Manrique et al. 2017). This promotes variation in life history traits (e.g., physiological, morphological), even at microgeographic (gradients) scales (Lozano et al. 2020; Mesquita et al. 2015; Pérez‐Mendoza et al. 2013). Sceloporus grammicus is an example of such intraspecific variation; for example, Díaz‐Marín et al. (2022) found changes in tibia length and body associated with perch height. Differences in coloration, body size, and head and limb proportions have also been found among contrasting habitats, some at small spatial scales (Díaz de la Vega‐Pérez, Barrios‐Montiel, et al. 2019; Díaz‐Marín et al. 2025; González‐Morales et al. 2021b). This could explain why we observed greater phenotypic (morphological) variation at the scale of sampling sites and not of regions, suggesting that S. grammicus is responding locally to environmental changes and landscape structure. On the other hand, the size of most phenotypic traits was concordant with S. grammicus sexual dimorphism (Díaz‐Marín et al. 2022; Lozano et al. 2020, 2023), alongside a consistent effect of snout‐vent length (SVL), indicating a strong association between morphological variation and body size (Leyte‐Manrique et al. 2017; Losos 1990). Interestingly, however, some phenotypic traits exhibited significant associations with landscape variables, demonstrating an additional landscape component that promotes morphological variation beyond the effects of sex and SVL.

Sceloporus grammicus follows the inverse pattern of Bergmann's rule, namely individuals at higher elevations have smaller body sizes (González‐Morales et al. 2023; Rivera‐Rea et al. 2023), corroborated by our results given the negative relationship between elevation and SVL. Specifically, individuals from the Iztaccihuatl (IZT‐F) site, which is located at the highest elevation (3372 m), had the smallest SVL (46.6 mm), while IZT‐A, located on the same mountain but at a lower elevation (2887 m), and Ostol (OST‐A), the lowest elevation site (2280 m), showed the largest SVL (52.8 and 65.5 mm, respectively). Likewise, the thermal quality of the habitat decreases with elevation, which restricts S. grammicus activity and foraging time, influencing in turn individual growth and body size (Domínguez‐Godoy, Gómez‐Campos, et al. 2020; Zúñiga‐Vega et al. 2008). This would primarily impact the highest elevation sites (IZT‐F, IZT‐A, TLA‐F), which exhibited the lowest air temperatures (19.3°C) and harboured individuals with the lowest SVL (50.2 mm). In contrast, Tlaloc (TLA‐A), Cerros Grises, and La Malinche (LAM‐A), the lower elevation sites (2677 m), had the highest air temperatures (21.2°C) and larger individuals (SVL, 58.6 mm), which suggests these sites could have higher thermal habitat quality, as described for S. grammicus at similar elevations (Díaz de la Vega‐Pérez, Barrios‐Montiel, et al. 2019; Domínguez‐Godoy et al. 2022; Rivera‐Rea et al. 2023). Further supporting this pattern, we documented a positive correlation between slope with aspect and air temperature; therefore, steep slopes facing south, southwest, west, and northwest (higher aspect values) tended to be warmer (Ostol and San Juan sites: OST‐A, OST‐F, SAJ‐A, SAJ‐F), allowing individuals better thermoregulation.

In addition to S. grammicus individuals meeting their thermal requirements at relatively low elevations, they also depend on an optimal diet that provides the necessary energy and promotes growth (Leyte‐Manrique and RamÍrez‐Bautista 2010). Montoya‐Ciriaco et al. (2020) found that S. grammicus consumes 25 arthropod families at elevations like LAM‐A, Cerros Grises, and TLA‐A, while at elevations comparable to TLA‐F, IZT‐A, and IZT‐F, feeding was reduced to 11 families. Another relevant aspect is that S. grammicus distributed on a forested landscape exhibited higher parasite load and abundance of the tapeworm Cyclophyllidea, which was associated with lower survival, in comparison with the agricultural landscape where no parasites were recorded (Montoya‐Ciriaco et al. 2020; Wolinska and King 2009). This could in part explain our findings since mass and body condition index (BCI) were higher at sites primarily farther from forested zones at lower elevations than in sites near forests at higher elevations. However, studies assessing how diet and parasite load influence S. grammicus body condition and health in contrasting landscapes at different elevations are needed to confirm this pattern.

The length and proportions of the limbs determine locomotor performance in lizards (Diaz‐Ortega et al. 2025; Irschick and Garland Jr. 2001; Reilly and Delancey 1997; Sinervo and Losos 1991). For instance, longer forearms and hindlimbs in S. grammicus inhabiting open landscapes may enhance their ability to climb irregular and three‐dimensional substrates and move in areas with little vegetation (Díaz‐Marín et al. 2022; Lozano et al. 2020), as documented in other lizard species (Melville and Swain 2000) and chameleons (Hopkins and Tolley 2011). It can also benefit in terms of increased sprint speed, territorial defence, prey capture, and predator evasion (Butler and Losos 2002; González‐Morales et al. 2021a; Losos et al. 2006; Melville and Swain 2000). Indeed, S. grammicus individuals showed longer limb lengths at sites with less agricultural cover or open areas with less vegetation and with steep slopes at Ostol, San Sebastián and San Juan sites (OST‐F, SAS‐A, SAS‐F, SAJ‐A, SAJ‐F). Allen's rule states that animals in colder climates tend to have smaller limbs to minimise heat loss (Allen 1877). Studies with lizards have shown both support and the inverse of this rule (Díaz‐Marín et al. 2025), related to the strong influence of thermoregulation processes and of microenvironment (Goldenberg et al. 2022; Rivas et al. 2018). Although we did not intend to test this pattern, it is interesting to note that limb length measured across all sampling sites showed positive correlations with air temperature, where the longest limb lengths were documented in Cerros Grises, the warmest study area.

Differential Signals of Potential Local Adaptation Along the Heterogeneous Landscape Gradient

The association between phenotypic traits and landscape features can be interpreted as evidence of adaptation (Butler and Losos 2002; Endler 1995; Campbell‐Staton et al. 2020; Valladares et al. 2014); however, it can also reflect phenotypic plasticity (Westneat et al. 2019). Therefore, incorporating a genetic component, like genotype–phenotype–environment associations, can strengthen such inference (Strickland et al. 2023). By combining GEA and GPA, we identified 72 candidate SNPs with potentially adaptive signals; additionally, some of these were consistent with the identified phenotype–landscape associations: elevation and snout‐vent length (Ele‐SVL) and distance to forests and body condition index (Dfz‐BCI). Altogether, these findings suggest that the observed phenotypic variation is not explained solely by plasticity and that it also involves local adaptation. Likewise, populations commonly differ in phenotypic traits and genetic composition across their distribution when they experience different landscape characteristics (environmental, topographic, anthropogenic). In this context, local conditions favour particular genotype and phenotype combinations among populations, affecting performance and promoting local adaptation (Soria‐Ortiz et al. 2025; Valladares et al. 2014). Indeed, this is consistent with the four adaptive units we identified for S. grammicus along the study area.

The GLMM and RDAs results for the LAM‐A‐Cerros Grises adaptive unit showed that sites at lower elevations, farther from forest zones, with steep slopes and more open habitats, were mostly associated with larger size individuals. Furthermore, Romero‐Báez et al. (2024) assessed gene flow among the 11 sampling sites; they found high gene flow between LAM‐A and Cerros Grises. Thus, the clustering of this adaptive unit could be related to such connectivity, which was favoured by, among other variables, substrate temperature (St), agricultural cover (Ac), and distance to agricultural zones (Daz) (GEA results). Overall, these variables may not only promote functional connectivity but likely also contribute to phenotypic homogenisation (larger sizes). This scenario is consistent with the expectation that, in heterogeneous landscapes or with low aggregation of habitat patches, local adaptation may be inhibited when selection is weak against gene flow (Forester et al. 2016; Garant et al. 2007). Genetic swamping, that is, the recurrent entry of potentially maladapted foreign alleles that limit local adaptive divergence (Lenormand 2002; Tigano and Friesen 2016), could explain the patterns observed for this adaptive unit.

The SAS‐A adaptive unit was surprising given that Romero‐Báez et al. (2024) found high levels of gene flow for this site at both regional and local scales. A plausible hypothesis is a scenario of strong selection despite gene flow, where migrant alleles or their offspring exhibit lower performance and their establishment is limited, thus maintaining local divergence (Tigano and Friesen 2016). However, demographic studies are necessary to corroborate this, by estimating fitness components (survival, reproductive success, recruitment) and comparing them between residents and migrants (e.g., ancestry assignment and identification of recent migrants). Consistently, SAS‐A exhibited the highest Dfz and the lowest NDVI values, while individuals showed the highest mass, longest tail length (TaL) and skull height (SkH), and among the highest BCI. In agreement, RDAs showed that SAS‐A aligned with the gradient associated with the environment (Distance to forest and to agricultural zones (Dfz, Daz) and NDVI), and with phenotypic variation (SkH, TaL, and partly BCI). Overall, concordance between the genomic signal and the landscape‐phenotypic context supports SAS‐A as an adaptive unit and local adaptation at microgeographic scales (Richardson et al. 2014).

The Tlaloc‐Iztaccihuatl (TLA‐A, TLA‐F, IZT‐A) adaptive unit showed a similar scenario as LAM‐A‐Cerros Grises; namely, with marked landscape heterogeneity among sites and with a certain level of gene flow at the regional scale (Romero‐Báez et al. 2024). Hence, the genetic exchange could be mitigating the expected local divergence of this unit (Forester et al. 2016; Tigano and Friesen 2016). On the other hand, these sites shared a similar elevation and some environmental variables (St, relative humidity (Rh)), as shown by RDA results. In agreement, Romero‐Báez et al. (2024) identified that St and Rh promoted functional connectivity at regional and local scales, respectively. Altogether, these patterns are consistent with a common environmental axis facilitating gene flow and similar adaptive composition, despite the contrasting agricultural‐forestry matrix. There are two non‐mutually exclusive explanations: genetic swamping, as proposed for the LAM‐A‐Cerros Grises adaptive unit, or that selection is relatively concordant along the shared environmental axis (Lenormand 2002; Tigano and Friesen 2016). Phenotypically, only a shared trend toward smaller forearm length (FoL; 7.3 mm) was observed, associated with higher NDVI values. In lizards, limb reduction has been linked to improved locomotor performance in habitats with high vegetation cover, as well as with effective heat dissipation (Díaz‐Marín et al. 2025; Herrel et al. 2002; Volynchik 2014), which is consistent with the observed St and NDVI at these sites.

The IZT‐F site is of particular interest because, in addition to being identified as another adaptive unit, Romero‐Báez et al. (2024) found it was significantly isolated, exhibiting the greatest genetic differentiation and the lowest levels of gene flow. They also found low connectivity associated with different variables depending on the scale: with higher Rh and lower St at the regional scale, and with higher natural cover (Nc) and lower air temperature (At) at the local scale. Consistently, our RDA‐GEA results showed IZT‐F projected toward the landscape characterised by high Ele, higher Rh, and lower St, while in the opposite direction toward more agricultural landscapes and lower Dfz. Regarding the RDA‐GPA results, IZT‐F was located in the opposite direction to the vectors associated with phenotypic traits of large size and BCI, consistent with S. grammicus smaller body size at higher elevations. These patterns suggest that landscape and environmental isolation are facilitating local adaptation in this distinct adaptive unit (Cheek et al. 2022; Garant et al. 2007; Yeaman and Whitlock 2011). Overall, the spatial configuration of the adaptive units is consistent with the functional connectivity framework reported by Romero‐Báez et al. (2024), namely connectivity‐promoting landscape variables aligning with shared adaptive composition within units, whereas connectivity‐inhibiting variables align with more differentiated units.

Association of Candidate SNPs With Landscape and Phenotype

Our findings support our expectation that candidate SNPs associated with phenotypic and landscape variables would be linked with genes related to physiological mechanisms associated with environmental stress and morphological variation. Functional enrichment showed an overrepresentation of molecular functions for four genes linked to candidate SNPs. We found that the frequency of three of these SNPs, annotated to ADCY10, LMOD3, and NINJ1, increased with elevation and decreased with snout‐vent length (SVL). Elevation imposes a selective regime on S. grammicus , since environmental conditions at high elevations are associated with lower thermal habitat quality and more restricted activity periods (Díaz de la Vega et al. 2019; Domínguez‐Godoy, Hudson, et al. 2020; Rivera‐Rea et al. 2023). Furthermore, it has been documented that S. grammicus resting metabolic rate can increase at high elevations, raising the energy demand for maintenance; also, that their diet is drastically impoverished, imposing additional energy constraints (Montoya‐Ciriaco et al. 2020; Plasman et al. 2020). Hence, selection could favour genotypes and phenotypes that maximise physiological homeostasis and performance with reduced maintenance costs, potentially at the expense of growth.

The concordant pattern observed for the three candidate SNPs and the enriched genes supports a mechanistic explanation. ADCY10 may act as an intracellular metabolic sensor (HCO3 −/CO2 and Ca2+) capable of modulating mitochondrial function through intracellular cAMP microdomains, which could favour a more stable supply of ATP during brief periods of activity in cold or hypoxic landscapes, typical of high elevations (Das et al. 2025; Ji et al. 2022; Nguyen et al. 2021; Pozdniakova and Ladilov 2018). Under this regime, the allocation of energy to maintenance and immediate performance could occur at the expense of growth, resulting in lower SVL. LMOD3, for its part, is a structural component of striated muscle associated with thin filament organisation and myogenesis; it also promotes myoblastic differentiation and proliferation via AKT/ERK (Lin et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2023), which could contribute to the maintenance of functional muscle under thermal and energetic constraints. This is relevant given that locomotor performance depends on body temperature and is associated with SVL and limb characteristics (Diaz‐Ortega et al. 2025; Domínguez‐Godoy, Gómez‐Campos, et al. 2020; González‐Morales et al. 2021a). Finally, NINJ1 introduces a complementary dimension of physiological cost, as it is a plasma membrane rupture effector in lytic cell death, modulating the release of damage signals and, consequently, the magnitude and duration of inflammation (David et al. 2024; Sahoo et al. 2024). Therefore, in energy‐constrained environments, variants that mitigate damage and inflammation costs could be particularly relevant by directly competing with maintenance and growth requirements.

The fourth SNP, annotated to STX16, was associated with increased distance to forest zones (Dfz) and body condition index (BCI), suggesting an adaptive mechanism linked to how S. grammicus maintains its energy balance along gradients of proximity to forests. STX16 is a Qa‐SNARE that participates in endomembrane traffic and autophagy, contributing to both autophagosome growth and autolysosome formation (Tang 2019). Functionally, this is related to a higher capacity for intracellular recycling (nutrient mobilisation, removal of damaged organelles, and adjustment of the anabolism‐catabolism balance), processes central to maintaining homeostasis when resources fluctuate or environmental stress increases (Gu et al. 2019; Tang 2019). Consequently, the positive association of Dfz and BCI could align with the hypothesis that, in landscapes farther from forests, variants linked to STX16 might favour a relatively higher BCI by optimising the efficiency of cellular recycling and the control of energy allocation. Thus, the SNPs annotated to ADCY10, LMOD3, and NINJ1 could capture adaptive responses in S. grammicus to cope with the heterogeneous altitudinal regime by prioritising energy efficiency, muscle function, and control of damage and inflammation. Additionally, STX16 might capture the adaptive variation associated with maintaining body condition through the regulation of endomembrane traffic and autophagy in landscapes with higher anthropogenic influence. Patterns that, respectively, could occur with greater intensity in IZT‐F and SAS‐A adaptive units, indicating potential local adaptations.

Conclusions

Our study is the first to detect potential local adaptation signals in Sceloporus grammicus by integrating phenotypic, genomic, environmental, and landscape data. Our findings showed that some phenotypic traits are associated with landscape variables, where certain associations were in addition consistent between GEA and GPA approaches. We identified 72 candidate SNPs with a potential adaptive response, based on which we identified four adaptive units across the study area. The spatial configuration of the adaptive units showed congruent patterns with the functional connectivity reported by Romero‐Báez et al. (2024). Furthermore, functional enrichment indicated overrepresentation of molecular functions for four genes, consistent with physiological processes and components of morphological variation (muscle) potentially relevant to adaptive responses. We acknowledge that these inferences constitute plausible hypotheses about how the mesquite lizard copes with restrictive environmental conditions and anthropised landscapes. These hypotheses should be assessed with physiological, translocation, or transcriptomic experiments, primarily because the functions of these genes have been described in mammals. It would be optimal to integrate physiological data when investigating adaptation processes, as in Araya‐Donoso et al. (2022) and Campbell‐Staton et al. (2020), which can provide valuable information to more rigorously support such mechanisms. Nevertheless, our findings suggest pathways through which S. grammicus could be coping with the challenges posed by heterogeneous and anthropogenic landscapes. Importantly, this scenario cannot apply to all lizards, particularly for species sensitive to land use changes and habitat loss (Doherty et al. 2020). It is crucial to expand landscape genomics studies to more lizard species to better understand their evolutionary potential and provide a more complete panorama, which would also contribute to strengthen management and conservation strategies on the face of the ongoing worldwide landscape modification.
