Powers of the arcsine and infinite classes of series involving central binomial coefficients Thanks: The first author was supported in part by the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada.
Karl Dilcher, Christophe Vignat

Abstract
. A general integral expression to transform power series is applied to arcsin ⁡ x \arcsin{x} and its positive integer powers. We concentrate on the first to the fourth powers and obtain infinite classes of new power series involving central binomial coefficients. Specializing the variable to appropriate simple values leads to different classes of series expansions for π \pi and some of its positive integer powers. We also discuss several limit expressions and connections with hypergeometric series.

Primary: 33E20; Secondary: 05A10, 33B10.

1.
Introduction

The well-known power series for
\arcsin{x}
and
(\arcsin{x})^{2}
,

(1.1)

\arcsin{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}}\frac{x^{2k+1}}{2k+1},\qquad\qquad(\arcsin{x})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2k}}{\binom{2k}{k}k^{2}},

have long been used as a source for series representations for
\pi
and

\pi^{2}
involving central binomial coefficients. This is achieved by
setting, for instance,
x=\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2},\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}
, 1, or other related algebraic numbers. These and
numerous further results can be found, for instance, in the much-cited and
interesting paper
[
7
]
by D. H. Lehmer. Other relevant publications include

[
2
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
12
]
.

It is the purpose of the present paper to use evaluations of certain integrals
(actually moments) of
\arcsin{x}
and
(\arcsin{x})^{2}
to obtain infinite
classes of extensions of the series in (
1.1
). This can also be done for
higher powers of
\arcsin{x}
, and we will carry this out for the third and
fourth powers. As a typical example we obtain, for instance, the series

(1.2)

\frac{2^{n+2}}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{4^{k}}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+n)}=\pi^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}\frac{4^{j}}{\binom{2j}{j}j^{2}},

valid for all even
n\geq 0
; see Corollary
5.3
. When
n=0
, this
reduces to the second identity in (
1.1
) for
x=1
. We will also obtain
various limit expressions, such as

(1.3)

\frac{2^{n+1}}{\binom{n}{n/2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{4^{k}}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+n)}\rightarrow\pi^{2}\quad\hbox{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty,

where
\binom{n}{n/2}
is considered a generalized binomial coefficient when

n
is odd. This follows from (
1.2
); see Corollary
8.1
.

In the short Section
2
we state and prove a general result which is
the basis for most of what follows. Sections
3
–
7
then
deal with results based on the first to the fourth powers, respectively, of
the
\arcsin{x}
. Section
8
contains some limit results, and we
conclude this paper with some results involving hypergeometric functions in
Section
9
.

2.
A general result

The main results in Sections
3
–
7
will all be based on the
following integral representation. We do not claim this to be original, but
we were unable to find it in the literature.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
f(x)
has the power series expansion

(2.1)

f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\cdot\frac{x^{k}}{k}\qquad(|x|<R),

where
R
is the radius of convergence. Then, for any integer
n\geq 0
and
real
x
with
|x|<R
, we have

(2.2)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\cdot\frac{x^{k+n}}{k+n}=f(x)x^{n}-n\int_{0}^{x}t^{n-1}f(t)dt.

Proof.

For
n=0
, the theorem is trivially true. For integers
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
,
we use integration by parts to get

\int_{0}^{x}\left(\frac{d}{dt}t^{n}\right)\frac{t^{k}}{k}dt=\left[\frac{t^{n+k}}{k}\right]_{0}^{x}-\int_{0}^{x}t^{n+k-1}dt,

which evaluates as

(2.3)

n\int_{0}^{x}t^{n-1}\frac{t^{k}}{k}dt=\frac{t^{k}}{k}x^{n}-\frac{t^{n+k}}{n+k}.

Multiplying both sides of (
2.3
) by
a_{k}
and summing over all
k\geq 1
,
then using (
2.1
), we get (
2.2
), as desired. Since we are dealing
with power series, the interchange of summation and integration is justified
within the radius of convergence.
∎

The fact that Theorem
2.1
requires
f(0)=0
is no loss of generality
since we can deal with
f(x)-f(0)
instead, if necessary.
Since most of the functions of interest to us are either even or odd, it is
convenient to reformulate Theorem
2.1
as follows.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose that
f(x)
is either an even or an odd function, analytic in a
neighbourhood of
0
, with power series expansion

(2.4)

f(x)=\sum_{k=1-\delta}^{\infty}c_{k}\cdot\frac{x^{2k+\delta}}{2k+\delta}\qquad(|x|<R),

where
\delta
is either
0
or
1
, and
R
is the radius of convergence.
Then, for any integer
n\geq 1
and real
x
with
|x|<R
, we have

(2.5)

\sum_{k=1-\delta}^{\infty}c_{k}\cdot\frac{x^{2k+\delta+n}}{2k+\delta+n}=f(x)x^{n}-n\int_{0}^{x}t^{n-1}f(t)dt.

Proof.

When
f
is even, then
a_{2k+1}=0
for all
k\geq 0
, and we set
c_{k}=a_{2k}

for
k\geq 1
and
c_{0}=0
. This is the case
\delta=0
. When
f
is odd, then

a_{2k}=0
for all
k\geq 0
, and we set
c_{k}=a_{2k+1}
, which is the case

\delta=1
.
∎

At this point, an important remark on the convergence of series is in order.
Using the well-known asymptotic relation

\binom{2k}{k}\sim\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}\quad\hbox{as}\quad k\to\infty

(see also (
8.10
) below), we see that the radius of convergence of both
series in (
1.1
) is
R=1
, while the series still converge at
x=1
.
By Abel’s limit theorem it is therefore legitimate to simply substitute
x=1

in the various series we deal with, to get identities that usually involve
\pi
.
This will be done throughout this paper, without further comments.

Binomial expressions are also often used in this paper, and the identities

(2.6)

\binom{n}{k}\frac{n+1}{k+1}=\binom{n+1}{k+1},\qquad\binom{2n-2}{n-1}=\frac{n}{2(2n-1)}\binom{2n}{n}

are particularly useful in the following sections.

3.
The case
f(x)=\arcsin{x}

In order to apply Corollary
2.2
, we need to evaluate

(3.1)

I_{\nu}^{(1)}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}t^{\nu}\arcsin{t}\,dt.

This evaluation is done in the following lemma and its proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

For integers
{\nu}\geq 0
we have

(3.2)

I_{\nu}^{(1)}(x)=\frac{1}{\nu+1}\left(f_{\nu}^{(1)}(x)\arcsin{x}+g_{\nu}^{(1)}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+h_{\nu}^{(1)}\right),

where for integers
\ell\geq 0
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle f_{2\ell}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{2\ell+1},\qquad f_{2\ell+1}^{(1)}(x)=x^{2\ell+2}-\frac{1}{4^{\ell+1}}\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1},

(3.4)

\displaystyle g_{2\ell}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\ell}}{(2\ell+1)\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{2j}{j}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2j},

(3.5)

\displaystyle g_{2\ell+1}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\cdot 4^{\ell+1}}\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)\binom{2j}{j}},

and
h_{\nu}^{(1)}=-g_{\nu}^{(1)}(0)
; in particular,

(3.6)

h_{2\ell}^{(1)}=-\frac{4^{\ell}}{(2\ell+1)\binom{2\ell}{\ell}},\qquad h_{2\ell+1}^{(1)}=0.

Proof.

The integral in (
3.1
) can be found as indefinite integrals in
[
9
]
,
separately for even and for odd
\nu
. In fact, if we set
a=1
in the
identities 1.7.3.2 and 1.7.3.3 in
[
9
]
, then we almost immediately get
(
3.2
)–(
3.5
). Since the two identities in question are indefinite
integrals, we need to take care of a constant of integration, say
c
, which
is then determined by the fact that
I_{\nu}^{(1)}(0)=0
. This means that

g_{\nu}^{(1)}(0)+c=0
, which in turn gives (
3.6
).
∎

With Lemma
3.1
we now obtain the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.2
.

For all even
n\geq 0
we have

(3.7)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{2k+n+1}\bigl(\frac{x}{2}\bigr)^{2k}=\frac{\binom{n}{n/2}}{(2x)^{n+1}}\left(2\arcsin{x}-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)\binom{2j}{j}}\right),

and for all odd
n\geq 1
,

(3.8)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{2k+n+1}\bigl(\frac{x}{2}\bigr)^{2k}=\frac{\bigl(\frac{2}{x}\bigr)^{n+1}}{(n+1)\binom{n+1}{\frac{n+1}{2}}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\binom{2j}{j}\bigl(\frac{x}{2}\bigr)^{2j}\right).

Proof.

We use Corollary
2.2
with
f(x)=\arcsin{x}
. Then by (
1.1
) we
have
\delta=1
and
c_{k}=\binom{2k}{k}4^{-k}
. When
n=0
, then (
3.7
) is
just the first identity in (
1.1
). When
n\geq 2
is even, we set

n=2\ell+2
, so that
\nu=2\ell+1
. Then (
2.5
) combined with
Lemma
3.1
almost immediately gives (
3.7
), where only
(
2.6
) also needs to be used.

Similarly, when
n\geq 1
is odd, we set
n=2\ell+1
, so that we take

\nu=2\ell
in Lemma
3.1
. Again, upon using (
2.6
), the identity
(
3.8
) follows immediately from (
2.5
), which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

For all integers
n\geq 0
we have

(3.9)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}(2k+n+1)}=\begin{cases}\binom{n}{n/2}\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1}}&\hbox{if $n$ is even},\\
\frac{2^{n-1}}{n\binom{n-1}{(n-1)/2}}&\hbox{if $n$ is odd}.\end{cases}

More concisely, for all
n\geq 0
we have

(3.10)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{4^{k}(2k+n+1)}=\binom{n}{n/2}\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1}},

where
\binom{n}{n/2}
is considered a generalized binomial coefficient in
the case of odd
n
.

Proof of Corollary
3.3
.

(
3.9
) follows immediately from Theorem
3.2
with
x=1
and the
fact that
\arcsin(1)=\pi/2
. It remains to prove (
3.10
) for odd

n=2\ell+1
. To do so, we write the relevant binomial
coefficients in terms of the gamma function:

(3.11)

\binom{n}{n/2}=\frac{\Gamma(2\ell+2)}{\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})^{2}},\qquad\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}=\frac{\Gamma(2\ell+1)}{\Gamma(\ell+1)^{2}}.

We now use Legendre’s duplication formula twice (see, e.g.,

[
8
, eq. 5.5.5]
):

\displaystyle\Gamma(2\ell+1)\sqrt{\pi}

\displaystyle=2^{2\ell}\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+1),

\displaystyle\Gamma(2\ell+2)\sqrt{\pi}

\displaystyle=2^{2\ell+1}\Gamma(\ell+1)\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2}).

Taking the product of these two identities and using the fact that

(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2})=\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})
,
we get

\Gamma(2\ell+1)\Gamma(2\ell+2)\pi=\frac{2^{4\ell+2}}{2\ell+1}\Gamma(\ell+1)^{2}\Gamma(\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})^{2}.

Combining this last identity with both identities in (
3.11
), we see that
the second part of (
3.9
) agrees with (
3.10
) for odd
n
, which
completes the proof.
∎

The next obvious special case of Theorem
3.2
, after
x=1
, would be

x=1/2
with
\arcsin{x}=\pi/6
. Rather than stating an analogue of
Corollary
3.3
, we give the first few explicit cases of (
3.7
)
for even
n=0,2,\ldots,10
.

Corollary 3.4
.

The following identities hold:

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{16^{k}(2k+1)}=\frac{\pi}{3}}
,

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{16^{k}(2k+7)}=\frac{20\pi}{3}-12\sqrt{3}}
,

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{16^{k}(2k+3)}=\frac{2\pi}{3}-\sqrt{3}}
,

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{16^{k}(2k+9)}=\frac{70\pi}{3}-\frac{169\sqrt{3}}{4}}
,

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{16^{k}(2k+5)}=2\pi-\frac{7\sqrt{3}}{2}}
,

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{16^{k}(2k+11)}=84\pi-\frac{1523\sqrt{3}}{10}}
.

The first one of these identities, at least, is well known and can be found,
for instance, in
[
7
, p. 451]
.

Another consequence of Theorem
3.2
is a pair of identities connecting
different power series.

Corollary 3.5
.

For all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
|x|<1
and integers
n\geq 0
we have

(3.12)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}x^{2k+2n+1}}{4^{k}(2k+2n+1)}=\frac{\binom{2n}{n}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{2^{2n+1}}\cdot\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{\binom{2j}{j}(2j+1)},

and for
n\geq 1
,

(3.13)

2n\binom{2n}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{2k+2n+1}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2k+2n+1}=\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\binom{2j}{j}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2j}.

Proof.

With the aim of dealing with the right-most sum in (
3.7
), we differentiate
the second identity in (
1.1
), obtaining

(3.14)

\frac{\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2k-1}}{\binom{2k}{k}k}.

This identity is also mentioned in
[
7
]
, along with a new proof. Using the
second identity in (
2.6
) and setting
k=j+1
in (
3.14
),
we get

(3.15)

\frac{2\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{\binom{2j}{j}(2j+1)}.

With this identity, the expression in large parentheses in (
3.7
) becomes

\displaystyle 2\arcsin{x}-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}-\sum_{j=n/2}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{\binom{2j}{j}(2j+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sum_{j=n/2}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{\binom{2j}{j}(2j+1)}.

Substituting this into (
3.7
), multiplying both sides by
x^{n+1}
, and
finally replacing the (even)
n
by
2n
, we get (
3.12
).

To prove (
3.13
), we differentiate the left identity in (
1.1
),
obtaining

(3.16)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{2j}{j}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2j}.

This is also a well-known identity which was used by Lehmer
[
7
]
. With
(
3.16
), the expression in large parentheses in (
3.8
) becomes

1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}-\sum_{j=\frac{n+1}{2}}^{\infty}\binom{2j}{j}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2j}\right)=\sum_{j=\frac{n+1}{2}}^{\infty}\binom{2j}{j}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2j}.

Substituting this into (
3.8
) and replacing the (odd)
n
by
2n-1
for

n\geq 1
, we immediately get (
3.13
).
∎

4.
A differential operator

Before continuing with higher powers of
\arcsin{x}
, we derive some results
concerning a differential operator, which will be useful in the following
sections. To see how this enters, we differentiate the right-hand sides of
(
3.1
) and (
3.2
), obtaining

(4.1)

\displaystyle({\nu}+1)x^{\nu}\arcsin{x}

\displaystyle=f_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)\arcsin{x}+\frac{f_{\nu}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad+g_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}-\frac{xg_{\nu}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

where for greater ease of notation we deleted the superscripts. For (
4.1
)
to hold for all
x
, we require both

(4.2)

f_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)=(\nu+1)x^{\nu}

and

(4.3)

xg_{\nu}(x)+(x^{2}-1)g_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)=f_{\nu}(x).

This last identity gives rise to the following definition: For a function

F(x)
, differentiable in a neighbourhood of 0, we define the operator

\mathcal{D}
by

(4.4)

\big(\mathcal{D}F\big)(x):=xF(x)+(x^{2}-1)\frac{d}{dx}F(x).

The following properties, with
c
an arbitrary constant, are easy to verify:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{D}c\big)(x)

\displaystyle=cx,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{D}\frac{c}{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\big)(x)

\displaystyle=0,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{D}\frac{\log(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1})}{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\big)(x)

\displaystyle=1.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is a linear differential operator of order 1, the identity
(
4.6
) guarantees that solutions to
\big(\mathcal{D}F\big)(x)=f(x)
,
for an appropriate given function
f
(here we will only be interested in
polynomials) are unique up to adding
c/\sqrt{x^{2}-1}
. We can therefore
consider the inverse operator
\mathcal{D}^{-1}
. It satisfies the
following property, which will be useful in later sections.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all integers
\ell\geq 1
we have, up to addition by
c/\sqrt{x^{2}-1}
on
the right,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{D}^{-1}x^{2\ell+1}\big)(x)

\displaystyle=g_{2\ell}^{(1)}(x),

(4.9)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{D}^{-1}x^{2\ell+2}\big)(x)

\displaystyle=g_{2\ell+1}^{(1)}(x)+\frac{1}{4^{\ell+1}}\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}\big(\mathcal{D}^{-1}1\big)(x),

where
g_{2\ell}^{(1)}(x)
and
g_{2\ell+1}^{(1)}(x)
are as given in
(
3.4
) and (
3.5
).

Proof.

This follows immediately from (
4.3
) and (
3.3
), along with the
linearity of the inverse operator
\mathcal{D}^{-1}
.
∎

5.
The case
f(x)=(\arcsin{x})^{2}

In analogy to Section
3
, we need to evaluate

(5.1)

I_{\nu}^{(2)}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}t^{\nu}(\arcsin{t})^{2}\,dt.

This is done as follows.

Lemma 5.1
.

For integers
{\nu}\geq 0
we have

(5.2)

I_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=\frac{1}{{\nu}+1}\left(f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)(\arcsin{x})^{2}+g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\arcsin{x}+h_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)\right),

where

(5.3)

f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=f_{\nu}^{(1)}(x),\qquad g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=2g_{\nu}^{(1)}(x),

and for integers
\ell\geq 0
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle h_{2\ell}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4^{\ell+1}}{(2\ell+1)\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{2j}{j}\frac{\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2j+1}}{2j+1},

(5.5)

\displaystyle h_{2\ell+1}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\cdot 4^{\ell+1}}\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+1}\frac{(2x)^{2j}}{\binom{2j}{j}j^{2}}.

Proof.

Computations led us to conjecture that
I_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)
is of the form
(
5.2
), where
f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)
,
g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)
, and

h_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)
are
polynomials with rational coefficients. We first consider these polynomials
as unknowns, and differentiating (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), we get

(5.6)

\displaystyle({\nu}+1)x^{\nu}(\arcsin{x})^{2}=\frac{d}{dx}f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)(\arcsin{x})^{2}+2f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)\frac{\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad+g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)\left(1-x\frac{\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\right)+\frac{d}{dx}g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)(1-x^{2})\frac{\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}+\frac{d}{dx}h_{\nu}^{(2)}(x).

We now equate coefficients of the transcendental functions
\arcsin{x}
and

(\arcsin{x})^{2}
, as well as the remaining terms, and we obtain

(5.7)

2f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)-xg_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)+(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=0,

as well as

(5.8)

\frac{d}{dx}f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=({\nu}+1)x^{\nu}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{d}{dx}h_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=-g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x).

From the first identity in (
5.8
), we have

(5.9)

f_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=x^{{\nu}+1}+c_{\nu},

where
c_{\nu}
are constants. Now by (
5.7
), (
5.9
) and
Lemma
4.1
, we have

(5.10)

\frac{1}{2}\cdot g_{\nu}^{(2)}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{-1}(x^{\nu+1}+c_{\nu})=g_{\nu}^{(1)}(x),

and in addition, by (
4.7
) we get
c_{2\ell}=0
and

c_{2\ell+1}=-\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}4^{-\ell-1}
. This is the first part of
(
5.3
), while (
5.10
) gives the second part of (
5.3
).

Finally, the second half of (
5.8
) immediately leads to (
5.4
) and
(
5.5
). In all cases the constants of integration are 0, which can be
seen by equating (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) and setting
x=0
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We now combine Lemma
5.1
with Corollary
2.2
, to obtain
the following result.

Theorem 5.2
.

For all even
n\geq 0
we have

(5.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2k}}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{n}{n/2}}{(2x)^{n}}\bigg(\arcsin^{2}{x}

Proof.

In analogy to Theorem

6.
The case
f(x)=(\arcsin{x})^{3}

In addition to the identities in (
1.1
), there are known power series
expansions for arbitrary positive integer powers of
\arcsin{x}
. Such
expressions can be found in the book by Schwatt
[
11
, p. 124]
or in the
more recent paper
[
1
]
. In general, these identities involve multiple
nested series, but in the case of powers 3 and 4, they can be written in terms
of fairly simple (generalized) harmonic numbers. We begin with the third power,
using a variant of the notation from
[
1
]
:

(6.1)

(\arcsin{x})^{3}=6\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}G(k)}{4^{k}(2k+1)}x^{2k+1},\qquad G(k):=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}.

Following the outlines of the previous two sections, we now set

(6.2)

I_{\nu}^{(3)}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}t^{\nu}(\arcsin{t})^{3}\,dt.

We then have

Lemma 6.1
.

For integers
{\nu}\geq 0
we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle I_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)=\frac{1}{{\nu}+1}\bigg(

\displaystyle f_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)(\arcsin{x})^{3}+g_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}(\arcsin{x})^{2}

\displaystyle+h_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)\arcsin{x}+u_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+w_{\nu}^{(3)}\bigg),

where

(6.4)

f_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)=f_{\nu}^{(1)}(x),\qquad g_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)=3g_{\nu}^{(1)}(x),

and for integers
\ell\geq 0
,

(6.5)

\displaystyle h_{2\ell}^{(3)}(x)

\displaystyle=3h_{2\ell}^{(2)}(x),\quad h_{2\ell+1}^{(3)}(x)=3h_{2\ell+1}^{(2)}(x)+\frac{3\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}}{2\cdot 4^{\ell+1}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+1}\frac{1}{j^{2}},

(6.6)

\displaystyle u_{2\ell}^{(3)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{-6\cdot 4^{\ell}}{(2\ell+1)\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}\bigg(\sum_{j=r}^{\ell}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\bigg)\binom{2r}{r}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2r},

(6.7)

\displaystyle u_{2\ell+1}^{(3)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{-3\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}}{4^{\ell+2}}\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}\bigg(\sum_{j=r+1}^{\ell+1}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\bigg)\frac{(2x)^{2r+1}}{(2r+1)\binom{2r}{r}},

(6.8)

\displaystyle w_{2\ell}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{6\cdot 4^{\ell}}{(2\ell+1)\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}},\quad w_{2\ell+1}^{(3)}=0.

Proof.

In analogy to Lemma
5.1
, we differentiate (
6.2
) and (
6.3
),
and after some easy manipulations we get

\displaystyle({\nu}+1)x^{\nu}

\displaystyle(\arcsin{x})^{3}=\frac{d}{dx}f_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)(\arcsin{x})^{3}+\bigg(3f_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)+(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}g_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)

\displaystyle-xg_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)\bigg)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}(\arcsin{x})^{2}+\bigg(2g_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)+\frac{d}{dx}h_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)\bigg)\arcsin{x}

\displaystyle+\bigg(h_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)+(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}u_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)-xu_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)\bigg)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.

This gives

(6.9)

3f_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)-xg_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)+(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}g_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)=0,

(6.10)

\frac{d}{dx}f_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)=({\nu}+1)x^{\nu}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{d}{dx}h_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)=-2g_{\nu}^{(3)}(x),

(6.11)

h_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)-xu_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)+(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}u_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)=0.

Now (

so that, for each
\nu\geq 0

where

.

By (

) we have

u_{\nu}^{(3)}(x)={\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\big(h_{\nu}^{(3)}\big)(x)

\nu

\nu
is even,
\nu=2\ell

(
6.12
) and (

(6.13)

u_{2\ell}^{(3)}(x)=\frac{-3\cdot 4^{\ell+1}}{(2\ell+1)\binom{2\ell}{\ell}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{\binom{2j}{j}{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(x^{2j+1})}{(2j+1)2^{2j+1}}+{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(c_{2\ell})(x).

Using (

When
\nu

(6.15)

u_{2\ell+1}^{(3)}(x)=\frac{-3\binom{2\ell+2}{\ell+1}}{2\cdot 4^{\ell+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{4^{j+1}{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(x^{2j+2})}{\binom{2j+2}{j+1}(j+1)^{2}}+{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}(c_{2\ell+1})(x).

4.9

) and (

) and

∎

In analogy to Theorems
3.2
and

, we obtain the following

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\frac{2}{3}\arcsin^{3}{x}-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\cdot\arcsin^{2}{x}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{\binom{2j}{j}(2j+1)}

\displaystyle+\arcsin{x}\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\bigg(\frac{(2x)^{2j}}{\binom{2j}{j}}-1\bigg)\frac{1}{j^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{2}\sum_{r=0}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\bigg(\sum_{j=r+1}^{n/2}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\bigg)\frac{(2x)^{2r+1}}{\binom{2r}{r}(2r+1)}\bigg),

,

\displaystyle\times\bigg(-\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\cdot\arcsin^{2}{x}}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\binom{2j}{j}\bigl(\frac{x}{2}\bigr)^{2j}+2\arcsin{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\binom{2j}{j}}{2j+1}\bigl(\frac{x}{2}\bigr)^{2j+1}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sum_{r=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\bigg(\sum_{j=r}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\bigg)\binom{2r}{r}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2r}-\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\bigg).

With the goal of applying Corollary

to the series (

),

Now we use Lemma
6.1
, separately for even

and odd

. Some straightforward manipulations then give (

)

\displaystyle+\arcsin^{2}{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\binom{2j}{j}\bigl(\frac{x}{2}\bigr)^{2j+1}}{2j+1}+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\arcsin{x}\sum_{r=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\bigg(\sum_{j=r}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\bigg)\binom{2r}{r}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2r}

We saw that the limits in Corollary

all come from the special case

in relevant earlier results. The following limit expressions are of a
different kind and are only valid for

.

Corollary 8.2
.

We have the following limits as
n\rightarrow\infty
:

(8.8)

\displaystyle(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{2k+2n+1}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2k}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

(8.9)

\displaystyle(2n+1)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{(2x)^{2k-1}}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+2n)}\rightarrow\frac{\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

uniformly on compact subsets of the interval
(-1,1)
.

Proof.

While originally these two limits were obtained from (
3.12
) and
(
5.17
), respectively, we present the following simpler proofs. We rewrite
the series in (
8.8
) as

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{\frac{2k}{2n+1}+1}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(x).

The well-known asymptotics for the central binomial coefficients imply, for

k\geq 1
,

(8.10)

\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}\cdot\frac{7}{8}\leq\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}\bigl(1-\frac{1}{8k}\bigr)<\binom{2k}{k}<\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi k}},

and with the right-most inequality we get

\left|a_{k}(x)\right|\leq\binom{2k}{k}\left|\frac{x}{2}\right|^{2k}<\frac{|x|^{2k}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}=:M_{k}.

Since
\sum M_{k}
converges for all
|x|<1
, we can use Tannery’s theorem for
the interchange of limit and infinite series; see, e.g.,
[
3
, p. 136]
.
Hence we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(x)\bigg)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{k}(x)\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{2k}{k}\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)^{2k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

where we have used (
3.16
) for the final equality.

Similarly, we rewrite the series in (
8.9
) as

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2k-1}}{\binom{2k}{k}k\big(\frac{2k-1}{2n+1}+1\big)}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}(x).

With the left inequalities of (
8.10
) we have

\left|b_{k}(x)\right|\leq\frac{8\sqrt{\pi k}}{7\cdot 4^{k}}\cdot\frac{2^{2k-1}}{k}|x|^{2k-1}=\frac{4}{7}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{k}}\cdot|x|^{2k-1}=:N_{k}(x),

and since
\sum N_{k}(x)
converges for
|x|<1
, we can use Tannery’s theorem
again. We then get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}(x)\bigg)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2k-1}}{\binom{2k}{k}k}=\frac{\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

where we have used (
3.14
).
Since in both cases we are dealing with power series, convergence is uniform on
compact subsets of the interval
(-1,1)
.
∎

9.
Connections with hypergeometric functions

In this section we mention some connections with hypergeometric series
(or functions), which are among the most important objects in the theory of
special functions and their applications. We recall that
generalized
hypergeometric functions
are defined by power series of the form

(9.1)

_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{k}\cdots(a_{p})_{k}}{(b_{1})_{k}\cdots(b_{q})_{k}}\cdot\frac{z^{k}}{k!},

with the Pochhammer symbol (or rising factorial) defined by
(a)_{0}=1
and

(a)_{k}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1),\qquad k\geq 1.

The most important special case is
{}_{2}F_{1}(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1};z)
, the
Gaussian
hypergeometric function
. Numerous properties can be found, e.g., in Chapters 15
and 16 of
[
8
]
. We are now ready to state and prove the following.

Corollary 9.1
.

For any integer
n\geq 0
, the identity (
3.7
) is equivalent to

(9.2)

_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},n+\tfrac{1}{2};n+\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\cdot{{}_{2}F_{1}(1,n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})}.

Proof.

We replace
n
by
2n
in (
3.7
) and note that Mathematica evaluates the
left-hand sum as

(9.3)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{2k+2n+1}\bigl(\frac{x}{2}\bigr)^{2k}=\frac{1}{2n+1}{{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},n+\tfrac{1}{2};n+\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})}.

For the sum on the right of (
3.7
), Mathematica gives

(9.4)

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{(2x)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)\binom{2j}{j}}=2\cdot\frac{\arcsin{x}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}-\frac{(2x)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)\binom{2n}{n}}{{}_{2}F_{1}(1,n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})},

where, by (
3.15
), the sum on the left is a partial sum of the whole series
which has
2\arcsin{x}/\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
as its generating function. Combining
(
9.3
) and (
9.4
) with (
3.7
), we get (
9.2
) after some
simplification.
∎

We remark that the identity (
9.2
) can also be obtained from the existing
literature. Indeed, from
[
10
, eq. 7.3.1.32]
we have

{}_{2}F_{1}(1,n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{{}_{2}F_{1}(1,2n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2};\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}})},

and from
[
10
, eq. 7.3.1.37]
,

{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},n+\tfrac{1}{2};n+\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})={{}_{2}F_{1}(1,2n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2};\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}})},

which combined immediately give (
9.2
). This approach can therefore be seen
as an alternative proof of the identity (
3.7
).

We now apply a similar approach to the identity (
5.11
) in
Theorem
5.2
.

Corollary 9.2
.

For any integer
n\geq 1
, the identity (
5.11
) is equivalent to

(9.5)

\displaystyle(2n-1)\,

\displaystyle{{}_{3}F_{2}(1,1,n;\tfrac{3}{2},n+1;x)}+\,{{}_{3}F_{2}(1,n,n;n+\tfrac{1}{2},n+1;x)}

\displaystyle=2n\,{{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},1;\tfrac{3}{2};\tfrac{x}{x-1})}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}(1,n;n+\tfrac{1}{2};x)}.

Proof.

We replace
n
by
2n
in (
5.11
). Then Mathematica identifies the
left-hand series as

(9.6)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{2k}}{\binom{2k}{k}k(2k+2n)}=\frac{x^{2}}{n+1}{{}_{3}F_{2}(1,1,n+1;\tfrac{3}{2},n+2;x^{2})},

and the right-most sum as

(9.7)

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(2x)^{2j}}{\binom{2j}{j}j^{2}}=\arcsin^{2}{x}-\frac{(2x)^{2n+2}}{2(n+1)^{2}\binom{2n+2}{n+1}}\,{{}_{3}F_{2}(1,n+1,n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2},n+2;x^{2})}.

Substituting (
9.6
), (
9.7
), and (
9.4
) into the modified
identity (
5.11
), we get after some simplification,

(9.8)

\displaystyle{{}_{3}F_{2}(1,1,n+1;\tfrac{3}{2},n+2;x^{2})}+\frac{1}{2n+1}\,{{}_{3}F_{2}(1,n+1,n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2},n+2;x^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2n+2}{2n+1}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}(1,n+1;n+\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})},

where we have used the well-known identity

\arcsin{x}=x\,{{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})}.

We now use another well-known identity, namely (see, e.g.,

[
8
, eq. 15.8.1]
)

{{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};\tfrac{3}{2};x^{2})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},1;\tfrac{3}{2};\tfrac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-1})},

and finally multiply both sides of (
9.8
) by
2n+1
and then replace

x^{2}
by
x
and
n+1
by

. This immediately gives (
9.5
).
∎

We were unable to find (

) in the literature. It is a somewhat unusual
identity in that it expresses the product of two
{}_{2}F_{1}
functions as a linear
combination of two
{}_{3}F_{2}
functions.
