Dorsal root ganglia CSF1 + neuronal subtypes have different impact on macrophages and microglia after spared nerve injury
Authors: Andreea Violeta Grosu, Roxana‐Olimpia Gheorghe, Alexandru Filippi, Alexandru Florian Deftu, Manon Isler, Marc Suter, Violeta Ristoiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsColony‐stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) is a growth factor secreted by dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons important for DRG macrophages and spinal cord (SC) microglia injury‐induced proliferation and activation, specifically released after spared nerve injury (SNI). In this study, we investigated if SNI‐induced CSF1 expression and perineuronal rings of macrophages around mouse DRG neurons vary between L3‐L5 DRG and with the neuronal type, and if the CSF1+ neuronal projections at the SC dorsal horns were associated with an increased microglial number in the corresponding laminae.MethodsSeven days after surgery, L3‐L5 DRG as well as their corresponding segments at the SC level were collected, frozen, and cut. DRG sections were double‐immunostained using antibodies against CSF1 and NF200, CGRP or IB4, while SC sections were immunostained using a fluorescent Nissl Stain and analyzed for CX3CR1‐GFP microglia number and distribution by an in‐house ImageJ Plug‐in.ResultsOur results showed that SNI‐induced CSF1 expression was common for all subtypes of mouse DRG neurons, being responsible for attracting more resident macrophages around them in a DRG‐dependent manner, with L4 showing the stronger response and CSF1+/NF200+ neurons showing the highest incidence. Even though the total number of microglia in the SC ipsilateral dorsal horns increased after SNI, the increase at their specific laminar projection sites did not mirror the incidence of DRG neuronal subtypes among CSF1+ neurons.InterpretationTaken together, these results contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the connection between CSF1 and macrophage/microglia response after SNI and emphasize the importance of considering L3‐L5 DRG individually when investigating SNI‐neuropathic pain pathogenesis in mice.

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral traumatic neuropathic pain, which manifests as a persistent chronic pain that can significantly impact a person's quality of life, is the result of neuronal dysfunction 1 , 2 and numerous neuro‐immune interactions 3 , 4 at both peripheral (PNS) and central nervous system (CNS) levels. Macrophages and microglia, which are the first line of defense in PNS 5 and CNS, respectively, 6 initiate complex molecular and cellular interactions with neurons in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) 7 , 8 , 9 and spinal cord (SC) dorsal horns 10 , 11 that contribute to the initiation and persistence of nerve injury‐induced neuropathic pain.

Colony‐stimulating factor 1 (CSF1), also known as macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF), is a growth factor secreted by DRG neurons important for both DRG macrophages and SC microglia injury‐induced proliferation and activation.

At the DRG level, CSF1, whose mRNA is expressed in neurons as soon as 1–2 days after spared nerve injury (SNI), 12 , 13 was shown to be important in attracting and maintaining perineuronal ring‐like clusters of resident macrophages, 9 , 14 since conditional deletion of Csf1 gene at PO4 after SNI 9 and inhibition of CSF1R 15 abrogated macrophages proliferation. Nevertheless, the ring‐like clusters of resident macrophages around DRG neurons are not limited to SNI‐neuropathic pain model, but they were also described 5 days after SNL, 16 7 days after sciatic nerve transection, 17 and 7 days after sciatic nerve ligation and transection. 18 However, CSF1 expression was not confirmed in these models, but it was confirmed at both gene and protein level 7 days after CCI in rat DRG neurons, 19 and at gene level in trigeminal ganglia 21 days after infraorbital nerve CCI. 20 The fact that the CCI‐induced CSF1 increase in rat DRG neurons was transient compared with SNI when measured at 42 days post surgery, 19 combined with the observation that CSF1 intrathecal delivery restored mechanical hypersensitivity completely suppressed by Csf1 gene deletion in DRG neurons, 12 suggests that CSF1 has an injury‐dependent release mechanism and that it is required and sufficient to induce neuropathic pain behavior.

Apparently, the expression of CSF1 varies between DRG neurons, with ~40% of small, ~43% of medium, and ~60% of large L4/L5 rat DRG neurons expressing CSF1 mRNA 2 days after SNI. 13 Surprisingly, the expression of CSF1 wasn't correlated with corresponding rings of macrophages around the expressing DRG neurons, since 7 days after SNI perineuronal rings of macrophages were preferentially described only around large NF200+ DRG neurons, while the smaller C‐neurons were relatively free of macrophages in L4/L5 rat DRG. 14 The fact that NF200+ DRG neurons correspond to Aβ and Aδ low threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMR) 21 may explain why mice in which Csf1 gene was deleted from sensory neurons did not develop mechanical hypersensitivity after SNI, 12 but it does not explain why small non‐peptidergic IB4+ C‐neurons and small peptidergic CGRP+ C‐neurons which also express CSF1 mRNA were described in Vega‐Avelaira's study as relatively free of macrophages after SNI. A more thorough investigation of the incidence of perineuronal rings of macrophages around C‐neurons subpopulations could provide more insight into these aspects.

CSF1 does not act only locally, at the DRG level, it also acts as secondary mediator between DRG and SC dorsal horn neurons. Studies have shown that 4 days after SNI, sensory neurons‐derived CSF1 is transported to the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horns where, upon release, it induces microglia expansion/proliferation through self‐renewal and not by monocytes infiltration, as well as their activation confirmed by increased expression of Iba1 and p‐p38. 12 , 13 After SNI, the majority of microglia were distributed within the territory occupied by peripherally axotomized primary afferents, although some spread was also seen into regions occupied by intact, ‘spared’ central projections of the sural nerve. 22 , 23 The central axon terminals of DRG neurons terminate in the dorsal horn of the SC, organized in layers of laminae (Rexed laminae) extending from the superficial to the deep dorsal horn, as follows: peptidergic C‐CGRP+ and Aδ‐CGRP+ nociceptors in Lamina I and outer Lamina II, non‐peptidergic C‐IB4+ nociceptors and C‐LTMR in Lamina II, Aδ‐LTMR in Laminae II‐III, and Aβ‐LTMR in Laminae III‐V. 21 , 24 , 25 One possible explanation for the high selectivity of the CSF1 for mechanical hypersensitivity could be that microglia at the projection sites of DRG central terminals are differently activated.

To complete the data about the role of CSF1+ DRG neurons in SNI‐induced neuropathic pain, in this study, we investigated how CSF1 expression was distributed between different subpopulations of DRG neurons (i.e. NF200+, CGRP+, and IB4+) in L3‐L5 DRG, 7 days after SNI lesion, and whether its expression level was associated with a higher incidence of ring‐like clusters of resident macrophages around them and correspondingly, with an increased microglial response at their SC projection site. Even though L3 DRG is important for SNI pathogenesis in mice since the injured fibers of common peroneal and tibial nerves predominantly originate in the L3 and L4 DRG, 26 the L3 DRG CSF1 expression was not investigated so far. A detailed analysis of the degree of microglial response after SNI per laminae, in the SC, was not previously performed either.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

A total of 39 adult (8–12 weeks) male and female CX3CR‐1GFP (B6.129P2(Cg)‐Cx3cr1tm1Litt/J) transgenic mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, Maine, USA) were randomly assigned to one of the following groups: (1) Control ‐ no interventions, (2) Sham ‐ nerve exposed and manipulated, and (3) Spared Nerve Injury (SNI) ‐ test group. The animals were housed in cages with ad libitum access to food and water in a climate‐controlled room, at a room temperature of 22 ± 0.5°C and a 12 h light/dark cycle.

Spared nerve injury surgery

All experimental procedures and protocols were carried out in accordance with the Directive 2010/63/EU and with the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Committee on Animal Experimentation of the Canton de Vaud, Switzerland, in accordance with the Swiss Federal Laws on Animal Welfare. Briefly, mice were placed under deep anesthesia using isoflurane (Piramal, Mumbai, Maharashtra, India) prior to the exposure of the sciatic nerve at thigh level. For Sham surgery, a piece of 6‐0 silk suture was placed under the common peroneal and tibial branches and left there for 7 days, while for SNI‐operated animals the two branches were ligated and cut distal to the ligature, followed by removal of a small portion of the nerve to prevent regeneration, as previously described. 27 All surgical procedures were performed on the left side of deeply anesthetized mice, and no surgery was performed on the right side.

DRG and SC dissection and tissue processing

Seven days after surgery, mice were injected with pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and perfused through the ascending aorta with ice‐cold 1X phosphate buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), before L3, L4, and L5 DRG, as well as their corresponding segments at the SC level, were collected. The tissue was additionally postfixed in 4% PFA for 1 h and 30 min for DRG, and for 3 h for SC, and then immersed in 20% sucrose overnight. For immunohistochemistry, DRG and SC segments were embedded in Tissue‐Tek® O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek, Alphen aan den Rijn, Netherlands) and frozen‐cut on a cryostat (Leica CM3050S, Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA) into 12 and 20 μm slices, respectively, which were placed on Superfrost™ Plus Adhesion Microscope Slides (J1800AMNZ; Epredia, Kalamazoo, Michigan, USA) and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

DRG sections were washed with 1X PBS and incubated with blocking solution (10% donkey serum in 1X PBS + 0.3% Triton X) for 1 h at room temperature. For all IB4 stainings and L5 DRG sections, prior steps of permeabilization and antigen retrieval were required before the blocking step. In this case, sections were first incubated with 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h and 45 min, followed by 3 cycles of 3 min‐incubations with Tris‐EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris Base, 1 mM EDTA solution, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 9.0) in the microwave (800 W). The blocking step was followed by a 3‐h incubation at room temperature for all IB4 and L5 DRG stainings, or an overnight incubation at 4°C for the rest of L3 and L4 DRG stainings with antibodies against: NF200 (1:500, rabbit, N4142, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), CGRP (1:1000, rabbit, T4032, BMA Biomedicals, Augst, Switzerland), CSF1 (1:300, goat, AF416, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and biotinylated isolectin B4 (IB4) (1:100, B‐1205, Vector laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) in 5% donkey serum in 1X PBS + 0.1% Triton X. After washing the primary antibodies with 1X PBS, DRG sections were incubated for 1 h and 30 min at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: donkey anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 350 (1:1000, A10039, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), donkey anti‐goat Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1500, A11058, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and Streptavidin ‐ Alexa Fluor 350 (1:400, S11249, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After three washes with 1X PBS, sections were cover‐slipped with Prolong Gold antifade reagent (P36930, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or FluorSave Reagent (Calbiochem, La Jolla, CA, USA) and left to cure overnight at room temperature in the dark.

Spinal cord sections were rehydrated in 1X PBS for 40 min and washed with 1X PBS + 0.1% Triton X for 10 min before being incubated with Neurotrace 435/455 Blue Fluorescent Nissl Stain (1:50, N21479, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. After two washes with 1X PBS + 0.1% Triton X and four washes with 1X PBS, sections were cover‐slipped with Prolong Gold antifade reagent (P36930, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and left to cure in the dark for at least 40 min before images were acquired.

Fluorescence microscopy

Images were captured using either Olympus IX73 fluorescent microscope, equipped with a Hamamatsu ORCA‐03G camera and processed with CellSens Dimension 1.11 software (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), or with Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 slide scanner using Zeiss ZEN 3.3 Blue software (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For DRG, images were acquired at 20× magnification, while for SC sections, images were acquired at 10× magnification for quantifying the number of microglia per lamina, and at 20× magnification for the reconstruction of the ipsilateral half of the SC.

Fluorescence microscopy on DRG sections

Every fourth section was analyzed, with a minimum of 3–4 sections/DRG and 3–4 animals/condition. DRG images were manually analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.53e, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), as follows: (1) to quantify the expression of CSF1 and the macrophage accumulation in the DRG, we counted the number of CSF1+ neurons and CX3CR1+ macrophages; (2) to quantify what type of neurons are the ones that express CSF1, the number of CSF1+ neurons that co‐express NF200, CGRP, or IB4 was calculated; and (3) to quantify the percentage of CSF1+ neurons that have perineuronal rings of macrophages, the number of CSF1+ neurons that have at least 50% of their perimeter surrounded by CX3CR1+ cells was determined (Figure S1). For CX3CR1+ macrophage quantification, images were processed by converting them to 8‐bit, subtracting the background and adjusting the threshold, in order to effectively enhance the contrast and delineate the cells from the background. To ensure accurate quantification, an additional step that involved applying a size threshold for the quantified particles was performed, to exclude the possibility of counting small artifacts. Next, the Analyze particle tool was used, precisely counting the number of macrophages within the images based on the defined parameters.

Fluorescence microscopy on SC sections

For the SC, 3 animals/condition and 13–20 sections/animal were analyzed, which were chosen at least 200 μm apart. To count microglia in different Rexed laminae (RL) of the SC, a Plug‐in in ImageJ 1.37v (NIH, USA) was developed (Figure 1). To gain positional information, the algorithm assumed the same orientation of all microscopy images, with the dorsal horns to the right. Both fluorescence images (Nissl and CX3CR1 staining) were preprocessed by converting them to 8‐bit images, subtracting the background and enhancing the contrast. (1) The gray matter area was identified based on the higher number of cells and higher interconnectivity of neurons in this region compared with those in the white matter (as reflected in higher Nissl staining), by using a Gaussian blur filter followed by the application of an intensity threshold. The boundary was further refined by filling holes inside the determined region and by a series of 2 dilation‐erosion cycles. (2) Next, the identified gray matter region was scanned in a medial to lateral direction, identifying the position of the isthmus, the longest anteroposterior span (anteroposterior diameter) and the lateral hemi‐diameter. The longest anteroposterior span was also scanned to identify the dorsal‐most and anterior‐most coordinates of the white matter in the image. (3) After splitting adjacent neuron images by a watershed algorithm, the size of all neuronal bodies was determined, and neurons were further divided in small/medium/large by applying size thresholds. Next, the local density was computed based on the centers of mass for all neurons, regardless of size. (4) The neuronal size and density data were used to construct a Voronoi diagram with neuronal bodies as centers for the Voronoi cells and the cells color‐coded depending both on neuronal size and local density of the cells. (5) From this color‐coded map, substantia gelatinosa was identified by color thresholds as a region with high density of small neurons and was further used to define Laminae II and III, as layers of about the same thickness. Lamina I was identified as the layer with lower neuronal density dorsal to Lamina II, not extending on either side to a point lower than the center of Lamina III. Next, Lamina VII was delimited as the anterior four ninths of the gray matter and Lamina VI as one tenth of the anteroposterior diameter, immediately posterior to Lamina VII. The remaining unallotted gray matter area was divided about equally between Lamina IV and V, with Lamina IV getting slimmer and Lamina V getting thicker toward the lateral margin of the white matter. (6) The CX3CR1+ microglia were identified from the green fluorescence channel and their centers of mass projected on the RL regions identified as above to attribute each microglia to a unique lamina. (7) The results were reported both as absolute number of microglia per lamina as well as microglial cell density in each lamina.

Spinal cord Rexed laminae analysis algorithm. The figure outlines the main steps of the algorithm used to attribute spinal cord (SC) microglia to Rexed Laminae, based on a fluorescent Nissl stain image of the neuronal bodies (blue) and a CX3CR1+ fluorescence image of microglia (green): (1) Identification of the gray matter area, (2) Collection of positional information, (3) Identification and classification of the neurons based on their size and the local density of neurons, (4) Construction of a Voronoi map of the gray matter and color thresholding, (5) ROI construction based on the logic shown on the bottom of the figure, and (6) Identification of microglia and overlay with Rexed lamina ROIs, to obtain (7) the number and density of microglia in each Rexed Lamina.

For the SC reconstruction, Adobe Illustrator software (version 25.1, Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) was used to first generate individual templates for each section. More specifically, a template was created for the ipsilateral half of the gray matter area by merging the bright field image with the CX3CR1+ staining, manually delineating the gray matter and assigning each microglia a marker (green dot). Subsequently, to create a sequential representation of the microglial distribution between L3 and L5 spinal segments, each two consecutive templates/sections were merged.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism version 9.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA, USA), using Two‐tailed Student's t‐test to compare the means of two groups and One‐Way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc test to compare multiple groups. All values were normalized to Control condition and presented as Mean ± Standard error of the mean (SEM). n represents the number of sections analyzed, pooled from 3 to 4 animals/condition. Statistical significance was considered as follows: p > .05 ns, *p < .05, **p < .01, ***p < .001, ****p < .0001.

RESULTS

CX3CR1 + macrophages accumulate differently in L3, L4, and L5 DRG after SNI

To investigate if the number of macrophages varies between L3, L4, and L5 DRG 7 days after SNI, the number of CX3CR1+ cells was determined on DRG sections. The results showed that in SNI animals the number of CX3CR1+ macrophages significantly increased compared with Sham condition in L3 and L4 DRG (L3 DRG, Sham: 1.28 ± 0.12, n = 11, and SNI: 2.04 ± 0.20, n = 11, p = .01; L4 DRG, Sham: 2.19 ± 0.15, n = 11, and SNI: 4.38 ± 0.29, n = 11, p < .0001, Figure 2A–D and Figure S2 for representative IHC images of all experimental conditions and all DRG), while for L5 DRG there was no significant difference (Sham: 1.35 ± 0.08, n = 11, and SNI: 1.68 ± 0.11, n = 12, p = .068, Figure 2E,F). Sham surgery had no effect on the number of L3 and L5 DRG macrophages compared with Control condition, but it induced a statistically significant increase in L4 DRG (Control: 1.00 ± 0.15, n = 11 and Sham: 2.19 ± 0.15, n = 11, p = .01, Figure 2D). Considering that all the DRG are coming from animals undergoing similar Sham surgery, this effect may suggest a higher sensitivity of L4 DRG to sciatic nerve manipulations. The comparison between the number of macrophages accumulated 7 days after SNI in L3, L4, and L5 DRG revealed that the strongest increase after the lesion was in L4 DRG (p < .0001 for L3 vs. L4 and for L4 vs. L5, and p = .719 for L3 vs. L5, Figure 2G).

CX3CR1 + macrophages that accumulate in L3, L4, and L5 DRG after SNI form perineuronal rings around CSF1 + neurons

Knowing that CSF1 acts as a chemoattractant agent for macrophages inside DRG 9 and that after SNI, macrophages form perineuronal rings around DRG neurons, 14 , 28 we explored whether the number of perineuronal rings of CX3CR1+ macrophages that form around mouse CSF1+ neurons varies between L3, L4 and L5 DRG, 7 days after SNI.

First, the number of CSF1+ neurons was determined on L3, L4, and L5 DRG sections. The results showed that while there were almost no CSF1+ neurons in Control condition, in Sham condition their number increased significantly in L4 DRG, but not in L3 and L5 DRG (L4 DRG, Control: 1.00 ± 0.44, n = 16 and Sham: 72.25 ± 13.02, n = 16, p = .006, Figure 3A–F). As discussed above, as all DRG are coming from animals undergoing similar Sham surgery, this effect may suggest a higher sensitivity of L4 DRG to sciatic nerve manipulations. Seven days after SNI, the number of CSF1+ cells significantly increased compared with Sham condition in all DRG (L3 DRG, Sham: 42.75 ± 6.34, n = 16, and SNI: 339.40 ± 36.84, n = 13, p < 0.0001; L4 DRG, Sham: 72.25 ± 13.02, n = 16, and SNI: 485.7 ± 29.52, n = 12, p < 0.0001, and L5 DRG, Sham: 11.00 ± 5.41, n = 16, and SNI: 152.5 ± 50.13, n = 15, p = .002, Figure 3A–F, and Figure S3 for representative IHC images of all experimental conditions and all DRG). The comparison between the number of CSF1+ neurons 7 days after SNI in L3, L4, and L5 DRG revealed that the strongest increase after the lesion was in L4 and L3 DRG, followed by L5 (p = .063 for L3 vs. L4, p = .007 for L3 vs. L5 and p < .0001 for L4 vs. L5, Figure 3G).

The results showed that in all DRG the CSF1+ neurons were surrounded by rings of CX3CR1+ macrophages, but in different proportions: in L3 DRG, ~ 26% (n = 12) of CSF1+ neurons had perineuronal rings of macrophages, in L4 DRG this percentage was increased to ~37% (n = 12), while in L5 DRG this percentage was only 15% (n = 8), Figure 4A–D.

After SNI, the incidence of CSF1 +/NF200 + neurons increased, while the number of CSF1 +/CGRP + and CSF1 +/IB4 + neurons decreased

Previously it was shown that 7 days after SNI, the perineuronal rings of macrophages were preferentially located around large NF200+ DRG neurons, while the smaller C‐neurons were relatively free of macrophages in L4/L5 rat DRG. 14 To confirm if this is the case with mouse DRG as well, including L3 DRG, and analyze the responses of all neuronal subtypes in L3‐L5 mouse DRG, we investigated what types of DRG neurons are CSF1+ neurons (i.e. NF200+, CGRP+, or IB4+) and if there is a difference in their incidence among DRG. Because we did not observe CSF1+ neurons in Control condition, we only analyzed sections from Sham and SNI conditions.

The results showed that there was no difference in the percentages of CSF1+/NF200+, CSF1+/CGRP+ and CSF1+/IB4+ 7 days after SNI compared with Sham condition in any of the three DRG, except for CSF1+/IB4+ which significantly decreased in L3 DRG (Figure 5A,B for L3 DRG, 5C and D for L4 DRG and 5E and F for L5 DRG and Table S1 with percentages and statistical analysis of CSF1+ neurons co‐stained with other neuronal markers out of the total number of CSF1+ neurons, pooled from 3 to 4 animals). The percentage of CSF1+ neurons that co‐expressed NF200 tended to increase compared with Sham, although not significantly, while the percentage of CSF1+ neurons that co‐expressed CGRP or IB4 decreased in all three DRG, although not significantly (Table S1). Cumulatively, there was an overall incidence in all 3 DRG of ~52% for CSF1+/NF200+ mechanoreceptors, of ~13% for CSF1+/CGRP+ peptidergic nociceptors and of ~37% for CSF1+/IB4+ non‐peptidergic nociceptors, with no differences between L3, L4 and L5 DRG for each neuronal type, 7 days after SNI (Figure 5G).

SNI‐induced CX3CR1 + microglia expansion in the ipsilateral SC (L3‐L5 segments) varies between laminae

Since the previous results showed that in all DRG, the CSF1+ neurons were surrounded by rings of CX3CR1+ macrophages, that among CSF1+ neurons in L3, L4, and L5 DRG the incidence of neuronal subtypes was similar (i.e. CSF1+/NF200+ > CSF1+/IB4+ > CSF1+/CGRP+), and considering that 52% of CSF1+ neurons have Aβ ‐ Αδ/NF200+ fibers which project to Lamina III–V, while 50% have either Aδ/CGRP+ fibres (~13%) or C/IB4+ and C/CGRP+ fibers (~37%) which project to Laminae I–II, we hypothesized that the number of microglia in the different dorsal horn laminae would mirror the incidence of DRG neuronal subtypes among CSF1+ neurons. Accumulating evidence from both preclinical animal studies and clinical brain imaging studies strongly supports that microglia activation and microgliosis are critical in the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain. 10 Specifically for SNI, both microglia activation (confirmed by increased expression of Iba1 and p‐p38) and microgliosis were also described. 12 , 13 In our study, the number of CX3CR1+ microglia was used as an indicator of microgliosis to evaluate de SNI‐induced response, either globally or specific per laminae, in the ipsilateral SC. The results showed that overall, there was a significant increase in the total number of CX3CR1+ microglia in the whole ipsilateral half of the SC 7 days after the lesion (Control: 1.000 ± 0.02, n = 47, Sham: 1.22 ± 0.04, n = 52 and SNI: 2.40 ± 0.11, n = 52, with p < .0001 for SNI vs. Sham, Figure 6A,B). In order to have a closer look on how the number of microglia varies among the laminae, we applied an in‐house developed ImageJ Plug‐in as described in the Methods section. The analysis showed that the number of microglia was significantly increased post‐SNI in each of the seven examined laminae compared with Sham condition (Figure 6C–I and Table S2 with the number of microglia/lamina, with p < .0001 for all 7 laminae). Nevertheless, contrary to our hypothesis, a comparison between laminae revealed a higher increase in the number of microglia in Lamina I and II, as opposed to Lamina V where there was the smallest increase (Lamina I: 3.56 ± 0.26, Lamina II: 3.55 ± 0.29, Lamina III: 2.44 ± 0.16, Lamina IV: 2.33 ± 0.11, Lamina V: 1.55 ± 0.07, Lamina VI: 1.75 ± 0.11 and Lamina VII: 2.23 ± 0.11, n = 52 for all, Figure 6J).

SNI‐activated CSF1 +/CGRP + and CSF1 +/IB4 + nociceptors induce a stronger increase in CX3CR1 + microglia number at their projection site compared with CSF1 +/NF200 + mechanoreceptors

To confirm if the number of CX3CR1+ microglia varies according to the type of projecting neurons (i.e. CSF1+/CGRP+ and CSF1+/IB4+ nociceptors in Laminae I and II, and CSF1+/NF200+ mechanoreceptors in Laminae III, IV and V), a cumulated comparison was performed. The results showed that 7 days after SNI, all CSF1+/CGRP+, CSF1+/IB4+, and CSF1+/NF200+ neuronal projections significantly increased the number of microglia compared with Sham condition in their corresponding laminae (Laminae I + II: Control: 1.00 ± 0.04, n = 47, Sham: 1.66 ± 0.08, n = 52 and SNI: 3.56 ± 0.26, n = 52, p < .0001 SNI vs. Sham, Figure 7A; Laminae III + IV + V: Control: 1.00 ± 0.03, n = 47, Sham: 1.25 ± 0.04, n = 52 and SNI: 2.15 ± 0.07, n = 52, p < .0001 for SNI vs. Sham, Figure 7B). A comparison between the projection areas in the SNI condition showed that CX3CR1+ microglia clustered more in Laminae I and II than in Laminae III, IV, and V combined, suggesting that signals coming from CSF1+/CGRP+ and CSF1+/IB4+ DRG nociceptors have a stronger influence on microglia (Laminae I + II: 3.56 ± 0.26, n = 52, Laminae III + IV + V: 2.15 ± 0.07, n = 52, p < .0001, Figure 7C). Furthermore, the reconstruction of L3‐L5 ipsilateral SC segments confirmed that after SNI, the microglial response in the SC in mice had the same somatotopic nature as it was previously shown for rats. 22 In addition, our data show that, in mice, microglia distribution also varies between spinal segments: in L5 area microglia mainly proliferated in the ventral horns, while in L4 and L3 areas, they proliferated both in the dorsal and ventral horns, with a gradual and expected decrease toward L2 and L6 (Figure 7D).

DISCUSSION

In this study we investigated if SNI‐induced CSF1 expression and perineuronal rings of macrophages around DRG neurons vary between L3‐L5 DRG and with the neuronal type, and if the CSF1+ neuronal projections at the SC dorsal horns are associated with an increased microglial number in the corresponding laminae. The results showed that 7 days after SNI, (1) L4 DRG followed by L3 and L5 DRG had an increased number of CSF1+ neurons that were all surrounded by perineuronal rings of CX3CR1+ macrophages; (2) in all L3‐L5 DRG, there was a similar increase of CSF1+/NF200+ neurons incidence, while for CSF1+/CGRP+ peptidergic nociceptors and CSF1+/IB4+ non‐peptidergic nociceptors the incidence decreased, with CSF1+/IB4+ neurons being more frequent than CSF1+/CGRP+ neurons; and (3) surprisingly, the higher incidence of CSF1+/NF200+ neurons was not associated with a higher number of microglia at laminae III‐V of the ipsilateral dorsal horn, since cumulated projections of CSF1+/IB4+ and CSF1+/CGRP+ neurons successfully activated more microglia in Laminae I + II.

Sensory neuron‐derived expression of CSF1 mRNA after SNI is induced 1 day after nerve injury, when 80% of ATF3+ lesioned mouse DRG neurons were CSF1+, and it persisted for 3 weeks when only 40% of ATF3+ DRG neurons continue to be CSF1+. 12 Previously it was shown that after SNI, in mice, ~40% of the injured common peroneal and tibial nerves originated in L3 DRG and ~35% in L4 DRG, while 15% of uninjured sural nerve fibers originated in L4 DRG and ~15% in L5 DRG. 26 In this study, we investigated the CSF1 expression in L3, L4 and L5 mouse DRG individually, 7 days after SNI, when most of the ATF3+ lesioned neurons would express CSF1. The individual approach was considered because examining the CSF1 expression in pooled L4/L5 DRG 9 , 12 may overlook the contribution of each ganglion and because L3 DRG, which consists primarily of injured neurons, may also contribute to CSF1 expression.

Our results showed that the number of CSF1+ neurons increased in all 3 DRG, with the strongest increase in L4 DRG, mirrored by a similar response of CX3CR1+ macrophages. These data confirm the previously reported data that sensory neuron‐derived CSF1, by engaging the CSF1R that is expressed by resident macrophages, triggers their nerve injury‐induced expansion in the DRG and further on the initiation and persistence of neuropathic pain phenotype. 9 The contribution of resident macrophages to mediating CSF1 effects in the first days after SNI was also confirmed in another study, in which by using CX3CR1GFP/+/CCR2RFP/+ reporter mice it was shown that CCR2+ blood monocyte infiltration was not required to expand CX3CR1+ resident macrophages in the DRG at 7 days after SNI, and that 97% of all resident macrophages in the sensory ganglia were CX3CR1+. 29 However, the increase was not the strongest in L3 DRG which contains primarily injured neurons, but in L4 DRG, which also contains uninjured sural fibers. This response was most probably due to the intermingling of injured and intact nerves specific for L4 DRG, which may allow cross‐excitation between cell bodies, 30 , 31 ephaptic cross talk along nerve fibers, 32 and further on sensitization of uninjured neuronal bodies which attract around them resident DRG macrophages. The individual variability in lumbar DRG associated with the presence of injured or intact neurons in different DRG was also described for SNI‐induced expression of NGF and BDNF: both neurotrophins increased in L3 DRG, only NGF increase and no change for BDNF in L4 DRG, and NGF decrease and no change for BDNF in L5 DRG. 33

The same individual variability between lumbar DRG could also be accounted for the surprisingly increased response of L4 DRG in Sham animals compared with Control condition, for both the number of CSF1+ neurons and CX3CR1+ macrophages. Apparently, the piece of 6‐0 silk suture placed under the common peroneal and tibial branches and left there for 7 days during Sham surgery sensitized more L4 DRG neurons than L3 and L5 DRG, although all DRG neurons could have been potentially sensitized. This response at 7 days after surgery could be predictive for a surprising Sham response shown recently at 4 weeks after SNI sugery. More precisely, the proteomic analysis of small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) purified from serum 4 weeks after SNI surgery revealed 274 gene products within sEVs, with 24 unique to SNI model, 100 to Sham surgery and 5 to Control samples. 34 Later on, the same group showed that the microglial uptake of sEVs isolated from the serum of Sham mice at 4 weeks after surgery induced stronger gene expression alterations compared with SNI sEVs; that is, in SNI sEV‐treated microglia 54 genes were upregulated while 1 was downregulated, while in Sham sEV‐treated microglia 787 genes were upregulated and 1487 genes were downregulated. 35 Possibly, these differences between SNI sEVs and Sham sEV‐treated microglia originate in the increased CX3CR1+ macrophages response we noticed at 7 days after SNI, but this assumption will require additional experiments to confirm.

In addition to previous data which showed a similar expansion of CX3CR1+/Iba1+ resident macrophages in pooled L4/L5 DRG at 4 days after SNI 9 or mainly in L4 DRG at 7 day after SNI, 29 we showed that 7 days after SNI, the proliferation of macrophages occurred distinctively in all DRG of origin for the sciatic nerve, following the same individual variation between DRG as for the number of CSF1+ neurons, that is, L4 DRG > L3 DRG > L5 DRG. Ring‐like clusters of resident macrophages were also described after SNL, 16 sciatic nerve transection 17 and sciatic nerve ligation and transection, 18 but they were not associated with CSF1 increased expression as after SNI. All together, these data suggest the existence of injury specific release of macrophages' chemoattractants agents by lesioned DRG neurons, and emphasize the importance of analyzing the individual contribution of L3‐L5 DRG for a better understanding of SNI‐induced neuropathic pain pathogenesis.

The incidence of CSF1+/NF200+ neurons was equally increased in all L3‐L5 DRG, followed by CSF1+/IB4+ non‐peptidergic neurons and CSF1+/CGRP+ peptidergic neurons, which also showed no difference between DRG. Previously, it was shown that in pooled L4/L5 rat DRG, 7 days after SNI, perineuronal rings of macrophages were located mainly around large NF200+ DRG neurons (37.6%), while the smaller C‐neurons (IB4+ non‐peptidergic and CGRP+ peptidergic) were relatively free of macrophages (6.4%). 14 Compared to mice, L4 and L5 DRG are of interest in the SNI rat pain model, since 98% of sciatic nerve fibers originate from the L4 and L5 DRG, while L3 DRG contains only 1.2% of the mid‐thigh sciatic afferents. 36 Assuming the same contribution of CSF1 in attracting macrophages around DRG neurons after SNI regardless of species, our results reinforce the existence of species‐specific anatomy or molecular signaling inside lumbar DRG of origin for the sciatic nerve and reconfirm the importance of animal pain model species. 26

Previously, we showed that 5 days after SNL perineuronal rings of Iba1+ resident macrophages developed preferentially in L5 DRG around NF200+ neurons, 37 similarly to the current results, while CGRP+ neurons were preferred against IB4+ neurons, in contrast to our present study. The higher affinity of NF200+ neurons for resident macrophages could be the expression of an increased sensitivity to traumatic injury, like after SNL. 38 , 39 However, since this was not yet documented for SNI, it is rather the expression of a preferential communication between large mechanoreceptive NF200+ neurons and macrophages, because depletion of macrophages with liposomal clodronate 1 day before SNI was not followed by tactile allodynia at 7 and 10 days post‐SNI in mice. 40 In the same study, the gene ontology analysis on L4/L5 DRG revealed that the genes associated with the development of tactile allodynia were almost entirely related to immune function, supporting a macrophage contribution, while genes related to the onset of cold allodynia were almost entirely related to neuronal function, that is, small C‐nociceptors. Knowing that cold allodynia developed 3 days after SNI, achieved peak levels 4–5 days after the lesion, and had a strong nociceptor component, 40 the role of the perineuronal rings of macrophages around small C‐neurons (IB4+ non‐peptidergic and CGRP+ peptidergic), which fully develop 7–10 days after the lesion, could be not in sensitizing small DRG neurons, but in promoting microglial‐induced central sensitization 41 and the switch from acute pain to chronic pain by central release of CSF1. 12 Even though it was performed in mice, the Cobos study did not consider the L3 DRG, which could have probably reinforced their data, considering its contribution to injured common peroneal and tibial nerves.

In this study, we also aimed to investigate if CSF1+ neuronal projections at the SC dorsal horns are associated with a higher number of microglia in the corresponding laminae, hypothesizing that perineuronal rings of macrophages will equally activate the neuronal subtypes (i.e. CSF1+/NF200+, CSF1+/IB4+, and CSF1+/CGRP+), and the most abundant neuronal subtype (i.e. CSF1+/NF200+) will induce a stronger microgliosis by releasing more CSF1 at the SC dorsal horns. 12 A variety of studies support the idea that CSF1‐CSF1R signaling is very important for nerve injury‐induced microglia response and subsequent neuropathic pain development. Thus, in the SC, CSF1R is expressed only in microglia, and not in neurons, astrocytes or oligodendrocytes 42 ; deletion of Csf1 gene from sensory neurons prevents nerve injury‐induced microglia activation 12 ; and intrathecal injection of CSF1 protein activated microglia and induced pain behavior even in the absence of an injury. 13 , 43 Our results, which showed that 52% of CSF1+ neurons had Aβ/NF200+ fibers, made us assume that more microglia will be activated in Laminae III–V than in the projection area of Aδ/CGRP+ fibers (~13%) or C/IB4+ and C/CGRP+ fibers (~37%) located in Laminae I–II. Since it was hard to distinguish between projection areas of CSF1+/CGRP+ and CSF1+/IB4+ neurons, we had to consider them all together. Overall, the number of microglia in Laminae III‐V represented 60% of the microglia present in Laminae I + II, indicating a stronger influence on microglia of CSF1+/IB4+ and CSF1+/CGRP+ DRG nociceptors, even though CSF1+/NF200+ were the most abundant neuronal subtype in all L3‐L5 DRG after SNI. This response could be due to different inputs received by SC dorsal horn neurons from DRG sensory neurons according to their subtype, or to a personalized reaction the microglia might have according to the lamina where they are located.

It was shown that tonic electric stimulation (180 s) with 5–100 Hz of rat L5 DRG results in progressive decline of Aα/ß‐fibers recruitment and frequency‐dependent reduction in the amplitude of action potential, possibly responsible for the analgesic effect of DRG stimulation shown in both animal and clinical studies. 44 The authors suggested that for this response to occur, the frequency of stimulation should be set to the lowest possible level, which, by extrapolating to in vivo condition, could be associated with the low intensity, tonic activation the perineuronal macrophages might induce in CSF1+/NF200+ after SNI. 45 In another study, it was shown that intense but non‐damaging, electrical stimulation (5 min at 10 Hz) of intact adult rat C‐fibers, but not Aβ/Aδ, alone, induced central sensitization characterized by mechanical hypersensitivity and significant microglial activation in the L4 spinal dorsal horn C‐fibers projection area, as expressed by significant upregulation of markers of microglial activation, including Iba1 and CSF1. 41 By putting together, the Hathway et al. data about the C‐fibers and the additional observation that the pattern of Iba1 expression in Sham‐operated and Aβ/Aδ group dorsal horn were indistinguishable, with Ting et al. data showing a reduction in the amplitude of action potentials upon low‐frequency stimulation of Aβ/Aδ‐fibers, the reconstituted puzzle that results is that the output of sensory DRG neurons at SC dorsal horns might differ, even though at periphery they could be equally activated. As CSF1 release by central axons of DRG sensory neurons is essential for microglia proliferation and activation, and all subtypes of DRG neurons express CSF1 after SNI, possible explanations for the reduced Laminae III‐V microglial number in our study could be: (i) CSF1 transported to the SC after nerve injury as soon as 4 days after injury 12 comes primarily from small CSF1+/IB4+ and CSF1+/CGRP+ DRG nociceptors, and less from CSF1+/NF200+, or (ii) CSF1 is equally transported to SC dorsal horns from all DRG neurons, but its release at the SC level is neuronal subtype‐ and time‐dependent, with CSF1+/NF200+ releasing less CSF1 7 days after SNI.

A specific location‐dependent reaction of the microglia according to the lamina, could be another explanation for the different degrees of microglia response in SC dorsal horns. Single‐cell RNA sequencing data showed that the majority of microglia in the L4‐L5 naive mouse SC exist in six transcriptional states (clusters 1–6), in addition to small populations (<2% of total) of proliferating (cluster 7 and 8) and vessel‐associated (cluster 10) microglia, accompanied by perivascular macrophages that express both macrophage and microglia genes (cluster 11). 46 Three days after SNI the authors showed a significant increase of proliferating microglia in clusters 7 and 8, but there are no data to associate these clusters with specific laminae in the SC.

No loss of neurons was shown to be required for the development of tactile allodynia after SNI, 47 but specific responses of microglia were actually shown after peripheral nerve injury. In contrast to SC injury or other direct CNS traumatic injuries when microglia transform into ameboid, intensely phagocytic cells, after peripheral nerve injury microglia have different profiles, depending on the location of the injury, the specific neuronal interactions, or time after injury. 48 In the SC, dorsal horns microglia do not enwrap neuronal cell bodies after peripheral nerve injury, as macrophages do in the DRG, but remain dispersed between them, as we have also shown. However, 7 days after SNL in rats, microglia that adhered to and engulfed both injured and uninjured myelinated axons were also described. 49 In the SC, ventral horns enwrapping microglia were described around ventral horn motoneurons 7 days after CCI, with microglia expressing less BDNF, fewer phagocytic markers and significantly more IL‐6 than in the SC dorsal horns. 50 CSF1 was shown to be necessary for microglial response in both cases, originating either from injured sensory neurons in the dorsal horns, or from motoneurons in the ventral horns. 48 , 51 Nevertheless, it cannot be ruled out that other changes in a CSF1‐independent manner could still occur, which could be responsible for the dorsal horns lamina specific SNI‐induced responses that we have shown, as well as for the increased number of microglia in the ventral horns that we have also noticed (Figure 7D), but we did not explore it in this study.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, this study showed that SNI‐induced CSF1 expression is common for all types of mouse DRG neurons (i.e. large NF200+ mechanoreceptors and small CGRP+ peptidergic and IB4+ non‐peptidergic nociceptors) and is responsible for attracting macrophages around them in a DRG‐dependent manner, with L4 showing the stronger response. Since L3 DRG in mice contributes significantly to injured common peroneal and tibial nerves, in contrast to rats, its study is highly relevant for SNI‐induced neuropathic pain pathogenesis. Surprisingly, the higher incidence of CSF1+/NF200+ neurons in all L3‐L5 DRG with CX3CR1+ macrophages around them was inversely correlated with the number of microglia at their laminar SC projections site, suggesting other mechanisms for microglial response than in DRG. In conclusion, if mice are the elective species for investigating SNI‐induced neuropathic pain, L3‐L5 DRG should be considered individually, since resident macrophages and SC microglia at their projection sites seem to have specific responses.
