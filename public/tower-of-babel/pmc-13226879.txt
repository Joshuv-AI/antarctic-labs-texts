Characteristics, treatment and survival of older patients with metastatic triple negative breast cancer from the ESME mBC real-world national cohort: a comparison with younger women
Authors: Mathilde Mairé, Fanny Bouteiller, Camille Chakiba, William Jacot, Romain Geiss, Mony Ung, Anthony Goncalves, Jean-Sébastien Frenel, Caroline Bailleux, Christophe Perrin, Vincent Massard, Audrey Mailliez, Christelle Jouannaud, François Cherifi, Thierry Petit, Catherine Terret, Alessandro Viansone, Lise Bosquet, Angéline Galvin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMetastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC) remains a major therapeutic challenge, particularly among older women, who are frequently underrepresented in clinical trials. This study aimed to describe the characteristics, treatment patterns, and prognostic factors for real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS) and overall survival (OS) in older (≥70 years) women with metastatic TNBC (mTNBC) compared to younger women. (40-69 years)MethodsA retrospective analysis of the UNICANCER ESME cohort was conducted, including 4246 women diagnosed with mTNBC between 2008 and 2021. Patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment data, and survival outcomes were collected. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess OS and rwPFS.ResultsOlder women (n = 987) presented with less aggressive tumors and were less likely to receive chemotherapy or targeted therapy compared to younger women (n = 3259). Median OS was 13.7 months in older women and 15.8 months in younger women, with no significant difference. Prognostic factors for OS and rwPFS included the number of metastatic sites, visceral metastasis, and metastasis-free interval, regardless of age. In older women, age over 80 was associated with poorer prognosis.ConclusionDespite differences in treatment patterns, survival outcomes were comparable between older and younger women with mTNBC. This real-world analysis underscores the importance of studying mTNBC in older populations, who are often excluded from clinical trials. Further research is needed to optimize treatment strategies for older women with mTNBC, especially with the introduction of new therapies and considering the real world data that allows for a better understanding of this population.Keywords: Metastatic breast cancer, Triple negative, Overall survival, Older patients

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancers (TNBC), are defined as tumors with negative Hormone Receptor (HR - less than 10% on immunochemistry) and without HER-2 overexpression. They account for 10 to 17% of all invasive breast cancers and are associated with poor prognosis [1,2]. TNBC are more frequent in young patients, tend to present with larger size than other subtypes of cancer and are usually high-grade, invasive ductal carcinomas [1]. Patients with TNBC experience higher rates of recurrence in the first 4 years after diagnosis and are more likely to experience visceral metastases than those with other types of breast cancer [3,4]. Overall survival (OS) for metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC) ranges to 15 months whereas it ranges around 45 to 50 months for other subtypes [5]. While OS has dramatically improved among HER2+ metastatic breast cancer patients, due to the release of several major HER2-directed therapies, this trend was not observed in the other subtypes, especially not in mTNBC patients [6]. mTNBC management remains challenging, and, to date, chemotherapy is the main first line recommended treatment for non BRCA-mutated mTNBC without significant PDL1 expression, with no specific recommendation regarding types of agent [7].

Age is a major risk factor of breast cancer and although breast cancer in advanced age has usually been associated with a slightly increased likelihood of favorable tumor biology, 10% of older women still develop TNBC [8]. For all breast cancers, tumor size and nodal involvement usually increase with age partly because of delayed diagnosis in older women [9]. To date, there is no published study focusing especially on older population with mTNBC and observational studies on mTNBC population are scarce, with small numbers and a low proportion of older subjects. In few studies including older patients, tumor size and nodal involvement were not significantly different according to age group [10,11] and the most frequent difference between younger and older patients was the indication for adjuvant therapy. Few studies have reported equivalent survival among older TNBC patients compared to younger patients despite the reduced percentage of patients receiving chemotherapy in this age group [[10], [11], [12]]. A better knowledge of older patients with mTNBC would permit to offer optimal care and issue recommendations [13].

Adults aged 65 years and older, and especially those over age 75, are underrepresented in cancer trials because of age limits for inclusion or comorbidities despite representing a growing segment of the population of the cancer patients [14]. Thus, data on older patients with mTNBC in real-life setting are particularly relevant. The UNICANCER ESME mBC (Epidemiological Strategy and Medical Economics-Metastatic Breast Cancer) national cohort includes all mBC patients primarily treated within the national network of Comprehensive Cancer Centers and therefore represents a unique, large-scale, real-world cohort.

The aim of this study was to describe characteristics, first-line treatments and prognostic factors for real-world progression free survival (rwPFS) and overall survival (OS) of women aged 70 and older (70+) with mTNBC, compared to younger women (aged 40-69) using the real-world ESME database.

Methods

Data source

The ESME research program collects real world data on oncology care for epidemiological research purposes. The mBC retrospective national cohort gathers data from all consecutive ≥18 years treated patients with mBC treatment initiated from 2008 in one of the 18 French Comprehensive Cancer Centers (clinicaltrials.gov; NCT 03275311). The ESME cohort includes only treated patients. Diagnostic, therapeutic and follow-up data (demographics, primary tumor, metastatic disease, treatment patterns and vital status) are collected routinely [15]. The collected information derives from the integration of patient-related data derived from medical records [16]. For the present study, data extraction took place on April 12, 2023.

Study population

Eligible patients in our study were female who had a diagnosis of mTNBC between January 1st, 2008 and December 31st, 2021. In accordance with the International Society of Geriatric Oncology (SIOG) definition of the ‘geriatric’ population in oncology, we set the cut-off at 70 years and older. We excluded patients with no informative data on mBC or on systemic treatment. Because they usually present with specific cancer characteristics and treatment pattern [17], patients younger than 40 years old were excluded. Tumoral subtype was defined with HER2 status and Hormone Receptor (HR) status derived from existing results about metastatic tissue when sampling where available, or, if not available with last available sampling on early disease. Tumors were defined as HR negative (HR-) if estrogen receptor and progesterone receptor expression was <10% (immunohistochemistry). HER2 immunohistochemical (IHC) score 0-1 or IHC score 2 + with a negative fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) classified the cancer as HER2 negative (HER2-). TNBC status was defined as negative HR and HER2 negative status.

Variables and definitions

De novo metastatic disease status was defined for patients who presented with metastases at the time of primary tumor diagnosis or developed them within 6 months from primary diagnosis. Metastasis-free interval (MFI) was defined as the time between initial diagnosis and metastatic relapse and categorized in three groups (<6 [de novo mBC], 6–24, >24 months). First-line mBC treatments were categorized as chemotherapy, targeted therapy or immunotherapy, endocrine therapy, and combinations of thereof. The first-line treatment was defined as intravenous (IV) or per os (PO) therapeutic regimen received between the metastatic diagnosis and the first progression, provided the treatment continued for more than 30 days following the date of metastatic disease. OS was defined as the time interval between the date of diagnosis of mBC and the date of death (any cause). rwPFS was defined as the time interval between the first-line initiation date and the date of the first disease progression (as considered by the reference physician) or death (any cause).

Statistical methods

Patients were divided into two groups: the younger 40-69 years old at mBC diagnosis) and the older group (≥70 years at mBC diagnosis). Qualitative variables were reported as frequency and percentages, continuous variables were reported as median and inter-quartile ranges. The median follow-up time was calculated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method [18]. OS and rw-PFS were estimated using Kaplan-Meier method. Multivariable analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards (PH) models. Prognostics factors included age at diagnosis, number of metastatic sites, presence of visceral metastases, metastasis-free interval (MFI) and year of diagnosis. Hazard ratios and their 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were reported at several time points (1, 3 and 5 years) in case of violation of the PH assumption.

Ethics approval and data protection

The ESME research program is managed by UNICANCER in accordance with current best practice guidelines and rules. It is supervised by a steering committee. The ESME MBC database was first authorized in October 2013 b y the French data protection authority (initial authorization DE-2013.-117) and obtained complementary authorization in October 2019 in compliance with the applicable European regulations. All data were obtained retrospectively, and no procedure was taken to recover unavailable data by contacting healthcare providers or patients. An independent scientific committee specifically approved the present work, and all patients have approved the use of their data.

Results

Patients, tumor and adjuvant treatment characteristics

A total of 27,769 women were diagnosed with mBC and included in the ESME cohort between January 2008 and Decembre 2021. Of those, 4246 (15.3%) presented with mTNBC and were aged 40 years and older, including 987 (23.2%) patients aged 70 years and older (Fig. 1).

Median age at metastatic diagnosis was 77 (interquartile range, IQR = 73-81) for women aged 70+ and 55 (IQR = 48-61) for the younger group. In the older group, 338 (34.2%) women had 80 years old or more. Women aged 70+ were more likely to present an invasive lobular carcinoma at initial diagnosis (11.0% vs 4.9%, p < 0.001) and less aggressive initial tumor (47.8% grade 3 vs 59.0%, p < 0.001) compared to women aged 40-69. Among patients with available data, only 29.3% of those aged 70 and older had a null ECOG PS, compared to 46.3% of younger patients. In contrast, BMI at metastatic diagnosis was statistically similar between the two age groups (p = 0.701).

Regardless of age group, 29.2% women presented with de novo mTNBC, 63.5% had visceral metastasis at diagnostic and 23.9% more than 3 metastatic sites. While bone-only metastasis was more frequent in older patients (13.9% vs 9.4%, p < 0.001) and isolated brain metastasis more frequent in younger patients (4.3% for 40-69 patients vs 2.6%, p = 0.018), the proportion of visceral metastasis (except brain) was similar between the two groups (58.8% in ≥70 and 59.8% in 40-69, p = 0.560). A larger proportion of 70+ patients had a metastasis-free interval (MFI) for initial diagnosis of 24 months or more compared to their younger counterparts (45.4% in 70+ vs 38.3% in 40-69 women, p < 0.001). Number of metastatic sites at diagnosis was similar between the two aged groups (75.8% in 40-69 patients and 77.1% in 70+ patients had less than 3 sites, p = 0.386).

Neo/adjuvant and first line metastatic treatment

Younger patients were more likely to receive IV chemotherapy/targeted therapy in adjuvant setting than their older counterparts (92.2% vs 59.4%, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table A).

At metastatic setting, 95.2% of women aged 40-69 and 93.8% of those aged 70+ received chemotherapy (Table 2). While older patients were more likely to receive PO chemotherapy (31.9% vs 21.2%, p < 0.001), women aged 40-69 were more likely to receive IV chemotherapy (85.9% vs 74.0%, p < 0.001) or targeted therapy (34.8% vs 15.5%, p < 0.001). The prescribed agent for PO chemotherapy was exclusively Capecitabine. For targeted therapy, the prescribed agent in 84.6% patients was Bevacizumab, for immunotherapy, 51.5% patients received Atezolizumab. Proportion of patients who received endocrine therapy as first-line metastatic therapy was not null and was greater in the group aged 70+ (11.3% vs 6.6%, p < 0.001). They received predominantly aromatase inhibitors and tamoxifen for endocrine therapy. Nine patients (2.5%) received anti androgenic therapy. A comprehensive list of the hormone therapies, targeted therapy and immunotherapy is provided in Supplementary Table B, C and D. Analysis of those patients who received hormone therapy found that more than 80% of them had HR status positive of the initial tumor, at initial diagnosis. Clinical features of this subgroup of patients who received endocrine therapy are presented in Supplementary Table E. Compared with patients who did not receive endocrine therapy, these patients had less aggressive tumors at diagnosis (27.0% grade 3 vs 58.8%), a longer metastasis-free interval (71.5% with MFI >24 months vs 37.3%), fewer metastatic sites (83.4% with fewer than three sites vs 75.5%), and a higher proportion of bone-only disease (33.7% vs 8.5%).

Survival outcomes by age groups

Median follow up duration was 74 months (95%CI [65.4; 81.4]) for patients aged 40-69 years old and 74.7 months (95%CI [62.2; 92.7]) for patients aged 70+. Median OS was 15.8 months (95%CI [15.1; 16.6]) for patients aged 40-69 years old and 13.7 months (95%IC [12.6; 15.1]) for women older than 70 years old (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Median rwPFS was similar between the two age groups (median rwPFS 5.4 vs. 4.9 months in patients aged 70 years and older and under 70 years, respectively, p = 0.52).

Overall survival (A) and Real-world progression free survival (B) in women aged 40-69 years old or 70 and older with mTNBC.

Prognostic factors for OS by age groups

Regardless of age group, the presence of more than 3 metastatic sites (p < 0.001), the presence of visceral metastases (p < 0.001) and a MFI of 6 month to 24 months (p < 0.001) were associated with shorter OS (Table 3). A time-varying effect was observed for the presence of visceral metastases and MFI, meaning that HRs were not constant-over time. Mainly, patients with visceral metastases had higher risk of all-cause death at 1 year, but this association did not remain significant after 3 and/or 5 years. While age was not associated with OS in women aged 40-69, age was identified as a prognostic factor for women aged 70+, with women aged 80 and older presenting with shorter OS than women aged 70-79 years old (p < 0.001).

Prognostic factors for rwPFS by age group

In the 70 and older subgroup, prognostic factors for rwPFS were age over 80 years old at mBC diagnosis (HR = 1.22 (1.07; 1.39), p = 0.004), metastatic free interval of 6 to 24 months compared to de novo mBC (HR = 1.81 (1.51-2.16), p < 0.001), number of metastatic sites >3 at mBC diagnosis (HR = 1.59 (1.34; 1.88), p < 0.001) and visceral metastasis (HR = 1.37 (1.19; 1.59), p < 0.001) (Table 4). Prognostic factors were identical for women aged 40-69 years old, except that the higher risk of recurrence among patients with visceral metastasis was observed exclusively during the first year after treatment initiation, due to a decreasing effect of HR over time.

Discussion

In this article, we aimed to describe characteristics, first-line treatment pattern, OS and rwPFS in women aged 70 and older with mTNBC, as compared to women aged 40-69 using data from a national French mBC cohort. The ESME program is a unique French real-life cohort on mBC including all adults with mBC treated in Comprehensive Cancer Centers. This large population enabled us to focus our study on older patients with mTNBC, a subgroup rarely analyzed. Overall, older patients presented with less aggressive disease and were less likely to receive chemotherapy or targeted therapy/immunotherapy at metastatic setting. When treated with chemotherapy, they more often received oral regimen. OS and rwPFS were not significantly different between the two age groups, with similar median OS (13.7 months for ≥70 vs 15.8 months for 40–69).

In the ESME cohort, mTNBC represented 17.4% of mBC, consistently with the 10% to 20% reported in literature [19]. In line with other studies, patients aged 70 and older represented approximately 20% of the mTNBC population [20]. At initial diagnosis, majority of TNBC are high grade invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) [21,22], in our population 81.0% patients had IDC and 56.4% had grade 3 initial tumor. Invasive lobular carcinomas were more frequent in older patients, which aligns with literature [21]. TNBC is usually described as an aggressive type of breast cancer with metastatic spread (excluding de novo metastatic cancer) described in the first 3 years [4] and more likely to visceral (60%) or brain (30%) metastases and less likely to metastasize to bones (10%) [1]. In our group of mTNBC patients, 60% of metastatic spread occurred in the first 2 years of diagnosis, and 73% in the first 3 years (including de novo metastasis), with a majority of visceral metastasis.

Regarding treatment, patients aged 70 and older were less likely to receive chemotherapy (93.8% vs 95.2%, p = 0.097) or targeted therapy (15.5% vs 34.8%, p < 0.001). In the study period, targeted therapy was mainly Bevacizumab. When treated with chemotherapy, patients aged 70+ were more likely to receive PO chemotherapy (31.9% vs 21.2%, p < 0.001).

In our population of patients who received treatment for mTNBC, OS and rwPFS were similar in the two age groups. Median OS was 15.3 months for the global mTNBC population, 13.7 months in patients older than 70 years and 15.8 months for younger patients. Our findings on OS in patients with mTNBC are in line with existing literature. Reported median overall survival ranges from 11.8 to 17.7 months, irrespective of treatment status [20,23,24]. Furthermore, a study focusing on a population similar to ours, including exclusively treated patients, reported a median overall survival of 13.3 months [24]. We identified similar prognostic factors for OS and rw-PFS, regardless of the age group: more than 3 metastatic sites, presence of visceral metastasis and MFI of 6 to 24 months were associated with shorter OS and rw-PFS. For patients aged 70+, increasing age was associated with poorer prognosis only after 80 years old. Those results are consistent with literature [20,25].

The large sample size enabled us to produce results on older patients, that are not currently available in the literature. The key strength of our study lies in its large sample size and its coverage of more than 95% of mBC patients treated in FCCCs which treat over a third of breast cancer patients in France. The ESME cohort also includes a long follow up, regular updates, a high quality-level of data collection, and a strict validation of data. Thus, our results could be considered as a good reflection of routine clinical practice in these comprehensive care centers.

Real-word data (RWD) provide an opportunity to study patients that are excluded from conventional cancer clinical trials and examine the covariates that influence the natural history of disease in these specific populations [26]. The use of RWD in high-quality observational studies can, according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology, advance evidence-based practice for cancer care and are complementary to randomized controlled trials [27]. For mTNBC, new treatment option such as immunotherapy (pembrolizumab), PARP inhibitors or antibody-drug conjugates (sacituzumab-govitecan) have recently been disclosed [[28], [29], [30]]. However, patients included in those studies were mainly aged less than 65: in the study by Robson and al. Median age was 45 years old; in the KEYNOTE-355 study and in Bardi and al.’ study, only 20% patients were aged 65 and older. Barriers identified for this under representation include unnecessarily strict inclusion or exclusion criteria, exclusion because of comorbidities beyond those specified by protocol, and presumed patient difficulties in trial participation. In addition, older patients reported limited access to information regarding clinical trials whereas they were as willing as younger patients to participate [9]. Knowing the underrepresentation of older patients in clinical trials, the ESME cohort provides information on this subgroup that are critical to better enable evaluation of benefit-risk of treatments in this population [14].

This study also presents some limitations. First, comorbidity data were unavailable, although known to influence outcomes and therapeutic options. Performance status was collected but missing for >60% of patients, limiting adjustment analyses as propensity scores. In the context of RWD, comparing the reported survival estimates to external studies is complex, mainly due to the distribution of confounding factors, which differs across observational studies, and the limited availability of subtype-specific studies on mBC. In addition, geriatric information such as cognitive status and functional status were not available. Those data are important for first line treatment's choice, especially in older population, and may affect survival, therefore, they may induce indication bias in survival analysis. A further limitation of our study is that the ESME cohort includes only treated patients. Those who did not receive treatment—potentially due to more advanced disease or poorer general condition, especially among older individuals—were excluded, possibly introducing a selection bias. In the study by Skinner et al. the authors examined treatment pattern and outcomes of patients with mTNBC from US community oncology centers. The proportion of patients without anti-cancer treatment, regardless of age group was 16.9%. They found that increasing age was associated with reduced likelihood of receiving systemic treatment and that median OS was significantly longer for treated patients than for untreated patients [20]. Patients under the age of 40 are also underrepresented in clinical trials, and data on this population are also lacking. However, they were not included in the present study because it focused on older subjects and young female patients represent a distinct population who have been the subject of a previous study [17].

In addition, because OS and PFS do not account for cause of death, cancer-specific survival (CSS) would have been another way to study survival. However, cause of death is usually not available in observational settings and, even when it is available, cause of death is often imprecise in older adults. Thus, relative survival is particularly relevant for future research in older populations because it avoids relying on the accurate attribution of cause of death, which is often challenging in older patients with multiple comorbidities and competing causes of mortality.

Regarding external validity, ESME cohort only selected patients treated in FCCCs, our population may not fully reflect the whole French TNBC population. Indeed, studies reported differences between BC patients regarding health center [31]. Older patients may be less frequently referred to specialized cancer centers due to their general health status or geographic constraints, potentially resulting in undertreatment that cannot be directly verified.

Treatment details were also limited and not analyzed here, while therapeutic options have evolved substantially in recent years (e.g., sacituzumab govitecan since 2021, pembrolizumab in selected patients). [26,27]. Despite these changes, outcomes remain comparable; ongoing analyses will further explore survival according to treatment received.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that although older treated patients tend to present with less aggressive mTNBC, and to be treated less intensively, their overall survival was not different from younger patients. It supports the importance of including older population on clinical trials or, otherwise, using RWD to assess treatment-related outcomes in this specific population. Response to treatment or toxicity, may vary with age and further analysis to study survival according to first-line treatment in older patients would be important for practitioner's decision.
