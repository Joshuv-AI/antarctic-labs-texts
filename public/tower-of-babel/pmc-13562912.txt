Efficacy and Safety of a Single‐Pill Triple Combination of Valsartan, Amlodipine, and Chlorthalidone in Patients With Essential Hypertension Inadequately Controlled on Dual Therapy With Valsartan and Amlodipine: A Randomized, Double‐Blind, Multicenter, Phase 3 Trial
Authors: In‐Jeong Cho, Soon Jun Hong, Min Gyu Kong, Sang‐Ho Jo, Eun Joo Cho, Cheol Ung Choi, Jung Hyun Choi, Dae‐Hee Kim, Ju Han Kim, Sang‐Hyun Ihm, Wook Bum Pyun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany hypertensive patients require three or more antihypertensive agents to achieve target blood pressure. This randomized, double‐blind, multicenter phase 3 trial conducted in South Korea evaluated the efficacy and safety of a single‐pill triple combination therapy with valsartan (Val), amlodipine (Aml), and chlorthalidone (CTD) in patients whose blood pressure remained inadequately controlled on Val/Aml dual therapy. Patients uncontrolled after 4 weeks of Val/Aml 80/5 mg were randomized 1:1 to either Val/Aml/CTD 80/5/12.5 mg or Val/Aml 80/5 mg using a double‐dummy design. After 2 weeks, doses were escalated to Val/Aml/CTD 160/5/25 mg or Val/Aml 160/5 mg, respectively, for a total treatment duration of 6 weeks. The primary endpoint was the change in mean sitting systolic blood pressure (MSSBP) from baseline to week 8. Of 193 randomized patients, 178 completed the study. The least squares mean ± SE change in MSSBP was −19.70 ± 1.31 mmHg in the Val/Aml/CTD group versus −8.71 ± 1.26 mmHg in the control group, yielding a statistically significant between‐group difference of −10.99 ± 1.81 mmHg (95% CI, −14.56 to −7.41; p < 0.0001). No serious adverse events occurred in the Val/Aml/CTD group, compared with an incidence of 1.98% in the control group (p = 0.4985). Triple combination therapy with Val/Aml/CTD demonstrated superior blood pressure reduction and a favorable safety profile in patients with essential hypertension inadequately controlled on Val/Aml dual therapy, supporting its use as an effective treatment option in this population. Introduction

Hypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and renal diseases and remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Despite the availability of effective antihypertensive therapies, optimal blood pressure (BP) control is still not achieved in a substantial proportion of patients, representing a persistent global health challenge [2].

Current guidelines recommend initiating antihypertensive treatment with first‐line agents, including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, and diuretics [3, 4]. In recent years, treatment strategies have shifted toward early combination therapy rather than stepwise monotherapy, as reflected in the European Society of Cardiology guidelines, which advocate initial low‐dose combination therapy to achieve faster and more effective BP control [3]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that, in patients who fail to reach target BP with conventional therapy, early intensification using agents with complementary mechanisms of action is more effective than dose escalation of existing medications [5, 6].

Triple fixed‐dose combination therapy has emerged as a promising strategy, offering enhanced BP‐lowering efficacy through synergistic pharmacological effects while improving treatment adherence by reducing pill burden. However, despite the well‐established antihypertensive efficacy and cardiovascular benefits of diuretics [7], their incorporation into combination regimens remains suboptimal in clinical practice, partly due to concerns regarding adverse effects such as electrolyte disturbances [8]. As a result, there remains a need for randomized clinical evidence evaluating the efficacy and safety of diuretic‐based triple combination therapy, particularly in patients with inadequate BP control on dual therapy.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a triple combination regimen consisting of valsartan, amlodipine, and chlorthalidone in patients with essential hypertension who remained inadequately controlled despite treatment with valsartan and amlodipine.

Participants and Methods

Study Design and Protocol

This phase 3 clinical trial was a randomized, double‐blind, multicenter study. Schematic representation of study designs is summarized in Figure 1. The institutional review board at each clinical site approved the study. The individuals aged over 19 years who had confirmed essential hypertension were screened. If an individual was selected as an eligible patient after the screening test, he or she received valsartan/amlodipine 80/5 mg for at least 4 weeks prior to the baseline visit. After treatment with the valsartan/amlodipine 80/5 mg combination therapy, patients were eligible for enrollment if they had a BP of 140 mmHg ≤ MSSBP (mean sitting systolic BP) < 200 mmHg, or, in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) accompanied by cardiovascular disease (CVD) or chronic kidney disease (CKD) accompanied by albuminuria (including microalbuminuria), 130 mmHg ≤ MSSBP < 200 mmHg. CVD included coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, aortic disease, heart failure, or left ventricular hypertrophy in individuals aged ≥50 years. At the baseline visit, the patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone 80/5/12.5 mg and placebo for valsartan/amlodipine 80/5 mg (study group) or placebo for valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone 80/5/12.5 mg and valsartan/amlodipine 80/5 mg (control group), using a double‐dummy design to maintain blinding. After 2 weeks of administration of investigational medicinal products, participants in the study group received valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone 160/5/25 mg and placebo for valsartan/amlodipine 160/5 mg, while those in the control group received placebo for valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone 160/5/25 mg and valsartan/amlodipine 160/5 mg. The patients visited the clinical sites at Week 4 and Week 8 for efficacy and safety assessments.

Schematic representation of study designs. Aml, amlodipine; CTD, chlorthalidone; Val, valsartan.

Study Population

Participants were eligible if they were aged ≥ 19 and if not receiving antihypertensive drugs, had an MSSBP ≥ 160 mmHg and < 200 mmHg. If receiving antihypertensive drugs, the MSSBP had to be ≥ 140 mmHg and < 180 mmHg. If they received antihypertensive drugs and had DM accompanied by CVD or CKD accompanied by albuminuria, the MSSBP had to be ≥ 130 mmHg and < 180 mmHg.

Participants were excluded if the MSSBP difference between arms was greater than 20 mm Hg or if the mean sitting diastolic BP (MSDBP) difference was greater than 10 mmHg. Additional exclusion criteria included specific medical conditions, such as secondary hypertension, cerebrovascular disease (obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, severe obstructive coronary artery disease, aortic coarctation, significant aortic or mitral stenosis, coronary intervention within 6 months, severe cerebrovascular disease within 6 months), serious ocular disease within 6 months, malignancy, and history of angioedema to angiotensin receptor antagonist.

Efficacy and Safety Evaluation

The primary efficacy endpoint was the change in MSSBP from baseline at Week 8 after administration of the investigational medicinal product. The secondary efficacy endpoints included (1) the change from baseline in MSSBP at Week 2 and Week 4; (2) the change from baseline in MSDBP at Week 2, Week 4, and Week 8; (3) BP control rate at Week 2, Week 4, and Week 8; and (4) BP response rate at Week 2, Week 4, and Week 8. BP control rate was defined as the proportion of subjects who achieved MSSBP < 140 mmHg and MSDBP < 90 mmHg in the general population, and as MSSBP < 130 mmHg and MSDBP < 80 mmHg in patients with DM accompanied by CVD or in patients with CKD accompanied by albuminuria. The BP response rate was defined as the proportion of subjects who achieved a reduction of ≥ 20 mmHg in MSSBP or ≥ 10 mmHg in MSDBP.

The safety assessment was conducted by adverse events (AEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), adverse drug reactions (ADRs), vital signs, laboratory test results, physical examination findings, and electrocardiograms for the duration of the study. Reference ranges for laboratory tests varied across participating institutions; therefore, values were defined as abnormally increased or decreased when they exceeded the upper limit or fell below the lower limit of each site's own reference range, respectively, and were classified as clinically meaningful by the responsible investigator.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic characteristics and baseline clinical characteristics were based on all randomized participants. Efficacy analyses were conducted using the full analysis set (FAS). Safety analyses were conducted using the safety set (SS). The per‐protocol set (PPS) included subjects from the FAS who completed the clinical trial without any major protocol deviations.

For continuous data, descriptive statistics are presented and the categorical data are presented as frequencies and ratios (%). For the efficacy analysis, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed using the baseline values as covariates to assess the differences between the groups with a two‐sided significance level of 5%. The least square (LS) mean and standard error (SE) for each administration group, the LS means for the difference between the two groups and the p‐value and 95% two‐sided confidence interval (CI) for both groups were presented. For efficacy variables in the FAS, missing values were imputed using the Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) method; baseline values were not replaced by post‐baseline values. Efficacy variables in the PPS and all safety variables were analyzed using observed cases only. The trial was designed to control the type I error rate for the primary endpoint only. Secondary endpoints and repeated time‐point analyses were pre‐specified but exploratory, and no formal adjustment for multiplicity was applied.

Sample Size Calculation

The objective of this clinical trial was to demonstrate the superiority of valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone 160/5/25 over valsartan/amlodipine 160/5 mg in terms of the change in MSSBP from baseline after 8 weeks of treatment in patients with essential hypertension who did not show an adequate response to 4 weeks of treatment with valsartan/amlodipine 80/5 mg. The sample size was to be determined with reference to studies comparing combination therapy with valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone versus valsartan/amlodipine. However, due to the absence of previous literatures, references were made to studies of agents within the same or similar pharmacological classes that reported changes in MSSBP [9, 10]. All the references compared the change in MSSBP at Week 8 from baseline and demonstrated the superiority of the treatment group over the control group. Accordingly, a meta‐analysis of these studies was conducted to estimate the effect size of the change in MSSBP at Week 8 from baseline between the treatment and control groups in the present clinical trial. Using a random‐effects model, the pooled effect size was calculated to be −0.59. Based on this result, the expected effect size for the change in MSSBP in this trial was assumed to be −0.59. Based on these assumptions, with a 1:1 allocation ratio, a two‐sided significance level of 5%, and a statistical power of 95%, the minimum required sample size was 76 participants per group. Considering a dropout rate of 20%, the required sample size increased to 95 participants per group. Therefore, a total of 190 participants were planned to be enrolled (PASS 21, NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA).

Results

Study Population

A total of 432 patients from 31 sites were screened, and 239 patients who did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria in the eligibility assessment were excluded. Accordingly, 193 patients were randomly assigned, including 92 in the study group and 101 in the control group. All 193 randomized participants were included in the SS. Of the 193 patients randomly assigned to groups in this clinical trial, 191 patients were included in efficacy analysis after excluding 2 patients due to missing efficacy data. The PPS included 151 participants, comprising 68 in the study group and 83 in the control group (Figure 2).

Demographic and Baseline Characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the randomized participants are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the overall study population was 61.30 ± 13.21 years, and 81.87% of participants were male. Smoking status and alcohol consumption patterns were comparable between the study and control groups, with no statistically significant differences observed. Anthropometric measures, including height and body weight, were similar between groups. The mean duration of essential hypertension was approximately 11 years in both groups. Baseline BP values were well balanced, with a MSSBP of 150.44 ± 10.39 mmHg and a MSDBP of 92.80 ± 9.93 mmHg in the overall population. No statistically significant differences were observed between the study and control groups across any baseline demographic or clinical variables, indicating adequate comparability of the treatment groups at baseline.

Primary Efficacy Outcomes

The primary analysis population for efficacy evaluation was the FAS and additional analyses were performed in the PPS, and the final efficacy conclusions were based on the FAS. At Week 8, the mean MSSBP was 130.79 ± 13.39 mmHg in the study group and 141.72 ± 14.28 mmHg in the control group, with a statistically significant difference between the groups (p < 0.0001). The LS mean ± SE change in MSSBP was −19.70 ± 1.31 mmHg in the study group and −8.71 ± 1.26 mmHg in the control group. The LS mean difference ± SE in the change in MSSBP between the treatment groups was −10.99 ± 1.81 mmHg (95% CI, −14.56 to −7.41), indicating a statistically significant greater reduction in the study group compared with the control group (p < 0.0001). The results were similar in the PPS as well. Figure 3 demonstrates changes in MSSBP from baseline at Week8 in the FAS and PPS.

Time course of LS mean change in MSSBP and MSDBP at Week 2, Week 4, and Week 8 is demonstrated in Figure 4. At Week 2, the LS mean ± SE change in MSSBP was −12.57 ± 1.21 mmHg in the study group and −6.51 ± 1.17 mmHg in the control group, with a significant between‐group difference (LS mean difference ± SE, −6.05 ± 1.68 mmHg; 95% CI, −9.38 to −2.73; p = 0.0004). At Week 4, the LS mean ± SE change in MSSBP was −17.34 ± 1.36 mmHg in the study group and −6.71 ± 1.31 mmHg in the control group, again showing a significant between‐group difference (−10.63 ± 1.89 mmHg; 95% CI, −14.36 to −6.90; p < 0.0001).

At Week 2, the LS mean ± SE change in MSDBP was −6.10 ± 0.79 mmHg in the study group and −3.00 ± 0.77 mmHg in the control group, with a significant between‐group difference (LS mean difference ± SE, −3.10 ± 1.10 mmHg; 95% CI, −5.28 to −0.93; p = 0.0054). Safety and Tolerability

Table 3 demonstrates adverse events and adverse drug reactions. All 193 randomized participants (92 in the study group and 101 in the control group) received at least one dose of the investigational medicinal product and were therefore included in SS. The overall incidence of AE was 13.04% in the study group and 9.90% in the control group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.4926). SAEs were reported in 1.98% of participants in the control group but were not reported in the study group; the between‐group difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.4985). The incidence of ADRs for which a causal relationship with the investigational medicinal product could not be excluded was 6.52% in the study group and 4.95% in the control group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.6382). No serious adverse drug reactions were reported. The changes in laboratory values from baseline to Week 8 are presented in Table S1. Compared with the control group, sodium and potassium showed a significantly greater numerical decrease from baseline, and creatinine and uric acid showed a significantly greater numerical increase from baseline, in the study group (all p < 0.05). However, there were no significant between‐group differences in the incidence of laboratory values that were abnormally increased or decreased beyond each site's reference range.

Discussion

The principal findings of the current study are (1) valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone showed significantly greater BP reductions compared with continued dual therapy of valsartan/amlodipine in patients with inadequately controlled hypertension, (2) valsartan/amlodipine/chlorthalidone achieved a better BP control and response rate than valsartan/amlodipine, and (3) these benefits were observed early after treatment initiation and were sustained throughout the study period, without a significant increase in adverse events. This study has several strengths, including its randomized, double‐blind design, the use of an active comparator, and the inclusion of a well‐defined population with uncontrolled hypertension despite standardized dual combination therapy. The consistency of results across multiple efficacy endpoints and time points further strengthens the robustness of the findings.

The recent hypertension guidelines state that most patients require two or more antihypertensive agents to achieve target BP, and therefore recommend initial dual‐combination therapy to facilitate rapid BP control [3, 4, 11]. In patients who fail to achieve adequate BP control with initial dual combination therapy, treatment options include increasing the dose of the existing agents or adding another antihypertensive drug to initiate triple therapy. Recently, several studies have proposed an alternative strategy in which multiple antihypertensive agents are administered in combination at low doses from the outset, rather than following the traditional stepwise approach of initiating monotherapy, increasing the dose, and subsequently adding combination therapy [12, 13, 14]. This strategy has attracted attention because the use of antihypertensive agents with different mechanisms of action may enable rapid BP control while potentially reducing adverse effects [13].

Combination treatments that include drugs having complementary mechanisms of action may provide benefits beyond improved/synergistic efficacy, including improved tolerability and prompt attainment of BP control [5]. In this study, we evaluated the BP lowering effect of adding chlorthalidone in patients whose BP was not adequately controlled with valsartan/amlodipine. The findings of this study provide randomized clinical evidence supporting the stepwise escalation from dual to triple therapy, particularly with the inclusion of a thiazide‐like diuretic. This aligns with guideline‐recommended treatment algorithms and provides practical evidence for clinicians managing patients with resistant or difficult‐to‐control hypertension. The observed between‐group difference in MSSBP of 10.99 mmHg is not only statistically significant but also clinically relevant. Prior epidemiological and interventional studies have consistently shown that even modest reductions in systolic BP are associated with substantial reductions in cardiovascular risk [15]. In this context, the magnitude of BP lowering observed in the present study suggests that the addition of chlorthalidone may translate into meaningful long‐term cardiovascular benefit, particularly in a population with persistent hypertension despite dual therapy.

A notable finding of this study is the rapid onset of antihypertensive effect. Significant between‐group differences were already evident at Week 2 and further increased at Weeks 4 and 8. Early BP control, ideally within 1–3 months, has been increasingly recognized as an important determinant of cardiovascular outcomes [16]. Delayed achievement of target BP is associated with prolonged exposure to elevated hemodynamic load and may attenuate the overall benefit of antihypertensive therapy. Therefore, the early and sustained BP reduction observed with triple therapy in this study supports the strategy of prompt treatment intensification in patients with uncontrolled hypertension.

Beyond MSSBP and MSDBP reduction, the addition of chlorthalidone significantly improved clinically relevant endpoints, including BP control and response rates. At Week 8, more than half of the patients in the triple therapy group achieved BP control, compared with approximately one‐third in the dual combination therapy group. The improved BP control rates observed in this study highlight the practical effectiveness of triple combination therapy in real‐world treatment pathways. Moreover, despite the greater BP reduction, the addition of chlorthalidone did not result in a significant increase in overall AEs, SAEs, or ADRs. Although sodium, potassium, creatinine, and uric acid showed significantly greater numerical changes from baseline in the study group compared with the control group, the incidence of laboratory values that were abnormally increased or decreased beyond each site's reference range did not differ between groups. This finding is clinically relevant, as concerns regarding electrolyte imbalance and metabolic adverse effects often limit the use of thiazide‐like diuretics. The favorable safety profile observed in this study supports the tolerability of chlorthalidone when used as part of combination therapy, particularly within a controlled dosing regimen. Although thiazide diuretics are widely used in hypertension management, chlorthalidone has pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic advantages over conventional thiazides, including a longer half‐life and more sustained antihypertensive effect [17]. The results of the current study can reinforce the role of chlorthalidone as an effective add‐on agent in patients who remain uncontrolled on renin–angiotensin system blocker and calcium channel blocker therapy.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the duration of the study was relatively short, and long‐term cardiovascular outcomes were not assessed. Second, the study population consisted predominantly of male participants, which may limit generalizability to female patients. This imbalance likely reflects the higher prevalence of hypertension requiring combination therapy among men in the recruited clinical settings, rather than a systematic exclusion of female participants during recruitment. Nonetheless, given known sex‐related differences in blood pressure response and drug metabolism, further studies with a more balanced sex distribution are warranted to confirm the generalizability of these findings to female patients. Third, the study did not directly compare chlorthalidone with other diuretics, such as hydrochlorothiazide. Fourth, since no formal adjustment for multiplicity was applied to the secondary endpoints and repeated time‐point analyses, the corresponding p‐values are nominal and should be interpreted with caution. The lack of multiplicity adjustment increases the risk of type I error, and these exploratory findings should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory.

Conclusions

The addition of chlorthalidone to amlodipine/valsartan therapy resulted in rapid, sustained, and clinically meaningful reductions in BP, with improved control rates and a favorable safety profile in patients with inadequately controlled hypertension on dual therapy. These findings support the use of chlorthalidone‐based triple combination therapy as an effective and well‐tolerated treatment strategy in this patient population.
