Effects of 6-week change-of-direction repeated sprint training on explosive power, sprint, and COD performance in U19 basketball players
Authors: Şakir Tüfekçi, Tahir Volkan Aslan, Yalın Aygün, Sacide Tüfekçi, Burak Canpolat, Cemal Gündoğdu, Göktuğ Norman, Hülya Berktaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBasketball requires repeated high-intensity actions such as sprinting, jumping, and change-of-direction (COD) movements. Introduction

Basketball is a high-intensity intermittent team sport that requires players to perform frequent accelerations, decelerations, short sprints, jumps, and rapid change-of-direction (COD) movements throughout a match [1–4]. During competition, U19 basketball players repeatedly execute brief maximal or near-maximal sprint efforts interspersed with multidirectional movements under conditions of limited recovery. Therefore, physical capacities such as lower-limb explosive power, sprint speed, and COD performance are considered critical determinants of basketball performance [5, 6]. Given the multidirectional nature of basketball, COD ability may be more relevant than linear sprint capacity alone for successful performance during offensive and defensive actions.

Within this context, repeated sprint training (RST) has emerged as an effective method for improving these specific performance components [7].

Defined as a series of short sprints (3–7 s) interspersed with brief recovery periods (< 60 s) and a work-to-rest ratio of approximately 1:3, repeated sprint training typically involves three or more sprint repetitions. This training modality has been shown to be highly effective in team sports such as basketball and soccer, where performance in decisive game moments can determine match outcomes [8–10].

For instance, while basketball players may perform between 105 repeated sprints lasting 2–6 s per game, soccer players execute roughly 1,000–1,400 brief high-intensity actions, including sprints every 90 s [10].

Accordingly, repeated sprint training has the potential to optimize explosive power and sprint ability by inducing physiological adaptations that enhance performance in high-intensity intermittent sports such as basketball [11]. These adaptations can improve sport-specific movement patterns—such as acceleration, change of direction, and jumping—thereby increasing players’ on-court effectiveness [12]. Therefore, examining the effects of repeated sprint training on basketball players’ explosive power, sprint speed, and change of direction performance holds substantial importance for both sport science and training methodology [13].

Repeated sprint-based exercises are characterized by several short sprints separated by brief recovery intervals [14]. Such exercises elicit metabolic responses similar to those occurring during actual games, including reductions in muscle pH, phosphocreatine, and ATP levels, activation of anaerobic glycolysis, and significant contributions from the aerobic energy system [9, 15, 16]. Consequently, RST has gained increasing popularity for the training and testing of team-sport athletes [9, 17].

Parallel research has demonstrated that sprint training involving short, maximal or near-maximal bouts (5–30 s) can improve athletes’ ability to repeatedly perform anaerobic efforts [18–20]. Furthermore, sprint training has been shown to enhance VO₂max and aerobic enzyme activity [18, 20–23]. These physiological adaptations align with the established effectiveness of high-intensity interval training in improving both aerobic and anaerobic performance, emphasizing the importance of short, explosive exercise bouts characteristic of sports like basketball [24]. Additionally, by optimizing both aerobic and anaerobic capacities, basketball players are able to sustain high performance levels throughout the duration of a game [25, 26].

It is important to distinguish between agility and change-of-direction (COD) performance, as these concepts are often incorrectly used interchangeably in sports science literature. Agility is defined as a rapid whole-body movement performed in response to an external stimulus and includes perceptual and decision-making components. In contrast, COD performance refers to pre-planned directional movements executed without reactive decision-making [27]. Since basketball involves both reactive and pre-planned multidirectional actions, distinguishing these constructs is essential when evaluating sport-specific performance adaptations. In the present study, the T-test was used to assess pre-planned directional movement ability and therefore should be interpreted as a measure of COD performance rather than agility.

Previous research has demonstrated that repeated sprint training (RST) can improve several performance-related variables in team-sport athletes, including sprint ability, explosive power, and high-intensity exercise capacity [14, 17, 18]. However, many RST protocols used in the literature are primarily based on linear sprinting patterns, despite the fact that basketball performance is characterized by frequent multidirectional movements and rapid changes of direction during both offensive and defensive actions. Recent studies have suggested that multidirectional or COD-based sprint training may induce distinct neuromuscular and mechanical adaptations compared with linear sprint training by increasing movement complexity, motor unit recruitment demands, and intermuscular coordination [28–31].

Although repeated sprint training has been extensively investigated, limited research has specifically examined the effects of COD repeated sprint training protocols in basketball players, particularly in U19 athletes. Furthermore, previous studies have generally focused on isolated performance outcomes such as sprint speed or fatigue resistance, while the combined effects of COD repeated sprint training on explosive power, sprint performance, and COD performance remain insufficiently explored. Given the multidirectional demands of basketball, investigating the effectiveness of COD-based repeated sprint training may provide more sport-specific insights for performance development in U19 basketball players.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of a six-week change-of-direction repeated sprint training program on explosive power, sprint, and COD performance in U19 basketball players. It was hypothesized that the intervention would improve these performance parameters.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study employed a pretest–posttest control group experimental design. Twenty male U19 basketball players aged between 18 and 19 years who regularly participated in basketball training voluntarily took part in the study. The inclusion criteria for participation were as follows: (a) having at least three years of basketball experience; (b) having no history of injury that could affect performance within the last 6 months; and (c) consistent participation throughout the study period. Exclusion criteria included participants who sustained injuries during the intervention or those who chose to withdraw before completing the study.

Sample size calculation

Before participant selection, the required sample size was determined using the G*Power software [32]. Based on an alpha level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, and a medium effect size (ES = 0.25), the calculation indicated that at least nine players per group would be sufficient. These parameters were selected according to conventional recommendations for behavioral and sports science research and in line with Cohen’s effect size classifications. The analysis indicated that a minimum of nine participants per group would be sufficient.

Initially, 25 athletes were enrolled in the study; however, three did not meet the inclusion criteria and two failed to attend training sessions or testing, resulting in their exclusion. Consequently, in accordance with the GPower analysis and randomization procedures, the final sample consisted of 20 participants—10 in the experimental group and 10 in the control group.

Participants were randomly assigned to groups using a simple randomization method based on a computer-generated random sequence. Allocation was performed by an independent researcher to minimize selection bias.

Ethics approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Inonu University Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences (Approval No: 2024/6279; Date: 24 September 2024). All participants were informed about the objectives, procedures, and requirements of the study and provided written informed consent prior to participation. This randomized controlled trial was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines.

Research protocol

Prior to the commencement of the study, participants underwent anthropometric assessments (height and body weight) and motor performance tests, including the Countermovement Jump (CMJ), Squat Jump (SJ), 30-meter sprint, and T-test, which were administered as pretests. The training program was implemented during the team’s preparatory phase and spanned six weeks, with training sessions scheduled three times per week. The intervention began after a two-week general preparation period. The six-week duration of the intervention was selected based on previous research demonstrating that short-term repeated sprint and high-intensity training interventions lasting approximately 4–6 weeks are sufficient to induce meaningful neuromuscular and performance-related adaptations in young athletes [18, 20, 21]. Previous studies have reported improvements in explosive power, sprint ability, and anaerobic performance following similar short-term training durations, suggesting that a six-week intervention period may be adequate to elicit measurable adaptations in basketball-specific performance parameters. Both groups continued their regular team training sessions, which consisted of technical and tactical drills conducted three to four times per week. The study was conducted during the preseason period.

In addition to the COD repeated sprint training sessions, participants continued their regular basketball team practices consisting of technical and tactical training conducted three to four times per week. No official matches were played during the intervention period due to the preseason phase of the season. Training sessions were scheduled on non-consecutive days to allow adequate recovery between repeated sprint sessions.

Athletes in the experimental group participated in change-of-direction repeated sprint training sessions three times per week (on non-consecutive days). The frequency of three COD repeated sprint training sessions per week was selected based on previous repeated sprint training studies demonstrating that 2–3 weekly sessions are effective for inducing neuromuscular and performance adaptations in team-sport athletes while allowing sufficient recovery between sessions [18, 31, 33]. In addition, training sessions were scheduled on non-consecutive days to minimize excessive fatigue accumulation. All training sessions and performance tests were conducted on an indoor basketball court with a standard wooden surface. All performance tests were conducted at the same time of day (± 1 h) for all participants to minimize circadian variation. Before each training session and testing session, participants performed a standardized 10-minute warm-up consisting of light running, dynamic stretching, and mobility exercises. Each session consisted of 2–4 sets of 4–8 repetitions over sprint distances of 20–40 m. Training intensity was controlled by instructing participants to perform all sprint efforts at maximal intensity (100%). To ensure maximal effort during training sessions and performance testing, participants received standardized verbal encouragement from the research staff throughout all sprint trials. Performance tests were conducted using electronic timing systems to provide objective measurement accuracy. Passive rest intervals of 30 s were provided between repetitions and 4 min between sets (Table 2).

Athletes in the control group continued their regular basketball practices and did not engage in any additional sprint-based exercises. After completion of the six-week training program, the same motor performance tests were administered again as posttests for both groups, marking the end of the data collection phase.

Internal training load variables such as rating of perceived exertion (RPE) and external load monitoring were not systematically recorded during the intervention period. However, all training sessions were supervised by the research team to ensure adherence to the prescribed training intensity and protocol structure.

The sprint distances used in the present study (20–40 m) were selected based on the movement demands of basketball, in which players frequently perform short maximal sprints interspersed with rapid directional changes over relatively short court distances. Previous repeated sprint training studies in basketball and team-sport athletes have similarly used sprint distances within this range to replicate sport-specific movement patterns and physiological demands [11, 31, 33]. The detailed structure of the training protocol is presented in Table 2, which systematically outlines the weekly progression of the repeated sprint training program in terms of sprint distance, repetitions, sets, rest intervals, and training intensity. The program was designed to progressively increase sprint distance and total workload volume over the six-week period. The initial two weeks involved short 20-meter sprints, followed by progressive increases to 30 m and 40 m in subsequent weeks. The incremental rise in repetitions and sets was intended to enhance the athletes’ speed endurance and sprint capacity.

Passive recovery periods of 30 s between repetitions and four minutes be-tween sets were incorporated to allow partial replenishment of phosphocreatine stores while maintaining high-intensity effort demands—thus providing a sport-specific conditioning model aligned with the intermittent, high-intensity nature of basketball.

Data collection instruments

The performance tests were administered in the following order: countermovement jump, squat jump, 30-m sprint, and T-test

Height and body weight measurements

Height and body weight were measured using standard anthropometric procedures with participants barefoot and wearing light sports clothing.

Countermovement jump (CMJ) test

Before performing the CMJ and SJ tests, players completed two to three submaximal practice jumps as a specific warm-up. During the test, participants stood on the jump mat with hands on hips, descended to a squat position with approximately 90° knee flexion, and then immediately jumped vertically as high as possible, landing with both feet simultaneously on the mat. Jump height was measured using an electronic jump mat (Smart Jump; Fusion Sport, Australia). Each athlete performed two trials, separated by 3 min of rest, and the higher value (in centimeters) was recorded for analysis [34].

Squat jump (SJ) test

In the SJ test, as in the CMJ, no arm swing was permitted. Participants began standing on the platform, then descended to a squat position (approximately 90–100° knee flexion) and held the position for 3 s before performing a maximal vertical jump. Literature indicates that a 3-second pause is the most commonly used standard for SJ testing [35, 36].

Squat jumps were measured using an electronic jump mat (Smart Jump; Fusion Sport, Australia). Each participant performed two trials, separated by 3 min of rest, and the best performance value was recorded.

30-meter sprint test

To assess sprint performance, a 30-meter sprint test was administered. Participants began from a standing start positioned 50 cm behind the first photocell gate and were instructed to start at their own discretion once ready. They were encouraged to complete the 30-meter distance as fast as possible and to continue running 5 m beyond the finish line to prevent deceleration before completing the sprint. Sprint times were measured in seconds using a dual-beam electronic timing system (Smart Speed; Fusion Sport, Australia). Each participant performed two trials, with 3 min of rest between runs to ensure maximal effort. The best performance time was recorded [37].

T- test

The T-Test was conducted on a setup forming a “T” shape, with 10 m of distance between cones in both the length and width directions and four designated con-tact points. The objective was to complete the movement sequence between the cones as quickly as possible. Participants began at cone A, sprinted forward to cone B, touched its top with the right hand, then side-shuffled left to cone C to touch it with the left hand. They then side-shuffled right to cone D to touch it with the right hand and finally side-shuffled left back to cone B, touching it before backpedaling to cone A to finish the test [38]. The test was performed twice, and the best performance time (in seconds) was recorded using an electronic timing system (Smart Speed; Fusion Sport, Australia).

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all variables satisfied the assumption of normality. Therefore, parametric tests were used for all analyses.

Between-group differences (experimental vs. control) for pre- and posttest values were analyzed using independent samples t-tests, while within-group changes (pretest vs. posttest) were evaluated using paired samples t-tests. The level of statistical significance was set at p < .05 for all analyses.

Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of changes in performance variables. Effect sizes were interpreted according to Hopkins et al. (2009) as trivial (0.0–0.2), small (0.2–0.6), moderate (0.6–1.2), large (1.2–2.0), and very large (> 2.0). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean (𝑥̄), standard deviation (SD), minimum–maximum values, and percentage change (%).

All results were summarized in tables, with significant differences indicated by *(p < .05).

Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of a six-week COD repeated sprint training program on explosive power, sprint, and COD performance in U19 basketball players. The main findings demonstrated that the intervention produced meaningful improvements in lower-limb explosive power, as evidenced by significant between-group differences in CMJ and SJ performance. In contrast, although favorable within-group improvements were observed in sprint and COD performance, these adaptations did not translate into statistically significant between-group differences. These findings suggest that COD repeated sprint training may be particularly effective for enhancing explosive neuromuscular performance, whereas improvements in more complex motor abilities such as sprint and COD performance may require longer intervention durations or more specific multidimensional training stimuli.

The results suggest that repeated sprint training may enhance the explosive force production capacity of lower-limb muscle groups. The significant increases in CMJ and SJ performance observed in the experimental group may be attributed to neuromuscular adaptations induced by high-intensity contractions and short recovery intervals during sprint efforts. These findings are consistent with the results of Burgomaster et al. (2005), Dawson et al. (1998), and MacDougall et al. (1998), who reported that short-term, high-intensity exercise protocols improve intramuscular phosphocreatine stores and anaerobic enzyme activity, thereby enhancing explosive performance. Moreover, meta-analyses have demonstrated that plyometric training positively influences muscle power, linear sprint speed, and change-of-direction ability [39]. The mechanism underlying these im-provements likely involves enhanced neuromuscular activation, increased motor unit recruitment, and greater firing frequency [39]. Similarly, research indicating that plyometric training increases jump height suggests that repeated sprint training may improve vertical jump performance through comparable physiological mechanisms [40].

The present findings are partially consistent with previous studies reporting improvements in explosive performance following multidirectional repeated sprint training interventions in basketball players. Recent multidirectional sprint training studies have similarly reported improvements in explosive performance following COD-based repeated sprint interventions in basketball players. In support of this interpretation, Attene et al. (2016) reported similar improvements in CMJ and SJ performance following multidirectional repeated sprint training, demonstrating that such protocols not only stimulate energy system adaptations but also enhance the elastic energy recovery capacity of the lower extremity muscles [31]. These adaptations are primarily associated with improved neuromuscular connectivity and greater synchronization of motor unit activation [18, 20, 21, 41]. Additionally, studies examining the relationship between sprint and jump performance have shown that in-creases in maximal strength and vertical jump height are directly associated with improvements in sprint performance [42]. Accordingly, repeated sprint training may enhance vertical jump performance through improvements in muscle–tendon mechanics and intermuscular coordination [28]. Additionally, COD-based sprint training may increase eccentric braking demands and horizontal force production during deceleration and reacceleration phases, which are considered critical biomechanical components of basketball-specific movements. Repeated exposure to these multidirectional force demands may contribute to improvements in lower-limb explosive performance and movement efficiency.

Although improvements were observed in the 30 m sprint and T-tests, these changes did not reach statistical significance between groups. However, the near-significant trend in sprint performance (p = .085), together with the significant within-group improvement in the experimental group, suggests that repeated sprint training may enhance short-term acceleration capacity in youth basketball players. This adaptation may reflect neuromuscular and mechanical improvements resulting from repeated maximal sprint efforts performed over six weeks. Similarly, the significant pre- to post-test improvement in T-test performance within the experimental group may indicate favorable adaptations related to change-of-direction ability, movement coordination, and neuromuscular control. Nevertheless, the lack of statistically significant between-group differences in sprint and COD performance suggests that these more complex motor abilities may require a longer intervention period or a more specific multidimensional training stimulus. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that while increases in maximal squat strength correlate with enhanced sprint and jump performance, the low variance explained by this relationship suggests that additional factors beyond strength contribute to these performance outcomes [43]. Similarly, research on short-term (4–6 week) sprint interventions has reported limited improvements in sprint performance [11]. Attene et al. (2016) also observed that both unidirectional and multidirectional sprint programs failed to produce significant differences in sprint times but led to improvements in fatigue index and ratings of perceived exertion (RPE) [31]. Thus, the findings of the present study align closely with those of previous research.

The six-week training program, implemented three times per week and characterized by short recovery intervals between high-intensity efforts, is consistent with the physiological demands of basketball [33, 44].

Such a training structure likely enhanced anaerobic energy production by repeatedly activating the phosphocreatine system. Furthermore, short rest intervals between sprints may have increased intramuscular lactate accumulation, contributing to improved lactate tolerance and metabolic endurance [9, 15].

Repeated sprint training may enhance the ability to sustain high-intensity efforts with minimal performance decrement, thereby improving anaerobic power, fatigue resistance, and recovery rate [45, 46]. Empirical evidence indicates that this training modality is particularly beneficial in intermittent team sports such as football and basket-ball, as it helps players maintain a high tempo throughout the game and minimizes performance decline between sprints [45, 47, 48]. Additionally, repeated sprint training improves both the efficiency of the phosphagen system and the recovery capacity of the aerobic system, leading to marked gains in overall physical endurance [49, 50].

Although the improvement in COD performance was not statistically significant in the present study, the reduction in T-test times suggests a positive adaptation in terms of change-of-direction speed and motor control. This effect may be attributed to enhanced neuromuscular coordination and increased efficiency of muscle spindle and proprioceptive feedback systems which are considered important mechanisms underlying improvements in change-of-direction ability and explosive performance [39, 40].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, the intervention period was limited to six weeks, which may not have been sufficient to produce larger adaptations in sprint and COD performance. Third, internal and external training load variables, such as rating of perceived exertion (RPE) and external workload monitoring, were not systematically assessed during the intervention period. Finally, although participants continued similar basketball training routines, external variables such as individual recovery quality, sleep patterns, nutritional habits, and daily physical activity could not be fully controlled. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes, longer intervention periods, and more comprehensive training load monitoring are recommended.

Practical applications

The findings of the present study suggest that COD repeated sprint training may be incorporated into basketball conditioning programs to improve lower-limb explosive power in U19 basketball players. Based on the current protocol, coaches may consider implementing COD repeated sprint sessions 2–3 times per week during the preseason period in combination with regular technical and tactical basketball training.

The use of short sprint distances (20–40 m) combined with multidirectional movement patterns may help replicate the movement demands of basketball competition while enhancing neuromuscular performance. However, because improvements in sprint and COD performance were limited between groups, coaches should integrate COD repeated sprint training alongside other sport-specific speed and agility-oriented training methods.

To minimize excessive fatigue accumulation, repeated sprint sessions should preferably be scheduled on non-consecutive training days and appropriately balanced with technical, tactical, and recovery sessions throughout the weekly training schedule.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that a six-week COD repeated sprint training program may contribute to improvements in explosive power, sprint, and COD performance in U19 basketball players. However, when compared with regular basketball training, statistically significant between-group differences were observed only for CMJ and SJ performance. Therefore, the superiority of the intervention appears to be primarily related to lower-limb explosive power development. Given the relatively small sample size and short intervention duration, further studies with larger samples and longer training periods are needed to better clarify the effects of COD repeated sprint training on sprint and COD performance.
