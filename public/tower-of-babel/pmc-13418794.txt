A comparison between CARLIN and DNA Typewriter in CRISPR-mediated lineage tracing
Authors: Fengshuo Liu, Xiang Zhang, Yipeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCARLIN and DNA Typewriter are two major breakthroughs in CRISPR-based lineage tracing technology. It is essential to understand the potential and performance of these methods in lineage tracing, which provides important guidance on experimental design.ResultsIn this study, we systematically compare these two strategies using a unified stochastic simulation framework with known ground-truth lineages. By explicitly modeling CRISPR editing dynamics, barcode evolution, and cell division processes, the framework enables quantitative benchmarking of lineage reconstruction accuracy across diverse experimental parameter regimes. Both methods are evaluated using multiple accuracy metrics, including Robinson–Foulds accuracy and triplet accuracy, allowing a comprehensive assessment of lineage reconstruction performance under various editing probabilities, sampling depths, and lineage lengths.ConclusionsDNA Typewriter consistently outperforms CARLIN in lineage reconstruction accuracy when sufficient numbers of recording targets are used, particularly in more cell divisions. Sequential and ordered recording in DNA Typewriter substantially reduces ambiguity in lineage inference compared to unordered CRISPR barcode editing. CARLIN’s lineage-recording potential exhausts rapidly under continuous induction, limiting its effectiveness in long-term lineage tracing. Triplet accuracy provides a more permissive and informative metric than Robinson–Foulds accuracy, especially under partial sampling scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-026-06496-y.

Introduction

Lineage tracing aims to reconstruct ancestry relationships among cells and connect those relationships to cell states, fate decisions, and clonal dynamics. While single-cell omics provides high-dimensional snapshots of cellular state, it does not directly encode true ancestry. Sequencing-based lineage tracing addresses this by coupling cell-state readouts with heritable molecular records that can be read out retrospectively [1–3]. CRISPR-based molecular recorders are particularly attractive because they can generate diverse, accumulating edits in situ and can be captured alongside transcriptomes in single-cell sequencing libraries [4, 5]. However, recorder designs differ substantially in how lineage information is written, and these differences translate into distinct error modes during lineage inference, making it difficult to quantify reconstruction error rates or to compare recorder designs fairly when ground truth is unavailable [2].

Two representative strategies illustrate this design space: (i) scar-based recorders that accumulate unordered indels and (ii) ordered recorders that append edits sequentially. Scar-based recorders such as CRISPR array repair lineage tracing (CARLIN) [4] rely on Cas9-induced double-strand breaks (DSBs) and stochastic repair to generate heritable “scars” in a target array, with Cas9 activity typically controlled by Doxycycline (Dox) induction [6]. CARLIN alleles are captured as transcripts and sequenced together with scRNA-seq, allowing lineage alleles to be paired with cell-state information. In practice, several features of scar-based recording can reduce usable information for inference. First, inter-target deletions can erase earlier edits and collapse distinguishable allele states, reducing effective diversity and increasing ambiguity. Approaches that bias repair toward insertions (e.g., TdT-assisted editing) can partially mitigate this loss by increasing allele diversity [7]. Second, because scars do not intrinsically encode event order, independently evolving lineages can converge to identical or highly similar alleles (mutation homoplasy), causing incorrect pairings and complicating phylogenetic reconstruction [8, 9]. Third, because individual targets become irreversibly mutated, the number of editable sites decreases over time, limiting the duration over which continuous recording can be sustained without sacrificing resolution.

Ordered recorders aim to directly encode temporal information to reduce ambiguity in inference. DNA Typewriter uses prime editing on a “DNA tape” composed of a sequence of partial CRISPR target sites [10, 11]. Initially, only the first site is active; each pegRNA (prime-editing guide RNA) mediates insertion of a k-mer that contains a pegRNA-specific insert barcode (insBC) and a short key that terminates the current site and activates the next. Because edits proceed sequentially along the tape, the temporal order of events can be recovered by the physical order of insertions, potentially reducing ambiguity in event ordering while avoiding DSB-associated inter-target deletions [10]. A related ordered-insertion design is peCHYRON [12]. Although these ordered systems are conceptually appealing, it remains unclear how much improvement they provide in reconstruction accuracy under realistic constraints (e.g., sampling depth, lineage length, and editing-probability budgets) without access to ground-truth lineages.

A major bottleneck in evaluating lineage recorders is validation. In most in vivo applications the true lineage is unknown, so reconstruction pipelines are rarely benchmarked quantitatively and error rates are difficult to estimate [2]. Moreover, observed performance depends jointly on recorder-specific editing statistics (e.g., deletion profiles, depletion of editable sites, insertion diversity) and the reconstruction algorithm, making it hard to disentangle recorder design from inference performance. This motivates simulation-based benchmarking, where ancestry is known by construction and both recorder dynamics and reconstruction procedures can be evaluated under controlled conditions. Pilarski et al. [13] performed simulations and assessed, using the Bayesian framework, the joint inference of time-scaled cell phylogenies and phylodynamic parameters from CRISPR lineage recordings with random or sequential edits. The simulated recording media in [13] have fixed number of targets whose edits take values within a finite state space, and edits occur in either sequential manner or non-sequential manner. The sequential-recording scenario is similar to DNA tape in Typewriter method, but the non-sequential-recording scenario is unlike CARLIN’s where inter-target large-segment deletions happen very often.

Here we benchmark two representative CRISPR recording strategies–CARLIN (unordered indel scars) [4, 6] and DNA Typewriter (ordered insertional records) [10, 11]–using ground-truth simulations. Building on our previously published stochastic lineage-tracing simulator [14], we extend the framework in two directions that are essential for an apples-to-apples comparison. First, we incorporate realistic CARLIN-specific constraints by using the actual designed reference architecture and modeling Dox-controlled editing with DSB repair outcomes that include random target cuts with cutting hotspots, indels, inter-target deletions, and progressive depletion of editable targets as sites are mutated or deleted. Second, we incorporate ordered DNA-tape recording by simulating sequential insertional edits along a tape and enabling order-aware reconstruction that exploits the physical ordering of recorded events. The simulator generates barcode evolution along a known cell-division tree and supports systematic sweeps over editing regimes and sampling depths, enabling direct evaluation of reconstruction performance under matched experimental conditions.

We evaluate reconstructed trees using complementary accuracy metrics. Robinson-Foulds (RF) accuracy provides a strict clade-matching measure that requires exact agreement of ancestral partitions. It was later generalized by Smith [15] that assigns a similarity score between two clades. Triplet accuracy quantifies whether relative relatedness among triplets of sampled cells is preserved, directly represents local ancestry relationships used in downstream biological interpretation [8]. A related work is found in [16] where Mulberry and Stadler performed a simulation on DNA Typewriter for lineage tracing, where RF distance and triplet score were used to evaluate the accuracy of rebuilt lineage tree. One major difference in our program is that it simulates the fundamental mechanisms of DNA tape evolution such as stochastic insBC insertions, DNA tape contractions, tape-specific insertion probabilities, etc., and parameters are tuned based on real experimental data. Another related work is [17] where Seidel et al developed a simulation and inference tool called SciPhy for lineage tracing using sequential genetic data, and applied it to real experimental data obtained via DNA Typewriter. SciPhy utilizes a Bayesian phylogenetic approach to estimate time-scaled phylogenies, and uses weighted RF metric and phylogenetic information metric (PI) to assess the quality of rebuilt lineage. Besides the different lineage reconstruction algorithm in this work, we also observe that triplet accuracy is a more informative metric. Furthermore, this work provides a controlled comparison of CARLIN and DNA Typewriter and yields quantitative guidance for recorder design and experimental planning.

Results

The primary goal of this work is to compare the two representative CRISPR recording strategies–CARLIN and DNA Typewriter - in lineage tracing. The phylogenetic tree is reconstructed using a NBJNF method (Neighbor Joining with No Filtering) [14] based on 1) CARLIN barcode alignment score (CARLIN), 2) DNA Tape alignment score (Typewriter), and/or 3) a combination of respective alignment scores from CARLIN and DNA Tape with equal weights (Dual). Each root cell is equipped with a CARLIN reference barcode and a DNA Tape, so that the comparison of lineage tracing accuracy from these two approaches is based on the same phylogenetic tree. The CARLIN barcode setting remains invariant - it consists of 10 editable targets that exactly matches the reference barcode in [4]. The CARLIN barcode is mutated with moderate probability, simulating medium Dox induction so that its potential does not exhaust too fast (see Sect. CARLIN reference barcode configuration and editing). The DNA tape evolves according to features and pattern observed from actual experimental data [10] (see Sect. DNA Typewriter configuration). Targets in DNA tape are all distinguishable.

In actual scRNA-seq experiments, common challenges are low RNA capture efficiency (dropout events), high technical noise, sample dissociation, etc., so that the well-sequenced alleles for analysis account for only a small fraction of a tissue, and the lineage tree is often a partial binomial tree. In this situation it is necessary to distinguish between paired nodes and singleton nodes. A paired/dividing node in the lineage tree is an internal node that has two direct children. A node having only one child is called a singleton node. In the case of full binomial tree, i.e., sample size is 100% (ss=1), every internal node is a paired node. If the sample size is less than 100%, the lineage tree is a partial binomial tree where some internal nodes are paired nodes while others are singleton. Paired nodes contain lineage information on how descendant cells are associated. Therefore, the accuracy of matched paired nodes is an important metric on the quality of rebuilt lineage (see Fig. 23 in [14]).

So, for the simulation with 5 cell divisions, we set ss=0.5, i.e., 50% of leaf cells are randomly sampled, and then compare lineage tracing accuracy of CARLIN, Typewriter, and dual algorithm. This program can also simulate asynchronous cell division and/or cell death/apoptosis, whose effect is analogous to the stochastic divisions in a heterogeneous population [13]. Each cell/node has a probability of clive at each cycle to survive into the next cycle. If a cell is marked dead, all of its descendants are marked dead. In this way the lineage tree will become a partial binomial tree with fewer internal nodes but high percentage of singletons, see Fig. 23 in [14]. Triplet accuracy is more informative than RF accuracy

The Robinson–Foulds accuracy that we adopt here is a strict metric because two clades are considered as a match if they both belong to the same generation and have the same leaf members. It contains not only the clade information but also the information of when a bi-partition occurs in a tree, though its numeric value tends to be small. A generalized RF metric called Phylogenetic Information (PI) was later proposed by Smith [15] that assigns a similarity score between two clades. For larger lineage trees, sometimes we are more concerned with the order of association of any three leaf nodes rather than the match of a clade, or the exact time(generation number) a bi-partition occurs. Triplet accuracy [8] appears to be a good metric in this case. In preceding sections with small trees, we see that triplet accuracy generally has a higher numeric value than RF accuracy. To obtain a comparison for larger trees between these two measurements, for each simulated lineage both RF accuracy and triplet accuracy are calculated via CARLIN and DNA Typewriter with moderate editing probability. Due to the heavy computational load for triplet accuracy (see Sect. Computational count), we are able to obtain the result with small sampling proportion ss. The comparison between RF and Triplet accuracy of CARLIN and Typewriter for 11 cell cycles with sampling size ss=0.05 is shown in Fig. 11. Methods

CARLIN reference barcode configuration and editing

In the simulation program actual CARLIN reference barcode (276-bp) is used. The hot spot of cut locus is 3bp 5’-upstream PAM in each of the 10 distinguishable targets, with small probabilities at 4bp, 5bp, 6bp upstream PAM, respectively (given that a cut occurs, at 3bp-60%, at 4bp-25%, at 5bp-10%, at 6bp-5%). Controlled Dox induction is reflected by a parameter mupb which is the probability of a cut at each target (if the target is still editable). The value mupb=0.2 represents low mutation/cut probability while mupb=0.4 represents moderate cut probability. If two or more cuts occur in the same round of editing, there is a probability of lgdelprob (default value 0.4) that inter-target deletion occurs. In this situation, two cut loci are randomly chosen from the cuts where the intermediate barcode segment is deleted.

At each cut locus, there will be a left end and a right end of the DSB (double-strand break). To process each end as cell tries to repair it, with respective probabilities conditional on the occurrence of a cut, there will be 0,1,2,3,4,5bp deletion (80%, 10%, 5%, 2.5%, 1.5%, 1%); and after that, with respective probabilities (90%, 5%, 2.5%, 1.25%, 0.75%, 0.5%) there will be 0,1,2,3,4,5bp insertion. Thus indels can occur, and mutation length can vary between 10bp deletion to 10bp insertion. (See the insertion/deletion data in Fig. 1D in [4]). If a target is uncut, or is cut but is perfectly repaired, then it is still editable in subsequent mutations. Otherwise, this target is mutated and will not be cut any more. However, it is possible that this target will be deleted if it lies in an inter-target segment deletion in future mutations.

We adjust parameters in the simulation program so that the mutation pattern will match that from actual experimental data. mupb is set at 0.2 to mimic low Dox induction. 5 division rounds are performed assuming a span of a 96-hour window, and this simulation is repeated 10 times. DNA Tape editing and evolution

The DNA tape consists of one or more tandem arrays of CRISPR-Cas9 target sites. The target sites on each array are edited in a sequential order, starting from the first site. Each edit includes an insertion of pegRNA-specific barcode (insBC) as well as a 3-bp key that activates the next monomer (so that the next target site in sequence becomes active). Because genome editing is sequential in this scheme, the temporal order of recorded events can be read out by their physical order along the array. DNA Typewriter configuration

In this simulation program the editing of each active site in a certain target in a certain cell is controlled by several parameters where editP is a primary one (editing probability with a default value 0.13). It is amplified by a random factor cellvital that introduces randomness in cell vitality. Currently cellvital is set as a random number in (0, 2.5) for each cell in each division cycle. We keep it as a separate factor for flexibility so that different functions can be implemented in future exploration. Each target has at most one active site, and active sites on different tandem arrays are edited independently. Not all targets have equal vitality because “some integrated tapes are more amenable to prime editing than others” [10], and this is controlled by a random but target-specific parameter tarvital ranging from 0.9 to 1.1. In each editing event, there are three possible outcomes, 1) the target site is skipped (unedited), 2) one insBC is inserted, 3) two insBCs are inserted with a much smaller probability (primarily controlled by 0.1editP). Although the tandem arrays are initially designed to have the same number of target sites, it is likely that a few arrays are corrupted and hence have shorter length [10]. The probability that a site is corrupted is controlled by a parameter corrP=0.125. As a result, some targets may have contractions. All these features are implemented in our simulation program.

These parameters are tuned so that the baseline design is closest to the empirical data [10]. Although the editing probability on a single target during short period of time is modest, the editing occasions become considerably high for longer editing duration with more targets. We use the same experimental design as in [10] to tune these parameters, that is, 13 targets are used, each with a tandem length of 5, and 19 insert BCs are applied for an editing duration of 20 cell divisions. Due to pre-existing heterogeneity in the TAPE-1 lentiviral library before integration, a few targets may have contractions (shorter tandem length). Lineage tree reconstruction

Lineage reconstruction algorithm is a very popular research topic in this literature. Some major classes include distance-based algorithms such as neighbor-joining [18]; parsimony-based [19] such as Cassiopeia [9]; and Bayesian inference methods [17]. In most of those algorithms, mutations need to be classified into distinguishable characters with finite mutational states, which not only simplifies simulation and empirical studies but also enables theoretical analysis [20]. In CRISPR barcode evolution process such as CARLIN, the barcodes are cut in a particular manner with certain level of randomness, and DSB repair will bring in another source of randomness. Mutated targets may differ largely, or differ slightly by a few bps. Theoretically there are infinite possibilities. How to classify mutated targets into finite states remains a question. In [14] several algorithms were tested to pair sibling nodes and rebuild parent barcodes, such as Root Matching Prioritized with reference to root barcode (RMP), Root Matching Prioritized without reference to root barcode (RMPNF), neighbor joining method with reference to root barcode (NBJ), and neighbor joining method without referring to root barcode (NBJNF). It was shown that NBJNF method generally yields higher accuracy than the other methods. Furthermore, adding a second CARLIN barcode does not significantly increase accuracy because the two barcodes sometimes yield competing information due to randomness in editing. Therefore, in this work we use just one CARLIN barcode and apply the NBJNF method to rebuild lineage.

The NBJNF method applies directly to DNA tape alleles in Typewriter method (where the root barcode is empty). The advantage that DNA Typewriter records temporal order of edits is naturally reflected when rebuilding the parent DNA tape. As seen in Fig. 18, the parent DNA tape consists of edited sites that are shared by both children alleles. So, the design of DNA Typewriter makes it easier to rebuild parent DNA tapes in lineage reconstruction. A pseudo code on NBJNF algorithm is provided in Algorithm 1, where the pairwise comparison of CARLIN alleles requires barcode alignment using dynamic programming. The pairwise comparison of Typewriter alleles uses site-by-site comparison, hence faster.Fig. 18Rebuild parent DNA tape

A dual algorithm that uses both CARLIN barcode and DNA tape to rebuild lineage is also tested. In this algorithm, to calculate the similarity score between two nodes, the two CARLIN alleles are aligned and the score is normalized using (CARLIN score)/(Max CARLIN score), and the two DNA Tape alleles are aligned and the score is normalized too (Tape similarity score)/(Max Tape similarity score). Lineage tree comparison and tracing accuracy

After the parameters are properly tuned and the phylogenetic tree is reconstructed, the next task is to evaluate the accuracy of rebuilt lineage. This is the primary advantage and value of simulation because the simulated phylogenetic tree can be used as a ground-truth to evaluate the accuracy of rebuilt lineages using a variety of algorithms. In [14] we used a RF-like metric to compare two binomial trees in a strict sense in that two clades (a clade is a set of all last-generation-descendants originated from an internal node) are considered as a match if they contain exactly the same members and the two internal nodes belong to the same generation. This RF metric is adopted again in this work, and its value equals (number of matched clades)/(total number of clades). In case of full binomial tree with depth n\ge 2, the total number of clades is equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^n-2$$\end{document}, where the root clade is excluded. Interested readers are referred to Figs. 22 and 23 in [14]. This metric is stricter than the regular RF metric in that the matched internal nodes should belong to the same generation, but on the other hand, it provides one of the important aspects to assess the quality of rebuilt lineage, hence can be used as a lower-bound on tracing accuracy.

However, very often in lineage tracing we are mostly interested in the order of association of cells in the last generation. As seen in Fig. 19A, we pick any three cells from the last generation, namely a, b, c, and assume that b, c are closer relatives than a. The rebuilt lineage in Fig. 19B correctly recovers the order of association of these three cells. Fig. 19C and D show scenarios of rebuilt lineages that do not correctly recover this order of association. All possible 3-combinations are checked to calculate the percentage of correctly recovered triplets, and this metric is called triplet accuracy. To be more specific, the triplet accuracy equals (number of correct triplets)/(total number of triplets). In [8] (see Box 2) the complement of triplet accuracy is adopted, that is, the percentage of wrongly recovered triplets which is called the triplet distance. Suppose there are n sampled cells, then the number of distinct triplets is n(n-1)(n-2)/6. In the case of full binomial tree, if the RF accuracy is 100%, then the triplet accuracy is 100% too.Fig. 19Triplet accuracy. A Ground truth lineage. B. Discussion

CRISPR-based lineage recorders provide a scalable route to reconstructing cell ancestry from sequencing data, yet the structure of the recorded signal strongly determines how much information is retained for downstream inference. Using a unified ground-truth simulation framework, we compared CARLIN and DNA Typewriter across a range of induction/editing regimes, sampling depths, and recorder design parameters. The CARLIN module simulates barcode cut and DSB repair in a stochastic manner that is different from widely used simplified models where mutation events are classified into finite states [9, 21, 22]. The Typewriter module simulates stochastic additions of insBC’s and possible DNA Tape contractions. Both methods are evaluated using multiple accuracy metrics, including Robinson–Foulds (RF) accuracy and triplet accuracy, allowing a comprehensive assessment of lineage reconstruction performance under varying editing probability, sampling depths, and lineage lengths. Conclusions

In this study, we systematically compare two CRISPR-based lineage tracing strategies–CARLIN and DNA Typewriter–using a unified stochastic simulation framework with known ground-truth lineages. By explicitly modeling CRISPR editing dynamics, barcode evolution, and cell division processes, the framework enables quantitative benchmarking of lineage reconstruction accuracy across diverse experimental parameter regimes. Across these benchmarks, Typewriter can substantially outperform CARLIN when the DNA tape contains sufficient recording targets, particularly for longer lineages where CARLIN’s editable sites are progressively depleted under sustained induction. This advantage is consistent with the core design principle of ordered recorders: sequential insertions preserve earlier information and encode temporal order directly, reducing ambiguity in reconstruction compared to unordered scar accumulation. This simulation framework also applies to related recorders such as GESTALT [23], scGESTALT [24], DARLIN [7] for scar-based technologies, and PEtracer [25] and peCHYRON [12] for sequential recorder systems. To simulate each of these technologies, the program should be customized to reflect the fundamental features of the editing dynamics for whatever recording medium is used.

These additions improve realism for the comparison task, but they also emphasize an important limitation: quantitative outcomes depend on the assumed editing dynamics and parameterization. As more experimental measurements become available for recorder-specific editing probabilities, deletion/repair profiles, and tape heterogeneity, the framework can be further refined and used to generate increasingly system-specific guidance for experimental design.
