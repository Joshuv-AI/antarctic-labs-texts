High-quality Chromosome-level genome assembly of male and female greater amberjack (Seriola dumerili)
Authors: Lei Chen, Yinjun Jiang, Binbin Tao, Yanlong Song, Yang Huang, Guangli Li, Wei Hu, Chunhua Zhu, Ji Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe greater amberjack (Seriola dumerili) is a species of significant commercial value due to the high nutritional value and tender meat of its flesh. The existing availability of genomic data, which is incomplete and discontinuous, limits molecular biology studies on greater amberjack. Furthermore, it is hard to distinguish the genders of amberjack from their appearance, which is a major drawback for artificial propagation. In this study, chromosome-level genomes of male and female greater amberjack were assembled using PacBio HiFi and Hi-C data. The male and female genomes were anchored on 24 chromosomes, with sizes of 637 Mb and 639.4 Mb, respectively. Genome assembly BUSCO scores were 98.5% and 98.71%, respectively. The functionally annotated genes are 24,112 (87.92%) in males and 23,571 (89.03%) in females, with BUSCO assessment scores of 90.23% and 90.31%, respectively. In conclusion, we successfully assembled high-quality chromosome-level genomes for male and female greater amberjack, providing valuable information for future molecular breeding, species conservation and evolutionary research of greater amberjack.Subject terms: Comparative genomics, Genomics

Background & Summary

The greater amberjack (Seriola dumerili) is classified under the genus Seriola, family Carangidae, and order Perciformes. It is a medium-sized carnivorous marine fish that is distributed in subtropical waters worldwide1. The greater amberjack is also an essential breeding object for marine farming due to its fast-growing rate, flavorful flesh with abundance of unsaturated fatty acids. Recent research has prioritized artificial breeding technology development, focusing on economic traits including reproductive development2–7, immunity8,9, nutrition10,11 and stress adaptation12–14. Among these, gonadal development15 and reproductive characteristics16, as well as the development of efficient oxytocics17,18, have received significant attention.

High-quality genomic data is fundamental for modern molecular research on greater amberjack. Additionally, greater amberjacks lack obvious secondary sexual characteristics even at spawning season, making gender discrimination based on morphology challenging. Therefore, constructing high-quality genomes of male and female greater amberjack will lay the foundation for elucidating the sex differentiation mechanism of greater amberjack. The earliest genomic resources (GCA_002260705.1)19,20 exhibit fragmented assemblies (contig N50: 0.25 Mb) and incomplete annotation (Table 1), while the more recent chromosome-level assembly (GCA_041682515.1)21 is limited to a single sex. The prevailing genome assembly paradigm synergistically integrates third-generation sequencing (TGS) for long-read scaffolding, next-generation sequencing (NGS) for short-read polishing of base-level inaccuracies, and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) to resolve chromosomal architectures—collectively enabling the construction of complete chromosome-scale assemblies through complementary technological advantages22. Complementary long-read isoform sequencing (Iso-seq) and short-read RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) further enhanced annotation comprehensiveness23.

In this study, we assembled male and female greater amberjack genomes using a combination of DNBSEQ short reads, PacBio HiFi long reads, and Hi-C scaffolding data, with Iso-seq and RNA-seq integrated for genome annotation (Fig. 1). The resulting genome assemblies spanned 637 Mb (male) and 639.4 Mb (female), both anchored to 24 chromosomes. Assembly quality was validated through multiple metrics: scaffold N50 values of 23.12 Mb (male) and 23.48 Mb (female), BUSCO (v5.8.3)24 completeness scores of 98.5%(male) and 98.7%(female), and short-read mapping rates of 99.47%(male) and 99.59%(female). Functional annotation revealed 24,112 (87.92%, male) and 23,571 (89.03%, female) protein-coding genes with homologs in public databases. We present the first high-quality, chromosome-level genome assemblies for both male and female greater amberjack. Our assemblies achieve comparable quality to the most recent chromosome-level assembly of greater amberjack-GCA_041682515.121 (Table 1, Figs. 2, and 3). These high-quality annotated genomes provide a foundation for molecular breeding, species conservation, and evolutionary studies of greater amberjack.

Methods

Sample collection

Three male and three female wild greater amberjack were caught off the coast of Fujian Province, China. A portion of the gonads was used for paraffin sections for sex identification25 (Fig. 4). Tissue samples including muscle, heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and gonad were collected from the 6 fish. These samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA sequencing. A portion of the muscle from a male and a female were for DNA sequencing, respectively. All experiments were conducted under the approval and regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese Academy of Science (keshuizhuan08529).

The morphological structure of the testis in the male and the ovary in the female greater amberjack.

Library construction, sequencing and data preparation

High-quality genomic DNA was isolated from muscle tissue for PacBio HiFi library construction. The genomic DNA extraction was performed following the standard protocol provided with the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). DNA quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometry, with quantification performed using a Qubit fluorometer. An initial 150-bp short-insert library was generated via DNBSEQ for genome survey analysis (Table 2). After obtaining 95.11 Gb (male) and 94.12 Gb (female) of high-quality filtered data, genomic DNA was sheared into 13–16 kb fragments using a Megaruptor system. Size selection was conducted with a SageELF instrument to construct PacBio HiFi libraries. DNA ends were repaired and ligated to SMRTbell adapters to generate 15-kb insert libraries. Finally, 23.70 Gb (male) and 24.69 Gb (female) of HiFi reads were produced on the PacBio Sequel II platform in circular consensus sequencing (CCS) mode (Table 3).

Genome survey

A k-mer survey analysis of the greater amberjack genome was conducted using the Jellyfish (V2.1.4)27. Initially, k-mer frequency distributions were calculated for k-values ranging from 17 to 31. The optimal k-mer size was determined to be 19 based on minimal k-mer multiplicity anomalies (Fig. 5). Genome characteristics were subsequently estimated by modeling the k-mer spectrum with GenomeScope (v2.0)28. Results indicated a male genome size of 636.21 Mb with 0.375% heterozygosity and 14.94% repetitive sequence content. The female genome was estimated at 630.31 Mb, exhibiting 0.46% heterozygosity and 14.48% repetitive sequences (Table 7).

Chromosome-level genome assembly of Greater amberjack

Chromosome-level genome assembly of Greater amberjack Chromosome-level de novo assemblies of male and female greater amberjack genomes were generated by integrating PacBio HiFi long reads with Hi-C scaffolding data (Table 8). Contig-level assemblies were produced using HiFiasm (v0.16.1)29. Hi-C reads were aligned to draft contigs with BWA (v0.7.17-r1188)30 using the mem module and processed through Juicer (v1.5.6)31 to calculate mapping efficiency metrics, including the proportion of valid/invalid interaction pairs. Chromosomal scaffolding was performed with 3D-DNA (v180922)32, followed by manual curation. The resulting 637 Mb (male) and 639.4 Mb (female) assemblies resolved 24 chromosomes (Fig. 6). Contact frequency matrices were generated by partitioning genomes into 1-kb bins and quantifying Hi-C read pairs between bin pairs, visualized as interaction heatmaps (Fig. 7).

Repeat annotation

Repetitive sequences were annotated through homology-based screening and de novo prediction. For homology detection, the RepBase (v21.12)33 library was employed via RepeatMasker (v4.0.7)34 to identify known repetitive elements. De novo prediction is based on sequence comparison, which involves creating a library of de novo35 repetitive sequences using LTR_FINDER (v1.06)36 and RepeatModeler (v2.0.1)34, and then predicting them with RepeatMasker34. The Tandem Repeats Finder (v4.09)37 was used to identify tandem repeats in the genome. Comprehensive repetitive element annotations are summarized in Table 9. Ultimately, repetitive sequences constituted 23.12% of the male genome and 23.48% of the female genome.

Protein-coding gene prediction and annotation

To predict gene structure, we combined de novo and evidence-based approaches (Table 8). De novo annotation was performed via Augustus (v3.3.2)38. Evidence-directed annotation combined transcriptomic evidence, homology evidence and cross-species protein sequences. Transcriptomic evidence (RNA-seq and Iso-seq data) aligned via Blat39 (http://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgBlat) (Table 10). Homology evidence from closely related species, such as Seriola rivoliana40, Seriola lalandi Dorsalis41, Danio rerio42, Trachinotus ovatus43, Seriola quinqueradiata44 and Seriola dumerili20. Integrated gene models were generated using Maker (v2.31.10)45.

High synteny between male and female greater amberjack genomes

To compare the chromosomal structural differences between the male and female genomes, we performed a whole-genome synteny analysis between them. Using the JCVI toolkit, we first identified orthologous gene blocks between the two genomes based on gene annotation results (parameters: jcvi.compara.catalog ortholog --cscore = 0.7 --no_strip_names), and further filtered reliable syntenic blocks (parameters: jcvi.compara.synteny screen --minspan = 30).

The results revealed a high degree of synteny between the male and female genomes (Fig. 8). Most chromosomal regions showed one-to-one syntenic correspondence, and no large-scale structural variations (such as chromosomal translocations, inversions, or duplications) were detected. This indicates that the macrochromosomal structure is highly conserved between male and female greater amberjack, with no obvious heteromorphic sex chromosomes formed.

Technical Validation

Genome evaluation

BUSCO (v5.8.3)21 is a Python-based software that evaluates the quality of transcriptome and genome assembly. In this study, the actinopterygii_odb12 library was utilized to evaluate assembly integrity. We used BUSCO to determine the completeness of the scaffold-level genome of male greater amberjack, which was 98.5%. The BUSCO assessment for the male annotated gene set was 90.23%. The BUSCO assessment for the female scaffold-level genome was 98.71%, and the annotated gene set was 90.31% (Tables 12, 13). The BUSCO evaluation shows high-quality genome assembly and gene prediction.
