Factors Influencing Racial Discrimination and Prevalence of Major Depressive Disorder Among Black Canadian Youths
Authors: Folajinmi Oluwasina, Andre M.N Renzaho, Delores Mullings, Joanna Henderson, Kwame McKenzie, Hayley Hamilton, Tolulope Sajobi, Ambikaipakan Senthilselvan, Cecile Rousseau, Bukola Salami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a complex and multifaceted mental health condition with far-reaching implications for youth well-being and society. This paper examined the prevalence of depressive symptoms consistent with probable MDD and their association with self-reported experiences of racial discrimination within the Black youth population in Canada. This study used a descriptive cross-sectional design. Questionnaires were administered via an anonymous online survey. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a self-report screening tool, with scores ≥ 10 indicating symptoms consistent with probable MDD rather than a clinical diagnosis. Experiences of discrimination were measured using self-reported items adapted from previously validated discrimination instruments. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression(p ≤ 0.05). A total of 933 Black youth participated in this survey. Black youth who reported unfair treatment due to their skin colour or race were 18 times more likely to express MDD than those who had not (OR: 18.38; 95% CI: 2.796–7.17 ). Similar associations were observed for self-reported discrimination based on language accent (OR = 4.40; 95% CI: 1.01–19.24) and culture (OR = 12.29; 95% CI: 1.47–12.53). Black youth who have been treated unfairly due to their culture were 12 times more likely to experience MDD than those who had not (OR: 12.29; 95% CI: 1.473–12.530). This study demonstrates a significant association between perceived unfair treatment and depressive symptoms (consistent with probable MDD) among Black Canadian youth. The findings suggest that racial, linguistic, and cultural discrimination are associated with poorer mental health outcomes in this group. Culturally sensitive interventions and policies that promote inclusivity and equity can help Black youth with MDD.

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) has become a severe health concern impacting youth worldwide, and Canada has identified youth depression as a growing public health concern. MDD is one of the most prevalent causes of disease burden worldwide and a general mental health condition that is both regularly occurring and persistent (Adams et al., 2019). The World Health Organization (WHO, 2021) reported that 10–20% of youths worldwide experience mental health challenges, with depression being one of the leading contributors to the global burden of disease among this age group. Black Canadian youth suffer specific mental health issues related to their racial identification (Brown & Jackson, 2020).

The high level of discrimination experienced by Black youth is likely to have a significant impact on their health and well-being because racism is a core driver of health inequities and has been linked to a wide range of physical, psychological, and academic outcomes throughout the lifespan (Benner et al., 2018; Jones & Neblett, 2017; Trent et al., 2019). According to the General Social Survey (GSS) by Statistics Canada (2019), 46% of the Black population report having experienced at least one form of discrimination in the last five years; this is far higher than for the non-racialized and non-Indigenous population (16%). The higher rate of discrimination indicates Black Canadian youth may have higher rates of depressive symptoms compared to their white counterparts.

Two studies found that exposure to racist experiences and messages of racial inferiority might be harmful to Black youth because they result in negative self-perceptions and a decrease in the youths’ sense of perceived control (Lambert et al., 2009; Utsey et al., 2013). Different kinds of discriminatory experiences cause a type of prolonged activation because they cause anticipatory stress before the stressor is met or persistent cognition after the stressor is no longer present. Other findings demonstrate higher levels of racial discrimination are connected to elevated levels of depressive symptoms among Black youths (English et al., 2014; Gaylord-Harden & Cunningham, 2009; Sellers et al., 2006b).

Despite the focus on diversity, structural and interpersonal racism, including unegqual treatment and restricted access to opportunities can still result in challenges within countries such as Canada that feature a variety of cultures. The Canadian legal framework prohibits discrimination on the grounds of ethnic origin, age, race, gender identity, colour, marital status, family status, nationality, religion, sexual orientation, handicap, or pardoned conviction for an offence (Canadian Human Rights Act, 1985). According to Statistics Canada (2019), approximately 50% of the Black population in Canada have encountered instances of discrimination and around 19% of Canadians aged 15 and above reported experiencing some form of discrimination or unfair treatment. The prevalence of discrimination varied significantly across different ethnocultural groups, and the higher rates experienced by Black people are likely attributed to their skin colour, race, or ethnicity. For example, 41% of people of African descent have been victims of discrimination or unjust treatment based solely on their race or colour of their skin. This ratio is almost 15 times higher than for non-Indigenous people who are not a visible minority (3%). More than one in four (27%) Black individuals have been discriminated against due to their race or culture, but just 2% of the population of non-Indigenous, non-visible minorities have met such treatment (Statistics Canada, 2019).

Instances of discrimination can lead to adverse outcomes for individuals (Berry, 2013; Dion, 2002). Increasing evidence suggests a longitudinal association between racial discrimination and depression symptoms. An examination of the correlation between occurrences of racial discrimination and subsequent manifestations of depressive symptoms lends credence to the notion that the impacts of racial discrimination endure over an extended period (Cave et al., 2020); this empirical evidence indicates a covariation between the escalation of racial discrimination and the rise in depressive symptoms. For example, Black youth who encounter higher levels of racial discrimination over time tend to exhibit a more pronounced rise in depressive symptoms compared to their white counterparts who experience fewer instances of racial discrimination (Brody et al., 2006). high levels of depressive symptoms when Furthermore, depressive symptoms are high among the Black youth who experience a significant number of racial discrimination cases in weeks when racial discrimination is greater than on weeks when it is less (Ong et al., 2009; Seaton & Douglass, 2014; Seaton & Iida, 2019). There is also a possibility that depressive symptoms can also affect the perception or recollection of discriminatory experiences in people. Yet, longitudinal research indicates that racial discrimination experiences are antecedents and predictors of depressive symptomatic changes over time, which can be interpreted as indicating that, instead of being caused by depression, discrimination is one of the factors causing it (English et al., 2014; Cave et al., 2020).

The problem of racial discrimination and its implication on the mental health of Black youth has become more and more popular and a concern.

Racial discrimination has been defined as unfair treatment based on race or ethnicity, which can affect mental health, including depression, among many racial and ethnic groups (Williams & Mohammed, 2019). Clark et al. (2016) reported that racial discrimination causes emotional trauma, isolation, and psychological distress among Black youth, which can make this population more susceptible to depression. Racism can be harmful to a person’s sense of self-worth, identity, and well-being (Adams et al., 2019), and can also chronically engage the body’s stress response systems (Clark et al., 2016). Discrimination may elevate stress hormones such as cortisol, which may disrupt mood control and cause a maladjustment in stress response, leading to a possibility of depression. The black youth can be subject to racist biases and negative self-images that indirectly lead them to develop depressive symptoms (Brown & Jackson, 2020). Internalized racism can also be experienced, whereby individuals internalize the prejudices and stereotypes in the society and develop a negative perception about their own race (Kessler et al., 2010); the negative beliefs may reduce self-esteem and self-image, thereby also predisposing the Black youth to depression. Additionally, the influence of some moderators, including sociodemographic factors such as gender, race, and ethnicity, may determine how racial discrimination affects depression in youths.

In light of the concerns about the mental well-being of Black youth in Canada and the relation to racial discrimination, this study examined the prevalence of MDD among Black youth and potential mediators that could explain how racial discrimination exerts its influence on depressive symptoms.

Methods

Study Setting and Sample Size Estimation

This study was conducted among Black youth (aged 16 to 30 years) living in Canada. According to the 2021 Canadian census, Canada was home to 1,547,870 individuals who identify as Black, representing 4.3% of the entire Canadian population (Statistics Canada, 2022). A proportional to size sampling method was used to select a representative sample of Black youth from Canada’s 10 provinces and three territories. We recruited until we reached a target of 933 participants. After collecting data, we reviewed the sample distribution by province/territory. When a province exceeded its proportional target, we stopped recruitment from that region and balanced the sample accordingly. Using a confidence interval of 95% and an error margin of 3%, the required sample size was 933. Patients or the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Study Design and Institutional Review Board Approval

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted using self-administered, anonymous questionnaires administered online. Respondents who self-identified as Black youth residing in Canada were included as participants. The study was carried out abiding by the Helsinki Declaration (Hong Kong Amendment) and Good Clinical Practice (Canadian Guidelines). Before participating, all individuals were given an online information booklet, and informed consent was obtained. The study was approved by the University of Alberta’s Health Ethics Research Board (Ethics ID: Pro00116630).

Data Collection and Outcome Measures

Online survey was used to collect data in February 2023, Social media, email listservs, and community networks, such as Black-led community organizations in Canada. People were advised to send the survey link to their communities to generate access to many provinces and territories The. Study data were collected and managed with the help of Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) electronic data capture tools (Harris et al., 2009, 2019). REDCap is a web-based software platform developed to facilitate data collection for research studies. It provides a safe place to capture and store data and offers features like the ability of the user to enter the validated data through the interactive user interface, audit trails to track the data manipulation and export activity, automated methods to download the data seamlessly to the popular statistical software packages and a tool to integrate and exchange the data with the external sources. The study data collection instruments were formulated using pertinent literature and questions of instruments that had been verified previously. Relevant demographic characteristics and discrimination variables were considered as the variables of interest. We included multiple discrimination variables. Importantly, discrimination based on race or skin colour, ethnicity, and culture was measured for the entire sample. In contrast, discrimination based on sexual orientation, religion, and disability applied only to subsamples within the total sample. These were included to capture broader discrimination experiences but were analyzed separately to avoid overgeneralization. The symptoms of MDD among the participants were assessed by using patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) (Kroenke & Spitzer, 2002).Data collection tools for this study were developed based on relevant literature and questions from previously verified instruments. The variables of interest included relevant demographic characteristics and discrimination variables. Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was utilized to evaluate the symptoms of MDD in participants (Kroenke & Spitzer, 2002). The PHQ-9 is a nine-item validated questionnaire with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.89, used to diagnose and examine the severity of depression (Kim & Lee, 2019; Kroenke et al., 2001). Each of the nine survey items is given a score between 0 (not at all) and 3 (nearly every day). Higher total scores (> 10) on the scale indicate higher levels of depression. (Kroenke & Spitzer, 2002). We examined the extent to which racial discrimination impacted individuals’ everyday life and their regular encounters with racial discrimination in their interactions with others. In total, there were 11 measures included in the analysis of the overall discrimination. Participants who indicated encountering any form of racial discrimination were requested to specify the main factor behind these occurrences, such as ethnicity, race or skin color, gender, sexual orientation etc.). These psychometric properties for racial discrimination measures were not provided out of concern for participant well-being and ethics. Racial discrimination issues are sensitive, so the research team prioritized participant comfort. We also noted that psychometric qualities may not apply universally across varied cultures due to the cultural heterogeneity of our participants, and the removal is meant to minimize simplifying complicated experiences. The study emphasizes its exploratory nature and focuses on key results, policy implications, and practical applications rather than psychometric analysis. To maintain confidentiality, measures are based on historical validity and reliability in similar circumstances, and participant anonymity is protected. This strategy decision supports research goals by prioritizing transparency and ethics.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was facilitated by SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26; IBM Corp, 2019). The demographic attributes of Black youth, along with their reactions to questions related to discrimination, are presented as absolute figures and proportions. The responses reported consist of complete data without any data imputation. Chi-square were conducted with a two-tailed significance level set at p ≤ 0.05. The goal of this analysis was to examine the relationship between the demographic characteristics of Black youth and their responses to questions regarding discrimination-related variables. Statistically significant variables (p ≤ 0.1) in the bivariate analysis were selected for inclusion in a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to compute odds ratios, which were used to assess the relationship between the predictor factors and the occurrence of MDD while controlling for the remaining variables included in the model.

Table 1 also shows the summary statistics of physical and mental well-being among Black youth.

Nearly half, 49.6% (463) participants reported that they are not experiencing unfair treatment due to their race or skin colour, while 50.4% (470) respondents indicated that they have been subjected to unfair treatment because of their skin colour or race. The majority, 79.0% (737) of respondents reported that they had not experienced unfair treatment because of their ethnicity while 21.0% (196) participants reported that they had experienced unfair treatment based on their race 73% (686) of the participants reported that they have not experienced unfair treatment due to their culture, while 26.5% (247) of the participants reported the incidence of unfair treatment based on their culture. About 48% (464) of the respondents had not experienced unfair treatment due to their language accent, while half of the respondents 50.3% 469), reported that they had experienced unfair treatment based on their language accent.

Logistic regression model predicting the relationship between the presence of MDD and racial discrimination among black youth

The logistic regression model consisted of 12 independent variables and had an Χ2 value of 46.35 (df = 12; n = 927) that was statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating the model could differentiate between Black youth with likely or unlikely MDD. The model accounted for 19.4% (Cox and Snell R2) to 29.8% (Nagelkerke R2) of the variance. Results of the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicate the model was appropriately fitted (χ2 = 7.42; p = 0.86) and accurately identified 96.2% of the cases.

Black youth who have been treated unfairly due to their race or skin colour were 18 times more likely to experience MDD than those who had not (OR = 18.38; 95% CI: 2.796–7.166). Black youth who have been treated unfairly due to their language accent were four times more likely to experience MDD than those who had not (OR = 4.40; 95% CI: 1.007–19.241). Black youth who have been treated unfairly due to their culture were 12 times more likely to experience MDD than those who had not (OR = 12.29; 95% CI: 1.473–12.530).

Discussion

This study revealed a significant correlation between instances of perceived unfair treatment and MDD among Black youth in Canada. Black youth subjected to unfair treatment due to their race or skin colour were 18 times more likely to experience MDD. This association demonstrates that racial discrimination negatively influences the mental health outcomes of Black youth in Canada. This result is consistent with other studies that reveal a significant relationship between racial discrimination and depressive symptoms (Cutrona et al., 2016; Tickamyer & Duncan, 1990; Williams et al., 2003). Their study also revealed that Black youth who reported being subjected to racial discrimination experienced higher symptoms of depression than Black youth people who reported being subjected to less prejudice. A previous survey found the increased incidence of discriminatory experiences within the Black community can be mostly attributed to higher levels of perceived discrimination based on factors such as ethnicity, skin colour, race, and culture (Canadian Human Rights Act. 1985; Berry, 2013).

We also note that the percentage of individuals identifying as Black who reported discrimination in 2019 (46%; Statistics Canada, 2019) was much higher than figures reported for 2014 (28%). The percentage of those who claimed to have been discriminated against due to the colour of their skin or their race was the main cause of the increase in the number as the percentage was nearly two times higher in 2019 (41%) than in 2014 (23%). The given increase in the numbers was seen before many significant Black Lives Matter Movement protests and events that happened in 2020 and practically highlighted several high-profile instances of misconduct, discrimination, and unfair treatment based on racial aspects. Racial discrimination in Black youth can cause helplessness, marginalization, and internalized racism, which can serve as moderators of MDD among Black youth.The results of this study also indicate Black youth who have experienced unfair treatment because of their language accent were four times more likely to experience MDD than those who had not. Our findings about the effects of language accent on the psychological health of Black adolescents is congruent with the research that indicates that there is a relationship between language-based discrimination and stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms (Buki et al., 2007; Gee et al., 2009). The risk of MDD could be affected by some negative experiences of language accents that may cause feelings of social exclusion and emotional issues.Furthermore, our study showed an association between culture and MDD, with those Black youth who had experienced unfair treatment because of their culture being 12 times more likely to have MDD than those who had not. This result shows the significant impact of cultural discrimination on mental health outcomes among the respondents and is in line with studies that also reveal the significant influence of cultural discrimination on the mental well-being of youth (Kessler et al. 2010; Sellers et al. 2006a). Experiences of cultural discrimination can result in identity conflicts, reduced self-esteem, and isolation, which are predictors of MDD.

Study Limitations

Data for this study were collected using an online survey. Snowball and convenience sampling was used and respondents may unintentionally have self-selected into the sample. The findings could have been different if this study had been carried out before the COVID-19 pandemic, which could have put our participants under more stress.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significant correlation between unfair treatment and MDD among Black Canadian youth. Specifically, the findings show that racial, language accent, and cultural prejudice harm the mental health of Black youth. The results emphasize the need for cultural sensitivity and inclusive practices in various settings to alleviate the adverse effects of unfair treatment on the mental health of these youths. Discrimination must be addressed in schools, workplaces, and health services to improve the mental health of this vulnerable population and more funding to support Black-led services. Culturally sensitive interventions and policies that promote inclusivity and equity may support Black youth with MDD. Future research is needed to consider intersectionality, such as race, gender, and sexual orientation, and how they influence unfair treatment and mental health outcomes in Black youth.
