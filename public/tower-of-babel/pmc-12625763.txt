Association between lumbopelvic pain in pregnancy and fear of childbirth: a multicenter cross-sectional case-control study
Authors: Celia García-Lucas, Esme Chen, Lola Serrano-Raya, Ana Boldó-Roda, María Dolores Arguisuelas, Luis Suso-Martí, Juan Pardo, Juan José Amer-Cuenca, Juan Francisco Lisón, Gemma Biviá-Roig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFear of childbirth is a common occurrence during pregnancy, causing unnecessary caesarean sections due to anxiety and pain catastrophizing. Lumbopelvic pain is also very prevalent during pregnancy and similarly linked to anxiety and pain catastrophizing. Although these two issues appear to have commonalities, their relationship has not been explored yet. This study aims to investigate the potential correlation between fear of childbirth and lumbopelvic pain during pregnancy, addressing a gap in the current literature.MethodsOnline surveys were emailed to pregnant women with lumbopelvic pain (n = 216) and without it (n = 207). We extracted our primary outcome, fear of childbirth, along with secondary variables including anxiety, depression, kinesiophobia, and catastrophizing.ResultsPregnant women with lumbopelvic pain had significantly higher fear of childbirth compared to those without lumbopelvic pain (p = 0.002). Moreover, fear of childbirth showed a significant positive correlation with anxiety, depression, kinesiophobia, and catastrophizing (p < 0.05).ConclusionPregnant women with lumbopelvic pain experience higher levels of fear of childbirth compared to those without lumbopelvic pain. Trial registrationThe study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identification number NCT05976854 on 10/08/2023.Keywords: Pregnancy, Low back pain, Pelvic girdle pain, Lumbopelvic pain, Fear of childbirth

Background

Fear of childbirth (FOC) is a common occurrence during pregnancy, affecting up to 20% of pregnancies in developed countries and reaching severe levels in 6–10% of pregnancies [1]. FOC is clinically important because it is a major contributor to unnecessary caesarean sections (C-sections) [1, 2]. Women with severe FOC are more inclined to choose C-sections, contributing to the global rise in non-medically indicated procedures, which carry greater risks for both mother and newborns than vaginal birth [1–3]. For the mother, C-sections are associated with higher rates of maternal death, postpartum infection and hemorrhage, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and burst abdomen [3, 4]. For newborns, C-sections can cause birth asphyxia, transient tachypnea, respiratory distress syndrome, and sepsis [3, 4]. Despite these risks, over 70% of gynecologists would allow C-section requests based on maternal fear, despite sufficient psychological treatment [5].

In trying to understand FOC, researchers have found that FOC is closely associated with anxiety [6–9], depression [7–9], pain catastrophizing [10, 11], and previous negative birth experiences, which can lead to post-traumatic stress disorder [12]. For example, FOC has been associated with multiple measures of anxiety [6, 11], which was also found to increase visits to the obstetrician regarding FOC and preference for elective C-Sect [7]. It has been proposed that FOC is a form of anxiety [13], while other research posits that anxiety is one main way by which FOC manifests itself [6]. In fact, both anxiety and depression are risk factors for and increase the prevalence of FOC [8, 9]. This explains observations that anxiety and depression disorders are twice as common among pregnant women with FOC compared to non-fearful pregnant women [14], and higher levels of FOC among pregnant women with anxiety and depression [6]. Similarly, preliminary studies have shown that pain catastrophizing, an excessively negative orientation to pain [15], is strongly associated with FOC, predicting its severity and influencing preference for elective C-Sects [10, 11].

Lumbopelvic pain (LPP), which encompasses both low back pain and pelvic girdle pain and may occur separately or simultaneously, is another common occurrence in pregnancy, as it is one of the most common complaints in the third trimester of pregnancy [16] and affects more than two-thirds of pregnant women [17]. Its etiology is considered multifactorial, involving hormonal changes (such as increased relaxin, estrogen, and progesterone) [18], biomechanical adaptations related to pregnancy (weight gain, altered posture, and increased lumbar lordosis) [19], and vascular factors [20]. Collectively, these changes contribute to greater ligamentous laxity, altered spinal and pelvic loading, and an increased susceptibility to pain.

LPP during pregnancy affects sleep, ability to work, and contributes to a threefold increase in the prevalence of postpartum depression [17]. In turn, infants of mothers with postpartum depression have poorer cognitive and emotional development [17]. Despite the prevalence and negative implications of LPP, little information about it is provided to expectant mothers. As a result, pregnant women may not disclose LPP-like symptoms to their healthcare providers [17]. Additionally, although clinical practice guidelines for the management of pelvic girdle pain already exist [21], their implementation in routine care remains limited, emphasizing the need to translate evidence-based recommendations into everyday clinical practice. Consequently, pregnant women with LPP may resort to unsafe self-management techniques, like NSAIDs and paracetamol, despite these being contraindicated in the third trimester of pregnancy [22]. Moreover, LPP may persist after pregnancy and become chronic in as many as 1 in 5 women who had LPP in pregnancy [23].

Studies show that low back pain is associated with pain catastrophizing [24–26], kinesiophobia [27], and anxiety [28, 29]. Pain catastrophizing and kinesiophobia are well-recognized in clinical studies to increase the risk of chronic low back pain and disability [24, 30]. During pregnancy, these relationships are also observed: early pregnancy pain catastrophizing has been associated with LPP [26], while late pregnancy pain catastrophizing has been associated with LPP interference [24]. Catastrophizing in pregnancy has also been associated with higher levels of postpartum LPP [25]. Moreover, kinesiophobia in pregnancy has been associated with LPP intensity and disability [27]. Likewise, the association between anxiety and low back pain is well-documented in studies [31, 32], as is the association between anxiety and LPP in pregnancy more specifically [28, 29]. To some extent, depression has also been associated with low back pain in pregnancy [29, 33]. In addition, women with LPP during pregnancy often report a disturbed body perception in their lumbopelvic region. Body perception refers to the conscious awareness and representation of one’s own body, and disturbances in this domain have been linked to pain- and movement-related fear as well as pain catastrophizing, further reinforcing the association between LPP and FOC [34].

Both FOC and LPP during pregnancy are associated with catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression, while LPP is also associated with kinesiophobia. Given these shared associations, it is reasonable to assume that FOC and LPP may be interrelated. The fear-avoidance model offers a useful framework to interpret this relationship: pain catastrophizing can heighten pain-related fear, which in turn fosters avoidance of movement [15]. Such avoidance behaviors may lead to guarded movements, reduced muscle strength, and decreased coordination, all of which contribute to lower pain tolerance and greater disability. Within this model, kinesiophobia is viewed as a central mechanism through which catastrophizing affects behavior, while anxiety is considered a vulnerability factor that amplifies fear responses and further exacerbates pain and disability [35]. From a psychoneuroimmunology perspective, prolonged stress responses may perpetuate cortisol dysfunction and inflammation, while exaggerated psychological reactions, such as catastrophizing, can consolidate fear-based pain memories and reinforce maladaptive avoidance behaviors [36]. Applying these ideas from the fear-avoidance model, one can reason that FOC may be associated with LPP in pregnancy via pain catastrophizing and kinesiophobia.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no study in the literature that has examined FOC in pregnant women with and without LPP. Our objective is to investigate the relationship between FOC and LPP in pregnancy, which is unknown, concerning kinesiophobia, catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression. We hypothesize that pregnant women with LPP are more likely to experience FOC, which can be interpreted with the fear-avoidance model in which pain catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and anxiety play a role.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a multicentric case-control study between August 2023 to November 2024. Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling technique through the Gynecology and Obstetrics departments of three hospitals in the Valencian Community: Hospital Sagunto (Valencia), Hospital IMED (Valencia) y Hospital Universitario de La Plana (Castellón).

This research was approved by the Corporate Ethics Committees for Clinical Research of the respective hospitals and the University CEU Cardenal Herrera Ethics Committee on Human Research (CEI21/061). It was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identification number NCT05976854 on 10/08/2023. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [37], and all procedures were conducted in line with the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Cases were defined as pregnant women who suffered from LPP with the location of pain confirmed as being in the lower back and/or pelvic girdle, in accordance with the standard definition of lumbopelvic pain during pregnancy. To ensure that LPP was pregnancy-related and not due to other mechanical causes, participants were initially screened by hospital gynecologists through clinical evaluation. Only those women whose pain onset occurred during pregnancy, whose clinical presentation was consistent with pregnancy-related LPP, and who reported a minimum score of 3/10 during the past week on the Visual Analog Pain Scale (VAS), and whose pain had persisted for at least two weeks (i.e., not occasional or sporadic episodes) were included. Controls were defined as pregnant women without LPP or with a pain score lower than 3/10 on the VAS.

Participants, regardless of whether they had PLPP, were identified through a two-step process. First, hospital gynecologists assessed whether they met the study’s eligibility criteria. If they met the criteria, they were invited to participate in the study and, if they agreed, provided written informed consent. This assessment was subsequently confirmed by the investigators at the university through a telephone interview. Our main variable (FOC) and secondary variables (anxiety, depression, kinesiophobia, and catastrophizing) were extracted from a Google Forms survey that was sent via email to all the participants of this study.

Study population

Our study population consisted of 423 pregnant women (216 cases and 207 controls) who were in the early third trimester of pregnancy from three hospitals.

Our inclusion criteria were pregnant women over 18 years of age, primiparous or multiparous, who were at the beginning of the third trimester of pregnancy. Our exclusion criteria were women with multiple pregnancies, previous spinal surgery, and psychiatric disorders.

Outcome measures

Demographic and anthropometric data

The participants were asked to provide the following information about themselves: age, height, weight, week of pregnancy, previous pregnancies, previous C-sections, history of lumbopelvic pain episodes, education level, and employment status.

Primary outcome

Fear of childbirth was assessed using the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ) version A [38], validated in Spanish [39]. This instrument consists of 33 items scored from 1 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater fear of childbirth. The Spanish version of this scale has shown excellent psychometric properties, with very high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.93; ω = 0.93) and good construct validity, supporting its use in Spanish-speaking populations [39].

Secondary outcomes

Anxiety and depression were measured using The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [40], validated in Spanish [41]. The HADS consists of 14 items, with two subscales: anxiety (HADS-A, 7 items) and depression (HADS-D, 7 items). Each item is rated from 0 to 3, so each subscale can have a maximum score of 21 points. Absence of anxiety or depression is scored between 0 and 7, medium levels are scored between 8 and 10, moderate levels between 11 and 14, and severe levels between 15 and 21. The Spanish version of the HADS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high test–retest reliability (correlation coefficients > 0.85) and excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86), supporting its validity and reliability [41].

Kinesiophobia was evaluated using the 11-item Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK-11) [42], validated in Spanish [43]. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 11 to 44. Higher scores reflect greater fear of movement. The Spanish TSK-11 demonstrated adequate psychometric properties, with internal consistency values of Cronbach’s α = 0.79 (chronic pain) and α = 0.81 (acute pain), acceptable stability over time and good construct, convergent, and predictive validity, supporting its use in Spanish-speaking populations [43].

Catastrophizing was assessed using The Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) [44], validated in Spanish [45, 46]. The PCS consists of 13 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 4 = all the time), with total scores ranging from 0 to 52; higher scores indicate greater levels of catastrophizing. The Spanish version has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties, with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.79) and strong test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.84). These findings support the use of the PCS as a reliable and valid tool for Spanish-speaking populations [46].

Statistical analysis

No previous studies are available on the comparison of FOC between pregnant women with and without associated LPP. Thus, we hypothesized that women with LPP would have a greater FOC compared to women without pain. We performed a power analysis using G*Power software v3.1.9.2 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany) [47] and found that a sample size of 402 participants would provide 85% statistical power at a 5% significance level (two-sided test) for a small effect size (d = 0.3) [12]. The sample size was increased by 5% to account for potential dropouts, resulting in a final sample size of 422 participants.

Compliance with the assumption of normality was assessed for each dependent variable and each study group using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Demographic and obstetric characteristics were compared between groups using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, since all continuous variables violated the assumption of normality. Results are presented in Table 1. Differences between the case (LPP) and control (no LPP) groups were analyzed using the Student’s t-test for independent samples (FOC) and the Mann–Whitney U test (kinesiophobia, catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression). To prevent an increase in type I error due to multiple statistical tests, a Bonferroni adjustment was applied. Consequently, the alpha level for the five group comparisons was set at 0.01 (0.05/5). Within the LPP group, bivariate correlations between fear of childbirth, kinesiophobia, catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression were examined using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Given that ten pairwise correlations were tested, the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold was set at 0.005 (0.05/10). The strength of the Spearman correlation was interpreted following the guidelines by Hopkins et al., where correlations ranging from 0.0 to 0.1 were considered trivial, 0.1 to 0.3 small, 0.3 to 0.5 moderate, 0.5 to 0.7 large, 0.7 to 0.9 very large, and 0.9 to 1.0 nearly perfect [48]. In addition, forward stepwise regression was used to identify the combination of variables (psychological factors, group status —LPP vs. no LPP—, previous pregnancies, weight, and education level) that best predicted FOC. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 for Windows (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Similarly, the Mann-Whitney U test demonstrated statistically significant differences between the two groups, with the LPP group exhibiting higher levels of kinesiophobia, catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between FOC and LPP in pregnant women with respect to anxiety, depression, catastrophizing, and kinesiophobia. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has explored the potential association between FOC and LPP during pregnancy. While previous research has examined FOC as a general concept [49] and its connection to labor pain [42, 43], our study is the first to specifically investigate the relationship between FOC and LPP during pregnancy. We predicted that FOC and LPP would be positively related to each other, and that kinesiophobia, catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression would play a role. Our findings partially support this hypothesis, filling a gap in the literature and providing new insights into the interplay between psychological and physical factors in pregnancy. Notably, pregnant women with LPP exhibited significantly higher levels of FOC compared to those without LPP. Furthermore, women with LPP showed higher levels of kinesiophobia, catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression. Within the LPP group, however, FOC was found to be moderately correlated with anxiety and depression, weakly correlated with catastrophizing, and not significantly associated with kinesiophobia.

In interpreting these results, it is important to acknowledge that the LPP group showed a higher prevalence of prior low back pain, greater weight, and a slightly lower educational level than the no-LPP group, which is consistent with existing literature identifying these variables as risk factors for LPP in pregnancy [24]. To ensure that weight and educational level did not influence our main findings, we included these variables in the linear regression model and confirmed that they are not predictors of FOC.

We hypothesized that the relationship between FOC and LPP could be explained by the fear-avoidance model. According to this model, fear-avoidance beliefs and avoidance behavior lead to pain and disability [15, 35]. Applying these concepts, it is plausible that FOC influences LPP and vice versa through pain catastrophizing and kinesiophobia, both of which are associated with fear avoidance and avoidance behavior. However, our results only partially support this reasoning: while catastrophizing showed a weak association with FOC, kinesiophobia was not significantly related. Instead, anxiety and depression emerged as the strongest correlates and predictors of FOC in women with LPP, suggesting that emotional vulnerability may play a more central role than fear of movement in this population.

An important concept of the fear-avoidance model is that the experience of pain is influenced by a mix of emotional, cognitive, biological, and behavioral factors. Our study focused on the psychological aspects of pain, which can have a profound impact on the experience of pain. Others have found that fear of pain can be more debilitating than pain itself [50]. For instance, individuals with chronic low back pain learn to associate fear and anxiety with actions that may exacerbate their pain. As a result, these individuals acquire avoidant behaviors that predict disability [50]. Consistent with this perspective, our regression analyses revealed that anxiety was the strongest independent predictor of FOC, followed by depression and catastrophizing. This highlights the role of negative cognitive-emotional processes in amplifying childbirth-related fears. Previous studies in pregnant populations have also supported the role of fear-avoidance in pregnancy-related pain. For instance, pregnant women with LPP or pelvic-girdle pain are more likely to exhibit fear-avoidance beliefs or have higher levels of negative thoughts [26, 50], and those with high fear-avoidance beliefs in pregnancy are at higher risk of having LPP at 6 months postpartum [51]. With regards to the topic of our study, LPP in pregnancy is also well-documented to be associated with fear-avoidant behaviors [50, 51], as well as pain catastrophizing [24–26], kinesiophobia [27], anxiety [28, 29], and depression [33].

FOC carries significant clinical implications. It is a major contributing factor for unnecessary C-Sects [1, 2, 10]., which are on the rise worldwide [1] and are associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes [3]. Examples of documented adverse maternal outcomes associated with FOC include higher rates of maternal death, postpartum infection, hemorrhage, and DIC, while adverse fetal outcomes include birth asphyxia, transient tachypnea of newborn, respiratory distress syndrome, and sepsis [3, 4]. Besides its association with C-sections, FOC is also associated with higher rates of epidural use in labor, longer labor times, increased labor pain, psychological problems during confinement, and postpartum depression [1, 52, 53]. Regarding labor pain, FOC has even been reported to be an independent predictor of perceived labor pain intensity [54]. Unsurprisingly, FOC also affects the subjective experience of childbirth, such that women with FOC are more likely to have negative childbirth experiences and feel dissatisfied postpartum [2]. FOC also affects daily life in pregnancy, as it has been linked to poor appetite, poor sleep, mood swings, and difficulty concentrating [2], as well as tachycardia, tenseness, and restlessness [55].

Importantly, recent Clinical Practice Guidelines for postpartum pelvic girdle pain identify C-sections as an established risk factor for persistent LPP [56], which highlights the reciprocal relationship between FOC, mode of birth, and long-term musculoskeletal outcomes. The 2021 Delphi consensus [57] further emphasizes the importance of addressing fear-based beliefs and implementing evidence-based strategies in clinical practice. This aligns with recent proposals to reframe unhelpful beliefs and empower pregnant women with strategies that are stable, safe, and self-manageable [58]. Given all these adverse implications, FOC is therefore important to study to improve labor and postpartum outcomes and decrease unnecessary elective C-sections.

LPP during pregnancy is another clinically significant issue, as it affects sleep, work, and mental health [17]. Disturbed sleep in pregnancy can be harmful, as studies have shown that abnormal sleep patterns in late pregnancy are associated with postpartum depressive symptoms and increased risk for C-sections, pre-eclampsia, and longer labor times [17]. Additionally, pregnant women with LPP who self-medicate to treat their pain may unknowingly take medications that can harm the fetus [22].

Therefore, developing effective interventions to reduce FOC and LPP is imperative. Currently, LPP is commonly managed with medications and physiotherapy [16], while FOC has been managed with education programs [2]. However, there is no standardized best-practice treatment for either condition [2]. Furthermore, existing interventions treat FOC and LPP as separate entities, lacking an approach that addresses both when they coexist. Our study underscores the importance of focusing particularly on emotional vulnerability factors, since anxiety and depression emerged as the strongest predictors of FOC in women with LPP. Given these findings, it becomes even more critical to address FOC when it accompanies LPP. An integrated biopsychosocial intervention could combine physical rehabilitation with targeted psychological strategies aimed at reducing anxiety, depression, and maladaptive cognitions, thereby tackling both conditions simultaneously.

Strengths and limitations

A notable strength of our study is the large sample size, which provides robust statistical power and increases the reliability of our findings. The multicentric design also enhances the generalizability of our results. Another strength is the comprehensive assessment of multiple psychological constructs, which allowed us to identify anxiety and depression as the strongest predictors of FOC in women with LPP. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. As a case-control study, we can only suggest associations between variables, not causation. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish causal relationships and further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the association between FOC and LPP. In addition, because the study was conducted in Spain, cultural and socioeconomic factors may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. In addition, as measurements were taken only at the beginning of the third trimester, future studies should include assessments both earlier and later in pregnancy to better capture temporal changes in FOC, LPP, and related psychological factors. Lastly, we acknowledge that our model explains only a limited proportion of the variance in FOC scores. This may reflect the influence of unmeasured external factors, such as the therapeutic relationship with healthcare providers or even media influences. Future studies should take these aspects into account to provide a more comprehensive understanding of FOC in pregnancy.

Conclusion

The results of this study primarily suggest that pregnant women with LPP experience higher levels of FOC compared to those without LPP. Furthermore, among women with LPP, anxiety and depression emerged as the strongest predictors of FOC, while catastrophizing showed only a small association, and kinesiophobia was not significantly related. Future studies should be designed with the intent to develop interventions to effectively manage FOC and LPP, for example, via randomized controlled trials to test the efficacy of pain education programs for managing anxiety, depression, kinesiophobia, and catastrophizing. Developing interventions to manage FOC and LPP is crucial, as this will hopefully decrease unnecessary elective Cesarean sections and improve labor and postpartum outcomes.
