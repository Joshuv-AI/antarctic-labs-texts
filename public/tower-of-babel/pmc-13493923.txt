Comfort-oriented stiffness sensitivity assessment of long-span cable-stayed bridges based on vehicle-bridge coupled vibration analysis
Authors: Fangyu Wu, Yonghui Fan, Ningbo Ye, Jiang Yuan, Hong Zhou, Qinfei Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBridge stiffness is a key factor governing both structural serviceability and vehicle ride comfort in long-span cable-stayed bridges. This study proposes a comfort-oriented stiffness sensitivity assessment framework rather than an inverse stiffness-identification method. The objective is not to uniquely estimate bridge stiffness from ride comfort, but to quantify how prescribed stiffness reductions in major structural components influence bridge deformation and vehicle ride comfort under controlled vehicle-bridge interaction scenarios. Using the Linyu Yangtze River Bridge as a case study, a deterministic traffic flow is established through static-effect equivalence to code-based lane loading, and the influences of stiffness reduction in the main girder, stay cables, and bridge towers on bridge deformation and vehicle ride comfort are systematically investigated. The results indicate that pavement roughness has a dominant influence on vehicle-level ride comfort, whereas global bridge deformation is governed primarily by component stiffness. Different components exhibit different sensitivity characteristics: stay cable stiffness mainly controls the overall vertical deflection of the bridge, main girder stiffness has a more pronounced influence on girder-end rotation and comfort-related vehicle responses, and tower stiffness is closely associated with stability-sensitive global deformation. The proposed framework is therefore not intended to replace conventional load-carrying capacity evaluation, or stiffness identification. Instead, it provides a supplementary vehicle-bridge-interaction response layer that helps determine whether prescribed stiffness variations are mainly reflected in structural deformation, user-perceived vibration, or stability-sensitive response amplification. This information can support component prioritization, deck-serviceability diagnosis, and serviceability-oriented maintenance decision-making for long-span cable-stayed bridges.Keywords: Vehicle-bridge coupled vibration, Cable-stayed bridge, Vehicle ride comfort, Stiffness reduction, Stiffness sensitivity assessment, Deterministic traffic flowSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the rapid development of transportation networks, long-span bridges have become essential components of river-crossing and sea-crossing infrastructure1,2. Structural stiffness is a fundamental parameter governing bridge safety, and serviceability3–5. It not only directly affects the dynamic response of bridges under moving loads, but also strongly influences the driving safety and ride comfort of vehicles crossing the bridge6,7. Conventional bridge design codes usually define stiffness requirements on the basis of static load effects and material strength8, with limited consideration of vehicle-bridge dynamic interaction and the associated vibration problems. Therefore, investigating the sensitivity of bridge and vehicle responses to stiffness-related parameters from the perspective of vehicle–bridge coupled vibration is of considerable importance for improving the theoretical basis of dynamic stiffness design and achieving a better balance between economy and serviceability.

As a key indicator of structural performance design, bridge stiffness has been widely studied from two main perspectives. The first concerns the evaluation of stiffness evolution and its influencing factors based on structural dynamic properties9–14. For example, Gonzales et al.15 measured bridge natural frequencies in different seasons to investigate the effect of ambient temperature on structural stiffness. Soyoz et al.16 analyzed modal data monitored over five years from a concrete bridge and employed neural-network techniques to determine the temporal variation in stiffness. Erduran et al.17 investigated the effect of bearing stiffness on bridge dynamic characteristics, and showed that it significantly affects both the magnitude and location of maximum bridge acceleration. The second perspective involves the development of new technologies and algorithms for stiffness identification18–20. McGetrick et al.21 proposed an algorithm for estimating overall bridge stiffness from vehicle vibration data, using acceleration measurements collected from instrumented vehicles as input. Zhang et al.22 developed a stiffness identification method based on microwave interferometric radar and rotational influence lines, and validated its effectiveness experimentally. Anastasopoulos et al.23 analyzed strain data from a steel bridge to derive temperature-insensitive strain modes and further explored methods for determining actual bridge stiffness from these strain patterns.

Overall, although substantial progress has been made in bridge stiffness research, existing studies mainly focus on stiffness identification and temporal evolution of stiffness in completed bridges. Most of them emphasize bridge responses such as acceleration and displacement, while paying relatively little attention to the respective contributions of different structural components to the overall stiffness performance of the bridge. In addition, traditional stiffness-related serviceability assessments are rarely linked directly to vehicle ride comfort under realistic vehicle-bridge interaction conditions. To address these issues, this study takes the Linyu Yangtze River Bridge as the engineering background and develops a ride-comfort-informed stiffness sensitivity assessment framework within a vehicle-bridge coupled vibration analysis. Vehicle ride comfort of on-bridge vehicles is adopted as an additional serviceability indicator, and the influence of main girder, stay cables, and towers on bridge stiffness is systematically investigated. The aim is to provide a reference for stiffness-sensitive component identification and serviceability-oriented design of similar bridges.

It should be emphasized that the present study does not attempt to uniquely identify the bridge components stiffness from ride comfort measurements alone. In this paper, “stiffness” refers to the equivalent component stiffness parameters represented in the finite-element model. The proposed framework is a forward parametric sensitivity assessment method, in which prescribed stiffness-reduction factors are introduced to examine their effects on bridge deformation and vehicle ride comfort under controlled traffic, speed, and pavement roughness conditions. Therefore, ride comfort is not used as a sole proxy for bridge stiffness, but as an additional serviceability-oriented response indicator within a vehicle-bridge interaction framework.

Vehicle-bridge coupling dynamic analysis model

Definition and scope of stiffness in this study

In this study, the stiffness reduction factor is defined as the ratio of the reduced component stiffness to its original design-state stiffness. A reduction factor of 0.8, for example, represents a 20% reduction in the corresponding component stiffness in the finite-element model. These reduction factors are not intended to represent a unique physical damage mechanism, but are used as generalized degradation-inspired variables for sensitivity assessment.

To avoid overinterpreting the prescribed stiffness reduction factors as direct damage-identification results, the stiffness reduction factors adopted in this study are interpreted as equivalent component-level stiffness variation scenarios. In practical bridge engineering, the same equivalent stiffness reduction may be caused by different deterioration mechanisms, such as cracking, corrosion, cable force variation, loss of composite action, or boundary-condition changes. Therefore, the relationship between the stiffness reduction factor and actual damage state is qualitative rather than one-to-one.

In this study, stiffness reduction factors close to the reference state are regarded as engineering-relevant deterioration-inspired scenarios, whereas very low stiffness factors are retained only as exploratory lower-bound cases for identifying sensitivity trends and possible threshold-like response amplification. In vehicle-bridge coupling dynamic analysis, vehicles are typically idealized as a combination of a rigid vehicle body, suspension system, and tires24,25. Each component possesses six spatial degrees of freedom26. To improve convergence efficiency and numerical robustness under multi-vehicle loading conditions, the Aitken iteration acceleration strategy is employed throughout the solution process.

In practical applications, weighted evaluation and comfort spectrum evaluation may yield different results, with the former generally being more conservative. Under the most unfavorable conditions, where the measured vibration exhibits a broadband spectrum and its one-third octave characteristics are comparable to the whole-body vibration exposure limit curve, the weighted vibration level may exceed the one-third octave level in the most sensitive frequency band, leading to overly conservative results. Under the most favorable conditions, where all vibration energy is concentrated within a single one-third octave band, the two methods give the same result. Therefore, the present study adopts the one-third octave comfort spectrum as the evaluation criterion.

It should be noted that the bridge-crossing process is generally a short-duration exposure event, and the actual exposure time on the bridge may in some cases be shorter than 1 min. In the present study, the 1-minute exposure limit was adopted as a unified evaluation criterion for two reasons. First, it provides a standardized and conservative reference for comparing the effects of different stiffness parameters under a consistent comfort framework. Second, for short-duration bridge-crossing scenarios, the use of the 1-minute limit is on the safe side, since shorter exposure durations generally permit higher comfort thresholds.

Case study analysis

Project overview

The Linyu Yangtze River Bridge is a three-tower cable-stayed bridge with a total length of 1080 m, as shown in Fig. 7. The tall towers adopt rigid tower-girder connections, while the short towers are equipped with vertical support systems. This is consistent with the boundary conditions in its finite element model. Meanwhile, the corresponding longitudinal and transverse restraints at the bridge abutments are represented by the simplified boundary conditions adopted in the bridge finite element model. It should be noted that the present study focuses on the global vehicle-bridge coupled dynamic response within a linearized structural analysis framework. Therefore, the nonlinear behavior of bearings and expansion joints under dynamic loading is not explicitly simulated.

Layout of Linyu Yangtze River Bridge (unit: m).

The girder adopts a steel-concrete composite structure. The main spans are constructed in steel, while the side spans use concrete girders, as shown in Fig. 8. The steel box-girder segment of the bridge is 744 m, with a cross-sectional width of 49 m and a girder height of 4.0 m at the centerline.

Standard girder cross-section layout (unit: m).

The bridge towers are centrally positioned on the deck, featuring single-column configurations in the transverse direction. The tall towers reach a total height of 184 m, while the short towers extend to 136 m. For this three-tower configuration, the central tower and the side towers play different structural roles in the global force transmission and deformation coordination of the bridge system. In particular, the central tower is more directly involved in maintaining the overall stability of the main-span system, while the side towers primarily provide boundary restraint and local stiffness coordination with the side spans.

Traffic flow simulation on the bridge

The characteristics of vehicles traveling on bridges, including vehicle type, axle load, headway, and traveling speed, are inherently random. For newly constructed bridges, these random parameters are usually estimated by referring to measured traffic data from similar bridges and then generating stochastic traffic flows through statistical simulation. However, such stochastic traffic simulation depends strongly on the availability and quality of field measurements, and the obtained results may vary with the selected samples and statistical assumptions. Therefore, although stochastic traffic flow is suitable for describing the probabilistic variability of actual traffic operations, it is not always the most practical approach for design-oriented parametric analysis.

In highway bridge design, the lane loads specified in current codes are established through statistical analysis and equivalence treatment of extensive traffic survey data. Therefore, they can be regarded as representative design-level traffic actions. Based on this idea, the present study adopts a deterministic traffic flow derived by reversing inference from code-based lane loading according to the principle of static-effect equivalence. The purpose of this deterministic traffic flow is not to reproduce the full stochastic characteristics of real traffic at the individual-vehicle level, but rather to construct a representative, repeatable, and computationally efficient loading scenario for vehicle-bridge coupled vibration analysis and stiffness sensitivity assessment.

For the Linyu Yangtze River Bridge, the vertical static displacements influence line at the mid-span of the left main span and the longitudinal displacement influence line at the tower top were first calculated under code-based lane loading, as shown in Fig. 9. Then, based on the principle of equivalent static bridge effects, the corresponding vehicle types, axle loads, vehicle spacing, and vehicle quantities were determined by reverse inference, thereby establishing the deterministic traffic flow for the subsequent coupled dynamic analysis.

The results indicate that the dynamic response of the bridge is closely related to its natural frequencies. The moving vehicle loads act as an excitation with generalized deflection frequencies. When the deflection frequency approaches a certain vertical natural frequency of the bridge, a first-type resonance condition may occur, leading to pronounced bridge vibration. For the Linyu Yangtze River Bridge, the first order generalized deflection frequencies at vehicle speeds of 80 km/h and 100 km/h are 0.250 Hz and 0.431 Hz, respectively. These values are close to the first-order vertical bending fundamental frequency (0.2555 Hz) and second-order vertical bending frequency (0.4481 Hz), respectively. The first-order vertical bending mode exhibits an anti-symmetric configuration, while the second-order mode displays a symmetric configuration. This explains why the dynamic vertical displacement response of the main girder at 100 km/h is smaller than that at 80 km/h.

For the girder vertical displacement, girder-end rotation, and tower top longitudinal displacement, the influence of pavement roughness is negligible and can be essentially disregarded. It should be noted that the above response characteristics are consistent with the Rayleigh damping parameters selected in this study. The dynamic response of the Linyu Yangtze River Bridge under moving vehicles is mainly governed by low-order global modes, as confirmed by the modal analysis in Table 6 and the dynamic response results discussed above. In the Rayleigh damping model, the modal damping ratio varies with frequency, so calibrating the damping coefficients from low-order frequencies tends to produce relatively larger damping in the higher-frequency range. For long-span cable-stayed bridges, this helps suppress spurious local high-frequency response components and numerical noise while preserving the dominant low-frequency global response relevant to bridge displacement and vehicle ride comfort evaluation.

Dynamic response of the vehicles under traffic flow

Theoretical and numerical analyses indicate that the most pronounced vertical vehicle vibrations occur when the deterministic traffic flow travels at 80 km/h. Light vehicles 23, 26, and 27, as well as heavy vehicles 3, 9, and 15, were selected to investigate their comfort curves under different pavement roughness conditions, where the roughness levels correspond to ISO 8608 Class A, Class B, and Class C, as defined in “Vehicle-bridge coupling model” section, and the results are shown in Fig. 15.

Comfort curves for different vehicles under varying pavement roughness conditions. (a) Vehicle 23; (b) vehicle 26; (c) vehicle 27;(d) vehicle 3; (e) vehicle 9; (f) vehicle 15.

The results show that, when vehicles 23, 26, and 27 travel at 80 km/h on Class C road surface, their vertical vibration comfort exceeds the 1-minute limit. On a Class B road surface, all three vehicles exceed the 25-minute limit but remain below the 1-minute limit. In contrast, when vehicles 3, 9, and 15 travel at 80 km/h on a Class C road surface, their vertical vibration comfort exceeds the 25-minute limit but remains below the 1-minute limit. On a Class B road surface, all of them exceed the 1-hour limit but remain below the 25-minute limit. These results also show that, under the highway traffic scenario considered in this study, light vehicles exhibit more pronounced vibration responses than heavy vehicles. Therefore, for comfort-oriented assessment of highway cable-stayed bridges, the comfort indices of light vehicles may be taken as the governing indicators. However, this conclusion is scenario-dependent and should not be generalized to industrial heavy-haul bridges or special heavy-load traffic conditions. In such cases, heavy-vehicle running safety may be more critical than light-vehicle ride comfort in governing the required bridge stiffness. The 25-minute and 1-hour limits are discussed here only to indicate the relative severity of vibration exposure, whereas the 1 min limit remains the governing criterion for comparative comfort assessment in this study.

Sensitivity of bridge responses and vehicle ride comfort to component stiffness variations

Sensitivity analysis of vertical component stiffness

To investigate the sensitivity of bridge responses and vehicle ride comfort to component-level stiffness variations, the deterministic traffic flow described in “Case study analysis” section was adopted as the representative highway vehicle loading scenario. The analysis was conducted by systematically reducing the equivalent stiffness of the main girder, stay cables, towers, and the combined girder-tower systems.

The stiffness reduction factors adopted in this section should be interpreted according to the engineering framework defined in “Definition and scope of stiffness in this study” section. Specifically, stiffness reduction factors from 1.0 to 0.6 are mainly used to represent engineering-relevant deterioration-inspired scenarios, ranging from mild to moderate equivalent stiffness degradation. In contrast, the lower stiffness factors from 0.5 to 0.2 are retained as exploratory lower-bound cases. These severe cases are not intended to represent routine bridge service conditions, but are used to reveal sensitivity trends, response amplification, and possible stability-sensitive behavior of the vehicle-bridge coupled system.

Following the analysis framework established above, the bridge responses considered herein include the vertical displacement at the mid-span of the left main span, the vertical rotational angle at the girder end, and the one-third-octave band comfort spectrum of highway vehicles.

Interpretation and limitations of conditional stiffness sensitivity results

Although the ride comfort requirement is satisfied in all successfully completed cases, this does not imply that ride-comfort-based analysis is unnecessary. A binary pass/fail comfort assessment only indicates whether the adopted comfort limit is exceeded. The more important information lies in the comparison between comfort-related vehicle responses and conventional structural response indices. For example, some stiffness-reduction cases produce substantial bridge deformation while still satisfying the comfort criterion, indicating that structural serviceability or stability-sensitive deformation may govern the acceptable stiffness lower bound. Conversely, pavement roughness may significantly deteriorate vehicle ride comfort while producing only limited changes in global bridge displacement. Therefore, ride comfort should be interpreted as a supplementary response indicator rather than as a standalone control criterion. This finding has practical engineering significance. If both structural deformation and comfort-related vehicle vibration deteriorate simultaneously, component stiffness degradation may need to be examined together with deck-surface condition. If comfort deteriorates while structural deformation remains nearly unchanged, the deck surface, expansion joints, local irregularities, and vehicle excitation conditions should be checked first. If structural deformation becomes excessive while comfort remains acceptable, conventional serviceability and stability-related indices should govern the assessment. In this way, the proposed framework provides additional information for distinguishing different sources and consequences of serviceability deterioration.

The stiffness reduction factors used in this study represent equivalent component-level stiffness variations rather than specific physical damage states. In actual bridge engineering, similar equivalent stiffness reductions may result from different deterioration mechanisms, including concrete cracking, steel corrosion, cable force loss, anchorage deterioration, bearing degradation, connection damage, or changes in boundary conditions. Conversely, the same physical damage mechanism may lead to different equivalent stiffness changes depending on its location, extent, and interaction with the global structural system. For this reason, the mapping between stiffness reduction factor and actual damage state is qualitative rather than deterministic.

The severe stiffness-reduction cases, especially those with reduction factors of 0.5 or lower, are mainly introduced to reveal response trends, sensitivity amplification, and possible lower-bound warning behavior. They should not be regarded as representative conditions for routine engineering design or normal bridge service states.

Temperature variation is another important factor that may affect vehicle-bridge interaction responses. The present study does not explicitly consider temperature-induced stiffness variation. Instead, it focuses on the mechanical sensitivity of bridge and vehicle responses to prescribed component stiffness reductions under fixed environmental conditions. Therefore, before comfort-related vehicle responses are used for practical condition assessment, temperature effects should be separated or compensated using field monitoring data.

Conclusions

This study proposed a comfort-oriented stiffness sensitivity assessment framework for long-span cable-stayed bridges by integrating deterministic traffic flow simulation, vehicle-bridge coupled vibration analysis, and one-third-octave band ride comfort assessment. Taking the Linyu Yangtze River Bridge as the engineering background, the effects of stiffness reduction in the main girder, stay cables, and towers on bridge deformation and vehicle ride comfort were systematically investigated. Based on the results obtained, the following conclusions can be drawn.

The deterministic traffic flow established through static-effect equivalence to the code-based lane loading reproduced the benchmark live-load effects with good agreement. The obtained D2/D1 ratios were approximately 1.01–1.02, and the RMSE value was 0.0065 m, indicating that the adopted traffic model can provide a representative and repeatable design-oriented loading scenario for subsequent vehicle-bridge coupled vibration analysis.

Different structural components govern different response indices. Its main engineering contribution is to provide a supplementary vehicle-bridge-interaction response layer within the conventional assessment framework. By comparing structural deformation indices with ride-comfort-based vehicle responses, the framework helps distinguish deformation-governed, comfort-sensitive, and stability-sensitive response characteristics. This study also has several limitations. The deterministic traffic flow adopted herein is intended as a representative and repeatable design-oriented loading scenario rather than a full probabilistic description of actual traffic operations. The stiffness reduction factors are equivalent component-level parameters and should not be interpreted as direct representations of unique physical damage mechanisms. In practical applications, the relationship between equivalent stiffness reduction and actual deterioration state should be established using field inspection, and structural health monitoring data. In addition, the present study focuses on mechanical sensitivity under fixed environmental conditions and does not explicitly consider temperature-induced stiffness variation. Although the proposed framework is applicable to similar long-span cable-stayed bridges, the quantitative conclusions obtained from the investigated bridge should not be directly generalized to bridges with substantially different span arrangements, tower-girder connection forms, cable layouts, boundary conditions, or traffic characteristics without bridge-specific recalibration.
