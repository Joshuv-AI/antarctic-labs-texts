Strategic Content Creation with GenAI: To Share or Not to Share?
Gur Keinan, Omer Ben-Porat

Abstract
We introduce a game-theoretic framework that examines strategic interactions between a platform and its content creators in the presence of AI-generated content. Our model’s main novelty is in capturing creators’ dual strategic decisions: Their investment in content quality and their (possible) consent to share their content with the platform’s GenAI, both of which significantly impact their utility. To incentivize creators, the platform strategically allocates a portion of its GenAI-driven revenue to creators who share their content. We focus on the class of full-sharing equilibrium profiles, in which all creators willingly share their content with the platform’s GenAI system. Such equilibria are highly desirable both theoretically and practically. Our main technical contribution is formulating and efficiently solving a novel optimization problem that approximates the platform’s optimal revenue subject to inducing a full-sharing equilibrium. A key aspect of our approach is identifying conditions under which full-sharing equilibria exist and a surprising connection to the Prisoner’s Dilemma. Finally, our simulations demonstrate how revenue-allocation mechanisms affect creator utility and the platform’s revenue.

Online Content platforms such as YouTube, Medium, and SoundCloud have become ubiquitous, hosting vast ecosystems of creators who aim to maximize their reach, engagement, and revenue. GenAI enables platforms to synthesize content by distilling and recombining existing creator contributions. For instance, a news platform could use GenAI tools to generate personalized summaries of the day’s news tailored to individual users. This enhances user satisfaction and opens new monetization channels. However, it also introduces two significant challenges: Sharing consent and content quality.

This paper pioneers the study of revenue sharing in the presence of strategic content creators and AI-generated content. We present a game-theoretic model consisting of a platform and strategic creators, in which the platform can generate content using GenAI tools and allocate its GenAI-driven revenue based on creator actions. Our framework incorporates creators’ dual strategic decisions: (i) the extent to which they share their content with the platform’s GenAI, ranging from full withholding to full sharing, and (ii) the body of content they create, each incurring its own cost. The platform selects a revenue-sharing mechanism rewarding creators based on these two axes of contribution, with the goal of optimizing for content quality, overall revenue, or a tradeoff between them.

1.1
Our Contribution

Our contribution is twofold. First and foremost, we introduce the first game-theoretic model that captures both content production and data sharing in Generative AI ecosystems, where creators
and
the platform are strategic. User traffic is allocated through a Tullock contest structure
[
42
]
, where creators and the platform’s GenAI system compete based on their relative content quality. Within this environment, each creator decides not only the quality of their content but also how much of it to share with the GenAI. The GenAI’s content quality is determined by the pool of shared creator content; thus, the platform should incentivize creators to produce and share content. To that end, we assume the platform can select a proportional allocation rule (see
Equation

1

\rho\in[0,1]

\rho

Our second contribution is technical. We study equilibria in the induced game, and focus on a subclass of equilibrium profiles we term
full-sharing equilibria
(FSE for abbreviation; see Definition

2
Model

We consider a platform that hosts content produced by a set of human creators, alongside content generated by a GenAI system controlled by the platform. We formally define the components of the interaction below.

Creators

We consider
n\geq 2
human content creators competing for user traffic and denote the set of creators by
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, with a typical creator indexed by
i\in[n]
. Each creator produces a
body of content
that encompasses multiple attributes: Quality, quantity, trend alignment, domain expertise or specialty, and so on. As is standard in prior work (e.g.,
[
52
,
50
]
), we aggregate these attributes into a single scalar, and without loss of generality, call it
content quality
. Formally, creator
i
selects a quality
x_{i}\in[0,\infty)
.

For each creator
i
, creating a content
x_{i}
incurs a production cost
c_{i}(x_{i})
. While we do not assume a particular form of the cost function, we have several structural assumptions that are standard in prior work. We assume that for every
i\in[n]
, the cost function
c_{i}
is non-decreasing, twice differentiable, and strictly convex. Moreover, we assume that
c_{i}(0)=0
,
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{dc_{i}(x)}{dx}=0
, and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{dc_{i}(x)}{dx}=\infty
. These assumptions ensure that not creating content is costless, and that the marginal cost of improving the quality by one unit is initially zero but becomes unbounded.

Beyond choosing the content quality, each creator also chooses their
sharing level

s_{i}\in[0,1]
. The sharing level represents the extent to which creator
i
allows their content
x_{i}
to be used by the GenAI system, ranging from complete withholding (
s_{i}=0
) to full sharing (
s_{i}=1
). Therefore, the strategy of each creator
i\in[n]
is a pair
(x_{i},s_{i})
. Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
denote the quality and sharing profiles, respectively. Additionally, for a creator
i\in[n]
, we let
\mathbf{x}_{-i}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{s}_{-i}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{n})
denote the quality and sharing profiles after omitting the
i
’th entry.

Generative AI

The platform operates a GenAI system, modeled as a non-strategic agent whose output quality depends on the aggregate quality of the content shared from creators,
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}s_{j}
. Its quality is given by
Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}
, where
\alpha>0
represents the efficiency of data usage.
2
2

2

We discuss extensions of some of our results to a more general functional form of
Q_{\text{AI}}
in
Section

6
.
Notably, if no data is shared (i.e.,
\mathbf{s}=0
), then
Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=0
. Whenever
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{s}
are clear from the context, we simplify the notation and use
Q_{\text{AI}}
.

User traffic and engagement

The platform attracts user traffic denoted by
T(\mathbf{x})
, which increases monotonically with the total quality of human content,
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}
, where we use
\|\cdot\|
without a subscript to denote the
\ell_{1}
norm throughout the paper. We model traffic as
T(\mathbf{x})=\mu\cdot\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}
,
where
\mu>0
is a baseline traffic parameter and
\gamma\in[0,1]
captures the elasticity of traffic with respect to the total human content quality
[
39
,
13
]
.
3
3

3

We assume that users are attracted to the platform due to its human-generated content, not the AI-generated content. The reason is that users tend to value originality and exhibit bias against AI-generated content when it lacks it
[
5
]
, which is the case in emerging domains where historical data is scarce
[
32
]
. We discuss extensions of our results to other form of traffic in
Section

6
.

Given a strategy profile
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
and the induced user traffic
T(\mathbf{x})
, users allocate their attention to human and AI-generated content according to a Tullock-like decision based on relative quality
[
42
]
. Specifically, the fraction of attention allocated to creator
i
’s content is
\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})}
, while the fraction allocated to AI-generated content is
\frac{Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})}
. In both cases, the denominator represents the total quality of created and generated content available on the platform.

Platform strategy

The platform may have multiple sources of revenue. However, to ease presentation, we focus on the user traffic it receives from its AI-generated content. Note that it depends on
Q_{\text{AI}}
, which in turn depends on human content. To obtain high-quality AI-generated content, the platform can allocate some of this revenue back to creators. Formally, the platform commits to an
allocation rule

f:[0,\infty)^{n}\times[0,1]^{n}\to\Delta^{n+1}
. It maps every strategy profile
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
to a vector
(f_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}),\ldots,f_{n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}),f_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}))
, where
f_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
represents the fraction of GenAI-driven revenue allocated to creator
i\in[n]
, and
f_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
is the fraction retained by the platform. Here,
\Delta^{n+1}
denotes the
(n+1)
-dimensional simplex.

While various allocation mechanisms are possible, we focus on the class of parameterized
proportional allocation rules
f_{\rho}
, formally defined below, and address other designs in
Appendix

E
. The revenue-allocation parameter
\rho\in[0,1]
, or simply the
allocation parameter
, specifies the fraction of GenAI-driven revenue allocated to creators, with the remainder
1-\rho
retained by the platform. Under this rule, the platform distributes the
\rho
-fraction among creators in proportion to their effective shared contributions
x_{i}s_{i}
, yielding

f_{i,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\begin{cases}\rho\cdot\frac{x_{i}s_{i}}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}}&\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}>0\\
0&\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}=0\end{cases},

(1)

and
f_{P,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
. We note that whenever
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}>0
, it holds that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\rho
and
f_{P,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=1-\rho
.

Revenue and utilities

Each human creator
i\in[n]
receives revenue from two sources and incurs a creation cost. The first revenue source is direct user traffic to their content, yielding a revenue of
T(\mathbf{x})\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}}
. The second revenue source is their allocated share of the GenAI-driven revenue, given by
T(\mathbf{x})\frac{Q_{\text{AI}}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}}\cdot f_{i,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
. Overall, creator
i
’s utility is

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})=T(\mathbf{x})\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}}+T(\mathbf{x})\frac{Q_{\text{AI}}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}}\cdot f_{i,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})-c_{i}(x_{i}).

The platform’s revenue corresponds to the portion of GenAI revenue it retains under the allocation rule
f_{\rho}
, which is given by

U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})=T(\mathbf{x})\frac{Q_{\text{AI}}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}}\cdot f_{P,\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}).

Stackelberg game

We model the interaction as a Stackelberg game, with the platform as the leader and the creators as followers. The platform first commits to a revenue-allocation rule
f_{\rho}
. This choice induces a subgame among the creators, denoted by
G(\rho)
. In this game, each creator
i\in[n]
simultaneously selects a strategy
(x_{i},s_{i})
to maximize their utility given the fixed rule
f_{\rho}
. The resulting profile
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
determines the user traffic
T(\mathbf{x})
, the quality of AI-generated content
Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
, and the resulting payoffs
U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})
and
U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})
. This structure enables the platform to steer creator behavior through the choice of the allocation parameter
\rho
.

2.1
Solution Concepts

We are interested in stable outcomes in the subgame
G(\cdot)
, where stability refers to pure Nash equilibrium (PNE). We highlight a class of PNEs that we call
F
ull
S
haring PN
E
, or FSE for abbreviation. In FSE, full content sharing arises endogenously: Each creator chooses to share their content because the platform’s revenue-allocation mechanism renders sharing a best response.

Definition 1

(FSE)
.

A strategy profile
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})
is an
FSE
in the subgame
G(\rho)
if for every creator
i\in[n]
,

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})\geq\sup_{x_{i}^{\prime}\in[0,\infty),\,s_{i}^{\prime}\in[0,1]}U_{i}((x_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{-i}),(s_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{1}_{-i});f_{\rho}).

FSEs are a special case of PNEs in which all creators fully share their content. While other equilibria involving partial or no sharing may exist, FSEs are appealing from the platform’s perspective: They maximize the quality of AI-generated content given the created content. Focusing on FSE is also practically motivated, as it prevents unauthorized use of content and disputes or resentment from creators who believe their content was used without consent. FSEs naturally extend to approximate equilibria: A strategy profile
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})
is an
\varepsilon
-FSE if no creator
i
has an
\varepsilon
-beneficial deviation.

To facilitate the analysis in the forthcoming sections, we also present the following auxiliary equilibrium concept called
E
nforced
S
haring PN
E
, or simply ESE.

Definition 2

(ESE)
.

A quality profile
\mathbf{x}
is an
ESE
in the subgame
G(\rho)
if for every creator
i\in[n]
,

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})\geq\sup_{x_{i}^{\prime}\in[0,\infty)}U_{i}((x_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{-i}),\mathbf{1};f_{\rho}).

To motivate
Definition

2
, imagine the auxiliary
enforced sharing game
, where
f_{\rho}
is fixed and creators
must
share their content, i.e.,
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
. ESEs allow us to analyze game dynamics by focusing solely on creators’ quality profiles
\mathbf{x}
. We remark that any quality profile
\mathbf{x}
such that
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})
is an FSE also constitutes an ESE.

3.1
Best Sharing Response and Uniqueness of ESE

We begin by analyzing the creators’ best responses under a fixed allocation rule
f_{\rho}
. First, we provide a full characterization of the optimal sharing level

for a given profile
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{-i})

Lemma 2
.

Fix an arbitrary creator
i

x_{i}>0
, and a profile
(\mathbf{x}_{-i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})
. Further, let
\tau_{i}
such that
\tau_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|+\alpha\mathbf{x}^{\top}_{-i}\mathbf{s}_{-i}}
. The optimal sharing level
s
of creator

w.r.t.
(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})

s\in\argmax_{s\in[0,1]}U_{i}((x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}),(s,\mathbf{s}_{-i});f_{\rho})
is given by

s^{\star}=\begin{cases}1&\rho>\tau_{i}\\
\textnormal{any number from }[0,1]&\rho=\tau_{i}\\
0&\rho<\tau_{i}\\
\end{cases}.

(2)

Due to space constraints, all proofs in this and subsequent sections are deferred to the appendix. The proof of
Lemma

2
involves showing that creator

when

\rho<\tau_{i}

2

Lemma

2
reveals an interesting asymmetry: Creators producing a large share of

have higher thresholds; thus, they are more inclined to withhold their data, as doing so weakens a powerful GenAI competitor. In contrast, creators with relatively low quality have stronger incentives to share. Notably, the sharing decision becomes effectively binary—each creator either fully shares or fully withholds, with no incentive to choose an intermediate level.

Another insight from
Lemma

2
concerns the case
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}
, where all creators initially withhold their data. Fix a quality profile
\mathbf{x}
, and notice that if the number of creators
n
is moderately large, the threshold
\tau_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}
becomes small for most creators. Consequently, even a low revenue-allocation parameter
\rho\approx\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}
can make it beneficial for the least productive creator, denoted by
i
, to deviate and set
s_{i}=1
. This deviation increases
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}
, thereby lowering the threshold
\tau_{j}
for other creators

\frac{x_{j}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha x_{i}}<\frac{x_{j}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}=\tau_{j}

.

For any fixed

3.2
Existence of FSE

We now examine conditions under which full sharing can arise in a PNE. For a profile to be an FSE, it must induce a quality profile that constitutes an ESE under enforced sharing. The uniqueness of the ESE implies that, if an FSE exists, both the creators’ content qualities and their sharing decisions are uniquely determined. Therefore, the remaining question is whether the provided revenue allocation is enough to induce such a profile in equilibrium.

First, we consider the two extreme cases,
\rho=0
and
\rho=1
. The former is trivial—reators can only be worse off from sharing; hence, withholding content is a dominant strategy, and no FSE exists in
G(0)
. The latter case is more interesting. If
\rho=1
, the platform allocates its entire GenAI-driven revenue to the creators. While this might not be practical, it provides a useful reference point for creator-centered ecosystems.

Proposition 4
.

The subgame
G(1)
admits a unique PNE, which is an FSE.

The result builds on a key structural property: When
\rho=1
, full sharing becomes a dominant strategy for every creator.
Lemma

2
offers intuition: As long as
\mathbf{x}
is strictly positive,
\rho=1
will surpass every possible threshold
\tau_{i}
, no matter the choice of
x_{i}
. This guarantees that any PNE must involve full sharing, and existence and uniqueness of the resulting quality profile then follow from
Lemma

3
. Extensions of
Proposition

4
to other forms of
Q_{\text{AI}}
are discussed in
Section

6
.

Generalizing
Proposition

4
beyond
\rho=1
requires careful attention. Although
Lemmas

2
and
3
provide a tight characterization of the (decoupled) best responses in

and

to induce an FSE.

Theorem 5
.

Fix any
\rho\in[0,1]
. For every

to be the unique positive value satisfying the equation

.

Fix some creator

4
The Platform’s Revenue Maximization Problem

We now turn to the platform’s strategic problem of selecting the revenue-allocation parameter
\rho\in[0,1]
that maximizes its retained revenue, subject to the constraint that creators respond by fully sharing their data. This induces a bilevel optimization problem: The platform acts as a Stackelberg leader, anticipating the equilibrium behavior of creators in response to its choice of
\rho

4.1

The platform’s optimization problem, which we term

\sup_{\rho\in[0,1]}\ U_{P}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})\quad\text{subject to }(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\mathbf{1})\text{ is an FSE}.

(PO)

Further, let

over

\mathcal{X}(\rho)

Finally, to allow efficient approximation, we impose additional regularity assumptions on the cost functions by assuming they are

Extensions

In this section, we provide several natural extensions of our framework and discuss their implications on our main results.

6.1
Multiple Topics

The following extension enables creators to allocate different efforts across various content topics, while the platform maintains a single revenue-allocation rule. Consider
K
distinct content topics. Each creator
i
now chooses a quality vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,K})\in[0,\infty)^{K}
and a sharing vector
\mathbf{s}_{i}=(s_{i,1},\ldots,s_{i,K})\in[0,1]^{K}
, where
x_{i,k}
and
s_{i,k}
represents their quality and sharing level in topic
k
, respectively. The cost function, which now gets
\mathbf{x}_{i}
as input, is convex but need not be separable across topics, i.e.,
c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\sum_{k}c_{i,k}(x_{i,k})
, for most results to hold.

Each topic
k
generates independent user traffic
T_{k}
, and the GenAI system’s quality is
Q_{\text{AI},k}
; both depend only on the restriction of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{s}
to topic
k
and have the same functional forms as in the single-topic case. The platform commits to a single allocation parameter
\rho\in[0,1]
across all topics. Under proportional allocation, creator
i
’s revenue from topic
k
is given by

R_{i,k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})=\frac{T_{k}(\mathbf{x})x_{i,k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j,k}+Q_{\text{AI},k}}+\frac{T_{k}(\mathbf{x})Q_{\text{AI},k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j,k}+Q_{\text{AI},k}}\cdot\frac{\rho x_{i,k}s_{i,k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j,k}s_{j,k}},

and its overall utility is
U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{i,k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})-c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.

Results

Lemma

2
extends to each topic independently: For fixed quality choices, the optimal sharing decision
s_{i,k}
for topic
k
follows the same threshold structure in
Equation

2
, with
\tau_{i,k}=\frac{x_{i,k}}{x_{i,k}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j,k}+\alpha_{k}\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j,k}s_{j,k}}
.
Lemma

3
holds as well—since the only interplay between different topics is through the cost function, the same analysis still applies.
Theorem

5
requires some modifications, but the general idea remains the same: A large enough
\rho
admits FSE. The main technical difference is establishing per-topic rational quality bounds
\mathbf{x}_{i,k}^{\max}
when the cost functions couple topics together. The approach of
Algorithm

1
generalizes to this setting too, but testing the condition in Line
7
becomes computationally intensive, as we need to check each of the
2^{K}-1
possible sharing deviations. This can be avoided when the cost functions are separable across topics.

6.2
Prior GenAI Data

Next, we allow the GenAI system to possess a fixed body of prior data, independent of the creators’ sharing contributions. Formally, we introduce a non-strategic creator indexed by
0
with fixed quality
x_{0}>0
and full sharing level
s_{0}=1
. The quality of the GenAI system then becomes
Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\alpha\left(x_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}s_{j}\right)
, so that even if no strategic creator shares content, the GenAI still attains positive quality
\alpha x_{0}
. This modification also affects user traffic. Essentially, we assume that there is another source of
x_{0}
body of content in the platform that is always available to the GenAI system.

Results

From creator
i
’s perspective, the new creator is just another content contributor with fixed quality and full sharing; thus, the threshold from
Lemma

2
updates accordingly to

\tau_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|+\alpha\mathbf{x}^{\top}_{-i}\mathbf{s}_{-i}+(1+\alpha)x_{0}}.

Notably, prior data
x_{0}>0
strictly decreases
\tau_{i}
, making full sharing more attractive for creators. The proof of
Lemma

3
requires a minor modification, as the dummy creator’s strategy is fixed. Nonetheless, the resulting subgame among the creators maintains the DSC property. All other results extend directly to this setting.

6.3
Alternative Optimization Objectives

While our analysis in
Section

4
focused on maximizing platform revenue (recall (
PO
)), there are other desirable objectives to consider. For instance, a platform may wish to balance its retained value with total content production and creator welfare. Our framework naturally extends to such cases, where the target function is Lipschitz continuous in the quality profile
\mathbf{x}
and the allocation parameter
\rho
. The platform’s problem becomes

\sup_{\rho\in[0,1]}\ f(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\quad\text{subject to }(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\mathbf{1})\text{ is an FSE}.

Natural choices for
f
include total content production
f(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}
, creator welfare
f(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})
, or regularized combinations such as

f(\mathbf{x},\rho)=(1-\lambda)U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})+\lambda\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}.

Results

The same algorithmic approach from
Algorithm

1
applies directly to these generalized objectives, with the approximation guarantees of
Theorem

8
carrying over after adjusting the constants
A
and
B
to account for the Lipschitz properties of
f
.

6.4
GenAI-Dependent Traffic

In our main model, user traffic
T(\mathbf{x})
depends solely on the quality of human-generated content. However, high-quality AI-generated content may also attract users to the platform. To capture this effect, we extend our traffic model to incorporate both human and AI contributions:

T(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\mu\left(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\right)^{\gamma}.

All other components of the model remain unchanged.

Results

Under this extended traffic model, the creator’s optimal sharing decision retains its threshold structure, with an adjusted threshold given by
\tau_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\left(\frac{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\left(x_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}x_{j}s_{j}\right)}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}s_{j}}\right)^{1-\gamma}-1\right]
.
Notably, larger values of
\gamma
decrease
\tau_{i}
, making sharing more attractive. This occurs because higher traffic elasticity amplifies the positive externality that sharing exerts on the platform’s traffic.

The analysis of ESE remains tractable under this extension. In the enforced sharing game where
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
, the traffic function simplifies to
T(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})=\mu(1+\alpha)^{\gamma}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}
. This expression is structurally identical to our original traffic model, differing only by a multiplicative scaling of the baseline parameter
\mu
by
(1+\alpha)^{\gamma}
. Consequently,
Lemma

3
applies directly: The subgame
G(\rho)
under enforced sharing admits a unique ESE. Since the utility function at
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
retains its structural properties, our main results carry over to this setting with adjusted constants. Specifically, the FSE existence condition (
Theorem

5
) holds by redefining
x_{i}^{\max}
as the unique positive solution to
c_{i}(x)=\mu(1+\alpha)^{\gamma}x^{\gamma}
. The approximation guarantees of
Theorem

8
follow similarly.

6.5
GenAI Quality Beyond Linearity

In our baseline model, the quality of the GenAI system is linear in the aggregate amount of shared content, given by
Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}s_{i}
. This specification is both analytically convenient and captures the essence of the strategic dynamics in the system. However, in practice, GenAI systems often exhibit diminishing marginal returns as the amount of training data grows
[
31
]
. To capture this effect, we consider the following generalization of
Q_{\text{AI}}
:

Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\alpha\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}s_{i}\right)^{\beta},\qquad\beta\in[0,1].

This form, introduced by
Yao et al. [50]
, captures diminishing returns in GenAI quality. Notice that when
\beta=1
, this formulation reduces to our original model.

Theoretical results.

When
\beta<1
, the enforced sharing game is no longer concave and therefore does not satisfy Rosen’s DSC condition. As a consequence, we lose general guarantees on the existence and uniqueness of an ESE. Indeed, in numerical experiments, we observe instances that admit multiple approximate ESEs, which can differ in the induced quality profiles. From a more positive perspective, several qualitative insights from our baseline model remain valid. First,
Proposition

4
transfers directly: When
\rho=1
, full sharing is a dominant strategy for every creator, and the subgame admits a unique PNE that is an FSE. Second, a weaker analogue of
Lemma

2
continues to hold. In particular, when no creator shares content, creator
i
has an incentive to deviate and share whenever
\rho>\frac{x_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}}
. This mirrors the baseline threshold and gives rise to a prisoner’s dilemma-like structure in large systems.

Simulation methodology.

To study equilibria under the generalized GenAI quality function with
\beta<1
, we rely on numerical methods. We focus on the enforced sharing game, where all creators are required to share (
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
), and compute an approximate ESE via best-response dynamics. Starting from an arbitrary initial quality profile, creators update their quality choices sequentially. Each best response in
x_{i}
is computed via exact one-dimensional optimization over the interval
[0,x_{i}^{\max}]
, where
x_{i}^{\max}
is the analytical upper bound derived in the paper. To ensure robust convergence, we employ a damping factor that interpolates between the current quality and the computed best response. We declare convergence once the maximum change in any creator’s quality falls below a tolerance
\varepsilon
. Across all instances we tested, these dynamics converged quickly to a stable quality profile, which we treat as an
\varepsilon
-ESE.

To test whether a converged profile constitutes an
\varepsilon
-FSE, we explicitly check unilateral deviations in the sharing decision. For each creator, we consider both
s_{i}=0
and
s_{i}=1
, and for each case, compute the optimal quality choice via one-dimensional optimization. If no such deviation yields a utility gain exceeding
\varepsilon
, we classify the profile as an
\varepsilon
-FSE. Notably, although multiple approximate equilibria may exist in this setting, our simulations reveal that they exhibit similar qualitative properties across different initializations.

7
Discussion and Future Work

We explored strategic data sharing and revenue allocation in content platforms with GenAI. We modeled the interaction between creators and the platform, where each creator chooses both the quality of their content and the extent to which they share it with the platform. The platform, which commits to an allocation rule, allocates them a portion of its GenAI-driven revenue based on their content qualities and sharing decisions. We characterized conditions for inducing an FSE, and proposed an efficient approximation algorithm for solving the platform’s revenue maximization problem (
PO
). Our simulations examined sensitivity and equilibrium outcomes.

We see several promising directions for future research. First, while our analysis focused on proportional allocation rules, other functional forms may lead to better outcomes. For instance, allocation rules that exhibit a staircase structure, providing high revenue for certain quality levels but diminishing afterward, could potentially yield more nuanced and efficient outcomes. Second, we restricted our attention to FSEs, but other PNEs (non-FSEs) may exist. Indeed,
Lemma

2
hints that asymmetric sharing, where only some creators choose to share content, can occur in equilibrium. Understanding which allocation rules can induce such equilibria and whether they lead to improved platform revenue remains an important open question. Third, extending our theoretical guarantees to more general GenAI quality functions with diminishing returns (
\beta<1
in
Section

6.5
) remains open. The loss of concavity invalidates our uniqueness results, and while simulations suggest equilibria persist with similar qualitative behavior, formal existence and characterization guarantees are lacking. Finally, our model considers a static interaction; incorporating dynamic elements such as creators entering or exiting the platform, evolving GenAI capabilities, or repeated interactions with learning could yield richer insights into long-term platform-creator relationships.

For each parameter combination, we initialized 150 independent game instances with randomly sampled cost coefficients. For each instance, we evaluated 100 equally-spaced values of

Remark 11

For the rest of the paper, we denote

as the unique positive solution to
c_{i}(x)=\mu x^{\gamma}
. This value is well-defined because the function

is strictly convex (as

is strictly convex and
x^{\gamma}
is concave) and satisfies

and
\lim_{x\to\infty}g(x)=\infty
. The boundary conditions imply that

must eventually become positive, ensuring it crosses zero at least once. Meanwhile, strict convexity ensures that the slope is strictly increasing, meaning

can cross the x-axis exactly once.

We establish existence and uniqueness by demonstrating that the enforced sharing game is monotone and the utility functions are strictly concave in each player’s action.

Step 1: Utility structure and gradient computation

Lemma

12
asserts that, under enforced sharing, creator
i
’s utility is:

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})=\frac{\mu(1+\alpha\rho)}{1+\alpha}\cdot(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)^{\gamma-1}\cdot x_{i}-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Let us denote the first term by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle=K\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left[(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)^{\gamma-1}\cdot x_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=K\left[(\gamma-1)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-1}\right]

\displaystyle=K\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\left[(\gamma-1)x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\right]

\displaystyle=K\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\left[\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right].

We denote this gradient by
A_{i}
, and note that
v_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=A_{i}-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})

Step 2: Strategy space consideration

13

[0,x_{i}^{\max}]

x_{i}^{\max}
is the unique positive root of
\mu x^{\gamma}=c_{i}(x)

Lemma

ensures that all rational strategies involve strictly positive quality choices, allowing us to focus on the interior of this space.

Step 3: Strict concavity of utility functions

We establish that

\mathbf{x}_{-i}
by demonstrating that
R_{i}
is concave in
x_{i}
, and then using the strict concavity of

and the fact that the sum of a concave and a strictly concave function is strictly concave. Taking the derivative of
A_{i}
with respect to
x_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle=K\left[(\gamma-2)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-3}\left(\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right)+\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\gamma\right]

\displaystyle=K\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-3}\left[(\gamma-2)(\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|)+\gamma\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\right]

\displaystyle=K\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-3}\left[(\gamma^{2}-2\gamma)x_{i}+(\gamma-2)\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|+\gamma(x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|)\right]

\displaystyle=K\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-3}\left[(\gamma^{2}-\gamma)x_{i}+(2\gamma-2)\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right]

\displaystyle=K(\gamma-1)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-3}\left[\gamma x_{i}+2\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right].

For

is non-positive since

\frac{\partial^{2}R_{i}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\leq 0
, establishing that

is concave in
x_{i}

c_{i}
is strictly convex,
-c_{i}
is strictly concave. Thus,
U_{i}=R_{i}-c_{i}

Step 4: Monotonicity of the game

To establish monotonicity, we need to show for any distinct

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(v_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-v_{i}(\mathbf{x}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})<0.

Substituting our gradient expressions:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(v_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-v_{i}(\mathbf{x}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(A_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime}))-(A_{i}(\mathbf{x})-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}))\right](x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-A_{i}(\mathbf{x}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i}).

For the second term, since

is strictly convex, we have

For the first term, we need to analyze

We now compute those partial derivatives. For

\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x})=K\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-3}(\gamma-1)\left[\gamma x_{i}+2\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right].

For

\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})=K\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left[\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}(\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|)\right].

Since

\displaystyle=K\left[(\gamma-2)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-3}(\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|)+\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\right]

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

is the unique optimum.

Next, we show that under
\rho=1
and full sharing, the utility function simplifies to a form that does not depend on
\beta
. When
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{s}=\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|
and
f_{i,1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})=\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}
. Substituting into the general utility expression:

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{1})=\mu\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}\cdot\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\beta}}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\alpha\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\beta}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\beta}}\cdot\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Combining the revenue terms over a common denominator and factoring, we obtain

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{1})=\frac{\mu\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}x_{i}(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\beta})}{(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\beta})\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}-c_{i}(x_{i})=\mu\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-1}x_{i}-c_{i}(x_{i}).

This simplified form is identical to that analyzed in the proof of
Lemma

3
for
\beta=1
. Since that analysis depends only on the structure
\mu\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-1}x_{i}-c_{i}(x_{i})
and not on
\beta
, we conclude that the enforced sharing game under
\rho=1
admits a unique ESE, which we denote by
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
.

To establish that
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{1})
is the unique FSE, consider any deviation by creator
i
to
(x_{i}^{\prime},s_{i}^{\prime})
. By a straightforward extension of
Lemma

15
to general
\beta
, we have
x_{j}^{\star}>0
for all
j
, ensuring
\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\star}_{-i}\right\|>0
. By the analysis above, any profitable deviation must have
s_{i}^{\prime}=1
. But then the best response in quality is
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}^{\star}
, since
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
is an ESE. Thus
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{1})
is an FSE, and uniqueness follows from the uniqueness of the ESE.

No other PNE exists

Suppose
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})
is an arbitrary PNE. By the extension of
Lemma

15
, we must have
x_{i}^{\prime}>0
for all
i\in[n]
. Since
n\geq 2
, this implies
\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{-i}\right\|>0
for each creator
i
. By the optimality of full sharing established above, we must have
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}
. But then the quality profile
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
must constitute an ESE, and by uniqueness,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\star}
. Therefore,
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{1})
is the unique PNE.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

We establish that when
\rho
satisfies the condition in the theorem statement, the unique ESE with full sharing constitutes the unique FSE of the game. The proof proceeds in three main steps: First, we analyze properties of the ESE; second, we demonstrate that full sharing is strictly dominant under the condition on
\rho
; and finally, we verify that the resulting profile is the unique FSE.

Step 1: Properties of the ESE profile

Recall that the existence and uniqueness of the ESE profile
\mathcal{X}(\rho)
are guaranteed by
Lemma

3
. By
Lemma

15
, any rational strategy must involve a strictly positive quality choice, which implies
\mathcal{X}_{i}(\rho)>0
for all
i\in[n]
. This allows us to use
Lemma

2
in the next step.

Step 2: Optimality of full sharing under the specified condition

We examine when full sharing (
s_{i}=1
) is the unique best response for creator
i
, given that all other creators
j\neq i
play
(x_{j}^{\star},s_{j}=1)
.

By
Lemma

2
, for any fixed quality
x_{i}>0
, full sharing is the unique optimal choice if and only if:

\rho>\tau_{i}(x_{i})\coloneqq\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}+\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|+\alpha\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|}=\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}+(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|}.

The derivative of
\tau_{i}
with respect to
x_{i}
is:

\frac{\partial\tau_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|}{(x_{i}+(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|)^{2}}>0.

This confirms that
\tau_{i}(x_{i})
is strictly increasing in
x_{i}>0
. Recall that
Lemma

13
ensures that for any rational quality choice, we must have
x_{i}\leq x_{i}^{\max}
. Since
\tau_{i}(x_{i})
is strictly increasing in
x_{i}
and any rational quality choice satisfies
x_{i}\leq x_{i}^{\max}
, the maximum threshold value for creator
i
occurs at
x_{i}=x_{i}^{\max}
, yielding:

\max_{x_{i}\in(0,x_{i}^{\max}]}\tau_{i}(x_{i})=\tau_{i}(x_{i}^{\max})=\frac{x_{i}^{\max}}{x_{i}^{\max}+(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|}.

The theorem’s condition requires:

\rho>\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{x_{i}^{\max}}{x_{i}^{\max}+(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|}.

This guarantees that for all creators
i\in[n]
and all rational quality choices
x_{i}\in(0,x_{i}^{\max}]
, the inequality
\rho>\tau_{i}(x_{i})
holds. Consequently, full sharing is the strictly dominant strategy for each creator at any rational quality level.

Step 3: Verification of FSE properties

Consider an arbitrary creator
i
contemplating a deviation from
(\mathcal{X}_{i}(\rho),1)
to any alternative strategy
(x_{i}^{\prime},s_{i}^{\prime})
.

By
Lemma

13
, if
(x_{i}^{\prime},s_{i}^{\prime})
is to be a beneficial deviation, we must have
x_{i}^{\prime}\leq x_{i}^{\max}
. For any such rational quality choice
x_{i}^{\prime}>0
, Step 2 has established that
s_{i}=1
strictly dominates any other sharing level when
\rho
satisfies the theorem’s condition. Therefore, regardless of the quality choice
x_{i}^{\prime}
, creator
i
would maximize utility by choosing
s_{i}=1
rather than any
s_{i}^{\prime}<1
.

This means that any rational deviation must effectively reduce to a quality-only deviation with full sharing, i.e.,
(x_{i}^{\prime},1)
. However, from the definition of an ESE,
\mathcal{X}_{i}(\rho)
is the unique best response to
(\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{\star},\mathbf{1}_{-i})
among all strategies with
s_{i}=1
. Therefore, no deviation to any
(x_{i}^{\prime},1)
can be profitable.

Combining these observations, we conclude that no creator
i
has a profitable deviation from
(\mathcal{X}_{i}(\rho),1)
, making
(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\mathbf{1})
a PNE. Moreover, uniqueness follows from the observation that any FSE must be an ESE when restricted to the subgame with
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
. Since
Lemma

3
establishes that the ESE is unique, there can be no other FSE. This completes the proof of
Theorem

5
.
∎

\displaystyle M(1-w)

Increasing both
w
and

by the same amount increases

2.

\displaystyle\max\{B(q_{1},u_{1},v_{1},w_{1},t),B(q_{2},u_{2},v_{2},w_{2},t)\}

\displaystyle\leq B(\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\},\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\},\max\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\max\{w_{1},w_{2}\},t).

Lemma 20

Proof of
Lemma

20
.

Define the following auxiliary functions:

\displaystyle F(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mu x(x+y)^{\gamma}}{x+(1+\alpha)y}-c(x),

\displaystyle U(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{x\in[0,x^{\max}]}F(x,y),

\displaystyle A(y,z,\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mu(1+\alpha\sigma)}{1+\alpha}z(z+y)^{\gamma-1}-c(z),

so that
\phi^{0}(y,z,\sigma)=U(y)-A(y,z,\sigma)
. We bound the sensitivity of
\phi^{0}
with respect to each variable.

Lipschitz constant in
z

U(y)
does not depend on
z
. We consider the
z
-derivative of
-A(y,z,\sigma)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial-A}{\partial z}(y,z,\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu(1+\alpha\sigma)}{1+\alpha}\left((z+y)^{\gamma-1}+z(\gamma-1)(z+y)^{\gamma-2}\right)+c^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu(1+\alpha\sigma)}{1+\alpha}(z+y)^{\gamma-2}(y+\gamma z)+c^{\prime}(z).

Bounding the absolute value of this derivative yields:

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(-A(y,z,\sigma)\right)\right|\leq\mu(Y_{LB})^{\gamma-2}(Y_{UB}+x^{\max})+M_{c^{\prime}}\leq B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma).

Lipschitz constant in
\sigma

U(y)
does not depend on
\sigma
. The only dependence comes from
A(y,z,\sigma)
. Compute:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left(-A(y,z,\sigma)\right)=-\frac{\partial A}{\partial\sigma}(y,z,\sigma)=-\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}z(z+y)^{\gamma-1}.

Lipschitz constant in
y

Both

and

First, for

:

and

(x^{\star}(y,\sigma)+y)^{\gamma-1}\leq(Y_{LB})^{\gamma-1}
,

\left|\frac{\partial V_{1}}{\partial\sigma}(y,\sigma)\right|\leq\mu x^{\max}(Y_{LB})^{\gamma-1}.

•

For
H(z,y,\sigma)
:

\frac{\partial H}{\partial\sigma}(z,y,\sigma)=K^{\prime}(\sigma)z(z+y)^{\gamma-1}.

Similarly,
z\leq x^{\max}
,
(z+y)^{\gamma-1}\leq(Y_{LB})^{\gamma-1}
,

\left|\frac{\partial H}{\partial\sigma}(z,y,\sigma)\right|\leq\mu x^{\max}(Y_{LB})^{\gamma-1}.

Summing, we get:

2\mu x^{\max}(Y_{LB})^{\gamma-1}\leq B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma).

Conclusion

Combining the three Lipschitz constants, we have:

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{1}(z_{1},y_{1},\sigma_{1})-\phi^{1}(z_{2},y_{2},\sigma_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)\left|z_{1}-z_{2}\right|+B(0,2,0,1,2-\gamma)\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|+B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right|.

This completes the proof of
Lemma

21

∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
\phi_{k}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
be the maximum utility gain player
k
can achieve by unilaterally deviating from the profile
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{1})
when the platform’s revenue sharing parameter is
\sigma
. For any profiles
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta
and
Y_{LB}\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-k}\right\|,\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{-k}\right\|\leq Y_{UB}
, and any
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
such that
\left|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\right|\leq\delta
, we have:

\left|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x},\rho)-\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\right|\leq\delta\left(B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)+B(0,(2+\alpha),0.5,2,3-\gamma)+B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)\right)

Proof of
Lemma

22
.

The deviation gain function
\phi_{k}
quantifies how much a player
k
can benefit by deviating from the strategy profile
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{1})
. To analyze this function, we introduce the notation
z_{k}
for player
k
’s quality and
y_{k}=\left\|\mathbf{z}_{-k}\right\|
for the sum of all other players’ qualities. This allows us to express
\phi_{k}
as a function of these scalar arguments:
\phi_{k}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)=\phi_{k}(z_{k},y_{k},\sigma)
.

Step 1: Lipschitz continuity of deviation gain function
\phi_{k}

According to
Lemma

2
, the optimal deviation involves either full sharing or no sharing. Thus, we can express the gain function as:

\phi_{k}(z_{k},y_{k},\sigma)=\max\left(\phi_{k}^{(s^{\prime}=0)}(z_{k},y_{k},\sigma),\phi_{k}^{(s^{\prime}=1)}(z_{k},y_{k},\sigma)\right),

where:

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{(s^{\prime}=1)}(z_{k},y_{k},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left[\max_{x_{k}^{\prime}\in[0,x^{\max}]}U_{k}((x_{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}_{-k}),\mathbf{1};f_{\sigma})\right]-U_{k}((z_{k},\mathbf{z}_{-k}),\mathbf{1};f_{\sigma}),

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{(s^{\prime}=0)}(z_{k},y_{k},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left[\max_{x_{k}^{\prime}\in[0,x^{\max}]}U_{k}((x_{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}_{-k}),(0,\mathbf{1}_{-k});f_{\sigma})\right]-U_{k}((z_{k},\mathbf{z}_{-k}),\mathbf{1};f_{\sigma}).

Recall that the Lipschitz constant of a maximum of two functions is at most the maximum of the Lipschitz constants of the two functions. Therefore, we can bound the Lipschitz constant of
\phi_{k}
by taking the maximum of the Lipschitz constants of
\phi_{k}^{(s^{\prime}=0)}
and
\phi_{k}^{(s^{\prime}=1)}
:

•

For the
z
coordinate:

\max\{B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma),B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)\}=B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma).

•

For the
y
coordinate:

\max\{B(0,2+\alpha,0,2,3-\gamma),B(0,2,0,1,2-\gamma)\}=B(0,2+\alpha,0,2,3-\gamma).

•

For the
\sigma
coordinate:

\max\{B(0,1,0,1,1-\gamma),B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)\}=B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma).

Writing it explicitly, we have:

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{k}(z_{k,1},y_{k,1},\sigma_{1})-\phi_{k}(z_{k,2},y_{k,2},\sigma_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)\left|z_{k,1}-z_{k,2}\right|+B(0,2+\alpha,0,2,3-\gamma)\left|y_{k,1}-y_{k,2}\right|+B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right|.

Step 2: Bounding the difference
\left|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x},\rho)-\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\right|

For profiles
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
with
\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta
, we have
\left|x_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k}\right|\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta
for any component
k
. For the sum of qualities of other players, we have:

\left|y_{k}-y^{\prime}_{k}\right|=\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-k}\right\|-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{-k}\right\|\right|\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-k}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{-k}\right\|\leq\sqrt{n-1}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-k}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{-k}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\delta.

Using the Lipschitz constant derived above and the mean value theorem, we can bound the difference:

\left|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x},\rho)-\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\right|\leq B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)\delta+B(0,2+\alpha,0,2,3-\gamma)\sqrt{n}\delta+B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)\delta.

Notice we can write the expression
B(0,2+\alpha,0,2,3-\gamma)\sqrt{n}
as
B(0,(2+\alpha),0.5,2,3-\gamma)
, as the third parameter affects the exponent of
n
in the definition of
B
. Thus, we can combine the terms to get:

\left|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x},\rho)-\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\right|\leq\delta\cdot\left(B(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)+B(0,(2+\alpha),0.5,2,3-\gamma)+B(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)\right).

∎

Lemma 23
.

Fix some
\delta>0
. Let two quality profiles
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta
, and two revenue sharing parameters
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
such that
\left|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\right|\leq\delta
. Then, the absolute difference in the platform’s revenue is bounded by:

\left|U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})-U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho^{\prime}})\right|\leq B(0,1+\gamma,0.5,1,1-\gamma)\cdot\delta.

Proof of
Lemma

23
.

We apply the triangle inequality to decompose the difference in utilities into two components:

\displaystyle\left|U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})-U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho^{\prime}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})-U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})\right|

\displaystyle+\left|U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})-U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho^{\prime}})\right|.

Letting
C=\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}
, we can express this as:

\displaystyle\left|U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})-U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho^{\prime}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|C(1-\rho)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}-C(1-\rho)\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|C(1-\rho)\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}-C(1-\rho^{\prime})\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\right|

\displaystyle=C(1-\rho)\cdot\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\right|+C\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\cdot\left|\rho^{\prime}-\rho\right|.

To bound the difference of powers
\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\right|
, we use the Mean Value Theorem. Let
g(v)=v^{\gamma}
for
v>0
, where
\gamma\in[0,1]
. By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists a value
\xi
between
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|
and
\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|
such that:

\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\right|=\left|g(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)-g(\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|)\right|=\left|g^{\prime}(\xi)\right|\cdot\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|\right|.

The derivative is
g^{\prime}(v)=\gamma v^{\gamma-1}
. Since
\xi\in[X_{LB},X_{UB}]
and
\gamma-1\leq 0
for
\gamma\in[0,1]
, the function
v^{\gamma-1}
is non-increasing, so:

\left|g^{\prime}(\xi)\right|=\gamma\xi^{\gamma-1}\leq\gamma(X_{LB})^{\gamma-1}.

Therefore,

\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\right|\leq\gamma(X_{LB})^{\gamma-1}\cdot\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|\right|.

By the reverse triangle inequality and the relation between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
norms in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have:

\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|\right|\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|\leq\sqrt{n}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\cdot\delta.

Combining, the first term is bounded by:

T_{1}=C\left|1-\rho\right|\cdot\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\right|\leq C\cdot\gamma(X_{LB})^{\gamma-1}\cdot\sqrt{n}\cdot\delta.

For the second term, since
\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|\leq X_{UB}
and
\left|\rho^{\prime}-\rho\right|\leq\delta
, we have:

T_{2}=C\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{\gamma}\cdot\left|\rho^{\prime}-\rho\right|\leq C\cdot(X_{UB})^{\gamma}\cdot\delta.

Combining both bounds and factoring out
\delta
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left|U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})-U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho^{\prime}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}+T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\gamma(X_{LB})^{\gamma-1}\cdot\sqrt{n}\cdot\delta+C\cdot(X_{UB})^{\gamma}\cdot\delta

\displaystyle=C\cdot\left(\gamma(X_{LB})^{\gamma-1}\cdot\sqrt{n}+(X_{UB})^{\gamma}\right)\cdot\delta.

Since
\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}\leq\mu
for any
\alpha>0
, we have:

\displaystyle\left|U_{P}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})-U_{P}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho^{\prime}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mu\cdot\left(\gamma(X_{LB})^{\gamma-1}\sqrt{n}+(X_{UB})^{\gamma}\right)\cdot\delta

\displaystyle\leq\left(B(0,\gamma,0.5,0,1-\gamma)+B(0,1,0,\gamma,0)\right)\cdot\delta

\displaystyle\leq B(0,1+\gamma,0.5,1,1-\gamma)\cdot\delta.

This completes the proof of
Lemma

23
.
∎

Lemma 24
.

Let
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in[0,1]
such that
\left|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\right|\leq\delta
, and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta

G(\rho)

G(\rho^{\prime})

\eta
-FSE in

-FSE in
G(\rho^{\prime})
.

Proof of
Lemma

24
.

Define
\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\}
to be the ESE profile among the two. Let
\phi_{k}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
denote the maximum utility gain player
k
can achieve by unilaterally deviating from the profile
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{1})
when the platform’s revenue sharing parameter is
\sigma

Lemma

22

\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}

From
Lemma

18

\left\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{-k}\right\|\geq\sum_{i}x_{i}^{\min}-\max_{k}x_{k}^{\min}\eqqcolon Y_{LB}

k
and any
\rho\in[0,1]

Lemma

13
that
\left\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{-k}\right\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\max}-\min_{k}x_{k}^{\max}\eqqcolon Y_{UB}
.

We now derive lower bounds for
\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-k}\right\|,\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{-k}\right\|

k

\left|\left\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{-k}\right\|-\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-k}\right\|\right|\leq\left\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{-k}-\mathbf{x}_{-k}\right\|\leq\sqrt{n-1}\left\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{-k}-\mathbf{x}_{-k}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\delta,

and the same holds for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}

Now, we analyze the deviation gain of any player from the profile

\mathbf{x}
is
\eta
-FSE in

\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\leq\left|\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x},\rho)-\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\right|+\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x},\rho).

Now, applying
Lemma

22
with

\tilde{Y}_{LB},\tilde{Y}_{UB}

\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\leq\delta\cdot\left(\tilde{B}(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)+\tilde{B}(0,(2+\alpha),0.5,2,3-\gamma)+\tilde{B}(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)\right)+\eta.

We now want to simplify the bracketed term. First, notice that

\displaystyle\tilde{B}(1,1,0,1,2-\gamma)+\tilde{B}(0,(2+\alpha),0.5,2,3-\gamma)+\tilde{B}(0,2,0,1,1-\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{B}(1,1,0,2,3-\gamma)+\tilde{B}(0,(2+\alpha),0.5,2,3-\gamma)+\tilde{B}(0,2,0,3,3-\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{B}(1,(1+2+\alpha+2),0.5,3,3-\gamma)

\displaystyle=\tilde{B}(1,(5+\alpha),0.5,3,3-\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq B(1,(5+\alpha)\cdot 2^{3}\cdot 2^{3-\gamma},0.5,3,3-\gamma)

\displaystyle=B(1,(5+\alpha)\cdot 2^{6-\gamma},0.5,3,3-\gamma),

where the second to last inequality follows from the definition of
B
and the fact that
\tilde{Y}_{LB}=0.5Y_{LB}
and
\tilde{Y}_{UB}=2Y_{UB}
. After this simplification, we get that:

\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\leq B(1,(5+\alpha)\cdot 2^{6-\gamma},0.5,3,3-\gamma)\cdot\delta+\eta,

which means that
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is a
(\eta+B(1,(5+\alpha)\cdot 2^{6-\gamma},0.5,3,3-\gamma)\delta)
-FSE in
G(\rho^{\prime})
, completing the proof of
Lemma

24
.

C.4
Strong Monotonicity and Lipschitz Continuity of ESE

Lemma 27
.

Assume that all the cost functions
c_{i}(x_{i})
are
\beta
-strongly convex. Then, for any
\rho\in[0,1]
, the enforced sharing game w.r.t.
\rho
is strongly monotone.

Proof of
Lemma

27
.

We extend the monotonicity result from
Lemma

3
to show that with
\beta
-strongly convex cost functions, the game becomes strongly monotone.

Recall from the proof of
Lemma

3
that:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(v_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-v_{i}(\mathbf{x}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-A_{i}(\mathbf{x}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i}).

We already established that the first term is non-positive:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-A_{i}(\mathbf{x}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})\leq 0.

The key difference now is that with
\beta
-strongly convex cost functions, we have a stronger bound on the second term. Specifically, for
\beta
-strongly convex functions:

(c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})\geq\beta(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})^{2}.

Summing over all players:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})\geq\beta\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})^{2}=\beta\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(v_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-v_{i}(\mathbf{x}))(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})\leq 0-\beta\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}=-\beta\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

This establishes that the game is strongly monotone with parameter
\beta
.
∎

Lemma 28
.

Assume that all the cost functions
c_{i}(x_{i})
are
\beta
-strongly convex. Then,
\mathcal{X}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the
\ell_{2}
-norm on the interval
[0,1]
, with Lipschitz constant at most
B(0,1/\beta,0.5,1,2-\gamma)
.

Proof of
Lemma

28
.

The proof proceeds in three main steps: (1) characterizing the equilibrium conditions, (2) establishing differentiability through the Implicit Function Theorem, and (3) bounding the derivative to obtain the Lipschitz constant.

Step 1: Characterizing the equilibrium

Denote
K(\rho)=\frac{\mu(1+\alpha\rho)}{1+\alpha}
. The utility for creator
i
under enforced full sharing (
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
) is given by
Lemma

12
:

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})=K(\rho)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-1}x_{i}-c_{i}(x_{i}),

Let us define
g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=K(\rho)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\left[\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right]-c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})
. Recall that the FOC for creator
i
in the ESE is
g_{i}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)=0
.

Step 2: Continuous differentiability of
\mathcal{X}(\cdot)

To establish Lipschitz continuity, we first show that
\mathcal{X}(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable using the Implicit Function Theorem (IFT).

We first verify that
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\rho)
is continuously differentiable in both
\mathbf{x}
and
\rho
:

•

K(\rho)=\frac{\mu(1+\alpha\rho)}{1+\alpha}
is clearly
C^{1}
in
\rho
.

•

By assumption, each
c_{i}(x_{i})
is at least twice differentiable, so
c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})
is
C^{1}
.

•

From
Lemma

15
and the arguments in the proof of
Theorem

5
, we know that
\mathcal{X}_{i}(\rho)>0
for all
i\in[n]
. Consequently, terms like
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}
are well-defined and
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{X}(\rho)
and are continuously differentiable as a composition of
C^{1}
functions.

Next, we need to verify that the Jacobian matrix
J_{x}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}
is invertible at
\mathcal{X}(\rho)
.
Lemma

27
establishes that when the cost functions
c_{i}(x_{i})
are
\beta
-strongly convex, the enforced sharing game is strongly monotone. Specifically, for any distinct
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in the strategy space, the gradient map
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\rho)
satisfies:

(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho)-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\rho))^{T}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})\leq-\beta\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

This condition of strong monotonicity for a continuously differentiable map
\mathbf{g}
implies that the symmetric part of its Jacobian,
J_{S}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\frac{1}{2}(J_{x}(\mathbf{x},\rho)+J_{x}(\mathbf{x},\rho)^{T})
, is negative definite. This, in turn, implies that the Jacobian
J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)
itself is invertible. To see why, assume for contradiction that
J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)
is not invertible. Then, there exists a non-zero vector
\mathbf{u}
such that
J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
. This would lead to:

\mathbf{u}^{T}J_{S}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u}^{T}J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}^{T}J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)^{T}\mathbf{u}).

Since
J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
, we get that
\mathbf{u}^{T}J_{S}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\mathbf{u}=0
, which contradicts the fact that
J_{S}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)
is negative definite. Therefore,
J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)
must be invertible.

By the Implicit Function Theorem,
\mathcal{X}(\cdot)
is a
C^{1}
function of
\rho
in
[0,1]
, with derivative:

\frac{d\mathcal{X}(\cdot)}{d\rho}=-\left(J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\rho}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho).

Step 3: Bounding the derivative norm

We now bound
\left\|\frac{d\mathbf{x}^{\star}}{d\rho}\right\|_{2}
to establish the Lipschitz constant. By the operator norm inequality:

\left\|\frac{d\mathcal{X}(\cdot)}{d\rho}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|J_{x}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\rho}\right\|_{2}.

First, we bound
\left\|J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)^{-1}\right\|_{2}
. Using standard results from variational inequalities (e.g.,
[
38
]
), we know that strong monotonicity of
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\rho)
implies that the symmetric part of the Jacobian
J_{S}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)
is negative definite with the following property: For any non-zero vector
\mathbf{v}
,

\mathbf{v}^{T}J_{S}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\mathbf{v}\leq-\beta\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

This means that the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric part
J_{S}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)
satisfies
\lambda_{\max}(J_{S})\leq-\beta
. Since
\beta>0
, we have
\lambda_{\max}(J_{S})<0
.
For a real invertible matrix
A
, if its symmetric part
S_{A}=(A+A^{T})/2
is negative definite and its largest eigenvalue is
\lambda_{\max}(S_{A})=-m<0
(where
m>0
), it is a known result that the norm of the inverse is bounded by
\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{m}
.
Applying this result to
J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)
, with
m=\beta
, we obtain:

\left\|J_{x}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)^{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}.

Next, we bound
\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\rho}\right\|_{2}
:
For each
i\in[n]
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial\rho}(\mathbf{x},\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial K(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\left(\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\left(\gamma x_{i}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{-i}\right\|\right).

Lemma

18
ensures that
X_{LB}\leq\left\|\mathcal{X}(\rho)\right\|
. Additionally,
Lemma

13
suggests that
\left\|\mathcal{X}(\rho)\right\|\leq X_{UB}
. Using the fact that
\gamma-2<0
, we have
\left\|\mathcal{X}(\rho)\right\|^{\gamma-2}\leq(X_{LB})^{\gamma-2}
. Also,
\gamma\mathcal{X}_{i}(\rho)+\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i}(\rho)\right\|\leq X_{UB}
and
\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}\leq\mu
. Therefore:

\left|\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial\rho}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\right|\leq\mu(X_{LB})^{\gamma-2}X_{UB}.

Using the Euclidean norm:

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\rho}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\rho)\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial\rho}\right)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{n}\cdot\mu(X_{LB})^{\gamma-2}X_{UB}.

Combining our bounds:

\left\|\frac{d\mathcal{X}(\cdot)}{d\rho}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\mu\sqrt{n}\cdot(X_{LB})^{\gamma-2}X_{UB}=\frac{\mu\sqrt{n}}{\beta}\frac{X_{UB}}{(X_{LB})^{2-\gamma}}.

Since
\mathcal{X}(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable on the compact interval
[0,1]
, its derivative is bounded. By the Mean Value Theorem, for any
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in[0,1]
:

\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\star}(\rho)-\mathbf{x}^{\star}(\rho^{\prime})\right\|_{2}\leq L\cdot\left|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\right|,

where
L=\sup_{\rho\in[0,1]}\left\|\frac{d\mathbf{x}^{\star}}{d\rho}(\rho)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\mu\sqrt{n}}{\beta}\frac{X_{UB}}{(X_{LB})^{2-\gamma}}\leq B(0,1/\beta,0.5,1,2-\gamma)
. The proof of
Lemma

28
is now complete.

\rho\in[0,1]
and let
\mu^{\prime}=\mu(1+\alpha\rho)
. For any sufficiently large
n
and
\mu
,
\left\|\mathcal{X}(\rho)\right\|
satisfies:

\frac{C_{\theta}}{2\alpha+2}<\frac{(\left\|\mathcal{X}(\rho)\right\|)^{\theta-\gamma}}{\mu^{\prime}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{a}^{-1}\right\|_{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}}<C_{\theta},

where
C_{\theta}
is a constant depending only on
\theta
,
\mathbf{a}^{-1}=(a_{1}^{-1},\dots,a_{n}^{-1})
, and
\left\|\mathbf{a}^{-1}\right\|_{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}^{-1})^{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}\right)^{\theta-1}
.

We now proceed to derive the approximation. We rely on the key relationships between
\left\|\mathcal{X}(\rho)\right\|
and its lower bound
X_{LB}(\rho)
established in the theorem above, which guarantees that for any
\rho\in[0,1]
:

X_{LB}(\rho)\leq\left\|\mathcal{X}(\rho)\right\|\leq R\cdot X_{LB}(\rho),

where
R=(2\alpha+2)^{1/(\theta-\gamma)}
and
X_{LB}(\rho)=\left(\frac{C_{\theta}\mu(1+\alpha\rho)\left\|\mathbf{a}^{-1}\right\|_{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}}{2\alpha+2}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta-\gamma}}
. Since the platform’s revenue is
U_{p}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})=\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}(1-\rho)(\mathcal{X}(\rho))^{\gamma}
, these quality bounds imply corresponding revenue bounds:

V_{LB}(\rho)\leq U_{p}(\mathcal{X}(\rho),\mathbf{1};f_{\rho})\leq R^{\gamma}\cdot V_{LB}(\rho),

where
V_{LB}(\rho)=\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}(1-\rho)(X_{LB}(\rho))^{\gamma}
.

Next, we derive an approximation by finding the
\rho
value that maximizes
V_{LB}(\rho)
. Substituting and simplifying:

\displaystyle V_{LB}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha}(1-\rho)\left[\left(\frac{C_{\theta}\mu(1+\alpha\rho)\left\|\mathbf{a}^{-1}\right\|_{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}}{2\alpha+2}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta-\gamma}}\right]^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=K\cdot(1-\rho)(1+\alpha\rho)^{p},

where
K
is a positive constant and
p=\frac{\gamma}{\theta-\gamma}
. Taking the derivative of
g(\rho)=(1-\rho)(1+\alpha\rho)^{p}
:

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-(1+\alpha\rho)^{p}+(1-\rho)p\alpha(1+\alpha\rho)^{p-1}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha\rho)^{p-1}[(p\alpha-1)-\alpha\rho(1+p)].

Setting
g^{\prime}(\rho)=0
and solving for
\rho
:

\rho_{crit}=\frac{p\alpha-1}{\alpha(1+p)}=\frac{\alpha\gamma+\gamma-\theta}{\alpha\theta}.

Under our assumption that
(\alpha+1)\gamma>\theta
, we have
\rho_{crit}\in(0,1)
, and the second derivative confirms this critical point maximizes
V_{LB}(\rho)
. We set
\rho_{approx}=\rho_{crit}
.

For sufficiently large
n
and
\mu
,
Proposition

6
guarantees that
\rho_{approx}
induces an FSE. This is crucial because it ensures our approximation is feasible within the constraints of the platform’s optimization problem.

Now we establish the approximation bound. Denote by
\rho^{\star}
an allocation parameter that achieves
OPT
(or a very close approximation). We leverage that
\rho_{approx}
maximizes
V_{LB}(\rho)
, so
V_{LB}(\rho_{approx})\geq V_{LB}(\rho^{\star})
. From our revenue bounds above,
V_{LB}(\rho^{\star})>\text{OPT}/R^{\gamma}
. Combining these:

V_{LB}(\rho_{approx})\geq V_{LB}(\rho^{\star})\geq\frac{\text{OPT}}{R^{\gamma}}.

Since
U_{p}(\mathcal{X}(\rho_{approx}),\mathbf{1};f_{\rho_{approx}})>V_{LB}(\rho_{approx})
, we have:

U_{p}(\mathcal{X}(\rho_{approx}),\mathbf{1};f_{\rho_{approx}})\geq\frac{\text{OPT}}{R^{\gamma}}=\frac{\text{OPT}}{(2\alpha+2)^{\frac{\gamma}{\theta-\gamma}}}.

Therefore,
U_{p}(\mathcal{X}(\rho_{approx}),\mathbf{1};f_{\rho_{approx}})\geq(2\alpha+2)^{-\frac{\gamma}{\theta-\gamma}}\cdot\text{OPT}
, establishing that our fixed-parameter approach achieves the claimed approximation ratio.
∎

D.1
Proofs for Multiple Topics

In this extension, each creator
i
chooses a quality vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,K})
and a sharing vector
\mathbf{s}_{i}=(s_{i,1},\ldots,s_{i,K})
. The utility function is separable in revenue across topics but coupled in costs:

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{i,k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};f_{\rho})-c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}).

Proposition 29

(Extension of
Lemma

2
to Multiple Topics)
.

Fix a quality profile
\mathbf{x}
and sharing decisions for all topics other than
k
, and for all creators other than
i
. The optimal sharing level
s_{i,k}
for creator
i
in topic
k
is given by the threshold policy:

s_{i,k}=\begin{cases}1&\rho>\tau_{i,k}\\
[0,1]&\rho=\tau_{i,k}\\
0&\rho<\tau_{i,k}\\
\end{cases},\quad\text{where }\tau_{i,k}=\frac{x_{i,k}}{x_{i,k}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j,k}+\alpha\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j,k}s_{j,k}}.

Proof.

The utility function
U_{i}
separates into topic-specific revenue terms. Crucially, the cost function
c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
depends only on quality
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and is independent of the sharing decision
\mathbf{s}_{i}
. Furthermore, the revenue
R_{i,m}
for any topic
m\neq k
is independent of
s_{i,k}
. Therefore, maximizing
U_{i}
with respect to
s_{i,k}
is equivalent to maximizing the revenue term
R_{i,k}
alone. The partial derivative is:

\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial s_{i,k}}=\frac{\partial R_{i,k}}{\partial s_{i,k}}.

The functional form of
R_{i,k}
is identical to the single-topic revenue function analyzed in
Lemma

2
, substituting the scalar quality
x_{i}
with the topic-specific quality
x_{i,k}
. Following the derivation in the proof of
Lemma

2
(specifically the derivative of Eq.
8
), we obtain the same condition:
\frac{\partial R_{i,k}}{\partial s_{i,k}}>0
if and only if
\rho>\tau_{i,k}
.
∎

Proposition 30

(Extension of
Lemma

3
to Multiple Topics)
.

For any fixed
\rho
, the subgame
G(\rho)
under enforced sharing (
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
) admits a unique ESE.

Proof.

We show that the game satisfies the Diagonally Strictly Concave (DSC) condition (or equivalently, that the gradient operator of the game is strictly monotone). The combined utility gradient vector for all players can be written as
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{R}
represents the vector of revenues and
\mathbf{C}
represents the vector of costs.

First, consider the revenue component. Since total revenue is the sum of independent revenues from topics
k=1\dots K
, the Jacobian of the revenue vector is block-diagonal, where each block corresponds to a single topic
k
. As established in the proof of
Lemma

3
, the single-topic revenue game is monotone (i.e., satisfies the negative definite property for the symmetric part of the Jacobian). The direct sum of monotone mappings is itself monotone. Thus,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}R_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}R_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{i})\leq 0
.

Second, consider the cost component. We assume
c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
is strictly convex. By definition of strict convexity, its gradient
\nabla c_{i}
is strictly monotone:

(\nabla c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime})-\nabla c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{i})>0\quad\text{for }\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{x}_{i}.

Even if
c_{i}
is not separable across topics (i.e., cross-derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}c_{i}}{\partial x_{i,k}\partial x_{i,m}}\neq 0
), strict convexity ensures the Hessian is positive definite.

Combining these, the game gradient
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
is strictly monotone. Along with the convexity and compactness of the strategy space (bounded by rational quality limits), this guarantees a unique equilibrium via Rosen’s theorem
[
35
]
.
∎

Theorem 31

(Extension of
Theorem

5
to Multiple Topics)
.

There exist topic-specific bounds
x_{i,k}^{\max}
such that if
\rho>\max_{i,k}\frac{x_{i,k}^{\max}}{x_{i,k}^{\max}+(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathcal{X}_{-i,k}(\rho)\right\|}
, the unique ESE is also the unique FSE.

Proof Sketch.

We generalize the concept of "rational quality bound" from
Lemma

13
. Since
c_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
is strictly convex and superlinear, the set of strategies yielding non-negative utility is bounded. Let
S_{i}
be the set of rational strategies for player
i
. We define
x_{i,k}^{\max}=\sup\{x_{i,k}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}\in S_{i}\}
. Because
\tau_{i,k}
is increasing in
x_{i,k}
(analogous to the single-topic case), the condition on
\rho
ensures that for every rational quality profile
\mathbf{x}
,
\rho>\tau_{i,k}(x_{i,k})
. This makes
s_{i,k}=1
a strictly dominant strategy for every topic
k
and every creator
i
. Since full sharing is dominant, the unique equilibrium must be the unique ESE of the enforced sharing game.
∎

Proposition 32

Proposition 33

(Robustness of

)
.

The introduction of fixed prior data

Proof.

In the enforced sharing game (

U_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mu(1+\alpha\rho)}{1+\alpha}(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+x_{0})^{\gamma-1}x_{i}-c_{i}(x_{i}).

The second derivative of revenue with respect to

D.4
Proofs for GenAI-Dependent Traffic

In this extension, the user traffic
T(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
depends on the total quality of both human and AI content:
T(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\mu\left(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\right)^{\gamma}
.

Proposition 34

(Threshold Derivation for GenAI-Dependent Traffic)
.

Fix an arbitrary creator
i
with a content
x_{i}>0
, and a profile
(\mathbf{x}_{-i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})
. Further, define
\tau_{i}
as

\tau_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\left(\frac{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\left(x_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}x_{j}s_{j}\right)}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}s_{j}}\right)^{1-\gamma}-1\right].

The optimal sharing level
s
of creator
i
w.r.t.
(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})
, i.e.,
s\in\argmax_{s\in[0,1]}U_{i}((x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}),(s,\mathbf{s}_{-i});f_{\rho})
is given by

s^{\star}=\begin{cases}1&\rho>\tau_{i}\\
\textnormal{any number from }[0,1]&\rho=\tau_{i}\\
0&\rho<\tau_{i}\\
\end{cases}.

(12)

Proof.

Let
D(s_{i})=\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},(s_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i}))
. Note that
D(s_{i})=A+\alpha x_{i}s_{i}
, where
A=\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}s_{j}
is independent of
s_{i}
.

The utility of creator
i
as a function of the continuous variable
s_{i}\in[0,1]
is:

U_{i}(s_{i})=\mu(D(s_{i}))^{\gamma}\cdot\frac{x_{i}(1+\alpha\rho s_{i})}{D(s_{i})}-c_{i}(x_{i})=\mu x_{i}(D(s_{i}))^{\gamma-1}(1+\alpha\rho s_{i})-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Differentiating
U_{i}
with respect to
s_{i}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial s_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mu x_{i}\left[(\gamma-1)(D(s_{i}))^{\gamma-2}(\alpha x_{i})(1+\alpha\rho s_{i})+(D(s_{i}))^{\gamma-1}(\alpha\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\mu x_{i}\alpha(D(s_{i}))^{\gamma-2}\left[(\gamma-1)x_{i}(1+\alpha\rho s_{i})+\rho D(s_{i})\right].

Substituting
D(s_{i})=A+\alpha x_{i}s_{i}
into the bracketed term:

\displaystyle[\dots]

\displaystyle=(\gamma-1)x_{i}+(\gamma-1)\alpha\rho x_{i}s_{i}+\rho A+\rho\alpha x_{i}s_{i}

\displaystyle=\rho A-(1-\gamma)x_{i}+\gamma\alpha\rho x_{i}s_{i}.

Since
\gamma,\alpha,\rho,x_{i}>0
, the term
\gamma\alpha\rho x_{i}s_{i}
is strictly increasing in
s_{i}
. This implies that
\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial s_{i}}
is strictly increasing with respect to
s_{i}
. Consequently, the maximum of
U_{i}(s_{i})
on
[0,1]
must occur at one of the boundaries,
s_{i}=0
or
s_{i}=1
.

Creator
i
chooses
s_{i}=1
if and only if
U_{i}(1)>U_{i}(0)
. Using the utility expression:

\displaystyle\mu x_{i}(D(1))^{\gamma-1}(1+\alpha\rho)

\displaystyle>\mu x_{i}(D(0))^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle 1+\alpha\rho

\displaystyle>\left(\frac{D(0)}{D(1)}\right)^{\gamma-1}=\left(\frac{D(1)}{D(0)}\right)^{1-\gamma}.

Solving for
\rho
, we obtain the threshold condition from the proposition statement:

\rho>\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\left(\frac{D(1)}{D(0)}\right)^{1-\gamma}-1\right]=\tau_{i}.

∎

Proposition 35

(ESE Uniqueness under GenAI-Dependent Traffic)
.

For any
\rho\in[0,1]
, the subgame
G(\rho)
under enforced sharing (
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}
) admits a unique ESE.

Proof.

Under enforced sharing, the total quality is
D(\mathbf{1})=\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+\alpha\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|=(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|
.
The traffic function simplifies to:

T(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})=\mu\left((1+\alpha)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\right)^{\gamma}=\mu(1+\alpha)^{\gamma}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}.

The utility function for creator
i
becomes:

\displaystyle U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle=T(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})\frac{x_{i}(1+\alpha\rho)}{D(\mathbf{1})}-c_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\mu(1+\alpha)^{\gamma}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma}\cdot\frac{x_{i}(1+\alpha\rho)}{(1+\alpha)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}-c_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\mu(1+\alpha)^{\gamma-1}(1+\alpha\rho)\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-1}x_{i}-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Let
\tilde{\mu}=\mu(1+\alpha)^{\gamma-1}(1+\alpha)=\mu(1+\alpha)^{\gamma}
. The utility takes the form
\frac{\tilde{\mu}(1+\alpha\rho)}{1+\alpha}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\gamma-1}x_{i}-c_{i}(x_{i})

This expression has the same form as the utility function in the baseline model (

), differing only by the constant scalar
\tilde{\mu}
instead of

. Since the scaling of the revenue term by a positive constant does not affect the strict concavity of the utility or the monotonicity of the game gradient, the existence and uniqueness of the ESE are preserved.
∎

D.5
Proofs for GenAI Quality Beyond Linearity

In this extension, the GenAI quality is given by
Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\alpha\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}s_{i}\right)^{\beta}
with
\beta\in(0,1]
.

Proposition 36

(Sharing Incentive under Universal Withholding)
.

Fix a creator
i
and a quality profile
\mathbf{x}
. Assume no other creator shares content (
\mathbf{s}_{-i}=\mathbf{0}
). Then, the utility of creator
i
is strictly increasing in
s_{i}
on the interval
(0,1]
if
\rho>\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}
, and strictly decreasing if
\rho<\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}
. Consequently, creator
i
has an incentive to deviate to full sharing (
s_{i}=1
) if and only if
\rho>\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}
.

Proof.

Let
X=\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|
be the total human quality. Since
\mathbf{s}_{-i}=\mathbf{0}
, the total shared content is
S(s_{i})=x_{i}s_{i}
.
If
s_{i}=0
, the utility is simply the direct traffic revenue share:
U_{i}(0)=\mu X^{\gamma}\frac{x_{i}}{X}-c_{i}(x_{i})
.

For any
s_{i}>0
, creator
i
is the sole contributor to the GenAI. Thus, their share of the AI revenue is
100\%
of the allocated pool (i.e.,
\frac{x_{i}s_{i}}{S(s_{i})}=1
). The GenAI quality is
Q(s_{i})=\alpha(x_{i}s_{i})^{\beta}
.
The utility function for
s_{i}>0
becomes:

U_{i}(s_{i})=\mu X^{\gamma}\left[\frac{x_{i}}{X+Q(s_{i})}+\frac{Q(s_{i})}{X+Q(s_{i})}\cdot\rho\cdot 1\right]-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Let
Y(s_{i})=Q(s_{i})=\alpha(x_{i}s_{i})^{\beta}
. Since
\beta>0
,
Y(s_{i})
is strictly increasing in
s_{i}
. We can analyze the utility with respect to
Y
:

U_{i}(Y)=\mu X^{\gamma}\left[\frac{x_{i}+\rho Y}{X+Y}\right]-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Differentiating with respect to
Y
:

\displaystyle\frac{dU_{i}}{dY}

\displaystyle=\mu X^{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\rho(X+Y)-(x_{i}+\rho Y)}{(X+Y)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mu X^{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\rho X+\rho Y-x_{i}-\rho Y}{(X+Y)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mu X^{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\rho X-x_{i}}{(X+Y)^{2}}.

The sign of the derivative is determined entirely by the numerator
\rho X-x_{i}
, which is constant with respect to
s_{i}
(and
Y
).

•

If
\rho X-x_{i}>0\iff\rho>\frac{x_{i}}{X}
, then
\frac{dU_{i}}{dY}>0
. Since
Y
increases with
s_{i}
,
U_{i}(s_{i})
is strictly increasing on
(0,1]
. The optimal strategy is
s_{i}=1
.

•

If
\rho X-x_{i}<0\iff\rho<\frac{x_{i}}{X}
, then
U_{i}(s_{i})
is strictly decreasing on
(0,1]
. The optimal strategy approaches
s_{i}\to 0
(or
s_{i}=0
exactly, by continuity from the right).

Thus, the condition
\rho>\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}
is necessary and sufficient for creator
i
to profitably deviate from a state of non-sharing to full sharing.
∎

Appendix E
Other Allocation Rules

In this section, we analyze two alternative functional forms to the proportional allocation rule examined in the main text. We demonstrate that the stability of FSEs depends not only on the total amount of revenue shared but also on how that revenue is distributed among creators. Specifically, we show that some intuitively appealing rules may fail to induce FSE even when the platform redistributes its entire GenAI-driven revenue (
\rho=1
).

We consider two alternative allocation rules parameterized by
\rho\in[0,1]
:

1.

Winner-Takes-All (WTA):
The entire
\rho
-fraction of AI revenue is allocated to the creators who provide the maximal effective contribution. Let
W(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\argmax_{j\in[n]}\{x_{j}s_{j}\}
. Then:

f_{i,\rho}^{WTA}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\begin{cases}\frac{\rho}{|W(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})|}&\text{if }i\in W(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\text{ and }\max_{j}x_{j}s_{j}>0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

2.

Binary Threshold with Equal Shares (BTES):
The
\rho
-fraction is distributed equally among all creators who fully opt-in (
s_{i}=1
), regardless of their quality contribution. Let
S_{1}(\mathbf{s})=\{j:s_{j}=1\}
. Then:

f_{i,\rho}^{BTES}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\begin{cases}\frac{\rho}{|S_{1}(\mathbf{s})|}&\text{if }i\in S_{1}(\mathbf{s})\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

While
Proposition

4
and
Theorem

5
establish that the proportional allocation rule admits FSE for sufficiently high
\rho
, the following proposition shows that such stability guarantees do not extend to these alternative mechanisms.

Proposition 37

(Instability under Alternative Rules)
.

Under either WTA or BTES allocation rules with
\alpha>0
and
n\geq 2
, there exist instances where no FSE exists, even with full revenue redistribution (
\rho=1
).

Proof.

We construct specific instances demonstrating the non-existence of FSE under each rule for
\rho=1
.

Part 1: WTA non-existence.

Consider any candidate full-sharing profile
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})
. If all qualities are identical, any small perturbation breaks the tie and guarantees the deviator a large utility increase, so the original profile cannot have been an FSE. If creators have different quality levels, let
i
be a creator such that
x_{i}<\max_{k}x_{k}
. Creator
i
is not in the winner set
W(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1})
and thus receives zero AI revenue. Their utility is purely derived from direct traffic:

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f_{1}^{WTA})=\mu(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)^{\gamma}\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}}-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Consider a deviation to
s_{i}^{\prime}=0
. The total shared content decreases, reducing the AI quality to
Q_{\text{AI}}^{\prime}=\alpha(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|-x_{i})<Q_{\text{AI}}
. Creator
i
still receives zero AI revenue, but benefits from reduced competition:

U_{i}((\mathbf{x},(0,\mathbf{1}_{-i}));f_{1}^{WTA})=\mu(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)^{\gamma}\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}^{\prime}}-c_{i}(x_{i}).

Since
Q_{\text{AI}}^{\prime}<Q_{\text{AI}}
, the traffic share
\frac{x_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|+Q_{\text{AI}}^{\prime}}
strictly increases, making the deviation profitable. If all qualities are identical, any creator can slightly increase their quality
x_{i}
to become the unique winner, capturing the entire AI revenue pot, which also destabilizes the profile. Thus, no FSE exists.

Part 2: BTES non-existence.

We provide a counterexample with
n=2
,
\alpha=\gamma=1
, and
\mu=10
. Let the cost functions be
c_{1}(x)=0.1x^{2}
and
c_{2}(x)=0.4x^{2}
.

First, we determine the unique ESE
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
under full sharing (
s_{1}=s_{2}=1
). In this state, both creators share, so
|S_{1}|=2
. The AI quality is
Q_{\text{AI}}=\alpha(x_{1}+x_{2})=\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|
. The utility for creator
i
is:

U_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{1};f^{BTES})=10\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\frac{x_{i}}{2\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}+\frac{1}{2}\left(10\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\frac{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}{2\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}\right)-c_{i}(x_{i})=5x_{i}+2.5\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|-c_{i}(x_{i}).

The first-order conditions
\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=0
yield:

5+2.5-0.2x_{1}=0\implies x_{1}^{\star}=37.5,

5+2.5-0.8x_{2}=0\implies x_{2}^{\star}=9.375.

The resulting total quality is
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\star}=46.875
. Creator 1’s utility in this potential FSE is:

U_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{1})=5(37.5)+2.5(46.875)-0.1(37.5)^{2}=187.5+117.1875-140.625=164.0625.

Now consider a deviation by Creator 1 to
s_{1}^{\prime}=0
(keeping
x_{1}=37.5
). They forfeit their share of AI revenue, but the AI quality drops to
Q_{\text{AI}}^{\prime}=\alpha x_{2}^{\star}=9.375
. Creator 1’s new utility is derived solely from direct traffic:

U_{1}^{dev}=10(46.875)\frac{37.5}{46.875+9.375}-0.1(37.5)^{2}=468.75\left(\frac{37.5}{56.25}\right)-140.625=171.875.

Thus, the deviation is profitable. Thus, the unique ESE is not stable under strategic sharing, proving the non-existence of FSE.
∎

The instability of these rules stems from a misalignment between contribution and reward. Under WTA, non-winning creators have no incentive to share, as doing so only strengthens the GenAI competitor without providing any compensation. Under BTES, high-quality creators (like Creator 1 in the proof) are undercompensated relative to their large contribution to the AI’s quality; they prefer to withhold data to weaken the AI rather than accept a diluted share of revenue equal to that of lower-quality contributors. The proportional allocation rule resolves this by linking rewards directly to the magnitude of contribution, ensuring that stronger creators are adequately incentivized.

Appendix F
Modeling Assumptions and Limitations

This section outlines key assumptions in our modeling framework and discusses their implications and limitations.

User Traffic Modeling

Our baseline model assumes that platform user traffic is driven entirely by human-created content, with AI-generated content affecting allocation but not the traffic volume. This encompasses the scenario of a fixed number of users as a special case when
\gamma=0
. In
Section

6
, we relax this assumption by considering a traffic model that incorporates both human and AI-generated content, and show that our main results carry over with appropriately adjusted constants. More general traffic models–such as those capturing nonlinear interactions between human and AI quality–remain unexplored.

Generative AI Quality Modeling

We model the quality of GenAI content as a linear function of the total shared creator data:
Q_{\text{AI}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\alpha\cdot\sum_{i}x_{i}s_{i}
. In practice, the relationship between training data quantity and model quality typically exhibits diminishing returns, consistent with established scaling laws in machine learning
[
30
]
. To address this,
Section

6
considers a generalized quality function
Q_{\text{AI}}=\alpha(\sum_{i}x_{i}s_{i})^{\beta}
with
\beta\in[0,1]
. While certain theoretical guarantees valid under the linear model do not hold in this generalized setting, our simulations suggest that the key qualitative insights persist. Other factors that may affect GenAI quality in practice–such as data diversity, representativeness, and complementarity among creators’ contributions–are not captured by our model and remain directions for future work.

Scalar Representation of Content Competitiveness

Each creator’s quality choice is represented as a single scalar
x_{i}\in[0,\infty)
, embodying the overall “competitiveness” of their content. This quantity encompasses various dimensions, including quality, quantity, trend alignment, expressiveness, domain specificity, and novelty. This modeling choice is a common simplification in the literature
[
52
,
50
]
, enabling tractable analysis while preserving the essence of competitive dynamics. However, real-world scenarios may involve more intricate representations, such as continuous vectors of features
[
25
]
, requiring entirely different analytical tools.

Continuous Data Sharing Decisions

Our model enables creators to select their sharing level continuously, represented by
s_{i}\in[0,1]
. In practice, platforms usually provide binary options. Nevertheless, all our results remain valid under binary sharing choices, as
Lemma

2
ensures that only binary sharing decisions are rational.

Static Game Formulation

Our model captures a one-shot interaction between the platform and creators. In practice, creator-platform relationships are ongoing, and the quality of GenAI systems depends on data accumulated over time. Such dynamic settings introduce richer strategic considerations, including the timing of sharing decisions and the platform’s ability to update its allocation rule. Modeling these interactions as repeated or sequential games constitutes a natural extension of our framework.
