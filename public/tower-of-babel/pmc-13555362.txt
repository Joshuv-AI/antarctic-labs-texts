The DNA/RNA Helicase SETX Maintains R‐Loop Homeostasis to Promote Chemoresistance in Ovarian Cancer
Authors: Zi‐Wei Hu, Tu‐Nan Xu, Zi‐Ying Wang, Xin Zheng, Duan‐Yong Feng, Xiao‐Min Wang, Er‐Suo Jin, Ming Ren, Xiao‐Min Yang, Xian‐Chao Zhang, Xuan Che, Wei‐Wei Pan, Zha‐Jun Zhan, Shu‐Qun Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOvarian cancer is a lethal malignancy that frequently develops resistance to platinum‐based chemotherapies. Elucidating the genes that regulate DNA damage repair could lead to more effective treatments for ovarian cancer. Senataxin (SETX), an RNA–DNA helicase, maintains R‐loop homeostasis to ensure replication fork progression, and its deficiency is linked to neurodegenerative diseases. However, its clinical relevance to solid tumors is not understood. We aimed to investigate the clinical significance and functional role of SETX in ovarian cancer progression and chemotherapy resistance. SETX expression was analyzed in ovarian cancer tissues, followed by CRISPR–Cas9‐mediated knockout, mechanistic analyses, and RNASEH1 rescue experiments to investigate its role in ovarian cancer progression and chemoresistance. SETX was significantly overexpressed in ovarian tumors, and its high expression was correlated with cisplatin resistance and poor patient survival. SETX depletion significantly impaired tumor cell proliferation and invasion and sensitized cells to cisplatin‐induced apoptosis. Mechanistically, SETX deficiency triggered pathological R‐loop accumulation, which was accompanied by replication stress, DNA double‐strand breaks, and activation of DNA damage response. RNA‐seq analysis also revealed splicing alterations and U2AF1 interaction. Critically, R‐loop degradation induced by RNASEH1 overexpression rescued the DNA damage phenotype and growth defects induced by SETX loss. These findings reveal a dual oncogenic role for SETX. Maintaining R‐loop homeostasis supports malignant progression while conferring chemotherapy resistance. Therefore, SETX represents a promising therapeutic target, as its inhibition simultaneously suppresses tumor growth and overcomes chemotherapy resistance in ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer, particularly high‐grade serous carcinoma (HGSOC), is a leading cause of gynecological cancer mortality worldwide [1]. HGSOC is typically diagnosed at an advanced stage and has a poor prognosis owing to its intrinsic malignancy and high propensity for progression [2]. Although first‐line chemotherapy with platinum‐based drugs is initially effective, acquired resistance eventually occurs in most patients, leading to treatment failure and disease recurrence [3].

A hallmark of ovarian cancer is profound genomic instability, especially in HGSOC, which is characterized by chromosomal instability (CIN) and high intra‐ and inter‐tumor heterogeneity [4, 5, 6]. Although genomic instability drives malignant evolution, it also presents a double‐edged sword; specifically, exceeding cellular tolerance thresholds can induce catastrophic DNA damage, potentially resulting in vulnerabilities that lead to cell dysfunction or even death [7, 8]. Therefore, cancer cells manage this instability through sophisticated regulatory mechanisms.

Genomic instability is closely linked to dysregulated R‐loop homeostasis [9]. An R‐loop is a three‐stranded RNA–DNA hybrid that represents a conserved feature of transcriptionally active chromatin [10]. Although the dynamics of R‐loops balance is crucial for genomic stability, pathological R‐loop accumulation suppresses tumor cell proliferation by inducing lethal replication stress and DNA damage. R‐loops accumulate at transcription–replication conflict sites, physically obstructing replication fork progression and causing fork collapse and subsequent DNA double‐strand breaks [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Consequently, unresolved R‐loops represent a major endogenous tumor‐suppressive mechanism that triggers DNA damage response (DDR) activation and replication catastrophe, which can halt malignant growth.

Senataxin (SETX) is a conserved RNA–DNA helicase that functions as a key guardian of favorable genome stability [16]. Its core function is to promptly clear R‐loops and ensure the normal progression of replication forks, thereby avoiding excessive replication stress and DNA damage [17, 18]. SETX mutations are well‐characterized in neurodegenerative disorders, where they cause disease through toxic R‐loop accumulation and neuroinflammation [19, 20, 21]. However, its expression patterns, functions, and clinical significance in solid tumors, especially ovarian cancer, remain unknown. Emerging evidence suggests that many tumors accumulate R‐loops through oncogene activation, transcriptional hyperactivity, or hormone signaling (e.g., estrogen in breast cancer) [22, 23, 24], but whether cancer cells upregulate SETX to counteract R‐loop‐mediated tumor suppression and whether this correlates with clinical outcomes remain unexplored.

Our clinical analyses revealed that SETX expression is significantly upregulated in ovarian cancer, suggesting that SETX is selectively upregulated in tumor cells to counteract the survival crisis induced by R‐loop accumulation. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that SETX deficiency triggers pathological R‐loop accumulation, which in turn leads to DNA double‐strand breaks, activation of the DNA damage response, and consequently the suppression of tumor growth. This R‐loop‐driven DNA damage underlies the dual role of SETX, which is essential for maintaining tumor cell survival and mediating chemoresistance. Furthermore, our data further implicate SETX in the regulation of alternative RNA splicing, suggesting a broader role in cellular homeostasis. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism through which ovarian cancer progression and chemosensitivity are coordinated, positioning SETX as a promising therapeutic target.

Material and Methods

Cell Culture and Stable Cell Line Generation With CRISPR/Cas9

Human ovarian cancer cell lines ES‐2, OVCAR‐3, and A2780 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. ES‐2 and A2780 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco). The OVCAR‐3 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco). Both media were supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) and 10% FBS (Gibco). All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All the cell lines were tested for Mycoplasma contamination.

SETX‐KO cells were generated using CRISPR/Cas9. Following transfection with SETX‐targeting CRISPR plasmids, cells were exposed to puromycin (2–10 μg/mL) for 72 h and seeded as single cells into 96‐well plates. Individual clones were screened by western blot analysis. The guide sequences used in this experiment are provided in the Table S1.

For cisplatin‐resistant cell lines, A2780, OVCAR‐3, and ES‐2 cells were continuously exposed to gradient concentrations of cisplatin (starting from 0.01 to 10 μg/mL over 6 months). The resulting cell lines exhibited markedly higher resistance to cisplatin compared with parental cells, as assessed by CCK‐8 cell viability assay following cisplatin treatment.

Clinical Ovarian Tissue Samples

The clinical specimens and paraffin‐embedded human tissue samples from patients with ovarian cancer and para‐cancerous tissues were obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of Jiaxing University. Tissue microarrays containing tumors and adjacent tissues from 102 patients with ovarian cancer (item number: OVC1022) were purchased from Guilin Panspectrum Biotechnology Co. Ltd.

Immunohistochemistry(IHC)

Tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight, dehydrated using an ethanol gradient (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm in thickness) were then blocked with goat serum (1 h, room temperature) and incubated with primary antibody at 4°C overnight. After PBST washes, HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied (30 min, room temperature). Signals were developed with DAB substrate, and sections were counterstained, dehydrated, cleared, and observed under a microscope for imaging.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton for 15 min, and blocked with 3% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C in a wet box, followed by Alexa Fluor‐conjugated secondary antibodies added in the dark for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min and sealed with an anti‐fluorescence quenching sealing agent. Images were captured using an Olympus FV3000 confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Cell Proliferation, Colony‐Formation Assays and Cell Viability Assays

For proliferation assays, 1 × 105 wild‐type or SETX‐KO cells were seeded into 6‐well plates, and cell numbers were counted at 24, 48, and 72 h to generate growth curves.

For soft agar colony formation, 2 × 105 cells were suspended in 0.35% agar and layered over 0.5% agar base in 6‐well plates. After 3 weeks, clones were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma‐Aldrich) dissolved in PBS and analyzed using ImageJ software.

For cell viability, 3000 cells/well were seeded in 96‐well plates. After drug treatment, CCK‐8 reagent (MedChemExpress) diluted 1:10 was added and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme immunoassay instrument. Quadruplicate wells were used for each condition.

Wound Healing and Transwell Assays

For wound healing, SETX‐KO ovarian cancer and control cells were seeded into 24‐well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well, and grown to confluence. A scratch was introduced with a pipette tip, and wound closure was photographed at 0, 11, and 22 h.

For transwell assays, cells suspended in serum‐free medium were seeded into upper chambers pre‐coated with Matrigel; lower chambers contained medium with 10% FBS. After 24 h, invaded cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted.

SA‐β‐Gal Assay

The cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA. An SA‐β‐Gal mixture was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime). Next, 500 μL of the SA‐β‐gal mixture was added to each well and incubated at 37°C. Cells were observed under a microscope and photographed. Cells stained blue were considered senescent cells, and the cell senescence rate was calculated (cell senescence rate = number of senescent cells in the field/total number of cells in the field).

Western Blotting

Protein lysates prepared in western and IP lysis buffers (Beyotime), supplemented with protease inhibitor, were quantified by BCA assay. Equal amounts of proteins (20 μg) were separated via SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), and blocked with 5% skim milk. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology). An ECL immunoblotting chemiluminescence kit (Merck) was further used. All primary and secondary antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Cell Cycle Detection

Cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin containing EDTA, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol precooled at −20°C overnight. Fixed cells were incubated with 100 mg/mL RNase A (BD Biosciences) for 30 min at 37°C and stained with PI staining solution (BD Biosciences) at 50 mg/mL in the dark for 30 min. Samples were subjected to cell cycle analysis using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Annexin V/PI Staining for Cell Apoptosis Detection

WT and SETX‐KO cells treated with cisplatin for 24 h were harvested, washed with ice‐cold PBS, and resuspended in 100 μL of 1× binding buffer. PI and Annexin‐V staining solution (MedChemExpress) were added (15 min, room temperature, dark). 1× binding buffer was added to terminate the staining, and fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry within 1 h.

RNA Isolation, qRT‐PCR and RNA‐Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent, followed by reverse transcription employing PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio). qPCR analysis was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara Bio) on a QuantStudio system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated by the ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate.

For RNA‐seq, libraries were prepared following Illumina protocols and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Reads were aligned to hg19 using HISAT2, and differential expression was analyzed with DESeq2 (|log2FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05).

Plasmid Transfection

Plasmids were transfected into cells using the Lipo3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein expression was measured at 48 h after transfection. For drug‐treatment experiments, the drug‐containing medium was replaced after 24 h. All indices were measured within 48–72 h after transfection.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation Assay (Co‐IP) and Mass Spectrometry

For Co‐IP, cells were lysed in western and IP lysis buffer (Beyotime) with protease inhibitor. Protein lysates were incubated with antibody‐conjugated protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress) overnight at 4°C. The magnetic beads were washed with the binding/elution buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer and analyzed by western blotting. Normal IgG served as negative control.

For mass spectrometry, HA‐SETX‐overexpressing cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with anti‐HA beads, separated by SDS‐PAGE, stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, and destained in deionized water to eliminate background interference. The samples were then subjected to Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry analysis.

Mouse Xenograft Models

Experimental mice were purchased from Jiangsu Jicui Pharmaceutical Kang Biotechnology Co. Ltd. 6–8‐week‐old BALB/C‐Nude female mice were maintained under Specified Pathogen Free (SPF) conditions. SETX‐KO or control OVCAR‐3 cells (5 × 106) suspended in PBS with Matrigel (1:1) were injected subcutaneously into both flanks. Tumor volume was measured every 2 days. Mice were euthanized when the tumor diameter reached 15 mm. BrdU was injected intraperitoneally 30 min before sacrifice. Tumor tissues were either fixed in 4% PFA for IHC or snap‐frozen for protein and RNA analyses.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Data were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance and Student's t‐tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. The log‐rank test was applied to the Kaplan–Meier analysis, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Levels of significance are represented by asterisks as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

SETX Is Overexpressed in Ovarian Cancer and Associated With Cisplatin Resistance and Poor Prognosis

To investigate the clinical relevance of SETX in ovarian cancer, we first assessed its expression pattern. IHC analysis of an ovarian cancer tissue microarray revealed significant SETX upregulation in tumor tissues compared with normal or benign ovarian tissues (Figure 1A,B). This finding was further confirmed by western blotting analysis, which demonstrated consistently higher SETX expression in ovarian cancer biopsies than in normal ovarian tissues (Figure 1C). Analysis of the GEO dataset confirmed that SETX mRNA expression was significantly elevated in ovarian cancer samples (Figure 1D). Notably, SETX expression correlated with clinical features of disease progression. In patients treated with cisplatin‐based chemotherapy, IHC staining revealed higher SETX protein levels in cisplatin‐resistant tumors than in sensitive tumors (Figure 1E). Furthermore, analysis of the TCGA ovarian cancer cohort revealed that SETX expression positively correlated with multiple core DNA damage response (DDR) genes (Figure 1F), suggesting a potential link between SETX and the DNA damage response network in ovarian cancer cells. Consistently, high SETX mRNA expression was significantly correlated with poor overall survival in patients with ovarian cancer (Figure 1G), suggesting that SETX overexpression may not only be a hallmark of ovarian cancer but also a marker of aggressive disease and an unfavorable prognosis.

SETX Is Required for the Proliferative Fitness of Ovarian Cancer Cells

Based on the clinical association between SETX overexpression and poor prognosis, we hypothesized that SETX functions as an oncogenic factor in ovarian cancer. To test this hypothesis, we generated stable SETX‐KO OVCAR‐3 and ES‐2 cell lines by Using CRISPR–Cas9 (Figure 2A). Knockout was validated by Sanger sequencing and complementation assays (Figures S1 and S3). SETX loss significantly suppressed the proliferation, as demonstrated by both cell counting assays (Figure 2B) and soft agar colony‐formation assays (Figure 2C), accompanied by reduced proliferation marker PCNA expression (Figure 2D). Flow cytometric analysis revealed G2/M phase arrest upon SETX knockout (Figure 2E), with concomitant p53 upregulation (Figure 2F). Additionally, SETX‐KO cells exhibited increased SA‐β‐gal activity which indicating enhanced senescence, suggesting that SETX loss results in the restoration of this tumor‐suppressive barrier (Figure 2G). We further found SETX knockout impaired cell migration and invasion, as shown by wound healing (Figure 2H) and matrigel invasion assays (Figure 2I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that SETX is essential for maintaining multiple malignant phenotypes of ovarian cancer cells, thereby positioning it as a key molecular driver of ovarian cancer pathogenesis.

SETX Deficiency Sensitizes Ovarian Cancer Cells to Cisplatin‐Induced Apoptosis

Next, we investigated the relationship between SETX and the cisplatin response. Cisplatin treatment of OVCAR‐3 and ES‐2 cells induced time‐ and dose‐dependent upregulation of SETX protein levels (Figure 3A,B). Consistently, three independently established cisplatin‐resistant cell lines (A2780, OVCAR‐3, and ES‐2) showed higher SETX expression than their parental sensitive counterparts (Figure 3C). This correlation strongly suggests that SETX is involved in the development of cisplatin resistance. Functionally, SETX‐KO cells exhibited markedly reduced viability upon cisplatin treatment compared with WT cells (Figure 3D), accompanied by increased cleaved caspase‐3 levels (Figure 3E). Flow cytometric analysis further showed that the combination of SETX knockout and cisplatin treatment led to a dramatic synergistic increase in the apoptotic cell ratio (Figure 3F). These results establish SETX as a critical survival factor induced by cisplatin stress that contributes to chemoresistance.

SETX Loss Triggers R‐Loop Accumulation and a Dysregulated DNA Damage Repair Response

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying SETX function, we performed transcriptome sequencing (RNA‐seq) based on WT and SETX‐KO cells. Given the clinical correlation between SETX expression and DDR genes (Figure 1F), we hypothesized that SETX loss might perturb the DNA repair response. GO and KEGG pathway analyses revealed significant enrichment of terms related to DNA double‐strand breaks (Figure 4A). Consistent with this, SETX knockout increased phosphorylation of the checkpoint kinase CHK1 (p‐CHK1), a hallmark of replication stress and elevated γ‐H2AX, a canonical marker of DNA double‐strand breaks (Figure 4B). This enhanced DNA damage was further confirmed by increased γ‐H2AX foci formation in SETX‐KO cells (Figure 4C). Focusing on the DNA repair genes highlighted in the RNA‐seq data, a volcano plot clearly highlighted a cluster of significantly altered DNA repair factors following SETX loss (Figure 4D). qRT‐PCR confirmed that the mRNA levels of key DNA repair‐associated genes, including POLK, SPIDR, REV3L, and TOP2B, were substantially elevated in SETX‐KO cells, whereas ERCC5, PRKDC, and RECQL mRNA expression were decreased (Figure 4E). Given the established function of SETX as an RNA–DNA helicase responsible for resolving R‐loops, we examined R‐loop levels and detected a significant increase in SETX‐KO cells using S9.6 antibody (Figure 4F). We further examined the mRNA levels of other R‐loop resolving factors and found no significant compensatory upregulation in SETX‐KO cells (Figures S2 and S3). Collectively, these results demonstrate that SETX deletion causes the accumulation of R‐loops and the concomitant activation of a global DNA damage response, thereby contributing to DNA damage signaling in ovarian cancer cells.

In addition to transcriptional changes, RNA‐seq analysis revealed widespread alternative splicing alterations, including in DDR pathway genes, along with U2AF1 interaction and elevated p‐SF3B1 in SETX‐KO cells (Figure S4). These data suggest a potential role for SETX in RNA splicing, although its functional contribution remains to be determined.

SETX Depletion Suppresses Tumor Growth and Amplifies DNA Damage In Vivo

To validate the tumor‐promoting function of SETX in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenograft models using control and SETX‐KO OVCAR‐3 and ES‐2 cells. SETX deficiency significantly impaired tumor growth (Figure 5A,B). Western blot analysis of tumor lysates revealed a decreased expression of the mitotic marker phospho‐histone H3 (p‐H3) in SETX‐KO tumors, increased PARP cleavage and elevated γ‐H2AX levels (Figure 5C). IHC staining confirmed increased cleaved caspase‐3 and γ‐H2AX, and reduced BrdU staining, in SETX‐KO tumors (Figure 5D). qRT‐PCR analysis revealed that DDR gene expression changes in tumor tissues were consistent with the in vitro findings (Figure 5E). These experiments conclusively demonstrate that SETX knockout suppresses tumor growth in vivo and recapitulates the DNA damage phenotype observed in cultured cells, thereby validating that SETX drives ovarian cancer malignancy by limiting DNA damage accumulation.

R‐Loop Accumulation Drives DNA Damage and Functional Defect Upon SETX Loss

To test whether the DNA damage observed upon SETX loss is principally driven by R‐loop accumulation, we performed a rescue experiment by overexpressing RNaseH1 in SETX‐KO cells, and RNaseH1 overexpression nearly abolished the S9.6 signal in SETX‐KO cells (Figure 6A). RNASEH1‐mediated R‐loop clearance completely reversed the elevated γ‐H2AX levels in SETX‐KO cells, as shown by both immunofluorescence and western blotting (Figure 6B,C). Furthermore, the impaired growth rate of SETX‐KO cells was significantly restored following RNaseH1 co‐expression (Figure 6D). Moreover, depletion of the R‐loops abolished the cisplatin‐sensitizing effect of SETX deletion (Figure 6E).

Collectively, these data provide clear evidence that R‐loop accumulation is a major driver of DNA damage and the subsequent proliferation defects caused by SETX depletion in ovarian cancer cells and contributes to the cisplatin‐sensitizing effect of SETX depletion. Thus, SETX maintains R‐loop homeostasis to promote tumor cell survival and chemotherapy resistance (Figure 7).

Discussion

HGSOC remains a major therapeutic challenge owing to its typical late diagnosis, high rate of metastasis, and susceptibility to chemotherapy resistance [25]. Here, we identify the RNA–DNA helicase SETX as a critical factor coordinating both malignant progression and cisplatin resistance. Clinically, SETX is significantly overexpressed in ovarian cancer tissues, particularly in those associated with cisplatin resistance and poor prognosis, suggesting that its upregulation is a survival strategy enabling cancer cells to adapt to transcriptional replication stress. Functionally, the absence of SETX disrupts R‐loop stability and is associated with replication stress and DNA damage, which not only directly inhibit tumor growth and invasion but also render cells exquisitely sensitive to cisplatin. These findings collectively establish that SETX is a key link coordinating the malignant progression of ovarian cancer and chemotherapy sensitivity.

Genomic instability is a hallmark of HGSOC and a known driver of tumorigenesis and progression [26, 27]. Pathological R‐loop accumulation is a key upstream event that can induce endogenous replication stress and DNA damage, thereby fueling genomic instability [15, 28]. HGSOC cells have evolved sophisticated molecular networks to actively manage R‐loop homeostasis. For example, the AKT1‐mediated recruitment of DHX9 [29] and RNA editing mediated by ADAR1 [30, 31] mitigate R‐loop‐induced replication stress. However, these mechanisms primarily involve indirect signaling‐dependent regulation. In contrast, our study identified SETX as a key cellular factor promoting R‐loop resolution, which ovarian cancer cells specifically hijack to sustain malignant proliferation. We demonstrate that the upregulation of SETX expression maintains R‐loop levels within a “pro‐tumorigenic window” that permits mutations and evolution but avoids triggering catastrophic growth arrest. This represents a fundamental repurposing of SETX in ovarian cancer, where it suppresses the tumor‐suppressive potential of R‐loops, converting a replicative threat into a survival advantage. Consequently, SETX knockout disrupts this balance, causing the R‐loop accumulation and DNA damage to exceed the repair capacity, thereby transforming replication stress–associated DNA damage from an evolutionary driver into lethal vulnerability.

Aberrant R‐loop accumulation is also closely related to tumor chemosensitivity [32, 33], and we further propose a “state synergy” model to explain SETX‐mediated cisplatin resistance. In SETX‐proficient cells, endogenous R‐loop stress is managed to preserve the repair capacity required to tolerate cisplatin‐induced exogenous damage. In contrast, SETX‐deficient cells have fragile genomes owing to endogenous damage. Cisplatin treatment creates a synthetic lethal interaction that overwhelms the cells, leading to apoptotic cell death and profound chemosensitization. This model aligns with recent findings that R‐loop‐associated double‐strand breaks in SETX‐deficient cells are repaired via break‐induced replication [34]. Our work provides the upstream explanation for this phenomenon by identifying the source of damage and the resulting cellular state, while anchoring these mechanisms within the specific clinical context of ovarian cancer.

Dysregulated alternative splicing is known to drive tumorigenesis and alter treatment sensitivity [35, 36, 37]. Beyond DNA metabolism, our RNA‐seq analysis links SETX loss to widespread alternative splicing alterations and elevated levels of spliceosome activation marker p‐SF3B1. We further identified U2AF1 as a SETX‐interacting protein, and mutations in this protein are known to lead to widespread splicing alterations [38]. Given that R‐loops can recruit U2AF1 to regulate splicing [39], and that U2AF1‐dependent splicing influences R‐loop dynamics in some tumors [40, 41], SETX‐mediated R‐loop clearance may indirectly impact splicing fidelity. However, how these changes contribute to SETX loss‐driven DNA damage and cisplatin resistance remains unconfirmed. Nevertheless, these data expand the functional scope of SETX beyond DNA metabolism and provide a basis for future investigations into the interplay between R‐loop homeostasis and RNA processing in ovarian cancer.

In summary, our study establishes SETX as a central driver of tumor progression and a compelling therapeutic target in ovarian cancer. Its expression level may serve as a prognostic and predictive biomarker. SETX itself is a promising therapeutic target, and targeting the SETX–R‐loop axis is expected to achieve dual therapeutic benefits by simultaneously inhibiting tumor growth and overcoming chemoresistance. Future studies should focus on validating this target in advanced models, developing SETX‐directed therapeutics, and exploring rational combinations of SETX inhibition with DDR inhibitors.
