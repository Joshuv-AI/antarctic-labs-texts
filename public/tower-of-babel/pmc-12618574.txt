Exploring mitochondrial ribosomal protein S12 as a novel target for non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Zihao Chen, Yu Jin, Shiyou Wei, Rongqiang Wei, Xinyu Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the expression and functional significance of mitochondrial ribosomal protein S12 (MRPS12) within NSCLC pathogenesis. The Cancer Genome Atlas dataset reveals a significant association between MRPS12 overexpression and unfavorable clinical outcomes in NSCLC patients. Single-cell RNA sequencing data unequivocally confirm MRPS12 overexpression across diverse NSCLC cancer cell populations. MRPS12 is also upregulated in locally-treated NSCLC tissues. shRNA-mediated gene silencing or CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knockout (KO) of MRPS12 impaired mitochondrial function, resulting in a reduction in oxygen consumption rate, marked declines of complex I activity and ATP levels, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, and an increase in reactive oxygen species production in NSCLC cells. Moreover, MRPS12 silencing or knockout attenuated cell viability, proliferation, and migratory capacity, while inducing apoptotic cell death in various NSCLC cell types. Conversely, ectopic MRPS12 overexpression conferred a pro-tumorigenic phenotype to NSCLC cells. In vivo studies demonstrated that MRPS12 silencing markedly inhibited the growth of subcutaneous xenografts derived from primary NSCLC cells in nude mice. The MRPS12-silenced NSCLC xenografts exhibited decreased ATP levels, elevated oxidative injury, diminished proliferation, inhibited Akt-mTOR activation and an increase in apoptosis. These findings support the important role of MRPS12 in supporting mitochondrial function and driving the malignant progression of NSCLC.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Cell biology, Diseases, Oncology

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most prevalent subtype of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases1–4. The incidence of NSCLC remains high globally, particularly in regions with high tobacco use and environmental pollution1,2. Current treatment modalities for NSCLC include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, with the choice of treatment largely depending on the stage of the disease at diagnosis3,4. Despite advancements in therapeutic options, the prognosis for NSCLC patients remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 25%, primarily due to late-stage diagnosis and the development of resistance to therapies3–5. Ongoing research is focused on improving early detection methods, understanding the molecular underpinnings of NSCLC, and developing novel therapeutics to enhance patient outcomes2,6–8.

Molecularly-targeted therapies have significantly advanced the treatment of NSCLC by targeting specific genetic alterations9,10. While inhibitors (or antibodies) for EGFR11, ALK12, ROS113, and BRAF V600E have shown remarkable efficacy7,9, ongoing research is focused on expanding the repertoire of targetable oncogenes and pathways, such as KRAS14, MET exon 14 skipping15, and RET fusions16 as well as non-coding RNAs17 and Akt-mTOR cascade18,19. Despite these advancements, challenges such as resistance mechanisms, intratumoral heterogeneity, and limited efficacy in patients without actionable mutations persist, highlighting the need for continued efforts to optimize and expand targeted therapies for NSCLC9,10.

Mitochondrial bioenergetics are essential for the survival and rapid proliferation of NSCLC cells20–24. Enhanced mitochondrial activity, a hallmark of NSCLC, facilitates increased ATP production and the generation of metabolic intermediates necessary for biosynthesis23. This metabolic hyperfunction supports tumor growth and survival in the often-challenging microenvironment of the tumor20–24. Furthermore, mitochondrial hyperactivity can confer resistance to apoptotic stimuli, promoting the persistence of cancer cells despite therapeutic interventions23. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying mitochondrial hyperfunction in NSCLC could identify novel therapeutic targets to disrupt these metabolic processes and improve patient outcomes20–24. Recent studies have shown that several key mitochondrial proteins are highly expressed in NSCLC and play a role in the progression of NSCLC20–24.

Our discovery for a novel mitochondrial target in NSCLC began with a comprehensive differential expression analysis of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs) in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, revealing several candidates consistently upregulated in malignant epithelial cells of NSCLC. To narrow our focus, we prioritized candidates whose elevated expression correlated significantly with poor patient survival. A subsequent literature review was then performed to ensure the novelty of our target by excluding previously studied MRPs, and we identified mitochondrial ribosomal protein S12 (MRPS12) as a promising target. It is an important component of mitochondrial protein synthesis25–27. As a constituent of the small 28S subunit of the mitochondrial ribosome, MRPS12 plays a role in translating mitochondrial-encoded proteins required for oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and ATP synthesis25. Encoded by a gene on chromosome 19q13.2, MRPS12 is synthesized in the cytosol and subsequently imported into the mitochondrial matrix, where it interacts with ribosomal RNA and other ribosomal proteins, ensuring accurate translation25–27. This function is important for the proper assembly and function of the electron transport chain25–28. In light of the established role of mitochondrial hyperfunction in NSCLC and the function of MRPS12 in mitochondrial protein synthesis, we sought to elucidate the expression, functional significance, and underlying molecular mechanisms of MRPS12 in NSCLC, aiming to provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of this disease.

Results

Increased MRPS12 expression is associated with worse clinical outcomes and higher disease-stage in NSCLC

To investigate the expression of MRPS12 in lung cancer, we analyzed TCGA datasets. Our results revealed a significant upregulation of MRPS12 transcripts in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) plus lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) tissues (“Tumor”) when compared to normal lung tissues (“Normal”) (Fig. 1A). This trend was observed consistently across LUAD or LUSC subtypes (Fig. 1B). Additionally, paired tissue analyses confirmed the elevated expression of MRPS12 in LUAD (Fig. 1C) or LUSC tissues (Fig. 1D) relative to their matched normal counterparts. Our analysis further elucidate a positive correlation between MRPS12 expression and tumor stage. The advanced-stage LUAD plus LUSC tumors, characterized by higher T-, N-, and stage- classifications, exhibited elevated MRPS12 transcripts (Fig. 1E–G). MRPS12 overexpression was particularly pronounced in tumors with advanced T stages (T2-T4) (Fig. 1E), N stages (N1-N3) (Fig. 1F), and overall stages II-III (Fig. 1G). However, no significant differential MRPS12 expression was observed between M1 and M0 patients (Fig. 1H).

The bioinformatics studies further revealed disparities in MRPS12 expression based on gender, with male LUAD plus LUSC patients exhibiting elevated MRPS12 levels (Fig. 1I). A positive association between smoking status and MRPS12 overexpression was observed in LUAD plus LUSC patients (Fig. 1J). Furthermore, the number of MRPS12 transcripts was significantly higher in deceased LUAD plus LUSC patients compared to surviving lung cancer individuals (Fig. 1K).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a significant correlation between MRPS12 overexpression and poor overall survival in LUAD plus LUSC patients (Fig. 1L). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that MRPS12 possesses a high capacity to distinguish between cancerous and normal tissues. The area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.912 for the combined LUAD and LUSC patient cohort (Fig. 1M), 0.868 specifically for LUAD patients (Fig. 1N), and 0.986 for LUSC patients (Fig. 1O). These results underscore the potential of MRPS12 as a biomarker for effective discrimination between malignant and non-malignant tissues. To investigate the association between MRPS12 expression and major oncogenic drivers in LUAD, we performed an analysis using TCGA data. As shown in Fig. 1P, we compared MRPS12 expression levels between samples harboring common mutations and their wild-type counterparts. Our results showed that MRPS12 expression was significantly elevated in samples with mutations in KRAS (Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog) and EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) compared to the wild-type groups (Fig. 1P). A particularly strong upregulation was observed in the KEAP1 (kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1) mutant subset (Fig. 1P). In contrast, no significant difference in MRPS12 expression was found between samples with ALK (anaplastic lymphoma kinase) or STK11 (serine/threonine kinase 11) mutations and their corresponding wild-type groups (Fig. 1P).

Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals significant overexpression of MRPS12 in cancer cells within NSCLC

An integrated analysis of NSCLC (LUAD and LUSC) single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA) data revealed a distinctive expression pattern of MRPS12 across various cell types. The cell type annotations, as provided by the original authors29, are visually represented in a dimensionality reduction diagram (Fig. 2A), delineating distinct cell populations. Additionally, the sources of the integrated data are summarized in Fig. 2B. To further elucidate the expression profile of MRPS12, a dot plot was generated (Fig. 2C), highlighting its differential expression across different cell types. The expression density of MRPS12 was also visualized (Fig. 2D), providing a detailed overview of its distribution within the lung cancer cellular landscape. These results demonstrated a preferential expression of MRPS12 in LUAD and LUSC cancer cells (Fig. 2D). To delve deeper into the role of MRPS12 in NSCLC cancer cell heterogeneity, cancer cell clusters were extracted and subjected to subclustering analysis (Fig. 2E-G). These analyses identified a significantly elevated expression of MRPS12 within the proliferating cancer cell subcluster (Fig. 2G) and LAMC2 (laminin subunit gamma 2) cancer subcluster (Fig. 2G), suggesting a potential association between MRPS12 and NSCLC proliferation.

We next performed a correlation analysis of MRPS12 expression within cancer cell cluster. The top 100 positively correlated genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses (Fig. 2H). Both analyses consistently highlighted significant enrichment in biological processes related to cellular respiration, mitochondrial ATP synthesis, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 2I, J). These findings suggest that MRPS12 may play a critical role in modulating mitochondrial respiration and energy metabolism within cancer cell subpopulation in NSCLC.

Based on the LUAD scRNA GEO data from GSE131907, we conducted a comprehensive analysis to investigate MRPS12 expression across diverse cellular populations in LUAD. The cell type annotations were obtained from the original publication30. In the LUAD primary tumor data, MRPS12 exhibited significant upregulation in epithelial cell populations, particularly within early-stage cancer tissues (Fig. 2K, L). The LUAD scRNA data (GSE131907) were then subjected to malignant epithelial cell isolation and subsequent subpopulation analysis based on molecular alterations, yielding seven distinct subset clusters (Fig. S2A). These clusters were functionally annotated based on their highly expressed genes and enriched Gene Ontology terms: Cluster 0 (Inflammatory, CCL20+), Cluster 1 (Translational, RPS10+), Cluster 2 (Keratinization, SPRR3+), Cluster 3 (Lipid Metabolism, CD36+), Cluster 4 (Proliferative, MKI67+), Cluster 5 (Ciliary, DNAI2+), and Cluster 6 (Angiogenic, COL4A2+) (Fig. S2B). A notable observation was the differential distribution pattern of MRPS12 across these molecular subpopulations, which varied significantly among normal tissue, early-stage, and advanced-stage LUAD, with minimal expression in normal tissue (Fig. S2C). In early-stage LUAD, MRPS12 expression was predominantly localized to the proliferative, inflammatory, and translational cell populations (Fig. S2C). However, in advanced-stage LUAD, the MRPS12 expression profile underwent a shift, exhibiting primary enrichment within the proliferative, lipid metabolism, ciliary, and angiogenic epithelial cell subpopulations (Fig. S2C). This stage-dependent differential enrichment of MRPS12 across different epithelial subpopulations based on molecular alteration strongly suggests a dynamic and adaptive re-orchestration of its cellular distribution.

In the LUAD brain metastasis (“mBrain”) tissues, MRPS12 demonstrated elevated expression in cancer epithelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 2M, N). The analysis of LUAD lymph node data revealed MRPS12 upregulation in lymph node metastatic cancer epithelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 2O, P). Also in the LUAD pleural effusion samples, MRPS12 expression was predominantly observed in the cancer cell populations (Fig. 2Q, R). Thus, MRPS12 is consistently found to be highly expressed in cancer epithelial cell populations across all types of LUAD samples analyzed.

Elevated MRPS12 expression in locally-treated NSCLC tissues and multiple NSCLC cell types

To investigate the expression of MRPS12 in locally-treated NSCLC patient tissues, we conducted a comprehensive analysis. NSCLC tumor samples (“T”) and corresponding adjacent normal lung epithelial tissues (“N”), from a total of twenty primary NSCLC patients of our institution were obtained. Analysis of tissue lysates revealed a significant upregulation (over three folds) of MRPS12 mRNA expression in NSCLC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, assessment of MRPS12 protein expression in five representative NSCLC patients (“T1”-“T5”) demonstrated elevated levels in tumor tissues (Fig. 3B), contrasting with the relatively low expression in paired normal tissues (Fig. 3B). A comprehensive analysis of all twenty pairs of tissue specimens corroborated a statistically significant upregulation of MRPS12 protein in NSCLC tissues (Fig. 3C). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a significant upregulation of MRPS12 protein expression in tumor tissues compared to paired normal samples from two representative patients (Fig. 3D). Normal tissues (panels ‘N’) showed low MRPS12 expression, while tumor tissues (panels ‘T’) exhibited robust cytoplasmic staining, indicating a marked increase in MRPS12 immunoreactivity (Fig. 3D). MRPS12 expression was subsequently tested across various NSCLC cell types, including primary human NSCLC cells (pNSCLC-1/2/3, derived three different patients)20,31,32 and A549 immortalized cell line. The qPCR assay results demonstrated a marked increase in MRPS12 mRNA levels in both primary and immortalized NSCLC cells, when compared to primary human lung epithelial cells (pEpi1 and pEpi220,31,32) (Fig. 3E). This upregulation was further validated by Western blotting, which showed significantly higher MRPS12 protein levels in NSCLC cells relative to the lower levels in the primary lung epithelial cells (Fig. 3F). Together, these results further underscore the upregulation of MRPS12 in NSCLC.

MRPS12 is important for maintaining mitochondrial function within NSCLC cells

The above correlation analysis of MRPS12 expression in cancer cell cluster of NSCLC indicates its significant involvement in mitochondrial respiration and energy metabolism in NSCLC. To probe the possible effect of MRPS12 silencing on mitochondrial function, we employed a shRNA approach within NSCLC cells. Lentiviral vectors carrying two separate shRNAs targeting MRPS12 (“shMRPS12-1#” and “shMRPS12-2#”, focusing on non-overlapping sequences) were transduced into pNSCLC-1 primary cells (as described previously20,31,32), yielding stable cells after puromycin selection. A substantial decrease in both MRPS12 mRNA and protein levels in these pNSCLC-1 primary cells was detected (Fig. 4A, B), whereas expression of MRPS7, a control gene, stayed unaffected (Fig. 4A, B).

To test whether MRPS12 silencing could affect mitochondrial functions, we first conducted seahorse assays, and the results showed that silencing MRPS12 using the above shRNAs significantly decreased both the basal and maximal oxygen consumption rates (OCR) in primary pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. 4C), indicating an inhibition of mitochondrial respiration. Simultaneously, a significant decline in mitochondrial complex I activity was detected in shMRPS12-expressing pNSCLC-1 cells, with an accompanying reduction in ATP levels (Fig. 4D). MRPS12 knockdown also triggered mitochondrial membrane depolarization in pNSCLC-1 cells, as evidenced by a shift in JC-1 fluorescence from red (aggregated form) to green (monomeric form) (Fig. 4E). A significant increase in both CellROX (red) and DCF-DA (green) fluorescence intensity was observed in shMRPS12-expressing pNSCLC-1 cells, suggesting elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and oxidative injury in the cancer cells (Fig. 4F, G). These findings were substantiated by a significant reduction in the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 4H). Furthermore, MRPS12 silencing resulted in lipid peroxidation in pNSCLC-1 cells, the latter was indicated by the marked increase of TBAR intensity (Fig. 4I).

To evaluate the applicability of MRPS12 knockdown across various NSCLC cell models, lentiviral delivery of shMRPS12-2# was used in primary human NSCLC cells from different patients (pNSCLC-2 and pNSCLC-3, as detailed previously20,31,32) and the immortalized A549 cell line. Stable cell populations were then generated using puromycin-selection culturing medium. Quantitative analysis revealed a consistent and significant reduction of MRPS12 mRNA in all treated NSCLC cells (Fig. 4J), whereas MRPS7 mRNA levels stayed unaffected (Fig. 4K). In both primary (pNSCLC-2, pNSCLC-3) and immortalized (A549) NSCLC cells, shRNA-mediated MRPS12 knockdown led to significant decreases in mitochondrial complex I activity and ATP production (Fig. 4L, M). Additionally, shMRPS12-2# induced mitochondrial membrane depolarization, as evidenced by an accumulation of JC-1 monomer (Fig. 4N). An increase in ROS production was observed, supported by the elevated CellROX red fluorescence in both primary and A549 NSCLC cells (Fig. 4O). These results together show that MRPS12 is important for maintaining mitochondrial function within NSCLC cells.

MRPS12 downregulation effectively attenuates NSCLC cell proliferation and migration

To elucidate the functional consequences of MRPS12 knockdown on NSCLC cell malignancy, we employed the two shRNAs targeting MRPS12 (shMRPS12-1# and shMRPS12-2#, as shown in Fig. 4). Suppression of MRPS12 expression by the applied shRNAs significantly attenuated cell viability in pNSCLC-1 cells, as assessed by the decreased CCK-8 optical density (Fig. 5A). Moreover, MRPS12 knockdown inhibited the colony formation of pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. 5B). Additionally, MRPS12 silencing inhibited cell proliferation, shown by the decreased ratio of nuclei with positive EdU incorporation (Fig. 5C). MRPS12 shRNA also markedly impaired the migratory and invasive capacities of pNSCLC-1 cells, as determined by “Transwell” and “Matrigel invasion” assays, respectively (Fig. 5D, E). The scramble control shRNA (shC), expectably, had no appreciable effect on the cellular behaviors examined (Fig. 5A–E). In the primary human NSCLC cells from two other independent patients (pNSCLC-2 and pNSCLC-3) and immortalized A549 cells, MRPS12 knockdown using shMRPS12-2# (Fig. 4) similarly inhibited proliferation, as evidenced by suppressed EdU incorporation (Fig. 5F), and reduced cell migration (Fig. 5G). These observations indicate that MRPS12 downregulation effectively attenuated NSCLC cell proliferation and migration.

MRPS12 downregulation triggers apoptosis within NSCLC cells

MRPS12 shRNA treatment led to significant reductions in viability, growth, and proliferation of NSCLC cells, we next hypothesized that apoptosis could be induced by MRPS12 silencing. As depicted in Fig. 6A, pNSCLC-1 cells transfected with shMRPS12 (shMRPS12-1# and shMRPS12-2#) exhibited significant upregulation of Caspase-3 activity compared to the control cells. It was accompanied by a marked increase in cytosolic cytochrome c levels (Fig. 6B). Additionally, the cleavages of Caspase-3, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), and Caspase-9 were triggered by MRPS12 shRNAs within pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. 6C). A significant elevation in TUNEL-positive nuclei was also detected within these pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. 6D), confirming the activation of apoptosis. This finding was further substantiated by the enhanced Annexin V staining in flow cytometry assays (Fig. 6E). Trypan blue staining assay results revealed a significant increase in cell death (with positive Trypan blue staining) within these MRPS12-silenced cells (Fig. 6F). Importantly, the Caspase-3 inhibitor z-DEVD-fmk and the pan-Caspase inhibitor z-VAD-fmk significantly ameliorated the cell viability reduction and cell death induced by shMRPS12-2# in pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. 6G, H). To explore the broader impact of MRPS12 knockdown on NSCLC cells, primary pNSCLC-2 and pNSCLC-3 cells, as well as the immortalized A549 cell line, were subjected to shMRPS12-2# treatment (see Figs. 4 and 5). A concomitant increase in Caspase-3 activity (Fig. 6I) and a higher proportion of TUNEL-positive nuclei (Fig. 6J) were observed in these cells. These findings collectively demonstrated that MRPS12 silencing induced apoptotic cell death within NSCLC cells.

To explore the impact of MRPS12 silencing in primary human lung epithelial cells, pEpi1 and pEpi2 cells were targeted with MRPS12 knockdown using the same shMRPS12-2# lentiviral construct, resulting in substantial MRPS12 mRNA downregulation (Fig. 6K). MRPS7 mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 6L). Despite MRPS12 silencing, no significant apoptosis was observed, as determined by nuclear TUNEL staining (Fig. 6M), and cell death remained minimal within the lung epithelial cell population (Fig. 6N). Cell viability (by CCK-8 assay, Fig. 6O), colony formation (Fig. 6P) and cell proliferation (by nuclear EdU incorporation assay, Fig. 6Q) were also not significantly impacted by MRPS12 knockdown in the epithelial cells. These results collectively demonstrate that normal lung epithelial cells tolerate MRPS12 suppression significantly better than NSCLC cells.

We utilized two independent shRNAs (shMRPS7-1# and shMRPS7-2#) to silence MRPS7 in pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. S3A). In contrast to the effects observed with MRPS12 silencing, MRPS7 silencing resulted in no significant changes to cellular ATP content, mitochondrial membrane potential, cell proliferation, or apoptosis in pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. S3B–E). To further investigate the roles of MRPS33 and MRPS14, two other MRPS subunits, we performed knockout experiments in A549 cells and assessed their impact on cell proliferation and apoptosis. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient knockout of MRPS33 protein in A549 cells stably expressing Cas9 construct plus the CRISPR/Cas9-MRPS33-KO puro-construct (koMRPS33) compared to control cells (koC) (Fig. S3F). Despite successful knockout, no significant differences were observed in cell proliferation, as measured by EdU incorporation (Fig. S3G), or cell viability, which wasassessed by CCK-8 assay (Fig. S3H). Similarly, TUNEL staining revealed no significant change in the percentage of apoptotic cells following MRPS33 KO compared to control A549 cells (Fig. S3I). Consistent with the MRPS33 knockout, Western blot analysis demonstrated knockout of MRPS14 protein in A549 cells stably expressing Cas9 construct and CRISPR/Cas9-MRPS14-KO puro-construct (koMRPS33) relative to control koC A549 cells (Fig. S3J). Similar to MRPS33, knockout of MRPS14 did not lead to significant alterations in cell proliferation (Fig. S3K), viability (Fig. S3L) and apoptosis (Fig. S3M). These results demonstrate that targeting different subunits of the mitochondrial ribosome can have distinct phenotypic consequences in NSCLC cells.

MRPS12 knockout inhibits mitochondrial function and malignant phenotypes in primary NSCLC cells

To circumvent potential off-target effects associated with shRNAs and achieve a complete knockout (KO) of MRPS12, we employed the CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing system. Lentiviral vectors containing the CRISPR/Cas9-MRPS12-KO puro-construct were delivered into Cas9-expressing pNSCLC-1 cells. Following puromycin selection, stable cells were established and screened for MRPS12 KO, leading to the creation of single-cell-derived MRPS12 KO pNSCLC-1 clones, named “koMRPS12-Cln1” and “koMRPS12-Cln2” (two independent colonies). Western blotting analysis confirmed the total absence of MRPS12 protein in the koMRPS12 pNSCLC-1 cells (Fig. 7A), while the expression of MRPS7 protein remained unchanged (Fig. 7A). MRPS12 KO pNSCLC-1 cells exhibited significant reductions in mitochondrial complex I activity (Fig. 7B) and ATP production (Fig. 7C). Additionally, MRPS12 KO resulted in mitochondrial depolarization, as indicated by an increase in JC-1 monomer accumulation (Fig. 7D). This was accompanied by an elevation in CellROX intensity, suggesting heightened ROS production and oxidative stress within MRPS12 KO NSCLC cells (Fig. 7E). Functionally, MRPS12 KO suppressed pNSCLC-1 cell proliferation and substantially decreased EdU incorporation (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, “koMRPS12-Cln1” and “koMRPS12-Cln2” pNSCLC-1 cells exhibited reduced migration and invasion capabilities in vitro (Fig. 7G, H). The CRISPR/Cas9-induced MRPS12 KO also triggered apoptosis in pNSCLC-1 cells, shown by an increase in the ratio of nuclei with positive TUNEL staining (Fig. 7I).

MRPS12 overexpression enhances mitochondrial function and malignant phenotypes in primary NSCLC cells

The findings presented herein demonstrate that MRPS12 depletion exerts a pronounced anti-cancer effect within primary and immortalized NSCLC cells. To explore the opposite effect, we overexpressed MRPS12 in pNSCLC-1 cells using a lentiviral construct. The resulting stable cells, designated “oeMRPS12”, exhibited a marked upregulation of MRPS12 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 8A, B), and MRPS7 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 8A, B). Importantly, ectopic MRPS12 expression led to a significant enhancement of mitochondrial function, causing augmented mitochondrial complex I activity (Fig. 8C) and ATP production (Fig. 8D). Cell viability, determined using the CCK-8 assay, showed a significant increase in pNSCLC-1 cells overexpressing MRPS12 (Fig. 8E). MRPS12 overexpression also significantly stimulated cell proliferation, as evidenced by a significant increase in nuclear EdU incorporation (Fig. 8F). Furthermore, MRPS12 overexpression enhanced cells’ migratory and invasive capacities (Fig. 8G, H), underscoring the role of MRPS12 in promoting more aggressive phenotypes in pNSCLC-1 cells.

Stable cell population were established following puromycin selection. These engineered NSCLC cells displayed a significant upregulation of MRPS12 mRNA expression (Fig. 8I), while the expression levels of the control gene MRPS7 remained unchanged (Fig. 8J). These cells exhibited enhanced proliferation, as evidenced by increased nuclear EdU staining (Fig. 8K), along with accelerated cell migration (Fig. 8L). These findings again emphasize the pivotal role of MRPS12 in regulating mitochondrial function and driving the malignant characteristics of NSCLC cells.

MRPS12 silencing or KO leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and growth inhibition in NSCLC xenografts

To study the functional significance of MRPS12 in NSCLC cell growth in vivo, we subcutaneously injected six million pNSCLC-1 cells, engineered with either shMRPS12-2# or shC, into the flank of the nude mouse. Observation started 14 days post-injection (“Day-14”). Tumor volume measurements every six days, from “Day-14” to “Day-56”, indicated markedly reduced growth in shMRPS12-2#-expressing pNSCLC-1 xenografts compared to shC controls (Fig. 9A). Day-to-day growth analysis, presented as mm3 per day, affirmed substantial tumor suppression in shMRPS12-1# xenografts (Fig. 9B). On “Day-56”, all pNSCLC-1 tumors were removed and weighed, revealing significantly lower masses in the shMRPS12-1# group than in the shC group (Fig. 9C). Mouse body weights stayed steady across both groups (Fig. 9D). Two pNSCLC-1 xenografts were carefully removed from both the shMRPS12-2# and shC groups. Each fresh tumor xenograft was cut into five sections for ensuing signaling analysis. In shMRPS12-2#-expressing pNSCLC-1 xenograft tissues, there was a substantial reduction in MRPS12 mRNA (Fig. 9E) and protein (Fig. 9F) levels, validating MRPS12 silencing. MRPS7 mRNA and protein expression levels were unchanged (Fig. 9E, F). The IHC images further affirmed the silencing of MRPS12 in shMRPS12-1# pNSCLC-1 xenografts (Fig. 9G).

In pNSCLC-1 xenograft tissues with silenced MRPS12, a significant reduction was observed in mitochondrial complex I activity (Fig. 9H), ATP levels (Fig. 9I), and the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 9J). Conversely, the intensity of TBAR, indicative of lipid peroxidation, was markedly increased (Fig. 9K). These findings provide evidence for impaired mitochondrial function in MRPS12-silenced pNSCLC-1 xenograft tissues. The results of the Ki-67 staining assay indicated that growth was inhibited in MRPS12-silenced pNSCLC-1 xenografts, as evidenced by a significant reduction in the ratio of Ki-67-positive nuclei (Fig. 9L). Early investigations have also indicated that mitochondrial hyper-function in NSCLC cells is important for the activation of the Akt-mTOR signaling cascade20,21, a pivotal pathway for NSCLC cell proliferation18,33,34. In this study, we observed a significant reduction in the levels of phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt) and phosphorylated S6 kinase 1 (p-S6K1) in MRPS12-silenced pNSCLC-1 xenograft tissues (Fig. 9M). Furthermore, IHC analyses corroborated these findings, demonstrating a decrease in p-Akt levels in the pNSCLC-1 xenograft sections treated with shMRPS12-2# (Fig. 9N). These results suggest that MRPS12 is an important regulator of the Akt-mTOR pathway activation in NSCLC. The MRPS12-silenced xenografts exhibited elevated levels of cytosolic cytochrome C (Fig. 9O), cleaved Caspase-3, and cleaved PARP-1 (Fig. 9P), supporting apoptosis activation. It was further substantiated by an increased percentage of TUNEL-positive nuclei in the MRPS12-silenced pNSCLC-1 xenograft sections (Fig. 9Q). These results strongly suggested that MRPS12 downregulation in pNSCLC-1 xenografts led to mitochondrial dysfunction and growth inhibition, Akt-mTOR inactivation, and apoptosis activation.

We also assessed xenograft growth by subcutaneously injecting koMRPS12 pNSCLC-1 cells and control (koC) cells into the nude mice. After 70 days, koMRPS12 xenografts were significantly smaller than controls (Fig. 9R), with no change in mouse body weight (Fig. 9S). Examination of tumor tissues confirmed the loss of MRPS12 protein (Fig. 9T) as well as decreased mitochondrial complex I activity (Fig. 9U) and ATP content (Fig. 9V) in koMRPS12 xenograft tissues, while MRPS7 protein levels were unchanged (Fig. 9T).

Discussion

The emergence of drug resistance and the heterogeneity of NSCLC have underscored the limitations of current therapeutic approaches1,7,8,10. Moreover, a substantial subset of patients harbors oncogenic alterations that remain undruggable, necessitating the identification of novel therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes1,7,8,10. Mitochondria are essential cellular organelles that orchestrate a diverse array of critical functions, including OXPHOS, ATP production, amino acid metabolism, macromolecular biosynthesis, fatty acid oxidation, and ionic homeostasis35–39. Furthermore, they serve as crucial platforms for signal transduction and apoptotic regulation35,40. Cancer cells, characterized by their metabolic reprogramming, frequently exhibit profound alterations in mitochondrial genes and function23. To sustain their rapid proliferation, malignant cells exhibit enhanced bioenergetic capacity, primarily driven by augmented ATP production through mitochondrial respiration23. Within the context of NSCLC, mitochondrial hyperfunction has emerged as a critical hallmark of tumorigenesis and progression20,21,23,41. Elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms underlying mitochondrial alterations in NSCLC remains a primary focus of ongoing research23,35–39,41.

Mounting evidence suggests that overexpression of several key mitochondrial proteins significantly contributes to NSCLC pathogenesis and progression. Zhou et al. demonstrated the oncogenic potential of POLRMT (RNA polymerase mitochondrial), showing it supports NSCLC growth by maintaining mitochondrial DNA integrity, regulating mitochondrial gene expression, and activating Akt-mTOR signaling22. The oncogenic role of YME1L, a mitochondrial ATPase, was also recently reported in NSCLC20. YME1L knockdown impaired mitochondrial function and hindered NSCLC cell growth, underscoring its critical role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and supporting NSCLC progression20. The aarF domain containing kinase 2 (ADCK2) is a mitochondrial kinase crucial for lipid metabolism and protein assembly and is found to be a promising therapeutic target for NSCLC21. ADCK2 depletion via genetic methods disrupted mitochondrial function and impeded NSCLC growth21. Agorreta et al. established TNF receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1) as a critical mitochondrial regulator influencing cell proliferation, survival, and mitochondrial function in NSCLC. Elevated TRAP1 expression correlated with increased disease recurrence, highlighting its prognostic and therapeutic potential24.

The results of this study support that MRPS12 is a novel therapeutic target for NSCLC. An extensive analysis of TCGA dataset revealed a robust correlation between MRPS12 overexpression and adverse clinical outcomes, higher tumor grade, along with other essential clinical parameters in NSCLC patients. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing data confirmed the elevated expression of MRPS12 across diverse cancer epithelial cell populations within NSCLC specimens. MRPS12 levels were also significantly upregulated in locally-treated NSCLC tissues and across a variety of primary/established human NSCLC cells. Silencing or knocking out MRPS12 resulted in considerable reductions in cell viability, proliferation, and migratory capabilities, while concurrently inducing apoptotic cell death in primary/established NSCLC cells. Conversely, ectopic overexpression of MRPS12 conferred a pro-tumorigenic phenotype. In vivo studies further substantiated these findings, revealing that MRPS12 silencing markedly inhibited the growth of subcutaneous xenografts derived from primary NSCLC cells in nude mice. Thus, these compelling evidence supporting the pivotal role of MRPS12 in NSCLC underscores its potential as a valuable therapeutic target and diagnostic marker.

MRPS12 is a component of the mitochondrial ribosome, the cellular machinery responsible for protein synthesis within mitochondria25–27. As a ribosomal protein, MRPS12 is implicated in accurate translation of mitochondrial DNA into proteins essential for various cellular functions25–27. Its precise role in mitochondria and human cells remains largely unclear. The results of the present study underscore the critical role of MRPS12 in sustaining mitochondrial function within NSCLC cells. The scRNA correlation analysis reveal that MRPS12 expression in NSCLC cancer cell cluster is significantly associated with genes involved in cellular respiration, mitochondrial ATP synthesis, and metabolic pathways. Utilizing both shRNA-mediated gene silencing and CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene KO techniques, we demonstrated that the depletion of MRPS12 significantly compromised mitochondrial respiration, leading to a pronounced reduction in complex I activity and ATP levels. This mitochondrial disruption was further characterized by mitochondrial membrane depolarization and a concomitant increase in ROS production, indicative of elevated oxidative stress. Conversely, the ectopic overexpression of MRPS12 resulted in enhanced complex I activity and elevated ATP production, thereby reinforcing its pivotal role in cellular energy metabolism. Importantly, MRPS12-silenced NSCLC xenografts exhibited diminished ATP levels and increased oxidative damage. Collectively, these findings affirm that MRPS12 is indeed important for mitochondrial integrity and functionality in NSCLC cells, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in this malignancy.

MRPS12 knockout cells exhibited residual survival and growth. This is consistent with prior studies where targeting essential mitochondrial genes in NSCLC cells using shRNA or CRISPR/Cas9 resulted in slowed proliferation rather than complete elimination20–22,42. Supporting this, the observed reduction in colony-forming efficacy was coupled with low levels of induced cell death, as our assays showed that MRPS12 shRNA or KO induced less than 30% cell death/apoptosis. This indicates that while MRPS12 suppression significantly impairs NSCLC cell growth, it is sub-lethal for the majority of cells. One plausible explanation is that this led to a moderate level of mitochondrial dysfunction.

The established classification of MRPS12 as a common essential gene in DepMap introduces critical considerations regarding its potential as a therapeutic target in NSCLC. While its pan-essential nature might imply limited selectivity, our findings reveal key distinctions that warrant further investigation into a potential therapeutic strategy. Specifically, a marked differential response was observed between normal and malignant cells. In contrast to NSCLC cells, MRPS12 silencing within normal lung epithelial cells did not result in significant apoptosis induction or elevated cell death. Furthermore, cell viability, colony formation, and proliferation metrics were not substantially altered by MRPS12 knockdown in these non-malignant cells. These data indicate tolerance of normal cells to MRPS12 suppression relative to NSCLC cells, strongly supporting the existence of a potential therapeutic window. The elevated basal expression of MRPS12 in NSCLC cells and tissues may render these malignant cells more susceptible to its functional perturbation. Notably, both shRNA-mediated silencing and CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of MRPS12 significantly impaired mitochondrial function and inhibited the growth of NSCLC cells in vitro and in vivo. Therefore, future research focused on the development of highly specific inhibitors is essential to evaluate the feasibility of exploiting the potential therapeutic value of MRPS12 in the treatment of NSCLC.

Methods

Bioinformatical studies

Raw RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) count matrices for Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma (LUSC) cohorts were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) public repository. Initial data processing involved normalization of counts to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) followed by a log2 (TPM + 1) transformation. The processed LUAD and LUSC datasets were then integrated, retaining genes common to both cohorts. Batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm from the ‘sva’ R package. Subsequently, we investigated the clinical relevance of MRPS12 by stratifying patients based on median expression levels and conducting survival analysis (‘survival’ R package). Furthermore, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (‘pROC’ R package) was utilized to evaluate the capacity of MRPS12 expression to discriminate between normal and malignant tissues.

Reagents and antibodies

Reagents and antibodies were obtained from the following sources: Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) for puromycin, cell culture DMEM medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), caspase inhibitors, polybrene, and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8); Thermo-Fisher Invitrogen (Suzhou, China) for fluorescent labeling reagents including 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU, C10339), terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL, C10618), DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, D1306), CellROX (C10422), DCF-DA (D399), and JC-1 (5,5’,6,6’-Tetrachloro-1,1’,3,3’-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide, T3168); and Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA) and Thermo-Fisher Invitrogen (Suzhou, China) for primary antibodies. Unless otherwise specified, viral vectors, nucleotide sequences, and the verified PCR primers were synthesized by Genechem (Shanghai, China).

Cells

A549 NSCLC cell line, generously provided by Dr. Xu at Soochow University21,31,43, was cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Additionally, primary NSCLC cells (pNSCLC-1, pNSCLC-2, and pNSCLC-3) and primary human lung epithelial cells (pEpi1 and pEpi2) were also obtained from Dr. Xu21,31,43,44. In brief, primary NSCLC cells were obtained by processing freshly resected tumor tissue under sterile conditions, involving mechanical mincing and enzymatic digestion to dissociate cells. The resulting single cell suspension was filtered, and red blood cells was lysed before viable cancer cells were counted and cultured in DMEM plus 15% FBS. These primary cultures were expanded through passaging, with characterization and quality control measures including mycoplasma testing and STR profiling employed to ensure their identity and viability. Tumor tissues were obtained with written informed consent from three male patients, aged 57, 61, and 63 years, all smokers, each diagnosed with LUAD harboring a KRAS mutation and PTEN depletion. These patients were treatment-naïve prior to surgical resection. For achieve epithelial cell primary culture, the EpCAM (Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule)-positive epithelial cells from paracancer normal tissues were enriched from single-cell suspensions using Magnetic-Activated Cell Sorting (MACS). Briefly, cells were labeled with anti-EpCAM MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Labeled cells were washed, resuspended in MACS buffer, and applied to a MACS separation column within a magnetic field. After washing the column to remove unlabeled cells, the column was removed from the magnetic field, and the retained EpCAM-positive fraction was eluted and epithelial cells were cultured in DMEM plus 10% serum medium. All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji University School of Medicine.

Tissues

This study involved twenty patients in authors institution, all diagnosed with primary NSCLC, whose tissue samples were obtained based on the protocols of early studies21,44. The patients information was listed in Table 1. Each participant gave written informed consent for the collection of tissues. The samples, comprising both freshly resected cancerous tissues and adjacent normal lung tissues, were promptly subjected to analysis based on the published protocols21,44. The protocols followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tongji University School of Medicine (BRC-2022-047).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) procedure in human tissues

Tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues are first collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 12 h before being dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. The tissue blocks are then sectioned, mounted on slides, and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 before being blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 min. After incubation with the primary antibody (ab154961, 1: 100, Abcam, Shanghai, China), the sections are stained for 10 min with diaminobenzidine (DAB) working solution, rinsed with deionized water, and then counterstained with hematoxylin for 5 min before being washed with PBS. Finally, the slides are sealed with a neutral mounting medium, and images are captured and analyzed under a brightfield microscope.

Western blotting

Tissue and cell samples were lysed using a radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Wuxi, China) that included protease inhibitors from the same manufacturer. Protein concentrations were measured with a BCA kit (Thermo-Fisher Invitrogen, Shanghai, China, 23227). Proteins were separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gels, with concentrations ranging from 10 to 12.5%, and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, IPVH00010). The membranes were then blocked and incubated overnight with primary antibodies, followed by a 50–60 min incubation with secondary antibodies. The detection of protein bands was carried out using an ECL (enhanced chemiluminescence) system. Un-cropped blot images are shown in Figure S1.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

RNA was isolated from cultured cells and tissue samples via employing the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026). RNA concentrations were quantified via UV spectrophotometry by measuring absorbance at 260 nm (A260). cDNA synthesis was then carried out via a Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). qPCR reactions were conducted on a Bio-Rad system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) using the SYBR Green real-time PCR Kit. The relative expression levels of mRNA were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) utilized as a reference gene.

shRNA

To suppress MRPS12 expression, shRNA-induced knockdown was performed on NSCLC cells and lung epithelial cells grown to 65% confluence in complete medium with polybrene. Lentiviral particles containing MRPS12 shRNA sequences in the GV369 construct, provided by Genechem (Shanghai, China), were introduced into the cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 12. After 48 h of infection, cells were selected using puromycin (2–3 μg/mL) over six passages to establish stable cell colonies. Two shRNAs were specifically used: shMRPS12-1# (target sequence: GCCTCTTCCATCAGGACCACTATTA) and shMRPS12-2# (target sequence: AAGCCCAACTCAGCCAATCGCAAGT). Control cells were infected with lentiviral particles carrying a scrambled non-sense control shRNA (“shC”, Genechem). The MRPS12 mRNA and protein levels in these stable cell colonies were regularly assessed. Silencing MRPS7 was through the same procedures using different shRNA sequences: shMRPS7-1# (target sequence: TGGGGCGGACCTTTGGATATATAAA) and shMRPS7-2# (target sequence: GAAGACCCAGTCATCAGTAAATTCA).

Gene knockout (KO)

To achieve MRPS12 KO in NSCLC cells, gene editing was performed using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The cells, cultured to 60% confluence in a medium containing polybrene, were initially infected with a Cas9-expressing lentivirus (Genechem, Shanghai, China). Following infection, stable clones expressing Cas9 were selected and were then transduced with a second lentivirus containing a CRISPR/Cas9 construct specifically targeting the MRPS12 gene, using a sgRNA sequence (targeting, GCTTGCGGGTAAACGTGCAC, PAM sequence: AGG) obtained from Genechem. After puromycin selection, individual cell clones were isolated and expanded in 96-well plates. The disruption of MRPS12 gene was confirmed through DNA sequencing, and KO status was further validated by Western blotting. Two stable knockout clones, labeled as “koMRPS12-Cln1” and “koMRPS12-Cln2,” were successfully established. Control cells underwent the same process but were infected with a lentivirus containing a CRISPR/Cas9 empty vector (“koC”), also provided by Genechem. Same procedures were employed to knockout MRPS33 or MRPS14 in A549 cells using specific sgRNAs: MRPS33, targeting, GCATGAGTTCAGCGTAAGTG, PAM sequence: TGG, MRPS14, targeting, GTTATGACGTCCCGTCCGCG, PAM sequence: TGG.

MRPS12 overexpression

To achieve MRPS12 overexpression, NSCLC cells were infected with a lentiviral vector encoding MRPS12 expression sequence (GV369 construct, provided by Genechem) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 11. This vector contained the hMRPS12[NM_021107.1] sequence without any tags, and cells were exposed to it for 40 h. Post-infection, cells were cultured in puromycin-containing medium to establish stable cell population over seven passages. MRPS12 mRNA and protein levels were routinely monitored in these stable cells. Control cells were transduced with an empty vector.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay. For this, 3000 cells per well were plated into a 96-well plate and incubated for 96 h. Then, 30 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added and left to react for 2 h. The reduction of the CCK-8 reagent, indicating cell viability, was measured by absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

Genetically treated NSCLC cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per 10 cm dish. After 10 days of incubation, switching medium every two days, colonies were fixed, stained, and counted by hand.

Transwell assays

To outline, NSCLC cells, with 10,000 cells per insert, were seeded onto the upper chamber of the “Transwell” inserts (Corning Life Sciences, Shanghai, China, 3422) and given 24 h to migrate. Cells that traversed to the lower side were fixed and stained using the crystal violet dye. In the case of cell invasion assays, the Transwell inserts were coated with Matrigel before use.

Nuclear EdU/TUNEL staining

NSCLC cells or lung epithelial cells, each with particular genetic alterations, were cultured in 12-well plates. For staining, TUNEL/EdU and DAPI dyes were applied. Cell images were then acquired using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope. The ratio of EdU/TUNEL-positive cells to DAPI-stained nuclei was calculated.

Other fluorescence staining assays

Cells treated as designated were incubated with specific fluorescence dyes (JC-1, CellROX, and DCF-DA), then washed to remove excess dye. The resulting fluorescence intensity was measured at the appropriate excitation wavelength using a Zeiss fluorescence microscopy, and representative images were taken.

Caspase-3 activity measurement

Caspase-3 activity was assessed using a colorimetric assay kit (Thermo-Fisher Invitrogen, KHO1091) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cell or tissue lysates (1 μg/μL, 25-30 μg per sample) were prepared and incubated with specific Caspase-3 substrate. The enzymatic cleavage generated a colorimetric signal, measured spectrophotometrically at 405 nm.

Annexin V-propidium iodide (PI) flow cytometry

NSCLC cells with specified genetic modifications were cultured for 72 h. Cells were harvested, washed, resuspended in 1× binding buffer with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) (Thermo-Fisher Invitrogen, V13242), then analyzed using a Beckman Coulter flow cytometer (Hong Kong, China).

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

OCR was quantitatively evaluated using the XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Seahorse Bioscience, Billerica, MA, 103677-100), adhering to well-established protocols45. NSCLC cells subjected to genetic modifications were treated in a sequential manner with specific pharmacological agents: 1 μM oligomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, O4876), 0.5 μM FCCP (carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone, Sigma-Aldrich, C2920), ands a combination of 0.5 μM antimycin A (Sigma-Aldrich, A8674) and rotenone (Sigma-Aldrich, R8875)45. This systematic approach facilitated the comprehensive assessment of various OCR parameters, including basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiratory capacity, and non-mitochondrial respiration. All results were normalized to intracellular protein concentrations to ensure accuracy and reproducibility of the data.

Assessment of mitochondrial complex I activity and ATP levels

Mitochondrial complex I activity was evaluated spectrophotometrically in cell and tissue lysates using a commercial colorimetric kit from Sigma-Aldrich (MAK359). The enzymatic process involved the conversion of NADH to NAD + , monitored at 380 nm, where a reduction in absorbance indicated complex I activity. Intracellular ATP contents were measured in the prepared cell and xenograft lysates following the manufacturer’s instructions, using a colorimetric assay kit (Abcam, Hangzhou, China, ab83355). For both analyses, 30 μL of lysates with a standardized protein concentration of 30 μg were utilized.

Lipid peroxidation measurement

Cellular or tissue lysates, normalized to a protein concentration of 30 μg per sample, were processed using a thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBAR) assay kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, Michigan, 10009055) and quantified malondialdehyde (MDA), a byproduct of lipid peroxidation. Lysates were incubated with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 95 °C for 55–60 min, forming an MDA-TBA adduct. The latter was detected spectrophotometrically at 550 nm, reflecting lipid peroxidation intensity.

GSH/GSSG ratio analysis

The ratio of GSH (reduced glutathione) to GSSG (oxidized glutathione) was measured using a kit from Thermo-Fisher Scientific Invitrogen (EIAGSHC). Lysates were treated with DTNB, glutathione reductase, and NADPH. The absorbance of the reaction mixture at 450 nm was recorded spectrophotometrically, with the GSH/GSSG ratio always normalized to the protein content of the lysate samples.

Xenograft experiments

The comprehensive methodologies were outlined elsewhere21. The nude mice (5–6 weeks old, weighing between 18.1-19.1 g, with equal male and female representation) were sourced from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) setting at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China). Subcutaneous xenograft tumors were generated by injecting six million pNSCLC-1 cells into the flank of each mouse. Tumor dimensions, body mass, and daily tumor growth rates (measured in mm³ per day) were assessed and calculated following established methods20,21. The anesthetic cocktail was prepared fresh on the day of use and administered via intraperitoneal (IP) injection at a dose of 100 mg/kg Ketamine and 10 mg/kg Xylazine. Animals were placed on a warming pad (set to 37 °C) during the procedure and recovery to maintain normothermia. Ophthalmic ointment was applied to prevent corneal drying. All animal-related procedures received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and the Animal Ethics Board of Tongji University School of Medicine (BRC-2022-047).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence staining

IHC and fluorescence staining techniques were utilized to analyze xenograft tumor sections. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned into 4 μm slices. Standard protocols were followed for deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer. Nonspecific binding was blocked before incubation with the primary antibody. IHC visualization was achieved using a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB chromogen. Alternatively, xenograft tumor sections were subjected to TUNEL staining using a commercial fluorescence kit (Biyuntian, Wuxi, China, a113) to identify apoptotic cells.

Statistical analysis

Each in vitro experiment was conducted five times (biological repeats). Data all followed normal distribution and were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software (Version 23.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). To compare two groups, unpaired Student’s t-tests were employed. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Scheffé's and Tukey’s post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was determined with a P-value threshold set at <0.05.
