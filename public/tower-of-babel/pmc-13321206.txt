Hydrogen-deuterium exchange points to inter-domain interactions in reverse gyrase that modulate conformational changes in positive DNA supercoiling
Authors: Vaibhav P. Mhaindarkar, Frederic Collin, Philipp Koldewey, Dagmar Klostermeier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reverse gyrase is a DNA topoisomerase that catalyzes positive DNA supercoiling in an ATP-dependent reaction. The enzyme consists of a helicase and a topoisomerase domain. The isolated helicase domain is a DNA-stimulated ATPase, and the isolated topoisomerase domain can relax supercoiled DNA. In reverse gyrase, these two domains need to cooperate to achieve positive supercoiling of DNA, presumably through conformational changes. Here, we probed conformational changes of Thermotoga maritima reverse gyrase on DNA and nucleotide binding using hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry. Although 5′-adenylyl-β,γ-imidotriphosphate binding does not induce major changes in HDX, binding of DNA leads to increased exposure of a region at the helicase-topoisomerase interface, encompassing R507 and E701 whose side chains engage in electrostatic interactions. Removing the charges in an R507A/E701A variant leads to a moderate increase in supercoiling activity, suggesting a slightly inhibitory role of these interactions. A R507C/E701C variant, crosslinked to fix the two cysteine side chains in a disulfide bond, on the other hand, is inactive. Collectively, these data show that rearrangements at the helicase and topoisomerase interface are important for the functional cooperation of the domains and for the enzymatic activity of reverse gyrase and provide a first structural model of the reverse gyrase/DNA complex.

DNA topoisomerases are enzymes that convert different topological forms of DNA (reviewed in (1, 2)). They are grouped into type I and II enzymes. Type II topoisomerases cleave both strands of their DNA substrate and catalyze DNA supercoiling, relaxation or decatenation through a strand-passage mechanism, in which one duplex region of the same or a different DNA molecule is passed through a transient break in another duplex region (3). Type I enzymes cleave only one strand of their DNA substrate. Most type I topoisomerases catalyze DNA relaxation, either by controlled rotation (4, 5) (type IB) or by strand passage (6) (type IA).

Reverse gyrases are special type IA topoisomerases that are capable of introducing positive DNA supercoils into DNA in an ATP-dependent reaction (7, 8, 9). The ATP dependence is conveyed by a helicase module that is either fused to a topoisomerase module in monomeric reverse gyrases (Fig. 1A), or noncovalently associates with a topoisomerase module to form a heterodimeric reverse gyrase ((10, 11); reviewed in (12)). The helicase module, with two globular domains (H1 and H2), structurally resembles superfamily 2 helicases (10). The topoisomerase module is a type IA topoisomerase (10), with domains T1-T4 assembled in a toroidal shape (13, 14). The interactions between the two modules that are key for their functional cooperation are largely unknown. Crystal structures of A. fulgidus and Thermotoga maritima reverse gyrase (13, 14) reveal a globular subdomain inserted into H2 of the helicase module, termed the latch, which has been suggested as a key player in interdomain communication (15). The latch consists of a globular domain that is not conserved in size or structure, which is connected to H2 by a two-stranded β-sheet formed by two short β-strands. Deletion of the entire latch abrogates reverse gyrase activity (16, 17), underpinning its important role in mediating functional interactions between the helicase and topoisomerase parts. When only the globular domain is deleted, reverse gyrase still introduces positive supercoils into DNA in an ATP-dependent reaction (15, 18, 19). A β-hairpin in which the two β-strands are connected by a short loop thus constitutes a minimal latch.

The isolated helicase module of reverse gyrase is a DNA-dependent ATPase and an ATP-dependent DNA helicase (20, 21). It operates as a nucleotide-driven molecular switch that alternates between an open ADP state with a low affinity and a closed ATP state with an increased affinity for double-stranded DNA (20, 22). The isolated type IA topoisomerase module relaxes DNA (15). Large movements in the topoisomerase domain have been postulated that are required to enable strand passage. According to this model, these motions start with an unlatching of T3 from H2, which is followed by an opening of the DNA-gate, brought about by a large-scale motion of T3 away from T1/T4 after DNA cleavage to enable strand passage. For religation of the cleaved strand, this motion is reverted (DNA-gate closing). DNA-gate opening and closing is then repeated to set free the DNA (13). The human type IA topoisomerase Top3B bound to DNA has recently been captured in a conformation with an open DNA-gate (23), and gate opening of bacterial topoisomerase IA has been inferred from an extension of DNA bound to the enzyme in magnetic tweezers studies (24). However, none of these motions has been observed for reverse gyrase.

The consequences of the conformational changes of the helicase module in response to nucleotide and DNA binding (22) for reverse gyrase are unknown. Also, the postulated conformational changes of the entire reverse gyrase have not been probed experimentally. Here, we used hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) experiments to chart regions in T. maritima reverse gyrase that change accessibility on binding of nucleotide and DNA. We show that nucleotide binding is not associated with major changes in amide proton accessibility, in agreement with no or minor conformational changes on nucleotide binding. In contrast, DNA binding reduces the accessibility of amide protons, both around the single-strand binding groove close to the catalytic tyrosine, as well as in parts of the helicase domain. Strikingly, a region at the helicase-topoisomerase interface, containing E507 and R701 that mediate electrostatic interactions across the helicase-topoisomerase interface, shows increased exposure on DNA binding, pointing to a DNA-induced conformational change. Removal of these charges leads to a slight increase in activity, whereas fixation of the interface by crosslinking these residues abrogates reverse gyrase activity. Overall, our data highlight the importance of a flexible helicase-topoisomerase interface to allow for conformational rearrangements during positive supercoiling and provide a first structural model of a rgyr/DNA complex.

Results

DNA- and nucleotide-induced conformational changes in reverse gyrase

To investigate conformational changes in reverse gyrase in response to nucleotide and DNA binding, HDX-MS experiments were performed with reverse gyrase in the absence and presence of 5′-adenylyl-β,γ-imidotriphosphate (ADPNP) and/or DNA (Fig. 1, B–E). As a DNA substrate, we used a DNA with a bubble formed by 30 single-stranded nucleotides in both strands that is flanked on each side by 10 base pairs of a GC-rich duplex region (Methods; ref. (25)). Mass-spec analysis of the peptides generated after proteolysis with pepsin revealed >90% coverage in the four experiments performed (Fig. S1). Deuterium uptake was analyzed after 10 s, 30 s, 100 s, 1000 s, and 10,000 s. Some peptides showed no changes in deuterium uptake on DNA binding, in others the uptake increased or decreased (Fig. S2). ADPNP caused only small changes in deuterium uptake in the peptides detected.

Changes in the rate of HDX were analyzed in pairwise comparisons: Nucleotide-induced conformational changes were identified from differences in HDX between reverse gyrase/ADPNP and reverse gyrase (Fig. 1B), and DNA-induced conformational changes were identified from differences in HDX between reverse gyrase/DNA and reverse gyrase (Fig. 1C). Nucleotide-induced conformational changes in the presence of DNA were analyzed by comparing HDX in rgyr/DNA/ADPNP and rgyr/DNA (Fig. 1D), and DNA-induced conformational changes in the presence of nucleotide were identified in comparisons of HDX in the rgyr/ADPNP/DNA and rgyr/ADPNP complex (Fig. 1E). The PyMOL files showing changes in HDX mapped on the rgyr structure are available as Supporting Material.

On ADPNP binding (Fig. 1B), overall changes in HDX are small, with changes ranging between −10% (decrease) and +6% (increase; Figs. S3A and S4A for time-dependent changes mapped on sequence and structure). Changes in HDX are observed in both the helicase and topoisomerase domains, with the strongest decrease in HDX in the topoisomerase domain (aa 748 to 750; T1). A slight increase in HDX is observed in the helicase domain (aa 271–275; H1). Notably, no significant change in HDX is observed in the nucleotide binding site in H1, around the conserved Walker A and Walker B motifs (aa 100–107 and aa 203–206, respectively).

On DNA binding, more substantial changes in HDX are detected (Figs. 1C, S3B and S4B for time-dependent changes mapped on sequence and structure), ranging from −24% to +20%. The DNA-induced changes in HDX are virtually identical for rgyr and for the cleavage-deficient rgyr_Y851F variant (Figs. S5, A and B and S6, A and B for time-dependent changes mapped on sequence and structure), suggesting that they reflect changes in HDX on DNA binding not associated with cleavage. The strongest decrease in HDX on binding of DNA is observed in domain T4, around the single-stranded DNA binding groove close to the catalytic tyrosine, specifically in aa 725 to 757 and aa 1011 to 1030 flanking the upper end of the extended groove. A smaller decrease is observed for aa 546 to 553 (T1) at the lower end of the groove. A protection from HDX in these regions is consistent with this groove serving as the primary DNA binding site. In the helicase domain, smaller decreases in HDX are observed in H1, and around aa 229 to 236, and aa 271 to 275. No protection from HDX is observed within the latch region (aa 389–459). Strikingly, DNA binding leads to an increased accessibility of a region in the helicase-topoisomerase interface between H2 and T3, comprising E507 in H2 and R701 in T1 (Fig. 1F). These residues connect the two modules through a network of electrostatic interactions (14). E507 is located at the C terminus of a long α-helix (aa 491–507), R701 at the N terminus of a helix (aa 701–710). These helices are aligned head-to-tail, leading to an addition of their helix dipoles. E507 interacts with the positive end of the helix comprising R701, and caps the first two amide groups at the N terminus of this helix. R507 interacts with the negative ends of the helix comprising R507 and of the first helix in T1. Minor increases in HDX were also observed in the C-terminal region of rgyr, in aa 1096 to 1102, which is near the exit site of the single-stranded DNA binding groove in T4.

When ADPNP binds to the rgyr/DNA complex, only minor changes in HDX ranging from −5 to +5% are observed (Figs. 1D, S3C, and S4C for time-dependent changes mapped on sequence and structure), again without changes in the Walker A and B motifs. DNA binding to the ADPNP complex elicits HDX changes in similar regions and to similar extents as in the absence of nucleotide (Figs. 1E, S3D, and S4D for time-dependent changes mapped on sequence and structure). Again, no changes are observed in the latch.

Role of E507 and R701 for reverse gyrase activity

The most striking change in HDX on DNA binding to rgyr was the increased accessibility at the helicase-topoisomerase interface, in regions comprising E507 in H2 and R701 in T1. To test whether the electrostatic interactions between these side chains and the surrounding helices plays a role in the cooperation of the helicase and topoisomerase domains in positive supercoiling of DNA, we generated rgyr_E507A/R701A, a variant in which both amino acids are replaced by alanines which do not engage in electrostatic interactions. We also generated rgyr_E507/R701C, in which both residues are replaced by cysteines. These cysteines are in a suitable distance for disulfide bond formation (modeled sulfur-sulfur distance 3.3 Å, Fig. S7A). SDS-PAGE analysis of rgyr, rgyr_E507A/R701A, and rgyr_E507C/R701C (Fig. S7B) showed a band corresponding to approx. 128 kDa for rgyr and rgyr_E507A/R701A, corresponding to the molecular mass expected from the amino acid sequences. In contrast, two bands were observed for rygr_E507C/R701C, one at approx. 128 kDa, and a second band corresponding to a higher molecular mass of approx. 180 kDa. Under reducing conditions, the higher-molecular-mass-species became less populated (2 mM BME) or almost disappeared (50 mM dithiothreitol), suggesting that this species is the result of spontaneous oxidation and intra- or inter-molecular disulfide bond formation between the cysteines introduced.

Next, we treated rgyr, rgyr_E507A/R701A, and rgyr_E507C/R701C with the zero-length crosslinker Cu(II)(1,10-phenanthroline)3 (Cu-Phen3; Fig. 2). Analysis by nonreducing SDS-PAGE after Cu-Phen3 treatment again revealed species with apparent molecular masses of approx. 128 kDa for rgyr and rgyr_E507A/R701A (Fig. 2A). For rgyr_E507C/R701C, the species with an apparent molecular mass of approx. 180 kDa was the predominant species after crosslinking (88%). Its molecular mass is between the expected molecular mass for monomeric and dimeric reverse gyrase (128 kDa and 256 kDa, respectively). To distinguish whether this band represents a monomer with an intra-molecular crosslink or a dimer with an inter-molecular crosslink, we performed mass photometry and mass spectrometry. Both methods revealed that this species is a rgyr monomer, with a similar molecular mass as WT rgyr (Figs. 2B, S8, and S9). Next, we analyzed the enzymatic activities of rgyr, rgyr_E507/R701A, and rgyr_E507C/R701C (Fig. 3). Rgyr introduces positive supercoils into plasmid DNA in an ATP-dependent reaction (7, 26). Under catalytic conditions (10 nM enzyme and 35 nM negatively supercoiled plasmid as a substrate), a broad distribution of different negatively and positively supercoiled species is observed after a reaction time of 60 min (Fig. 3A) Rgyr_E507A/701A also shows positive supercoiling activity on a similar timescale. In comparison to the WT, this variant shows an increased processivity, with fewer intermediates present, and a different endpoint of the reaction, with a topoisomer distribution that is centered at higher levels of positive supercoiling. By contrast, rgyr_E507C/701C shows very little supercoiling activity. The residual supercoiling activity observed might be caused by traces of noncrosslinked enzyme (Fig. 3B). We also tested the activities under reducing conditions (Fig. S10). Here, the activities of rgyr and rgyr_E507C/R701C are virtually identical, both in time and as a function of enzyme concentration, showing that the mere introduction of cysteines at positions 507 and 701 is not responsible for the deleterious effect observed in the oxidized form. Overall, we show that removing the electrostatic interactions around E507 and R701 at the helicase-topoisomerase interface of rgyr leads to a moderate increase in supercoiling activity, whereas fixation of the interface by a disulfide bond between C507 and C701 abrogates supercoiling activity. These observations indicate that flexibility of the helicase-topoisomerase interface is key to the functional cooperation of these modules in positive supercoiling by rgyr. Possibly, crosslinking at this interface prevents conformational changes that have been postulated to occur in the catalytic cycle, but so far have not been observed experimentally.

Discussion

Protection from amide proton exchange due to binding

HDX experiments with rgyr in the absence and presence of nucleotide show little protection of amide protons due to binding of ADPNP. No changes were observed around the nucleotide binding site on H1, around the Walker A and B motifs, although > 80% of rgyr are bound to ADPNP under the experimental conditions used according to the nucleotide affinities measured before (7, 26) and the enzyme shows activity at ATP concentrations much lower than those used here (26). A lack of HDX changes on binding, termed type-0 behavior, is observed when the ligand binds to solvent-accessible residues without altering the energy landscape of protein conformations (27) (“lock-and-key” model (28)). The lack of HDX changes in other regions, outside the nucleotide binding site, is in agreement with single-molecule FRET data showing little conformational changes on ADPNP binding to the helicase domain (22), with the AlphaFold 3 (AF3)-predicted structural models of rgyr in the apo state and the ATP-bound form (Fig. S11), and with crystal structures of rgyr without and with ADPNP-bound (13). DNA binding leads to larger protection of amide protons against deuterium exchange. Protected areas are observed in T4 of the topoisomerase domain, at the ends of the extended binding groove for single-stranded DNA around the catalytic tyrosine. The helicase domain contains a canonical nucleic acid binding site of superfamily 2 helicases that stretches across the surfaces of both H1 and H2. However, only moderate protection was observed in H1, and little protection in H2. The protection on H1 clusters on one side of the domain, facing toward H2. No protection was observed at the latch, although it has been implicated in DNA binding (16, 22) and the DNA cleavage/religation reaction (15, 29). However, the interaction of the latch depends on the nucleotide state of the helicase domain and thus occurs transiently, which may not be picked up in HDX experiments.

The changes in HDX on ADPNP or DNA binding are very similar regardless whether the second ligand is bound. Thus, there is little indication for cooperativity in binding of ADPNP and DNA to rgyr, which would lead to new regions becoming protected if the second ligand is prebound. This observation is consistent with the previously reported lack of cooperativity in DNA and ADPNP binding (20).

Increased HDX at the helicase-topoisomerase interface: evidence for a conformational change on DNA binding

Large-scale conformational changes of rgyr during positive supercoiling have been postulated, with T3 swinging away from T1/T4 and back twice during the catalytic cycle (13). This opening and closing of the DNA-gate has not been observed for rgyr, but has been inferred from substantial length changes of DNA in magnetic tweezers experiments with topoisomerases I and III (24). The increased accessibility at the helicase-topoisomerase interface that we observe here is independent of DNA cleavage and the presence of nucleotide. Thus, it must reflect a DNA-induced conformational change of the enzyme, without opening of the DNA-gate. Structures of type IA topoisomerases in the apo and DNA-bound forms reveal little indication for DNA-induced conformational changes (30, 31, 32, 33, 34). In contrast, the isolated helicase domain of rgyr closes the cleft between H1 and H2 on binding of DNA when nucleotide is bound (22). The previously reported unwinding activity of rgyr implies that such a conformational change also occurs in the context of rgyr (21), although it may be attenuated (20). The closing of the helicase core would exert a pulling force on the latch, which would rationalize changes in HDX at the interface of the helicase and topoisomerase domains. It is conceivable that the DNA-induced changes at the helicase-topoisomerase interface we observe here reflect a prior conformational change in preparation for unlatching the topoisomerase domain and opening of the DNA-gate.

The side chains of E507 and R701 engage in a network of electrostatic interactions at the helicase-topoisomerase interface (see Results). Both side chains are highly conserved among reverse gyrases (83%/89% according to an alignment of 105 reverse gyrases (12)). Only 12 out of the 105 rgyrs in this alignment have no positively charged amino acid at the position corresponding to 701 in the T. maritima enzyme, 5 out of these 12 also do not have a charged residue at position 507 (one even has a positively charged R, Table S2). There are 21 rgyrs without E or D at the position corresponding to 507. Most of these have either K or R at position 701, seven have apolar residues (4× L, 2× V, 2× P). Only four rgyr have apolar residues in both positions (G/V, G/L, 2× V/L; Table S2). This distribution suggests that electrostatic interactions are prevalent modulators of the interactions at the helicase-topoisomerase interface, but inter-domain communication is also possible through hydrophobic interactions.

AF3 predictions of the rgyr/DNA complex produced models in which the DNA does not form a bubble and does not interact with rgyr. Predictions in the presence of DNA and ATP, on the other hand, generated models with a DNA bubble bound to the enzyme (Figs. 4A and S12, A–C for a comparison of rgyr, rgyr_E507C/R701C, and rgyr_E507A/R701A). As our HDX data and the AF3-predicted models suggest that addition of nucleotide to the DNA complex does not lead to major structural changes, we used structural models of the enzyme in the presence of DNA and ATP for further interpretation.

In all models, the structure of rgyr is predicted with high confidence levels, whereas the confidence levels for the bound DNA are low to very low (Fig. S12D). The predicted structure of the rgyr/DNA complex in the presence of ATP shows the topoisomerase domain of rgyr in an overall conformation similar to the enzyme without DNA bound (both 4ddu and AF3 model, Fig. 4, A and B). The main differences between the apo and DNA-bound forms are in the helicase domain. The comparison reveals an appreciable change in the position of H2, which moves closer to H1, and, as a consequence, pulls the latch away from T3 (the relative positions of the latch and H2 are virtually unchanged) (Fig. 4B). Such a conformation could represent rgyr with an unlatched topoisomerase domain, but with a closed DNA-gate (above). Due to the low confidence levels (Fig. S12D), the coordinates of the DNA should be interpreted with caution. In the model of the rgyr/DNA complex, the DNA forms a wide-open bubble that binds across the front side of rgyr (Fig. 4A), with one of the duplex regions bound to the helicase domain, mostly to H1. One strand of the single-stranded bubble connects from the helicase domain to the DNA binding groove of T4 in the topoisomerase domain, and the second strand follows a path along the latch (without contacting it) and the hole in the toroidal structure formed by T1-T4, resuming base-pairing in the duplex at the end of T4. The position of the DNA bubble in the single-stranded DNA binding groove in T4 is consistent with the positions of single-stranded DNA bound to type IA topoisomerases according to structural data (33, 34), the position of the duplex in the helicase domain is consistent with single-stranded RNA bound to the helicase Vasa (36). The predicted binding mode is also consistent with the observed HDX protection patterns on the surface of H1 and around the single-stranded binding groove in T4 (Fig. 4C). Notably, the distance (Cα-Cα) between R507 and E701 increases from 7.2 Å in the absence of DNA to 15.1 Å on DNA binding, in agreement with the observed increase in accessibility of this region in the DNA-bound state. Finally, the predicted DNA binding mode is also consistent with the electrostatic surface potential of rgyr (Fig. 4D), particularly with the strongly positively charged regions in H1 around the fork, where the double-stranded DNA splits up into the single-stranded bubble.

Although the position of the DNA is predicted with low confidence, it forms an open DNA bubble bound at the front of the enzyme, with the duplex regions and the single-stranded regions in overall similar locations in all models for rgyr/DNA complexes we generated (Figs. Collectively, the binding mode without passing of the single-stranded region through the toroidal hole may thus be more realistic.

A functional role for the latch

Finally, we compared the DNA-induced conformational changes in rgyr representatives containing latches of different sizes. Rgyr_minlatch is a variant of rgyr whose latch lacks the globular domain and just consists of the small β-sheet, closed by a hairpin. Rgyr from Pyrococcus furiosus (rgyr_Pfu(maxlatch)), on the other hand, contains a much larger globular domain than the T. maritima enzyme (12, 18, 19). Apart from the latch, AF3 predicts very similar structures of the three enzymes in the absence of DNA (Fig. S15). The only difference is a slightly more closed conformation of the helicase domain in rgyr_Pfu(maxlatch), possibly caused by the large latch that pushes H2 somewhat toward H1 even in the absence of DNA. In the DNA complexes, the DNA is bound in similar modes to all three enzymes, with the only difference that one of the unpaired strands passes through the hole of the topoisomerase domain in rgyr, whereas both strands of the bubble bind to front on rgyr_minlatch and rgyr_Pfu(maxlatch) (Fig. S15; before). On DNA binding, the H2 domain moves toward H1 in all three enzymes (Fig. 5). However, the extent of this motion is smaller than in rgyr both with a small and a large latch. In rgyr_minlatch, the smaller motion if sufficient to completely detach the tiny latch from T3 of the topoisomerase domain. The motion in rgyr_Pfu(maxlatch) is larger than in rgyr_minlatch, and sufficient to detach the large globular part of the latch from T3. The overall motion is smaller than in rgyr, though, as H2 is already closer to H1 in the absence of DNA. Notably, the topoisomerase domain of rgyr_Pfu(maxlatch) also moves by rotating slightly on DNA binding. Collectively, these models suggest an overall plasticity of reverse gyrases with latches of different sizes, and a rather passive role of the latch based on sterical effects, as proposed earlier (19). Enzymes with large latch domains may adopt slightly different resting conformations, with the helicase domain somewhat more closed in the apo state, and their structural changes on DNA binding may extend further to the topoisomerase domain. On the other hand, the structural models also support the notion that DNA-induced closing of the helicase core and the detachment of the latch from the topoisomerase domain may be a conserved feature among reverse gyrases, irrespective of the size of the latch.

Conclusions

Overall, we show that the functional cooperation between the helicase and topoisomerase domains of reverse gyrase in positive supercoiling of DNA requires substantial flexibility at the helicase-topoisomerase interface to enable large-scale conformational changes. Fixation of this interface is associated with a loss in activity. HDX data point to a conserved binding mode of reverse gyrase to a DNA substrate with an internal bubble, and support a common structural model for rgyr/DNA complexes. The structural model suggests that the helicase module may simply act as a nucleotide-dependent duplex binding module whose DNA-induced conformational change draws the latch away from the topoisomerase domain and provides space for processing of the single-stranded DNA regions by the topoisomerase domain.

Experimental procedures

Site-directed mutagenesis

T. maritima reverse gyrase variants R507A, E701A, R507A/E701A, and R507C/E701C were generated by site-directed mutagenesis following the QuikChange protocols. Primers were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (desalted, Table S1 for sequences).

Protein purification

T. maritima reverse gyrase, the cleavage-deficient Y851F variant, and the variants R507/E701A and R507/E701C were purified as described (18). Briefly, cells were disrupted in 50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 800 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME) using a microfluidizer. Reverse gyrase was purified to homogeneity by chromatography on Ni2+-NTA-sepharose, followed by cleavage of the His6 tag with TEV protease, removal of uncleaved fusion protein and the tag by Ni2+-NTA sepharose, removal of DNA by chromatography on Q-sepharose, and a final size-exclusion step on an S200 column in 50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 μM Zn(OAc)2, and 2 mM BME.

HDX-MS

Samples for HDX contained 25 μM reverse gyrase or the cleavage-deficient rgyr_Y851F variant in 50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 μM Zn(OAc)2, and 2 mM BME, either without ligands (sample 1), with 25 μM DNA (sample 2), with 2 mM ADPNP (sample 3,) or with DNA and ADPNP (sample 4). As a DNA substrate, we used a bubble, formed by annealing of oligonucleotides

bubble_30_f (5′-GCGCTAGCCGGCGACACACATCAAGGATCCTCGTAGCCAAGCCCCCGGGC-3′) and bubble_30_r (5′-GCCCGGGGGCAGACAATGCGCCTACAATTGCTGTTCAGGACGGCTAGCGC-3′).

The underlined regions form duplex regions flanking a single-stranded bubble formed by the remaining nucleotides.

Preparation of individual samples for HDX-MS from the sample stock solutions was aided by a two-arm robotic autosampler (LEAP Technologies) essentially as previously described (37). HDX reactions were initiated by 10-fold dilution with buffer (20 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 μM Zn(OAc)2, and 2 mM BME) prepared with 99.9% D2O. After incubation for 10, 30, 100, 1000, or 10,000 s at 25 °C, the exchange was stopped by mixing with an equal volume of predispensed quench buffer (400 mM KH2PO4/H3PO4, 2 M guanidinium chloride; pH 2.2) temperated at 1 °C. After mixing, 100 μl were injected into an ACQUITY UPLC M-Class System with HDX Technology (38). Non-deuterated samples were prepared similarly by 10-fold dilution in buffer prepared with double-distilled H2O. The injected HDX samples were flushed out of the sample loop (50 μl) with H2O + 0.1% (v/v) formic acid at 100 μl/min flow and delivered to a column containing immobilized porcine pepsin for proteolysis at 12 °C. The resulting peptic peptides were collected on a trap column (2 mm × 20 mm) filled with POROS 20 R2 material (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and kept at 0.5 °C. After 3 minutes of digestion and trapping, the trap column was placed in line with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 1.7 μm; 1.0 mm × 100 mm column (Waters), and the peptides eluted with a gradient of H2O + 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (eluent A) and MeCN + 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (eluent B) at 30 μl/min flow rate as follows: 0 to 7 min/95-65% A, 7 to 8 min/65-15% A, 8 to 10 min/15% A. The outlet of the C18 column was connected to a Synapt G2-Si mass spectrometer (Waters) where peptides were ionized by electrospray ionization (capillary temperature 250 °C, spray voltage 3.0 kV). Mass spectra were acquired with the software MassLynX MS version 4.1 (Waters) over 50 to 2000 m/z in enhanced high definition MS (39, 40) or high definition MS mode for non-deuterated and deuterated samples, respectively. Lock mass correction was implemented with [Glu1]-Fibrinopeptide B standard (Waters). During separation of the peptides on the ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column, the pepsin column was washed three times through injection of 80 μl of 0.5 M guanidine hydrochloride in 4% (v/v) MeCN each. To minimize peptide carry-over, blank runs (injection of double-distilled H2O instead of sample) were performed between each sample. All measurements were carried out in triplicates.

Peptides were identified and evaluated for their deuterium incorporation with ProteinLynx Global SERVER 3.0.1 (PLGS) and DynamX 3.0 softwares (both Waters) as described previously (37). Raw data of deuterium uptake by the identified peptides and residue-specific HDX are provided in the Supporting Information (Source Data). The data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (41) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD074413.

The PyMOL files showing changes in HDX mapped on the rgyr structure (Figs. 1, S4, and S6) are available as Supporting Material.

Crosslinking

The buffer of rgyr, rgyr_E507C/R701C, and rgyr_E507A/R701A enzymes were changed to 50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 μM ZnCl2 by size-exclusion chromatography on Micro Bio-Spin 30 columns (BioRad). The zero-length crosslinker Cu-Phen3 was prepared by adding a 3-fold molar excess of 1,10-phenanthroline (40 mM in 50 mM KH2PO4) to CuSO4 (13.3 mM). Crosslinking reactions were performed with 10 μM rgyr and 125 μM Cu-Phen3 at 37 °C for 1 h, and Cu-Phen3 was removed by size-exclusion chromatography on Micro Bio-Spin 30 columns.

Mass photometry

Samples containing rgyr and rgyr_E507/R701C in 50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 M NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 μM Zn(OAc)2 without and with Cu-Phen3 treatment were subjected to mass photometry. Data were analyzed using the TwoMP (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford). Measurements were done in 6 well silicon gaskets (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford) attached to ready-to-use sample carrier slides (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford). Proteins were diluted to 20 nM protein in buffer right before the measurement. Prior to the addition of protein, 18 μl buffer was added to the gasket, and the focus position was adjusted to optimize the contrast at the glass-solution interface. For each measurement, 2 μl of the 20 nM rgyr stock solution was added to the gasket containing the 18 μl buffer and briefly mixed. A 60 s movie was recorded immediately after mixing, using a field of view of 16.9 × 12.0 mm2 and 48 frames per second (AcquireMP, Refeyn Ltd, Oxford). Movie processing and event detection was performed using the DiscoverMP software (Refeyn Ltd). The measured contrasts were converted to mass using β-amylase (A8781-1VL, Sigma-Aldrich) as a contrast-to-mass protein calibrant (masses: 56 kDa, 112 kDa, 224 kDa), which was measured once on every gasket. Mass histograms were generated and analyzed in Origin. Monomeric rgyr was quantified from the sum of all molecules with masses between 100 to 170 kDa, dimeric rgyr as the sum of all molecules with masses between 235 to 315 kDa.

Mass spectrometry

Samples containing 1 μM rgyr and rgyr_E507/R701C in 50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 M NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 μM Zn(OAc)2 without and with Cu-Phen3 treatment were analyzed by LC-MS. Separation by reversed-phase chromatography was performed on a nanoAcquity system (Waters), using a Poroshell 300 SB-C8 column (5 μm, 1.0 × 75 mm2, Agilent) at 80 °C (HotDog 5090, Prolab). Separation was achieved with H2O/MeCN (9:1) + 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (eluent A) and MeCN/H2O (9:1) + 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (eluent B) as eluents, and a gradient from 10% B to 65% B over 10 min at a flow rate of 100 μl/min. Proteins were detected at 220 nm. ESI-MS was performed on a Xevo G2 QTof instrument (Waters) in positive sensitivity mode, using 3.0 kV (capillary), a sampling cone of 30 V, a desolvation temperature of 400 °C, and a source temperature of 120 °C.

Positive DNA supercoiling

Positive supercoiling reactions were performed with 35 nM negatively supercoiled pUC18 in 50 mM Tris/HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 μM ZnCl2, 2 mM BME, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 2 mM ATP, at 75 °C. BME was omitted in reactions with Cu-Phen3 crosslinked proteins. For time-dependent assays, 10 nM enzyme was used. Aliquots were taken at indicated time points, and reactions were stopped by rapid cooling on ice, followed by addition of 0.5% (w/v) SDS, 10 mM EDTA, and 5% (v/v) glycerol (final concentrations), respectively. For concentration-dependent assays, the enzyme concentration was varied, and each reaction was incubated for 1 h. Topoisomer distributions were analyzed using two-dimensional agarose gel electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels. Chloroquine (10 μg/ml) was added in the second dimension to resolve negatively and positively supercoiled topoisomers.
