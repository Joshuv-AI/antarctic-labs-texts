DOF gene family in P. sativum (L.): comprehensive genomic identification, phylogenetic examination, evolutionary growth, and expression analysis
Authors: Mengyu Zhao, Hui Zhou, Qiaohui Yang, Lijuan Qin, Manming Li, Xin Zhang, Yu Fan, Jingjun Ruan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPea (Pisum sativum), one of the earliest domesticated crops, belongs to the subfamily Papilionoideae within the legume family. This annual cold-season legume demonstrates strong adaptability, a brief growth period, and considerable genetic diversity. DNA binding with one finger (DOF) represents a significant family of transcription factors, and research indicates that several members of this family play crucial roles throughout the plant life cycle and in responses to abiotic stresses. However, a comprehensive analysis of the DOF family in P. sativum remains to be undertaken.ResultsIn this study, we identified and characterized 37 DOF genes in pea (Pisum sativum, hereafter referred to as PsDOF). We performed a comprehensive analysis of the evolutionary relationships, classifications, and gene duplication events associated with these PsDOF genes. The 37 PsDOF members were categorized into 9 subfamilies and 1 unclassified subfamily. Across the evolutionary process, the B1, B2, C2.1, and D1 subfamilies could have experienced marked expansion. Segmental duplications likely played a crucial role in the expansion of the DOF family. To comprehensively examine the evolutionary relationships within the DOF family among diverse plant species, comparative genomic maps of pea were constructed alongside representative species, and gene comparisons were conducted. Subsequently, we investigated these PsDOF genes’ expression characteristics in different growth and developmental processes as well as in stress response processes. Notably, we identified several candidate genes associated with tissue formation and responses to abiotic stress, including PsDOF3, PsDOF8, PsDOF10, and PsDOF35.ConclusionsThis article presents a comprehensive overview of the characteristic expression patterns of PsDOF genes across various tissues, developmental period, and stress responses. Our results provide a foundation for a more comprehensive understanding and investigation of the PsDOF family in pea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12321-2.

Background

DOF domain proteins represent plant-specific transcription factors containing a highly conserved DNA-binding region. They are unique to gymnosperms, angiosperms, and certain lower algae, forming a subfamily within the zinc finger protein family [1]. DOF transcription factors were first identified in maize [2]. The DOF family constitutes a distinct class of zinc finger transcription factors [3], characterized by a conserved region of approximately 50 amino acids forming a C2–C2 zinc finger structure [4], which binds specifically to DNA motifs containing the 5’-T/AAAAAG-3’ core sequence [5]. Typical DOF proteins range from 200 to 400 amino acids in length and include a conserved N-terminal DOF domain (comprising ~ 52 amino acids) and a variable C-terminal transcriptional regulatory region with lower conservation [2, 5–7]. In addition to the DOF domain, these proteins contain a bipartite nuclear localization signal that partially overlaps with the DNA-binding region [8] and a transcriptional activation domain at the C-terminus [9]. Specific amino acids within both the DOF domain and the C-terminal region interact with DNA targets to regulate diverse physiological and developmental processes [10]. Structurally, most DOF proteins possess four functional components: a highly conserved N-terminal DOF domain (~ 50–52 amino acids), an oligomerization site, a transcriptional regulatory domain, and a nuclear localization signal. However, many DOF members feature only a single DNA-binding and oligomerization domain [11]. Compared with other transcription factor families, DOF genes are less numerous in plant genomes, and their copy numbers vary significantly among species [12].

The DOF transcription factor family is widely conserved among spermatophytes and early land plants, including Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and Physcomitrella patens [13]. The presence of a single DOF gene in C. reinhardtii suggests an early origin through duplication in green algae [14]. The absence of DOF genes in Rhodophyta and Bacillariophyta indicates that DOF transcription factors emerged before the divergence of Chlorophyta and terrestrial plants [15]. The subsequent expansion of this family accompanied the evolution of complex transcriptional regulatory networks in plants [12]. Before the divergence of bryophytes and angiosperms, only a few ancestral DOF genes existed; later, large-scale duplications during angiosperm diversification expanded the family. In Arabidopsis thaliana, 37 DOF-coding genes have been identified, although multiple stop codons within At1G65935 suggest that it may be a pseudogene [7]. Phylogenetic analyses of A. thaliana and Oryza sativa have distributed DOF members into four and six subfamilies, respectively [16]– [17]. Moreover, DOF genes have been identified in numerous plant species—including physic nut [18], canola [19], tomato [20], apple [21], Brachypodium distachyon [22], maize [23], wheat [24], moso bamboo [25], sweet pepper [26], ginseng [27], tartary buckwheat [28], sweet sorghum [19], foxtail millet [29], pitanga [30], blueberry [31], eggplant [32], tea plant [33], lotus [34], olive [35], and cashew [36]—highlighting the evolutionary conservation and functional diversity of this gene family.

Functionally, DOF transcription factors in monocots and dicots regulate gene expression involved in seed development, dormancy, germination, and overall plant growth. Genome-wide studies of the DOF family thus offer critical insights into plant evolution and environmental adaptation. For instance, AtDOF6 interacts with AtTCP14 to inhibit seed germination [37]; StDOF1 is highly active during potato tuber formation under short-day conditions [38]; and AtDOF4.7 influences floral organ morphogenesis by regulating cell wall hydrolase genes [11]. In barley, HvDOF17 and HvDOF19 promote seed initiation via hydrolase-related genes [39]– [40], while in tobacco, NtBBF1 modulates root growth through auxin-dependent regulation of the rolB promoter [41]. Overexpression of AtDOF5.4/AtOBP4 results in dwarfism due to suppressed cell proliferation [42], whereas OsDOF12 inhibits brassinosteroid signaling, leading to shorter, erect rice plants with smaller leaves and panicles [43]. In wheat, TdDOF influences stem structure and stress responses through downregulation of NAC and CEP genes [44]. Additionally, SCAP1 plays a vital role in guard cell differentiation and stomatal development in A. thaliana [45]. Single-cell sequencing revealed that AtDOF5 regulates early cotyledon vasculature formation through direct control of BZIP9, SWEET11/12, and SULTR2 [46]. In fruit crops, overexpression of DzDOF2.2 in durian accelerates auxin–ethylene crosstalk, triggering premature ripening [47]. In maize, ZmDOF3 represses starch biosynthesis [48], whereas ZmDOF36 promotes starch accumulation by activating ZmAGPS1a, ZmAGPL1, and ZmISA1 [49]. DOF transcription factors are also broadly involved in plant responses to environmental stresses such as extreme temperature, drought, salinity, and pH fluctuation [50]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that DOF genes regulate both biotic and abiotic stress responses. For example, CDF3 enhances osmotic tolerance in A. thaliana, whereas its mutation increases sensitivity to drought and cold [51]; ThDOF14 mediates sodium and osmotic stress through interaction with TheIF1A [52]; BnCDF1 improves cold resistance in Brassica [53]; and JRDOF3 in walnut enhances heat tolerance by upregulating heat shock proteins [54]. Overexpression of MdDOF54 enhances photosynthetic performance and drought endurance in apple [55], while CmDOF12 and CmDOF20 in chrysanthemum are responsive to ABA and SA signaling [56]. In rice, OsDOF15 regulates ethylene-mediated root inhibition under saline stress [57]. Collectively, DOF genes act as central regulators integrating multiple environmental and hormonal pathways.

Pea (Pisum sativum) is a widely cultivated legume grown across Europe, North America, China, and Australia [58]– [59]. Owing to its high levels of protein, carbohydrates, dietary fiber, antioxidants, and micronutrients, pea serves as a valuable food and feed crop. It ranks among the world’s most productive legumes, with yields comparable to those of beans and bananas [60]. Domesticated approximately 7,000 years ago in Turkey [61], pea is also one of the oldest known cultivated species. It contributes to reducing risks of cardiovascular diseases and cancer [62]– [63], and its proteins are hypoallergenic and easily digestible [64]. In this study, we conducted a systematic genome-wide investigation of the pea DOF gene family, analyzing its phylogeny, structural features, evolutionary dynamics, and expression diversity. Comparative analyses involving rice and A. thaliana (with 30 and 36 DOF genes, respectively) revealed insights into gene structure, motif composition, duplication events, and chromosomal distribution, thereby elucidating the evolutionary trajectory of DOF transcription factors in pea. Moreover, we examined PsDOF expression patterns under various hormonal and stress conditions, as well as across different tissues and developmental stages. These findings provide valuable resources for further functional characterization and evolutionary understanding of the PsDOF gene family.

Results

Verification of PsDOF family genes in P. sativum

Using two BLAST methods, every conceivable PsDOF genes in P. sativum genome were identified (Table S1). Subsequently, the genes obtained were referred to as PsDOF1 to PsDOF37 according to their specific locations on the P. sativum chromosomes. A thorough examination was carried out to identify their basic signatures, featuring open reading frames, Molecular mass of protein, points of zero charge, and intracellular distribution. Among the 37 PsDOF proteins, PsDOF1 has the largest size with 501 amino acids, while PsDOF5 is the smallest, with only 162 amino acids. The molecular weights of the proteins fluctuate between 18.34 kD (PsDOF5) to 54.62 kD (PsDOF1). The pI values fluctuate between 4.55 (PsDOF27) to 9.54 (PsDOF11), averaging 8.06. Every PsDOF proteins contain the DOF domain, that is crucial for their role as transcription factors. In the anticipated subcellular localization findings, 36 PsDOF proteins are located within the nuclear region, while 1 (PsDOF5) is found within the cytoplasm.

Phylogenetic examination and multi-sequence alignment of PsDOF family genes

To clarify the evolutionary relationships of the PsDOF family in Pisum sativum, we constructed a phylogenetic tree including 37 PsDOF and 36 AtDOF proteins (Fig. 1, Table S1). Using a previously established classification system [18], the PsDOF proteins were grouped into nine major subfamilies (A–D2) and one unclassified protein, PsDOF7, reflecting their evolutionary conservation relative to A. thaliana. This indicates that most PsDOF subfamilies have been stably retained during pea evolution. Notably, PsDOF7 diverges from all AtDOF subfamilies, suggesting a species-specific evolutionary innovation. Similar patterns of divergence and potential neofunctionalization are observed in other legumes, such as soybean and Cajanus cajan, highlighting lineage-specific expansion within the Leguminosae. Among these subfamilies, D1 contains the largest number of members (14 PsDOF proteins). Phylogenetic analysis reveals strong genetic affinities between certain PsDOF and AtDOF homologs, with bootstrap support ≥ 70. For instance, PsDOF14 clusters with AtDOF4.1, suggesting that these genes may share analogous structures and functional roles in development and physiology. Other subfamilies, including B1, B2, and C2, display moderate expansion and varying degrees of homology with AtDOFs, implying functional diversification possibly associated with tissue-specific or stress-responsive roles. Structural features and regulatory elements of PsDOF genes implicate functional specialization in pea

Gene structure analysis of the 37 PsDOF genes in the pea genome (Fig. 3, Tables S1/S2) revealed variation in exon number from 1 to 3. Fourteen genes (37.84%) contain a single exon, distributed across six subfamilies (A, B2, C2.2, D1, D2, and UC), while 22 genes (59.46%) possess two exons, and only PsDOF15 contains three exons. The D1 subfamily exhibits the most complex gene structures, with members having 1, 2, or 3 exons, whereas subfamily B1 contains genes with either one or two exons. Despite these differences, genes within the same subfamily generally share similar exon-intron arrangements, reflecting evolutionary conservation. Using the MEME online tool, ten conserved motifs were identified across the 37 PsDOF proteins, reflecting the structural organization of their amino acid sequences (Fig. 3). Motifs 1 and 9 are widely conserved across most PsDOF proteins, suggesting their fundamental role in maintaining the core DOF domain structure and DNA-binding functionality. In contrast, other motifs display subfamily-specific distribution, indicating potential roles in functional diversification. For instance, subfamily D1 contains all ten motifs, with motifs 2, 4, 6, and 7 being unique to this subfamily, while motifs 3, 8, and 10 are shared between subfamilies C1 and D1. Subfamilies B1, B2, C2.1, C2.2, D2, and the unclassified protein all include motifs 1 and 9, whereas subfamily A contains only motif 1, highlighting a simpler structural pattern.The positional arrangement of motifs further supports subfamily conservation: motifs 1 and 9 are generally located at the N- and C-terminal regions, respectively, whereas motif 4 predominantly occurs at the N-terminus of D1 members. These patterns indicate that while the overall protein architecture is conserved within subfamilies, subfamily-specific motifs may contribute to functional specialization, such as differential regulation of transcription, tissue specificity, or stress-responsive activity.

To explore the potential regulatory roles of PsDOF genes, we analyzed cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of all 37 genes. A total of 92 cis-elements, associated with 51 biological functions, were identified and classified into eight categories: light-responsive, hormone-responsive, stress-related, development-related, wound-responsive, promoter-associated, binding site-related, and others (Table S3). Light-responsive elements were the most prevalent, indicating that PsDOF genes are likely regulated by photoperiod or light signaling. Promoter-associated elements, including TATA-box and Plant-AP-2-like sites, were present in all PsDOF genes, reflecting conserved transcriptional initiation mechanisms. Hormone-responsive elements were widely distributed: nearly all PsDOF genes contained ABA- and MeJA-responsive elements (~ 97%), while estrogen (~ 81%), gibberellin (~ 43%), salicylic acid (~ 41%), and auxin (~ 41%) elements were moderately represented. Ethylene-responsive elements were rare (~ 3%). Stress-related cis-elements associated with drought, anoxia, fungal elicitors, and other abiotic stresses were common, highlighting the potential roles of PsDOF genes in environmental adaptation. Additionally, nine elements were linked to developmental processes, including cell cycle regulation, circadian rhythm, and tissue-specific expression. Overall, the distribution of cis-elements suggests that PsDOF genes contribute to organ development and mediate responses to multiple abiotic and biotic stresses in pea.

Genomic distribution and gene replication of PsDOF genes

An overall count of 37 PsDOF genes were identified, with an uneven distribution across chromosomes 1R through 7R (Fig. 4, Table S1). Among these genes, chromosome 1R contains the highest number of PsDOF members (8 genes, approximately 21.62%), whereas chromosome 6R has the fewest members (2 genes, approximately 5.41%). Chromosomes 2R, 3R, 5R, and 7R each contain 5 PsDOF genes (approximately 13.51%). Additionally, several tandem duplications have been observed in the PsDOF family of P. sativum (Fig. 4, Table S4). Two contiguous replications including four PsDOF genes were identified on chromosomes 1R and 2R, all belonging to the same subfamily. For example, PsDOF2 and PsDOF3, as well as PsDOF11 and PsDOF13, are arranged in tandem genes in the D1 subfamily. Furthermore, 6 sets of partial replications (replications of extensive chromosomal areas) were identified within the PsDOF genes (Fig. 5, Table S5). Chromosomes 1R and 7R each house the most PsDOF members (3 genes), while chromosomes 2R and 4R contain the fewest (1 gene each). Among all identified PsDOF genes, the D1 subfamily features three segmental duplications (involving six PsDOF genes), while subfamilies B2, C1, and C2.1 each have only one segmental duplication (involving two PsDOF genes). Comparative genomic analysis of PsDOF genes

To clarify the phylogenetic relationships among PsDOF proteins in different species, we constructed syntenic alignments fueled by homologs from P. sativum and six indicative species. These species include three monocots (B. distachyon, Sorghum bicolor, O. sativa) and three dicots (A. thaliana, Capsicum annuum, Vitis vinifera) (Fig. 6, Table S6). Thirty PsDOF proteins illustrate homologous similarities alongside those in (A) thaliana (20 genes), Oryza sativa (3 genes), (B) distachyon (4 genes), S. bicolor (5 genes), (C) annuum (19 genes), and V. vinifera (24 genes). The abundance of syntenic homologs shared by P. sativum and these representative species (A. thaliana, O. sativa, B. distachyon, S. bicolor, C. annuum, and V. vinifera) are 34, 3, 5, 7, 25, and 39, respectively. Among the orthologous pairs in the three dicotyledonous plants, P. sativum shares a relatively high proportion (accounting for over 50%) of PsDOF genes with C. annuum, A. thaliana, and V. vinifera, accounting for 51.35%, 54.05%, and 64.86%, respectively. Twenty PsDOF genes paired with at least two genes from these dicotyledonous plants, mainly including A. thaliana and V. vinifera. The D value of 6.20, which is significantly positive, indicates an excess of intermediate-frequency alleles and suggests that some PsDOF genes may be maintained by balancing selection rather than evolving strictly neutrally.

Transcriptional patterns of PsDOFs in various plant tissues

Tissue-specific expression patterns revealed that several PsDOF genes exhibit preferential expression: a subset (e.g., PsDOF1, PsDOF16, PsDOF24, PsDOF32) was predominantly expressed in roots, whereas others (PsDOF3, PsDOF8, PsDOF21, PsDOF25, PsDOF28, PsDOF30, PsDOF36) showed stem-preferential expression. Certain genes (PsDOF7, PsDOF8, PsDOF22, PsDOF26) were more abundant in seeds, while PsDOF10 and PsDOF35 were enriched in pods. Transcription patterns of PsDOF genes in response to different treatments

To further investigate whether the transcriptional patterns of PsDOF genes in pea (P. sativum) respond to various abiotic stresses, seedlings were exposed to nine treatments, including PEG (10%), NaCl (5%), heat (40 °C), and cold (4 °C). The expression profiles of 16 PsDOF genes were analyzed by qRT-PCR across different tissues (Figure S3). Many PsDOF members exhibited significant up- or downregulation under these conditions, indicating their responsiveness to environmental fluctuations. Notably, several genes displayed tissue- and stress-specific expression patterns: under heat stress, PsDOF8, PsDOF21, PsDOF22, PsDOF24, and PsDOF36 were rapidly upregulated in roots, stems, and leaves within two hours; PsDOF26 showed transient upregulation followed by downregulation under PEG treatment; and PsDOF32 exhibited gradual induction in leaves and stems after heat exposure, while being suppressed in leaves under other stress conditions. Some genes demonstrated contrasting transcriptional dynamics across stresses—for example, PsDOF22 was upregulated by NaCl but exhibited a transient increase followed by decline in leaves. Other genes, such as PsDOF35 and PsDOF8, showed initial induction followed by downregulation under multiple treatments.

Moreover, PsDOF genes displayed coordinated expression patterns across tissues and developmental stages. Correlation analysis revealed that subsets of genes (PsDOF21, PsDOF22, PsDOF24; PsDOF1, PsDOF10, PsDOF35) exhibited significant positive relationships across different organs and during fruit development, whereas certain pairs (PsDOF21 with PsDOF25 and PsDOF1) were negatively correlated (Figures S1, S2, S4). Collectively, these results highlight root-preferential, organ-specific, and stress-inducible expression as key functional patterns of the PsDOF gene family, suggesting their roles in environmental adaptation and organ development.

Discussion

PsDOF gene arrangements and phylogenetic analyses

In the present research, an aggregate of 37 PsDOF genes were identified. The proportion of PsDOF genes to the overall count of genes in the P. sativum genome is approximately 0.06%, which is comparable to that of cassava (0.06%) [65], higher than that in A. thaliana (0.13%) [16], maize (0.05%) [23], and soybean (0.05%) [66], but lower than that in barley (0.07%) [67], rice (0.08%) [16], S. italica (0.08%) [29], and tomato (0.10%) [68]. These findings provide important perspectives on the evolutionary and diversification processes of the DOF genes across various species. Each gene encodes a protein with significant structural variations, underscoring the complexity of P. sativum (Figures S1 and S2, Table S1). The DOF gene possesses a well-conserved DOF domain located at the N-terminus, comprising 52 individual copies of amino acids, together with a relatively conserved transcription regulation domain at the C-terminus [2, 5–7]. A total of 5 PsDOFs (13.51%) were categorized as E-box binding proteins, while 30 PsDOFs (81.08%) were categorized as G-box binding proteins, and 7 PsDOFs (18.92%) were identified as non-G-box binding proteins. This ratio might correspond to that observed in dicotyledonous plants and Leguminosae species [26, 30, 69]– [70]. The Cys2-Cys2 zinc finger gene sequence within the DOF domain is capable of binding to the core sequence of the upstream promoter region of the target gene, 5ʹ-(AT)/AAAG-3ʹ [71–73], which is crucial for function. These proteins interact with other proteins and specifically bind to DNA elements [1, 74]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions remain to be fully elucidated. Previous studies have suggested that the DOF domain functions in both protein interaction and DNA element binding, primarily through the Cys2-Cys2 zinc finger gene sequence binding to upstream promoters within the DOF domain [71–73].

Based on the constructed phylogenetic tree [16], the members of these nine subfamilies are preserved and closely resemble those found in A. thaliana [16], rice [16]– [17], millet [28], pepper (C. annuum) [26], and grape (V. vinifera) [75]. The diversity of DOF proteins may have originated as early as the emergence of terrestrial plants, possibly even prior to the divergence of green algae and terrestrial plant ancestors [7]. Furthermore, an unclassified gene (UC, PsDOF7) has received independent support within the topological structure of DOF proteins, suggesting further differentiation of the DOF genes in P. sativum; this phenomenon has additionally been noted in some dicotyledonous plants, like E. uniflora [30] and S. melongena [32]. While certain proteins may have undergone significant evolution or expansion, the genetic distances among different families tend to converge. In plants of the same family, the genetic distances of DOF proteins in C3 or C4 plants may be closer. The DOF proteins in these legumes likely originated from one or more ancestral proteins, and their origins predate the extraction of green algae and land plant progenitors [15]. The proliferation of DOF family genes is associated with the advent of various mechanisms for transcriptional regulation during the evolution of plants [12]. Based on the topology tree analysis, the D1 subfamily is the most numerous in P. sativum (n = 14, 37.84%), while the A, C3, D2, and UC subfamilies are the least numerous (n = 1, 2.70%), differing from (A) thaliana. Throughout the lengthy evolutionary history of dicotyledonous plants, the resemblance in member numbers indicates that these particular DOF gene subfamilies have experienced significant expansion. The number and proportion of entities within subfamilies B2 and D1 are notably greater than those present in S. melongena [32] and (B) napus [19], paralleling findings in A. thaliana [74]. However, whether this differentiation benefits legumes remains to be determined.

The number of exons in P. sativum varies from 1 to 3 (Fig. 3). Most PsDOF genes (n = 23, 62.16%) contain two or more exons. In most cases, the gene structure patterns among subfamilies are similar, but there are significant differences among different populations. In this study, 14 (37.84%) PsDOF genes have no introns, which is lower than that in wheat (T. aestivum, 45.83%) [24], A. thaliana (48.65%) [16], and rice (53.33%) [16]– [17]. Furthermore, most of these intron-deficient DOF genes are found within the D1 subfamily, which may facilitate the increase in the DOF genes and the emergence of innovative roles in pea. Previous research has indicated that genes with few or no introns typically exhibit lower expression levels in plants [75]. – [76]. Among these DOF genes, the positions of introns in the open reading frame (ORF) vary. However, within the basic region of the DOF domain, the positions and phases of introns are highly conserved (Fig. 3). Our research indicates that the expression of PsDOF genes may largely not based on the abundance of introns and exons in these genes. Some genes with rapid or high expression may have an average intron density, suggesting that the expression of these genes may depend on specific developmental stages and environmental stresses. For example, under PEG, salt, cold, and heat stress, the expression of PsDOF8 in stems increased rapidly, indicating that this gene may have a specific response to these abiotic stresses.

Tandem duplications can vary in size in response to changing environmental conditions, thereby maintaining a stable number of genes with similar functions without further increasing genetic complexity during evolution [77]– [78]. Consequently, tandem and segmental duplications that facilitate gene family expansion play crucial roles in genome evolution. In this study, we identified two pairs of tandemly duplicated PsDOF genes (approximately 10.8%), all within the same subfamily, and six pairs of segmentally duplicated genes (approximately 32.4%), involving twelve PsDOF members. Notably, nine members of the D1 subfamily participated in these duplication events, which likely contributes to its predominance within the PsDOF family, similar to patterns observed in A. thaliana [16], rice [16]– [17], and C. annuum [26]. Tandem duplications may particularly facilitate amplification of genes with conserved functions, whereas segmental duplications can introduce redundancy or allow acquisition of new functions, thereby supporting functional diversification [79]– [80]. Collectively, these duplication events have likely driven the expansion of the PsDOF gene family in pea, underpinning both evolutionary retention and adaptation to diverse environmental conditions.

Patterns of expression and function assessment of PsDOFs

In this study, we analyzed the transcription profiles of 16 PsDOF genes exhibiting substantial phylogenetic divergence across different organs and developmental stages of pea grain (Figs. 8 and 9). Nearly all PsDOF genes displayed significant differential expression, with fold changes exceeding two-fold. For example, PsDOF3 of the D1 subfamily showed the highest expression in stems, roots, leaves, pods, and seed coats, paralleling the expression pattern of its Arabidopsis homolog CIB2, which regulates root and stem development and activates FT protein through heterodimer formation with CIB1/4/5, thereby influencing flowering and grain filling [81]– [82]. PsDOF16 (B1 subfamily) was predominantly expressed in roots and stems, similar to its homolog At3g55370, a nuclear-localized DOF transcription factor expressed in roots and responsive to salicylic acid, whose overexpression results in leaf yellowing and impaired root development [16]. Another homolog, At1g07640, is induced by injury, MeJA, and insect feeding, and promotes glucosinolate biosynthesis [16]. PsDOF35 peaked in roots, pods, and leaves, exhibiting dynamics akin to At5g62430 (D1 subfamily), a DOF-type zinc finger protein involved in regulating flowering time via interactions with LKP2 and FKF1, with expression oscillating under constant light and predominantly localized in vascular tissues and stomata [16]. Additionally, orthologs in soybean (GmDOF4 and GmDOF11) directly activate genes for fatty acid biosynthesis and downregulate storage protein genes, suggesting that related PsDOF members may similarly modulate metabolic processes [66]. Correlation analysis revealed that most PsDOF genes exhibited significant positive co-expression across five plant tissues (Figure S2), indicating potential cooperative roles in organ development. During fruit development, transcript levels of 37 PsDOF genes were highest in the initial and intermediate seed filling stages. PsDOF1 maintained relatively stable expression across all stages, while PsDOF16 and PsDOF21 peaked during early and mid-stages, suggesting their involvement in seed development regulation.

Pea is inherently frost- and drought-tolerant, and its adaptability is supported by complex endogenous signaling networks and transcriptional regulation, as observed in related legumes (V. faba and A. hypogaea) [83]– [84]. To explore the roles of PsDOF genes in stress adaptation, we systematically analyzed their expression under various abiotic stresses (Figures S3, S4). Under heat stress, several PsDOF genes were significantly upregulated in roots, stems, and leaves, with variation depending on treatment duration. Notably, PsDOF1 was broadly induced under most stresses, implying a central role in environmental adaptation. Other genes, such as PsDOF26, PsDOF28, and PsDOF32, showed pronounced induction under heat, potentially facilitating stomatal expansion and enhancing leaf respiratory activity. Comparative analysis with other species highlights subfamily-specific functional patterns: in Camellia sinensis, D1 subfamily members (CsDOF13–16) are key in drought response; in tomato, B1 subfamily member SlDOF32 exhibits root-preferential expression; BnaDOF C2.1 members show elevated expression in young roots and stems, whereas over 50% of Groups A and B1 members are preferentially expressed in stems and young roots. These patterns, along with the associative network among PsDOF genes, suggest coordinated regulatory frameworks linking stress response and hormonal signaling. For instance, PsDOF25 and PsDOF21 display strong positive correlations under various hormonal treatments, implying complementary roles in regulating internal metabolic processes. Overall, these findings indicate that PsDOF genes may play multifaceted roles in organ development, stress adaptation, and hormonal regulation, with orthologous relationships to Arabidopsis DOF genes providing valuable functional insights and testable hypotheses for pea.

Conclusion

Overall, we conducted a comprehensive genomic analysis of the DOF gene family in pea (P. sativum). The 37 PsDOF members were classified into 9 subfamilies and one unclassified gene. Additionally, we investigated their evolutionary connections, gene configurations, conserved elements, and expression profiles. The results indicate that both tandem and segmental duplications play significant roles in the expansion of the DOF family in P. sativum. Tandem duplications appear to have a considerable contribution to the evolutionary expansion of DOF genes in P. sativum. Furthermore, we observed the expression patterns of DOF in different tissues. Interestingly, we identified some candidate genes associated with tissue development, such as PsDOF3, PsDOF8, PsDOF10, and PsDOF35. These results lay the groundwork for additional studies on the PsDOF family in P. sativum.

Materials and methods

Gene identification

The latest high-quality reference genome of pea (P. sativum) was obtained from the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) GenBank website, with the accession number GCF_024323335.1 [85]. Initially, a total of 36 DOF proteins from A. thaliana (AtDOF) were utilized to search for candidate DOF proteins in the P. sativum genome using the BLASTp program [86]. First, with BLASTp (score value ≥ 100 and e-value ≤ 1e − 10, https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), all possible DOF proteins were identified from the P. sativum genome referring to trihelix protein sequences of A. thaliana. Then, the hidden Markov model (HMM) file corresponding to the DOF trihelix domain (PF02701) was downloaded from the PFAM protein family database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) [87–89]. We used both HMMER3.0 (default parameters) with a cutoff of 0.01 (http://plants.ensembl.org/hmmer/index.html) and SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) to ascertain the presence of the DOF domain. Ultimately, we identified 37 PsDOF proteins. We then used these PsDOF proteins as the original inquiry for further verification in the NCBI protein database. Finally, the ExPASy online program (https://www.expasy.org/) was employed to analyze the basic characteristics of the DOF genes in P. sativum, including sequence length, molecular weight, isoelectric points, and subcellular localization.

Examination of DOF gene structures and conserved sequence motifs

Using the standard settings of the ClusterW program, 37 DOF proteins from P. sativum were aligned in multiple sequence projects to analyze their specific domains [90]. MEGA 7.0 was utilized to annotate the conserved domains within the amino acid sequences of the DOF proteins. The Gene Structure Display Server online software was employed to analyze the exon-intron substructure of the PsDOF genes [91]. Subsequently, the MEME web (http:/meme.nbcr.net/meme/intro.html) application was utilized to assess conserved motifs and structural discrepancies in these DOF proteins: optimum motif width of 6 ~ 200, and maximum number of motifs, 10 [92]. We used the gene structure display server (GSDS: http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn) online program to analyze the exon–intron structure of the PsDOF genes based on CDS length and corresponding full-length sequence. Additionally, the PlantCARE web (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) application was used to identify cis-acting elements in the upstream 2000-bp region of the promoters associated with the 37 PsDOF genes [93].

Distribution across chromosomes and gene duplication

Initially, based on the physical locations of these genes in the annotation file, all PsDOF genes were assigned their corresponding chromosomal details. Subsequently, Circos software (http://circos.ca/software/download/) was utilized to analyze the chromosomal location information of these PsDOF genes [94]. The Multiple Collinearity Scanning tool (MCScanX) was employed to investigate gene duplication events using default parameters [87, 95]. By utilizing the dual synteny plotter project in TBtools software (v1.120), we assessed the homology of the DOF genes among P. sativum and six other plants: (A) thaliana, O. sativa, (B) distachyon, S. bicolor, (C) annuum, and V. vinifera [96].

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of the PsDOF family

Based on the classification of AtDOF proteins, the 37 DOF proteins in pea are divided into nine primary subfamilies. Additionally, a multi-species phylogenetic tree was constructed in this study, which includes DOF protein sequences from P. sativum and six other plant species, all of which were sourced from the UniProt website [85]– [86].

Botanical materials, cultivation conditions, and abiotic stresses in P. sativum

‘Zhongwan 6’ has been cultivated in the greenhouse facilities at Guizhou University farm. The cultivation of peas involves potting the plants in a mixture of soil and vermiculite, after which they are placed in the greenhouse. Plants were grown in plastic pots (15 cm diameter × 20 cm height) filled with a 1:1:1 mixture of peat, vermiculite, and perlite, providing adequate drainage and nutrient availability. For each treatment, five seedlings per pot were used, with three pots per treatment, totaling 15 plants per stress condition. All pots were randomly arranged in the greenhouse to minimize positional effects, and plants were regularly watered to maintain uniform soil moisture prior to stress application. The greenhouse is regulated to maintain a diurnal cycle of 16 h at 25℃ and 8 h at 20℃, with a relative humidity of 75%. Irrigation with pure water is conducted every 5 days. During the early ripening stage of pea, samples are carefully collected from the roots, stems, leaves, pods, and seeds. Abiotic stress treatments were applied to three-leaf stage (14-day-old) pea seedlings of uniform size and health. Seedlings were grown under controlled conditions (16 h light/8 h dark, 25℃, 60% relative humidity) prior to stress treatment. The following stresses were imposed: Drought stress: 10% PEG 6000 solution; Salt stress: 5% NaCl solution; Heat stress: 40℃; Cold stress: 4℃. For each treatment, seedlings were sampled at 0 h (control), 2 h, and 24 h after initiation. Whole seedlings were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80℃ until RNA extraction. Each stress experiment included three biological replicates to ensure reliability.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from pea tissues (0.1 g) thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen using the Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen DP452, Beijing, China). RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and RNA purity and concentration were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Only RNA samples with clear rRNA bands and an A260/A280 ratio between 1.9 and 2.1 were used for cDNA synthesis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software (Table S8). The expression analysis was performed with three biological replicates and three technical replicates per sample. ACTIN and EF1α were used as internal reference genes for normalization. qRT-PCR reactions were conducted on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan) in a 20µL reaction volume. The PCR cycling conditions were: 95℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 30 s, with a melt curve analysis from 65℃ to 95℃ to verify specificity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻(ΔΔCt) method [97].

Statistical analysis

The least significant difference (LSD) test was conducted using a significance level of 0.05 in JMP 6.0 software. Histograms were plotted using Origin 2016 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, Massachusetts, USA). The correlation coefficients for the PsDOF genes were calculated using the Pearson correlation program in SigmaPlot 12.0 software. The Pearson correlation matrix for the DOF genes in was constructed using R 2.11, and network analysis was performed with Cytoscape 2.7.0 software. A correlation coefficient was deemed significant when P < 0.05.
