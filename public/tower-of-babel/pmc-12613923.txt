Curcumin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles: a promising approach to liver fibrosis prevention
Authors: Pegah Hasanzade, Ghasem Mosayebi, Ali Ganji, Shohreh Fahimirad, Ali Ghazavi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver fibrosis is a progressive pathological condition that results from chronic liver injuries, including viral infections, alcohol abuse, and metabolic-associated fatty liver disease. Although curcumin, a polyphenolic compound from turmeric, exhibits potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, its clinical utility is limited by poor solubility and low bioavailability. To address this, chitosan-based curcumin nanoparticles (CUR-CS NPs) have been developed to enhance curcumin’s therapeutic potential.MethodsCUR–CS NPs were synthesized by ionic gelation and characterized by DLS, FE-SEM, and zeta potential analyses. Encapsulation efficiency (EE%), drug content (payload), and in vitro release were quantified spectrophotometrically at 425 nm. Male C57BL/6 mice received CCl₄ for 4 weeks, with concurrent treatment of free curcumin or CUR–CS NPs (2 mg/kg). Serum biochemical markers (ALT, AST, NO), histopathology, pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine gene expression, and Th17/Treg ratios were assessed.ResultsCUR–CS nanoparticles exhibited a mean size of 282.7 ± 65.6 nm, zeta potential + 28.1 mV, and smooth spherical morphology, with an encapsulation efficiency of 83.0 ± 1.8% and a payload of 62 µg CUR/mg CS. The in vitro release showed a biphasic pattern (16–81% over 72 h). In vivo, AST, ALT, and NO levels were significantly reduced (P < 0.05), and the Th17/Treg ratio decreased (P = 0.04), indicating hepatoprotection and immune modulation. Gene expression analysis revealed significant suppression of TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, and α-SMA, with upregulation of TGF-β (P < 0.05), confirming anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects of CUR–CS NPs.ConclusionBy suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ; P < 0.05) and reducing serum AST, ALT, and NO levels (P < 0.05), while restoring the Th17/Treg balance (P = 0.04) and enhancing TGF-β expression (P < 0.05), CUR–CS NPs demonstrated statistically validated hepatoprotective, anti-fibrotic, and immunomodulatory effects. These quantitative outcomes confirm CUR–CS NPs as a dual-action nanotherapeutic platform with measurable efficacy for liver fibrosis management.Keywords: Curcumin, Chitosan, Nanoparticles, Liver fibrosis, Inflammation, TGF-β, Th17/Treg

Introduction

Liver fibrosis is a dynamic pathological process characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, primarily collagens, resulting from chronic hepatic injury. Common etiologies include viral hepatitis, excessive alcohol consumption, and metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) [1]. Fibrogenesis is primarily driven by the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), which transdifferentiate into myofibroblast-like cells and secrete fibrotic mediators such as alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen types I and III [2, 3]. Despite extensive research efforts, there is currently no FDA-approved treatment for liver fibrosis [4].

Curcumin (CUR), a hydrophobic polyphenolic compound derived from the rhizomes of Curcuma longa, exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-proliferative, and anti-fibrotic properties [5]. Numerous preclinical studies have highlighted its potential in mitigating hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis [6, 7]. However, curcumin’s clinical translation has been hindered by its poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, and low systemic bioavailability [8].

Nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems offer a promising strategy to overcome these limitations. Among them, chitosan-based nanoparticles have garnered attention due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, mucoadhesiveness, and capacity to enhance intestinal absorption [9–11]. Chitosan, a natural cationic polysaccharide, can effectively encapsulate hydrophobic drugs like curcumin, thereby improving solubility, stability, and bioavailability [12].

In the context of liver fibrosis, targeting oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune dysregulation is crucial for therapeutic intervention. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ exacerbate liver damage and HSC activation [12–15]. Additionally, the imbalance between Th17 cells, which promote inflammation and fibrosis, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which exert anti-inflammatory effects, plays a pivotal role in disease progression [16, 17].

Previous studies have demonstrated the anti-fibrotic potential of curcumin, but its efficacy remains limited in vivo due to pharmacokinetic challenges [18, 19]. Therefore, nanoparticle-mediated delivery represents an innovative and effective approach to potentiate curcumin’s therapeutic actions.

This study aims to evaluate the anti-fibrotic effects of curcumin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (CUR-CS NPs) in a murine model of CCl₄-induced liver fibrosis. We hypothesize that CUR-CS NPs can attenuate liver fibrosis more effectively than free curcumin by improving drug delivery, suppressing pro-fibrotic cytokines, and modulating immune responses.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

A total of 42 male C57BL/6 mice (22 ± 7 g) were obtained from Pasteur Institute of Iran (Karaj, Iran). The animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 3 °C, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Following a two-week acclimatization period, mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 7 per group): negative control, positive control (CCl₄ only), sham (vehicle), curcumin, chitosan, and curcumin-loaded chitosan nanoparticle (CUR-CS NP) groups.

Liver fibrosis was induced in all groups, except the negative control, by intraperitoneal injection of carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄, 0.5 mL/kg; 1:1 v/v in olive oil) administered twice weekly for four weeks.

The curcumin-treated mice received 2 mg/kg free curcumin by intraperitoneal injection, twice per week for 28 days, concurrently with CCl₄ administration. The CUR-CS NP group received an equivalent curcumin dose (2 mg/kg, based on encapsulated drug content) using the same dosing schedule and route [20].

Preparation of curcumin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles

CUR-CS NPs were synthesized using the ionic gelation method. Briefly, curcumin (Sigma-Aldrich (USA)) was dissolved in absolute ethanol (1.5 mg/ml). A 0.2% (w/v) chitosan (Merck (Germany)) solution was prepared in 2% acetic acid, and pH was adjusted to 5.2 using NaOH. Curcumin was dissolved in ethanol, followed by the addition of 25 µL Tween 80, then added dropwise to the chitosan solution (CUR: CS = 5% w/w) under stirring at 600 rpm. The mixture was continuously stirred for 1 h to ensure complete dispersion and homogenization of curcumin within the chitosan matrix. Then, tripolyphosphate (TPP) solution (0.15% w/v in distilled water) was added dropwise (20% v/v of chitosan solution) as a crosslinking agent under constant stirring for 3 h. The resulting nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was collected to determine the optical density (OD) of free curcumin for calculating the encapsulation efficiency (EE%). The CUR–CS nanoparticle pellet was resuspended and stored in 3% (w/v) sucrose solution at − 20 °C until further analysis [21, 22].

Characterization of nanoparticles

The average particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). FTIR spectra of chitosan, curcumin, and CUR–CS nanoparticles were recorded using an FTIR spectrophotometer (range 4000–400 cm⁻¹) to identify functional groups and confirm interactions between components. Surface morphology was evaluated using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM), and zeta potential was determined using a SZ-100 Zetasizer [23].

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and Payload (µg/mg) were calculated using UV–Vis spectrophotometry at 425 nm after measuring the amount of unencapsulated curcumin in the supernatant [21, 24]:

EE% = [(Initial CUR - Free CUR in supernatant) / Initial CUR] × 100

EE% = [(Initial CUR - Free CUR in supernatant) / Initial CUR] × 100

Payload (µg/mg)=) Encapsulated CUR (mg)×1000(/Weight of chitosan used (mg)

Payload (µg/mg)=) Encapsulated CUR (mg)×1000(/Weight of chitosan used (mg)

In vitro drug release study

To evaluate the release profile, 50 mg of CUR-CS NPs were suspended in 5 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 1% Tween-80 and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. Samples were collected and centrifuged at predetermined time intervals (15 min, 30 min, 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, and 72 h), and the supernatant was analyzed spectrophotometrically to determine curcumin concentration [25]:

Drug release (%) = [Amount of CUR released / Total CUR loaded] × 100

Drug release (%) = [Amount of CUR released / Total CUR loaded] × 100

Histological analysis

The mice anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine/xylazine cocktail. The animals were euthanized via cardiac puncture. Liver samples were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 6 μm thickness, and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s Trichrome for histopathological evaluation under light microscopy [26].

Biochemical analysis

Blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged at 350×g for 10 min. Serum levels of ALT and AST were measured using commercial kits (Darman Faraz Kav, Iran). Nitric oxide (NO) levels were determined using the Griess reaction method.

Gene expression analysis (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue using an RNA-plus solution and reverse-transcribed into cDNA (Yekta Tajhiz, Iran). Gene-specific primers for TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, TGF-β, and α-SMA, as well as GAPDH (housekeeping gene), were designed using Allele ID 6.0 software and verified via NCBI BLAST. Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a LightCycler® 96 system (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

Flow cytometry

Liver mononuclear cells were isolated using mesh filtration. For each sample, 4 × 10⁵ cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies: CD4, CD25, and FOXP3 for Treg analysis, and CD4 and IL-17 for Th17 analysis.

All antibodies were purchased from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA). Samples were analyzed on a BD FACScan™ flow cytometer, and data were processed using FlowJo v10 software. The Th17/Treg ratio was calculated as the percentage of CD4⁺IL-17⁺ cells divided by the percentage of CD4⁺CD25⁺FOXP3⁺ cells [27].

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to compare group means. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Characterization of nanoparticles

The synthesized curcumin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (CUR-CS NPs) had an average particle size of 282.7 ± 65.6 nm and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.36, while plain chitosan nanoparticles measured 501.5 ± 108.1 nm, as determined by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1a-b). The FTIR spectrum of CUR–CS nanoparticles displayed a broad O–H/N–H band (~ 3400 cm⁻¹), a shifted carbonyl peak (~ 1620 cm⁻¹), and new phosphate bands (~ 1230, 1125 cm⁻¹), indicating effective crosslinking and curcumin encapsulation within the chitosan matrix. (Fig. 1c). Zeta potential measurements indicated surface charges of + 28.1 mV for CUR-CS NPs and + 19.7 mV for chitosan NPs (Fig. 1d), suggesting adequate colloidal stability. FE-SEM images revealed spherical morphology and smooth surface characteristics for both nanoparticle types (Fig. 2).

The spectrum of CUR–CS NPs exhibited a broad O–H/N–H band at 3412 cm⁻¹, a shifted carbonyl peak at 1618 cm⁻¹, and new phosphate peaks at 1234 and 1125 cm⁻¹, confirming hydrogen bonding, TPP-mediated crosslinking, and curcumin encapsulation within the chitosan network. Encapsulation efficiency and drug release

CUR–CS nanoparticles exhibited a high encapsulation efficiency of 83.0% and a drug content of approximately 62 µg CUR per mg CS, confirming efficient incorporation of curcumin into the chitosan network.

Sustained release period. Approximately 16% of curcumin was released within the first 15 min, increasing to 32% at 30 min, 49% at 1 h, and 62% at 2 h. Subsequently, a slower and more gradual release was observed, reaching 69% at 4 h and 74% at 6 h, eventually plateauing at around 81% between 12 and 72 h (Fig. 3).

Drug release. Cumulative in vitro release of curcumin from CUR–CS nanoparticles at 37 °C. (16% at 15 min, 32% at 30 min, 49% at 1 h, 62% at 2 h, 69% at 4 h, 74% at 6 h, 76% at 12 h, 79% at 24 h, and 81% at 72 h)

This biphasic release behavior is characteristic of diffusion-controlled mechanisms commonly observed in polymeric nanoparticles. The initial burst release can be attributed to the rapid desorption of surface-associated curcumin. In contrast, the subsequent sustained phase corresponds to the diffusion of entrapped drug molecules through the hydrated chitosan matrix. The extended release observed over 72 h highlights the potential of CUR–CS nanoparticles as an effective controlled-release delivery system, capable of maintaining therapeutic levels for prolonged periods.

Histopathological evaluation: Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

H&E-stained liver sections showed severe necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and structural disorganization in the positive control group (Fig. 4a). In the curcumin and chitosan groups, moderate hepatocellular damage was observed, including ballooning degeneration and mild infiltration (Fig. 4c–d). Notably, liver tissues from the CUR-CS NP-treated group exhibited near-normal architecture with minimal inflammatory changes (Fig. 4e). The negative control group showed intact hepatic structure (Fig. 4b).

(c) Chitosan-treated: moderate cellular damage. (d) Curcumin-treated: moderate protection. (e) CUR-CS NP-treated: significant preservation of liver structure

Serum biochemical markers

AST and ALT levels

As shown in Fig. 5, serum levels of AST and ALT were significantly elevated in the positive control group compared to the negative control (P < 0.05), confirming that CCl₄ caused hepatocellular injury. Treatment with CUR–CS nanoparticles, free curcumin, and chitosan significantly reduced the levels of both enzymes relative to the positive control group (P < 0.05 for all comparisons). The CUR–CS NP–treated group exhibited the most substantial reductions in AST (P < 0.0001) and ALT (P = 0.0023), indicating superior hepatoprotective efficacy. Additionally, ALT and AST levels in the curcumin and chitosan groups remained significantly higher than those in the negative control (P < 0.05), suggesting that their protective effects were partial but not complete. The sham group also showed significantly higher AST levels compared to the negative control (P < 0.05). Overall, these findings demonstrate that CUR–CS NPs effectively reduced hepatic enzyme elevations and provided statistically significant protection against CCl₄-induced liver damage.

(a) A significant increase was observed in the positive control group (*P < 0.01), while treatment groups, especially CUR-CS NPs, showed marked reduction (****P < 0.0001). (b) Positive control group had significantly elevated ALT (*P = 0.0184), which decreased significantly in treatment groups (**P = 0.0023, ****P < 0.0001). (c) Treatment with curcumin and CUR-CS NPs significantly reduced NO levels compared to positive control (**P = 0.003, ***P < 0.0001)

Nitric oxide (NO)

Serum nitric oxide (NO) levels were significantly elevated in the positive control group compared with the negative control (P < 0.05), confirming oxidative stress and inflammatory activation following CCl₄ exposure. Treatment with CUR–CS nanoparticles markedly reduced NO concentration compared to the positive control (P < 0.0001) and showed a greater effect than free curcumin (P = 0.003). The curcumin group also demonstrated a moderate but significant reduction relative to the positive control (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that CUR–CS NPs exert a strong antioxidative effect, effectively reducing excessive nitric oxide production associated with liver fibrosis. (Fig. 5c)

Flow cytometry: Th17/Treg ratio

As presented in Fig. 6, the Th17/Treg cell ratio was markedly increased in the positive control group compared with the negative control (P = 0.01), reflecting immune imbalance associated with CCl₄-induced inflammation. Treatment with CUR–CS nanoparticles significantly reduced this ratio relative to the positive control (P = 0.04), restoring immune homeostasis. The free curcumin and chitosan groups also showed moderate decreases compared with the positive control (P < 0.05), although the effect was less pronounced than that observed with CUR–CS NPs. These findings indicate that CUR–CS nanoparticles effectively modulate the immune response by rebalancing Th17 and Treg cell populations, contributing to their overall immunoprotective activity against liver fibrosis.

Th17/Treg ratio in liver-infiltrating lymphocytes (Flow Cytometry). CUR-CS NP treatment significantly decreased the Th17/Treg ratio compared to positive control (P = 0.04), indicating immunomodulation

Gene expression

TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ

The expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ was markedly upregulated in the positive control group compared with the negative control (P < 0.05 for all), confirming activation of inflammatory signaling during CCl₄-induced liver injury. Treatment with CUR–CS nanoparticles, free curcumin, and chitosan significantly downregulated these cytokines relative to the positive control. The CUR–CS NP group exhibited the greatest suppression of TNF-α (P = 0.0004), IL-6 (P = 0.0052), and IFN-γ (P = 0.0023), showing stronger anti-inflammatory efficacy than free curcumin or chitosan. Moreover, expression levels in the sham group remained higher than those in the negative control (P < 0.05), while CUR–CS NP–treated mice demonstrated values approaching normal baseline levels (P < 0.0001). These results indicate that CUR–CS nanoparticles effectively inhibit hepatic inflammation by suppressing key pro-inflammatory mediators involved in fibrogenesis. (Figs. 7a-c).

TGF-β

Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 7d the expression level of TGF-β was significantly lower in the positive control group than in the negative control (P < 0.05), indicating suppression of this regulatory cytokine during fibrosis progression. The negative control group also showed significantly higher TGF-β expression compared with the sham group (P < 0.05). Importantly, treatment with CUR–CS nanoparticles led to a significant upregulation of TGF-β compared with both the positive control and sham groups (P < 0.05), while expression in the CUR–CS NP group was also higher than in the free curcumin group. These findings suggest that CUR–CS NPs promote immunoregulatory and tissue-repair responses through the enhancement of TGF-β expression.

α-SMA

As shown in Fig. 7e, expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)—a key marker of hepatic stellate cell activation—was markedly increased in the positive control group compared with the negative control (P < 0.05), confirming enhanced fibrogenic activity following CCl₄ administration. Treatment with CUR–CS nanoparticles significantly reduced α-SMA expression relative to the positive control (P < 0.05) and produced a greater decrease than free curcumin or chitosan. Expression levels in the CUR–CS NP–treated mice approached those of the negative control group, indicating effective suppression of hepatic stellate cell activation and attenuation of fibrosis.

Discussion

Liver fibrosis remains a primary global health concern, characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and excessive extracellular matrix accumulation that ultimately leads to cirrhosis and liver failure. Despite decades of research, no antifibrotic therapy is currently available, underscoring the urgent need for effective and well-tolerated therapeutic options [4, 28]. Conventional agents, such as curcumin, show considerable promise due to their pleiotropic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties; however, their poor aqueous solubility and rapid metabolism have significantly limited their clinical translation [29, 30].

The present study directly addresses this long-standing limitation by developing curcumin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (CUR-CS NPs) designed to enhance bioavailability and enable targeted hepatic delivery. The integration of chitosan’s mucoadhesive and immune-modulatory properties with a sustained-release curcumin formulation offers a dual mechanism that improves drug stability while modulating immune responses. Unlike conventional formulations, CUR-CS NPs combine physicochemical optimization with immunological targeting, representing a significant step toward multifunctional nanotherapy for chronic liver injury [7, 31].

Previous studies have confirmed the therapeutic potential of chitosan-based curcumin nanoparticles for treating hepatic injury. However, most of these studies have primarily focused on optimizing physicochemical properties or evaluating antioxidant efficacy, neglecting the immunological mechanisms that contribute to fibrosis regression. In contrast, the present study expands this understanding by combining immunophenotypic (Th17/Treg) and molecular (TGF-β/Smad) analyses. We found that curcumin-chitosan nanoparticles (CUR-CS NPs) not only provide hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects but also help modulate immune balance. This dual-level evaluation sets our research apart from previous formulations and offers a new immunological perspective on curcumin nanotherapy for liver fibrosis.

Nanoparticle characterization and drug delivery efficiency

The CUR-CS nanoparticles displayed optimal physicochemical characteristics, including a nanoscale diameter (~ 282 nm), a moderately positive zeta potential (+ 28.1 mV), and a uniform spherical morphology [32]. Such features are recognized to facilitate endocytic uptake by hepatocytes and Kupffer cells while promoting mucoadhesion and prolonged intestinal residence time, thereby improving oral bioavailability. Notably, the sustained-release behavior observed in vitro indicates that CUR-CS NPs can maintain therapeutic concentrations of curcumin over an extended period, addressing one of the primary limitations of free curcumin—its rapid systemic clearance and low solubility [33, 34].

Hepatoprotective and anti-fibrotic effects

The histopathological and biochemical data jointly demonstrate that CUR-CS NP administration markedly restores hepatic integrity in fibrotic mice. The observed decrease in necrosis, inflammatory infiltration, and collagen deposition correlates with improved functional markers (ALT, AST), suggesting not only protection against cellular injury but an intriguing active modulation of fibrogenic cascades [35].

Mechanistically, this hepatoprotection likely arises from the synergistic interplay between curcumin’s antioxidant/anti-inflammatory roles and chitosan’s bioadhesive carrier behavior, which prolongs hepatic retention. Comparable outcomes have been reported in recent nano-curcumin systems showing enhanced hepatic regeneration and reduced ECM accumulation. Thus, our findings provide experimental reinforcement that nanocarrier-mediated bioavailability enhancement directly translates into improved antifibrotic efficacy, supporting the repositioning of curcumin within nanotherapeutic frameworks for liver disease [36, 37].

Reduction of oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is a central orchestrator of hepatic fibrogenesis, primarily through the activation of Kupffer cells and stellate cells, as well as the release of profibrotic cytokines. The marked decline in serum nitric oxide (NO) following CUR-CS NP treatment reflects a restoration of the hepatic redox balance. NO overproduction triggers peroxynitrite generation and lipid peroxidation, perpetuating cellular injury; its suppression, therefore, represents a mechanistic pivot point in fibrosis attenuation.

Recent evidence confirms that nano-curcumin platforms more effectively scavenge ROS and normalize antioxidant enzyme activities compared with free curcumin [38].In this framework, our results extend the paradigm by demonstrating that oxidative stress mitigation is not merely a downstream consequence but a critical mediator of the observed antifibrotic phenotype in CUR-CS NP–treated animals [39].

Inhibition of hepatic stellate cell activation

Downregulation of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) indicates that CUR-CS nanoparticles effectively prevent the transdifferentiation of quiescent hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) into myofibroblasts, thereby interrupting the pathological overproduction of extracellular matrix. These nanoparticles do more than just act as a delivery vehicle for antioxidants; they also appear to engage key intracellular signaling pathways. For instance, in activated HSCs, curcumin has been shown to suppress NOX expression and reduce the phosphorylation of Smad3C in response to TGF-β stimulation [40]. Additionally, studies on nano-curcumin suggest that it dampens inflammatory signaling [38]. Together, these findings imply that CUR-CS nanoparticles may provide dual modulation of both the TGF-β/Smad and NF-κB pathways [39, 41].

Immune modulation: Th17/Treg axis

A key finding of this study is the significant reduction in the Th17/Treg ratio in the CUR-CS NP-treated group. Th17 cells, via IL-17 production, are known to promote fibrogenesis, whereas Tregs are anti-inflammatory and contribute to tissue repair. Restoration of the Th17/Treg balance suggests an immunomodulatory effect of curcumin that extends beyond its antioxidant properties. These results align with earlier research that links immune cell polarization with liver fibrogenesis [42]

Cytokine modulation

The CUR-CS NP formulation led to significant downregulation of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ. These cytokines not only perpetuate inflammation but also enhance HSC activation and collagen synthesis [43, 44]. Their suppression likely contributes to the improved histological and biochemical parameters observed.

Interestingly, TGF-β expression was found to be elevated in the CUR-CS NP-treated group. The simultaneous reduction in the Th17/Treg ratio and increase in TGF-β expression observed in this study highlight a coordinated immunoregulatory shift in the hepatic microenvironment. TGF-β is a context-dependent cytokine that governs CD4⁺ T-cell fate: in an inflammatory milieu rich in IL-6, it drives Th17 differentiation through STAT3 and RORγt activation, whereas under low-inflammatory conditions, it promotes Treg induction via FOXP3 stabilization [45, 46]. Thus, the elevated TGF-β levels in our model, alongside suppressed IL-6 and TNF-α, suggest a regenerative, pro-Treg role rather than a pro-fibrotic one.

Recent in vivo studies have confirmed that nanocurcumin formulations can downregulate Th17 cytokines (IL-17, IL-22) and concurrently expand FOXP3⁺ Tregs, thereby restoring immune homeostasis [47, 48]. These effects are partly mediated through inhibition of STAT3 signaling, which is critical for Th17 lineage commitment. Meanwhile, chitosan-based nanocarriers have been reported to condition dendritic cells toward a tolerogenic phenotype, elevating IL-10 and TGF-β secretion and thereby favoring Treg polarization [3].

In the context of liver fibrosis, this dual mechanism — enhanced TGF-β–FOXP3 signaling and constrained STAT3-Th17 activity — may underlie the attenuation of hepatic inflammation and stellate cell activation observed in our model. The data thus support a model in which CUR-CS NPs re-establish an anti-inflammatory, Treg-dominant equilibrium through contextual modulation of TGF-β signaling, simultaneously preserving its immunoregulatory functions while preventing excessive fibrogenic Smad activation. This duality warrants further investigation, but the observed increase may reflect reparative rather than pathological signaling.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study shows that curcumin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (CUR-CS NPs) provide a versatile therapeutic approach for liver fibrosis. By improving bioavailability, enabling sustained release, and targeting the liver, CUR-CS NPs enhance the biological effectiveness of curcumin. This results in the simultaneous suppression of oxidative stress, inflammation, and the activation of hepatic stellate cells.

Beyond these typical effects, our findings highlight an additional immunomodulatory aspect—specifically, the restoration of the Th17/Treg balance and the controlled upregulation of TGF-β. This indicates that CUR-CS NPs help reprogram the hepatic immune microenvironment towards resolution rather than progression. This dual action, which combines pharmacokinetic optimization with immune regulation, sets CUR-CS NPs apart from previous single-function nanoformulations and emphasizes their role as active molecular modulators rather than mere carriers.

Future perspectives

Overall, this research contributes to the growing field of mechanism-driven nanotherapeutics for chronic liver disease, offering new insights into how delivery systems can integrate anti-oxidative, anti-fibrotic, and immune-restorative functions. Future studies should focus on: 1- Examine molecular signaling pathways underlying the Th17/Treg shift—particularly STAT3, FOXP3, and Smad-dependent cross-talk—to clarify the immunological basis of fibrosis regression. 2- Characterizing macrophage and dendritic-cell responses following CUR-CS NP treatment to identify their contribution to Treg induction and immune tolerance. 3- Conduct translational studies integrating pharmacokinetic, safety, and immunodynamic endpoints to support eventual clinical testing.

Together, these efforts will deepen our understanding of how nanotherapeutics can simultaneously deliver drugs and reshape immune balance, advancing toward next-generation immuno-nanomedicine for liver fibrosis and related chronic inflammatory diseases.
