HOPE Kids 2: Phase 3, Randomized Trial of Voxelotor in Children With SCD and Conditional Cerebral Blood Flow Velocities

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildren with SCD have elevated stroke risk, correlated with cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV). HOPE Kids 2 was a phase 3, multicenter, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial evaluating the effect of voxelotor on CBFV. Participants aged 2 to < 15 years with SCD (HbSS/HbSβ0) and conditional CBFV (170 to < 200 cm/s) were randomized 1:1 to voxelotor (N = 120) or placebo (N = 116) for 96 weeks; 195 (83%) were in sub‐Saharan Africa. The primary endpoint, CBFV change from baseline to Week 24, was significantly greater with voxelotor (least‐squares mean change: voxelotor [n = 114] −12.06 cm/s, placebo [n = 108] −4.29 cm/s; difference −7.77 cm/s; 95% CI −13.18 to −2.37; p = 0.0048), with significant difference sustained through Week 48. Hemoglobin levels increased at Weeks 24 and 48 with voxelotor. Drug administration was paused in May 2024 when an imbalance of deaths was observed between treatment groups; subsequently, the trial discontinued when all active voxelotor trials were discontinued in September 2024. At study end, median (range) exposure was 84 (3–104) weeks for voxelotor and 84 (3–106) weeks for placebo. The most common SCD‐related treatment‐emergent adverse event was sickle cell anemia with crisis (voxelotor 59.2%; placebo 37.9%). All deaths (voxelotor n = 8; placebo n = 2) were considered unrelated to study drug by treating investigators. Overall, voxelotor showed CBFV reduction in children with SCD and conditional CBFV, suggesting HbS polymerization inhibition has a potential therapeutic role for this group; nevertheless, additional studies evaluating potential regional risks for sickle cell anemia with crisis or mortality are warranted. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04218084

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a global health challenge, affecting millions of people worldwide [1, 2]. In more than 50% of countries where the prevalence of SCD is high, total SCD mortality remains a top‐20 cause of death in people aged < 50 years, with children < 5 years old bearing the brunt of the mortality burden, particularly in sub‐Saharan Africa [1]. Silent cerebral infarction or overt stroke caused by abnormality of cerebral blood flow (CBF) leading to ischemia or hemorrhage is a potentially devastating, persistent complication of severe SCD—homozygous hemoglobin S (HbSS) or hemoglobin S beta‐thalassemia (HbSβ0) genotypes—and a cause of motor disability, cognitive morbidity, and mortality [3]. Before implementation of current management approaches, overt stroke risk was reported to be 200–400 times that of the general population [4], with stroke occurring in ~10% of children with SCD [5, 6].

To assess stroke risk, CBF velocity (CBFV) in the major intracranial arteries is measured by transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography [7]. An abnormal CBFV (≥ 200 cm/s) indicates substantially higher risk of stroke compared with normal CBFV (< 170 cm/s) [8, 9, 10, 11], and evidence‐based treatment recommendations to mitigate risk have been established [12, 13, 14]. A “conditional” CBFV (170 to < 200 cm/s) indicates elevated risk compared with normal CBFV, yet few clinical trials have been conducted in this group and currently, clinical observation with repeat TCD is recommended, although management varies in practice [6, 10, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Generally, conditional CBFV is an indication to either start or optimize hydroxyurea therapy [19].

In SCD, CBFV increases as compensation for severe anemia [20, 21, 22], and encroaches on the cerebrovascular reserve, limiting response to increased metabolic demands and/or other stimuli (e.g., during hypercapnia), with the risk of ischemic brain damage [20, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Along with elevated blood pressure, history of transient ischemic attack or acute chest syndrome, anemia is among the recognized risk factors for cerebrovascular accidents in SCD [26], and CBFV is inversely correlated with hemoglobin levels [21, 27, 28, 29].

The sickle hemoglobin (HbS) polymerization inhibitor voxelotor has demonstrated increases in hemoglobin in patients with SCD [30, 31]. We hypothesized that, by increasing hemoglobin concentrations, voxelotor could reduce CBFV and the HOPE Kids 2 trial was designed to evaluate voxelotor in children with SCD and conditional CBFV. The trial had completed an initial 48‐week period and was ongoing when, in May 2024, study drug administration was paused following observation of an imbalance of deaths between the voxelotor and placebo arms. In September 2024, a preliminary dataset from several voxelotor studies (HOPE Kids 2, NCT04218084; RESOLVE, NCT05561140; RETRO, NCT04930328; and PROSPECT, NCT04930445) called into question the benefit–risk profile of voxelotor, the manufacturer voluntarily withdrew voxelotor from clinical use, and all active voxelotor clinical trials were discontinued pending further analysis [32, 33, 34]. Analyses are now complete, and we report the final results of the HOPE Kids 2 trial.

Methods

HOPE Kids 2 (NCT04218084; C5341021, formerly GBT440‐032) was a multinational, randomized, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind, phase 3 clinical trial. The trial was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all applicable country‐specific laws and regulations. Institutional review boards/ethics committees at each participating site approved the protocol and participants' parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent. The sponsor conducted data analysis and all authors had access to primary clinical trial data. The protocol is available on ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04218084).

Participants

Eligible participants had SCD with documented genotype HbSS or HbSβ0, were aged 2 to < 15 years, and had CBFV 170 to < 200 cm/s and hemoglobin 5.5–10.5 g/dL at screening. Participants receiving stable‐dose hydroxyurea were eligible, but participants initiating hydroxyurea after randomization were discontinued from the study. A history of vaso‐occlusive crises (VOCs) within the past year was not required for eligibility, but participants reporting > 10 VOCs requiring hospitalization or emergency department/clinic visits within the past year were excluded. Further eligibility criteria are given in Supporting Information Methods.

Participants who developed an abnormal CBFV study value were discontinued from the study. Specifically, for CBFV ≥ 220 cm/s, the participant discontinued immediately (confirmation assessment was not required). For CBFV in the range 200 to < 220 cm/s, repeat assessment was required within 2–4 weeks; if the repeat CBFV was ≥ 200 cm/s, the participant was discontinued from the study.

Interventions

The trial included a screening period (up to 35 days), treatment period (up to 96 weeks), and an end‐of‐trial visit 4 weeks after the last study–drug dose. Using a central Interactive Response System, participants were randomized (1:1) to receive once‐daily oral voxelotor or placebo. Participants ≥ 12 years old received a fixed 1500‐mg/day dose of voxelotor; participants < 12 years old received weight‐based doses (1500‐mg/day equivalent). Adherence was calculated as described in Supporting Information Methods. Randomization was stratified using permuted blocks within strata by hydroxyurea use (yes; no), age (2 to ≤ 8; > 8 to < 15 years), screening CBFV (170 to < 185; 185 to < 200 cm/s), and geographic region (Africa, including Middle East and North Africa; rest of the world).

Assessments

Nonimaging pulsed TCD was performed by sponsor‐trained local sonographers at screening and Weeks 8, 16, 24, 28, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, and the end‐of‐study visit or early discontinuation, using a dedicated Multi‐Dop T digital machine (Compumedics DWL, Singen, Germany) as described in Supporting Information Methods. Hematology and serum chemistry were assessed as described in Supporting Information Methods.

During the trial, VOCs were prospectively recorded by the investigator's assessment and reporting of a crisis event, as defined in the protocol (see Supporting Information Methods). Safety was assessed as described in Supporting Information Methods. Investigators reported stroke as an adverse event (AE) according to their clinical assessment; imaging was not required.

When study–drug administration was paused, visits and assessments continued in accordance with the protocol. At study termination, participants who had not discontinued or completed the trial were requested to attend an early‐discontinuation visit within 2 weeks, and the end‐of‐study visit 4 weeks later.

Endpoints and Statistical Analyses

Final analyses are reported, using data collected through the last participant's last visit on November 6, 2024 (database lock April 16, 2025). The pause in study–drug administration > 4 months before study termination hindered interpretation of Week‐96 analyses: participants did not resume study drug, yet fulfilled intention‐to‐treat (ITT) criteria (Supporting Information Methods). Therefore, prespecified secondary Week 96 endpoints (changes from baseline to Week 96 and time‐to‐event analyses over the 96‐week period) were considered exploratory.

The primary efficacy endpoint was change from baseline at Week 24 in CBFV, specifically time‐averaged maximum of the mean velocity (TAMMV) in the middle cerebral artery, as measured by TCD. Secondary endpoints prespecified in the statistical analysis plan were change from baseline in CBFV at Weeks 48 and 96; time to conversion to abnormal CBFV (≥ 200 cm/s); time to reversion to normal CBFV (< 170 cm/s); the proportion with CBFV response (reduction ≥ 15 cm/s) at Weeks 24, 48, and 96; change in hemoglobin from baseline at Weeks 24, 48, and 96; change from baseline in markers of hemolysis (unconjugated bilirubin, percentage of reticulocytes, absolute reticulocytes, and lactate dehydrogenase [LDH]) at Weeks 24, 48, and 96; and annualized incidence rate of reported VOCs.

The primary efficacy analysis included all randomized participants according to treatment group as randomized (ITT population). Assuming a difference in mean change from baseline in CBFV of 15 cm/s and common standard deviation of 24 cm/s, with a 20% drop‐out rate, a sample size of 112 participants per group was sufficient to provide > 95% power to detect the targeted treatment effect at a 2‐sided significance level of 5%. To control the overall significance level for the study at a 2‐sided 5% level, a fixed‐sequence hierarchical test procedure was used to formally evaluate voxelotor versus placebo at Weeks 24 and 48, testing primary and key secondary endpoints in the following order: (1) CBFV change from baseline at Week 24 (primary endpoint), (2) CBFV change from baseline at Week 48, and (3) CBFV response rate at Week 24. Subsequent endpoints and subgroup analyses were not adjusted for multiplicity.

For CBFV, screening measurements were used as baseline values. Analyses excluded measurements after new hydroxyurea initiation. Measurements obtained ≤ 8 weeks after red blood cell (RBC) transfusion were imputed using the last CBFV value before the transfusion. Change in CBFV from baseline to Week 24 was evaluated using a mixed‐model for repeated measures (Supporting Information Methods). Changes in CBFV from baseline to Weeks 48 and 96, and for subgroups to Week 24, as well as in hemoglobin and markers of hemolysis, were analyzed similarly, with a single model incorporating data from all timepoints. The proportion of participants with a hemoglobin response (> 1 g/dL increase from baseline) at Weeks 24 and 48 was calculated considering participants newly initiating hydroxyurea before Week 24/48, RBC transfusion within 8 weeks before the Week 24/48 assessment, or with missing hemoglobin assessment as nonresponders. Subgroup analyses for the primary endpoint were performed for prespecified groups based on sex, geographic region, and baseline age, hydroxyurea use, and CBFV value. Cutpoints for age, geographic region, and CBFV value were based on randomization stratification factors, with age subgroups 2 to ≤ 8 and > 8 to < 15 years chosen to evenly balance by age distribution using the expected median age of 8 years. An additional age subgroup analysis defined subgroups of ≥ 2 to < 4; 4 to < 12; and ≥ 12 to < 15 years. Analyses based on data to Week 96 estimated treatment effects and corresponding confidence intervals without formal hypothesis testing.

Rates of CBFV response (reduction from baseline ≥ 15 cm/s) were compared using an exact Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel general association test as described in the Supporting Information Methods. Rates and time to conversion to abnormal CBFV (≥ 200 cm/s) were estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis as described in the Supporting Information Methods. Time to reversion to normal CBFV (< 170 cm/s) was summarized descriptively.

Rates of VOC were calculated for total participant‐years on treatment (time from randomization to the earliest of last study–drug dose, initiation of hydroxyurea after baseline for participants not receiving hydroxyurea at baseline, and study end). Annualized incidence rates of VOC were estimated using a negative binomial model with treatment group as an independent variable and adjusting for baseline hydroxyurea use, age group, and baseline CBFV subgroup. Stroke was evaluated as an additional exploratory endpoint.

Results

Participants

HOPE Kids 2 was conducted at 21 institutions in 8 countries (Egypt, Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, Oman, Saudi Arabia, the United Kingdom, and the United States). Between November 2020 and January 2023, 236 participants were enrolled; 120 were randomly assigned to voxelotor and 116 to placebo (Figure S1). Most participants were enrolled in sub‐Saharan Africa (Table 1); the majority were in Nigeria (Table S1).

Treatment groups were generally well balanced for baseline characteristics (Table 1), with ~30% in both groups receiving hydroxyurea. For VOC history, the groups appeared imbalanced, as fewer participants in the voxelotor arm had zero VOCs within the past 12 months (47.5% vs. 58.6%). In addition, mean baseline leukocyte values were higher in the voxelotor arm.

During the trial, similar proportions of each group had ≥ 1 on‐treatment RBC transfusion (voxelotor 32/120 [26.7%], placebo 32/116 [27.6%]). By trial end, the median (range) time on study for the voxelotor and placebo groups, respectively, was 96.1 (3, 119) and 96.1 (5, 123) weeks; median (range) exposure to study drug was 84 (3, 104) and 84 (3, 106) weeks. The mean (SD) dosing regimen adherence was high in both groups [voxelotor 96 (8)%; placebo 97 (6)%]. Before the dosing pause, all participants had reached Week 48 (or discontinued early for other reasons). However, most participants had discontinued study drug before Week 96 due to the pause in study drug administration.

CBF Velocity

The decrease from baseline in CBFV at Week 24 was significantly greater for the voxelotor group than the placebo group (Figure 1A). Least‐squares (LS) mean changes from baseline were −12.06 cm/s (95% CI −15.82 to −8.31) for the voxelotor group and −4.29 cm/s (95% CI −8.17 to −0.40) for placebo (statistically significant difference −7.77 cm/s; 95% CI −13.18 to −2.37 cm/s; p = 0.0048). Across subgroups, analyses showed considerable overlap in the placebo‐adjusted change from baseline in CBFV at Week 24 (Figure 2). Although analyses should be evaluated with caution as some subgroups were small (9–15 participants per treatment arm), the strongest voxelotor response (> 15 cm/s reduction) occurred in adolescents (aged ≥ 12 to < 15 years) whereas the youngest group (≥ 2 to < 4 years) unexpectedly improved substantially with placebo (Figure 2). At Week 48, the difference between the voxelotor and placebo groups remained statistically significant, with least‐squares mean changes from baseline of −10.33 (95% CI −14.14 to −6.52) and −3.86 (−7.77 to 0.05) cm/s for the respective groups (difference −6.47 cm/s; 95% CI −11.93 to −1.01 cm/s; p < 0.05).

At Week 24, more participants in the voxelotor arm had a CBFV response (≥ 15 cm/s reduction from baseline) (Table S2). At this timepoint, 38.2% of the voxelotor group and 25.3% of the placebo group had a CBFV response (difference in adjusted CBFV response rates: 12.8%, 95% Cl 0.9%–24.8%; p = 0.049). The proportions remained similar at Week 48 (difference: 12.4%, 95% Cl 0.3%–24.5%; p = 0.069).

During the 96‐week timeframe, conversion to abnormal CBFV (≥ 200 cm/s) was recorded for 51 participants (voxelotor: 22; placebo: 29; HR 0.62; 95% CI 0.35–1.09; Figure S2). Most participants in both groups achieved ≥ 1 CBFV value < 170 cm/s during the study (Table S3). For participants with Week 24 and 48 measurements, 32/108 (29.6%) of the voxelotor group and 19/106 (17.9%) of the placebo group had CBFV < 170 cm/s at both timepoints (Table S4).

Hemoglobin and Markers of Hemolysis

At Weeks 24 and 48, changes from baseline in hemoglobin were higher for the voxelotor group than the placebo group (least‐squares mean difference [95% CI] at Week 24: 0.7 [0.4–1.1; p = 0.0001]; at Week 48: 1.0 [0.6–1.4] g/dL; p < 0.0001) (Figure 1B and Table S5). For subgroups according to age, changes from baseline in hemoglobin at Weeks 24 and 48 were greater in the voxelotor group than the placebo group in older children, although analyses should be evaluated with caution as some subgroups were small (Figure S3). For the overall group, a higher proportion of participants receiving voxelotor had hemoglobin increases > 1 g/dL from baseline at Week 24 (42.6% vs. 9.1% with placebo) and Week 48 (37.4% vs. 11.2% with placebo) (Table S6). At both Week 24 and 48, CBFV tended to decrease when hemoglobin increased (Figure S4).

For markers of hemolysis, the voxelotor group had reductions from baseline in indirect bilirubin throughout the study, with significant differences from placebo at Weeks 24 and 48 (Figure 3). For the percentage of reticulocytes, absolute reticulocytes, and LDH, changes from baseline were not different from placebo (Figure 3 and Table S5).

VOC and Stroke

During the 12 months pre‐baseline, the mean (SD) rate of VOCs was 0.9 (1.2) events/year for the voxelotor group and 0.7 (1.0) events/year for the placebo group. During the study, annualized adjusted incidence rates (events/year) were 1.1 in the voxelotor group and 0.6 in the placebo group (incidence rate ratio 1.9; 95% CI 1.3–2.7; p = 0.0006) (Table 2). When evaluated by subgroups based on VOC history (0 vs. ≥ 1 VOC in the past 12 months), participants with a history of no VOCs had a lower incidence of VOC during the trial in both treatment groups, and the difference between treatment groups in on‐study VOC rates remained similar within each subgroup (Table S7).

During the study, stroke was not reported for any participants in the voxelotor group (0 of 120 participants) but was reported for three of 116 participants in the placebo group (cerebrovascular accident in two participants, hemorrhagic stroke in one participant).

Safety

Non‐SCD‐related TEAEs were reported for similar proportions of the voxelotor (77.5%) and placebo (78.4%) groups (Table 3). Most of these TEAEs were Grade 1 or 2, and the proportion of participants with Grade ≥ 3 TEAEs was similar for the treatment groups. The most common non‐SCD‐related TEAEs of any grade were pyrexia and malaria. Most non‐SCD‐related TEAEs were balanced between treatment groups; only pyrexia, upper respiratory tract infection, and cough had incidence ≥ 5% higher with voxelotor than placebo (Table 3). Non‐SCD‐related serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported for 38.3% of the voxelotor group and 30.2% of the placebo group (Table 3). Few participants had a TEAE leading to study drug discontinuation (voxelotor 6/120 [5.0%], placebo 3/116 [2.6%]) (Table S8). All non‐SCD‐related TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation were also SAEs. Most non‐SCD‐related TEAEs were unrelated to study drug, with 9/120 participants (7.5%) in the voxelotor group and 4/116 (3.4%) in the placebo group having TEAEs considered drug‐related by the treating investigator (Table S9). One SAE (hyperbilirubinemia, in the voxelotor group) was considered related to study drug by the investigator.

SCD‐related TEAEs were reported for a higher proportion of the voxelotor group than the placebo group (Table 4). The difference was primarily due to TEAEs of sickle cell anemia with crisis, reported in 71/120 (59.2%) participants in the voxelotor group and 44/116 (37.9%) in the placebo group. Grade 3 SCD‐related TEAEs were reported for 35 participants (29.2%) in the voxelotor group and 19 (16.4%) in the placebo group, and Grade 4 in 1 (0.8%) and 0 participants, respectively. Three SCD‐related TEAEs were considered drug‐related by the investigator (sickle cell anemia with crisis in two participants in the voxelotor group, and dactylitis in one participant in the placebo group). When evaluated by geographic region (Table S10), no difference was observed between treatment groups in the incidence of SCD‐related TEAEs in regions outside of sub‐Saharan Africa (voxelotor: 15/23 [65.2%], placebo: 11/18 [61.1%]) while in sub‐Saharan Africa, a difference was observed (voxelotor: 56/97 [57.7%], placebo: 38/98 [38.8%]). SCD‐related TEAEs also appeared more common in partici‐pants who received concomitant antimalarial prophylaxis other than proguanil; however, a similar pattern was observed in the voxelotor and placebo arms (Table S10). For upper respiratory tract infection and malaria, the majority of infections were not associated with SCD‐related TEAEs. There was also no substantial association with pyrexia, as the majority of participants with sickle cell anemia with crisis in both treatment arms did not have pyrexia within 3 days before or after onset (voxelotor: 64/71 participants, placebo: 42/44 participants; Table S11).

SCD‐related SAEs were reported for 41.7% of the voxelotor group and 19.8% of the placebo group (Table 4); none were considered related to study drug by the investigator. Five SCD‐related TEAEs led to treatment discontinuation (all were sickle cell anemia with crisis in the voxelotor group). One participant (in the voxelotor group) had an SCD‐related TEAE leading to dose reduction and nine had SCD‐related TEAEs leading to drug interruption (five voxelotor and four placebo) (Table S8).

Ten treatment‐emergent deaths were reported: 8/120 (6.7%) in the voxelotor group and 2/116 (1.7%) in the placebo group (Table S12). In all cases, the treating investigator considered the event unrelated to study drug. The participants died between 21 and 670 days after starting the study, with most fatal cases in the voxelotor group occurring > 200 days after starting treatment. For participant characteristics, no pattern was apparent for participants' age or CBFV; one participant was receiving hydroxyurea, and all fatal events occurred in countries in sub‐Saharan Africa (Table S12). SCD‐related TEAEs, in particular sickle cell anemia with crisis, were reported at the time of six deaths in the voxelotor group, whereas SCD‐related TEAEs were not reported for either death in the placebo group. Five participants with fatal TEAEs were receiving antimalarial treatment as part of the fatal event (four voxelotor, one placebo). For three participants, malaria was included as part of the preferred term or the term reported by the investigator, and a positive malarial blood test was recorded. Of these three participants, two were on malaria prophylaxis with proguanil. Among the other participants who died, two received acute antimalarial treatment despite negative testing at the time of the fatal event. Of these, one participant had previously had malaria with a positive malaria test ~1 year before the fatal event and was receiving proguanil prophylaxis, and had recovered from a further TEAE of malaria 1 week before death from anemia/sickle cell anemia with crisis (Table S12). In addition to the treatment‐emergent deaths, there were two deaths that were not treatment‐emergent: one participant died during the screening period, having completed informed consent but before randomization, and one participant died 110 days after the dosing pause (Table S12).

Discussion

HOPE Kids 2 is the first randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study evaluating the effect of voxelotor on CBFV in participants with SCD and conditional CBFV values. The trial met its primary endpoint, with statistically significant improvement in CBFV at Week 24, although the observed magnitude of benefit was lower than the targeted effect used in the trial design. Improvements were consistent across subgroups and durable, with results sustained to Week 48. Furthermore, 29.6% of the voxelotor group versus 17.9% of the placebo group had CBFV < 170 cm/s, a cutpoint associated with lower risk of stroke, at both Weeks 24 and 48. A higher proportion of participants on the voxelotor arm had > 15 cm/s CBFV improvement, with the difference reaching statistical significance at Week 24.

During the trial, progression to an abnormal CBFV (≥ 200 cm/s), a value that markedly increases stroke risk [35], was recorded for 22 participants in the voxelotor arm and 29 in the placebo arm. It is noteworthy that there were no strokes during the study on the voxelotor arm whereas three strokes were reported on the placebo arm, although it is important to bear in mind that stroke was not precisely defined in the protocol and that one stroke was hemorrhagic in nature. Moreover, HOPE Kids 2 was not designed to assess stroke as an outcome, and further work would be needed to determine whether voxelotor reduces stroke incidence in patients with SCD and conditional CBFV.

In the placebo arm, we anticipated an increase in CBFV, based on prior reports [19], but observed CBFV improvement of −4.29 cm/s at Week 24. The reduction could not be attributed to hydroxyurea initiation or transfusions, as analyses excluded any measurements affected. However, 9% of the placebo group had hemoglobin increases > 1 g/dL at Week 24, possibly resulting from more consistent care or follow‐up while participating in a clinical trial, and these increases could partly explain the observed CBFV reductions. In addition, the natural history of CBFV in SCD is not clearly defined, particularly in patients with conditional values, with some study reports suggesting CBFV increases in most children, but others finding that CBFV decreases after 5 years of age [35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. It is therefore unclear whether a natural decrease, possibly accentuated by regression to the mean, could have contributed to the overall reduction in the placebo group, highlighting the paucity of studies in this area and the need for more CBFV research in children with SCD.

It is notable that the baseline hemoglobin in this study was nearly 1 g/dL lower than in the pivotal HOPE study and may reflect the much lower proportion receiving hydroxyurea [30]. In the voxelotor arm of HOPE Kids 2, hemoglobin increased from baseline to Weeks 24 and 48, in line with improvements previously reported for voxelotor [30, 31]. Consistent with the mechanism of action, there was an associated improvement in indirect bilirubin, a marker of hemolysis. Other markers of hemolysis (LDH and reticulocytes) were not meaningfully improved versus placebo. It is possible that the voxelotor dose was insufficient to reduce hemolysis adequately to impact LDH and reticulocyte counts, yet we also observed large variability in LDH and reticulocyte changes, which may have been partly due to the use of two central laboratories, and the well‐known challenges of inconsistent measurements in children.

The mechanism of action of voxelotor has generated concerns regarding the potential for hindered oxygen offloading and/or that the benefit of hemoglobin improvement is secondary to a hypoxia response. If this were the case, hemoglobin improvements in HOPE Kids 2 would not have been expected to improve CBFV and potentially would have worsened it. Instead, the observed statistically significant improvement in CBFV provides reassurance that voxelotor treatment is not associated with hindered oxygen offloading.

In HOPE Kids 2, acute pain events were assessed in two ways. Firstly, VOCs were defined as a composite of acute painful crisis and/or acute chest syndrome, and to document a VOC, investigators confirmed the participant had moderate‐to‐severe pain that had no other explanation, lasted ≥ 2 h, and required analgesics prescribed by a healthcare professional. During the trial, the incidence of VOCs was low in both treatment groups but was significantly higher with voxelotor. This finding was unexpected given that previous studies have shown no increase, yet post hoc analyses suggested that baseline differences between treatment groups would not necessarily fully explain the difference in on‐study rates.

Acute pain events were also assessed as TEAEs, specifically SCD‐related TEAEs reported by treating investigators as sickle cell anemia with crisis. A safety signal was observed, with a notable increase with voxelotor: the placebo group in HOPE Kids 2 had nearly twofold lower incidence than the voxelotor group. Again, this contrasts with the pivotal HOPE study, which showed no increase in SCD‐related TEAEs with voxelotor versus placebo [30]. Although the between‐group baseline differences in propensity for acute pain events may have contributed, these results are concerning and require further evaluation. No clear explanation for the imbalance in SCD events was determined but the increase in acute pain events in this population of patients with SCD could suggest that voxelotor lacks the ability to adequately reduce hemolysis‐driven inflammation, a hypothesis potentially supported by the lack of improvement in LDH and reticulocyte count.

An imbalance in fatal events was observed in HOPE Kids 2, with eight deaths in the voxelotor arm (6.7%) and two in the placebo arm (1.7%). The observed imbalance raises concerns and, together with voxelotor data that emerged concurrently from the RESOLVE study, contributed to the trial Sponsor's decision to pause study drug administration and subsequently to discontinue the study. Evaluation of fatal events did not identify any unexpected causes of death attributable to voxelotor: in each case, fatal events were consistent with known causes of death in people with SCD or the region where the fatal event occurred (e.g., malaria). Furthermore, the only commonality among all deaths was participant location in sub‐Saharan Africa, specifically Nigeria or Kenya. It could be speculated that trial location may have an effect on observed mortality, however, no clear mechanism has been identified to explain a regional effect of voxelotor on mortality and the cause of the imbalance in deaths between groups remains unclear based on available evidence.

In conclusion, this study is the first to show that voxelotor treatment, like hydroxyurea, is able to reduce CBFV in children with SCD and conditional CBFV. These data support the hypothesis that voxelotor‐mediated increase in hemoglobin can be associated with improvements in CBF and provide evidence to allay concerns about hindered oxygen offloading. Acute pain events were imbalanced between the treatment arms, and it is possible that the level of hemolysis reduction by voxelotor in this study was insufficient to improve pain rates and possibly even led to increased frequency compared with placebo. Lastly, the imbalance in mortality between the study groups raises concerns about the impact of voxelotor on these events, which is currently unexplained. With all deaths occurring in sub‐Saharan Africa and a similar trend not seen in voxelotor studies conducted outside this region, the impact of multiple factors associated with geographical differences on the observed death imbalance requires further study. Thus, the benefit of voxelotor on the cerebrovascular system needs to be balanced with further insight regarding the safety signals that emerged during this study.
