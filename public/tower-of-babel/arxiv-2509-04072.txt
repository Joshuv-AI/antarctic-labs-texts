LibriQuote: A Speech Dataset of Fictional Character Utterances for Expressive Zero-Shot Speech Synthesis
Gaspard Michel, Elena V. Epure, Christophe Cerisara

Abstract
Text-to-speech (TTS) systems have recently achieved more expressive and natural speech synthesis by scaling to large speech datasets. However, the proportion of expressive speech in such large-scale corpora is often unclear. Besides, existing expressive speech corpora are typically smaller in scale and primarily used for benchmarking TTS systems. In this paper, we introduce the LibriQuote dataset, an English corpus derived from read audiobooks, designed for both fine-tuning and benchmarking expressive zero-shot TTS system. The training dataset includes 12.7K hours of read, non-expressive speech and 5.3K hours of mostly expressive speech drawn from character quotations. Each utterance in the expressive subset is supplemented with the context in which it was written, along with pseudo-labels of speech verbs and adverbs used to describe the quotation ( e.g. “he whispered softly” ). Additionally, we provide a challenging 7.5 hour test set intended for benchmarking TTS systems: given a neutral reference speech as input, we evaluate system’s ability to synthesize an expressive utterance while preserving reference timbre. We validate qualitatively the test set by showing that it covers a wide range of emotions compared to non-expressive speech, along with various accents. Extensive subjective and objective evaluations show that fine-tuning a baseline TTS system on LibriQuote significantly improves its synthesized speech intelligibility, and that recent systems fail to synthesize speech as expressive and natural as the ground-truth utterances. The dataset and evaluation code are freely available. Audio samples can be found at https://libriquote.github.io/ .

Introduction

Recently, text-to-speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved by scaling to large-scale, multi-domain speech corpora
(
Shen et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Kharitonov et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023a
;
Anastassiou et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
, exhibiting remarkable speech naturalness, quality and voice following abilities.
These large-scale corpora typically contain speech derived from audiobooks
(
Kahn et al. 2019
;
Pratap et al. 2020
;
Kang et al. 2024
)
or in-the-wild data
(
He et al. 2024
)
.
While in-the-wild speech may include diverse forms of expressivity and speaking style, it is sometimes believed that audiobook speech corpora may lack such expressiveness
(
He et al. 2024
)
.
In contrast, we claim that human reading in audiobooks, especially readings of fictional works, offer extensive prosodic variability, as they often involve an alternation between neutral, non-expressive speech and expressive, sometimes emotional, utterances.
Neutral speech usually corresponds in a book to narration parts, these story fragments enunciated by the narrator – may it be a first or third person view, or free indirect speech – while expressive speech is employed during dialogues between fictional characters.
Dialogue is an essential narrative function
(
Genette 1983
)
that allows fictional character to portray themselves, expressing aspects of their personality, emotional states, and their own representation of the fictional world.
As the story unfolds, characters navigate dynamically through their own emotion arc
(
Vishnubhotla et al. 2024
)
, which is often portrayed during audiobook reading.
Indeed, numerous blog posts dedicated to amateurs and written by professional narrators emphasize the importance of
impersonation
, or the act of bringing life to fictional characters
(
Gonzalez 2021
;
Browm 2021
;
Goodwin 2024
)
.

In this work, we put fictional characters at the forefront, and propose LibriQuote, a large-scale dataset of expressive utterances derived from LibriVox

Notably, LibriQuote differs from standard large-scale audiobook datasets

;

;
Kang et al. 2024
)
by segmenting speech utterances in two ways: character utterances that involve expressive and diverse spech, and narration parts that correspond to a neutral, formal reading style.
In addition, we provide as side information the context in which each character utterance appears, supplemented with pseudo-labels of potential speech verbs and adverbs used to describe the utterance (
e.g.
‘‘he whispered softly’’), automatically annotated and further validated by humans.
These pseudo-labels provide rich information that we further exploit to construct an expressive data subset.
Through an extensive alignment pipeline involving audio preparation and transcription from LibriVox, text matching from Project Gutenberg

https://www.gutenberg.org/
and quotation detection
(

Qualitative analysis of the data reveals that LibriQuote contains a large set of emotions and accents, facilitating the training and benchmarking of expressive TTS systems, as depicted in Figure

Our objective and subjective experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of LibriQuote.
When evaluated on our test set, modern open-source TTS systems fail to synthesize utterances as expressive and as natural as the ground-truth quotations, suggesting that further research should be conducted to build more expressive systems.
When used as a fine-tuning corpora, LibriQuote-
train

Besides, we derive an expressive data subset using pseudo-labels of speech verbs and adverbs, which we show is a data-efficient alternative to the full LibriQuote-
train

Our contribution are as follows:

•

We release publicly LibriQuote, a dataset derived from audiobooks dedicated to zero-shot TTS. LibriQuote comes with standard train/dev/test splits. LibriQuote-
train
comprises 5.3K hours of training quotations, as well as 12.7K hours of training narration segments, while LibriQuote-

•

We release publicly the code and evaluation samples used in objective and subjective experiments to encourage benchmarking on LibriQuote-

.

•

We show through extensive objective and subjective evaluations that modern TTS systems fail to synthesize utterances as expressive and as natural as in real audiobooks.

•

We show that fine-tuning a baseline TTS system on LibriQuote-

Existing Datasets

We briefly review the composition and characteristics of existing datasets used for zero-shot speech synthesis.

Audiobook-based Speech Datasets

Audiobooks have traditionally been the de-facto open-source data available for training TTS systems.
The LibriVox project records thousands of audiobooks derived from public-domain books, read by volunteered speakers.
LibriSpeech
(
Panayotov et al. 2015
)
, a 1000-hours corpus of English speech data extracted from LibriVox that covers 2400 speakers, has been extensively used in the literature to train and benchmark Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and TTS systems.
LibriTTS
(
Zen et al. 2019
)
provides an enhanced version of LibriSpeech, with better audio quality and utterance segmentation, but supports a smaller amount of training data, and LibriSpeech-PC
(
Meister et al. 2023
)
restored the punctuation and capitalization of LibriSpeech audio transcripts.
Libri-Light
(
Kahn et al. 2019
)
is the largest English audiobook corpus of open data, with approximately 60,000 hours of English speech derived from LibriVox, but does not provide audio transcriptions, making it unpractical to train supervised ASR or classic TTS systems.
The MLS datasest
(
Pratap et al. 2020
)
is composed of 50,000 hours of multilingual audiobooks and proposes audio transcriptions that were aligned with the original text.
LibriHeavy
(
Kang et al. 2024
)
improved Libri-Light by aligning audio segments derived from recordings and their text counterpart in the book from which the segment was taken, but do not provide book contextual information in the vicinity of audio segments.
These 30-seconds audio segments are created at sentence boundaries, thus they may contain multiple quotations and narration parts, alternating between neutral (narration) and expressive (quotation) speech.
Because of this alternation patterns, complex pitch distributions arise in each training instance, which may hinder the learning of TTS systems.
In contrast, we extract speech from a subset of LibriVox, Fiction Novels, separating training instances between neutral (narration) and expressive (quotation) segments, preserving contextual information around quotations.
In fiction, quotations from fictional characters is an essential communicative medium between the reader and the characters, revealing their emotions and personality through dialogue.

Expressive Speech Datasets

Audiobook narration typically alternates between neutral, narrative speech and expressive speech during character acting.

Székely et al. 2012
design a system to capture expressive speech utterances from audiobooks, and validate their approach on a single Hungarian audiobook.
To the best of our knowledge, no work have focused on capturing expressive audio segments in audiobooks at a large scale.
However, several efforts towards the creation of expressive read speech corpora have been made. Emotional speech datasets
(
Busso et al. 2008
;
Cao et al. 2014
;
Livingstone and Russo 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2021
)
that assign discrete emotion labels to speech utterances have been extensively used to evaluate emotional synthesize of TTS systems
(
Jiang et al. 2023b
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.
The L2-ARCTIC corpus
(
Zhao et al. 2018
)
proposes an 11 hour corpus with ten speakers, spanning 5 different accents.
EXPRESSO
(
Nguyen et al. 2023
)
introduces an high-quality dataset of read and improvised speech across 26 expressive styles.
In LibriQuote, we create audio segments that match character utterances and which are thus likely to be more expressive than narration parts.
Besides, we extract narrative information indicating
how
an utterance should be spoken, providing a natural language pseudo-label of expressivity.

LibriQuote Dataset

This section describes the steps involved in preparing LibriQuote, followed by descriptive statistics. Then we present our methodology to extract narrative information for each utterance. Figure
1
provides an overview of the full process.

Dataset Construction

We mainly follow the recipe of LibriHeavy
(
Kang et al. 2024
)
to align audio files downloaded from LibriVox to their original text.
However, we filter out books that are unlikely to contain fictional characters, such as bibliographical work or other type of non-fictional works.
We describe the filtering and alignment stages below.

Audio Preparation

We start by browsing the LibriVox API
3
3

3

https://librivox.org/api/info
to collect recording information.
We only extract English recordings corresponding to Fiction books.
This step is necessary to filter out non-fiction books that are unlikely to contain fictional characters.
Each recording is downsampled to 16kHz for further processing, but we provide links to download the original audio files as well, allowing LibriQuote to support systems operating on higher audio quality.
Note that LibriVox also contains
Dramatic Readings
, which are recordings where each fictional character is acted by a unique speaker.
We also applied the alignment process described below to these recordings, but do not include them in LibriQuote as they would require an additional speaker diarization step.
We plan to release these alignments in future works.

Text Preparation

For each audio recording, we download the corresponding book text from Project Gutenberg.
Some books have different text providers, but we found by manual inspection that automatically
downloading from these websites was either too complicated, or resulted in texts with very poor quality often due to OCR errors.
In such cases, we browse Project Gutenberg to search for the target book, and discard it if not available on the platform.
We then perform quotation detection using BookNLP
4
4

4

https://github.com/booknlp/booknlp/blob/main/booknlp/english/litbank˙quote.py
on each book.
We discard every book containing less than
20
quotations, as we found that these book were typically showing quotation detection errors.
For other books, we keep quotation positions in the original text for further alignments.

Audio Transcription

We start by segmenting each audio recording into 30-second segments, with a 2 second overlap at each side.
These segments are then transcribed with a Zipformer-Transducer ASR model trained on LibriSpeech
5
5

5

https://github.com/k2-fsa/icefall/pull/1058
, and combined by leveraging word-level timestamps to obtain a full timestamped transcript of the recording.

Text-Audio Alignment

Since audio recordings often involve a full chapter, this step is necessary to find the corresponding chapter text within the full original book, and to have fine-grained alignment at the word level.
This alignment process involves two stages.
In the first stage,
close matches
between a transcription and the book text are constructed.
These
close matches
correspond to
(i,j)
pairs where
i
is an index in the transcription and
j
an index in the book text.
Then, a coarse alignment is produced by taking the longest chain of pairs
(i_{1},j_{1}),\dots,(i_{N},j_{N}),
such that
i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{N}
and
j_{1}\leq\dots\leq j_{N}
.
The second stage produces a final alignment by concatenating Levenshtein alignments
(
Lcvenshtcin 1966
)
between the transcription and the text segment produced by the longest chain of pairs.
We refer the reader to
Kang et al. 2024
for additional information regarding the alignment process.

Quotation Alignment

The output of the text-audio alignment is an array of words in the original text, timestamped in the audio recording.
We leverage the timestamps and the output of the quotation detection system to segment each audio into a set of read quotations.
Additionally, we build audio segments from narration paragraphs (
i.e.
parts that are not quotations) in each book.
The final construction stage involved assigning contextual information around quotations.
Based on the alignments, we match each quotation with a window of around
100
words occurring before and after it, with boundaries defined by paragraphs.
With LibriQuote, we release the preprocessed texts as well as full paragraph and quotation alignments.
Thus, it is possible to derive a contextual window of any desirable length around quotations.

Dev & Test Sets

We designed the development and test sets to reflect the following challenge in expressive TTS:
given a reference timbre from neutral speech and the text to generate, can we synthesize a quotation with the correct level of expressiveness?

Each quotation serves as the expressive speech, and we match them with the nearest narration utterance from the book context that acts as the neutral reference speech.
Without the context, the task can be particularly hard as the quotation text itself
may be misleading with regard to the true emotion, which may rather be specified in the context.
As an example, the exclamation mark in the quotation
“It’s so good!” he whispered softly
might suggest it shall be uttered with a loud voice, but the narrative information clarifies that it is actually being whispered.
Therefore, we also provide contextual information in the vicinity of quotations to encourage further research on predicting expressivity from textual context for audiobook synthesis.

The development dataset contains randomly selected utterances from speakers and books in the train set.
It thus does not encompass zero-shot speech synthesis, but aims at evaluating expressive synthesis from neutral speech for seen speakers.
The test set contains 8 male and 7 female unseen speakers, for a total of 7.5 quotation hours.
We ensure no speaker overlap with LibriSpeech
train
and
dev
speakers.

Distinguishing between ”story” (what is happening) and ”discourse” (how it is told) is a key aspect of narrative understanding.
NLP research has extensively focused on various aspects of
narrative discourse
, as theorized by
Genette 1983
.
Recently, a variety of works explored the usage of Large Language Models (LLMs) to model elements of narrative understanding, such as dialogue
(
Michel et al. 2025
)
, character roles and information extraction
(
Stammbach, Antoniak, and Ash 2022
;
Gurung and Lapata 2024
)
, narrativity
(
Hatzel and Biemann 2024
)
or structure
(
Soni et al. 2023
)
.

The current work is grounded in the perspectival issues of fictional characters, including point of view and dialogues, anchored in the
voice
linking function of Genette’s theoretical framework.
In fiction, utterances are sometimes supplemented with
narrative information
in the form of speech verbs and adverbs (
e.g. “he whispered softly”
).
These indicators have been shown to produce positive effects during reading, such as enhancing reading comprehension
(
Wolters, Kim, and Szura 2022
)
or character identification
(
Van Krieken, Hoeken, and Sanders 2017
)
.
For audiobook narrators, they are a valuable resource to set the tone and point of view of the character.
Other contextual information such as the emotional context of a character are likely to also impact speech delivery, but are often prone to subjective interpretation and are harder to automatically infer
(
Vishnubhotla et al. 2024
)
.
Thus, by extracting these narrative cues and filtering out less expressive utterances (
e.g. “she said”
), we can derive a high-quality subset that spans a broad prosodic range.

Method

LLMs have shown promising results in literary dialogue and point of view understanding
(
Piper and Bagga 2024
;
Michel et al. 2025
)
.
Our experiments involved prompting
Phi-4
8
8

8

https://huggingface.co/microsoft/phi-4
to extract
narrative information
for a target quotation in a contextual window of one paragraph before and after the quotation.
We use few-shot prompting with 5 examples.
Prompt details can be found in the Technical Appendix.
To mitigate extraction errors and keep only informative narrative content, we replace all quotations in context with special markers, such that only narration and structure remains.
Besides, we experimented with prompting the LLM to self-report a
confidence score
on a 1-to-10 scale that we used to prune-out unconfident predictions in order to maximize precision (Phi-4
{}_{\text{conf}}
).

Validation

In an initial phase developed to setup guidelines and discussing complex cases, two annotators processed independently 100 quotations.
After discussion and once an agreement was reached, a single annotator continued 300 extra instances, resulting in 400 quotations annotated with
verbs
,
adverbs
,
nouns
and
adjectives
.
We provide in Table
2
the results for verbs and adverbs with Phi-4.
Results for other LLMs, nouns and adjectives and runtimes can be found in the Technical Appendix.
We see that Phi-4 performs relatively well at extracting the narrative information, but still suffers from hallucinations – in particular for adverbs – as shown by the precision score.
When pruning out all predictions with a confidence score lower than 10, Phi-4
{}_{\text{conf}}
excels in terms of precision at the cost of lower recall for both verbs and adverbs.
A high precision is desirable: we want to avoid quotations to be wrongly affected with expressive speech verbs and adverbs.

Filtering

We extract narrative information on LibriQuote-
train
using Phi-4
{}_{\text{conf}}
, yielding a (potentially empty) set of predicted verbs and adverbs for each quotation.
To produce a filtered subset
\mathbf{Q}_{f}
, we first include all quotations that had a non-empty adverb pseudo-label regardless of the predicted speech verb, as adverbs often give precise information of how a quotation is uttered (
e.g.
“he said cautiously”).
Then, based on a manually defined set of
expressive
speech verbs
9
9

9

We kept 89 out of 201 speech verbs from the following list:
https://archiewahwah.wordpress.com/speech-verbs-list/
, we include all quotations (which were not kept in the first step) that have a verb falling into that list.
As a result,
\mathbf{Q}_{f}
, contains 377,776 quotations (11% of the full quotation train set) for a total of 379 hours.
We believe
\mathbf{Q}_{f}
can be used for data-efficiency, or can foster analysis on the alignment between written narrative information and uttered speech.

Experiments

In this section, we describe our main LibriQuote experiments and further evaluations on benchmark datasets.

LibriQuote Test Set

Baselines

To evaluate to which extent state-of-the-art TTS systems are able to synthetize expressive speech, we compare three TTS systems: SparkTTS
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, F5-TTS
(
Chen et al. 2024
)

and MaskGCT
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
.
These models were chosen based on their availability and performance on standard benchmarks.
SparkTTS is an autoregressive TTS model based on Qwen2-0.5B.
It generates global and semantic discrete tokens derived from BiCodec
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, that are then converted back to raw audio.
Global tokens capture speaker information such as timbre, while semantic tokens capture fine-grained semantic information.
F5-TTS is a non-autoregressive flow matching TTS system based on Diffusion Transformer
(
Peebles and Xie 2023a
)
.
MaskGCT is a non-autoregressive TTS system, that follows a
mask-and-predict
paradigm.
It follows a two-stage process: semantic tokens derived from a VQ-VAE model with Wav2Vec-Bert 2.0 features are predicted in a first step, and then used to condition the prediction of acoustic tokens.
They were all trained with approximately 100K hours of speech data.
We use publicly available checkpoints for each model and do not modify generation parameters.
Baselines details are available in the Technical Appendix.

Metrics

Our evaluation follows the
cross-sentence
design
(
Le et al. 2023
)
: a narration utterance of 2 to 15 seconds is used as audio context to guide the generation of a quotation utterance.
For objective metrics, we report Word Error Rate (WER) computed with Whisper-large-v3
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
to measure speech intelligibility and speaker similarity between the synthesized speech and the original ground-truth speech (SIM-O). We employ a WavLM-large based speaker verification model
(
Chen et al. 2022
)
to extract speaker embeddings and calculate cosine similarities.
We evaluate prosody similarity with the ground truth quotation using Mel Cepstral Distortion (MCD) and F0 Pearson correlation (FPC) following
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
.
We also evaluate emotion and accent similarity (E-Sim, A-Sim) by leveraging representations from dedicated pretrained models: Emotion2Vec
(
Ma et al. 2024
)
and an ECAPA-TDNN model trained on Common Voice
(
Desplanques, Thienpondt, and Demuynck 2020
;
Ardila et al. 2020
;
Zuluaga-Gomez et al. 2023
)
.
Similarity scores are calculated by computing the cosine similarity between the ground-truth and synthesized speech representations.
For subjective metrics, we employ Mean Opinion Score (MOS, rated from 1 to 5 with 0.5 intervals) to assess naturalness, and Comparative MOS (CMOS, rated from -3 to 3) to measure the degree of expressivity with respect to the ground-truth.
We randomly selected 2 samples per speaker in LibriQuote-
test
, for a total of 30 samples.
Each sample was judged by 5 raters.
More details can be found in the Technical Appendix.

Analysis

As shown in Table
3
, the ground-truth WER is relatively high compared to standard audiobooks datasets such as LibriSpeech.
We believe this high WER is likely due to an higher expressiveness in our test samples.
SparkTTS achieves the lowest WER (5.5) across models, but the lowest SIM-O.
In contrast, MaskGCT achieves the highest WER and SIM-O.
F5-TTS and MackGCT synthesize speech with higher accent and emotion similarity than SparkTTS.
However, in terms of prosody similarity, SparkTTS performs better, with an higher FPC and lower MCD.
The subjective evaluation reveals that all models fail to synthesize speech as expressive as the ground-truth utterances: the latter were consistently preferred over synthesized speech in the CMOS experiment.
In addition, ground-truth quotations appear more natural than generated speech, with a MOS of 3.58, while F5-TTS achieves the best MOS of 3.47.
These results suggest that the
cross-sentence
task of LibriQuote-
test
is challenging, even for modern TTS systems.

Experiments

We evaluate how

Conclusion

This work introduces LibriQuote, a publicly available speech dataset derived from audiobooks, designed for training and benchmarking expressive zero-shot TTS systems.
The LibriQuote training set contains 12,720 hours of neutral, non-expressive narration utterances and 5359 hours of more expressive speech in the form of quotations from fictional characters in books, while the test set comprises 7.5 hours of quotations from 15 speakers.
Our qualitative analysis reveal that LibriQuote-
test
covers a large set of emotions and accents.
Additionally, we supplement each utterance with the book context in which it was written and provide pseudo-labels for the speech verbs and adverbs used to introduce each quotation.
Objective and subjective evaluations show that the TTS systems we evaluated fail to synthesize speech as expressive as the ground-truth utterances and that LibriQuote-
train
improves speech intelligibility when used as a fine-tuning corpus.
We believe that the release of this dataset can foster research on expressive TTS and on contextual conditioning for audiobook synthesis.

Limitations

This work bears several limitations.
First, we did not filter LibriQuote utterances that exhibit very high WER between the transcript and ground-truth text as done in previous work.
Thus, LibriQuote might contain a small number of outliers, which can hinder learning of ASR or TTS systems.
We plan to perform WER-based filtering in a future dataset release.
Second, our objective analysis of expressiveness remains limited, with metrics such as FPC and MCD that assume a ground-truth sample is deterministic, hence penalizing diversity in generation.
We believe subjective listening tests are more suited for evaluation of expressiveness by providing human ratings.
Third, our training experiments were solely conducted with SparkTTS, an autoregressive TTS system which bears its own limitations (such as low speaker similarity), and did not involve other system types.
However, we believe that these baseline system results are still a valuable source of information.
Finally, we experimented with narrative context conditioning in utterance synthesis, but we believe further research should be conducted on how TTS systems may benefit from it.

Ethical Considerations

This work is purely dedicated to research projects.
While TTS models continue bridging the gap towards human naturalness and expressiveness, numerous dangerous applications arise, such as voice spoofing, or the forced replacement of talented voice actors by AI narrators.
Therefore, we believe it is of paramount importance to ensure responsible and ethical applications of TTS.

The human study involved participants recruited on a voluntarily basis from our organization, and we ensure each listening test was completed within working hours.

Quotations and Pitch Variation

Audiobook narrators typically alternate between neutral utterances of narration, and expressive utterances in the form of character quotations.
Sometimes, they may even completely incarnate a character: for instance, a male speaker might raise his pitch to quote a small female child character.
This pitch variation reflects in datasets such as LibriHeavy: audio utterances are typically built by taking around 30 seconds of audio, randomly segmenting at sentence boundaries.
Hence, they might contain a various number of quotations, leading to very complex pitch distributions.
As a result, Text-to-speech (TTS) systems trained on such audio segments might be hindered by being trained to reproduce these complex distributions without access to meaningful information, such as the fact that a raise in pitch might be favored by the impersonation of a little girl.

To illustrate this effect, we used the
small

We found that 25% of audio segments contained 1 to 12 quotations.
Then, we calculate pitch standard deviation using Parselmouth

The results are displayed in Figure

We see that pitch distributions are typically more spread when the number of quotations in the segments increases.
This result is supported by looking at Spearman

In comparison, LibriQuote-

Details - Baselines

In this section, we describe in more details the baselines used in our experiments.

SparkTTS

is an autoregrssive TTS system that builds upon Qwen2.5-0.5M.
It also uses BiCodec, a speech codec model that transforms 16kHz sampling rate speech into two types of discrete tokens: global and semantic tokens.
Global tokens are derived from representations of an ECAPA-TDNN model fine-tuned for speaker-verification, hence capturing speaker-related information such as timbre.
The ECAPA-TDNN representations are mapped to a set of 32 hidden features using learned laten queries, which are then discretized using Finite Scalar Quantization
(
Mentzer et al. 2023
)
.
Semantic tokens are derived from representations of the 11th, 14th and 16th layers of Wav2Vec 2.0 (XLSR-53)
(
Pasad, Shi, and Livescu 2023
)
.
These features are then quantized using single-codebook vector quantization, using factorized codes as done in DAC
(
Kumar et al. 2023a
)
.
Tokens are converted back to raw audio using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) Decoder composed of ConvNeXt blocks.
The language modeling framework is used to learn to generate semantic tokens, conditioned on the text-to-synthesis tokens and global tokens (which are prepended in the autoregressive sequence).

Figure 5:
t-SNE projection of accent representations of LibriQuote-
test
quotations.

F5-TTS

is a non-autoregressive diffusion TTS system based on Flow Matching that does not rely on phonetic alignment nor duration predictor, operating on 24kHz sampling rate speech.
During training, following the text-guided speech infilling task, speech mel spectrograms are first masked and then concatenated with text tokens.
The resulting sequence is inputted to a Diffusion Transformer (DiT)
(
Peebles and Xie 2023b
)
, which learns to generate the masked spectrogram region from gaussian noise using Flow Matching.
During inference, the reference speech mel spectogram serves as the unmasked region, and is concatenated with the text to synthesize the input sequence.
Sway sampling is used along with an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) solver to generate mel spectrograms that are converted back using Vocos
(
Siuzdak 2024
)
.

MaskGCT

is also a non-autoregressive TTS system, that follows a two-step masked generative modeling paradigm.
It leverages two speech codec models to produce semantic and acoustinc discrete units from 24kHz sampling rate speech.
Semantic tokens are derived from representations of the 17th layer of W2v-BERT 2.0, that are converted to discrete units using a single codebook of size 8,192 via classic Vector Quantization (VQ).
Acoustic tokens are produced following RVQGAN
(
Kumar et al. 2023b
)
, and 8 codebooks of size 8,192 are used via Residual VQ (RVQ).
To generate raw audio, MaskGCT follows a two-step process: first, it uses a Transformer to generate semantic tokens by iteratively predicting masked tokens conditioned on the text to synthesize, prompt tokens and previously generated semantic tokens ; second acoustic tokens are generated following a similar iterative procedure, but using an architecture similar to SoundStorm
(
Borsos et al. 2023
)
, and the generative process is conditioned with the prompt acoustic tokens and previously predicted semantic tokens.
Finally, predicted acoustic tokens are converted back to raw audio using Vocos.
Additionally, MaskGCT employs a Flow-Matching duration predictor to predict the target speech duration during inference.
