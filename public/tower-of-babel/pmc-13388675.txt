Early hexapod genomes reveal the deep origin of insect gustatory and odorant receptors
Authors: Robert F Mitchell, Kevin M Moran, Duane D McKenna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsect smell and taste is primarily mediated through a chemoreceptor superfamily that includes three lineages of gustatory receptors—sugar, carbon dioxide, and bitter—and a distinct lineage of odorant receptors. Although sugar gustatory receptors can be traced back to ancestral crustaceans, the evolutionary origins of the remaining lineages have been unclear. Here, we resolve this history through homology-driven searches and phylogenetic analyses across insect and non-insect hexapod genomes. We uncover several previously unrecognized gustatory receptor clades, including a fifth insect chemoreceptor lineage that we term relict gustatory receptors, which expanded in the non-insect hexapod order Diplura but dwindled in extant insects. We also identify a gustatory receptor lineage in non-insect hexapods that is sister to insect odorant receptors, and which provides strong evidence that odorant receptors arose from within the sugar gustatory receptors. These findings clarify relationships among the insect gustatory and odorant receptor lineages and illuminate the origins and subsequent diversification of chemoreception across Hexapoda.Subject terms: Molecular evolution, Chemical ecology, Entomology

Introduction

Insects navigate their environment primarily through chemical cues1, which necessitates a sophisticated and expansive chemosensory system composed of vast arrays of chemoreceptor proteins2. Chemoreceptors are among the largest gene families in insects3, and they evolve rapidly via a birth-and-death model3,4 that yields highly divergent repertoires across insect taxa5–7. These receptors largely fall into two ancient radiations that date at least to the earliest animals: the ionotropic receptors (IRs8) and the chemoreceptor superfamily (CRS2,9,10), the latter of which includes the families of insect gustatory receptors (GRs) and odorant receptors (ORs). IRs are associated with multiple sensory modalities, including chemoreception11, hygroreception12, and thermoreception13, while the CRS derives from a larger clade of seven transmembrane domain ion channels14, and appears to mediate most chemoreception in insects, including both gustation and olfaction2.

Four lineages of the CRS are recognized within insects: three referred to as GRs (the sugar GRs, bitter GRs, and carbon dioxide GRs), and a fourth of ORs2. The sugar GRs are solely associated with the detection of sugars15,16, while bitter GRs include receptors for plant secondary compounds17,18, as well as fructose19 and cuticular hydrocarbons20. The carbon dioxide (CO2) GRs comprise only 2–3 copies in many holometabolous insects21, where they mediate the detection of gaseous carbon dioxide22, but expansions of this lineage in other insect orders hint at additional olfactory or gustatory functions23,24. ORs are the primary means of olfaction, and they have radiated extensively within insects, mediating diverse ecological interactions including the detection of host volatiles and pheromones25–28.

Sugar GRs have been traced to crustaceans29, but the various putative clades of bitter GRs identified from early-divergent hexapods and crustaceans are of uncertain relation to those of insects29,30, while CO2 GRs are currently known only from winged insects (Pterygota)30–32. ORs are similarly restricted to Insecta, despite intensive surveys of crustacean and hexapod genomes29,30,32–35. They share structural hallmarks with GRs, including a specific distribution of seven transmembrane domains, an inverted topology relative to GPCRs, and a conserved motif in the C-terminus2. However, the phylogenetic placement and evolutionary origin of ORs have remained unclear, with the lineage appearing abruptly in early-divergent insects (Archaeognatha) before radiating extensively in the silverfish (Zygentoma) and other insects33,34.

To resolve these uncertainties, we manually annotated chemoreceptors from several early-divergent hexapod and insect genomes. Phylogenetic analyses revealed multiple novel lineages of GRs and established a clear progression of chemosensory evolution from early hexapods to insects. One clade—provisionally termed the relict GRs—is extraordinarily expanded in the hexapod order Diplura and remains present but diminished in many insect orders. Finally, we identified a lineage of GRs in non-insect hexapods that we propose are the precursors of insect ORs.

Results

A novel and conserved lineage of GRs spans the insect tree of life

Research on antennal transcriptomes from longhorned beetles (Cerambycidae) revealed a highly divergent GR absent from previous annotations of the family36. We identified homologous genes across multiple insect orders, typically one per genome in Holometabola, and as small, divergent radiations in the older insect groups Paleoptera and Polyneoptera (Fig. 1). Initial phylogenies suggested that this lineage was ancient and distinct from the recognized subfamilies of the insect CRS (ORs + sugar, bitter, and CO2 GRs), supporting the existence of a fifth insect CRS lineage, which we have termed the “relict GRs”.

Relict GRs exhibited a patchy distribution, and they were entirely absent, or nearly absent, from some insect orders (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1). Most notably, relict GRs appeared to be absent from the megadiverse orders of flies (Diptera), moths (Lepidoptera), and wasps (Hymenoptera), except for mandibulate moths (Micropterigidae) and sawflies (Pamphiliidae, Xiphydriidae). The distribution was also highly unpredictable; for instance, in the order Psocodea, relict GRs were only recovered within the booklouse family Liposcelididae (deeply nested in Psocodea and sister to the parasitic lice37), a situation that must have arisen through rapid lineage sorting, multiple instances of loss, or some mix thereof.

We further explored the origin of relict GRs by conducting an intensive manual annotation of GRs from non-insect hexapods and early-divergent insects, including the non-insect hexapod order Diplura and the early-divergent insect orders Archaeognatha and Zygentoma38. A previous effort to annotate the dipluran Campodea augens (Diplura: Campodeidae) revealed an extraordinary radiation of 2431 IRs, but a very small set of 41 GRs, with only 13 sugar GRs recognizable as a known lineage in insects30. We instead annotated the genome of the dipluran Catajapyx aquilonaris (Diplura: Japygidae)39 and identified 547 GRs, including 517 GRs (plus 9 additional splice isoforms) related to the novel insect lineage (CaquGR31–547; Table 1 and Supplementary Data 2).

We next identified 154 GRs from the genome of the jumping bristletail Machilis hrabei (Archaeognatha: Machilidae) (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 3), which included at least two relict GRs (MhraGR43–44) and representatives of the sugar (28 GRs; MhraGR2–29), CO2 (13 GRs; MhraGR30–42), and bitter (110 GRs; MhraGR45–154) GR lineages, and we confirmed the presence of 5 ORs (MhraOR1–533). Of those lineages, only sugar GRs were also observed in C. aquilonaris (10 GRs; CaquGR1–10). Annotation of M. hrabei proved difficult due to assembly gaps, and based on orphaned exons, we estimate that >100 GRs remain to be annotated. However, these fragments largely appear to be paralogs of our annotated models of bitter GRs.

Silverfish and firebrats (Zygentoma) are hypothesized to be the first branch of insects subsequent to Archaeognatha38. They possess a complete insect OR system, i.e., one that includes a radiation of ligand-binding ORs and the specialized OR co-receptor, Orco, of which a single copy is retained in all other insects33,34. We identified 112 GRs + 127 splice isoforms from the genome of the firebrat Thermobia domestica (Zygentoma: Lepismatidae) (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 4), including representatives of sugar (2 GRs; TdomGR1–2), CO2 (16 GRs; TdomGR3–18 + 13 splice isoforms), and bitter (94 GRs; TdomGR19–112 + 112 splice isoforms). We confirmed the previous OR annotation33, but we corrected and slightly expanded this repertoire by adding four additional ORs (TdomOR32-35) and two additional splice isoforms, and we formalized the proposed alternative splice of the Orco gene33 (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 5). Relict GRs were absent from the genome of T. domestica, but we annotated a single, partial model from the genome of the related silverfish Lepisma saccharinum (LsacGR1; Supplementary Data 6). Thus, we speculate that the absence of relict GRs from T. domestica is a secondary loss.

Relict GRs radiated to an extreme in C. aquilonaris, and BLAST searches (unpub. data) indicate a similarly large radiation was present but overlooked in C. augens. These two species represent two major divisions of Diplura (Japygoidea and Campodeoidea, respectively40), so it is likely that relict GRs constitute the bulk of the chemoreceptor superfamily in most dipluran species. It is possible that relict GRs approximate the function of bitter GRs in Diplura, given their inverse relationship in abundance. Relict GRs continued into Insecta but diminished rapidly after Archaeognatha, and the unpredictable recovery of relict GRs throughout the remainder of Insecta suggests a conserved and important, though not irreplaceable, function for the remaining genes.

Phylogenetic analyses confirmed the distinctiveness of relict GRs and their evolutionary relationship with bitter and CO2 GRs (Fig. 2). We propose that these three lineages form a “bitter GR supergroup,” arising from a common ancestor which existed prior to the divergence of Insecta and Diplura. Members of this supergroup retain the shared genomic structure documented previously from many insect GRs and GR-like proteins, consisting of a long first exon, a second exon of 69–72 bp, a third exon of 90 bp, and a short terminal exon, all separated by phase 0 introns2. We observed this structure consistently in Diplura, Archaeognatha, and Zygentoma (barring rare and clearly secondary acquisitions of additional introns in the long exon), and it is only in Pterygota that structural divergence becomes frequent.

Basal hexapod GRs and an evolutionary origin for ORs

During the investigation of relict GRs, our analyses revealed two additional GR lineages mostly restricted to the non-insect hexapods (Fig. 2). One lineage was previously identified in the dipluran C. augens (28 GRs; CaugGR14–41), and we supplement it here with 13 GRs in C. aquilonaris (CaquGR18–30; Table 1 and Supplementary Data 2). This lineage is almost entirely lost in the Insecta, with a single copy conserved in Archaeognatha (MhraGR1; Supplementary Data 3) and none recovered in Zygentoma or more derived insects (Fig. 3). We have provisionally named this GR family the “basal hexapod GRs”, or hexapod GRs, with “basal” referring to the phylogenetic position of this clade among GRs in the Hexapoda, not to imply strict restriction to non-insect hexapods or ancestral function. Hexapod GRs constitute a small expansion in Diplura, and they are unrelated to the bitter supergroup, explaining the difficulty categorizing them in an earlier phylogeny of dipluran GRs30. Their early-divergent position in our tree suggests that clarifying their evolutionary history will require broader sampling of early hexapod lineages (Protura, Collembola) and closely related crustaceans (e.g., Remipedia).

A second lineage, identified in Diplura and Collembola, consists of genes overlooked in previous genome annotations. These GRs are recovered as sister to insect ORs with full support, and thus we have provisionally named this family the “precursor ORs” (Fig. 4). Notably, precursor ORs are absent from Archaeognatha, but species in this order instead possess the earliest true ORs (MhraOR1–533), a lineage that is correspondingly absent in non-insect hexapods. Precursor ORs were represented in Diplura by a small radiation of five genes in C. augens (added here as CaugGR42–46) and seven genes in C. aquilonaris (CaquGR11–17). We also identified at least one copy from genomes of three collembolan species (ScurGR1, LcurGR1, and OflaGR1–4; Table 1 and Supplementary Data 6). No copies were detected in the only publicly available genome of the remaining early-divergent hexapod order, Protura, but that genome was incomplete and highly fragmented.

Our phylogeny indicates that ORs and precursor ORs are related (Fig. 4), and that this small radiation in hexapods is the hitherto absent missing link between GRs and ORs34. This suggests a scenario in which a common ancestor evolved prior to hexapod diversification, which then gave rise to ORs alongside the origin of insects. Additionally, ORs and precursor ORs were strongly supported as sister to the sugar GRs, an unexpected relationship that was only recovered when precursor ORs were included in the tree (Fig. 4). The function of precursor ORs remains unknown, but additional study of their expression, ligands, and folding will help clarify whether they were indeed the first steps toward insect olfaction.

Cryptic diversity in the chemoreceptor superfamily

Our findings reveal substantial hidden diversity within the CRS and address longstanding questions involving the origin of insect chemosensation, but they raise a further question: how many CRS lineages remain unidentified in insect genomes? Our search methodology incorporated multiple homology-based methods, but these all inherently lose effectiveness as divergence increases—and divergence within the chemoreceptor superfamily is extreme. For instance, relict GR orthologs in the beetle families Cerambycidae (A. glabripennis) and Tenebrionidae (Tribolium castaneum) shared <40% amino acid identity despite being in the same beetle infraorder (series), Cucujiformia. BLAST searches of the genome of A. glabripennis using its 234 described GR copies36 produced only a single weak hit to its relict GR, even at very permissive e-values of 1.0. Similarly, the dipluran precursor ORs were only evident following an intensive manual annotation of the relict GRs. Thus, it is likely that lineages of the CRS continue to escape detection and that our own effort, while exhaustive, remains incomplete.

The haphazard evolution of chemosensory genes also hinders their discovery. For instance, relict GRs appear to be lost (or nearly so) in the most intensively studied insect orders of Diptera, Lepidoptera, and Hymenoptera, which partly explains how they have escaped notice. We suspect that novel CRS lineages have already been annotated in other genome projects (and indeed, copies of relict GRs had sporadically been predicted by NCBI pipelines), yet they may not have been assigned to the CRS due to their divergent sequences or partial annotations. Furthermore, diminished CRS lineages could easily be misclassified within the dominant clade of insect bitter GRs due to phylogenetic artifacts and limited taxon sampling.

Taken together, these observations further support the hypothesis that cryptic lineages of GRs and related genes remain to be found in insect and arthropod genomes14, and their identification will require meticulous genome surveys and creative methodologies. For instance, a combination of sequence and structural homology (i.e., distance comparisons between folded proteins) was effective in identifying additional GRs within Drosophila melanogaster10. The seven TM domains of ORs were key to their original discovery and description41,42, and recent structural comparisons have helped establish the larger, ancestral clade of 7TM ion channels that extends to prokaryotes14. These challenges also highlight the need for deeper genome sampling, especially at the base of the hexapod tree (Protura and Remipedia43), where chemoreceptor evolution will be critical to understanding the origin of both olfaction and terrestriality in hexapods.

Methods

Species selection and sequence data

We determined the distribution of relict GRs in insects by sampling genomes from all available insect orders on NCBI. Genomes were sampled across diverse families of each order (when possible) until the presence of relict GRs was confirmed, or the available high-quality genomes in that order were exhausted. However, for the megadiverse orders of Coleoptera, Lepidoptera, Diptera, Hymenoptera, and Hemiptera, all available high-quality genomes were searched exhaustively. Data were sourced from the NCBI repository prior to March 2025, except for the genome of Priacma serrata, where an unpublished genome (McKenna et al. in prep) was used to confirm fragmented models of relict GRs identified from the genome deposited at NCBI. A full list of taxa and genome versions used in this study is provided in Supplementary Data 1. The relict GRs in insects are conserved compared to other CRS lineages, and after generating an initial reference set, genuine copies were typically straightforward to identify via low e-values in BLAST, DIAMOND, or HMMER searches. However, all genomic hits were incorporated into a broadly sampled phylogenetic analysis of GRs to verify that they grouped with relict GRs.

We also scanned genomes of the early-divergent hexapod order Diplura to determine the ancestry of relict GRs and precursor ORs (Supplementary Data 1). Relatively few copies of precursor ORs were identified from the two available dipluran genomes, so to better anchor that phylogeny, we also scanned for precursor ORs from selected genomes of two other orders of early-divergent hexapods: the springtails Lepidocyrtus curvicollis (GenBank WGS accession CAXYGN01), Orchesella flavescens (CAXIUI01), and Sinella curviseta (RBVU03) (Collembola: Entomobryidae) and the proturan Sinentomon sp. (JASGXE01) (Protura: Sinentomidae).

Annotation methodology

All GRs were annotated in Geneious Prime (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, NZ). Relict GRs were initially discovered in a partial transcript expressed in an antennal transcriptome of the eucalyptus longhorned borer Phoracantha recurva (Coleoptera: Cerambycidae), following tBLASTn searches of the transcriptome using the GR protein sequences of the Asian longhorned beetle, Anoplophora glabripennis, as queries36. We then used the sequence data from P. recurva in tBLASTn searches of the A. glabripennis genome to identify a full-length ortholog, AglaGR144. This copy was used to identify orthologous genes from other beetle genomes, with each new gene in turn used to continue identifying relict GRs from all additional insect genomes.

For most genomes, the simple presence or absence of relict GRs was determined by serial BLAST searches. However, to better determine how the relict GRs and other lineages of the CRS evolved in insects, we manually annotated the full complement of chemosensory receptors from the genomes of the dipluran Catajapyx aquilonaris (Diplura: Japygidae), the jumping bristletail Machilis hrabei (Archaeognatha: Machilidae), and the firebrat Thermobia domestica (Zygentoma: Lepismatidae) (Supplementary Data 2–5). An initial reference set of GRs was constructed from published annotations of the crustacean Daphnia pulex29 (Anomopoda: Daphniidae), the springtail Holacanthella duospinosa32 (Collembola: Neanuridae), the dipluran Campodea augens30 (Diplura: Campodeidae), the mayfly Cloeon dipterum44 (Ephemeroptera: Baetidae), and the damselfly Calopteryx splendens31 (Odonata: Calopterygidae). The OR lineage was previously annotated from M. hrabei and T. domestica33,38, but the T. domestica genome used in our study is more recent and complete, and so we re-analyzed this genome for additional OR genes using the known ORs as queries.

Genomes were searched against this initial reference set with BLAST45, DIAMOND v2.1.946, and HMMER v3.447, the combination of which significantly improved recovery as it helped compensate for any idiosyncratic weakness in an individual program. Output of each search was filtered to best hits and converted to GFF tracks that were overlaid onto a genome and reviewed manually. Periodically during the annotation process for each genome, we would extract reviewed GRs and ORs, add them to the reference set, and reprocess the genome. This iterative strategy significantly improved the ability of the software to find exons, as well as find divergent lineages overlooked by the initial scan. Candidate chemosensory receptors were confirmed by (a) the presence of seven transmembrane domains using DeepTMHMM48; (b) observing for a terminal exon that began with the conserved amino acid motif XXXXXXTYXXXXXQ, where X is generally a nonpolar residue2; and/or (c) establishing homology with other CRS members in the same genome. Genes were not annotated if they were <300 bp in length or consisted of a single partial exon.

Annotated GRs for each species in this study were named numerically, starting at GR1 (if no known prior annotation effort), GR4 (if a holometabolous insect order where GR1–3 are reserved for the conserved CO2 GRs), or the next highest GR number following the most recent available annotation. Clades of related genes were named in sequence. Suffixes of NTE, CTE, and INT were applied to indicate missing N-terminal, C-terminal, or internal sequence; if multiple suffixes were required, then they were reduced to a single letter (e.g., NI indicates missing N-terminal and internal sequence). We assigned a suffix of PSE to apparent pseudogenes, which were defined by the presence of nonsense or frameshift mutations, splice site mutations, and/or indels. GRs and ORs frequently present as multiple copies of a long first exon that alternatively splice with conserved C-terminal exons2, so we assumed alternative splicing when this pattern was observed. For alternative splices, we assigned a lowercase letter alphabetically in order of increasing distance from the conserved C-terminal exons or “conserved core”. Thus, if a conserved core was overlooked, this strategy allowed clean reassignment of long first exons without affecting the lettering of exons that are still associated with the other conserved core.

Phylogenetic analyses

The phylogenetic dataset included 1,084 GR and OR genes across 37 species. As a base we included complete, published annotations of CRS genes from a distant arthropod, the centipede Strigamia maritima49, and a representative species from each of the three general divisions of Insecta: the Paleoptera (the damselfly C. splendans), the Polyneoptera (the termite Zootermopsis nevadensis23), and the Holometabola (the honey bee Apis mellifera50). To this, we added existing annotations of GRs and ORs from C. augens and T. domestica as cited above, alongside our own annotations of C. aquilonaris, M. hrabei, T. domestica, and springtail precursor ORs. We also manually annotated representative relict GRs from selected species within each insect order where they were identified (Supplementary Data 1 and 6) and included these in our phylogeny. Representative species were chosen based on quality of genome (preferring chromosome level assemblies), prior annotations, minimal intron complexity, and model organism status. Finally, the conserved lineages of carbon dioxide GRs and Orco were supplemented by including published representatives from Anopheles gambiae (Diptera: Culicidae), A. glabripennis, Bombyx mori (Lepidoptera: Bombicidae), and Drosophila melanogaster (Diptera: Drosophilidae)21,36,51.

Pseudogenes were excluded from the analysis, as they might no longer be functionally constrained, and we excluded most incomplete gene models to reduce effects of missing data (unless these genes were necessary to represent diminished lineages, e.g., LsacGR1NTE was the only available representative relict GR from the order Zygentoma). Finally, we excluded TdomGR110 and TdomGR111 from the T. domestica annotation. These two unusual GRs are members of the bitter supergroup that possess extraordinarily long first exons (686 and 895 amino acids, respectively) and are likely to be a derived lineage within Zygentoma.

Protein alignments were generated using FAMSA v2.2.352 with default settings and MAFFT v7.50553 using the E-INS-i algorithm, which is specifically designed for the structural configuration of our target proteins (multiple conserved regions, including transmembrane domains, separated by variable-length loop regions). For each alignment, four supermatrices were constructed to evaluate sensitivity to column filtering and explicitly test robustness to both alignment method and trimming strategy (using trimAL v1.254). Matrices consisted of: (1) the full untrimmed alignment, (2) removal of columns containing ≥75% gap characters (equivalent to trimAl -gt 0.75 -st 0), (3) removal of columns containing ≥50% gap characters (trimAl -gt 0.50 -st 0), and (4) trimAl --gappyout, a maximum likelihood–informed heuristic that dynamically determines gap thresholds.

Matrices were analyzed under a maximum likelihood (ML) framework in IQTREE v 2.0.755, with ModelFinder56 used to identify the best-fitting substitution model and nodal support assessed with UFBoot2 ultrafast bootstrap57. ModelFinder selected JTT + F + R10 as the best fitting model for the 75% and 50% gap-cull matrices for both the MAFFT and FAMSA alignments, and this model choice was subsequently applied to the remaining matrices. To further account for potential site-heterogeneous substitution processes, we generated posterior mean site frequency (PMSF) versions of each matrix. For each alignment and trimming combination, the initial maximum likelihood tree inferred under JTT + F + R10 was used to estimate site-specific amino acid frequency profiles. Phylogenies were then re-estimated in IQ-TREE v2 under the LG + C60 + G + F model, which approximates mixture model behavior at reduced computational cost58.

In total, sixteen phylogenetic analyses were conducted (2 alignment methods × 4 trimming strategies × ±PMSF; Supplementary Data 7). Trees were rooted on the base of the centipede GR clade (S. maritima), under the assumption that orthology between these and the hexapod clades was restricted to the most recent common ancestor. Trees were visualized using condensed clade representations based on the results of the FAMSA trimAL gappyout matrix. Only minor differences in support existed between the major clades in the alternative trees, with topology differing slightly within respective subfamilies and a small subset of the M. hrabei genes inconsistently placed between MAFFT and FAMSA. A complete, colorized tree derived from the FAMSA trimAL gappyout matrix, with clades visualized, is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1, and support for major clades across all sixteen analyses is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.
