Transgenerational consequences of grandparents’ smoking on grandchildren’s development: a systematic review
Authors: Suci Puspita Ratih, Wan Yuen Choo, Nik Daliana Nik Farid, Nohan Arum Romadlona, Mika Vernicia Humairo, Nina Rini Suprobo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveResearch on the impact of smoking behaviour across generations has primarily focused on grandmaternal smoking during pregnancy. However, the broader multigenerational effects of smoking behaviour, notably through environmental and behavioural pathways, remain underexplored. This study evaluated previous studies on the possibility of transgenerational transmission, rather than in utero transmission, regarding the effects of grandparental smoking behaviour on offspring’s development outcomes.DesignThis study is a systematic review with qualitative evidence synthesis.Data sourcesA comprehensive search was conducted across multiple online databases, including PubMed, EBSCOhost, Web of Science and Scopus. Introduction

Transgenerational transmission of health outcomes can occur through maternal and paternal lines and is distinct from intergenerational transmission.1,3 In the maternal lineage, intergenerational effects extend to the third generation (ie, the grandchildren of the first generation). In contrast, in the paternal lineage, they reach only the second generation (the immediate offspring).2 3 Inheritance beyond these respective generational boundaries is classified as transgenerational.2 In other words, the transgenerational transmission of the effects might occur beyond in utero and germline transmissions.4

Environmental exposure can be transmitted across generations through early epigenetics, childhood experiences and learning behaviours.5,7 The ecological transmission across generations can affect health outcomes in future offspring.7 Similarly, disease risk can be transmitted across generations, influencing the health of subsequent offspring.3 4 8 Research on the cross-generational transmission of health issues in humans and animals has revealed various pathways through which these effects occur.9,14 However, studies on germline involving non-genomic transmission inheritance in humans remain limited compared with those in animals.15 16

In the context of smoking behaviour, previous studies have examined how the smoking behaviour of grandparents may affect the health of their offspring, focusing on the effects of maternal smoking during pregnancy on the health of the mother’s future children.17,22 Conversely, transmissions besides in utero transmissions across generations remain an underexplored area of research. Studies have suggested that socio-economic, behavioural and family factors could contribute to these effects.23,27 Investigating whether these transmission patterns can be repeated across subsequent generations is crucial, as social and physical environmental factors can influence gene expression and health outcomes.2 A similar review has begun to explore the intergenerational transmission of health-related behaviours. For example, Chambers et al. conducted a systematic review examining grandparents’ influence on their grandchildren’s cancer risk factors, including smoking, diet, physical activity and weight.28 However, this review primarily focused on behavioural and caregiving influences and did not address the biological or transgenerational mechanisms by which grandparents were exposed.

Longitudinal family studies that extend beyond traditional birth cohorts raise important questions regarding how grandfathers’ smoking behaviour may influence the developmental and behavioural outcomes of their grandchildren. A deeper understanding of these behavioural and environmental transmission pathways is essential to generate evidence that can inform and enhance the effectiveness of tobacco control policies.29,31 Accordingly, this study systematically reviewed the existing literature on the transgenerational transmission of grandparents’ smoking behaviour and its potential effects on grandchildren’s development, with a focus on methodological approaches, developmental outcomes and key findings from the included studies.

Methods

Search strategy and databases

A systematic search was conducted across several online databases, including PubMed, Web of Science (WoS), SocIndex (via EBSCOhost), Psychology and Behavioural Science Collection (via EBSCOhost), and Scopus. An alternative online search was conducted using Google Scholar through Publish or Perish. The search parameters were restricted to studies published in English and peer-reviewed journals. No limitations were imposed on publication dates to maximise the retrieval of relevant articles. The search concluded on 31 August 2024. Initially, the search employed Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) within PubMed to systematically identify relevant articles. Pertinent terms identified through PubMed’s MeSH were subsequently used to conduct searches across other databases and Google Scholar. The detailed search terms are provided in online supplemental table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Population

For clarity, the study child in this review is referred to as generation 3 (G3), parents as generation 2 (G2), grandparents as generation 1 (G1) and great-grandparents as Generation 0 (G0).

This review defined transgenerational transmission as the environmental and behavioural transmission of health outcomes that may occur in grandchildren on the paternal line and great-grandchildren on the maternal line.2 3 Therefore, this study included only grandfathers (G1) and earlier generations on the paternal side, great-grandmothers (G0) and earlier generations on the maternal side and the study child (G3). Studies that focused exclusively on grandmothers (G1) or grandmaternal smoking during pregnancy (GMSDP) were excluded, as such exposures reflect in utero or intergenerational pathways rather than actual transgenerational effects.

Exposure

This review systematically examined literature documenting the smoking behaviours of grandfathers, great-grandfathers and great-grandmothers, along with their potential effects on the developmental outcomes of the study children, with particular attention to the possibility of transgenerational transmission.

Outcomes

The initial primary outcomes of interest included physical, mental, behavioural and cognitive development. This review also included studies that analysed outcomes related to socio-economic well-being, including factors such as food intake, education and shelter. This systematic review considered the effect on grandchildren in various countries, regions and settings.

Study design and setting

This study exclusively included longitudinal studies with no restrictions on the study location or period. Only the original studies were included in this review.

Data screening

The screening process was based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram (figure 1).32 The studies included in this review met the defined eligibility criteria carefully, focusing on longitudinal designs that explored how smoking behaviours might affect later generations. Each study presented original data examining both behavioural and developmental outcomes among grandchildren (G3). Overall, the quality of the reviewed studies was generally strong, with most showing sound methodology and clearly defined measures of exposure and outcomes.

Identification, screening and inclusion of reviewed articles based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram.

Two reviewers (SPR and NRS) reviewed the titles and abstracts of the identified studies using Rayyan.ai to select those that met the inclusion criteria. Rayyan.ai is a web-based systematic review management tool that facilitates blinded screening and conflict resolution through artificial intelligence-assisted tagging and filtering.33 The reviewers uploaded all identified records and manually screened each title and abstract, and the platform’s AI suggestions were used only to support, not replace, reviewer judgement. Differences in opinion were addressed through discussions with other members of the review team (CWY and ND). Subsequently, three reviewers (SPR, NAR and MVH) independently evaluated the full texts of these potentially suitable studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussions with another review team member (CWY and ND). During this stage, studies that only measured grandmothers’ smoking during pregnancy (GMSDP) were excluded because we exclusively reviewed studies exploring the transgenerational effects of grandparents’ smoking without in utero transmission, both in paternal and maternal lineages. Studies that did not involve humans were also excluded.

Data synthesis

A coding sheet was created for data extraction and synthesis. Three reviewers (SPR, CWY and ND) extracted and reported data using a coding sheet. The extracted information included details such as authors, titles, years of publication, study location, sampling method, variables, study design, methods, timing of data collection, characteristics of the study population, statistical methodologies employed, outcomes relevant to the review and any identified limitations and biases. The risk of bias (quality) was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cohort Studies to evaluate article quality. The quality appraisal results were considered during the interpretation of the findings; however, no studies were excluded based on quality. Despite minor limitations, the included studies were retained owing to their overall strong methodological quality based on JBI appraisal. Cohen’s kappa test was employed to test inter-rater reliability using Stata Now 18.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). The agreement between reviewers 1 and 2 and between reviewers 2 and 3 was moderate (71.43%, K=0.44). The agreement between reviewers 1 and 3 was high (100%, K=0.1). The overall agreement among the three reviewers was substantial (K=0.64).

Owing to the diversity of reports on relationships/effect measurement, this study provides a narrative synthesis structured around the characteristics of the reviewed studies, data sources used in the studies, sampling procedures, years to follow-up, measurements of exposure, outcomes, other factors and measurements of association. This review also compared the methods and findings between studies, particularly those with the same outcomes. Additionally, this review discusses the gaps in the methods and conclusions of the reviewed articles.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, review or reporting of this research. Our commitment to PPI will be realised during the dissemination, on peer-reviewed publication of the manuscript. A key part of our dissemination plan, to be enacted after this research is accepted for publication, is to collaborate with the public to share the results through an open-access article.

Results

Study identification and quality assessment

Based on a systematic search of online databases, this review identified 1812 articles from PubMed, 876 articles from Scopus, 766 articles from EBSCOhost, and 434 articles from WoS. Additionally, 245 articles were identified from Google Scholar, totalling 4133 articles. After removing 217 duplicates, the remaining articles were 3916 (figure 1). After screening the articles based on the eligibility criteria, 37 were included in the full-text assessment. Furthermore, 30 articles were excluded for the following reasons: exposure measurement focused solely on grandmaternal smoking during pregnancy (GMSDP) (n=20); lack of measures of association (n=2); absence of smoking behaviour data for either the G0 or G1 generation (n=4); and study design not being longitudinal (n=4). A total of 7 articles were assessed for quality assessment.

Table 1 presents the results of the quality assessment of the reviewed studies. According to the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cohort Studies, one key criterion assesses whether participants were free of the outcome at baseline, meaning that the condition of interest had not yet developed prior to the exposure being measured. In three of the reviewed articles, this information was not clearly reported, making it uncertain whether the studied children were free of the outcome (smoking behaviour) at the start of follow-up.34,36 Additionally, two articles did not clearly describe the statistical strategies to address potential confounding factors.37 38 Overall, most studies met the JBI quality criteria, demonstrating appropriate recruitment, reliable exposure and outcome measurement, adequate follow-up and sound analytical approaches.

Characteristics of the reviewed studies

The seven articles included in this review examined the transgenerational effects of smoking behaviour on the health of offspring. While one study also measured GMSDP, allowing assessment of in utero transmission through the offspring, it was included in the review primarily for its analysis of transgenerational pathways rather than direct effects of GMSDP.38 The studies were published between 2006 and 2023, with most conducted in Western countries. Three were based in Avon, England,37,39 two in the USA,34 35 and one each in Ireland40 and Norway.36 The generations studied ranged from great-grandparents to grandchildren, with a fairly balanced examination of the effects of grandparents’ smoking on grandchildren’s behaviour and physical development.

Behavioural outcomes were assessed at different ages, including adolescence, young adulthood and adulthood, though adolescent studies did not analyse smoking due to the low prevalence in that age group.34,36 Physical development outcomes were primarily related to body composition and anthropometric indicators, including birth weight, body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference.38,40 Two studies have focused on body composition, both originating from the same cohort, with one emphasising the role of paternal and male ancestral smoking (online supplemental table 2).38 39 One study focused on cognitive outcomes (IQ).37

Data source and sampling procedure of the reviewed studies

The reviewed studies employed various longitudinal datasets and sampling approaches to analyse the transgenerational effects of smoking behaviour. Some studies followed families prospectively, beginning with parents or grandparents, whereas others traced relationships retrospectively from the grandchildren. Three articles explored data from the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC) in England, which began in 1991–1992 with over 14 541 pregnancies.37,39 Parents provided information about their parents and grandparents, allowing researchers to map up to four generations (G0–G3).

Mejía-Lancheros et al40 analysed data from the Lifeways Cross-Generation Cohort Study (Ireland, Lifeways Study), which started in 2001, collecting data from 1124 pregnant mothers (G2), their partners and both sets of grandparents (G1). This study followed 1068 children (G3) from birth to ages 5 and 9, providing multi-generational data on health and socio-economic factors. In Norway, the Tromsø Study offered a 42-year follow-up (1974–2016), tracing families retrospectively by identifying grandchildren in the latest survey and linking them with their parents and grandparents.36 The sample included 5725 maternal and 4057 paternal lineage cases.36

Two studies from the USA provided additional perspectives. Bailey et al used data from the Seattle Social Development Project and the Intergenerational Project, beginning in 1985, to analyse 208 families across three generations, with teachers also reporting behavioural data.34 Vandewater et al combined three national longitudinal datasets—the Panel Study of Income Dynamics, the Child Development Supplement and the Transition to Adulthood survey to explore the transmission of smoking behaviours.35 Their sample included grandparents, parents and up to two siblings (G3) per family, who were followed from childhood into young adulthood (ages 18–27).

Measurements of variables of the reviewed studies

Measurements of ancestors’ smoking behaviours varied throughout the study. In some studies, grandparents and parents self-reported their smoking behaviour, whereas in others, data were provided by subsequent generations. Three articles indicated that grandparents and parents themselves recorded their smoking behaviours during their participation in the study,34 35 40 whereas in others, the information was reported by their children or grandchildren due to missing data from earlier waves. For instance, some studies relied on parental reports about the smoking of their parents and grandparents,37,39 whereas one study36 used grandchildren’s reports for both generations. This procedure was conducted because data on smoking behaviour by G1 and G2 were not available in previous waves.36

Definitions of smoking behaviour also varied. In Vandewater et al, grandparents were classified as never-smokers if they never reported smoking across the study period and as ever-smokers if they were former or current smokers.35 Grandchildren were identified as current smokers if they reported smoking in the latest survey wave. Other studies assessed behavioural outcomes such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), oppositional defiant disorder and conduct disorder, with some relying on self-reports and others on teacher assessments.34 Physical outcomes included birth weight, BMI, waist circumference and measures of body composition, recorded at different ages and analysed as continuous variables.38,40 One study assessed cognitive outcomes, measuring grandchildren’s IQ using standardised Wechsler tests at ages 8 and 15.37

All studies also adjusted for a range of covariates across generations. For G1 and G0, these included demographic factors, social class and age at the birth of the next generation. For G2, covariates covered demographics, socio-economic position, parental age and prenatal exposures such as alcohol and energy intake. In G3, covariates included gestational age, total energy intake and peer smoking during adolescence. Some studies have further incorporated contextual variables, such as family income, national gross domestic product and economic cycles, during the grandparents’ birth period. Together, these measurements provided a comprehensive framework for assessing the multigenerational effects of smoking behaviours (online supplemental table 3).

Statistical approach of the reviewed studies

The majority of the reviewed studies used sufficient statistical approaches to test the associations between grandparents’ smoking behaviour and grandchildren’s development, with some studies reporting how they handled missing data. Regarding the association tests, the reviewed studies used various statistical analyses. Bailey et al employed a latent variable to examine the relationship between substance use and problem behaviours across three generations.34 Their analysis involved confirmatory factor analysis to validate latent constructs for each generation, followed by structural equation modelling to assess intergenerational pathways. Vandewater et al applied logistic regression models to test direct and indirect effects of grandparents’ smoking on grandchildren’s smoking, using the Sobel test to assess mediation by parental smoking and correcting for family clustering with robust standard errors.35

Other studies used regression-based approaches tailored to their specific outcomes. Mejia-Lancheros et al employed χ2 tests, analysis of variance and logistic regressions to assess associations between parental and grandparental smoking and children’s weight status at different ages, stratified by sex.40 Golding et al examined grandchildren’s IQ using regression models adjusted for grandparents’ socio-economic characteristics while excluding parental and child variables to isolate generational effects.37 In another study, Golding et al analysed body composition using one- and two-tailed tests, depending on hypothesised directions, and explored sex interactions through multiple regression models.38 Gregory et al followed a multi-stage approach combining descriptive, power and regression analyses, applying different significance thresholds for maternal and paternal lineages.39

Sari et al employed a control function approach, using three-stage probit regression models, to account for both direct and indirect smoking effects across generations.36 The first stage estimated grandparents’ smoking probabilities, followed by analyses of parental and grandchildren’s smoking behaviours while adjusting for relevant covariates. Mediation was tested using Sobel’s product coefficient method to differentiate direct from indirect pathways. Collectively, these studies have demonstrated methodological diversity, ranging from latent variable and structural models to regression and mediation analyses. This approach highlighted a comprehensive and statistically rigorous approach to understanding the transgenerational effects of smoking behaviour.

Transgenerational effects of smoking behaviour on grandchildren’s development

Three reviewed studies that assessed the effects of grandparents’ smoking behaviour on grandchildren’s behaviour showed varied results. Bailey et al revealed a modest general continuity in the transmission of the use of drugs from G1 to G2.34 Drug-specific effects were observed for cigarette smoking, showing continuity across generations (G1–G2), but were not applied to binge drinking and marijuana use. However, a low significance of the direct effects of G1’s substance use on G3’s behaviour was observed.

The other two studies measured G3’s smoking behaviour as the primary outcome. Vandewater et al showed that the direct link between G1’s ever-smoking and G3’s current smoking was not statistically significant.35 The study also showed that the relationship between grandparents’ (G1) and young adults’ (G3) smoking was significantly mediated by parental (G2) smoking. Sari et al showed that the smoking behaviour of maternal grandparents has a significant adverse effect on their grandchildren’s likelihood of smoking, even after accounting for parents’ smoking habits during their children’s upbringing.36 Additionally, the study revealed matrilateral bias, indicating that smoking behaviour appears to be more strongly transmitted through the maternal side of the family.

The three studies that examined grandchildren’s physical development focused mainly on BMI and body composition, such as fat and lean mass. Mejia-Lancheros et al found no significant overall association between grandparental smoking and grandchildren’s overweight or obesity, though paternal grandmother smoking was linked to a higher risk of overweight or obesity among granddaughters at age 9, even after adjusting for paternal smoking.40

Golding et al and Gregory et al reported that ancestral smoking, particularly when initiated before puberty, was associated with greater fat mass in grandchildren.38 39 Golding et al found strong associations between early smoking in great-grandfathers and grandfathers and increased fat mass, about 3–6 kg higher in their granddaughters and great-granddaughters, with no effect on lean mass.38 Gregory et al observed similar sex-specific patterns: male and female grandchildren of grandfathers who began smoking before puberty had significantly higher fat mass at ages 17 and 24, with effects more pronounced for females and along the maternal line.39

The studies highlighted sex-specific transgenerational effects of smoking on offspring’s health. Golding found that grandparents’ regular smoking during adolescence was associated with greater fat mass in grandchildren, with stronger and more consistent effects in grandsons than in granddaughters, suggesting that male descendants may be more sensitive to ancestral smoking exposures.38 Similarly, Mejia-Lancheros reported that paternal smoking had a stronger influence on sons, increasing their risk of overweight or obesity at age 5, whereas paternal grandmother smoking was more strongly linked to higher adiposity in granddaughters at age 9.40

Additionally, Golding et al found cognitive implications, showing that grandsons demonstrated lower verbal and performance IQ at age 8 when their paternal grandmother had smoked during pregnancy, whereas granddaughters exhibited lower total IQ at age 15 when their paternal grandfather had been a smoker.37 Together, these findings suggest that early-life smoking in ancestors may have transgenerational effects on both body composition and cognitive development, with notable differences by sex and lineage.

Strengths and limitations of the reviewed studies

Most of the reviewed studies reported strengths and limitations of their studies. Vandewater et al35 analysed longitudinal data and a population-representative sample, ensuring generalisability to the US population, high follow-up rates and reduced bias. However, it lacked causal conclusions, did not account for family data dependencies and missed factors, such as grandparental cohabitation and confounding variables. Mejía-Lancheros et al40 examined cross-generational health patterns, focusing on the effect of paternal smoking on offspring overweight/obesity with a robust longitudinal design across three generations. Its limitations include a small sample size, self-reported paternity and incomplete smoking data. Golding et al37 benefited from the careful timing of data collection, reduced bias related to IQ and enhanced accuracy because of parental familiarity with grandparents’ smoking habits. However, it has limitations, such as the lack of replication studies, reliance on parental reports and selective fertility bias. Gregory et al39 used dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) for more accurate body composition measurements and yielded consistent results across two ages but suffered from recall bias, missing data on smoking onset and a lack of replication studies. Sari et al36 offered insights into intergenerational smoking behaviour transmission in Northern Norway; however, its geographical focus limited the generalisability of the study results, and it lacked data on grandparental cohabitation and broader family dynamics. The summary of statistical approaches and results of the study can be found in online supplemental table 4.

Discussion

This systematic review explored the effects of ancestral smoking behaviour on subsequent generations through environmental and behavioural transmission in contrast to in utero transmission, which can occur through grandmaternal smoking during pregnancy. The review included seven eligible articles that aligned with the research questions. Compared with studies that exclusively analysed grandmaternal tobacco use during pregnancy (with 20 articles excluded during the screening process), the environmental and behavioural pathways in cross-generational smoking impact studies remain underexplored. This may be related to the availability of longitudinal data measuring smoking behaviour in multiple generations, whereas multigenerational effects through transgenerational transfer can be adequately assessed in third generations or higher.3 41 Regarding the study location, no records were found evaluating the cross-generational effects of grandparents’ smoking on grandchildren’s development in Asian countries, despite relatively high smoking rates in the region.42 43 This presents a significant research gap that warrants further exploration in the future.

Regarding the quality of the reviewed studies, the methodological quality of the included studies was generally strong according to the JBI appraisal, with most demonstrating appropriate recruitment, reliable exposure and outcome measurement and adequate follow-up. However, a few studies lacked clarity regarding participants’ baseline status and statistical control for confounders, which may have influenced the precision of their reported associations. These limitations highlight the need for future longitudinal studies with clearer reporting and stronger adjustment for potential confounding to strengthen the evidence on transgenerational effects of smoking.

The reviewed studies identified four primary outcome categories for grandchildren: problematic behaviours, smoking behaviours, anthropometry and IQ. Family size is one of the notable methodological issues that must be considered when exploring multigenerational associations.1 Smaller families (single-child families) showed a higher incidence of outcomes than bigger families (families of ≥5 children).1 Other outcomes related to the environmental and behavioural pathways have been explored less frequently. In contrast, various outcomes have been investigated in the in utero pathway. In addition to the anthropometry of the children, studies on grandmaternal smoking during pregnancy have also measured asthma,1944,47 lung function,47 early onset myopia18 and autism spectrum disorders.48 Although the direct effects of grandparents’ smoking behaviour on these health issues through environmental and behavioural pathways have not been conclusively demonstrated, it is possible that conditions, such as asthma, could have been influenced by environmental factors, such as physical, social and economic environments,49 potentially linked to grandparents’ smoking behaviours in the past. Smoking behaviour is widely known to be significantly associated with socioeconomic status (SES), with a greater rate or frequency of smoking in individuals with lower SES than in those with higher SES.50,53 Chambers et al revealed that grandparents often exposed their grandchildren to cigarette smoke, ignored parents’ preferences about avoiding secondhand smoke and demonstrated smoking behaviours that appeared to encourage similar habits among their grandchildren.28 The review highlights the need to include grandparents in health promotion strategies.

In terms of residency, the reviewed studies did not specifically address whether grandchildren lived separately from or under the same roof as their grandparents, which could influence direct exposure.36 54 Direct exposure through secondhand smoke at home could influence physical and cognitive development as well as behaviour in grandchildren.55,59 Those relationships require further exploration in cross-generational studies.

Regarding the measurement of grandparents’ smoking behaviour, the reviewed studies employed self-reports or recall from subsequent generations. The accuracy of recalling past smoking behaviour is still debated. Although some studies agreed that this method could be valid, some bias exists regarding memorising certain quantitative details.60 61 This could also result in limited data on exposure variables, as was the case in the studies by Gregory et al39 and Golding et al,38 which excluded from the analyses the insufficient data of grandparents on their smoking behaviours. Assessing smoking behaviour is considered more valid through careful assessment using clinical methods, as self-reporting of the behaviour is reliable only over a short period.62,64 Moreover, although most articles employed adequate analytical techniques, some studies did not explicitly address how they handled missing values. Addressing missing information is necessary when handling longitudinal data, as it may affect the power of the study; therefore, appropriate methods, such as inverse probability weighting or multiple imputation, can be conducted.65 66

Two studies found that parental factors mediated the effects of smoking behaviour on grandchildren’s behaviour through Sobel tests.35 36 Mediation analysis can contribute to more valid causal interpretations and assumptions.67 Various mediation tests have been introduced and can be used by considering type I error rates and statistical power.68 The reviewed studies also indicated that the probability of G3’s smoking was doubled when G2 smoked, as G2’s smoking behaviour had more long-lasting effects on their direct generations.35 36 Parental smoking can influence children to have a more favourable attitude towards smoking behaviour and could encourage smoking initiation in children.69 When discussing cross-generational effects, environmental factors such as exposure to cigarettes at a young age may influence disease risks in future generations, which in some instances involves epigenetic transmission and learning behaviour.5 7 Moreover, a study on miRNAs in animals showed that a small dose of nicotine could alter the expression of miRNAs in the studied generations (G1) and their next generations (G2 and G3), affecting the tendency to become addicted to nicotine in G2 and G3.70

In this review, grandparents’ smoking indirectly influenced their grandchildren’s behavioural disorders, including the tendency to have ADHD.34 This finding is in line with that of Yim et al17 who analysed GMSDP, which was found to be significantly associated with ADHD in grandchildren. Although the study on the heritable effects of environmental and behavioural factors is limited, a study on animals found that paternal nicotine exposure can affect the brain and behaviour of offspring, which may be repeated in future generations.71

The reviewed studies also showed that grandparents’ smoking status was significantly associated with their grandchildren’s physical development, especially fat mass and obesity/overweight status.38,40 Although the associations between grandparents’ smoking and grandchildren’s physical development were mostly explored through in utero pathways, findings in this study indicate possibilities of transgenerational transmission of the effects on physical aspects in grandchildren. This finding is in line with that of Northstone et al72 who stated that paternal smoking in childhood contributed to obesity in the next generation and indicated the potential transgenerational effects of prenatal exposure to smoke. Similarly, this study also found that grandparents’ smoking behaviour can influence grandchildren’s cognitive development. This is in line with a survey by Pembrey et al,73 who found that children whose fathers were exposed to smoking prenatally had lower average IQ scores, especially in the verbal component. Moreover, environmental factors, especially during childhood, can shape children’s cognitive and neural development through learning opportunities provided by the environment.74

This systematic review has several limitations. First, there is a significant heterogeneity in the statistical approaches and outcomes measured in the reviewed studies, which may affect the synthesis of the results and validity of the conclusions. Second, we did not conduct the search procedures on grey literature and publications written in other languages, resulting in a biased sample of the available literature. Despite the limitations, our systematic review offers several strengths, such as identifying gaps in the current literature and suggesting directions for future research in this field. This systematic review also followed rigorous methodologies that minimised selection bias and allowed the integration of diverse studies, thus enhancing the generalisability and robustness of the findings. The findings of this review also have important implications for tobacco control policies. Although While most existing policies target the prevention of direct and secondhand exposure, the evidence of transgenerational effects suggests that the health consequences of smoking may extend well beyond the immediate generation. Strengthening tobacco control strategies to emphasise intergenerational risks and promoting cessation efforts across all life stages, including adolescence and preconception periods, could help reduce the long-term, multigenerational burden of tobacco use.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights significant gaps in our understanding of the transgenerational effects of smoking behaviour, specifically those independent of the widely studied in utero pathway of grandmaternal smoking during pregnancy. These findings suggest that ancestors’ smoking behaviour can affect grandchildren’s behavioural, physical and cognitive development, including behavioural disorders, increased risk of obesity and reduced IQ through environmental and behavioural pathways. Parental factors potentially mediated the effects. However, these effects have not been conclusively demonstrated; thus, further research is needed to better understand the mechanisms of environmental and behavioural transmissions and the role of parental factors as mediators. Future studies should focus on improving data accuracy, employing robust measurement techniques and addressing gaps related to missing data and direct exposure of grandchildren living on the same roof as their grandparents. In addition, future research exploring the cross-generational effects of smoking in regions with high smoking rates such as Asia is necessary.
