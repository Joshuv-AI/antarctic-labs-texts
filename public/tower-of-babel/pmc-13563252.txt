Biomimetic nanodelivery of antagomiR-155-5p ameliorates wear particle-induced osteolysis by dual regulation of Gas6/Axl-mediated macrophage inflammation
Authors: Taihe Liu, Sipeng Lin, Chenhao Pan, Yifan Yu, Haopeng Sun, Junxi Chen, Muyun Tan, Changchuan Li, Shixun Li, Siyuan Tan, Gang Zeng, Haoxian Liu, Wingcheuk Ko, Jing Xu, Yujun Sun, Phei Er Saw, Yue Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAseptic loosening (AL) is the primary cause of prosthesis failure after total joint replacement, which is triggered by wear particle-induced macrophage activation, excessive inflammatory factor secretion and subsequent periprosthetic osteolysis. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) act as key regulators of this pathological cascade, yet the specific functional miRNAs and their precise regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely clarified.MethodsmRNA-seq and miRNA-seq were performed in titanium particle (TiPs)-stimulated macrophages to screen osteolysis-associated miRNAs, with expression validation conducted in clinical periprosthetic osteolytic tissues. In vitro functional and mechanistic assays were used to verify the miR-155-5p/Gas6/Axl regulatory axis, and a mouse calvarial osteolysis model was applied for in vivo validation. Macrophage membrane-camouflaged PLGA-PEI nanoparticles loaded with antagomir-155-5p (Anta-155@MNPs) were constructed, and their local therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in murine osteolysis models.ResultsmiR-155-5p was significantly upregulated in both TiPs-stimulated macrophages and clinical periprosthetic osteolytic tissues. Functionally, miR-155-5p promoted Stat1/p65 phosphorylation, proinflammatory cytokine production and M1 macrophage polarization by inhibiting the Gas6/Axl pathway via dual mechanisms: Mafb-mediated Gas6 transcriptional repression and Adam10-dependent Axl cleavage inhibition. In vivo, this axis exacerbated TiPs-induced calvarial osteolysis, while local Anta-155@MNPs administration markedly mitigated bone resorption and tissue inflammation.ConclusionmiR-155-5p drives TiPs-induced periprosthetic osteolysis via the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway, and targeted inhibition of miR-155-5p is a promising strategy to prevent artificial joint AL.The translational potential of this articleThis study identifies the miR-155-5p/Gas6/Axl axis as a novel therapeutic target for AL, and the targeted Anta-155@MNPs delivery system provides a feasible preclinical strategy for clinical translation to treat wear particle-induced osteolysis.

Introduction

Total joint replacement (TJR) serves as the mainstream therapeutic modality for end-stage osteoarticular disorders. However, prosthesis failure is associated with a poor patient prognosis and imposes a substantial economic burden [1], [2]. By 2030, 635,000 total hip replacement surgeries and 1.28 million total knee replacement surgeries are predicted to be conducted yearly in the United States, and more than 10% will need to undergo revision procedures because of loosening [2]. Aseptic loosening (AL) is among the leading causes for the first-time revision of TJR and the failure of revision, accounting for at least 50% of all total hip arthroplasty (THA)revisions according to most national registry systems and more than 40% of late total knee arthroplasty (TKA) revisions [[3], [4], [5]]. The top indication for re-revision was AL, which constituted 34.8% of all cases, followed by periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), which represented 27.8%, and instability, which accounted for 13.9% [3], [[6], [7], [8]]. However, at present, effective strategies for preventing AL are unavailable. Revision surgery, the only treatment method, causes secondary trauma to patients and imposes a substantial social burden. Therefore, investigating the mechanism of AL and developing targeted treatment strategies are necessary. AL of artificial joints is driven by multiple interrelated mechanisms, including wear particle-induced inflammation, stress shielding, and prosthetic micromotion [9]. Among these mechanisms, wear particles shed from the prosthesis surface play a dominant role: they actively recruit macrophages and trigger a cascade of cellular responses that ultimately promote periprosthetic osteolysis [10]. This particle-mediated pathway is currently recognized as the primary pathogenic factor underlying AL of artificial joints [1]. Within the periprosthetic tissue, the persistent presence of wear particles, such as titanium particles (TiPs), drives macrophages to undergo M1 polarization and secrete a proinflammatory secretome. This dysregulated macrophage response subsequently inhibits osteoblastogenesis, promotes osteoclastogenesis, and ultimately triggers osteolysis, culminating in AL [9], [11], [12]. Therefore, identifying the core regulators governing wear particle-induced inflammatory responses in macrophages will lay a critical foundation for the development of future therapeutic strategies.

To identify the critical transcriptomic shifts driving this pathology without empirical bias, we first performed high-throughput mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) on TiPs-stimulated macrophages. This screening revealed that the TAM (Tyro3, Axl, Mertk) receptor tyrosine kinase family, specifically the Gas6/Axl signaling axis, emerged as a primary hub cascade robustly dysregulated under particle stress. Canonical Gas6/Axl signaling serves as a critical homeostatic rheostat that modulates downstream networks, including the JAK–STAT, NF-κB, and PI3K–Akt pathways, to govern cell survival and inflammatory responses [13]. While therapies targeting Gas6/Axl have shown efficacy in mitigating acute inflammation in various organ systems [[14], [15], [16]], its exact functional dynamics within the unique chronic microenvironment of wear particle-induced osteolysis remain poorly defined, and the mechanism causing its pathological alteration remains an enigma.

Given that transcriptional networks and receptor expressions are tightly orchestrated by noncoding RNAs at the post-transcriptional level, we subsequently sought to uncover the upstream epigenetic brakes driving this Gas6/Axl dysregulation. Through miRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) coupled with bioinformatic target prediction, miR-155-5p—a potent master regulator of both innate and adaptive immunity [17]—was identified as the key upstream post-transcriptional regulator directly targeting the Gas6/Axl axis. While miR-155-5p has been extensively documented to promote inflammatory responses in rheumatoid arthritis [18] and is implicated as a central pathogenic factor in osteolysis and other skeletal diseases [[19], [20], [21]], its specific role as the upstream aberrant switch responsible for Gas6/Axl signaling disruption in aseptic loosening remains unknown. Therefore, the specific scientific question driving this study is: how does the particle-induced upregulation of miR-155-5p pathologically deactivate or reshape the immunomodulatory Gas6/Axl axis to perpetuate macrophage inflammation, and can this axis be therapeutically rebalanced to halt periprosthetic osteolysis? We hypothesized that miR-155-5p acts as an upstream master switch, and that targeted silencing of this single miRNA could globally rescue the downstream Gas6/Axl-mediated homeostatic network.

To address this scientific question and achieve precise therapeutic intervention at the osteolytic site, a robust delivery system capable of navigating the periprosthetic inflammatory microenvironment is required. Macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles have garnered increasing attention for managing inflammatory disorders due to their superior biocompatibility, inherent inflammation-homing capabilities, and ability to sequester decoy cytokines [22]. Macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles have also been widely applied in the treatment of orthopedic diseases [[23], [24], [25]]. By cloaking engineered miRNA inhibitors (antagomirs) within these biomimetic vehicles, it is possible to achieve highly specific, localized gene silencing at the bone-implant interface.

In this research, we comprehensively validated the miR-155-5p/Gas6/Axl regulatory axis and evaluated a translationally viable nanotherapeutic strategy. We demonstrated that both Gas6 and Axl expressions are abnormally altered in clinical periprosthetic synovium and TiPs-treated macrophages. Utilizing genetic silencing Axl, we verified that Axl signaling acts as a critical driver of TiPs-induced STAT1/p65 activation and M1 polarization. Mechanistically, we elucidated that miR-155-5p promotes this inflammatory cascade via a unique dual-targeted mechanism: transcriptionally inhibiting Gas6 through Mafb repression and suppressing protective Axl cleavage via Adam10 inhibition. Finally, we engineered macrophage membrane-camouflaged PLGA-PEI nanoparticles loaded with antagomir-155-5p (Anta-155@MNPs). These biomimetic nanoparticles successfully targeted the inflammatory loci, suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and effectively ameliorated TiPs-induced calvarial osteolysis in vivo. Collectively, our findings uncover a novel epigenetic mechanism driving particle-induced synovitis and establish a promising, macrophage-targeted biomimetic strategy for preventing and treating AL.

TiPs promote proinflammatory cytokine production in both AL patient synovium and macrophages

Image analysis (Bresalier's method) was performed to quantify the staining intensity scores: the scores for TNF-α were 0.49 ± 0.14 in the FHN group and 1.94 ± 0.24 in the AL group (P < 0.05) and the scores for IL-6 were 0.31 ± 0.25 in the FHN group and 1.71 ± 0.19 in the AL group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A and B). We performed mRNA profiling and pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to identify key regulators of TiPs-induced macrophage inflammation. A volcano plot (Fig. S1A) showed that 1486 genes were upregulated (log2 fold change [log2FC] > 1, P < 0.05) and 1144 genes were downregulated (log2FC < −1, P < 0.05) in TiP-stimulated macrophages. All the DEGs were uploaded to the STRING database to construct a protein–protein interaction network (PPIN), and the CytoHubba plugin was used to screen the hub genes (Fig. S1B). However, the list of hub genes was extensive and included well-known inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF and IL6). Our primary goal was to identify the core upstream regulator. We performed gene set enrichment analysis to narrow the list of candidate hub genes, and the results revealed that the top three activated genes were related to the inflammatory response, JAK–STAT signaling, and TNF-α-mediated NF-κB signaling; notably, Axl was annotated in the inflammatory response gene set (Fig. S1C). We further annotated the DEGs using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, and the top 10 enriched pathways are shown in Fig. S1D; among these pathways, TNF signaling and NF-κB signaling were the most prominent biological pathways that were changed in TiPs-stimulated macrophages. Confocal imaging of immunofluorescence staining for p65 (a marker of NF-κB) and Stat1 revealed that after 4 h of TiPs stimulation, the transcription factors Stat1 and NF-κB translocated into the macrophage nucleus. These findings indicated that both the JAK–STAT pathway and the NF-κB pathway are involved in TiPs-induced macrophage activation, which is consistent with the KEGG pathway enrichment results (Fig. 1E).

Based on these bioinformatics findings and the literature suggesting an association between Axl and inflammatory pathways, we constructed an Axl-centered PPIN to identify potential interaction modules that may regulate or be regulated by Axl (Fig. S1E). This network revealed that multiple cytokine families, including Il6, Il1b, Tnf, and Ifn1b, and their regulatory mediators (Socs1 and Socs3) directly interact with Axl. These results collectively suggest that Axl is a key regulator of TiPs-induced macrophage inflammation.

Finally, we investigated the expression of Gas6 and Axl in the periprosthetic microenvironment using tissue immunofluorescence (IF) staining. Synovial IF staining showed significantly more Axl+/Cd68+ (macrophage-specific marker) and Gas6+/Cd68+ double-positive macrophages in the AL group than in the FHN group, indicating that Gas6/Axl signaling plays an important role in periprosthetic inflammation (Fig. 1F and G).

The Gas6/Axl pathway enhances TiPs-Induced inflammatory responses and proinflammatory cytokine production in macrophages

With prolonged TiPs stimulation, both the Axl transcript and protein expression levels were significantly increased (Fig. 2A and B). Flow cytometry further confirmed the concurrent upregulation of Axl expression on the macrophage surface: the percentage of Axl+ macrophages was 4.2 ± 1.1% in the PBS group compared with 22.3 ± 4.5% in the TiPs group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C, Fig. S2C). We first evaluated the knockdown efficiency of three Axl-targeting shRNAs and selected shAxl#1 for subsequent experiments (Fig. S2A and B). Compared with control shNC lentivirus, Axl knockdown significantly reduced the TiPs-induced phosphorylation of p65 and Stat1, as reflected by decreased p-p65/p65 and p-Stat1/Stat1 ratios (Fig. 2D and S2D). Cellular immunofluorescence staining further showed that Axl knockdown inhibited the translocation of p65 and Stat1 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus (Fig. 2E). Together, these results demonstrated that Axl is critical for the activation of inflammatory pathways (e.g., JAK–STAT and NF-κB signaling) in TiPs-stimulated macrophages. Using a time course of 1, 2, 4, and 8 h, we found that Axl knockdown reduced the production and transcript levels of proinflammatory factors at all tested time points (Fig. 2F and G). Additionally, after 6 h of TiPs stimulation, flow cytometry analysis of iNOS (a marker of M1 polarization) and CD206 (a marker of M2 polarization) showed that Axl knockdown decreased the percentage of iNOS+ macrophages by 22.0 ± 3.1% (P < 0.05) and increased the percentage of CD206+ macrophages by 15.3 ± 2.2% (P < 0.05). These changes specifically reduced the abundance of the iNOS+/CD206- macrophage subset, directly confirming an association between Axl expression and TiPs-induced M1 polarization of macrophages (Fig. 2H, Fig. S2E). We further investigated the relationship between Axl activity and titanium particle (TiPs)-induced inflammation in macrophages. Macrophages were pre-incubated with R428, a specific Axl activity inhibitor, for 24 h. The results demonstrated that inhibition of Axl activity markedly downregulated the expression of TNF-α and IL-6 (Fig. S2G), and significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of p65 and STAT1 (Fig. S2H). We also examined the expression and secretion of Gas6, and TiPs stimulation significantly increased Gas6 protein levels in whole-cell lysates and its concentration in conditioned medium (Fig. 2I and J). Preincubation of macrophages with recombinant mouse Gas6 further increased the expression and production of proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 2K and L).

Axl is cleaved by ADAM10 in TiPs-Stimulated macrophages

Investigating the upstream mechanisms regulating the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway is critical for developing targeted therapeutic strategies for AL. A structural analysis of the Axl receptor revealed three distinct domains: the extracellular domain, transmembrane domain, and intracellular domain. Notably, the extracellular domain can be cleaved and released into the extracellular microenvironment, where it may function as a decoy receptor to antagonize Gas6/Axl signaling activation [26]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that a disintegrin and metallopeptidase domain 10 (ADAM10) acts as the primary sheddase responsible for cleaving the Axl receptor and generating the soluble form of Axl (sAxl). However, the role of sAxl in regulating inflammatory responses remains largely unclear [[27], [28], [29]]. We preincubated macrophages with recombinant mouse Axl (rmAxl) for 24 h to investigate sAxl functions. Compared with the control treatment, rmAxl treatment significantly reduced the expression of M1 polarization markers (Nos2 and Cd86) (Fig. 3B). Concurrently, reverse transcription–quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT–qPCR) revealed that rmAxl also decreased the transcript levels of Tnf and Il6 (Fig. 3C).

We next analyzed ADAM10 expression and localization to explore its role in Axl shedding. Immunoblotting revealed that TiPs stimulation had little effect on the expression of pro-ADAM10 dimers but significantly reduced the level of mature ADAM10 (Fig. 3D and S3A). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining showed that TiPs stimulation induced the translocation of cytoplasmic ADAM10 to the cell membrane (Fig. 3E), suggesting that ADAM10 may mediate Axl shedding through direct interactions with membrane-localized Axl.

We pretreated macrophages with either (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, an ADAM10 agonist) or GI254023X (an ADAM10 inhibitor) prior to TiPs stimulation to confirm ADAM10-dependent Axl shedding. Activating ADAM10 with EGCG increased sAxl production in the supernatant and decreased the expression of membrane-bound Axl (Fig. 3F and G, Figure S3B), demonstrating that even with reduced overall expression, TiP-recruited membrane-localized ADAM10 still retained the ability to cleave Axl.

Mafb transcriptionally represses Gas6 production in TiPs-Stimulated macrophages

MAF bZIP transcription factor B (Mafb), a member of the Maf family of transcription factors, is strongly associated with macrophage differentiation [30]. Previous studies targeting Mafb have shown that it mitigates inflammation by inhibiting the cellular uptake of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [31]. We first analyzed the Gas6 promoter sequence (−2000 to +100 bp relative to the transcription start site [TSS]) using the JASPAR database to investigate the transcriptional regulation of Gas6 by Mafb. This analysis identified a MAF recognition element (MARE) and predicted Mafb as a putative transcriptional regulator of Gas6 signaling, along with the predicted binding motif (Fig. 3H–J). In support of this prediction, reanalysis of existing Mafb-targeted ChIP-seq datasets (GSM1964739 and GSM1964741) revealed the significant enrichment of Mafb at the Gas6 promoter region, further suggesting that Mafb may exert its biological effects through the regulation of Gas6 transcription (Fig. 3I). We cotransfected HEK293T cells with a luciferase reporter plasmid containing the Gas6 promoter and either a Mafb overexpression plasmid or an empty vector control (vector NC) to directly validate the Mafb-mediated regulation of Gas6 transcription. Dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that luciferase activity decreased after the transfection of increasing amounts of the Mafb overexpression plasmid, confirming that Mafb functions as a negative transcriptional regulator of Gas6 (Fig. 3K). We next examined the dynamics of Mafb expression upon TiPs stimulation: RT–qPCR and western blot analyses showed that Mafb expression was downregulated in a time-dependent manner following TiPs treatment (Fig. 3L and M, and S3C). We then validated the efficacy of three Mafb siRNA sequences and a Mafb overexpression plasmid (Fig. S3D and E), and selected si-Mafb#1 and si-Mafb#2 for subsequent experiments based on their silencing efficiency. Functional assays showed that Mafb knockdown increased Gas6 expression, whereas Mafb overexpression reduced Gas6 protein levels (Fig. 3N and S3F).

miR-155-5p targets Mafb and ADAM10 to regulate the Gas6/Axl pathway and TiPs-Induced macrophage inflammation

We performed miRNA-seq of the control group and TiPs-stimulated group, and a volcano plot of the miRNA-seq data was subsequently generated (Fig. S4A). Using the ENCORI database, we first predicted potential miRNAs that target Mafb and ADAM10. We then intersected these two candidate miRNA sets with the differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) identified in our miRNA-seq data. This analysis revealed that only miR-155-5p has the potential to coregulate both Mafb and ADAM10 (Fig. 4A). Based on the RNA-seq data, we constructed a dual-layer miRNA–mRNA coexpression network at www.omicsnet.ca. miR-155-5p was identified as a core mediator that potentially regulates multiple mRNAs, indicating its robust regulatory capacity in TiPs-induced inflammation (Fig. S4B). Genes targeted by miR-155-5p were strongly correlated with the hub gene network (including Axl), suggesting that miR-155-5p may modulate Axl-driven inflammatory responses (Fig. S4C). TiPs stimulation resulted in 50 upregulated miRNAs (log2FC > 1, P < 0.05) and 23 downregulated miRNAs (log2FC < −1, P < 0.05). We validated the top 6 differentially expressed miRNAs (|log2FC| < 4, P < 0.01) using RT–qPCR, and only miR-155-5p and miR-155-3p were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4B). We then synthesized miR-155-5p/3p inhibitors and their negative controls, which were transfected into macrophages. The therapeutic effect of Anta-155@MNPs was assessed in vitro and in vivo

In vivo experiments were also performed to verify the regulatory effects of the nanoparticles. The mice were randomized into the following six groups subjected to different treatments: (i) the sham group; (ii) TiPs group; (iii) TiPs + NC@MNP group; (iv) TiPs + Anta-155 group; (v) TiPs + Anta-155@NP group; (vi) TiPs + Anta-155@MNP group. Mouse calvaria were collected for micro-CT scanning and immunostaining. We locally injected mice with calvarial osteolysis with gradient doses of Anta-155@MNPs (0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 nmol per mouse) to determine the optimal dose. Micro-CT analysis of the BMD in the osteolytic area allowed us to generate a dose–response curve, with an EC50 of 2.43 nmol/mouse (Fig. S6B). After 14 days, no significant differences in body weight were observed between the groups (Fig. S6C), and no toxicity symptoms (e.g., altered mental status, abnormal activity, anorexia, diarrhea, or constipation) were noted. Serum analyses revealed that alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels in the treatment groups were within normal ranges, with no significant differences compared with those in the control group (Fig. S6D and E). Additionally, a histopathological examination of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney from mice receiving local nanoparticle injections revealed no obvious abnormalities (Fig. S6F). Micro-CT scanning and 3D reconstructions revealed that compared with Anta-155@NPs or Anta-155 alone, Anta-155@MNPs alleviated TiPs-induced calvarial osteolysis more significantly (Fig. 7A). Compared with the Anta-155 and NC@MNP groups, the Anta-155@MNP group presented significant increases in both the BV/TV and BMD (Fig. S7A and S7B). This group also exhibited reduced TNF-α and IL-6 production (Fig. 7B,S7C, and S7D) and the induction of osteocalcin expression (Fig. 7C and S7E). For in vivo distribution studies, we labeled the antagomir with Cy7 and prepared nanoparticles using the same method. In vivo fluorescence imaging showed that the nanoparticles remained stable for 14 days after local injection, whereas the fluorescence intensity from the Cy7-labeled antagomir alone gradually decreased (Fig. S7F). Fluorescence imaging of the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and lung), skull, and brain confirmed the exclusive distribution of nanoparticles in the skull, with no detectable signal in other organs (Fig. S7G). We collected mouse serum to measure the concentrations of TNF-α and IL-6. The results revealed that local injection of titanium particles increased serum levels of these inflammatory factors, whereas additional local administration of Anta-155@MNP partially reversed this effect (Fig. S7H). In summary, Anta-155@MNPs reprogrammed the osteolytic microenvironment and inhibited TiPs-induced osteolysis.

Discussion

Wear particle-induced osteolysis is a primary driver of aseptic prosthetic loosening—a major complication affecting the long-term survival of orthopedic implants. In the chronic periprosthetic microenvironment, macrophages are the most abundantly recruited cell type and play a central role in mediating the inflammatory and osteolytic responses triggered by wear debris [11], [32]. Upon stimulation with wear particles, macrophages are prone to secrete large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6. These cytokines not only promote osteoclastogenesis to accelerate bone resorption but also inhibit osteoblastogenesis. Additionally, direct inhibition of osteoblast function byTiPs represents another key contributor to periprosthetic osteolysis. Collectively, these findings highlight macrophages as critical therapeutic targets for alleviating particle-induced osteolysis, while the upstream regulators governing macrophage-mediated inflammation remain to be fully elucidated [1]. In this study, we identified Axl as a pivotal regulator that controls downstream STAT1 and NF-κB activation, macrophage polarization, and cytokine production. mRNA profiling confirmed that Axl is a hub gene, as Axl knockdown significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-6 expression as well as STAT1/NF-κB phosphorylation. Through the construction of an miRNA–mRNA coexpression network, miR-155-5p was predicted to regulate the network of hub genes, including Axl. Mechanistically, miR-155-5p promoted Gas6/Axl-mediated macrophage inflammatory responses by inhibiting ADAM10-dependent soluble Axl generation and repressing Mafb, a transcriptional inhibitor of Gas6.

In vivo experiments demonstrated that miR-155-5p significantly exacerbated TiPs-induced osteolysis via Axl: Axl knockdown alleviated TNF-α and IL-6 production, whereas miR-155-5p agomir injection reversed these effects. We further generated PLGA nanoparticles loaded with the miR-155-5p antagomir and encapsulated them in macrophage membranes. These nanoparticles were characterized for their zeta potential and size, and their ability to transfect macrophages and sequester cytokines was validated. Local injection of Anta-155@MNPs markedly increased the BMD and BV/TV, decreased TNF-α and IL-6 expression, and promoted OCN expression in the TiPs-modulated microenvironment.

In general, titanium particles have been indicated to act as inflammatory stimuli that activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs) and Toll-like receptors (TLRs) [12], [33], leading to the phosphorylation of STING [34], the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the activation of other inflammatory pathways [35], but the connections between PRRs and the inflammatory response in the osteolytic environment remain unclear. In our study, mRNA profiling identified Axl as a critical differentially expressed gene (DEG) that is involved in enriched inflammatory pathways. Notably, conflicting findings concerning the role of the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway in regulating inflammation—whether it promotes or suppresses inflammation—have been reported. For instance, Gas6/Axl binding has been shown to switch macrophages from an antibacterial phenotype to an M2-polarized state in response to pneumococcal infection [36]. Axl-deficient (Axl−/−) macrophages exhibit enhanced T-cell-induced M1 polarization in response to Trypanosoma cruzi infection [37]. In contrast, DeBerge et al. found that Axl activation promotes glycolytic metabolism and a proinflammatory macrophage phenotype. Additionally, they generated Axl cleavage-resistant mice; in these mice, impaired Gas6/Axl signaling was shown to protect cardiac tissue from ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) [38]. In patients with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), elevated Axl signaling promotes hepatic fibrosis and inflammation in Kupffer cells. Notably, bemcentinib—a specific Axl inhibitor—has been shown to alleviate experimental NASH [29]. The role of Gas6 in regulating inflammation remains controversial. Zagórska et al. recognized that Axl was activated and highly expressed when macrophages were challenged by inflammatory stimuli and that Mertk, another receptor in the TAM family that has an affinity for Gas6, was responsible for immune tolerance and homeostasis maintenance [39]. These results indicate that clear inflammatory stimuli, such as IRI and LPS, can activate Axl and promote intracellular and tissue inflammation. However, the role of Axl in diseases such as NASH and OA (osteoarthritis) is unclear and requires further in-depth research. The molecular biological events mediated by the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway are heterogeneous. When macrophages are exposed to inflammatory stimuli, the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway can mediate the activation of downstream inflammatory signaling pathways. In contrast, when apoptotic bodies are present extracellularly, Gas6 can also bind to phosphatidylserine on apoptotic bodies and bridge Axl to complete efferocytosis, thereby clearing apoptotic bodies and reducing tissue inflammation levels [32]. It is hypothesized that upon titanium particle stimulation, macrophages phagocytose non-degradable inert particles rather than apoptotic cells, and Gas6 cannot form the physiological "PS-Gas6-Axl" bridging complex. Furthermore, the particles themselves cause pro-inflammatory cascade and osteoclast formation [1]. This pathological phagocytosis possibly rewires the downstream signal transduction direction of Axl activation. In the present study, the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway acts as a proinflammatory mediator in macrophages stimulated with TiPs. In this context of TiPs-induced inflammation, the expression levels of both Axl and Gas6 are elevated. Notably, knocking down Axl expression modulates proinflammatory signaling pathways, including the JAK–STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways, as evidenced by the reduced phosphorylation of Stat1 and p65 and impaired nuclear translocation of these two inflammatory transcription factors. Additionally, the Gas6/Axl pathway positively regulates TiPs-induced cytokine production and M1 macrophage polarization. To further clarify the comprehensive regulatory landscape of Axl in macrophage activation, future studies will employ more systematic and in-depth strategies. Specifically, we will stimulate macrophages with multiple pathogenic stimuli, including TiPs, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and apoptotic cells, in combination with Axl knockdown and pharmacological inhibition using R428. Subsequently, multi-omics high-throughput analyses, including transcriptomics and proteomics, will be performed to identify the global downstream effectors and signaling pathways governed by Axl. This integrative approach will help establish a complete regulatory network centered on Axl and provide deeper insights into its functional versatility in modulating inflammatory responses during aseptic loosening.

In the field of therapeutic strategies for bone and joint disorders, microRNAs (miRNAs) offer distinct advantages [40], [41]. MiRNAs can precisely target and regulate the expression of genes closely associated with skeletal disorders [42], [43]. Additionally, their structural characteristics and molecular length increase their stability in the complex in vivo environment and facilitate membrane penetration to bind to intracellular targets [44]. With the support of appropriate carrier systems, microRNAs (miRNAs) can be precisely delivered to affected joint tissues [45]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) also significantly affect TiPs-induced osteolysis [46]. For example, previous research has shown that miR-92a targets and degrades KLF4, thereby altering the level of the p110δ transcript to regulate titanium particle (TiPs)-associated osteolysis [47]. Building on the aforementioned studies, we performed miRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) and constructed an miRNA–mRNA coexpression network. Through this analysis, miR-155-5p was predicted to be associated with the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway and the hub gene network, highlighting its potential to regulate TiP-induced biological processes. miR-155-5p has been well studied in diabetes, hypertension and inflammatory neural diseases as an initiator of inflammation and a key regulator of target genes [[48], [49], [50]]. However, the specific role of miR-155-5p in the osteolytic microenvironment remains incompletely elucidated. In our study, miR-155-5p promoted the TiP-induced activation of inflammatory pathways and cytokine production—effects that could be partially inhibited by Axl knockdown. Consistent with these findings, miR-155-5p also drove the M1 polarization of macrophages. Mechanistically, miR-155-5p downregulated Mafb and ADAM10 to regulate Gas6 and Axl, respectively. In vivo experiments further confirmed that the miR-155-5p/Axl axis exacerbated TiPs-induced osteolysis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that miR-155-5p acts as a key mediator of TiPs-induced inflammation and osteolysis via the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway, thereby providing novel targets for the prevention of periprosthetic osteolysis.

Multiple strategies using PLGA-based nanoparticles have been developed, leveraging their safety and biocompatibility for preventing aseptic prosthetic loosening [51]. Lin et al. developed si-Neat1@MPs—microparticles that load the si-lncRNA Neat1 onto a PLGA-PAMAM platform—to alleviate titanium particle (TiPs)-induced osteolysis by targeting the lncRNA Neat1 [35]. Moreover, Xie et al. engineered PLGA-based exosome (PLGA-Exos) carriers derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) for sustained delivery to osteolytic sites [52]. However, in the prosthetic microenvironment associated with AL, macrophages are extensively recruited. Owing to their phagocytic activity, the administration of drugs alone results in rapid clearance. Macrophage-biomimetic nanoparticles therefore exhibit significant potential as drug delivery platforms for this scenario [53]. We constructed a macrophage membrane-coated PLGA-PEI complex that encapsulates antagomiR-155-5p (Anta-155@MNP). We evaluated its efficacy in knocking down miR-155-5p, characterized the protein markers on the nanoparticles, and demonstrated its ability to alleviate osteolysis, inhibit cytokine production, and promote osteoblastogenesis. In miRNA therapy, off-target effects are among the key factors limiting therapeutic efficacy. With respect to the off-target effects of Anta-155@MNPs, our in vivo fluorescence imaging results showed that the nanoparticles were localized only to the skull region during the experiment, with no distribution detected in other organs or tissues. Furthermore, the ability of the coated cell membrane to target inflammatory regions is enhanced; together, these features endow it with an extremely low risk of off-target effects. In summary, Anta-155@MNPs directly target miR-155-5p, a key molecule that drives TiPs-induced inflammatory responses via the Gas6/Axl pathway. The macrophage membrane coating increases its sustained efficacy and targeting ability, providing novel insights for future therapeutic development.

Our study has several limitations. In the context of AL, we evaluated only the osteolytic effect of titanium particles, whereas other wear particles, such as CoCrMo, ceramics, and UHMWPE, can similarly induce periprosthetic osteolysis [54]. Therefore, the effect of silencing the miR-155-5p/Axl axis on osteolysis caused by these other types of wear debris warrants further in-depth investigation. Another notable limitation is the small clinical sample size, with only 6 AL and 6 FHN synovial samples included in the final analysis. Although these clinical data support the aberrant activation of the miR-155-5p/Gas6/Axl axis observed in vitro and in vivo, they only provide preliminary evidence of its clinical relevance. The limited cohort size restricts statistical robustness and generalizability of the clinical findings. Larger multicenter cohorts are required in future studies to further validate the clinical significance of this regulatory axis. In this study, we employed a mouse calvarial osteolysis model for in vivo animal experiments to investigate the specific mechanism of osteolysis induced by wear particles. This model is also widely used in research within this field. While the in situ implant model presents inherent challenges in isolating the specific contribution of wear particle-induced osteolysis—due to the presence of multiple confounding factors including surgical trauma, foreign body reaction, and concurrent mechanical stress—it remains the most clinically relevant preclinical model for evaluating implant-related complications [55]. To validate our findings under conditions that more closely approximate human joint replacement, we will establish an aseptic loosening model via titanium rod implantation into the femoral medullary cavity in subsequent studies [56]. This model faithfully recapitulates the core biomechanical features of clinical aseptic loosening, including physiological weight-bearing stress, implant micromotion, and synovial fluid pressure gradients, which cannot be fully replicated in the calvarial osteolysis model. Further optimization of Anta-155@MNPs could focus on engineering the macrophage membrane—for instance, by overexpressing cytokine receptors and TAM receptors—to better elucidate its potential for neutralizing proinflammatory factors.

In conclusion, this study revealed that miR-155-5p, a therapeutic target, promotes TiPs-induced macrophage inflammation and osteolysis via the Gas6/Axl signaling pathway. It also revealed the intrinsic connection between miR-155-5p and the Gas6/Axl axis, which is mediated by Adam10 and Mafb. Furthermore, we developed Anta-155@MNPs, which exhibit excellent efficacy in alleviating TiP-induced osteolysis. These findings reveal a novel mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of AL and provide new insights for the development of therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Clinical specimens

Based on the tissue origin and anatomical location, while excluding core confounding factors relevant to the study and adhering to ethical requirements, we selected synovial tissues from patients with femoral head necrosis (FHN) as the control group for periprosthetic tissues from patients with AL. After informed consent was obtained from the patients, all clinical specimens were collected from the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital. These specimens included synovial membranes from 8 patients with FHN and 7 patients with AL of hip prostheses(After excluding slices with quality defects, the samples finally enrolled in the experiment were derived from 6 FHN patients and 6 AL patients). Patient demographics and baseline characteristics are presented in Table S3 and Table S4.

Animal surgery

Ten-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Animal Laboratory of Sun Yat-sen University for the establishment of a cranial osteolysis model and subsequent in vivo experiments. Under anesthesia, a 10-mm midline sagittal incision was made on the mouse skull to expose the calvarium. A gelatin sponge (0.5 × 0.5 × 0.3 cm3) was then placed at the bregma for subsequent injection. In the experiment displayed in Fig. 5, the mice were divided into six different groups of six mice each as follows: (1) the sham-operation group; (2) the titanium particle-treated control group; (3) the group treated with titanium particles, NC agomir and sh-NC lentivirus; (4) the group treated with titanium particles, NC agomir and sh-Axl lentivirus; (5) the group treated with titanium particles, miR-155-5p agomir and sh-NC lentivirus; and (6) the group treated with titanium particles, miR-155-5p agomir and sh-Axl lentivirus. Three milligrams of titanium particles was suspended in 100 μl of a mixture of PBS or shRNA lentivirus and miRNA agomir. The mixture for each mouse contained 35 μl of virus fluid (1×10^8 TU/ml titer) and 65 μl of the miRNA agomir solution (3 nmol of agomir dissolved in 65 μl of ddH2O). For the in vivo experiments assessing the nanoparticles, the mice were divided into six different groups of six mice each as follows: (1) a sham-operation group; (2) a control group treated with titanium particles; (3) a group treated with titanium particles and NC@MNPs; (4) a group treated with titanium particles and antagomir-155-5p; (5) a group treated with titanium particles Anta-155@NPs; and (6) a group treated with titanium particles and Anta-155@MNPs. Each mouse was injected with nanoparticles containing 3 nmol of antagomir/NC or 3 nmol of antagomir alone. All the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following intraperitoneal anesthesia on postoperative Day 14, after which their calvaria were harvested. Mouse venous blood was collected from the tail vein. This in vivo animal experiment was approved by and conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Sun Yat-sen University.

Micro-CT scanning

Calvarial micro-CT scanning and 3D reconstructions were fully described in our previous studies [57]. All calvaria were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and scanned using a microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) system (ZKKS-MCT-Sharp, Zhongke Kaisheng Medical Technology). The acquired data were further subjected to quantitative analysis and three-dimensional (3D) model reconstruction using the accompanying software (ZZKS-Micro-CT4.1), with the scanning parameters set at 60 kV and 667 μA. Subsequently, a region of interest (ROI) with dimensions of 1 × 3 × 3 mm3 was defined to measure bone mineral density (BMD), the ratio of bone volume to total volume (BV/TV), and the area of osteolysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining and tissue immunofluorescence (IF-tissue) staining

For the synovial membrane samples, the tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by dehydration with xylene, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. After overnight fixation with 4% PFA, the mouse calvarial specimens were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, pH 7.4; E1171; Solarbio) for three weeks. The decalcified samples were subsequently embedded in paraffin blocks at 54 °C and sectioned.

For IHC staining, histological sections were heated for deparaffinization, followed by rehydration using gradient concentrations of xylene and ethanol. Next, 0.1% Triton X-100 (9002–93–1; Sigma–Aldrich) was used for permeabilization, and pepsin (ZLI-9013; ZSGB-BIO) was used for antigen retrieval. After the sections were incubated with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA; CCS30014.01, Molecular Research Center, Inc.) for 1 h, they were incubated with diluted primary antibody overnight at 4 °C (Note: 4 °C is the standard temperature for the primary antibody incubation; adjust if your protocol uses a different condition). The sections were then washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash), followed by an incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit, ab6721; Abcam) at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the sections were stained sequentially with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; P0203; Beyotime Biotechnology) and hematoxylin (C0107; Beyotime Biotechnology). After staining, the sections were mounted with neutral balsam and observed under a microscope (NI-U, Nikon).

The intensity score was evaluated according to the Bresalier scoring system. All histological sections were examined by two pathologists in a double-blind manner. Each pathologist randomly selected 10 regions per section and assigned a staining score using a specific scale: 0 (no staining), 1 (weak staining), 2 (moderate staining), and 3 (strong staining). The corresponding frequency grades (F0–F3) were recorded based on the number of regions falling into each score category. The final intensity score was calculated using the following formula: Σ (0 × F0 + 1 × F1 + 2 × F2 + 3 × F3).

For tissue immunofluorescence (IF) staining, the sections were first subjected to heating, deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. The sections were subsequently coincubated with primary antibodies derived from different hosts (rat or mouse), followed by an incubation with the corresponding fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies. After staining, the sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (P0131; Beyotime Biotechnology), and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (BX63; Olympus). The percentage of positive cells for both the IHC and tissue IF samples was calculated using Image J software.

Titanium particles (TiPs) preparation

Our previous study described the complete procedure for the generation of sterilized TiPs [58]. The original TiPs solution (<20 μm, W08A030, Alfa Aesar) was filtered through Isopore filter membranes (10 μm/1.2 μm/0.2 μm pore sizes, TCTP04700/RTTP04700/GTTP04700, Millipore) in batches. Afterward, we removed the particles from the membranes and immersed them in 75% ethanol in a culture dish for 48 h. The dish was placed in a ventilated area and the TiPs were thoroughly dried. The filtered particles were further sterilized using ethylene oxide and subjected to endotoxin detection (Limulus assay, EC80545, Bioendo), which requires <0.25 EU/ml. After they were weighed, the particles were diluted in PBS to a concentration of 9 × 10−3 g/ml for in vitro experiments. Each mouse received a local injection of 3 mg titanium particles to establish a skull osteolysis model.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 macrophages (CL-0190, Procell) were utilized in this research. High-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (C11995500BT; Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099141; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (SV30010; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was prepared for RAW264.7 cell cultivation. Before titanium particle stimulation or an incubation with other compounds, the macrophages were seeded in 6- or 12-well plates and incubated for 24 h until they reached 80% confluence. According to previously published studies, macrophages were treated with titanium particles for 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h to conduct subsequent experiments [34], [35], [47]. To induce osteoclast differentiation, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL RANKL for 5 consecutive days.

Lentivirus, siRNA, agomir and antagomir transfection

The lentiviruses for knocking down Axl (NCBI GeneID: 26362) were constructed by Cyagen, and NC lentiviruses were designated as controls. Lentiviruses (1 × 107 TU/ml) were added to the complete medium and incubated with cells for 48 h to ensure stable transfection; subsequently, puromycin was applied to select the stably transfected cell strains.

Plasmids expressing Mafb (NCBI GeneID: 16658), siRNAs targeting Mafb and Gas6 (NCBI GeneID: 14456) and agomir/antagomir of miR-155-5p were constructed by GenePharma, and the mock plasmid, NC siRNA and NC agomir/antagomir were used as controls. After the macrophages were seeded into plates and reached 60% confluence, the plasmid/siRNA/agomir/antagomir–Lipofectamine LTX (15338100; Thermo Fisher Scientific) complex diluted in OptiMEM (31985070; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the cells and incubated for 24 h. After replacing the transfection medium with complete medium, further experiments could be conducted.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from seeded macrophages using an EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (B0004D, EZBioscience), and the RNA concentration was evaluated using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (ND-2000; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Next, the extracted RNA was mixed with 4×Reverse Transcription Master Mix (A0010GQ, EZBioscience) and reverse transcribed to obtain cDNA, which was subsequently blended with mRNA/miRNA primer, ddH2O and 2×SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (ROX2 plus) (A0001-R2, EZBioscience), and RT–qPCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler 96 Real-Time PCR system (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc.). The expression data were analyzed with the comparative 2−ΔΔCq method. For the analysis of miRNAs, the extracted RNA was mixed with 4× microRNA reverse transcription mix (EZB-miRT3, A0010GQ, EZBioscience) and a specific reverse primer to generate cDNA. The related primer sequences are presented in Table S2.

Western blot

The detailed process of Western blot has been described previously. Briefly, mixtures of whole-cell lysate, PMSF (P0100-1; Solarbio), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (CW2383; CW Biotech) were consecutively centrifuged and quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific); loading buffer was added, and the samples were denatured. After electrophoresis and electrotransfer, the membrane containing proteins was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (ST023; Beyotime) and incubated with diluted primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After the membranes were incubated with HRP-linked antibodies, we added Super ECL detection reagent (36208ES60; Yeasen) to the immunoblots, which were subsequently visualized using a digital imaging system (Syngen G:BOX Chmi XT4). In this study, GAPDH, β-actin, and β-tubulin were selected as internal controls. The main reason for choosing these three molecules was to expose the target proteins in the same Western blot, eliminating the need to run parallel gels. For some target proteins with molecular weights similar to that of a certain internal reference, an alternative internal reference had to be selected. GAPDH, β-actin, and β-tubulin served as stable internal controls in the macrophage model stimulated by wear particles [35], [59], [60].

Cytokine assays and ALT and BUN evaluations

The culture medium from adherent macrophage cultures was collected and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant, which was assessed using TNF-α- and IL-6-specific instant enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (EMC102a.96 for the TNFα ELISA and EMC004.96 for the IL-6 ELISA; Neobioscience). After the mouse blood samples were harvested, ALT levels were measured with an Amplex Red Alanine Aminotransferase Activity Assay Kit (P2711S; Beyotime), and BUN levels were measured with a Urea Nitrogen Assay Kit (S0574S; Beyotime).

For cytokines neutralization assays, RAW264.7 macrophages were stimulated with titanium particles (TiPs) for 24 h. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to completely remove residual cells and particles. Equal volumes of the cell-free supernatant were incubated with 200 μg/ml Anta-155@NPs or Anta-155@MNPs at 37 °C for 2 h with gentle shaking. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min to pellet the nanoparticles. The concentrations of TNF-α and IL-6 in the supernatant were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunofluorescence staining of cells and confocal laser scanning microscopy

The prepared macrophages were transferred to confocal plates (BDD011035; Jet Bio Filtration Co., Ltd.) for TiP stimulation, immunostaining, and scanning. After fixation with 4% PFA, 0.1% Triton X-100 was added for permeabilization of the cell membranes. Afterward, the cells were blocked with 1% BSA and subsequently incubated with diluted primary antibodies overnight. After three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies. Finally, all the cells in the confocal plates were incubated with DAPI and antifade mounting medium (HNFD-02; HelixGen Co., Ltd.) and observed under a confocal microscope (LSM 710; Carl Zeiss). A fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay was performed with a FISH kit for miR-155-5p (GenePharma). The probe was labeled with Cy3, and the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73).

Flow cytometry

After incubation and TiPs stimulation, the macrophages were removed from the plates and placed into glass tubes for flow cytometry. Then, we centrifuged the tubes and washed the cells with PBS. The cells were fixed with fixation medium (Medium A) (GAS001S100; Thermo Fisher Scientific), permeabilized with permeabilization medium (Medium B) (GAS002S5; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and incubated with a PE-Cy7-conjugated iNOS monoclonal antibody and APC-conjugated CD206 monoclonal antibody. We directly incubated the cells with an APC-conjugated Axl monoclonal antibody after fixation to detect Axl expression on the cell membrane. Cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSVerse, BDBiosciences).

Dual luciferase reporter assay

A pGL3-Basic plasmid containing the Gas6 promoter constructed by IGEBio was used to validate the regulatory effect of Mafb on the promoter of Gas6. HEK293T cells were seeded in a 12-well plate and cultured until they reached 80% confluence. After the cells were transfected with plasmids carrying the Gas6 promoter, vector- and Mafb-overexpressing plasmids at different ratios were transfected. Luciferase activity was detected with Firefly/Renilla Luciferase Assay Reagent by SYNERGY H1 (Bio-Tek) and normalized to Renilla luciferase activity in cells expressing pRL-TK plasmids.

RNA-seq and analysis

The procedure for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was described in detail in our previous study. Briefly, following 24 h of stimulation with titanium particles (TiPs), total RNA was extracted from macrophages. After quality control was performed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and qPCR, cDNA libraries were constructed. Specifically, ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) were removed to enrich mRNAs and noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), which were then fragmented, reverse transcribed into fragmented cDNAs, modified by the addition of a poly(A) tail, and ligated with Illumina sequencing adapters. The cDNAs were subsequently digested with uracil-N-glycosylase (UNG), subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis for size selection, and amplified by PCR. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. After the quality control analysis, the clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) using MapSplice, and gene-level read counts were quantified with HTSeq. Small RNAs (18–30 nt) were enriched by PAGE, ligated with 3′ and 5′ adapters, reverse-transcribed, and amplified by PCR. The 140–160 bp PCR products were gel-purified to construct the cDNA library, which was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw reads were filtered to generate clean tags by removing low-quality, adapter-contaminated, and short (<18 nt) reads. Clean tags were mapped to GenBank, Rfam, and the mouse reference genome to discard rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, snoRNA, and mRNA-degradation fragments. Known miRNAs were identified against miRBase (Release 22), and novel miRNAs were predicted using Mireap_v0.2. miRNA expression was normalized to TPM.

In this study, the volcano plots, GSEA and KEGG enrichment analyses, as well as the Venn diagrams were all generated on www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en, while the multi-layered miRNA-Gene co-expression network was generated on www.omicsnet.ca. The dataset has been assigned the accession number: PRJNA1372466 and PRJNA1456954.

Preparation of PLGA nanoparticles carrying RNA and PEI complexes

Mixing RNA (antagomir of miR-155-5p) and branched PEI resulted in the formation of complexes, which were subsequently encapsulated in PLGA nanoparticles. First, 1 mg of synthetic RNA was mixed with 150 μL of DEPC-treated water containing 0.5% (w/v) branched PEI and then incubated at room temperature for 30 min. This solution was subsequently mixed with an appropriate amount of 3% (w/v) PLGA polymer solution and subjected to 3 min of sonication on ice to form a water-in-oil (W/O) nanoemulsion. The emulsion was then gradually added to 5 mL of a 2% (w/v) PVA aqueous solution and subjected to sonication in an ice bath for 1 min to form a water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) emulsion, followed by stirring at room temperature for 12 h to evaporate the dichloromethane. The resulting nanoparticles were then recovered by centrifugation at 20,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and washed three times with DEPC-treated water to remove unencapsulated RNA. The obtained RNA-PEI PLGA nanoparticles were then ready for further use.

Preparation of macrophage membrane-coated nucleic acid-loaded PLGA nanoparticles

Extraction of Macrophage Membrane Vesicles: RAW 264.7 macrophages were washed with PBS (containing 0.01 M Tris and 0.001 M MgCl2) and then suspended in buffer. The cell suspension was homogenized using an IKA-T10 homogenizer at 22,000 rpm for 1 min, followed by centrifugation at 800 rpm for 15 min to remove precipitated impurities. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 20,000 rpm to obtain the cell membrane vesicles, which were then set aside for subsequent use.

Preparation of Cell Membrane-Coated RNA Nanoparticles: The cell membrane vesicles were mixed with the aforementioned RNA-loaded PLGA nanoparticles (with a membrane protein/PLGA weight ratio of 2:1), followed by sonication under ice bath conditions using a 200 W probe sonicator for 2–3 min. The resulting mixture was sequentially extruded through 1000 nm, 400 nm, 200 nm, and 100 nm polycarbonate membranes. Finally, the excess cell membrane material was removed by ultracentrifugation.

Determination of the particle size and zeta potential, and transmission electron microscopy observations of the morphology of nanoparticles

The obtained nanoparticles were diluted 1:50 with ultrapure water, and the particle size and surface zeta potential in pure water were measured using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer Mastersizer 3000. For transmission electron microscopy observations, 10 μL of the nanoparticle suspension was deposited onto a copper grid, negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid, and then observed using a JEOL 2010 transmission electron microscope.

Statistics

All experiments were independently repeated three or more times to mitigate bias. To avoid conflating technical and biological variation, n is defined as the number of biologically independent units: (i) biologically independent replicates for in vitro experiments, (ii) individual mice per group for in vivo studies, and (iii) individual patient samples for clinical tissue analyses. The data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism (ver. 9.3.0), and normality was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The significance of differences between two groups was determined using a t test, whereas one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons of one or two variables among multiple groups along with Bonferroni correction. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference between the intervention groups and control groups. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. The data are shown as the means±SDs.
