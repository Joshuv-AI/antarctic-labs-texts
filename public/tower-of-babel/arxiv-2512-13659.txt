When is a cut and project set substitutional?
Edmund Harriss, Henna Koivusalo, James J. Walton

Abstract
. Cut and project sets are obtained by projecting an irrational slice through a lattice to a lower dimensional subspace. Under standard conditions, the resulting pattern has no translational periods even though it retains some regularity of the lattice. Cut and project sets are one of the archetypical examples of patterns featuring aperiodic order, the other construction methods being by substitution and matching rules. Many early examples of aperiodic tilings, including the famous Penrose and Ammann–Beenker tilings, have a description from all of these methods. In this article we answer the following question, in the case of a Euclidean total space: what property of the cut and project data characterises when the resulting cut and project sets may also be defined by a substitution rule?

1.
Introduction

A
tiling

\cT
of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a collection
(T_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of topologically regular subsets (i.e., equal to the closures of their interiors) that intersect only on their boundaries and cover all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
Delone set
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a collection of points that is relatively dense and uniformly discrete. We use the term pattern to refer to both tilings and Delone sets (or variants of these where tiles or points may carry labels). The field of Aperiodic Order
[
BG13
]
studies patterns that have no translational periods but feature a good amount of structure, in some sense. The main methods for producing aperiodic order are by matching rules, substitutions and cut and project.

The matching rule tilings are colections of shapes that can tile the plane but only non-periodically
[
Ber66
,
Gar89
,
Rob71
,
Kar96
,
JR21
]
, such as the famous Penrose tiles and the recently discovered Hat einstein
[
SMKGS24
]
. The discovery of such tilesets in the ’60s and ’70s invigorated the study of tilings and patterns, with a quest to discover general methods that could create such tiles. Two powerful methods were developed, hierarchic/substitution methods that expanded and replaced tiles to create larger patches and projection methods that brought tilings down from higher dimensions. Many classical examples of matching rule tilings, examples, such as the Penrose tiling and the Hat tiling that can be generated in both of these ways.

Let us first focus on tile substitutions (or, used somewhat less commonly, Delone set substitution
[
LW03
]
). Symbolic substitutions are classical in Dynamics
[
Mor21
,
BI94
]
, and symbolic substitutions have relevance in tiling theory and vice versa
[
Fog02
]
. Loosely speaking, they are defined by inflate and replace rules that produce arbitrarily large patches of patterns. Proving that a given matching rule enforces hierarchy (often described by some substitution) is one of the more common approaches to proving aperiodicity of a matching rule tiling. For example, the Penrose tilings
[
Pen80
]
and many other classical examples have a description from substitutions as well as from matching rules.

We note at this point that a substitution rule
\sigma
defines not a single tiling, but rather a collection of finite patches that, in turn, defines a collection of admitted tilings that are all
locally isomorphic
(at least in the translationally repetitive/primitive case), that is, they all have the same finite patches, up to translation. In the nonperiodic case, there are uncountably many such tilings, even modulo translation equivalence, which together form a
tiling space
or
translational hull

\Omega

[
Sad08
,
AP98
]
. The tiling space is a topological dynamical system, when equipped with a natural topology and the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-action of translation. The original substitution acts continuously on
\Omega
and, as a result of aperiodicity, is a homeomorphism on
\Omega
. That is, it is invertible: each tiling
\cT\in\Omega
has a unique substitutive pre-image
\cT^{\prime}=\sigma^{-1}(\cT)\in\Omega
. This result is known as unique composition, or recognisability; see
[
Sol98
]
for primitive stone inflation rules or
[
Wal25
]
for a more general statement in the non-repetitive case that extends beyond tilings, to general so-called
L
-sub FLC pattern spaces.

The third method is by via cut and project schemes. Cut and project sets are obtained by taking an irrational slice through a lattice and projecting it to a lower dimensional subspace. More formally, a cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
consists of the following data: two (positive-dimensional) transversal subspaces
\phy
,
\intl<\mathbb{R}^{k}
, respectively called the
physical
and
internal
space, and a lattice
\Gamma<\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We restrict here to a Euclidean
total space

\mathbb{R}^{k}
(although it is also of interest to allow non-Euclidean internal and total space). Let
\pi_{\vee}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\phy
be the projection with respect to the decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{k}=\phy+\intl
. Then, given a
window

W\subset\intl
, we define the
cut and project set
:

\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W)\coloneqq\pi_{\vee}(\Gamma\cap(W+\phy)).

In other words, we
cut
the lattice
\Gamma
with the
strip

W+\phy
, and then
project
it to the physical space. For further details, see Section
2.4
.

Throughout, we use the standard assumptions that
\pi_{\vee}
is injective on
\Gamma
and that the projection of
\Gamma
to the internal space is dense (otherwise, certain reductions can be performed). We also take the projection of the lattice to internal space to be injective, which ensures non-periodicity. It is not hard to then show that
\cps
is a Delone set if
W
is compact and topologically regular (which we always will do in this article).

Just as how substitutions do not merely define single tilings, a cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
with window
W
more naturally defines a whole translational hull
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
of Delone sets which, due to the above restrictions, are all locally isomorphic, non-periodic and repetitive. This set of patterns is defined by taking non-singular translates of the lattice (those not intersecting the boundary of the strip), then cutting and projecting this, and completing this collection by adding any limiting patterns corresponding to the singular translates.

The cut and project method was first given by Meyer in 1972 as a tool to study certain problems in harmonic analysis and Diophantine approximation
[
Mey72
]
. Just as many of the early substitution patterns were proven to have matching rule descriptions and vice versa, many cut and project patterns can also be generated from these other descriptions. For example, it was shown by de Bruijn in 1981 that this is true of Penrose tilings
[
dB81
]
.

Comparing the patterns that can be generated by these different methods is of fundamental importance to the theory of Aperiodic Order. It was shown by Goodman–Strauss in 1998 that essentially every substitution tiling can be enforced, in a certain sense, as a matching rule tiling
[
GS98
]
(also see
[
Moz89
]
for similar considerations in the special case of Wang tiles). For a certain class of cut and project schemes, Bédaride and Fernique
[
BF17
]
found a characterisation of the existence of matching rules (also see
[
Bur88
,
Lev88
,
Soc90
,
BF15
,
FL24
]
for earlier and supplementary results). The problem of characterising those substitution tilings with a ‘nice’ cut and project description is closely related to the elusive Pisot conjecture
[
Lee23
,
ABB+15
,
AH14
]
, towards which so far only partial progress has been made
[
Bar18
,
Que87
,
ABB+15
]
.

The current article tackles the reverse problem: characterising precisely when cut and project sets are also substitutional. Some past work exploring this problem from various perspectives include
[
BSJ91
,
Ple00
,
HL04
,
MMP21
,
KL23
]
. Our work extends a programme of establishing precise criteria on the cut and project data that correspond to natural properties of the resulting patterns. For polytopal windows, it is known that the complexity function
p(r)
(giving the number of patches of each radius
r
) grows polynomially, with power determined algebraically by the ranks of intersections of the projected lattice with supporting subspaces of the window, see
[
Jul10
,
KW21
,
Wal24
]
. The property of linear repetitivity
[
LP02
]
requires, additionally, that the lattice is in some sense positioned in a badly approximable way with respect to the physical space, see
[
HKW14
,
KW22
,
Wal24
]
.

Many insights and results of the current article are rooted in the work of
[
HL04
,
Har04
]
. However, a key departure from this is a great simplification and clarification on what is meant by ‘substitutional’. Whilst there are some simple notions in the case of 1-dimensional tilings, when moving to higher dimensions it is less obvious how one defines the notion of being substitutional, in a way that encompasses examples such as the Penrose tilings, where either one must allow for non-stone inflations (where inflated tiles do not have support exactly equal to their replacement), or one needs to apply a local rule so as to work with so-called
mutually locally derivable
(MLD) tilings, the Robinson triangle tilings, which do permit a stone-inflation rule. The notion of a local derivation is fundamental in Aperiodic Order
[
BG13
]
and will be explained further in Section
2
. Cut and project sets are Delone sets, rather than tilings, so implicitly we already perform such MLD transformations when comparing to the case of tilings. Thus, it seems natural to work with a notion of substitutional that is invariant under MLD equivalence.

In fact, the notion of a local derivation is integral to our working definition of being substitutional here. We aim to characterise those cut and project sets which we can locally derive from some scaled pattern that is locally indistinguishable from it (that is, in the repetitive case here, in the same local isomorphism class). So as to avoid confusing the set-ups and terms, we have chosen to call such cut and project sets
substitutional
.

Thus, we have chosen the following definition for a Delone set
\cps\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or tiling) to be substitutional with respect to some linear isomorphism
L\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
: there is a Delone set
\cps^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

•

there is a local derivation from
L(\cps^{\prime})
to
\cps
and

•

\cps^{\prime}
is locally indistinguishable from
\cps
.

For more detail, see Section
2.3
. Our definition is very closely related to the standard notion of being generated by substitution for tilings, as we will demonstrate in Section
7
. When
L
is expansive, by recognisability
[
Sol98
,
Wal25
]
(and since our cut and project sets are non-periodic and repetitive), the first condition is equivalent to the stronger condition that “there is a local derivation from
L(\Lambda^{\prime})
to
\Lambda
, and vice versa” i.e.,
L(\cps^{\prime})
is MLD to
\cps
. Similarly, the second condition is equivalent to
\cps
and
\cps^{\prime}
being locally isomorphic i.e., they are locally indistinguishable (see Definition
2.5
) from each other.

We are now ready to state our main theorem. For the precise statement, see Theorem
3.1
.

Informal statement of Theorem
3.1
.

The cut and project sets determined by the data as above are substitutional with respect to some linear expansion
L\colon\intl\to\intl
if and only if there is a linear map
M\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfying:

(1)

M(\Gamma)=\Gamma
;

(2)

M(\phy)=\phy
with
M|_{\phy}=L
;

(3)

M(\intl)=\intl
and

(4)

a translate of
W
is
constructable
from
M(W)
. In other words,
W
can be written using finitely many translates of
M(W)
, and the set operations: intersection, union and complement. The allowed translations are the projected elements of
\Gamma
.

We note that our results throughout also allow for our windows to be multicoloured, even though we only speak of the single-coloured version in this introduction, for simplicity.

The first three conditions depend only on the cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{R}^{k},\phy,\intl,\Gamma)
, which need to be preserved by the linear automorphism
M
. The final condition says that
W
is also fixed by
M
, up to ‘mutual constructability’. The notion of constructability is explained in more detail in Section
2.5
. It is shown that, when the above theorem is satisfied, we necessarily also have that
M(W)
is constructable from
W
. This condition is slightly weaker (owing to our notion of substitutional being MLD invariant) to the window being defined by a certain type of GIFS (graph iterated function system). That this notion is related to the notion of a cut and project Delone (multi)set being substitutive is already known, see for instance
[
Lee23
]
. Whilst the window being a special form of attractor for a GIFS is not necessary for our substitutional property, we show that it is closely related: up to MLD equivalence, the window must (up to a translation) be a GIFS of the special form described in Corollary
7.9
.

This theorem has many corollaries. For instance, we quickly derive an explicit characterisation of the possible physical spaces
\phy
and lattices
\Gamma
which allow for substitutional cut and project sets. Namely, if
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
(which is without loss of generality, up to reparameterisation), it is necessary and sufficient for the slop of
\phy
to be a quadratic irrational, see Theorem
3.20
. We are also able to show that there are essentially (that is, up to reparametrisation) only countably many substitutional cut and project sets (with Euclidean total space), see Theorem
3.19
. We also demonstrate that variations of the above substitutionality definition and assumptions on the cut and project sets lead to quantifiable changes in the statements. These allow us to, for instance, classify exactly what expansive substitution maps
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
the translational hull supports (Theorem
3.5
) and which exact positionings of the lattice leads to LIDS
[
BG13
]
patterns, loosely speaking, those which substitute to
themselves
under an expansive substitution map.

We are able to derive a much simpler (and more easily checked) criteria on the window
W
when restricting to the case that
W
is polytopal. The main result here states the following; for a precise statement, see Theorem
3.14
.

Informal statement of Theorem
3.14
.

Let
\cps
be a cut and project set determined by the data above, where
W
is a polytope, and let
L\colon\phy\to\phy
be expansive. Then
\cps
is substitutional with respect to some power of
L
if and only if there is a linear map
M\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfying the conditions (1–3) from Theorem
3.1
(see above), and
W
satisfies:

•

the supporting hyperplanes of
W
are invariant under some power of
M
, and

•

the differences of the vertices of
W
can be expressed as projected elements of
\tfrac{1}{M}\Gamma
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
.

The techniques for proving these theorems build on a vast range of methodology from the literature. It is not too hard to show that if an
M
such as in the statement of both of the main theorems exists, the corresponding cut and project set is substitutional. A key clue for this direction of the proof are analyses of local derivations and local isomorphisms in the cut and project context which correspond, respectively, to translations and reconstructions of the window. These techniques are particularly inspired by
[
BG13
, Chapter 7]
and
[
BSJ91
]
. The converse direction is less obvious. Finding such an
M
when
\cps
is substitutional, we need to lift the substitutional property of the cut and project set into the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Here, properties of local inflation-deflation symmetries (LIDS) on Delone sets play an important role. These techniques utilise
[
BG13
, Chapter 5]
. Throughout, the acceptance domain formalism is important, as is the stabiliser rank computations for pattern analysis in the polytopal case (based on e.g.,
[
KW21
,
KW22
]
). We summarise the crucial background findings on acceptance domains, local derivations and local isomorphisms, and LIDS, in Section
4
. Many of the consequences of the main theorem rely on the torus parametrisation of the translational hull
\Omega
(see Theorem
3.5
), that is, the maximal equicontinuous factor of
\Omega
, a tool used frequently for cut and project schemes see e.g.,
[

]
.

2.
Problem set-up, notation, and basic properties

In this section we define Delone (multi)sets and recall some basic properties, such as notions of
local derivability
and
indistinguishability
. Many of these properties have analogous definitions for tilings. In Section
7
we will explain how to compare the properties defined in the Delone context, to the corresponding tiling properties in principle, and demonstrate that the results from this and later sections apply to tilings just as well as Delone sets. In particular, we show that the definition of a Delone set being
substitutional
given below in Subsection
2.3
is analogous to the classical definition of tilings or Delone (multi)sets being generated by substitution rules.

In Subsection
2.4
we define Euclidean cut and project sets and go on to define and investigate other properties required to state our main theorems, in particular, in Subsections
2.5
and
2.6
we relate local derivability and local indistinguishability precisely to the cut and project set up.

2.1.
Delone (multi)sets

Throughout, let
E\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
be some
d
-dimensional vector space (
d\geq 1
), which will be the ambient space of our patterns. Fix a norm on
E
. We let
B(x,r)
denote the closed ball of radius
r
centred at
x\in E
. A
Delone set
is a subset
\cps\subset E
which is both
uniformly discrete
(there exists some
r>0
so that every
r
-ball in
E
intersects at most one point of
\cps
) and
relatively dense
(there exists some
R>0
so that every
R
-ball intersects at least one point of
\cps
).

Given
\mathbf{\Lambda}=(\cps_{1},\ldots,\cps_{\ell})\subset E^{\ell}
, we denote its
support
by
\cps=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{\Lambda})\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}\Lambda_{i}
. We call
\mathbf{\Lambda}
a
Delone multiset
if
\cps
is a Delone set
1
1

1

It is usual to further demand that each
\Lambda_{i}
is Delone, although here it seems more natural to not demand this, in analogy with tilings (where not all tile types necessarily appear relatively densely).
. Although a Delone multiset is a subset of the
\ell
-fold product of the ambient space
E
, we visualise it as a finite collection of uniformly discrete sets in
E
, where the points of
\cps_{i}\subseteq\cps
are those labelled with ‘colour’
i
. No complications arise from points of different colours occupying the same point of
E
, we can simply view that point as having multiple colours. We may define the translate
\mathbf{\Lambda}+x=(\Lambda_{i}+x)_{i=1}^{\ell}
of a Delone (multi)set, and say it is
non-periodic
if
\cps\neq\cps+x
for all
x\in E\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Similarly, if
L\colon E\to E
is a linear automorphism, we may define
L\cps=(L\Lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
. Henceforth, we will drop the bold font on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and work with Delone sets and multisets interchangeably.

Given a Delone (multi)set
\cps
, a patch is given by taking a finite selection of points in
\cps
. More precisely, for a bounded subset
U\subset E
, we may define the patch
\cps\langle U\rangle\subset\cps
by
(\cps\langle U\rangle)_{i}\coloneqq\Lambda_{i}\cap U
. A
patch
in
\cps
is then simply a patch of the form
\cps\langle U\rangle
for bounded
U\subset E
i.e., a finite selection of points from
\cps
(recording their colours, in the multiset case).

In what follows we will use the word ‘pattern’, to encompass both Delone (multi)sets and tilings. For the pattern
\cP=\cps
which, for simplicity, we assume is a Delone (multi)set for now,
x\in E
and
r\geq 0
, we define

(2.1)

\cP[x,r]\coloneqq\cP\langle B(x,r)\rangle-x.

If
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\cP)
, we in particular call
\cP[x,r]
an
r
-patch
. The square brackets indicate that an equivalence relation has been introduced: since we ‘recentre’, by translating
x
over the origin in Equation
2.1
,
\cP[x,r]=\cP[y,r]
can be interpreted as ‘the
r
-patches centred at
x
and
y
in
\cP
are the same, modulo translation from
x
to
y
’. More generally, given a bounded subset
U\subset E
, we define a
U
-patch
to be a patch of the form
\cP[x,U]\coloneqq\cP\langle U+x\rangle-x
, where again (for later convenience) we restrict to
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\cP)
(for Delone sets). Restricting
r
- or
U
-patches to be centred on
\mathrm{supp}(\cP)
is a convenience that allows the following simpler definitions (otherwise, we need to modify them slightly):

Definition 2.1
.

A pattern
\cP
has
finite local complexity
(
FLC
) if, for each
r\geq 0
, there are only finitely many
r
-patches in
\cP
. Equivalently, for all bounded
U\subset E
, there are only finitely many
U
-patches.

Definition 2.2
.

A pattern
\cP
is
repetitive
if, for each
r\geq 0
, there is some
R=R_{r}>0
so that, for each
r
-patch
P
in
\cP
and all
x\in E
, there is some
y\in B(x,R)
with
\cP[y,r]=P
. In other words, all patches appear relatively densely.

We henceforth assume that all patterns are FLC. In fact, for most of the paper (from Section
3
onwards), all patterns will also be repetitive.

2.2.
Local derivability and indistinguishability

We will use the notions of local derivability and indistinguishability to give a highly practical (especially in the context of cut and project sets) definition of patterns being ‘substitutional’.

Definition 2.3
.

A pattern
\cQ
is
locally derivable
(
LD
)
from
a pattern
\cP
if there is some
derivation radius

c\geq 0
satisfying the following: whenever
\cP[x,c]=\cP[y,c]
for
x,y\in E
, then
\cQ[x,0]=\cQ[y,0]
, in which case we write
\cP\LD\cQ
. If
\cP
is locally derivable from
\cQ
and vice versa, we call them
mutually locally derivable
and write
\cP\MLD\cQ
.

A pattern
\cQ
being LD from
\cP
may be thought of as saying that one may redecorate
\cP
to obtain
\cQ
using a rule that is ‘local’, in the sense it can only inspect regions to a bounded radius in
\cP
. One may also consider that, in this case, ‘
\cP
is detailed enough so that its patches, to some bounded radius, determine
\cQ
’.

Remark 2.4
.

It is easy to show that we can increase the ‘
0
’ for the patches in
\cQ
in Definition
2.3
with any
r\geq 0
, by increasing the derivation radius by
r
. In particular, if
\cQ=\cps
is a Delone (multi)set and
r>0
is such that all points of
E
are within distance
r
of a point of its support, clearly in Definition
2.3
it is necessary and sufficient for a local derivation
\cP\LD\cQ
that, for any
x
and
y\in\Lambda
(rather than all of
E
), there is some
c\geq 0
so that if
\cP[x,c]=\cP[y,c]
then
\cQ[x,r]=\cQ[y,r]
.

Definition 2.5
.

A pattern
\cP
is
locally indistinguishable
(
LI
)
from
a pattern
\cQ
, and we write
\cP\LI\cQ
, if every patch of
\cP
appears, up to translation, in
\cQ
. If
\cP
is locally indistinguishable from
\cQ
and vice versa, we call them
locally isomorphic
and write
\cP\LIs\cQ
.

Remark 2.6
.

Note that, in contrast to
[
BG13
]
, we do not use the terms
locally isomorphic to
and
locally indistinguishable from
interchangeably, with the latter being only a one-sided relation here. This non-transitive version is very useful, though, for example in defining the hull of a pattern.

The proof of the following is a simple exercise:

Lemma 2.7
.

If
\cP\LI\cQ
and
\cQ
is FLC then
\cP
is also FLC. If, additionally,
\cQ
is repetitive, then so is
\cP
and
\cQ\LI\cP
, that is,
\cP\LIs\cQ
.

Definition 2.8
.

For an FLC pattern
\cP
we let its (
translational, continuous
)
hull

\Omega_{\cP}
be the space of patterns locally indistinguishable from
\cP
, with the local topology.

See
[
BG13
, Section 5.4]
for the definition of the local topology. In short, it may be derived from a uniformity, or a metric, which considers patterns to be ‘close’ if they agree on a large ball about the origin, up to a small translation. One may equivalently take
\Omega_{\cP}
as the completion of the orbit
\cP+E
of translates of
\cP
. It follows quickly from the definitions that
\cP\LI\cQ
if and only if
\Omega_{\cP}\subseteq\Omega_{\cQ}
.

For a proof of the below, in a generalised setting, see
[
Wal25
]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
\cP\LD\cQ
, with derivation radius
c
, then this may be uniquely extended to a surjective factor map
f\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cQ}
with
f(\cP)=\cQ
. This map satisfies the following: given
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega_{\cP}
, if
\cU[x,c]=\cV[y,c]
then
(f\cU)[x,0]=(f\cV)[y,0]
. In particular, each element of
\Omega_{\cQ}
is LD, with derivation radius
c
, from an element of
\Omega_{\cP}
.

More generally, for spaces of patterns
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
, we say that a map
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
is a
local derivation map
if it satisfies the conclusion of the above, that is, there exists some
c\geq 0
for which, for all
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega
, if
\cU[x,c]=\cV[y,c]
then
f(\cU)[x,0]=(f\cV)[y,0]
. In particular, for each
\cU\in\Omega
, we have
\cU\LD f(\cU)
. This is the geometric analogue of a ‘sliding block code’ (between two orbit closures) from Symbolic Dynamics.

A local derivation map
f\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cQ}
, as induced by
\cP\LD\cQ
from Lemma
2.9
, is injective (and thus is a homeomorphism) if and only if
\cP\MLD\cQ
, in which case the inverse
f^{-1}
is the derivation map induced by
\cQ\LD\cP
. For further details, see, for instance,
[
Wal25
]
.

2.3.
Substitutional patterns

We now use the notions of LD and LI to give an intrinsic definition of when a pattern is ‘substitutional’ and derive some simple consequences. The idea of this definition is simple: when dealing with sufficiently nice patterns generated by an ‘inflate, replace’ substitution rule, for any such pattern one always has a predecessor pattern, also generated by the substitution, for which applying substitution recovers the original pattern (in other words, the inflated ‘super-pattern’ produces the original when we apply the replacement rule to it). Conversely, such a predecessor pattern determines a substitution rule generating these patterns. We make the existence of such a predecessor the defining property. In the below, the ‘inflate’ is given by the map
L
, and the ‘replace’ (on the inflated structure) is given by the local derivation:

Definition 2.10
.

Let
L\colon E\to E
be a linear isomorphism and
\cP
a pattern in
E
. We say that
\cP
is
L
-substitutional
(or
L
-sub
, for short) if there is another pattern
\cP^{\prime}
, called a
predecessor
, for which
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
and
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
. That is:

(1)

\cP
is locally derivable from
L\cP^{\prime}
and

(2)

\cP^{\prime}
is locally indistinguishable from
\cP
.

We will refer to any linear automorphism
L\colon E\to E
on the ambient space of a pattern as an ‘inflation’, whether or not it is expansive. However, in most places in this paper (except when considering symmetries), we have in mind that
L
is expansive:

Definition 2.11
.

A linear map
L\colon E\to E
is called
expansive
if every eigenvalue of
L
is strictly greater than
1
in modulus. Similarly, a linear map is called
contractive
if all its eigenvalues are strictly less than
1
in modulus.

One may show that being expansive is equivalent to the property that, with respect to some appropriate norm on
E
, there is some
\lambda>1
with
\|L(x)\|\geq\lambda\|x\|
for all
x\in E
. Given a fixed, expansive
L
, we always assume such a
\lambda>1
and norm are taken (the norm, otherwise, does not affect properties of relevance in this paper, so this may be done freely).

We say that
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
is a
substitution map
on a space of patterns
\Omega
if
\sigma=S\circ L
, where
L\colon\Omega\to L\Omega
is the expansion map
\cU\mapsto L\cU
and
S\colon L\Omega\to\Omega
is a local derivation map. For an
L
-sub pattern
\cP
, with predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
, by Lemma
2.9
we always have an induced
subdivision map

S\colon\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}\to\Omega_{\cP}
, which is a surjective local derivation map. Often, we at least have
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
(see Definition
2.18
below, Theorem
2.19
, but also generally in the repetitive case), so that
\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}=\Omega_{L\cP}
and thus subdivision defines a map
S\colon\Omega_{L\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
i.e., we may apply subdivision to
any
inflated pattern from the hull of
\cP
. We may then define the (necessarily surjective) substitution map as
\sigma\coloneqq S\circ L\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
i.e., the map which ‘inflates, then subdivides’. If all elements of
\Omega
are locally isomorphic (i.e., in the repetitive case), clearly each
\cP\in\Omega
is
L
-sub, with predecessor any element of
\sigma^{-1}(\cP)
.

If one requires the predecessor to be literally equal to the original in Definition
2.10
we obtain the following notions, see
[
BG13
, Definition 5.15]
(and
[
Sol98
]
, where the notion of a pseudo self-affine pattern is considered).

Definition 2.12
.

We call
\cP

pseudo self-affine
(or say that
\cP
has a
local scaling property
), with respect to a linear map
L\colon E\to E
, if
L\cP\LD\cP
. We say that
\cP
has a
local inflation deflation symmetry
(or is
LIDS
, for short), with respect to
L
, if
L\cP\MLD\cP
.

If, instead, we drop the local derivation, we define the following notion:

Definition 2.13
.

We say that a linear map
L\colon E\to E
is a
symmetry
of
\cP
if
L\cP\LI\cP
.

When we have such a symmetry, we obtain a continuous map
L\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{L\cP}\subseteq\Omega_{\cP}
, given by
\cU\mapsto L\cU
. For repetitive patterns,
L\cP\LI\cP
automatically implies that
L\cP\LIs\cP
(see Lemma
2.7
), and thus
L
defines a homeomorphism
L\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
, with inverse
L^{-1}
, which is also a symmetry. Thus,
L
is in fact a ‘symmetry’ of the LI-class of
\cP
(the hull of
\cP
, in the repetitive case), rather than of just
\cP
itself, and the set of symmetries of
\cP
(or
\Omega_{\cP}
) is a group. Note, however, that a symmetry need not fix
\cP
itself, nor indeed any element of the hull, see Example
2.15
below.

Example 2.14
.

Trivially, any pattern
\cP
is
L
-sub for
L=\Id
. The notion is more relevant when
L
is expansive. Of course,
L=\Id
is also a symmetry of any pattern
\cP
.

Example 2.15
.

We include here the familiar example of the Penrose tiling for comparison, and to demonstrate how the properties above relate to the set-up of a Penrose tiling. For definitions relating to this example, see
[

Let

A linear map

Example 2.16
.

Let
\cps^{\prime}

Example 2.17
.

Let
\cP
be (a translate of) the periodic unit square lattice in
E=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
L(x)=\lambda x
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\cP
is
L
-sub, where we may take
\cP^{\prime}=\cP
(or any translate of
\cP
). Indeed, trivially,
\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}
. And, since
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, it is not hard to see that
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
; the local derivation is given by replacing each inflated square by its covering of
\lambda^{2}
unit squares (and perhaps applying a fixed translation), which is clearly a local derivation. So
\cP
is
L
-sub. However,
L\cP^{\prime}
is not LD from
\cP
for

. Indeed, the translational symmetries of

.

\Diamond

It will be crucial in many of our proofs for the LD of Definition
2.10
to be an MLD, and that
\cP
and
\cP^{\prime}
are in fact locally isomorphic:

Definition 2.18
.

We say that
\cP
is
strongly
L
-substitutional
(or
L
-Sub
, for short) if there exists some
\cP^{\prime}
for which
L(\cP^{\prime})\MLD\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}
.

Note that we use an upper case in Sub as shorthand for being
strongly

L
-substitutional. Recall that
\cP
is called
aperiodic
if its hull
\Omega_{\cP}
consists of only non-periodic patterns. In rather general circumstances, patterns which are
L
-sub are automatically
L
-Sub, when aperiodic:

Theorem 2.19

(
[
Wal25
]
)
.

Let
\cP
be an
L
-sub FLC pattern in
E
, where
L\colon E\to E
is expansive, with predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
. Then
\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}
, so we have a substitution map
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
. Moreover,
\cP
is strongly
L
-Sub, that is
L\cP^{\prime}\MLD\cP
or equivalently
\sigma\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
is injective, if and only if
\cP
is aperiodic.

The above is an extension of the classical result from
[
Sol98
]
, on recognisability or unique decomposition in the geometric setting (see also
[
Mos92
,
Mos96
,
BSTY19
,
BPRS25
]
for recognisability results in the symbolic setting). In fact, for the repetitive case at hand, it may be derived from a combination of the results from
[
Sol98
,
Sol07
,
PS01a
]
(along with showing that a power of substitution must have a fixed point). In short, aperiodicity is equivalent to there being a
unique
inflated pattern
L\cU^{\prime}\in L\Omega
subdividing to a given
\cU\in\Omega
, in which case
L\cU^{\prime}
can be derived from
\cU
using a local rule.

The upshot from all of the above is, that in all reasonable cases of FLC
L
-sub patterns (namely, when
L
is expansive, or
\cP
is repetitive), we do have a subdivision map
S\colon\Omega_{L\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
and substitution map
\sigma\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
. Moreover, if we additionally assume aperiodicity and that
L
is expansive, we have that
L
-sub and
L
-Sub are equivalent. Similarly, in this case, being pseudo self-affine is equivalent to LIDS, with respect to
L
. However, we preserve the distinctions, as the definition of

-sub seems the most natural extension of the idea of a tiling (or Delone set) being substitutional in the classical sense. Compare to Examples
2.16
and
2.17

L
-sub, even though in the proofs we will rely on the implied stronger property

-Sub.

Lemma 2.20
.

If
\cP
is
L
-sub,
L
-Sub, or has
L
as a symmetry, and
\cP\LIs\cQ
then
\cQ
has the same property.

Proof.

If
\cP\LIs\cQ
then
\Omega_{\cP}=\Omega_{\cQ}
. Subdivision
S\colon\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}\to\Omega_{\cP}
is surjective, so there exists some
L\cQ^{\prime}\in\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}
with
S(L\cQ^{\prime})=\cQ
. Since
S
is a local derivation map, we have that
L\cQ^{\prime}\LD\cQ
. Moreover, since
L\cQ^{\prime}\in\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}
, equivalently
\cQ^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\cP^{\prime}}
, we have
\cQ^{\prime}\LI\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP\LIs\cQ
, that is,
\cQ^{\prime}\LI\cQ
, as required. If
\cP
is also
L
-Sub, then subdivision defines an MLD
L\cQ^{\prime}\MLD\cQ
. It is not hard to see that, for patterns
\cU\LI\cV
and a local derivation map
f
(as in Lemma
2.9
), we also have
f(\cU)\LI f(\cV)
. Indeed, the finite patches of
f(\cU)
are determined, under
f
, by those of
\cU
, and similarly for
f(\cV)
, which contain at least all of those of
f(\cU)
since
\cU\LI\cV
. We see that, if
\cU\LIs\cV
, then
f\cU\LIs f\cV
. In our case, starting with
\cP\LIs\cQ
and applying the local map
S^{-1}
, we see that
L\cP^{\prime}\LIs L\cQ^{\prime}
, equivalently,
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cQ^{\prime}
. Thus
\cQ\LIs\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cQ^{\prime}
so
\cQ\LIs\cQ^{\prime}
, as required. The proof for
L
being a symmetry is similar (and simpler).
∎

In particular, for the hull of a repetitive pattern
\cP
, all elements
\cQ\in\Omega_{\cP}
have
\cQ\LIs\cP
(Lemma
2.7
) and thus are also
L
-sub; of course, in this case, one may take any
\cQ^{\prime}\in\sigma^{-1}(\cQ)
as a predecessor for
\cQ
. Iterating substitution, and choosing pre-images, we may thus always construct a hierarchy of patterns
\cQ_{i}\in\Omega_{\cP}

\cdots\LD L^{2}\cQ_{2}\LD L\cQ_{1}\LD\cQ_{0}=\cQ\LD L^{-1}\cQ_{-1}\LD L^{-2}\cQ_{-2}\LD\cdots

where each LD is induced by subdivision
S\colon\Omega_{L^{i}\cP}\to\Omega_{L^{i-1}\cP}
in the obvious way, and we have
\sigma(\cQ_{i+1})=\cQ_{i}
.

Being
L
-sub or
L
-Sub is also invariant under MLD equivalence. Note, however, that whilst being pseudo self-affine (or LIDS) are MLD-invariant, they are not LI invariant. On the other hand, symmetries are not MLD invariant.

Lemma 2.21
.

If
\cP
is
L
-sub,
L
-Sub, LIDS or pseudo self-affine with respect to
L
, and
\cQ\MLD\cP
, then
\cQ
has the same property.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.9
, we may extend the MLD to a derivation map of hulls
g\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cQ}
, where
\cU\MLD g\cU
for all
\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}
, where
g\cP=\cQ
. Let
\cQ^{\prime}\coloneqq g(\cP^{\prime})\in\Omega_{\cQ}
, so
\cQ^{\prime}\LI\cQ
and
\cP^{\prime}\MLD\cQ^{\prime}
. Since
L\cQ^{\prime}\MLD L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP\MLD\cQ
, we have
L\cQ^{\prime}\LD\cQ
, so
\cQ
is
L
-sub. If
\cP
is
L
-Sub, then we have
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
. Using a similar argument to the last proof, we have
\cQ^{\prime}\LIs\cQ
(as
g\cP^{\prime}=\cQ^{\prime}
and
g\cP=\cQ
, so
\cQ^{\prime}\LIs\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP\LIs\cQ
). Since
L\cQ^{\prime}\MLD L\cP^{\prime}\MLD\cP\MLD\cQ
, we see that
L\cQ^{\prime}\MLD\cQ
and
\cQ
is
L
-Sub, as required. The proof for LIDS/pseudo self-affine with respect to

are simpler, as in this case we just have that
\cP^{\prime}=\cP
and thus we take
\cQ^{\prime}=\cQ
.
∎

2.4.
Euclidean cut and project sets

We now define the main object of study in this paper, cut and project sets.

Definition 2.22
.

Let
\tot
be a
k
-dimensional vector space, called the
total space
. We let
\phy
be a dimension
d>0
subspace of
\tot
, called the
physical space
, and
\intl
be an
n\coloneqq k-d>0
dimensional subspace of
\tot
that is complementary with
\phy
, called the
internal space
(so
\phy\cap\intl=\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
\tot=\phy+\intl
). Let
\Gamma
be a full rank lattice of
\tot
.

Denote by
\pi_{\vee},\pi_{<}
the projections to
\phy
and
\intl
, respectively, with respect to the decomposition
\tot=\intl+\phy
. For any subset
A\subset\tot
, use the shorthands
A_{\vee}\coloneqq\pi_{\vee}(A)
and
A_{<}\coloneqq\pi_{<}(A)
, and similarly
a_{\vee}\coloneqq\pi_{\vee}(a)
and
a_{<}\coloneqq\pi_{<}(a)
for any
a\in\tot
. We always assume that
\Gamma
is irrationally positioned relative to the the physical and internal spaces, in the following sense:

•

\pi_{\vee}|_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma\to\phy
is injective;

•

\pi_{<}|_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma\to\intl
is injective;

•

\Gamma_{<}\subset\intl
is dense.

The tuple
\mathcal{S}=(\tot,\phy,\intl,\Gamma)
is called a
k
-to-
d
cut and project scheme
.

We define the
star map
by
e^{\star}\coloneqq\pi_{<}\circ\pi_{\vee}^{-1}(e)
on elements
e\in\Gamma_{\vee}
, which defines a (discontinuous) isomorphism
\star\colon\Gamma_{\vee}\to\Gamma_{<}
.

Remark 2.23
.

In the above definition, injectivity of
\pi_{\vee}|_{\Gamma}
guarantees that the star map is well defined. Injectivity of
\pi_{<}|_{\Gamma}
is equivalent to non-periodicity of the resulting patterns.

To produce a cut and project set from a scheme, we choose a window in its internal space:

Definition 2.24
.

A subset is
topologically regular
if it is the closure of its interior. A (
multi-coloured
)
window
of a cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
is a finite collection
W=(W_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
of
window components

W_{i}\subset\intl
, which are compact and topologically regular.

We say that
W
is in
non-singular position
if each
\Gamma_{<}\cap\partial W_{i}=\emptyset
. In this case, we define the associated the
cut and project set
as the Delone (multi)set
\cps(\mathcal{S},W)=(\Lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
where

\Lambda_{i}\coloneqq\{e\in\Gamma_{\vee}\mid e^{\star}\in W_{i}\}=(\Gamma\cap(\phy+W_{i}))_{\vee}\ .

More generally, we say that a Delone (multi)set
\cps^{\prime}
is
generated by the cut and project scheme

\mathcal{S}
(and window
W
) if
\cps^{\prime}\in\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)\coloneqq\Omega_{\cps}
, where
\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W+s)
for some (equivalently, any) non-singular translation
W+s
of
W
.

Given a window, we define the set
\mathscr{S}\subset\tot
of
singular points
as those
x\in\tot
for which
x_{<}\in\bigcup_{g\in\Gamma_{<}}\partial W+g
. We let
\mathscr{N}=\tot\setminus\mathscr{S}
denote the
non-singular points
. Note that, in particular, for
x\in\intl
we have that
x\in\mathscr{S}
if and only if
x\in\bigcup_{g\in\Gamma_{<}}\partial W+g
, otherwise
x\in\mathscr{N}
.

Frequently one does not need the window to be multi-coloured. In this case, we take
W
itself as simply a compact, topologically regular subset of
\intl
, and take
\cps
as an (uncoloured) Delone set. As we will see in Proposition
4.9
, reducing to the single-colour case often results in little loss of generality. However, allowing for multi-coloured windows can have practical advantage in applying our results directly to given examples, such as schemes that are naturally defined using partitions of the window but also those whose internal space is the product of a Euclidean space with a finite Abelian group, see Example
2.27
below.

Notice that, since we assume
W
is topologically regular,
\mathscr{N}
is a countable union of open dense sets in
\intl
and is thus dense (similarly,
\mathscr{S}
is also dense). This does not require that the window is
regular
, that is, the measure
|\partial W|=0
. In the regular case,
|\mathscr{S}_{<}|=0
, so
\mathscr{N}_{<}
is full measure in
\intl
, and the same holds for the singular and non-singular points in
\tot
.

Remark 2.25
.

Since we restrict to repetitive patterns, generating the hull using non-singular translates of the window, the
W_{i}
being topologically regular is not a particularly restrictive condition. Indeed, otherwise, replacing each
W_{i}
with
\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{int}(W_{i}))
does not change
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
but makes the window topologically regular. One advantage is that, for a fixed scheme
\mathcal{S}
, there is now only one choice of window
W
, up to translation, which produces
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
, see Lemma
4.19
.

Remark 2.26
.

All cut and project sets as defined above are repetitive (this follows by Lemma
4.4
by the same proof as
[
BG13
, Proposition 7.5]
) and aperiodic. So the elements of
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
are all locally isomorphic. In particular, by Lemma
2.20
, we have that all elements of
\Omega
are
L
-sub (or
L
-Sub) if and only if some particular element
\cps\in\Omega
is.

Example 2.27
.

The vertices of the Penrose tiling are given as a cut and project set with an internal space
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times C_{5}
, where
C_{5}=\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}
, see
[
BG13
, Example 7.11]
. The window is a union of four pentagons, occupying the components of the internal space corresponding to non-trivial elements of
C_{5}
. Considering each pentagon to have a different colour does not affect the MLD class. Lattice elements may be used to translate each window component to
W_{i}-(\gamma_{i})_{<}
, all in the trivial component
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{[0]\}
. The result will be equal to a Penrose pattern but where each of the sub-Delone sets of different colours are non-trivially translated relative to each other. However, doing this does not affect the MLD class. So (most of) our results apply directly to examples of this form: when the internal space is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times G
for some finite Abelian group
G
, a process like this can always be done.

\Diamond

2.5.
Constructability between windows

Constructability between windows was introduced in

Informally, given a fixed cut and project scheme

is

W^{\prime}
as a finite Boolean expression in terms of
W
and its translates under
\Gamma_{<}
. However, this definition lacks important nuance, as the next example demonstrates:

Consider the windows
W^{\prime}=[0,1]^{2}
, the unit square in
\intl=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
W=W^{\prime}\cup(W^{\prime}+(1,0))\cup(W^{\prime}+(0,1))
. That is,
W
is an ‘L’ shape. Suppose that
(1,0)\in\Gamma_{<}
. Then it is almost the case that
W^{\prime}=W\cap(W+(1,0))
, although not quite: this intersection contains an extra
1
-dimensional segment
[(1,1),(1,2)]\subset\partial W
. We could attempt to remove this segment using further Boolean operations on
\Gamma_{<}
translates of
W
and its complement, but it can well be the case that there is no element in
\Gamma_{<}
that would fully remove this segment without also removing interior points of
W^{\prime}
. However, once the windows are moved into non-singular position, no lattice points ever hit this segment, so the definition of constructability should be blind to such inconsequential features.

One route to a more precise definition is to consider equality between the windows only on the non-singular points. An alternative, neater way, and the one given in
[
BSJ91
]
, is to redefine the standard Boolean operations by always taking the closure of interior at each step, which removes awkward features such as in teh example above. Another advantage of this approach is that this makes acceptance domains (see Definition
4.1
) topologically regular and thus also windows. This is the definition we give underneath.

Notation 2.28
.

For
U\subset\intl
we define
\varkappa(U)\coloneqq\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{int}(U))
. We denote:

U\wedge V\coloneqq\varkappa(U\cap V),\ U\vee V\coloneqq\varkappa(U\cup V),\ \neg U\coloneqq\varkappa(\intl\setminus U).

Let
\mathscr{B}
be the collection of topologically regular subsets of
\intl
. It is not hard to check that
\varkappa(U)\in\mathscr{B}
for all subsets
U
, that
\varkappa=\Id
on
\mathscr{B}
and that, for
U
,
V\in\mathscr{B}
, we have
U\vee V=U\cup V
and
\neg U=\mathrm{cl}(\intl\setminus U)
. Moreover, one may check that this makes
\mathscr{B}=(\mathscr{B},\wedge,\vee,\neg)
a Boolean algebra.

Given
W=(W_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
, with each
W_{i}\in\mathscr{B}
, let
\mathscr{B}(W)
be the sub-Boolean algebra of
\mathscr{B}
generated by
\{W_{i}+g\mid i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\},g\in\Gamma_{<}\}\subset\mathscr{B}
. That is,
\mathscr{B}(W)
consists of all
U\in\mathscr{B}
which may be constructed as a finite expression in terms of the subsets
W_{i}+g
, for
g\in\Gamma_{<}
, and the operations
\wedge
,
\vee
and
\neg
.

Definition 2.29
.

Fix a cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
and two windows
W
,
W^{\prime}\subset\intl
. We say that
W^{\prime}

is constructable from

W
if
\mathscr{B}(W^{\prime})\subseteq\mathscr{B}(W)
. We call the windows
mutually constructable
if
W^{\prime}
is constructable from
W
and vice versa, that is,
\mathscr{B}(W)=\mathscr{B}(W^{\prime})
.

To unpack the above definition somewhat, for simplicity suppose that both
W
and
W^{\prime}
are single-coloured (so we may just take
W=W_{1}
,
W^{\prime}=W_{1}^{\prime}
). If
W^{\prime}
is constructable from
W
then, by definition,
\mathscr{B}(W^{\prime})\subseteq\mathscr{B}(W)
. This is equivalent to
W^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
: Indeed,
W^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W^{\prime})
, so if
\mathscr{B}(W^{\prime})\subseteq\mathscr{B}(W)
then
W^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
. Conversely, if
W^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
then also
W^{\prime}+g\in\mathscr{B}(W)
for each
g\in\Gamma_{<}
, since all terms of a given Boolean expression may be translated by

to provide another for the translate. Since these generate
\mathscr{B}(W^{\prime})
, we have
\mathscr{B}(W^{\prime})\subseteq\mathscr{B}(W)
.

Now,
W^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
simply means that
W^{\prime}
can be given a finite expression in terms of the translates
W+g
, for
g\in\Gamma_{<}
, and the Boolean operations above. Using the De Morgan Laws (and that
\vee=\cup
on
\mathscr{B}
), such an expression may always be re-arranged to an equation of the form

(2.2)

W^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\ \ \ \text{ for }\ \ \ X_{i}=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m_{i}}W_{ij},

where each
W_{ij}=W+(g_{ij})_{<}
or
W_{ij}=\neg W+(g_{ij})_{<}
, where the
g_{ij}\in\Gamma
. Intuitively, we may construct
W^{\prime}
from intersections, unions and
\Gamma_{<}
translates of
W
, up to negligible topological defects (more precisely, see also Lemma
4.3
). The case of a multi-coloured window is similar: in this case, we now take each
W_{ij}=W_{m}+(g_{ij})_{<}
or
W_{ij}=\neg W_{m}+(g_{ij})_{<}
for some index
m
in Equation
2.2
, to construct each window component
W^{\prime}_{p}
.

2.6.
The torus parametrisation

For a hull
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
of cut and project sets, we now recall the
torus parameterisation
,
\tau\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{T}
, where
\mathbb{T}=\tot/\Gamma
. This map is defined as follows. For each
x\in\mathscr{N}
(equivalently, such that
(\Gamma+x)_{<}\cap\partial W=\emptyset
), we associate a
non-singular
cut and project set
\cps_{x}\in\Omega
, defined by

\cps_{x}=\pi_{\vee}((\Gamma+x)\cap(\phy+W))

(and similarly for a coloured window, where
(x+\gamma)_{\vee}\in\cps_{x}
is given colour
i
when
(x+\gamma)_{<}\in W_{i}
). We then define
\tau(\cps_{x})\coloneqq[x]\in\mathbb{T}
. All other elements
\cps\in\Omega
, which are called
singular
, may be written as a limit of non-singular patterns
\cps_{x_{n}}\to\cps
, where
[x_{n}]\to[x]
for some
[x]\in\mathbb{T}
, and we may set
\tau(\cps):=[x]
, which can be shown to be well-defined. Clearly, for
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we have
x\in\mathscr{N}
if and only if
x+\gamma\in\mathscr{N}
. Hence, the singular and non-singular points
x\in\tot
correspond to elements of
\mathbb{T}
, whose pre-images under
\tau
are the singular and non-singular elements of
\Omega
, respectively. Note that
\tau
defines a surjective factor map, where
\phy
acts on
\mathbb{T}
by
x\cdot[u]=[u+x]
. That is,
\tau(\cps+x)=\tau(\cps)+[x]
for any
x\in\phy
. Moreover, it is not hard to see that, for
s\in\intl
with
W-s
non-singular, we have that
\tau(\cps(\mathcal{S},W-s))=\tau(\cps_{s})=[s]
, since
(\gamma+s)_{<}\in W
if and only if
\gamma_{<}\in W-s
.

The idea of the above may be stated quite intuitively: if
\tau(\cps)=[x]
, this means that
\cps
may be considered as the cut and project set given by cutting and projecting the translated lattice
\Gamma+x
. One just needs to bear in mind that, if
[x]
is singular, we need to be careful in selecting points from the translated lattice when they project to the boundary of the window. In particular, there are multiple different such cut and project sets for this translate, depending on which lattice points hitting the window boundary to include or exclude; in other words, depending on how we approach the singular pattern with non-singular ones. The non-singular points of
\mathbb{T}
are precisely those with single pre-images under
\tau
, and the singular points are precisely those with multiple pre-images. For more detail, see e.g.
[
Hun15
]
.

3.
Characterisation of cut and project schemes that are substitutional

We can now state our main theorems. Recall Definition
2.18
of
L
-Sub, shorthand for strongly
L
-substitutional. Recall that since cut and project sets are aperiodic, by Theorem
2.19
,
L
-Sub is equivalent to the weaker notion of being
L
-substitutional, when
L
is expansive. Further, by Lemma
2.20
, for any element from the hull of locally isomorphic patterns is also
L
-sub. Hence, the result below also characterises when a hull of Euclidean cut and project sets satisfies
L
-sub.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=(\tot,\phy,\intl,\Gamma)
be a Euclidean cut and project scheme with window
W
, and let
L:\phy\to\phy
be linear. The cut and project sets in
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
are
L
-Sub if and only if there is a linear map
M\colon\tot\to\tot
satisfying the following:

(1)

M(\Gamma)=\Gamma
;

(2)

M(\phy)=\phy
with
L=M|_{\phy}
;

(3)

M(\intl)=\intl
, and we define
A\coloneqq M|_{\intl}
;

(4)

a translate
W-s
of
W
, for some
s\in\intl
, is mutually constructable from
A(W)
.

If
L
is expansive, then
A=M|_{\intl}
is contractive and we may replace (4) with the weaker condition

(4a)

a translate
W-s
of
W
, for some
s\in\intl
, is constructable from
A(W)
.

The proof of this result will be given in Section
5
. A few remarks are in order.

Remark 3.2
.

(a)

Similar results on sufficiency of properties similar to (1–4) have appeared before in the literature
[
HL04
,
KL23
,
AFHI11
]
; more of the work is in formally proving they are also necessary.

(b)

Note that Theorem
3.1
applies directly to single- and multi-coloured windows. Also, as discussed in Example
2.27
, it is indirectly but easily applied to the case of internal spaces with multiple Euclidean components, such as seen in the standard Penrose cut and project scheme, through a simple (MLD) conversion to the single-component setting considered here.

(c)

For readers interested in defining explicit substitution rules, note that the construction of (a translate of)
W
from
A(W)
determines the actual substitution rule on given patterns (how points of inflated patterns are replaced with clusters), as explained through Proposition
4.21
and Theorem
4.22
. See also Theorem
3.5
below, which describes the corresponding substitution map on the hull.

(d)

We will see later that, the window
W
of an
L
-Sub cut and project set need not be (even up to translation) the attractor of a GIFS. However, once we decompose the window into enough acceptance domains, see Definition
4.1
, it is an attractor of a GIFS, see Corollary
7.9
.

Given a Euclidean total space, lattice and physical space, it is natural to ask if this triple permits an internal space and window making (expansive) substitutional patterns. This is answered in the result below, where we call a linear automorphism
M\colon\tot\to\tot

hyperbolic
if
\tot=A+B
is the sum of
M
-invariant subspaces, with
M
expansive on
A
and contractive on
B
. Note that the condition that
[\phy]=\phy/\Gamma
is dense in
\mathbb{T}
is equivalent to our requirement that
\Gamma_{<}
is dense in
\intl
in all cut and project schemes, but without reference to a particular
\intl
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\tot
,
\phy
and
\Gamma
be given, with
[\phy]
dense in
\mathbb{T}=\tot/\Gamma
and
\Gamma\cap\phy=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

There is an internal space
\intl
, expansive
L\colon\phy\to\phy
, and a window
W
so that the cut and project set corresponding to
W
and the scheme
(\tot,\phy,\intl,\Gamma)
is
L
-Sub.

(2)

There exists a hyperbolic linear automorphism
M\colon\tot\to\tot
, with
M(\Gamma)=\Gamma
, for which
\phy
is

M
-invariant, and
M|_{\phy}
is expansive.

In this case,
L=M|_{\phy}
and
\intl
is the contracting subspace of
M
with
A=M|_{\intl}
. Moreover, there is a finite collection of lattice points
Z\subset\Gamma
and such a window

, invariant in the sense that

(3.1)

W=\bigcup_{z\in Z}A(W)+z_{<}.

Remark 3.4
.

Since

is a contraction, the collection of maps

is known as an
iterated function system
and for any choice of finite

[
Fal03

. A particular choice of

3.3
in Section

.

The next theorem is a classification result, on all possible substitution maps
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
on a hull of Euclidean cut and project sets. We assume from here on that all substitution maps are injective. Note that injectivity is automatic when

is expansive (see Theorem
2.19

-Sub, with respect to a given predecessor: In this case,
\sigma=S\circ L
where, for each

L\cps\MLD S(L\cps)=\sigma(\cps)
.

To state the theorem, we need to introduce some notation. Given an automorphism

with

(as provided by Theorem
3.1
), we let
M_{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
denote the induced automorphism
M_{\mathbb{T}}[x]\coloneqq[Mx]
on the torus. Given also some

, we let
T_{u}=T_{u}^{M}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}

from Section

Theorem 3.5
.

For a Euclidean cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
with window

, suppose that the hull

consists of

-Sub patterns. Let

Remark 3.6
.

(a)

Theorem

(c)

The next results concerns a characterisation of the case where

.

Consider a Euclidean cut and project scheme

Example 3.8
.

Consider

\Diamond

In this aperiodic setting, with

.

Consider a hull of Euclidean cut and project sets

3.1.
Results for polytopal windows

In the case that
W
is polytopal, we may describe the constructability conditions in the results above in more detail. We begin with the definition of polytopal and the corresponding supporting hyperplanes.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
W\subset\intl
be compact and topologically regular. An (
affine
)
hyperplane

H\subset\intl
is translation of a codimension
1
subspace. We call a hyperplane
H\subset\intl

if there exists some
w_{H}\in H\cap W
and an open neighbourhood
U_{H}\subset\intl
of

so that
W\cap U_{H}=H^{+}\cap U_{H}
, where
H^{+}
is a closed half-space for

(i.e., the closure of a connected component of
\intl\setminus H
). We call
W

if
\partial W
is contained in a finite collection
\mathscr{H}
of supporting hyperplanes. A multi-coloured window
W=(W_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
is called

if each

is polytopal, in which case we let
\mathscr{H}
be the union of the supporting hyperplanes of the
W_{i}
.

For an affine hyperplane
H
we let
V(H)\coloneqq H-H
, that is, the subspace translate of
H
containing the origin. We let
\mathscr{H}_{0}\coloneqq V(\mathscr{H})=\{V(H)\mid H\in\mathscr{H}\}
denote the collection of
supporting subspaces
of the window i.e., the subspaces parallel to its faces.

Remark 3.11
.

In Definition
3.10
, each
H\cap W
, for
H\in\mathscr{H}
, is a ‘face’ of the polytope (or possibly containing a union of faces). Then, selecting

in the interior of a face, in a small neighbourhood about

the polytopal window will be the same as a closed half-space. We note that any supporting hyperplane

of

must be a member of
\mathscr{H}
or else the hyperplanes of
\mathscr{H}
would not cover
\partial W_{i}
.

We leave it as an exercise to check that Definition
3.10
is equivalent to
W
being a finite union of convex polytopes, where a
convex polytope
is a convex hull of finitely many points with a non-empty interior. In particular, the requirement that
W
is compact, equal to the closure of its interior and has boundary contained in a finite union of affine hyperplanes means that
W
is a finite union of convex polytopes given as closures of some connected components of
\intl\setminus(\bigcup_{H\in\mathscr{H}}H)
.

Assume now that the necessary conditions (1–3) from Theorem
3.1
hold for some fixed linear automorphism
M\colon\tot\to\tot
, that is:

(1)

M(\Gamma)=\Gamma
;

(2)

M(\phy)=\phy
, where we denote
L\coloneqq M|_{\phy}\colon\phy\to\phy
;

(3)

M(\intl)=\intl
, where we denote
A\coloneqq M|_{\intl}\colon\intl\to\intl
.

The existence of such an
M
concerns only the scheme
\mathcal{S}
but not the window. We now also assume that
W
is polytopal (possibly multi-coloured) and that

is expansive. Without loss of generality in the results below, we will assume that

has a vertex over the origin, which simplifies some statements. One immediate consequence of this assumption is that

is in a singular position.

Definition 3.12
.

We say that

is

If we merely have

of the expansion, and so it makes no difference to the characterisation whether we take

Definition 3.13
.

We call elements of
\Gamma_{<}

integral

The polytope

.

Of course, if

is connected, it is necessary and sufficient for rationality that each edge

of adjacent vertices in

v_{2}-v_{1}
rational. Generally, since we assume that

being rational. The above definition is given to be invariant under translating the window.

Our main characterisation for substitutional polytopal cut and project schemes may now be stated:

Theorem 3.14
.

Assume that conditions (1–3) of Theorem

hold, where
L\colon\phy\to\phy
is expansive, and that

is polytopal. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

there is some

so that all elements of
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
are
L^{m}
-sub;

(2)

W
is FD-invariant with respect to
A^{p}
, for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and
W
is rational.

By inspecting the proofs, the powers

and

The result below generalises the well-known case of Sturmian words (see also Theorem
3.20
), in the 2–to–1 case to general dimensions and general polytopal windows. Recall the torus parametrisation

from Section

Theorem 3.15
.

Assume that the elements of
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
, for polytopal

L^{m}
-sub for some

. Then
\cps\in\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
is LIDS (equivalently, pseudo self-affine, or fixed by a substitution map

of the expansion, if and only if
\tau(\cps)=[u]
for
u_{<}\in\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
.

Recall that we have chosen our polytopal windows to have a vertex over the origin in the above statement. So, when the hull admits an expansive substitution, those Delone (multi)sets

by one are precisely those given by cutting and projecting translations of the lattice which are rational, in the internal space component. More specifically (see Proposition

Example 3.16
.

Denote

3.2.
Countability of substitutional Euclidean cut and project schemes

In this, and the following subsections, we will derive some simple consequences of the theorems above.

Definition 3.17
.

We say that two (Euclidean) cut and project schemes
\mathcal{S}=(\tot,\phy,\intl,\Gamma)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=(\tot^{\prime},\tot_{\vee}^{\prime},\tot_{<}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
if there is a linear isomorphism
f\colon\tot\to\tot^{\prime}
for which
f(\phy)=\tot_{\vee}^{\prime}
,
f(\intl)=\tot_{<}^{\prime}
and
f(\Gamma)=\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Clearly, by choosing an isomorphism
\Gamma\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, every Euclidean cut and project scheme is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\phy,\intl,\mathbb{Z}^{k})
for some choice of physical and internal space. That is, modulo isomorphism, we may take
\Gamma
as the standard integer lattice. Note that if
\mathcal{S}
is isomorphic, under
f
, to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, then the elements of
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
are equal to those of
\Omega(\mathcal{S^{\prime}},f(W))
, up to a linear rescaling of the physical space (compare with Lemma
5.1
). So, if we only wish to consider cut and project sets up to rescaling by a linear automorphism, we need only consider cut and project schemes up to isomorphism. Note that the property of being substitutional is preserved by linear automorphisms, and the map
L
only changes to
f\circ L\circ f^{-1}
.

In the proof of the next theorem we will need the following notion.

Definition 3.18
.

For each
i,j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, let
f_{i,j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a contraction. Assume that the tuple
(W_{1},\dots,W_{\ell})\subset\mathbb{R}^{r\ell}
of compact non-empty sets satisfies that for each
i=1,\dots,\ell
,

(3.2)

W_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}f_{(i,j)}(W_{j}).

We then call the tuple
(W_{1},\dots,W_{\ell})

attractor of a graph directed iterated function system (GIFS)
. Given the maps
f_{(i,j)}
, the existence of a unique tuple of compact, non-empty sets
(W_{1},\dots,W_{\ell})
satisfying (
3.2
) is guaranteed by standard theory (see e.g.
[
MW88
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 3.19
.

Up to isomorphism, there are only a countable number of cut and project schemes
\mathcal{S}
for which there is some window
W
making the elements of
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
all
L
-sub for some expansive
L
. For each such scheme
\mathcal{S}
, there are only countably many such windows
W
, up to translation.

Proof.

Consider a scheme
\mathcal{S}
, which we may assume, by the above, has
\tot=\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. By Theorem
3.3
, we must have that
\phy
and
\intl
are the expanding and contracting subspaces, respectively, of some hyperbolic linear map
M
on
\tot
, with
M(\mathbb{Z}^{k})=\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, which thus corresponds to a matrix in
\mathrm{GL}(k,\mathbb{Z})
, of which there are countably many.

Now fix a cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
satisfying the above, thus the necessary conditions (1–3) of Theorem
3.1
hold, with respect to
M
. We also obtain a contraction
A\colon\intl\to\intl
. In the polytopal case, since the possible set of locations
\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
of vertices is countable, it quickly follows that there are only countably many polytopal windows making
\Omega
consist of
L
-sub patterns.

For general windows, things are slightly more complicated. However, we will see in Corollary
7.9
that,
W
is a finite union of subsets
W_{1}^{\prime}
, …,
W_{\ell}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
, so that for every
i=1,\dots,\ell
,

W^{\prime}_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}A(W_{j})+g_{j},

where for each
j=1,\dots,\ell
, the map
x\mapsto A(x)-g_{j}
is a contraction, with
g_{j}\in\Gamma_{<}
. Clearly, the tuple
(W_{1}^{\prime},\dots,W_{\ell}^{\prime})
is then the attractor of a GIFS and hence uniquely characterised by the above equality. Since
\Gamma_{<}
is countable, there are only countably many such choices of maps, so there are only countably many attractors of GIFS of this form. It follows that there are only countably many such windows
W
, up to translation.
∎

3.3.
Characterisation of substitutional cut and project sets in
2
–to–
1
case

Theorems
3.1
and
3.14
give a full characteristion of those cut and project sets which are substitutional. However, they are both by nature somewhat non-constructive, relying on the statement ‘there exists a linear map
M
’. In this section we demonstrate that they can, however, be used to characterise substitutional cut and project sets in concrete terms. Versions of the following result are well-known (see, for example
[
Fog02
, Chapter 6]
), but depending on the exact set-up, there are minor discrepancies in formulation. In particular, our notion of being substitutional allows for adding locally derivable labels when defining the substitution rule (see Example
3.21
).

Theorem 3.20
.

Consider a cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
with
k=2
,
d=1
,
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\phy=\langle(1,\alpha)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
for some irrational
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, if the cut and project sets of
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
are
L
-sub with
L
expansive, for some
\intl
and
W
, then
\alpha
must be a quadratic irrational. Conversely, if
\alpha
is a quadratic irrational, there is a unique choice of
\intl
, and there are choices of windows
W
, for which the elements of
\Omega
are
L
-Sub, with
L
expansive. In this latter case,
W
may be taken, for example, to be any union of closed intervals with rational endpoints.

In this case,
\cps\in\Omega
is pseudo self-affine, fixed by some (expansive) substitution
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
, if and only if
\tau[\cps]=[u]
for
u_{<}\in\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
.

Proof.

Assume first that such choices
W
, and
\intl
exist for which
\cps
is
L
-sub (equivalently,
L
-Sub, using that
L
is expansive). We know from Theorem
3.1
that there is a linear automorphism
M\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
which preserves
\Gamma
and
\mathbb{E}_{\vee}
. In particular,
(1,\alpha)
is an eigenvector for an integer matrix, which makes
\alpha
a quadratic irrational.

Now assume that
\alpha
is a quadratic irrational, and denote
\ell\coloneqq\phy
. By Theorem
3.20
, we need to find
M\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
with
M\ell=\ell
, with
M|_{\phy}
expansive. This makes
\phy
an eigendirection of
M
, and we will then choose
\intl
to be another one.

Without loss of generality, we may take
0<\alpha<1
: Otherwise, it is not hard to see that there is some
T\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
for which
\ell^{\prime}=T\ell
intersects the line segment between
(1,0)
and
(1,1)
. In this case,
\ell^{\prime}=\langle(1,\alpha^{\prime})\rangle
where
0<\alpha^{\prime}<1
remains a quadratic irrational (since it has a rational expression in terms of
\alpha
). The below argument constructs
M\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
with
M\ell^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}
and so may use
T^{-1}MT\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
with
T^{-1}MT\ell=\ell
.

The continued fraction expansion of
\alpha
is eventually periodic. Let us first investigate the case when
\alpha
has a periodic continued fraction expansion, so
\alpha=[\overline{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}]
, where the notation
\overline{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}
means that the finite word
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
is repeated indefinitely. Set the notation

A\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\ \textrm{and}\ B\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\ ,\ \text{ thus }A^{a_{j}}B=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{j}&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\ .

It is a matter of a simple computation to check that if a line
k
has the slope
s
(i.e.,
k=\langle(1,s)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
), then
A^{a_{j}}Bk
has the slope
\frac{1}{a_{j}+s}
. If we now let

M=A^{a_{0}}BA^{a_{1}}B\dots BA^{a_{n}}B

and apply the observation on the slopes above repeatedly to
\alpha
, we see that the slope of
M\ell
reduces to

\frac{1}{a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{\ddots+\frac{1}{a_{n}+\alpha}}}}=\alpha\ ,

using
\alpha=[\overline{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}]
. Thus,
M\ell=\ell
. The matrix
M
also preserves
\Gamma
as it is an integer matrix of determinant
\pm 1
, and hence is the matrix required.

We now check the case for an
\alpha
with an eventually periodic continued fraction expansion
\alpha=[c_{0}\dots c_{m},\overline{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}]
. Denote by
\beta=[\overline{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}}]
the irrational corresponding to the periodic part of the continued fraction expansion, and denote by
\ell^{\prime}
the line of slope
\beta
. Analogously to the argument above, applying
P\coloneqq A^{c_{0}}B\dots BA^{c_{m}}B
to
\ell^{\prime}
produces a line of slope

\frac{1}{c_{0}+\frac{1}{c_{1}+\frac{1}{\ddots+\frac{1}{c_{n}+\beta}}}}=\alpha\ .

That is,
P\ell^{\prime}=\ell
. Hence, choosing
M^{\prime}=A^{a_{0}}BA^{a_{1}}B\dots BA^{a_{n}}B
and
M=PM^{\prime}P^{-1}
gives a matrix for which
M(\ell)=PM^{\prime}P^{-1}(\ell)=PM^{\prime}(\ell^{\prime})=P(\ell^{\prime})=\ell
. Notice that
M^{\prime}
and
P
are integer matrices of determinant
\pm 1
and in particular,
M
preserves
\Gamma
. Since
M(\ell)=\ell
, with the latter irrational (i.e., not intersecting non-zero lattice points), it is clear that the eigenvector
(1,\alpha)
has a real, irrational eigenvalue
\lambda
, in particular
|\lambda|\neq 1
. Replacing
M
with
M^{-1}
, if necessary, it follows that
M|_{\phy}
is expansive, so the second claim now follows from Theorem
3.3
.

If we now take
W\subset\intl
to be polytopal, it is a union of closed intervals. The only supporting subspace is
\{\mathbf{0}\}
so the scheme is FD-invariant (as is always the case in codimension
1
). Thus,
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
consists of
L^{m}
-sub elements, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, if and only if
W
is rational, by Theorem
3.14
(in fact, if all the boundary points of
W
are integral, it is not hard to show that we may take
m=1
here, as clearly
W
is constructable from
A(W)
in this case). Equivalently, if we choose a translate of
W
so that some boundary point is rational, all vertices of
W
are rational.

The final statement, on the pseudo self-affine elements, follows directly from Theorem
3.15
, at least when we choose one vertex over the origin. If, instead, vertices are merely in
\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
, then the result clearly still holds, as translating a boundary point over the origin just changes the torus parametrisation by addition of an element in
\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
.
∎

In the Sturmian case (where the window is a projection of
[0,1]^{2}
), similar but slightly different results have appeared before, that connect the property of being substitutive with the slope being quadratic irrational. For instance, in
[
PT11
, Theorem 4.1]
, the slope (taken with

) needs to be a Sturm number i.e., with Galois conjugate not in

[

(or its inverse, if

We get the more satisfying condition of the slope just needing to be a quadratic irrational. Part of the explanation of the difference is that, in our result (which also needs to respect the geometric structure and not merely the symbolic sequence), we may choose an appropriate projection to the physical space i.e., choose the internal space, which is determined as the contracting eigenspace of
M\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. In the result of
[
Lam98
]
, the orthogonal projection is used instead. The below explains this discrepancy with an explicit example:

Example 3.21
.

Consider the cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
with
\tot=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and

\alpha=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
,

,

and

Explicitly, this can be realised as follows. First, each Delone set is MLD to the tiling of

), so any rational window defines substitutional cut and project sets, for an appropriate power.

The choice of window for the classical Ammann–Beenker is given by
[0,1]^{4}_{<}
, or any translate of this, which is clearly rational. In fact, for the translate

, where

is the centre of the octagon

is non-singular, so defines a literal cut and project set
\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W)
. Let us now indicate how to construct this

from lattice translates of

: The vertices of

are
\Gamma_{<}
translates of the corresponding vertices of

W=\bigcup_{g\in X}A(W)-g_{<}.

Here

25

\Gamma

\mathbf{0}
, providing the centred
A(W)
in the above union, one
g_{<}
for each vertex of
W
, and another two
g_{<}
from each edge of
W
such that the translates of
A(W)
by these cover the edge. See Figure

.

Since the make up of

Generally, the substitution rules may not be so simple. Consider, for instance, a non-convex polytopal window, such as a star shape. It may be impossible to tile (a translate of)

with

(or

Up to adding this information through an MLD recolouring, though, it is not hard to see that one may always at least replace points with clusters only depending on the colour of a point.

4.
Acceptance domains, the limit translation module, and local derivation for cut and project sets

In this section we introduce some constructions and tools needed to prove Theorems
3.1
and its corollaries from Section
3
. Namely, we define and check basic properties of acceptance domains, the limit translation module, and local isomorphisms and local derivations, in the context of cut and project sets. In the next section, we then apply these techniques in the proof of Theorem
3.1
and related results in the case of general windows. Further tools specific to the polytopal window case will be given in the Section
6
, where Theorems
3.14
and
3.15
are proved.

4.1.
Indicator subsets and acceptance domains

Fix a Euclidean cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
and, for now, non-singular window
W\subset\intl
. Let
\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W)
. Recall that, for bounded
K\subset\phy
, we have
K
-patches
\cps[x,K]
for
x\in\cps
, defined as
\cps[x,K]=\cps\langle K+x\rangle-x
. For any
K
-patch
P
of
\cps
, we have the
indicator set

(4.1)

\cps_{P}\coloneqq\{x\in\cps\mid\cps[x,K]=P\}\

i.e., the centres of occurrences of
P
. This is a slight abuse of notation since
\cps_{P}
may depend on
K
as well as
P
; one may like to think of
P
as a patch which records the existence but also the absence of any points potentially being in
K
. By repetitivity,
\cps_{P}
is also a Delone set. In fact, it turns out to also be a cut and project set with window
W_{P}
constructable from
W
, which we will call an acceptance domain.

Sometimes, especially for reducing complexity in practical applications, it can be convenient to work with a more flexible notion of patches. We define an
indicator
to be a set of pairs
P=(P_{i}^{\mathrm{in}},P_{i}^{\mathrm{out}})_{i=1}^{\ell}
of finite sets
P_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
P_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\subset\phy
, indexed over the colours
i=\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
of the window (in the single-coloured case, we just drop the indices
i
). We define its associated
indicator set
to be

\cps_{P}\coloneqq\{x\in\cps\mid x+y\in\cps_{i}\text{ for each }y\in P_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}\text{ and }x+y^{\prime}\notin\cps_{i}\text{ for each }y^{\prime}\in P_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\}.

In other words, for
x\in\cps
, we determine if
x\in\cps_{P}
simply by checking, for each colour
i
, whether certain displacements from
x
are in
\cps
and colour
i
, or that certain such displacements do not occur. Thus, it is obvious that
\cps\LD\cps_{P}
. In the single-coloured case, we instead have just one pair
P^{\mathrm{in}}
and
P^{\mathrm{out}}
.

Without loss of generality (since
\cps-\cps\subset\Gamma_{\vee}
), we may assume that each
P_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}\subset\Gamma_{\vee}
. Indeed, otherwise,
\cps_{P}=\emptyset
. Similarly, we assume that
P_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\subset\Gamma_{\vee}
as, otherwise,
x+y^{\prime}\notin\cps
for any
x\in\cps
so no relevant information is added. Moreover, if
x\in\cps
,
x+y\in\cps
then
x^{\star}\in W
and
x^{\star}+y^{\star}\in W
, thus
x^{\star}\in W-y^{\star}\subset W-W
. Thus, we assume that
(P_{i}^{\mathrm{in}})^{\star}\subset W-W
, since otherwise
\cps_{P}=\emptyset
. Similarly, we assume that
(P_{i}^{\mathrm{out}})^{\star}\subset W-W
, as otherwise
x+y\notin\cps
for any
x\in\cps
and again this adds no information in this case. In summary, we will assume that each
P_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
P_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\subset\mathscr{C}_{\vee}
for the
cylinder

\mathscr{C}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma_{<}\in W-W\}
of lattice points contained in the thickened physical space
\phy+(W-W)
. We denote the lifted versions of these sets as

\Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{in}}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma_{\vee}\in P_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}\},\ \Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{out}}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma_{\vee}\in P_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\}\subset\mathscr{C}.

Given our restrictions, to define an indicator it is thus equivalent to choose the finite lifted subsets of
\mathscr{C}
. In the special case of a
K
-patch
P=\cps[x,K]=(P_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
, we define its associated indicator by

\Gamma^{\mathrm{in}}_{P_{i}}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{K}\mid\gamma_{\vee}\in P_{i}\},\ \Gamma^{\mathrm{out}}_{P_{i}}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{K}\mid\gamma_{\vee}\notin P_{i}\},\ \text{ where }\Gamma_{K}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathscr{C}\mid\gamma_{\vee}\in K\}.

By the above discussion, it is clear that the indicator set
\cps_{P}
results in the same definition as before, since
P_{i}=(\Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{in}})_{\vee}
and
(\Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{out}})_{\vee}
excludes all possible points which
could
be in
P
, but are not. Note that
\Gamma_{K}
is a finite set, since it is contained in the bounded set
K+(W-W)
, which is the disjoint union
\Gamma_{K}=\Gamma^{\mathrm{in}}_{P_{i}}\cup\Gamma^{\mathrm{out}}_{P_{i}}
.

Definition 4.1
.

For an indicator (or patch)
P
, defined by
\Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
\Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{out}}\subset\mathscr{C}
, we define its
acceptance domain
as

W_{P}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(\left(\bigwedge_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{in}}}W_{i}-\gamma_{<}\right)\wedge\left(\bigwedge_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{P_{i}}^{\mathrm{out}}}\neg W_{i}-\gamma_{<}\right)\right)\wedge W,

using the Boolean operators
\wedge
,
\neg
from Notation
2.28
.

Remark 4.2
.

In the single-coloured case, we have the slightly simpler formula

W_{P}\coloneqq\left(\bigwedge_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{P}^{\mathrm{in}}}W-\gamma_{<}\right)\wedge\left(\bigwedge_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{P}^{\mathrm{out}}}\neg W-\gamma_{<}\right)\wedge W.

We may remove the final
\wedge W
term if
P
is a
K
-patch with
\mathbf{0}\in K
, since we always have
\mathbf{0}\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{in}}_{P}
in this case (so
\wedge W
is already included in the first intersection). In several proofs below, the single- and multi-coloured cases are proved very similarly, so to save on technical notation we will often only present the single-coloured case (or give brief notes on how the proofs are generalised), as the changes needed are otherwise obvious. However, our definitions and results will all be given for both the single- and multi-coloured cases.

The lemma below gives the reassuring result that, in practice, we can essentially replace the Boolean operations, as used above, with the standard ones. The advantage of using their modifications, from Notation
2.28
, is that the resulting acceptance domains are compact and topologically regular, so are themselves windows according to Definition
2.24
(and even polytopes, in the case
W
is polytopal).

Lemma 4.3
.

If
W^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
then
\partial W^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{N}
, explicitly,
\partial W^{\prime}
is contained in the finite union of boundaries
\partial W_{ij}=\partial(W)+g_{ij}
, where the
g_{ij}\in\Gamma_{<}
are those used to express
W^{\prime}
in terms of
W
, as in Equation
2.2
. Let
W^{\prime\prime}
be given by replacing each operation
\wedge
,
\vee
and
\neg
in this expression with its standard Boolean counterpart. Then, for
x\in\mathscr{N}
, we have that
x\in W^{\prime}
if and only if
x\in W^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

It is not hard to show that, for any
U
,
V\in\mathscr{B}
, we have that
\partial(U\vee V)\subseteq\partial(U)\cup\partial(V)
,
\partial(U\wedge V)\subseteq\partial(U)\cup\partial(V)
and
\partial(\neg U)=\partial U
. Applying this observation repeatedly to an expression
W^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
, as in Equation
2.2
, we see that
\partial W^{\prime}
is contained in the finite union of boundaries
\partial W+g_{ij}
, where the
g_{ij}\in\Gamma_{<}
are the translations of
W
(or
\neg W
) used in the expression. Thus, in particular,
\partial W^{\prime}\subset\mathscr{N}
. The same is true, by the same argument, for
W^{\prime\prime}
. One may check that, for any
U
,
V\in\mathscr{B}
, we have that
U\vee V
,
U\wedge V
and
\neg U
agree with
U\cup V
,
U\cap V
and
\intl\setminus U
, respectively, on all points
x\notin\partial(U)\cup\partial(V)
. Thus, given
x\in\mathscr{N}
, it follows that
x\in W^{\prime}
if and only if
x\in W^{\prime\prime}
since, inductively under each application of an operation,
x
does not belong to the boundaries of any of the intermediate sets.
∎

The following lemma shows that, when non-empty, indicator sets are themselves cut and project sets, with windows constructable from the original:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W)
with
W
non-singular and
P
be a patch (or, more generally, an indicator). Then
x\in\cps_{P}
if and only if
x^{\star}\in W_{P}
, equivalently,
\cps_{P}=\cps(\mathcal{S},W_{P})
. In particular,
\cps_{P}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
W_{P}\neq\emptyset
, in which case
\cps_{P}
is a cut and project set with scheme
\mathcal{S}
and window
W_{P}
constructable from
W
.

Proof.

We just present the single-coloured case, with the multi-coloured version being similar. By definition,
\cps_{P}\subset\cps\subset\Gamma_{\vee}
. So suppose that
x\in\Gamma_{\vee}
. Then
x\in\cps
if and only if
x^{\star}\in W
. In this case,
x\in\cps_{P}
if and only if
x+y\in\cps
for each
y=\gamma_{\vee}\in P_{\mathrm{in}}=(\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}^{P})_{\vee}
and
x+y\notin\cps
for each
y\in P_{\mathrm{out}}=(\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}^{P})_{\vee}
. Applying the star map (which is a homomorphism) these are equivalent to
x^{\star}+y^{\star}\in W
i.e.,
x^{\star}\in W-y^{\star}=W-\gamma_{<}
, and
x^{\star}\notin W-\gamma_{<}
, respectively, that is (with
x^{\star}\in W
),

x^{\star}\in\left(\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{P}_{\mathrm{in}}}W-\gamma_{<}\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{P}_{\mathrm{out}}}\intl\setminus(W-\gamma_{<})\right)\cap W.

Since
x^{\star}\in\mathscr{N}
, by Lemma
4.3
the standard Boolean operations may be replaced with the modified ones of Notation
2.28
, so this is equivalent to
x^{\star}\in W_{P}
, by Definition
4.1
, as required. By definition,
W_{P}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
, so it is constructable from
W
, and compact since
W_{P}\subseteq W
. If
\cps_{P}\neq\emptyset
then, since
\cps_{P}=\cps(\mathcal{S},W_{P})
, we have
W_{P}\neq\emptyset
. Conversely, if
W_{P}\neq\emptyset
, we have
\mathrm{int}(W)\neq\emptyset
(since
W_{P}\in\mathscr{B}
), so
\cps_{P}\neq\emptyset
by density of
\Gamma_{<}
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The requirement that
\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W)
for
W
non-singular allows for the given simpler statement above. For a singular cut and project set, one needs to be careful in defining which acceptance domain
x^{\star}
should belong to when it hits boundary points, which will be determined by the way in which
\cps
is given as a limit of non-singular cut and project sets.

Notation 4.6
.

For bounded
K\subset\phy
, let
\mathscr{A}_{K}=\mathscr{A}_{K}(W)
denote the set of all acceptance domains of
K
-patches. In the case that
K=B(\mathbf{0},r)
, that is, when
P
is an
r
-patch, we call
W_{P}
an
r
-acceptance domain
and let
\mathscr{A}_{r}
denote the set of
r
-acceptance domains over all
r
-patches.

Lemma 4.7
.

The set
\mathscr{A}_{K}
of acceptance domains of
K
-patches tile
W
, in the sense their union is
W
and their interiors are disjoint.

Proof.

By the previous lemma,
x^{\star}\in W_{P}
for any
x\in\cps
, where
P=\cps[x,K]
. Moreover, since
x\in\cps
if and only if
x^{\star}\in W
, all points of
(\Gamma_{<})\cap W
belong to some
K
-acceptance domain. We have that
\Gamma_{<}
is dense in
W
, so the acceptance domains cover a dense subset of
W
. Moreover, the union of acceptance domains is closed, since
\Gamma_{K}
is clearly finite and each
W_{P}
is closed. So the union of the
K
-acceptance domains is closed, and thus equal to
W
.

Now suppose that
\mathrm{int}(W_{P})\cap\mathrm{int}(W_{P^{\prime}})\neq\emptyset
for distinct
K
-patches
P
and
P^{\prime}
. Since
\Gamma_{<}
is dense, there must exist
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
\gamma_{<}\in\mathrm{int}(W_{P})\cap\mathrm{int}(W_{P^{\prime}})
. But then, by the previous lemma,
P=\cps[\gamma_{\vee},K]=P^{\prime}
, so
W_{P}=W_{P^{\prime}}
, as required.
∎

The following is an elementary fact, and we omit the proof.

Lemma 4.8
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, for sufficiently large
r>0
each
W_{P}\in\mathscr{A}_{r}
has diameter at most
\varepsilon
.

Our main theorems apply to Euclidean cut and project schemes where the windows can be multi-coloured. The below shows that there is no loss of generality, though (at least for those referencing the MLD-invariant notions of
L
-(S)sub, LIDS etc.), in working with single-coloured windows.

Proposition 4.9
.

For a Euclidean cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
and multi-coloured window
W=(W_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
, there is another, single-coloured window
W^{\prime}
that is mutually constructable from
W
. If each
W_{i}
is polytopal then so is
W^{\prime}
and, in this case,
W
is rational and FD-invariant with respect to a linear map
A\colon\intl\to\intl
if and only if
W^{\prime}
is.

Proof.

Given a multi-coloured window
W=(W_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
, the window
(W_{i}+(\gamma_{i})_{<})_{i=1}^{\ell}
is clearly mutually constructable from
W
, for any choice of
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma
. In particular, by taking appropriate
\gamma_{i}
, we may assume, up to MLD equivalence, that the
W_{i}
are all disjoint. If we then drop colours from
W
, this clearly produces a single-coloured window
W^{\prime}
that, by definition, is constructable from
W
. Conversely, using Lemma
4.8
, find an
r
-acceptance domain
W^{\prime}_{P}
of
W^{\prime}
with diameter
\varepsilon
, where
2\varepsilon
is strictly smaller than the minimal distance between two of the colour components (which, as above, we assume is strictly positive). By density of
\Gamma_{<}
, we may cover each
W_{i}
with translates
W^{\prime}_{P}+g
, for
g\in Z_{i}
, for some finite set
Z_{i}\subset\Gamma_{<}
, where each
(W^{\prime}_{P}+g)\cap W_{i}\neq\emptyset
. By definition, the
W^{\prime}_{P}+g
are constructable from
W^{\prime}
, thus so is each
W_{i}=\bigcup_{g\in Z_{i}}(W^{\prime}\cap(W^{\prime}_{P}+g))
, as required.

By construction, since we only moved window components by lattice vectors in the above argument, it is clear that the properties of being polytopal, rational and FD-invariant are preserved.
∎

4.2.
The limit translation module for Euclidean cut and project sets

The following definition can be found in
[
BG13
, Section 5.1.2]
and will be crucial in determining structure on the cut and project scheme from an
L
-sub cut and project set.

Definition 4.10
.

For a Delone (multi)set
\Lambda
and bounded
K\subset E
, let

D_{K}=D_{K}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\left\{(y-x)\in E\mid\Lambda[x,K]=\Lambda[y,K]\text{ for some }x,y\in\cps\right\}.

That is,
D_{K}
is the set of displacements between translation-equivalent
K
-patches in
\Lambda
. In the particular case of
K=B(\mathbf{0},r)
, we write
D_{r}\coloneqq D_{K}
. We let
\Delta_{K}=\Delta_{K}(\Lambda)=\langle D_{K}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
be the
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by
D_{K}
, denoted
\Delta_{r}
for
K=B(\mathbf{0},r)
. Clearly, for
K\subseteq K^{\prime}
, we have
D_{K}\supseteq D_{K^{\prime}}
and thus
\Delta_{K}\supseteq\Delta_{K^{\prime}}
. Similarly, for any two bounded
K
,
K^{\prime}\subset E
we have that
\Delta_{K\cup K^{\prime}}\subseteq\Delta_{K}\cap\Delta_{K^{\prime}}
. The
limit translation module

\Delta=\Delta(\Lambda)
is given by the inductive limit of this diagram of Abelian groups, that is

\Delta=\bigcap_{\mathrm{bdd}K\subset E}\Delta_{K}=\bigcap_{r>0}\Delta_{r}\ .

In the literature, it is more usual to define
D_{K}
by letting the
x
,
y\in E
, not just
\cps
. However, this does not affect the limit translation module. Indeed, let
D^{\prime}_{K}
denote this standard version of
D_{K}
. Obviously,
D^{\prime}_{K}\supseteq D_{K}
. On the other hand, let
r>0
be such that every
x\in E
is within
r
of some point of
\cps
. Then, if
\cps[x,K+B_{r}]=\cps[y,K+B_{r}]
, we also have
\cps[x+z,K]=\cps[y+z,K]
for some
z\in B_{r}
, where
x+z\in\cps
. Provided
\mathbf{0}\in K
, we have
y\in\cps
too and thus,
D^{\prime}_{K+B_{r}}\subseteq D_{K}
.

The following result (see
[
BG13
]
) is a simple exercise:

Proposition 4.11
.

If
\Lambda^{\prime}\LI\Lambda
then
\Delta(\Lambda^{\prime})\leq\Delta(\Lambda)
. Similarly, if
\Lambda\LD\Lambda^{\prime}
then
\Delta(\Lambda)\leq\Delta(\Lambda^{\prime})
. In particular, if
\Lambda\LIs\Lambda^{\prime}
or
\Lambda\MLD\Lambda^{\prime}
then
\Delta(\Lambda)=\Delta(\Lambda^{\prime})
.

We now make some simple observations regarding the return vectors and the limit translation module of an
L
-Sub pattern, and then a cut and project set. The below follows directly from Proposition
4.11
and Definition
2.10
:

Corollary 4.12
.

Suppose that
\cps
is
L
-Sub. Then
\Delta=L(\Delta)
.

Similarly, if
\cps
is
L
-sub we at least have
L(\Delta)<\Delta
when
\cps
is repetitive or
L
is expansive (as, by Theorem
2.19
, we have that
\cps\LIs\cps^{\prime}
, its predecessor, in this case). The following slightly more specific version of this type of result will be used elsewhere:

Lemma 4.13
.

Suppose that
\cps
is
L
-sub, with predecessor
\cps^{\prime}\LIs\cps
(for instance,
L
is expansive or
\cps
is repetitive). Then there exists some
\kappa\geq 0
so that, for all bounded
K
and
v\in D_{K}
, we have that
L(v)\in D_{K^{\prime}}
, provided that
K^{\prime}\subseteq L(K)_{-\kappa}
, where
L(K)_{-\kappa}
is the set of points whose
\kappa
-balls are fully contained in
L(K)
.

Proof.

We have a predecessor
\cps^{\prime}
for which
L(\cps^{\prime})\LD\cps\LIs\cps^{\prime}
. From
\cps^{\prime}\LIs\cps
, we have
D_{K}=D_{K}(\cps)=D_{K}(\cps^{\prime})
(and similarly for
K^{\prime}
satisfying the statement). So, given
v\in D_{K}
, there exist
x
,
y\in\cps^{\prime}
with
v=x-y
and
\cps^{\prime}[x,K]=\cps^{\prime}[y,K]
. Then, clearly,
(L\cps^{\prime})[L(x),L(K)]=(L\cps^{\prime})[L(y),L(K)]
. Let
L\cps^{\prime}\LD\cps
have derivation radius
c
. It follows that
\cps[L(x),L(K)_{-c}]=\cps[L(x),L(K)_{-c}]
. Let
r>0
be such that all points of the ambient space are within distance
r
of a point of
\cps
. Then there exists some
z
with
\|z\|\leq r
with
\cps[L(x)+z,L(K)_{-(c+r)}]=\cps[L(y)+z,L(K)_{-(c+r)}]
and
L(x)+z
,
L(y)+z\in\cps
. Taking
\kappa=c+r
, we see that
(L(x)+z)-(L(y)+z)=L(v)\in D_{L(K)_{-\kappa}}
, as required.
∎

Corollary 4.14
.

There exists some
C>0
so that, for sufficiently large
r
, if
v\in D_{r}
then
L(v)\in D_{Cr}
, where we may take

if

is expansive. In particular, for some sufficiently large

, for all

we have that
L(v)\in D_{0}=\cps-\cps
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma

4.3.
Local isomorphism and local derivations for cut and project sets

The concepts of local isomorphism and local derivations have simple descriptions in the context of cut and project sets. First, we consider those which are locally isomorphic. Note that, given our definitions,
\cps\LI\cps^{\prime}
if and only if
\cps\LIs\cps^{\prime}
for cut and project sets
\cps
and
\cps^{\prime}
, since all our cut and project sets are repetitive.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a cut and project scheme,

and
W^{\prime}
be windows,
\cps\in\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
and
\cps^{\prime}\in\Omega(\mathcal{S},W^{\prime})

\cps\LIs\cps^{\prime}
if and only if
W=W^{\prime}+s
for some

.

Proof.

It also follows directly from our definitions that translating the window does not affect
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
, so clearly if
W=W^{\prime}+s
then
\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W^{\prime})
and all these cut and project sets are locally isomorphic. Conversely, suppose that
\cps\LIs\cps^{\prime}
. By repetitivity, without loss of generality
\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W)
and
\cps^{\prime}=\cps(\mathcal{S},W^{\prime})
with
W
and
W^{\prime}
non-singular and containing the origin. By density of
\Gamma_{<}
and
W
being compact and equal to the closure of its interior, for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
, there is a sufficiently large
r>0
so that
P_{r}=\cps\langle B(\mathbf{0},r)\rangle\subset\cps
is such that the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood

of
P_{<}
covers
W
. Since
P
also appears in
\Lambda^{\prime}
, up to translation, the same properties hold for
W^{\prime}+s_{\varepsilon}
for some
s_{\varepsilon}=\gamma_{<}\in\Gamma_{<}
. Thus,
P_{<}\subset W,W+s_{\varepsilon}\subset N_{\varepsilon}
, where
N_{\varepsilon}
is an
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
P_{<}
. It follows that the Hausdorff distance between
W
and
W^{\prime}+s_{\varepsilon}
converges to

as

, and it easily follows that

for some

, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, with

.
∎

We now want to show that constructability is necessary and sufficient for local derivability. However, it is also useful to see how a local derivation can be explicitly described by a construction between windows. We do this through the following lemma and proposition. In these results, if
W
is multi-coloured, we let

also denote the union of its colour components, where in each instance this should be clear, given the context.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{B}(W)
, that is,
A
is constructable from
W
, where we assume that
A\subseteq W
. Then
A=W_{P}
for some indicator
P
.

Proof.

Since

, we may express

in terms of
\Gamma_{<}
translates of
W
, as in Equation
2.2
. Namely, we may write
A
as a union of sets

of the form

X=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}W_{j},

where each
W_{j}=W+(g_{j})_{<}
or
\neg W+(g_{j})_{<}
for
g_{j}\in\Gamma
. Without loss of generality (since we assume that

and thus each

) we may include
-\wedge W
into the above. Thus, similarly to in the discussion to the definition of indicators, we may assume without loss of generality that each

belongs to the cylinder

. Hence, defining

by letting

be the set of

and

∎

Proposition 4.21
.

Consider a cut and project scheme
\mathcal{S}
and two windows

and

for it, in non-singular position. Denote
\cps\coloneqq\cps(\mathcal{S},W)
and
\cps^{\prime}\coloneqq\cps(\mathcal{S},W^{\prime})

as

(4.2)

Proof.

By Lemma

, we can indeed write each

from

. Thus, we just need to show that

Since
\cps^{\prime}
,

(the latter due to each

if and only if

For a single-coloured window, in particular, if we may take each

\cps=\cps(\mathcal{S},W)

Remark 4.23
.

Perhaps in contrast to other references, we do not assume that
\cps^{\prime}
is a cut and project set in the above, the proof above derives this fact (something which we need later). For simplicity, we do assume that
\cps^{\prime}\subset\Gamma_{\vee}
, although an inspection of the proof of the second direction reveals the extent to which this is necessary. If this is dropped then, instead,
\cps^{\prime}
is a union of translates of cut and project sets (note that such an object may not be constructable from a cut and project scheme, by choosing algebraically incommensurate shifts in the physical space). One could, in principle, handle this situation by allowing different translates to be applied to the cut and project set for each colour component of a window. However, this will not be required later, so we again do not say much more on this technicality.

Proposition 5.3

The map

Proof.

Suppose that

L
-sub patterns, recall that substitution

Proposition 5.4
.

Consider a hull of Euclidean cut and project sets
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
. Let

satisfying (1–3) of Theorem
3.1
be given, which thus defines a toral automorphism

. Then, for any substitution map
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
with inflation

, we have
\tau\circ\sigma=T_{u}\circ\tau
for some
[u]\in\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, we may express this

as

[u]=\tau(\sigma\cps)-M_{\mathbb{T}}(\tau(\cps))

for any
\cps\in\Omega
. We have that
\mathscr{N}+[u]=\mathscr{N}
and
\mathscr{S}+[u]=\mathscr{S}
. Any other substitution map
\sigma^{\prime}\colon\Omega\to\Omega

\tau\circ\sigma^{\prime}=T_{u}\circ\tau

\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma
.

Proof.

Let
\cps\in\Omega
be arbitrary,
[a]\coloneqq\tau(\cps)
and
[b]\coloneqq\tau(\sigma\cps)\in\mathbb{T}
. Since
\sigma=S\circ L
satisfies
\sigma(\cps+x)=\sigma(\cps)+Lx

\tau(\sigma(\cps+x))=\tau(\sigma(\cps)+Lx)=\tau(\sigma(\cps))+[Lx]=[b]+M_{\mathbb{T}}[x]

for all
x\in\phy
. On the other hand,

T_{u}(\tau(\cps+x))=T_{u}([a]+[x])=M_{\mathbb{T}}[a+x]+([b]-M_{\mathbb{T}}[a])=M_{\mathbb{T}}[x]+[b]

so
\tau\circ\sigma=T_{u}\circ\tau
on the translational orbit
\cps+\phy\subset\Omega
for the above choice of
[u]=[b]-M_{\mathbb{T}}[a]
. Since this orbit is dense, and
\tau
,
T_{u}
and
\sigma
are continuous,
\tau\circ\sigma=T_{u}\circ\tau
on all of
\Omega
, as required.

Now, suppose that
\cps\in\Omega
is singular, so there exists some other
\cps^{\prime}\neq\cps
with
\tau(\cps)=\tau(\cps^{\prime})=[x]\in\mathbb{T}
, where
x\in\mathscr{S}
. By the above,
\tau(\sigma\cps)=T_{u}(\tau\cps)=T_{u}(\tau\cps^{\prime})=\tau(\sigma\cps^{\prime})
. Since
\sigma
is injective, we have
\tau(\sigma\cps)=\tau(\sigma\cps^{\prime})
for distinct
\sigma\cps\neq\sigma\cps^{\prime}
, so each is singular. Thus,
\sigma
maps singular points to singular points. Similarly,
\sigma
maps non-singular points to non-singular points. Indeed, if
\cps
is non-singular but
\sigma\cps
is not, we have
\tau(\sigma\cps)=\tau(\cps^{\prime})
for some
\cps^{\prime}\neq\sigma\cps
. We may write
\cps^{\prime}=\sigma\cps^{\prime\prime}
, since
\sigma
is surjective. From
\tau(\sigma\cps)=\tau(\sigma\cps^{\prime\prime})
, applying
T_{u}\circ\tau=\tau\circ\sigma
, we have
T_{u}(\tau\cps)=T_{u}(\tau\cps^{\prime\prime})
. But
\cps\neq\cps^{\prime\prime}
(or else
\sigma\cps=\sigma\cps^{\prime\prime}=\cps^{\prime}
). Since
T_{u}
is injective, we see that
\tau\cps=\tau\cps^{\prime\prime}
, contradicting that
\cps
is non-singular.

We see that
\sigma
, and thus
T_{u}[x]=M_{\mathbb{T}}[x]+[u]
, preserves the singular and non-singular points of
\mathbb{T}
. Since
M\phy=\phy
and
M\Gamma=\Gamma
, we have
M_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathscr{N})=\mathscr{N}
, so
\mathscr{N}+[u]=\mathscr{N}
and similarly
M_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathscr{S})=\mathscr{S}
so
\mathscr{S}+[u]=\mathscr{S}
, as required.

Finally, suppose that
\sigma^{\prime}
also satisfies
\tau\circ\sigma^{\prime}=T_{u}\circ\tau
. Let
\cps\in\Omega
be non-singular (thus, by the above,
\sigma\cps
and
\sigma^{\prime}\cps
are also non-singular). Then

(\tau\circ\sigma)(\cps)=(T_{u}\circ\tau)(\cps)=(\tau\circ\sigma^{\prime})(\cps).

Since
\sigma\cps
is non-singular and
\tau(\sigma\cps)=\tau(\sigma^{\prime}\cps)
, we must have that
\sigma\cps=\sigma^{\prime}\cps
. But then
\sigma(\cps+x)=\sigma(\cps)+Lx=\sigma^{\prime}(\cps)+Lx=\sigma^{\prime}(\cps+x)
, so
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
on the dense orbit
\cps+\phy
in
\Omega
. By continuity,
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
.

for all
\cps\in\Omega
. Then, given any
\cps\in\Omega
with
\tau\cps=[v+s]
for some
v\in\phy
, take in particular
[u]=[(v-L^{p}(v))+(s-A^{p}(s))]
and let
\sigma
be the associated substitution. Then

\tau(\sigma\cps)=T_{u}(\tau\cps)=[M^{p}(v+s)]+[u]=[L^{p}(v)+A^{p}(s)+(v-L^{p}(v)+s-A^{p}(s))]=[v+s],

so
\tau(\sigma\cps)=\tau\cps
. Thus,
\sigma(F)\subseteq F
for the fibre
F=\tau^{-1}[v+s]
. By assumption,
F
is finite. Moreover, since
\sigma
is bijective, some power
\sigma^{i}
is the identity on

, thus each element of
F
is fixed by the (invertible) substitution
\sigma^{i}
, which has inflation map
L^{m}
for some multiple
m
of

and (A) holds, as required.
∎

6.
Substitutional polytopal windows

In this section we prove Theorem
3.14
, giving a simple necessary and sufficient condition for a cut and project scheme with polytopal window to produce substitutional cut and project sets, as well as Theorem
3.15
, which characterises the LIDS (equivalently, pseudo self-affine) points in terms of the rational points. Recall, from Section
3
, the finite sets
\mathscr{H}
and
\mathscr{H}_{0}
of supporting hyperplanes and subspaces, respectively. The following concept will be useful, especially when analysing the complexity of polytopal cut and project sets:

Definition 6.1
.

A subset
f\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{0}
of exactly
n=\dim(\intl)
elements is called a
flag
if

\bigcap_{V\in f}V=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Similarly, a subset
f\subseteq\mathscr{H}
of
n
elements is called a
flag
when the intersection is a singleton, equivalently,
V(f)
is a flag.

The following stabiliser subgroups will be particularly useful in establishing that (1) implies (2) in Theorem
3.14
.

Definition 6.2
.

For a subspace
X\leqslant\intl
, we define the
stabiliser
subgroup

\Gamma^{X}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid X+\gamma_{<}=X\}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma_{<}\in X\}\leqslant\Gamma.

In particular, for each
V\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
we have a stabiliser
\Gamma^{V}
. For a translation
Y\subset\intl
of a subspace
V(Y)
, we similarly define the
stabiliser
subgroup

\Gamma^{Y}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid Y+\gamma_{<}=Y\}=\Gamma^{V(Y)}.

In particular, for each
H\in\mathscr{H}
we have a stabiliser
\Gamma^{H}
.

Throughout this Section, as in Section
3.1
, we assume that the necessary conditions (1–3) of Theorem
3.1
(see also Theorem
3.3
) hold, relative to a hyperbolic map
M\colon\tot\to\tot
, restricting to an expansion
L\colon\phy\to\phy
on the physical space. A marked difference to the non-polytopal case is the second conclusion of the following:

Lemma 6.3
.

If
W
is FD-invariant then
A(X)=X
for any subspace
X
that is an intersection of subspaces from
\mathscr{H}_{0}
. In particular,
A
is diagonalisable and has only real, irrational eigenvalues of modulus strictly less than one.

Proof.

Since each
V\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
is invariant under
A
, the same is true of intersections
X
of such subspaces. Intersecting all but one of the elements of a flag in
\mathscr{H}_{0}
(of which there must be one, or else
W
would not be compact) defines a
1
-dimensional subspace
Y
, and these subspaces span
\intl
. Since each such subspace is invariant, we see that
A
is diagonalisable with real eigenvalues. They must be irrational, or else
M
has a rational eigenvalue too, which consequently has corresponding eigenvector
\gamma
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
(this is most easily seen by reparameterising to
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, so that
M
is represented by an integer matrix). Since
\intl
is the contracting subspace of
M
, it follows that
\gamma\in\intl
, contradicting that
\intl\cap\intl=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
∎

6.1.
Proof that face direction invariant and rational implies substitutional

In this subsection we prove the first direction of Theorem
3.14
, establishing that a cut and project scheme is substitutional when it is FD-invariant and rational. So, in this subsection we will assume:

(1)

face direction invariance with respect to
A^{p}
, for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
;

(2)

rationality.

For the proof below we assume for simplicity (and without loss of generality, as neither FD-invariance, rationality or being substitutional depends on the translate of
W
used) that
W
is in
singular
position, with a vertex
v
over the origin. Moreover, by Proposition
4.9
, we may assume throughout that
W
is single-coloured.

Theorem 6.4
.

For
W
as above, we have that
W
is constructable from
A^{m}(W)
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, the elements of
\Omega
are
L^{m}
-Sub.

Proof.

By rationality (and our choice of translate above), the vertices of
W
belong to
\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
, that is,
\mathrm{Ver}(W)\subset\frac{1}{N}\Gamma_{<}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We also have that
A
maps
\Gamma_{<}
isomorphically to itself, so similarly for
\frac{1}{N}\Gamma_{<}
. Then
A
quotients to an isomorphism
A\colon Q\to Q
on the quotient group
(\frac{1}{N}\Gamma_{<})/\Gamma_{<}
, which is finite since
\frac{1}{N}\Gamma_{<}
is finite index in
\Gamma_{<}
. It follows that
A^{m}
acts as the identity on
Q
for a sufficiently high power
m=jp
, with
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, by assumption,
A^{m}
acts as the identity on
\mathscr{H}_{0}
. Note that
A^{m}
permutes the connected components of
\intl\setminus\bigcup_{H\in\mathscr{H}_{0}}
. By taking an even larger power, if necessary, we may assume that
A^{m}
acts as the identity on this finite set. Henceforth we set
m=1
, to simplify notation, by starting with some such power of
M
.

Construct
B\in\mathscr{B}(A(W))
containing a ball around the origin, but also with very small diameter
\kappa
. For
v\in\mathrm{Ver}(W)
, define
B^{\prime}_{v}\coloneqq(B+\gamma_{<})\wedge A(W)
for
\gamma_{<}
very close to
Av\in\mathrm{Ver}(AW)
. It is not hard to see that, if
\kappa
is small enough and
\gamma_{<}
is close enough to
Av
, then
B_{v}\coloneqq B^{\prime}_{v}+(v-Av)\subset W
and
B_{v}=W\cap U
on some ball
U
centred at
v
. Indeed, by our choice of the power of
A
, a small region around the vertex at
v
transforms, under
A
, to a translate of this region in
A(W)
about
Av
. Since
v-Av\in\Gamma_{<}
, we also have
B_{v}\in\mathscr{B}(A(W))
. Thus, by taking the union of elements
B_{v}
over all
v\in\mathrm{Ver}(W)
, we define a set
B_{0}\in\mathscr{B}(A(W))
with
B_{0}\subseteq W
which contains a neighbourhood of the vertex set in
W
.

The above may now be repeated on higher dimensional cells. For example, for each edge
e
between vertices
v
,
w\in\mathrm{Ver}(W)
, start with
B_{v}
as above. By rationality,
N(w-v)\in\Gamma_{<}
. Moreover, by Lemma
6.3
, so is
A^{i}(N(w-v))
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, since the line parallel to
e
(which is an intersection of hyperplanes of
\mathscr{H}_{0}
) is invariant under
A
. Since
A
is contractive, it follows that the subspace parallel to
e
contains a dense subset in
\Gamma_{<}
. Thus, by taking sufficiently many translates of
B_{v}^{\prime}
with elements of
\Gamma_{<}
lying on this line, we may cover a neighbourhood of the edge
e\subseteq W
with a union of such elements, which by construction is some subset
B_{e}\in\mathscr{B}(A(W))
. Moreover, if we initially intersect
B_{v}
with a much smaller diameter set containing
v
(which we may always easily construct), we may easily ensure that
B_{e}
does not intersect
W
across any other face not incident with
e
(except perhaps near the vertices
v
and
w
, but these are already correctly covered by
B_{v}
and
B_{w}
). Thus, taking the union
B_{1}
of these sets and
B_{0}
, we have constructed a subset
B_{1}\in\mathscr{B}(A(W))
with
B_{1}\subseteq W
and covering a neighbourhood of its
1
-skeleton.

The above argument may be repeated to construct
B_{2}
, …,
B_{n}\in\mathscr{B}(W)
, where
B_{n}=W
, where in each step we observe that, for each cell of
W
, we have that
\Gamma_{<}
is dense in the parallel subspace, using Lemma
6.3
. Thus,
W\in\mathscr{B}(A(W))
and we have proved (4a). Since we assume (1–3) throughout this section, and
L
is expansive, we have that
\Omega
consists of
L^{m}
-sub patterns for the appropriate power
m
noted at the start of the proof, by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Since
W
(without any translation) is constructable from
A^{m}(W)
for a suitable power
m\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows quickly from Theorem
3.9
that all rational translates (in the internal space) are LIDS. In fact, we have the following more specific result; note that an analogous statement and proof works whenever
\tau\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{T}
has finite fibres, and
\tau\circ\sigma=T\circ\tau
where
T=M_{\mathbb{T}}^{m}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

There is an (injective) substitution map
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
, with inflation
L^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, this satisfies
\tau\circ\sigma=T\circ\tau
, where
T[x]=[M^{m}(x)]
. We have that every
\cps\in\Omega
with
\tau\cps=[u]
, for
u_{<}\in\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
, satisfies
\sigma^{\ell}(\cps)=\cps+e
for some power
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in\phy
. In particular, all such elements are LIDS.

Proof.

Since, by the above proof,
W
is constructable from
A^{m}(W)
, by Theorem
3.5
we have a substitution map
\sigma
for which
\tau\circ\sigma=T\circ\tau
, since we may take
s=u=\mathbf{0}
in that result. Here, we take the power
M^{m}
in place of
M
, so the inflation map is
L^{m}
on
\phy
. To simplify notation, we replace
M
with
M^{m}
at this point (so, for instance, we replace
A^{m}
with
A
).

Take
\cps
as in the statement and write
u=v+s
, where
v=u_{\vee}
and
s=u_{\vee}\in\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
. So
s=\frac{1}{N}\gamma_{<}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. As in the previous proof, consider a power
A^{\ell}
of
A
which acts as the identity on the quotient
\frac{1}{N}\Gamma_{<}/\Gamma_{<}
. Thus, in particular,
A^{\ell}(s)-s\in\Gamma_{<}
so

T^{\ell}[u]=[M^{\ell}(u)]=[L^{\ell}(v)+A^{\ell}(s)]=[L^{\ell}(v)+s]=[L^{\ell}(v)-v]+[v+s]=[v^{\prime}]+[u],

where
v^{\prime}=L^{\ell}(v)-v\in\phy
. Then, for any
\cps^{\prime}\in\Omega
with
\tau\cps^{\prime}=[u]
, we have that

\tau(\sigma^{\ell}(\cps^{\prime})-v^{\prime})=\tau(\sigma^{\ell}\cps^{\prime})-[v^{\prime}]=T^{\ell}(\tau\cps^{\prime})-[v^{\prime}]=[u],

so the map
\cps^{\prime}\mapsto\sigma^{\ell}(\cps^{\prime})-v^{\prime}
sends the (finite) fibre
\tau^{-1}[u]
to itself. Since this map is injective, a power is the identity, so it follows that all such patterns are fixed under some power of
\sigma^{\ell}
, followed by a translation and thus they are LIDS.
∎

We will see a converse to the above in a later result, that the LIDS points
\cps\in\Omega
satisfy
\tau\cps=[u]
for
u_{<}\in\mathbb{Q}\Gamma_{<}
.

6.2.
Proof that substitutional implies face direction invariant and rational

Throughout this subsection we assume that
\Omega=\Omega(\mathcal{S},W)
, for
W
polytopal (and, without loss of generality by Proposition
4.9
, single-coloured), consists of
L
-Sub cut and project sets, where
L
is expansive. If they are all, instead, merely
L^{m}
substitutional for some
m>1
then we pass to the corresponding power
M^{m}
. This does not affect the properties of FD-invariance or rationality, nor conditions (1–3) required of the linear automorphism
M^{m}\colon\tot\to\tot
. Thus, the elements of
\Omega
are assumed to be
L
-Sub, so (1–4) of Theorem
3.1
hold.

Lemma 6.6
.

We have that
W
is FD-invariant with respect to some power
A^{\ell}
.

Proof.

Since the elements of
\Omega
are
L
-Sub,
W
is constructable from some translate of
A(W)
, by Theorem
3.1
. By Lemma
4.3
, we may thus cover
\partial W
by a finite set of translates of
\partial A(W)
. Since
A
is a linear automorphism it is bijective as a function
V\mapsto A(V)
on codimension
1
subspaces. In particular, the supporting subspaces
\mathscr{H}_{0}
of
W
are in bijective correspondence with those of
A(W)
under the map
A
. Then, if
A(V^{\prime})\notin\mathscr{H}_{0}
for some
V^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
, then there must be some
V\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
with
V\neq A(V^{\prime})
for all
V^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
. Then it is impossible to cover
\partial W
using a finite number of translates of
\partial A(W)
. Thus,
A
defines a bijection
A\colon\mathscr{H}_{0}\to\mathscr{H}_{0}
and, since
\mathscr{H}_{0}
is finite, some power
A^{\ell}
acts as the identity on
\mathscr{H}_{0}
.
∎

Given the above lemma, we will henceforth assume FD-invariance throughout the remainder of this subsection. Thus,
A
is diagonalisable with real, irrational eigenvalues strictly less than
1
in modulus (by Lemma
6.3
). To simplify constructions, we will now position the window so that
W
it is constructable from
A(W)
, rather than merely a translate of it being so.

Lemma 6.7
.

For an appropriate translate of
W
, we have that
W
is mutually constructable from
A(W)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.14
(4) we already know that
W-s
is mutually constructable from
A(W)
for some
s\in\intl
. Thus,
W-(s+A(x))
is mutually constructable from
A(W-x)=A(W)-A(x)
for all
x\in\intl
, so we just need
x\in\intl
with
s+A(x)=x
, equivalently
(\Id-A)(x)=s
. We have that
\Id-A
is invertible (since
A
is contractive, so
1
is not an eigenvalue), so such an
x\in\intl
exists, as required.
∎

Our proof of this direction of Theorem
3.14
will end up establishing that
\Omega
consists of low complexity patterns, in the sense below. In the case that
L
is a similarity, we would know that the elements of
\Omega
are linearly repetitive, in which case the property would hold automatically. However, we are able to show this without restricting to the case of
L
being a similarity.

Definition 6.8
.

We say that
\Omega
has
low complexity
(and say it has property
C
, for short) if there is some
C>0
so that for some (equivalently any)
\cps\in\Omega
, the number of
r
-patches is bounded above by
Cr
for all
r\geq 1
.

The proof of rationality now breaks into two steps. Firstly, we will show that the stabilisers
\Gamma^{V}
of
V\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
have sufficiently high rank to force
C
, making use of results from
[
KW22
]
(see also
[
Wal24
]
for the case of non-convex windows). Amongst other properties, this will imply that singleton intersections of
\Gamma_{<}
translates of the supporting subspaces are rational, that is, they are contained in some
\frac{1}{N}\Gamma_{<}
. We then show that all supporting hyperplanes are in fact rational translates of the supporting subspaces.

Lemma 6.9
.

Each
V\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
has a basis in
\Gamma^{V}_{<}
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
V\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
, with parallel supporting hyperplanes
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{\ell}\}\subset\mathscr{H}
, where we denote
H\coloneqq H_{1}
. By FD-invariance, we have that
\{A(H_{1}),\ldots,A(H_{\ell})\}
are the supporting hyperplanes of
A(W)
parallel to
V
.

By Lemma
6.7
,
W\in\mathscr{B}(A(W))
, so by Lemma
4.3
we have that
\partial W\subset\bigcup_{g\in Z}\partial A(W)+g_{<}
for a finite set
Z\subset\Gamma
. In particular, for a generalised ‘face’
F=\partial W\cap H
, which is a compact set with non-empty interior in
H
, we have
F\subset\bigcup_{g\in S}\partial A(W)+g_{<}
. We may write
\partial A(W)
as the union of closed sets
F_{H^{\prime}}\coloneqq\partial A(W)\cap A(H^{\prime})
, over all
H^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
. Of course,
(F_{H^{\prime}}+x)\cap F
has zero measure for
A(H^{\prime})+x\neq H
, so we must have that
F
is contained in the union of
F_{H_{i}}+g_{<}
with
A(H_{i})+g_{<}=H
. By passing to a sufficiently large power, if necessary, we may ensure that the
F_{H_{i}}
have very small diameter relative to some ball contained in
F\subset H
, small enough so that we need at least
n
distinct translates
F_{H_{i}}+(g_{j})_{<}\subset H
used in covering
F
, for some fixed
i
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where
\{(g_{j}-g_{1})_{<}\mid j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}\}
is a basis for
V
and each
g_{j}\in\Gamma
. Thus, the projected lattice points
(g_{j}-g_{1})_{<}\in\Gamma_{<}\cap V=\Gamma_{<}^{V}
span
V
, as required.
∎

Since each
\Gamma^{V}_{<}
contains a basis for
V
, it is rank at least
n-1
. In fact, by FD-invariance and the fact that
A
is a contraction, we in fact know that it must have strictly higher rank than this. We aim to show that their ranks are, in fact, generally as high as they can be, which will prove
C
, the low complexity condition. To this end, we introduce the following technical lemma:

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
f=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}\}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{0}
be a flag and let
G_{m}\coloneqq\Gamma^{V_{m}}
. For
m=0
, …,
n
, we also define
V_{m}^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}
(with
V_{0}^{\prime}\coloneqq\intl
),
G_{m}^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}G_{i}
(with
G_{0}^{\prime}\coloneqq\Gamma
),
X_{m}\coloneqq\langle G_{m}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
and
X_{m}^{\prime}\coloneqq\langle G_{m}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
(hence
X_{0}^{\prime}=\tot
). Then

(1)

X_{i}^{\prime}=X_{1}\cap X_{2}\cap\cdots\cap X_{i}
for
i=0
, …,
n
;

(2)

X_{i}^{\prime}+X_{i+1}=\tot
for
i=0
, …,
n-1
.

Proof.

We have that (1) is true for
i=0
since
X_{0}^{\prime}=\tot
, which is also the vacuous intersection of subspaces of
\tot
in (1), by convention. Moreover,
X_{0}^{\prime}+X_{1}=\tot+X_{1}=\tot
, so (2) is trivial.

So suppose that both (1) and (2) hold for
i=m-1
, with
m\leq n
. By a standard dimension formula,

\dim(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m-1})+\dim(X_{m})=\dim((X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m-1})\cap X_{m})+\dim((X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m-1})+X_{m}).

By induction, we thus have
\dim(X_{m-1}^{\prime})+\dim(X_{m})=\dim(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m})+k
. By definition,
\dim(X_{m})=\rk(G_{m})
and
\dim(X_{m-1}^{\prime})=\rk(G_{m-1}^{\prime})
so that

(6.1)

\rk(G_{m-1}^{\prime})+\rk(G_{m})=\dim(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m})+k.

On the other hand, using the short exact sequence

0\to G_{m-1}^{\prime}\cap G_{m}\to G_{m-1}^{\prime}\times G_{m}\to G_{m-1}^{\prime}+G_{m}\to 0,

we have the rank formula
\rk(G_{m-1}^{\prime})+\rk(G_{m})=\rk(G_{m}^{\prime})+\rk(G_{m-1}^{\prime}+G_{m})
. Combining with Equation
6.1
, we see that

(6.2)

\rk(G_{m}^{\prime})+\rk(G_{m-1}^{\prime}+G_{m})=\dim(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m})+k.

Since each
G_{i}\leqslant X_{i}
, it is clear that
\rk(G_{m}^{\prime})\leq\dim(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m})
. Similarly, since
G_{m-1}^{\prime}+G_{m}\leqslant\Gamma
, we have that
\rk(G_{m-1}^{\prime}+G_{m})\leq k
. So both of these inequalities must in fact be equalities to ensure Equation
6.2
holds, so that

\rk(G_{m}^{\prime})=\dim(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m})\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \rk(G_{m-1}^{\prime}+G_{m})=k.

Since
X_{m}^{\prime}\coloneqq\langle G_{m}^{\prime}\rangle\leqslant X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m}
and
\dim(X_{m}^{\prime})=\rk(G_{m}^{\prime})=\dim(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m})
, we have
X_{m}^{\prime}=X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m}
, as required by claim (1) of the lemma for
i=m
.

To prove (2), now suppose additionally that
m<n
. So (1) holds for
i=m
, by the above, and (2) holds for
i=m-1<n-1
. We have that
G_{m}^{\prime}+G_{m+1}
is a lattice in its span, denoted

Z\coloneqq\langle G_{m}^{\prime}+G_{m+1}\rangle=\langle G_{m}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle G_{m+1}\rangle=X_{m}^{\prime}+X_{m+1}=(X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m})+X_{m+1}\ .

Recall (Lemmas
6.3
and
6.6
) that
A
is diagonalisable, with eigenvectors that may be chosen to lie on the lines given as the intersections of all but one of the hyperplanes of the flag
f
. Take any such line
Y
, where
A(Y)=Y
, say
A(y)=\lambda y
, where
|\lambda|<1
since
A
is contractive. We claim that, for any
V_{i}\in f
, if
Y\subseteq V_{i}
then
Y\subseteq X_{i}
. Indeed, take any
y\in Y
. By Lemma
6.9
,
\Gamma_{<}^{V_{i}}
contains a basis for
V_{i}
and hence there is at least some
x\in X_{i}
so that
x_{<}=y
. We may write
x=v+y
for
v=x_{\vee}\in\phy
. Then
M^{-n}(\lambda^{n}x)=\lambda^{n}L^{-n}(v)+\lambda^{n}A^{-n}(y)=\lambda^{n}L^{-n}(v)+y
. Since
L
is expansive,
L^{-n}
is contractive so that
L^{-n}(v)\to\mathbf{0}
, and so of course
\lambda^{n}L^{-n}(v)\to\mathbf{0}
too as
n\to\infty
. Thus,
y_{n}\coloneqq M^{-n}(\lambda^{n}x)\to y
as
n\to\infty
. But
X_{i}
is invariant under
M
and
M^{-1}
, so each
y_{n}\in X_{i}
. Since
X_{i}
is closed, we have that
y\in X_{i}
, as required.

Thus, for each eigenline
Y\subseteq\intl
defined by the flag, we must have that
Y\subseteq X_{1}\cap X_{2}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m}=X_{m}^{\prime}
, or
Y\subseteq X_{m+1}
(since every such

Definition of classical tiling substitutions

In this final section, we compare the notion of a pattern being
L
-sub to standard notions, for Delone (multi)sets and tilings, being generated by substitution rules. Throughout this section,

will be assumed to be expansive.

7.1.
L
-sub patterns versus substitutional Delone (multi)sets

The following is a widely used notion
[
LW03
,
Lee23
]
of a Delone (multi)set being substitutional:

Definition 7.1
.

A Delone (multi)set
\cps=(\cps_{i})_{i\leq m}
is called a
substitution Delone multiset
(
SDM
) if
\cps
is a Delone (multi)set and there exists an expansive map
L\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and finite sets
\mathcal{D}_{ij}
for
i
,
j\leq m
such that, for each
i\leq m
,

(7.1)

\Lambda_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}(L\Lambda_{j}+\mathcal{D}_{ij})\ ,

where the unions on the right-hand side are disjoint.

Firstly, we observe that an SDM, with expansion
L
, is clearly
L
-sub, where Equation
7.1
provides a local rule
L\cps\LD\cps
. More precisely, suppose the maximal norm of element over all
\mathcal{D}_{ij}
is
c
. Suppose that
(L\cps)[x,c]=(L\cps)[y,c]
. Then, for each
j
and
z\in B_{c}
, we have
x-z\in L\cps_{j}
if and only if
y-z\in L\cps_{j}
. Thus, for all
i
and
j
, we have
x\in L\cps_{j}+D_{ij}
if and only if
y\in L\cps_{j}+D_{ij}
, that is,
x\in\cps_{i}
if and only if
y\in\cps_{i}
, showing that
L\cps\LD\cps
, with derivation radius
c
.

Of course, it is not true that every Delone (multi)set
\cps
which is
L
-sub is an SDM, since the former captures the idea that
\cps
is any element in a hull of substitutional patterns, whereas Definition
7.1
captures the notion of a pseudo self-affine point, substituting to itself under a (very particular kind of) substitution rule. However, we will show that a repetitive
L
-sub pattern, at least up to a ‘local recolouring’ (see Definition
7.3
), is one generated in the hull of an SDM.

For the result below recall that, generally, for a space
\Omega
of patterns (in this case, Delone (multi)sets), a substitution is a map
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
, defined by
\sigma=S\circ L
where
S
is a local derivation map. All
L
-sub patterns (for repetitive patterns, or
L
expansive, as here) uniquely define such a substitution map.

Proposition 7.2
.

For any FLC pattern space, with substitution
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
, we have that
\sigma^{n}
has a fixed point for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

For a proof of the above, see
[
Wal25
]
, which applies to non-repetitive pattern spaces too (and defines a notion of
\Omega
being FLC, although for the repetitive examples considered here this is automatic).

Definition 7.3
.

For Delone (multi)sets
\cps
and
\cps^{\prime}
, we say that
\cps^{\prime}
is a
local recolouring
of
\cps
if
\mathrm{sup}(\cps)=\mathrm{sup}(\cps^{\prime})
and
\cps\MLD\cps^{\prime}
.

Usually, we will recolour by labelling points with their
r
-patches, which is clearly an MLD relabelling. Of course, a local relabelling extends over all Delone sets in a hull (or one may define the notion for a space of such patterns, analogously to definition of a derivation map).

Definition 7.4
.

We say that a substitution rule
\sigma=S\circ L\colon\Omega\to\Omega
on a hull of Delone (multi)sets is
colour-defined
(or
CD
, for short) if the subdivision rule
S
may defined by

(7.2)

S(L\cps)_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}(L\Lambda_{j}+\mathcal{D}_{ij}).

If the unions on the right-hand side are disjoint, we call a CD substitution rule
non-overlapping
.

Remark 7.5
.

In other words, a substitution rule is CD and non-overlapping when it is a rule of the form given in Definition
7.1
. We use the non-overlapping condition since Definition
7.1
is how it is often stated in the literature, although note that it seems quite natural to not require this condition generally, analogously to how for substitutions of tilings (such as the Penrose or Ammann–Beenker tile substitutions) one often considers rules which, when applied to the tilings, can have non-disjoint substitutes of different tiles. The only restriction in a substitution being colour-defined is that the rule must only replace points with clusters depending just on the colour of points i.e., not using information from other nearby points. Thus, by adding extra information to points, using local patches, it is not hard to see why the following holds:

Proposition 7.6
.

For any substitution rule
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
on a hull of Delone (multi)sets, by applying a local recolouring of points (by labelling points by their
r
-patches, for sufficiently large

), the resulting substitution map on the hull of recoloured Delone (multi)sets may be described by a CD and non-overlapping substitution.

Proof.

We begin by showing we may enhance the colouring to define Delone (multi)sets with (non-overlapping) colours allowing for a CD substitution rule. We will then refine the argument to show it can be made non-overlapping.

Let
\kappa>0
be such that, for any
\cps^{\prime}\in\Omega

L\cps^{\prime}
, where

Given any

7.2.
L
-sub tilings versus substitution tilings

In the above section, we showed how a standard notion of a Delone (multi)set being substitutional corresponds rather directly (essentially, “up to MLD”) to our notion of
L
-sub. In this section, we relate
L
-sub with the classical notion of being substitutive for tilings.

Given a tile substitution rule
\sigma
(satisfying standard properties, such as FLC and primitivity), for each tiling
\cT
admitted by
\sigma
, it is well-known that there is always a ‘predecessor’ tiling
\cT^{\prime}\in\Omega
for which
\sigma\cT^{\prime}=\cT
, see for instance
[
AB98
]
. Substitution is defined by inflation followed by a subdivision rule
S
on tiles, so
S(L\cT^{\prime})=\cT
. Since subdivision
S
is clearly a local derivation, we have that
L\cT^{\prime}\LD\cT
. And since
\cT^{\prime}
belongs to the translational hull of
\cT
, we have that
\cT^{\prime}\LI\cT
, establishing that
\cT
is
L
-sub.

The relationship in the converse direction is established by a result of Solomyak
[
Sol97
]
(and earlier, in the planar case, of Priebe–Frank and Solomyak
[
PS01b
]
), namely, that a pseudo self-affine tiling is self-affine, up to MLD. Recall that a tiling
\cT
is pseudo self-affine if it is repetitive and
L\cT\LD\cT
, for an expansion
L
.

So, suppose that
\cT
is a repetitive and
L
-sub pattern. It may be shown (see, for instance,
[
Wal25
]
) that, at least for some power
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the associated substitution map
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
has some fixed point
\sigma^{n}(\cT^{\prime})=\cT^{\prime}
. Thus,
L^{n}\cT^{\prime}\LD\cT^{\prime}
, so
\cT^{\prime}\in\Omega
is pseudo self-affine. The result of
[
Sol97
]
then says that
\cT^{\prime}\MLD\cT^{\prime\prime}
, where
\cT^{\prime\prime}
is fixed by a stone inflation substitution, with expansion perhaps some further power of
L^{n}
. Thus, the hull
\Omega
is, up to MLD, generated by a standard tile stone inflation rule. With the above, this establishes that our notion of
L
-sub only differs from other standard notions up to redecorating our patterns up to MLD.
