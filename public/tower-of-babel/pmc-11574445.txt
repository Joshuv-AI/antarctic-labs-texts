Peer review of the pesticide risk assessment of the active substance gibberellic acid (GA3)
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Fernando Álvarez, Maria Arena, Domenica Auteri, Sofia Batista Leite, Marco Binaglia, Anna Federica Castoldi, Arianna Chiusolo, Angelo Colagiorgi, Mathilde Colas, Federica Crivellente, Chloe De Lentdecker, Isabella De Magistris, Mark Egsmose, Gabriella Fait, Franco Ferilli, German Giner Santonja, Varvara Gouliarmou, Katrin Halling, Laia Herrero Nogareda, Alessio Ippolito, Frederique Istace, Samira Jarrah, Dimitra Kardassi, Aude Kienzler, Anna Lanzoni, Roberto Lava, Renata Leuschner, Alberto Linguadoca, Jochem Louisse, Christopher Lythgo, Oriol Magrans, Iris Mangas, Andrea Mioč, Ileana Miron, Tunde Molnar, Laura Padovani, Vincenzo Padricello, Martina Panzarea, Juan Manuel Parra Morte
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conclusions of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) following the peer review of the initial risk assessments carried out by the competent authorities of the rapporteur Member State Slovenia and co‐rapporteur Member State Slovakia for the pesticide active substance gibberellic acid and the considerations as regards the inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 are reported. The context of the peer review was that required by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659. The conclusions were reached on the basis of the evaluation of the representative use of gibberellic acid as a plant growth regulator on seedless grapes (field use). The reliable end points, appropriate for use in regulatory risk assessment, are presented. Missing information identified as being required by the regulatory framework is listed. Concerns are reported when identified.Keywords: GA3, gibberellic acid, peer review, pesticide, plant growth regulator, risk assessment

SUMMARY

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659, lays down the procedure for the renewal of the approval of active substances submitted under Article 14 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. The list of those substances is established in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 686/2012 as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2016/183. Gibberellic acid is one of the active substances listed in that Regulation.

In accordance with Article 1 of Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, the rapporteur Member State (RMS), Slovenia and co‐rapporteur Member State (co‐RMS), Slovakia, received an application from the European Gibberellic Acid Task Force II (Valent Biosciences LLC (Sumitomo Chemical Agro Europe SAS), Fine Agrochemicals Ltd., Nufarm GmbH & Co. KG, L. Gobbi s.r.l., Globachem NV and Aifar SpA) for the renewal of approval of the active substance gibberellic acid. In addition, the applicants submitted an application for inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005.

An initial evaluation of the dossier on gibberellic acid was provided by the RMS in the renewal assessment report (RAR) and subsequently, a peer review of the pesticide risk assessment on the RMS evaluation was conducted by EFSA in accordance with Article 13 of Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659. The following conclusions are derived.

The use of gibberellic acid according to the representative use as a plant growth regulator on seedless grapes via spray application to improve fruit quality and yield by cluster stretching, berry thinning and berry sizing in Southern EU results in sufficient efficacy as plant growth regulator.

The assessment of the data package revealed no issues that need to be included as critical areas of concern with respect to the identity, physical, chemical and technical properties of gibberellic acid or the formulation for representative uses, and analytical methods.

In the area of mammalian toxicology, issues that cannot be finalised and critical areas of concerns were not identified.

In the area of residues in food and feed, the consumer risk assessment cannot be finalised until information on the nature of the residues and their relevance for consumers becomes available. The criteria for potential inclusion in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 are either not fulfilled (criteria I, II and III) or cannot be evaluated as no or insufficient data are available (criterion IV and V) for gibberellic acid.

In the area of environmental fate and behaviour, satisfactory information on the aerobic route of degradation of gibberellic acid in soil was not available. Consequently, the risk assessment to soil and aquatic organisms and the groundwater exposure assessment for any transformation products that might be formed from gibberellic acid in soil cannot be finalised, as well as the groundwater exposure assessment for metabolite AS7. Information is also missing regarding the effect of the water treatment processes on the nature of the residues of both the active substance and its identified metabolites that might be potentially present in groundwater and surface water, when surface water or groundwater is abstracted for the production of drinking water. This data gap leads to the consumer risk assessment from the consumption of drinking water being not finalised.

In the area of ecotoxicology, the risk assessment for soil microorganisms and non‐target terrestrial plants could not be finalised due to the lack of suitable data.

Gibberellic acid is unlikely to meet the criteria for endocrine disruption both for humans and non‐target organisms according to points 3.6.5 and 3.8.2 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605.

BACKGROUND

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, 1 as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659 2 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Regulation’), lays down the provisions for the procedure of the renewal of the approval of active substances, submitted under Article 14 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. 3 This regulates for the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) the procedure for organising the consultation of Member States, the applicant(s) and the public on the initial evaluation provided by the rapporteur Member State (RMS) and/or co‐rapporteur Member State (co‐RMS) in the renewal assessment report (RAR), and the organisation of an expert consultation where appropriate.

In accordance with Article 13 of the Regulation, unless formally informed by the European Commission that a conclusion is not necessary, EFSA is required to adopt a conclusion on whether the active substance can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided for in Article 4 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 within 5 months from the end of the period provided for the submission of written comments, subject to an extension of an additional 3 months where additional information is required to be submitted by the applicant(s) in accordance with Article 13(3). Furthermore, in accordance with Article 13(3a), where the information available in the dossier is not sufficient to conclude the assessment on whether the approval criteria for endocrine disruption are met, additional information can be requested to be submitted in a period of minimum 3 months, not exceeding 30 months, depending on the type of information requested.

In accordance with Article 1 of the Regulation, the RMS Slovenia and co‐RMS Slovakia received an application from the European Gibberellic Acid Task Force II (Valent Biosciences LLC (Sumitomo Chemical Agro Europe SAS), Fine Agrochemicals Ltd., Nufarm GmbH & Co. KG, L. Gobbi s.r.l., Globachem NV and Aifar SpA) for the renewal of approval of the active substance gibberellic acid. In addition, the applicants submitted an application for inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. 4 Complying with Article 8 of the Regulation, the RMS checked the completeness of the dossier and informed the applicants, the co‐RMS (Slovakia), the European Commission and EFSA about the admissibility.

The RMS provided its initial evaluation of the dossier on gibberellic acid in the RAR, which was received by EFSA on 27 February 2019 (Slovenia, 2019).

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA distributed the RAR to the Member States and the applicants, the European Gibberellic Acid Task Force II, for consultation and comments on 22 May 2019. EFSA also provided comments. In addition, EFSA conducted a public consultation on the RAR. EFSA collated and forwarded all comments received to the European Commission 25 July 2019. At the same time, the collated comments were forwarded to the RMS for compilation and evaluation in the format of reporting table. In addition, the applicants were invited to respond to the comments received. The comments and the applicants' response were evaluated by the RMS in column 3 of the reporting table.

The need for expert consultation and the necessity for additional information to be submitted by the applicants in accordance with Article 13(3) of the Regulation were considered in a telephone conference between EFSA and the RMS on 10 September 2019. On the basis of the comments received, the applicants' response to the comments and the RMS's evaluation thereof, it was concluded that additional information should be requested from the applicants and that EFSA should conduct an expert consultation in the areas of mammalian toxicology and ecotoxicology.

In addition, following a consultation with Member States in the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Meeting PREV 26 (March 2020) and its follow up TC (9 July 2020), it was considered necessary to apply an additional clock stop of 30 months in accordance with Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659, to be able to conclude whether the approval criteria for endocrine disruption in line with the scientific criteria for the determination of endocrine disrupting properties, as laid down in Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605, 5 are met.

The outcome of the EFSA‐RMS telephone conference, together with EFSA's further consideration of the comments, is reflected in the conclusions set out in column 4 of the reporting table. All points that were identified as unresolved at the end of the comment evaluation phase and which required further consideration, including those issues to be considered in an expert consultation, were compiled by EFSA in the format of an evaluation table.

The conclusions arising from the consideration by EFSA, and as appropriate by the RMS, of the points identified in the evaluation table, together with the outcome of the expert consultation and the written consultation on the assessment of additional information, where these took place, were reported in the final column of the evaluation table.

A final consultation on the conclusions arising from the peer review of the risk assessment and on the proposal for inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 took place with Member States via a written procedure in August 2024.

This conclusion report summarises the outcome of the peer review of the risk assessment of the active substance and the formulation for representative use, evaluated on the basis of the representative use of gibberellic acid as a plant growth regulator on seedless grapes, as proposed by the applicants. THE ACTIVE SUBSTANCE AND THE FORMULATION FOR REPRESENTATIVE USES

Gibberellic acid is a trivial name for (3S,3aS,4S,4aS,7S,9aR,9bR,12S)‐7,12‐dihydroxy‐3‐methyl‐6‐methylene‐2‐oxoperhydro‐4a,7‐methano‐9b,3‐propenoazuleno[1,2‐b]furan‐4‐carboxylic acid or (3S,3aR,4S,4aS,6S,8aR,8bR,11S)‐6,11‐dihydroxy‐3‐methyl‐12‐methylene‐2‐oxo‐4a,6‐ethano‐3,8b‐prop‐1‐enoperhydroindeno[1,2‐b]furan‐4‐carboxylic acid (IUPAC). This substance is considered by the International Organization for Standardization not to require a common name. As a synonym is also commonly used GA3.

The formulation for representative use for the evaluation was ‘Progibb 40 SG’ (GA3 40%SG; ABG‐3206), a water‐soluble granule (SG) containing 400 g/kg gibberellic acid.

The information on the active substance and the formulation for representative use, including the co‐formulant(s) in the formulation, has been considered in the overall assessment during the peer review. None of the co‐formulant(s) is an unacceptable co‐formulant listed in Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, 7 nor considered as an active substance in accordance with Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. Details on the composition of the formulations cannot be reported in conclusions because of the provisions in Article 63(2)(d) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, however this information was fully available and evaluated during the peer review. Proposal for classification of the formulation according to Regulation (EC) 1272/2008 8 was provided by the applicants and assessed by the RMS (please see Volumes 3 CP of the RAR).

The representative use evaluated comprises outdoor foliar spraying as a plant growth regulator on seedless grapes to improve fruit quality and yield by cluster stretching, berry thinning and berry sizing in Southern EU (SEU). Full details of the GAP can be found in the list of end points in Appendix B.

Data were submitted to conclude that the representative use of gibberellic acid proposed at SEU level results in sufficient efficacy as plant growth regulator on seedless grapes, following the guidance document SANCO/2012/11251‐rev. 4 (European Commission, 2014b).

CONCLUSIONS OF THE EVALUATION

General aspects

With regard to the mammalian toxicity information available for the formulation for representative use ‘Progibb 40 SG’, studies were performed for acute toxicity endpoints. Regarding the co‐formulants contained in ‘Progibb 40 SG’, sufficient toxicological data were available for all components. The peer review experts considered that the available toxicological information sufficiently addressed the genotoxicity and repeated dose toxicity potential of ‘Progibb 40 SG’ over the short‐ and long‐term and no concerns were identified. 9

The availability of ecotoxicity data with the formulation for representative use was discussed at the experts' meeting. 10 Furthermore, the experts also discussed the data retrieval search and the available data for the individual components. No concerns were identified.

A data gap has been identified for a search of the scientific peer‐reviewed open literature on the active substance and its relevant metabolites, dealing with side effects on the environment and published within 10 years before the date of submission of the dossier, to be conducted and reported in accordance with the EFSA guidance on the submission of scientific peer‐reviewed open literature for the approval of pesticide active substances under Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 (EFSA, 2011) (see Section 10).

IDENTITY, PHYSICAL/CHEMICAL/TECHNICAL PROPERTIES AND METHODS OF ANALYSIS

The following guidance documents were followed in the production of this conclusion: European Commission (2000a, 2000b, 2010).

The individual specifications were based on batches from industrial and pilot scale productions. Based on data of the renewal procedure it is proposed the reference specification to be updated with a unified specification covering all sources. The proposal is that the minimum purity of the active substance as manufactured be maintained as for the first approval (850 g/kg), but levels of some of the significant impurities are changed and Fumonisin B1 and Fumonisin B2 are specified as relevant impurities (they were not included in the current reference specification). The sum of Fumonisin B1 + Fumonisin B2 must not exceed 200 μg/kg. 11 It is noted that evaluation of the toxicological relevance of one of the impurities cannot be concluded (see Section 2), and as a consequence, new data such as spectral data, content of the impurity before and after the storage of the formulation and method for analysis of the relevant impurity in the formulation might be required. The batches used in the (eco)toxicological studies support the proposed updated reference specification (see Sections 2 and 5). A FAO specification does not exist for this substance.

The main data regarding the identity of gibberellic acid and its physical and chemical properties are given in Appendix B.

Methods are available for the generation of data required for the risk assessment. Adequate high‐performance liquid chromatography–UV spectroscopy (HPLC‐UV) analytical methods are available for the determination of gibberellic acid content in the technical materials and the formulation for representative use. Appropriate methods exist for the determination of the content of the relevant impurities in the technical materials, however a method for their determination in the formulation is missing (data gap, see Section 10).

Methods of analysis for monitoring of gibberellic acid in food of plant origin might be required pending on the final residue definition for monitoring (see Section 3). Methods of analysis for monitoring of gibberellic acid in food of animal origin are not required as EU MRLs are not proposed. Liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) method exists for monitoring of gibberellic acid in soil with a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.01 mg/kg, however, as the DT90 is less than 3 days, there is no need for a monitoring method. Determination of gibberellic acid in drinking and surface water can be done by LC–MS/MS with a LOQ of 0.1 μg/L. Pending on the final residue definitions for monitoring in soil and surface water, additional method(s) might be required (see Section 4).

Data gaps were identified for methods of analysis for monitoring of gibberellic acid in air and in body fluids and tissues (see Section 10).

MAMMALIAN TOXICITY

The following guidance documents were followed in the production of this conclusion: European Commission (2003, 2012), EFSA (2014, 2017a), and ECHA (2017).

Gibberellic acid was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Meeting PREV 25 (March 2020) and at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference (TC) 125 (January 2024) and follow up TC 131 (March 2024).

The proposed unified reference specification for the renewal is considered to be supported by the batches used in the toxicological studies. Fumonisins B1 and B2 are mycotoxins of known toxicological profile (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2018) and considered as toxicologically relevant impurities. The proposed maximum level of 200 μg/kg (for the sum of B1 and B2, in line with Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006 12 ) in the specification is acceptable since an exposure at the acceptable daily intake (ADI) value of gibberellic acid will result in a value for fumonisins below the group tolerable daily intake (TDI) of 1.0 μg/kg body weight (bw) per day for Fumonisins B1‐4 (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2018). For another impurity, quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) alerts were identified, and in the absence of other data, its toxicological relevance cannot be concluded (data gap, see Section 10).

No toxicokinetics (absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME)) and comparative in vitro metabolism studies are available. The peer review concluded that, based on structural similarity between gibberellic acid and gibberellins (GA4/GA7) for which ADME studies are available, these studies are deemed unnecessary. In addition, the in silico predictions comparing the toxicity profiles of gibberellic acid and gibberellins, which gave similar results (no different alerts), further support that these studies are not needed. The residue definition for body fluids and tissues is defined as gibberellic acid.

In the acute toxicity studies, gibberellic acid has shown low acute toxicity when administered orally, dermally or by inhalation to rats. It is not a skin irritant, skin sensitiser or eye irritant. In addition, gibberellic acid is negative in an in vitro phototoxicity assay. Although the phototoxic potential has not been assessed at wavelengths in UV‐B range, this is deemed not necessary based on the fact that maximum absorption is at around 200 nm and that the molar absorption coefficients at or above 290 nm are < 10 L cm−1 mol−1.

Short‐term oral toxicity studies in rats and limited data with dogs and mice were provided. The overall no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) is 391 mg/kg bw per day based on decreased body weight gain and epithelial hyperplasia in the urinary bladder observed in males at the highest dose in a 90‐day rat study. A short‐term study in dogs was not available and has not been considered necessary based on the low toxicity profile of gibberellic acid.

Based on the available genotoxicity studies, gibberellic acid is unlikely to be genotoxic. 13

Long‐term and carcinogenicity GLP studies are not available; two mouse studies identified in the literature were considered not reliable since they presented severe limitations (e.g. no purity reported, only one dose used). The peer review concluded that the long‐term and carcinogenicity studies can be waived based on low toxicity observed in the short‐term toxicity studies in rats, and considering that gibberellic acid is unlikely to be genotoxic and that it constitutes a natural component of the diet. Nevertheless, the lack of long‐term and carcinogenicity studies has been taken into account for setting reference values (see below).

In the two‐generation rat study, the reproductive toxicity NOAEL is 2397 mg/kg bw per day (highest dose); the overall parental NOAEL is 767 mg/kg bw per day based on increased incidence of loose stool and slight decrease in body weight gain, caecal distension and slight increase in liver weight (relative to body weight); the offspring LOAEL (lowest observable adverse effect level, as a NOAEL could not be established) is 233 mg/kg bw per day based on increased incidence of omphalocele. 14

For developmental toxicity, in both the rat and rabbit teratogenicity studies, the developmental and maternal NOAEL is 1000 mg/kg bw per day (highest dose).

For neurotoxicity, although studies are not available, gibberellic acid does not have a (developmental) neurotoxic potential based on lack of structural alerts for neurotoxicity and no related findings from the available regulatory studies. No immunotoxicity is expected since gibberellic acid does not show effects on the immune system in the available standard toxicity studies.

The ADI is 0.23 mg/kg bw per day, based on the offspring LOAEL of 233 mg/kg bw per day from the two‐generation study in rats (based on increased incidence of omphalocele), and applying a standard uncertainty factor (UF) of 100 plus an additional UF of 10 (to cover extrapolation from sub‐chronic to chronic toxicity, the use of a LOAEL and the uncertainties related to the limited available dataset). It is noted that in the previous conclusion (EFSA, 2012), a different ADI was set (0.68 mg/kg bw per day).

The acute reference dose (ARfD) is 0.78 mg/kg bw, based on the offspring LOAEL of 233 mg/kg bw per day from the 2‐generation study in rats (based on increased incidence of omphalocele) and applying a standard UF of 100 plus an additional UF of 3 to cover the use of a LOAEL. It is noted that in the previous conclusion (EFSA, 2012), an ARfD was considered not justified.

The systemic acceptable operator exposure level (AOEL) and systemic acute acceptable operator exposure level (AAOEL) is 0.23 mg/kg bw (per day), based on the LOAEL from the two‐generation study in rats and applying a standard UF of 100 plus an additional UF of 10 to cover the use of a LOAEL and the uncertainties related to the limited available dataset (including lack of data to set an oral absorption value). It is noted that in the previous conclusion (EFSA, 2012), a different AOEL was set (0.68 mg/kg bw per day) and an AAOEL was not established.

Dermal absorption is 2.2% for both the concentrate and the dilution.

For non‐dietary exposure, estimates were provided with the EFSA calculator (EFSA, 2014). Considering the representative use with ‘Progibb 40 SG’ as plant growth regulator in seedless grapes, the estimated operator exposure is below the (A)AOEL for all methods of applications without personal protective equipment. For bystanders and residents as well as for workers, the estimated exposure is below the (A)AOEL.

Regarding metabolites, pending further identification and quantification of their levels (e.g. for the groundwater metabolite AS7) (see Sections 3, 4 and data gaps in Section 9.1), further assessment of their toxicological relevance might be required.

RESIDUES

The assessment in the residue section is based on the OECD following documents: OECD (2009, 2011), European Commission (2011, 2015) and JMPR (2004, 2007).

Guidance compliant plant or livestock metabolism studies were not submitted and the available information on the metabolism of gibberellins in plants is only derived from published literature. From these studies it could be concluded that three main routes of metabolism of gibberellins (including gibberellic acid) in plants were identified: hydroxylation, glucose conjugation and epoxidation. Identification of the precise plant metabolites formed from gibberellic acid and a relevance assessment for consumers of those metabolites are missing (data gap, see Section 9.1).

Residue trials on seedless table grape varieties in the SEU zone covering the critical GAP conditions and supported by freezer storage stability data were submitted and five of them were found valid. The samples were analysed for gibberellic acid and residues in treated and control samples were below the LOQ of 0.05 mg/kg. However, it is noted that these residue trials only investigated the levels of gibberellic acid. Neither hydroxylated metabolites and their glucoside conjugates, nor epoxy metabolites were quantified in these trials (data gap, see Section 9.1). This information is needed to decide on the potential inclusion of gibberellic acid in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 or, in case this is not possible, to establish adequate residue definitions for risk assessment and enforcement.

Other studies were not submitted and are not required for the representative use.

From an investigation in 17 honey samples from China, transfer of gibberellins to honey was demonstrated, though it was not clear whether the crops that were foraged by bees were subject to treatment with gibberellins at any point (Wang et al., 2017). Even if in this study gibberellic acid was not detected in honey, the occurrence of its plant metabolites cannot be completely ruled out from the representative use as gibberellic acid applied to seedless grapes between the growth stages BBCH 55 to BBCH 75, i.e. during flowering. Therefore, data are required to demonstrate that residues of gibberellic acid and its relevant plant metabolites in honey, resulting from the representative use, will not exceed the natural background levels (data gap, see Section 10).

An indicative consumer risk assessment has been performed with the available information on the residue levels of gibberellic acid (see Appendix B). However, the consumer risk assessment is considered not finalised until information on the nature of the residues and their relevance becomes available. In addition, the consumer risk assessment from the consumption of drinking water could not be finalised, as satisfactory information was not available to address the effect of water treatment processes on the nature of the residues that might be present in surface water and groundwater, when surface water or groundwater is abstracted for the production of drinking water (see Sections 4 and 9.1).

Information on the natural background levels of gibberellic acid in grapes has been provided from the scientific peer‐reviewed literature. In these papers the natural background concentration of gibberellic acid at harvest in seedless grapes and in seeded grapes was measured. The levels of naturally occurring gibberellic acid in grapes at maturity, measured in these studies, are at least two orders of magnitude lower than the LOQ of 0.05 mg/kg used in the residue trials provided. Therefore, it cannot be concluded that the use of gibberellic acid as a plant protection product in grapes would result in residue levels similar to the levels in seedless grape berries. In order to decide on the appropriateness of inclusion of gibberellic acid in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 or whether MRLs are needed, further data on the natural background levels of gibberellins (including gibberellic acid) and the levels and relevance of gibberellic acid metabolites in plant commodities other than grapes will be needed (data gap, see Section 10).

With regard to the five assessment criteria according to the Commission guidance SANCO/11188/2013 Rev. 2 (European Commission, 2015) for potential inclusion of the substance in Annex IV of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, i.e. approval as basic substance (criterion I), listed in Annex I of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 (criterion II), having no identified hazardous properties (criterion III), natural exposure is higher than the one linked to the use as plant protection product (criterion IV) and consumer exposure is not expected considering the representative uses (criterion V), the criteria are either not fulfilled (criteria I, II and III) or cannot be evaluated as no or insufficient data are available (criterion IV and V) for gibberellic acid.

ENVIRONMENTAL FATE AND BEHAVIOUR

The rates of dissipation and degradation in the environmental matrices investigated were estimated using FOCUS (2006) kinetics guidance. In soil laboratory incubations under aerobic conditions in the dark, gibberellic acid exhibited very low persistence, forming seven major metabolites (i.e. observed at levels > 10% applied radioactivity (AR) or > 5% AR on consecutive sampling intervals or > 5% AR and increasing at the end of the study). These metabolites have been tentatively identified as various multiple oxidation, reduction and decarboxylation products of gibberellic acid and were assigned as AS1 (maximum observed level 39.6% AR, 1 DAT (day after treatment)), AS2 (max 10.6% AR, 1 DAT), AS3 (max 11.0% AR, 3 DAT), AS4 (max 11.9% AR, 3 DAT), AS5 (max 27.1% AR, 3 DAT), AS6 (max 25.1% AR, 3 DAT) and AS7 (max 42.4% AR, 7 DAT). Further attempts to positively confirm the identity of these metabolites have proven to be largely inconclusive and data to clearly establish the route of degradation of gibberellic acid in soil, where chromatographically resolved fractions > 10% AR were identified, were not available. Therefore, the route of degradation of gibberellic acid in soil cannot be considered addressed. Consequently, the risk assessment to soil and aquatic organisms and the groundwater exposure assessment for any transformation products that might be formed from gibberellic acid in soil are not finalised for the representative use (see Section 9.1). In addition, ‘unknown metabolites’ occurring at levels up to 27.1% AR were measured in the aerobic soil metabolism study. No information was provided if there was any single component occurring at levels > 10% applied radioactivity or > 5% AR on consecutive sampling intervals (data gap, see Section 9.1). In the absence of robust and reliable kinetic assessment of the soil residue data to determine metabolite DT50 values (with the exception for metabolite AS1 which exhibited very low to low persistence), as a worst‐case assumption a soil DT50 of 5 days was assumed for all metabolites in the exposure modelling. However, it should be noted that this assumption might not be valid for the metabolite AS7 and the fraction ‘unknown metabolites’. 15

Mineralisation of [methylene‐14C]‐gibberellic acid to carbon dioxide accounted for 39%–62% AR after 121 days. The formation of unextractable residues for this radiolabel accounted for 21%–39% AR after 121 days. A batch adsorption/desorption study that uses the indirect method in five soils was available for gibberellic acid. However, this study failed a number of the quality checks that are included in the OECD 106 evaluators checklist (EFSA, 2017b). Therefore, the adsorption endpoints derived from this study are highly uncertain and should not be used in the environmental exposure modelling. However, as the derived sorption endpoint (geometric mean K FOC 1.6 mL/g) for modelling purpose is very conservative, it can be concluded that further data on the adsorption/desorption behaviour of gibberellic acid is not needed to complete the environmental exposure assessment for the representative use applied for. There was no indication that soil adsorption of gibberellic acid was pH dependent in the range of pH of agricultural soils (the pKa of 4.1 indicates that significant dissociation would be expected across this range).

In laboratory incubations in dark aerobic natural sediment water systems, gibberellic acid exhibited low persistence, forming the major metabolites WS1 (max. ca. 35% AR in water and 11.6% AR in sediment), WS3 (max. 27% AR in water but only 5% AR in sediment) and WS4 (max. ca 11% AR in water but only 4% AR max. in sediment). Tentative structures for the metabolites were proposed on the basis of extensive LC–MS/MS analysis. Metabolite WS1 was found to be composed of 2 components WS1a and WS1b, each > 10% AR and each with the same molecular mass as gibberellic acid. These components were positively identified as isogibberellic acid and gibberellenic acid, respectively. However, as a worst‐case for the purposes of the aquatic exposure assessment, WS1 was assumed to be one component based on a combined maximum occurrence in the water/sediment systems of 46.5% AR. The unextractable sediment fraction for the methylene 14C radiolabel accounted for 26%–36% AR at study end (100 days). Mineralisation of this radiolabel accounted for 12%–25% AR at the end of the study. The rate of decline of gibberellic acid in a laboratory sterile aqueous photolysis experiment was slow relative to that occurred in the aerobic sediment water incubations. Gibberellenic acid and isogibberellic acid were the major (> 10% AR) metabolites formed in both the irradiated and non‐irradiated samples.

The necessary surface water and sediment exposure assessments (predicted environmental concentrations (PEC) calculations) were carried out for gibberellic acid, the aquatic metabolites WS1, WS3 and WS4, and the soil metabolites AS1, AS2, AS3, AS4, AS5, AS6 and AS7 using the FOCUS (FOCUS, 2001) step 1 and step 2 approach (version 3.2 of the Steps 1–2 in FOCUS calculator).

The necessary groundwater exposure assessments were appropriately carried out using FOCUS (European Commission, 2014a) scenarios and the models PEARL 4.4.4, PELMO 5.5.3 and MACRO 5.5.4 16 for gibberellic acid and its soil metabolites. In the absence of reliable metabolite formation fractions, separate, stand‐alone simulations were performed for each metabolite, assuming application as parent, using pseudo application rates adjusted to account for the maximum observed percentage of each metabolite in soil and a relative molecular weight correction factor. In addition, in the absence of laboratory‐derived data on their fate and behaviour in the environment, conservative assumptions and reasoning have been employed to select worst‐case modelling endpoints for the metabolites. However, for the metabolite AS7, the worst‐case soil DT50 value of 5 days may not apply, 17 and therefore the groundwater exposure assessment for the soil metabolite AS7 could not be finalised (see Section 9.1). The potential for groundwater exposure from the representative use on seedless grapes by gibberellic acid and its soil metabolites AS1, AS2, AS3, AS4, AS5 and AS6 above the parametric drinking water limit of 0.1 μg/L was concluded to be low in geoclimatic situations that are represented by all 7 FOCUS groundwater scenarios.

The applicants did not provide appropriate information to address the effect of water treatment processes on the nature of the residues that might be present in surface water and groundwater, when surface water or groundwater is abstracted for the production of drinking water. This has led to the identification of a data gap and results in the consumer risk assessment from drinking water not being finalised (see Sections 3 and 9.1).

The PEC in soil, surface water, sediment and groundwater covering the representative use assessed can be found in Appendix B of this conclusion. A key to the wording used to describe the persistence and mobility of the compounds assessed can be found in Appendix C of this conclusion.

ECOTOXICOLOGY

The risk assessment was based on the following documents: European Commission (2002), SETAC (2001), EFSA (2009), EFSA PPR Panel (2013) and EFSA (2013).

Gibberellic acid was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Meeting PREV 26 (March 2020) and at its follow up TC (29 April and 9 July 2020) and at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' TC 129 (January–February 2024).

The batches used in the ecotoxicological studies support the proposed updated reference specification.

Toxicity data on all the different taxa of non‐target organisms were not always available with gibberellic acid. When not available, data on gibberellins (GA4/GA7) were used for risk assessment. The applicants did not submit any information in support of the read across between gibberellins (GA4/GA7) and gibberellic acid. However, based on the structural similarity between gibberellic acid and gibberellins (GA4/GA7) and other available information (e.g. bridging of ADME data from gibberellins (GA4/GA7) to gibberellic acid, acute toxicity data with mammals, aquatic organisms and bees), the use of data from gibberellins (GA4/GA7) for the risk assessment of gibberellic acid was supported.

For the available chronic studies, EC10/EC20 18 estimates were not provided (data gap, see Section 10).

Based on the available information, low risk to birds was concluded for all the different routes of exposure for the representative use under assessment. No chronic data were available on birds, however low chronic risk was concluded considering (i) an endpoint 100 times lower than the no observed effect level (NOEL) derived from the short‐term study, (ii) the evidence showing that gibberellins, including gibberellic acid, are widespread in nature and present in different matrices such as vascular plants, seeds, fruits, etc.

Low acute and long‐term risk to wild mammals was concluded for all the different relevant routes of exposure. The long‐term endpoint for the risk assessment of wild mammals was discussed at the Peer Review Experts' Meeting 26. 19

Following the data gap identified in Section 3, additional assessment may be needed for birds and mammals when exposed to potential pertinent plant metabolites.

Based on the available data, low risk to fish, aquatic invertebrates and algae was concluded by using FOCUS Step 1 PECsw. Nevertheless, a study with a second algal species should be provided (data gap, see Section 10). For aquatic macrophytes, data with the active substance and the formulation for representative use were available both with Lemna sp. and Myriophillum sp. showing Myriophillum to be the most sensitive aquatic macrophyte. The experts at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Meeting 26 agreed that the stimulating effects observed are adverse considering the specific mode of action of gibberellic acid, i.e. plant growth hormone. In the case of gibberellic acid, although a definitive endpoint could not be derived (ErC50 was a lower than value), the experts agreed that low risk could be concluded. This conclusion was reached considering that at the lowest tested dose the effects observed were above but close to 50% and in view of the high margin of safety in the risk assessment. The need for additional data should be further considered at MS level if a lower margin of safety is obtained.

Low risk to pertinent aquatic metabolites (WS1, WS3, WS4, AS1, AS2, AS3, AS4, AS5, AS6, AS7) was concluded based on a screening assessment (metabolites considered to be 10 times more toxic than the parent).

Low risk to honeybees was concluded for all the different routes of exposure using both the SANCO and the EFSA Guidance (EFSA, 2013; European Commission, 2002), except for the treated crop in the chronic risk assessment. However, considering that (i) the exposure toxicity ratios (ETR) were close to the trigger and that (ii) gibberellic acid is present in the environment in different matrices like plants, seeds, overall low chronic risk was concluded. For honeybee larvae, only a single exposure study was available with gibberellins (GA4/GA7). Although according to EFSA (2013), a repeated exposure study should be available, considering the high margin of safety in the risk assessment and the profile of the active substance, no further data are deemed necessary. However, the need for further data may be considered at MS level for product authorisation in case of a different outcome in the risk assessment. No data were available to assess the potential sub‐lethal effects of gibberellic acid on honeybees and for addressing the risk from exposure to metabolites occurring in pollen and nectar (data gap, see Section 10). No data were available on accumulative effects and on wild bees.

Low risk to non‐target arthropods, earthworms and soil macro‐organisms other than earthworms (based on a qualitative assessment) 20 was concluded for the representative use. No reliable studies were available to assess the risk to soil microorganisms, leading to an issue that cannot be finalised (see Section 9.1). Low risk to earthworms was concluded for the pertinent soil metabolites (AS1‐AS7). However, since no data are available for the parent compound on soil microorganisms, the risk assessment could not be performed for the soil metabolites as well (issue not finalised, see Section 9.1).

The available data and risk assessment for non‐target terrestrial plants was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Meeting 26. 21 Due to the lack of a suitable endpoint, the risk assessment could not be performed, leading to an issue that cannot be finalised (see Section 9.1).

In view of the data gap identified in Section 4 to address the route of degradation in soil, the conclusion on the risk assessment for aquatic and soil organisms should be considered provisional (see Section 9.1).

Low risk to organisms involved in biological methods for sewage treatment was concluded.

ENDOCRINE DISRUPTION PROPERTIES

The assessment of the endocrine disrupting (ED) properties of gibberellic acid was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' meetings PREV 25 (March 2020), PREV 26 (March 2020) and its follow up TC (9 July 2020) and at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' TC 124 (January 2024).

With regard to the assessment of the ED potential of gibberellic acid for humans according to the ECHA/EFSA guidance (2018), in determining whether gibberellic acid interacts with the oestrogen, androgen and steroidogenesis (EAS) and thyroid (T) mediated pathways, the number and type of effects induced and the magnitude and pattern of responses observed across studies were considered. Additionally, the conditions under which effects occur were considered, in particular, whether or not endocrine‐related responses occurred at dose(s) that also resulted in overt toxicity. The assessment therefore provides a weight‐of‐evidence (WoE) analysis of the potential interaction of gibberellic acid with the EAS and T signalling pathways using the available evidence in the data set.

The T‐modality was considered sufficiently investigated and T‐mediated adversity was not identified. Therefore, it was concluded that the ED criteria are not met for the T‐modality (scenario 1a of the ECHA/EFSA guidance (2018)).

For the EAS‐modalities, the data package for adversity was incomplete; however, no EAS‐mediated adversity was observed in the available dataset. The endocrine activity was considered sufficiently investigated only for the E‐modality, for which a ToxCast ER pathway model is available and negative for both agonist and antagonist pathways. For the A and S modalities, overall, it was concluded that the ED criteria are not met considering the existing data for the structurally similar gibberellin substances (GA4/GA7) for which no EAS‐mediated adversity was observed in a complete dataset. Therefore, it was concluded that the ED criteria are not met for the EAS‐modalities (scenario 2a(ii) and scenario 1a, of the ECHA/EFSA guidance (2018) for the E‐modality and for the AS‐modalities, respectively).

For mammals as non‐target organisms, the same conclusion as for humans applies.

For non‐target organisms other than mammals, an amphibian metamorphosis assay (AMA) with gibberellic acid and a fish short‐term reproduction assay (FSTRA) with gibberellins (GA4/GA7) were available to sufficiently investigate the endocrine activity. The studies including their related findings, the WoE and its related uncertainties were discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' TC 124.

The available AMA was considered reliable with minor restrictions owing to some limitations related to the lack of information on water iodine content and scoliosis observed in controls and treated animals. 22 A statistically significant increase in wet body weight (WBW) at both day 7 and day 21, and in snout‐to‐vent length (SVL) at day 21 was observed in a dose–response manner. While there were no statistically significant changes in the developmental stage at any time, a slightly higher number of animals at the highest concentration appeared to be at a stage > NF60 compared to the control animals at study termination. Moreover, all the animals > NF60 showed all growth parameters coherent with their advanced stage, compared to the other animals. Thyroid histology, however, did not show any relevant change.

When considering all the available information, the WoE and its related uncertainties (see Table 1), it can be concluded that it is unlikely that gibberellic acid may elicit endocrine‐related adverse effects through interference with the hypothalamus‐pituitary‐thyroid (HPT) axis.
