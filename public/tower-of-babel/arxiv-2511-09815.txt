Traversable wormhole with double trace deformations via gravitational shear and sound channels
Fitria Khairunnisa, Hadyan Luthfan Prihadi, M. Zhahir Djogama, Donny Dwiputra, Freddy Permana Zen

Abstract
We investigate how non-local gravitational couplings from double-trace deformation between two asymptotic boundaries of an AdS 5 black brane can lead to the violation of the Averaged Null Energy Condition (ANEC). The first-order gravitational perturbations backreact with the background metric at second-order, creating a wormhole opening. The wormhole becomes traversable in both the gravitational shear and sound channels within the hydrodynamic approximation. This shows that dynamical metric perturbations can facilitate information transfer in a purely gravitational setting, with the emergence of G N G_{N} indicating the gravitational origin. For the shear channel, we consider three different coupling configurations, whereas for the sound channel, we vary both the speed of sound and the attenuation constant, as these parameters control the wormhole traversability. Furthermore, we obtain late-time power-law behavior in the ANEC using fitting function and present a generalization that applies to both shear and sound channels. Due to its propagating nature, the sound channel exhibits late-time power-law remnants at low sound speed similar to the vector diffusive probes, but it prefers an exponential decay at higher sound speed similar to the scalar non-diffusive probes, as the power-law exponent weakened with increasing sound speed. For superluminal sound channels, the wormhole opens for an extremely brief duration at late insertion times, rendering it non-traversable.

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{g_{tt}(UV)}{UV}dUdV+g_{xx}(UV)\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}

(31)

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta}{2\pi UV}g_{xx}(UV)\mathfrak{h}_{tx}(U,V,z)(UdV-VdU)dx

\displaystyle+2g_{xx}(UV)\mathfrak{h}_{zx}(U,V,z)dzdx.

In this setup,
T_{VV}^{(2)}
is defined in the low-temperature regime (large
\beta
) since we are working in low energy, hydrodynamic limit
58
. It is evaluated at
U=0
and takes the form (Appendix A),

8\pi G_{N}\langle\hat{T}_{VV}^{(2)}\rangle=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{32\pi^{2}V^{2}}\langle\partial_{z}\mathfrak{h}_{tx}\partial_{z}\mathfrak{h}_{tx}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\beta),

(32)

with the
\mathcal{O}(\beta)
terms are subleading in the hydrodynamic limit. Using the point-splitting method and ignoring the
\mathcal{O}(1)
term, it is now written as

\displaystyle 8\pi G_{N}\langle

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{VV}^{(2)}\rangle=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{32\pi^{2}V^{2}}

(33)

\displaystyle\times\lim_{\textbf{x}^{\prime}\rightarrow\textbf{x}}\partial_{z}\partial_{z^{\prime}}\langle\mathfrak{h}_{tx}(U,V,z)\mathfrak{h}_{tx}(U^{\prime},V^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle\bigg|_{U=0}.

Here,
\langle\mathfrak{h}_{tx}(U,V,z)\mathfrak{h}_{tx}(U^{\prime},V^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle
is one of the Green’s function of the tensor field
\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}
in the bulk, denoted as

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=\langle\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x})\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}(\textbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle,

(34)

where
\textbf{x}=(r,t,\vec{x})
and
\textbf{x}^{\prime}=(r^{\prime},t^{\prime},\vec{x}^{\prime})
.

Due to the deformation, the bulk tensor field
\mathfrak{h}_{MN}
evolves with the unitary time-evolution operator
U(t,t_{0})=\mathcal{T}\{e^{-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{1}\delta H(t_{1})}\}
in the interaction picture, where

\displaystyle\delta H(t_{1})=\int d^{3}x_{1}

\displaystyle Q^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

(35)

\displaystyle\times T_{h,\mu\nu}^{(L)}(-t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})T_{h,\alpha\beta}^{(R)}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),

is the double-trace deformation. The left and right boundary is related through it. The bulk two-point function is then modified by
\delta H(t_{1})
as

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}^{(H)}(\textbf{x})\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}^{(H)}(\textbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle U(t,t_{0})^{-1}\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}^{(I)}(\textbf{x})U(t,t_{0})

\displaystyle\times U(t^{\prime},t_{0})^{-1}\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}^{(I)}(\textbf{x}^{\prime})U(t^{\prime},t_{0})\rangle,

where
(H)
and
(I)
in the superscript denote the Heisenberg and Interaction picture, respectively. For simplicity, the superscript
(I)
will be omitted hereafter.

For small
\delta H(t_{1})
perturbation, the Green’s function in the first order of
\delta H(t_{1})
is given by

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{(1)}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{1}\langle[\delta H(t_{1}),\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x})]\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}(\textbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle+i\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}dt_{1}\langle\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x})[\delta H(t_{1}),\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}(\textbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{1}d^{3}x_{1}Q^{\lambda\rho\gamma\sigma}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\langle[T_{h,\lambda\rho}^{(R)}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x})]T_{h,\gamma\sigma}^{(L)}(-t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}(\textbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle+\textbf{x}\leftrightarrow\textbf{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{1}d^{3}x_{1}Q^{\lambda\rho\gamma\sigma}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\langle[T_{h,\lambda\rho}^{(R)}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x})]\rangle\langle\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}(\textbf{x}^{\prime})T_{h,\gamma\sigma}^{(R)}(-t_{1}-i\beta/2,\vec{x}_{1})\rangle+\textbf{x}\leftrightarrow\textbf{x}^{\prime}.

We use the large-N factorization to go from the second to the third line and we use the fact that operators in the left asymptotic boundary commutes with the ones in the right asymptotic boundary, assuming that
\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x})
lives in the right region. Introducing the Wightman and retarded bulk-to-boundary propagators,

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}^{W}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle\equiv-i\langle\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x})T_{h,\lambda\rho}^{(R)}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\rangle,

(38)

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle\equiv-i\langle[\mathfrak{h}_{\mu\nu}(\textbf{x}),T_{h,\lambda\rho}^{(R)}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})]\rangle,

(39)

the first-order Green’s function is then written as

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{(1)}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{1}d^{3}x_{1}Q^{\lambda\rho\gamma\sigma}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

(40)

\displaystyle\times K_{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}^{W}(\textbf{x}^{\prime};-t_{1}-i\beta/2,\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle\times K_{\alpha\beta,\gamma\sigma}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})+\textbf{x}\leftrightarrow\textbf{x}^{\prime}.

Here, we perform a shift
t_{1}\rightarrow t_{1}-i\beta/2
to go from the right to the left asymptotic boundary.

Therefore, to calculate the ANEC in eq. (
33
), we need to calculate the Green’s functions
G_{tx,tx}^{(1)}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})
and
G_{zx,tx}^{(1)}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})
. However, we need to first specify the coupling functions
Q^{\lambda\rho\gamma\sigma}(t,\vec{x})
. There are three cases that can be considered here,

Case I:

\displaystyle Q^{\lambda\rho\gamma\sigma}(t,\vec{x})=A(t)\delta_{t}^{\lambda}\delta_{x}^{\rho}\delta_{t}^{\gamma}\delta_{x}^{\sigma},

Case II:

\displaystyle Q^{\lambda\rho\gamma\sigma}(t,\vec{x})=B(t)\delta^{\lambda}_{t}\delta_{x}^{\rho}\delta_{z}^{\gamma}\delta_{x}^{\sigma},

Case III:

\displaystyle Q^{\lambda\rho\gamma\sigma}(t,\vec{x})=C(t)\delta_{z}^{\lambda}\delta_{x}^{\rho}\delta_{z}^{\gamma}\delta_{x}^{\sigma}.

We also need to explicitly calculate the bulk-to-boundary propagators

\displaystyle K_{tx,tx}^{W}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),\;\;\;K_{tx,tx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),

\displaystyle K_{tx,zx}^{W}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),\;\;\;K_{tx,zx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),

\displaystyle K_{zx,zx}^{W}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),\;\;\;K_{zx,zx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),

and calculate these propagators by first performing a Fourier transform from a retarded bulk-to-bulk propagators in Fourier space
G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{\text{ret.}}(r,r^{\prime},\omega,k)
,

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{\text{ret}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=\int

\displaystyle\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{\text{ret.}}(r,r^{\prime},\omega,k)

(41)

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i\vec{k}(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})},

then evaluating one of the bulk point to the asymptotic boundary coordinate. The Wightman propagator can be obtained from the retarded Green’s function using the relation (Appendix B)

G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{W}(r,r^{\prime},\omega,k)=i\frac{e^{\omega/T}}{e^{\omega/T}-1}\text{Im}G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{\text{ret.}}(r,r^{\prime},\omega,k).

(42)

III.2.1
Green’s Function Calculations

To obtain the bulk-to-boundary propagators, we first need to solve the classical equations of motion for
\mathfrak{h}_{tx}
and
\mathfrak{h}_{zx}
. The linearized Einstein’s equation

R_{MN}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2}(Rg_{MN})^{(1)}-\Lambda h_{MN}=0,

(43)

gives us coupled second-order differential equations. We also consider the Fourier modes of the tensor fields,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{tx}(r,t,z)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\omega d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}h_{t}(r)e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}},

(44)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{zx}(r,t,z)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\omega d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}h_{z}(r)e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}},

(45)

where we assume that the tensor fields only propagate in the
z
direction so that
\vec{k}=k\hat{z}
. The equations of motion in the Fourier modes are

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}_{z}-h_{t}^{\prime}\partial_{r}

\displaystyle\log\bigg(\frac{g_{tt}g_{rr}}{\sqrt{|g|}g_{xx}}\bigg)

(46)

\displaystyle-h_{t}\bigg(\frac{k^{2}g_{rr}}{g_{xx}}\bigg)-h_{z}\bigg(\frac{k\omega g_{rr}}{g_{xx}}\bigg)=0,

\displaystyle h_{z}^{\prime\prime}-h_{z}^{\prime}\partial_{r}

\displaystyle\log\bigg(\frac{g_{rr}}{\sqrt{|g|}}\bigg)

(47)

\displaystyle-h_{z}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}g_{rr}}{g_{tt}}\bigg)-h_{t}\bigg(\frac{k\omega g_{rr}}{g_{tt}}\bigg)=0,

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\omega g_{xx}h_{t}^{\prime}-kg_{tt}h_{z}^{\prime}=0.

(48)

From equation (
46
), we can solve for
h_{z}(r)
and obtain

h_{z}=\frac{g_{xx}}{k\omega g_{rr}}\bigg\{h_{t}^{\prime\prime}-h_{t}^{\prime}\partial_{r}\log\bigg(\frac{g_{tt}g_{rr}}{\sqrt{|g|}g_{xx}}\bigg)-h_{t}\bigg(\frac{k^{2}g_{rr}}{g_{xx}}\bigg)\bigg\}.

(49)

Inserting this to (
48
), we get equation of motion for the perturbation,

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}

\displaystyle-h_{t}^{\prime\prime}\partial_{r}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{|g|}g_{rr}}{g_{xx}^5}\bigg)-g_{rr}\bigg[\omega^{2}g^{tt}+k^{2}g^{xx}

(50)

\displaystyle+g^{xx}\partial_{r}\bigg(\frac{g_{xx}^{5}}{\sqrt{|g|}g_{rr}}\partial_{r}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{|g|}}{g_{xx}^{4}}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg]h_{t}^{\prime}=0.

We now proceed to find the solution for the equation of motion. For simplicity, we consider a
(4+1)
-dimensional AdS black hole spacetime in the
(t,u,\vec{x})
coordinates,

ds^{2}=\frac{1}{u}\big(-f(u)dt^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}\big)+\frac{du^{2}}{4u^{2}f(u)},

(51)

where
f(u)
satisfy
f(u_{H})=0
, with
u_{H}
be the horizon’s radius and
f(0)=1
from the AdS boundary condition. This metric can be obtained by defining
u=r^{2}
in Eq. (
9
). In this case, we have

g_{rr}(r)\rightarrow g_{uu}(u)=\frac{1}{4u^{2}f(u)},\;\;\;g_{xx}=\frac{1}{u},\;\;\;\sqrt{|g|}=\frac{1}{2u^{3}}.

(52)

The equation of motion for
h_{t}(u)
then becomes

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u)

\displaystyle-h_{t}^{\prime\prime}(u)\partial_{u}\log(u^2g_{uu})

(53)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle ug_{uu}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}g^{tt}}{u}+k^{2}\bigg)h_{t}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{u}\partial_{u}\log(u^2g_{uu})h_{t}^{\prime}=0.

We also assume that the black hole is non-extremal so that
g_{uu}(u)
has an order-1 pole near the horizon,

g_{uu}(u)\approx\frac{1}{4u_{H}^{2}f^{\prime}(u_{H})(u-u_{H})}.

(54)

The solutions with infalling boundary condition at the horizon in the hydrodynamic limit with
(\omega,k^{2})\ll 1
are given by (Appendix C)

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}h_{t}^{0}+\omega kh_{z}^{0}}{H_{1}(0)(i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2})}\left(1-\frac{u}{u_{H}}\right)^{-i\omega/\nu}

(55)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\times\bigg(u-i\omega H_{1}(u)+k^{2}H_{2}(u)+\dots\bigg),

\displaystyle h_{z}^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega kh_{t}^{0}+\omega^{2}h_{z}^{0}}{f(u)H_{1}(0)(i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2})}\left(1-\frac{u}{u_{H}}\right)^{-i\omega/\nu}

(56)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\times\bigg(u-i\omega H_{1}(u)+k^{2}H_{2}(u)+\dots\bigg),

where
\dots
are the terms with
\mathcal{O}(\omega^{2},\omega k^{2},k^{4})
and higher order. Here,
H_{1}(u)
and
H_{2}(u)
are defined as

\displaystyle H_{1}(u)=

\displaystyle\frac{u}{\nu}\int_{u}^{u_{H}}\bigg(\frac{u_{H}^{2}|F^{\prime}(u_{H})|}{u_{1}^{2}f(u_{1})}-\frac{1}{u_{H}-u_{1}}\bigg)du_{1},

(57)

\displaystyle H_{2}(u)=

\displaystyle\frac{u}{2}\int_{u_{H}}^{u}\frac{(u_{1}^{2}-u_{H}^{2})}{u_{1}^{2}f(u_{1})}du_{1},

and

\mathcal{D}_{T}\equiv\frac{H_{2}(0)}{H_{1}(0)},

(58)

is the diffusion constant for the tensor perturbations. For a Scwarzschild-AdS
5
black hole with
f(u)=1-(\frac{u}{u_{H}})^{2}
, we recover
\mathcal{D}_{T}=\frac{\beta}{4\pi}
, which is consistent with
58
.

We can switch from
(t,u,\vec{x})
coordinates to the tortoise coordinates
(t,r^{*},\vec{x})
using the relation

r^{*}(u)=\int^{u}\sqrt{-\frac{g_{uu}(u_{1})}{g_{tt}(u_{1})}}du_{1}.

(59)

Near the horizon, following relation applies

r^{*}(u)\approx\frac{1}{\nu}\log|u_{H}-u|.

(60)

Thus, we have the near horizon solutions in the form of

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{t}(r^{*})\approx

\displaystyle\frac{k^{2}h_{t}^{0}e^{-i\omega r^{*}}u_{H}^{i\omega/\nu+1}}{H_{1}(0)(i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2})},

(61)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{z}(r^{*})\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{2}h_{z}^{0}e^{-i\omega r^{*}}u_{H}^{i\omega/\nu+1}}{f(r^{*})H_{1}(0)(i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2})}.

Furthermore, we can calculate the bulk-to-bulk retarded Green’s function in Fourier space

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}^{\text{ret.}}(r^{*},r^{*\prime},\omega,k)

\displaystyle=-i\int dtd^{3}xe^{i\omega t-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\Theta(t)

(62)

\displaystyle\times\langle[T_{\mu\nu}(r^{*},t,x),T_{\alpha\beta}(r^{*\prime},0,0)]\rangle,

analogous to the calculations performed with the
U(1)
Maxwell gauge field
1
. One can do this by first solving the homogeneous equations of motion for the respective fields in the hydrodynamic limit which has a diffusive pole. In our case, the solutions are given by Eq. (
61
). Then, the bulk-to-bulk retarded Green functions ansatz in Fourier space that are also consistent with
58
;
28
;
15
are given by

\displaystyle G_{tx,tx}^{\text{ret.}}(r^{*},r^{*\prime},\omega,k)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}e^{-i\omega(r^{*}-r^{*\prime})}}{(i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2})},

(63)

\displaystyle G_{tx,zx}^{\text{ret.}}(r^{*},r^{*\prime},\omega,k)

\displaystyle\approx i\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}\omega ke^{-i\omega(r^{*}-r^{*\prime})}}{(i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2})},

(64)

\displaystyle G_{zx,zx}^{\text{ret.}}(r^{*},r^{*\prime},\omega,k)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}\omega^{2}e^{-i\omega(r^{*}-r^{*\prime})}}{(i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2})},

(65)

up to some proportionality constant. The factor
\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}
is added to ensure that the Green’s functions are dimensionally correct. The difference between our calculations and the previous works is that we generalize the diffusion constant
\mathcal{D}_{T}
for arbitrary function
f(u)
.

Now, we want to calculate the bulk-to-boundary correlators in real space by inverse Fourier transform

\displaystyle G_{tx,tx}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}G_{tx,tx}(r^{*},r^{*\prime},\omega,k)

(66)

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})}.

For
G_{tx,tx}^{\text{ret.}}
, it is given by

\displaystyle G_{tx,tx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}\frac{e^{-i\omega(r^{*}-r^{*\prime})}}{i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}

(67)

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{(V/V^{\prime})^{-i\omega/2\pi T}}{i\omega-\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})}}{(V/V^{\prime})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}}\Theta(V-V^{\prime}),

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function obtained from closing the
\omega
contour down and pick up a pole in the lower-half plane. Using the relation in Eq. (
42
), the Wightman Green’s function is given by

\displaystyle G_{tx,tx}^{W}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{4}\Theta(V-V^{\prime})

(68)

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})}e^{-i\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}/2T}}{\sin(\frac{\mathcal{D}_Tk^2}{2T})(V/V^{\prime})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}}.

by choosing to close the contour down we pick up a pole at
\omega=-i\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}
and considering the near-horizon limit where
U\rightarrow 0
.

The bulk-to-boundary correlators are obtained by taking the limit
r^{*}\rightarrow 0
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle K_{tx,tx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{(Ve^{-2\pi Tt_{1}})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}}

(69)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\times\Theta(V-e^{2\pi Tt_{1}}),

\displaystyle=\Theta(V-V_{1})2\pi T\mathcal{D}_{T}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\times V\partial_{V}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})}}{(V/V_{1})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}},

(70)

for the retarded propagator and

\displaystyle K_{tx,tx}^{W}(\textbf{x}^{\prime};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}^{\prime}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{\sin(\frac{\mathcal{D}_Tk^2}{2T})}

(71)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times\frac{e^{-i\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}/2T}}{(V^{\prime}e^{-2\pi Tt_{1}})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}}\Theta(V^{\prime}-e^{2\pi Tt_{1}})

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi T\mathcal{D}_{T}}{2}\Theta(V^{\prime}-e^{2\pi Tt_{1}})V^{\prime}\partial_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\times\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}^{\prime}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{\sin(\frac{\mathcal{D}_Tk^2}{2T})}\frac{e^{-i\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}/2T}}{(V^{\prime}/V_{1})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}},

for the Wightman propagator. Note that the dependence on
V_{1}
cancels after multiplying the retarded and the Wightman bulk-to-boundary propagators. This means that there is no dependence on the insertion time
t_{0}
of the first-order Green’s function
G_{\mu\nu.\alpha\beta}^{(1)}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}^{\prime})
. However, as mentioned in
1
, the result should indeed depends on the insertion time. To overcome this problem, we use the perscription used in
15
, so that the propagators become

\displaystyle K_{tx,tx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=\Theta(V-V_{1})2\pi T\mathcal{D}_{T}V

(72)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times\partial_{V}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{(V/V_{1}-1)^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}},

\displaystyle=-\Theta(V-V_{1})\frac{V}{V_{1}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{(V/V_{1}-1)^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}+1}},

and

\displaystyle K_{tx,tx}^{W}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi T\mathcal{D}_{T}}{2}\Theta(V-V_{1})V\partial_{V}

(73)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\times\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}^{\prime}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{\sin(\frac{\mathcal{D}_Tk^2}{2T})}\frac{e^{-i\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}/2T}}{(V/V_{1}-1)^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4}\Theta(V-V_{1})\frac{V}{V_{1}}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}}{\sin(\frac{\mathcal{D}_Tk^2}{2T})}\frac{e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{(1-V/V_{1})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2T}+1}}.

By taking
t_{1}\rightarrow-t_{1}-i\beta/2
for the Wightman propagator, we get

\displaystyle K_{tx,tx}^{W}(\textbf{x};-t_{1}-i\beta/2

\displaystyle,\vec{x}_{1})=-\frac{i}{4}VV_{1}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}

(74)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\times\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}^{2}k^{2}}{\sin(\frac{\mathcal{D}_Tk^2}{2T})}\frac{e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{(1+VV_{1})^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2T}+1}}.

These propagators have qualitatively similar behaviors at late time limit (
V/V_{1}\gg 1
) while maintaining the desired analytical properties connecting the retarded and Wightman propagators
15
.

The other propagators such as
K_{tx,zx}
and
K_{zx,zx}
can also be obtained from those derivations. Here, we have

\displaystyle K_{tx,zx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\approx

\displaystyle-\Theta(V-V_{1})\frac{V}{V_{1}}

(75)

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{i\mathcal{D}_{T}^{3}k^{3}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{(V/V_{1}-1)^{\frac{\mathcal{D}_{T}k^{2}}{2\pi T}+1}},

\displaystyle K_{zx,zx}^{\text{ret.}}(\textbf{x};t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\approx

Remarks on the Localized Perturbations

In the previous section, we showed that the ANEC for both shear and sound channel perturbations exhibits a power-law scaling at late times. On the other hand, at early times or as soon as the perturbations are inserted at time
t_{0}
, the wormhole immediately becomes traversable as
\langle\hat{T}_{VV}^{(2)}\rangle
is negative right after
V=V_{0}
(see Figures
1
and
3
). However, this might not be the case when we consider a local setting in the double-trace deformation, such that the one considered in
16
. In this section, without performing an explicit analytical computation, we provide a brief discussion of how localized perturbations could alter the traversability of the wormhole.

Instead of inserting the double-trace deformation at time
t_{0}
via Dirac delta function and integrating throughout all space, we could insert a local perturbation at time
t_{0}
and at position
\vec{x}_{0}
at both asymptotic boundaries. This could be achieved by choosing the function
A(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})
in the coupling function
Q^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})
as

\displaystyle A(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=\delta\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\big((t_{1}-t_{0})-|\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{0}|/v_{B}\big)\bigg)

(108)

\displaystyle=\tilde{V}_{0}\delta(V_{1}-\tilde{V}_{0}),

where
\tilde{V}_{0}\equiv V\big|_{t=\tilde{t}_{0}}
with
\tilde{t}_{0}\equiv t_{0}+|\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{0}|/v_{B}

64
;
65

v_{B}

\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{VV^{\prime}\Theta(V-\tilde{V}_{0})}{(1+V^{\prime}\tilde{V}_{0})(V/\tilde{V}_{0}-1)}\frac{kk^{\prime}e^{i\vec{k}^{\prime}\cdot(\vec{x}^{\prime}-\vec{x}_{1})}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{1})}}{\sinh\big(\frac{k^\prime v_s}{2T}\big)}

\vec{x}_{1}

\displaystyle 8\pi G_{N}

\displaystyle\langle\hat{T}_{VV}^{(2)}\rangle=\frac{3a}{2^{6}\pi^{3}T^{6}}\int d^{3}x_{1}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{-kk^{\prime}\vec{k}\cdot\vec{k}^{\prime}}{\sinh\big(\frac{k^\prime v_s}{2T}\big)}\frac{\real(\mathcal{Q}(V^{\prime},\tilde{V}_{0},k))\imaginary(\mathcal{R}(V,\tilde{V}_{0},k^{\prime}))}{P(V,\tilde{V}_{0})}.

(112)

VI
Summary and Discussions

This work extends previous studies
27
;
1
on traversable wormholes via double trace deformation. Instead of using scalar or vector field, we perturb the AdS
5
black brane background with gravitational/tensor perturbations that are dual with energy momentum tensor operators on its boundary. The approach is carried out through linear perturbation analysis in the shear and sound channels.

Gravitational shear channel.
We consider three different coupling functions,
Q^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(t,\vec{x})
, for the shear channel, denoted as case I, case II, and case III. We find that each case results in a different opening of the wormhole,
\Delta U^{(2)}
, as seen in Figure
2
, with the order of traversability from the largest to the smallest being
tx-tx,tx-zx,
and
zx-zx
. We know that
tx-tx
is a momentum density-momentum density correlation. This type of coupling gives the strongest traversability compared with momentum-shear and shear-shear interactions.

We also note that, in subsection
III.2.1
, we generalize the tensorial diffusion constant,
\mathcal{D}_{T}
, for an arbitrary function
f(u)
that can recover
\mathcal{D}_{T}=1/4\pi T
for Shwarzschild AdS
5
blackhole which has
f(u)=1-u^{2}/u_{H}^{2}
, which is consistent with the results of previous studies
55
;
58
. Thus, one can verify if we could get different diffusion constants for different forms of
f(u)
that could possibly affect the the wormhole opening. If one apply
f(u)
for a black hole with momentum relaxation
1
, one could obtain
\mathcal{D}_{T}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}_{c}
.

Gravitational sound channel.
For the sound channel, we find that its traversability is smaller than that of all the shear channels. Nevertheless, we observe some interesting findings regarding this channel. When plotting the ANEC integral, as seen in Figure
2
versus Figure
3
, the turning point of the graph, corresponds to maximal traversability, is different. For the shear channel case, the turning point is shown when
V_{0}=1
, equivalent with
t_{0}=0
. However, with the sound channel, the wormhole becomes most traversable when the insertion time is
t_{0}<0
, indicating that we need to activate the perturbations earlier.

This raises further questions regarding the underlying mechanism. We speculate that this comes from the propagating nature of the gravitational sound channel. At low speed, the perturbations behaves more like a diffusive probes similar with
U(1)
conserved current operators where it is found that maximal traversability in obtained when
t_{0}=0
. As the sound speed becomes larger, however, the response becomes less diffusive and prefers propagating wave-like properties. In that case, as can be seen in Figure
4
, the time for maximal traversability is shifted to
t_{0}<0
. This behavior might be linked to the findings of
15
, which showed that sound modes with
v_{s}>v_{B}
can have different scrambling properties as some portion of information scramble at a speed larger than
v_{B}
.

Another aspect of the sound channel perturbations is the influence of the attenuation constant on the wormhole traversability. The result can be seen in Figure
5
, where a higher attenuation constant leads to less traversability for early
t_{0}
while it increases traversability at later times. This seems to contradict the behavior of the ANEC in the diffusive modes. In the sound channel, however, the relevant quantity controlling traversability is the ratio
\bar{\Gamma}_{s}/v_{s}^{2}
. Therefore, the effect between
\bar{\Gamma}_{s}
and
v_{s}
compete with each other at both early and late insertion times, leading to the result found in Figure
5
. Furthermore, once the perturbations become superluminal, as can be seen in Figure
6
, the opening of the wormhole becomes too transient such that the wormhole becomes non-traversable at late insertion time, although the turning point returns to
t_{0}\approx 0
.

Power-law tail of the ANEC
. We also perform data fitting and find that the ANEC for both shear and sound channels can be well described by

\mathcal{A}_{\text{new}}(V_{0})=-\frac{V_{0}}{V_{0}^{(c+1)}+1}\frac{a}{[\ln(V_0+1/V_0)]^{b}},

(115)

where
\{a,b,c\}
are the fitting parameters. The parameters
b
and
c
control the late-time behavior of the ANEC, investigating whether it favors purely exponential decay or includes a power-law factor. At late time (
t_{0}\gg 1
), the function is given by Eq. (
107
). For the shear channel, we get a comparable result with
1
where the value of
c
remains close to unity, thereby reducing to the old model (see Table
2
). However, for the sound channel,
c
shifts away from 1 and
b
becomes smaller as the speed of sound is increased, as can be seen in Table
3
and Figure
9
. This behavior shows that the power-law factor of the sound channel weakened as the sound speed is increased. Instead of power-law, the response is more characterized by exponentially decaying modes, as the ratio
c/b
gets larger, associated with propagating waves. This is indeed consistent with the behavior of the sound channel perturbations that prefer ballistic propagation instead of diffusion at large
v_{s}
. Therefore, the late-time gravitational sound modes lie in an intermediate regime as they interpolate between the vector diffusive behavior and the scalar propagating behavior, depending on the relative strength of the sound speed and the attenuation constant.

Bound on information transfer
. An important distinction between the gravitational vector type perturbation,
\mathfrak{h}_{tx}
and
\mathfrak{h}_{zx}
, versus pure vector perturbation
A_{\mu}
is that the energy-momentum tensor
\langle T_{VV}\rangle
for vector fields depends solely on the gauge fields
A_{V}
, while the gravitational contribution explicitly involves the gravitational constant
G_{N}
. The appearance of
G_{N}
encodes the backreaction of spacetime geometry, shifting the horizon and allowing the signal/information to be sent. It also underlines gravitational nature of the interaction, distinguishing it from other gauge interactions.

One can calculate the upper bound
1
;
23
of how much information can be transferred through the wormhole opening caused by gravitational interaction, denoted as
N_{\text{bits}}
. The number of bits
N_{\text{bits}}
is upper-bounded by
\Delta U
times momentum of the shockwaves
p^{U}
, which is roughly equal or less than
(r_{H}^{d-1})/G_{N}
in a so called probe limit. This limit is obtained from the assumption that the backreaction from a particle with positive energy that is sent through the wormhole is substantially smaller than the backreaction from gravitational perturbations that open the wormhole. In our case,
\Delta U
is proportional to
8\pi G_{N}\int\langle\hat{T}_{VV}^{(2)}\rangle dV
, while
8\pi G_{N}\langle\hat{T}_{VV}^{(2)}\rangle\sim\mathcal{O}(h_{MN}^{2})
(see Eq. (
22
)). Therefore, to have
N_{\text{bits}}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
, the gravitational perturbations should be at least
h_{MN}\sim\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{G_{N}})
, whereas
h_{MN}\sim\mathcal{O}(G_{N})
leads to
N_{\text{bits}}\sim\mathcal{O}(G_{N})
, which is vanishing in a semiclassical limit.

Future works
. So far, the construction of traversable wormholes with double trace deformation has been accomplished by using bosonic operators such as scalar, vector, and, in this paper, tensor operators. Then it is interesting to see if this procedure can be developed for fermionic operators. Unlike scalar, vector, and tensor, there are some subtleties when choosing the boundary condition in fermionic interaction. This is because when the bulk action vanishes at the boundary, we can add boundary terms to the action that satisfy the variational principles, and different boundary terms can lead to different conformal field theories
45
. Therefore, one could produce a different kind of deformation. In other contexts, the lack of fermionic superradiance is an advantage to construct traversable wormholes in a Kerr black hole
37
;
52
. The bosonic superradiance prevents wormholes from being defined at the off-axis region
7
, while fermions can avoid this problem. By having traversable wormholes from more physical black holes (especially near the extreme limit), it can open up the possibility of observing wormholes in the future. Wormhole traversability from fermionic operators will be presented in the near future.

Another interesting aspect to explore is the study of traversable wormhole for various black hole backgrounds, especially involving scalar or vector hair, which have attracted significant interest until very recently
32
;
12
;
11
;
59
;
60
;
75
. It is shown that black hole parameters can be influenced by the value of the scalar field at the asymptotic AdS boundary, which corresponds to a deformation in the boundary CFT. One might also expect that the boundary deformation could influence the diffusion constant or the speed of sound of the theory. Furthermore, the use of rotating shock waves
51
;
61
;
62
;
60
might also influence the traversability of a wormhole, especially in rotating backgrounds
7
, or even from its quantum counterpart called the Exotic Compact Object (ECO)
19
. The angular momentum of the shock waves gives an enhanced upper bound for the Lyapunov exponent in the context of gravitational scrambling dynamics.

Lastly, this work may also be connected to inflationary cosmology, where it suggests that primordial gravitational perturbations, generated during inflation epochs, originated from quantum fluctuations in the early universe
66
;
14
. Analogous to the tensor perturbation, cosmological perturbations can also be classified as scalar, vector, or tensor perturbations depending on their behavior under spatial coordinate transformations.
14
. Furthermore, in the aforementioned work, gravitational waves are represented as second-order perturbations. In our study, the metric perturbations are also treated at second order, which motivates us to find out if such gravitational wave perturbations may create a traversable wormhole. Additionally, a recent proposal suggests the connection between inflationary cosmology and Anti-de Sitter wormholes, initiated by an effort to address the problem of the initial conditions for inflation
6
.
