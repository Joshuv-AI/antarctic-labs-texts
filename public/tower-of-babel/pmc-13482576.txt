Extraction-dependent phenolic shifts drive antioxidant and multi-enzyme inhibitory activities of Onosma rechingeri methanolic extracts
Authors: Mamdouh Alshammari, Cengiz Sarikurkcu, Fevzi Bardakci, Mousa Alreshidi, Mohd Adnan, Arif Jamal Siddiqui, Bektas Tepe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The bioprospecting of Onosma species for multifunctional phenolics is gaining momentum, yet the impact of extraction methods on bioactivity is underexplored. This study compared methanolic extracts of O. rechingeri obtained via ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE-ME), maceration (MAC-ME), and Soxhlet (SOE-ME). Total phenolic and flavonoid content was highest in SOE-ME. Luteolin-7-glucoside and hesperidin were abundant in MAC-ME, while SOE-ME was richest in rosmarinic acid. SOE-ME showed superior electron-transfer antioxidant activity, while metal chelation favored UAE-ME and MAC-ME. Enzyme inhibition varied by extraction: MAC-ME was most effective against AChE and α-amylase, SOE-ME against tyrosinase. Correlation analysis revealed distinct compound-activity associations: tyrosinase aligned with electron transfer, AChE and α-amylase with chelation. Docking suggested luteolin-7-glucoside as a strong α-amylase binder (–9.09 kcal/mol) and rosmarinic acid as a promising in silico binder of AChE and TYRP1. Overall, extraction strategy significantly alters chemical profiles and bioactivity, but these findings should be interpreted as preliminary because they are based on crude extracts, in vitro assays, correlation analysis with a limited sample set, and docking predictions; therefore, they highlight the need for targeted fractionation and further biological validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56378-9.

Introduction

Since ancient times, medicinal plants have been integral to human health care, serving as foundational sources for traditional healing practices and modern therapeutic agents1. Throughout history, diverse cultures have utilized plant-based preparations to manage various ailments, ranging from minor infections to chronic diseases2. Contemporary pharmacological studies underline the substantial bioactivity potential inherent in plant-derived extracts, attributing significant biological effects primarily to their phytochemical components, particularly phenolic compounds and flavonoids. These compounds are widely recognized for their antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and enzyme inhibitory properties, which contribute prominently to therapeutic efficacy and nutraceutical applications3,4.

Plant-derived natural products have been widely investigated for their diverse biological effects, notably antioxidant properties and inhibitory activities against critical enzymes involved in human diseases, such as cholinesterases, α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and tyrosinase5,6. Among numerous plant genera, members of the genus Onosma have gained substantial scientific attention due to their potent bioactivity profiles7,8. Recent research highlighted that extracts of Onosma species exhibit pronounced antioxidant activities, effectively neutralizing free radicals and preventing oxidative stress-related cellular damage9,10. Moreover, these plants have demonstrated considerable enzyme inhibitory potentials, particularly toward acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE)—enzymes implicated in neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s disease—as well as α-amylase and α-glucosidase, key therapeutic targets in diabetes management11,12. Additionally, Onosma extracts have been documented as promising natural inhibitors of tyrosinase, a critical enzyme regulating melanin biosynthesis, indicating their potential value in cosmetic and pharmaceutical formulations aimed at hyperpigmentation disorders13. However, comprehensive phytochemical and bioactivity assessments remain limited for several underexplored species, emphasizing the necessity of further research to fully uncover the therapeutic capabilities of Onosma rechingeri Riedl and related taxa.

Although several Onosma species have ethnobotanical relevance (e.g., traditional remedies and use of root-derived pigments)7,8, there are, to the best of our knowledge, no reported ethnomedicinal or food-related records specifically for O. rechingeri. This lack of prior use information and the absence of any phytochemical/bioactivity report make O. rechingeri a suitable target within our broader bioprospecting effort on endemic Turkish Onosma taxa to reveal their pharmacological potential.

Considering the current gaps in scientific literature regarding the phytochemical and biological characteristics of O. rechingeri, the primary goal of this study was to comprehensively evaluate, for the first time, its chemical composition and associated biological properties. Specifically, qualitative and quantitative phytochemical profiling was performed via spectrophotometric determination of total phenolic and flavonoid contents, and detailed LC-ESI-MS/MS analyses. Additionally, multiple complementary antioxidant methods—including phosphomolybdenum assay, CUPRAC (Cupric Reducing Antioxidant Capacity) reducing power, FRAP reducing power, DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging, and ferrous ion chelation assays—were employed to thoroughly assess the antioxidant potential of O. rechingeri extracts. The enzyme inhibitory activities were evaluated against AChE, tyrosinase, and α-amylase to determine possible therapeutic applications. Furthermore, correlation analyses were conducted to elucidate the relationship between phenolic constituents and their bioactivities. Finally, molecular docking studies were carried out to understand the interactions of selected major compounds (hesperidin, luteolin 7-glucoside, and rosmarinic acid) with the active sites of these enzymes. This multifaceted approach not only aims to deliver valuable novel insights into O. rechingeri but also seeks to provide a preliminary phytochemical and bioactivity basis for future fractionation, mechanistic validation, and safety assessment before any pharmacological or nutraceutical relevance can be inferred.

Material and method

Chemicals

Gallic acid, (+)-catechin, pyrocatechol, chlorogenic acid, 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, (−)-epicatechin, caffeic acid, syringic acid, vanillin, taxifolin, sinapic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, rosmarinic acid, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, pinoresinol, quercetin, luteolin, and apigenin were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Fluka (St. Louis, MO, USA) supplied vanillic acid, 3-hydroxybenzoic acid, 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid, apigenin 7-glucoside, luteolin 7-glucoside, hesperidin, eriodictyol, and kaempferol. Verbascoside, protocatechuic acid, and hyperoside were obtained from HWI Analytik (Ruelzheim, Germany). HPLC-grade methanol (99.9%) and formic acid were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), respectively. Ultrapure water (18 MΩ) was generated using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore Co., Ltd.). Additionally, methanol and ethyl acetate were obtained from Carlo Erba Reagents (Milan, Italy), and ultrapure water was also prepared using the Millipore Milli-Q Plus system (Millipore Bedford Corp., Bedford, MA, USA).

Plant material

The aerial portions of O. rechingeri were harvested from the roadside at the 15th km of the Adıyaman–Çelikhan highway in Adıyaman Province, Turkey (elevation: 1.200 m; coordinates: 37°55′693″ N, 38°17′061″ E) on 23 June 2023. The collection of plant material was carried out with the necessary permissions obtained from the relevant local authorities and in accordance with applicable institutional and national regulations governing the collection of wild plant species. All field studies and sampling procedures complied with relevant international guidelines, including the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction and the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES). Specimen authentication and deposition (Herbarium No. Binzet 202011) were carried out by Prof. Dr. Rıza Binzet of the Department of Biology, Mersin University, Mersin, Turkey.

Methanol extraction

Methanol extracts were prepared according to Zengin, et al14. using three approaches:

Maceration (MAC):

Five grams of dried plant material were combined with 100 mL of methanol and left to macerate for 24 h at room temperature (20–25 °C) under temperature-controlled conditions. During maceration, agitation was applied using a magnetic stirrer (150 rpm) to enhance extraction efficiency.

Ultrasound-Assisted Extraction (UAE): The same plant-to-solvent ratio (1:20 w/v) was subjected to ultrasonic irradiation for one hour using an open-system ultrasonic water-bath device (bath-type) (Sonorex Digitec DT 510 H, Bandelin Electronic GmbH & Co. KG, Berlin, Germany). The ultrasound bath operated at 40 kHz and 300 W in continuous mode (no ON/OFF pulse cycles). To minimize temperature rise during sonication, ice packs were used and the extraction temperature was maintained at approximately 30 °C.

Soxhlet Extraction (SOE):

Standard Soxhlet apparatus was used to extract compounds with methanol over a period of six hours at the boiling temperature of methanol (approximately 65 °C).

Following extraction, all solutions were filtered and concentrated to dryness under reduced pressure at 40 °C. The dried residues were stored in amber vials at 4 °C until further analysis. Extraction efficiencies for MAC, SOE, and UAE were calculated as 2.87%, 2.53%, and 1.86%, respectively.

Quantification of phenolic constituents

To determine the total phenolic content, 0.25 mL of the sample solution was combined with 1 mL of diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (1:9 dilution) and thoroughly mixed. After 3 min, 0.75 mL of 1% sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) solution was added. The mixture was then incubated at room temperature for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 760 nm. Results were expressed in terms of gallic acid equivalents15.

For the total flavonoid content, 1 mL of the sample solution was mixed with 1 mL of 2% aluminium trichloride (AlCl₃) prepared in methanol. A blank was also prepared by mixing 1 mL of the sample with 1 mL of methanol without AlCl₃. After a 10-minute incubation at room temperature, the absorbance of both the sample and the blank was recorded at 415 nm. The final absorbance was calculated by subtracting the blank value from that of the sample, and total flavonoid content was reported as rutin equivalents15.

A previously developed and validated method—known for its simplicity, speed, reproducibility, and sensitivity—was employed to simultaneously quantify 31 phenolic compounds using LC–ESI–MS/MS16. The analyses were performed using an Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity liquid chromatography system coupled with a 6420 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved on a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (100 mm × 4.6 mm i.d., 2.7 μm particle size). The mobile phase, consisting of 0.1% formic acid and methanol, was optimized to enhance chromatographic resolution of isomeric compounds and to improve detection sensitivity for many analytes. Specifically, solvent A was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water, and solvent B was methanol. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0.00–3.00 min, 2% B; 6.00 min, 25% B; 10.00 min, 50% B; 14.00–17.00 min, 95% B; and returned to 2% B at 17.50 min. The column temperature was kept constant at 25 °C, with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and an injection volume of 2.0 µL.

The mass spectrometer was connected to the LC system via an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface and operated in both positive and negative multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) modes. The ESI source settings were as follows: capillary voltage at − 3.5 kV, gas temperature at 300 °C, gas flow rate at 11 L/min, and nebulizer pressure at 40 psi. Compound identification was achieved by comparing retention times and MRM ion transitions with those of authentic standards, as detailed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Assessment of biological activities

The total antioxidant capacity of the samples was assessed using the phosphomolybdenum assay. For this, 0.2 mL of the sample solution was mixed with 2 mL of a reagent composed of 0.6 M sulfuric acid, 28 mM sodium phosphate, and 4 mM ammonium molybdate. The mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 90 min, after which absorbance was measured at 695 nm15,17–20.

To evaluate DPPH radical scavenging activity, 1 mL of the sample was added to 4 mL of a 0.004% DPPH solution in methanol. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and absorbance was recorded at 517 nm15,17–20.

ABTS radical cation scavenging activity was determined by generating ABTS⁺• radicals via reaction of 7 mM ABTS with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate, allowing the solution to stand in the dark for 12–16 h at room temperature. Prior to the assay, this solution was diluted with methanol to reach an absorbance of 0.700 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. Then, 1 mL of the sample was added to 2 mL of the diluted ABTS solution, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at room temperature for 7 min before absorbance was measured at 734 nm15,17–20.

Ferrous ion chelating activity was measured by adding 2 mL of the sample to 0.05 mL of 2 mM FeCl₂ solution, followed by 0.2 mL of 5 mM ferrozine to initiate the reaction. A blank was prepared similarly, but using water instead of ferrozine. Absorbance was measured at 562 nm after a 10-minute incubation at room temperature15,17–20.

CUPRAC (cupric ion reducing antioxidant capacity) was assessed by mixing 0.5 mL of sample with a pre-prepared reaction mixture consisting of 1 mL each of 10 mM CuCl₂, 7.5 mM neocuproine, and 1 M ammonium acetate buffer (pH 7.0). A blank was prepared without CuCl₂. After 30 min of incubation at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 450 nm15,17–20.

For the FRAP (ferric reducing antioxidant power) assay, 0.1 mL of sample was mixed with 2 mL of FRAP reagent prepared by combining acetate buffer (0.3 M, pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl₃ in a 10:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio. After a 30-minute incubation at room temperature, absorbance was recorded at 593 nm15,17–20.

α-Amylase inhibitory activity was determined using the Caraway–Somogyi iodine/potassium iodide (IKI) method. In a 96-well plate, 25 µL of sample was combined with 50 µL of α-amylase in phosphate buffer (pH 6.9 with 6 mM NaCl) and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The reaction was started by adding 50 µL of 0.05% starch solution. A blank was prepared without the enzyme. After another 10-minute incubation at 37 °C, the reaction was stopped with 25 µL of 1 M HCl, followed by addition of 100 µL of IKI solution. Absorbance was measured at 630 nm, and blank absorbance was subtracted from that of the sample21.

Tyrosinase inhibition was analyzed using a modified dopachrome assay with L-DOPA as the substrate. In a 96-well plate, 25 µL of the sample was mixed with 40 µL of tyrosinase solution and 100 µL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), then incubated for 15 min at 25 °C. The reaction was initiated with 40 µL of L-DOPA. A blank was prepared without the enzyme. After 10 min at 25 °C, absorbance was measured at 492 nm21.

Cholinesterase inhibition was determined via Ellman’s method. In a 96-well plate, 50 µL of the sample was mixed with 125 µL of DTNB and 25 µL of AChE enzyme solution in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), followed by a 15-minute incubation at 25 °C. The reaction was initiated with 25 µL of acetylthiocholine iodide (ATCI). A blank was prepared in the absence of the enzyme. After 10 min, absorbance was measured at 405 nm, and the blank value was subtracted21. For radical scavenging, metal chelation, and enzyme inhibition assays, IC₅₀ values were defined as the sample concentration required to reduce the initial activity by 50%. EC₅₀ values for reducing power and phosphomolybdenum assays were calculated as the concentration corresponding to an absorbance of 0.500. The biological activities of the extracts were reported as mg of standard equivalent per gram of extract, and compared against reference compounds including Trolox, EDTA (disodium salt), galanthamine, kojic acid, and acarbose, which served as positive controls.

Statistical treatment

All experiments were performed in triplicate. To assess statistical significance, Tukey’s test was applied. As previously mentioned, antioxidant activities of the extracts were evaluated using a variety of analytical methods, each designed to assess a distinct mechanism of antioxidant action. Consequently, the results from different assays are not directly comparable. To enable cross-method comparison, Relative Antioxidant Capacity Index (RACI) values were calculated22. Additionally, the correlation between individual assay results and their corresponding RACI values was assessed separately.

To compute RACI values, the mean and standard deviation were first calculated for each assay. The individual data points were standardized by subtracting the mean from each value and dividing the result by the standard deviation. This normalization process was applied independently for each antioxidant test. Total RACI scores were then obtained by averaging the RACI values across all antioxidant assays conducted for a given sample, including total phenolic and flavonoid content.

Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22.0 to explore the relationship between the primary phytochemical classes—namely phenolics and flavonoids—and their biological activity.

Molecular docking–based assessment of interactions between major phytochemicals and human enzymes

In this work, molecular docking simulations were employed to investigate the binding propensities of three principal phytochemicals—hesperidin, luteolin-7-glucoside, and rosmarinic acid—identified in O. rechingeri. These compounds were computationally evaluated against three biologically significant human enzymes: pancreatic α-amylase (AAMY), acetylcholinesterase (AChE), and tyrosinase-related protein 1 (TYRP1). The objective was to clarify the molecular basis of the enzyme inhibitory effects observed experimentally for extracts obtained via maceration, Soxhlet extraction, and ultrasound-assisted extraction. The docking analyses were designed to prioritize major constituents that may contribute to the observed enzyme inhibitory profiles and to generate mechanistic insights that complement the in vitro data. By targeting enzymes central to carbohydrate digestion, cholinergic neurotransmission, and melanogenesis, this in silico strategy facilitated the preliminary recognition of phytochemicals with potential therapeutic significance.

High-resolution crystallographic structures were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, including human α-amylase co-crystallized with montbretin A (PDB ID: 4W93; 1.35 Å), human AChE in complex with tacrine (PDB ID: 7XN1; 2.85 Å), and TYRP1 bound to tropolone (PDB ID: 5M8O; 2.50 Å). High-resolution X-ray crystallographic structures were selected because experimentally determined coordinates provide reliable atomic-level definition of the active site architecture and binding pocket geometry, which is essential for accurate structure-based docking simulations. The presence of co-crystallized ligands further allowed grid calibration and validation of the docking protocol23. Three-dimensional structures of hesperidin, luteolin-7-glucoside, and rosmarinic acid were constructed using ChemOffice v19.1 and exported in MOL format for subsequent docking analyses24. To verify the reliability of the docking protocol and establish comparative benchmarks, the native co-crystallized ligands—montbretin A, tacrine, and tropolone—were re-docked into their respective active sites. The resulting binding scores (kcal/mol) were used as reference values for assessing the affinities of the phytochemicals. Overall, this combined computational approach provides deeper insight into enzyme–ligand interactions and supports the identification of bioactive constituents from O. rechingeri with prospective pharmaceutical relevance.

Preparation of protein and ligand structures for molecular docking

Before docking analyses, the crystallographic structures of the target enzymes—AAMY, AChE, and TYRP1—were structurally optimized using Discovery Studio Visualizer24. All non-essential components, including crystallographic water molecules and metal ions, were removed to enhance the reliability of the docking calculations. Both the selected phytochemicals and the native co-crystallized inhibitors were subjected to energy minimization using Open Babel, applying the MMFF94 force field in combination with the conjugate gradient optimization method (convergence criterion: 10⁻⁷)25. The MMFF94 force field was selected due to its well-established parameterization, which enables accurate representation of bond geometries and non-covalent interactions in small to medium-sized organic compounds26,27. Following optimization, ligands were saved in MOL2 format, and all protein–ligand systems were subsequently converted to PDBQT format using AutoDock Tools v1.5.728 to ensure compatibility with the AutoDock Vina docking platform.

Docking simulations and interaction analysis

Molecular docking calculations were performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.729, implemented in conjunction with AutoDock Tools. During system preparation, polar hydrogen atoms were retained, non-polar hydrogens were removed, and appropriate partial charges were assigned using the Gasteiger method for ligands and the Kollman scheme for protein receptors. Any imbalance in the total charge of the protein structures was corrected prior to docking.

A semi-flexible docking protocol was employed, in which ligands were allowed full conformational flexibility while the protein receptors were maintained in a rigid state. Docking grids were centered on the binding sites defined by the positions of the native co-crystallized ligands, thereby restricting the search space to experimentally validated active pockets. The grid box dimensions and center coordinates were defined as follows: for AAMY, 18 × 18 × 18 Å³ (center: x = 11.57, y = 86.33, z = 156.33); for AChE, 15 × 15 × 15 Å³ (center: x = 48.32, y = − 39.99, z = − 30.07); and for TYRP1, 14 × 14 × 14 Å³ (center: x = 121.08, y = 278.18, z = 216.89).

For each ligand–enzyme system, 20 independent docking runs were conducted with an exhaustiveness parameter of 64 to ensure thorough sampling of the conformational space. The resulting poses were clustered according to geometric similarity and ranked based on predicted binding free energies (ΔG, kcal/mol). The highest-ranked pose from each cluster, corresponding to the lowest ΔG value, was selected for subsequent analysis.

Protein–ligand interactions, including hydrogen bonds, π–π stacking interactions, and van der Waals contacts, were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio Visualizer30. This analysis provided molecular-level insights into the interaction patterns underlying the inhibitory effects of the major phytochemical constituents of O. rechingeri on the target enzymes.

Results

Chemical compositions of the extracts

The methanolic extracts of O. rechingeri exhibited comparable but extraction-dependent totals of phenolics and flavonoids (Fig. 1). SOE-ME and UAE-ME contained higher total phenolics than MAC-ME (34.31 and 33.58 vs. 30.93 mg GAEs/g), and a similar pattern was observed for total flavonoids (32.95 and 31.49 vs. 27.87 mg REs/g), indicating that the intensified techniques outperformed simple maceration (Fig. 1).

Targeted phenolic profiling revealed luteolin 7-glucoside, hesperidin, and rosmarinic acid as the predominant constituents across extracts (Table 1; Fig. 2). Luteolin 7-glucoside was abundant in all extracts; however, MAC-ME contained a significantly higher level (4136 µg/g) than SOE-ME (4014 µg/g) and UAE-ME (4013 µg/g), which did not differ from each other according to Tukey’s HSD test (p < 0.05; Table 1). A similar extraction-dependent pattern was observed for hesperidin, with MAC-ME showing the highest and statistically distinct concentration (3304 µg/g), followed by statistically comparable SOE-ME (2898 µg/g) and UAE-ME (2749 µg/g). In contrast, rosmarinic acid was favored by intensified extraction, with all three extracts differing significantly from each other in the order SOE-ME (4182 µg/g) > UAE-ME (3971 µg/g) > MAC-ME (2931 µg/g). These values also provide comparative benchmarks for the dominant phenolics of O. rechingeri, indicating that glycosidic flavonoids were relatively enriched by maceration, whereas rosmarinic acid was maximized by Soxhlet extraction.

According to data presented in the table, the same integrated pattern was observed for several minor constituents. Hyperoside and chlorogenic acid were significantly highest in MAC-ME, whereas 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillin, ferulic acid, luteolin, and protocatechuic acid reached their highest statistically distinct levels in SOE-ME. Pinoresinol, p-coumaric acid, and syringic acid were also higher in the intensified extracts than in MAC-ME, although SOE-ME and UAE-ME were not significantly different from each other for these compounds. Antioxidant activities of the extracts

The antioxidant profile varied according to both the analytical assay employed and the extraction-related composition of each methanolic extract (Table 2). Overall, SOE-ME tended to perform best in reducing power and radical-scavenging assays, showing the lowest EC₅₀/IC₅₀ values in CUPRAC (0.85 mg/mL), FRAP (0.626 mg/mL), DPPH (1.20 mg/mL), and ABTS (1.120 mg/mL), relative to UAE-ME and MAC-ME. For these four assays, Tukey’s test indicated significant differences among all extract treatments, with SOE-ME showing significantly stronger activity than UAE-ME and MAC-ME, while UAE-ME was also significantly more active than MAC-ME (p < 0.05). By contrast, the phosphomolybdenum assay showed only minor variation among extracts (1.44–1.50 mg/mL). These differences were not statistically significant among MAC-ME, SOE-ME, and UAE-ME, as indicated by the same superscript letter. Metal-chelating capacity followed an opposite trend, with MAC-ME and UAE-ME (≈ 1.03–1.05 mg/mL) outperforming SOE-ME (1.24 mg/mL). In this assay, MAC-ME and UAE-ME did not differ significantly from each other but both were significantly more active than SOE-ME. As expected, positive controls were substantially more active. The integrated RACI score similarly placed SOE-ME highest (0.635), UAE-ME intermediate (0), and MAC-ME lowest (− 0.632) (Fig. 3).

Enzyme inhibitory activities of the extracts

Enzyme inhibition was extraction-dependent (Table 3). For AChE, MAC-ME showed the greatest potency (IC₅₀ = 1.78 mg/mL), outperforming UAE-ME (2.19 mg/mL) and SOE-ME (2.36 mg/mL). Statistically, MAC-ME was significantly more active than SOE-ME, whereas UAE-ME occupied an intermediate position and did not differ significantly from either MAC-ME or SOE-ME. Tyrosinase inhibition favored SOE-ME (1.85 mg/mL) over UAE-ME (1.90 mg/mL) and MAC-ME (1.96 mg/mL). For tyrosinase, SOE-ME was significantly more active than MAC-ME, while UAE-ME was statistically intermediate and not significantly different from either of them. For α-amylase, MAC-ME again led (0.98 mg/mL), followed by UAE-ME (1.14 mg/mL) and SOE-ME (1.35 mg/mL). In this assay, all three extracts differed significantly from each other, following the order MAC-ME > UAE-ME > SOE-ME. Reference inhibitors were markedly stronger.

Linking phenolic profiles to assay outcomes

Redox-based assays were tightly aligned (e.g., DPPH–FRAP r = 0.996; CUPRAC–FRAP r = 0.995), whereas chelation (FICA) opposed them (e.g., FICA–ABTS r = − 0.996) (Table 4). Enzyme readouts split into two clusters: AChE and α-amylase inhibition correlated negatively with electron transfer metrics (AAIA–DPPH r = − 0.986; AChEIA–CUPRAC r = − 0.844) but positively with FICA (AAIA r = 0.895; AChEIA r = 0.712); by contrast, tyrosinase inhibition tracked electron transfer strength (TIA–CUPRAC r = 0.929; TIA–FRAP r = 0.899) and was inversely related to FICA (r = − 0.811). Total phenolics/flavonoids mirrored the electron transfer block (TPC–FRAP r = 0.866; TFC–CUPRAC r = 0.930) and were inversely associated with AChEIA/AAIA (e.g., TFC–AAIA r = − 0.964). Among individual markers, luteolin showed the strongest electron transfer coupling (to FRAP r = 0.996) and anti-FICA trend (r = − 0.971); rosmarinic and 4-hydroxybenzoic acids similarly supported electron transfer (to CUPRAC r = 0.885 and 0.901). Conversely, glycosides such as luteolin-7-glucoside and hesperidin associated with AChEIA/AAIA (r = 0.859/0.860 and 0.755/0.708), suggesting possible, but not definitive, associations between these glycosides and the two enzyme-related response patterns. Because the correlation analysis was based on only three extract types, these relationships should be regarded as exploratory rather than causal (Table 4).

Discussion

Chemical composition

This study provides the first chemical profile of O. rechingeri, enabling genus-level comparisons with congeneric taxa analyzed by similar extraction and workflows from the same research line—an important point for methodological consistency21,31–34. Across methods, methanolic extracts of O. rechingeri contained moderate-to-high totals of phenolics and flavonoids, with intensified techniques (Soxhlet, ultrasound) outperforming maceration for overall yield. This pattern echoes prior Onosma reports where extractive strength is a key determinant of phenolic recovery and where weaker/less polar conditions (e.g., EtOAc) are consistently poor in screened phenolics21,31,34.

Targeted profiling identified luteolin-7-glucoside, hesperidin, and rosmarinic acid as recurrent major constituents in O. rechingeri. The prominence of rosmarinic acid is concordant with several Onosma species—O. gigantea, O. frutescens, O. sericea, and O. ambigens—in which rosmarinic acid or flavone glycosides dominate methanolic profiles31,32,34. At the same time, O. rechingeri shows a nuanced extraction dependence: Soxhlet and ultrasound favored higher rosmarinic acid, whereas maceration slightly preserved glycosides (luteolin-7-glucoside, hesperidin). Such divergence likely reflects differential stability/solubility of phenolic subclasses across thermal and mass-transfer regimes—glycosides being comparatively stable under mild conditions35, while intensified techniques better liberate ester-linked phenolics like rosmarinic acid36.

Secondary features of the O. rechingeri profile also align with genus-level trends. Hyperoside, chlorogenic acid, pinoresinol, 4-hydroxybenzoic and vanillic acids were consistently detected, broadly mirroring the phenolic “palette” reported for O. aucheriana, O. frutescens and O. sericea32. Conversely, the non-detection of (+)-catechin, (–)-epicatechin, gallic acid, quercetin and verbascoside in O. rechingeri resembles the absence/trace levels of these analytes in certain congeners profiled under comparable conditions32. Taken together, these parallels suggest a chemotaxonomic signature for the genus centered on rosmarinic acid and flavone glycosides, with species- and method-specific shifts in relative abundance.

From a functional standpoint, the predominance of rosmarinic acid, luteolin derivatives and hesperidin—compounds repeatedly linked to antioxidant and enzyme-modulatory activities in Onosma—is consistent with the bioactivity patterns reported for methanolic and aqueous extracts in related species21,31,33,34. Although the present work focuses on methanolic extracts, prior studies show that water extracts can be comparatively rich in several phenolic acids and flavonoids21,31, hinting that a broader solvent panel for O. rechingeri (including water) may further augment specific phenolic subclasses.

Antioxidant activity

The present work constitutes the first systematic appraisal of the antioxidant profile of O. rechingeri. Comparable trends have been reported for congeners of the genus. Methanolic preparations of O. mutabilis and O. ambigens were likewise dominant in radical-scavenging and reducing-power tests, a behaviour ascribed to their dense phenolic/flavonoid matrices34,37. A similar hierarchy emerged in O. lycaonica versus O. papillosa, where the more phenolic-rich extract of the former out-performed its counterpart across CUPRAC, FRAP, DPPH and ABTS assays38. Against this backdrop, the RACI recorded here positions SOE-ME at the upper end of the activity range that typifies the genus.

Notably, the chelation profile of O. rechingeri mirrors the inter-specific divergence documented for O. trapezuntea and O. rigidum—the latter displaying comparatively weaker reducing power yet superior metal-binding capacity39. Such concordance suggests that chelating efficiency may depend less on extract redox density than on the abundance of ortho-dihydroxylated or carboxyl-rich sub-fractions selectively enriched by milder, non-thermal protocols40.

Chemical profiling revealed luteolin-7-glucoside, hesperidin and rosmarinic acid as leading constituents of SOE-ME. Each compound possesses well-documented antioxidant properties that rationalise the assay outcomes: rosmarinic acid donates electrons rapidly and quenches reactive species in both cell-free and cellular systems41; thermal-stabilised luteolin-7-glucoside shows enhanced radical-scavenging and promotes endogenous redox defences42; and hesperidin affords marked protection against oxidative stress in chemical and biological models43. The elevated temperatures inherent to Soxhlet extraction plausibly facilitated the liberation of these relatively polar phytochemicals—an effect likewise observed for O. ambigens where rosmarinic acid and luteolin-7-glucoside predominated after hot methanol extraction34. Their complementary mechanisms—electron donation (rosmarinic acid), hydrogen-atom transfer (luteolin-7-glucoside) and modulation of cellular antioxidant enzymes (hesperidin)—provide a coherent molecular explanation for the superior electron-transfer but moderate chelating performance of SOE-ME.

Taken together, the data indicate that O. rechingeri possesses a measurable antioxidant profile, particularly in electron-transfer assays, that is broadly consistent with patterns reported for several congeners. Direct superiority over related species should be avoided because assay conditions, extraction protocols, and reporting formats differ among studies.

Enzyme inhibition activity

This work also provides the first account of enzyme inhibition by O. rechingeri and shows a clear extraction-dependence across targets. The methanolic macerate most strongly inhibited AChE and α-amylase, whereas Soxhlet methanol was slightly superior against tyrosinase; rank orders were conserved when activities were expressed as reference‐drug equivalents, indicating assay-level consistency rather than scale artifacts. These patterns align with genus-level observations that polar phenolic-rich extracts of Onosma often drive cholinesterase, tyrosinase and carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzyme inhibition, while activity magnitudes vary with solvent system and processing (time/temperature) (e.g., MeOH/EtOAc > water in many cases). Studies on O. heterophyllum, O. ambigens, O. tauricum var. tauricum, and other congeners similarly reported meaningful anti-AChE/anti-tyrosinase/anti-amylase actions and frequently noted positive associations with total phenolics or specific flavonoid/phenolic acids, though the most active extract sometimes differed by species and enzyme21,34,44,45.

Two reports especially contextualize our extraction effect. First, in O. bourgaei and O. trachytricha, methanolic extracts inhibited α-glucosidase/α-amylase and cholinesterases, and the two species showed comparable tyrosinase inhibition, underscoring that digestive-enzyme versus cholinesterase/tyrosinase profiles can diverge even under a single solvent regime12. Second, comparative work on additional Onosma taxa confirmed that switching among EtOAc/MeOH/H₂O shifts inhibitory strength and that MeOH extracts are often (not invariably) among the most active37. Consistent with the literature, MAC-ME showed the strongest inhibition of AChE and α-amylase, whereas SOE-ME exhibited slightly better tyrosinase inhibition. This pattern may indicate that mild maceration helps retain heat-sensitive constituents contributing to AChE/α-amylase inhibition, while Soxhlet extraction promotes the release/enrichment of phenolics that are more relevant to tyrosinase inhibition.

The phytochemical context of O. rechingeri supports a constituent-driven hypothesis. Chromatography identified luteolin-7-glucoside, hesperidin and rosmarinic acid among the high-abundance metabolites. Luteolin-7-glucoside has been shown to inhibit AChE (with only weak tyrosinase/BChE effects), consistent with our AChE-weighted activity in MeOH extracts; thus luteolin-7-glucoside likely contributes to the cholinesterase signal46. Rosmarinic acid is a plausible multi-target contributor: it inhibits AChE/BChE and also suppresses pancreatic α-amylase and tyrosinase in vitro, matching the cross-target activity we observe47–49. Hesperidin (and its glycosylated analogs) inhibits α-glucosidase50 and has documented effects on cholinesterases in vivo51; notably, hesperidin acts as a non-competitive tyrosinase inhibitor in cell-free assays, which could rationalize a modest contribution to our tyrosinase profile while more strongly supporting carbohydrate-enzyme modulation.

Taken together, the abundant phenylpropanoids/flavone glycosides in O. rechingeri provide a mechanistic basis for the observed multi-enzyme inhibition and for the extraction-specific differences (e.g., maceration favoring labile luteolin-7-glucoside-like contributors to AChE versus Soxhlet favoring rosmarinic acid-dominated fractions relevant to tyrosinase).

Computational interaction profiling

The binding affinity of luteolin-7-glucoside to AAMY is highly comparable to the value previously reported in the literature for this compound (from O. bourgaei and O. trachytricha) binding to AAMY (ΔG= − 9.22 kcal/mol), which was calculated using AutoDock 4.2.612. The consistency between the two docking results supports the validity of our docking outcome, albeit, small differences were observed in the interacting amino acid residues, which may be attributed to variations in the docking search algorithms or the initial ligand configurations used. Rosmarinic acid, in the context of AChE, exhibited a highly favorable binding energy in the docking study, indicating its potential as a hit ligand. Rosmarinic acid was reported to inhibit AChE and BChE with Ki values ranging from the micromolar to the picomolar scale, with the most pronounced inhibitory effect observed against AChE, for which an experimental Ki value of 42.52 pM was reported47. Thus, the docking affinity of rosmarinic acid in our study supports its prioritization for further experimental testing against AChE. Luteolin-7-glucoside and rosmarinic acid exhibited significantly more favorable docking scores against the human TYRP1 enzyme compared to the inhibitor tropolone, thereby suggesting that these compounds may merit further TYRP1-focused validation. No computational or experimental data reporting the interaction between luteolin-7-glucoside and TYRP1 could be found in the literature; therefore, the luteolin-7-glucoside–TYRP1 interaction and its promising binding affinity presented in this study represent a new finding. In contrast, rosmarinic acid has been shown to exhibit favorable interactions with TYRP1 in a previous computational docking and experimental study, demonstrating tight binding within the enzyme’s active site (ΔG= − 7.38 kcal/mol) compared with the co-crystallized inhibitor tropolone52. Therefore, when considered alongside the existing literature, our docking study suggests that rosmarinic acid could represent a computationally prioritized candidate for subsequent TYRP1 inhibition assays.

Conclusions

This work shows that extraction modality is a decisive lever for tuning both the phenolic architecture and the bioactivity profile of O. rechingeri methanolic extracts. Intensified techniques (Soxhlet and ultrasound) enriched total phenolics/flavonoids and strengthened electron-transfer antioxidant readouts, whereas maceration preserved glycosidic phenolics that coincided with stronger AChE and α-amylase inhibition. Multivariate correlations resolved two activity clusters: electron-transfer measures covaried and aligned with tyrosinase inhibition, while diverging from metal chelation and from AChE/α-amylase responses. These associations are hypothesis-generating rather than causal, especially because they were derived from a small number of extract types. Docking analyses provided supportive but preliminary molecular-level predictions by revealing target-consistent interactions and competitive binding poses for the major constituents across AChE, TYRP1, and α-amylase.

Notwithstanding these strengths, the study is limited by reliance on crude methanolic matrices, in vitro chemical assays, and docking-level inference without kinetic or cellular confirmation. Matrix effects, potential assay interferences, and the absence of permeability/metabolic data could bias apparent potencies. Future efforts should pursue bioassay-guided fractionation to deconvolute synergistic versus single-agent effects; enzyme kinetics to define mechanisms and inhibition constants; and cell-based models (e.g., melanocytes, neuronal and intestinal lines) to validate functional relevance and cytocompatibility. Orthogonal in silico refinements (MD, MM-GBSA, free-energy calculations) and, where feasible, structural biology would improve target assignment. To support pharmaceutical or nutraceutical translation, these findings should next be tested in vivo using appropriate efficacy and safety paradigms (e.g., animal models relevant to oxidative stress–linked cognitive or metabolic dysfunction, and pigmentation-related readouts), alongside pharmacokinetic/bioavailability profiling and acute–subchronic toxicity assessment. For nutraceutical development specifically, additional studies such as simulated gastrointestinal digestion, stability under processing/storage, and formulation/encapsulation strategies should be conducted to define dose-relevant exposure and reproducible performance. Finally, process optimization toward greener, scalable extraction (e.g., UAE with response-surface design, solvent selection guided by phenolic subclass targets) may enable standardized preparations tailored to antioxidant, cosmeceutical, or carbohydrate-digestive enzyme–modulating applications. Collectively, O. rechingeri emerges as a tractable source of multi-target phenolics whose activity spectrum can be purpose-built through extraction-informed design.
