Infection after Endovascular Repair of Complex Aortic and Thoracoabdominal Aneurysms with Fenestrated and Branched Devices: A Systematic Descriptive Review
Authors: Andreas L Tsimpoukis, Christos F Pitros, Fotios O Efthymiou, Spyros I Papadoulas, John D Kakisis, Konstantinos G Moulakakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndograft infection after fenestrated or branched endovascular aortic repair (F/BEVAR) is an uncommon yet serious complication due to the involvement of the visceral branches and the complexity of the required treatment. This systematic review aimed to descriptively synthesize reported cases of infection after F/BEVAR, with an emphasis on clinical presentation, microbiology, associated aortoenteric fistulas (AEFs), management strategies, and reported outcomes. A systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, with MEDLINE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library searched up to September 14, 2025; eligible reports included case reports and series describing F/BEVAR-related infections. Eleven studies encompassing 26 patients were identified. The weighted mean patient age was approximately 73 years, and 77% of patients were male. The weighted mean interval from repair to infection was approximately 20 months. Microbiological findings were heterogeneous, with both Gram-negative and Gram-positive organisms and several polymicrobial infections reported. AEFs were confirmed in at least 8/26 (31%) patients. Surgical explantation with reconstruction was performed in 12/26 patients (46%), conservative therapy in 12/26 (46%), and graft-preserving procedures in 2/26 (8%). The overall 30-day mortality rate was 4/26 (15%): 3/12 after graft explantation and reconstruction, 1/12 after conservative treatment, and 0/2 after graft-preserving procedures. Among the 22 patients who survived 30 days, 18 (82%) were alive at the last reported follow-up: 9/9 after graft explantation and reconstruction, 7/11 after conservative treatment, and 2/2 after graft-preserving procedures. Infection after F/BEVAR requires individualized management and has been reported to have a heterogeneous clinical presentation, microbiological findings, and treatment strategies. Given the limited evidence base, consisting primarily of case reports and small case series, these findings should be interpreted descriptively. Multicenter collaboration and standardized reporting are needed to better characterize this complication and inform future management strategies.Keywords: Aortic aneurysm, thoracoabdominal; Endovascular aneurysm repair; Prosthesis-related infections; Systematic review

INTRODUCTION

Fenestrated or branched endovascular aortic repair (F/BEVAR) has emerged as a transformative technique for treating thoracoabdominal and other complex aortic pathologies; this has fundamentally reshaped the management paradigm for these challenging conditions [1]. The adoption of F/BEVAR has expanded rapidly in recent years, driven by advancements in device technology and growing physician expertise [2].

Aortic endograft infection (AEI) following F/BEVAR is uncommon, but is one of the most devastating complications due to the anatomical location of the graft in the mid-abdominal or descending thoracic aorta and the need to maintain visceral artery perfusion [1]. As these infections are rare, diagnostic and therapeutic strategies are largely guided by principles established for the broader management of aortic graft and endograft infections [3]. When infections occur, they often have catastrophic consequences, including life-threatening sepsis, aortoenteric fistula (AEF) formation, pseudoaneurysm development, and the need for high-risk reintervention, with substantial associated mortality [3,4].

Managing infected F/BEVAR devices poses significant challenges that are distinct from those encountered in conventional AEI [1]. Complete device explantation followed by in situ or extra-anatomic reconstruction is generally considered the definitive treatment; however, it is frequently prohibitively risky in frail patients or when visceral incorporation renders device removal complex [5]. Alternative approaches, such as partial explantation, radical debridement, long-term antibiotic therapy, and complex reconstructive techniques, have been proposed; however, outcomes remain variable, and the optimal management strategy remains uncertain [6].

This review aimed to systematically identify and descriptively synthesize reported cases of infection following F/BEVAR, focusing on the clinical presentation, timing of onset, microbiological patterns, association with AEF, and reported management strategies. Given the rarity of this complication and the nature of the available evidence, the analysis was intended to be descriptive rather than comparative.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This systematic review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [7].

1) Search strategy

We systematically searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to September 14, 2025, without language or date restrictions. The search combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to infection and endovascular aortic repair (EVAR) using Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT). The complete search strings for each database are listed in Table 1. In addition, we manually reviewed the reference lists of the relevant articles to identify additional eligible studies. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (ID: CRD420251126793). Although the registered protocol specified the inclusion of English-language studies and the exclusion of conference abstracts, the eligibility criteria were subsequently broadened to include non-English studies and selected conference abstracts with extractable relevant data due to the limited number of reported cases.

2) Study identification and bias evaluation

The records identified in each database search were collected using Mendeley software (Elsevier B.V.). The Ablebits Duplicate Remover add-in (4 Bits Ltd.) was used to eliminate duplicates using Microsoft Excel version 2021 (Microsoft Corp.). No attempts were made to obtain unpublished data. Two reviewers (ALT and KGM) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. A PRISMA flow diagram summarizes the study selection process (Fig. 1). Domain-level methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists [8] for case reports and for case series separately, and was visualized using the Risk Of Bias VISualization (ROBVIS) online application [9] as “traffic light” plots. Judgments were based on yes/no/unclear responses and mapped to low, high, and moderate categories for visualization purposes.

3) Eligibility criteria

We included case reports, case series, and cohort studies that reported infections following F/BEVAR. Studies were excluded if they were non-human (in vitro or animal), reviews, letters, editorials, or lacked extractable data.

4) Data extraction

From each included study, we extracted the year of publication, study duration, patient demographics and comorbidities, clinical presentation of infection with the corresponding Management of Aortic Graft Infection Collaboration (MAGIC) diagnostic criteria, type of implanted device (fenestrated, branched, or hybrid), identified microorganisms, and details of the treatment strategy (surgical or conservative, including the type of reconstruction or antimicrobial regimen). Information on minor procedures (dental, endoscopic, or urologic) performed after F/BEVAR and antibiotic prophylaxis was also recorded. We also collected data on outcome measures, namely length of stay, 30-day mortality, reinfection, and follow-up duration. Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers using a standardized form.

5) Outcomes

The primary objective of this review was to describe the characteristics of infections after F/BEVAR, including the timing of presentation, clinical features, microbiological patterns, and management strategies. The reported outcomes, including 30-day mortality, survival (defined as being alive at the last reported follow-up), and reinfection (defined as recurrence of infection during follow-up), were summarized descriptively. Weighted means for patient age and the interval from F/BEVAR to infection were calculated using the number of patients in each study as weights.

RESULTS

1) Patient demographics and comorbidities

A total of 26 patients with infection after F/BEVAR were identified in 11 studies: 9 case reports [5,10-17] and 2 case series, Figueroa et al. [18] (n=6) and Campolmi et al. [19] (n=11). The patients were predominantly male (20/26, 77%) with a weighted mean age of approximately 73 years.

Comorbidities were reported inconsistently. Among patients with available data (n=16), the most common conditions were hypertension (88%, 14/16), hyperlipidemia (81%, 13/16), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (63%, 10/16), smoking (56%, 9/16), coronary artery disease (38%, 6/16), and peripheral artery disease (25%, 4/16). The weighted mean interval from F/BEVAR to the diagnosis of infection was approximately 20 months (range, 1-84 months). In the 6-patient by Figueroa et al. [18], patients were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists class III, without further details on comorbidities (Table 2).

2) Methodological quality assessment

Methodological appraisal using the JBI critical appraisal checklists showed that the included case reports generally fulfilled most of the core reporting domains, particularly for patient demographics, clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, intervention, and post-intervention outcomes. The most frequent limitation was incomplete reporting of adverse events or complications. The case series demonstrated greater variability in methodological reporting, with the abstract-only study showing several incompletely reported domains. The domain-level assessments are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2.

3) Clinical presentation and diagnostic imaging

Among the 8 case reports that reported presenting symptoms, the most frequent were fever (6/8), abdominal or back pain/discomfort (5/8), weight loss (4/8), and malaise. Sepsis was reported in 2/8 cases and melena in 1/8. Patient-level presenting symptoms were not reported in the series by Figueroa et al. [18] or Campolmi et al. [19].

No included studies reported minor procedures (dental, endoscopic, or urologic) performed after F/BEVAR before infection onset, and the use of antibiotic prophylaxis was not reported (Table 2).

4) MAGIC criteria and diagnostic confirmation

Among the 9 case reports with sufficient data for assessment using the MAGIC criteria, all cases (9/9, 100%) met the definition of diagnosed AEI.

Major criteria were fulfilled in all cases (9/9, 100%). Within the clinical/surgical domain, AEF was identified in 4 cases (44%). Major radiologic criteria on computed tomography (CT) were observed in 6 cases (67%), while positive intraoperative, aspirate, or graft cultures were reported in 7 cases (78%).

Minor criteria complemented these findings in all the cases (9/9, 100%). Clinical signs such as fever occurred in 6 cases (67%), minor radiologic findings, including positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) findings, in 5 cases (56%), and laboratory abnormalities such as positive blood cultures or elevated inflammatory markers in 8 cases (89%).

Campolmi et al. [19] reported 11 cases, all diagnosed according to the MAGIC criteria, with at least one additional criterion reported for each case, resulting in 11/11 diagnosed AEIs; however, individual patient-level MAGIC components were not sufficiently detailed for independent reassessment.

Figueroa et al. [18] reported 6 cases without sufficient individual-level clinical, radiologic, or laboratory data; thus, MAGIC assessment was not possible (Table 3).

5) Microbiology and antibiotic therapy

Among the 9 individually reported patients, positive microbiological findings were reported in 8/9 (89%); the single culture-negative case had received broad-spectrum antibiotics for several weeks before sampling [5]. Among the 7 cases with specific organism-level data, Gram-negative bacteria were identified in 5/7 (71%), including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella oxytoca, Moraxella osloensis, and Bacteroides fragilis. Gram-positive bacteria were identified in 4/7 (57%), including Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus parasanguinis, and Parvimonas micra. Anaerobic organisms were identified in 3/7 (43%), and fungal infection was reported in 1/7 (14%). These categories were not mutually exclusive because several infections were polymicrobial.

In the series by Figueroa et al. [18], recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) were reported in 3 patients, Salmonella bacteremia in 2, and Q fever in 1; the causative organisms of the UTIs were not specified. In the series by Campolmi et al. [19], causative microorganisms were identified in all 11 patients, with heterogeneous mono- and polymicrobial infections.

Antibiotic duration varied considerably across studies. Among the individual case reports with a finite reported treatment duration (n=4), the median duration was 10 weeks (range, 6-18 weeks), whereas 4 patients received lifelong suppressive therapy; treatment duration was not sufficiently reported in one case [16]. In the series by Campolmi et al. [19], the mean duration of antimicrobial therapy was 18 months in conservatively managed patients and 6 months in surgically treated patients. In the series by Figueroa et al. [18], 5 patients considered unfit for open conversion were managed conservatively with lifelong antibiotic therapy.

Initial antimicrobial regimens varied and generally consisted of intravenous broad-spectrum or culture-directed therapy followed by targeted treatment based on microbiological findings. Reported oral suppressive regimens included fluoroquinolones, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, metronidazole, and rifampin-containing combinations (Table 4).

6) Treatment strategy

① Surgical management

Of the 26 reported patients, complete or partial graft explantation with reconstruction was performed in 12 (46%). Surgical strategies varied considerably. Antimicrobial grafts were used in 5 patients, autologous vein reconstruction in 2, and human cryopreserved allografts with visceral debranching and aortic stump closure in 2. A composite bovine/biosynthetic Y graft was used in one patient, and partial excision of FEVAR graft with preservation of the fenestrated cuff followed by reconstruction with a rifampin-soaked bifurcated graft was performed in one. Procedural details regarding the type of reconstruction were not available for one patient from the series by Figueroa et al. [18]. Three patients [13,14,17] underwent staged axillofemoral bypass before graft explantation and reconstruction. In Manunga et al. [5], Terry et al. [14], and Bugna et al. [17], surgical explantation followed an initial period of conservative treatment. In addition, one patient in the series by Campolmi et al. [19] who was initially managed conservatively required urgent explantation because of worsening sepsis. One case involving chimney endovascular aneurysm repair (ChEVAR) was included as part of the series by Campolmi et al. [19] and was retained to preserve the integrity of the original dataset (Table 5).

② Conservative and graft-preserving management

Of the 26 reported patients, 12 (46%) were managed conservatively. Percutaneous drainage was reported in 5 cases (4 from the series by Campolmi et al. [19] and 1 individual case), while the remaining patients received antibiotics alone or had no clearly reported use of drainage. Graft-preserving procedures were performed in 2 patients (8%). One patient underwent duodenal repair with perigraft and retroperitoneal washout and omental reinforcement [15], while the other underwent sacotomy with duodenal repair and omentoplasty without graft explantation (Table 5) [19].

③ Outcomes

Hospital stay was variably reported across studies and could not be reliably pooled because patient-level data were unavailable in several reports. The overall 30-day mortality rate was 4/26 (15%). When stratified by final treatment strategy, 30-day mortality was 1/12 (8%) for conservative management, 3/12 (25%) following graft explantation and reconstruction, and 0/2 for graft-preserving surgery. These findings are descriptive and likely reflect differences in patient selection (Table 5).

Confirmed AEF or aortoduodenal fistula (ADF) was reported in at least 8/26 patients (31%): 4 individual cases [11,14-16] and 4 patients from the series by Campolmi et al. [19]. Hanif et al. [13] additionally described a presumed, scarred-over ADF, whereas fistula status was not reported for the 6 patients in the series by Figueroa et al. [18]. All 8 patients with confirmed AEF/ADF underwent surgical treatment, with or without graft explantation. The 30-day mortality rate among these patients was 2/8 (25%).

Follow-up duration varied substantially. Among the individual case reports with post-discharge follow-up, follow-up ranged from 3 to 48 months. Campolmi et al. [19] reported a mean follow-up of 15 months (range, 3-37 months), whereas Figueroa et al. [18] reported a mean survival of 6±9 months after AEI diagnosis. Among the 22 patients who survived 30 days, 18 (82%) were alive at the last reported follow-up: 9/9 (100%) after graft explantation and reconstruction, 7/11 (64%) after conservative management, and 2/2 (100%) after graft-preserving procedures. Among patients who survived graft explantation and reconstruction, no reinfections were reported during the available follow-up. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of substantial differences in patient selection and follow-up duration (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

Infection after F/BEVAR is a rare and devastating complication. In our review, the weighted mean patient age was approximately 73 years, and comorbidities were common among patients with available data. Symptoms were diverse and often nonspecific, including abdominal or back pain, weight loss, malaise, and fever. This illustrates the diagnostic challenge of graft infection in the elderly, especially when symptoms resemble more prevalent conditions such as musculoskeletal pain or malignancy. Laboratory findings, when reported, commonly included elevated leukocyte counts and C-reactive protein levels. Imaging findings were not uniform across cases. When available, computed tomography angiography and/or fluorodeoxyglucose-PET/CT supported the diagnosis by demonstrating features such as perigraft inflammation, fluid, or gas, consistent with the MAGIC diagnostic criteria [20].

Notably, infections tended to present after a prolonged interval following the index procedure, with a weighted mean interval of approximately 20 months (range, 1-84 months). Late infections may reflect hematogenous seeding or bacterial translocation, whereas early infections may be related to perioperative contamination. These findings are consistent with the broader EVAR literature; a meta-analysis by Argyriou et al. [21] reported a mean interval of 25 months (range, 1-128 months) from implantation to diagnosis.

Microbiological data, when reported at the organism level, demonstrated substantial heterogeneity. Among the 7 individual cases with specific organism-level data, Gram-negative bacteria were identified in 5/7 (71%), while Gram-positive bacteria were identified in 4/7 (57%); these categories were not mutually exclusive because several infections were polymicrobial. AEF or ADF was confirmed in at least 8/26 (31%) patients. In such cases, direct communication between the aorta and gastrointestinal tract may facilitate bacterial translocation and polymicrobial infection, including enteric Gram-negative organisms and anaerobes [21,22]. However, the limited number of cases and heterogeneous microbiological reporting preclude a reliable assessment of the association between fistula status and specific microbial patterns. Close clinical, laboratory, and imaging follow-up is therefore warranted. In the available EVAR literature [22-24], complete explantation with debridement and postoperative antimicrobial therapy is considered the preferred definitive source-control strategy; however, the optimal management of infected branched and fenestrated aortic endografts remains undefined. In our review, several reconstructive strategies were used, reflecting the absence of a single ideal conduit for reconstruction after infected F/BEVAR explantation. The small number of patients precluded meaningful comparison according to conduit type. Approaches included antimicrobial-coated prostheses, autologous vein grafts, cryopreserved allografts, and bovine or biosynthetic grafts.

In the present review, the overall 30-day mortality rate was 4/26 (15%). The 30-day mortality rates were 1/12 (8%) for conservative treatment, 3/12 (25%) for graft explantation and reconstruction, and 0/2 for graft-preserving procedures. Among patients who survived 30 days, all 9 patients who underwent graft explantation and reconstruction were alive at the last reported follow-up, with no reported reinfections, compared with 7/11 (64%) patients managed conservatively. However, these findings are descriptive and are subject to substantial selection bias and differences in follow-up duration. Previous AEI series similarly demonstrate that endograft explantation can provide definitive source control but is associated with substantial perioperative risk, whereas conservative treatment is generally reserved for selected patients who are poor surgical candidates [25,26].

Given the complexity of these cases, treatment should be centralized in high-volume aortic centers where expertise in visceral reconstruction and multidisciplinary collaboration can optimize outcomes [22,27]. Omental reinforcement was reported in only a limited number of cases in this review. In the broader aortic graft infection literature, omental coverage has been used as an adjunct to in situ reconstruction to provide vascularized tissue around the reconstructed graft [27,28]. However, it represents an additional operative step and may not be feasible in unstable or physiologically frail patients.

In conservative and graft-preserving cases, treatment was influenced by patient-specific factors, including patient preference [14], advanced age and severe comorbidities [12,18,19], and the anticipated risks of supraceliac cross-clamping [15] or complex surgical dissection [11]. Notably, the same factors that may have favored the initial use of F/BEVAR, including advanced age, COPD, coronary disease, and prior abdominal surgery, may also render subsequent open explantation high risk, creating a challenging treatment dilemma.

Conservative therapy was administered in nearly half of cases. Treatment consisted of long-term antimicrobial therapy, often culture-directed when microbiological data were available, with percutaneous drainage reported in 5 cases based on the available data. Although some patients achieved prolonged infection control, late deaths were more frequent among conservatively managed patients; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in patient selection and follow-up duration. These findings are consistent with previous EVAR infection studies, in which conservative management is generally reserved for patients with high operative risk and may be less likely to achieve definitive infection control [23-25]. Nevertheless, antibiotic therapy remains an essential component of management, particularly in patients undergoing partial explantation or graft-preserving procedures.

Notably, in our review, both patients who underwent graft-preserving surgery were alive at the last reported follow-up, suggesting that this less radical approach may be considered in selected patients, although the evidence remains limited. In Wang et al. [15], the patient underwent duodenal repair, extensive sac and retroperitoneal washout with bacitracin-containing saline, and omental coverage of the exposed graft, followed by lifelong suppressive antibiotic therapy. In the series by Campolmi et al. [19], one patient underwent sacotomy, duodenal fistula repair, and omentoplasty without graft explantation, followed by prolonged suppressive antimicrobial therapy. These findings are consistent with the broader aortic graft infection literature describing graft-preserving or less radical approaches in patients considered unfit for high-risk surgery [22,25,26].

The duration of antimicrobial therapy varied widely across studies. Among the individual case reports with a finite treatment duration, the median duration was 10 weeks (range, 6-18 weeks), whereas lifelong suppressive therapy was used in 4 patients. There is no consensus regarding the optimal duration of antimicrobial therapy. Strategies for antimicrobial management are largely based on the broader AEI literature. When infected material is completely removed and reconstruction is performed, a minimum of 6 weeks of postoperative antimicrobial therapy is generally recommended, whereas prolonged or lifelong suppressive therapy may be considered when infected prosthetic material remains or complete source control cannot be achieved [22,29,30].

The series by Campolmi et al. [19] included 11 of 26 patients and therefore had a substantial influence on the overall descriptive findings. Compared with the individual case reports, this series reported a more structured multidisciplinary approach and more frequent use of conservative management. Given its relatively large contribution to the overall cohort, this institutional weighting should be considered when interpreting treatment distribution and outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the evidence base consisted almost entirely of case reports and small case series and is therefore highly vulnerable to publication and survivorship bias. Unfavorable or fatal courses may be underreported, potentially leading to overestimation of favorable outcomes. Second, treatment allocation was non-random and strongly influenced by frailty, anatomy, operative risk, and surgeon judgment, creating substantial confounding by indication and limiting any comparison of treatment outcomes. Third, reporting was heterogeneous and incomplete, including variable details on clinical presentation, microbiology, drainage, branch management, and follow-up. Fourth, follow-up duration was highly variable and relatively short in several cases; therefore, survival at the last reported follow-up should not be interpreted as a robust estimate of long-term prognosis. Fifth, one included study was available only as a conference abstract, which provided limited patient-level clinical and outcome data and introduced additional uncertainty and potential reporting bias. Because this study contributed 6 of 26 patients, its inclusion may have influenced the descriptive estimates, particularly treatment distribution and outcomes. A formal sensitivity analysis excluding this study was not performed due to the small sample size and descriptive nature of the dataset. Finally, the inclusion of one ChEVAR case and deviations from the original PROSPERO eligibility criteria should be considered when interpreting the findings.

CONCLUSION

Infection after F/BEVAR is a severe complication that requires complex, individualized management. The reported strategies range from graft explantation and reconstruction to graft-preserving procedures and conservative antimicrobial therapy, reflecting differences in anatomy, source control, patient fitness, and operative risk. Current evidence is limited, highlighting the need for multicenter collaboration and standardized reporting to define optimal treatment strategies and improve outcomes in these high-risk patients.
