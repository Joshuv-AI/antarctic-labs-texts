The risk spillover between geopolitical risk and China's 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries
Authors: Qingqiao Huang, Bin Wang, Jing Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpillover effect refers to the phenomenon that when an economic entity, industry or market encounters a change or shock, its impact is transmitted to other entities, industries or markets. In recent years, 5G, semiconductors and rare earths have successively become geopolitical battlegrounds. In this paper, sparrow search algorithm was used to optimize the parameters of variational mode decomposition (VMD), then use the optimized VMD to decompose the geopolitical risk (GPR), 5G, semiconductor and rare earth time series data, and exploit fuzzy c-means clustering algorithm to obtain low-frequency and high-frequency time series. Finally we investigated the connectedness between GPR and China's 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries based on TVP-VAR model in different cycles, as well as between these industries. The findings reveal that there exists risk spillover between GPR and the three industries, which is significantly enhanced under the impact of geopolitical events. In addition, there is heterogeneity in the risk spillover between GPR and these three industries in different cycles. Notably, in the short term, the GPR is the main source of risk, and in the long term, the semiconductor industry has the great impact on GPR, 5G and rare earth industries, indicating that the semiconductor industry has very important geopolitical significance.Keywords: Geopolitical risk, 5G industry, Semiconductor industry, Rare earth industry, TVP-VAR, Risk spillover

Introduction

The fifth-generation mobile technology (5G) is becoming a disruptive technology capable of connecting people and machines and is critical for the development and deployment of Industry 4.0, Intelligent Transportation Systems, the Internet of Things, and Smart Cities [1]. It is projected that 5G technology will generate a global economic value of around $13.2 trillion by 2035 [2]. Due to the huge impact of 5G on the economy and society, it has sparked not only competition between companies but also geopolitical confrontation between countries. Currently, 5G technology is mainly controlled by a handful of countries, including the U.S., China, South Korea, Japan, Finland and Sweden. Given China's leading position in 5G technology, the U.S. government perceives China as posing a dual threat to its geopolitical and economic power, alleging that Chinese companies may engage in espionage through 5G networks, posing a threat to national security, and has united its allies to boycott China's 5G technology [3]. Consequently, the U.S. launched a global "technological cold war" [4]. In 2018, the U.S. blacklisted Huawei equipment and banned the use of Huawei hardware [5]. One year later, the U.S. added Huawei to the "Entity List" and banned the sale of semiconductors and other products to Huawei [6]. The geopolitical risk (GPR) has a strong negative impact on firm-level investments [7]. Changes in 5G-related policies due to geopolitical factors may prompt investors to withdraw funds or turn to other markets, which may cause instability and liquidity problems in the market.

Semiconductors are widely used in the digital economy and are a necessary foundation for it [8] and a key driver for the electronics industry as well as the military [9]. China lags behind in semiconductors, with 95 % of China's 30 largest conglomerates relying on imports for computer and server CPU-related chips [10]. In recent years, due to geopolitical influences, China has been striving to achieve self-reliance in science and technology. China's top chipmaker Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation (SMIC) made 7 nm technology breakthrough in just 2 years [11]. To strengthen the U.S. leadership in semiconductors, the U.S. President Joe Biden signed the CHIPS and Science Act (CHIPS) in August 2022 to provide huge subsidies for U.S. semiconductor production and R&D. This was followed by the cooperation among the U.S., South Korea, Japan and Taiwan, organized by the U.S. to restructure the global semiconductor supply chain in an attempt to exclude China from the supply chain [12]. China still heavily relies on foreign technology in the semiconductor field, and the threat of decoupling the current interconnected supply chain could have a significant impact on the development of Chinese semiconductors [13]. GPR has become an important source of supply chain risk for enterprises in the context of the continuous globalization of supply chains. The recent trade restrictions that the U.S. placed on China's semiconductor sector serve as an example of this [14]. Especially, the U.S. blacklisted Huawei equipment and banned the use of Huawei hardware in 2018. Hence, GPR may lead to a decline in investor confidence in the semiconductor market, which in turn triggers panic selling or investment withdrawal, leading to sharp fluctuations in stock prices and affecting market liquidity.

Rare earth elements are not only indispensable raw materials for modern high-tech industries, but also the key to achieving sustainable development goals in the "dual carbon" era, and have become a geostrategic resource [15]. China has the world's largest rare earth reserves and dominates the global rare earth market [16]. In 2010, China temporarily suspended exporting rare earths to Japan due to the China-Japan dispute over the Diaoyu Islands [17]. Afterward, China cut its rare earth export quota by 40 % and increased some of its rare earth export taxes on the grounds of protecting the environment, conserving resources and eliminating smuggling crimes [18]. In 2012, the U.S., the European Union (EU), and Japan complaint to the World Trade Organization (WTO), which ultimately ruled that China had violated the free trade agreement [19]. Rare earths could be a part of geopolitics and could become a key bargaining chip as the Sino-U.S. trade war proceeds [20]. Despite the friction between China and the U.S. has been escalating, the final list of taxable commodities released by the U.S. government excludes rare earths. Geopolitical events such as the Sino-U.S. trade war exacerbate the uncertainty in the rare earth supply chain [21]. And high GPR can impact positively on rare earth prices, highlighting its strategic and hedging value in turbulent times caused by geopolitical events [22]. To maintain the stability of rare earth prices, policymakers should set up an early warning system for hazards and a safeguard against geopolitical threats that could jeopardize rare earth security and economic activity. Investors should be likely to pay more attention to government policy changes on rare earths, including export controls, tariffs and environmental regulations, to assess potential market shocks.

Geopolitics focuses on political power and diplomatic history about economic resources and geographic space [23]. GPR refers to the risks associated with wars, terrorism, and tensions between countries, which undermine normal international relations [24]. GPR can have a significant impact on an industry or even global markets. Recent reports by the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund have expressed growing concern about GPR, which has come to be seen as a key indicator of economic success [25]. Due to the ongoing stimulation of events like anti-globalization, trade protectionism, technology blockades, COVID-19, and energy competition, GPR has continued to rise in recent years, and geopolitical issues have grown more serious. There is evidence to suggest that GPR has impacted on emerging economies seriously [26]. As the second-largest economy, China is inevitably affected by geopolitics. Specially, 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries are impacted greatly by GPR. Therefore, it is interesting to investigate the impacts of GPR on China's 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries, as well as the spillover effects between these industries. As the investment in the three strategic industries of 5G, semiconductors and rare earths is mainly driven by the decisions of the government and policy makers, the behavior of investors is affected by these decisions. Extreme geopolitical events may make policy changes, trigger panic among investors, and lead to abnormal market volatility, which in turn can affect market returns [[25], [27], [28], [29]]. As a result, investors must take precautions to lessen the negative effects of various uncertainties, and policymakers must be aware of their influence in order to regulate appropriately.

Theoretically, the information transmission process in financial markets is intricate. The mechanism of financial risk contagion is characterized as the quick transfer of risk to other institutions in the financial system (market) when a financial institution (market) is impacted, see Refs. [[30], [31]]. Morales and Andreosso-O 'Callaghan [32] pointed out that "contagion" means that the affected markets encounter the same problems as the source countries, but many countries do not face the same issues as the source country after the financial crisis. Zhou and Liu [33] considered that the traditional definition of "contagion" actually refers to "spillover effect", which is the phenomenon of high transmission risk caused by cross-market linkage. Based on this, we believe that the spillover effect refers to the phenomenon that when an economic entity, industry or market encounters certain changes or shocks, the impact is not limited to the entity or industry, but spreads or conducts to other entities, industries or markets. For example, a geopolitical event could cause a major shock to a particular industry, such as the semiconductor industry, which could further affect the performance of the 5G and rare earth industries through changes in supply chains or market confidence. Billio et al. [31] and Diebold and Yilmaz [[34], [35]] and other references used the more commonly used "connectedness" to describe the relationships between financial markets. Therefore, we use the term "spillover" and "connectedness" in the analysis below.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. Firstly, most references utilized qualitative methodologies to study the impact of geopolitical events on individual markets such as 5G, semiconductors, or rare earths [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. This paper employs a quantitative analysis method to investigate the risk spillover between GPR and China's 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries, as well as between these industries. Secondly, as the existing literature rarely explored the short- and long-term influencing factors, this paper uses variational mode decomposition (VMD) method to decompose series, and divides the long-term (low-frequency) and short-term (high-frequency) series by the fuzzy c-means clustering (FCM) algorithm to research the short- and long-term spillover effect. Finally, this paper introduces a combination of the sparrow search algorithm (SSA), VMD method, and FCM algorithm, along with dynamic connectedness method based on the time-varying parameter vector autoregression (TVP-VAR) to study the dynamic connectedness between different markets. This approach can capture the time-varying spillover effects between different markets while largely preserving the original economic information of the series.

The structure of the rest of the article is as follows: the literature review is provided in Section 2. The method is introduced in Section 3. The data description is in Section 4. The empirical analysis and robustness test are presented in Section 5. The conclusion is in Section 6.

Literature review

In recent years, the linkage between GPR and equity, energy markets has been hotly discussed. And the significant impact of GPR has been demonstrated [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46], [47]]. However, while we infer that the 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries are likely to be hit by GPR, unfortunately, there is little literature that quantitatively validates this and places these three interconnected industries in a single framework for analysis.

The emergence of 5G technology has sparked significant geopolitical pressures and also has garnered considerable attention from academics. One particular area of focus is the leadership in 5G technology, which has led to intense debates among scholars. Some studies discussed the leadership of 5G technology through 5G-related patents [[24], [38], [48], [49], [50], [51]]. Parcu et al. [48] argued that global competition should be based on owning patents related to rare technologies, rather than sheer quantity, and the U.S. is the current leader in 5G technology, followed by China, Japan, and South Korea. Rühlig and Brink [49] analyzed China's persistent efforts in international standardization of 5G from a techno-nationalist perspective, highlighting China's potential to reshape global technology standards. Some studies criticized China's 5G [[52], [53]], which reached the conclusion that the Chinese government may use 5G networks for spying on other countries. On the other hand, some scholars argued that the U.S. sees China as a threat to its national interests and hegemonic position [[3], [19]]. Triolo [54] pointed out that if the U.S.-China relations continues to deteriorate, the decades of technological cooperation and supply chain optimization will be disrupted, straining existing alliances and trade relations, and impeding global technological innovation. Numerous scholars have discussed the attitudes of countries worldwide towards China's 5G. These countries can be divided into three camps: those that support China's 5G technology (e.g., Russia, Qatar, most of Southeast Asia); those that boycott China's 5G technology (e.g., the U.S., Canada, the UK, the EU, Australia, and Japan); and those that remain neutral (e.g., Serbia) [[6], [55], [56], [57]]. Bojić et al. [55] argued that maintaining a neutral geopolitical status regarding 5G may be favorable for global balance and that the new technology will serve as a basis for multilateral dominance.

As the core of emerging information technology industries such as 5G and artificial intelligence, as well as a strategic industry for safeguarding national security and economic development, the semiconductor industry has become another focus of geopolitics. China is in the midst of a transition from relying on technology from multinational corporations to pursuing higher levels of technological autonomy. It lacks an independent voice in key semiconductor technologies [[13], [58]]. Some scholars have analyzed the reasons why China's semiconductor industry is lagging. Yu et al. [59] highlighted the lack of advanced semiconductor technology knowledge and patent has hindered Chinese companies from participating in the industry's next-generation co-licensing networks and technology alliances. Malkin and He [60] found that the U.S. imposes increasing restrictions on transactions between Chinese semiconductor firms and non-U.S. business partners through extraterritoriality, which is achieved through geo-economic tools such as export restrictions, military alliances, etc. Ren et al. [61] pointed out that China's trade in semiconductor equipment is highly dependent on Europe, U.S., Japan and South Korea, which have established exclusive innovation networks and trade bulwarks. Capri [62] pointed out that the decoupling of U.S. semiconductor firms from China's semiconductor-related industries would have a traumatic impact on the entire technology sector and change the global economic landscape. Zhang [63] argued that although decoupling may work in the short term in industries such as semiconductors, it could lead to China becoming a stronger competitor in the high-tech sector in the long term. Peters [11] revealed that Taiwan is of crucial geostrategic importance, with its TSMC enterprise being a world leader in semiconductor manufacturing, which is highly likely to deepen the U.S.-Sino conflict.

Rare earths are likely to become geopolitical levers due to geopolitical issues arising from technologies such as 5G and semiconductors. Effective rare earth mining and utilization can stimulate industrial upgrading in multiple industries and lead to the emergence of several new strategic industries, both of which are crucial for a country's development [64]. Several scholars have studied China's world leading position in the rare earth market, where China has the largest rare earth reserves and is a global leader in rare earth processing [[21], [65]]. China's leading position in the rare earth market remains undisputed, despite many political and industrial efforts by other countries and a decline in China's market share of rare earths [[66], [67]]. Geng et al. [68] noted that geopolitics have disrupted global supply chains and made prices volatile for more than a decade. Several scholars have discussed whether rare earths would be used as economic and political weapons against other states in future conflicts [[69], [70], [71]]. Klinger [71] pointed out that China, due to the geopolitical dispute over the Diaoyu Islands between China and Japan in 2010, temporarily suspended its rare earths exports to Japan, causing a global market panic and political crisis. Some scholars argued that China's near monopoly in the global rare earth market threatens to regional stability [61], while others argued that domestic concerns about resource conservation and environmental preservation drove China's 2011 rare earth export strategy [[18], [72]]. Schmid [14] emphasized that U.S. is almost entirely dependent on China for its rare earth imports and that the Sino-U.S. trade war has exacerbated uncertainties in the rare earth supply chain. These uncertainties were accentuated by COVID-19, which led to supply chain disruptions [73]. Fan et al. [36] quantitatively found that geopolitics significantly affects the trade of rare earths, with unit import prices of rare earths being positively correlated with GPR, while the total value of imports was negatively correlated with GPR, and the value of imports negative correlation seems to be strongest for rare earths from China.

The traditional efficient market hypothesis (EMH) holds that the market investors are rational and make choices based on maximizing utility. A "rational person" participating in the market activities will promptly gather all the information about the expected stock profits and respond promptly to changes in the stock price. But in real markets, investors often overreact or underreact to stock prices and fail to process information rationally [74]. Investors tend to build portfolios to reduce risk. Logically, for practical investment purposes, investors should pay closer attention to the magnitude and direction of the contribution of different asset classes in their investment portfolio to the spillover of net returns. In line with this realistic investment expectation, more and more literatures used Diebold and Yilmaz's [[34], [75]] intuitive spillover approach to study overall and net directional spillovers between different markets (assets) [76].

Research based on "connectedness" is considered to be at the heart of systemic risk measurement and financial asset management [35], in which the breadth and depth of risk contagion was determined by the degree of relevance. Many scholars have made significant contributions in connectedness. For instance the tail reliance between a particular institution and the entire financial system is measured using the conditional value at risk (CoVaR), was proposed by Adrian and Brunnermeier [77]. The change in a single firm's return rate under exceptional events is measured using the marginal expected gap (MES) was proposed by Acharya et al. [78]. The connectedness indicators aforementioned between financial systems are widely used, but it has certain drawbacks. For example, CoVaR disregards the financial system's general structural characteristics. An extreme incident does not always follow from a higher MES. One of the most widely used VAR-based methods (DY for short) is the connectedness measurement based on VAR model and generalized prediction error variance decomposition (GFEVD), proposed by Diebold and Yilmaz [[34], [35], [75]]. It can better describe the direction and intensity of risk transmission. More and more literature emphasizes the importance of connectedness methods in revealing the spillover effect of the entire network [[79], [80], [81]]. However, Antonakakis et al. [82] pointed out that the above-mentioned DY connectedness method has a high degree of subjectivity and arbitrariness when choosing the size of rolling window, which may lead to the loss of initial observations in data samples and bias in the results. Therefore, Antonakis et al. [82] proposed a time-varing financial risk spillover network analysis framework based on the TVP-VAR model and the risk spillover network construction method of Diebold and Yilmaz [35]. This method does not need adjusting the window width and hence preserves the valid information in the original sample, which makes up for the deficiency of dynamic risk spillover analysis in Ref. [35].

The above method cannot characterize the interaction between GPR and the three industries of 5G, semiconductors and rare earths on different time scales. In order to analyze the risk spillover effect in different periods, Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) can effectively separate the high-frequency and low-frequency components of financial time series, which has a certain guiding effect on the actual investment operations [[83], [84]]. However, the Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) proposed by Dragomiretskiy and Zosso [85] can decompose the original signal into several spectral domain components with specific bandwidths. Based on its excellent performance, it has been successfully applied to time series, fault detection and biomedical signal analysis [[86], [87]]. Compared with EMD and its variants, VMD has complete mathematical theory support, which can refrain from the problems of error accumulation and inefficiency caused by iterative calculation. Compared with Empirical Wavelet Transform (EWT), VMD does not need to set the boundary frequency of the modes and has a higher signal-to-noise ratio. However, two key parameters in VMD decomposition, i.e. the number of layer and penalty factor, will affect the decomposition effect. To solve this problem, an optimization algorithm can be used to select the appropriate parameter combination adaptively. Sparrow search algorithm (SSA) [88] shows certain advantages in terms of high search accuracy, fast convergence rate and strong stability. It can effectively avoid getting stuck in local optimal problems, and overcome the drawbacks of methods such as particle swarm optimization and whale optimization algorithm. Therefore, this paper chooses SSA algorithm for optimization.

Methodology

Like [89], we utilize the SSA to optimize the parameters of VMD algorithm, then decompose GPR, 5G, semiconductor and rare earth time series data by the optimized VMD, and exploit FCM algorithm to obtain low-frequency and high-frequency time series, according to Ref. [74]. Finally we investigates the dynamic connectedness between GPR and China's 5G, semiconductor, rare earth industries based on TVP-VAR model in different cycles, as well as between these industries.

Data

Caldara and Iacoviello [24] constructed a GPR index, which is a measure of adverse geopolitical events and relative risks based on newspaper articles reporting geopolitical tensions. It is closely related to military conflicts between countries, nuclear tensions, threats of war, and threats of terrorism, and it is a reliable indicator for assessing international tensions [90]. We used the global GPR data downloaded from https://www.matteoiacoviello.com/gpr.htm on November 6, 2023.

The CSI 5G Industry 100 Index (No. 931491) represents the 5G industry variable (5G), which selects 100 securities of listed companies in the 5G industry chain from the Shanghai and Shenzhen markets as index samples, including but not limited to 5G network construction, communication equipment materials, terminal equipment and application scenarios and other subdivisions to reflect the overall performance of listed companies in 5G industrial chain in Shanghai and Shenzhen markets. The CSI Semiconductor Industry Index (No.931865) represents the semiconductor industry variable (SE), which selects no more than 40 securities of listed companies whose business involves semiconductor materials, equipment and applications as index samples from the listed companies in Shanghai and Shenzhen markets to reflect the overall performance of securities of listed companies in the semiconductor core industry. The CSI Rare Earth Industry Index (No. 930598) represents rare earth industry variable (RE) is represented by, which takes the securities of listed companies involved in rare earth mining, processing, trade and application as samples to reflect the overall performance of the securities of listed companies in the rare earth industry. The data for the 5G, SE and RE variables are obtained from the official website of CSI (https://www.csindex.com.cn/#/). Note that China Securities Index Co., Ltd. (CSI), a financial market index provider, was jointly funded in August 2005 by China's two major stock exchanges, the Shanghai Stock Exchange and the Shenzhen Stock Exchange (https://www.csindex.com.cn/#/about/overview).

For these indices, the CSI Semiconductor Industry Index started the latest, on January 3, 2017. Therefore, in order to ensure the synchronization of time, this paper selects the daily data from January 3, 2017 to October 30, 2023, and excludes the data of non-common days to obtain 1656 sets of sample data. This time period includes major events such as the U.S.-China trade war, COVID-19, and the Russia-Ukraine conflict. The data are processed by first-order difference, Rit=lnTit−lnTit−1, where Tit and Tit−1 correspond to the data of the i variable on time t and time t−1, respectively.

Empirical results

SSA-VMD

The response of financial and economic systems to exogenous shocks of different time frequencies is uneven [91]. Low-frequency modes seize long-term trends in time-series fluctuations, while high-frequency modes have a significant impact on short-term fluctuations [92]. It is important to distinguish between short- and long-term risk transmission paths, where the short-term ones are related to investors' short-term decision-making behavior, while the long-term ones are related to policymakers' significant long-term decisions [93]. However, medium-term spillovers are the transition between short- and long-term spillovers, with blurred boundaries and without specific economic significance [94].

In order to examine the systematic risk spillover between GPR and the 5G industry, semiconductor industry, and rare earth industry, and to analyze the short-term and long-term influencing factors, this paper uses the VMD algorithm to decompose the variables and then reconstructs them using the FCM algorithm [95], resulting in long-term and short-term series. However, it should be noted that the number of decomposition and the penalty factor of the VMD algorithm have a great influence on the decomposition effect. To minimize subjective bias, this paper employs the SSA algorithm to optimize the parameters in the VMD algorithm. We choose the minimum envelope entropy as the fitness objective function to improve the accuracy of VMD results. The SSA parameters are set as follows: the maximum number of iterations is 30, and the population size is 10. Let (K, α) be the optimal parameters obtained after VMD optimization by SSA. Descriptive statistics

This paper examines the risk spillover effects of 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries under the influence of GPR from the perspective of dynamic connectedness. At the same time, it investigates the differential performance of spillover effects in different cycles to determine whether the industry's risk spillover mechanism is mainly driven by short-term or long-term events.

Firstly, according to the BIC criterion, the optimal lag orders for the original, short-term and long-term return series are 1, 8 and 10, respectively. The TCI indices in Table 4 suggests that there is some correlation between GPR, 5G industry, semiconductor industry and rare earth industry, and there is a risk contagion mechanism. Observed from the original return series, the TCI is 38.5 %, indicating that 38.5 % of the shocks come from the interactions among system members. The GPR's own shock drive is the largest at 96.7 %, and the remaining 3.3 % comes from the system's drive. The 5G industry contributes the most to the semiconductor industry's spillovers at 34.2 %, while the semiconductor industry also contributed the most to the spillover of the 5G industry at 32.7 %. This indicates that there are strong bidirectional spillover effects between the 5G industry and the semiconductor industry, indicating that the 5G industry and the semiconductor industry have a close linkage. In addition, the spillover contribution of GPR to the three industries does not differ much. From the values of "TO" and "FROM", it can be seen that 5G and semiconductor industries are both the main transmitters and receivers of spillover effects. From the value of "NET", it can be seen that the rare earth industry generates negative spillover effects, indicating its vulnerability and the impact of geopolitical instability.

Spillover effects over different periods are discussed next. Both short-term and long-term TCIs have increased relative to the original return series, indicating an increased short- and long-term correlation and increased risk between the GPR, 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth sectors. In addition, there is little difference between the short-term and long-term TCIs, suggesting that the impact of major events on the original return series is dominated by a combination of short-term and long-term spillover factors. Overall, the GPR generates fewer own shocks in the short and long term, with more shocks spreading to other members of the system. In addition, GPR generates larger short- and long-term return spillovers than original return spillovers for the 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries, especially in the short term. In the short term, GPR is the largest net transmitter of spillovers and the semiconductor industry is the largest net recipient. However, in the long term GPR becomes the largest net receiver and the semiconductor industry becomes the largest net transmitter, and it is the only net transmitter.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the connectedness between system members during geopolitical events, it is of vital importance to consider the time-varying characteristics of spillovers. Fig. 2 illustrates the dynamic total connectedness between the GPR, 5G industry, semiconductor industry, and rare earth industry gains under different cycles. During geopolitical events, there have been significant changes in the connectedness of the entire system. During the onset of the Sino-U.S. trade war in 2018, COVID-19 in 2020, and the Russia-Ukraine war in 2022, the TCI shows an upward trend. This indicates that the connectedness between the three industries strengthens, leading to expanded channels of risk spillovers. Additionally, it highlights the significant impact of the GPR on these industries. In 2018, the United States levied tariffs on goods from China, frictions continued to escalate, leading to the Sino-U.S. trade war broke out, and TCI increased. In October 2019, substantial progress in Sino-U.S. negotiations lead to the suspension of tariffs and the easing of trade tensions, causing a decline in TCI values. The COVID-19 pandemic has impacted heavily on three industries, leading to a sharp rise in the TCI. As China's experience in responding to the epidemic continues to accumulate, the epidemic has been effectively controlled and relevant economic and regulatory policies were operated effectively, the TCI has shown a downward trend. The TCI of the original series showed a minimum value in early 2022, and the TCI that fell to the lowest point rose again in February 2022 when the Russia-Ukraine conflict broke out. This conflict impacted the promotion of 5G, the sales of semiconductors, and the supply, demand, and prices of rare earths in China. Furthermore, the short-term TCI shows more frequent fluctuations, indicating that major events have a sustained impact on the 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries. On the other hand, the long-term TCI more clearly demonstrates the impact of geopolitical events on these three industries. Overall, the connectedness of these industries increases significantly during major events and gradually decreases when the geopolitical situation becomes more stable.

Dynamic total connectedness.

Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5 show the net spillover effects of GPR, 5G industry, semiconductor industry and rare earth industry. The net spillover effects of the short-term and long-term return series are more pronounced than that of the original series. Since the members of the system are interconnected, the net spillover index has been constantly changing, with the GPR and the three industries shifting roles between receiver and transmitter. After the outbreak of the Sino-U.S. trade war, the semiconductor industry is a net receiver of short-term spillovers but a net transmitter of long-term spillovers. The semiconductor industry has suffered from export restrictions and technology blockades, thereby bearing the spillover of risks in the short term. Companies face the challenges of unavailability of technology and market uncertainty, hitting their profit and growth expectations. However, with the development and gradual maturity of domestic technology, this industry may pass risks to the global semiconductor market in the long term, especially in competition with foreign manufacturers. The rare earth industry is a net transmitter of short-term spillovers but a net recipient of long-term spillovers. Relatively speaking, China's rare earth industry passed the risk to the outside world through export restrictions and price manipulation in the early days of the trade war. This has led to market volatility in countries dependent on rare earth resources, creating a pass-through effect of short-term risk spillovers. Although it has the ability to pass on risks in the short term, in the long run, the rare earth industry may encounter technological substitution and intensified market competition, and eventually become a recipient of risks. After the outbreak of COVID-19, GPR is a net transmitter of short-term spillovers but a net recipient of long-term spillovers; the semiconductor industry is a net recipient of net receiver of short-term spillovers and a net transmitter of long-term spillovers. Geopolitical risks spread rapidly in the short term, becoming a net carrier of risk spillovers. Competition among countries for medical resources and industrial chains has intensified, rapidly affecting the global supply chain and forming short-term instability. In the longer term, as countries adjust to the new normal, the risks posed by the pandemic may also turn into a global burden to be shared, the 5G industry is a net receiver of long-term spillovers. The rare earth industry is a net recipient of long-term spillovers, but shifts from a net receiver to a net transmitter in short-term spillovers. In addition, the short-term spillovers from the 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries reached unprecedented levels at the beginning of the outbreak. After the outbreak of the Russia-Ukraine war, the roles played by GPR, the 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries in the short- and long-term spillovers are largely consistent with those during the previous epidemic.

As can be seen from Fig. 7, GPR has a greater impact on the 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries in the short term, i.e., GPR is a major source of systemic risk. As a world leader in 5G technology and rare earths, as well as the world's largest semiconductor market, China's 5G, semiconductor and rare earths industries have attracted the attention of many investors from China and other countries, and investment and financing activities have been relatively active. However, China's 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries are also inevitably in the geopolitical spotlight. During geopolitical events, negative market expectations, panic and biased investment behavior often lead to the spread of market risks [[97], [98]]. Short-term spillovers often arise from investors' short-term investment behaviors. The occurrence of geopolitical events, such as the Russia-Ukraine war, has led to a heightened sense of panic among investors. Consequently, this has further intensified their pessimistic outlook on China's 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries. As a result, the spillover effects between geopolitical events and all three industries have increased. Geopolitical risks often cause market unrest and turbulence, and investors usually adopt a cautious attitude in the face of uncertainty, delaying investment and other behaviors. Such psychological changes cannot be ignored, especially in the technology industry, where a calm assessment of market trends and dynamic changes is crucial to the development of enterprises. In the short term, market volatility may trigger broader financial risks and create systemic challenges.

Fig. 8 shows the long-term net pairwise spillovers. It can be seen that the connectedness between GPR and the 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries has increased significantly since the outbreak of COVID-19. The connectedness between GPR and three industries remains strong in 2022 and 2023 under multiple geopolitical contexts such as COVID-19 and Russia-Ukraine war. The epidemic has significant impacts on three industries. On the one hand, the shutdown and trade restrictions during the epidemic may lead to problems such as industrial chain disruption in the three industries, and on the other hand, the epidemic has accelerated the process of Industry 4.0, and the demand for 5G equipment, semiconductors and rare earths has risen sharply, thus exacerbating the instability of the three industries. In 2021, the spillover effects between GPR and the semiconductor industry rose sharply, which may be due to the severe shortage of global chips due to geopolitical events such as the controversy over the source of the epidemic [99]. Meanwhile, the spillovers between GPR and the rare earth industry have also risen sharply. This may be due to the Chinese government's merger of three rare earth state-owned enterprises into China Rare Earth Group to increase its pricing power and production efficiency [100], deepening geopolitical anxieties in many countries. With the escalation of Russia-Ukraine war, U.S. allies other countries in NATO have imposed severe sanctions on Russia, the global semiconductor industry chain has been affected, China's semiconductor industry will inevitably be affected, and China's 5G industry has also been affected, such as China's 5G mobile phone exports have been halved. In addition, due to the irreplaceable nature of rare earths in the high-tech and defense fields, China's rare earth industry has also been affected to a certain extent [[44], [96]].

It can also be seen from Fig. 8 that there is a strong connectedness between semiconductor and 5G industries, semiconductor and rare earth industries in the long-term, indicating that the semiconductor industry has a great impact on both 5G and rare earth industries, which is due to that semiconductors are the key components of 5G equipment, and rare earths are indispensable raw materials for semiconductors. In addition, the semiconductor industry has the greatest impact on GPR among the three industries, and the connectedness between the semiconductor industry and GPR is the strongest, indicating that the semiconductor industry has very important geopolitical significance. As China is leading in 5G technology, the U.S. has severely banned Chinese companies such as Huawei from purchasing its semiconductor products. In addition, Taiwan is the world's largest producer of advanced chips, and it is also one of the world's most dangerous geopolitical flashpoints. In August 2022, the U.S. Speaker of the House of Representatives Nancy Pelosi visited Taiwan, and Taiwan's leader, Tsai Ing-wen, expressed her willingness to co-operate in building a sustainable supply chain for her so-called "democracy chip" [101]. A few days later, Biden signed the "CHIPS", promising subsidies to TSMC, and cooperation in the new Chip 4 alliance began in earnest [11]. In August 2023, Biden signed an executive order restricting U.S. entities from investing in Chinese semiconductors and microelectronics, quantum information technology, and artificial intelligence [102]. Policies related to these geopolitical events may be an important reason for exacerbating spillovers in the semiconductor industry. In summary, long-term spillovers are related to policies formulated by policymakers and spread gradually through the industrial chain.

Robustness tests

Robustness test is conducted by changing the range of advance prediction step H of the generalized prediction error variance decomposition [26]. To ensure the robustness of the empirical results, we set the advance prediction step H of the GFEVD to 15 and 20 for testing, and the results are shown in Figures A1 and A2in the Appendix. The empirical results after the test are largely consistent with those before the test. The results of the study similarly show that the connectedness between the whole system and system members rises significantly in the presence of geopolitical events. In addition, there are differences in the connectedness between GPR and different industries under different cycles. GPR is the main source of systemic risk in the short term. The semiconductor industry has the largest impact on GPR among the three industries in the long term, and there is strong connectedness between semiconductors and the 5G industry, as well as the rare earth industry. Therefore, the empirical results are robust.

Conclusion

Spillover effect refers to the phenomenon that when an economic entity, industry or market encounters a change or shock, its impact is transmitted to other entities, industries or markets. This paper studies the risk spillover between GPR and China's 5G, semiconductor, and rare earth industries by combining the SSA algorithm, the VMD method, and TVP-VAR connectedness approach. The results show that, firstly, there is some risk spillover between GPR, the 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries. Secondly, the risk spillover between the whole system and system members rises significantly under the impact of geopolitical events, while the risk spillover decreases gradually when the geopolitical situation is more stable. Furthermore, there is heterogeneity in the risk spillover between GPR and different industries under different cycles. Finally, GPR has a greater impact on the 5G, semiconductor and rare earth industries in the short term, i.e., GPR is the main source of systemic risk. The semiconductor industry has the greatest impact on GPR among the three industries in the long term, and the connectedness between the semiconductor industry and GPR is the strongest, suggesting that the semiconductor industry is of great geopolitical importance. In addition, there is strong connectedness between the semiconductor and 5G industries and the semiconductor and rare earth industries in the long term, suggesting that the semiconductor industry has a large impact on both the 5G and rare earth industries.

Our findings have implications for policymakers and investors in some respects. First, given the linkages between GPR and the three industries as well as between industries, Chinese policymakers and investors should not focus only on a single industry, especially during geopolitical events, but should also focus on related industries with significant spillover relationships. Furthermore, there are differences in the connectedness between GPR and different industries under different cycles, and investors should prudently assess the direct and indirect risks that GPR brings to the three industries. Policymakers should reasonably judge the impact of geopolitical events and formulate corresponding risk prevention and response decisions for different industries. For example, for 5G, we should promote the establishment of an international consensus on 5G security and strengthen the construction of security trust mechanism.
