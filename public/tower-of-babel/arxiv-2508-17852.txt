Abstract
Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) with energy harvesting (EH) are expected to play a vital role in intelligent 6 6 G systems, particularly in applications such as industrial sensing and control, where continuous operations and sustainable energy management are essential. Given their limited energy resources, WSNs require energy-efficient operations to maintain long-term performance. Their deployment in real-world scenarios is challenged by highly dynamic environments, where EH conditions, network scale, and arrival data rate changes over time. In this work, we consider system dynamics that yield different learning tasks, where the space of decision variables remains fixed but the strategies are variable, and learning domains, where both the decision space and the strategies evolve over time. A scenario in which a design based on the cross-domain lifelong reinforcement learning (CD-L2RL) framework is required. More specifically, we focus on an energy-efficient WSN design under changing EH conditions and network scale. Towards this, we propose a CD-L2RL algorithm that leverages prior experience to accelerate adaptation across multiple tasks and domains. Unlike conventional methods, such as modeling the system with Markov decision process (MDP) or Lyapunov optimization solution, which often assume fixed or slowly changing environments, our solution exhibits rapid policy adaptation by reusing knowledge from previously encountered tasks and domains that ensure continuous operations. We validate the effectiveness of our approach through extensive simulations under different environmental settings. The results show that the proposed solution enables faster adaptations up to 35 % 35\% compared to the standard reinforcement learning (RL) method and up to 70 % 70\% compared to the Lyapunov-based optimization methods. It also achieves a higher total amount of harvested energy, demonstrating strong potential for deployment in dynamic 6 6 G WSNs .

Multi-task learning
assumes that all tasks come from the same domain, i.e., they share common state-action space structures, where domains are higher-level abstraction that group together multiple structurally related tasks.
It focuses on jointly learning policies for these related tasks to improve generalization.
Typically, task-specific policies are expressed as combinations of shared basis components drawn from a global knowledge source

In contrast,

These tasks may have different state or action space dimensions and cannot be represented in a shared input-output space directly.

31

This projection mechanism enables multi-task learning between structurally dissimilar tasks.
In both paradigms, it is crucial that each task retains its own reward function to reflect distinct operational objectives, especially in cross-domain settings where environmental and structural variations are more substantial.

This approach ensures fast convergence, stable control, and robust performance under dynamic

This work frames dynamic

This enables continual adaptation and warm-start over tasks and domains without retraining from scratch.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to realize

We assume that the communication systems follow queuing models under channel dynamics and limited communication resources.
For any

III
Optimization-Based Solution

Although classical
MDP
-based methods can theoretically handle stochastic control problems, they suffer from high computational complexity in large state spaces, making them impractical for real-time operations in large-scale networks.
To address these challenges, we adopt the Lyapunov optimization, a powerful framework for online control in stochastic systems with time-averaged constraints
[
14
]
.
This method provides a low-complexity approach that does not require prior knowledge of system statistics and is well-suited to the energy management needs of dynamic
WSNs
.

To handle time-averaged constraints, we set virtual queues, then define a concatenated vector of the queues as
\Theta_{t}=[q_{0,t},\ldots,q_{N,t}]
.
Following that, we define the Lyapunov function to measure the congestion in these queues as follows

L(\Theta_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}q_{n,t}^{2}.

(6)

Then, the Lyapunov drift can be obtained as
\Delta\Theta_{t}=\mathbb{E}\Biggl\{L[\Theta_{t+1}]-L[\Theta_{t}]\Biggl|\Theta_{t}\Biggl\}
.
According to the Lyapunov optimization theory
[
3
]
, enforcing constraints (
5h
) and (
5d
) is equivalent to minimizing the drift
\Delta\Theta_{t}
, and optimizing the objective function with the constraints is equivalent to optimizing the drift-plus-penalty defined as
\Delta\Theta_{t}-\beta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{N}p^{j}_{n,t}\alpha^{j}_{n,t}\bigg|\Theta_{t}\bigg]
, where the parameter
\beta\geq 0
stands for the penalty weight, which represents the importance of the objective function compared to the constraints at every time slot.
Therefore, the optimization problem (
5
) can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\underset{\left(p_{0}^{j},\alpha_{n}^{\prime j},\alpha_{0}^{j},\alpha_{n}^{j}\right)_{t}}{\text{min}}\penalty

\displaystyle\Delta\Theta_{t}-\beta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n,t}^{j}\alpha_{n,t}^{j}\bigg|\Theta_{t}\bigg]

(7a)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\penalty

\displaystyle\eqref{eqn:time_constraint_1},\eqref{eqn:time_constraint_2},\eqref{eqn:queuing_stability_constraint},\eqref{eqn:Battery_capacity_constraint},\eqref{eqn:TX1_Power_Constraint},\eqref{eqn:TXn_Power_Constraint},\eqref{eqn:queue_dynamics_constraint},\eqref{eqn:queue_dynamics},\eqref{eqn:battery_queue_dynamics}.

According to (
6
) and the Lyapunov drift, the objective function in (
7
) depends on variables at time slot
t+1
.
Therefore, the original problem must be reformulated as a sequence of per-slot optimization problems, each solved at time
t
, to obtain the solution iteratively.
Although the exact drift-plus-penalty expression involves variables at time slot
t+1
, it can be upper bounded using only the variables at time
t
.
Deriving such an upper bound simplifies the optimization problem.
To this end, we compute an upper bound for the term
q_{n,t+1}^{2}-q_{n,t}^{2}
as follows

\displaystyle q_{n,t+1}^{2}-q_{n,t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\max\left\{q_{n,t}-d_{n,t},0\right\}+a_{n,t}\right)^{2}-q_{n,t}^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle\leq d_{n,t}^{2}+a_{n,t}^{2}+2q_{n,t}(a_{n,t}-d_{n,t})

\displaystyle\leq(1-2\varrho)d_{n,t}^{2}+A^{2}+2\varrho A,

where
\varrho
refers the capacity of data buffers, and
A
is the maximum arrival data.
From (
8
), the upper bound of the drift-plus-penalty can be calculated as follows

\displaystyle\Delta\Theta_{t}-\beta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{N}p^{j}_{n,t}\alpha^{j}_{n,t}\bigg|\Theta_{t}\bigg]\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}(1-2\varrho)d_{n,t}^{2}+A^{2}+2\varrho A-\beta p^{j}_{n,t}\alpha^{j}_{n,t}.

(9)

From (
9
), we observe that the upper bound consists of both variable-dependent terms and constants.
To design a tractable control policy, we focus only on minimizing the terms that depend on the current decision variables at time slot
t
, namely
\sum_{n=0}^{N}(1-2\varrho)d_{n,t}^{2}-\beta p^{j}_{n,t}\alpha^{j}_{n,t}
.
The remaining terms such as
A^{2}
and
2\varrho A
are constant with respect to the optimization variables and can therefore be omitted from the per-slot problem.
This leads to the following simplified optimization problem that guides the control decisions at each time slot

\displaystyle\underset{\left(p^{j}_{0,t},{\alpha^{\prime}}^{j}_{n,t},\alpha^{j}_{0,t},\alpha^{j}_{n,t}\right)_{t}}{\text{min}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}(1-2\varrho)d_{n,t}^{2}-\beta p^{j}_{n,t}\alpha^{j}_{n,t}

(10a)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\penalty\ \eqref{eqn:time_constraint_1},\eqref{eqn:time_constraint_2},\eqref{eqn:queuing_stability_constraint},\eqref{eqn:Battery_capacity_constraint},\eqref{eqn:TX1_Power_Constraint},\eqref{eqn:queue_dynamics_constraint},\eqref{eqn:queue_dynamics},\eqref{eqn:battery_queue_dynamics}.

From (
10
), we can affirm that the optimization problem is non-convex due to two key factors.
First, the data transmission term
d_{n,t}
defined in (
2
) is concave, but its square

d_{n,t}^{2}
appears in the objective, making it a non-convex composition.
Second, the term
p^{j}_{n,t}\alpha^{j}_{n,t}
is bilinear, which is inherently non-convex.
Therefore, standard convex optimization techniques are not directly applicable.
It is important to emphasize that the original long-term stochastic optimization problem has been decoupled via the Lyapunov optimization framework into a sequence of per-time-slot problems.
While Eq. (
10
) is written in a generic form for all
t
, it serves as a decision-making template that is instantiated and solved separately at each time slot using the current system state.
At each time
t
, the observed queue states, battery levels, and channel conditions are substituted into Eq. (
10
), producing a concrete, time-indexed instance of the optimization problem.
Solving this problem yields the optimal control decisions (e.g., power and time allocations) for that specific slot.
Because the system state evolves dynamically due to stochastic arrivals and fading, this process must be repeated at every time slot to ensure responsiveness to changing conditions.
While convex relaxation techniques may be employed to approximate a solution
[
14
,
32
]
, in this paper we adopt an alternating optimization technique
[
33
]
to iteratively solve each per-slot problem.
The full procedure is presented in Algorithm

Reinforcement Learning-Based Solution

Due to the absence of reliable models in dynamic wireless sensor environments, we adopt a model-free
RL

[

,
7
,
22
,
23
]

This is particularly suitable for our setting, where transition dynamics and reward functions evolve over time.
To address the need for fine-grained control over continuous-valued actions such as power and time allocations, we employ

IV-A

MDP
Formulation

We model the decision-making problem in dynamic
WSNs
as a
MDP
, formally defined as a tuple
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma\rangle

[
35
]
, where

•

State space

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{6N}
: The system state at the
t
th time step includes, for each
\text{TX}_{n}
, the queue length, battery level, communication channel gain, and
EH
channel gain.
This results in a
6N
-dimensional state vector as
\bm{s}_{t}=[q_{0,t},\dots,q_{N,t},b_{1,t},\dots,b_{N,t},h_{0,t},\dots,h_{N,t},h^{\prime}_{1,t},\dots,h^{\prime}_{N,t}]
.

•

Action space

\mathcal{A}
: Contains all possible actions where an action
\bm{a}=[p_{0},\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{N},\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{N}]\in\mathcal{A}
defines the decision variables, where
\alpha_{n}
is the fraction of each time slot used for
(TX_{n},RX_{n})
data transmission,
\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
is the fraction of each time slot used for
(TX_{n},RX_{n})

EH
process, and
p_{0}
is the transmission power of
\text{TX}_{0}
.

•

Transition function

\mathcal{P}(\bm{s}^{\prime}|\bm{s},\bm{a})
: Defines the probability distribution over the next state
\bm{s}^{\prime}
, conditioned on the current state
\bm{s}
and action
\bm{a}
. Transitions are governed by queue dynamics, battery updates, and stochastic channel variations.

•

Reward function

\mathcal{R}(\bm{s},\bm{a})
: The reward function is based on the negative of the objective (
5
), penalizing higher energy consumption, augmented with penalty terms to account for the constraints, satisfying queueing stability. The optimization problem (
5
) enforces hard constraints, while the
RL
reward penalizes constraint violations, allowing temporary violations during learning. The reward function is given as
\mathcal{R}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t},\bm{a}^{j}_{t})=-\sum_{n=0}^{N}p^{j}_{n,t}\alpha^{j}_{n,t}-\nu\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(b^{j}_{t}-B)+\sum_{n=0}^{N}(q^{j}_{n,t}-d^{j}_{n,t})\bigg)

\nu

In a more general dual formulation, each constraint would have its own penalty coefficient (i.e., dual variable), reflecting its individual impact on the objective.
In our simulations, we adopt a unified coefficient
\nu
as a design simplification that empirically balances these trade-offs effectively.

•

Discount factor

\gamma\in[0,1]
: Models the trade-off between immediate and future rewards. We typically use
\gamma\approx 1
to prioritize long-term performance.

This MDP formulation provides a structured framework for applying
RL
to learn adaptive control strategies under uncertain and time-varying conditions in
WSNs
.
Let
S_{d}
and
A_{d}
be the dimensionality of the state space and the action space, respectively.

IV-B

Training Algorithm

As this work involves continuous state and action spaces, we adopt a
PG
method to directly optimize a parameterized control policy via gradient ascent
[
34
]
.
Specifically, we define a stochastic policy
\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{j}_{t}\mid\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
that maps states to distributions over actions, where
\bm{\theta}
denotes the vector of trainable parameters.
The objective is to maximize the expected cumulative reward over trajectories
[
36
]

J(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}\mathcal{R}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t},\bm{a}^{j}_{t})\right],

(11)

where
\tau=(\bm{s}^{j}_{0},\bm{a}^{j}_{0},\dots,\bm{s}^{j}_{T},\bm{a}^{j}_{T})
is a
trajectory
, i.e., a sequence of states and actions experienced by the agent when interacting with the environment under policy
\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}}
over a finite horizon
T
.
We model both the policy
\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{j}_{t}\mid\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
and the state-value function
V_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
using
neural networks (NNs)
, parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\phi}
, respectively.
These networks are trained using
PG
optimization methods, as described below.
Let
N_{d}
the number of layers in the neural networks.
To optimize this objective, we apply the
PG
theorem
[
37
]
, which gives

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{j}_{t}\mid\bm{s}^{j}_{t})G_{t}\right],

(12)

where
G_{t}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\mathcal{R}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t^{\prime}},\bm{a}^{j}_{t^{\prime}})
is the empirical return from time
t
onward.
Using raw returns introduces high variance into the gradient estimates.
To address this, we define the advantage function
[
38
]
as
A(\bm{s}^{j}_{t},\bm{a}^{j}_{t})=G_{t}-V(\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
, where
V(\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
is the state-value function learned by a critic network.
Substituting this into the gradient yields a variance-reduced estimator, where
G_{t}
will be replaced by
A(\bm{s}^{j}_{t},\bm{a}^{j}_{t})
.
This leads to an actor-critic architecture, where the actor updates the policy using gradients and the critic estimates the value function for advantage computation.
The advantage is computed using the temporal difference (TD) error
[
39
]

A(\bm{s}^{j}_{t},\bm{a}^{j}_{t})=\mathcal{R}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t},\bm{a}^{j}_{t})+\gamma V(\bm{s}^{j}_{t+1})-V(\bm{s}^{j}_{t}).

(13)

As discussed above, we first defined the policy gradient objective, then introduced the baseline to reduce variance, and finally adopted the
temporal-difference (TD)
-based advantage used in actor-critic training.
To handle continuous control variables such as transmission power and time allocation, we model the policy using a Gaussian distribution
[
34
]
as
\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{j}_{t}\mid\bm{s}^{j}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t}),\Sigma_{\bm{\theta}}),
where
\mu_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
and
\Sigma_{\bm{\theta}}
are the mean and covariance of the action distribution, parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
.
The policy parameters are updated using stochastic gradient ascent as
\bm{\theta}\leftarrow\bm{\theta}+\alpha\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J(\bm{\theta})
, where
\alpha
is the learning rate.
We denote the parameters of the value function (critic) as
\bm{\phi}
, and the policy (actor) parameters as
\bm{\theta}
.
The critic estimates the state-value function
V_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
for computing advantages.
The critic is trained by minimizing the squared error between predicted and empirical returns as
\bm{\phi}\leftarrow\bm{\phi}-\wp\nabla_{\bm{\phi}}\left(V_{\bm{\phi}}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t})-G_{t}\right)^{2}
, where
\wp
is the critic learning rate.

Proposed Solution based on A Cross-Domain Lifelong Reinforcement Learning

The overarching objective of this work is to develop a control framework that adaptively optimizes energy and time scheduling policies in
WSNs
deployed in dynamic and heterogeneous environments.
These environments evolve over time due to variations in external factors such as
EH
efficiency, wireless channel conditions, and data traffic loads.
This inherent variability gives rise to a sequence of learning problems, each characterized by a specific combination of system parameters and control requirements, for example, a high data arrival rate with low
EH
efficiency, or a low-latency requirement under severe channel fading.
Such combinations impact how energy and transmission decisions must be made to maintain stable and efficient network operations.
To address this diversity in a structured way, we distinguish between two key concepts:
tasks
and
domains
.

Definition 1
.

A
task
refers to a single learning problem under a fixed environmental configuration. Formally, a task is modeled as a
MDP
, defined by the tuple
\mathcal{F}^{j}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}^{j},\mathcal{R}^{j},\gamma^{j}\rangle
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
.
Each task encapsulates a stationary system behavior over a period and defines a unique objective through its reward function and transition dynamics.

Definition 2
.

A
domain
is a higher-level abstraction that groups together multiple structurally related tasks.
Tasks within the same domain share consistent input and output representations (i.e., the same dimensions for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
) but may differ in specific parameters such as
EH
conditions, channel profiles, or arrival rates.
A domain thus captures a family of
MDPs
that operate under similar structural assumptions but distinct environmental regimes.
For instance, a domain could correspond to a class of
WSN
deployments characterized by shared topologies and action constraints but different
EH
efficiencies or communication noise levels.

Traditional optimization and
RL
techniques typically focus on single-task or single-domain scenarios, limiting their ability to generalize or adapt to changing conditions.
To overcome this, we propose a
CD-L2RL
framework, which integrates ideas from both multi-task and cross-domain learning.
While the proposed solution is grounded in the
MDP
framework, it mitigates high-dimensional complexity by leveraging shared latent structures and sparse policy representations.
This enables efficient transfer across tasks and domains, avoiding the scalability limitations of classical
MDP
approaches.
The proposed method partitions the overall learning problem into a set of domains, each containing a sequence of tasks.
Within a domain, task-specific policies are learned as sparse combinations of shared components, enabling efficient intra-domain knowledge transfer.
Across domains, where state and action spaces may vary, transfer is achieved through domain-specific projection matrices that align heterogeneous tasks with a shared latent representation (i.e., an abstract feature space that captures common structure across tasks).
The
CD-L2RL
framework is designed not only to optimize policies within individual tasks but also to accelerate adaptation in new domains by leveraging previously acquired knowledge.
The following subsections describe the knowledge transfer mechanism and the architectural components that enable this continual and adaptive learning process.

V-A

Cross-Domain Knowledge Transfer Mechanism

To enable
lifelong reinforcement learning (L2RL)
across diverse operational regimes, our framework employs a hierarchical knowledge structure that facilitates efficient policy transfer across task domains.
Specifically, we assume a sequence of domains
\mathcal{C}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}^{K}
, where each domain
\mathcal{C}^{k}
is defined by a shared state space
\mathcal{S}^{k}
and action space
\mathcal{A}^{k}
, along with consistent input-output representations.
Within each domain, the agent encounters a set of tasks
\mathcal{F}^{j},\ j\in\{1,\cdots,M\}
, each corresponding to a stationary
MDP
characterized by domain-consistent representations but differing in reward functions or environmental parameters (e.g.,
EH
conditions, arrival rates).The goal is to learn a set of optimal task-specific policies

\bm{\Pi}^{\star}=\left\{\bm{\pi}^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{\text{opt}}^{j}}\right\}_{j=1}^{M},\quad\text{with parameters}\quad\bm{\Theta}^{\star}=\left\{\bm{\theta}_{\text{opt}}^{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}.

Within each domain
\mathcal{C}^{k}
, all tasks share a common policy parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{K_{d}}
, where
K_{d}=|\mathcal{S}^{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{A}^{k}|
.
To promote intra-domain knowledge reuse, reusing knowledge between tasks that belong to the same domain, we assume that policy parameters for task
j\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
are generated via a sparse linear combination over a domain-specific basis matrix
\bm{G}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{d}\times r}
, where
r
denotes the number of latent basis components used to represent task-specific policies within each domain
\mathcal{C}^{k}
.
The linear combination over a domain-specific basis matrix is defined as follows

\bm{\theta}^{j}=\bm{G}^{k}\bm{v}^{j},\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{v}^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},

(14)

where
\bm{v}^{j}
is a sparse coefficient vector encoding the contribution of each latent basis to the task’s policy.
Collecting all coefficient vectors in domain
\mathcal{C}^{k}
gives
\bm{V}^{(j\in\mathcal{C}^{k})}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times|\mathcal{C}^{k}|}
.
There is no relation between the domain-specific basis matrix
\bm{G}^{k}
and the empirical return
G
.
There is no relation between coefficient vectors
\bm{V}^{(j\in\mathcal{C}^{k})}
and the value functions
V
.

To enable transfer across domains with different input-output structures, we introduce a global shared knowledge repository
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
that contains
r
latent components applicable across all domains.
The dimensionality
d
defines the size of the shared latent space used for cross-domain knowledge transfer.
The matrix
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
serves as a global knowledge base consisting of
r
latent components, each of dimension
d
.
Each domain
\mathcal{C}^{k}
is equipped with a projection matrix
\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{d}\times d}
that maps the global latent space to the domain-specific policy space.
This yields the decomposition
\bm{G}^{k}=\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}
.
Substituting into the parameter formulation gives the cross-domain decomposition as
\bm{\theta}^{j}=\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}\bm{v}^{j}
.
This decomposition allows each task policy to be synthesized from a small number of shared components, linearly combined via task-specific weights and projected to the appropriate domain representation.
The sparsity of
\bm{v}^{j}
encourages compact task representations and supports transfer by reusing latent structures across both tasks and domains.
The agent sequentially interacts with tasks from various domains without prior knowledge of the number or order of domains or tasks.
It collects trajectories from each task and incrementally updates the shared knowledge base, domain projections, and sparse codes to refine its generalization capabilities.
This formulation supports both intra-domain and cross-domain transfer: within a domain, all tasks share the same projection, enabling efficient local adaptation; across domains, the shared latent base
\bm{U}
ensures coherence and scalability.
Building upon this foundation, the next subsection details how this knowledge is integrated into the
L2RL
framework,
enabling the agent to adapt efficiently as new tasks arrive sequentially.

V-B

Cross-Domain Learning Framework

We propose a
CD-L2RL
framework that enables continual adaptation to new tasks by efficiently reusing shared knowledge across domains.

PG
methods
[
39
]
represent the agent’s policy
\bm{\pi}
as a function defined over a vector
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of control parameters.
With this parameterized policy, we can compute the optimal parameters
\bm{\theta}^{*}
that maximize the expected average reward as follows

\Gamma(\bm{\theta}^{j})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}^{j}(\bm{s}_{t+1},\bm{a}_{t+1})\right]=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{j}}p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})d\bm{\tau},

(15)

where
\mathbb{H}^{j}
is the set of all possible trajectories,
\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}^{j}(\bm{s}^{j}_{t+1},\bm{a}^{j}_{t+1})
is the reward of trajectory
\bm{\tau}
, and
p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})=Q_{0}(s_{0})\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(\bm{s}^{j}_{t+1}|\bm{s}^{j}_{t},\bm{a}^{j}_{t})\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{a}^{j}_{t}|\bm{s}^{j}_{t})
is the probability of
\bm{\tau}
with initial state distribution
Q_{0}:\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
. By integrating all possible trajectories
\bm{\tau}
weighted by their likelihood
p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})
under the policy
\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}
, the expected return
\Gamma(\bm{\theta}^{j})
quantifies the overall performance of the policy for task
j
.
Given the decomposition
\bm{\theta}^{j}=\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}\bm{v}^{j}
, the objective for lifelong policy learning across domains is given as follows

\displaystyle L_{M}

\displaystyle(\bm{U},\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{1}},\dots,\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigg\{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{k}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}^{k}}\min_{\bm{v}^{j}}\left[-\Gamma(\bm{\theta}^{j})+\mu_{1}\|\bm{v}^{j}\|_{1}\right]+\mu_{2}\|\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\|_{F}^{2}\bigg\}+\mu_{3}\|\bm{U}\|_{F}^{2},

(16)

where
\mu_{1}
,
\mu_{2}
, and
\mu_{3}
are regularization weights.
The
L_{1}
norm of
\bm{v}^{j}
is used to approximate the true vector sparsity.
We employ regularization via the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
to avoid overfitting on both the shared knowledge base
\bm{U}
and each of the group projections.
To enable efficient policy learning in a lifelong setting, it is necessary to avoid reliance on full trajectory distributions across all tasks.
The expected return objective for each task
j
, given in Equation (
15
), requires access to all possible trajectories, which is impractical for continual learning.
To address this, we reformulate the lifelong optimization objective using a surrogate that approximates the expected return through a lower-bound-based Taylor expansion.
We begin with the original task-specific objective

\min_{\bm{v}^{j}}\left[-\Gamma(\bm{\theta}^{j})+\mu_{1}\|\bm{v}^{j}\|_{1}\right],\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{\theta}^{j}=\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}\bm{v}^{j}.

(17)

Following the approach in
[
30
]
, we consider the lower bound of
\Gamma(\bm{\theta}^{j})
by defining

\displaystyle\Gamma_{L,\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j})\propto-\int_{\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{j}}p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})\log\left[\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})}{p_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})}\right]d\bm{\tau},

(18)

which leads to the equivalent minimization problem

\min_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}\int_{\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{j}}p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})\log\left[\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})}{p_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})}\right]d\bm{\tau}.

(19)

Since computing this term remains computationally intensive, we adopt an approximation based on a second-order Taylor expansion around a surrogate solution
\bm{\rho}^{j}
defined as
\bm{\rho}^{j}=\bm{\theta}^{j}+\eta\mathcal{\Im}^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{j}}\Gamma_{L,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{j})
, where
\eta
is a learning rate and
\mathcal{\Im}
is the Fisher information matrix.
Using the identity

\log p_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}(\bm{\tau})=\log Q_{0}(\bm{s}_{0})+\sum_{t=1}^{T^{j}}\log p(\bm{s}_{t+1}|\bm{s}_{t},\bm{a}_{t})+\sum_{t=1}^{T^{j}}\log\bm{\pi}_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}(\bm{a}_{t}|\bm{s}_{t}),

(20)

the first-order gradient becomes
\nabla_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}\Gamma_{L,\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j})=-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\tau}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}}\left[\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})\sum_{t=1}^{T^{j}}\nabla_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}\log\bm{\pi}_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}(\bm{a}_{t}^{j}|\bm{s}_{t}^{j})\right]
.
Then, the second derivative is
\bm{\aleph}^{j}=-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\tau}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}}\left[\mathcal{\Re}^{j}(\bm{\tau})\sum_{t=1}^{T^{j}}\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}\log\bm{\pi}_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}}(\bm{a}_{t}^{j}|\bm{s}_{t}^{j})\right]_{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}=\bm{\rho}^{j}}
.
We now apply a second-order Taylor expansion of
-\Gamma(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j})
around
\bm{\rho}^{j}
as

\displaystyle-\Gamma(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j})\approx-\Gamma(\bm{\rho}^{j})+(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}-\bm{\rho}^{j})^{\top}\nabla(-\Gamma)(\bm{\rho}^{j})+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}-\bm{\rho}^{j})^{\top}\bm{\aleph}^{j}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}-\bm{\rho}^{j}).

(21)

Since
\bm{\rho}^{j}
is a stationary point of the lower-bound objective, we have
\nabla(-\Gamma)(\bm{\rho}^{j})=0
.
Thus, the linear term vanishes.
We also discard the constant term
-\Gamma(\bm{\rho}^{j})
as it does not influence the optimization.
Substituting
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}=\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}\bm{v}^{j}
, we obtain
-\Gamma(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j})\approx\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{\rho}^{j}-\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}\bm{v}^{j}\right\|_{\bm{\aleph}^{j}}^{2}
, where
\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{A}}^{2}:=\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}
.
Plugging this approximation into the full lifelong objective (
16
) yields
\hat{L}_{M}(\bm{U},\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{1}},\dots,\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{K}})
, where
\Gamma(\bm{\theta}^{j})
will be replaced by
\Gamma(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j})
.
This formulation eliminates dependence on trajectory sampling while preserving expressiveness and structure in the
CD-L2RL
.

We fit the policy parameters in two stages.
Upon observing a new task
j\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
, we first estimate the task-optimal policy parameters
\bm{\rho}^{j}
via a policy gradient update, along with the associated curvature matrix
\bm{\aleph}^{j}
, which approximates the local Hessian at
\bm{\rho}^{j}
.
Then, we obtain the task-specific coefficient vector
\bm{v}^{j}
by solving the following
least-absolute-shrinkage-and-selection-operator (LASSO)
-like optimization to promote sparsity in the solution by penalizing the sum of absolute values of the coefficients

\bm{v}^{j}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\bm{v}}\left\|\bm{\rho}^{j}-\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}\bm{v}\right\|_{\bm{\aleph}^{j}}^{2}+\mu_{1}\|\bm{v}\|_{1}.

(22)

To update the shared knowledge components incrementally, we compute the task-specific statistics as
\bm{X}^{j}=\bm{v}^{j}(\bm{v}^{j})^{\top},\ \bm{Y}^{j}=\bm{\rho}^{j}(\bm{v}^{j})^{\top}
.
These statistics are used to recursively update cumulative cross-domain sufficient statistics for each domain as

\bm{X}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leftarrow(1-\eta_{a})\bm{X}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}+\eta_{a}\bm{X}^{j},\qquad\bm{Y}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leftarrow(1-\eta_{a})\bm{Y}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}+\eta_{a}\bm{Y}^{j},

(23)

where
\eta_{a}\in(0,1)
is the update rate controlling the influence of the current task.
Finally, the group-specific projection matrix is updated using the pseudoinverse-based least-squares solution as
\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leftarrow\bm{Y}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\left(\bm{X}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\right)^{\dagger}
, where
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse (used to obtain a minimum-norm least-squares solution even when the matrix is not invertible).
This update yields the minimum-norm solution to the least-squares problem
\min_{\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}}\left\|\bm{Y}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}-\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{X}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
, ensuring stable and consistent refinement of the domain projection matrices even in the presence of noisy or underdetermined data.
Having updated
\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}
, the policy parameters for task
j
are reconstructed as
\bm{\theta}^{j}=\bm{\Psi}^{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\bm{U}\bm{v}^{j}
, from which the policy
\bm{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}^{j}}
can be evaluated and refined through additional local learning if needed.

CD-L2RL
algorithm is shown in Algorithm

.

Simulation Results

This section evaluates the performance of the the
EH
framework and the proposed algorithms, including
CD-L2RL
,
PG
-
RL
, and the Lyapunov optimization through simulations conducted under dynamic and realistic
WSN
conditions.
The aim is to benchmark its adaptability and learning performance in comparison with two well-established baselines: the Lyapunov optimization and
PG
-
RL
.
The experiments are designed to emulate a range of non-stationary scenarios, including time-varying energy harvesting capabilities, stochastic data arrival rates, and fluctuating wireless channel conditions.

The simulation environment consists of multiple sensor nodes communicating wirelessly while harvesting energy.
To emulate realistic
WSN

The channel gains for data transmission and wireless energy transfer are independently sampled from Rayleigh distributions at each time step.

VI-C

Complexity Comparison

This subsection consolidates and compares the computational complexities of the three methods evaluated, the Lyapunov Optimization,
PG
-
RL
, and the proposed
CD-L2RL
algorithm, across algorithmic structure, computational cost, and scalability in dynamic
WSN
environments.

The Lyapunov Optimization
operates with low per-slot computational complexity by solving a constrained, non-convex problem through alternating minimization over a small number of control variables (e.g., power and time allocations).
The per-slot complexity is approximately
\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{alt}}\cdot C_{\text{sub}})
, where
N_{\text{alt}}
is the number of alternating iterations, and
C_{\text{sub}}
depends on system dimensionality and the cost of evaluating nonlinear expressions.
This method scales linearly with the number of nodes but lacks the adaptability required in non-stationary environments.

PG
-
RL
methods, particularly actor-critic architectures based on policy gradients, offer greater flexibility by learning directly from interaction with the environment.
They incur high computational cost.
The complexity per time step is
\mathcal{O}(N_{d}S_{d}A_{d})
for both forward and backward passes through the neural networks.
For
T
training iterations and accuracy
\epsilon
, the total training complexity becomes
\mathcal{O}(TN_{d}S_{d}A_{d}/\epsilon^{2})
.
Although parallelization and batching improve runtime, the sample inefficiency and need to relearn policies per task make
PG
-
RL
less suitable for lifelong deployment.

CD-L2RL
achieves a balance between efficiency and adaptability through modular design and hierarchical knowledge sharing.
The per-task complexity is governed by

•

\mathcal{O}(r\cdot K_{d})
for computing task-specific policies from shared representations,

•

\mathcal{O}(r\log r)
for solving the LASSO problem during sparse coding,

•

\mathcal{O}(r^{2}\cdot d)
for updating the shared latent base,

•

\mathcal{O}(r\cdot K_{d}^{2})
for updating group-specific projections.

Assuming
r,d\ll K_{d}
, the dominant cost scales linearly in the task dimensionality, making
CD-L2RL
well-suited for real-time, large-scale, and cross-domain learning in dynamic environments.
Having established the convergence and computational characteristics of the algorithms, we now evaluate their robustness to dynamic
EH
conditions and generalization across tasks and domains.

VII
Conclusion

The conclusion of this study underscores the success of the proposed
CD-L2RL
framework in optimizing long-term energy consumption in dynamic
WSNs
environments.
The
CD-L2RL
algorithm outperforms traditional optimization and
RL
methods by efficiently adapting to non-stationary conditions and leveraging knowledge transfer across both tasks and domains.
By framing dynamic
WSN
control as a multi-task learning problem,
CD-L2RL
learns from a sequence of tasks, each reflecting a unique environmental configuration, while generalizing its experience across multiple domains with structurally related system dynamics.
The simulation results confirm that the
CD-L2RL
approach achieves faster convergence, lower energy consumption, and better adaptability to dynamic environments.
Specifically, the algorithm demonstrates superior performance in maintaining queue stability and minimizing energy consumption under varying
EH
conditions and network traffic scenarios.
These results highlight the strength of the proposed
CD-L2RL
approach in enabling real-time, sustainable operations of
WSNs
in highly dynamic environments.
The findings suggest that the
CD-L2RL
framework is a promising solution to enhance the sustainability and efficiency of future
WSNs
, paving the way for more resilient and adaptive communication systems in the era of
6
G and beyond.
Future research will aim to enhance the scalability of the proposed algorithm and explore its integration into distributed systems such as autonomous vehicle networks, smart grids, and edge computing environments.
