Cell identity and filament architecture shape intercellular communication in the terminal heterocystous cyanobacterium Richelia rhizosoleniae SC01
Authors: Sepehr Bardi, Mercedes Nieves-Morión, Rachel A. Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntercellular communication is essential for metabolite exchange and cellular coordination in filamentous cyanobacteria, yet how filament organization influences this process remains poorly understood, particularly in terminal heterocyst-forming strains. Here, we compared the facultative symbiont Richelia rhizosoleniae SC01 (hereafter Richelia; terminal heterocysts) with the free-living Anabaena sp. PCC 7120 (hereafter Anabaena sp.; intercalary heterocysts) and a septal junction mutant of Anabaena with impaired intercellular communication (CSVT22, ∆fraC-∆fraD), using fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) and fluorescence loss in photobleaching (FLIP). Richelia SC01 and Anabaena showed similar recovery (R) rates, indicating comparable average molecular exchange through septal junctions, with no significant correlation between R and filament length. By contrast, CSVT22 showed reduced recovery and accelerated, irregular decay, indicative of both disrupted septal connectivity and altered regulation of septal junctions, resulting in heterogeneous and less efficient intercellular exchange. Differences in fluorescence decay speed across cell types and strains suggest that cell identity and filament organization, including filament taper, shape patterns of intercellular exchange. FLIP analyses showed that Anabaena consistently exhibited greater exchange asymmetry, whereas Richelia SC01 vegetative cells displayed comparatively more symmetric exchange, despite pronounced filament tapering and terminal heterocyst organization. Transmission electron microscopy further showed that septal nanopores in Richelia SC01 are similar in size to those of Anabaena but exhibited marked variation in number per septal disk, suggesting a potential structural basis for variation in intercellular exchange patterns. Collectively, our findings show that combining FRAP and FLIP provides a quantitative framework to dissect the kinetics and spatial organization of intercellular exchange in filamentous cyanobacteria.IMPORTANCEFilamentous cyanobacteria rely on intercellular exchange through septal junctions to coordinate metabolism and development, yet the principles governing multicellular communication in bacterial filaments remain poorly understood. Here, we show that intercellular exchange emerges from the interplay between septal junction properties, cell identity, and filament architecture, generating distinct communication patterns across cyanobacterial lifestyles. Notably, despite its pronounced filament tapering and terminal heterocyst organization, Richelia SC01 exhibited comparatively symmetric exchange, demonstrating that filament polarity alone does not determine communication patterns. Our findings reveal that cyanobacterial multicellularity arises from the integration of structural organization and cell-specific properties, providing a framework for understanding coordinated physiology in filamentous cyanobacteria, including symbiotic and nitrogen-fixing systems.

INTRODUCTION

Multicellularity and cellular differentiation likely originated with filamentous microbes (1–4). One of the foundational characteristics of multicellular organisms is the presence of structures that enable coordinated activity between cells through intercellular communication. Among prokaryotes, filamentous cyanobacteria stand out as some of the earliest examples of multicellularity and cellular differentiation (2, 4), where cytoplasmic bridges play crucial roles in coordinating cellular activity (5). In eukaryotes, we see phylogenetically widespread examples of such structures enabling cytoplasmic continuity: “plasmodesmata” in land plants; “gap junctions” in animals; and “septal pores” in fungi (6).

Filamentous cyanobacteria that differentiate into distinct cell types, e.g., heterocyst-forming cyanobacteria, represent ideal model systems to study cooperation between two distinct cell types: vegetative cells and heterocysts. Under nitrogen-limiting (diazotrophic) conditions, heterocysts differentiate to perform N2 fixation. Depending on the strain, heterocysts can be single and positioned terminally or in set intervals along the filament (intercalary) (7). Consequently, the proportion of heterocysts along the filament (heterocyst frequency) is influenced by environmental conditions and varies among cyanobacteria strains. In particular, cyanobacteria that form symbiotic relationships with terrestrial plants can have 3- to 10-fold (up to 60% in land plant symbioses) higher heterocyst frequency than their free-living counterparts (8). Vegetative cells are always present and function to reduce CO2 through oxygenic photosynthesis and provide energy for N2 fixation. Metabolite exchange between the two cell types, where nitrogen-enriched metabolites from heterocysts (9–12) are exchanged for photosynthates from the vegetative cells (mainly as sucrose) (13–16), is well documented under diazotrophic conditions. Thus, both cell types are mutually dependent and require intercellular communication for metabolite exchange and growth of the whole filament.

In the model heterocyst-forming cyanobacterium Anabaena sp. PCC 7210, rapid intercellular exchange of metabolites and signaling molecules is enabled through cytoplasmic connections between adjacent cells, formed by septal junctions. The septal junctions in filamentous cyanobacteria traverse the septal peptidoglycan layer through perforations called nanopores to establish a cytoplasmic continuity between neighboring cells (17–19). Several proteins, including FraC and FraD (5, 20, 21) and SepJ (also known as FraG) (22), function in the formation of the septal junctions. Nanopores have been visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of purified peptidoglycan murein sacculi in several different filamentous cyanobacteria, including Anabaena sp. PCC 7210, and are considered key components enabling intercellular exchange (23, 24). In Anabaena sp. PCC 7120, the formation of nanopores requires AmiC-type amidases (AmiC1, Alr0092; AmiC2, Alr0093) that perforate the septal peptidoglycan, while AmiC3 (All1140) influences nanopore width, and a LytM-factor regulator activates AmiC1 to control the process; inactivation of any of these proteins results in reduced nanopore abundance and impaired intercellular molecular exchange (23, 25–27). Thus, filamentous heterocyst-forming cyanobacteria provide an ideal model system to investigate the links between cell differentiation, multicellular organization, and intercellular communication.

Intercellular molecular exchange in filamentous cyanobacteria has been commonly studied using fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis. FRAP relies on sequesterable fluorescent tracers (e.g., calcein, 5-carboxyfluorescein, and esculin), photobleaching, and real-time live cell imaging (28–30). In FRAP, the recovery rate of fluorescence in the bleached cell directly reflects the diffusion rate and serves as a measure of intercellular communication rates. It assumes that intercellular communication is through simple diffusion (28–30). While the influence of nanopore size and abundance per disk on recovery rates has been characterized in vegetative cells of Anabaena sp. PCC 7210 (23), the influence of other characteristics at the filament level, such as filament length, cell size, and heterocyst position, on intercellular diffusion dynamics remains largely unexplored. A second complementary live cell imaging technique that also relies on retainable fluorophores, fluorescence loss in photobleaching (FLIP), can be used to quantify the intercellular exchange along filaments by measuring the spreading decay of fluorescence to the surrounding cells due to continuous bleaching of one cell. However, FLIP has so far only been used to study intercellular communication through the continuous periplasm of cyanobacterial filaments (31). FLIP can therefore enable the quantification of the rate and symmetry of intercellular exchange along cyanobacterial filaments, and, when combined with FRAP, provides a comprehensive framework to resolve both local cell-to-cell exchange and filament-scale transport dynamics.

Here, we characterized the kinetics and structural aspects of intercellular communication in the marine filamentous facultative cyanobacterial symbiont Richelia rhizosoleniae strain SC01 (hereafter Richelia SC01) that differentiates terminal heterocysts and can form symbioses with chain-forming diatoms (32). There are two other Richelia strains (ReuHH01 and RintRC01) which live in symbiosis with diatoms, but as endobionts. The filaments of Richelia SC01 exhibit a tapering to that of one endobiont, ReuHH01, with vegetative cell diameters decreasing with distance from the terminal heterocyst (33, 34). In symbiosis, Richelia strains (including SC01) are the primary source of nitrogen to their respective host (35, 36), and strategically, Richelia SC01 attaches externally to the diatom with its heterocyst. Thus, determining Richelia’s capacity for intercellular communication and its spatial organization within filaments is important to understand the cellular basis of symbiont-host interaction. We further compared intercellular communication in Richelia SC01 with the model cyanobacterium Anabaena sp. PCC 7120 (hereafter Anabaena) and the Anabaena double ∆fraC-∆fraD mutant (hereafter CSVT22) using FRAP and FLIP methods. The double mutant, CSVT22, shows a phenotype resembling Richelia SC01: short filaments and terminal heterocysts, and is largely impaired in intercellular communication. Given the importance of septal structures in intercellular communication, we additionally imaged and characterized the nanopores of Richelia SC01. The combined use of FRAP and FLIP revealed that intercellular exchange in Richelia SC01 occurs at rates comparable to Anabaena yet differs in spatial asymmetry and cell type-specific dynamics, revealing heterogeneous patterns of exchange. Our study demonstrates that filament architecture and lifestyle shape the organization but not the overall capacity of intercellular communication in filamentous cyanobacteria.

RESULTS

Morphological comparison of cyanobacterial strains

To establish structural characteristics relevant to the FRAP and FLIP analyses, we measured filament length, heterocyst frequency, heterocyst position, and cell cross-sectional area for all strains grown under the same diazotrophic lab conditions (Fig. 1A). We additionally collected images of wild R. rhizosoleniae from a confocal image archive of plankton samples from Tara Oceans (37) and previously reported (38, 39). The latter images represent the symbiotic state for Richelia SC01, as all images show R. rhizosoleniae living in symbioses with its preferred diatom host Chaetoceros sp. (32).

Cell cross-sectional area varied significantly within strains. In Anabaena strains, heterocysts were consistently larger than vegetative cells, whereas no significant differences between cell types were observed in Richelia SC01 (Fig. 1B). Between Richelia strains, vegetative cells of SC01 were 1.7-fold larger than those of wild Richelia, while heterocyst size did not differ significantly.

Of the laboratory strains, Richelia SC01 showed the largest cell areas overall, with heterocysts and vegetative cells being 1.4- to 1.7-fold and 1.6- to 2-fold larger, respectively, than those of Anabaena and CSVT22. In terms of cell shape, Richelia strains exhibited lower circularity, indicating more elliptical elongated cells than Anabaena strains in both cell types (Fig. S1). Within strains, only Richelia SC01 showed cell type-specific differences in shape, with vegetative cells presenting more elongated morphologies than heterocysts (Fig. S1). The previously reported taper in the Richelia SC01 filaments was also noted in both the lab and wild strains. In contrast, all Anabaena strains did not differ significantly in cell shape between cell types. Accordingly, the longest cell diameter in Richelia SC01 was approximately 2.6-fold larger than in wild Richelia for both cell types, and vegetative cells were also larger than those of Anabaena (~1.4- to 1.5-fold), whereas heterocyst diameter did not differ significantly among strains. Altogether, only Anabaena strains showed significant cell type differences in shape and size, with heterocysts being larger and less elliptical than vegetative cells.

The cell density per filament varied with strains. For filaments of similar length, the number of cells differed between Richelia SC01 and wild Richelia (12 ± 7 and 6 ± 1 cells per filament, respectively; Fig. 1C). For Anabaena, we observed high variability in filament length after handling (16 ± 17 cells per filament), whereas for the CSVT22 mutant, we observed the shortest filaments (4 ± 1 cells per filament). The high variation in Anabaena is likely a result of handling, which resulted in high fragmentation of filaments. Richelia SC01 and Anabaena had the largest ranges of filament length, ranging from 3–45 cells and 3–123 cells, respectively.

Heterocyst frequency (number of heterocysts/total number of cells) was highest in CSVT22 after 48 h in diazotrophic conditions (24.0% ± 1.8%), comparable to wild Richelia (16.9%) and Anabaena (13.0% ± 2.1%), and lowest in Richelia SC01 (7.9% ± 0.7%; Fig. 1D). Heterocyst positioning within filaments was strain specific. Both Richelia strains had exclusively terminal heterocysts. In the CSVT22 mutant, most filaments contained terminal heterocysts (82%), whereas 10% lacked heterocysts and 7% had intercalary heterocysts. In Anabaena, potential filament breakage during handling resulted in a mixed distribution of heterocyst positions, with filaments containing terminal (59%), both terminal and intercalary (26%), or exclusively intercalary heterocysts (11%; Fig. 1E).

Overall, the cyanobacterial strains analyzed exhibit substantial morphological variation in cell types and filament architecture, providing the structural framework required for subsequent analyses to understand intercellular exchange.

Intercellular communication in cyanobacterial filaments differs between strains and cell types

Cells (heterocyst or vegetative cell) labeled with 5-CF were successfully photobleached in all three cyanobacteria strains, allowing measurement of fluorescence recovery by FRAP experiments (Fig. 2A). Although 5-CF labeling indicated viable cells, two distinct behaviors were observed independently of strain and cell type after photobleaching: some cells showed fluorescence recovery, while others remained bleached, indicating the absence of lateral communication with their neighboring cells (Fig. 2B). The time required to reach the 90% saturation recovery after photobleaching differed among all strains as well as between cell types in Richelia SC01 and CSVT22 (Fig. 2B). For example, 90% saturation in vegetative cells and heterocysts was reached approximately after 15 s and 21 s, respectively, in Richelia SC01; 19 s and 20 s, respectively, in Anabaena; and 22 s and 23 s, respectively, in CSVT22 (Fig. 2B).

We measured the average fluorescence recovery for vegetative cells and heterocysts, defined as the percentage of the prebleach fluorescence intensity reached after 30 s of recovery, to quantify the extent of fluorescent 5-CF available for intercellular exchange within the whole filament. We took into consideration the overall filament length (Fig. 1C) to calculate the theoretical maximum fluorescence intensity recovery percentage (Fmax) if all the 5-CF taken up would be freely available and all intercellular communication opened. Consequently, Fmax was lower for the short filament of CSVT22 than in the longer filaments of Anabaena and Richelia SC01 (Fig. 2E).

We observed a significant cell type-specific difference in average fluorescence recovery for Richelia SC01 and CSVT22, but not for Anabaena: heterocysts exhibited a lower average recovery than vegetative cells (Fig. 2E). In Richelia SC01, average recovery was 23% ± 18% in heterocysts compared to 49% ± 18% in vegetative cells (Fmax = 92%), while in CSVT22 values were 3% ± 4% and 12% ± 8%, respectively (Fmax = 67%). In vegetative cells, average fluorescence recovery was highest in Richelia SC01 (49% ± 18%), intermediate in Anabaena (26% ± 15%; Fmax = 91%), and lowest in CSVT22 (12% ± 8%; Fig. 2B and E).

We therefore first focused on characterizing the capacity of intercellular exchange in single cell types within filaments. The recovery rate constant (R; s−1), derived from a mathematical fit of range-normalized FRAP recovery profiles (all exhibiting similar AIC range), was used to compare strains and cell types (Fig. 2C, Table 1). Our results were consistent with previous work under similar diazotrophic conditions on vegetative cells of Anabaena and CSVT22 (Fig. 2C). Independent of the recovery profiles, we found that the overall distribution of R values was similar between Richelia SC01 and Anabaena, and both strains had significantly higher and larger ranges in R values than CSVT22. When comparing cell types, Richelia SC01 and CSVT22 showed lower R values and range in heterocysts, whereas Anabaena showed significant differences in R range but not values between cell types (Fig. 2C, Table 1). Overall, our results indicate that each individual cell type can vary its intercellular communication capacity with its neighbors within the filament. However, it remains unclear whether the differences in this capacity are due to filaments, strains, cell types, and/or position within the filament.

We then classified cell types according to their R values, and cells with R < 0.01 s−1 were classified as non-communicating, as previously defined (5, 42). We measured the proportion of non-communicating cell types in each strain: Richelia SC01 exhibited similar proportions of non-communicating heterocysts and vegetative cells (28% and 7%, respectively) as Anabaena (14% and 6%, respectively), contrasting with CSVT22 (63% and 39%, respectively; Fig. 2D). Both cell types in Anabaena and only vegetative cells in Richelia SC01 had significantly lower proportions of non-communicating cells compared to CSVT22 (Fig. 2D). Taken together, our results suggest that each cell in a filament, independent of the strain, can autonomously regulate its communication through cytoplasmic connections with its neighboring cells.

Our FRAP data demonstrated that the isolated cell behavior depended on filaments. We then assessed the performed linear regressions to compare how filament length affected intercellular communication parameters (fluorescence recovery and recovery rate; Table 2). In all cases, correlations were weak (R2 < 0.5) and/or not significant, indicating that intercellular communication did not systematically change with filament length. These results indicate that the high variability in Anabaena filament length did not systematically influence fluorescence recovery measurements despite its effect on the theoretical maximum recovery (Fmax). Overall, independent of cell type and strains, our results highlight that the intercellular communication capacity of each cell in a filament is controlled at the single cell level.

Decay kinetics, decay mechanisms, and patterns of intercellular exchange in FLIP assays vary strongly with strain and cell type

Given the results of the FRAP assays, we then used FLIP experiments (Fig. S2) to evaluate whether (i) the position of cells in a filament and/or (ii) the spatial distribution of intercellular exchange between neighboring cells are differently affected. To assess the FLIP results, we fitted our normalized fluorescence decay profiles onto a stretched exponential (StrExp) model (equation 4), from which we extracted for every cell on the filament, differentially distant from the continuously bleached cell, the following parameters: the time constant (τ or Tau) describing the speed of fluorescence decay, the stretching exponent (h) indicating hetero- to homogeneity extent of decay (estimated by the curvature extent of fitted curve), and the time-dependent rate coefficient [k(t)] describing how the loss of fluorescence evolves over time (43).

We first compared the characteristic decay time (τ) of cells in a filament at several distances from the bleached cell, counted in cell distance (two- and five-cell distances; see the supplemental methods). When comparing the average τ of cells within a two-cell distance from the bleached cell, CSVT22 filaments bleached in a vegetative cell decayed significantly faster (τ = 58.5 ± 37.4 s, n = 60) than Anabaena vegetative cells (τ = 96.4 ± 64.6 s, n = 25) or Richelia SC01 heterocysts (τ = 134 ± 110 s, n = 32; Fig. 3A). In the five-cell range (restricted to Anabaena and Richelia SC01), Richelia SC01 filaments bleached in a vegetative cell decayed significantly faster (τ = 79.0 ± 30.2 s, n = 15) than in Richelia SC01 heterocyst (τ = 165 ± 113 s, n = 32) or Anabaena vegetative cell (τ = 122 s ±66.3, n = 25; Fig. 3B).

To elucidate the factors driving fluorescence decay kinetics, we applied a linear mixed-effects model (LMM; Fig. 4A; Fig. S3) with log-transformed τ as the dependent variable. Among multiple variables tested, including strain, bleached cell type, cell type, heterocyst frequency, filament length, and model root mean squared error (RMSE)—the distance from the bleached cell was one of the strongest predictors of τ, consistent with diffusion-mediated decay along filaments (Fig. 4B). Importantly, bleaching of Richelia SC01 vegetative cells produced a significant negative effect on τ indicating faster decay, whereas Richelia SC01 filaments overall, including heterocyst, showed a significant positive effect on τ (slower decay). In contrast, heterocyst frequency, cell type, and filament length had no significant effect, highlighting that spatial position and cell type of bleaching are the primary determinants of filament decay dynamics (Fig. 4B).

To further explore the mechanisms underlying the distance-dependent and cell type differences in fluorescence decay revealed by the kinetic analysis of τ, we classified the nature of fluorescence loss using a qualitative framework adapted from Wüstner et al. (43), based on three variables: τ, the characteristic decay time reflecting the overall speed of fluorescence loss, h, the shape parameter indicating the curvature of the decay curve, with lower values (<0.5) corresponding to irregular or disconnected decays, and k(t), the instantaneous decay rate describing how the loss of fluorescence evolves over time (Table 3; Fig. S4). Each decay curve was assigned to one of six categories: diffusion-limited (inside or outside the ROI), binding/barrier-limited, or diffusion-limited, mono-exponential (bleach-limited), very low h (disconnected/ambiguous), or unclassified. As expected for diffusion-mediated decay, h values decreased (curves became more compressed) with increasing distance, resulting in a higher proportion of cells with low h (<0.5; Fig. 5). In Richelia SC01, heterocyst bleaching produced a higher fraction of very low h cells than bleaching of vegetative cells at both two-cell (39% and 6%, respectively) and five-cell (61% and 28%, respectively) distances (Fig. 5), indicating more irregular or disconnected decay in heterocysts. By contrast, Anabaena and CSVT22 showed no significant cell type-dependent differences, and CSVT22 filaments contained a smaller proportion of very low h cells overall, reflecting more uniform fluorescence decay (Fig. 5).

Septal structure features of Richelia SC01 differ from Anabaena genotypes

To account for the selective change in intercellular communication, we isolated the peptidoglycan (PG; murein) sacculi from Richelia SC01 filaments and visualized them by TEM (Fig. 7; Fig. S5). In order to predict the cell type of the PG disks observed in the TEM micrographs, we compared the diameters of septal PG disks observed by TEM with the estimated diameters of heterocysts and vegetative cells measured in confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images. We also considered the likelihood of the cellular origin of the PG sacculi based on the heterocyst frequency (Fig. 1D and E). For example, the measurements by TEM and CLSM for vegetative cells of Richelia SC01 were similar (Fig. S6), and as such, the PG disks were likely derived from vegetative cells. The average septal PG disks were significantly larger in Richelia SC01 (2.77 ± 0.703 µm; n = 10) compared to reported measures of Anabaena vegetative cell septal PG disks (1.431 μm ± 0.413 µm; n = 22) and CSVT22 (0.952 μm ± 0.261 µm; n = 11) (44). Nanopore size was relatively consistent across septa and within the range reported for Anabaena, Nostoc punctiforme, and symbiotic Nostoc azollae (Table 4). In contrast, the number of nanopores per septal disk showed high variability, which may contribute to the observed variation in intercellular exchange patterns.

Fra and AmiC homologs in the Richelia SC01 genome

To understand the possible molecular mechanisms enabling the single-cell selective control of intercellular communication we demonstrated above, we used sequences of Anabaena septal protein sequences as queries to identify putative homologs of FraC (WP_088243366.1, E-value = 4e−40), FraD (WP_088243365.1, E-value = 6e−125), FraE (WP_088243364.1, E-value = 4e−111), and SepJ (WP_088243279.1, E-value = 9e−110) in Richelia spp. strains (Table S1). Sequence alignments were performed in Clustal and confirmed that homologs were present in Richelia SC01 (Table S1). Based on the predicted synteny in the Richelia SC01 genome (RrhiSC01), fraC, fraD, and fraE form a gene cluster, while fraE was notably absent from the genomes of the other symbiotic Richelia spp. strains (RintRC01 and ReuHH01; Table S1). Using the nanopore-associated protein sequences of Anabaena as queries, putative homologs of AmiC1 (Alr0092) and AmiC2 (Alr0093) were identified in Richelia SC01 genome as (WP_088240876.1, E-value = 0.0), AmiC3 (All1140; WP_088240073.1, E-value = e−158), and the LytM regulatory factor Alr3353 (WP_088240267.1, E-value = e−100; Table S1).

DISCUSSION

Filamentous cyanobacteria establish functional multicellularity through septal junctions that connect adjacent cells and permit gated exchange by simple diffusion (5, 29, 51), yet how cell type, filament architecture, and lifestyle shape exchange dynamics remains poorly understood. Here, we addressed this gap by comparing intercellular communication in three strains with contrasting filament architecture: Anabaena as an intercalary heterocyst-forming freshwater model; CSVT22 as a communication-impaired Anabaena mutant with terminal heterocysts (21); and Richelia SC01, a marine facultative diatom symbiont forming tapered filaments with terminal heterocysts (32, 52). Our study attempted to uncover how intercellular communication is controlled at both the single-cell and filament level.

Under nitrogen limitation, all strains resulted in the expected phenotype, including differentiated heterocysts, with morphological features (cell diameter and filament length) consistent with earlier reports (53). Successful 5-CF labeling was achieved in all three strains, including Richelia SC01 for the first time, demonstrating its suitability for studying intercellular communication in this cyanobacterium. It is important to note that fluorophores, e.g., 5-CF, calcein, and esculin, differ in molecular size (376.3, 622.5, and 340.3 Da, respectively), molecular charge, and immobility fractions, which alter the fluorophores’ effective exchange behavior (30). Therefore, observed differences between our results and previous studies may reflect tracer-specific properties, highlighting the importance of considering the fluorophore used in each study.

FRAP recovery rates in Richelia SC01 were similar to those of Anabaena, indicating that heterocyst position (terminal vs intercalary) does not strongly influence overall exchange capacity under the conditions tested. Here, Richelia SC01 and CSVT22 showed significant cell type-specific recovery rates, with vegetative cells recovering faster than heterocysts. Earlier FRAP studies (30, 42) have found similar results with Anabaena using different fluorophores (calcein and esculin, respectively). Combined, these results suggest that differential connectivity between cell types may represent a conserved feature among heterocyst-forming cyanobacteria rather than a strain-specific trait. Cyanophycin plugs at heterocyst cell poles may contribute to differential recovery between cell types by restricting inward fluorophore flow, as previously demonstrated in Anabaena mutants lacking polar cyanophycin (28, 40). Further supporting this, an Anabaena mutant with inactivated fraH, which produces heterocysts lacking the characteristic intracellular honeycomb membrane structures associated with cyanophycin plugs, showed increased rates of calcein transfer from vegetative cells to heterocysts compared to wild type (54). Notably, the Richelia SC01 genome encodes both cyanophycin synthase (WP_088243624.1) and cyanophycinase (WP_088243625.1), and cyanophycin granules have been observed in wild Richelia sp. (52). Hence, a similar impact of cyanophycin plugs acting as barriers to intercellular communication could explain a slower recovery in the heterocysts of Richelia SC01.

The presence of non-communicating cells observed here in both Richelia SC01 and Anabaena is consistent with previous observations in Anabaena (42), where communicating cells in Richelia SC01, together with the presence of putative homologs of FraC, FraD, FraE, and SepJ, suggest a similar regulatory mechanism potentially supporting coordinated intercellular exchange. Additionally, non-communicating heterocysts in Richelia SC01 may partly reflect senescent heterocysts, as previously noted for aging heterocysts in Anabaena filaments using esculin (30).

FLIP analysis complemented FRAP by allowing observations of the continuous fluorescence decay dynamics, and thus, we moved from a single-cell analysis to along filaments. Distance from the bleached cell was the strongest determinant of decay speed, consistent with diffusion-mediated exchange, but similar to the FRAP assays, strain- and cell type-specific differences emerged. Notably, CSVT22 showed the fastest fluorescence decay in FLIP despite displaying the slowest recovery in FRAP, and this pattern persisted even after accounting for the smaller filament size and correspondingly lower theoretical fluorescence reservoir of CSVT22 (Fig. 2E). These results in CSVT22 are consistent with previous evidence that loss of FraC/FraD function affects both septal connectivity and the regulation of intercellular exchange (20, 21, 55). The stretched exponential classification of decay profiles further supports our interpretation. In CSVT22, decay profiles rarely fell into ambiguous or disconnected categories, indicating that fluorophore exchange remained continuous across cells despite impaired regulation. In contrast, Richelia SC01 heterocysts frequently exhibited very low stretching parameter values, consistent with limited or heterogeneous connectivity.

Beyond differences in decay kinetics, FLIP revealed distinct exchange patterns between strains. Anabaena displayed consistently higher asymmetry in fluorescence loss patterns than Richelia SC01, independent of bleach cell type or neighboring cell identity. This asymmetry reflects differences in exchange between the two neighboring cells of a bleached cell rather than a directional bias of transport. The greater asymmetry observed in Anabaena may result from increased variability in septal junction activity among cells or from less coordinated regulation of septal junctions at opposite cell poles. Our data cannot distinguish between these possibilities, but they suggest that intercellular exchange is less uniformly organized in Anabaena than in Richelia SC01. Interestingly, despite its polarized filament morphology, including tapering and a terminal heterocyst, Richelia SC01 exhibited comparatively symmetric patterns among its vegetative cells. This suggests that morphological polarity does not necessarily translate into asymmetric intercellular exchange and points instead to a potential role for differences in the regulation of cell-cell communication. In the symbiotic context, where Richelia SC01 attaches to its diatom host at its terminal heterocyst (32), symmetric vegetative cell connectivity may facilitate efficient redistribution of fixed nitrogen from heterocysts toward vegetative cells and ultimately to the host, which is consistent with the rapid nitrogen transfer shown previously for this symbiosis (36). The reliance on a single terminal heterocyst may favor the maintenance of intercellular exchange along the filament, as disruption of connectivity could limit nitrogen transfer to cells located farther from the heterocyst. Furthermore, it is informative to consider heterocyst frequency in Richelia SC01 relative to other known cyanobacterial symbioses. Cyanobacteria, when associated with terrestrial plants, can exhibit substantial increases in heterocyst differentiation compared to when they are in a free-living state. Reported frequencies, however, vary widely and range 1%–60% depending on the symbiotic system (8). For example, frequencies of 10%–30% are reported in lichen symbioses compared to up to 60% in angiosperm-associated systems such as Gunnera (8). The heterocyst frequency of Richelia SC01 (7.9% ± 0.7%) is closer to values reported for free-living heterocystous cyanobacteria. Our demonstration of efficient and symmetric intercellular exchange in Richelia SC01 supports the capacity of this lineage to sustain such bidirectional metabolite redistribution along short filaments. A direct test of intercellular communication dynamics in the presence of the diatom host would be a valuable next step, as physical attachment and metabolic integration with the host may alter the asymmetry, magnitude, and rate of exchange observed here under free-living laboratory conditions.

To investigate whether the nanopore array could differ in Richelia SC01, the septal peptidoglycan of Richelia SC01 was extracted and visualized by TEM and compared with earlier reports of other cyanobacteria, including facultative (N. punctiforme) and obligate (N. azollae) symbionts of plants. This is consistent with the presence of orthologs of key Anabaena proteins (AmiC1: Alr0992; AmiC2: Alr0093; AmiC3: All1140; and the AmiC regulatory factor LytM: Alr3353) encoded in the Richelia SC01 genome involved in nanopore formation in septal disks (56). The nanopore size and abundance in Richelia SC01 were within the range reported for other heterocystous cyanobacteria, including both free-living and symbiotic strains. Although Richelia SC01 vegetative cells possessed significantly larger septal disks than those of Anabaena, nanopore abundance was similar between strains, resulting in a lower nanopore abundance per unit disk area in Richelia SC01. The septal disk area, nanopore abundance, and nanopore diameter of Richelia SC01 were, however, not significantly correlated (Fig. S7), indicating that these structural features vary independently. Additionally, there was high variation in nanopores per disk, and occasionally larger pores were observed in Richelia SC01 septa, which is similar to observations recently reported in septal disks of vegetative cells and a hormogonia mutant (∆fraI) of the facultative symbiont Nostoc punctiforme (49). As suggested by Janović et al. (49), the presence of larger pores may explain higher rates of communication in some cells.

More broadly, our findings contribute to a growing appreciation that regulated intercellular communication through cytoplasmic bridges is a recurrent and functionally important feature across the tree of life. Chaigne and Brunet (6) proposed that cytoplasmic bridges may have first served a structural role in the evolution of multicellularity before later enabling nutrient exchange and intercellular communication. Previous studies have established that septal junction structure and regulation govern intercellular communication. Our results further show that cell identity and filament organization influence how exchange is distributed within multicellular filaments, indicating that cyanobacterial multicellularity is shaped by the integrated effects of structural connectivity, regulatory control, and filament architecture.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains and growth conditions

Anabaena sp. strain PCC 7120 (7) and the Anabaena ∆fraC-∆fraD double mutant (CSVT22) (21) were grown photoautotrophically in liquid BG11 medium (7) under continuous shaking (100 rpm) and constant light (42 μE m−2 s−1) at 30°C. The ferric ammonium citrate in the original medium recipe was replaced with ferric citrate (Sigma-Aldrich). Heterocyst differentiation was induced in Anabaena and CSVT22 by harvesting filaments grown on BG11 (5–7 days after inoculation), washing three times with BG110 medium (lacking NaNO3), and resuspended in the same medium for 48 h prior to live-cell imaging experiments. Richelia SC01 was grown photoautotrophically in SO medium (SN lacking NaNO3) (57) on a 14 h:10 h light:dark cycle (15 ± 5 μE m−2 s−1) at 26°C under continuous shaking (80 rpm, 10 mm radius of rotation). Richelia SC01 was inoculated into fresh SO medium 6–7 days prior to live-cell imaging experiments.

Filament morphology

Triplicate cultures of each strain were grown under their respective conditions (BG110 for 48 h for Anabaena and CSVT22, and SO medium for Richelia SC01), and brightfield micrographs were acquired to quantify filament length, heterocyst frequency, and heterocyst position. For each replicate, 100 filaments were measured to capture intra-strain variability, and only intact filaments were included in the analysis. Heterocysts of Anabaena and CSVT22 were identified by mixing cell solutions (1:10) with alcian blue (1% [wt/vol] in 95% ethanol) for 5–10 min (58). See the supplemental methods for details on counting criteria (Table S4). Images of wild Richelia rhizosoleniae were obtained from an image archive of plankton from the Tara Oceans (37) under accession number S-BSST529 at the EMBL-EBI repository BioStudies (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies).

Purification, imaging, and analysis of murein sacculi from Richelia SC01

Richelia SC01 cells in late stationary growth phase (20–30 days after transfer to SO media) were harvested by gravity filtration (Whatman Cyclopore Track Etched Membrane, 47 mm, 5 µm pore size), flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further handling (hereafter, pellet). The pellets were processed as previously described (17). Samples were then imaged with a Zeiss Libra 120 Plus electron microscope at 120 kV. See the supplemental methods for details on processing of the TEM micrographs.

FRAP and FLIP analyses

Aliquots (1 mL) of cell culture were harvested by centrifugation, washed in respective N-deplete growth medium (BG110 for Anabaena and CSVT22 and SO for Richelia SC01), resuspended in 1 mL of fresh growth medium, and amended with 4 µL 5-carboxyfluorescein Diacetate, Acetoxymethyl Ester (5-CF; Molecular Probes, Invitrogen) in Dimethyl sulfoxide (5 mg/mL). Initial tests with calcein (Molecular Probes, Invitrogen) in Richelia SC01 showed no uptake of the dye; therefore, we used 5-CF (data not shown). All centrifugation steps here and after were at a speed of 20,800g and 1 min in duration. Subsequently, the cell suspension was incubated in the dark in respective growth conditions (see above) for 1 h. Filaments were then processed as previously described (21). Small agar blocks were cut out and transferred to 35 mm µ-dishes (Ibidi GmbH, Graefelfing, Germany), and measurements were carried out at respective growth temperatures, i.e., 30°C for Anabaena and the strain CSVT22 and 26°C for Richelia SC01. Note that experiments with Anabaena and CSVT22 were carried out within 48 h of inducing heterocyst differentiation by removal of N. For strain CSVT22, only filaments with two vegetative cells and a terminal heterocyst were chosen for the experiments.

FRAP and FLIP experiments were carried out with a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal laser scanning microscope and a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective (Zeiss, Germany). The samples were excited with the Argon laser (488 nm), and emission of 5-CF was observed at 500–527 nm with the use of the main dichroic beam-splitter 488. Uptake of 5-CF in Richelia SC01 could be observed and differentiated from autofluorescence (Fig. S8). Images (512 by 512 pixels and 16-bit depth) were acquired with laser power set to 0.3%–2.5%, and care was taken not to saturate the detector (GaAsP) by adjusting the gain between 500 and 700. The pinhole was set to 150 µm for all experiments. Only filaments that fit within the field of view were observed and measured. For most images, a zoom of 1.8× was used, except for longer Anabaena filaments that did not fit the frame, for which a zoom of 1.2×–1.5× was used. Parallel brightfield images were acquired for Richelia SC01. The frame interval for both FRAP and FLIP experiments was 1 s, and three prebleach images were acquired prior to the start of an experiment. For FRAP experiments that lasted 30 s, the recovery of fluorescence was measured for 27 frames after one photobleaching event (100% laser power, 30 iterations). For FLIP experiments that lasted 60 s, photobleaching occurred at 10 s intervals (80% laser power, 30 iterations), and the decay of fluorescence in every cell within a filament was measured between bleaching events (six bleaching events in total).
