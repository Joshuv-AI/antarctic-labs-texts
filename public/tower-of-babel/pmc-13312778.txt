Who wants to pay more taxes for better long-term care services: evidence from OECD surveys
Authors: Sylvain Botteron, Christophe Courbage, Joël Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examines the factors influencing individuals’ willingness to pay higher taxes to support long-term care (LTC) services, a pressing issue as aging populations drive rising LTC demand and public spending.MethodsWe use data from the 2020 and 2022 waves of the OECD’s “Risk that Matter” survey, a cross-national survey examining people’s perceptions of the social and economic risks they face. We employ generalized linear mixed-effects models configured as logistic regression models, including country-level random intercepts and country-level covariates. We also conduct standard logit regressions for individual countries to analyze local nuances further.ResultsThe findings reveal that higher income, older age, female gender, left-leaning political orientation, and caregiving responsibilities significantly increase the likelihood of willingness to pay for improved LTC services. At the country level, greater LTC spending as a share of GDP, higher trust in government, and policies offering cash benefits to carers also play a significant role.ConclusionsOverall, the results indicate strong support for additional LTC funding among sizeable demographic groups, especially older respondents and those in higher-income groups. At the same time, political orientation and certain socio-demographic characteristics are associated with differences in support. These findings are relevant for policymakers considering reforms to improve the provision of LTC services.Keywords: Long-term care, Willingness to pay, Taxes, Social contributions

Background

The demographic shift towards an aging population, particularly the rising number of elderly individuals in most industrialized nations, is leading to an increased demand for long-term care (LTC). Public expenditures cover most LTC costs, averaging about 1.8% of GDP across OECD countries in 2021 [1]. Projections indicate that this percentage is anticipated to at least double by 2060 [2], placing significant pressure on public budgets. Without structural reforms to boost GDP and reduce LTC spending, governments need to find additional revenue to address the rising public LTC expenses, primarily through increases in personal income taxes and social security contributions. Understanding the factors that influence individuals’ acceptance of higher taxes or social contributions is thus essential for policymakers to decide about the resources allocated in financing LTC. Insights into these factors can help design LTC policies that more effectively distribute fiscal burdens and gain public support. Enhanced public acceptance may, in turn, facilitate the implementation of tax increases and improve tax compliance and overall revenue collection [3].

Research on the willingness to pay (WTP) for improved LTC services through higher taxes or social contributions and its determinants is scarce and country-specific. Most studies rely on state preference methods, such as contingent valuation or discrete choice experiments, and focus on specific national or local institutional settings. For instance, Kitajima [4] used survey data from Tokyo municipalities and contingent valuation methods to show a strong positive association between WTP for public LTC insurance and age, consistent with the hypothesis that proximity to care needs increases perceived benefits. Brau and Lippi Bruni [5], employing discrete choice modeling with an Italian sample, stressed the positive association between income and WTP for public LTC coverage, reflecting lower marginal utility losses from taxation among higher-income individuals. More recently, Amilon et al. [6], relying on discrete choice experiments with data for Denmark, showed that WTP for social LTC insurance, using household taxes as the payment vehicle, is positively associated with age, being politically oriented towards the left and being employed, and is negatively associated with having a high level of education, having right-wing political preferences, having children and having used LTC or work experience of the LTC sector. These findings highlight the coexistence of self-interest and experiential effects. While proximity to LTC risks is expected to increase WTP, familiarity with the LTC system may also raise awareness of inefficiencies, potentially dampening support. In related work, Amilon et al. [7] employed alternative discrete choice experiments to show that lower trust in institutions is associated with higher WTP for specific LTC services in Denmark, suggesting that institutional trust influences not only the level but also the form of support for public LTC spending.

A related strand of the literature addressed the broader subject of WTP higher taxes in return for better public healthcare. For instance, Gugushvili [8], using survey data from 27 countries and contingent valuation methods, found that individuals with higher satisfaction with healthcare services, higher levels of education, and greater wealth were more likely to support paying more taxes for healthcare improvements. Similarly, Habibov et al. [9], examining data from 29 post-communist countries through contingent valuation, reported that individuals with higher social and institutional trust and left-leaning ideological orientations were more likely to favor tax increases to enhance healthcare in specific regions. While informative, these studies do not explicitly focus on LTC, which differs from healthcare in important respects: LTC is strongly age-related, often involves informal caregiving, and entails long-term fiscal commitments that may alter individual cost–benefit calculations.

Finally, a broader literature on welfare attitudes stresses that people’s preferences regarding income distribution usually reflect their perceived self-interest in specific types of welfare provision [10]. For instance, older people favor pension spending over education [11], while individuals with higher socio-economic status favor benefits provisions that reflect previous contributions [12]. However, financial self-interest alone does not fully account for the varying levels of support for redistribution, pointing towards a significant role for social preferences and beliefs about fairness and equity in shaping public policy preferences (see e.g. [13]). LTC, situated at the intersection of ageing, health, and family responsibility, provides a particularly salient context in which these competing mechanisms may operate.

This article contributes to the literature on contingent valuation of LTC services in several important ways. First, it provides one of the few large-scale cross-national analyses of individuals’ willingness to pay higher taxes or social contributions for improved LTC services, using data from the 2020 and 2022 waves of the OECD’s “Risk that Matter” survey covering 25 OECD countries, including major European welfare states (such as Germany, France, and Sweden), Anglo-Saxon countries (such as the United Kingdom, the United States, and Canada), and non-European OECD members (including Japan and South Korea). This broad geographical scope allows us to assess whether theoretically hypothesised determinants of WTP, such as age, income, political orientation, trust, and caregiving responsibilities, operate consistently across diverse institutional and cultural contexts. Second, by exploiting two survey waves, we strengthen the robustness of our findings and enable limited cross-temporal comparisons of attitudes toward LTC financing during a period marked by heightened awareness of social risks. Third, we explicitly combine individual-level characteristics with country-level factors, thereby linking micro-level preferences to macro-institutional arrangements.

Methodologically, we employ multilevel logistic models with country-level random intercepts to account for the hierarchical structure of the data and unobserved cross-country heterogeneity, and complement them with country-specific regressions to capture national differences.

Our findings reveal that individual characteristics such as higher income, older age, being female, left-leaning political orientations, and existing LTC responsibilities or needs are consistently associated with a greater likelihood of agreeing to pay for increased taxation to better access LTC services. Additionally, country-level factors, including the country’s LTC expenditure as a percentage of GDP, respondents’ trust in government institutions, and cash benefits policy for carers, significantly affect the WTP. Taken together, the results point to strong support for additional LTC funding among sizeable demographic groups, especially older respondents and those in higher-income groups. Differences across groups remain, in particular by political orientation in some countries, which can matter for how reforms are communicated and designed.

Methods

Study design

We use data from the 2020 and 2022 waves of the OECD’s “Risk that Matter” survey [14, 15], a cross-national survey examining people’s perceptions of the social and economic risks they face. The survey is repeated cross-sectional, drawing a new quota-based sample in each wave rather than following the same individuals over time. Thus, differences between 2020 and 2022 reflect population-level changes under different contexts, not within-person change.

The survey comprises two sections: the core questionnaire, gathering views on societal risks, and the background questionnaire, recording respondents’ socio-economic characteristics. It has expanded from 21 countries in the initial wave to 25 in 2020 and 27 in 2022 [1]. Quotas based on sex, age, education, income, and employment ensure samples reflect each country’s population, with weights addressing representation issues ([16], Box 1.1). Each weighted sample includes 1,000 respondents per country, without adjustments for population size differences. Since country-level weighting is applied, no additional post-collection weights were used, and missing observations minimally affect sample distribution.

To maintain uniformity across the datasets, Latvia and the United Kingdom are excluded from the 2022 sample as they were not present in 2020. Israel is excluded due to issues related to the “Political Orientation” variable, while Mexico and Turkey are omitted due to the unavailability of data on the “LTC share of GDP” variable. These adjustments result in a final sample of 22 countries, as reported in Fig. 1. After excluding missing observations, the final sample includes 22,655 respondents in 2020 and 22,278 in 2022, with missing data primarily due to non-responses to the dependent variable.

Country-specific variables are sourced from the OECD iLibrary. If a country does not report a particular variable for the year under analysis, we use data from the closest available year.

Dependent variable

The dependent variable is derived from one question of the ‘core questionnaire’ of the OECD “Risk that Matter” survey, which was formulated as follows in 2022:

Would you be willing to pay an additional 2% of your income in taxes/social contributions to benefit from better provision of and access to LTC services for elderly people (including e.g. home, community-based, institutional care)?1

The answer to this question is binary and respondents could choose amongst the options “yes” and “no”. This question addresses the respondent’s willingness to support fiscal measures for enhancing LTC services. While this variable aligns with the classification of WTP studies employing a dichotomous (closed-ended) question format [see 17], we recognize certain methodological limitations. The survey does not provide a specific monetary value for LTC services, leaving respondents to estimate this based on a proposed 2% income tax increase. Additionally, the fixed tax increase and lack of variation in LTC service levels prevent us from evaluating respondents’ sensitivity to pricing or service scenarios.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, agreement to a tax/social contribution increase is moderate amongst respondents. In 2020, the average agreement rate was 34.56%, declining to 29.61% in 2022. The median agreement rate across countries also declined from 36.04% in 2020 to 29.28% in 2022 (a 6.76 percentage-point decrease). While agreement remains in the same broad range, changes are heterogeneous across countries, with some experiencing sizeable shifts between waves.

Slovenia and Ireland had the highest agreement rates, with Slovenia nearing 50% in 2020. Estonia’s support remained stable at 36.72% in 2022, down slightly from 37.30% in 2020, similar to Chile. Italy and Poland showed consistently moderate support, around 20–25%, particularly in 2020. Germany’s agreement fell significantly from 36.04% in 2020 to 25.27% in 2022, while the USA saw a slight change, from 26.68% in 2020 to 28.09% in 2022, indicating more stable support.

Independent variables

This study examines individual and country-level factors influencing WTP for increased LTC-related taxes or social contributions. Variables are selected based on theoretical relevance, prior research, and survey data availability, while addressing multicollinearity and statistical concerns. Determinants are grouped into socio-demographic, socio-economic, LTC exposure, political orientation, and country-level characteristics.

Socio-demographic and economic variables are known to shape preferences for LTC services [4, 8]. We included respondents’ gender, age group, marital status (married/partnered vs. not), household size (1–15 members), and the presence of children under 18. Household income is measured as equivalized disposable annual income after taxes and transfers, adjusted for household size and categorized into quartiles. Housing type (own, rent, or other arrangement) and education level (mandatory school, high school, or higher education) are also included.

Exposure to LTC significantly influences perceptions of LTC financing [18]. Variables include caregiving responsibilities (e.g., caring for an elderly family member), current LTC needs, and concerns about future access to quality LTC for elderly family members, categorized as concerned, not concerned, or undecided.

Political beliefs are well-known to shape opinions on social welfare programs and taxation policies (see Habibov et al. [9]). Although data on political orientation are collected only in 2022, we include this variable to examine its impact on fiscal attitudes toward LTC. Respondents identified their preferred political party in a hypothetical election, classified using the Chapel Hill Expert Survey (CHES). We used the LRGEN variable, which provides an expert-based assessment of a party’s ideological stance on the left-right spectrum [19].

Parties with identifiable CHES scores were grouped into left (0–3), center (4–6), and right (7–10), with all remaining responses coded as “Other”.

Older respondents consistently showed higher agreement rates. Individuals with personal experiences in LTC, such as caring for an elderly family member or having personal LTC needs, also showed higher agreement rates. Political orientation influenced support, with left-leaning respondents showing the highest agreement, followed by centrists and right-leaning individuals.

Finally, we examine a range of country-level variables. One important factor is a country’s LTC share of GDP, which quantifies the percentage of national resources allocated to financing LTC across providers and schemes. Indeed, higher existing allocations to LTC may reduce individuals’ willingness to contribute further.

Trust in institutions has also been highlighted as a significant driver of support for higher taxation in previous research (see Habibov et al. [9], Amilon et al. [7]). We used data from a poll conducted in 2020 by the Gallup World Poll (GWP), as reported in the OECD Government at a Glance report [20]. The poll measures trust in government as the percentage of individuals who express confidence in their national government.

We include life expectancy at age 65 as an indicator of potential LTC needs, as a longer life expectancy at older ages is associated with higher demand for LTC services. Similarly, the proportion of the population aged 65 and older, representing demographic pressures on LTC systems, may also shape attitudes toward higher taxes.

Direct cash benefits to carers, which involve financial assistance to informal caregivers, are also considered. These benefits, often provided as allowances or payments [21], may raise awareness of the importance of public funding for LTC and its allocation.

Finally, we account for income inequality, measured by the Gini Coefficient, as attitudes toward higher LTC taxes may be influenced by their potential to reduce disparities in wealth distribution.

Together, these variables provide a comprehensive understanding of the national factors that shape public support for LTC policies.

Econometric specification

We employ generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) configured as logistic regression models. We implemented these models using the lme4 package in R. Multilevel random intercept models yield more accurate results than standard logistic regression models, as they consider the nested nature of the data [22]. We also interpret the results using Average Marginal Effects (AMEs), calculated with the marginaleffects package, which supports mixed-effects models incorporating random effects from the lme4 package. These offer a more intuitive understanding of changes across nested models compared to shifts in coefficients [23].

The coefficients and are the individual-level and country-specific predictors, respectively. Finally, is the random intercept for each country , capturing unobserved variability across countries not explained by observed variables.

We also conduct standard logit regressions for individual countries to explore local nuances and assess consistency. Furthermore, we apply our modeling approach to the 2020 dataset as a backtest to confirm the stability and generalizability of our findings across different datasets and temporal contexts.

We treat all categorical variables as nominal, regardless of whether ordinal or nominal. This allows for a more straightforward interpretation of the coefficients.

We analyze intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) from our multilevel models to assess the data’s hierarchical structure. The null model, without predictors, shows that 4.8% of total variation is attributable to country-level differences, supporting the use of multilevel modeling.

In the full model, including individual- and country-level predictors, the ICC decreases to 1.8%, indicating that the predictors explain much of the between-country variability. However, the residual ICC suggests unmeasured country-level factors still influence the outcome, highlighting the importance of addressing these through the model’s random effects.

Starting with socio-demographic and economic factors, women are consistently more likely than men to support increased contributions for better LTC services, aligning with research showing women are generally more pro-welfare than men (see e.g. [24, 25]).

We also observe that older respondents strongly support increased taxation for LTC services. This supports the notion that fiscal policy attitudes among older individuals are influenced by self-interest, as corroborated by Kitajima [4] and Amilon et al. [6], who suggest that proximity to potential LTC needs drives this support.

Individuals with higher education appear to agree more with the proposition than those who have only completed mandatory education, as supported by Gugushvili [8].

Higher-income groups are more inclined to support higher taxes for better LTC services, reinforcing the established connection between financial security and willingness to invest in public health services, as supported by Habibov et al. [9] and Gugushvili [8].

Moving to variables capturing respondents’ awareness of LTC issues, we find that individuals with LTC responsibilities for an elderly family member show significantly higher agreement for the tax increase. Similarly, respondents with personal LTC needs or concerns about an elderly family member’s LTC access are likelier to endorse the contribution increase. These findings are consistent with Costa-Font and Rovira-Forns [18] and Kitajima [4], who found that exposure to LTC issues influences support for related policies.

Respondents aligned with right-wing political parties show lower support for increased contributions to improve LTC services compared to those favoring left-wing parties. This highlights the role of political ideology in shaping welfare policy preferences, supporting Fong [13]’s claim that financial self-interest alone does not explain the diversity in public policy support. These findings align with Amilon et al. [6], who observed similar patterns in Denmark regarding LTC services at home.

Although not consistently significant, factors such as the presence of children, housing tenure, and household size exhibit a coherent directional influence across both study years. A notable exception is marital status, which shows opposite non-significant effects across years with negligible overall impact. This aligns with offsetting mechanisms as those without a partner may expect greater formal LTC use [26], but single-adult households face tighter budgets and higher poverty risk, making a 2% income-based contribution more burdensome and dampening support [27].

Regarding country-specific variables, we first find that higher national expenditure on LTC as a proportion of GDP is associated with a lower individual WTP additional taxes. This result may reflect a ’thermostatic’ response, where individuals adjust their support based on perceptions of the adequacy of existing tax levels, as noted by Wlezien [28].

Conversely, higher trust in government consistently correlates with greater willingness to support higher LTC contributions in both survey years. This finding resonates with Habibov et al. [9], who identified institutional trust as having a significant favorable influence on the WTP for improved public healthcare provision. Yet, these results contradict the ones of Amilon et al. [7] showing surprisingly that lower trust in institutions is associated with higher WTP for specific LTC services in Denmark. This discrepancy might come from the definition of trust used by Amilon et al. [7], which is based on the belief that welfare state institutions will produce intended results, while we define trust as confidence in the national government.

Lastly, direct cash benefits to carers are strongly linked to a higher likelihood of supporting the tax in both 2022 and 2020.

Individual country regressions

Figures 2 and 3 in the Appendix show country-by-country logit regressions as heatmaps. Each column is a country, and each cell reports a coefficient (log-odds), with stars indicating statistical significance. We mainly discuss the 2022 heatmap (Fig. 3), which includes a richer set of covariates, notably political orientation. Figure 2 (2020) is shown for comparison. Given the sample size of roughly 1,000 respondents per country, the regressions’ explanatory power is lower than the aggregate multilevel model, which leverages 23,000 observations for a broader analysis.

Age is the clearest and most consistent result across countries. Compared with the 18–29 group, the coefficients for ages 50–59 and 60+ are positive in all 22 countries in 2022. The 60+ effect is statistically significant in 18 countries and is especially large in Estonia, Portugal, Slovenia, Korea, Italy, Belgium and Ireland. It is smaller (and often not significant) in Lithuania, Norway and the United States. This shows that the age pattern seen in the pooled model reflects a broad tendency across countries rather than being driven by a small number of cases.

Concern about future access to LTC also shows a similar pattern across countries. Being concerned is associated with a higher willingness to contribute in 21 out of 22 countries. The coefficient is particularly large in Denmark and is statistically significant in Switzerland, Austria, Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands. Chile is the only country with a slightly negative coefficient.

Political orientation in 2022 points in the same direction across most countries, but the size of the association differs. Identifying with a right-leaning party is linked to lower willingness to pay in 19 of 22 countries, and the effect is statistically significant in 11. The largest negative coefficients are found in Germany, Austria and Denmark, followed by Norway, Switzerland, Finland, Spain, the Netherlands, the United States, Estonia and Chile. In Italy and Portugal, the coefficient is close to zero, and in Lithuania it is positive. This means that the pooled result mainly reflects the countries where the difference between left and right is strongest and most precisely estimated.

Gender differences are also present in many countries, but not in all. The coefficient for women is negative in 18 of 22 countries and statistically significant in Slovenia, Estonia, Canada, Denmark and Finland, with the largest negative estimates in Denmark and Finland. Italy and Germany show positive coefficients, and the Netherlands and Poland show slightly positive coefficients, suggesting that the gender association depends on country context in a small set of cases.

Variables capturing exposure to care needs show more mixed patterns. Being responsible for an elderly family member is positive in 18 countries and statistically significant in several (notably Belgium, Finland, France, Austria, Poland, Lithuania and Italy), but it is negative in Denmark (and also in Ireland). Having LTC needs in the household is positive in 15 countries but negative in 7, and it is statistically significant only in Switzerland and the Netherlands. These results may partly reflect limited precision for less common household situations within country samples, and they may also reflect differences across countries in how people relate family needs to formal LTC provision.

Finally, socio-economic variables (education, income, housing tenure, and household size) show weaker and less consistent patterns across countries. Income effects are clearly positive in Spain and Portugal (especially at higher income quartiles), but are small or mixed elsewhere. Renting (relative to owning) is significantly negative in Ireland, Korea and Canada, but significantly positive in Austria. This helps explain why pooled results for these variables are often smaller and less stable than those for age, concern about access, and (in many countries) political orientation.

Sensivity checks

We validate our binary GLMM using standard diagnostic tests, treating observations as conditionally independent after accounting for country-level random intercepts. A Q–Q plot of the random intercepts (Fig. 4) suggests approximate normality, with minor tail deviations that are common in practice [29]. A Shapiro–Wilk test does not reject normality () [30]. GVIF values between 1 and 2 indicate no problematic multicollinearity. As additional checks, we re-estimate the main specifications using alternative link functions (logit and probit), and results remain stable with negligible differences in AIC and BIC [31]. We also estimate reduced models (Table 3), including specifications omitting political orientation in the 2022 wave, and the main coefficients remain stable. Following recommendations for non-linear models, we interpret results using average marginal effects (AMEs) to facilitate comparisons across specifications [32, 33]. The AME comparisons indicate significant differences only for “LTC share of GDP” and “Trust in government.” Finally, given the limited number of countries, we also estimate specifications with upper-level predictors in separate regressions to limit overfitting risks and to confirm the robustness of the main patterns [8, 34]. We also test whether the association between baseline LTC spending and willingness to increase contributions is sensitive to how the country fiscal context is measured. Because the number of countries is limited, we add these country-level variables as extensions of the reduced specification (Table 3), rather than including many country controls at once in the full model. This reduces overfitting risk and keeps the interpretation of country-level predictors more stable. First, we add country-level measures of the revenue structure (revenues from social security contributions and from income/profit/capital taxes) as broad indicators of the financing environment. Second, we add government spending shares on health, education, and social expenditure to assess whether the LTC spending effect is capturing overall public spending levels. Across both waves, the LTC spending share remains negatively associated with willingness to contribute, and this result is not materially altered by either set of controls. Importantly, these additions do not materially change the main individual-level results (Table 3, additional columns). We report further robustness checks in Appendix Tables 4 and 5. First, we estimate year-specific linear probability models (LPMs) with country fixed effects and standard errors clustered by country, including interactions between age and income (2020 and 2022) and, in 2022, between age and political orientation. The interaction terms are small. Joint tests show no evidence of ageincome interactions in 2020 () and only weak evidence in 2022 (), while agepolitical orientation interactions are not jointly significant in 2022 (). Substantively, the interaction models are close to additive and do not change the main conclusions: support increases strongly with age, and this age gradient is broadly similar across income groups and political groups. Second, we estimate pooled models with country and year fixed effects (country indicators and a 2022 indicator). We report results from a fixed-effects logit model and from a weighted LPM that uses fitted probabilities from the logit model to address the non-constant variance implied by a binary outcome. The results are consistent with the baseline findings, and the year effect indicates lower support in 2022 compared with 2020, conditional on country fixed effects and respondent characteristics (Table 5). Taken together, these checks suggest that the results do not depend on the choice of functional form and are not driven by unobserved country-specific factors that are constant over time.

Discussion

Our study reveals several key factors shaping individuals’ willingness to pay higher taxes for improved LTC services.

Gender differences are notable, with women more likely to support higher contributions. This likely reflects their greater role in informal caregiving, which increases awareness of LTC challenges, and a self-interest effect, as women’s longer life expectancy and higher morbidity in older age raise the likelihood of needing formal LTC and the perceived value of publicly funded services [35, 36].

Age-related patterns show that older individuals are more inclined to support increased LTC funding, possibly due to their awareness of future needs or valuing accessible services, while younger people may prioritize other financial concerns. Higher education also boosts support, as educated individuals may be more aware of societal LTC needs and feel a stronger civic responsibility to address them.

Political orientation is another important factor, with individuals leaning left politically showing more support for increased LTC funding. This aligns with broader ideological beliefs about the role of government in providing social services and redistributing resources. Left-leaning respondents emphasize social equality and solidarity, while right-leaning individuals prioritize personal responsibility and lower taxes. For policymakers, this highlights the importance of considering the political climate and public sentiment when proposing changes to social contributions.

Higher-income respondents are also more likely to support increased social contributions. This is somehow expected as individuals from relatively wealthy households are more likely to be able to contribute a higher proportion to support LTC services. However, as Sumino [37] suggests, increasing the financial burden on these groups could lead to resistance, especially if they feel overtaxed or the benefits of increased funding are not clearly communicated.

Individuals with existing LTC needs and obligations are more likely to agree to the contribution increase, underscoring the influence of self-interest in shaping attitudes toward social welfare policies. This suggests that public education campaigns highlighting the potential need for future LTC services could enhance support for increased funding.

At the country level, a higher share of GDP spent on LTC is associated to lower support for increased contributions. One potential concern is the possibility of reverse causality between government LTC spending and individual willingness to pay additional taxes for LTC. Government budget allocations are typically determined through complex legislative processes and are less likely to be immediately influenced by individual willingness to pay. Therefore, we interpret the negative association between higher national LTC expenditure and lower willingness to pay additional taxes as being possibly due to perceptions of adequate funding or skepticism about spending effectiveness. Lower trust in government also reduces support, highlighting the importance of institutional trust in gaining public support for policy initiatives. Support is higher in countries offering direct cash benefits to informal carers, suggesting that compensating caregivers can foster broader backing for LTC funding reforms.

Some limitations to this study need to be mentioned. First, the reliance on self-reported responses may introduce self-reporting bias. Second, the data are repeated cross-sectional: the 2020 and 2022 waves surveyed different respondents, limiting within-person inferences and causal interpretation. Third, the two waves were conducted in different contexts. RTM 2020 took place during COVID-19, when heightened concern about health-system vulnerabilities may have increased support for public LTC funding [16], whereas RTM 2022 coincided with rising prices and a cost-of-living crisis, which could have constrained willingness to accept a 2% tax/social contribution increase [1]. Differences between waves should therefore be interpreted as population-level variation across contexts rather than within-person change [38]. Fourth, the 2022 wave added illustrative examples to the question related to LTC services (home-, community-based, and institutional care), introducing a potential framing effect. Fifth, the binary dependent variable may oversimplify attitudes, and the fixed 2% contribution requires respondents to infer the services purchased, increasing cognitive burden and limiting sensitivity analyses. The question also omits budget trade-offs or alternative funding mechanisms, so estimated associations reflect support under a specific tax-increase framing, which could affect the magnitude or direction of group differences under other policy scenarios. Finally, the small number of countries reduces the statistical power of country-level estimates.

Conclusion

While these findings do not establish causality, they provide important insights for public authorities aiming to address the complex social dynamics of LTC funding. By understanding the varying demographic, social, and political factors that influence the willingness to pay higher taxes for enhanced LTC services across OECD countries, as well as the specific characteristics of each country, policymakers can adapt their communication strategies and reforms accordingly. In doing so, our findings can help inform the development of policy measures that are better aligned with public sentiment, ultimately fostering a more resilient and sustainable LTC system.
