Remodified Ni/Al2O3 Catalysts for the Gas-Phase Reductive Amination of 1‑Octanol: Reconfiguration of Acid–Base Sites Enhances Catalytic Performance
Authors: Hao Wu, Zhengang Hou, Xiaoping Wang, Dianhua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In industrial applications, the amination of long-chain fatty alcohols to synthesize fatty amines still faces challenges such as unstable reaction rates and low product yields. Therefore, developing a catalyst for the amination of long-chain fatty alcohols that achieves high yields remains a significant challenge. Herein, we report that a series of Ni–Re/Al2O3 catalysts were synthesized and characterized for the continuous gas-phase reductive amination of 1-octanol with ammonia under atmospheric pressure. The results indicate that the presence of Re not only promotes hydrogen spillover and accelerates the reduction of Ni species but also weakens Ni–O–Al interactions and reconstructs the surface acid–base properties. The resulting balanced distribution of weakly basic, moderately basic, and strongly basic sites regulates primary amine selectivity, with yield exhibiting a volcano-shaped relationship with Re content. Under the optimal conditions, the Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst achieves 99.5% conversion and 64% monoamine selectivity. This study reveals the synergistic effect of Re in regulating metal–support interactions and acid–base sites, which provides a rational design strategy for non-noble catalysts in the reductive amination of long-chain alcohols.

Introduction

The gas-phase reductive amination of alcohols with ammonia offers a sustainable and efficient method for synthesizing primary amines, essential intermediates in industries like pharmaceuticals, surfactants, and corrosion inhibition. − This process follows a hydrogen-borrowing mechanism, starting with alcohol dehydrogenation, followed by condensation with ammonia to form an imine, and concluding with hydrogenation to yield the amine. − Compared with conventional amination routes based on halogenated substrates or preformed carbonyl compounds, this process eliminates stoichiometric waste and produces only water as a byproduct, making it an attractive green synthetic strategy. −

Despite these advantages, the reductive amination of long-chain alcohols remains challenging. The intrinsically low dehydrogenation activity of long-chain fatty alcohols, their high boiling points, and competitive adsorption of ammonia and its derivatives on catalyst surfaces often lead to low overall efficiency and undesired byproducts such as nitriles and secondary amines. − Therefore, developing catalysts that exhibit high activity, primary amine selectivity, and long-term stability under continuous-flow operation is crucial for practical and industrial applications. −

Nickel-based catalysts have attracted considerable attention as cost-effective and abundant alternatives to noble metals for hydrogenation and amination reactions. , However, strong metal–support interactions (MSI) between Ni and Al2O3 often lead to the formation of inert spinel phases such as NiAl2O4, resulting in poor reducibility, low dispersion, and rapid deactivation. ,, To overcome these limitations, bimetallic modification has been widely adopted to tune the electronic and structural properties of Ni catalysts. ,,− Among various promoters, Re has demonstrated exceptional potential in hydrogenation and reductive amination systems due to its ability to enhance hydrogen activation and migration via the hydrogen spillover effect and to improve the reductive properties of Ni while suppressing the formation of inert NiAl2O4 species. , For example, Ni–Re/Al2O3 catalysts have shown improved dispersion, stability, and activity in both hydrogenation and amination reactions. ,

The surface acid–base properties of Ni-based catalysts play a critical role in the adsorption and transformation of alcohols, ammonia, and imine intermediates. − The balance between weak, medium, and strong acid–base sites strongly influences imine adsorption configuration and hydrogenation behavior, thereby governing primary amine selectivity. , Recent studies have shown that tuning the metal–support interface and acid–base site distribution can effectively enhance ammonia adsorption and reaction pathway selectivity in gas-phase amination reactions. ,, Nevertheless, a systematic understanding of how Re incorporation reconfigures surface acid–base functionality and thereby governs reaction pathways in the reductive amination of long-chain alcohols remains elusive. ,

In this study, a series of Ni–Re/Al2O3 catalysts were prepared and investigated for the gas-phase reductive amination of 1-octanol in a fixed-bed reactor. Multiple characterization methods were employed to analyze the effect of Re doping and the balanced distribution of acid and base sites on the catalyst surface on the target product yield, while also examining the influence of reaction conditions on catalytic performance. Results indicate that moderate addition of Re modifier significantly improves catalytic performance. Under optimal conditions, the Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst achieved a 99.5% conversion rate for OL and a 64% selectivity for OA.

Experimental Section

Materials

Nickel nitrate (Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O, 98 wt %), ortho-xylene (99 wt %), and biphenyl (BP, 99.5 wt %) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Ammonium perrhenate (NH4ReO4, 99 wt %) was obtained from Shanghai Yanlu Manman Technology Co., Ltd. Activated aluminum oxide (Al2O3, 99 wt %) was purchased from Shanghai Sinoptinciple chemical technology Co., Ltd. N-Octanitrile (ON, 98 wt %) and trioctylamine (TOA, 99 wt %) were both obtained from Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd. 1-Octanol (OL, 99.9 wt %) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. N-Octylamine (OA, 99 wt %) was purchased from Shanghai Naicheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Dioctylamine (DOA) was purchased from Shanghai Chuangsai Technology Co., Ltd. H2 (>99.999%) and N2 (>99.999%) were purchased from Air Liquide (Shanghai) Compressed Gas Co., Ltd. NH3 (>99.999%) was purchased from Shanghai Haoqi Gas Co., Ltd.

Catalyst Preparation

Ni/Al2O3 and Ni–Re/Al2O3 catalysts were prepared by impregnating activated alumina with Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O and NH4ReO4 precursors using an initial wet impregnation method. The aluminum oxide used is in granular form, with a particle size range of 40–60 mesh; this granular form was chosen primarily because it facilitates handling during the preparation process and is suitable for large-scale production. The alumina was precalcined at 600 °C for 6 h before being mixed with the precursors dissolved in deionized water. Mix thoroughly, then let it rest for 24 h. The mixture was dried at 110 °C overnight and calcined at 500 °C for 4 h in air. The resulting samples were denoted as Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalyst, where Ni loading is fixed at 10 wt % and x represents Re loading ranging from 0 to 3 wt %. Single-metal nickel and Re loaded on Al2O3 are referred to as Ni/Al2O3 and Re/Al2O3, respectively.

Catalytic Experiment

The reductive amination reaction of 1-octanol was conducted in a fixed bed equipped with a stainless steel reactor (inner diameter 7 mm, length 405 mm). Before commencing the experiment, the influence of diffusion on catalytic performance was first ruled out. The porous structure of supported catalysts often gives rise to a certain degree of internal diffusion. To eliminate the effects of internal diffusion, it is usually necessary to control the particle size of the catalyst. Consequently, this study screened Ni–Re/Al2O3 catalysts with particle sizes of 20–40 mesh, 40–60 mesh and 60–80 mesh for evaluation of the amination of 1-octanol, with the results shown in Figure S1. The results indicate that, under identical reaction conditions, the conversion rates of the 20–40 mesh, 40–60 mesh and 60–80 mesh catalysts were essentially consistent. This indicates that the effect of internal diffusion is largely eliminated under the 40–60 mesh conditions. As the reaction conversion rate is high, approaching 100%, the reactants are consumed almost immediately upon reaching the catalyst surface. This suggests that the reaction rate is sufficiently fast; consequently, once the reactants come into contact with the catalyst surface, there is virtually no time for external diffusion to occur. Therefore, the influence of external diffusion is not considered in this experiment.

Typically, 1.08 g of catalyst was loaded into the constant-temperature zone of the reactor. Next, the reactor was leak-tested and purged with nitrogen. Subsequently, the catalyst was heated to 500 °C with a heating rate of 2 °C/min. Under atmospheric pressure, reduction was performed for 4 h using a mixed gas flow of 50 mL/min H2 and 50 mL/min N2. The system was then cooled to the reaction temperature. After stabilizing the flow rates of NH3, H2, and N2, 1-octanol was pumped into the reaction tube for catalytic testing. Catalyst performance was analyzed and discussed based on 24-h reaction results. Gas-hourly space velocity (GHSV) was calculated as the feed rate of reactants per unit time divided by catalyst mass. Reaction products were analyzed using gas chromatography (GC-8950, Shanghai Tianpu Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd.) equipped with a hydrogen flame ionization detector and an HP-5 column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm). The sampling method employed in this study was liquid-phase sampling. Once the reaction reached a steady state, samples were collected at 12 h intervals; the corresponding internal standards and solvents were added, and the samples were subsequently analyzed using a gas chromatograph to determine the concentrations of the reaction products. The following equations were used to calculate the OL conversion, product selectivity, product yield and carbon balance, with biphenyl as the internal standard and o-toluene as the solvent: XOL=nOL,0−nOL,1nOL,0×100% 1 Si=ninOL,0−nOL,1×100% 2 Yieldi=νOL,ini1nOL0×100% 3 Carbonbalance=∑(νOL,ini1+nOL1)nOL0×100% 4 where X OL denotes the conversion of OL, S i denotes the selectivity of the product, n OL,0 and n OL,1 represent the molar quantity of OL in the reactants and products, respectively, n i represents the molar quantity of the product, including ON, DOA, and TOA, νOL,i represents the stoichiometric coefficient of OL.

Characterization

N2 physical adsorption–desorption analysis was performed at Micromeritics ASAP 2020. The specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution of the catalyst were calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET), Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH), and t-Plot methods. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed on a Bruker ADVANCE D8 XRD instrument using a Cu Kα radiation source (4 kW, 40 kV, 100 mA), scanning 2θ from 10 to 90° at 10°/min. H2-TPR was performed on a Quantachrome Chembet Pulsar TPR/TPD equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). Typically, 0.1 g of sample was heated to 400 °C and purged for 1 h under a 50 mL/min pure helium (He) atmosphere. The sample was then cooled to 30 °C and reduced by hydrogen gas flow at 30 mL/min. The temperature was raised from 30 to 750 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, with the composition of the desorbed gases analyzed by TCD. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha instrument with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) at 12 kV and 6 mA. The energy range was scanned from 0 to 1200 eV with 1 eV steps. All binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Catalyst surface morphology was analyzed by field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FEI Tecnai F20 TEM) with energy-dispersive spectroscopy and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (JEOL JSM-7800F Prime SEM). The instrument operated at a maximum acceleration voltage of 200 kV. NH3-TPD and CO2-TPD (programmed temperature desorption) were performed on a Chembet Pulsar TPR/TPD with TCD quantum analyzer. Typically, 50 mg of sample was heated to 300 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min and purged for 2 h under pure He atmosphere at 60 mL/min. It was then cooled to 50 °C and adsorbed for 3 h using a mixed gas (10% vol NH3/He or CO2/He) at a flow rate of 60 mL/min. Following desorption, the sample was purged for 1 h with a 20 mL/min He flow. After raising the temperature from 50 to 750 °C at 10 °C/min, the outlet gas was desorbed for TCD analysis. Elemental composition and content in the sample were determined using a Varian 710ES ICP-OES. Prior to analysis, samples were acidified with a mixed solution of HNO3 and H2O2 at 120 °C for 30 min to ensure complete dissolution, followed by dilution to volume with ultrapure water. The samples used for XRD and those used for XPS characterization were both subjected to calcination and reduction; the reduced catalysts have been specifically marked in the manuscript, with the reduction conditions being 4 h at 500 °C. The catalyst samples used for H2-TPR, TEM, some XRD, CO2-TPD and NH3-TPD characterization were subjected only to calcination. However, the procedures for CO2-TPD and NH3-TPD characterization included a reduction step, with the reduction conditions being 4 h at 500 °C.

Results and Discussion

Characterization Results

The pore characteristics of Al2O3 and Ni-based catalysts were examined using the BET equation. Figure shows the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of the calcined alumina support and Ni-based catalyst. According to the IUPAC classification, all samples exhibited Type IV isotherms with H4-type hysteresis loops, indicating the presence of irregular channel systems, as shown in Figure B. Structural properties of the support and catalysts, such as specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter, are presented in Table S1. After Ni metal loading, the specific surface area and pore volume of the catalyst decreased significantly. Following Re metal doping, the specific surface area decreased, but its pore size and pore volume increased. This phenomenon is most likely attributable to Re particles entering the mesopores of alumina, occupying the original space, thereby significantly reducing the number of micropores in the alumina and causing a decrease in its overall specific surface area. The specific surface area of Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalysts ranges from 113.3 to 127.8 m2/g. Compared to undoped Ni/Al2O3 catalysts, Redoped Ni/Al2O3 catalysts exhibit increased specific surface area, pore size, and pore volume. Re metal doping may enhance the resistance of the catalyst to sintering, preserving larger pore sizes and pore volumes.

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm (A) and pore distribution of the samples (B) of Al2O3 and Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalysts.

The crystal structures of Ni and Ni–Re catalysts supported on Al2O3 were characterized by XRD, and the results are shown in Figures and S2. Figure presents the XRD patterns of the calcined (unreduced) and reduced Ni/Al2O3 and Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalysts, while Figure S1 shows the reduced Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalysts with varying Re contents. The XRD patterns of Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalysts with different Re contents are essentially identical. For all samples, characteristic diffraction peaks of the γ-Al2O3 support (PDF#00-0134) were observed at 2θ = 37.4°, 46.2°, and 67.3° (JCPDS 48-0367). Compared to the undoped sample, Re doping significantly weakened the intensity of the Al2O3 diffraction peak and broadened its shape from relatively sharp to wider, indicating that the introduction of Re may have reduced the crystal order of the carrier and altered its surface structural characteristics. This is typically associated with enhanced metal–support interactions. NiO diffraction peaks were observed on unreduced Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalysts, with 2θ = 43.3° and 62.8°, corresponding to the (200) and (111) planes of NiO (PDF#03-6920), indicating that Ni is primarily dispersed on the Al2O3 surface in the form of NiO; furthermore, the intensity of these diffraction peaks gradually decreased as the Re content increased, and the peaks shifted toward higher angles. After reduction, the reduced Ni/Al2O3 catalyst exhibited distinct metallic Ni0 diffraction peaks at 2θ = 44.5°, 51.9°, and 76.7°, corresponding to the (111), (200), and (220) crystal planes of Ni (PDF#97-3397). In contrast, the NiO diffraction peak intensity in the reduced Ni/Al2O3 and Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst was significantly weakened, with the peak positions exhibiting a slight overall shift toward lower angles. This indicates that Re introduction significantly enhances Ni species dispersion and suppresses grain growth. Notably, characteristic diffraction peaks for Re or ReO x were not detected in the XRD patterns of all Ni–Re catalysts, typically attributed to low Re content and highly dispersed species.

XRD patterns of (A) unreduced Ni/Al2O3, (B) unreduced Ni-2Re/Al2O3, (C) reduced Ni/Al2O3, and (D) reduced Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalysts.

Figures and S3 and Table S3 present the H2-TPR curves and fitted data tables for Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalysts with varying Re contents. As shown in Figures and S3, the Ni/Al2O3 catalyst doped with Re exhibits a distinct low-temperature peak a, primarily corresponding to the reduction of ReO x and the reduction of NiO species closely associated with ReO x clusters. , Reduction peak b originates from the weak interaction between NiO and the carrier, while reduction peak c stems from the strong interaction between NiO and the carrier. − It can be observed that with increasing Re content, peaks b and c associated with NiO gradually shift toward lower temperatures, with peak areas first decreasing and then increasing. This indicates that Re addition enhances the interaction between NiO species and the Al2O3 support, which leads to better dispersion and more efficient reduction of NiO species to metallic Ni. Reduction peak d is attributed to NiAl2O4, which strongly interacts with the Al2O3 support. , The reduction peak d shifts toward lower temperatures with increasing Re content, but beyond a certain Re concentration, it transforms into a reduction peak associated with NiO species. This indicates that Re addition suppresses NiAl2O4 formation. Table S3 shows that the total H2 consumption of Ni–xRe/Al2O3 catalysts consistently exceeds that of Ni/Al2O3 catalysts, reflecting both a higher proportion of reducible Ni species and the contribution from ReO x reduction. After accounting for the contribution of ReO x reduction, the Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst exhibits the largest peak area for the NiO-related reduction peak, indicating the most effective reduction performance. This is further corroborated by the reduction degree data in Table S3. The total H2 consumption values (Table S3) were obtained by integrating the TPR profiles after baseline correction. It should be noted that the contribution from ReO x reduction cannot be quantitatively separated without independent calibration. Therefore, the H2 consumption data are used here for qualitative comparison of reducibility rather than precise quantification. The theoretical H2 consumption was calculated based on the stoichiometry of NiO and ReO x reduction. The differences between the actual and theoretical values are minimal and within an acceptable range, indicating complete reduction of NiO and ReO x , within the limits of experimental error.

The surface valence states and compositions of the reduced Ni/Al2O3 and Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalysts were analyzed via XPS (Figure and Table ). As shown in Figure , both catalysts show Ni0 2p3/2 and Ni2+ 2p3/2 components, with Ni0 (852.6 eV) accounting for just 7.01% in reduced Ni/Al2O3 catalyst, while Ni2+ (855.82 eV) is dominant (92.99%). This suggests a significant presence of Ni2+ species postreduction. , Notably, the binding energy of Ni2+ 2p3/2 near 856 eV typically indicates strong Ni2+–Al2O3 interactions, which increase Ni2+ binding energy and reduce its reducibility. , The introduction of 2 wt % Re increased the proportion of Ni0 2p3/2 (852.78 eV) in reduced Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst to 16.02%, indicating that Re significantly enhances the exposure/apparent reduction state of surface Ni0. The discrepancy between the XPS results and H2-TPR reduction degree can be explained by the different experimental conditions and measurement techniques. XPS reflects the surface Ni2+ content after exposure to air, leading to partial reoxidation of Ni species, while H2-TPR measures the overall reducibility of the catalyst in a hydrogen atmosphere. The surface Ni0 species detected by XPS likely undergo partial reoxidation upon exposure to air, while the bulk NiO is fully reduced under H2-TPR conditions, leading to higher reducibility. Concurrently, the Ni0 binding energy exhibited only a minor shift (ΔBE ≈ +0.18 eV), indicating that Re modulates the electronic environment of Ni without altering its dominant valence state assignment. More crucially, the total Ni loading verified by ICP-OES was nearly identical, allowing us to reasonably infer that the increase in XPS signal intensity and Ni/Re atomic ratio primarily resulted from enhanced enrichment or exposure of Ni on the surface, rather than an increase in total Ni content. As shown in Figure S4, the reduced Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst exhibits multicomponent peaks within the 40–48 eV range, indicating the coexistence of ReO x species with multiple valences and low-valent Re species on the surface. Typically, the Re 4f7/2 of metallic Re0 resides near 40 eV, while ReO2 (Re4+) and higher-valent ReO x species (Re6+/Re7+) appear in the higher binding energy region (42–47 eV). The components labeled at 40.7–40.9 and 43.1–43.3 eV in Figure S4 align with the typical characteristics of coexisting low-valent Re and ReO x species. The absence of Re diffraction peaks in XRD further supports the presence of Re in a highly dispersed, low-concentration form rather than as detectable large crystalline phases.

TEM analysis revealed the metal dispersion and particle size distribution on the catalyst surface. Figure I shows that Re doping significantly reduced the size of Ni particles while enhancing their spatial uniformity. HAADF-STEM and EDX elemental mapping results, shown in Figure II, further confirm the uniform distribution of Ni and Re across the Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst. This uniform distribution indicates that Re enhances metal–support interactions, suppressing Ni aggregation tendencies and promoting more uniform anchoring of Ni on the Al2O3 surface. HRTEM images in Figure S5 confirm these observations. Distinct striations with a lattice spacing of 0.239 nm correspond to the (100) plane of metallic Re over Re/Al2O3 catalyst. For Ni/Al2O3, a lattice spacing of 0.205 nm corresponds to the (111) plane of Ni. The Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst shows a slightly increased lattice spacing of 0.214 nm, suggesting that Re incorporation alters the local lattice environment of Ni, likely due to Ni–Re interactions or strain at the metal–support interface. It should be noted that the presence of metallic Ni observed in HRTEM does not contradict the XPS results, where Ni2+ species appear dominant. HRTEM provides localized structural information from selected regions, while XPS reflects the average chemical state of the outermost surface. Moreover, surface Ni0 species are prone to partial reoxidation upon exposure to air prior to XPS measurement, which may lead to an underestimation of the metallic Ni fraction. In addition, Ni nanoparticles may exhibit a core–shell structure consisting of a metallic Ni0 core and an oxidized Ni2+ shell. Therefore, the combined results suggest that metallic Ni species are indeed formed after reduction, while the catalyst surface is partially oxidized under ambient conditions, leading to the apparent discrepancy between TEM and XPS observations.

The acid sites of the catalyst enhance ammonia adsorption and activation, while the base sites facilitate hydrogen adsorption and activation, enabling control over the selectivity of the product. − The acid–base properties of the catalyst were characterized by TPD analysis of NH3 and CO2 (Figure ; Tables S5 and S6). The acid–base properties of the Remodified catalysts were evidenced by systematic changes in both the intensity and distribution of acidic and basic sites. NH3-TPD profiles exhibited three distinct desorption regions at 70–200, 300–350, and above 350 °C, which can be assigned to weak, medium, and strong acid sites, respectively. Correspondingly, CO2-TPD profiles displayed three desorption regions below 150 °C, between 150 and 450 °C, and above 450 °C, associated with weak, medium, and strong base sites. With increasing Re loading, the intensity and area of the weak acid desorption peaks increased noticeably, accompanied by a gradual shift toward higher temperatures, indicating enhanced stability of weak acid sites. Meanwhile, the population of weak base sites increased progressively upon Re incorporation. Notably, although the amount of strong base sites increased, their desorption peaks shifted to lower temperatures, suggesting a decrease in their intrinsic basic strength. These variations in acid–base characteristics are primarily attributed to the incorporation of ReO x species into the catalyst, leading to the formation of specific Re–O-support structures. In addition, Re doping effectively suppresses the formation of NiAl2O4 species, as evidenced by H2-TPR results, which contributes to the modulation of strong basic sites and promotes a more favorable acid–base synergy in the Remodified catalysts.

Catalytic Performance

Catalyst Screening

A series of experiments were conducted on nickel-based catalysts with varying nickel loadings, and the corresponding data are presented in Figure S6. The results indicate that the 20 wt % nickel-based catalyst exhibited the best overall performance. However, the pure 20 wt % nickel catalyst underperformed in terms of catalytic efficiency and stability, falling significantly short of expectations. To enhance its catalytic performance, the 20 wt % nickel-based catalyst underwent metal modification, and its catalytic activity was subsequently evaluated in the amination reaction of OL, as shown in Figure S7. Interestingly, the modified catalyst exhibited a decrease in performance, suggesting that the high nickel loading had a detrimental effect on the catalyst’s efficacy. Although the 20 wt % Ni catalyst initially exhibited the highest baseline activity, bimetallic modification of this high-loading catalyst unexpectedly led to a decline in performance. In contrast, Re modification of the 10 wt % Ni catalyst yielded superior overall catalytic efficiency. Consequently, the 10 wt % Ni formulation was selected for further optimization (Figure S8). Catalytic efficiency was assessed based on the conversion rate of OL and selectivity toward OA. A series of nickel-based catalysts with varying rhenium contents were prepared for the gas-phase reductive amination of OL. The influence of the Re promoter on the catalytic performance of the nickel-based catalysts was thoroughly investigated, as shown in Figure and Table S7. Initially, the Ni/Al2O3 catalyst showed low OL conversion (9.7%) and OA selectivity (40.1%), suggesting limited catalyst activity. However, the Ni-0.5Re/Al2O3 catalyst reached 90% OL conversion and modestly improved OA selectivity (48.1%), highlighting the beneficial effect of Re in promoting OL conversion. Subsequent optimization of Re content maintained high OL conversion while substantially increasing OA selectivity (64.0%) and suppressing byproducts, attributed to acid–base synergism. TPD analysis confirmed that Re restructured acid–base sites on the catalyst surface, preserving weak acid sites for amination activity while enhancing surface basicity, collectively optimizing OA production.

Effect of Reaction Conditions

The influence of reaction parameters-including reaction temperature, NH3/OL molar ratio, H2/OL molar ratio, and space velocity-on catalytic performance was investigated on the Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst, as shown in the figure. In Figure A, the conversion rate of 1-octanol steadily increases with rising temperature, reaching a maximum at 220 °C. The selectivities for OA, DOA, and TOA progressively decrease, while the selectivity for ON continuously increases. High temperatures not only promote the dehydrogenation of OL but also catalyze the dehydrogenation of the intermediate imine, yielding the byproduct ON. This leads to a decrease in the selectivity of OA, with corresponding reductions in the selectivity of DOA and TOA. Therefore, the optimal temperature is 200 °C. The effect of the H2/OL molar ratio on catalytic performance is shown in Figure B. As the H2/OL molar ratio increases, the OL conversion rate remains largely unchanged. The OA selectivity first increases and then decreases; the byproduct ON selectivity first decreases and then increases, while the DOA and TOA selectivities exhibit a trend of first decreasing and then increasing. Changes in hydrogen concentration may induce hydrogen overflow on the catalyst surface, affecting the hydrogen activation process and influencing OA selectivity. Excessively high hydrogen concentrations occupy active sites on the catalyst surface, leading to the dehydrogenation of the imine intermediate to form ON instead of continuing hydrogenation to produce OA. Comprehensive analysis indicates that a molar ratio of H2/OL of 10 is optimal. The effect of NH3/OL molar ratio on catalytic performance is shown in Figure C. Increasing the NH3/OL molar ratio leads to an increase in OA selectivity up to a maximum, followed by a decline, whereas the selectivity of DOA and TOA decreases initially, reaching a minimum, and then increases. ON selectivity shows a moderate decrease. At low ammonia concentrations, OL primarily forms OA via the reductive amination pathway. With limited ammonia supply as a nitrogen source, OA production remains relatively stable, though selectivity is not particularly favorable. Excess ammonia accelerates the overamination reaction, causing OA to continue reacting with ammonia to form DOA and TOA, thereby reducing the selectivity for OA. Therefore, the optimal NH3/OL molar ratio is determined to be 12.

Effects of reaction parameters on the catalytic activity of the Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst. Effects of the (A) temperature; (B) molar ratio of H2/OL; (C) molar ratio of NH3/OL; and (D) GHSV. Reaction conditions: (A) H2/OL = 10, NH3/OL = 12, GHSV = 4732 mL/g·h; (B) 200 °C, NH3/OL = 12, GHSV = 4732 mL/g·h; (C) 200 °C, H2/OL = 10, GHSV = 4732 mL/g·h; (D) 200 °C, H2/OL = 10, NH3/OL = 12.

The effect of gas-hourly space velocity (GHSV) on catalytic performance is shown in Figure D. As the space velocity increases, the feedstock conversion increases to a maximum and then decreases. Similarly, the yield of the main product OA rises to a peak before declining. In contrast, the selectivity of byproducts DOA and TOA decreases to a minimum and subsequently increases, while the selectivity of ON increases monotonically. With increasing space velocity, the reaction time of the reactants at the active sites of the catalyst decreases, thereby reducing the catalytic activity. Thus, the optimal GHSV is determined to be 4732 mL/g·h.

Catalytic Stability

Catalytic stability tests were conducted on the Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst, with results shown in the Figure . During the 140 h reaction period, the catalyst exhibited generally stable performance with only a slight decline in activity. The OL conversion decreased marginally to 92.58%, while the OA selectivity declined to 56.84% at the end of the test. This minor deactivation may be associated with the partial loss of Re species during the reaction. In the initial 0–90 h period, the OL conversion remained nearly constant, while the OA selectivity increased and the ON selectivity decreased, indicating a gradual adjustment of the catalytic surface toward a more favorable reaction pathway. Overall, the catalyst maintained good stability under continuous-flow conditions over 140 h, with only minor fluctuations in selectivity and a slight downward trend in activity.

Research on Acid–Base Synergistic Reaction Pathway

According to the widely accepted reaction mechanism, , this reaction pathway involves: dehydrogenation of OL to form octanal; condensation of octanal with ammonia to generate the intermediate primary imine; and hydrogenation of the primary imine to OA. NH3-TPD and CO2-TPD analyses demonstrate that Re modification redistributes surface acid–base properties, leading to enhanced weak acid sites, a dominant increase in medium-strength basic sites, and a reduction in the combined total of weak and strong base sites (Figure ). We can infer that this optimized acid–base environment plays a crucial role in C–N bond formation, stabilization of the imine intermediate, and the hydrogenation reaction of the imine (Scheme S1). Weak acid sites associated with ReO x -related structures are capable of stabilizing electrophilic intermediates and transition states during C–N coupling reactions, whereas medium-strength basic sites-attributed to NiO and metallic Ni species-facilitate proton transfer and dehydration steps. The resulting imine intermediates are stabilized by weak acidic sites, preventing uncontrolled secondary condensation.

Furthermore, the balanced distribution of weak acidic and medium-strength basic sites provides a favorable environment for the hydrogenation of imine intermediates. Meanwhile, the redistribution of basic sites, as evidenced by TPD analysis, is likely to moderate excessive basicity, thereby suppressing excessive nucleophilicity and overalkylation. − Overall, the amination of OL over Remodified catalysts can be rationalized as an acid–base-controlled process governed by the synergistic interplay between weak acid and medium-strength base sites.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the performance of Ni–xRe/Al2O3 as a catalyst for the atmospheric-pressure gas-phase reductive amination of OL to prepare primary amines. Results indicate that the introduction of Re significantly enhances the catalytic activity of Ni/Al2O3. The enhanced catalytic performance is primarily attributed to the synergistic effects of Re, which not only promotes hydrogen spillover and accelerates Ni species reduction but also weakens Ni–O–Al interactions, leading to the reconstruction of acid–base properties on the catalyst surface. The Remodified catalyst exhibits a balanced distribution of weak acid and moderately basic sites, facilitating the adsorption and activation of alcohols and ammonia, thereby promoting the selective synthesis of primary amines. The optimal Ni-2Re/Al2O3 catalyst achieves a 99.5% conversion for OL and 64% selectivity for OA. This study reveals a volcano-type relationship between Re content and catalytic performance, emphasizing the critical role of acid–base site distribution equilibrium in regulating reaction pathways.
