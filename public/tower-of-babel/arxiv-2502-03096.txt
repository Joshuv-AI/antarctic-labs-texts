BGK model for rarefied gas in a bounded domain
Hongxu Chen, Christian Klingenberg, Marlies Pirner

Abstract




Abstract:
We study the Bathnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) equation in a smooth bounded domain featuring a diffusive reflection boundary condition with general collision frequency. We prove that the BGK equation admits a unique global solution with an exponential convergence rate if the initial condition is a small perturbation around the global Maxwellian in the
L^{\infty}
space.
For the proof, we utilize the dissipative nature from the linearized BGK operator and establish an
L^{2}
coercive estimate. Next, we derive the a priori estimate by obtaining an
L^{\infty}
bound on the nonlinear operator; this requires a delicate analysis to manage its intrinsic nonlinear structure. Finally, we establish the
L^{\infty}
stability estimate and introduce sequential arguments for the nonlinear BGK operator, thereby concluding both well-posedness and positivity.

1.
Introduction

The dynamics of a monatomic gas without chemical reactions is known to be described by the celebrated Boltzmann equation. But the complicated structure of the collision operator has long been a major obstacle in developing efficient numerical methods
[
15
]
. Under certain assumptions, the complicated interaction terms of the Boltzmann equation can be simplified by a so called BGK approximation, consisting of a collision frequency multiplied by the deviation of the distributions from local Maxwellians. This approximation is constructed in a way such that it has the same main properties of the Boltzmann equation namely conservation of mass, momentum and energy. In addition
,
it has an H-theorem with an entropy inequality leading to an equilibrium which is a Maxwellian.
Our interest in this kind of models comes from the fact that it is
used a lot by engineers, chemists and physicists and in numerical applications, see for
example
[
45
,
49
,
54
]
. BGK models give rise to eﬃcient numerical computations, which are asymptotic preserving, that is they remain eﬃcient even
approaching the hydrodynamic regime
[
8
,
9
,
22
,
24
,
23
,
28
,
46
,
20
]
.

It is used in many applications and there exist many extensions to deal with gas mixtures, ellipsoid statistical (ES-BGK) models, polyatomic molecules, chemical reactions or quantum gases; see for example
[
34
,
39
,
31
,
29
,
53
,
43
,
38
,
13
,
3
,
55
,
32
,
44
,
50
,
11
,
12
,
4
]
.

In this paper, we consider the initial-boundary value problem of the BGK equation in a smooth bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

(1.1)

\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla_{x}F=\nu(M(F)-F),

where
F=F(t,x,v)\geq 0
stands for the velocity distribution function of gas particles with velocity
v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
at time
t\geq 0
and position
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
M(F)
is the local Maxwellian defined as

(1.2)

M(F)(t,x,v)=\frac{\rho(t,x)}{(2\pi T(t,x))^{3/2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{|v-U(t,x)|^{2}}{2T(t,x)}\Big),

where
\rho,U
and
T
correspond to the macroscopic quantities given by the moments of
F
:

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(t,x,v)\mathrm{d}v,

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)U(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(t,x,v)v\mathrm{d}v,

\displaystyle 3\rho(t,x)T(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(t,x,v)|v-U(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}v.

The collision frequency
\nu
takes the following form: for some constants
\eta,\omega
:

\displaystyle\nu(x):=\rho^{\eta}T^{\omega}.

From the numerical point of view, the BGK model considerably simplifies the situation. But mathematical analysis is not necessarily easier, because the relaxation operator involves more non-linearity compared to the bilinear collision operator of the Boltzmann equation.
In
[
47
]
, Perthame established the global existence of weak solutions in whole space for the BGK model with constant collision frequency. Regularity and uniqueness were considered in
[
48
]
under the local existence framework in the torus. In a near-a-global-Maxwellian regime, the global existence in the whole space in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a polynomial convergence to equilibrium was established in
[
7
]
. In
[
56
]
, for a wide class of non-trivial collision frequencies, the existence of a unique global smooth solution is established in the torus under a close-to-equilibrium assumption on the initial data and an exponential decay estimate is established in a high order energy norm. There are also various extensions of the previous result to more complicated BGK-type equations as the BGK equation for gas mixtures in
[
5
]
, the ellipsoidal BGK model
[
57
]
, relativistic and quantum BGK models
[
40
,
6
]
. Moreover, a method to construct sharp convergence rates for the BGK equation is given in
[
1
,
2
]
. All results here, concerning exponential convergence to equilibrium are in the torus and use a high order energy method to show exponential convergence for the non-linear BGK equation in a close-to-equilibrium regime.
On the other hand, there are very few studies on the boundary value problem of the BGK problem.

Reflective boundary conditions play a role in many applications. Therefore, several numerical methods for the BGK equation with reflective boundary conditions have been proposed in the literature, e.g.
[
33
,
51
,
9
]
, also focusing on approaches preserving at the discrete level the asymptotic limit towards Euler equations up to the wall, thus ensuring a smooth transition towards the hydrodynamic regime
[
9
]
.
Therefore, in this article, we aim to provide a theoretical foundation for the boundary value problem and construct a unique global solution to the BGK equation with the diffusive reflection boundary condition. We note that the diffusive boundary condition is one of the most important reflection-type boundary conditions, and it corresponds to the no-slip boundary condition in the hydrodynamic limit, cf.
[
52
]
.

In the presence of the boundary, due to the characteristic nature, the kinetic equation exhibits singularities near the boundary
[
37
,
36
,
41
,
17
,
18
,
21
]
, the high order energy method and Fourier transform method(cf.
[
25
]
) become unavailable. To address the challenges posed by the nonlinear BGK operator, in the paper we focus on constructing a low-regularity solution, specifically achieving
L^{\infty}_{x,v}
control without relying on the embedding
H^{2}_{x}\subset L^{\infty}_{x}
. The linear BGK operator possesses a dissipative property for the microscopic components in the
L^{2}_{v}
energy estimate, which allows us to manage the additional nonlinearity introduced by the BGK operator by seeking a solution in the space
L^{2}_{x,v}\cap L^{\infty}_{x,v}
. Guo proposed this
L^{2}_{x,v}\cap L^{\infty}_{x,v}
framework in
[
35
]
, which established global well-posedness and exponential convergence to the global Maxwellian for the Boltzmann equation including diffuse and specular boundary condition. This breakthrough has significantly advanced the study of the boundary value problem of the Boltzmann equation, we refer to
[
42
]
for the specular boundary and
[
14
,
19
,
10
,
16
]
for intermediate status between pure diffuse reflection and pure specular reflection.
Our main purpose in this paper is to propose an effective method to construct the BGK solution in the low regularity space
L^{2}_{x,v}\cap L^{\infty}_{x,v}
. Thus we only focus on the classical diffuse reflection boundary condition as mentioned earlier. We expect that our methodology can be applied to investigate the relevant problems, such as the well-posedness theory under other boundary conditions, the stationary problem, the regularity issues, the hydrodynamic limits, etc.

To the end, we denote the boundary of the phase space as

\gamma:=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\}.

Let
n=n(x)
be the outward normal direction at
x\in\partial\Omega
. We decompose
\gamma
as

\begin{split}&\gamma_{-}=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}:n(x)\cdot v<0\},\\
&\gamma_{+}=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}:n(x)\cdot v>0\},\\
&\gamma_{0}=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}:n(x)\cdot v=0\}.\end{split}

The diffusive reflection boundary condition is prescribed for the incoming phase space:

\displaystyle F(t,x,v)|_{\gamma_{-}}

\displaystyle=c_{\mu}\mu(v)\int_{n(x)\cdot u>0}F(t,x,u)(n(x)\cdot u)\mathrm{d}u,

where
\mu
corresponds to the normalized global Maxwellian:

\displaystyle\mu(v):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}}.

The constant
c_{\mu}=\sqrt{2\pi}
is chosen to satisfy
\int_{n(x)\cdot v<0}c_{\mu}\mu(v)|n(x)\cdot v|dv=1
so that
c_{\mu}\mu(v)|n(x)\cdot v|
is a probability measure on the half velocity space
\{\mathbb{R}^{3}:n(x)\cdot v<0\}
. Note that the mass flux is vanishing at the boundaries, namely

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(n(x)\cdot v)F(t,x,v)\mathrm{d}v=0,\ x\in\partial\Omega.

We seek the solution around the global Maxwellian, which takes the form
F=\mu+\sqrt{\mu}f
. Then, the following equation for
f
can be derived

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\mathcal{L}f=\Gamma(f)\quad\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&f(t,x,v)|_{\gamma_{-}}=c_{\mu}\sqrt{\mu(v)}\int_{n(x)\cdot u>0}f(t,x,u)\sqrt{\mu(u)}|n(x)\cdot u|\mathrm{d}u\quad\text{for }x\in\partial\Omega,\\
&f(0,x,v)=f_{0}(x,v).\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
\mathcal{L}
is a linearized collision operator, and
\Gamma
a nonlinear collision operator. To define these operators, we first denote the orthonormal basis

\displaystyle\chi_{0}(v):=\sqrt{\mu(v)},\ \ \chi_{i}(v):=v_{i}\sqrt{\mu(v)},\ i=1,2,3,\ \ \chi_{4}(v):=\frac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\sqrt{\mu(v)}.

We denote
\mathbf{P}f
as the macroscopic quantities, which is defined as the
L^{2}_{v}
projection of
f
onto the subspace spanned by
\chi_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}f

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=0}^{4}\langle f,\chi_{i}\rangle\chi_{i}=a(x)\chi_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}b_{i}(x)\chi_{i}+c(x)\chi_{4},

with

a(t,x):=\langle f,\chi_{0}\rangle,\ \ \mathbf{b}(t,x)=(b_{1}(t,x),b_{2}(t,x),b_{3}(t,x)),\ b_{i}(t,x):=\langle f,\chi_{i}\rangle\ \text{ for }i=1,2,3;\ \ c(t,x):=\langle f,\chi_{4}\rangle,

where we have taken the usual inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{v})
:

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(v)g(v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

The linear operator
\mathcal{L}
is then defined as
\mathcal{L}f=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})f
. The nonlinear operator
\Gamma(f)
is defined as the remaining term in the BGK operator (
1.1
):

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Gamma(f):=\frac{\nu(M(\mu+\sqrt{\mu}f)-\mu-\sqrt{\mu}f)}{\sqrt{\mu}}-(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})f.

Here we highlight that
\Gamma(f)
is a nonlinear operator of
f
, which exhibits a higher degree of nonlinearity compared to the bilinear Boltzmann operator. The derivation of
\mathcal{L}f
and the explicit expression of
\Gamma(f)
can be obtained by performing a Taylor expansion around the equilibrium state
(\rho,u,T)=(1,0,1)
.
For the detailed derivation and the associated properties, we refer to the next section ((
2.6
) and (
2.7
) in Lemma
1
).

We denote a velocity weight as

(1.5)

w(v):=(1+|v|)^{\beta}e^{\theta|v|^{2}},\ \ \begin{cases}&\beta\geq 0\text{ for }0<\theta<\frac{1}{4},\\
&\beta>\frac{3}{2}\text{ for }\theta=0.\end{cases}

Such choice of weight guarantee
w^{-2}(v)\in L^{1}_{v}
and
w(v)\sqrt{\mu}\lesssim 1
.

Now we state our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
\Omega
is bounded and smooth. There exists a constant
0<\delta\ll 1
such that

if the initial condition
F_{0}(x,v)=\mu+\sqrt{\mu}f_{0}(x,v)\geq 0
satisfies
\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sqrt{\mu}f_{0}(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}x=0
and

\|wf_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}<\delta,

then there exists a unique solution
F(t,x,v)=\mu+\sqrt{\mu}f(t,x,v)\geq 0
to the problem (
1.3
)

such that
\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sqrt{\mu}f(t,x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}x\equiv 0
, and the following estimate holds true:

\|wf(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\leq Ce^{-\lambda t}\delta.

Here
C>1
,
0<\lambda<1
are constants.

Remark 1
.

The linearized BGK operator
\mathcal{L}f=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})f
corresponds to the microscopic component of
f
. Here, the first component
f
serves as a damping factor, while the second component
\mathbf{P}f
is a compact operator on
L^{2}_{v}
.

For the Boltzmann operator
Q(F,F)
, the linearized operator is given by
\mathcal{L}_{Q}(f):=-\frac{Q(\mu,\sqrt{\mu}f)+Q(\sqrt{\mu}f,\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu}}
. By the Grad estimate in
[
30
]
, this operator can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{Q}f=\nu(v)f-Kf,\ \ Kf=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{k}(v,u)f(u)\mathrm{d}u,\ \ \mathbf{k}(v,u)\lesssim\frac{e^{-C|v-u|^{2}}}{|v-u|}.

Compared with the linear BGK operator, the damping factor is given by
\nu(v)\sim(1+|v|)^{\gamma}
. In the case of hard sphere
\gamma=1
, it provides extra damping. In the case of Maxwell molecule
\gamma=0
, this coincides with the damping factor in the BGK operator.

The integral operator
Kf
is also a compact operator on
L^{2}_{v}
. Under a polynomial or exponential weight
w
from (
1.5
), the kernel
\mathbf{k}(v,u)
enjoys(hard sphere potential)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(v,u)\frac{w(v)}{w(u)}\lesssim\frac{e^{-C|v-u|^{2}}}{|v-u|},

\displaystyle w(v)Kf=w(v)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{k}(v,u)f(u)\mathrm{d}u\lesssim\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{-C|v-u|^{2}}}{|v-u|}\mathrm{d}u\lesssim\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}.

The linearized BGK operator exhibits a similar but more regular property. Since
\mathbf{P}
is an
L^{2}_{v}
-projection onto its kernel, and given the constraint
\theta<\frac{1}{4}
in (
1.5
), we have:

\displaystyle w(v)\mathbf{P}f\lesssim w(v)\sum_{i=0}^{4}\chi_{i}(v)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi_{i}(u)f(u)\mathrm{d}u\lesssim\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\sum_{i=0}^{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\chi_{i}(u)w^{-1}(u)\lesssim\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}.

The integral kernel of the linearized BGK operator does not exhibit a singularity in
|v-u|
. We expect this smoother structure to enhance the regularity of solutions to the BGK equation in boundary value problems, compared with the regularity of the Boltzmann equation studied in
[
37
]
. This will be left for future study.

Remark 2
.

While the linearized BGK operator has a simpler structure than its Boltzmann counterpart, its nonlinear term
\Gamma(f)
is more complex. The nonlinear Boltzmann operator
\Gamma_{Q}(f,f)=\frac{Q(\sqrt{\mu}f,\sqrt{\mu}f)}{\sqrt{\mu}}
has a bilinear form. This structure yields the key weighted estimate:
\|\nu^{-1}w\Gamma_{Q}(f,f)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\lesssim\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}^{2}
in the
L^{2}_{x,v}-L^{\infty}_{x,v}
argument. Furthermore, the sequential argument and uniqueness proof follow directly, as the bilinearity implies:

\displaystyle\|\nu^{-1}w[\Gamma_{Q}(f_{1}-f_{2},f_{1})+\Gamma(f_{2},f_{1}-f_{2})]\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\lesssim\|w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}[\|wf_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}+\|wf_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}].

In contrast, the nonlinear BGK operator
\Gamma(f)
is defined in (
2.7
) via a Taylor expansion, and exhibits a more intricate nonlinearity. Although the last term in (
2.7
) appears to be trilinear, the coefficients
\mathcal{Q}_{ij}
in (
2.5
) depend nonlinearly on the macroscopic quantities
(\rho,U,T)
of
F
, which again depend on the perturbed solution
f
. This nonlinear dependency poses a major challenge for deriving the necessary
L^{\infty}_{x,v}
estimate for
\|w\Gamma(f)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}
. We refer to Lemma
5
for details.

Moreover, new sequential equations and stability estimate
\|w\Gamma(f_{1})-\Gamma(f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}
need to be introduced to establish the existence, uniqueness, and positivity of the solution. We refer to the stability estimate in Section
2.3
and the new sequential argument in Section
4.1
.

Moreover, to establish the solution’s existence, uniqueness, and positivity, we must introduce a new iterative scheme and a corresponding stability estimate of the nonlinear operator
\|w\Gamma(f_{1})-\Gamma(f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}
. We refer to the stability estimate in Section
2.3
and the sequential argument in Section
4.1
.

As explained in Remark
2
, our main contribution in this paper can be summarized as follows:

(1)

Nonlinear estimates:
We derive the
L^{\infty}_{x,v}
estimate for the nonlinear BGK operator
\Gamma(f)
and establish the a priori estimate in the
L^{2}
-
L^{\infty}
framework,

(2)

Well-posedness:
We derive the
L^{\infty}_{x,v}
stability estimate for
\Gamma(f)
and establish a new iterative scheme to prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution.

(3)

Positivity:
We establish the positivity of the solution through a new sequential argument.

Outline.
In Section
2
, we will derive the expressions of the BGK operator
\mathcal{L}
and
\Gamma
, and establish their fundamental properties. In Section
3
, we will derive the
L^{2}
estimate for the linear BGK equation by leveraging the coercive property of
\mathcal{L}
. Finally, in Section
4
, we conclude Theorem
1
by constructing the
L^{\infty}
estimate through the method of characteristics and employing an iterative argument for the existence and uniqueness of the solution.

Lemma 4

We can control the macroscopic quantities using the

f

If

\displaystyle\|\rho-1,U,T-1\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim\delta.

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|Q_{i}|\mathrm{d}\vartheta\lesssim 1,

(2.12)

We can estimate the density as

\displaystyle|\rho(t,x)-1|=\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}[\mu+\sqrt{\mu}f]\mathrm{d}v-1\Big|\leq\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sqrt{\mu}w^{-1}\mathrm{d}v\leq C\delta.

Then we estimate the momentum as

\displaystyle|\rho(t,x)U(t,x)|=\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}[\mu+\sqrt{\mu}f]v\mathrm{d}v\Big|\leq\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sqrt{\mu(v)}w^{-1}(v)|v|\mathrm{d}v\leq C\delta.

Thus

\displaystyle|U(t,x)|\leq\frac{C\delta}{\inf\{\rho(t,x)\}}\leq\frac{C\delta}{1-C\delta}\leq 2C\delta.

Last we compute the energy as

\displaystyle|3\rho(t,x)T(t,x)-3|=\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\mu+\sqrt{\mu}f)|v-U(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}v-3\Big|

\displaystyle\leq|U(t,x)|^{2}+\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sqrt{\mu(v)}w^{-1}(v)|v-U(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2}+\|wf\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sqrt{\mu(v)}w^{-1}(v)[|v|^{2}+|U(t,x)|^{2}]\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2}+C\delta(C+(2C\delta)^{2})\lesssim\delta.

With

\displaystyle T(t,x)-1=\frac{\rho(t,x)T(t,x)-1}{\rho(t,x)}-\frac{\rho(t,x)-1}{\rho(t,x)},

we derive that,

\displaystyle|T(t,x)-1|\lesssim\frac{\delta}{1-C\delta}+\frac{\delta}{1-C\delta}\lesssim\delta.

We conclude (
2.10
).

Next we prove (
2.11
). Recall the definition of
\rho_{\vartheta},U_{\vartheta},T_{\vartheta}
in Lemma
1
, from (
2.10
) it is straightforward to verify that for some
C

(2.13)

\displaystyle|\rho_{\vartheta}-1,U_{\vartheta},T_{\vartheta}-1|\leq C\delta.

By the property of
Q_{i}
in (
2.8
), we apply (
2.13
) to control the denominator as

\displaystyle 1\lesssim r_{1,i}(1-C\delta)^{r_{2,i}}(1-C\delta)^{r_{3,i}}\leq R_{i}(\rho_{\vartheta},T_{\vartheta}).

We control the numerator as

\displaystyle P_{i}(\rho_{\vartheta},U_{\vartheta},T_{\vartheta})\lesssim\sum_{m\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}|a_{m}|(1+C\delta)^{m_{1}}(C\delta)^{m_{2}+m_{3}+m_{4}}(1+C\delta)^{m_{5}}\lesssim 1.

Last we prove (

\displaystyle P_{ij}(\rho_{\vartheta},v_{1}-U_{\vartheta,1},v_{2}-U_{\vartheta,2},v_{3}-U_{\vartheta,3},U_{\vartheta,1},U_{\vartheta,2},U_{\vartheta,3},T_{\vartheta})

\displaystyle|e^{2\lambda t}w(\nu_{p}(f_{1})-\nu_{p}(f_{2}))f_{1}|\lesssim\|e^{\lambda t}w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\|e^{\lambda t}wf_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\lesssim\delta\|e^{\lambda t}w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}.

Thus (
2.23
) holds for
\Gamma_{2}
.

Next we estimate
\Gamma_{3}(f)=\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq 4}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(f)(1-\vartheta)\mathrm{d}\vartheta\langle f,\chi_{i}\rangle\langle f,\chi_{j}\rangle\mu^{-1/2}
. We compute that

\displaystyle e^{2\lambda t}|w\Gamma_{3}(f_{1})-w\Gamma_{3}(f_{2})|\leq w\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq 4}\int_{0}^{1}[\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(f_{1})-\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(f_{2})](1-\vartheta)\mathrm{d}\vartheta\langle e^{\lambda t}f_{1},\chi_{i}\rangle\langle e^{\lambda t}f_{1},\chi_{j}\rangle\mu^{-1/2}

\displaystyle+w\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq 4}\int_{0}^{1}|\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(f_{2})|(1-\vartheta)\mathrm{d}\vartheta[\langle e^{\lambda t}(f_{1}-f_{2}),\chi_{i}\rangle\langle e^{\lambda t}f_{1},\chi_{j}\rangle+\langle e^{\lambda t}f_{2},\chi_{i}\rangle\langle e^{\lambda t}(f_{1}-f_{2}),\chi_{j}\rangle]\mu^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|e^{\lambda t}wf_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}^{2}\|w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}+\|e^{\lambda t}w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}[\|e^{\lambda t}wf_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}+\|e^{\lambda t}wf_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}]

(2.25)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\|e^{\lambda t}w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}.

Here we have applied (
2.12
) and (
2.17
). Thus (
2.23
) holds for
\Gamma_{3}
.

Last we estimate
\Gamma_{4}(f)=\nu_{p}(f)\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq 4}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(f)(1-\vartheta)\mathrm{d}\vartheta\langle f,\chi_{i}\rangle\langle f,\chi_{j}\rangle\mu^{-1/2}
. We compute that

\displaystyle e^{2\lambda t}|w\Gamma_{4}(f_{1})-w\Gamma_{4}(f_{2})|\leq e^{2\lambda t}|\nu_{p}(f_{1})-\nu_{p}(f_{2})|w\int_{0}^{1}|\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(f_{1})|(1-\vartheta)\mathrm{d}\vartheta\langle f_{1},\chi_{i}\rangle\langle f_{1},\chi_{j}\rangle\mu^{-1/2}

\displaystyle+|\nu_{p}(f_{2})|e^{\lambda t}|w\Gamma_{3}(f_{1})-w\Gamma_{3}(f_{2})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|e^{\lambda t}w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\|e^{\lambda t}wf_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}^{2}+\delta\|e^{\lambda t}wf_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\|e^{\lambda t}w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\lesssim\delta\|e^{\lambda t}w(f_{1}-f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}.

In the RHS of the first line, we have applied (
2.24
), (
2.12
). In the last line, we have applied (
2.1
), (
2.11
), and (
2.25
).

We conclude the lemma.

3.
L^{2}
dissipation estimate

In this section, we consider the solution to the linearized BGK equation

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\mathcal{L}f

\displaystyle=g,

with the source term
g=g(t,x,v)
satisfying

(3.2)

\mathbf{P}g=0.

The boundary condition of
f
is given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle f(t,x,v)|_{\gamma_{-}}=c_{\mu}\sqrt{\mu(v)}\int_{n(x)\cdot u>0}(n(x)\cdot u)f(t,x,u)\sqrt{\mu(u)}\mathrm{d}u.

We denote

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}f:=c_{\mu}\sqrt{\mu(v)}\int_{n(x)\cdot u>0}(n(x)\cdot u)f(t,x,u)\sqrt{\mu(u)}\mathrm{d}u.

We will prove the following
L^{2}
-dissipation result.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Omega
be an arbitrary bounded and
C^{1}
domain. There exists
0<\lambda\ll 1
such that if the initial data
f_{0}
and source data
g
satisfy (
3.2
) and

\|f_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}g(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty,

then there exists a unique solution to the problem

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\mathcal{L}f=g,\\
&f(0,x,v)=f_{0}(x,v),\ f|_{\gamma_{-}}=P_{\gamma}f.\end{cases}

Moreover, it holds that

(3.5)

\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\lesssim e^{-2\lambda t}\Big\{\|f_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}g(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big\},\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

To prove Proposition
2
, we need to have the following
L^{2}
dissipation estimate of the macroscopic quantities. Denote

\displaystyle|f|_{2,+}^{2}:=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{n(x)\cdot v>0}|f(t,x,v)|^{2}(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x},\ \mathrm{d}S_{x}\text{ is the surface integral.}

Lemma 8
.

Suppose
f
solves the following equation,

(3.6)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\mathcal{L}f=g,

with boundary condition (
3.3
). Here
g
does not need to satisfy the condition (
3.2
). It holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbf{P}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim G(t)-G(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})f\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|g(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\int_{0}^{t}|(I-P_{\gamma})f(s)|_{2,+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
G(t)
is a functional of
f(t,x,v)
such that
|G(t)|\lesssim\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}
holds true for any
t\geq 0
.

The proof of the macroscopic dissipation estimate is standard. For completeness, we refer to the proof provided in the appendix.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

We prove the decay estimate (
3.5
). Multiplying (
3.4
) with
e^{\lambda t}
we get

(3.7)

[\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\mathcal{L}](e^{\lambda t}f)=\lambda e^{\lambda t}f+e^{\lambda t}g.

Applying Green’s identity to (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\|e^{\lambda t}f(t)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})e^{\lambda s}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}|(I-P_{\gamma})e^{\lambda s}f(s)|_{2,+}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

(3.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}g(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Here, we have applied
\mathbf{P}(e^{\lambda t}g)=0
. We have also applied the following coercive property of the diffuse reflection boundary condition:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f(t,x,v)|^{2}(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{n(x)\cdot v>0}|(I-P_{\gamma})f+P_{\gamma}f|^{2}(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{n(x)\cdot v<0}|P_{\gamma}f|^{2}(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{n(x)\cdot v>0}|(I-P_{\gamma})f|^{2}(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x}+2\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{n(x)\cdot v>0}fP_{\gamma}f(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x}

\displaystyle-2\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{n(x)\cdot v>0}|P_{\gamma}f|^{2}(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|P_{\gamma}f|^{2}(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}S_{x}

\displaystyle=|(I-P_{\gamma})f|_{2,+}^{2}+\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{n(x)\cdot u>0}(n(x)\cdot u)f(u)\sqrt{\mu(u)}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}S_{x}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\times[(2-2)\int_{n(x)\cdot v>0}c_{\mu}\mu(v)(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{\mu}\mu(v)(n(x)\cdot v)\mathrm{d}v]

\displaystyle=|(I-P_{\gamma})f|_{2,+}^{2}.

Next, we apply Lemma
8
to (
3.7
), then we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}\mathbf{P}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim G(t)-G(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|\lambda e^{\lambda s}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,v}}\mathrm{d}s

(3.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}g(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}|e^{\lambda s}(I-P_{\gamma})f(s)|_{2,+}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

where
|G(t)|\lesssim\|e^{\lambda t}f(t)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}
. Therefore, multiplying (

) by a small constant
\varepsilon
and adding the resultant to (
3.8
), we obtain that for some
C>0

\displaystyle(1-C\varepsilon)\|e^{\lambda t}f(t)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\Big\{(1-C\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}\mathbf{P}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\Big\}

\displaystyle+(1-C\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{t}|e^{\lambda s}(I-P_{\gamma})f(s)|_{2,+}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C(\lambda+\lambda^{2})\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+C\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}g(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Since
\|e^{\lambda s}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\|e^{\lambda s}\mathbf{P}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}=\|e^{\lambda s}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}
, we further obtain that for

\displaystyle\|e^{\lambda t}f(t)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C(\lambda+\lambda^{2})\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}f(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+C\|f(0)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\lambda s}g(s)\|_{L^{2}_{x,v}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Last we let
\lambda\ll 1
be such that
C(\lambda+\lambda^{2})\leq\varepsilon
, then the above estimate gives the desired decay estimate (
3.5
). We conclude the proof of Proposition
2
.
∎
