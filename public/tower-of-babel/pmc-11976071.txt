The impact of fixed and removable dental prostheses on the periodontal health of abutment teeth: A Retro-Prospective Comparative Study
Authors: Arwa U Alsaggaf, Ahlam Almuwallad, Lina Mirza, Layan Alhemayed, Halema Zamzami, Ehab Azab, Hesham Alhazmi, Amin Marghalani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeriodontal disease is the most prevalent oral condition worldwide, resulting from a prolonged inflammatory reaction triggered by the buildup of dental plaque. Many studies have concluded that there is a strong association between periodontal health and tooth restorations, as the periodontium must be healthy for the restoration to last.Aimto evaluate the impact of fixed and removable dental prostheses on the periodontal health of abutment teeth.MethodologyThis was a retro-prospective cohort study of 112 abutment teeth collected from 34 patients who received a total of 47 fixed (81 abutments) and 14 removable (31 abutments) dental prostheses treated by senior dental students under supervision during the past two years. Patients were recalled for clinical and radiographic examinations to assess periodontal parameters of abutments, including pocket depth (PD), clinical attachment loss (CAL), presence of plaque, and bleeding on probing (BOP), and to evaluate fixed and removable prostheses.ResultsMost of the prostheses were fixed (72.32 %), and most of them were bridges (60.71 %) located in the anterior region of the mouth (58.56 %). The study findings showed a significant increase (mean = 0.46 mm) in clinical attachment loss after prosthesis placement (p < 0.01), while no significant difference was observed in pocket depth (p = 0.062). Furthermore, there was a significant increase in plaque at the follow-up visit compared to the baseline visit (p < 0.01) for only the fixed dental prostheses. There was no significant difference between fixed and removable prostheses regarding their impact on the periodontal health of abutments.ConclusionThe study found that abutment teeth had increased clinical attachment loss after receiving dental prostheses, highlighting the need for regular maintenance and patient education to maintain periodontal health.Keywords: Periodontal Health, Dental Prosthesis, Fixed prosthesis, Removable prosthesis, Prosthesis abutment, Periodontal parameters

Introduction

Periodontal health is characterized by the absence of inflammatory periodontal disorders, which allows individuals to function normally and prevents further complications. Those who have effectively treated periodontal disease and strive to maintain a clinically inflammation-free oral condition fall into this category of healthy periodontium (Chapple et al., 2018). However, periodontal disease is the most prevalent oral condition worldwide and results from a prolonged inflammatory reaction triggered by the deposition of dental plaque (Raitapuro-Murray et al., 2014). Periodontal disease has a significant impact on a person’s oral health and quality of life (Meusel et al., 2015, Needleman et al., 2004). This inflammatory process can ultimately lead to tooth loss if left untreated (Papapanou et al., 2018).

Dental prosthetic treatment is directed toward replacing lost teeth to allow for normal function and aesthetics, beside improve patient quality of life. (Øzhayat and Gotfredsen, 2019, AlSaggaf et al., 2024, Montero et al., 2013). Depending on the patient's needs, fixed and/or removable prostheses supported by the oral mucosa, natural teeth, or dental implants are provided (Malik & Yazdanie, 2021). There may be circumstances where the use of a Fixed Dental Prosthesis (FDP) is restricted by the patient’s general health, oral health, abutment tooth condition, financial limitations, or time constraints (de Pablo et. al., 2009) The cost-effective alternative treatment option is Removable Partial Dentures (RPD), which preserve oral tissue (Almeida et al., 2020). However, from a psychological standpoint, the FDP is preferable for most patients (Malik and Yazdanie, 2021, Petridis and Hempton, 2001).

It is well-established that there is a strong association between periodontal health and tooth restoration. A healthy periodontium is essential for the long-term success of the restoration and for maintaining the stability of the teeth (Basnyat et al., 2020, Malik and Yazdanie, 2021, Muddugangadhar et al., 2011, Mojon et al., 1995, Hsu et al., 2015). Furthermore, FDP or RPD can be used as a restorative treatment option in patients with a history of periodontitis without further compromising periodontal condition (Montero et al., 2022 Jun, Gotfredsen et al., 2022 Jun).

Gingivitis and periodontitis are the major consequences of improper prosthodontic treatment (Basnyat et al., 2020, Srimaneepong et al., 2022). Numerous clinical studies have investigated the significant influence of dental restorations on periodontal health (Malik and Yazdanie, 2021, Basnyat et al., 2020, Milardović Ortolan et al., 2012, Zlatanovska et al., 2017). Cleanability is influenced by many factors, some of which include subgingival margin placement, insufficient marginal adaptation, and poorly contoured restorations. These factors can adversely affect periodontal health and lead to localized periodontal inflammation (Milardović Ortolan et al., 2012). Therefore, patients who have undergone oral rehabilitation must be closely monitored and encouraged to practice good oral hygiene to ensure successful treatment (Oruba et al., 2014, Tada et al., 2015, Dula et al., 2015).

Few studies have focused on the effects of FDP on periodontal health and RPD as alternative treatment options for replacing missing teeth (Hind Majed, 2021, Basnyat et al., 2020, Milardović Ortolan et al., 2012). Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of FDP and RPD on the periodontal health of abutments.

Materials and Methods

Ethical approva l

In January 2023, the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee granted full approval to the principal investigator regarding the ethics of the scientific research to conduct the study at the Umm Al-Qura University Dental Teaching Hospital in Makkah, Saudi Arabia (UQUDentH). (number: HAPO-02-K-012–2023-01–1397).

Study Design and Participant selection

This retro-prospective study included adult patients who (1) received a FDP or RPD from senior dental students under the direct supervision of a prosthodontist with at least six months before the study began, and (2) underwent a periodontal clinical parameter assessment at a re-evaluation visit before receiving prosthetic treatment (baseline for this study). The patients had a follow-up periodontal assessment after the start of the study. Patients with uncontrolled systemic diseases, those on long-term medications affecting the periodontium, individuals with active periodontal disease, or those who had undergone periodontal surgical therapy were excluded from the study. Convenience sampling was used to select participants, and a total of 67 individuals were contacted to attend the follow-up visit.

Dental prosthesis

The selection of the type of prosthetic treatment was based on the agreed treatment plan by academic instructor at least 6 months before the recruitment. Mostly it was chosen based on the number of missing teeth, distal abutments availability, and crown/root ratio. (McCracken WL., 1958; Jacobs DJ et al., 2002) FDP were fabricated as porcelain-fused-to-metal using Nickle chromium alloys, glass ceramics or zirconia using Zirconium oxide (Jacobs DJ et al., 2002; Wassell R et al., 2002a’ 2002b). While RPD were fabricated as metallic RPDs using Cobalt chromium alloys and Polymethyl methacrylate acrylic resin (McCracken WL., 1958; Ben-Ur Z et al., 1999, Becker CM et.al., 1994, LaVere AM et al., 1973).

Follow-up visit data

Prior to data collection, all participants signed informed consent forms.

During the visits, the following information was collected: age, gender, date of visit, their current oral hygiene regimen and whether they had received proper oral hygiene instructions after prosthetic treatment. Previous periodontal evaluation records (baseline) were also reviewed. The same charts used to assess periodontal clinical parameters in baseline was used for follow-up visit.

Oral and radiographic examination

Basic diagnostic instruments, including a mouth mirror, periodontal probe, and explorer, were used to perform the oral examinations. The hard and soft tissues of the abutments were examined using a periodontal chart approved by the UQUDentH. A periodontal probe was used to record the periodontal parameters of the abutments, including PD and CAL at six sites per tooth. In addition, a binary (yes/no) registration system was used to assess BOP (Mühlemann & Son, 1971) and the presence or absence of dental plaque (Ainamo & Bay, 1975). Radiographs were taken using the bite-wing technique to evaluate the prosthesis margins and to ensure that there was no excess cement as well as caries for all prosthesis.

Evaluation of prostheses

All abutments of FDP or RPD were examined to identify any defects that could affect the periodontium. For all prosthesis caries, recurrent caries, and mobility were evaluated. For FDP, the following types of defects were inspected: open margin, sensitivity, overcontoured margin, undercontoured margin, chipped porcelain, tight contact, open contact, high occlusion, and excess cement. (Pjetursson et al., 2015). For RPD, the following aspects were examined: clasp fracture, rest fracture, loss of retention, discoloration, and food impaction (Bergman et al., 1982). Following clinical examination, patients received reinforced instructions on oral hygiene maintenance and the use of interdental brushes and super floss.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted for the study participants. The Shapiro–Wilk test showed that normal distributions were not detected. Two nonparametric tests were used: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare mean pocket depth and clinical attachment loss between baseline and follow-up visits (6 months or more), and the McNemar test to assess plaque and bleeding on probing during the same visits. Additionally, logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between prosthesis type and failure, adjusting for age and gender. Data analysis was performed using Stata version 18, with a significance level of 5 % (α = 0.05).

Results

In this study, 34 patients were included, with a mean follow-up period of 11.46 months (95 % CI: 10.34–12.59). More than half of the prostheses (56.25 %) were for females, and the majority (65.77 %) were located in the anterior region of the mouth. Of all the prostheses, 58.56 % were in the upper dental arch. Among the prostheses, 72.32 % were FDPs, while 27.68 % were RPDs. For the FDP, 60.71 % were bridges (see Table 1). The mean number of missing teeth substituted by FDPs was 0.85 (95 % CI: 0.67–1.03), compared to 2.92 (95 % CI: 1.66–4.20) for RPDs.

Discussion

This study was conducted to evaluate periodontal health of the abutment of dental prostheses provided by dental students at the UQUDentH in an academic setting. The study included 112 abutments that were treated within the past two years, with a minimum follow-up period of six months. This study showed an increased clinical attachment loss in the abutments of both prostheses. These findings were consistent with a previous study (Almeida et al., 2019) that focused on RPD and reported a similar difference in clinical attachment loss. However, it is important to stress that this increase in clinical attachment loss is not solely due to the detrimental effects of prostheses on periodontal health. The role of patient follow-up in clinical studies cannot be overstated, as patients with prosthesis issues were more likely to be present for follow-up visits, which may have influenced the observed increase in clinical attachment loss.

A previous study (Silness et al.) indicated that several reasons, including open and overhanging margins, surface roughness, and overcontoured prostheses, cause poor gingival health surrounding crowns. These factors make it difficult to maintain proper oral hygiene and compromise gingival health. Consistent with this study, we observed a significant increase in plaque accumulation in patients after FDP compared to pre-prosthetic treatment. This finding suggests that FDP may contribute to increased plaque accumulation, which is consistent with the study conducted by Zlatanovska et al. (2017). Zlatanovska et al. reported that after providing instructions to maintain oral hygiene, they observed a subsequent improvement in oral hygiene and a reduced plaque index. These findings offer a promising outlook, suggesting that the negative impact of FDP on oral health can be minimized through proper patient education and oral hygiene instructions.

In contrast to our findings, a 15-year follow-up study by Valderhaug et al. 1993 reported no significant difference in plaque index values between teeth with crowns and control teeth. The discrepancy in the results could be attributed to the multiple follow-ups in their study, suggesting that the impact of FDP on plaque accumulation varies depending on many factors.

In terms of prosthetic failures that contributed to increased plaque accumulation, the study’s findings revealed that the most common reasons for FDP failure were open margins (32 %), overcontoured margins (30 %), and sensitivity (15 %). Suggested factors contributing to the complications include the lack of highly skilled technicians or advanced equipment to achieve optimal precision and quality in the fabrication of dental prostheses in the teaching hospital dental laboratory, which may affect the quality of the lab work. A systematic review conducted by Pjetursson et al. (2015) evaluated the five-year survival rate of tooth-supported FPDs as well as the incidence of complications. It identified marginal discoloration as the most frequent complication, occurring in 21.4 % of patients with metal-ceramic restorations, followed by chipped porcelain (8.6 %) and ceramic fracture (5.0 %). Another systematic review evaluated the five-year survival and complications of single crowns and found that ceramic chipping occurred in 2.6 % of metal-ceramic crowns, followed by marginal discoloration (1.8 %) (Sailer et al., 2015). In addition, a systematic review in 2022 showed that monolithic zirconia crowns’ most common complications were increased bleeding on probing, secondary caries, bacterial plaque, and crown chipping and fracture (Leitao et al., 2022, Vozzo et al., 2023). However, Alarcón-Sánchez et al. Reported that zirconia prostheses displayed less harm to periodontal health with reduced levels of inflammation and were easier to maintain clean compared to metal-ceramic ones (Alarcon-Sanchez et al., 2023). Resin-bonded FPDs were not included in the present study due to patient preferences. However, the biological and mechanical complications associated with them are unique compared to the other types of FPDs requiring different patient criteria. These complications include debonding (37.32 %), re-cementation (21.83 %), fracture of the prosthesis framework (8.45 %), chipping (7.04 %), caries (7.04 %), and periodontal problems (2.82 %) (Santos et. Al., 2023). Implant prostheses and overdenture could also be considered for future studies as they present different complications and risks toward periodontal health (de Castro et al., 2022, Motta and V. C. F., Santos L.E., Da Silva M.A., Leite G.B., Fernandes J.C., Fernandes G.V. , 2023).

Although the study has several strengths, such as a standardized treatment approach and consistency in work procedures performed by the same dental laboratory. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The limited sample size which limits the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, extending the follow-up period might help in assessing any potential complications or changes that may arise over time and provide a better understanding of the long-term effects of dental prostheses on periodontal health. Finally, a randomized clinical trial might provide a more concise result. These recommendations would provide insightful information about patients’ experiences and opinions regarding treatment outcomes and might be helpful for future studies to generate more robust and generalizable findings on the impact of dental prostheses provided by dental students.

Conclusion

The findings show an increase in clinical attachment loss for the abutment teeth after receiving prostheses. Furthermore, there was an increase in plaque accumulation with fixed dental prostheses.
