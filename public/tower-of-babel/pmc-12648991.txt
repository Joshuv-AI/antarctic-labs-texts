How does the mapped ICD data in an EHR system compare to the hospital DAD data in Alberta, Canada?
Authors: Namneet Sandhu, Debbie Onos, Bing Li, Danielle A Southern, Jeffrey A Bakal, Abdel Aziz Shaheen, Glenda Tower, Kathleen Addison, Sarah Whittle, Hude Quan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveCoding by human coders is burdensome to healthcare systems requiring advanced computerized techniques. Epic has collaborated with Intelligent Medical Objects (IMO) solution to integrate a solution where the clinical interface terminology is mapped to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). This study assesses the agreement between the solution mapped ICD-10-CA codes in Alberta, Canada’s EHR system (based on Epic) to the human coded ICD-10-CA codes in hospital Discharge Abstract Database (DAD).Design and settingIn this retrospective analysis conducted in Alberta, Canada, we linked records in the acute care hospital DAD with the province wide EHR system for admissions between April 2021 and March 2024.Main outcome(s) and measure(s)The primary outcome was the level of agreement between the solution mapped ICD-10-CA codes from the ‘hospital problem list’ in the EHR and hospital DAD data. The analysis was conducted at 3-digit and 4-digit ICD code level for main diagnosis and any diagnosis, and further stratified by physician specialty, hospital type and location, and length-of-stay.ResultsA total of 603,437 unique hospital records were linked between hospital DAD and EHR. The average level of agreement at 3-digit level of ICD-10-CA code for main diagnosis was 47.5% and any diagnosis was 37.0%. The average number of diagnoses coded by human coders in hospital DAD was higher than the solution mapped data in EHR. The agreement varied by specialty and length-of-stay with specialties with more complex patients and longer stays showing the lowest levels of agreement.ConclusionLevel of agreement between solution mapped EHR and hospital DAD for ICD-10-CA data was low, indicating significant differences between terminology mappings and the coding process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13716-3.Keywords: International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Electronic health records, Mapped ICD codes

Introduction

Hospital data, specifically administrative coded data, are collected by human coders who code the data using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) system developed by the World Health Organisation (WHO) [1]. Several countries have established standardized guidelines and developed training programs to train human coders in a systematic manner. Globally, although this is a well-established system to collect administrative data, coding by human coders remains a challenging, time-consuming, and resource-oriented process that has become burdensome to the healthcare systems [2–6]. In Canada, pressures to meet coding quotas due to limited budgets and staffing and varying jurisdictional requirements in areas where national directives are either absent or inconsistent have been well recognized [7–9]. Australia as well, faced with similar issues of increased workload pressures and backlogs, had its health information management and clinical coding workforce reports express a major need for more efficient coding processes [10].

Several solutions have been suggested to mitigate the challenging clinical coding situation met by the professional coders worldwide. Among these are system level improvements to chart documentation, implementation of standardized templates in electronic medical records (EMRs) and automated clinical coding [7, 10]. A global effort is seen in building new systems that include Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques such as computer-assisted coding (CAC) to reduce this burden and strengthen coded data quality [2, 4–6, 10–13]. Improved coding processes have been reported with these new AI techniques where clinical coders remain an essential part of the automated clinical coding [10]. Even World Health Organization has developed 11th version of ICD with enhanced digital capabilities allowing integration of ICD-11 into software services, including coding support applications [14].

In Canada, current practice involves trained clinical coders coding hospital data to administrative health database known as hospital Discharge Abstract Database (DAD) using standard coding guidelines developed by the national Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI). All human coders are required to complete a 2-year program offered through an accredited institution and obtain certification by Canadian Health Information Management Association (CHIMA). Human coders generally read through various documentation in the patient chart such as patient discharge summary, progress notes by physician or nurse, consultations, orders, imaging reports, etc. to abstract required information to ICD-10-CA following CIHI standards and classification systems. ICD-10-CA is the tenth revision of ICD developed by WHO with Canada specific enhancements established by CIHI.

In Alberta, Canada, a new Electronic Health Record (EHR) system called Connect Care by health information system vendor, Epic Systems Corporation® (referred as Epic in the next sections), has been rolled out at Alberta Health Services (AHS) sites across the province [15]. The term ‘EHR’ is referring to Connect Care in the remaining sections of this paper.

Terminology solutions are implemented to support clinicians in documenting clinically relevant information at the point of care. In Alberta, the Intelligent Medical Objects (IMO) Core CA solution (i.e., the version that supports ICD-10-CA specifically) has been implemented as the interface terminology, which includes SNOMED CT concept and classification (ICD-10-CA) codes [16, 17].

The comprehensive EHR system in Alberta, implemented for better integrated clinical care, provides an ideal opportunity to assess the solution mapped ICD-10-CA codes (obtained through mapping terminologies to classification disease codes) from the point of care clinical data. By comparing the solution mapped codes in the EHR to the human-coded hospital DAD codes, we aim to establish a foundational understanding of the mapping solution’s current performance and identify opportunities for improvement – whether in the mapping processes themselves or through implementation of advanced applications to support more efficient collection of accurate and complete ICD coded data. Given hospital DAD is a validated database, used for both administrative and research purposes, it serves as an ideal reference standard for ICD-10-CA coded data to compare the EHR solution mapped codes with [18, 19].

Therefore, the overall objective of this study is to assess the agreement between the coding through the mapping solution in EHR and the human coding approach using hospital DAD.

Methodology

Study setting and data sources

Connect Care: In Alberta, Canada, Connect Care (by Epic) is a province wide comprehensive EHR system that captures a wide array of health information such as provider clinical notes, laboratory data, procedures and medications, and health problems, etc. as the patient encounters the healthcare system [15]. This new system has advanced features and functionalities facilitating integrated care across the entire province, serving a population of approximately 4.4 million. Epic has collaborated with Intelligent Medical Objects (IMO) to support a digital solution that maps IMO’s proprietary clinical interface terminology to International Classification of Diseases-10-Canada (ICD-10-CA) and SNOMED codes [16, 17]. As outlined by Rosenbloom et al., clinical interface terminology refers to the “systematic collection of health care-related terms” supporting clinicians in capturing patient information in electronic computer systems [20]. IMO’s interface terminology solution consists of a vast list of clinical terms which are designed to be used as a structured pick list, where each interface term has a proprietary identifier and has been mapped to both SNOMED CT and ICD-10-CA. At the point of care, clinicians search and select terms from the interface terminology to populate a patient’s hospital problem list. In this paper, the pre-mapped ICD-10-CA codes, linked to the interface terminology selections in the hospital problem list, are referred to as the solution mapped codes.

Hospital DAD data: The data abstracted by human coders builds the hospital Discharge Abstract Database (DAD). Hospital DAD captures demographic, administrative and clinical encounter data on discharge from acute care facilities. Collection of hospital DAD data is mandated for inpatient visits by CIHI and all hospitals across Canada (except Quebec) capture and report this data [21].

Study population

All patient data for this study cohort linked in-patient encounter hospital DAD and the patient’s hospital problem list records within the EHR from April 2021 to March 2024. As outlined above, as pre-mapped ICD-10-CA codes are linked to the terminology selections in the hospital problem list, the encounter records with hospital problem list were linked to hospital DAD. The linkage was completed using the Contact Serial Number (CSN) which is the unique identifier for each encounter record.

Data analysis

Main diagnosis in hospital DAD

A diagnosis type is assigned to each diagnosis that coders abstract and code. There are several diagnosis types specified by CIHI, among these are: main diagnosis which refers to the condition most responsible for patient’s hospital stay, type 1 diagnosis which refers to existing condition prior to admission and meets certain significance criteria as outlined by CIHI and other diagnosis types that can be found in CIHI’s documented guidelines [22].

Primary diagnosis in Epic

In the EHR, physicians search and select terms, from the interface terminology, to document the patient’s clinically relevant problems and conditions throughout the hospital encounter, onto the hospital problem list. Other care providers such as nurses can also enter information in the hospital problem list. The ICD-10-CA maps are pre-assigned to each term and do not change based on the context or use case. Of all problems entered in the hospital problem list, primary diagnosis is described as clinician’s indication of the most responsible diagnosis or the principle problem reflecting a patient’s reason for presentation or stay in the hospital at the time of discharge from the inpatient encounter. Whereas any diagnosis in this paper is referring to a diagnosis in any position.

Agreement

The primary outcome of this study was the level of agreement between the solution mapped ICD-10-CA codes from hospital problem list in the EHR and the corresponding ICD-10-CA codes abstracted by human coders in the hospital DAD for the linked encounter records. In Canada, hospital DAD and other administrative databases have been validated and widely used as reference standard in research studies [18, 19]. The agreement was assessed independently for the main diagnosis and any diagnosis. In this paper, any diagnosis agreement refers to the agreement between a diagnosis in any position in hospital DAD and any position in the hospital problem list.

Agreement was assessed in two ways: (1) using number of codes in hospital DAD as the denominator, and (2) using number of codes in the hospital problem list in EHR as the denominator. The agreement rate calculations were as follows:

Any diagnosis agreement (hospital DAD as a reference standard) = (Number of matched diagnoses in both hospital problem list and hospital DAD/Number of diagnoses in hospital DAD) x 100%.

Any diagnosis agreement (EHR hospital problem list as a reference standard) = (Number of matched diagnoses in both EHR hospital problem list and hospital DAD/Number of diagnoses in EHR hospital problem list) x100%.

Main diagnosis agreement = (Number of primary diagnoses in hospital problem list in EHR matching main diagnoses in hospital DAD/Total number of encounter records with primary diagnosis) x 100%.

Agreement was assessed at both 3-digit ICD level and 4-digit ICD level for higher precision. For example, a 3-digit code for diabetes is E10 whereas diabetes with coma is more specified with the 4-digit code of E10.0. Text Box A in the Additional File provides examples of 3-digit and 4-digit ICD-10-CA codes for other conditions. It is possible to capture ICD codes up to 5-digits which are even more specific, however, this study conducted a preliminary assessment only at a 3-digit and 4-digit level.

The agreement was stratified by provider specialty, length-of-stay (LOS), and hospital characteristics like hospital type (teaching or non-teaching) and location (urban or rural). Provider specialties were grouped into four categories: [1] Surgeons, [2] General Practitioners/Family Physicians, [3] Internists, and [4] Other, which included all other specialties such as anaesthesiology, dermatology, psychiatry, and diagnostic radiology, amongst others. The percentages of codes ending with 0.8 (refers to other forms of disease) or 0.9 (refers to the disease, unspecified) were also compared between hospital problem list and hospital DAD. The agreement was also determined at the conditions level by assessing six specific conditions, including common chronic and acute conditions, namely diabetes, hypertension, stroke, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and hospital-acquired infections, using validated ICD-10-CA codes as shown in Additional File Text Box B.

Standard deviations were computed to assess the dispersion in the agreement rate, and ANOVA was used to compare the differences in means in the groups. All statistical analysis was completed using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4.

Results

A total of 603,437 admission records were linked between hospital DAD and Connect Care. Urban hospitals accounted for 89.7% (541 098/603 437) of the admission records, and most patients (75.9%) had a length-of-stay of ≤ 7 days (Table 1).

Our study found that 37.0% of the solution mapped codes (a direct translation based solely on the term selected) at a 3-digit and 23.9% at a 4-digit aligned with the codes assigned by clinical coding specialists (who review the entire record and implement coding standards and guidelines) (Table 2a and Fig. 1a) when hospital DAD was used as the denominator. The agreement improved to 47.5% for primary diagnosis at a 3-digit level and to 31.8% at a 4-digit level (Table 2a and Fig. 1b). Although the maximum possible number of diagnoses that can be captured in the EHR is higher than hospital DAD (50 vs. 25), the average number of diagnoses coded by human coders in hospital DAD was found to be higher than the number of diagnoses through the mapping solution in EHR (4 vs. 2). The percentage of unspecified codes was higher in the solution mapped coded data compared to coders-coded data in hospital DAD (37.0% vs. 22.9%; not shown in results). With EHR hospital problem list as the denominator, a higher agreement rate was observed (Table 2b). For instance, the overall agreement for any diagnosis at 3-digit level and 4-digit level was 58.0% and 39.4% respectively. The results were statistically significant with p-values < 0.01.

The average agreement varied by physician specialty, which ranged from 31.0% to 38.9% for any diagnosis and 42.9% to 58.4% for primary diagnosis at a 3-digit ICD code level. Compared to other physician groups, the surgery group demonstrated the highest average agreement for main (primary) diagnosis (58.4%) at a 3-digit level. Internists and general practitioners had nearly the same agreement at the 3-digit level for primary diagnosis (47.9% and 46.7%, respectively), whereas the agreement differed for any diagnosis (31.0% vs. 38.9%). Teaching hospital settings exhibited slightly higher agreement than non-teaching hospitals for primary diagnoses (48.8% vs. 47.0%). However, this pattern reversed when the agreement was compared for any diagnoses (33.4% for teaching vs. 38.7% for non-teaching). Longer lengths-of-stay, which likely involves more complex patients, demonstrated lower agreement compared to shorter lengths-of-stay for primary and any diagnoses fields. Urban hospitals also had a slightly higher agreement than rural hospitals (Table 2a, Fig. 1a and b).

Figure 2 presents the agreement at the condition level. The agreement for main (primary) diagnosis of certain acute conditions like myocardial infarction (64.7%) and congestive heart failure (56.2%) was relatively higher than other conditions such as stroke (39.0%). For any diagnoses fields the agreement was much lower and ranged between 22.2% and 37.7% for all the conditions tested.

Discussion

This study assessed the ICD-10-CA codes obtained through the mapping solution embedded in the EHR by comparing with hospital DAD data coded by human coders and found a low agreement between the two approaches with an average of 37.0% at a 3-digit and 23.9% at a 4-digit ICD code level for any diagnosis when hospital DAD was used as a denominator. The agreement improved to 47.5% at 3-digit and 31.8% at 4-digit level for main diagnosis. The agreement rate varied by physician specialty, hospital type and location. Shorter length-of-stay had higher agreement than longer length-of-stay. When EHR hospital problem list was used as a denominator, the overall agreement improved with an average of 58.0% at a 3-digit level and 39.4% at a 4-digit level. Compared to agreement rate with hospital DAD as the denominator, the agreement rate with hospital problem list as a denominator is higher, indicating that the mapped codes are matching to hospital DAD codes to a large extent but the problem lists are not fully complete to reach an agreement closer to 100%. Overall, the low agreement shows that the mapped codes using the mapping solution approach are not ideal for research or surveillance use in the current stage. This study provides a valuable insight and foundational starting point in this area where a huge potential exists for improvement in EHR coded data using more advanced coding techniques. The study also highlights the importance of human coders who remain essential for collection of accurate and complete coded diagnoses and interventions.

There are several factors that are potentially leading to the low agreement observed in this study. First, each individual clinical term selected by physicians in the hospital problem list get solution mapped to the ICD-10-CA code based on only the text description; whereas, the coding in hospital DAD considers an entire encounter that entails combination of clinical diagnoses with comorbidities, physician notes and the encounter details as per the standards and guidelines by CIHI. Due to this, a single term is unlikely to replace the need for the context of amalgamating and summarizing an entire patient encounter. Additionally, physicians do not have the same level of knowledge of coding guidelines as the trained coding specialists. Consequently, as recognized by Tang et al., there are differences in terminology physicians use in capturing clinical diagnoses and the standardized disease classification system used by coders. Their study highlighted clinical terminology as more appropriate for clinical care and standardized disease classification by coders more important for epidemiological research and resource planning [8]. It also indicated that translating from one to another is a fundamental problem as the two do not always correlate (i.e., a clinical diagnosis may not correlate to an ICD-10 code). Furthermore, health care providers often use other sections of the EHR such as consult or progress notes and discharge summaries that allow free text entry to capture patient history and diagnosis related information. As such, the hospital problem list field, which uses a predefined set of terms, may not always be populated with an entire list of clinical conditions if those conditions are already documented in other free text sections of the EHR. Additionally, if care providers are not mandated to re-enter the diagnosis information in the hospital problem list, the likelihood of capturing conditions in this field remains low, especially if documentation in clinical notes or discharge summary is deemed sufficient and adequate for clinical care purposes. The higher agreement observed for primary diagnosis (47.5%) vs. any diagnosis (37.0%) in our study is indicating that clinicians are more often capturing primary or main diagnoses in the hospital problem list.

From physician’s point of view, their focus is on documenting clinically accurate information which does not necessarily meet or abide by the coding guidelines as physicians are not required to obtain coding training in Canada. Clinical documentation is the input data that is used to create output data by trained coding specialists using coding guidelines. Clinicians’ perspective about documentation may differ, leading to variations in selecting a single term in the hospital problem list. As such, an initial diagnosis added may not be revised to account for the events during the hospitalization or the comorbidities affecting the stay, or the appropriate level of specificity may be missing. For example, a patient diagnosed with alcohol-related hepatitis may not have the initial alcohol use disorder (AUD) added to the hospital problem list, or a clinician may decide to document acidosis versus respiratory acidosis or lactic acidosis. Study by Klappe et al. investigated factors that influenced problem list use by care providers in the Netherlands and found that physicians lacked specific guidelines on effectively utilizing problem list, were not aware of the potential benefits of problem list, did not perceive problem list added value in their daily practice, and did not consider re-use of problem list data as the primary goal of health care [23]. Our future work in this area will delve more into understanding physician documentation behaviour and their perception of the value of the problem list by interviewing physicians working in the inpatient settings in Alberta. These interviews will help us understand whether physicians are populating the hospital problem list to the full extent and the factors that contribute to variable use of this field amongst the physicians. In the current study, for readers to understand the context better, two use case scenarios are provided in the supplementary material illustrating how problem list solution mapped data varies from human coded data by coding specialists who are required to use standard guidelines and complete encounter information to code patient conditions (see Additional File 1).

It is important to note that an increase in big databases across healthcare avails opportunities for the development of sensitive AI technology, enabling searching of structured and unstructured data spanning the entirety of electronic health records, as well as having the capability to consider context. As mentioned above, computer-assisted coding (CAC) is one such example of a semi-automated process that assists clinical coding using a natural language processing (NLP) technique. As Campbell et al. illustrated in their narrative literature review, CAC has been suggested to improve transparency, compliance, consistency, accuracy, and productivity in the clinical coding process [10, 24, 25]. CAC is a semi-automated coding process that uses NLP to perform a preliminary scan of EMR documentation proposing clinical codes to human coders who then review, edit and validate the codes [10, 13, 26]. It’s a semi-automated technique as it requires human coders to review and validate codes. This combination of technology and coders brings a new modified role for clinical coders as they perform more value-added work as clinical coding analysts or editors validating clinical codes to ensure the CAC consistency [10, 13]. Studies have reported increased coders satisfaction rate with CAC implementation as it supports clinical coding efficiency [10, 27]. Connect Care provides a suitable environment for integration of advanced techniques such as CAC for enhancing the coding processes in Alberta, Canada. Some jurisdictions in Canada have already implemented such advanced techniques for an efficient clinical coding practice [28]. Additionally, as ICD-11 supports advanced digital capacities including coding support applications, integrating it into the current EHR offers a major opportunity to take advantage of the innovative and more sophisticated AI techniques for improvements in coded data.

Our study has some limitations that are important to note. First, we are comparing with hospital DAD, which, although a validated source of ICD-coded data collected using standardized guidelines, may still have some flaws as its quality depends on multiple factors such as chart documentation and human resource availability [7]. Our team plans to conduct chart recoding of a small set of charts in Alberta to compare with EHR codes. Second, this study was conducted during the initial years of Connect Care’s implementation, as such, the users (for instance, physicians) may still be in the early stages of learning the new interface. Thus, the agreement in the coded ICD data may improve as the users gain more experience.

Our future research will entail interviews with clinicians providing inpatient care to gain an in-depth understanding of coded data collection process and workflows that may be leading to discrepancies in the ICD-coded data generated using the mapping solution in Connect Care and coders coded data in hospital DAD. Apart from potential discrepancies resulting from mapping, the interviews will seek to understand which aspects of physician behaviour are acting as contributing factors. We aim to interview physicians from different specialties with varying lengths of working experience to understand if documentation of diagnosis information varies by specialty, number of practice years, as well as other potential influencing factors such as involvement of medical residents or trainee students in the care and chart documentation, and the role of varying shift work, etc. Our research will shed light on areas for improvement of completeness and quality of ICD-coded data, adding more value to the rich clinical data that Connect Care already avails.

Conclusion

This study found a low agreement between the solution mapped codes in Connect Care and the coded data by human coders, signifying the important role of human coders in capturing coded data.
