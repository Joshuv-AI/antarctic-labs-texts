Microglial galectin-3 disrupts parvalbumin interneurons and hippocampal synchrony, driving cognitive deficits
Authors: Min Jia, Hua Shao, Si-qi Ma, Wen-xue Liu, Meng Cai, Tong Zhu, Shan Xu, Gui-zhou Li, Shuai-fei Lu, Jin-chun Shen, Jiang Chen, Yun Stone Shi, Kenji Hashimoto, Guang-fen Zhang, Mu-huo Ji, Jian-jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE), a devastating neurological complication of systemic inflammation, affects approximately 70% of patients with sepsis. It not only increases mortality but also leaves survivors with persistent cognitive deficits. However, the mechanisms underlying SAE progression remain incompletely understood. Here, using a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced mouse model of SAE, we identify microglial galectin-3 (Gal-3) as a central pathogenic mediator driving systemic inflammation-induced cognitive impairment. Mechanistically, systemic LPS challenge robustly upregulates microglial Gal-3, which in turn activates Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) signaling and promotes NLRP3/AIM2 inflammasome assembly. This microglia-driven inflammatory cascade substantially exacerbates local oxidative stress, leading to selective structural and functional impairment of hippocampal parvalbumin (PV) interneurons. Dysfunction of these critical interneurons disrupts theta/gamma oscillations, impairs excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance and synaptic plasticity, and ultimately results in severe cognitive decline. Supporting this pathogenic cascade, pharmacological inhibition of Gal-3 with TD139 effectively suppresses TLR2/inflammasome activation, attenuates oxidative stress, and prevents memory deficits. Conversely, targeted rAAV-mediated overexpression of Gal-3 in microglia is sufficient to recapitulate neuroinflammation, PV-interneuron injury, oscillatory abnormalities, and cognitive impairment. Finally, chemogenetic reactivation of hippocampal PV interneurons using DREADDs restores theta/gamma oscillations and ameliorates LPS-induced cognitive deficits. Together, our findings define a coherent pathogenic axis linking microglial Gal-3 upregulation to PV interneuron-dependent network desynchronization and highlight Gal-3 as a promising therapeutic target for inflammation-associated cognitive disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03889-x.

Introduction

Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is a debilitating and diffuse brain dysfunction driven by a severe systemic inflammatory response rather than direct central nervous system (CNS) infection [1]. Clinically, SAE is characterized by acute delirium, coma, and persistent cognitive deficits, and it serves as a paradigm for how systemic inflammation—triggered by sepsis, aging, or surgical trauma [2–7] —can drive sustained neuroinflammation and long-term neurological impairment. Both epidemiological and preclinical studies have shown that peripheral immune challenges can disrupt CNS homeostasis, leading to persistent synaptic dysfunction and memory impairment. At the center of this pathological process are microglia, the resident macrophages and principal innate immune cells of the brain [8–10]. Although microglial activation is essential for normal CNS surveillance, maladaptive hyperactivation under systemic inflammatory conditions can trigger a cascade of neurotoxic events, including excessive cytokine release, oxidative stress, aberrant synaptic engulfment, and lysosomal accumulation associated with defective phagocytosis [3, 10–13]. Historically, despite their remarkable morphological and functional heterogeneity across development and disease, microglia were simplistically classified into a resting state (M0) and two activation states: M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory) [14]. However, this dichotomy has been superseded by advances in high-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which have identified multiple distinct microglial subpopulations with unique transcriptomic signatures and functional phenotypes across development, aging, and disease states. Notable examples include disease-associated microglia (DAM), a presumably protective and phagocytic population, and neurodegenerative microglia (MGnD), which represent a dysfunctional phenotype [15, 16]. Furthermore, proliferative-region-associated microglia (PAM) emerge during development, sharing a transcriptional profile enriched in DAM [17]. In the aging brain, recent studies have identified a distinct subpopulation termed lipid-droplet-accumulating microglia (LDAM), characterized by age-dependent lipid accumulation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and robust release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [11]. Precisely defining these diverse microglial states and clarifying their functional contributions will be essential for the development of targeted therapies for neurological disorders. Nevertheless, despite these advances in microglial phenotyping, a critical knowledge gap remains: the molecular sensors within microglia that translate peripheral inflammatory signals into specific disruptions of hippocampal circuitry remain incompletely understood.

Emerging evidence identifies galectin-3 (Gal-3), an evolutionarily conserved β-galactoside-binding lectin, as a potent regulator of neuroinflammation [18–20]. Gal-3 is markedly upregulated in reactive microglia across various neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s disease [21–25], where it often accumulates in lysosomes and orchestrates immune responses [25–27]. However, it remains elusive whether microglial Gal-3 acts merely as a bystander biomarker or serves as a central pathogenic hub that actively drives systemic inflammation-induced cognitive decline. Furthermore, how Gal-3-mediated microglial activation propagates damage to specific neuronal populations and impairs higher-order network dynamics, such as neural oscillations, has yet to be fully elucidated.

Cognitive functions, particularly memory encoding and retrieval, depend critically on the precise synchronization of neuronal networks, a process strongly governed by fast-spiking parvalbumin (PV)-expressing interneurons in the hippocampus [28–30]. PV interneurons are indispensable for generating theta and gamma oscillations and maintaining synaptic plasticity [31–33]. However, due to their high metabolic demand, PV interneurons are uniquely vulnerable to redox dysregulation and oxidative stress [34–37]. We postulated that Gal-3 might be the critical upstream trigger that initiates this oxidative damage, thereby uncoupling the neuro-glial metabolic balance and disrupting the PV interneuron-dependent rhythmic synchronization required for cognition.

To address this central mechanistic question, we hypothesized that microglial Gal-3 acts as a pivotal sensor and driver of neuroinflammation-induced cognitive impairment. Specifically, we propose a pathogenic cascade in which systemic inflammation-induced upregulation of microglial Gal-3 activates TLR2 signaling and promotes NLRP3/AIM2 inflammasome assembly; the resulting oxidative stress then selectively impairs hippocampal PV interneurons, disrupts theta–gamma oscillations and synaptic plasticity, and ultimately leads to cognitive decline.

To systematically test this hypothesis, we employed a step-wise experimental paradigm in a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) mouse model [38–40], focusing our analysis on the hippocampus given its critical vulnerability to neuroinflammation and its central role in cognitive function. First, we characterized LPS-induced microglial activation in the hippocampus, highlighting a Gal-3-dependent mechanism accompanied by aberrant lysosomal accumulation. Second, to establish necessity, we utilized pharmacological blockade (TD139) to ascertain whether inhibiting Gal-3 could mitigate TLR2/inflammasome activation and rescue cognitive deficits. Third, to demonstrate sufficiency, we employed rAAV-mediated microglia-specific overexpression to verify whether targeted Gal-3 upregulation could independently recapitulate the oxidative stress, PV-interneuron dysfunction, and cognitive deficits. Finally, to confirm the downstream effector of this cascade, we used chemogenetic (DREADD) tools to reactivate PV interneurons, testing whether directly restoring network synchronization could bypass the upstream microglial toxicity and rescue synaptic plasticity and cognitive decline. Together, this multidimensional approach delineates a coherent pathogenic cascade linking microglial Gal-3 to downstream PV interneuron dysfunction. Furthermore, it highlights microglial Gal-3 as a critical mechanistic driver and validates its pharmacological blockade as a viable therapeutic strategy for inflammation-related cognitive disorders.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics statement

C57BL/6J male mice (6–8 weeks) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co. Ltd., Nanjing, China. C57BL/6Smoc-Pvalbem1(T2A−iCre)Smoc (Pvalb-iCre; Cat. NO. NM-KI-200098) mice were obtained as a gift from Prof. Yun Stone’s laboratory. R26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato (Cat. NO. NM-KI-225042) and C57BL/6Smoc-Cx3cr1em1(iCre)Smoc (Cx3cr1-iCre; Cat. NO. NM-KI-200079) mice were all purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc., Shanghai, China. All mice were housed in specific pathogen-free laboratory conditions with a controlled temperature of 23 ± 1 ℃, relative 50%-60% humidity, and a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to standard chow diet and water. The experimental protocol for this animal study was approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Jinling Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University and conformed to relevant regulations.

Establishment of sepsis-induced SAE model and drug administration

To establish the SAE mouse model, LPS (L4130, Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany) was dissolved in 0.9% saline and administered via a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a dose of 4 mg/kg body weight (BW), according to previous studies with minor modification [38–41]. As demonstrated by Huang et al. and detailed in the Materials and Methods section [38], preliminary evaluations using 1–5 mg/kg LPS (i.p.) revealed a dose-dependent severity of systemic inflammation, which closely paralleled the extent of neuroinflammation. In their study, mortality rates were approximately 10% and 40% in mice receiving 4 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg LPS, respectively. Therefore, to ensure a robust systemic inflammatory response while strictly adhering to IACUC ethical guidelines regarding animal welfare, a dose of 4 mg/kg LPS was selected for the present study. The control mice received an equivalent volume of normal saline.

The Gal-3 inhibitor TD139 (GB0139, S0471) was purchased from Selleck, Shanghai, China. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: Saline + Vehicle group, Saline + TD139 group, LPS + Vehicle group, and LPS + TD139 group. TD139 was dissolved in a vehicle solution (containing a volume ratio of 2% DMSO + 10% PFG300 + 88% normal saline) to a final concentration of 0.75 mg/ml. TD139 (15 mg/kg BW) or an equivalent volume of the vehicle solution was given by intraperitoneal injection once a day for 15 consecutive days, starting 1 h before LPS injection and ending 2 h before the final behavioral test. The dose of TD139 was referred to a previous study [19].

Genotyping

Mice were genotyped for wild-type, PV-icre, or Cx3cr1-icre by PCR. The PV-Cre: tdTomato (PV-TD) double transgenic mice were obtained by crossing the PV-iCre mice to R26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato mice. Briefly, DNA from tails was extracted using the extraction Kit (DP304-03, TIANGEN, Beijing, China) and amplified by PCR using the 2× Taq Plus Master Mix (Dye Plus) (P212-03, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with the following primers (5′–3′). For PV-iCre mice (wild type(W): GCAGGCATGGTGACTAACAACAC; mutant(M): GGTGGTGTCCGATTGGTACAGC); for tdTomato mice (W: AAGGGAGCTGCAGTGGAGTA and CCGAAAATCTGTGGGAAG TC; M: GGCATTAAAGCAGCGTATCC and CTGTTCCTGTACGGCATGG); for Cx3cr1-icre mice (common(C): CCGCCAGACGCCCAGACTA; W: AGCCGGAAGCCCAAGAGCATC; M: TGCTGCTGCCCGACAACCAC).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data processing

The RNA-seq assay and analysis were performed by Jiangbei New Area Biopharmaceutical Public Service Platform Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China. The quality of RNA-seq reads was evaluated through Fastp (10.1002/imt2.107). Reads were aligned to the GRCm39 Mus musculus genome using STAR v2.7.10 under default settings. For each sample, a gene count matrix was created with salmon v1.9.0. Downstream analysis was conducted using the DEseq2 v1.40.2 package in R. Genes having a normalized count beneath 10 were excluded from downstream analysis. Significantly dysregulated genes were determined by a false discovery rate of less than 0.05. Differential expression analysis between conditions was executed with the ‘DESeq’ function with default parameters. Log2 fold-change shrinkage was carried out on the result of differential expression analysis. Heatmaps of DEGs with a p-value < 0.05 were visualized using pheatmap v1.0.12 employing Z-scores. A K-means algorithm was utilized for unsupervised clustering of gene sets with similarities following LPS injection. Chosen gene set enrichment analysis of gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway was performed with ClusterProfiler v4.6.0. Networks of murine Lgals-3 first neighbor interactors were created utilizing the STRING protein database. The maximum number of additional interactors was restricted to 20, with a confidence cutoff set at 0.4. Subsequently, the network was condensed to proteins associated with DEGs (Padj ≤ 0.05), and the corresponding log2 fold changes were assigned to the color of the node fill.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

In this study, the hippocampus was quickly harvested from mice across different experimental groups. Based on our previous research [42], RNA extraction was conducted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen). Immediately following RNA isolation, DNase I treatment was performed to ensure DNA contamination was minimized. RNA quality and quantity were assessed utilizing a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer, with an acceptable 260/280 nm absorbance ratio maintained within the range of 1.8 to 2.2, ensuring RNA integrity and suitability for downstream applications. For mRNA expression analysis, 1.0 µg of total RNA was used as input. Reverse transcription was performed employing the SuperscriptTM IV Reverse Transcriptase enzyme (18090010, ThermoFisher Scientific) to generate cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was then conducted on a LightCycler 1.5 instrument (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), in conjunction with the QuantiTech SYBR Green PCR kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocols. Data acquisition and analysis were carried out according to standard procedures, with expression levels normalized using the 2−ΔΔCT method relative to the reference gene gapdh. the forward (F) and reverse (R) primer sequences (5’→3’) employed in this study are as follows: lgals3 (F: TAATCAGGTGAGCGGCACAG; R: GCTAAGGCATCGTTAAGCGAAA), tlr2 (F: GCATCCGAATTGCATCACCG; R: GCAGAACAGCGTTTGCTGAA), and gapdh (F: ATGTGTCCGTCGTGGATCTG; R: AGTTGGGATAGGGCCTCTCTT).

Stereotaxic injections of recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV)

Heterozygous PV-iCre+/− or Cx3cr1-iCre+/− mice were used for virus injections. Briefly, mice anesthetized with 1.5–1.8% isoflurane, and 400 nl rAAV2/9-Ef1a-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-EYFP-WPRE-hGH polyA (for PV-iCre+/− mice; 2 × 1012 vg/ml titers) or rAAV2/6-CAG-DIO-galectin-3-2a-EGFP-WPRE-hGH pA (for Cx3cr1-iCre+/− mice; 5 × 1012 vg/ml titers), which was constructed by BrainVTA Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China), was injected into 4 sites of the hippocampus, respectively. The stereotaxic coordinates were determined by the Mouse Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates (2nd Edition). Hippocampal injections were performed at the following coordinates relative to Bregma: (1) AP -1.94 mm, ML ± 1.50 mm, DV -1.70 (from dura); and (2) AP -3.08 mm, ML ± 3.15 mm, DV -3.25 mm (from dura). Representative illustrations are shown in as shown in Figs. 9A and 10A. As a control, the virus rAAV2/9-Ef1a-DIO-EGFP-WPRE-hGH pA or rAAV2/6-CAG-DIO-EGFP-WPRE-hGH pA was injected, respectively. After 4 weeks of expression, PV-iCre mice were injected with clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) (HY-17366, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) intraperitoneally at a dose of 1.0 mg/kg once a day 30 min before each behavior test during the behavioral test session. All Mice were sacrificed for further analysis 2 h after the final injection of CNO or the final behavioral test. For western blot, the hippocampal tissues were stored in -80 ℃ refrigerator before use; for immunofluorescence (IF) staining, Mice were transcardially perfused with 1× PBS followed by 4% PFA, and the brains were then fixed overnight in 4% PFA overnight, dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution, cryoprotected in OCT, and storage at – 80 ℃ refrigerator before sectioning.

Behavioral tests

All behavioral tests were conducted in sound-proofed rooms between 9:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m. Experimenters were blinded to the experimental groups. Behavior was recorded and analyzed using a computer-operated video tracking system developed by Shanghai Softmaze Information Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). During each testing interval, the chambers were cleaned using MB-10 (100 ppm, CL-4136-15, CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) solution, followed by thorough drying with paper towels.

Open field test (OFT)

The OFT was used to assess exploratory and anxiety-like behaviors in mice on day 7 after LPS injection. A white open box (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) was divided into 16 squares, with the middle four squares defined as the central area and the rest as the surrounding area. A mouse is placed at the center of the box and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The total distance traveled in the arena and the time spent in the central area were automatically recorded by VisuTrack software (XR-VT).

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The NOR test was carried out 24 h after the OFT by using the same chamber at day 8 post-LPS stimulation. The experiment was divided into two sessions: training and testing. In the training session, mice were gently placed in the chamber containing two similar objects (named A1 and A2) for exploring 30 min. Twenty-four hours after training, one of the objects was replaced with object B, which has a different shape and color compared with object A. The duration of the testing session was lasting for 10 min. Discrimination ratio = new object exploring time / (new object exploring time + old object exploring time)%.

Y maze test (YMT)

At day 11 post-LPS stimulation, a spontaneous alternation test of the YMT was conducted to assess short-term spatial working memory. Mice were gently placed at the center of the maze and allowed to explore freely for 8 min and the sequence of the mice entering each arm was recorded by an experimenter who was blind to the experimental conditions. Spontaneous alternation (%) = number of spontaneous alternations / (number of total arm entries − 2) × 100%.

Contextual fear conditioning (FC) test

At day 12 post-LPS stimulation, the FC test was performed to evaluate the associative learning and memory of mice in this experiment according to the previous study [43]. The experiment was divided into two phases: training and testing. In the training phase, each mouse was placed in the experimental chamber and allowed to explore freely for 180 s, followed by exposure to a tone stimulus (70 dB, 3 kHz) for 30 s, and given a foot shock (2 s, 0.75 mA) beginning at the last 2 s of the tone stimulus, followed by a 30 s adaptation period without any stimulation. After that, the mice were returned to their home cage. Twenty-four hours later, a contextual FC test was conducted to evaluate long-term contextual fear memory, which is dependent on the hippocampus. The mice were placed in the original training chamber in without any stimulation for 5 min. which was automatically monitored by a video-tracking system (XR-XC404). Freezing behavior was defined as the absence of visible movement, except for respiration, detected as immobility periods more than 0.75 s.

BV2 microglial cells cultures, treatments, and preparation of conditioned medium

BV2 microglial cell lines, purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (M8-0701, Guangzhou, China), were cultured in a DMEM medium (11995065, Gibco™, Thermofisher scientific) containing 10% FBS (10091148, Gibco™, Thermofisher scientific; FBS210025, Jet Biofil, Guangzhou, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin medium and maintained in a controlled atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 ℃. Microglia-like BV2 cells were seeded on 6-well plates (TCP011006, Jetbiofil) at a density of 10.0 × 104 cells per well. The next day, the cells were then treated with 1 µg/mL LPS, 1 µM Gal-3 protein (active) (ab224635, Abcam, Cambridge, England), 10 µM Gal-3 inhibitor TD139, and equivalent vehicle for 18 h, respectively. Saline, LPS, or Gal-3 were added to the culture medium 30 min before the application of TD139 or Vehicle. The following day, the medium was removed, and the cultured cells were washed twice with PBS to eliminate the treatments. Subsequently, the cells were further cultured for an additional 2 days in the culture medium without any treatments to collect conditioned medium (CM).

Western blot

Hippocampal tissues of mice were collected under 2% isoflurane anesthesia after the final behavioral test and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer containing fresh phosphatase and protease inhibitors (A32961, Thermo Fisher Scientific-CN, Shanghai, China) added 1 mM PMSF (ST506, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). For highly expressed proteins, dorsal and ventral hippocampi were lysed separately. For lower-abundance targets (e.g., inflammasome components and cytokines), total hippocampal lysates were utilized to ensure sufficient protein yield for reliable quantification, working in a complementary manner with region-specific immunofluorescence. Protein concentration was determined by a BCA protein assay kit (ZJ102, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). Twenty or thirty milligrams of protein per lane were separated by 4–20% Hepes-Tris gels (LK206, Epizyme Biotech), 4–20% Bis-tris MOPS gels (LK409, Epizyme Biotech), 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels (PG212; PG213, Epizyme Biotech), and then transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF or 0.22 μm PSQ membranes (IPVH00010; ISEQ00010, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The blots were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in 1× TBS with 1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature (RT) and then incubated at 4 ℃ overnight with the following primary antibodies at a concentration of 1:1000: mouse anti-Gal3 (60207-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-CD11 antigen-like family member B (CD11b) (ab184307, Abcam), rabbit anti-ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1) (016-20001, Wako, Osaka, Japan; 680023, Zenbio, Chengdu, China), rat anti-CD68 (MCA1957GA, Bio-rad, California, USA), rabbit anti-lysosomal associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) (ab208943, Abcam), mouse anti-NLRP3 (68102-1-Ig, Proteintech), mouse anti-absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) (sc-293174, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Texas, USA), rabbit anti-apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) (10500-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-Caspase-1 (24232, CST, Massachusetts, USA), rabbit anti-IL-1β (ab234437, Abcam), rabbit anti-TLR2 (DF7002, Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, USA), rabbit anti-IL-18 (57058, CST), rabbit anti-Caspase-11 (R23724, Zenbio), rabbit anti-IL-6 (500286, Zenbio), rabbit anti-TNFα (346654, Zenbio), rabbit anti-PV (ab181086, Abcam), rabbit anti-cathepsin B (CatB) (A0967, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). rabbit anti-cathepsin D (CatD) (ab75852, Abcam), rabbit anti-gasdermin D (GSDMD) (ab219800, Abcam), rabbit anti-NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) (1:1000, A22149, Abclonal), rabbit anti-βIII tubulin (R011968, Epizyme Biotech), rabbit anti-β actin (20536-1-AP, Proteintech; LF202, Epizyme Biotech), and mouse anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). After washing with 1× TBST 3 times for 20 min, the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated according to the source of first antibodies at a concentration of 1:10,000: goat anti-rabbit IgG (SA00001-2, Proteintech), goat anti-mouse IgG (SA00001-1, Proteintech), and goat anti-rat IgG (SA00001-15, Proteintech) for 1 h at RT, then washed with 1× TBST 3 times for 20 min and developed with chemiluminescence detection kits (BeyoECL Star, P0018AM; BeyoECL SuperMoon, P0018HM, Beyotime) and analyzed by FIJI software (Version 2.3.0/1.53q, NIH, Maryland, USA).

Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunocytochemistry (ICC) staining

In vitro phagocytosis assay of microglial phagocytosis

The phagocytosis assay of microglial BV2 cells was conducted under the previous study [44] with minor modifications. For in vitro phagocytosis assays, BV2 cells were plated in a 12-well plate for 24 h at a density of 40,000 cells/well along with a round-shaped 18-mm coverslip (#1 thickness, GG-18, Neuvitro Corporation, Washington, USA) precoated with 1× PLL in a DMEM medium + 10% FBS medium. Following treatments with LPS or Gal-3 with or without TD139, and vehicle solutions for 18 h, wells were rinsed with 37 ℃ DMEM 3 times, and 40 µl Latex beads (0.5 μm mean particle size, L3280-1ML, Sigma) in 400 µl DMEM + 1% FBS medium were added per well, followed by incubation of the plates at 37 ℃ for 2 h. After aspirating the beads and media, cells were rinsed with 1× PBS twice and incubated in wells with 4% PFA plus 4% glucose for 10 min at 37 ℃ to fix cells. Subsequently, the plate was washed with 1× PBS 3 times for 10 min. Co-localization staining for Iba-1, Gal-3, and CD68 was performed according to the methods of ICC staining. Cells can be visualized using a Zeiss confocal microscope (LSM880) at a magnification of 40 × zoom 3 under a z-stack (15 μm with an interval of 0.75 μm). Image analysis and phagocytosis quantification were accomplished using Imaris, Fiji, and Graphpad Prism software.

Lipid peroxidation malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

The MDA concentration was measured by a Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (S0131S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fresh hippocampi were homogenized by RIPA lysis buffer at a ratio of 1:10. After sample preparation, the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, to facilitate the subsequent calculation of the content of MDA in tissues per unit protein weight. Adding 0.1 ml of homogenate, lysis buffer, PBS, or other appropriate solution as a blank control in a centrifuge tube, add then 0.1 ml of the above different concentrations of standards for making a standard curve, and add 0.1 ml of the sample for determination; then add 200 µl of the MDA detection working solution. After mixing, heat at 100 ℃ in a boiling water bath for 15 min. Subsequently, the centrifuge tubes were cooled in a water bath to RT and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min at RT. The supernatant (200 µl) was added to a 96-well plate and then measured the absorbance at 532 nm using a microplate reader. The molar concentration of MDA was calculated based on the standard curve. The MDA content was presented in the original sample as µmol/mg protein through the protein content per unit weight.

Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) assay

The total antioxidant capacity in hippocampi was assayed by the T-AOC assay kit (AKAO012M, BC1310, Solarbio, Beijing, China) by the ferric-reducing ability of plasma (FRAP) methods. For the fresh hippocampus, 100 µl of ice-cold 1× HBSS solution was added to every 20 mg of tissue, sonicated to fully disrupt the tissue, centrifuged at approximately 10,000 g for 5 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent assays. The concentration of proteins in the samples was determined by the BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were used in each group. According to the manual, FRAP working solution (180 µl) was added to each well of a 96-well plate. The blanks control wells were added 5 µl distilled water and the standard curve detection well was added 5 µl of various concentrations of FeSO4 standard solutions, and then gently mixed several times. After incubating at 37 ℃ for 10 min, the absorbance was measured at 593 nm under a microplate reader. The total antioxidant capacity was calculated of the samples according to the standard curve.

ROS assay

The quantitative assay of intracellular levels of ROS in BV2 cells was determined using the Reactive oxygen species assay kit with CM-H2DCFDA (S0035S, Beyotime). As per the manual, CM-H2DCFDA (5 mM) was freshly diluted 1000-fold in a serum-free culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 5 µM. Subsequently, the cell culture medium was removed, and an adequate amount of diluted CM-H2DCFDA (5 µM) was added to comprehensively cover the cells. About 1 ml of diluted CM-H2DCFDA per well of an 18-mm micro coverslip in a 12-well plate and incubated in a 37 ℃ cell incubator for 30 min, and then washed the cells 3 times with serum-free culture medium to thoroughly eliminate the residual CM-H2DCFDA. After that, the live cell was imaged using Zeiss confocal microscopy (LSM880) with an excitation at 488 nm at a magnification of 40× zoom 3 under a z-stack (10 μm with an interval of 1.0 μm).

In vivo electrophysiology

LFP recordings in vivo were performed as our previously described [45]. One week before LPS stimulation, the mice were anesthetized using 1.5–1.8% isoflurane with 100% O2, and craniotomies were performed under stereotaxic guidance. For mice that underwent stereotaxic surgery for viral construct infusion, a recovery period of at least two weeks was allowed before craniotomy and microelectrode implantation. After microelectrode implantation, animals were given an additional 10 days to recover before LFP recordings were initiated, ensuring full recovery and recording stability. After stabilizing the skull, a rectangular craniotomy (2 mm × 2 mm) was carried out to expose the brain, and the dura was opened. An eight-channel microelectrode (KD-MWA), purchased from Kedou Brain-Computer Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China, was implanted into the dorsal hippocampal CA1 (AP: -2.70 mm; ML: -2.50 mm; DV: -1.75 mm) and ventral hippocampal CA1 (AP: -3.16 mm; ML: -3.50 mm; DV: -4.0 mm). The electrode position was determined correctly in advance, a small amount of DIO (C1038, Beyotime) is dipped in the end of the electrode wire, and the position of the electrode end was observed by staining with DAPI after 1 week. Three screws were fixed to the skull and connected to stainless steel wires as ground, then the electrodes were fixed using dental adhesive resin cement Super-bond C&B (13301100, Sun Medical, Shiga, Japan). Thirteen days after LPS injection, the mice were placed into the chamber of contextual FC test to move freely and local field potentials (LFP) were recorded using the OmniPlex (Version 1.20.0, Plexon, Texas, USA) or Apollo™ (RHD1024A, KedouBC, Suzhou, China) in vivo multichannel recording system. In our study, the frequency bands were classified as theta oscillations (4–8 Hz), alpha oscillations (8–12 Hz), beta oscillations (12–30 Hz), and gamma oscillations (30–80 Hz). The signals were filtered with a passband of 0.3–300 Hz, then further amplified and digitized at 2 kHz. All data were analyzed with Neuroexplorer software (V5.310, Plexon) software.

In vitro electrophysiology

Two hours after the contextual FC test, field excitatory postsynaptic potential (fEPSP) LTP recordings of Schaffer collateral – CA1 projection in acute hippocampal slices were performed as previously described [42]. Briefly, 350 μm thickness acute coronal hippocampal slices) were prepared in 95% O2 / 5% CO2 perfused high-sucrose cutting solution, containing (in mM) KCl 2.5, 1.25 NaH2PO4 1.25, NaHCO3 25, CaCl2 0.5, MgSO4 7, sucrose 210, D-glucose 10 and Na-ascorbate 1.3 ,with vibratome (VT1000s, Leica, Germany), and transferred into artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF, in mM, NaCl 119, KCl 2.5, CaCl2 2.5, MgSO4 1.3, NaHCO3 26.2, NaH2PO4 1 and D-glucose 11, with 95% O2 / 5% CO2 saturated perfusion) at 32 °C for 20 min and the room temperature (approximate 23 °C) for over 40 min to recovery. The slices were continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF at 3–4 mL/min flow rate for recoding. The 1–2 MΩ borosilicate recoding electrode filled with ACSF and the concentric bipolar stimulation electrode (CBBRC75, FHC, USA) were placed at CA1 stratum radiatum with ~ 200 μm distance from each other. Four fEPSPs per minute were recorded. The fEPSP baseline was set as 40% of the maximum rise slope and recorded for 20 min. High-frequency stimulation containing four 100 Hz/1 s pulses with 20 s interval was introduced for LTP stimulation. Then, fEPSPs were recorded for an hour to assessed the LTP magnitude. The rise slope of fEPSP was obtain to analyze.

For spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic current (sIPSCs) recording, we used 3–4 MΩ recoding electrode filled with the high-chloride intracellular solution (in mM, CsMeSO3 70, CsCl 70, NaCl 8, HEPES 10, EGTA 0.3, Mg-ATP 4, Na3-GTP 0.3 and QX-314 5, adjusted pH7.3-7.4 with CsOH, 290–295 mOsm/L) to whole-cell clamp the CA1 pyramidal neurons at -70 mV. AMPA receptor antagonist NBQX (10 µM, Alomone Labs, Isreal) and NMDA receptor antagonist D-AP5 (100 µM, Alomone Labs) were added in the perfused ACSF to isolate the GABAA receptor-mediated sIPSCs at -70 mV holding. The amplitude, frequency and interevent interval of sIPSC was obtain to analyze.

Action potentials (APs) and excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) ratios were recorded in the CA1 region of acute hippocampal slices from PV-tdTomato and wild-type mice, respectively. These slices were prepared according to the same protocol described above for fEPSP recordings. For AP recordings, borosilicate recording electrodes (3–5 MΩ) were filled with a K+-based intracellular solution containing (in mM): K-gluconate 120, KCl 20, HEPES 10, EGTA 0.5, MgCl2 2, Na2-ATP 2 and Na3-GTP 0.3 (290–295 mOsm/l; pH 7.3 adjusted with KOH). To evoke AP firing, 300 ms current stimuli from − 10 to 250 pA in 10 pA increments were injected into whole-cell-patched neurons. For E/I ratio analysis, evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (eEPSCs) and evoked inhibitory postsynaptic currents (eIPSCs) were recorded. A Cs+-based intracellular solution was used, containing (in mM): CsMeSO3 135, NaCl 8, HEPES 10, EGTA 0.3, Mg-ATP 4, Na3-GTP 0.3, spermine 0.1 and QX-314 5 (290–295 mOsm/l, pH 7.3 adjusted with CsOH). eEPSCs and eIPSCs were evoked by stimulating the Schaffer collateral with a concentric bipolar stimulation electrode (CBBRC75, FHC, USA) using the same stimulus intensity. The eEPSCs were recorded at a holding potential of -70 mV, and the eIPSCs at 0 mV. The E/I ratio from each neuron was calculated as the absolute value of eEPSC amplitude divided by eIPSC amplitude.

Electrophysiological data were acquired using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier and an Axon Digidata 1550 digitizer (Molecular Devices, USA), filtered at 2 kHz and sampled at 50 kHz digitized frequency.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Data distribution was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test and it was presented as the mean ± SEM along with dot plots. Additionally, a two-tailed Student’s t-test, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni or Tukey’s post hoc test was employed for analysis, as deemed appropriate. A P value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Systemic LPS elicits robust microglial activation in hippocampus

Based on previous studies examining the impact of systemic inflammation on brain function [36, 38–40] and the pivotal role of the hippocampus in learning and memory consolidation [46, 47], we first characterized microglial activation in the hippocampus following systemic neuroinflammation induced by intraperitoneal LPS injection. By analyzing representative dorsal (dCA1) and ventral (vCA1) CA1 regions, we found that microglial numbers were comparable between groups in the dCA1 (P = 0.3050), whereas LPS administration markedly increased microglial number in the vCA1 (P = 0.0089; Fig. 1A). Morphological profiling showed that microglia in both dCA1 and vCA1 adopted a robust reactive phenotype after systemic LPS challenge (Fig. 1B). Quantitative analyses revealed enlarged somata (dCA1: P < 0.0001; vCA1: P < 0.0001; Fig. 1C), increased soma solidity (dCA1: P = 0.0009; vCA1: P < 0.0001; Fig. 1D), and pronounced process retraction, as evidenced by shorter maximal processes (dCA1: P = 0.0029; vCA1: P < 0.0001; Fig. 1E), fewer terminal branches (dCA1: P < 0.0001; vCA1: P < 0.0001; Fig. 1E), and reduced Sholl intersections (dCA1: P < 0.0001; vCA1: P < 0.0001; Fig. 1F). Western blot corroborated these structural changes, showing significant upregulation of canonical activation markers: Iba-1 (dorsal: P = 0.0286; ventral: P = 0.0286), CD11b (dorsal: P = 0.0281; ventral: P = 0.0047), the phagolysosomal proteins CD68 (dorsal: P = 0.0286; ventral: P = 0.0286) and LAMP1 (dorsal: P = 0.0011; ventral: P = 0.0030) in both hippocampal poles (Fig. 1G). Immunofluorescence validated these molecular signatures: co-localization of CD11b with the microglia-restricted marker TREM119 was significantly increased in LPS-exposed dCA1 and vCA1 (dCA1: P = 0.0260; vCA1: P = 0.0022; Fig. 1H). Consistent with the biochemical data, intracellular CD68 signal within microglia was also markedly intensified (dCA1: P = 0.0115; vCA1: P = 0.0003; Fig. 1I), confirming enhanced lysosomal activity across both hippocampal subfields.

Transcriptomic profiling of LPS-evoked microglial activation

To delineate the molecular circuitry driving microglial reactivity, we performed deep RNA-sequencing on hippocampal tissue collected 10 days after LPS injection. Differential expression analysis identified 347 robustly altered transcripts relative to saline controls, including 265 upregulated and 82 downregulated genes (Suppl. Figure 1 A–B). Intriguingly, 33 of the induced genes encoded lysosomal components, indicating a pronounced pro-phagocytic signature (Suppl. Figure 1 C). Gene-ontology enrichment highlighted functional clusters related to extracellular-matrix organization, metallo- and dipeptidase activities, MHC class II engagement, ferric-chelate and cupric reductase activities, and NADPH-dependent metal reduction (Suppl. Figure 1D). KEGG pathway mapping further linked these DEGs to diabetic cardiomyopathy, phagosome maturation, ECM-receptor crosstalk, protein digestion/absorption, AGE–RAGE signaling, rheumatoid arthritis, hematopoietic lineage commitment, antigen processing and presentation, inflammatory bowel disease, and asthma (Suppl. Figure 1D). A high-confidence STRING interactome revealed dense, functionally coherent networks among these candidates (Suppl. Figure 1E). To validate key molecular nodes, we quantified hippocampal galectin-3 (Gal-3, encoded by lgals3) and Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) mRNA by qRT-PCR at day 10 post‑LPS, and measured Gal‑3, TLR2, and TLR4 protein levels by western blot at 12 h and day 10 post‑LPS. Both Gal‑3 and TLR2 transcripts were significantly upregulated (Gal‑3: P = 0.0004; TLR2: P = 0.0003; Suppl. Figure 1 F), and their protein levels remained persistently elevated at both time points (LPS 12 h: Gal‑3: P = 0.0004; TLR2: P = 0.0132; LPS 10 d: Gal‑3: P = 0.0020; TLR2: P = 0.0001; Suppl. Figure 1G). In contrast, TLR4 expression was increased only at 12 h and returned to baseline by day 10 after LPS administration (LPS 12 h: P = 0.0217; LPS 10 d: P = 0.7242; Suppl. Figure 1G). These findings are consistent with the transcriptomic data and implicate Gal-3-associated TLR2 signaling in LPS-induced microglial activation.

LPS selectively induces Gal-3 in hippocampal microglia with lysosomal enrichment

To define the neuroinflammatory milieu, we quantified TLR2 and Gal-3 protein levels in hippocampal lysates. Western blot revealed robust elevations of both Gal-3 (P = 0.0047) and TLR2 (P = 0.0011) in LPS-treated mice, with comparable up-regulation across hippocampal poles (Fig. 2A and Suppl. Figure 2A). Immunofluorescence confirmed a marked increase in hippocampal Gal‑3 signal (P = 0.0022; Fig. 2B and Suppl. Figure 2B). Co-localization analysis showed that this induction was largely restricted to microglia, as Gal-3 fluorescence overlapped almost exclusively with Iba-1 + cells rather than GFAP+ astrocytes (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2C). Consistent with this finding, Gal-3 intensity within microglia increased dramatically after LPS administration (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2C), whereas astrocytic Gal-3 remained unchanged (P = 0.1295; Fig. 2C). Notably, Gal-3 accumulated densely within CD68+ lysosomal compartments of hippocampal microglia (P = 0.0002; Fig. 2D and Suppl. Figure 2C), supporting a lysosome-centered role for Gal-3 in LPS-induced microglial activation in hippocampus.

Gal-3 blockade with TD139 rescues LPS-induced cognitive deficits

To determine whether Gal-3 inhibition could alleviate neuroinflammation-associated cognitive impairment, mice received the Gal-3 inhibitor TD139 or vehicle following LPS challenge (Fig. 3A). In the open-field test (OFT), no group differences were observed in total distance traveled (F(1,38) = 0.5989, P = 0.4438; Fig. 3B) or time spent in center (F(1,38) = 0.04269, P = 0.8374; Fig. 3B), excluding locomotor confounds. Object-exploration time was also comparable among groups (F(1,38) = 0.7374, P = 0.3967; Fig. 3C). In the novel object recognition test (NORT), LPS-treated mice receiving vehicle exhibited a markedly reduced discrimination index compared with controls, whereas TD139 treatment fully restored this deficit (F(1,38) = 4.797, P = 0.0347; Fig. 3C). Likewise, spontaneous alternation in the Y-maze test (YMT) was impaired by LPS and rescued by TD139 administration (F(1,38) = 4.313, P = 0.0446; Fig. 3D). In the contextual fear conditioning (FC) test, no group differences were observed in immediate post-shock freezing (F(1,38) = 1.032, P = 0.3126; Fig. 3E), indicating comparable shock sensitivity. However, 24 h later, LPS-vehicle mice displayed significantly impaired contextual memory, and this impairment was completely reversed by TD139 treatment (F(1,38) = 5.306, P = 0.0268; Fig. 3E). Thus, these findings indicate that pharmacological inhibition of Gal-3 effectively rescues LPS-induced deficits in recognition memory, working memory, and contextual memory, without altering baseline locomotor activity or shock responsiveness.

Gal-3 blockade with TD139 suppresses LPS-induced microglial activation

Western blot showed that TD139 abolished the LPS-induced upregulation of canonical activation markers in hippocampal lysates, including Iba-1 (F(1,24) = 7.234, P = 0.0128), CD11b (F(1,16) = 5.386, P = 0.0338), Gal-3 (F(1,24) = 4.661, P = 0.0411), TLR2 (F(1,20) = 5.216, P = 0.0334), Lamp1 (F(1,20) = 5.643, P = 0.0276), and CD68 (F(1,20) = 4.521, P = 0.0461) (Fig. 3F). Immunofluorescence further confirmed that LPS increased intra-microglial Gal-3 in the hippocampus, and that TD139 treatment fully normalized this elevation (F(1,24) = 6.920, P = 0.0147) (Fig. 3G and Suppl. Figure 2D). In BV2 microglial cells, TD139 similarly reduced LPS-driven Gal-3 accumulation within lysosomes (F(5,114) = 13.57, P < 0.0001; Suppl. Figure 3A) and decreased Gal-3 release into the conditioned medium, as well as intracellular Gal-3 levels in cell lysates (CM: F(5,18) = 8.377, P = 0.0003; cell lysate: F(5,18) = 7.836, P = 0.0005; Suppl. Figure 3B). Consistently, immunocytochemistry (ICC) staining demonstrated that TD139 inhibited LPS-induced TLR2 upregulation (F(5,90) = 8.960, P < 0.0001; Suppl. Figure 4A) and reduced its association with the lysosomal marker CD68 and with phagocytosed beads (F(5,138) = 27.33, P < 0.0001; Suppl. Figure 4B). Collectively, these data indicate that Gal-3 blockade disrupts Gal-3-dependent TLR2 signaling and lysosome-centered microglial activation, thereby restraining microglial reactivity.

TD139 limits LPS-induced NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasome assembly and cytokine release in hippocampal microglia

Western blot analysis showed that systemic LPS robustly upregulated core inflammasome components in hippocampal lysates, including NLRP3 (F(1,12) = 4.915, P = 0.0467), ASC (F(1, 16) = 14.28, P = 0.0016), AIM2 (F(1,20) = 4.605, P = 0.0443), Caspase-1 (F(1,12) = 23.25, P = 0.0004), and Caspase-11 (F(1,12) = 14.34, P = 0.0026). TD139 treatment abolished these elevations (Fig. 4A). Representative immunofluorescence images further confirmed that LPS increased the intracellular volume of both NLRP3 (F(1,28) = 5.347, P = 0.0283) and AIM2 (F(1,28) = 7.085, P = 0.0127) within Iba-1+ microglia, whereas TD139 restored both to baseline levels (Fig. 4B, C). Concomitantly, TD139 suppressed the downstream cytokine burst, normalizing hippocampal levels of IL-1β (F(1,12) = 4.952, P = 0.0460), IL-18 (F(1,12) = 9.534, P = 0.0094), IL-6 (F(1,12) = 5.269, P = 0.0405), and TNF-α (F(1,12) = 9.490, P = 0.0095) (Fig. 5D). The Gal-3 inhibitor TD139 also reversed LPS-driven up-regulation of pyroptosis executor Gsdmd (F(1,12) = 5.011, P = 0.0449) and lysosomal proteases Cathepsin D (F(1,12) = 7.379, P = 0.0187) and Cathepsin B (F(1,12) = 16.92, P = 0.014) (Fig. 5D). Together, these findings indicate that Gal-3 inhibition suppresses upstream pro-inflammatory signaling, inflammasome activation, and downstream inflammatory effector responses in hippocampal microglia following systemic inflammation.

TD139 protects hippocampal PV interneurons from LPS-evoked oxidative injury

Systemic LPS challenge increased hippocampal malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, and reduced total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), whereas TD139 treatment abolished both changes (MDA: F(1,16) = 5.547, P = 0.0316, Fig. 5A; T-AOC: F(1,16) = 7.58, P = 0.014; Fig. 5B). Consistent with these findings, western blot analysis showed that TD139 reversed the LPS-induced downregulation of PV (F(1,20) = 4.714, P = 0.0421) and the upregulation of NOX4, a major contributor to reactive oxygen species production in the central nervous system [48]) (F(1,20) = 4.664, P = 0.0435) (Fig. 5C). In BV2 microglial cells, LPS markedly increased intracellular ROS levels (F(5,54) = 21.99, P < 0.0001; Suppl. Figure 5A) and elevated NOX4 together with Gal-3 expressions in Iba-1+ microglia (F(5,114) = 21.15, P < 0.0001; Suppl. Figure 5B). Immunofluorescence further showed that LPS shortened the soma-to-soma distance between PV-interneurons and adjacent microglia, indicative of reactive microglial clustering; TD139 restored this distance to control levels (F(1,60) = 4.296, P = 0.0425; Fig. 5D and Suppl. Figure 6A) and simultaneously reduced the number of microglia surrounding PV cells (F(1,60) = 4.305, P = 0.0423; Fig. 5D and Suppl. Figure 6A). Moreover, markers of oxidative DNA damage were markedly elevated in PV interneurons following LPS exposure,, including 8-OH-dG (F(1,15) = 14.70, P = 0.0016) and γH2AX (F(1,15) = 19.12, P = 0.0005) (Fig. 5E, F and Suppl. Figure 6A, C).Markers of oxidative DNA damage were markedly elevated within PV interneurons after LPS [8-OH-dG and γH2AX] (Fig. 5E, F and Suppl. Figure 6A, C). Together, these findings indicate that Gal-3 inhibition mitigates oxidative stress and DNA damage in hippocampal PV interneurons during LPS-induced systemic inflammation.

Gal-3 inhibition with TD139 preserves PV interneuron function during LPS-induced systemic inflammation

In tdTomato reporter mice, tdTomato fluorescence overlapped almost completely with PV immunoreactivity, confirming the fidelity of PV interneuron labeling (Fig. 6A). LPS did not alter the co-localization ratio of tdTomato+ cells with PV (P = 0.2475; Fig. 6B and Suppl. Figure 6D), but significantly reduced PV signal intensity in the hippocampus (P < 0.001; Fig. 6B and Suppl. Figure 6D). Concordantly, GABA immunofluorescence and PV+/GABA+ co-labeling were significantly diminished across CA1 subfields (GABA: P = 0.0002; PV+/GABA+: P = 0.0002; Fig. 6C and Suppl. Figure 6E). Functional assessment using c-Fos+ labeling showed a reduced number of PV+/c-Fos⁺ interneurons after LPS (F(1,44) = 8.571, P = 0.0054; Fig. 6D and Suppl. Figure 6F), indicating suppressed neuronal activity. Likewise, the proportion of activated PV interneurons among total PV cells was significantly decreased (F(1,44) = 11.58, P = 0.0014; Fig. 6D and Suppl. Figure 6F). TD139 treatment fully reversed these deficits, restoring PV interneuron dendritic arborization (F(1,20) = 15.33, P = 0.0009; Fig. 6E and Suppl. Figure 6G) and reinstating PV interneuron activation to control levels. LPS also impaired action potentials (AP) firing in PV interneurons, as evidenced by a flatter input–output curve (F(78, 540) = 1.930, P < 0.0001; Fig. 7A-C), an increased rheobase (F(1, 20) = 4.693, P = 0.0425; Fig. 7D) and a prolonged inter‑spike interval during 250‑pA suprathreshold stimulation (F(1, 20) = 5.308, P = 0.0321; Fig. 7E). TD139 largely reversed these AP deficits, while vehicle‑ and TD139‑treated control groups were comparable across AP parameters (Fig. 7B-E). These findings indicate that TD139 restores the intrinsic excitability of PV interneurons under LPS-induced systemic inflammation. Thus, Gal-3 blockade preserves both the molecular identity and functional integrity of PV interneurons during systemic inflammation.

Gal‑3 blockade with TD139 restores LPS‑disrupted hippocampal theta–gamma oscillations and rescues synaptic function and plasticity

To assess network-level effects, we performed in vivo extracellular local field potential (LFP) recordings across hippocampal CA1 region (Fig. 6F and Suppl. Figure 6H). LPS markedly suppressed theta power relative to controls, and TD139 fully reversed this deficit (F(1,20) = 4.715, P = 0.0421; Fig. 6G). Alpha and beta rhythms were unchanged (alpha: F(1,20) = 0.8233, P = 0.3750; beta: F(1,20) = 0.9442, P = 0.3428; Fig. 6H, I). Gamma power was likewise diminished by LPS and restored to control levels by TD139 (F(1,20) = 4.659, P = 0.0432; Fig. 6J). These results indicate that Gal-3 inhibition selectively restores theta and gamma oscillatory activity, thereby reinstating the rhythmic substrates for cognitive processing disrupted by systemic inflammation.

To further evaluate synaptic and cellular function, we performed in vitro electrophysiological recordings. LPS selectively reduced the amplitude of spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs) (Fig. 8A-C; Amplitude: F(1, 76) = 4.129, P = 0.0456; Fig. 8C), whereas sIPSCs frequency remained unchanged (Frequency: F(1, 76) = 0.4605, P = 0.4994; Fig. 8D). Consistently with this interpretation, TD139 normalized the LPS‑elevated eEPSC/eIPSC (E/I) ratio without affecting controls (Fig. 8E, F; E/I ratio: F(1, 32) = 5.688, P = 0.0232; Fig. 8F). Input–output analysis further that LPS reduced the EPSP slope across all fiber volley amplitudes, an effect that was corrected by TD139 (Slope: F(27, 240) = 0.7274, P = 0.8472; Saline + Vehicle vs. LPS + Vehicle: F(9, 120) = 1.979, P = 0.0474; LPS + Vehicle vs. LPS + TD139: F(9, 100) = 2.960, P = 0.0037; Fig. 8G, H). Likewise, LTP was impaired by LPS and rescued by TD139 (Fig. 8I-K; slope: F(1, 24) = 5.490, P = 0.0226; Fig. 8K). Together, these findings demonstrate that Gal-3 blockade repairs both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic dysfunction and restores activity-dependent synaptic plasticity in hippocampal circuits exposed to systemic inflammation.

Hippocampal microglial Gal-3 overexpression is sufficient to drive neuroinflammation and oxidative stress–mediated PV-interneuron dysfunction, culminating in cognitive decline

To determine whether microglial Gal-3 is causally sufficient to drive neuroinflammation and behavioral impairment, we virally overexpressed Gal-3 in hippocampal microglia (Fig. 9A). Representative immunofluorescence images showed a robust expansion of the microglial population in hippocampal CA1 region, as indicated by increased numbers of Iba-1+ cells (P = 0.0002) and EGFP+/Iba-1+ cells (P = 0.0007; Fig. 9B). This was accompanied by elevated intracellular Gal-3 with pronounced Gal-3 accumulation within CD68+ lysosomes (both P < 0.0001; Fig. 9C). Consistently, morphometric analysis showed that Gal-3-overexpressing microglia exhibited a reactive phenotype characterized by increased soma volume and greater soma solidity (both P < 0.0001; Fig. 9D). In the OFT, Gal-3 overexpression did not affect locomotor activity (distance P = 0.9654), but induced anxiety-like behavior, as reflected by reduced time spent in the center area (P = 0.0020, Fig. 9E). Moreover, Gal-3 overexpression produced broad cognitive deficits, including a reduced discrimination index in NORT (discrimination index, test phase P = 0.0031; Fig. 9F), decreased spontaneous alternation in the YMT (P = 0.0343; Fig. 9G), and diminished freezing in contextual FC test (test phase P = 0.0085; Fig. 9H). Biochemically, western blot analysis demonstrated upregulation of microglial innate immune markers, including Gal-3 (P = 0.0002), TLR2 (P = 0.0043), Iba-1 (P = 0.0022), and CD11b (P = 0.0152), as well as lysosomal markers CD68 (P = 0.0411) and Lamp-1 (P = 0.0281; Suppl. Figure 7A). Notably, Gal-3 overexpression alone recapitulated the inflammasome-related molecular program observed after systemic LPS challenge, with significant increases in NLRP3 (P = 0.0286), AIM2 (P = 0.0087), ASC (P = 0.0022), Caspase-1 (P = 0.0286), Caspase-11 (P = 0.0087), IL-1β (P = 0.0087), IL-18 (P = 0.0286), IL-6 (P = 0.0286), TNFα (P = 0.0286), Gsdmd (P = 0.0286), Cathepsin D (P = 0.0087) and Cathepsin B (P = 0.0087; Suppl. Figure 7B). These findings indicate that elevated microglial Gal-3 in the hippocampus is sufficient to activate the NLRP3/AIM2 inflammasome axis and trigger cognitive dysfunction. Together with the TD139 loss-of-function data, these results support both the sufficiency and necessity of Gal-3 in this pathogenic pathway. In parallel, hippocampal oxidative stress was markedly exacerbated, as evidenced by increased lipid peroxidation (MDA, P = 0.0317; Suppl. Figure 7C) and reduced total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, P = 0.0079; Suppl. Figure 7D). This redox imbalance coincided with decreased PV expression (P = 0.0411) and increased NOX4 (P = 0.0022; Suppl. Figure 7E), consistent with oxidative stress–associated compromise of PV interneuron integrity. Supporting a direct microglia-PV interaction, immunofluorescence analysis revealed reactive microglial clustering around PV interneurons, an approximately 50% reduction in soma-to-soma distance (P = 0.0005; Suppl. Figure 7F), and a more than twofold increase in the number of microglia around to each PV cell (P < 0.0001; Suppl. Figure 7F). Furthermore, oxidative DNA damage (8-OH-dG) was markedly elevated within PV interneurons (P = 0.0003; Suppl. Figure 7G). At the circuit level, in vivo recordings revealed a selective reduction in theta (P = 0.0016) and gamma (P = 0.0031) power, whereas alpha (P = 0.1274) and beta (P = 0.1709) power remained unchanged (Suppl. Figure 7H). Collectively, these findings indicate that hippocampal microglial Gal-3 overexpression is sufficient to induce inflammasome-driven neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, thereby disrupting PV interneuron function and network oscillations, and ultimately impairing cognition.

Chemogenetic activation of PV interneurons rescues LPS-induced network dysfunction and cognitive deficits

To determine whether re-engaging PV interneurons can counteract neuroinflammation-related deficits, we delivered a Cre-dependent rAAV encoding the excitatory DREADD hM3D(Gq) to PV-iCre mice and activated it with clozapine-N-oxide (CNO). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed robust viral expression, with EGFP labeling observed in the majority of PV interneurons, indicating strong colocalization (Fig. 10A). CNO markedly elevated the proportion of c-Fos+ PV-interneurons (F(2,15) = 136.5, P < 0.0001; Fig. 10B), confirming effective neuronal activation. Locomotor activity remained unaltered (total distance: F(1,36) = 0.0002, P = 0.9875; time spent in center: F(1,36) = 0.06374, P = 0.8021; Fig. 10C), indicating that the behavioral effects were not confounded by alterations in motor activity or anxiety-like behavior. Behavioral testing further showed that chemogenetic activation of PV interneurons significantly improved recognition memory, as reflected by an increased discrimination index in the test phase of the NORT (discrimination index, training: F(1,36) = 0.3141, P = 0.5787; test: F(1,36) = 5.545, P = 0.0241; Fig. 10D), and enhanced working memory, as indicated by increased spontaneous alternation in the YMT (Y-maze alternation: F(1,36) = 6.442, P = 0.0156; Fig. 10E), within the LPS-treated cohort. Contextual fear memory was similarly restored, as shown by increased freezing during the retention test (training: F(1,36) = 0.06134, P = 0.8058; test: F(1,36) = 4.814, P = 0.0348; Fig. 10F). At the network level, CNO restored the LPS-induced suppression of theta (F(1,24) = 4.496, P = 0.0445) and gamma (F(1,24) = 4.489, P = 0.0447) oscillatory power, whereas alpha (F(1,24) = 0.4286, P = 0.5189) and beta (F(1,24) = 0.002049, P = 0.9643) power remained unchanged (Fig. 10G). Together, these data identify PV interneurons hypoactivity as a critical node linking systemic inflammation to cognitive impairment and demonstrate that targeted chemogenetic activation of PV circuits is sufficient to restore network oscillatory activity and behavioral function.

Discussion

Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as a major driver of cognitive dysfunction across a broad spectrum of neurological and systemic disorders [49–52]. In the present study, using an LPS-induced mouse model of sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE), we identify hippocampal microglial galectin-3 (Gal-3) as a central pathogenic hub linking systemic inflammation to circuit dysfunction and cognitive decline. Integrating molecular, cellular, electrophysiological, and behavioral data, we delineate a coherent mechanistic cascade in which systemic inflammatory challenge induces microglial Gal-3 upregulation, which in turn engages TLR2 signaling and promotes NLRP3/AIM2 inflammasome assembly. This inflammatory amplification enhances local oxidative stress, selectively disrupts parvalbumin (PV) interneuron integrity and function, impairs hippocampal theta–gamma oscillations and synaptic plasticity, and ultimately leads to cognitive deficits (Fig. 11). Notably, both pharmacological Gal-3 inhibition with TD139 and chemogenetic reactivation of PV interneurons restored network dynamics and improved cognition, providing strong proof-of-concept for the pathogenic axis identified in this study.

Fig. 11Systemic LPS administration elevates microglial galectin-3 (Gal-3), which triggers toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) activation, disrupts the integrity of the parvalbumin (PV)-expressing interneuron network, impairs theta-gamma oscillations and synaptic plasticity, and ultimately contributes to cognitive dysfunction

Systemic LPS administration elevates microglial galectin-3 (Gal-3), which triggers toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) activation, disrupts the integrity of the parvalbumin (PV)-expressing interneuron network, impairs theta-gamma oscillations and synaptic plasticity, and ultimately contributes to cognitive dysfunction

Neuroinflammation is implicated not only in chronic neurodegenerative disorders but also in acute perioperative syndromes, including postoperative delirium and postoperative cognitive dysfunction [53–56]. Within this framework, our data identify microglial Gal-3 as both an initiator and an amplifier of the neuroinflammatory cascade leading to cognitive impairment. Systemic LPS challenge elicited a regionally biased microglial response in the hippocampus, characterized by marked proliferation, morphological transformation (enlarged somata, retracted processes), and increased expression of activation markers (Iba-1, CD11b) and phagolysosomal proteins (CD68, LAMP1). Transcriptomic profiling highlighted Gal-3 and TLR2 among the most robustly induced genes. Importantly, Gal-3 upregulation was largely restricted to microglia with minimal astrocytic overlap, and accumulated within CD68⁺ lysosomes, consistent with exacerbated phagolysosomal activity. Gain-of-function experiments strengthened the causal inference, demonstrating that selective Gal-3 overexpression in hippocampal microglia was sufficient to reproduce key features of the LPS phenotype, including enhanced phagocytosis, activation of the Gal-3/TLR2 pathway, oxidative stress, and cognitive decline. Together with prior studies linking Gal-3-driven microglial activation to chronic neuroinflammation, neuronal injury, and disease progression [20, 24, 25, 57, 58], our findings support the view that Gal-3 is not simply a biomarker, but a central regulator and promising therapeutic target in this pathogenic process.

Our data further refine the downstream signaling mechanism by identifying the Gal-3–TLR2–inflammasome axis as a major driver of persistent neuroinflammatory injury. Although systemic LPS is classically recognized to initiate acute innate immune responses through TLR4, our temporal analysis revealed that this TLR4 upregulation was transient, peaking at 12 h post-LPS and returning to baseline by day 10. In contrast, TLR2 remained persistently elevated during the later stage, which coincided with the critical window of cognitive impairment and the time point selected for our transcriptomic and protein analyses. In parallel, LPS markedly increased the expression of both Gal-3 and TLR2, which acted upstream to promote NLRP3/AIM2 inflammasome assembly in microglia. Consistently, systemic LPS challenge upregulated core inflammasome components, including NLRP3, ASC, AIM2, and caspase-1/11, as well as downstream proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-18, and IL-6 in hippocampal tissue, all of which have been strongly implicated in cognitive impairment across multiple disorders [59–61]. Importantly, TD139, a selective Gal-3 inhibitor, effectively abolished inflammasome activation and the associated cytokine surge, indicating that Gal-3 acts upstream of this inflammatory cascade. This temporal pattern suggests a context-dependent shift in Gal-3 receptor usage during disease progression. While the initial inflammatory response is likely triggered by LPS–TLR4 signaling, the sustained interaction between microglial Gal-3 and TLR2 appears to dominate the later phase of chronic neuroinflammation and circuit dysfunction. TLR2 activation at this stage may arise secondarily from the initial LPS–TLR4 response or from the release of endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [62], but our findings indicate that the Gal-3–TLR2 interaction serves as a key executor of prolonged pathology. Moreover, TD139 prevented the LPS-induced upregulation of lysosomal cathepsins D and B, linking Gal-3 signaling to lysosomal destabilization [63, 64] and microglial phagocytosis [65–67], both of which are known triggers of inflammasome activation. The consequent oxidative stress was evident—manifesting as elevated hippocampal MDA, increased NOX4 (a driver of oxidative stress [48, 68]), and higher microglial ROS production.; notably, all of these changes were mitigated by TD139. Collectively, these findings support the conclusion that the Gal-3–TLR2–inflammasome axis is a dominant driver of the oxidative and neuroinflammatory milieu during endotoxemia, whereas Gal-3 blockade effectively restrains microglial reactivity and limits secondary neuronal injury.

At the cellular level, PV interneurons represent a critical subclass of inhibitory neurons that regulate network oscillations and modulate neural activity through feedforward and feedback inhibition [69, 70]. This function is essential for maintaining the precise excitation-inhibition balance required for sustained information processing, working memory, and sensory integration [71–74]. Owing to their fast-spiking properties, PV interneurons rely heavily on robust mitochondrial metabolism, rendering them particularly vulnerable to metabolic and oxidative stress relative to other neuronal populations [35, 75–77]. Although systemic inflammation and the resulting accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are likely to affect multiple hippocampal cell types—including excitatory pyramidal neurons and other interneuron subtypes such as SST, CCK, and VIP neurons—our data identify PV interneurons as a critical pathological bottleneck. In our model, systemic LPS challenge, acting through Gal-3-driven microglial activation, markedly compromised both the structure and function of PV interneurons. Specifically, LPS exposure reduced PV protein expression and dendritic arborization in hippocampal CA1 region, decreased GABA immunoreactivity and PV/GABA co-labeling, and significantly lowered the number of active PV neurons, as indicated by reduced PV/c-Fos double-positive cells. Importantly, our findings further suggest a targeted microglia-mediated attack on PV interneurons. Reactive microglia preferentially clustered around PV neurons, as reflected by a shorter soma-to-soma distance and greater microglial encirclement, while oxidative DNA damage markers, including 8-OH-dG and γH2AX, accumulated specifically within PV cells. These observations indicate that PV interneurons are especially susceptible to the toxic microenvironment generated by Gal-3-dependent neuroinflammation. This selective vulnerability translated into pronounced circuit dysfunction. Consistent with the pacemaker role of PV interneurons, in vivo recordings revealed a selective reduction in theta and gamma power in the hippocampal CA1 region, whereas alpha and beta oscillations were relatively preserved. Notably, microglia-specific overexpression of Gal-3 alone was sufficient to reproduce this theta–gamma disruption, supporting a causal link between microglial Gal-3 activation and inhibitory circuit failure. At the synaptic level, LPS flattened the input–output curves, indicative of reduced synaptic efficacy, impaired long-term potentiation (LTP), decreased sIPSC amplitude, an increased E/I ratio in CA1 pyramidal neurons, and reduced action potential firing in hippocampal PV interneurons. Together, these changes point to weakened inhibitory control and impaired PV interneuron function. Crucially, our chemogenetic experiments demonstrated that selective reactivation of PV interneurons via hM3Dq was sufficient to restore theta/gamma oscillations and ameliorate LPS-induced cognitive deficits. This finding supports a mechanism of “functional bypass,” in which the exogenous excitatory drive provided by DREADD activation overrides the inflammation-induced suppression of PV interneuron firing. By restoring the pacemaker function of PV interneurons, the network synchronization required for memory encoding can be re-established even in the presence of upstream inflammatory stress. These results further suggest that the pyramidal network remains intrinsically functional but critically depends on intact PV-mediated synchronization. Whether this restoration of neuronal activity also exerts secondary neuroprotective effects by retrogradely modulating microglial reactivity through neuron–microglia crosstalk [78–80]) remains an important question for future investigation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our model primarily reflects acute to subacute endotoxemia. Longitudinal studies will be necessary to determine whether TD139 treatment or PV-interneuron activation can prevent persistent cognitive deficits and attenuate chronic microglial priming or long-term synaptic loss. Second, although the present study focused on the Gal-3/TLR2/inflammasome axis, transcriptomic analyses implicated additional pathways, including extracellular matrix remodeling, phagosome maturation, and AGE–RAGE signaling. The extent to which these processes interact with Gal-3 signaling remains to be clarified. Third, although our viral strategy employed a specific promoter to restrict Gal-3 overexpression to microglia and thereby minimize off-target expression in neurons or astrocytes, the physical spread of rAAV resulted in broad expression throughout the hippocampus rather than confinement to a single subregion. Nevertheless, because systemic LPS induces widespread Gal-3 upregulation across the hippocampal formation, this broad microglial manipulation likely captures the overall inflammatory pathology at the network level. In addition, constitutive rAAV-mediated expression lacks temporal precision; future studies using inducible approaches will be important for dissecting the stage-specific dynamics of microglial Gal-3 signaling. Fourth, although our representative analyses focused on tTlr$he hippocampal CA1 region because of the high vulnerability of its PV interneurons and its canonical role in theta–gamma oscillations [32, 81, 82], other hippocampal subfields, particularly the dentate gyrus (DG), are also crucial for memory encoding and consolidation through mechanisms involving neurogenesis and experience-activated neuronal ensembles [83–85]. A more comprehensive characterization of the microglial Gal-3 cascade across the entire hippocampal network, including the DG, will therefore be essential. In addition, cognitive tasks such as NOR and Y-maze spontaneous alternation depend on distributed hippocampal–cortical circuits, particularly those involving the perirhinal and prefrontal cortices. Although systemic LPS likely induces concurrent microglial Gal-3 upregulation in these cortical regions, our targeted viral manipulations underscore the essential role of the hippocampus as a critical bottleneck in this process. Indeed, Gal-3 dysregulation confined to hippocampal microglia was sufficient to disrupt the broader circuitry required for these behaviors. Future studies mapping Gal-3-dependent inflammatory dynamics across interconnected cortical regions will be necessary to establish a more complete systems-level view. Finally, to avoid the severe confounding effects of electrode-induced gliosis on microglial morphological quantification and ROS measurements, our biochemical, electrophysiological, and behavioral assays were conducted in standardized, parallel cohorts rather than sequentially within the same subjects. While this is a standard approach to preserve tissue integrity, it necessitates a convergent interpretation of the causal chain rather than a direct within-subject demonstration.

Conclusions

n this study, we identify hippocampal microglial Gal-3 as a crucial sensor and amplifier of neuroinflammation-induced cognitive impairment. Our data delineate a coherent pathogenic cascade in which systemic inflammatory challenge induces robust upregulation of microglial Gal-3, which subsequently engages TLR2 signaling and drives the assembly of the NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes. This inflammatory activation amplifies local oxidative stress, creating a toxic microenvironment that selectively compromises the structural and functional integrity of parvalbumin (PV) interneurons. The resulting loss of PV-mediated inhibitory control disrupts hippocampal theta–gamma synchronization, impairs synaptic plasticity, and ultimately leads to cognitive decline. Importantly, two independent rescue strategies—pharmacological inhibition of Gal-3 with TD139 and chemogenetic reactivation of PV interneurons—effectively reversed these network and behavioral abnormalities, providing strong proof-of-concept for this pathogenic pathway. Together, these findings establish a mechanistic link between systemic neuroinflammation and hippocampal circuit failure, and nominate microglial Gal-3 and PV interneuron circuitry as translationally relevant therapeutic targets for inflammation-associated cognitive disorders.
