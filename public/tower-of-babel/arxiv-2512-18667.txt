Abstract
. Noise is a major bottleneck in today’s quantum computing, stemming from decoherence, gate imperfections and other hardware limitations. Accurate noise fingerprints are essential, yet undocumented noise model differences between Quantum Ecosystems undermine core functionality, such as compilation, development and debugging, offering limited transferability and support for quantum software engineering (QSE) tasks. We propose a new research direction: live empirical noise fingerprinting as a lightweight QSE-oriented “noise fingerprinting”. Though explored in physics as device-level diagnostics, we reposition them as a QSE paradigm: we propose leveraging classical shadow tomography to enable a new generation of techniques. As a first step, we introduce SimShadow , which prepares reference states, applies shadow-tomography-inspired estimation and constructs deviation fingerprints. Initial experiments uncover systematic discrepancies between platforms (e.g. Frobenius distances up to 7.39) at up to 2.5 × 10 6 2.5\times 10^{6} lower cost than traditional methods. SimShadow opens new directions for noise-aware compilation, transpilation, cross-platform validation, error mitigation, and formal methods in QSE.

2.1.
The Noise Fingerprinting Paradigm

Existing vendor-supplied noise models provide the closest available characterisation of device behaviour, but they are necessarily simplified, offer limited transparency and detail, and are updated only periodically (e.g. every 24 hours). We advocate for a new paradigm centred on live, empirical, and descriptive noise fingerprinting, designed to be transparent and accessible, with three key properties:

Empirical.

A fingerprint is generated from
empirical data
by actively probing the target system through measurement, whether a simulator or hardware; it is not a theoretical model or a manufacturer’s datasheet. Here, the fingerprint represents the
actual
, “de facto” behaviour of the system, not the “de jure” behaviour described in its documentation. Our approach grounds noise understanding in the observable reality of systems, unlike techniques such as process tomography
(
9
)
or gate set tomography
(
6
)
, which provide physically complete but exponentially costly noise characterisations impractical beyond a few qubits.

Live and Updatable.

Unlike static characterisation, our fingerprint is
live
: it can be generated quickly and updated repeatedly. This is vital for hardware, where calibration drifts within hours, and for software, where fingerprints can be generated on demand for specific versions or commits in a CI/CD pipeline. Prior QSE methodologies, mainly focused on testing and benchmarking
(
12
)
, have typically focused on execution speed
(
30
;
17
;
22
)
. Our approach captures fidelity and consistency by transforming noise from a static property into a dynamic, observable state of the system.

Descriptive and Actionable.

Randomised benchmarking
(
25
)
yields only an aggregate fidelity score, revealing nothing about
how
or
why
simulators differ. We instead propose fingerprints as richer signatures: a
descriptive, structured signature
as a matrix of deviations. Its patterns may reveal the
type
,
strength
, and
asymmetries
of noise, making fingerprints potentially
actionable
. For example, developers might use them to debug portability issues
(
32
;
24
;
26
;
34
)
, or in guiding compiler optimisations
(
28
;
33
)
.

2.2.
The
SimShadow
Protocol

Our protocol, instantiated in the
SimShadow
prototype, is a multi-stage process that creates a compact yet comprehensive and interpretable signature of a simulator’s noise.

1. Core Concepts of Quantum Measurement.

We briefly recall the basics of quantum measurement
(
35
)
. A (pure) quantum state is represented by a ket
|\psi\rangle
in a complex Hilbert space; its dual bra is
\langle\psi|=(|\psi\rangle)^{\dagger}
. For a single qubit, the general state is a
superposition

|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle
. An
observable

O
is a Hermitian matrix whose eigenvalues are the possible measurement outcomes, and the expected result of measuring
O
on
|\psi\rangle
is
\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle
. For an arbitrary
n
-qubit system, the full statistical description is given by its density matrix
\rho
, which requires
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
parameters and thus scales exponentially.

2. Shadow-Inspired Estimation.

To create noise fingerprints efficiently, we produce an ordered collection of measurement outcomes that captures key deviations
without estimating the density matrix
. Our
shadow-inspired estimation
differs from
shadow tomography

(
2
;
1
)
, which approximates the density matrix, and from
classical shadows

(
18
;
8
)
, which create classical data structures for later prediction of arbitrary observables. Our fingerprints keep neither and go directly from outcomes to deviation signatures tailored specifically for QSE. For our fingerprinting use case with a fixed, small set of observables, we measure each observable directly in its eigenbasis. This is simpler and more efficient for a fixed set of targets than the full random-basis approach of classical shadows.

3. Prepare a Diverse Set of Reference States.

The protocol begins by preparing a small but diverse set of well-characterised quantum states
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
. The choice of these states is critical.
SimShadow
uses a strategic selection of
k
states to ensure the fingerprint has no blind spots:

•

Computational Basis States
(e.g.
|00\rangle,|11\rangle
): Sensitive to population-related errors like amplitude damping.

•

Superposition States
(e.g.
|+\rangle|0\rangle
1
1

1

|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
): Essential for detecting coherence errors like phase damping.

•

Entangled States
(e.g.
|\Phi^{+}\rangle
Bell state): To probe correlated errors like crosstalk. For two qubits, we use the maximally entangled
|\Phi^{+}\rangle=(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
Bell state.

4. Construct the Deviation Fingerprint.

After evolving each reference state under noise, we estimate a selected set of Pauli observables
\{O_{j}\}
(including single-qubit operators and two-qubit correlators
2
2

2

Two-qubit correlators (e.g.
X_{1}X_{2},X_{1}Z_{2}
) capture correlations between qubits, beyond what single-qubit (e.g.
X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1}
) measurements reveal.
). For each reference state
|\psi_{i}\rangle
(rows) and observable
O_{j}
(columns), we form the fingerprint matrix
F\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
. Each entry is the deviation between observed and ideal expectations:

F_{i,j}\;=\;\langle\psi_{i}|O_{j}|\psi_{i}\rangle_{\text{observed}}\;-\;\langle\psi_{i}|O_{j}|\psi_{i}\rangle_{\text{ideal}}\,

F
is a state–observable grid of deviations, not a density matrix. We compute
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{ideal}}
analytically and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{observed}}
via
SimShadow
. We compare platforms using the Frobenius distance
\lVert F^{(A)}-F^{(B)}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}(F^{(A)}_{i,j}-F^{(B)}_{i,j})^{2}}
, which measures the total magnitude of element-wise differences between two fingerprint matrices.

5. Algorithmic Analysis.

The final fingerprint matrix
F
is passed to our analysis algorithm, which performs two tasks: channel classification and parameter estimation.

Channel Classification.
We extract six statistical features from
F
: (1) mean deviation
\mu_{F}=\frac{1}{kn}\sum_{i,j}F_{i,j}
, (2) standard deviation
\sigma_{F}
, (3) Frobenius norm
\|F\|_{F}
, (4) sparsity
s=\frac{|\{(i,j):|F_{i,j}|<\tau\}|}{kn}
(with threshold
\tau=0.001
), (5) maximum absolute deviation, and (6) variance pattern (variance of column variances). Physics-informed decision rules then classify the noise type:

\displaystyle\text{classification}=\begin{cases}\text{phase\_damping}&\text{if }s>0.12\\
\text{amplitude\_damping}&\text{if }|\mu_{F}|>0.13\\
\text{depolarizing}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

These thresholds are calibrated for our experimental parameters (0.05, 0.10, 0.08); automated calibration for different ranges is future work.

Parameter Estimation.
For each noise type, we estimate parameters using calibrated formulas. For depolarizing noise with probability
p
, we use
p\approx|\mu_{F}|/C_{\text{dep}}
where
C_{\text{dep}}\approx 2.14
(calibrated). For amplitude damping with rate
\gamma
, we use an ensemble:
\gamma\approx\frac{1}{2}(|\mu_{F}|/C_{\text{amp}}+\text{var\_pattern}/0.001)
where
C_{\text{amp}}\approx 1.44
. For phase damping with rate
\lambda
, we use
\lambda\approx(1-s)\cdot C_{\text{phase}}
where
C_{\text{phase}}\approx 0.094
. These constants are calibrated for our parameter ranges; dynamic adaptation for different ranges is future work.

3.
Emerging Findings

To provide preliminary evidence for the feasibility and utility of our fingerprinting vision, we conducted a rigorous experiment to characterise and compare the noise models of Qiskit and Cirq. This section details our experimental setup and presents the key emerging results.

3.1.
Experimental Setup

Evaluation Questions.

Our experimental evaluation is designed to answer four key questions (EQs) regarding
SimShadow
’s capabilities:

EQ1:

Noise Channel Identification:
Can
SimShadow
correctly identify different noise types based on their fingerprints?

EQ2:

Cross-Platform Comparison:
Can
SimShadow
detect and quantify differences between platforms with identical configurations?

EQ3:

Scalability Analysis:
How does the efficiency of
SimShadow
compare to traditional methods as system size increases?

EQ4:

Parameter Estimation Accuracy:
How accurately can
SimShadow
estimate the strength of a configured noise model?

Evaluation Protocol.

We designed a protocol adapted from standard practices in quantum system characterisation
(
11
;
25
)
. We used
k=9
reference quantum states prepared with standard minimal circuits, strategically chosen to be sensitive to a wide range of error mechanisms: (i) 4 computational basis states to probe population-related errors, (ii) 4 superposition states to probe coherence errors, and (iii) 1 entangled state (
|\Phi^{+}\rangle
Bell state) to probe correlated multi-qubit errors. For each state, we measured
n=15
single- and two-qubit Pauli observables to cover local and correlated errors. The evaluation comprised 54 configurations (9 states
\times
3 noise types
\times
2 platforms) with noise parameters 0.05, 0.10, and 0.08 for Depolarising, Amplitude Damping, and Phase Damping respectively, with 500 quantum shots per measurement configuration.

3.2.
Emerging Results

Result 1: Characterising and Comparing Noise Models (EQ1 & EQ2).

The primary capability of
SimShadow
is to generate visually distinct fingerprints that characterise noise and enable cross-platform comparison.
Figure 2
shows the fingerprints for three noise types on both Qiskit and Cirq. The results are twofold. First, the fingerprints for each noise channel have a unique structure, enabling automatic noise identification. Second, and more critically for software engineering, the visual differences between platforms are stark. To quantify this, our analysis uses the Frobenius distance
(
14
)
, which measures the total magnitude of the difference between two fingerprint matrices. A distance of 0 signifies identical behaviour, while a larger value indicates a more pronounced systematic difference. The Frobenius distances between platform fingerprints were 7.18 for depolarising noise, 6.60 for amplitude damping, and 7.39 for phase damping. These values are approximately
9
–
10\times
larger than the expected Frobenius distance from statistical noise alone (estimated as
\sqrt{135}\times\sqrt{2}\times 1/\sqrt{500}\approx 0.74
, where
\sqrt{135}
reflects the Frobenius norm being the square root of a sum over 135 matrix elements,
\sqrt{2}
accounts for the variance of the difference between two independent noisy measurements, and
1/\sqrt{500}
is the shot-noise uncertainty per measurement), confirming that the simulators exhibit pronounced systematic differences despite identical configurations. Notably, we observe that Qiskit fingerprints exhibit approximately 2
\times
larger magnitudes than Cirq fingerprints (standard deviation 0.67 vs 0.31), indicating fundamental differences in how the platforms implement noise models beyond mere pattern variations.

Yes, the unique patterns in fingerprints can be used to identify noise types

Result 2: Scalability and Practicality (EQ3).

A key justification for our approach is its efficiency.

The fingerprinting approach is exponentially more scalable than traditional methods, making it a viable tool for validating realistic quantum software systems.

Result 3: Quantitative Analysis and Insights (EQ4).

Beyond visual inspection,
SimShadow

44.5\times
difference in execution speed between the simulators for this task, with Cirq achieving 140,221 shots/sec compared to Qiskit’s 3,148 shots/sec. Our parameter estimation algorithms demonstrate the feasibility of extracting noise strength from fingerprints, achieving within 3% error for phase damping parameters (see

2.2

Answer to EQ4:
Yes, fingerprints can be used to estimate noise parameters with reasonable accuracy, demonstrating their utility for deep quantitative analysis and debugging.

4.
Research Agenda for QSE

The ability to efficiently generate empirical noise fingerprints is not only a characterisation technique: it opens a broader research agenda for QSE. By treating fingerprints as actionable artefacts, we can envision a new generation of tools that account for the noisy, platform-dependent reality of quantum computing and integrate this knowledge directly into core QSE tasks.

Today’s Use Cases.
We examine two scenarios from today’s quantum software ecosystem that expose persistent pain points and show how fingerprints could provide new leverage.

Scenario 1: Cross-Platform Portability

Consider developers in academia and industry whose algorithm achieves 92% accuracy on Qiskit’s simulator, but drops sharply when ported with equivalent noise parameters to Cirq.

Problem:
Although the algorithm and configurations are identical, the platforms differ in how they
realise
noise models. Frameworks such as XACC
(
27
)
have improved code portability but do not provide tools to access, characterise, or validate open-source or live noise models across platforms. Furthermore, limited access to costly hardware means many developers, especially in education and research, must rely on simulators whose noise models are often opaque and proprietary. These undermine reproducibility, complicate debugging, and make benchmarking inconsistent.

Solution:
Fingerprint comparisons could generate quantitative reports and visual heatmaps that show precisely
how
noise models differ. Such artefacts are easy to interpret, even for junior developers and students, and would reduce dependence on costly hardware, improve reproducibility, and enable fair, consistent cross-platform testing in education, research, and industry.

Scenario 2: Enabling Independent Characterisation for Engineers

Consider a hardware or compiler engineer working on a new device.

Problem:
Vendors provide simplified noise models that may quickly become outdated as noise drifts.
Genetic algorithms have been used to model noise as descriptive functions
(
31
)
, but this approach is ill-suited for cross-framework comparison, requires manual specification of noise types, and shows limited accuracy on real devices.

Solution:
Our lightweight protocol allows engineers to generate a live, empirical fingerprint of the hardware’s present noise profile. This enables tuned calibration, adapted compiler optimisations, and error mitigation tailored to the device’s behaviour
today
rather than outdated assumptions.

Tomorrow’s Tools.
We have presented a new idea: noise fingerprinting inspired by classical shadows, as a practical solution to the critical QSE challenge in simulator validation.
Our prototype,
SimShadow
, demonstrates that this approach is efficient, scalable, and capable of uncovering and quantifying significant discrepancies between industrial-scale platforms. Nonetheless, our characterisation depends on the chosen states and observables, and establishing “ground truth” is challenging, as differences may reflect valid design choices.
This opens the door to further experimentation on refined fingerprint estimation, tailored to the precision needs of development, testing, and debugging contexts.
We envision this idea becoming a cornerstone for a new generation of QSE tools:

Standardised Benchmarking:
We envision a benchmark suite, ‘SimBench’, integrated into CI/CD pipelines to provide a “nutrition label” for simulator behaviour and prevent regressions.

Hardware-Informed Simulation:
By fingerprinting real hardware, we can quantify the “realism” of simulators and guide the development of more faithful models.

Noise-Aware Tooling:
Compilers, formal methods, and fuzzers could explore adapting their strategies based on empirical fingerprints.

Educational Artefacts:
Fingerprints as teaching aids to make noise behaviour visible and interpretable without access to hardware, lowering the barrier for students and junior developers.

We plan to extend our study to additional platforms and hardware backends, refine and tailor estimation methods for higher precision, and integrate fingerprints into development and testing tools.
In this way, we aim to assess how fingerprints can directly support portability, reproducibility, error diagnosis, and developer reasoning in practice within QSE.
We believe this work represents a foundational step toward building a robust validation infrastructure necessary for a mature QSE ecosystem.
