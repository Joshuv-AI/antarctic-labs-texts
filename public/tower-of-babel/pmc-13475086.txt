Targeting GRP75 by natural compound polyphyllin II triggers mitochondrial calcium overload and pyroptosis to potentiate cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Yuting Wang, Jingwen Dong, Yu Long, Liping Li, Cuicui Sun, Ruoqi Li, Jiayu Zhang, Yichen Liu, Zipeng Yao, Shuqi Wang, Ke Li, Mingxiao Yin, Xinbo Zhou, Na Zhang, Hongbin Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has emerged as a pivotal cancer treatment by activating antitumor immunity. However, its clinical efficacy remains limited in many patients, highlighting the need for combination strategies to overcome resistance. Inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) represents a promising approach to remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and improve ICB efficacy.MethodsA high-throughput screen of a natural compound library identified potent ICD inducers. Polyphyllin II (PPII), a bioactive component from Paris polyphylla, was selected for further investigation. Its effects on ICD markers, tumor growth, and immune activation were evaluated in vitro and in vivo. Limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry was employed to identify the direct target of PPII, followed by mechanistic studies using molecular and immunological assays.ResultsPPII was identified as a potent ICD inducer, stimulating the release of high mobility group box 1, ATP, and calreticulin from tumor cells. PPII suppressed tumor growth and enhanced antitumor immunity by promoting dendritic cell maturation and antigen cross-presentation, leading to CD8+ T-cell activation. Mechanistically, PPII directly bound to glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75), enhancing endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondrial tethering, provoking endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitochondrial calcium overload, and promoting cytochrome c release and caspase-3 activation. This cascade ultimately triggered gasdermin E (GSDME)-mediated pyroptosis. Furthermore, PPII synergized with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 therapy by reprogramming the tumor immune microenvironment and promoting systemic antitumor immunity.ConclusionOur findings identify GRP75 as a novel therapeutic target for cancer immunotherapy and highlight PPII-driven immune reprogramming as a translatable strategy to potentiate ICB efficacy through the induction of immunogenic pyroptosis.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study identifies polyphyllin II (PPII), a natural compound, as a novel and potent ICD inducer that triggers immunogenic pyroptosis through gasdermin E (GSDME) cleavage. Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has advanced rapidly in recent years due to its potential for selective tumor targeting and the induction of systemic immune responses.1 Diverse therapeutic modalities, such as immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and adoptive cell therapy, have achieved remarkable clinical success in cancer treatment.2 3 However, a substantial proportion of patients exhibit limited or no response to these treatments, resulting in a low overall clinical response rate.4 A major barrier to effective immunotherapy is the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which is characterized by T-cell exhaustion and the accumulation of immunosuppressive cells.5 Consequently, reversing the immunosuppressive TME and converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones remains a pivotal challenge in current cancer immunotherapy.

A promising strategy to reprogram the immunosuppressive TME is the induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells. During the process of ICD, dying tumor cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as surface-exposed calreticulin (CRT), high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and ATP, which collectively stimulate adaptive immune response.6 The immunogenicity of cell death is modality-dependent. While apoptosis is generally non-immunogenic, other forms of programmed cell death such as pyroptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis are strongly associated with ICD.7 Pyroptosis, a lytic and inflammatory form of cell death mediated by gasdermin family proteins, is of particular therapeutic interest. On activation, gasdermin proteins oligomerize to form plasma membrane pores, leading to osmotic cell rupture8 and the release of inflammatory cytokines (eg, interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18) and DAMPs, thereby eliciting a robust antitumor immune response.9 Although conventional chemotherapy drugs can induce pyroptosis,10 their clinical utility is limited by high toxicity, drug resistance, poor selectivity, and the need for high doses. Consequently, developing novel, potent, and selective inducers of pyroptosis to achieve effective ICD has become a critical research priority.

Glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75), also known as Mortalin or heat shock protein family A member 9 (HSPA9), is a member of the conserved heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) chaperone family.11 GRP75 primarily localizes to mitochondria but also presents at the cell membrane, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and mitochondrial-associated ER membranes (MAMs).12 Notably, MAMs serve as a critical interface for mitochondrial-ER communication, playing a central role in maintaining calcium homeostasis and regulating lipid metabolism.13 14 At MAMs, GRP75 bridges the ER inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R) with the mitochondrial voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1). This IP3R-GRP75-VDAC1 ternary complex facilitates the direct transfer of Ca²+ from the ER to the mitochondria, a process critical for maintaining intracellular Ca²+ homeostasis and regulating cell fate.15 Through this mechanism, GRP75 promotes tumor progression by enhancing cancer cell metabolism, inhibiting apoptosis, and promoting chemoresistance.12 Although accumulating evidence suggests that GRP75 is strongly implicated in cancer initiation and progression,16–18 its potential role in regulating ICD remains unexplored.

To identify novel ICD inducers, we established a dual screening system based on ICD reporter genes and T-cell activation.19 Using this platform to screen a natural product library, we identified polyphyllin II (PPII), a bioactive component derived from Paris polyphylla,20 as a candidate that potently activated ICD. Although PPII is known to possess antitumor activity,21 22 its capacity to induce ICD has not previously been reported. In this study, we demonstrate that PPII enhances tumor immunogenicity and promotes the activation of dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells. PPII treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth in vivo in a manner that depended on DCs and CD8+ T cells. Mechanistically, we found that PPII binds directly to GRP75, leading to mitochondrial calcium overload and ER stress, which ultimately induce pyroptosis. This pyroptotic cell death facilitated antigen cross-presentation by DCs and enhanced T-cell activation, collectively amplifying antitumor immunity. Our findings reveal the translational potential of PPII as a chemo-immunotherapeutic agent capable of improving outcomes in cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Cell culture

B16, A375, MDA-MB231 tumor cells and HaCaT, NIH/3T3 cells were purchased from the Institute of Basic Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). 4T1 cells were kindly gifted by Dr Ke Li (Institute of Medicinal Biotechnology, Chinese Academy of Medical Science). The HEK293T cell line used for lentiviral packaging was kindly provided by Dr Guohui Wan (School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China). Mouse hybridoma cell B3Z was kindly gifted by Dr Nilabh Shastri (University of California, Berkeley, California, USA). The B16-ovalbumin (OVA) and MC38-OVA cell lines were established by stably transfecting the parental B16 and MC38 cells with the plasmid pCI-neo-mOVA (Addgene #25099). All cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium 1640 (RPMI-1640) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. All cell lines tested negative for Mycoplasma contamination.

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from femoral bone marrow of 6-week-old C57BL/6 mice and cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium supplemented with 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, mouse granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (mGM-CSF, 20 ng/mL), and mouse interleukin-4 (mIL-4, 20 ng/mL). The medium was replaced with fresh medium containing cytokines every 2 days.

Chemicals, antibodies and plasmids

Natural product chemical monomers were obtained from TargetMol (Beijing, China). Additional reagents and commercial assay kits used in this study are detailed in online supplemental table S1, while online supplemental tables S2 and S3 in the supporting information list the antibodies used. The HMGB1-Gluc reporter plasmid was kindly gifted by Professor Xiaojun Xia (Collaborative Innovation Center for Cancer Medicine, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, Guangzhou, China). The plasmids pLKO.1, psPAX2, and VSVG were generously provided by Professor Guohui Wan (Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China).

Measurement of dendritic cells and T-cell activation

To evaluate DCs’ activation and antigen-presenting capacity, B16-OVA or MC38-OVA cells (3×105) were plated in 6-well plates and treated with PPII (6 µM) for 6 hours, followed by thoroughly washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and an additional 18-hour incubation. The treated cells were subsequently co-cultured with BMDCs (3×105) for an additional 24 hours. Following co-culture, cells were harvested and stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against CD11c, CD40, CD86, CD80, MHC-I, MHC-II, and H-2Kb-SIINFEKL complexes. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of these surface markers on DCs was quantified by flow cytometry with a Guava EasyCyte instrument, and data were analyzed with GuavaSoft V.3.1.

To assess T-cell activation, B3Z cells were added to the previously co-cultured BMDCs at a 5:1 ratio and further incubated for 24 hours. After centrifugation (400×g, 5 min), the culture supernatants were collected for IL-2 and interferon (IFN)-γ quantification via ELISA. The harvested cells were surface-stained with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-CD8 and anti-CD69 antibodies, followed by intracellular staining for IFN-γ and granzyme B (GzmB) after fixation/permeabilization.

LacZ activity measurement was conducted in parallel using 96-well cultures. B3Z cells were lysed in Tris-based buffer (250 mM, pH 8.0; 0.1% Triton X-100; 50 µL/well). After three freeze-thaw cycles, 50 µL of PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 100 µL of substrate solution (1 mg/mL chlorophenol red-β-D-galactopyranoside) were added to each well. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 12–18 hours until color development was detectable, and absorbance was measured at 580 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using the 3-(4, 5-dimethyl thiazol-2-yl)−2, 5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates and treated with varying concentrations of PPII. After 24 hours of incubation, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by an additional 4 hours of incubation. The supernatant was then discarded, and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well. Plates were vortexed for 20 min, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm.

In vitro measurement of ICD

The induction of ICD was detected by monitoring cell surface exposure of CRT, extracellular release of HMGB1, and ATP secretion. B16 or A375 cells were transfected with the HMGB1-Gluc reporter plasmid using vigofect and selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 3~4 weeks to establish stable cell lines. To measure HMGB1-Gluc activity, B16 or A375 cells stably expressing HMGB1-Gluc were plated in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 hours. The cells were subsequently treated with specified concentrations of PPII for an additional 24 hours. Following treatment, culture supernatants were collected, and HMGB1-Gluc activity was quantified using the Renilla Luciferase Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence was recorded using a microplate reader, and the relative fold-change was calculated against vehicle-treated controls. Essentially similar experimental procedures were used to measure extracellular ATP levels in culture supernatant using the ENLITEN ATP Assay System (Promega) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For detecting cell surface-exposed CRT, tumor cells treated with PPII were harvested, washed two times with PBS, and stained with a fluorescently labeled CRT-specific antibody at 4°C for 30 min. CRT exposure on the cell surface was detected and quantified by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence imaging.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ in cell culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (R&D Systems; IL-2: SM2000, IFN-γ: MIF00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) were performed using the UniPeak U+One Step RT-qPCR SYBR Green Kit (Vazyme). The gene expression levels were finally normalized to GAPDH/β-actin and analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are listed in online supplemental table S4.

In vitro evaluation of pyroptosis

To examine the morphological changes, the cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with PPII at indicated concentrations. Images were acquired using a Zeiss microscope (Axio Vert.A1), with representative images selected from at least three randomly chosen fields. For the SYTOX Green uptake assay, A375 cells were seeded in 6-well plates overnight and treated with 6 µM PPII for varying durations. Following treatment, cells were incubated with 500 nM SYTOX Green at 37°C for 10 min, and fluorescence images were captured by Zeiss microscope. The release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was measured using the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay kit (Promega, G1780) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Animal studies

C57BL/6J, BALB/c and BALB/c Nude mice (6–8 weeks old, female) were purchased from HFK Bio-Technology (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Female animals were involved, and sex was not considered as a biological variable. All animal studies were approved by the Institute of Medicinal Biotechnology (IMB) animal ethics committee, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (approval numbers: IMB-20240514D802, IMB-20240708D202, IMB-20240827D202, IMB-20241225D801, IMB-20250304D601, IMB-20250609D801, IMB-20250627D803, and IMB-20250627D802). For in vivo study, B16 (3×105 cells) or 4T1 (1×106 cells) cells were inoculated subcutaneously into the right flank of C57BL/6J mice or BALB/c nude mice. On tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³, PPII (2, 5, and 10 mg/kg) was administered through intratumoral injection every other day. Anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) (clone 29F.1A12) was administered via intraperitoneal injection (100 µg per mouse) every other day. Tumor growth and body weight of the mice were assessed every 2 days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: 1/2×length×(width)². At the experimental endpoint, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for flow cytometry analysis to evaluate tumor-infiltrating immune cells. To elucidate the role of GRP75 in antitumor effects, wild-type mice inoculated with GRP75-knockdown B16 tumors were monitored for tumor growth.

For the in vivo tumor vaccination and rechallenge experiment, C57BL/6J mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank with 1×10⁵ B16 cells that had been either treated with 20 µM PPII or subjected to three cycles of freeze-thawing in liquid nitrogen. Prior to inoculation, cell viability was evaluated using a propidium iodide (20 µg/mL) staining assay. The viability was >95% in the DMSO-treated (control) group, approximately 50% in the PPII-treated group, and 0% in the freeze-thawed (non-immunogenic death) groups. Mice receiving DMSO-treated cells rapidly developed primary tumors at the right flank. 7 days later, 3×10⁵ live B16 cells were injected into the contralateral flank. Tumor growth was subsequently monitored. To deplete DCs in vivo, mice were intravenously administered cytochrome c (Cyt c) at a dose of 5 mg per mouse on days −1, 6, and 13. In the lung metastasis model, C57BL/6J mice were intravenously injected with 5×10⁵ B16 cells via the tail vein. Starting the day after inoculation, the mice were treated with PPII (0.5 or 1 mg/kg) through intraperitoneal administration, once every 3 days. On day 14 post-inoculation, the mice were euthanized, and the intact lungs were harvested to quantify the number of B16 lung metastatic nodules. Representative lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently processed for H&E staining.

For the distal tumor model, BALB/c mice were inoculated with 1×106 4T1 cells on the right flank on day 0, followed by subcutaneous injection of 1×106 4T1 cells on day 5 on the contralateral sites. Mice received intratumoral injections of PPII and intraperitoneal injections of anti-PD-1 every other day, and tumor growth was monitored at both the primary and distant sites.

Analysis of the effectiveness of combination therapy

The effectiveness of combination therapy is evaluated via the fractional product (fp), which is defined as fp = I1+2/(I1 + I2 − I1 × I2). Here, fp >1 indicates a synergistic effect, fp = 1 indicates an additive effect, and fp <1 indicates an antagonistic effect. I1 and I2 denote the average inhibition rates of each individual drug, whereas I1+2 represents the average inhibition rate achieved with the combined use of the two drugs.23

Flow cytometric analysis of immune cells in tumors and tumor-draining lymph nodes

Tumor tissues were collected and mechanically dissociated, followed by enzymatic digestion in a medium containing 1 mg/mL type IV collagenase, 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich), and a Cell Stimulation Cocktail (phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate/ionomycin with brefeldin A) at 37°C for 30 min. The digested tissue was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific for major immune subsets: T cells (CD45, CD3, CD8, IFN-γ, GzmB), DCs (CD45, CD11c, CLEC9A, CD40, CD80, CD86, MHC-I), macrophages (for polarization: CD45, CD11b, F4/80, CD86, MHC-II, CD206), regulatory T cells (Tregs) (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD25, Foxp3), and myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) subsets (CD45, CD11b, MHC-II, F4/80). The tumor-draining lymph nodes (tDLN) were ground in PBS and filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD45, CD11c, CLEC9A, CCR7, CD80, and CD86. Flow cytometry was performed with the Guava easyCyte platform, and data were analyzed using the GuavaSoft V.3.1 software. Gating strategies are presented in online supplemental figure S9.

shRNA knockdown

The shRNAs targeting the respective target proteins were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the plasmids and the viral packaging plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G) using Vigofect transfection reagent (Vigorous Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s instructions. At 72 hours post-transfection, the culture medium containing viral particles was collected and filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane. The filtrate was then added to the target cell culture in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Solarbio). Puromycin selection was applied to generate stable cell lines. The knockdown effect was assessed by western blot analysis of whole cell protein extracts. The target sequences of shRNAs are listed in online supplemental table S5.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout

SgRNAs were inserted into LentiCRISPRv2 vector. HEK293T cells in 10 cm dishes were transfected with 5 µg lentiviral plasmid, 5 µg psPAX2, and 1 µg VSVG. At 72 hours post-transfection, the culture medium containing viral particles was collected and filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane. B16 cells were transduced for 24 hours, then cultured in puromycin-supplemented medium. The sgRNA sequences targeting gasdermin E (GSDME) are listed in online supplemental table S6.

Colony formation assay

A375 or B16 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Following a 2-day incubation, cells were treated with compounds at the indicated concentrations for 7–10 days. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and colonies were counted using ImageJ software.

Western blotting and coimmunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed with lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 5 mM EGTA, 1 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 5 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 0.5% Triton X-100 and 1 mM DTT) supplemented with protease inhibitor on ice for 30 min. The supernatant was collected following centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4°C. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay, and equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5×loading buffer and denatured by heating at 95°C for 10 min. The samples were subsequently analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and western blotting. Blots were visualized using the Tanon 5200 image system.

For coimmunoprecipitation, cells were treated with PPII and then lysed using NP-40 lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. The cell lysate (600 µL) was mixed with 4 µL of anti-GRP75 or anti-IgG antibody and 40 µL of Protein A/G magnetic beads, overnight at 4°C on a rotator. The beads were then washed three times with cold lysis buffer. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 1×loading buffer at 95°C for 10 min. The eluates were subsequently analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting.

Limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry assay

To identify potential protein targets of PPII, a limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry (LiP-MS) assay was performed.24 A375 cells were treated with DMSO or PPII (6 µM) for 4 hours, followed by protein extraction in a native lysis buffer to maintain protein conformations. For the limited proteolysis step, proteinase K was added at an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:100 and incubated for 5 min at 25°C. The reaction was immediately quenched by boiling, followed by standard global trypsin digestion. Resulting peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium (https://proteomecentral.proteomexchange.org) via the iProX partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD077862.

Cellular thermal shift assay

A375 or B16 cells (5×106) were digested, resuspended in DMEM, and treated with DMSO or PPII at 37°C for 1 hour. The cell suspension was aliquoted into seven PCR tubes (100 µL per tube). The tubes were heated at designated temperatures for 3 min and cooled at room temperature for 3 min. Samples were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and room temperature, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was mixed with 5×loading buffer, denatured by heating at 95°C for 10 min, and analyzed by western blotting.

Drug affinity responsive target stability assay

Total cellular protein of A375 or B16 cells was extracted using NP40 lysis buffer and quantified via the Bradford assay. Aliquots containing 100 µg of protein were incubated with DMSO or PPII at the indicated final concentrations at 4°C for 8 hours. After incubation, Pronase was added at a mass ratio of 1:100 (Pronase to total protein) and digestion proceeded for 30 min at 37°C. The reaction was terminated by adding 5×SDS loading buffer and boiling at 95°C for 10 min, followed by western blot analysis.

Proximity ligation assay

The in situ proximity ligation assay was performed using the Duolink In Situ PLA kit (Sigma-Aldrich) to detect the interactions between VDAC1 and IP3R. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and subsequently permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 hour. After blocking with Duolink blocking buffer for 2 hours at room temperature, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in the blocking buffer (anti-VDAC1, 1:200; anti-IP3R, 1:200). After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with proximity ligation assay (PLA) probes for 1 hour at 37°C. The ligation solution was then added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to hybridize with the PLA probes and form a closed circle. Next, cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled oligonucleotides and polymerase at 37°C for 90 min. Prior to imaging, nuclei were stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Olympus). Subsequent image processing and analysis were conducted with Olympus cellSens software (V.3.1.1) and ImageJ.

Mitochondrial Ca2+ measurement

Mitochondrial Ca²+ levels were measured using the fluorescent indicator Rhod-2 AM. Following treatment, cells were loaded with 2 µM Rhod-2 AM in DMEM at 37°C for 30 min, washed two times with PBS, and resuspended in PBS for analysis. Mitochondrial localization of Rhod-2 was confirmed by confocal microscopy (Olympus). The change in Ca²+ concentration was quantified by flow cytometry based on the MFI.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

Mitochondrial membrane potential was detected using the fluorescent probes TMRM and JC-1. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates overnight and treated as indicated. For TMRM staining, cells were collected following treatment, washed with PBS, and incubated with TMRM dye at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. The stained cells were then washed three times with PBS, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry. For JC-1 staining, the assay was performed using Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Olympus).

Detection of cytosolic cytochrome c

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates overnight and treated as indicated. Following treatment, cells were harvested and washed two times with cold PBS. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 200 µL of permeabilization buffer (containing 100 µg/mL digitonin, 75 mM NaCl, 8 mM Na₂HPO₄, 250 mM sucrose, and protease inhibitors, pH 7.4) and lysed on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation (4°C, 21, 000×g, 5 min), the supernatant (cytosolic fraction) was carefully collected. The pellet containing mitochondria was washed two times with cold PBS containing protease inhibitors under the same centrifugation conditions and then lysed in 150 µL of radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer. Samples were then subjected to immunoblot analysis.

Surface plasmon resonance analysis

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements were performed at room temperature using an XLEMENT WeSPR One Biomolecular Interaction Analyzer. Recombinant human GRP75 protein was immobilized onto a carboxylated sensor chip according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PPII was dissolved in running buffer, 1× phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST) containing 5% DMSO, and the flow path was equilibrated with the same buffer. PPII solutions at the indicated concentrations were injected into the system at a flow rate of 40 µL/min for 150 s. The binding kinetics were determined using the WeSPR One software (V.5.3.22).

Molecular docking

Protein-ligand docking was conducted using Schrödinger Glide (2024–1). The crystal structure of GRP75 (PDB: 6P2U) was prepared with Protein Preparation Wizard: hydrogen/protonation treatment (Epik, pH 7.0) and restrained minimization (OPLS4). Ligands were prepared with LigPrep (conformers sampled). A cubic grid with 10 Å sides was centered on the binding site. Docking was carried out in standard precision mode with default settings. The resulting poses were ranked by GlideScore, and the top-scoring conformation was selected for further analysis.

Overall survival analysis

The GEPIA2 database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) was used to generate Kaplan-Meier survival curves based on GRP75 expression across multiple cancer types.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.5. Data are presented as mean±SD and were evaluated using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests for comparisons between two groups, or one-way/two-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple groups. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High-throughput screening identifies PPII as a potent ICD inducer

To identify novel inducers of ICD, we performed a high-throughput screen using a dual-assay system25 (figure 1A). First, 535 distinct compounds from a natural bioactive library were screened for their ability to induce HMGB1 release, a key hallmark of ICD, using an HMGB1-Gluc reporter system. In the primary HMGB1-Gluc reporter screen, 29 compounds that met the predefined cut-off of a >five-fold increase in luciferase activity relative to the vehicle control were identified. The hit compounds were then assessed in a B3Z T-cell activation assay, in which B16-OVA cells pretreated with hit compounds were co-cultured with BMDCs and B3Z cells (a CD8+ T-cell hybridoma specifically recognizing the OVA epitope). T-cell activation was quantified via an IL-2 promoter-driven β-galactosidase (LacZ) reporter, and the secondary screen narrowed this selection to 19 compounds with an OD590>0.5. (The full screening parameters, comprehensive profiles of the identified hit compounds are summarized in online supplemental figure S1A,B). A dual-parameter scatter plot was used to evaluate the hit compounds based on their ability to induce both HMGB1 release and LacZ T-cell activation (figure 1B). Notably, PPII, an active compound extracted from P. polyphylla, concurrently induced markedly high activity in both HMGB1-Gluc and LacZ assays (figure 1B,C), second only to the positive control Raddeanin A.19

We next evaluated the cytotoxicity of PPII against multiple tumor cell lines using an MTT assay. Results demonstrated that PPII displayed potent cytotoxicity against human (A375) and mouse (B16) melanoma cells, as well as human (MDA-MB-231) and mouse (4T1) breast cancer cells, with half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values ranging from 3 to 5 µM (figure 1D and online supplemental figure S2A). Similarly, a colony formation assay showed that PPII treatment markedly reduced the colony-forming capacity of both B16 and A375 cells (figure 1E). Notably, PPII reduced the viability of A375 and B16 cells in a dose-dependent manner while showing minimal toxicity toward the normal liver cell line THLE-3, as well as other representative normal cell types including mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH-3T3) and human immortalized epidermal cells (HaCaT) (online supplemental figure S2B). Given its selective cytotoxicity, we evaluated PPII’s ability to trigger ICD. PPII treatment resulted in a dose-dependent increase in HMGB1-Gluc activity (figure 1F) and ATP secretion (figure 1G) in both B16 and A375 cell lines. Furthermore, flow cytometry and confocal microscopy demonstrated that PPII treatment significantly upregulated the surface expression of CRT in B16 cells (figure 1H,I). Similar results were recapitulated in A375 cells (online supplemental figure S2C,D), further confirming the ICD-inducing effect of PPII. To further evaluate the ICD potency of PPII, we benchmarked it against doxorubicin (DOX), a well-established ICD-inducing chemotherapeutic agent.26 At equimolar concentrations, PPII triggered significantly higher levels of ICD hallmarks compared with DOX, including HMGB1 release, ATP secretion, and CRT surface exposure in B16 and A375 cells (online supplemental figure S2E–H), indicating the potent ICD-inducing capabilities of PPII relative to established ICD-inducing agents.

To further validate the ICD-inducing capacity of PPII in vivo, we employed a well-established preventive tumor vaccination model in C57BL/6 mice, a gold-standard methodology for assessing ICD (figure 1J). Mice were immunized subcutaneously on the right flank with either PPII-pretreated or freeze-thawed B16 melanoma cells, a model characterized by high aggressiveness and low immunogenicity. 7 days later, the same mice were challenged on the opposite (left) flank with live, untreated B16 cells. We observed that all mice immunized with either DMSO-treated or freeze-thawed B16 cells developed tumors on the left flank after rechallenge (figure 1K). In contrast, approximately 30% of mice vaccinated with PPII-treated dead cells remained tumor-free for 15 days post-challenge (figure 1K). Furthermore, analysis of tumor growth curves revealed that mice immunized with PPII-treated B16 cells exhibited significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with the other two groups (figure 1L). To validate the broad efficacy of PPII as a tumor vaccine, we employed the 4T1 breast cancer model, where PPII-pretreated cells achieved a 66.7% tumor-free rate after rechallenge (online supplemental figure S2I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PPII-induced ICD can serve as an effective tumor vaccine, preventing tumor initiation and growth. Overall, these findings confirm that PPII is a potent ICD inducer.

PPII-treated tumor cells promote DCs-mediated T cells activation

As DAMPs released during ICD—including HMGB1, ATP, and CRT—are critical for activating antigen-presenting cells and initiating adaptive immunity, we next examined whether PPII-treated tumor cells could enhance the activation of DCs and T cells. Using the B16-OVA cells and BMDCs co-culture system (figure 2A), we found that 6 µM PPII-treated B16-OVA cells robustly promoted the maturation of BMDCs, as indicated by enhanced surface expression of CD40, CD80, CD86, MHC-I, and MHC-II (figure 2B–F). A similar effect was observed in MC38-OVA cells co-cultured with BMDCs (online supplemental figure S3A–E). Furthermore, BMDCs co-cultured with PPII-pretreated tumor cells exhibited increased surface expression of the SIINFEKL epitope (OVA 257–264) in complex with major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I (figure 2G), indicating improved antigen cross-presentation. Moreover, PPII treatment significantly upregulated the expression of key genes involved in antigen processing and presentation in BMDCs, including the MHC-I subunit B2m, the peptide-trimming enzyme Erap1, and the essential peptide-loading transporters Tap1 and Tap2 (figure 2H). These data demonstrate that PPII-induced ICD promotes DCs maturation and enhances their antigen-presenting capacity.

We subsequently evaluated PPII-mediated T-cell activation in a triple-cell co-culture system containing B16-OVA cells, BMDCs, and OVA-specific B3Z CD8+ T cells (figure 2A). PPII treatment markedly increased LacZ reporter activity in B3Z cells (figure 2I), along with elevated secretion of T cell-derived cytokines IL-2 and IFN-γ (figure 2J,K). Similarly, the expression levels of the activation marker CD69 (figure 2L), as well as the effector molecules GzmB and IFN-γ (figure 2M,N), were notably increased in B3Z cells co-cultured with PPII-treated tumor cells and BMDCs. To confirm that this immunogenic effect was specifically driven by ICD rather than general cell death, we compared PPII treatment to freeze-thawed (necrosis) and paclitaxel (apoptosis) treatment B16-OVA cells. PPII-treated cells induced significantly higher BMDCs maturation and subsequent B3Z T cells activation (CD69 upregulation, IFN-γ and GzmB secretion) than either necrosis or apoptosis controls (online supplemental figure S3F–H). Furthermore, as tumor cells lacking MHC-I and the necessary co-stimulatory signals (CD80/CD86), PPII-treated B16-OVA cells failed to activate T cells in the absence of BMDCs (online supplemental figure S3I,J), demonstrating that cross-presentation by professional antigen-presenting cells is strictly required for T-cell activation. Taken together, these results indicate that PPII-induced ICD enhances DCs maturation and antigen cross-presentation, leading to potent activation of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells.

PPII inhibits tumor growth by activating antitumor immunity

To evaluate the effect of PPII on immune-mediated tumor control in vivo, C57BL/6 mice bearing subcutaneous B16 melanomas were treated with intratumoral (i.t.) injections of PPII (figure 3A). Our results demonstrated that treatment with PPII (at doses of 2, 5, and 10 mg/kg) significantly suppressed tumor growth (figure 3B,C) and reduced tumor weight (online supplemental figure S4A) compared with controls, achieving tumor inhibition rates of 49.1% at 5 mg/kg and 63.6% at 10 mg/kg. Similarly, PPII also exhibited potent antitumor activity in a 4T1 breast cancer model (figure 3D,E and online supplemental figure S4B). Importantly, i.t. administration of PPII did not induce observable systemic toxicity, as evidenced by stable body weight (online supplemental figure S4C), normal serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels (online supplemental figure S4D,E), absence of histopathological abnormalities in major organs (online supplemental figure S4F) and unchanged organ indices (online supplemental figure S4G).

To characterize the immune response in vivo, we performed tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TILs) profile analysis on tumor tissues and tDLNs from PPII-treated B16 tumor-bearing mice. We found that PPII significantly increased the infiltration of CD11c+ CLEC9A+ type 1 conventional dendritic cells (cDC1) and CD8+ T cells within the tumor (figure 3F). Consistent with the in vitro findings, cDC1 in PPII-treated B16 tumors exhibited elevated surface expression of costimulatory molecules CD40, CD80 and CD86, as well as antigen-presenting molecules MHC-I (figure 3G). Moreover, we found a profound increase in the levels of the effector molecules GzmB and IFN-γ derived from CD8+ T cells in the PPII-treated group, indicating enhanced cytotoxic function of T cells within the TME (figure 3H). Furthermore, in tDLNs, PPII treatment significantly increased the infiltration of cDC1s (online supplemental figure S5A) and upregulated their expression of CCR7 (online supplemental figure S5B), a key chemokine receptor essential for DCs lymph node homing and T-cell priming.27

To determine the functional contribution of DCs in PPII-mediated antitumor effect, we depleted DCs in vivo by administering Cyt c28 (figure 3I). Strikingly, DC depletion by Cyt c abolished the antitumor effect of PPII (figure 3J and online supplemental figure S5C,D), indicating that the function of DC is essential for the antitumor activity of PPII. Notably, in BALB/c nude mice with impaired T-cell function, PPII failed to inhibit B16 tumor growth (figure 3K and online supplemental figure S5E,F), confirming the essential role of T cells in PPII‐driven antitumor immunity. Finally, we evaluated the antitumor effect of PPII on a melanoma lung metastasis model by intravenous injection of B16 cells into C57BL/6 mice. Intraperitoneal administration of PPII markedly reduced the number of lung metastatic nodules (figure 3L) and preserved the normal histological architecture of the lung tissue (figure 3M). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PPII exerts its antitumor effects by activating DCs and promoting CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity.

PPII triggers GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in tumor cells

To determine the cell death modality induced by PPII, we pretreated tumor cells prior to DMSO or PPII treatment with inhibitors of distinct death pathways: z-VAD-FMK (ZVF, pan-caspase inhibitor), necrostatin-1 (necroptosis), 3-methyladenine (autophagy inhibitor), ferrostatin-1 (ferroptosis inhibitor), LDC7559 and mannose29 (pyroptosis inhibitor). Results indicated that PPII-induced cell death in A375 cells was markedly attenuated by the pan-caspase inhibitor ZVF and pyroptosis inhibitor mannose (figure 4A), suggesting a caspase-dependent pyroptotic process. Consistently, PPII treatment triggered characteristic pyroptotic morphology, including membrane blebbing and cellular swelling, in both A375 and B16 cells (figure 4B). PPII-induced pyroptotic death was further confirmed by time-dependent SYTOX Green uptake (figure 4C) and dose-dependent LDH release (figure 4D,E), another key indicator of pyroptosis. Similar morphological changes and increased LDH release were observed in PPII-treated MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 cells (online supplemental figure S6A–C).

Since pyroptosis is executed by cleaved gasdermin proteins, we examined whether PPII acts through this mechanism by analyzing the cleavage of key gasdermin members. PPII treatment induced dose-dependent cleavage of GSDME (figure 4F), whereas it did not affect gasdermin C (GSDMC) (figure 4G) or gasdermin D (GSDMD) (figure 4H). This specificity is consistent with the inhibitor results, in which mannose (which blocks GSDME-mediated pyroptosis), but not the GSDMD inhibitor LDC7559, reduced PPII-triggered cell death (figure 4A). Likewise, PPII-induced GSDME cleavage was also observed in MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 cells (online supplemental figure S6D,E). Consistent with GSDME activation, PPII triggered cleavage of its upstream activator, caspase-3 (figure 4I), indicating activation of the caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptotic pathway. Notably, quantitative PCR analysis revealed that PPII did not upregulate GSDME at the transcriptional level (online supplemental figure S6F,G), suggesting that GSDME activation occurs post-translationally.

We next asked whether pyroptosis is involved in PPII-induced immunogenic characteristics. Both pan-caspase inhibitor ZVF and pyroptosis inhibitor mannose reversed the ICD triggered by PPII, as evidenced by reduced secretion of HMGB1 and ATP (figure 4J,K and online supplemental figure S6H,I). Further supporting this, in a co-culture system, ZVF and mannose markedly reduced the upregulation of CD80, CD86, and MHC-I-SIINFEKL on BMDCs (online supplemental figure S6K–M), as well as the elevation of CD69 on B3Z cells induced by PPII-treated tumor cells (online supplemental figure S6N). To definitively establish GSDME as the executioner molecule of PPII-induced ICD, we generated GSDME-knockout (GSDME-KO) B16 cells using CRISPR/Cas9. Strikingly, on GSDME-KO, the PPII-induced release of ICD markers, including HMGB1 release, ATP secretion and CRT exposure, was nearly completely abrogated (figure 4L,M and online supplemental figure S6J). Furthermore, co-culturing these PPII-treated GSDME-KO cells with BMDCs resulted in a profound failure to induce DC maturation and T-cell activation (figure 4N–Q). These data confirm that GSDME is the indispensable executor of PPII-induced immunogenic pyroptosis. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PPII induces tumor ICD via caspase-3-dependent cleavage of GSDME, leading to pyroptosis.

GRP75 is a direct target of PPII in the induction of ICD and pyroptosis

To identify the cellular target through which PPII induces pyroptosis, we employed LiP-MS by comparing proteolytic peptide profiles between full and limited proteolytic digestion from lysates treated with or without PPII. The full list of identified proteins is provided in online supplemental table S7. This analysis identified nine high-confidence candidate proteins based on the fold change in structurally differential peptides (figure 5A and online supplemental figure S7A). Notably, among these leading candidates, only knockdown of GRP75 by specific shRNA, but not of other identified proteins, significantly decreased PPII-induced HMGB1-Gluc activity (figure 5B), suggesting GRP75 as a functional target of PPII. Accordingly, knockdown of GRP75 markedly attenuated PPII-induced LDH release (figure 5C and online supplemental figure S7B) and pyroptotic morphology (figure 5D and online supplemental figure S7C). Western blot analysis further confirmed that GRP75 knockdown reduced PPII-induced cleavage of GSDME (figure 5E and online supplemental figure S7D). Collectively, these observations suggest that GRP75 is essential for PPII-induced pyroptosis and subsequent ICD.

The direct interaction between PPII and GRP75 was further confirmed by cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). In A375 cells, PPII treatment induced marked thermal destabilization of GRP75 (figure 5F), a finding that was consistently reproduced in B16 cells (online supplemental figure S7E). At 50°C, GRP75 stability decreased in a PPII concentration-dependent manner (figure 5G and online supplemental figure S7F). In contrast, no significant change in GRP75 stability was observed at 37°C (online supplemental figure S7G,H), indicating that PPII specifically reduces the thermal stability of GRP75 without affecting its basal stability under physiological conditions. Consistent with these results, under a fixed enzyme-lysate ratio, a progressive reduction in GRP75 stability was detected with increasing concentrations of PPII (figure 5H and online supplemental figure S7I). SPR assay further revealed that PPII bound to GRP75 with an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 1.06 µM (figure 5I), confirming their high-affinity binding. To elucidate the structural basis of this interaction, we performed molecular docking using the GRP75 crystal structure (PDB: 6P2U). The docking model revealed that PPII engages in favorable hydrogen bond interactions with key GRP75 residues, including Glu318, Ser321, and Ser322 (figure 5J). Binding mode analysis further suggests that Ser320 in GRP75 may also play a potential role for the interaction. We thus individually mutated these residues to alanine (Glu318A, Ser320A, Ser321A, Ser322A) to identify the most critical residues for the PPII-GRP75 interaction. Alanine substitution mutagenesis indicated that Ser320A and Ser322A mutants, but not wild-type proteins, failed to restore PPII‐triggered HMGB1‐Gluc activity when it was reintroduced into GRP75-knockdown cells (figure 5K). These findings suggest that PPII binds to GRP75 at two key residues, Ser320 and Ser322. Clinically, high GRP75 expression correlates with poorer patient survival in several cancer types, including breast cancer, melanoma, and lung adenocarcinoma (online supplemental figure S7J). Collectively, these findings consistently demonstrate that GRP75 is a specific target for the action of PPII.

PPII triggers ER stress and mitochondrial calcium overload

GRP75 serves as a central hub at ER-mitochondria contact sites, where it bridges the IP3R on the ER with the VDAC1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane (figure 6A).30 This physical coupling enables efficient Ca²+ transfer from the ER lumen to the mitochondria, a process critical for energizing mitochondrial function.31 Given the central role of GRP75 in this interorganellar communication, we investigated whether PPII affects the integrity of the IP3R-GRP75-VDAC1 complex. Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that PPII treatment enhanced the interaction of GRP75 with both IP3R and VDAC1 (figure 6B). Consistent with this, proximity ligation assay (PLA) demonstrated an increased number of VDAC1-IP3R complexes following PPII exposure (figure 6C), suggesting that PPII promotes or stabilizes complex assembly. To assess the functional consequences of this enhanced complex formation, we measured mitochondrial Ca²+ levels using the fluorescent indicator Rhod-2 AM. PPII treatment induced a marked increase in mitochondrial Ca²+, which was abolished in GRP75-knockdown A375 cells (figure 6D and online supplemental figure S8A). A consistent result was observed in the B16 cell line (online supplemental figure S8B,C), confirming the specificity of the action of PPII through GRP75. Collectively, these results indicate that PPII binds to GRP75 and abnormally promotes the assembly of the IP3R-VDAC1 complex, leading to sustained Ca²+ efflux from the ER into the mitochondria.

Given that luminal Ca²+ is a critical cofactor for chaperone function,15 we hypothesized that disrupting calcium homeostasis would impair protein folding and trigger ER stress. To test this, we assessed the activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR). Indeed, PPII treatment led to a marked upregulation of key ER stress markers, including phosphorylation of IRE1α, PERK, and its downstream effector eIF2α, confirming UPR activation (figure 6E and online supplemental figure S8D). Moreover, the ER stress inhibitor 4-phenylbutyric acid significantly attenuated PPII-induced surface exposure of CRT (figure 6F and online supplemental figure S8E), and PPII failed to enhance CRT externalization in GRP75-knockdown cells (figure 6G and online supplemental figure S8F). These results suggest that PPII triggers ER stress through GRP75, thereby promoting CRT translocation.

It is well documented that calcium overload dissipates mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and disrupts mitochondrial function.32 Using JC-1 and TMRM staining, we further confirmed that PPII treatment induced a dose-dependent loss of ΔΨm, which was abrogated in GRP75-knockdown cells (figure 6H,I and online supplemental figure S8G–I). The collapse of ΔΨm compromises the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane, leading to the release of cytochrome c from the intermembrane space into the cytosol.33 Consistent with this, PPII triggered cytosolic cytochrome c release and subsequent cleavage of caspase-9 and caspase-3, while this effect was absent in GRP75-knockdown cells (figure 6J). This finding aligns with our earlier observation that GRP75 knockdown attenuates PPII-induced GSDME cleavage (figure 5E and online supplemental figure S7D), supporting a model in which PPII-triggered mitochondrial calcium overload initiates mitochondrial dysfunction, Cyt c release, and ultimately caspase-3-dependent cleavage of GSDME to execute pyroptosis. Collectively, these data suggest that PPII disrupts ER-mitochondrial calcium coupling via GRP75, driving a cascade of ER stress, mitochondrial calcium overload, and resultant pyroptosis.

PPII potentiates the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy and promotes systemic antitumor immunity

Given the ability of PPII to induce pyroptosis and the subsequent ICD, we sought to evaluate its potential to enhance immunotherapy in vivo. We tested whether PPII could synergize with an anti-PD-1 antibody in a B16 tumor model (figure 7A), which is typically resistant to ICB.34 Mice were treated with PBS, PPII alone, anti-PD-1 alone, or the combination. As shown in figure 7B,C, the combination treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth with a 68.3% inhibition rate, surpassing PPII monotherapy (45.7%) and anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy (20.1%). The calculated fp for the combination therapy was 1.21, indicating a clear synergistic effect in solid tumor therapy. Tumor weight analysis corroborated these findings, with the combination treatment achieving the most significant tumor suppression (figure 7D and online supplemental figure S9A). Importantly, safety evaluations revealed no significant body weight loss (online supplemental figure S9B), and histological H&E examinations of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) (online supplemental figure S9C) showed no evidence of systemic toxicity in the combination cohort. Furthermore, the combination treatment also significantly prolonged the survival of B16 tumor-bearing mice (figure 7E). Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence analysis of tumor tissues revealed that the combination treatment of PPII and anti-PD-1 exhibited a significant increase in terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) and cleaved caspase-3 signals (online supplemental figure S9D), coupled with a marked reduction in Ki67 staining (online supplemental figure S9D). Cleaved GSDME was elevated in both PPII and combination groups (online supplemental figure S9D), confirming the induction of pyroptosis in vivo. Collectively, these results demonstrate that combining PPII with anti-PD-1 induces tumor cell pyroptosis and substantially inhibits tumor cell proliferation in vivo.

To elucidate the immune mechanisms underlying these effects, we characterized PPII’s impact on the TME. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the combination of PPII and anti-PD-1 significantly increased intratumoral infiltration of cDC1s and CD8+ T cells (figure 7F,G), and enhanced the expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC-I on cDC1s (online supplemental figure S9E), as well as the effector molecules IFN-γ and GzmB in CD8+ T cells (figure 7H,I). Given that MDSCs and Tregs are the major immunosuppressive populations within the TME, we further quantified the proportions of these populations. Notably, PPII and anti-PD-1 combination treatment markedly reduced the proportions of both Tregs and MDSCs within tumors (figure 7J,K). Furthermore, while neither monotherapy altered the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the combination treatment significantly converted the TAM population toward an M1-dominant phenotype, as evidenced by an increased M1/M2 ratio (figure 7L). These findings indicate that PPII sensitizes tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy by remodeling the immunosuppressive TME. Furthermore, consistent with GRP75’s central role, neither PPII monotherapy nor its combination with anti-PD-1 suppressed the growth of B16 tumors expressing GRP75-targeting shRNA (figure 7M), confirming that the antitumor effect of PPII is GRP75-dependent. Collectively, these findings position PPII as a promising candidate for combination regimens with anti-PD-1 antibodies to improve therapeutic outcomes.

Finally, we evaluated whether PPII-induced ICD could generate a systemic antitumor response. Using a bilateral 4T1 tumor model, PPII was administered into the primary tumor, while the contralateral tumor remained untreated (figure 7N). Intratumoral PPII injection alone significantly inhibited the growth of both the injected primary tumor by ~50.1% and the untreated distant tumor by ~57.9% (figure 7O,P and online supplemental figure S10A–D). To substantiate the generation of systemic antitumor immunity, we analyzed TILs within the distant, untreated tumors. Flow cytometry revealed a significant increase in the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and cDC1s, accompanied by a marked decrease in the frequencies of immunosuppressive Tregs and MDSCs in the distant TME (online supplemental figure S10E). More importantly, the combination of PPII and anti-PD-1 achieved a more pronounced tumor suppression, as assessed in tumor growth and tumor size (figure 7O,P and online supplemental figure S10A–D). This abscopal effect was substantially enhanced when PPII was combined with systemic anti-PD-1 administration. While anti-PD-1 monotherapy suppressed distant tumor growth by ~40.7%, the combination with PPII achieved a superior tumor growth inhibition of ~73.5% (figure 7O,P). These results collectively suggest that PPII potentiates the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy and promotes robust systemic antitumor immunity, supporting its combination with anti-PD-1 antibodies to improve the outcomes of cancer immunotherapy.

Discussion

The immunosuppressive TME often hinders endogenous immune responses and proves insufficient to control tumor progression, highlighting the need for exogenous interventions to elicit robust antitumor immunity. ICD, a form of cell death that activates adaptive immunity, has emerged as a promising strategy to reshape the TME and improve immunotherapy outcomes. In this study, using an ICD-specific reporter screening, we identified PPII as a potent ICD inducer capable of stimulating adaptive antitumor immunity. We demonstrated that PPII significantly suppresses tumor growth and enhances the sensitivity of tumors to PD-1 blockade therapy by inducing ICD, which releases tumor-associated antigens and danger signal molecules through direct targeting of GRP75 (figure 7Q). These findings suggest that PPII can convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, revealing a novel therapeutic strategy for patients resistant to current immunotherapy.

Previous studies reported that PPII induces protective autophagy and apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells,22 modalities generally considered as non-immunogenic. Here, we found that PPII exerts its antitumor effect primarily through ICD. PPII treatment triggered robust release of DAMPs (including HMGB1, ATP, and CRT) from tumor cells, thereby enhancing tumor immunogenicity and reprogramming the TME through recruitment and activation of DCs and T cells. Importantly, we found that PPII induces pyroptosis in tumor cells. These findings are consistent with recent evidence that pyroptosis potently stimulates antitumor immunity and sensitizes tumors to ICB therapy.35 Furthermore, we confirmed that PPII-triggered pyroptosis is mediated specifically by GSDME, rather than GSDMC or GSDMD. Inhibition of pyroptosis reversed PPII-mediated ICD and the subsequent activation of DCs and T cells, confirming the functional link between these processes. Our results establish that GSDME-dependent pyroptosis and ICD are critical for the antitumor activity of PPII.

A central finding of this study is that PPII targets GRP75 to modulate ER-mitochondrial contact sites, thereby affecting the biological functions of MAMs and triggering ER stress along with mitochondrial calcium overload. In recent years, mitochondrial calcium overload has gained considerable attention in tumor immunotherapy. Inducing calcium overload in tumor cells has emerged as an effective strategy to enhance the antitumor immune response.36 In particular, calcium signaling represents a promising target for combination immunotherapy aimed at modulating the TME.37 38 As specific membrane contact sites between ER and mitochondria, MAMs are critical not only for maintaining calcium homeostasis but also for determining cell fate. Recent studies have highlighted that ER-mitochondria contacts serve as key platforms for phospholipid peroxidation, thereby driving ferroptosis.39 This suggests that enhanced ER-mitochondrial tethering could favor ferroptotic death. Although PPII significantly strengthened the interaction between VDAC1 and IP3R, typically indicative of closer organelle juxtaposition and enhanced MAMs activity, we did not observe hallmarks of ferroptosis. Indeed, ferroptosis inhibitor failed to rescue PPII-induced cell death (figure 4A), clearly excluding this pathway. Notably, we found that PPII induces the upregulation of GPX4 (online supplemental figure S8J), a key suppressor of ferroptosis. Furthermore, recent study indicates that cytoplasmic cytochrome c can inhibit ferroptosis by binding to inositol polyphosphate-4-phosphatase type I A.40 Together, these factors likely explain why enhanced MAMs assembly on PPII treatment does not culminate in ferroptosis, highlighting the complexity of cell-fate decisions at the ER-mitochondria interface. This complexity is further underscored by recent findings: RCN1, a calcium-binding protein, dynamically modulates the IP3R-GRP75 complex in a calcium-dependent manner,41 and MAMs disassembly destabilizes this complex to drive chemoresistance.42

At the molecular level, PPII specifically binds to GRP75, a key molecular chaperone localized at MAMs that mediates the assembly of the IP3R-VDAC1 complex and maintains the structural and functional integrity of MAMs. Interestingly, CETSA results revealed that PPII reduces the thermal stability of GRP75. This finding contrasts with the conventional expectation that ligand binding generally stabilizes protein structure, yet it aligns with several reports indicating that certain ligands can induce protein destabilization.43 44 We speculate that PPII may force GRP75 to adopt a thermodynamically unstable, non-native conformation. Notably, at physiological temperature (37°C), PPII did not alter GRP75 stability, indicating that the PPII-induced destabilization of GRP75 occurs specifically under thermal stress conditions. While this study establishes that PPII binds to GRP75, the precise conformational and functional changes in GRP75 and subsequently modulating the assembly of the IP3R-VDAC1 complex remain to be fully elucidated. Unraveling this mechanism represents an important direction for future research. In summary, as a core MAMs component amenable to small-molecule targeting, GRP75 holds translational promise for reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Of note, its family member GRP78 has been shown to promote metabolic reprogramming and therapeutic resistance in pancreatic cancer via nuclear translocation.45 Therapeutic modulation of GRP75 to influence MAMs function may thus offer a novel therapeutic strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

Combination strategies designed to enhance the efficacy of ICB represent a promising frontier in cancer immunotherapy. Current clinical efforts are exploring the integration of ICD inducers with ICB monotherapy.46 47 Our results demonstrate that combining PPII with anti-PD-1 antibodies markedly remodels the tumor immune microenvironment. This combination promotes the maturation of CLEC9A+ cDC1, shifts macrophage polarization from an M2 to an M1 phenotype, ultimately increasing the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Such remodeling, particularly the marked infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, is clinically associated with improved treatment response and prolonged patient survival.48 Notably, combination therapy effectively reversed the immunosuppressive TME by reducing intratumoral MDSCs and Tregs populations, both of which are known to promote tumor progression through cell-contact inhibition and metabolic interference.49 50 This synergistic action functions by simultaneously “stepping on gas” via ICD to initiate immune priming and “releasing the brake” via ICB to sustain effector function, thereby potentiating the entire cancer-immunity cycle. This mechanistic rationale provides a compelling combinatorial strategy for tumors resistant to conventional immunotherapy.

In summary, our findings establish that PPII is a potent ICD inducer through its ability to trigger GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. This process activates cDC1s and CD8+ T cells within TME, resulting in robust antitumor immunity. Mechanistically, PPII specifically binds to GRP75, inducing ER stress and mitochondrial calcium overload, which in turn drives pyroptosis and ICD. This cascade remodels the TME and sensitizes tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy. Our work identifies GRP75 as a novel therapeutic target for cancer immunotherapy and highlights PPII-driven immune reprogramming as a translatable strategy to enhance the efficacy of existing ICB therapies.
