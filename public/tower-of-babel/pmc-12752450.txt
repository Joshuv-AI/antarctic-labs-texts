A novel biomimetic nanoplatform amplifies ferroptosis for specific cGAS-STING pathway activation to enhance hepatocellular carcinoma chemo-immunotherapy
Authors: Hulinyue Peng, AQian Chang, Huizhong Zhang, Xiaohan Xu, Wenqi Wang, Ke Zhang, Jingwen Yang, Sha Xie, Xiaoyu Peng, Xingbin Yin, Changhai Qu, Jing Fu, Jian Ni, Xiaoxv Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow immunogenicity remains a critical barrier to effective immunotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway is a promising strategy for HCC treatment; however, achieving specific activation of this pathway remains a major challenge. Herein, we report a novel biomimetic nanoplatform (PZ@M-T) that triggers ferroptosis to specifically activate the cGAS-STING pathway. This study utilized a hollow ZIF-8 metal-organic framework (MOF) coated with mesenchymal stem cell membrane (MSCm) as a biomimetic carrier of polyphyllin II (PPⅡ) functionalized with triphenylphosphine (TPP) via copper-free click chemistry for mitochondrial targeting. Acid-responsive PPⅡ release in the tumor microenvironment (TME) induced robust ferroptosis in HCC cells, eliciting mitochondrial stress to cause endogenous mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release, which specifically initiated the cGAS-STING pathway with the cooperation of Zn2+. This activation drove dendritic cell (DC) maturation, repolarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from the M2 to M1 phenotype, enhanced cytotoxic T cell infiltration, and suppressed regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby collectively driving a robust anti-tumor immune response that significantly inhibited HCC progression. This innovative nanotherapeutic platform provides new promising strategy for tumor immunotherapy via specific activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and offers new insights into advanced chemo-immunotherapeutic approaches.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03870-5.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, cGAS-STING pathway, Polyphyllin Ⅱ, Ferroptosis, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a persistent global health challenge, characterized by aggressive tumor progression, high recurrence rates, and limited therapeutic options beyond early surgical resection [1, 2]. Immunotherapy has revolutionized HCC treatment, with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) as a representative approach reinvigorating anti-tumor T cell responses [3–5]. However, due to intrinsic tumor immune evasion and immunosuppressive microenvironments, the response rate to ICIs remains only 15% to 30%, far from an ideal level [1, 6, 7].

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway has emerged as a key target to overcome this limitation, as it bridges innate and adaptive immunity [8, 9]. By sensing abnormal double-stranded DNA, cGAS activates STING to induce type I interferons (IFN-I) and proinflammatory cytokines, this not only activates antigen-presenting cells for T cell priming but also reshapes the TME to promote T cell infiltration [10, 11]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop therapeutic strategies that can specifically activate the cGAS-STING pathway in order to re-initiate immune responses at tumor sites and treat HCC.

Polyphyllin Ⅱ (PPⅡ), a spirostane-type triterpenoid compound extracted from Paridis Rhizoma, exerts anti-HCC effects through multiple mechanisms including apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, invasion and metastasis inhibition and ferroptosis [12–15]. Notably, PPII-induced ferroptosis leads to a massive release of endogenous mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which acts like a “biological detonator”, precisely triggering a “chain reaction” in the cGAS-STING signaling network and transforming localized cell death events into a signal for systemic immune activation, thereby activating the immune response. The dual synergistic mode of “chemotherapy-induced killing + immune activation” endows PPⅡ with great potential for HCC treatment. However, due to inherent limitations in PPⅡ use, such as low bioavailability, hepatotoxicity, and poor targeting, there is an urgent need to find a suitable drug delivery method [16].

ZIF-8 Metal-organic framework (MOF) is composed of Zn2+ and 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM), and it possesses unique properties, such as pH-sensitive degradation and good biocompatibility [17–19]. Considering that a hollow ZIF-8 has a larger specific surface area and pore size than a compared to solid ZIF-8, tannic acid (TA) is used as an etchant to convert the hydrophobic surface of ZIF-8 into a hydrophilic surface, promoting the migration of H+ ions into the interior of ZIF-8 and disrupting the internal Zn-N bonds to form a hollow structure, thereby achieving efficient loading of PPⅡ [20].

Although nanomaterials exhibit enormous potential for drug delivery, they are confronted with the risk of being recognized and eliminated by the body’s immune system, which can render their effective delivery to tumor sites rather challenging [21, 22]. For example, Xie et al. utilized mesenchymal stem cell membranes (MSCm) to coat hollow manganese dioxide nanoparticles loaded with paclitaxel, demonstrating promising tumor targeting and a lower drug clearance rate [23]. Inspired by this study, we utilized the unique tumor-homing property and excellent immunocompatibility of MSCm to perform biomimetic camouflage of nanomaterials, aiming to achieve prolonged in vivo retention and substantial accumulation at tumor sites.

Additionally, the excellent mitochondrial targeting function of triphenylphosphine (TPP) demonstrated in previous studies caught our attention [24–27]. After nanoparticles reach the tumor site, TPP can further target drugs to the mitochondria, enhancing the ferroptosis-inducing effect and thereby exacerbating mitochondrial damage, stimulating mtDNA release, and promoting tumor immunogenicity. Copper-free click chemistry provides a feasible strategy for modifying TPP. This method does not require the introduction of exogenous catalysts, avoiding the risk of metal residues, and enables rapid and precise molecular coupling under physiological conditions, significantly enhancing the safety and controllability of nanoparticle modification [28–32].

Herein, inspired by the significant potential of the cGAS-STING pathway to enhance immunotherapy of HCC, a novel biomimetic nanoplatform (PZ@M-T) was constructed to specifically activate and potentiate STING-mediated immunotherapy (Fig 1). Firstly, TA was employed to etch the ZIF-8 MOFs, generating hollow ZIF-8 frameworks (TA-ZIF-8) that were subsequently loaded with the chemotherapeutic drug PPⅡ, yielding PPⅡ/ZIF-8 (PZ) with high drug-loading efficiency. Secondly, the surface of PZ was coated with MSCm to create PZ@M, which facilitated enhanced tumor accumulation. Finally, TPP was conjugated to PZ@M through copper-free click chemistry, culminating in the formation of PZ@M-T. Upon intravenous administration, PZ@M-T exploited the combined targeting capabilities of MSCm and TPP to precisely target tumor mitochondria. Once PZ@M-T was internalized, the released PPⅡ can trigger potent ferroptosis in tumor cells, causing mitochondrial damage, disrupting cellular antioxidant homeostasis, and activating the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway initiated a dual-pronged immunomodulatory cascade, promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation and subsequent T-cell activation, while also polarizing tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from the pro-tumoral M2 phenotype to the anti-tumoral M1 phenotype. These processes collectively remodeled the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and potentiated systemic antitumor immunity. As a novel biomimetic nanoplatform integrating chemotherapy and immunotherapy, PZ@M-T provides valuable insights for the treatment of HCC.

Synthesis of PPⅡ/TA-ZIF-8 (PZ), PZ@M, and PZ@M-T

Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (1 mmol) and 2-MIM (32 mmol) were separately dissolved in 10 mL of methanol, then mixed and stirred at 800 rpm for 2 h. ZIF-8 nanoparticles were obtained by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min), washing, and vacuum drying (60 °C, 24 h).

The synthesis of TA-ZIF-8 has been described in a previous study [20]. In summary, ZIF-8 (0.1 g) was dispersed in 2 mL of deionized water. Then, TA (0.25 g) was dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water and introduced into the ZIF-8 solution. The mixture was then stirred for 15 min and aged at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, TA-ZIF-8 nanoparticles were obtained by centrifugation, washing, and vacuum drying.

PZ was synthesized via the solvent precipitation method. First, PPⅡ (100 mg) was dissolved in 20 mL of methanol, and then TA-ZIF-8 (25 mg) was dispersed in the above solution. The mixture was stirred at 800 rpm for 72 h, centrifuged for 30 min, and washed with methanol. The supernatant was collected to calculate the loading content (LC) of PPⅡ using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with the following equation: LC = (total PPⅡ - unbound PPⅡ)/total PZ, and the precipitate was then dried under vacuum to obtain PZ nanoparticles.

MSCs were incubated with 30 µg/mL Ac4ManNAz (N3) for 72 h and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to collect N3-labeled MSCs. MSCm were collected using a membrane and cytosol protein extraction kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) and then washed three times with PBS [30]. For the synthesis of PZ@M, PZ (1 mg/mL) was mixed with MSCm (2 mg/mL) in equal volumes, and the mixture was sonicated at 200 W for 4 min (sonication for 2 s, intermittence for 2 s) on ice and then centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 4 °C) for 30 min. Finally, PZ@M was washed twice with deionized water and then freeze-dried [33, 34].

To synthesize PZ@M-T, PZ@M (0.1 mg/mL) was mixed with DBCO-PEG-TPP (0.1 mM), and the mixture was stirred at 1200 rpm for 2 h [30]. The PZ@M-T nanoplatform was obtained by centrifugation, washing with deionized water and freeze-drying.

Characterization of PZ@M-T

The surface morphology and elemental distribution of PZ@M-T were observed by transmission electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS, Tecnai G2 F30, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The hydrodynamic particle size, and Zeta potential of PZ@M-T were measured by a laser particle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano S90, Malvern, UK). A Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher, USA) was then used to record the FT-IR spectra of PZ@M-T. The surface chemical composition and ionic valences were obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 advance, Bruker, Germany) was used to record the XRD spectra of PZ@M-T. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of TA-ZIF-8 and PZ@M-T was performed using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG-DSC 3+, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Furthermore, HPLC was utilized to investigate the release profile of PPⅡ. The retention of MSCm protein and other specific proteins (CD44, CD90 and CD105) was determined by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).

In vitro stability assay

First, PZ@M-T (1 mg) was dispersed in 10 mL of 10% FBS (v/v) solution and incubated at 37 ℃ in a constant temperature oscillator for 7 days. The size and Zeta potential of PZ@M-T were assessed daily.

In vitro release

The release of PPⅡ from PZ@M-T was detected using the dialysis method. PZ@M-T was suspended in PBS (pH 5.5 or 7.4, containing 1% Tween-80). Then, 200 µL of the solution was aspirated at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h and immediately replenished. The content of PPⅡ was determined and analyzed using HPLC.

CCK assay

HepG2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates overnight and treated with PPⅡ, PZ, PZ@M, and PZ@M-T for 24 h. After adding CCK-8 and incubating for 2 h, cell viability was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Investigation of the forms of cell death caused by PZ@M-T

HepG2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates overnight and pretreated with Z-VAD-FMK (20 µM), Necrostatin-1 (40 µM), Disulfiram (1 µM), Ferrostatin-1 (1 µM), and Liproxstatin-1 (1 µM) for 4 h, followed by treatment with PZ@M-T for 24 h. The cell viability was detected using the CCK-8 assay.

Cellular uptake and uptake pathway

Cou-6 was used as a fluorescent probe to label nanoparticles. PBS, Cou-6, Cou-6/PZ, and Cou-6/PZ@M-T (equal to Cou-6 1 µg/mL) were incubated with HepG2 cells and THLE-2 cells, respectively. Nanoparticle uptake was observed and photographed using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, TCS SP8, Leica, Germany), and then measured using flow cytometer (FACSCantoTM Ⅱ, BD Bioscience, USA).

To clarify the internalization pathway of PZ@M-T, HepG2 cells were pre-treated with five inhibitors targeting different endocytic pathways for 30 min, followed by incubation with PZ@M-T for 1 h. The inhibitors were: Amiloride hydrochloride hydrate (macropinocytosis inhibitor, 100 µM), Cytochalasin D (actin-dependent phagocytosis inhibitor, 2.5 µg/mL), Methyl-β-cyclodextrin (caveolin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor, 10 mM), Filipin complex (caveolin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor, 10 µg/mL), and Chlorpromazine hydrochloride (clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor, 10 µg/mL).

Lysosomal escape assays

After incubation with Cou/PZ@M-T for 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, and 2 h, HepG2 cells were stained with LysoTracker Deep Red (100 nM) for 30 min, and stained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min. The fluorescence images were analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, TCS SP8, Leica, Germany).

Evaluation of mitochondrial targeting capability

After incubation with the fluorescent probe for 4 h, HepG2 cells were stained with Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos (200 nM) for 30 min, and stained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min. The fluorescence images were analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, TCS SP8, Leica, Germany).

Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation (LPO) detection

HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates overnight. PBS, TA-ZIF-8, PPⅡ, PZ, PZ@M, and PZ@M-T (equivalent to 5 µg/mL PPⅡ) were added and incubated for 24 h. Then 20 µM of RhoNox-1 was added and incubated at 37 ℃ for 30 min. The fluorescence of Fe2+ was observed by fluorescence inverted microscope (IX71, Olympus Corporation, Japan) and the level of Fe2+ was detected by flow cytometer (FACSCantoTM Ⅱ, BD Bioscience, USA).

For the detection of LPO, 2 µM of BODIPY 581/591 C11 was added and incubated at 37 ℃ for 30 min. The fluorescence and quantitative results were detected by fluorescence inverted microscope and flow cytometry, respectively.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation

HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates overnight and treated with PBS, TA-ZIF-8, PPⅡ, PZ, PZ@M, and PZ@M-T for 24 h. Then 10 µM of DCFH-DA was incubated with cells at 37 ℃ for 20 min. The fluorescence and quantitative results of ROS were detected by fluorescence inverted microscope and flow cytometry, respectively.

Glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) detection

After 24 h treatment, 10 mM of GSH was mixed with 10 µL of sample and incubated at 25 ℃ for 30 min. The fluorescence and quantitative levels for GSH were determined by fluorescence inverted microscopy and a microplate reader at 412 nm.

According to the instructions of the kit, 200 µL of a TBA working solution was mixed with 100 µL of sample and incubated at 90℃ for 30 min. The level of MDA was then determined by a microplate reader at 532 nm.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) detection

After 24 h treatment, the cells were incubated with 5 µL of a JC-1 working solution at 37℃ for 20 min. The fluorescence and quantitative results were detected by fluorescence inverted microscopy and flow cytometry, respectively.

Mitochondrial structural observations

After 24 h treatment, the cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 12 h, fixed with 1% osmic acid for 2 h, dehydrated with ethanol, polymerized with epoxy resin to make sections, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and then the mitochondrial structure was observed with a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400, Japan).

MtDNA detection

After 24 h of drug intervention, the cells were divided into two equal parts. The cytosolic fraction was isolated from one part using a Mitochondria/Cytosol Fractionation Kit (ab65320, Abcam) to analyze cytosolic components. Total DNA was extracted from whole-cell and cytosolic fractions using the QIAamp DNA kit (56304, QIAGEN, Germany). The mtDNA was represented by the gene NADH dehydrogenase 1 (ND1) of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, and the nuclear DNA (nDNA) was represented by 18S ribosomal RNA (18S rRNA). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) reactions were performed using the TB Green Premix EX Taq™ Ⅱ (RR820A, Takara, Japan). The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and the sequences were as follows: ND1 (forward primer 5’-ATGGCCAACCTCCTACTCCT-3’, reverse primer 5’-GCGGTGATGTAGAGGGTGAT-3’); 18 S rRNA (forward primer 5’-CATTCGAACGTCTGCCCTATC-3’, reverse primer 5’-CCTGCTGCCTTCCTTGGA-3’). The purity of the cytosolic fraction was verified by quantifying 18 S rRNA, and the cytosolic mtDNA level was calculated using the formula: Cytosolic mtDNA/(Whole-cell mtDNA/Whole-cell nDNA).

Western blot assays

After 24 h of drug intervention, proteins in cells were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Then, membranes were incubated with anti-GPX4, anti-SLC7A11, anti-ACSL4, anti-TFR1, anti-cGAS, anti-p-STING, anti-STING, anti-p-TBK1, anti-TBK1, anti-p-IRF3, anti-IRF3, and anti-IFN-β antibodies overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-secondary antibody for 2 h. Finally, the blots were visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) method.

IFN-γ, IFN-β, IL-6 and TNF-α enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After 24 h of drug intervention, the levels of IFN-γ, IFN-β, IL-6, and TNF-α in cell supernatant were detected using ELISA kits.

Animal and tumor-bearing mouse model

Healthy male C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks, 20 g) were purchased from Sipeifu Biological Technology Co. Ltd (Beijing, China) and subcutaneously injected with Hepa1-6 cells (5 × 106 cells/mL, 100 µL). All procedures for the experiment were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval number: 20250306-1138).

In vivo targeting capability

Cou-6-labeled nanoparticles were injected into tumor-bearing mice via the tail vein. Fluorescence imaging was measured using BioLight GelView 6000 M system at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h. After 24 h, the major organs and tumors were excised for biodistribution study.

In vivo antitumor efficacy

C57BL/6J mice with a tumor of approximately 50 mm3 were randomly divided into eight groups: (1) normal saline, (2) TA-ZIF-8, (3) anti-PD-L1, (4) PPⅡ, (5) PZ, (6) PZ@M, (7) PZ@M-T, (8) PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 (equivalent to 5 mg/kg PPⅡ). The nanoparticles were administered via tail vein injection every other day, followed by an intraperitoneal injection of 5 mg/kg of anti-PD-L1 on the next day. The weights and tumor volumes of tumor-bearing mice were measured daily. After five treatments, blood samples were collected for biochemical assays, and the tumors and major organs were excised for further histopathological examination.

Immunological effects

To investigate the effect of immunogenic cell death (ICD), the slices of tumors were stained with CRT, HMGB1. To investigate the mechanism of combined therapy, tumor slices were stained with CD274 to detect the expression of PD-L1; stained with T-cell Immunoglobulin and Mucin Domain-containing Molecule 3 (Tim-3) to assess the exhaustion level of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs); stained with CD8 and CD103 to identify tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells and tissue-resident memory CD8⁺CD103⁺ T cells; and stained with CD8 and Granzyme B to identify cytotoxic T lymphocytes cells.

The levels of IFN-γ, IFN-β, IL-6 and TNF-α in tumors were detected by ELISA. For flow cytometry analysis, tumor tissues were digested to single cell suspension using a syringe. For the DC maturation ability, single cell suspensions of tumors were blocked on ice for 30 min and further incubated with PE anti-CD11c, FITC anti-CD80, and APC anti-CD86 antibodies. For the macrophages detection, cell suspensions were incubated with PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-CD45, FITC anti-F4/80, PE/Cy7 anti-CD206 and PE anti-CD86 antibodies. For the CD4+ T and CD8+ T cell activation study, cell suspensions were incubated with PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-CD45, FITC anti-CD3, PE anti-CD4 and PE/Cy7 anti-CD8a antibodies. For regulatory T cells (Tregs) analysis, cell suspensions were incubated with PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-CD45, FITC anti-CD3, PE anti-CD4, PE/Cy7 anti-CD25 and Alexa Fluor® 647 anti-Foxp3 antibodies.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as means ± SD. One-way ANOVA were used to analyze statistical significance, which was carried out in GraphPad Prism 10.0.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of the PZ@M-T nanoplatform

In order to efficiently load PPⅡ (Fig. S1) onto Zn-based nanomaterials, ZIF-8 MOFs were synthesized, and then the weak acidity of TA was utilized to transform the hydrophobic surface of ZIF-8 into a hydrophilic one, promoting the transfer of H+ ions into the interior of ZIF-8 and disrupting the Zn-N bonds, thereby synthesizing TA-ZIF-8 with a hollow structure. which provides more space for PPⅡ loading, and thus PZ was synthesized [35]. An ultrasonic disruptor was then used to coat N3-labeled MSCm onto the surface of PZ to synthesize PZ@M. Finally, TPP with its mitochondrial-targeting function was attached to the surface of the nanoplatform using copper-free click chemistry to obtain the PZ@M-T (Fig. 2A).

(H) FT-IR spectra of PZ, TPP and PZ@M-T. (I) XPS spectra of PZ@M-T. (J) EDS mapping of PZ@M-T. (K) Loading contents of PPⅡ in PZ, PZ@M, and PZ@M-T (n = 3). (L) Stability evaluation of PZ@M-T within 7 days as assessed by particle size and Zeta potential (n = 3). (M) Release profiles of PPⅡ under different conditions (n = 3)

TEM images showed that the ZIF-8 were nanometer-sized dodecahedral particles, and that TA-ZIF-8 was transformed into a hollow structure (Fig. 2B-C). The final PZ@M-T nanoplatforms were dodecahedral structures with apparent membrane coating (Fig. 2D). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the mean hydrodynamic diameters of ZIF-8 and TA-ZIF-8 were 203.43 ± 1.59 nm and 213.97 ± 2.44 nm, respectively. Interestingly, the diameters of PZ@M-T (222.8 ± 2.07 nm) were increased only slightly after the subsequent preparation process (Fig. 2E, Fig. S2). We found that the Zeta potential of TA-ZIF-8 (−20.73 ± 0.42 mV) was significantly changed from ZIF-8 (13.63 ± 0.59 mV), due to the successful coordination of TA with ZIF-8, which caused TA-ZIF-8 to shift from a positive to negative Zeta potential (Fig. 2F). Due to the electrostatic adsorption of PPⅡ with ZIF-8, encapsulation of MSCm, and the coordination of TPP, the Zeta potential of PZ@M-T shifted to −7.54 ± 0.17 mV.

To investigate the N3 incubation concentration, we conducted the CCK-8 assay and determined that the concentration of N3 was 30 µg/mL (Fig. S3). To confirm the successful labeling of N3 on the MSCm, we used DBCO-FITC, which can undergo copper-free click chemistry with N3, to observe the fluorescence. The results showed that the green fluorescence was significantly enhanced in the N3 incubation group, indicating the successful labeling of N3 (Fig. S4). SDS-PAGE was then used to evaluate the coating effect of MSCm. The results showed that the protein bands of PZ@M-T were consistent with as those of the MSCm (Fig. 2G). On the other hand, PZ@M and PZ@M-T also expressed key markers (CD44, CD90, and CD105) of the MSC membrane surface, indicating the successful retention of MSCm-derived proteins on the surface of the nanocarriers (Fig. S5).

FT-IR spectra were used to identify the synthesis of PZ@M-T (Fig. 2H, Fig. S6-7). The peaks at 760 and 420 cm-1 were ascribed to the in-plane or out-of-plane bending of the imidazole ring and the stretching vibration of Zn-N in ZIF-8, respectively. After TA coating, the characteristic C = O peak and characteristic C-O peak were detected at 1705 and 1065 cm− 1 in the spectrum of TA-ZIF-8, and the same peaks were also visible in the spectrum of PZ and PZ@M-T. Additionally, the spectrum of PZ retained absorption peaks at 980 cm⁻¹ and 900 cm⁻¹, which are attributed to the fact that the aglycones of steroidal saponins in PPⅡ have a spiro ketal structure. Absorption peaks at 1464 cm⁻¹, 954 cm⁻¹ and 688 cm⁻¹ were observed in both the spectra of TPP and PZ@M-T. Among them, the absorption peak at 1464 cm⁻¹ may be related to the symmetric C-H stretching, while the peak at 688 cm⁻¹ is mainly attributed to the vibrational stretching of P = O [26, 36, 37]. In addition, a major peak was observed at 1706 cm⁻¹ in the TPP spectrum, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching vibration of C = O. Since TA also contains this functional group, this peak was also detected in the PZ@M-T spectrum. An obvious absorption peak corresponding to the P-C stretching vibration appeared at 1100 cm⁻¹ in the TPP spectrum; however, this peak was not observed in the PZ@M-T spectrum. It is speculated that this phenomenon may be due to the interference of the strong absorption peak of the C-O bond in TA-ZIF-8. Moreover, the absorption peak of PZ@M-T at 1065 cm⁻¹ was also significantly broadened. XPS and TEM-EDS were also utilized for the investigations of chemical composition and elemental mapping (Fig. 2I-J), and the spectra of PZ@M-T revealed the existence of C, O, Zn, N and P, which is consistent with the theoretical elemental composition of PZ@M-T.

The XRD patterns were used to analyze the crystallographic structures of ZIF-8, TA-ZIF-8, and PZ@M-T. As shown in Fig. S8-9, the XRD diffraction peaks of ZIF-8 and TA-ZIF-8 basically matched, indicating the successful synthesis of TA-ZIF-8. However, PZ@M-T exhibited a broadened peak in the range of 15–30°, however, the overall peak shape was similar to that of TA-ZIF-8, suggesting that PZ@M-T may mainly exist in an amorphous state, which was speculated to be related to the encapsulation of the cell membrane. The nitrogen adsorption experiment indicated that the specific surface area of TA-ZIF-8 was 124.85 m²/g, the pore volume was 0.14 cm³/g, and the pore size was 3.15 nm, making it a mesoporous material (Fig. S10-S11). TGA data showed that TA-ZIF-8 and PZ@M-T had a certain degree of thermal sensitivity, with a weight loss of less than 10% at temperatures below 200 °C, but with significant weight loss at temperatures above 200 °C (Fig. S12). In addition, the loading contents of PPⅡ in PZ, PZ@M, and PZ@M-T were 34.95%, 25.91% and 14.37%, respectively, demonstrating the effective loading of PPⅡ (Fig. 2K, Fig. S13).

Since PZ@M-T will be used in physiological environments, we used FBS-containing buffer to examine its stability at 37 °C. The results demonstrated that there were no significant changes in the particle size or Zeta potential of PZ@M-T within 7 days, indicating that the PZ@M-T is stable in FBS-containing buffer (Fig. 2L). Considering the acidic characteristics of TME, we also compared the release characteristics of PPⅡ under different pH conditions (Fig. 2M). The data revealed that free PPⅡ could be rapidly released at both pH 5.5 and pH 7.4, with a cumulative release rate exceeding 95% within 2 h. Compared to a pH of 7.4, PPⅡ in PZ@M-T exhibited a faster initial release rate at a pH of 5.5, with a cumulative release rate of 61.23% within 2 h, demonstrating significant pH responsiveness. Additionally, the cumulative release rate reached 70.99% after 24 h, higher than with a pH of 7.4 (66.39%). These findings provided evidence for the effective release of PPⅡ from PZ@M-T in the acidic TME.

The cell selectivity and targeting performance of PZ@M-T in vitro

To evaluate the selective targeting ability of PZ@M-T towards cancer cells, we used CLSM and flow cytometry to detect the intracellular distribution of nanoparticles. The results demonstrated that in HepG2 cells, the Cou-6/PZ@M and Cou-6/PZ@M-T groups exhibited the strongest fluorescence signals, clearly indicating that both MSCm coating and TPP modification facilitated the intracellular accumulation of nanoparticles (Fig. S14A). This observation was further supported by quantitative data, which consistently confirmed enhanced delivery capacity of the modified nanoparticles compared to unmodified counterparts (Fig. S14B-C). Moreover, in normal liver THLE-2 cells, the fluorescence intensities of the Cou-6/PZ@M and Cou-6/PZ@M-T groups were significantly lower than those of the unmodified Cou-6 and Cou-6/PZ groups (Fig. S15). This differential uptake pattern is likely attributed to the tumor-homing property of MSCm: the membrane coating enables nanoparticles to preferentially target and be internalized by tumor cells, while minimizing non-specific accumulation in normal liver cells.

The strong lipophilicity of the triphenyl structure is the basis of TPP’s targeting performance [38], to evaluate the ability of PZ@M-T to target mitochondria, we treated HepG2 cells with Cou-6-fluorescently labeled different nanoparticles and observed their uptake under a CLSM. As a result, the fluorescence intensity of the Cou-6-labeled nanoparticles in HepG2 cells treated with PZ@M-T was significantly higher than that in the PZ@M group and PZ group (Fig. 3A). Moreover, colocalization analysis of the intracellular Cou-6 fluorescence and mitochondrial fluorescence, as assessed by the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) revealed that the PCC of the PZ@M-T treatment group was 0.81, which was much higher than that of the other groups, further indicating that the modification of TPP facilitated the accumulation of PZ@M-T in the mitochondria of HepG2 cells.

After confirming the excellent mitochondrial targeting performance of PZ@M-T, we used five types of endocytic inhibitors to investigate the cellular internalization pathway of PZ@M-T. The data indicated that Filipin complex, Methyl-β-cyclodextrin, or Chlorpromazine hydrochloride significantly reduced the cellular uptake of PZ@M-T, while Amiloride and Cytochalasin D had no obvious inhibitory effect (Fig. S16). Specifically, the inhibition rates of Filipin complex and Methyl-β-cyclodextrin were approximately 10%, whereas Chlorpromazine hydrochloride exerted a much stronger inhibitory effect (over 50%). These findings confirmed that PZ@M-T was internalized by HepG2 cells primarily through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, with a minor contribution from caveolin-mediated endocytosis.

Furthermore, we also conducted a more in-depth study on whether PZ@M-T possesses the ability to escape from lysosomes. The results of CLSM demonstrated that with the extension of time, the green fluorescence of the PZ@M-T gradually increased, further indicating a gradual increase in the uptake of PZ@M-T (Fig. S17). At 15 min, the green fluorescence of the PZ@M-T highly overlapped with the red fluorescence of lysosomes. As the time extended to 30 min, a portion of PZ@M-T had escaped from the lysosomes. By 2 h, PZ@M-T had been widely distributed in the cytoplasm. These results confirmed that PZ@M-T possessed a distinct lysosomal escape property, which facilitated its subsequent translocation to mitochondria.

In general, the above results indicated that, benefiting from the effect of the MSCm coating, PZ@M-T possessed excellent tumor cell selectivity. Its cellular internalization mainly occurred through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and the modification of TPP endows it with distinct lysosomal escape ability and efficient mitochondrial targeting ability.

Ferroptosis promotion of PZ@M-T in vitro

To determine the efficacy of PZ@M-T, we conducted in vitro antitumor studies using a HepG2 cell model. First, CCK8 assay results confirmed the safety of the carrier material TA-ZIF-8 at concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 µg/mL (Fig. S18). The cytotoxic effects of PPⅡ, PZ, PZ@M and PZ@M-T on HepG2 cells were dose-dependent, with the IC50 value of PPⅡ being approximately 5 µg/mL. Notably, PZ@M-T exhibited the strongest cytotoxic effects at different concentrations, with the cytotoxicity of PZ being significantly higher than that of free PPⅡ, and the cytotoxicity of PZ@M was further enhanced compared to PZ. All these results indicated that the encapsulation of PPⅡ with TA-ZIF-8, the coating with MSCm, and the modification with TPP all contributed to enhancing the cytotoxicity of PPⅡ (Fig. S19). In order to investigate the specific mechanism of cell death caused by PZ@M-T nanoparticles, we conducted the CCK-8 cell viability assays involving Z-VAD-FMK (apoptosis inhibitor), Necrostatin-1 (necrosis inhibitor), Disulfiram (pyroptosis inhibitor), Ferrostatin-1 (ferroptosis inhibitor) and Liproxstatin-1 (ferroptosis inhibitor). And the results showed the ferroptosis inhibitors including Ferrostatin-1 and Liproxstatin-1 significantly increased the viability of HepG2 cells, and this effect was significantly better than that of the Z-VAD-FMK. In contrast, after treatment with the Necrostatin-1 and Disulfiram, there was no significant difference in cell viability compared with the control group. These results indicated that the ferroptosis plays a dominant role in the process of PZ@M-T nanoparticles induce HepG2 cell death (Fig. 3B).

Inducing ferroptosis in HepG2 cells is one of the key mechanisms of PPⅡ in treating HCC [15]. To investigate whether PZ@M-T can further enhance ferroptosis, we detected four ferroptosis-related phenotypes: ferrous iron accumulation, antioxidant imbalance, mitochondrial damage, and ferroptosis-related proteins expression [39, 40]. RhoNox-1 reacts with Fe2+ to produce a red fluorescent product, rendering it suitable for detecting Fe2+ in live cells [41]. After treatment with PZ@M-T, the intensity of red fluorescence within cells significantly increased, indicating excessive accumulation of Fe2+ within HepG2 cells (Fig. S20). Flow cytometry results were consistent with the fluorescence findings, further indicating that PZ@M-T significantly increased the content of free Fe2+ in HepG2 cells, thereby facilitating the Fenton reaction (Fig. 3C).

An imbalance in the intracellular antioxidant system is also one of the hallmark events of ferroptosis, including excessive production of ROS and MDA, accumulation of LPO, and depletion of GSH [42–44]. We used the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe to qualitatively visualize and quantitatively measure intracellular ROS levels. The findings showed that the PZ@M-T group exhibited the strongest green fluorescence intensity and the highest proportion of ROS-positive cells, indicating that PZ@M-T further enhanced ROS production and exacerbated the imbalance of the antioxidant system (Fig. 3D-E).

The LPO generation in HepG2 cells after treatment with different nanoparticles was detected using BODIPY 581/591 C11 as a fluorescence probe. Compared with free PPⅡ, PZ, and PZ@M groups, HepG2 cells treated with PZ@M-T showed a significant increase in LPO levels, thus indicating promotion of LPO accumulation (Fig. 3F-G). Additionally, intracellular GSH and MDA levels were quantitatively measured, and GSH fluorescence intensity was visualized. These results demonstrated that after treatment with PZ@M-T, intracellular GSH content significantly decreased, with the weakest fluorescence intensity, while MDA levels significantly increased, indicating a loss of cellular antioxidant capacity and the accumulation of peroxides, further exacerbating ferroptosis (Fig. S21-23).

Mitochondrial damage and ferroptosis induced by PZ@M-T

As the energy metabolism factory of cells, mitochondria are deeply affected by ferroptosis, with mitochondrial morphological changes being particularly prominent [45]. Therefore, we observed the mitochondrial structures via biological TEM. Compared to the control and TA-ZIF-8 groups, mitochondrial structures in the PPⅡ, PZ, PZ@M and PZ@M-T treatment groups underwent significant changes, with mitochondria transforming from rod-shaped to round, reductions or disappearance of mitochondrial cristae, and the appearance of numerous vacuolar structures in the cells (Fig. 4A).

(The red arrows indicate the mitochondria.) (B) Fluorescence images of JC-1 monomers and JC-1 aggregates for evaluating MMP in HepG2 cells. (C, D) Analysis of MMP in HepG2 cells using ImageJ (n = 3). Loss of MMP is one of the typical characteristics of mitochondrial dysfunction. We examined the MMP using a JC-1 fluorescent probe, and the results indicated that the red fluorescence of mitochondrial aggregates was significantly reduced after nanoplatform treatment, while the green fluorescence of mitochondrial monomers was significantly increased compared to the control and TA-ZIF-8 groups (Fig. 4B-D), indicating obvious mitochondrial depolarization. The above findings suggested that the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent cellular ferroptosis might be important mechanisms through which PZ@M-T enhanced cellular cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells.

To gain insights into the important role of ferroptosis in this study, we examined the expression of relevant proteins through Western blot assays. As shown in Fig. 4E-I, the expression levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 were both significantly decreased in the PZ@M-T group compared to the control and PPⅡ groups. In contrast, the expression levels of ACSL4 and TFR1 were enhanced by over 3 times compared to the control group. These results indicated that PZ@M-T dramatically enhanced ferroptosis in HepG2 cells and regulated ferroptosis-related genes, suggesting a crucial function for PZ@M-T in tumor ferroptosis.

Specific activation of the cGAS-STING pathway by PZ@M-T

Inducing ferroptosis, which causes changes in mitochondrial structure and dysfunction, is an effective strategy for promoting mtDNA release and activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway [46, 47]. After confirming that the nanoplatform could efficiently promote mtDNA release (Fig. 5 A), we detected the expression levels of proteins related to cGAS-STING signaling pathways (Fig. 5B, Fig. S24). The expression levels of cGAS and IFN-β in the PZ@M-T group were enhanced by over 8.8 and 9.7 times, respectively, compared to the control group. Additionally, the ratios of p-STING/STING, p-TBK1/TBK1, and p-IRF3/IRF3 were significantly increased. These results demonstrated that PZ@M-T had the ability to activate the cGAS-STING pathway.

Activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway mainly induces the release of proinflammatory factors such as IFN-β, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 to modulate cellular immune responses. Therefore, we used ELISA to quantitatively detect the above proinflammatory factors in HepG2 cells. As a result, compared with the control and TA-ZIF-8 groups, the levels of IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α were obviously enhanced in PZ@M-T group (Fig. 4C-F). It is worth noting that the levels of the above inflammatory factors were increased over 1.9, 2.0, 1.4, and 1.6 times, respectively, after PZ@M-T administration compared with free PPⅡ administration, indicating a massive release of proinflammatory factors.

To further validate that the observed cGAS-STING activation and subsequent immune response are specifically mediated by the cGAS-STING axis, we performed pharmacological inhibition experiments using cGAS-IN-1 and STING-IN-2. And the results indicated that pretreatment with cGAS-IN-1 significantly reduced the expression of cGAS and IFN-β, as well as the phosphorylation levels of STING, TBK1, and IRF3. In contrast, STING-IN-2 had no significant effect on cGAS expression, but markedly decreased the phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3, and downregulated IFN-β production (Fig. 5G, Fig. S25). These results collectively demonstrated the specific activation of the cGAS-STING pathway by PZ@M-T. Given that the cGAS-STING pathway exerts its immune-regulatory function mainly through driving the secretion of proinflammatory factors, we conducted the ELISA assay to detect the pro-inflammatory factors in the cell supernatants after treatment with cGAS-IN-1 and STING-IN-2. And the results confirmed that pretreatment with cGAS-IN-1 or STING-IN-2 prior to PZ@M-T treatment significantly abrogated the PZ@M-T-induced upregulation of IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 5H-K), suggesting that the immune activation effect was significantly inhibited. Overall, these collective findings provided compelling evidence supporting that PZ@M-T specifically activated the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and triggered the subsequent release of proinflammatory factors.

Inhibitor verification of ferroptosis caused by PZ@M-T

To determine whether ferroptosis induced by PZ@M-T was the primary driver of cGAS-STING signaling pathway activation, HepG2 cells were pretreated with Ferrostatin-1 and Z-VAD-FMK, followed by assessments of MMP changes and mtDNA release. As shown in Fig. 5A, pretreatment with either Ferrostatin-1 or Z-VAD-FMK led to a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity of JC-1 aggregates, which suggested that PZ@M-T-induced mitochondrial depolarization was notably attenuated, with mitochondrial damage alleviated and mitochondrial function tending toward normalization. Notably, the rescuing effect of Ferrostatin-1 on MMP was significantly more pronounced than that of Z-VAD-FMK. Similarly, mtDNA release assays revealed that pretreatment with Ferrostatin-1 significantly suppressed the release of mtDNA induced by PZ@M-T (Fig. 5B). In contrast, Z-VAD-FMK only exerted a trend toward reducing mtDNA release, with no statistically significant difference observed compared to the PZ@M-T alone group. Collectively, these data implied that PZ@M-T-induced ferroptosis may have served as the primary contributor to mitochondrial damage, rather than apoptosis.

Building on these observations, we further sought to confirm whether ferroptosis directly regulated the downstream cGAS-STING signaling activation. First, the expression of GPX4 (a well-established hallmark of ferroptosis) was examined, and the results further confirmed that ferroptosis was the primary form of cell death induced by PZ@M-T in HepG2 cells (Fig. 5C, Fig. S26). Compared with the PZ@M-T alone group, treatment with either of the ferroptosis inhibitors (Ferrostatin-1 or Liproxstatin-1) significantly reduced the intracellular expression levels of cGAS and IFN-β, as well as the phosphorylation levels of STING, TBK1, and IRF3. In contrast, Z-VAD-FMK had no significant effect on the expression of these cGAS-STING pathway-related proteins. Similarly, in the ELISA analysis of proinflammatory cytokines, ferroptosis inhibitors significantly reduced the secretion of IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α induced by PZ@M-T, whereas Z-VAD-FMK did not suppress the production of these cytokines (Fig. 5D-G). These results collectively indicated that PZ@M-T-induced ferroptosis mediated mitochondrial damage and subsequent mtDNA release, which in turn drove the activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and the secretion of proinflammatory factors, thus confirming the causal role of ferroptosis in PZ@M-T-triggered immune activation. Therefore, inducing ferroptosis and promoting mtDNA release might be an effective strategy for PZ@M-T to activate the cGAS-STING pathway (Fig. 5H).

MSCm-mediated in vivo targeted delivery of PZ@M-T

Previous studies have confirmed that MSCs possess tumor tropism [48–50]. Therefore, in this study, MSCm was coated onto the surface of PZ to achieve efficient delivery to liver tumor tissues. In order to evaluate the ability of PZ@M-T to specifically target liver tumor, the in vivo fluorescence imaging of mice was performed at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h after tail vein injection. As shown in Fig. 6A, the liver tumor-bearing mice injected with PZ@M and PZ@M-T exhibited intensive fluorescence in the tumor tissues. The strongest fluorescence was observed at 12 h, and this fluorescence was maintained for up to 24 h in the liver tumors, indicating a substantial accumulation of PZ@M and PZ@M-T in the tumor. Furthermore, the fluorescence intensity of the isolated tumors in the PZ@M and PZ@M-T groups was significantly stronger than that in the Cou-6 and Cou-6/PZ groups, further confirming that MSCm coating can enhance the tumor targeting abilities of PZ@M and PZ@M-T (Fig. 6B-C). Though we also inevitably observed fluorescent signals in other tissues including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, the intensity was significantly weaker than that of tumor tissues (Fig. 6D). We speculated that this finding might have resulted from the liver and kidneys both being organs with the most critical metabolic and excretory functions.

In vivo antitumor effects in the Hepa1-6 HCC model

In vitro antitumor studies have demonstrated that PZ@M-T exhibited excellent chemotherapeutic effects and immune-activating properties. Inspired by these findings, we investigated its in vivo antitumor activity using a Hepa1-6 HCC model (Fig. 7A). During the 9-day treatment, the tumor volumes of mice were recorded daily and the tumor inhibition rates were calculated, showing that PZ@M-T significantly inhibited the development of liver tumors in mice, with a tumor inhibition rate of 76.7%, which was obviously higher than that of TA-ZIF-8 (12.8%), PPⅡ (30.2%), PZ (43.5%), and PZ@M (58.0%) groups (Fig. 7B-C, Fig. S27-28). This finding was primarily attributed to an increased accumulation of PZ@M-T at the tumor sites and the combined use of chemotherapy and immunotherapy. No significant difference in efficacy was observed between the anti-PD-L1 treatment group alone (66.7%) and the PZ@M-T treatment group; however, it is noteworthy that the optimal efficacy was obtained with PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 treatment, with a tumor inhibition rate exceeding 86.6%.

Furthermore, we dissected and weighed the tumors at the end of the treatment, as shown in Fig. 7D and E, and the weights of tumors were significantly inhibited in the PPⅡ, PZ@M, and PZ@M-T treatment groups compared with the control and TA-ZIF-8 groups, which was consistent with the results for tumor volume. In contrast, the PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 group had the most significant inhibition of tumor growth, probably because anti-PD-L1 effectively enhanced tumor ICD and further activated the immune response of mice.

In addition, the biological safety of PZ@M-T was evaluated, and the mice treated with PZ, PZ@M, and PZ@M-T remained normal throughout the experiment without abnormal behavioral changes or weight loss (Fig. 7F, Fig. S29). Blood samples were collected for routine hematological tests and liver and kidney function assessments. The results revealed that, aside from the PPⅡ monotherapy group, no significant alterations were detected in the biomarkers of biochemical and hematological analyses across all other treatment groups (Fig. S30-31). This finding suggested that PZ@M-T exhibits favorable biocompatibility, whereas PPⅡ alone may induce mild hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity.

Finally, in the H&E staining sections of each group, there was no visible damage to the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidneys (Fig. S32). In conclusion, PZ@M-T was biocompatible and relatively safe in terms of the administration time and dosage. Subsequently, we carried out H&E staining observations of the tumor tissues, and the results showed that PZ@M-T and PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 treatment ruptured the nuclei of tumor cells, with cytoplasmic staining becoming lighter and the tumor tissues becoming necrotic (Fig. 7G). Furthermore, we also detected the Zn2+ concentration in tumors and the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney). Compared with the control group, the results indicated that the Zn2+ concentration in tumor of the PZ@M-T group was significantly elevated (Fig. S33). However, the Zn2+ concentration in the major organs did not show significant differences among the groups. The data demonstrated that the MSCm coating could enhance tumor accumulation, and no obvious off-target accumulation was observed in major organs.

Systemic immune activation in vivo of PZ@M-T

Triggering the cGAS-STING signaling pathway is crucial for establishing an effective innate immune response, including stimulation of DC maturation and induction of TAMs to polarize toward the M1 phenotype, both of which play a significant role in regulating the body’s spontaneous antitumor immune response [51]. Additionally, activation of the cGAS-STING pathway can indirectly promote T cell activation and differentiation by inducing antigen-presenting cells (such as DCs and macrophages) to secrete IFN-Ⅰ and proinflammatory factors [52–54]. Therefore, the cGAS-STING pathway, a key bridge connecting innate and adaptive immunity, plays a critical role in tumor immune regulation (Fig. 8A). Based on these observations, we speculated that the excellent antitumor activity of PZ@M-T is closely associated with the comprehensive activation of the immune response.

Encouraged by the results of previous in vitro immunological studies, the immunological activation effects of different nanoplatforms were further explored. The combination of PZ@M-T and anti-PD-L1 demonstrated excellent efficacy for inhibiting liver tumors in mice, and we speculated that the introduction of anti-PD-L1 would enhance the occurrence of tumor ICD. Therefore, we used immunofluorescence staining to analyze the levels of CRT and HMGB1 in tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. 8B-E, compared with the control group, CRT levels were significantly elevated and HMGB1 levels were reduced after PZ@M-T treatment. Notably, CRT levels in the anti-PD-L1 treatment group were significantly higher than those in the PZ@M-T group, while HMGB1 levels were similar between the two groups. Additionally, combination therapy with anti-PD-L1 further increased CRT levels and decreased HMGB1 levels. These findings indicated that both PZ@M-T and anti-PD-L1 contribute to ICD activation, exerting a synergistic effect to enhance immune responses and antitumor activity.

In addition, we used flow cytometry to assess the maturity of DCs in tumor tissues, and the results showed that the ratio of CD86+CD80+ cells was significantly increased in mice treated with PZ@M-T (20.60%), compared to the control (2.86%), TA-ZIF-8 (2.99%), PPⅡ (6.35%), PZ (8.56%), and PZ@M (15.62%) groups, indicating that the DCs in the PZ@M-T group were highly mature (Fig. 8F-G). The combination of PZ@M-T and anti-PD-L1 (36.24%) further enhanced this DC maturity, promoting the transition of DCs from an “antigen-capture state” to an “immune-activated state”.

Previous studies have shown that the absolute dominance of M2-TAMs and the significant deficiency of M1-TAMs in the TME of HCC constitute a core characteristic of immune suppression [55, 56]. Prompting TAMs to repolarize from the M2 phenotype to the M1 phenotype, and thereby transforming the tumor from a “cold” state to a “hot” state may be one of the most important strategies by which PZ@M-T initiates tumor immunity. Therefore, we investigated the TAM repolarization in liver tumors through flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 8H-I and Fig. S35, the ratio of M2-TAMs (CD86−CD206+) was obviously decreased in the PZ@M-T group and PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 groups. Meanwhile, the ratio of M1-TAMs (CD86+ CD206−) in the two groups was increased to 9.14% and 13.6%, respectively, which was significantly higher than in the control (1.79%) and the PPⅡ (3.63%) groups, further confirming the significant immunotherapeutic effects induced by PZ@M-T.

PZ@M-T promoted T cell immune activation and Proinflammatory cytokine release

The cGAS-STING pathway exerts multifaceted regulation over T cell-mediated immune responses by integrating innate immune sensing with adaptive immunity, orchestrating a hierarchical sequence of molecular and cellular events. Upon activation, STING triggers transcriptional programs in antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby enhancing the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I/Ⅱ molecules and co-stimulatory molecules, and potentiating T cell activation. Concurrently, STING-mediated induction of IFN-I and pro-inflammatory cytokines reshapes the cytokine environment, promoting CD4+ T cell proliferation and enhancing the cytotoxic potential of CD8+ T cells, while inhibiting the expansion of Tregs [57, 58].

Based on this information, we used flow cytometry to analyze the T lymphocyte infiltration into tumors after various treatments. The PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 group had the highest proportion of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells, followed by the PZ@M-T group and then anti-PD-L1 monotherapy group. Conversely, the lowest proportion of Tregs was observed in the PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 group, indicating that the PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 treatment had the most significant ability to activate T cells (Fig. 9A-D, Fig. S36).

Additionally, given the important mediating roles of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory factors in the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, we used ELISA to quantitatively measure the levels of IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α in tumor tissues. These data suggested that PZ@M-T alone and in combination with anti-PD-L1 promoted the secretion of IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 9E-H).

To systematically explore the antitumor immune mechanism of the combined administration of PZ@M-T and anti-PD-L1, we analyzed the expression of key immune-related molecules in tumor tissues and T cells. First, the protein level of PD-L1 in tumor tissues was detected using immunofluorescence. The results showed that compared with the control group, the fluorescence intensity of PD-L1 was significantly decreased in the anti-PD-L1 monotherapy group, PZ@M-T monotherapy group, and PZ@M-T plus anti-PD-L1 combination group, among which the combination group exhibited the most prominent downregulation effect (Fig. 9I). This finding suggested that PZ@M-T can significantly downregulate the expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells, thereby alleviating the immunosuppressive state of the TME [59]. Next, we detected the expression of Tim-3 (a T-cell exhaustion marker) on the surface of CD8⁺ T cells via fluorescent staining [60]. The results demonstrated that compared with the control group, the fluorescence intensity of CD8⁺ T cells was significantly enhanced in all drug-administered groups, while the fluorescence intensity of Tim-3 was reduced to varying degrees, with the combination therapy group showing the weakest Tim-3 intensity (Fig. S37). These results indicated that PZ@M-T can reduce the functional exhaustion of CD8⁺ T cells by downregulating the Tim-3-mediated exhaustion pathway; meanwhile, the addition of anti-PD-L1 more significantly reversed T-cell exhaustion, thus exhibiting the strongest immunotherapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, we analyzed the expression of CD103 and Granzyme B in tumor tissues. CD103 is a key marker of tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM), and the enrichment of CD103⁺ TRM is generally associated with the local expansion of tumor-specific T-cell clones [61]. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of CD103 was significantly increased in both the PZ@M-T monotherapy group and the combination group, with the combination group showing the most significant effect (Fig. S38). This suggested that the combination therapy more effectively promoted the expansion of tumor-specific TRM cells in the TME. In addition, Granzyme B is a critical cytotoxic effector molecule of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), and its expression level directly reflects the killing capacity of CTLs [62]. The results revealed that the fluorescence intensity of Granzyme B in the combination group was significantly higher than that in the PZ@M-T monotherapy group and anti-PD-L1 monotherapy group, indicating that the CTL-mediated killing effect was most prominent in the combination therapy group (Fig. 9J).

Collectively, these results confirmed that the combined administration of PZ@M-T and anti-PD-L1 exerted a potent synergistic antitumor immune effect through multiple pathways. The underlying mechanisms include: downregulating PD-L1 to reverse immunosuppression, downregulating Tim-3 to reverse T-cell exhaustion, promoting the expansion of CD103⁺ TRM to strengthen tumor-specific immune surveillance, and increasing Granzyme B expression to improve CTL-mediated cytotoxicity.

Discussion

HCC remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies globally, with limited treatment options for advanced-stage patients [1, 2]. In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative strategy for the treatment of HCC, particularly with ICIs targeting PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA-4. Clinical trials such as KEYNOTE-224 and IMbrave150 have demonstrated that ICIs, whether used as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapeutic agents, can significantly improve the overall survival rate and objective response rate in patients with advanced HCC [63, 64]. However, the clinical benefits of ICI-based therapy are still limited by several key challenges. First, the TME of HCC comprises an extremely complex composition, including tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, and extracellular matrix, with significant inter-patient differences in the composition and function of these components. This high inter-individual heterogeneity directly results in a generally low response rate to ICI therapy among patients, and some patients even exhibit a complete non-response [6, 7]. Second, the TME of HCC possesses typical immunosuppressive characteristics, which are mainly manifested as follows: extensive infiltration of Tregs with immunosuppressive functions, enrichment of M2 macrophages that can secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines, and high expression of PD-L1 molecules on the surface of tumor cells and stromal cells. These factors collectively construct a robust “immunosuppressive barrier”, which not only severely impairs the cytotoxic activity of effector immune cells such as CD8⁺ T cells but also hinders the effective initiation and sustained exertion of anti-tumor immune responses [65]. Furthermore, this immunosuppressive TME can induce functional exhaustion of effector T cells, which further reduces the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs after they relieve immune checkpoint inhibition.

Against this backdrop, PZ@M-T, developed in this study, showed promising potential in HCC immunotherapy. Its immune activation effect mainly stemmed from the fact that the loaded PPⅡ triggered ferroptosis in cells, thereby activating the endogenous cGAS-STING pathway. This unique “chemotherapy-immunotherapy synergy” highlighted the great potential of PPⅡ in the treatment of HCC. In addition, the combination of the MSCm biomimetic strategy and TPP targeting modification endowed the nanoparticles with excellent tumor-targeting ability, which also provided a necessary condition for effectively reactivating anti-tumor immune responses.

Due to the strong immunosuppressive properties of the TME and the high tendency of drug resistance, monotherapy often has limited efficacy in the clinical treatment of HCC. Therefore, the combination of PZ@M-T with anti-PD-L1 therapy may serve as a more optimal treatment strategy. As expected, among all treatment groups, the PZ@M-T combined with anti-PD-L1 group exhibited the strongest efficacy in reversing immunosuppression and activating anti-tumor immunity in vivo. Specifically, on the one hand, this combined therapy effectively activated the cGAS-STING pathway, promoted the maturation and antigen presentation of DCs, and thereby activated the anti-tumor immune response mediated by CD8⁺ T cells; on the other hand, the combined therapy significantly reduced the expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells, decreased T cell exhaustion, enhanced the cytotoxic capacity of CTLs, and thus markedly reversed immunosuppression.

Nevertheless, despite the promising prospects of these research findings, several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, it is still necessary to verify the pharmacodynamic effects and immune activation ability of PZ@M-T in orthotopic HCC animal models. This is because subcutaneous tumor models cannot reproduce key features such as the interaction between tumor cells and surrounding liver tissue, vascular invasion, and the unique composition of hepatic stromal cells, and thus cannot fully simulate the TME of HCC. Second, conducting long-term safety evaluations of PZ@M-T is also highly worthy of further investment in future research, to assess potential risks such as possible chronic organ damage or cumulative toxicity.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel biomimetic nanoplatform was constructed using ZIF-8 MOF that possessed the ability to specifically activate the cGAS-STING pathway via ferroptosis. The PZ@M-T nanoplatform specifically targeted HCC by leveraging the homing capacity of MSCm and the mitochondrial targeting properties of TPP. Within the acidic TME of HCC, the ZIF-8 rapidly disintegrated to release PPⅡ, which, in turn, highly effectively induced ferroptosis in HCC cells. During this sequence of events, morphological and functional damage to mitochondria led to the release of abnormal mtDNA, which triggered a cascade of responses within the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. This cascade not only promoted the maturation of DCs and the polarization of M2-TAMs toward M1-TAMs, but also enhanced the infiltration of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells at the tumor site, thereby comprehensively activating the immune response and demonstrating excellent anti-HCC efficacy. Overall, this rationally engineered immunotherapeutic nanoplatform paves the way for a new paradigm in the development of immune-nanotherapeutics with enhanced antitumor efficacy.
