Beyond contextual smoking norms: The importance of family-level nicotine use risk factors among rural and nonrural youth living in Arkansas
Authors: Ashley H. Clawson, Jeff Thostenson, Alison H. Oliveto, Mary J. Bollinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study identified the associations between home-based nicotine use risk factors (sibling nicotine use; permissive home/car smoking rules; and parental approval of youth nicotine use) and nicotine use among youth in Arkansas, a rural state overburdened by tobacco, after accounting for county-level adult smoking rates.MethodsData were from the 2021 Arkansas Prevention Needs Assessment, a state-wide, school-based survey (N = 41,707) of 6th, 8th, 10th, and 12th graders. Youth self-reported on lifetime and past-month nicotine use and independent variables [home/car smoking rules, sibling nicotine use, parental approval of child nicotine use]. Models were executed on the full sample and stratified by rurality.ResultsIn the full and nonrural models, higher county-level adult smoking rates were associated with youth nicotine use. In all models, after adjusting for county-level adult smoking, youth with permissive smoke-free home/car rules, perceived parental approval of child nicotine use, and sibling nicotine use were more likely to report lifetime and past-month nicotine use.ConclusionsAddressing home-based nicotine use risk factors may reduce nicotine use among rural and nonrural youth, regardless of the prevalence of adult smoking in their communities. Multi-level interventions are needed to reduce youth exposure to smoking in their communities and home-based nicotine use risk factors.

Introduction

Arkansas is a rural state—45% of residents live in rural counties versus 20% nationally (Seo and Goodman, 2025)—overburdened by tobacco (Truth Initiative, 2019). Rural populations have earlier tobacco and nicotine use (TU/NU) initiation, higher youth and adult TU/NU rates (with variability by product type), lower quit ratios, and strong intergenerational patterns of TU/NU (Bernat and Choi, 2018; Parker et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2016, Roberts et al., 2020; Talbot et al., 2019; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024; Wiggins et al., 2020). Rural-urban youth TU/NU disparities may be impacted by differences in exposure to TU/NU risk factors across socioecological levels, such as the community and home levels (Jackson and Dickinson, 2009).

At the contextual level, higher county-level adult smoking—an indicator of community smoking norms—increases risk for youth smoking (Thrul et al., 2014). Research on how county-level adult smoking rates relate to youth use of other nicotine products is lacking. This is a critical gap because youth are increasingly using non-cigarette products (Han et al., 2025) and understanding how county-level adult smoking impacts youth use of various nicotine products could inform policy and prevention interventions in a state with poor overall tobacco control (American Lung Association, 2026), and ultimately help to reduce youth geographic TU/NU disparities (Roberts et al., 2020; Talbot et al., 2019; Wiggins et al., 2020). Therefore, the current study investigated how county-level adult smoking rates relate to youth use of varied nicotine products, and if this association differed based on rurality.

Rural youth also experience more family-level TU/NU risk factors than non-rural youth: They are more likely to live with caregivers who use nicotine products, be exposed to environmental tobacco smoke, and be offered nicotine products by caregivers, and less likely to have home smoking rules and receive anti-nicotine socialization from caregivers (Bernat and Choi, 2018; Kegler and Malcoe, 2005; Mantey et al., 2021; Roberts et al., 2020). Sibling TU/NU is an understudied risk factor among rural youth. In smaller, rural Midwest samples (N = 663; N = 279), sibling vape use was associated with greater risk of vape use (Hesse and Fite, 2024), and sibling use of vapes and traditional tobacco were related to increased risk for youth TU/NU (Fite et al., 2018).

Overall, this paper investigated how a community factor [county-adult smoking rate (Thrul et al., 2014)] and numerous home-based factors [sibling TU/NU (Leonardi-Bee et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2018), permissive home/car smoking rules (Emory et al., 2010), and parental approval of child TU/NU (Sargent and Dalton, 2001)] related to youth TU/NU in a rural state with high tobacco burden (Truth Initiative, 2019), as well as identified differences in these associations based on within-state variability in rurality. Importantly, this paper specifically aimed to advance our understanding of how home-based TU/NU risk factors relate to youth TU/NU, after adjusting for county-level adult smoking. This is important because interventions aiming to reduce youth rural-urban TU/NU disparities will benefit from understanding what home-based factors influence youth TU/NU, regardless of the prevalence of adult smoking in the communities where youth live. This paper had two objectives. The first was to determine whether rurality differences exist in youth exposure to county-level adult smoking rates and home-based TU/NU risk factors. The second was to identify the associations between county adult smoking rates and family-level TU/NU risk factors with youth TU/NU (lifetime and past-month) among the full sample of Arkansas youth, as well as among samples stratified based on the rurality of the school location. Findings may inform multi-level interventions aiming to reduce child exposure to household and contextual TU/NU-risk factors and reduce youth TU/NU.

Methods

Study design and population

Youth-reported data were from the 2021 Arkansas Prevention Needs Assessment (APNA), a state-wide, school-based survey based on the validated Risk and Protective Framework (Arkansas Department of Human Services, 2021). All public schools with 6th, 8th, 10th, or 12th graders were invited to participate in APNA. School, and therefore student, participation was achieved in 70 of our 75 counties. There was variability in the rate of eligible student participation by county, but the 2021 dataset was deemed representative of the students in the state (Arkansas Department of Human Services, 2021). Teachers administered surveys to students between November 2021 to January 2022 using a protocol that maximized confidentiality and accuracy.

In 2021, valid data (N = 55,449) were obtained from 16,231 6th, 16,680 8th, 13,114 10th, and 9424 12th graders across 70 of our 75 counties. This paper included students with complete data on all variables of interest (N = 41,707); 13,742 cases were excluded due to missing data (24.78%). The rates of missing data for all variables ranged from 0% to 13.35%, with relatively similar missing rates by sex, race/ethnicity, and grade (Table 1). Age, sex, and race/ethnicity were included as covariates in primary models. The subsample of students attending rural schools included 18,756 students with complete data (77.8% of the rural sample); the subsample of students attending nonrural schools included 22,951 students with complete data (73.3% of the nonrural sample). This study was performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and was deemed not human subjects research by our institutional review board on July 15, 2025 (#299089).

Measures

Youth self-reported on all demographics, including age, sex, and race and ethnicity. Response options are presented in Table 1.

Nicotine use

Youth self-reported on lifetime and past-month use of cigarettes, smokeless tobacco, and nicotine vapes; response options were yes and no. Using responses on each of these questions, two binary composite variables were calculated: 1) lifetime use of any of these nicotine products and 2) past-month use of any of these nicotine products.

Home and car smoking rules

Youth answered a question asking, “Which statement best describes rules about smoking inside your home or your family cars?”. Response options were as follows: smoking is not allowed anywhere inside the home or cars, smoking is allowed in some places or some times in the home or cars, smoking is allowed anywhere inside the home or cars, there are no rules about smoking inside the home or cars, and I do not know.

Sibling use of cigarettes and vapes

Youth answered two questions about sibling nicotine use: 1) “Have any of your brothers or sisters ever smoked cigarettes?” and 2) “Have any of your brother or sisters ever used a vaping product like e-cigarettes, e-cigars, or e-hookahs?”. Responses on these two questions were used to create a composite variable summarizing sibling use of these nicotine products, with the following response options: no siblings at home, sibling use cigarettes and vapes, siblings use cigarettes only, siblings use vapes, and siblings use neither cigarettes or vapes.

Parental perspectives on child nicotine use

Youth were asked, “How wrong do your parents feel it would for you to smoke tobacco?”; responses options were: very wrong, wrong, a little bit wrong, and not at all wrong.

School rurality

School location was used to identify the county where the school was located and then each county was coded using the county-level USDA rural-urban continuum codes (United States Department of Agriculture, 2024); all non-metro categories were coded as rural.

County-level adult smoking rates

School location was used to identify the county where the school was located and then the associated county-level adult smoking rates were drawn from 2021 CDC PLACES data (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023). To be on a more comparable scale with the categorical factors, the raw county-level adult smoking rate was re-scaled for a 1-unit increase in this continuous variable to represent a change of 5% in the rate. Models were fit on this re-scaled variable.

Statistical analysis

Rural and nonrural youth variables were compared using Student's t-tests, Wilcoxon Rank-sum tests, or χ2 tests. Generalized linear mixed models with a logit link function were modeled for each binary TU/NU dependent variable. Each independent variable (county adult smoking rate, home/car smoking rules, parental perspectives on child TU/NU, and sibling TU/NU) was included in each model along with age, sex, and race/ethnicity for adjustment. Models were clustered by school and executed on the full sample and stratified by school rurality. SAS (version 9.4) PROC GLIMMIX was used.

Results

About 45% of youth attended rural schools. Youth attending rural schools had 43% higher lifetime TU/NU (23.4% vs. 16.4%) and 51% higher past-month TU/NU relative to youth attending nonrural schools (12.8% vs. 8.5%; Table 1). Compared to nonrural youth, youth attending rural schools were more likely to attend schools in counties with higher county-level adult smoking, more likely to have siblings who use nicotine, and less likely to have comprehensive home/car smoking rules (i.e., smoking not allowed anywhere) and perceive that their parents disapprove of child TU/NU.

Across the state, youth in counties with higher smoking rates have increased odds of having a lifetime TU/NU history; this association was also significant for youth of nonrural counties, but not for youth of rural counties (Fig. 1). After adjusting for county-level adult smoking in the full and stratified samples, home-based TU/NU risk factors were related to youth lifetime TU/NU. Compared to youth with comprehensive home/car smoking rules, those with no rules (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.57, 1.97) or with smoking being allowed anywhere (OR = 1.89, CI: 1.63, 2.20) or in some places (OR = 1.66, CI: 1.52, 1.81) were more likely to have a lifetime TU/NU history. This finding holds true for rural and nonrural youth. In the full and stratified samples, compared to youth who report their parents strongly disapprove of child TU/NU, youth who perceive their parents have less disapproval were more likely to have used nicotine (Full sample ORs ranging from 2.16 to 4.38). Sibling TU/NU was associated with increased risk for TU/NU in all models (Full sample ORs ranging from 2.42 to 6.18). The results for past-month TU/NU were largely congruent to those for lifetime TU/NU (Fig. 2).

Across lifetime and past month nicotine product-specific outcomes, the results remained consistent, with some variability in the strength of associations across products (Supplementary Materials-Tables 1–3 and Figs. 1–3). For example, in models examining lifetime and past-month cigarette use among the full sample, higher county-level adult smoking (OR = 1.32, CI: 1.21, 1.45 and OR = 1.43, CI: 1.20, 1.70, respectively), permissive home/car smoking rules (excluding the “I do not know” option”, ORs ranged from 1.78 to 2.57), parental approval of child TU/NU (ORs ranged from 1.99 to 7.16), and sibling TU/NU (excluding the “no siblings at home” option, ORs ranged from 1.55 to 5.98) were associated with greater odds of youth cigarette use (Supplementary Materials-Table 1 and Fig. 1). The pattern of results was similar among rural and nonrural youth, except that (as with the models examining youth use of any nicotine product), county-level adult smoking rates were not significantly associated with cigarette use among rural youth. Supplemental Materials-Tables 2–3 and Figs. 2–3 detail the models investigating youth smokeless tobacco use and nicotine vape use, highlighting similar patterns: County-level adult smoking was related to youth smokeless tobacco use and vaping in the full and nonrural models, but not the rural models, and home-based TU/NU risk factors were related to greater odds of smokeless tobacco and vaping in all models.

Discussion

Arkansas is a rural state overburdened by tobacco and tobacco-caused disease (Seo and Goodman, 2025; Truth Initiative, 2019). This study identified that home-based TU/NU factors were associated with youth lifetime and past-month TU/NU across the full sample of youth living in a rural state with high tobacco burden as well among youth attending schools located in rural and nonrural counties, after accounting for county-level adult smoking. This study is among the first to quantify the associations between the home environment and youth TU/NU in the context of county-level adult smoking, and to identify that these associations hold regardless of the adult smoking rates in youths' communities, which in Arkansas averages 20% (University of Wisconsin Population Health Institute, 2026). Future research should investigate the potential of home-based interventions for reducing youth TU/NU and ultimately reducing rural-urban tobacco disparities. The significance of this finding is echoed in our findings documenting that youth attending schools located in rural counties were more likely to have used nicotine and had greater exposure to county-level adult smoking and home-based TU/NU risk factors. This documents how within-state variability in rurality affects youth TU/NU even within a rural state, aligns with the existing research documenting rural-urban TU/NU disparities among youth (Bernat and Choi, 2018; Parker et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2020, Roberts et al., 2016; Talbot et al., 2019; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024; Wiggins et al., 2020), and reifies the urgent need for multi-level interventions to eliminate them.

The current findings align with prior work describing how socializing influences across ecological levels impact youth TU/NU (Jackson and Dickinson, 2009). Our study expands upon prior research (Thrul et al., 2014) by documenting a positive association between county-level adult smoking rates and youth odds of lifetime and current use of cigarettes, smokeless tobacco, and nicotine vapes in the full sample and among nonrural but not rural youth, adjusting for home-based factors. This highlights the importance of policy-level interventions to reduce adult smoking (e.g., policies supporting cessation) because they may not only reduce smoking among adults but may also reduce the risk of youth TU/NU given we identified county-level adult smoking as a risk factor for youth use of various nicotine products. The nonsignificant association between county-level adult smoking rates and TU/NU among rural youth may suggest that home-based factors are stronger risk factors among rural youth in Arkansas and/or that other community-level factors are more salient correlates of TU/NU for these youth (e.g., rural cultural assets, tobacco norms and attitudes, marketing exposure)(Talbot et al., 2019).

After adjusting for county-level adult smoking rates, home-based factors emerged as consistent predictors of youth TU/NU in the full sample of youth living in a rural state with high tobacco burden, as well as in the stratified samples of youth attending rural and nonrural schools. Youth living in homes with partial or no home/ car smokefree rules, youth who perceived that their parents approved of youth TU/NU, and youth with siblings who used cigarettes or vapes were more likely to have used any nicotine product in their lifetime and in the past month. Our findings expand on prior research that characterized home smoke exposure, parental approval of TU/NU, and sibling TU/NU as risk factors for youth TU/NU by identifying their consistent associations with youth use of various nicotine products among rural and nonrural youth, even after accounting for youth's exposure to county-level adult smoking (Fite et al., 2018; Hesse and Fite, 2024; Roberts et al., 2020). This research augments prior research on shared and unique TU/NU risk factors among rural and nonrural youth (Roberts et al., 2020).

Arkansas is one of the 12 states included in Tobacco Nation, a cluster of Southern and Midwestern states with persistently higher tobacco use and tobacco-caused disease relative to other parts of the US (Truth Initiative, 2023). Future research could build on this cross-sectional work by determining if interventions addressing home-based factors reduce TU/NU among youth living in tobacco-burdened states, and if intervention effects are moderated by the prevalence of adult smoking in their communities or rurality. However, both state-wide and broader comprehensive tobacco control and prevention programs are also needed to eliminate geographic tobacco disparities. Overall, multi-level interventions are needed to reduce youth exposure to smoking in their communities (e.g., policy-level and community-level interventions) and exposure to home-based risk factors for nicotine use [e.g., family-based interventions (Thomas et al., 2016)], as well increase youth access to nicotine use prevention and cessation resources across systems (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024).

Study limitations include a lack of information on other TU/NU risk factors (e.g., parental and peer TU/NU, policies, tobacco marketing exposure), an inability to determine causality, and limitations in the generalizability to different geographic areas. However, our large sample size with students from 70 of our 75 counties in a rural state with significant tobacco burden (N = 41,707) may generalize to other similar states, such as those in Tobacco Nation. This study did not assess youth use of all nicotine products which provides an underestimate of youth TU/NU in Arkansas; we also lacked county-level information about adult use of nicotine products other than cigarettes. However, it hypothesized that the same pattern of results would emerge if other nicotine products were included. Nevertheless, future work should re-examine these associations with more comprehensive assessments of youth and adult TU/NU. Despite these limitations, the results provide preliminary support for the need for interventions that target community-level and home-based TU/NU risk factors to reduce TU/NU among youth living in rural, tobacco burdened states.

Conclusions

This study advances the literature by identifying that: 1) higher county-level adult smoking rates were associated with increased odds of youth TU/NU and that 2) after adjusting for county-level adult smoking, permissive comprehensive smoke-free home/car rules, perceived parental approval of child TU/NU, and sibling TU/NU remained significantly associated with increased odds of youth TU/NU. These findings suggest that clinical and public health interventions aiming to reduce home-based TU/NU risk factors may benefit youth, regardless of rurality or county-level adult smoking, an important intervention facet for youth living in rural areas with high levels of adult smoking. Multi-level interventions aiming to reduce child exposure to household and contextual TU/NU-risk factors, prevent TU/NU, and support youth cessation are critically needed, especially for youth living in rural states with high tobacco burden.
