Kidney failure risk equation to predict kidney transplant failure in individuals with an eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m2: a UK Renal Registry external validation and recalibration study
Authors: Sherry Masoud, Aisha Bello, Shalini Santhakumaran, Anna Casula, Francesa Maher, Fran Benoy-Deeney, James Medcalf, Rupert Major, Dorothea Nitsch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo externally validate and subsequently repurpose/recalibrate the easily accessible kidney failure risk equation (KFRE) for a prevalent transplant population with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)<30 mL/min/1.73 m². It also aims to evaluate the recalibrated KFRE’s potential to improve clinical practice with a clinical utility assessment.DesignRetrospective cohort study using UK Renal Registry data.Setting68 adult UK kidney centres.Participants4092 patients with grafts at least 2 years old and eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m2 from 2009 to 2018.Primary outcome measureDeath-censored allograft failure at 2 years, defined as dialysis initiation, re-transplantation or, in the absence of the former two, the recorded date of transplant failure.Prognostic model validationThe KFRE was calculated at baseline using the 2-year, 8-variable non-North American KFRE, and performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-statistic and calibration plots. The model was recalibrated using Cox Regression (2009–2013 cohort) and temporally validated using the 2014–2018 cohort. Clinical utility was assessed using decision-curve analysis, estimating per-100-patient gains in timely planning and reductions in unnecessary interventions compared with eGFR triggers.ResultsThe original KFRE had excellent discrimination but was miscalibrated, underpredicting graft failure. Temporal validation demonstrated that the performance of the recalibrated KFRE could be maintained across time periods (Harrell’s C-index of 0.81 (95% CI 0.80 to 0.83); O/E (Observed/Expected events) ratio 1.00 (95% CI 0.93 to 1.07). For this underserved population, we present an easily accessible model that is robust to temporal and case-mix differences, demonstrates clinical utility and has the potential to improve daily clinical practice.Keywords: Risk management, Nephrology, Prognosis

Introduction

Renal transplantation offers the best long-term outcomes for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).1 2 However, despite improvements in short-term graft survival, there has been little improvement in medium-to-late outcomes, with increasing numbers returning to dialysis.3 Patients experiencing graft failure should be prepared for this in a timely manner, both to increase the chances of early re-transplantation and to ensure a smooth transition to the patient’s preferred subsequent treatment. It is well established that late referral and unplanned dialysis starts are associated with worse outcomes in the chronic kidney disease (CKD) population.4 Similarly, those returning to dialysis after graft failure without definitive access have a higher risk of death and infectious complications.5 6 The UK Renal Registry (UKRR) dataset shows that 52% of patients with failed grafts return to dialysis during an inpatient stay, suggesting many return to dialysis in an unplanned or unprepared manner, with similar findings from other UK and North American studies.7,9

Predicting graft failure is not always straightforward, and numerous risk prediction models have been developed.10 Almost all models were developed and validated to make predictions at, or shortly after, the time of transplantation, and therefore are of less clinical relevance and difficult to pragmatically apply when making decisions several years after transplantation. By contrast, one model (iBox) has been validated to be predictive at any time after transplantation and incorporates histological, immunological and functional data11 to generate medium-term to long-term prognostic estimates (3-year, 5-year and 7-year graft failure risk).11 Nevertheless, its use in routine UK practice is limited—access requires arrangements with the Paris transplant group, and many long-term stable recipients may lack recent histology or donor-specific antibody (DSA) data needed to run the model. In addition, while iBox provides robust medium-term to long-term prognostic information, its 3-year risk horizon may not correspond to the point at which patients are typically engaged in planning for dialysis or re-transplantation. By contrast, the kidney failure risk equation (KFRE) provides a 2-year risk estimate, which aligns better with clinical pathways that recommend referral and preparation at least 12 months prior to dialysis or re-transplantation.12 13 The KFRE is widely validated, has been incorporated into the National Institute of Clinical Excellence native CKD guidelines, requires only routinely collected demographic and biochemical data and is familiar to most nephrologists.14,17 Importantly, many renal centres have embedded KFRE calculators into their electronic systems, making them immediately accessible at the point of care and reinforcing their practical value in routine clinical decision-making. The KFRE has also been validated in North American transplant cohorts10 18 19 and a single-centre UK transplant study (4-variable and 8-variable KFRE),20 but to our knowledge, has not yet been validated in a nationwide UK or European transplant population.

In this study, we externally validated the KFRE for predicting graft failure in UK recipients with low kidney function (<30 mL/min/1.73 m²) and a possible ‘failing’ transplant using UKRR data, and we updated the model for this population. In the absence of a single international consensus definition of a ‘failing’ transplant, both British and American Transplantation Society groups broadly distinguish two clinical categories: (1) stable but low function likely to benefit from multidisciplinary advanced kidney care input and (2) progressive decline with anticipated graft loss within approximately 12 months.12 21 The stable-low category is difficult to define by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) alone, as discussed in the 2022 KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) controversies conference.22 CKD complications often emerge when eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m², yet many allografts remain durable at this level, and some, especially extended-criteria grafts, start near it. Accordingly, we selected recipients with eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m² as a high-risk yet clinically actionable cohort with residual equipoise/uncertainty, in whom KFRE could guide referral and care planning for subsequent graft failure.

Materials and methods

Transplant cohort, variables and outcome assessment

Since 2007, all 68 UK adult renal centres have reported quarterly (3-monthly) data on patients receiving kidney replacement therapy (KRT) to the UKRR for audit purposes. The UKRR does not hold biochemistry data for Scottish units, so these units were excluded. We included adult 1st and 2nd kidney transplant recipients (2009–2018) with ≥2 years of failure-free survival. From these, we retained patients with (1) at least one proteinuria measurement and (2) eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m² within the same quarter (calculated from a single raw creatinine value using the CKD-EPI 2009 equation without ethnicity adjustment).23

To exclude transient acute kidney injury (AKI), we removed cases in which the next scheduled quarterly (90-day) creatinine fell by at least a third of the index creatinine that generated the eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m², as this is the inverse of the 1.5-fold increase. If this data point was missing, we proceeded to the next quarter; however, we only allowed one missing data point. If a patient was ineligible based on the AKI criteria above, they could be included in subsequent quarters if they met the proteinuria and eGFR criteria at a later date without again triggering the AKI exclusion criteria. The date of inclusion in the study was defined by the date of index creatinine, generating an eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m² (as long as the aforementioned proteinuria and AKI criteria were also met), but patients could only be included once.

The year 2009 was chosen because spot proteinuria reporting via V.3.14 of the UKRR dataset was established at this time, and 2018 was chosen to allow a minimum follow-up of 2 years prior to the COVID pandemic. Given that the risk of acute transplant rejection decreases significantly after the first year and a previous study showed that the KFRE is more accurately calibrated in transplant patients beyond 2 years, we elected to include patients in our cohort only once they were 2 years post-transplant.10 When only protein-creatinine ratios were available, these were converted to albumin-creatinine ratios (ACR) to enable calculation of KFRE (see online supplemental text 1 for the formula).24

The 2-year risk of transplant failure was calculated using the 8-variable non-North American KFRE (online supplemental text 2), which incorporates calcium, albumin, bicarbonate and phosphate, as well as sex, age, eGFR and urine ACR.15 16 In a preliminary analysis on a smaller cohort, this model slightly outperformed the 4-variable KFRE with regard to discrimination, visual inspection of calibration plots and calibration in the large. This was expected, given that failing transplants often have poorer anaemia, bicarbonate and phosphate control compared with the native CKD population,25 26 and hypoalbuminaemia is associated with poorer graft outcomes.27 28 The 8-variable was therefore selected over the 4-variable KFRE. A complete case analysis was performed; however, patients with missing data were compared with those included in the cohort with regard to baseline characteristics and outcomes. Comorbidity data regarding patients with transplants were augmented using Hospital Episode Statistics for England and Patient Episode Dataset for Wales. The primary outcome was death-censored allograft failure at 2 years, defined as dialysis initiation, re-transplantation or in the absence of the former two, date of transplant failure to avoid excluding those who opt for conservative treatment after graft failure. The date of transplant failure is provided by centres directly to UKRR and via our linkage with National Health Service Blood and Transplant (NHSBT). This is in contrast to previous validation studies which have only included change in KRT.

Model performance measures and sample size

A maximum sample size of 4092 patients was available, and therefore, the calibration slope could be reported with a 95% CI width of 0.289.29 Discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index with 95% CIs calculated using standard errors following the method of Kang et al.30 The model performance at 2 years was of primary interest, so observations were truncated at 2 years. This was to ensure that model coefficients were more specific to short-term risk prediction, as the effect of risk factors could vary over time, and models with a longer horizon have been shown to overestimate the risk of kidney failure.13

Model recalibration

Recalibration was used to improve the fit of the 2-year, 8-variable KFRE model. This recalibrated model was, in turn, validated using temporal validation by splitting the data into a training set (2009–2013) and a validation dataset (2014–2018). This process is considered intermediate between internal and external validations.35 It enables assessment of a model’s performance across time periods, non-random differences in case mix and reflects the envisioned real-life behaviour of the model when applied to new patients. Recalibration was performed on the training set and then verified on the validation dataset. Recalibration was achieved using Cox regression by36:

Estimating a new baseline survival at 2 years by setting the linear predictor of the original KFRE model as the offset.

Clinical utility

Clinical utility was assessed using the decision curve analysis (DCA),37 38 a methodology applied to the 2014–2018 validation dataset to evaluate how well the recalibrated 2-year KFRE supported decision-making across a range of predefined threshold probabilities. In DCA, the net utility reflects the relationship between true positives (patients correctly identified for referral, ie, those whose KFRE is over a threshold and whose grafts fail within 2 years) and false positives (patients unnecessarily referred, ie, those whose KFRE is over a threshold and whose grafts have not failed within 2 years). We compared the net utility of KFRE-based thresholds (eg, 40% risk of ESKD within 2 years) to simple eGFR-based strategies, with referral triggered once eGFR drops below fixed cut-offs (<15 mL/min/1.73 m² or <20 mL/min/1.73 m²). This methodology, previously applied in advanced CKD populations,39 does not require data on actual referrals for dialysis or relisting, as the calculation relies only on eGFR values and dates, KFRE variables and kidney failure dates. A predefined 40% threshold was selected based on its proposed suitability for planning dialysis access and transplantation in native CKD populations.16 40 The net utility of both the KFRE-based and eGFR-based triggers at this threshold on the decision curve was then compared.

Results

There were 4092 patients included in the cohort from 2009 to 2018 who had received their first or second transplant between 1985 and 2016. The mean (SD) age was 53 (14.6) years. Of the cohort, 3645 (89.1%) were first transplant recipients, 3038 (74.2%) were deceased donor recipients and 712 (17.4%) had received pre-emptive transplants. The median graft age was 7.1 years, with a median eGFR of 24.9 mL/min/1.73 m2 and a median ACR of 13.1 mg/mmol. Baseline characteristics for each of the KFRE risk groups can be found in table 1. Those in the highest risk group were younger, male and had older grafts, with a higher proportion of living kidney donor transplants and native disease due to diabetes and glomerulonephritis. HLA mismatches were similar across risk groups. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar between those with missing data and those included in our complete case analysis (online supplemental table 1), with a slightly higher proportion of the missing data cohort having diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Losses to follow-up were minimal (4 patients).

External validation of the original KFRE and model recalibration

The original 8-variable, 2-year KFRE had good discrimination with Harrell’s C-index of 0.81 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.82) and clear separation of risk groups (figure 1a). The KFRE does not account for the competing risk of death; nonetheless, cumulative incidence function curves (which treat death as a competing event) were closely matched to Kaplan-Meier estimates for each risk category (figure 1b), with only mild deviation observed in the higher risk groups after 15 months. However, the original 8-variable, 2-year KFRE consistently underpredicted the risk of allograft failure, particularly for those in high-risk groups (figure 1c). Stratification by age of graft, living versus deceased donor kidney transplant and HLA mismatches did not show evidence of improved calibration in a particular subgroup (online supplemental figures 1–3). We, therefore, proceeded with recalibration as described in the methods. A comparison of the discrimination and calibration performance measures for the original 8-variable KFRE and the three recalibrated Cox models is presented in table 3. Recalibrated model 2, which involved estimating a new baseline survival at 2 years, as well as a single slope factor, performed best when validated on the contemporary 2014–2018 cohort (figure 1d, table 3). Clinical utility

DCA demonstrated that the recalibrated KFRE (model 2) outperforms eGFR triggers of <20 mL/min/1.73 m2 and <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 at relevant threshold probabilities (figure 2). When compared with a late clinical trigger of an eGFR<15 mL/min/1.73 m2 at a 40% threshold probability, the recalibrated KFRE was able to identify nine extra patients per 100 that would progress to transplant failure by 2 years and identify 13 more patients per 100 for whom intervention could be delayed. The median time to transplant failure for patients with a 2 year KFRE risk of ≥40% using recalibrated model 2 was 10.2 months (IQR 5.9–15.7).

Discussion

External validation

Our study is the first UK study to externally validate the KFRE in a large failing transplant population using real-world data covering England, Wales and Northern Ireland. In keeping with previous studies conducted in North America and a small UK study, we found that the original KFRE demonstrated excellent discrimination of 0.81 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.82).1018,20 We also found no improvement in calibration performance regardless of donor type (living or deceased) or graft vintage (2–5, 5–10 and >10 years), consistent with Chu et al’s findings.10 In contrast to these studies, we used a more restricted and understudied but clinically important cohort with an eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m2 and found the KFRE consistently underpredicted the risk of graft failure, with this discrepancy being particularly pronounced in high-risk groups. This is perhaps not surprising, as failing transplants frequently proceed to kidney failure at an accelerated rate, with McCaughan et al demonstrating a mean eGFR loss of 7.9 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the 12 months prior to graft failure.41 We propose that a kidney transplant, being a single organ and specifically a single transplanted organ, has a more limited reserve than a native kidney. This diminished reserve may explain the observed undercalibration seen across all risk groups, but it is more pronounced in high-risk groups and provides a logical basis for the accelerated rate of failure often observed in these organs. Indeed, a propensity-matched analysis comparing transplant to native CKD patients demonstrated a lower cumulative incidence of ESKD initially, which exceeded that of native CKD by 5 years and was 2.3-fold higher by 10 years.42

Model recalibration

Given that calibration is often considered the most essential element of a risk prediction tool, and large datasets like the UKRR provide a valuable opportunity for recalibration, we updated the original 8-variable KFRE for our target transplant population.43 Indeed, many prognostic models fail to transport to different settings and are often discarded in favour of new model development, leading to a plethora of models and wasting available research.43 We used temporal validation to assess the transportability of our recalibrated model across time periods and different case mixes, a process considered intermediate between internal and external validation.44 We performed a complete case analysis, including only those with creatinine and proteinuria data, so particularly for the latter, bias may have occurred by excluding individuals where testing of this had not occurred. The rate of graft failure was, however, similar among both those with complete and missing data. The KFRE does not account for competing risk of death, but only 6% of the cohort had died prior to graft failure, and the cumulative incidence (treating deaths as a competing risk) and Kaplan-Meier curves (treating deaths as censored) were similar. We did not consider death as a competing risk because, as a recalibration of an existing model, we followed the same method as the original KFRE. A recent study by Ramspek et al13 found that the predictive performance of the 2-year KFRE in patients with advanced CKD was similar before and after accounting for competing risks. However, death as a competing risk should be considered if developing a new prediction model, particularly for longer-term risk horizons.

It is possible that incorporating donor characteristics or immunological variables, such as findings from kidney biopsies or the presence of DSAs, may have enhanced the predictive accuracy of the original model, particularly for high-risk groups where it exhibited significant miscalibration. However, these variables were not available to us, are not always readily available in clinical practice and limit the practicality of the model. Regardless of variable availability or their impact on model practicality, the recalibrated model demonstrated uniform performance within the validation cohort, including in the high-predicted and observed risk groups. We suggest that this consistent performance is attributable to the fact that proteinuria and eGFR remain downstream markers of graft damage, irrespective of the cause. Indeed, proteinuria has been shown to be a powerful independent predictor of graft failure, irrespective of histology or DSAs.45,47 It should also be noted that the purpose of the model is not to guide treatment decisions; rather, it is to promote open and honest discussions when reversible causes have been addressed to the best of the treating team’s abilities.

When comparing the recalibrated KFRE to the well-calibrated full iBox model (which includes histological and DSA inputs and has been tested geographically), the discrimination of our model 2 was similar, with a Harrell’s C-index of 0.81 (95%CI 0.80-0.83) and the iBox showing a C-index ranging between 0.80-0.81, depending on the validation cohort.11 The discrimination of our model was also comparable to that of the simplified iBox model, which includes proteinuria, eGFR and DSAs and provided a Harrell’s C-index of 0.81 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.82).11 The iBox model offers 3-year, 5-year and 7-year predictions.11 The 3-year horizon may be too early to support the practical planning and patient engagement required to prepare for transplant failure, as highlighted previously. In addition, it is notable that most risk evaluation time points in the iBox derivation cohort occurred relatively early, between 1 and 2 years post-transplant, with sensitivity analyses assessing performance primarily within 4–5 years (mean 2.3 years, SD 1.3). By contrast, our cohort had a median graft age of 7.1 years, representing patients with established chronic allograft injury and the cumulative burden of immunological and non-immunological insults over time. In this context, grafts with persistently low function may appear clinically stable yet remain at high risk of decompensation, making the timing of failure difficult to anticipate. The KFRE is particularly valuable in such circumstances: by generating short-term risk estimates from routinely available clinical data, it can help to identify recipients with stable but low graft function who are most likely to benefit from early referral to multidisciplinary advanced kidney care teams, thereby facilitating timely preparation for dialysis or re-transplantation.

Finally, the KFRE in our cohort was calculated at baseline only (once eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m2), whereas in clinical practice, patients are repeatedly assessed at each follow-up visit. Further work could explore the value of serial KFREs or the rate of change of KFRE risk, and externally validate future cohorts. Nonetheless, this model still provides a useful adjunct to initiating and guiding conversations about planning for transplant failure, supported by ongoing qualitative studies, such as IN-FAKT, which is exploring patient, family and clinician experiences, as well as priorities around transplant failure.48 Predicting the risk of transplant failure may be especially useful for patients who are planning for conservative care after transplant failure.

Clinical utility

Our study offers insight into the clinical impact of the recalibrated 8-variable KFRE in a failing transplant population by assessing clinical utility through DCA, which incorporates measures of discrimination and calibration.37 We show that patient intervention on the basis of a recalibrated KFRE assessment was the model of choice compared with using eGFR cut-offs of <20 mL/min/1.73 m2 and <15 mL min/1.73 m2 across a range of appropriate threshold probabilities, including the often cited 40% risk threshold.37 However, we were only able to compare the recalibrated KFRE with simple eGFR-based guidelines, which is not a true representation of daily clinical practice, where decisions are often based on more factors than eGFR, such as the rate of renal function decline and transplantation factors, both immunological and non-immunological, as well as the physician’s experience. That said, currently 52% of transplant recipients return to dialysis during an acute inpatient admission, while some of these cases may indeed be unavoidable due to intercurrent illness or admission to manage late transplant rejection, there is clearly room for improvement.7,9 In addition, DCA only assesses the net utility of true positives, avoiding unnecessary interventions (false positives), and would not account for the wider patient-focused, clinical and potentially economic effects, which a move towards using the recalibrated KFRE as an adjunct to guide decision-making may have. Similar to the original KFRE, the recalibrated version could underpin guidelines for transplant patients, with an online calculator made available to clinicians.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have shown using real-world data from Northern Ireland, England and Wales that in a large failing transplant population with an eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m2, the orignal 8-variable 2-year KFRE consistently underpredicts the risk of transplant failure. We recalibrated the KFRE and used temporal validation to demonstrate that the model maintains excellent discrimination and its calibration is robust to possible differences in case mix, demonstrating the real-life envisioned behaviour of the model in a contemporary UK setting. Further validation would be necessary to confirm utility elsewhere. The net clinical utility of the recalibrated KRE compared with eGFR-based triggers was also shown using DCA. In an underserved and difficult-to-manage failing transplant population who often return to dialysis in an unplanned manner, the recalibrated KFRE provides a practical and easy-to-use tool to help guide discussions and planning for subsequent transplant failure, with the potential to provide tangible support for daily clinical practice.
