Unreliability in Practical Subclasses of Communicating Systems
Amrita Suresh, Nobuko Yoshida

Abstract
Systems of communicating automata are prominent models for peer-to-peer message-passing over unbounded channels, but in the general scenario, most verification properties are undecidable. To address this issue, two decidable subclasses, Realisable with Synchronous Communication ( rsc ) and k k -Multiparty Compatibility ( k k -mc ), were proposed in the literature, with corresponding verification tools developed and applied in practice. Unfortunately, both rsc and k k -mc are not resilient under failures: (1) their decidability relies on the assumption of perfect channels and (2) most standard protocols do not satisfy rsc or k k -mc under failures. To address these limitations, this paper studies the resilience of rsc and k k -mc under two distinct failure models: interference and crash-stop failures . For interference, we relax the conditions of rsc and k k -mc and prove that the inclusions of these relaxed properties remain decidable under interference, preserving their known complexity bounds. We then propose a novel crash-handling communicating system that captures wider behaviours than existing multiparty session types (MPST) with crash-stop failures. We study a translation of MPST with crash-stop failures into this system integrating rsc and k k -mc properties, and establish their decidability results. Finally, by verifying representative protocols from the literature using rsc and k k -mc tools extended to interferences, we evaluate the relaxed systems and demonstrate their resilience.

Introduction

Asynchronous processes that communicate using First In First Out
(FIFO) channels
[
12
]
, henceforth referred to as FIFO systems, are widely
used to model distributed protocols,
but their verification is known to be computationally challenging.
The model is Turing-powerful for even just two processes communicating via two unidirectional FIFO channels

[
12
]
.

To address this challenge, several efforts have focused on identifying practical yet decidable subclasses – those expressive enough to model a wide range of distributed protocols, while ensuring that verification problems such as reachability and model checking remain decidable.
Most FIFO systems assume
perfect
channels, which is too restrictive
to model the real-world distributed phenomena where
system failures often happen.
This paper investigates whether two practical decidable subclasses of communicating systems,

Realisable with Synchronous
Communication
(
rsc

[

(

when integrated with two different kinds of

contracts

[

]
and
session types

[
3

6
]
. We say a system is
resilient
under a given failure model
if (i) the inclusion remains decidable, and (ii) the verification properties
of interest remain decidable under that failure model.

Failures in communications.

A widely studied failure model in FIFO systems
is
lossy channels

[

Abdulla and Jonsson

Restricting the channel behaviour.

To define decidable subclasses,
many works
study how to restrict read and write access to channels. For two-process (binary) FIFO systems, the notion of
half-duplex
communication was introduced in
[
15
]
, where at most one direction of communication is active at any time. For such systems, reachability is decidable in polynomial time. However, generalising this idea to the multiparty setting often yields subclasses that are either too restrictive or lose decidability.

Di Giusto et al.
[
19
,
20
]
extended this idea to multiparty systems while preserving decidability, resulting in the notion of systems
realisable with synchronous communication
(
rsc

They showed that this definition overlaps with that in

[

for mailbox communication. However, in the
case of peer-to-peer communication where the two definitions differ,

rsc

[

Another approach to restricting channel behaviours is to bound the length of the channel. Lohrey
[

A decidable bounded approach,

This property is

Checking

and OCaml
[

Combining the two approaches.

As far as we are aware, the intersection between expressive, decidable subclasses and communication failures is less explored.
Lozes and Villard
[
39

studied reliability in binary half-duplex systems and showed that
many communicating contracts can be verified with this model.
Inspired by this, we investigate whether practical
multiparty

subclasses,

Although failure models such as lossy channels are well studied, the complexity of verification in their presence is often very high — for instance, reachability in lossy systems is non-primitive recursive
[

Contributions and outline.

The main objective of this paper is to investigate
whether multiparty adaptations of half-duplex systems
(
rsc
and
k
-mc
) retain both their expressiveness for modelling real-world protocols and the decidability of their inclusions, with preserved complexity, under two distinct kinds of communication failures: interferences and crash-stops.
§
2
introduces preliminary notions, notably FIFO
systems and interference models;
§
3

studies
rsc
under interference, and shows that relaxing certain conditions on matching send and receive actions retains both expressiveness and decidability (Theorem
19
).
§
4

examines
k
-mc
with interferences, and proposes a relaxed version,
k
-wmc
(weak
k
-mc
), by weakening the
safety
condition. We prove that checking the
k
-wmc
property remains decidable under interferences (Theorem
26
).
§
5
introduces
the FIFO systems with crash-stop
failures (called
crash-handling systems
), and shows that
checking
rsc
and
k
-wmc
under crash-stop failures
is decidable (Theorems
32
and
33
);
§
6

defines a translation from (local) multiparty session types (MPST) to
crash-handling systems and
proves that this translation preserves trace semantics.
This implies the decidability of
rsc
and
k
-mc
within
the asynchronous MPST system extended to crash-stop failures
(Theorem
40
); §
7
evaluates
protocols
from the literature extending the existing tools with support for interferences;
and §
8
concludes with further related and future work.
Proofs are provided in the appendix.
The tools and benchmarks are publicly available from
https://github.com/NobukoYoshida/Interference-Tool

Preliminaries

For a finite set
\Sigma
, we denote by
\Sigma^{\ast}
the set of finite words over
\Sigma
, and the empty word with
\varepsilon
. We use
|w|
to denote the length of the word
w
, and
w_{1}\cdot w_{2}
indicates the concatenation of two words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
. Given a (non-deterministic) finite-state automaton
\mathcal{A}
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
the language accepted by
\mathcal{A}
.
Consider a finite set of processes
\mathbb{P}
(ranged over by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}},\ldots
or occasionally by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r_{2}}},\ldots
) and a set of messages
{\Sigma}
. In this paper, we consider the
peer-to-peer
communication model; i.e., there is a pair of unidirectional FIFO channels between each pair of processes, one for each direction of communication. In our model, processes
act either by point-to-point communication or by internal actions (actions local to a single process). Moreover, in this setting, we consider messages to be atomic, akin to letters of an alphabet.

Let
\mathit{Ch}

=\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}\mid{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\neq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}\text{ and }{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}\in\mathbb{P}\}
be a set of
channels
that stand for
point-to-point links.
Since we are considering the peer-to-peer model of communication,
there is a unique process that can send a message to (or dually, receive a message from) a particular channel.
An action
a=({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}},!,\textit{m})\in\mathsf{Act}
indicates that a
process
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
sends a message
m
on the channel

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}
. Similarly,
a=({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}},?,\textit{m})\in\mathsf{Act}
indicates that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
receives a message
m
on the
channel
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
.
We henceforth denote an action
a=({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}},\dagger,\textit{m})\in\mathsf{Act}
, where
\dagger\in\{!,?\}
, in a simplified form as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{\dagger}\textit{m}
.
An
internal action

\mathit{c}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
means that process
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}

performs the action
c
.
We define a finite set of actions as
\mathsf{Act}\subseteq(\mathit{Ch}\times\{!,?\}\times{\Sigma})\cup\mathsf{Act}_{\tau}
where

\mathsf{Act}_{\tau}
is the set of all internal actions.

Definition 2

(FIFO automaton)
.

A FIFO automaton
\mathcal{A}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}
, associated with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
, is defined as
\mathcal{A}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}=(Q_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}},\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}},q_{0{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}})

Q_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
is the finite set of control-states,

\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\subseteq Q_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\times\mathsf{Act}\times Q_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}

is the transition relation, and

q_{0{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}\in Q_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
is the initial control-state.

Note that in this model, there are no final or accepting states.

The set of outgoing channels of process
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}

is represented by
\mathit{Ch}_{\texttt{o},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}=\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\}
.
Similarly,

\mathit{Ch}_{\texttt{i},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}\in\mathbb{P}\setminus{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\}
is the set of incoming channels of
process
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
.

Given an action
a
,
an
active process
, denoted by
\mathsf{proc}(a)
,
is defined as:

\mathsf{proc}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}!{m})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
and
\mathsf{proc}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}?{m})={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}
. Similarly,
\mathsf{ch}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}!{m})=\mathsf{ch}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}?{m})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}
.

We say a control state
q\in Q_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
is a
sending state
(resp.

) if all its outgoing transitions are labelled by send (resp. receive) actions. If a control state is neither a sending nor receiving state, i.e., it either has both send and receive actions or neither, then we call it a

. We say a sending (resp. receiving) state is

if all the send (resp. receive) actions from that control state are to the same process.
Like for finite-state automata,
we say that a FIFO automaton

\mathcal{A}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}=(Q_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}},\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}},q_{0{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}})
is

if for all transitions
(q,a,q^{\prime}),(q,a^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime})\in\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
,
a=a^{\prime}\implies q^{\prime}=q^{\prime\prime}
.
We write

q_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}q_{n+1}
for
(q_{1},a_{1},q_{2})\cdots(q_{n},a_{n},q_{n+1})\in\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
.
Unless specified otherwise, we consider non-deterministic automata,
allowing mixed states, and all states do not have to be directed.

In this paper, we do not restrict

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}
continues to send it.

•

Lossiness
: Consider the execution
e_{\ell}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}!{start}~.~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}?{start}~.~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}!{data}~.~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}?{data}~.~

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}!{end}}
. Here, the message
end
has been lost, which means process
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}
will be stuck waiting for process
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}}
to either send
data
or
end
.

•

Out-of-order
: Let
e_{o}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}!{start}~.~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}?{start}~.~{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}!{data}}~.~{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}!{end}}~.~{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}?{end}}~.~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}}}}!{ack}~.~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}}}}?{ack}~.~{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}}}}?{data}}.

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}}}}!{err}~.~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{r}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{s}}}}}?{err}
. In this case, the order of
data
and
end
has been swapped in the queue, which leads to a configuration where the error message is sent.

As shown in
[
1
,
23
]
, for communicating automata with lossiness, the reachability set is recognisable, and the reachability problem is decidable. In the case of out-of-order scheduling, it is easy to see that the problem reduces to reachability in Petri nets. It is less clear, but it can also be reduced to Petri net reachability problem in case of corruption. We recall these proofs in Appendix
A
.
However, the complexity of reachability for these systems is
very high – it is non-primitive recursive for lossy
systems
[
46
]
, and Ackermann-hard for corruption
and out-of-order
[
14
]
. Hence, it is still worth exploring subclasses in the presence of errors.

3
RSC systems with interferences

We first extend the definitions of synchrony in systems from
[
20
]
to consider possible interferences. The main extension relates to the definition of
matching pairs
. Intuitively, matching pairs refer to a send action and the corresponding receive action in a given execution. In the presence of interferences, it is not necessary that the same message that is sent is received (due to corruption), or that the
k
-th send action corresponds to the
k
-th receive action (due to lossiness or out-of-order).
Hence we extend the definition of matching pairs.

Definition 7

(Matching pair with interference)
.

Given an execution
e=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
, if there exists a channel
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}
, messages
m,m^{\prime}\in{\Sigma}
and
j,j^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
where
j<j^{\prime}
, and the following four conditions:

(1)

a_{j}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}!{m}
;

(2)

a_{j^{\prime}}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}?{m^{\prime}}
;

(3)

a_{j}
is the
k
-th send action to

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}
in
e
; and

(4)

a_{j^{\prime}}
is the
k^{\prime}
-th receive action on

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}
in
e
,
then we say that
\{j,j^{\prime}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
is a
matching pair with interference
, or
i
-matching pair.

Note that if
m=m^{\prime}
and
k=k^{\prime}
, we are back to the original
definition of matching pairs in
[
19
, Section 2]
, which we shall refer to henceforth as
perfect matching pairs
. When we refer to a matching pair, we mean either a perfect or
i
-matching pair. Moreover, our formalism allows for a single message to have more than one kind of interference, e.g. the same message can be corrupted and received out-of-order.

Example 8
.

Consider the following execution
e=a_{1}\ldots a_{5}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}!{a}\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}}}!{b}\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}}}?{b}\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}!{c}\cdot{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}?{c}}
. For the channel
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
, we have a perfect matching pair
\{2,3\}
which corresponds to the actions
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}}}!{b}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}}}?{b}

If the message

If the message

If the trace with an appended action as follows:

-matching pairs

.

We now modify the definition of
interactions
from
[
19
]
as follows.

Definition 9

(Interaction)
.

An
interaction
of
e
is either a (perfect or
i
-) matching pair, or a singleton
\{j\}
such that
a_{j}
is a send action and
j
does not belong to any matching pair (such an interaction is called unmatched send).

Given
e=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
, a set of interactions
\nu
is called a
valid communication
of
e
if for every index
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, there exists exactly one interaction
\chi\in\nu
such that
j\in\chi
. I.e., we need to ensure that every action in
e
belongs to exactly one interaction in the valid communication. We denote by
\textsf{Comm}(e)
the set of all valid communications associated to
e
.

Example 10
.

Revisiting Example
8
, given the execution

e=a_{1}\ldots a_{5}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}!{a}\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}}}!{b}\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}}}?{b}\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}!{c}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}?{c}}
,
there are two valid communications,

\nu_{1}=\{\{1,5\},\{2,3\},\{4\}\}
and

\nu_{2}=\{\{1\},\{2,3\},\{4,5\}\}
,
and
\textsf{Comm}(e)=\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}
.

For the rest of this section, when we refer to an execution, we are referring to a tuple
(e,\nu)
such that
\nu\in\textsf{Comm}(e)
. We say that two actions
a_{1}
,
a_{2}

commute
if
\mathsf{proc}(a_{1})\neq\mathsf{proc}(a_{2})
and they do not form a matching pair.

Given an execution
(e,\nu)
such that
e=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
and
\nu\in\textsf{Comm}(e)
, we say that
j\prec_{e,\nu}j^{\prime}
if (1)
j<j^{\prime}
and (2)
a_{j}
,
a_{j^{\prime}}
do not commute in
\nu
.
We now graphically characterise
causally equivalent
executions, using the notion of a conflict graph. This allows us to establish equivalences between different executions in which actions can be interchanged.

Definition 11

(Conflict graph)
.

Given an execution
(e,\nu)
, the conflict graph
\mathsf{cgraph}(e,\nu)
is the directed graph
(\nu,\rightarrow_{e,\nu})
where for all interactions
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\in\nu
,
\chi_{1}\rightarrow_{e,\nu}\chi_{2}
if there is
j_{1}\in\chi_{1}
and
j_{2}\in\chi_{2}
such that
j_{1}\prec_{e,\nu}j_{2}
.

The conflict graph corresponding to Example
10
,

\mathsf{cgraph}(e,\nu_{1})
, is:
\{1,5\}
\{2,3\}
\{4\}

Two executions
(e,\nu)
and
(e^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
are causally equivalent, denoted by
(e,\nu)\sim(e^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
, if their conflict graphs are isomorphic.

We are now ready to define
i
-rsc
systems, which is the extension of
rsc
to include interferences. Intuitively,
i
-rsc
executions can be reordered to mimic rendezvous (or synchronous) communication. In the case with interference, we enforce that every valid communication is equivalent to a RSC execution.

Definition 12

(
i
-RSC
system)
.

An execution
(e,\nu)
is
i
-rsc
if all matching pairs in
\nu
are of the form
\{j,j+1\}
. A system
\mathcal{S}
is
i
-rsc
if for all tuples
(e,\nu)
such that
e\in\mathsf{executions}(\mathcal{S})
and
\nu\in\textsf{Comm}(e)
, we have
\mathsf{cgraph}(e,\nu)=\mathsf{cgraph}(e^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
where
(e^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
is an
i
-rsc
execution.

Example 13
.

From Ex.
10
,
(e,\nu_{2})
is an

-rsc
execution, but
(e,\nu_{1})
is neither an

-rsc

has to be sent before message

is received by process
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}
while message

has to be sent before the corresponding message (which is now

due to corruption) is received by process
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}
.

This is the strictest version, however, this can be adapted to include only one communication by assuming a single instance of
\nu
instead of all. We formalise our observation about non-
i
-rsc
behaviours from Example

13
, and show that

i
-rsc
still maintains the good properties of the conflict graph as in
[

]
.

Lemma 14
.

An execution
(e,\nu)
is causally equivalent to an
i
-rsc
execution iff the associated conflict graph
\mathsf{cgraph}(e,\nu)
is acyclic.

A borderline violation for interferences defined below is a key concept for the decidability of
rsc
systems. Intuitively, it provides a “minimal counter-example” for non-
rsc

Definition 15

(Borderline violation)
.

An execution
(e,\nu)
is a borderline violation if
(1)

(e,\nu)
is not causally equivalent to an
i
-rsc
execution,
(2)

e=e^{\prime}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}?{m}
for some execution

such that
(a)
for all
\nu^{\prime}\in\textsf{Comm}(e^{\prime})
,
(e^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
is equivalent to an
i
-rsc
execution and
(b)
there exists
\nu_{1}\in\textsf{Comm}(e^{\prime})
such that
(e^{\prime},\nu_{1})
is an
i
-rsc
execution.

Lemma 16
.

\mathcal{S}
is
i
-rsc
if and only if for all
e\in\mathsf{executions}(\mathcal{S})
and
\nu\in\textsf{Comm}(e)
,
(e,\nu)
is not a borderline violation.

Following the same approach as in
[
19
]
, we show that inclusion into the
i
-rsc
class is decidable. For simplicity, we construct the following sets:
\mathsf{Act}_{nr}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}!?{m}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}!{m}\in\mathsf{Act},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}?{m^{\prime}}\in\mathsf{Act}\}\cup\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}!{m}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}!{m}\in\mathsf{Act}\}
and
\mathsf{Act}_{?}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}?{m}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}?{m}\in\mathsf{Act}\}
. Note that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{c}}}!?{m}
could include a send-receive pair where the message sent is different from the one received. This ensures inclusion of matching pairs due to corruption. An
i
-rsc
execution can be represented by a word in
\mathsf{Act}_{nr}^{*}
and a borderline violation by a word in
\mathsf{Act}_{nr}^{*}.\mathsf{Act}_{?}
. We first show that the set of borderline violations is regular.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
with
\mathsf{product}(\mathcal{S})=(Q,\Sigma,\mathit{Ch},\mathsf{Act},\delta,q_{o})
. There is a non-deterministic finite state automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{bv}}
computable in time
\mathcal{O}(|\mathit{Ch}|^{3}|{\Sigma}|^{2})
such that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{bv}})=\{e\in\mathsf{Act}_{nr}^{*}.\mathsf{Act}_{?}\mid\exists\nu\in\textsf{Comm}(e)\text{ such that }(e,\nu)\text{ is a borderline violation}\}
.

Next we show that the subset of executions
\mathsf{executions}(\mathcal{S})
that begin with an
i
-rsc
prefix and terminate with a reception is regular.
The construction of the automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{rsc}}
recognising such a language mimics the
i
-rsc
executions of the original system
\mathcal{S}
, storing only the information on non-empty buffers, guessing which is the send message that will be matched by the final reception.

For the following result, we let the size
|\mathcal{A}|
of an automaton
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\delta,q_{0{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{}}}})
be
|Q|+|\delta|
. Moreover, the size
|\mathcal{S}|
of a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{A}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}})_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\in\mathbb{P}}
=
\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}\in\mathbb{P}}|\mathcal{A}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}}|
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a FIFO system. There exists a
non-deterministic finite state automaton

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{rsc}}
over
\mathsf{Act}_{nr}\cup\mathsf{Act}_{?}
such that

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{rsc}})=\{e\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}?{m}\in\mathsf{Act}_{nr}^{*}.\mathsf{Act}_{?}\mid e\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{q}}}}}?{m}\in\mathsf{executions}(\mathcal{S})
and
\exists\nu\in\textsf{Comm}(e)
such that
(e,\nu)
is an
i
-rsc
execution
\}
, which can be constructed
in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{|\mathbb{P}|+2}|\mathit{Ch}|^{2}\times 2^{|\mathit{Ch}|})
, where

is the size of
\mathcal{S}
.

Using the above lemmas, we derive the following main theorem in this
section, which states that the inclusion of
an
i
-rsc
system is decidable; and the complexity is comparable to that of checking inclusion to
rsc
[
19
, Theorem 12]
.

Theorem 19
.

Given a system
\mathcal{S}
of size
n
, deciding whether it is an
i
-rsc
system can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{|\mathbb{P}|+2}|\mathit{Ch}|^{5}\times 2^{|\mathit{Ch}|}\times|\Sigma|^{2})
.

8
Conclusion and further related work

In this paper, we derived decidability and complexity results
for two subclasses,
rsc
and
k
-mc
,
under two types of communication failures: interferences and
crash-stop failures.
In the absence of errors,
rsc
systems and
k
-mc
systems are incomparable, even if we restrict the analyses to
1
-mc
systems. For example,
[
37
, Example 4]
is
1
-mc
but not
rsc
. Conversely,
[
19
, Example 4]
is
rsc
but does not satisfy the progress condition, and hence is not
k
-mc
for any
k\in{\rm Nature}
. Despite these distinctions, both classes aim to generalise the
concept of half-duplex communication to multiparty systems.
This serves as our primary motivation for examining failures in a uniform way across both
rsc
and
k
-mc
systems.

In the interference model,
we introduced
i
-rsc
systems,
which relax the matching-pair conditions in
rsc
;
and
k
-wmc
which omits the progress condition
to accommodate a model with no final states.
We proved that the inclusion problem for these relaxed
properties remain decidable within the same complexity class
as their error-free counterparts.
The evaluation results in
§
7
confirm
that relaxed systems are more resilient than the original ones.

As the second failure model, we investigated crash-stop failures. We
defined crash-handling communicating systems which strictly include the class of local types with crash-stop failures. We also proved that both
rsc
and
k
-mc
properties are decidable for this class. Note that multiparty session types
with crash-stop failures studied in
[
6
]

are limited to
synchronous
communications. Meanwhile, the asynchronous setting in

[
3
]

restricts expressiveness
to a set of local types projected from global types (which is known to be
less expressive than those not using global types

[
45
]
). Therefore, both of these systems are strictly subsumed by
our crash-handling system as proven in Theorem

40
.

Integrating the
k
-mc
-checker and the
ReSCu
tool (with support for crash-stop failures) into the Scala toolchain of
[
3
]
is a promising direction for future work, potentially enabling the verification of a broader class of programs than those considered in
[
45
,
3
]
.

Due to the need to model failures in real-world distributed systems, various failure-handling systems have been studied in the session types literature, e.g., affine session types
[
40
,
24
,
31
]
, link-failure
[
2
]
and event-driven failures
[
49
]
. Interpreting their failures into our framework would offer a uniform
analysis of behavioural typed failure processes.

Appendix A
Proofs from §
2

We sketch the proofs of reachability under the presence of errors. The proof is well-known for FIFO systems with lossiness and out-of-order errors, but we for completeness sake, we include it here.

FIFO systems with lossiness.

As shown in
[
16
]
, for lossy systems, the reachability set is recognisable, and the reachability problem is decidable.

Lemma 41

(
[
16
]
)
.

For FIFO systems with lossiness, the reachability set is recognisable.

Proof.

The proof follows from the fact that upward-closed sets are recognisable. Moreover, the complement of the reachability set of lossy FIFO systems is upwards-closed (under the subword ordering). Therefore, the reachability set is recognisable (since recognisable sets are closed under complementation).
∎

FIFO systems with out-of-order errors.

For FIFO systems with out-of-order errors, reachability is decidable.

Lemma 42
.

For FIFO systems with out-of-order errors, reachability is decidable.

Proof.

FIFO systems with out-of-order errors can be seen as FIFO systems with bags, or multisets. Loosely speaking, this can translate to a vector addition system with states (VASS), and
[
14
]
shows that reachability is Ackermann-complete for VASS.
∎

FIFO systems with corruption.

In case of corruption, the reachability problem is decidable.

Lemma 43
.

For FIFO systems with corruption errors, the reachability problem is decidable.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}

The protocol.

We assume a subset of processes to be
proposers
, i.e. processes that can propose values. The consensus algorithm ensures that exactly one value among the proposed values is chosen. A correct implementation of the protocol must ensure that:

•

Only a value that has been proposed will be chosen.

•

Only one single value is chosen by the network.

•

A process never knows that a value has been chosen unless the value has actually been chosen.

The Paxos setting assumes the customary asynchronous, non-Byzantine model, in which:

•

Agents operate at arbitrary speed, may fail by crashing, and may restart. However, it is assumed that agents maintain persistent storage that survives crashes.

•

Messages can take arbitrarily long to be delivered, can be duplicated, and can be lost or delivered out of order, but they are not corrupted.

Paxos agents implement three roles: i) a proposer agent proposes values towards the
network for reaching consensus; ii) an acceptor accepts a value from those proposed, whereas
a majority of acceptors accepting the same value implies consensus and signifies protocol
termination; and iii) a learner discovers the chosen consensus value.

The implementation of
the protocol may proceed over several rounds. A successful round has two phases: Prepare
and Accept.
The protocol ensures that in the case where a consensus value
v
has already been chosen
among the majority of the network agents, broadcasting a new proposal request with a higher
proposal number will result in choosing the already chosen consensus value
v
. Following this
fact, we assume for simplicity that a learner has the same implementation as a proposer.

•

Requirement 1: An acceptor must accept the first proposal that it receives, i.e. for the acceptor, there must be a path from the initial state, which accepts the first proposal it gets.

•

Requirement 2: If multiple proposals are chosen, they all have the same proposal value.
