MS2 Virus-like Particles as a Versatile Peptide Presentation Platform: Insights into the Deterministic Abilities for Accommodating Heterologous Peptide Lengths
Authors: Mei Dang, Long J Wu, Si R Zhang, Jian R Zhu, Yu Z Hu, Chen X Yang, Xiao Y Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractVirus-like particles (VLPs) are nanostructures with the potential to present heterologous peptides at high density, thereby triggering heightened immunogenicity. RNA bacteriophage MS2 VLPs are a compelling delivery platform among them. However, a notable hurdle arises from the immune response toward MS2 coat protein, swiftly eliminating subsequent vaccinations via the same vector. Although larger inserts effectively mask carrier epitopes, current research predominantly focuses on displaying short conserved peptides (<30 aa). A systematic evaluation regarding the deterministic ability of MS2 VLPs as a platform for presenting heterologous peptides remains a gap. In light of this, we employed the “single-chain dimer” paradigm to scrutinize the tolerance of MS2 VLPs for peptide/protein insertions. The results unveiled functional MS2 VLP assembly solely for inserts smaller than 91 aa. Particularly noteworthy is the largest insertion achieved on the MS2 VLPs to date: the RNA helicase A (RHA) dsRNA-binding domains (dsRBD1). Attempts to introduce additional linkers or empty coat subunits fail to augment the expression level or assembly of the MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD1, affirming 91 aa as the upper threshold for exogenous protein presentation. By illuminating the precise confines of MS2 VLPs in accommodating distinct peptide lengths, our study informs the selection of appropriate peptide and protein dimensions. This revelation not only underscores the scope of MS2 VLPs but also establishes a pivotal reference point, facilitating the strategic manipulation of MS2 VLPs to design next-generation epitope/antibody-based therapeutics.Keywords: virus-like particles (VLPs), MS2 VLP, peptide display, RNA helicase A (RHA) dsRNA-binding domain 1 (dsRBD1)

Introduction

Virus-like particles (VLPs) are nanostructures formed by self-assembling proteins, closely resembling native viruses in morphology but lacking viral genetic material, rendering them noninfectious to host cells. An appealing feature of VLPs is their ability to present heterologous peptides in a highly multivalent manner, enhancing their efficacy, even at relatively low concentrations. This characteristic, coupled with their small size and favorable safety profile, has sparked cumulative research on VLPs since 19681 in diagnostics, vaccines, and therapeutic modalities.

Among diverse VLPs, RNA bacteriophage MS2 VLPs stand out as a compelling delivery platform. They facilitate genetic manipulation and present antigenic determinants in dense repetitive arrays.2,3 Additionally, MS2 VLPs can package RNAs located at the 5′ terminus of the pac site,4,5 enabling RNA delivery and establishing a rigorous genotype/phenotype linkage on a single platform. Moreover, they exhibit excellent adjuvant properties, eliciting both humoral and cell-mediated immunity.6−8 When modified with a ligand, MS2 VLPs achieve tissue-specific targeting, enhancing the effectiveness of the carried agents.

Structurally, the icosahedral capsid of MS2 spontaneously assembles from 180 copies of a single coat protein (Figure 1A,B), measuring 22–29 nm in diameter. The basic MS2 capsid can form a mix of T = 3 and T = 4 structures in which T = 3 virions are favored by the ordered genome.9 The crystal structure revealed that the C-terminal α-helices of two coat protein monomers interdigitate to form noncovalent dimers in the capsid, with AB-loops (Gly13, Gly14, and Thr15) prominently protruding out of the VLP surface (Figure 1C),10,11 offering accessibility and regular geometric spacing for foreign peptides presentation. Such features solidify MS2 VLPs as a promising choice in vaccinology.

The most exciting application of MS2 VLPs lies in vaccine development, where they have been employed to display Zika virus potential B-cell epitopes, leading to the neutralization of Zika virus infection of monkey kidney cells.12 Likewise, the immunization of mixed MS2-L2 VLPs, specifically MS2-31L2/16L2 VLPs and MS2-consL2 (69–86) VLPs, has been shown to protect mice against infection caused by multiple high-risk HPV types associated with cervical cancer and a low-risk HPV type linked to genital warts and recurrent respiratory papillomatosis.13−15 Beyond acting as antigen carriers, MS2 VLPs are also under investigation as vehicles for precise targeting and binding of cellular receptors.16

Despite the promising feature of MS2 VLPs in peptides presentation, a significant hurdle lies in the immune response directed toward MS2 coat protein per se rather than the carried inserts, leading to rapid recognition and clearance of subsequent vaccinations using the same vectors. Peter G. Stockley and colleagues have shown that as the size of the insets increases, the amount of the chimeric VLPs recognized by immobilized polyclonal anti-MS2 coat protein IgG decreases,17 indicating that larger inserts could effectively mask the underlying carrier epitopes. Yet, current research predominantly focuses on displaying short conserved peptides, with insertions, typically less than 30 amino acids,18−20 There is a lack of systematic information regarding the deterministic ability of MS2 VLPs as a platform for presenting heterologous peptides.

In this study, we sought to address this gap by adopting the “single-chain dimer” paradigm3 and inserting 13 model peptides/proteins of various lengths (ranging from 10 to 238 aa) into the AB-loop of the second subunit. Our findings revealed that insertions exceeding 100 aa were not expressed in a soluble form, while those below 100 aa showed detectable soluble expression. Additionally, only inserts smaller than 91 aa did we observe functional vehicle assembly. Specifically, we emphasized the RNA helicase A (RHA) dsRNA-binding domain 1 (dsRBD1), which to date represented the largest insertion (91 amino acids) on the MS2 VLP platform. Attempts to introduce additional linkers or empty coat subunits did not increase the expression level or assembly of the MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD1, confirming the length of 91 amino acids as reaching the upper limit of the MS2 VLPs for exogenous proteins presentation.

Our data provide crucial insights into the capabilities of MS2 VLPs in accommodating specific peptide lengths, conclusively establishing the threshold insertion length as 91 aa. This determination serves as a valuable reference for selecting appropriate sizes of the peptides and proteins for practical work with MS2 VLPs. It also opens exciting opportunities for identifying mimics with more complex conformations or larger epitopes and antibodies or targets, facilitating the harnessing of the full potential of MS2 VLPs as a versatile peptide presentation platform for designing next-generation epitope/antibody-based therapeutics.

Results

The Fusion of dMS2 with Peptides or Proteins Shorter than 100 aa Resulted in Soluble Expression

Thirteen model peptides or proteins with diverse lengths (10–238 amino acids) were genetically fused into the AB-loop of C-terminal half of dMS2 (Figure 2A). The general expression levels of these chimeric proteins were assessed by SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure 2B). The results revealed that the insertion of short peptides (10–38 aa), including HBVsAg, GnRH, P14, CoxA16, and the EV71 epitope, demonstrated successful soluble expression. In contrast, as the size of the insets exceeded 100 aa, represented by Ago1 mid domain, Csy4, App1766, the GST tag, and eGFP, the resulting dMS2 chimeric proteins were predominantly observed as inclusion bodies. Intriguingly, despite being found in insoluble aggregates, dMS2 fused with the SUMO tag, dsRBD1, and dsRBD2 remained detectable in the supernatant. Remarkably, the lengths of these three inserts ranged between 91 and 100 amino acids, suggesting the capability of dMS2 to bear inserts shorter than 100 amino acids while maintaining soluble expression.

The Recombinant dMS2-dsRBD1 Demonstrated the Ability to Self-Assemble into MS2 Chimeric VLPs

Considering that SDS-PAGE could only track the denatured mode of proteins but not higher-order states (Figure 3A), Superdex 75 column chromatography was employed to evaluate the capacity of the recombinant dMS2-dsRBD1 and dMS2-dsRBD2 for spontaneous VLP assembly (Figure 3B). The results showed that dMS2 in the absence of any inserts revealed a single normally distributed curve with a peak output volume of 63.0 mL. Unlike the dMS2, dMS2-dsRBD1 and dMS2-dsRBD2 exhibited aberrant chromatographic curves, with the latter showing more pronounced irregularities. Specifically, dMS2-dsRBD1 displayed two peaks, with a major peak at 52.7 mL and a minor peak at 61.6 mL, both of which preceded 63.0 mL. In contrast, dMS2-dsRBD2 manifested a minor peak at 58.0 mL and a major peak much later at 67.4 mL.

To further visually confirm VLPs formation, chimeric dMS2-dsRBD1 and dMS2-dsRBD2 were subjected to TEM analysis (Figure 3C). The results showed that the dMS2 without any inserts was normally assembled with a uniformly distributed particle size of approximately 25 nm. In contrast, MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD1 exhibited heterogeneous sizes, ranging from 50 to 100 nm, consistent with the corresponding chromatographic behaviors. However, we failed to acquire the particles of MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD2, implying the incapability of dMS2-dsRBD2 for self-assembly, which is also in line with the observed chromatographic curve.

MS2 Chimeric VLP Displaying dsRBD1 Possesses Specific dsRNA Binding Activity

Given the essential role of dsRBD1 and dsRBD2 in binding specific dsRNA in previous studies (Figure 4A),21 we aimed to investigate whether such interaction persists or even improves when utilizing MS2 chimeric VLPs displaying dsRBD1. Structural prediction analysis showed that the dsRNA-binding sites of dsRBD1 within dMS2 were exposed, with Ala53 in the second MS2 coat potentially establishing polar interactions with the guanine nucleotide (Figure 4B). To delve further into the interplay between dMS2-dsRBD1 and dsRNA, EMSA analysis was performed (Figure 4C). The results demonstrated that akin to isolated dsRBD1, MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD1 retained the capacity to bind the dsRNA probe. The observed binding was concentration-dependent, evident by the intensified bands at higher protein concentrations. Typically, the migration of dMS2-dsRBD1 bands was slower than that of dsRBD1 due to the disparities in molar weights. It is worth noting that the retarded bands using dMS2-dsRBD1 were more pronounced than those using dsRBD1, indicative of enhanced binding activity. Moreover, the negative control using dMS2 ruled out false positive effects caused by carrier epitopes, further substantiating that the binding interaction was independent of empty MS2 VLPs. However, in the case of MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD2, the bands were corrupted (data not shown), suggesting that the insertion of dsRBD2 might not only perturb the particle assembly but also impact the proper folding of dsRBD2 itself.

Neither Flexible Linkers nor Reduced Insertion Density Could Enhance Chimeric MS2 VLPs Displaying Large Fragments

The failure to acquire assembled particles of MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD2 led us to speculate that the larger inserts may create stronger static clashes, thus impeding polymerization and self-assembly. As such, linker structures were introduced as a strategy to reduce steric hindrance and optimize the MS2 VLP platform. Three representative proteins, dsRBD1, dsRBD2, and eGFP, were individually inserted to evaluate the effects of linkers on the soluble expression as well as self-assembly. Nevertheless, no significant enhancement in soluble expression levels was observed (Figure 5A). Further TEM imaging demonstrated that similar to dMS2, particles formed by the dMS2-linker were uniformly distributed, measuring approximately 30 nm in diameter (Figure 5B). Additionally, akin to particles formed by dMS2-dsRBD1, particles formed by dMS2-linker-dsRBD1 exhibited inhomogeneity with a slightly larger size, ranging from 50 to 125 nm in diameter. Despite our efforts, assembled particles formed by dMS2-dsRBD2 remained elusive.

Furthermore, considering that larger insertions still adversely affected correct folding, even with the use of flexible linkers, we next pursued reducing the density of proteins displayed on MS2 VLPs to further alleviate the surface tension and suppress the defects imparted by insertions. To this end, the dual promoter plasmid pETDuet-1 was employed to coexpress an empty MS2 coat along with dMS2-linker. The same three proteins, dsRBD1, dsRBD2, and eGFP, were utilized (Figure 5C). However, contrary to expectations, the target bands were visible only in the precipitated form. Collectively, our findings uncovered that neither flexible linkers nor reduced insertion density led to improvements in soluble expression and self-assembly into particles, indicating that MS2 VLPs can tolerate insertions up to 91 nm in length, nearly reaching the maximum limit of this platform.

Discussion

Since its initial discovery in 1968,1 VLPs have garnered increasing interest in research. From 2000 to 2023, the study of VLPs has witnessed a steady and notable growth, with 11,075 publications dedicated to this topic (Figure 6A). The intrinsic characteristics of VLPs have rendered them with diverse applications, particularly in vaccine development and as platforms for displaying exogenous peptides. Table 1 provides illustrative examples of VLP representatives, ranging from the relatively small PP7VLPs (∼17 nm in diameter) to larger P22 VLPs (56–62 nm in diameter). Among the myriad of VLPs, MS2 VLPs, derived from the RNA bacteriophage MS2, have emerged as one of the most promising candidates. Over the past two decades, interest in MS2 VLPs has persisted, resulting in 129 relevant articles retrieved from the WoS database (Figure 6A). CiteSpace clustering analysis has delineated the predominant research areas focused on MS2 VLPs, encompassing vaccine development, epitope presentation, drug delivery, and self-assembly (Figure 6B). Despite the widespread use of MS2 VLPs for displaying peptides, the majority of previous research has primarily focused on short peptide insertion and epitope selection, leaving the exact tolerance of insertions on the MS2 platform relatively unexplored. Hence, this study aims to delve into the potential of MS2 VLPs in effectively presenting exogenous peptides, providing a valuable reference and guidance for their further development and utilization in various fields.

Summary of VLPs as Peptide Display Platforms

Although the N-terminus,20,22 FG-loop,23 and AB-loop regions all offer potential sites for accommodating foreign peptides, the AB-loop is particularly appealing due to its status as the immunodominant epitope on wild-type MS2 and its prominent protrusion from the surface of MS2 VLPs (Figure 1A,B). As such, in this study, we concentrate exclusively on exploring the tolerance of the AB-loop for peptide insertion. Of note, given that the insertions fused or conjugated within the AB-loop could interfere with protein folding, we adopted the paradigm of “a single chain dimer”, wherein the two coat proteins are genetically fused.3 The rationale of utilizing this version is supported by two reasons: first, the dimer structure represents the basic subunit of MS2 VLPs, with each VLP particle consisting of 180 coat protein monomers forming 60 A/B dimers and 30 C/C dimers (Figure 1C). As a result, the fusion of the two coat proteins is not expected to disrupt the assembly of VLPs particles. Second, the use of a single-chain dimer is a widely adopted strategy across various VLP systems for epitope presentation, effectively addressing the issue of improper protein folding caused by peptides/proteins insertions.12,24

Here we successfully constructed the MS2 display platform (Figure 2A), which exhibits remarkable solubility and self-assembly capacity (Figure 2B and Figure 3C). By inserting 13 model peptides or proteins with varying lengths, we observed that the soluble expression of dMS2 chimeric proteins could only be achieved when the insertions were less than 100 amino acids, as demonstrated by dsRBD1, SUMO tag, and dsRBD2. Once the length of insertions exceeds this threshold, the dMS2 chimeric proteins tend to form inclusion bodies. Notably, besides the lengths, the expression and solubility of the dMS2 chimeric proteins were also affected by the source and physiochemical properties of the insertions. For instance, compared to short peptide insertions ranging from 10 to 38 aa, such as HBVsAg, GnRH, CoxA16 epitope, and EV71 epitope, the expression level of dMS2-P14 was comparatively weaker (Figure 2B). This phenomenon could be attributed to the inherent activity of P14, as which was designed based on the anticancer peptide KLA.25,26 It may induce cell apoptosis during expression, thereby impacting the normal expression of the chimeric protein. Additionally, App1766, falling within the range of 200–300 aa, displayed partial solubility (Figure 2B), which might be influenced by its unique physicochemical properties as an outer membrane protein of Klebsiella pneumoniae, potentially adsorbing onto E. coli and inducing cell lysis,27 thereby allowing partial expression in the supernatant. Moreover, despite forming inclusion bodies, the expression levels of dMS2-GST and dMS2-eGFP revealed relatively higher levels. This could be explained by the fact that both the GST tag and eGFP are commonly used to promote protein soluble expression.28−30 Efforts to refold the inclusion bodies with 8 M urea yielded undesirable outcomes (data not shown), indicating irreversible damage to the correct folding and assembly of MS2 due to the insertion of excessively long foreign proteins. These results highlighted the critical role of the length and identity of the insertions in determining the efficiency of the MS2 VLPs display, underscoring the importance of carefully selecting appropriate insertions when using the MS2 VLP display platform.

Upon further investigation of the self-assembly capacity, it was observed that recombinant dMS2-dsRBD1 demonstrated self-assembly, giving rise to particles with diameters ranging from 50 to 100 nm (Figure 3C). Conversely, the dMS2-dsRBD2 failed to form assembled particles. This distinction was corroborated by the observation in the chromatographic curves of both the dMS2-dsRBD1 and dMS2-dsRBD2 samples, where the double peaks signified their heterogeneous nature (Figure 3B). Furthermore, the retention time of the major peak of dMS2-dsRBD2 was delayed relative to that of dMS2-dsRBD1 and dMS2, validating the inability of forming MS2 VLPs upon insertion of dsRBD2. Further structural predictions unveiled that unlike dMS2-dsRBD1, the formed dMS2-dsRBD2 can lead to the disruption of the MS2 dimer structure (Figure 7B). This disruption could be attributed to the large fluctuations in the extra N- and C-terminal regions of dsRBD2 relative to dsRBD1 (Figure S2). These fluctuations disrupt the β strands formed around the AB-loop of the second MS2 coat protein used for foreign peptide or protein insertions. In particular, these fluctuations may induce the formation of an additional α helix region or a substantial disordered region, thereby interfering with the interaction interface between the two MS2 coat proteins and ultimately leading to the distortion of the MS2 dimer structure, which likely accounts for the inability to obtain functional particles.

Moreover, the formed MS2-dsRBD1 VLPs exhibited concentration-dependent binding to dsRNA probes (Figure 4C), and the binding capacity of dMS2-dsRBD1 to dsRNA was notably stronger than that of dsRBD1 alone, as evidenced by the relatively brighter shifted bands. This heightened binding interaction can be attributed to the high-density presentation of dsRBD1 within the context of MS2 VLPs, thus amplifying its biological activities. In contrast, in the case of displaying dsRBD2, the EMSA band was disrupted (data not shown), implying that the insertion of dsRBD2 not only impacted the self-assembly of MS2 VLPs but also potentially altered its intrinsic dsRNA binding characteristics. One possible speculation is that the active site of dsRBD2 may be casually occluded during the improper folding process.

How can the display efficiency of MS2 VLPs be improved to tolerate the larger sequence? The larger inserts may generate stronger steric hindrance, thereby deterring polymerization and self-assembly. In the pursuit to improve display efficiency and tolerate larger sequences, we devised a linker structure strategically positioned around the insertion site to ameliorate steric hindrance originating from the insertion of large foreign segments (Figure S1). This linker amalgamates the commonly used poly G sequence with established Q25 and P6 sequences from prior research.31,32 However, the results demonstrated minimal distinctions. Analogous to cases in the absence of linker, the MS2-linker VLPs displaying dsRBD1 exhibited partial assembly, while larger sequences, such as eGFP, also triggered aggregation. Similarly, the assembly of MS2 linker VLPs displaying dsRBD2 proved failed, which aligns with the structure prediction analysis suggesting that the insertion of dsRBD2 could disrupt MS2 dimer packing (Figure 7B), paralleling the scenario lacking a linker. Furthermore, in an effort to reduce the density of displayed foreign peptides, we integrated a single MS2 coat protein into the vector, aiming for its expression as a dimer to participate in the MS2-linker VLP assembly. This approach was intended to ensure that the presentation of foreign peptides remained below 90, thereby curtailing steric hindrance caused by high-density display and fostering VLP formation. Unsatisfactorily, the results yielded limited success in enhancing the efficiency of the MS2 VLP platform. These findings substantiate that MS2 VLPs can tolerate insertions up to 91 aa, nearly reaching the maximum limit of this platform. Further studies are warranted to modify the linker sequence and elucidate the assembly behavior to better understand VLP formation.

The identification of the dsRBD1 as the largest insertions displayed in MS2 VLPs to date, opens up new avenues for engineering the MS2 VLP platform to determine mimics of more complicated conformations or larger epitopes. In addition, it provides valuable references for simultaneously displaying two or more inserts, such as antibody mimetic or disease-related peptides, enabling the development of bispecific or multispecific antibodies/mimetics with potential applications in personalized vaccines and biotechnological research. Moreover, the capacity to accommodate such large insertions on MS2 VLPs holds the potential for developing vaccines that confer protection against multiple related pathogen strains, thereby expanding vaccine coverage. By selecting appropriate foreign peptides, one can design vaccines with enhanced immunogenicity and broad-spectrum protective effects, offering an effective means for preventing infectious diseases. Beyond its immunologic implications, this platform also allows for studying the structure and function of foreign proteins, charting an essential trajectory for future research pursuits.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the deterministic abilities of MS2 VLPs as a versatile peptide presentation platform. The identification of a threshold insertion length of 91 amino acids expands our understanding of the limits and repertoires of MS2 VLPs for exogenous protein presentation. The implications of these findings are substantial for the design and development of VLP-based vaccines and targeted therapeutics, unlocking new opportunities to fully harness the potential of MS2 VLPs in biomedicine. Further studies exploring a wider range of insertions and functional domains will undoubtedly build upon this foundation and propel advancement in the field of VLP-based peptide presentation platforms.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The bacterial strains and plasmids utilized in this study are detailed in Table S1. E. coli Trans5α and Trans1-T1 phage-resistant chemical competent cells (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were employed as cloning hosts. Additionally, E. coli BL21 (DE3) was designated the expression host. The gene sequence encoding the MS2 coat protein (UniProt ID: P03612) was synthesized by Synbio Technologies (http://www.synbio-tech.com.cn/).

Plasmid Construction

The two tandem MS2 coat subunits were cloned into pET28b. Briefly, the upstream copy of the MS2 coat gene was amplified and digested with NcoI and BglII restriction enzymes. The digested fragment was then ligated into pET28b (Annoron Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) that was linearized with NcoI and isocaudarner BamHI, generating intermediate construct pET-sMS2. As for the downstream subunit of the MS2 coat, it was subjected to mutagenesis using the KOD-Plus-mutagenesis kit (TOYOBO Co., Ltd., Japan) to introduce two mutation sites (i.e., BamHI and SalI) between codons 14 and 15. Subsequently, the mutated MS2 was amplified and cloned into the pET-sMS2 using EcoRI and HindIII sites, resulting in the recombinant vector pET-dMS2 (Figure 2A). The pET-dMS2 vector served as the backbone for displaying exogenous peptides and proteins in the following studies.

To investigate the precise tolerance of the coat protein toward insertions, a series of plasmids were generated by fusing various-length sequences encoding eGFP, GST, App 1766, Csy4, Ago1 mid domain, dsRBD2, SUMO, dsRBD1, EV71, CoxA16, p14, GnRH, and HBVsAg between BamHI and SalI sites in the pET-dMS2 vector above. Detailed information on the inserted peptides or proteins is illustrated in Figure 2A.

Additionally, for the control experiments involving MS2 VLP displaying dsRBD1 and dsRBD2 in subsequent functional assays, the genes encoding dsRBD1 and dsRBD2 were individually cloned into the pET-28b vector incorporating an N-terminal (His)6-SUMO tag.

Moreover, a pET-dMS2-linker vector was created to introduce the GGGGSQGVSDLVGLPNQICLQKTTSTILKP (G4SQ25) and PAQCSEGGGGS (P6G4S) linkers flanking the constituted BamHI and SalI sites (Figure S1). This was achieved using the pEASY-Uni Seamless Cloning and Assembly kit (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The plasmid denoted as pETDuet-tMS2-linker was engineered by retaining the cascade version of coat proteins derived from the pET-dMS2-linker plasmid and appending another single MS2 coat, which was respectively inserted into the pETDuet vector under two T7 promoters (Figure S1). Similarly, several representative insertions were introduced into both pET-dMS2-linker and pETDuet-tMS2-linker.

All of the resulting recombinant constructs described above were verified by sequence analysis. All the referring primers (Table S2) were synthesized by Genewiz (https://www.genewiz.com.cn/).

Protein Expression and Purification

To assess the expression levels of the recombinant proteins, each plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells and induced using 0.4 mM Isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 14 h at 20 °C. The harvested cells were resuspended in buffer A (25 mM Tris, 25 mM KH2PO4, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM dithiothreitol, pH 7.5), and subjected to ultrasonication (Scientz, JY92-IIN; Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China) for cell lysis. Followed by centrifugation, the soluble cytoplasmic fractions and precipitated proteins were identified using SDS-PAGE.

For the purification of dMS2, dMS2-dsRBD1, and dMS2-dsRBD2, the crude supernatants were initially precipitated by adding saturated ammonium sulfate to 30% (v/v). The resulting precipitates were resuspended in buffer B (25 mM Tris and 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.5). The suspensions were loaded onto a Q-Sepharose anion exchange column (GE HealthCare Technologies Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), pre-equilibrated with five-bed volumes of 25 mM Tris-buffer (pH 7.5). Following a wash step with 5-bed volumes of mono Q buffer (10 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol, pH 7.5), the target proteins were eluted with a linear gradient of 250 mM to 1 M NaCl. The eluted fractions were then pooled and further purified via gel filtration using a Superdex 75 (GE HealthCare Technologies Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) pre-equilibrated with gel filtration buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 4 mM dithiothreitol, pH 7.5.

For the control experiments involving MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD1 and dsRBD2 in subsequent EMSA analysis, recombinant dsRBD1 and dsRBD2 using the pET-SUMO expression vector were purified through Ni-NTA affinity columns (GE HealthCare Technologies Inc., Chicago, IL, America) and eluted with a linear gradient of imidazole. The protein peak fractions from the Ni column were dialyzed overnight against buffer B supplemented with P19 proteinase for (His)6-SUMO tag cleavage. Subsequently, the fractions were reloaded onto a Ni-NTA column to thoroughly remove the (His)6-SUMO tag. The flowthrough was then dialyzed against buffer C (25 mM Tris, 250 mM NaCl; pH 7.5).

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) Analysis

The purified proteins (dMS2, dMS2-RBD1, and dMS2-RBD2) were diluted in buffer B and gently applied to carbon-Formvar-coated copper grids. After 3 min of adsorption, the grids were subjected to three consecutive 1 min washes with deionized water. Subsequently, negative staining was performed using a 1% uranyl acetate aqueous solution for 10 min at room temperature. The stained grids were carefully loaded into a Tecnai-G200 transmission electron microscope (FEI Company, Oregon, USA), operating at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV for imaging and analysis.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

The biotin-labeled GC10 double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) probe (5′-GCGCGCGCGC-3′) was custom-synthesized by GenScript (http://www.genscript.com/). Purified MS2 VLPs displaying dsRBD1 and dsRBD2 in buffer C were individually mixed with EMSA/Gel-Shift binding buffer supplemented with 10 U of RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, America) and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min to minimize nonspecific interactions. Subsequently, 5 nmol of dsRNA probe was added to each sample and incubated for an additional 20 min. The resulting samples were resolved on 4% native polyacrylamide gels and then transferred onto nylon membranes. A series of sequential steps, including cross-linking, blocking, detecting, washing, and staining, were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions provided with the Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Beyotime Biotech Inc., Shanghai, China). Data acquisition was performed using an imager (Tanon, 5200; Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

For the control experiments, identical RNA probing processes, as described above, were performed for dsRBD1, dsRBD2, and MS2 VLPs to enable a comparative analysis of their respective interactions with the dsRNA probe.
