Evolution and regional disparities in guideline recommendations and evidence levels for structural heart disease
Authors: Yuxiang Tang, Aijin Xie, Yiming Yan, Guangzhi Zhao, Yi Sun, Fengwen Zhang, Shouzheng Wang, Cheng Wang, Fang Fang, Chuangshi Wang, Xiangbin Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySignificant advances have been made in the field of structural heart disease (SHD), yet guideline characteristics and evolution across regions remain uncertain. This study aims to analyze the distribution of class of recommendation and level of evidence (LOE) of SHD guidelines across American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA), European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and Chinese cardiovascular societies. We evaluated 1,867 recommendations from prior and current guidelines. In current guidelines, strong recommendations (Class I/III) constituted 53.2%, yet only 8.1% (38/470) were supported by highest-quality evidence (LOE A). Regional variations existed, with ACC/AHA showing predominant LOE B recommendations (56.3%) while ESC and Chinese guidelines featured mainly LOE C (79.4% and 69.5%, respectively). Longitudinal analysis revealed overall evidence improvement. These findings highlight persistent challenges in aligning recommendation strength with evidence quality across healthcare systems. Additionally, the disparity across regions underlines the importance of establishing international collaborative mechanisms to coordinate guideline development processes.

Introduction

Structural heart diseases (SHDs), primarily including valvular heart disease (VHD) and congenital heart disease (CHD), have become a focal area in cardiovascular medicine with advancements in development of innovative devices and treatment techniques.1 The prevalence of VHD is growing worldwide due to improved survival and aging populations, affecting more than 70 million people, including an estimated 25 million patients in China.2 The birth prevalence of CHD has also increased over the past 40 years, attributable to improved diagnosis and interventional developments.3 Meanwhile, reported global CHD-related mortality has decreased substantially, reflecting the progress of treatment and increasing evidence foundation.3,4

Pathologically, SHDs arise from anatomical abnormalities in the structure of the heart, leading to various cardiac conditions such as pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, and arrhythmias.5 Such complex conditions have highlighted the growing significance of anatomy-based interventional treatments, such as transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) and drive the development of anatomy-based treatments with techniques and devices.6 The emerging interventional procedures are established as equally critical as surgical approaches in disease management.7 Furthermore, TAVR volumes exceeded isolated surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) by 2016 and all SAVR by 2019.8 With the ongoing clinical investigations into tricuspid TEER9,10 and transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR),11 this field is poised for continued expansion in the future.12 Emerging basic and translational research in CHD, like the development of biodegradable materials, has the potential to transform pediatric cardiovascular medicine.13 These innovations are expected to expand the scope of interventional treatments for CHD. Consequently, SHD has increasingly become a distinct topic in academic conferences and forums, and dedicated SHD centers are established in hospitals worldwide for enhanced treatment and management.14 However, the extent to which this shift is reflected in the clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) and the evolution of the underlying evidence are unclear.

In the United States, American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) have developed a series of CPGs covering the full spectrum of cardiovascular diseases and procedures.15 Similarly, European Society of Cardiology (ESC), as the largest cardiovascular society in Europe, has issued CPGs that have had a profound impact on clinical practice in many regions worldwide.16 By contrast, Chinese (CHN) guidelines and consensus are often published in various journals by different medical associations and play a crucial role in guiding clinical practice in China.17 Despite the widespread use of evidence-based methods in guideline development, strategies for managing certain diseases could vary across countries and regions, tailored to guide management within specific populations.18,19 Collectively, guidelines from these regions provide the most comprehensive reflection of progress and evidence base in this field.

This study aims to examine and compare guidelines with regard to SHDs from different regions over the past 20 years, depicting the current distribution of class of recommendation (COR) and the supporting level of evidence (LOE) as well as the temporal changes. We investigate these guidelines by diseases (VHD and CHD) and their subspecialties to explore potential differences. Additionally, we analyze the evolution of intervention and surgical recommendations across guidelines to present the updates in mainstream treatments. It will demonstrate a clear picture of recommendations and the underlying evidence in this rapidly developing field and provide valuable guidance for future research directions.

Results

Overall characteristics of guideline recommendations

Our analysis encompassed a total of 1,867 recommendations from ACC/AHA, ESC, and CHN guidelines. Current guidelines comprised 884 recommendations: 180 (20.4%) from CHN guidelines (5 documents, 2016–2023), 451 (51.0%) from ACC/AHA guidelines (2 documents, 2018, 2020), and 253 (28.6%) from ESC guidelines (2 documents, 2020, 2021). Overall, approximately half were categorized as strong recommendations under Class I and III, with a minority supported by LOE A (Table 1). Compared with the prior guidelines, the percentage of Class III decreased, with an increase shown in Class II. The proportion of LOE A/B recommendations elevated, alongside the substantial drop in LOE C. From the prior ones to the current, therapeutic recommendations continued to dominate, comprising two-thirds or higher, followed by diagnostic ones. When analyzed by regions (ACC/AHA and ESC), similar results were observed. Comparison of recommendations by classes and evidence levels across guidelines, diseases, and subspecialties

In current guidelines, the distribution of recommendations varied significantly across regions. CHN guidelines demonstrated the highest proportion of I-A and II-A recommendations. This contrasted with the lower proportions of <3% in ESC and ACC/AHA guidelines. In comparison, ACC/AHA guidelines had the highest proportion of I-B and II-B recommendations, with each around 25%, while more than three-quarters of recommendations were designated as I-C and II-C in ESC guidelines (Figure 1; Figure S1). When examining the changes longitudinally, both ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines exhibited a marked shift toward higher evidence quality. ACC/AHA guidelines demonstrated a marked upward shift in I-A, II-A, I-B, and II-B recommendations and a downward shift in I-C and II-C. A parallel but less substantial shift was seen within the ESC guidelines. Further, the distribution of recommendations in the current guidelines was presented by diseases, as shown in Figure 2. For CHD, there was few recommendations supported by LOE A in ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines, while CHN guidelines included some. Comparative analysis of current guidelines by subspecialties for each disease

Moreover, we examined the LOE A recommendations overall and for each COR by subspecialty in all current guidelines. The proportion of recommendations classified as LOE A varied significantly across subspecialties of VHD. In the ACC/AHA guidelines, it ranged from 0% for tricuspid valve diseases (TVDs) and mixed valve diseases to 15.3% for aortic valve diseases (AVDs). For the ESC guidelines, the range was from 0% for both mitral valve diseases (MVDs) and TVDs to 18.2% for general principles. However, in CHN guidelines, the variation was from 0% for prosthetic valves management to 17.1% for AVDs, with a higher proportion of LOE A for Class I recommendations in certain subspecialties. It highlighted the disparities in high-quality evidence across different specialized areas and guidelines (Figure 3A). Regarding CHD, due to the lack of LOE A recommendations for ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines, it was totally absent in major subspecialties like shunt lesions and right-sided lesions. The evolving trend of intervention and surgery recommendations across guidelines

To illustrate the impact of technological advancements on treatment, we calculated the proportions of intervention and surgery in total recommendations separately, and their evolving trend from the prior guidelines to the current was assessed. As for VHD, the proportion of interventional recommendations in ESC guideline increased from 19.8% to 27.1%, while that of surgical recommendations showed a decrease. ACC/AHA guidelines exhibited similar patterns, though the changes were less pronounced. A similar trend was observed for CHD in ESC guidelines, with doubled interventional therapy recommendations and dramatically dropped surgical recommendations, while ACC/AHA guidelines showed slight increases in both (Figure 4; Table S3). Similar literature analyses were performed for surgery and intervention (Figures S5 and S6). It showed that the total publications and RCTs had a larger increase in intervention than surgery in both AVDs and MVDs. The figure of surgery in the shunt lesions remained stable while that of interventional treatments increased substantially. Discussion

This study provides a systematic comparison of SHDs guidelines across ACC/AHA, ESC, and CHN guidelines, integrating cross-sectional, longitudinal, and disease-specific analyses. Our comprehensive analysis of 884 recommendations revealed that strong recommendations (Class I and III) predominate across current guidelines, yet most lack high-quality supporting evidence. The limitation has not substantially improved over the past two decades in ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines, while Chinese guidelines lack longitudinal comparison data.

Our analysis demonstrates a persistent evidence-practice gap, with strong recommendations comprising nearly half of all current guidelines (53.2%) while most lacking high-quality supporting evidence. The predominance of recommendations without LOE A support reflects inherent challenges in conducting high-quality randomized controlled trials in structural heart diseases, including patient heterogeneity, ethical constraints, and long-term follow-up requirements.20 The complexity of SHD management has further complicated guideline development. Compared to prior guidelines, the proportion of strong recommendations has declined while those requiring clinical judgment (Class II) have increased across both VHD and CHD domains. This shift reflects the evolving technological landscape, where innovations like TAVR have transformed treatment paradigms through iterative device improvements and refined patient selection criteria.21 In aortic valve replacement, operators should consider multiple patient-specific factors, including the choice between surgical and interventional approaches as well as valve type selection, underscoring the importance of personalized treatment strategies.22

Longitudinal trends demonstrated substantial improvement in ACC/AHA guidelines, with significant increases in LOE B recommendations and corresponding decreases in LOE C recommendations. This improvement may reflect methodological enhancements introduced in 2011 that better integrated high-quality observational studies when RCT data were limited.23,24,25 In contrast, ESC guidelines show more modest improvement, with LOE C recommendations still exceeding two-thirds of total recommendations, partially reflecting the continued predominant role of clinical experience in European practice.26 Chinese guideline longitudinal trends cannot be systematically evaluated due to lacking historical versions, reflecting relative lag in Chinese guideline development.

Regional differences in evidence evaluation reveal significant methodological disparities that may overshadow true evidence variations. Current ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines show that only a minority of Class I recommendations are supported by LOE A, consistent with previous studies.27 This contrasts with Chinese guidelines, which demonstrate a relatively higher proportion of LOE A supporting Class I recommendations. However, recent comprehensive analyses of Chinese cardiovascular guidelines suggest that the higher LOE A proportion may reflect differences in evidence evaluation methods and inconsistent standards rather than superior evidence quality.28,29,30 Wang et al. found that 22.7% of recommendations in Chinese cardiovascular guidelines were classified as LOE A, significantly higher than the 9.0% reported in ACC/AHA guidelines.29 However, their detailed study revealed only 5.5% of supporting evidence came from Chinese RCTs, with many guidelines lacking appropriate reference citations and unclear evaluation standards.29 These apparent evidence quality differences likely reflect systematic methodological disparities in guideline development. Similar methodological differences have been documented in international guideline comparisons, where identical clinical evidence led to divergent recommendations due to different evidence evaluation standards, committee composition, and voting mechanisms.30 The higher LOE A proportions in Chinese guidelines may reflect more lenient evidence grading standards and lack of transparent methodological frameworks rather than research superiority. Similarly, the predominance of LOE C recommendations in ESC guidelines may reflect stricter evidence evaluation standards and the traditional role of expert opinion in European practice. The persistent gap between recommendation strength and supporting evidence quality emphasizes the importance of establishing standardized, transparent evidence evaluation systems and the urgent need to narrow evidence gaps through innovative research methods and international collaboration. Additionally, the small sample sizes of recommendations may result in less reliable proportion estimates, which possibly explained the observed regional differences to some extent. For instance, the apparently higher proportion of LOE A recommendations in Chinese VHD guidelines than other regions was partially influenced by the limited number of recommendations (Figure 3).

Beyond regional variations, certain subspecialties face universal evidence challenges that transcend geographic boundaries. Our subspecialty analysis identified widespread scarcity of high-quality evidence across VHD and CHD recommendations, with systematic differences among disease categories. High-quality evidence for TVDs is almost completely absent in ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines, while Chinese guidelines even lack recommendations in this area. Recommendations for complex congenital lesions and prosthetic valve management consistently show evidence gaps across all three regions, indicating universal challenges rather than region-specific limitations. The relatively higher proportion of LOE A recommendations in Chinese CHD guidelines may be attributed to China’s large CHD patient population, providing a foundation for large-scale clinical trials.31,32 These subspecialty evidence quality differences provide important reference for future research priority setting.

Regional differences in therapeutic approach evolution reflect different healthcare systems’ adaptation to technological advances. The pronounced shift toward interventional therapies in ESC guidelines, particularly evident in CHD management, represents a fundamental transformation in treatment paradigms and demonstrates a more pronounced trend compared to other regions.21,33 ACC/AHA guidelines showed relatively modest changes, suggesting more conservative technology adoption patterns potentially influenced by regulatory frameworks, healthcare system structures, and clinical practice patterns.34

In conclusion, existing SHD guidelines show strong COR I and III recommendations lacking robust evidence, indicating limited progress in high-level evidence despite improved overall quality. Moreover, disparities in evidence distribution across regions are apparent in the current guidelines, suggesting clinical practice variations across different healthcare systems. Addressing these challenges requires future guideline development to commit to methodological standardization, promote international cooperation to bridge regional gaps, and prioritize high-quality research in subspecialty areas with significant evidence gaps.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, insufficient methodological transparency in Chinese cardiovascular guideline development processes without a core centralized issuing body and inconsistent evidence evaluation standards among different guidelines may affect the reliability of LOE grading and accuracy of international comparisons. Second, the merging of Class IIa and IIb recommendations may have masked important distinctions in recommendation strength within the uncertain recommendation category. This consolidation, while following established practices in previous comparative guideline studies,27 potentially obscures the proportion of weaker versus stronger uncertain recommendations across guidelines. Third, the absence of prior versions of Chinese guidelines prevented temporal analysis of practice evidence patterns. Fourth, differences in regional guideline update cycles created temporal inconsistencies that may bias cross-regional comparison results, particularly in rapidly evolving areas such as transcatheter interventions. Fifth, while our bibliometric analysis broadly quantified research output trends, it could not assess literature quality and impact, and publication volume alone may not adequately reflect evidence strength and quality. Last, how the technological advances shape the recommendations is not analyzed. Future research could employ content analysis methods to assess in detail how these technological advances influence guideline recommendation development and updates, which would provide a more comprehensive perspective on understanding technology-driven changes in modern SHD management.

Experimental model and study participant details

We focused on VHD and CHD to provide a more straightforward representation of the relevant field. Data were extracted and analyzed based on guidelines from the ACC/AHA, ESC, and CHN clinical guidelines which were published between June 1, 2004 to June 1, 2024. The ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines were retrieved from their respective websites (https://www.acc.org/guidelines#doctype; https://www.escardio.org/Guidelines/Clinical-Practice-Guidelines) using the keywords/topics “valvular heart diseases” and “congenital heart diseases”, while CHN guidelines, often published in academic journals, were sourced through a systematic search of the Chinese Biomedical Database (SinoMed, http://www.sinomed.ac.cn/) and the Wanfang Med Database (http://med.wanfangdata.com.cn/). Details of the search keywords and strategies are provided in Table S4. To ensure consistency and comparability, only guidelines classified by the Class of Recommendation (COR, including Class I/II/III) and Level of Evidence (LOE, including LOE A/B/C) were included in the main analysis. Expert consensus documents, focused updates, interpretation, translation or analysis of guidelines were excluded. The document screening process is illustrated in Figure S7. To assess the effects of non-COR/LOE classification system on the results, a sensitivity analysis by including one document using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria was performed, where the GRADE classification was converted into LOE/COR classification (details shown in Table S5). For ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines, the most recent version was defined as the “current version”, while the previous one was considered the “prior version”. For CHN guidelines, only the current version was available and analyzed. Meanwhile, sensitivity analyses across all versions of ACC/AHA and ESC guideline documents were performed to evaluate the robustness of the overall longitudinal trend. The results of all sensitivity analyses are shown in the supplemental materials (see Figures S8 and S9, and Tables S6–S8).

In addition, a PubMed search was conducted to quantify publications related to VHD and CHD between 2008 and 2023, corresponding to the time span between the earliest and the most recent guideline to analyze the evidence landscape. The search strategies (Table S9) were designed to capture all types of studies, RCTs and studies specifically focusing on surgical and interventional approaches respectively. A single reviewer (Y.Y.) screened the documents, with verification by two additional reviewers (A.X. and Y.T.). Specific guideline titles and DOI information are provided in Table S10. The definition of COR and LOE in each guideline series are shown in Table S11.

Method details

Data extraction

All recommendations from the guidelines of three regions (ACC/AHA, ESC, and CHN) were extracted and analyzed by COR and LOE. They were classified by specialty (i.e., VHD and CHD) and further divided by subspecialty (Table S12). In addition, each recommendation was categorized into one of four classifications (therapeutic, diagnostic, follow-up, or others) according to its function. Therapeutic and diagnostic recommendations were further subdivided by its action, with the definitions for each category provided in Table S13. As some recommendations involved multiple actions in a single item, it was counted in all relevant categories. All three authors independently quantified and classified the recommendations, and any discrepancies were discussed with a senior author (Chuangshi Wang.), with unresolved issues referred to another senior author (X.P.).

Quantification and statistical analysis

For ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines, a longitudinal comparison was made between the current and prior versions to evaluate trends in the evolution of recommendations. Heat maps were utilized to illustrate changes in COR and LOE over time. For each disease, a cross-sectional comparison of current guidelines from three regions was performed to highlight differences in recommendations and the underlying evidence. Stacked percentage charts were used to compare the evidence structure for CHD and VHD. We also reported the proportion of recommendations supported by LOE A for each subspecialty area overall and by COR. Since Class II recommendation highlights areas with conflicting evidence, indicating a need for further research, we calculated the proportion of Class II for each subspecialty. Subspecialties with more than 50% Class II were identified as requiring critical attention. Moreover, to better understand the impact of technological advancements on treatment, line plots were used to compare the shifts in the proportion of surgical and interventional recommendations from the prior version to the current ones for each disease among the CHN, ACC/AHA, and ESC guidelines.

Categorical variables (COR, LOE, and classification categories) were presented as counts with percentages. Comparisons between prior and current guidelines within regions and across ACC/AHA, ESC, and CHN guidelines were performed using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by including additional guideline versions (ACC/AHA and ESC VHD guidelines) or incorporating GRADE-based recommendations (CHN guidelines). Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare recommendation distributions across different guideline versions and assess the robustness of the main findings. All statistical tests were 2-sided and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and RStudio (version 2024.04.2+764) software.
