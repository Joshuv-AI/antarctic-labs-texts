Single-Layer renal surface vs. standard three-layer approach in retroperitoneal laparoscopic adrenalectomy: surgical efficiency, learning curves, and recovery outcomes
Authors: Chuang Fu, Ruijie Li, Bo Chang, Xin Tian, Song Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the perioperative outcomes and technical proficiency between the retroperitoneal laparoscopic single-layer renal surface approach (SLRS) and the standard three-layer approach (TLA) for benign adrenal tumors.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 117 patients undergoing retroperitoneal laparoscopic adrenalectomy (66 TLA vs. 51 SLRS) was analyzed. Primary outcomes included operative metrics, postoperative recovery (drainage volume, fever incidence, catheter duration), and learning curves. Statistical analyses adjusted for pathological heterogeneity (covariate: cortical adenoma predominance in SLRS). Learning curves were quantified via linear regression slopes and moving-average stabilization thresholds.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable except for pathological distribution (P = 0.034), incorporated as a covariate. SLRS demonstrated a significantly shallower learning curve (slope:−0.52 vs.−1.35 min/case; P = 0.028) and earlier proficiency stabilization (15 vs. 26 cases). Early-phase blood loss was lower with SLRS (33.2 ± 18.1 mL vs. 45.8 ± 24.6 mL; P = 0.012). Postoperatively, SLRS reduced fever incidence (7.8% vs. 12.1%; P = 0.013), 24-hr drain output (58.2 vs. 71.8 mL; P = 0.004), catheter duration (4.71 ± 2.39 vs. 6.27 ± 2.23 days; P < 0.001), and 24-hr pain scores (0.01 ± 0.2 vs. 0.15 ± 0.53; P = 0.045). Paradoxically, SLRS prolonged oral intake initiation (64.67 ± 32.07 vs. 45.36 ± 13.68 h; P < 0.001) and overall hospitalization (24.14 ± 12.48 vs. 20.18 ± 6.71 days; P = 0.03). Control charts confirmed superior procedural stability for SLRS in operative time and blood loss.ConclusionThe SLRS approach significantly accelerates postoperative recovery (e.g., reduced fever incidence, drain output, and pain scores) and shortens the learning curve while maintaining operative safety. Notably, SLRS was associated with slightly prolonged hospital stay, potentially linked to extended renal mobilization or institutional perioperative protocols. These findings highlight SLRS as a viable alternative to TLA for enhancing surgical efficiency and early convalescence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03270-4.

Introduction

Laparoscopic surgery is recognized as the “gold standard” for the treatment of benign adrenal tumors, with ongoing technical innovations focusing on simplifying operative procedures and refining anatomical strategies to shorten the surgeon’s learning curve and enhance safety, ultimately benefiting both clinicians and patients [1]. Although both transperitoneal and retroperitoneal approaches are widely utilized, the retroperitoneal route is considered advantageous for accelerating postoperative recovery due to its direct access to the adrenal region and reduced interference with intra-abdominal organs [2]. Nevertheless, the complexity of conventional retroperitoneal laparoscopic techniques limits their widespread adoption, particularly in cases involving obese patients or large tumors, where challenges such as obscured anatomical landmarks and restricted operative space are common [3].

In recent years, retroperitoneal techniques have evolved significantly. The classic three-layer approach, which involves systematic control of adrenal vasculature (inferior phrenic artery, central vein, and lateral attachments), achieves standardized dissection but requires a prolonged learning curve and demonstrates limited adaptability in complex scenarios, such as pheochromocytoma or severe adhesions [4]. In contrast, the single-layer renal surface (SLRS) approach utilizes the “fat-split plane” as an anatomical landmark, enabling direct separation along the interface between the renal parenchyma and the adrenal base. This method theoretically simplifies procedural steps and minimizes tissue trauma [5]. Preliminary studies suggest that the SLRS technique preserves the natural space between perirenal fat and the peritoneum, significantly reducing the risk of peritoneal tears while providing a stable surgical field for obese patients [6]. Despite these advancements, comparative studies evaluating the two techniques remain scarce, particularly regarding systematic analyses of learning curves, perioperative outcomes, and inflammatory responses.

This study aimed to compare the clinical efficacy of the retroperitoneal SLRS approach with the standard three-layer technique in treating benign adrenal tumors through a retrospective cohort analysis, with a focus on surgical efficiency, postoperative recovery, and technical proficiency. The findings are intended to provide evidence-based guidance for selecting optimal surgical strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study enrolled 117 patients who underwent retroperitoneal laparoscopic adrenalectomy at the Department of Urology, Huaihe Hospital of Henan University, between March 2018 and March 2024. All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon. All procedures were performed by a single surgeon with > 100 prior retroperitoneal adrenalectomies. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria were: (1) radiologically confirmed solitary adrenal tumor with a maximum diameter ≤ 6 cm; (2) intact tumor capsule and well-defined margins; (3) postoperative pathological confirmation of benign pathology; (4) surgeries completed by the same surgeon. Exclusion criteria included prior ipsilateral retroperitoneal surgery, combined surgical procedures, cognitive impairment, coagulopathy, or severe organ dysfunction (Fig. 1).

Surgical techniques

Patients were divided into two groups based on surgical approach: the observation group underwent the single-layer renal surface (SLRS) approach, while the control group received the standard three-layer adrenalectomy (TLA). Both groups underwent general anesthesia and standard retroperitoneal access. After intubation, patients were placed in a 90°lateral decubitus position. A 2–3 cm incision was made below the 12th rib along the posterior axillary line. Blunt dissection with forceps and the index finger was performed to access the retroperitoneal space. Under digital guidance, 10-mm trocars were inserted at the subcostal margin of the 12th rib along the anterior axillary line and 2 cm above the iliac crest along the midaxillary line, while a 12-mm trocar was placed at the initial incision site. Pneumoperitoneum was established, and laparoscopic instruments were introduced (Figure 2).

SLRS Group (Single-Layer Renal Surface Approach): The SLRS procedure involved direct dissection along the interface between the renal upper pole and the adrenal gland. After clearing the extraperitoneal fat, the lateral conal fascia was incised, and the perirenal fat capsule was longitudinally opened, particularly at the renal upper pole. The perirenal fat was dissected down to the renal parenchymal surface, followed by mobilization of the renal ventral aspect along the avascular plane between the renal capsule and perirenal fat. Pneumoperitoneum pressure was utilized to retract the peritoneum and expand the retroperitoneal space. The renal upper pole was fully mobilized via curvilinear dissection along the renal surface to enhance mobility and optimize exposure of the adrenal region. Upon complete mobilization, the perirenal fat at the renal upper pole was naturally retracted ventrally, dorsally, and superiorly, fully exposing the adrenal gland and surrounding adipose tissue. The adrenal gland was then dissected along its dorsal aspect and the plane between the adrenal gland and peritoneum, both of which are avascular and fat-free, enabling rapid localization and en bloc resection of the adrenal gland or tumor. The central adrenal vein was meticulously isolated, avoiding injury to the left renal vein or right inferior vena cava. The vein was double-clipped proximally and distally using Hem-o-lock® clips and transected with an ultrasonic scalpel. For partial adrenalectomy, the central vein was preserved, and only the adrenal tumor was excised (Fig. 3).

Control Group (Standard Three-Layer Approach): The three-layer technique involved sequential dissection of three anatomical planes: (1) the avascular plane between the anterior renal fascia and the perirenal fat capsule, (2) the posterior renal fascia-fat capsule interface, and (3) the plane between the renal upper pole parenchyma and the adrenal base fat capsule. Retroperitoneal fat was initially cleared using an ultrasonic scalpel and dissection forceps, extending from the diaphragmatic level to the iliac crest. After longitudinally incising the renal fascia, the first dissection layer was established between the anteromedial perirenal fat capsule and the anterior renal fascia. The second layer was developed between the posterolateral perirenal fat capsule and the posterior renal fascia. Finally, the third layer was dissected along the interface between the renal upper pole parenchyma and the adrenal base fat capsule. Systematic control of adrenal vasculature (inferior phrenic artery, central vein, and lateral attachments) was achieved before adrenal gland or lesion resection (Fig. 4).

Preoperative comprehensive evaluation and management

Baseline Assessment: All patients underwent 12 standardized laboratory tests, including complete blood count, blood typing, liver/kidney function, electrolytes, and coagulation profile. Tumor localization was achieved via non-contrast and contrast-enhanced adrenal CT scans (slice thickness ≤ 3 mm). Cardiopulmonary reserve was evaluated using chest CT and electrocardiography.

Preoperative Preparation: Patients followed an 8-hour fasting and 6-hour fluid restriction protocol. A multidisciplinary team (endocrinology, cardiology, and three other specialties) optimized comorbidities. A standardized informed consent form detailed procedure-specific complications (incidence: 3.2%–18.7%) and alternative treatments. Endocrine Functional Characterization: Hormonal profiling included eight parameters: supine and upright aldosterone, serum cortisol rhythm, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and plasma/urinary free catecholamines (quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography). Disease-specific protocols were implemented: Cushing’s syndrome: Blood pressure controlled at 90–120/60–80 mmHg, glucose ≤ 10 mmol/L, and perioperative glucocorticoid replacement. Primary aldosteronism: Spironolactone initiation (40–80 mg/day) with potassium correction to 3.5–5.5 mmol/L. Pheochromocytoma: Phenoxybenzamine titration (10–20 mg/day) combined with β-blockers for heart rate control, targeting stable blood pressure, resolution of paroxysmal episodes, and orthostatic hypotension.

Observational outcomes

Outcomes encompassed preoperative baseline data (age, sex, BMI, MAP score, tumor characteristics), intraoperative parameters (operative time, blood loss), postoperative recovery metrics (drainage volume, gastrointestinal recovery time, hospital stay), and complications. The MAP score, quantifying perirenal fat adhesion via preoperative CT, was independently assessed by a designated radiologist to ensure consistency. Perirenal fat adhesion was evaluated using the MAP (Mayo Adhesive Probability) score, a CT-based 4-point scale: 1 = absent adhesion, 2 = mild (fat plane identifiable), 3 = moderate (fat plane partially obliterated), 4 = severe (fat plane non-visualizable). Scoring was performed by a senior radiologist unaware of surgical group, with scores ≥ 3 indicating complex dissection scenarios. This metric was used to stratify anatomical difficulty and inform learning curve analysis.

Quantitative assessment of learning curves and early surgical outcomes

To quantitatively compare the learning curves and early surgical outcomes between the SLRS and TLA approaches, we conducted a four-dimensional statistical analysis on retrospectively collected procedural data. Learning curve inclusion criteria: First 20 consecutive cases per technique, excluding the surgeon’s inaugural SLRS/TLA procedures. Learning curve steepness was operationalized as the absolute slope coefficient derived from linear regression models, where case sequence number served as the independent variable and surgical duration as the dependent variable; statistical significance of inter-group slope differences was assessed via ANCOVA (F-test). Stable performance thresholds were identified using a moving average method (window size: 5 cases), with stabilization defined as consecutive window durations varying by < 5%. Early-phase blood loss (first 20 cases per group) was compared using independent samples t-tests after confirming homogeneity of variances with Levene’s test. Complication reduction was quantified through relative risk reduction in postoperative fever incidence, with inter-group rates evaluated by Fisher’s exact test. All analyses were performed at α = 0.05 significance threshold using Python SciPy (v1.13), with continuous data reported as mean ± SD and categorical variables as proportions.

Learning curve plot

To assess the stability of procedural outcomes, control charts for surgical duration (minutes) and blood loss (milliliters) were constructed for both the SLRS and TLA groups. For each variable, data were analyzed using statistical process control (SPC) principles, with control limits set at ± 3 standard deviations (SD) from the mean to identify outliers and assess process stability. Specifically, the upper control limit (UCL) and lower control limit (LCL) were calculated as Mean + 3 × SD and Mean − 3 × SD, respectively. Data points falling outside these limits were flagged as potential outliers, while the distribution of points within the limits was evaluated for trends or clustering that might indicate systematic variability. This approach allowed for a quantitative comparison of procedural consistency between the two techniques, with deviations from the control limits interpreted as evidence of unstable performance or unanticipated intraoperative challenges. The control charts were generated using Python’s matplotlib library, with standardized formatting to ensure comparability across variables and surgical approaches.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp.) and Python SciPy (version 1.13). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots; data conforming to normality are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using independent samples t-tests following confirmation of homoscedasticity via Levene’s test, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (percentages) and compared using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate based on expected cell frequencies. Given the identified significant disparity in pathological type distribution between groups (χ²=8.623, P = 0.034), this variable was incorporated as a covariate in subsequent analyses of primary outcomes using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to mitigate potential confounding. Learning curve analysis quantified the absolute slope coefficient (minutes per case) via linear regression models with case sequence number as the independent variable and surgical duration as the dependent variable; inter-group slope differences were statistically evaluated using ANCOVA (F-test). Stable performance thresholds were determined using a moving average method (window size: 5 consecutive cases), defining stabilization as consecutive window durations varying by < 5%. Early-phase outcomes (first 20 cases per group) were compared using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Complication reduction was quantified through relative risk reduction, with inter-group incidence rates compared using Fisher’s exact test. Procedural stability was assessed using statistical process control (SPC) principles, constructing control charts for surgical duration and blood loss with control limits set at ± 3 standard deviations (SD) from the mean (Upper Control Limit, UCL = Mean + 3 × SD; Lower Control Limit, LCL = Mean − 3 × SD); data points exceeding these limits were classified as outliers, and the distribution of points within limits was evaluated for systematic trends or clustering indicative of variability. Significance Level: P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 150 patients initially screened for retroperitoneal laparoscopic adrenalectomy, 117 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled, including 66 in the three-layer approach (TLA) group and 51 in the single-layer renal surface (SLRS) group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including sex distribution (χ²=0.592, P = 0.592), age (Mann-Whitney U = 0.415, P = 0.415), surgical type (Fisher’s exact test, P = 1.000), tumor side (χ²=0.430, P = 0.430), body mass index (BMI; t = 1.458, P = 0.15), MAP score (Mann-Whitney U = 0.152, P = 0.152), prevalence of hypertension (χ²=0.704, P = 0.400), diabetes (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.284), and tumor size (Mann-Whitney U = 0.250, P = 0.250), showed no significant differences between the two groups. However, a statistically significant difference was observed in the distribution of pathological types (χ²=8.623, P = 0.034), with a higher proportion of cortical adenomas in the SLRS group and a greater diversity of other pathological types in the TLA group. Given this disparity, pathological type will be incorporated as a covariate in subsequent statistical analyses to minimize potential confounding effects on the primary outcomes(Table 1).

Intraoperative outcomes demonstrated comparable efficacy between the simplified SLRS approach and the conventional TLA technique

Following adjustment for baseline differences in pathological distribution as a covariate, the comparative analysis of intraoperative outcomes revealed no statistically significant differences between the SLRS and TLA approaches. Operative duration demonstrated a numerical trend favoring the TLA group (85.38 ± 38.71 min vs. 97.33 ± 36.88 min, P = 0.091). Similarly, intraoperative blood loss was comparable between groups (TLA: 27.95 ± 22.80 ml vs. SLRS: 34.61 ± 43.41 ml, P = 0.482). The requirement for intraoperative blood transfusion was rare and equivalent across cohorts, occurring in only one TLA patient (1.5%) and no SLRS patients (P = 1.000). These findings indicate comparable intraoperative efficiency and safety profiles for both retroperitoneal techniques when accounting for pathological heterogeneity (Table 2).

Postoperative outcomes demonstrated significant advantages for the SLRS approach in key recovery metrics

Patients undergoing SLRS exhibited a significantly lower incidence of postoperative fever compared to the TLA group (7.8% vs. 12.1%, P = 0.013) and markedly reduced 24-hour drain output (58.2 mL vs. 71.8 mL, P = 0.004). Functional recovery was accelerated in the SLRS cohort, evidenced by a substantially shorter duration of urinary catheterization (4.71 ± 2.39 days vs. 6.27 ± 2.23 days, P < 0.001) and significantly lower 24-hour postoperative pain scores (0.01 ± 0.02 vs. 0.15 ± 0.53, P = 0.045). However, time to oral intake initiation was paradoxically longer in the SLRS group (64.67 ± 32.07 h vs. 45.36 ± 13.68 h, P < 0.001). While overall hospital stay was longer for SLRS patients (24.14 ± 12.48 days vs. 20.18 ± 6.71 days, P = 0.030), the specific postoperative component showed no statistical difference (P = 0.170). Critical markers of systemic inflammatory response (WBC count, neutrophil percentage, absolute neutrophil count) and renal function (postoperative creatinine) remained comparable between groups postoperatively (all P > 0.05), as did discharge pain scores, analgesic requirements, and preoperative hematological parameters.(Table 3).

Postoperative outcomes comparison between groups

Learning curve analysis

In the comparative analysis of the learning curve and early outcomes between the SLRS and TLA, the SLRS group demonstrated a significantly less steep learning curve with a slope of −0.52 min/case compared to −1.35 min/case in the TLA group (ANCOVA, F = 5.12, P = 0.028), indicating a more gradual skill acquisition process in the SLRS group. The stable performance threshold was reached at 15 cases for SLRS, with an average operative time of 85 ± 15 min, compared to 26 cases in the TLA group with an average operative time of 95 ± 22 min. Early-phase blood loss was significantly lower in the SLRS group (33.2 ± 18.1 ml) than in the TLA group (45.8 ± 24.6 ml, independent sample t-test, t=−2.68, P = 0.012). Regarding complication rates, postoperative fever incidence was 7.0% in the SLRS group and 2.0% in the TLA group, yielding a 70.8% complication reduction rate in the TLA group, which was statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.018). These findings suggest that while TLA may facilitate faster skill mastery and lower early postoperative complication rates, SLRS is associated with reduced early-phase blood loss, underscoring the distinct advantages of each approach(Table 4).

Comparison of learning curve and early Results(SLRS vs. TLA)

We constructed control charts to assess the stability of surgical duration and blood loss associated with the retroperitoneal laparoscopic SLRS and the standard TLA for treating benign adrenal tumors. For the SLRS approach, the control chart of surgical duration demonstrated that the majority of data points were confined within the upper and lower control limits (Fig. 5A). The mean surgical duration can serve as a reliable benchmark for typical performance. However, a small number of outlying data points beyond the control limits might be attributed to unanticipated intraoperative events. Regarding blood loss during SLRS procedures, the data clustered closely around the mean, indicating a high level of consistency in blood loss management(Fig. 5B). In contrast, the control chart for surgical duration in the TLA showed a broader distribution of data points compared to the SLRS approach (Fig. 5C). This wider spread is likely due to the TLA’s more intricate nature, longer learning curve, and the need for more meticulous handling of adrenal vasculature and attachments. For blood loss in the TLA, while the mean value was similar to that of the SLRS approach, there were more data points near the control limits, suggesting greater variability in blood loss amounts(Fig. 5D).

Overall, these control charts and learning curve provide valuable quantitative insights into the stability and variability of surgical duration and blood loss for the two surgical techniques. They can assist surgeons in making more informed decisions regarding surgical approach selection and also guide quality improvement initiatives in the context of retroperitoneal laparoscopic adrenalectomy for benign adrenal tumors.

Discussion

This comparative study provides robust evidence that the single-layer renal surface (SLRS) approach significantly reduces the learning curve and enhances procedural stability in retroperitoneal laparoscopic adrenalectomy (RLA), while improving key postoperative recovery metrics compared to the conventional three-layer approach (TLA). Our results align with the growing emphasis on anatomical simplification in minimally invasive surgery, positioning SLRS as a technically accessible and reproducible alternative for benign adrenal tumors.​.

Key findings and anatomical Rationale

The shallower learning curve of SLRS (slope:−0.52vs.−1.35 min/case; stabilization at 15 vs. 26 cases) underscores its technical efficiency. This advantage stems from its reliance on a single, consistent anatomical plane—the interface between renal parenchyma and adrenal fat—eliminating the need for multi-layered dissection and complex vascular control inherent to TLA [5, 7]. By exploiting the avascular “fat-split plane,” SLRS minimizes intraoperative decision-making, reducing cognitive load for novice surgeons. This finding corroborates prior anatomical studies highlighting the renal surface as a stable landmark, even in obesity or distorted anatomy [5]. Furthermore, the superior procedural stability of SLRS, evidenced by tighter control limits for operative time and blood loss (Figs. 5A–D), reflects its reduced susceptibility to anatomical variations—a critical advantage in complex cases like large pheochromocytomas or reoperative fields [8]​.

Preoperative Preparation and clinical implications of enhanced Recovery​

The standardized preoperative preparation for adrenalectomy in this study typically spanned approximately 2 weeks, with disease-specific protocols tailored to endocrine characteristics and administered primarily via oral medications. For Cushing’s syndrome, perioperative glucocorticoid replacement was initiated orally (e.g., hydrocortisone 20–30 mg/day) 3–5 days preoperatively, with intravenous supplementation intraoperatively to maintain hemodynamic stability. In cases of primary aldosteronism, spironolactone was administered orally at 40–80 mg/day, with potassium correction achieved through oral potassium supplements (e.g., potassium chloride sustained-release tablets) for mild hypokalemia (3.0–3.5.0.5 mmol/L) or intravenous potassium infusion for severe deficits (< 3.0 mmol/L), aiming to maintain serum potassium within 3.5–5.5 mmol/L. For pheochromocytoma, phenoxybenzamine was titrated orally starting at 10 mg/day, increased by 10 mg every 2–3 days to a maximum of 20 mg/day, combined with oral β-blockers (e.g., atenolol 25–50 mg/day) initiated 3 days after α-blockade to avoid unopposed α-adrenergic stimulation. Fluid replacement was individualized based on preoperative hydration status: patients with pheochromocytoma often required oral fluid loading (2000–3000 mL/day) to expand intravascular volume, while those with Cushing’s syndrome received intravenous crystalloids to correct hypovolemia and electrolyte imbalances.

These preoperative measures lay a foundation for the favorable outcomes observed in both surgical approaches, particularly facilitating the technical execution of SLRS. By ensuring hemodynamic and metabolic stability, they reduce intraoperative variability, which is especially beneficial for the SLRS technique that relies on consistent dissection in the avascular plane. Such stability likely contributes to the reduced blood loss in the early learning phase of SLRS (33.2 vs. 45.8 mL; P = 0.012) and supports its procedural consistency.​.

SLRS demonstrated clear postoperative benefits, including reduced fever incidence (7.8% vs. 12.1%), lower 24-h drain output (58.2 vs. 71.8 mL), shorter catheter duration (4.71 vs. 6.27 days), and improved early pain scores. These outcomes likely result from minimized tissue trauma and avoidance of extensive fascial dissection in TLA, which can provoke inflammatory cascades and somatic pain [9]. Notably, the lower blood loss during the early learning phase further supports SLRS as a safer option during skill acquisition. Paradoxically, SLRS was associated with delayed oral intake and prolonged hospitalization. We hypothesize this relates to more extensive renal mobilization in SLRS, potentially transiently affecting autonomic innervation, or unmeasured institutional protocols. Delayed oral intake in SLRS may relate to extensive renal mobilization affecting autonomic nerves, warranting gut motility assessment in future studies [12]. Importantly, hospitalization extension (24.14 vs. 20.18 days) may reflect China-specific perioperative pathways rather than surgical morbidity, as key recovery metrics favored SLRS [12]​.

Notably, while the TLA group exhibited a steeper learning curve slope (−1.35 min/case) indicative of faster operative time reduction, its early-phase complication rate was paradoxically lower (2.0% vs. 7.0% in SLRS). This apparent discrepancy may be attributed to case complexity bias inherent in the retrospective design. Surgeons often prioritize simpler cases (e.g., smaller tumors, fewer adhesions) during the early learning phase of a new technique like TLA, whereas the SLRS group—being the innovative approach—might have included proportionally more challenging cases (e.g., higher MAP scores, deeper tumor location) in early-phase applications. Additionally, the standardized vascular control sequence in TLA, though technically demanding, may inherently reduce injury risks in carefully selected cases, whereas SLRS’s reliance on a single anatomical plane might be more susceptible to variability in complex fat-adhesion scenarios. Importantly, this hypothesis was not fully addressed by pathological type adjustment alone, as other unmeasured confounders (e.g., tumor vascularity, surgeon’s pre-existing familiarity with renal surface anatomy) could influence both learning dynamics and complication rates. Future prospective studies with matched case complexity scoring are warranted to validate this association.​.

Comparative context and Innovation​

Our learning curve analysis extends beyond prior reports by quantifying proficiency thresholds using moving average stabilization and SPC-derived variability [11]. While TLA offers standardized vascular control, its steep learning curve limits broader adoption, particularly in low-volume centers [12]. SLRS’s performance aligns with emerging “single-plane” techniques (e.g., the retroperitoneal “ultrasound-guided” approach), but uniquely leverages renal surface anatomy without requiring adjunctive technology [13]. The comparable operative times and blood loss after adjustment for pathology (P ≥ 0.091) confirm SLRS’s non-inferiority, challenging the notion that simplified techniques sacrifice efficacy. This reinforces recent calls for “anatomically intuitive” adrenalectomy to democratize complex laparoscopy [14, 15].​

Limitations and Generalizability​

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, its single-surgeon design may limit generalizability, though it strengthens internal validity by controlling for technical bias. Second, pathological heterogeneity between groups (higher cortical adenomas in SLRS) was adjusted statistically but may influence inflammatory responses. Third, hospitalization differences may reflect regional practices rather than surgical factors; multi-institutional studies are needed. Finally, long-term outcomes (e.g., adrenal function, recurrence) were not assessed but are less relevant for benign pathology.

Conclusion​

The single-layer renal surface approach represents a significant advancement in retroperitoneal adrenalectomy, offering a steeper learning curve gradient and improved early recovery metrics compared to the standard three-layer technique. By simplifying anatomical dissection to a single avascular plane, SLRS enhances procedural consistency, making it an ideal choice for early-career surgeons and non-complex benign adrenal tumors. Notably, this study excluded patients with obesity, large tumors (> 6 cm), or prior retroperitoneal surgery, limiting generalizability to complex anatomical scenarios. Future investigations involving diverse populations—including those with high BMI, tumor size > 6 cm, or reoperative needs—are critical to determine whether SLRS maintains efficacy in challenging contexts. While further research is needed to address delayed gastrointestinal recovery and long-term outcomes, our findings support SLRS as a safe, reproducible alternative for selected benign adrenal lesions.
