Abstract
The evolution of radio access networks (RANs) toward virtualization and openness creates new opportunities for flexible, cost-effective, and high-performance deployments. Achieving real-time and energy-efficient baseband processing on commercial off-the-shelf platforms, however, remains a critical challenge. This article explores how single instruction multiple data (SIMD) architectures can accelerate RAN workloads. We first outline why key physical-layer functions, such as channel estimation, multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) detection, and forward error correction, are well aligned with SIMD’s data-level parallelism. We then present practical design guidelines and prototype results, showing significant improvements in throughput and energy efficiency compared to conventional CPU-only processing, while retaining programmability and ease of integration. Finally, we discuss open challenges in workload balancing and hardware heterogeneity, and highlight the role of SIMD as an enabling technology for flexible, efficient, and sustainable 6G-ready RANs.



I
Introduction

The architecture of the radio access network (RAN) is continuing to evolve in the transition from 4G to 5G and beyond to 6G. This evolution is driven by several key factors. First, RANs must meet some performance requirements, including support for higher data rates, lower latency, and operation across a wide range of frequency bands. Second, there is a desire to get more vendors to open RAN architectures, enabling multi-vendor interoperability and reducing dependence on proprietary hardware. Third, the emergence of new deployment models such as cloud RAN and edge computing necessitates more flexible and software-centric RAN architectures. These trends are motivating a shift away from hardware-defined solutions toward more modular and virtualized designs that can adapt to future requirements with greater agility.

The traditional RAN (tRAN) architecture has been widely deployed in 4G and 5G. In a tRAN system, most of the baseband processing is performed on dedicated hardware such as field programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), digital signal processors (DSPs), and application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs). These platforms offer low latency and some level of programmability, which made them attractive for initial deployments. They are, however, expensive to develop and difficult to upgrade as network requirements change. Because the hardware and software are usually provided by a single vendor, operators face vendor lock-in and limited interoperability. For these reasons, the industry has started to explore alternative architectures that are easier to maintain.

Virtualized RAN (vRAN) is one alternative architecture. In vRAN, the baseband functions are implemented in software and run on commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) servers instead of specialized hardware. This makes the system easier to update and manage using general-purpose computing resources. vRAN also supports more flexible deployment models, such as centralized or cloud-based processing. The open RAN (O-RAN) framework builds on the idea of vRAN by defining standardized interfaces between different parts of the RAN

A key feature of vRAN is that it runs mainly on general-purpose processors (GPPs), typically x86-64 processors, instead of dedicated baseband hardware. These CPUs are flexible and widely available, allowing operators to implement RAN more easily and reduce deployment complexity. While GPUs are explored for specific use cases, their higher power consumption and integration challenges have limited mainstream adoption. To fully benefit from CPU-based processing, the software must be optimized to take advantage of the CPU’s architecture. One important technique is single instruction, multiple data (SIMD), which has been a hot topic in modern vRAN and O-RAN baseband implementations. SIMD allows the same operation to be applied to multiple data elements in parallel. It has been widely used in fields like computer vision and image processing, where parallel operations on thousands of pixels are required in real-time tasks such as autonomous vehicles and robots

The result highlights the potential for PHY-layer software optimization that directly reduces processing latency in real-world RAN deployments.

In this article, we introduce the concept of SIMD to communication engineers and show how it can be applied in modern RAN architectures. While SIMD is one of the features in general-purpose CPUs and widely used in domains like computer architecture and image processing, it is not explicitly addressed in 3GPP or O-RAN standards and remains underutilized in many wireless PHY-layer implementations. We provide a more detailed introduction to the evolution of different RAN architectures, highlighting the growing role of software in baseband processing. Then, we explain the fundamentals of SIMD and why it is well suited for the parallel, structured computations found in wireless systems. Next, we explore the use of SIMD in a design example of MIMO detection under realistic 3GPP parameters, demonstrating its potential in vRAN deployments. We also analyze its effectiveness compared to GPUs and FPGAs and conclude with use cases and future research directions.

II
The Evolution of RAN architecture

The structural shift from hardware-centric tRAN to software-defined architectures has created an environment where SIMD acceleration can be directly integrated into the baseband processing stack. In vRAN and O-RAN, virtualization abstracts network functions from hardware, enabling flexible placement of processing tasks and dynamic allocation of resources. This flexibility allows developers to incorporate SIMD-optimized libraries into PHY-layer functions such as MIMO processing, which was previously not possible in fixed DSP pipelines of legacy RANs. In this section, we examine how these architectural properties enable practical SIMD deployment for baseband processing.

II-A

The vRAN architecture

vRAN refers to the software-defined implementation of RAN functions that enables baseband functions, or at least some of them, to run on GPPs, such as x86-64 CPUs, virtually on top of COTS computing platforms. The vRAN architecture separates software from hardware, enabling RAN workloads to operate on GPP-based servers. Virtualization on x86-64 platforms allows a unified software stack to support a wide range of deployment scenarios from small cells to macro cell base stations. Industry developers can implement RAN functions using their familiar programming languages such as C or C++, and port them across processors with minimal effort through recompilation. In contrast, tRAN architectures perform PHY processing on hardwired fixed-function silicon, which requires hardware upgrades for new services. Legacy systems also depend on hard-coded hardware description languages (HDLs) such as Verilog. These are specialized programming languages used to describe digital circuit behavior at the register transfer level (RTL). This approach ties the design to specific hardware and require proprietary software tools, limiting innovation due to the need for developers with specialized expertise.

The evolution of vRAN architecture from virtualized network functions (VNFs) to cloud-native or containerized network functions (CNFs) significantly enhances operational efficiency. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, VNFs run within virtual machines (VMs) and require full guest operating systems (OSs) over a hypervisor, a software layer that creates and manages multiple VMs on a physical COTS server. While VNFs provided the first step toward virtualization, they are resource-heavy and slower to scale. CNFs, in contrast, operate as lightweight containers generated by a container engine. This containerized structure eliminates the need for a full guest OS, enabling faster scaling, more efficient resource utilization, and improved software portability. CNF-based architectures also allow services to run across future processor generations through simple recompilation and lightweight container orchestration. Although we do not cover the CNF structure in detail, Kubernetes-based deployment is one of the most commercialized container orchestrators for vRAN solutions. It further enables mobile network operators (MNOs) to flexibly manage, scale, and launch new services
[
5
]
. The CNF-based vRAN architecture also supports the transition to a disaggregated and interoperable RAN structure aligned with O-RAN principles. This cloud-native evolution creates a flexible and portable software environment that supports SIMD-accelerated baseband processing within GPPs.

II-B

The O-RAN architecture

O-RAN extends the principles of vRAN by introducing open standard interfaces and enabling interoperability among equipment from different vendors
[
6
]
. Although commercial adoption is still limited compared to tRAN and vRAN, O-RAN’s main motivation is cost reduction through disaggregation of PHY-layer functions and increased vendor competition. Open interfaces also allow local vendors to enter the RAN ecosystem, promoting diversity and strengthening technology sovereignty. In the long term, O-RAN adoption is expected to help maintain 5G leadership and influence the development of 6G systems.

At the functional level, O-RAN separates legacy RAN modules into the radio unit (RU), distributed unit (DU), and centralized unit (CU), connected by standardized interfaces such as fronthaul and midhaul. This functional split allows independent development and deployment of each unit, increasing flexibility in vendor selection and network design. Reducing the dimensionality of signals sent over the fronthaul is also an active research topic

, as it can significantly lower the processing load between the RU and DU.

In O-RAN deployments, the pursuit of both energy efficiency and low latency aligns closely with the capabilities of SIMD-based acceleration. As 6G moves toward higher performance, greater flexibility, and improved sustainability, split-aware functional optimization will be essential for reducing baseband energy consumption

. At the same time, achieving real-time performance requires lightweight and accelerated implementations of the DU and RU. Maximizing the use of internal processing resources becomes critical as 6G faces strict latency requirements and processes much larger data. In this context, SIMD-based acceleration enables vRAN and O-RAN platforms to meet high throughput and real-time processing demands. It also supports the energy efficiency and latency goals of next-generation RAN systems.

III
SIMD fundamentals and integration into PHY

III-A

SIMD fundamentals

SIMD is a parallel computing architecture that enables a single instruction to operate on multiple data elements. It was originally introduced by Intel through extensions such as MMX, SSE, and AVX, and is now widely supported across modern CPU architectures. Compared to non-SIMD execution, SIMD enables fine-grained data-level parallelism directly on each CPU core and can achieve speedups of 8x to 16x for signal processing tasks.

SIMD acceleration is realized by defining specialized data types (e.g.,
__m256
and
__m128
for Intel AVX) and applying vectorized arithmetic instructions such as
add
,
subs
,
mul
, and
div
. Fig.
3
demonstrates this concept using a simple element-wise array addition example. The left side of the figure shows a standard scalar loop, which processes one element at a time. The right figure shows SIMD intrinsics (e.g.,
__m256_add_pd
for double precision and
__m256_add_ps
for single precision) that process multiple elements in parallel within a single CPU instruction like four packed-double (PD) or eight packed-single (PS) values in a 256-bit register. This example highlights how SIMD exploits data-level parallelism to accelerate computation. A key practical advantage of SIMD intrinsics is their compatibility with standard C/C++ environments, including compilers (e.g., GCC) and debuggers (e.g., GDB). This compatibility simplifies development and debugging across generations of Intel CPUs and similar architectures. Provided as C function-like application programming interfaces (APIs), SIMD intrinsics directly map to specific SIMD assembly instructions and offer much better readability than raw assembly. These features make SIMD a portable and sustainable option for PHY-layer processing.

The more efficiently communication engineers optimize an algorithm for SIMD, the greater the acceleration gains they can achieve. Modern CPUs support extended SIMD instruction sets such as Intel AVX2 and AVX-512, which provide 256-bit and 512-bit vector registers, respectively. These extensions enable high-throughput floating-point operations (FLOPs), essential for computationally intensive MIMO workloads involving dense matrix and vector algebra. Fully leveraging SIMD requires careful memory alignment, loop unrolling, and load/store optimizations to maximize the benefit of register-level bandwidth. Moreover, SIMD allows precise control over the trade-off between performance and numerical accuracy. For example, packing single-precision (16-bit) floating-point elements instead of double-precision (32-bit) floating-point elements into a 256-bit register
__m256
can nearly double the throughput with minimal degradation.

III-B

SIMD integration into PHY

SIMD shows its benefits when applied to heavy PHY operations in modern RAN systems. In the context of vRAN and O-RAN, SIMD can accelerate a wide range of PHY-layer blocks, including MIMO detection, precoding and channel estimation. Among these, MIMO detection is chosen as the primary focus of this article because it is computationally demanding yet algorithmically structured, making it an ideal candidate for SIMD-based acceleration. As shown in Fig.
3
, the fused multiply-add (FMA) example maps directly to intermediate operations in MIMO receivers such as covariance calculation. These repetitive linear algebraic tasks benefit significantly from SIMD’s data-level parallelism through vectorized additive and multiplicative instructions. We use MIMO detection as a case study to demonstrate how SIMD can be systematically integrated into broader PHY-layer pipelines in vRAN and O-RAN systems. Offloading these tasks to SIMD-enabled software modules allows vRAN systems to achieve lower latency and higher throughput without the need for GPUs or FPGAs.

Fig. 4:
SIMD-accelerated MIMO detection algorithm implemented in the software-defined DU. The LMMSE detection process, consisting of LU decomposition, forward/backward substitution, and matrix inversion, is efficiently executed using SIMD instructions by exploiting vector-level parallelism.

SIMD-based baseband processing also runs directly on CPU cores, minimizing PCIe-related data transfer overhead and enabling tight timing control. These benefits are especially valuable in commercial vRAN and O-RAN systems that rely on fully software-defined signal chains running on COTS servers. Intel’s recent white paper demonstrates the viability of this approach by implementing a SIMD-accelerated polar decoder using AVX-512 for 5G control channels, showing that even latency-sensitive L1 modules can be handled entirely in software on x86-64 platforms
[
9
]
. This prior work targets a simple decoding task with low computational complexity and small payloads, such as control channels.

We extend this approach to large-scale data processing, where MIMO detection involves far larger payloads and tighter transmission time interval (TTI) constraints. By demonstrating that SIMD can handle these demanding baseband operations with high efficiency, we highlight the broader applicability of software-defined PHY acceleration in real-time, high-throughput scenarios beyond limited control-plane use cases. Furthermore, we provide a feasibility for scaling to the massive antenna arrays anticipated in 6G deployments.

This C++-based simulator emulates the behavior of commercial base station equipment running on COTS servers. It includes key PHY modules compliant with NR standards and serves as a testbed to examine how SIMD integration affects the performance of virtualized baseband systems under various MIMO workloads. This setup serves as a reference platform to evaluate the potential performance gains of SIMD acceleration under conditions representative of current and near-future vRAN and O-RAN deployments.

We also analyze the maximum data rates of PHY-layer and evaluate the scalability of SIMD acceleration under different MIMO configurations. To estimate the theoretical peak rates, we consider a 5G NR system operating with maximum MCS level(27), which uses 256-QAM modulation and a high coding rate. Such performance confirms that all PHY computations can be completed within the 1 TTI deadline. The evaluation results validate that software-defined base stations can meet real-time constraints without external accelerators. This efficiency reduces CPU clock cycles and power consumption during intensive matrix operations, leading to significant energy savings.

Higher MIMO configurations (e.g.,

Wider SIMD lanes and efficient vector load/store operations increase the data rates that can be processed in a single TTI while still meeting latency requirements. This capability supports the realization of fully software-defined indoor vRAN deployments with Gbps-level throughput.

In vRAN systems, several acceleration options exist for PHY-layer processing, including CPU-based SIMD, GPU-based single instruction, multiple threads (SIMT), and FPGA solutions. Among these, SIMD operates directly on general-purpose CPUs and is well-suited for latency-sensitive workloads. It also executes vectorized instructions on CPU registers with minimal control overhead and data transfer latency.

Table
I
demonstrates the strengths and weaknesses of GPUs in vRAN PHY acceleration. GPUs offer massive thread-level parallelism and can achieve very high throughput. In signal processing tasks, however, they often require substantial software tuning to reach peak performance. SIMT execution also faces warp divergence, where threads within a warp take different control paths, and scheduling overhead. These factors introduce latency variability and reduce energy efficiency. They also make GPUs less suitable for latency-critical real-time workloads.

FPGA-based solutions provide deterministic latency and excellent energy efficiency. They also deliver high timing precision, which is essential for time-critical PHY-layer tasks. These benefits come with trade-offs, however, as they require long development cycles, reliance on proprietary toolchains, and limited flexibility after deployment. Such constraints reduce agility and slow adaptation to evolving RAN requirements, particularly in software-defined environments.

SIMD applies uniform vector operations across aligned data vectors. This approach eliminates the per-thread divergence and unpredictable delays seen in GPUs. Its software-oriented design removes the need for device passthroughs or vendor-specific drivers and allows seamless integration within CNF-based vRAN deployments. Overall, SIMD presents a balanced combination of scalability, low integration overhead, and compatibility with standard x86-64 server platforms. This balance makes it attractive for near-term 5G and emerging 6G RAN deployments, where ease of deployment, real-time performance, and software sustainability are crucial.

VI
Future outlook

SIMD-based acceleration is a practical approach for scalable and efficient MIMO processing in vRAN and O-RAN deployments. In this article, an NR-compliant, end-to-end link-level simulator on a COTS platform was used to emulate commercial base station operations. Results show that SIMD can significantly improve the performance of key PHY-layer functions such as matrix inversion and signal detection. Modern CPUs equipped with advanced SIMD instruction sets (e.g., AVX2 and AVX-512) provide substantial acceleration, reinforcing SIMD’s role as an practical enabler in software-defined RAN environments. Beyond demonstrating technical feasibility, it is essential to understand how SIMD can be applied in diverse use cases and what future research directions can extend its impact.

VI-A

Use cases and future research directions

The application of SIMD goes beyond PHY-layer testing and extends to diverse deployment scenarios. Several representative examples include:

•

Distributed MIMO (D-MIMO)
: In D-MIMO
[
12
]
, spatially distributed RUs cooperate to serve users, creating high fronthaul traffic and a heavy computational load on the DU. SIMD can accelerate both fronthaul compression using block floating-point (BFP) quantization and centralized baseband processing. These support real-time coordination across DUs and enable efficient, scalable D-MIMO operation.

•

Green 6G RAN
: As 6G targets sustainability and carbon neutrality, energy efficiency in RAN architectures is a critical concern
[
13
,
14
]
. SIMD-based processing addresses this by executing PHY-layer tasks efficiently on general-purpose CPUs. It reduces data movement and avoids the overhead of device passthrough. Its deterministic execution and tight software integration make it well-suited for energy-aware baseband pipelines in Green RAN deployments.

•

Indoor fixed wireless access (FWA)
: FWA is a cost-effective broadband alternative to fiber and delivers high-speed internet to fixed locations without the need for physical cabling
[
15
]
. It is particularly valuable in rural areas, helping bridge the internet access gap. It is also well-suited for enterprise or industrial sites that require rapid, flexible deployments. In O-RAN deployments running on COTS servers, FWA’s high bandwidth and predictable traffic patterns create heavy PHY-layer processing demands. SIMD acceleration can handle these workload efficiently.

•

Artificial intelligence (AI) RAN
: While AI/ML workloads typically run on GPUs or tensor processing units (TPUs), low-level PHY tasks can be offloaded to SIMD. This frees the accelerators to focus on control-plane functions within RAN intelligent controllers (RICs), including resource scheduling, energy-efficient operation, and quality of experience (QoE) optimization. As AI RAN architectures evolve toward 6G, this funtional split between SIMD and AI accelerators is expected to become important for achieving both real-time performance and system stability.

VI-B

The road ahead: hybrid and heterogeneous computing

Hybrid and heterogeneous computing techniques will play a critical role in future RAN architectures, combining (1) SIMD for deterministic, fine-grained PHY acceleration, (2) GPUs/TPUs for AI control plane processing, and (3) FPGAs and photonic accelerators for ultra-low-latency or high-throughput tasks supporting macro cells. Particularly, considering the characteristics of optical fiber and related components in O-RAN fronthaul, photonic accelerators can offload many baseband processing blocks. Their ultrafast, power-efficient capabilities complement electronic SIMD and FPGA solutions.

In this broader vision, SIMD is not only an optimization but a cornerstone of scalable, agile, and maintainable PHY-layer processing. Its inherent compatibility with COTS servers and software stacks provides a pathway to realizing open, flexible, and high-performance RANs for 5G-Advanced and beyond. We hope this work encourages deeper exploration of SIMD-based acceleration strategies within the wireless research and communication engineering communities.
