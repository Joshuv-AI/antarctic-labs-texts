Knowledge, attitude, and behavior regarding the implementation of shared decision-making among advanced practice nurses in Taiwan
Authors: Nai-Chen Lu, Su-Ying Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch on shared decision-making (SDM) mainly focuses on physicians and patients, with less study on advanced practice nurses (APNs), who have expertise and participate in decision-making. Understanding APNs’ SDM knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors could enhance patient outcomes, especially in Taiwan where patient involvement in medical decisions is limited. This study examined how SDM knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors influence SDM implementation among APNs in Taiwan.MethodsThis study aims to examine the factors influencing and the relationships among SDM knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors of Advanced Practice Nurses (APNs) in Taiwan. This study employed a cross-sectional design and surveyed 112 Advanced Practice Nurses (APNs) in Taiwan via an online survey. Data collected via a questionnaire on SDM knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors were analyzed for influencing factors. Descriptive statistics and univariate linear regression were used.ResultsAttitude was the primary predictor of SDM behavior, strongly linked to effort and the quality of implementation. Practical SDM experience also boosted APNs’ effort in carrying out the process. This study identified gaps in APNs’ risk communication knowledge, especially in understanding relative risk and natural frequency formats.ConclusionsSDM training should incorporate risk communication strategies, reflective learning, simulations, practice, and structured feedback to enhance APNs’ understanding of SDM core concepts and promote consistent clinical application.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Trial registrationNot applicable. This study did not involve any health care intervention on human participants.

Introduction

Shared decision-making (SDM) is a model emphasizing collaboration between healthcare professionals and patients. Physicians provide evidence-based information on treatment options, helping patients make informed choices aligned with their preferences and values. Patients uncertain about screening, treatments, or major procedures seek guidance from healthcare professionals who analyze benefits and risks using evidence-based medicine and clinical experience. This support helps patients make decisions that match their personal values [1–3]. Through exchanging information and deliberating, healthcare professionals and patients ultimately reach a treatment decision that best reflects the patients’ needs and values [3]. This model replaces the traditional one-way information exchange between healthcare professionals and patients or their families with a two-way communication method [4]. This type of two-way process is considered a form of patient-centered care that enables patients to take an active role in making medical decisions [1]. SDM helps patients understand the benefits and risks of various treatments, which can reduce the likelihood of unnecessary interventions resulting from limited healthcare knowledge [5].

Due to patriarchal cultural influences, patients in Asia often adopt a more passive role in medical decision-making [6]. In Taiwan, physicians typically provide medical information to patients or their families and make decisions on the patients’ behalf [7]. Since 2016, Taiwan has begun implementing Shared Decision-Making (SDM) in hospitals to enhance communication between healthcare professionals and patients, increase patients’ health literacy, reduce anxiety, and strengthen the physician–patient relationship. In 2017, SDM was officially included in hospital accreditation standards to foster two-way communication between healthcare providers and patients [8]. In addition, the Ministry of Health and Welfare made SDM a core goal in 2018 to enhance healthcare quality and patient safety by encouraging institutions to use various technologies to improve communication and involve patients and families in decision-making [9].

In clinical practice, physicians often face time constraints and may underestimate patients’ preferences for participation, which serves as a structural barrier to SDM [40]. Advanced practice nurses (APNs) have specialized nursing skills and the ability to make differential diagnoses. In Taiwan, APNs deliver direct patient care and assist physicians by explaining and coordinating treatment decisions. During shared decision-making (SDM), APNs act as essential intermediaries, facilitating communication between patients and their families, aiding in treatment planning, evaluating the benefits and risks, and supporting patients throughout their decision-making process [10]. Therefore, APNs are crucial for advancing SDM, boosting patient engagement, and enhancing treatment outcomes.

A previous study rooted in behavioral theory examines nurse anesthetists’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors in managing pain. Their understanding and outlook shape their professional actions. This study is theoretically grounded in the Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) model, which posits that individuals’ professional behaviors are shaped by their level of knowledge, attitudes, and subsequent practice. Shared decision making (SDM) is a complex professional behavior requiring both cognitive understanding and attitudinal readiness. Therefore, the KAP model provides an appropriate framework for examining SDM-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among advanced practice nurses.

Knowledge serves as the foundation for behavior, affecting treatment accuracy, clinical decision-making, and precise recommendations. The influence of knowledge is indirect. Attitude is crucial in shaping, understanding, and predicting behavior. It encourages the development of behavioral intentions and guides the execution of actions to reach particular goals. Simply possessing knowledge does not directly cause behavior [11, 12]. Behavior is influenced by knowledge and attitude, where attitude motivates behavioral intention and drives action. Having a positive attitude influences how well behaviors are performed. Therefore, implementing SDM relies on knowledge and attitude as key factors influencing behavior. Healthcare professionals with positive attitudes and sufficient knowledge are more likely to perform effectively during SDM [2, 13]. The clinical expertise of APNs, their positive attitude toward SDM, and the decision-making context are key to effective SDM implementation [14].

Historically, medical decision-making in Taiwan has been predominantly physician-led within a hierarchical clinical culture. However, under the current national SDM policy mandate, this paradigm is shifting toward a model where healthcare providers facilitate informed choices, returning decision-making autonomy to patients and their families. Within this evolving landscape, Advanced Practice Nurses (APNs) play a pivotal role as agents of change. Therefore, the KAP model provides an appropriate framework for examining how SDM-related knowledge and attitudes influence the actual practices of APNs during this policy-driven transition.

Despite the clear policy mandate for SDM, APNs in Taiwan often face significant role tensions rooted in a hierarchical medical culture. Historically, the dominance of physician authority has limited the professional autonomy of nursing staff, creating a structural barrier where APNs may feel hesitant to initiate shared decisions. These structural factors including traditional power dynamics and restricted clinical decision-making scope may hinder the translation of SDM knowledge into practice. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate how APNs’ internal cognitive and attitudinal factors interact with these external pressures, as this understanding is essential for empowering APNs to navigate these tensions and successfully implement patient-centered care.

The conceptual framework of this study (Fig. 1) integrates individual predictors with structural determinants of SDM implementation. Grounded in the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) model, it posits that SDM Knowledge serves as the cognitive foundation, while SDM Attitudes act as the core driver and mediator for SDM Behaviors.

This framework explains how demographic factors and individual readiness (KAP) interact with systemic institutional pressures to shape the actual performance of SDM in clinical settings.

Studies on SDM mainly focus on physician-patient interactions, with limited attention to APNs’ role. APNs have expertise and participate in decision-making. Understanding their knowledge, attitude, and behavior in SDM can improve their support and promote a patient-centered healthcare model. This study aims to examine these factors’ relationships among APNs in Taiwan.

Methods

Research instrument

Demographic information and SDMrelated variables were gathered. These included gender, age, educational level, service department, professional title, hospital accreditation level, total years of nursing experience, SDM practice years, participation in SDM training, extent of SDM implementation (such as explaining disease management options and alternatives, providing comparative treatment information, understanding patients’ preferences, analyzing the pros and cons of options, and supporting decision-making aligned with patient values), and the geographic region of the hospital.

In this study, the KAP framework was operationalized through specific definitions tailored to SDM. Knowledge involves the comprehension of evidence-based risk presentation and the structured steps of the SDM model. Attitude encompasses the APN’s disposition toward empowering patients and their internal motivation to uphold patient autonomy. Behavior focuses on the observable actions taken during patient encounters to facilitate a collaborative choice. These definitions served as the basis for the instruments used to evaluate APN readiness for SDM implementation.

Knowledge of SDM was evaluated with the SDM knowledge scale developed by Forcino et al. [13] which was initially created to evaluate the knowledge of physicians and nurse practitioners regarding SDM. The SDM knowledge scale consists of 10 items with yes or no answers. Correct responses earn 1 point, while incorrect ones earn 0 points, resulting in a maximum score of 10. The original version of the scale was in English. For this study, Two experts fluent in both English and Chinese, with medical knowledge, translated the scale into Chinese. Another bilingual expert back translated it into English. An English-language expert compared the original and back-translated versions. Discrepancies were fixed by adjusting the Chinese translation for accuracy and cultural relevance. The internal consistency of the dichotomous SDM knowledge scale was assessed using the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20), which is mathematically equivalent to Cronbach’s alpha for binary items. The KR-20 coefficient for the 10-item knowledge scale was 0.60.

Attitudes towards SDM were measured using the Chinese version of the nursing shared decision-making attitude scale developed by Hsu [15]. Originally, this scale was developed to assess oncology nurses’ attitudes toward SDM. It consists of 11 items divided into two dimensions: empathetic communication and mastery learning. Participants respond using a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores reflect more positive attitudes toward SDM. The original scale’s Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92 overall, with 0.87 for empathetic communication and 0.88 for mastery learning. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.94.

Two SDM behavior constructs were assessed. This study’s framework for SDM behavior was built on Elwyn et al.‘s [16] Three-Step Model, which highlights the core behavioral elements: ‘Team Talk’, ‘Option Talk’, and ‘Decision Talk’ [42]. It first measures how much effort APNs put into promoting patient participation in decision-making, using a 5-item observational scale called OPTION5. The alignment is as follows: Item 2 (Partnership) supports ‘Team Talk’; Items 1 and 3 (Problem definition and Risk communication) assess the pedagogical efforts for ‘Option Talk’; and Items 4 and 5 (Preference elicitation and Decision-making) capture the complexity of ‘Decision Talk’. This was evaluated with the OPTION5 scale by Elwyn [16], translated into Chinese by Hou [17].

This scale consists of five items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale with the following anchors: 0 (no effort), 1 (minimal effort), 2 (moderate effort), 3 (skilled effort), and 4 (exemplary effort). Higher scores reflect increased effort in engaging patients in shared decision-making (SDM). Originally, the scale had a Cronbach’s alpha of .95; in this study, it was .94. The second construct was the quality of SDM implementation, assessed through the shared decision-making questionnaire physician version developed by [18] and translated into Chinese by [19]. The initial version of this questionnaire was created to evaluate students’ self-assessment of SDM quality. This study adapted the questionnaire to assess APNs’ perception of SDM implementation quality. The scale consists of nine items rated on a 6-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating ‘completely disagree’ and 6 indicating ‘completely agree.’ Higher scores signify a greater perceived quality of SDM implementation. The original scale had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93, and in this study, it was slightly higher at 0.94.

Data management and statistical analysis

Participants were recruited through a web-based anonymous survey distributed via professional social media groups and nursing networks. This strategy was chosen to facilitate nationwide outreach and to include APNs from diverse geographic locations and various tiers of healthcare institutions. All participants were screened to confirm they met the professional criteria for APNs in Taiwan before proceeding to the survey. Between November 20, 2022, and March 10, 2023, a total of 168 individuals accessed the survey, with 164 giving informed consent (consent rate = 97.6%). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics, such as percentages and mean scores, were used to summarize demographic data and SDM knowledge, attitude, and behavior. Univariate linear regression analysis was performed to explore how demographic factors and SDM knowledge and attitudes are associated with SDM behavior. (i.e., respondents’ level of effort in implementing SDM and their implementation quality). Variables found significant in univariate analysis were used in multivariate linear regression to assess the relationship between demographic factors, SDM knowledge, attitudes, and SDM behavior. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of a regional hospital in Tainan, Taiwan (TMANH111-REC016). The study’s purpose was explained on the first page of the online survey. Participants had to give informed consent before starting the survey. To ensure eligibility, an initial screening question was used to verify that each participant was a certified APN; general registered nurses were automatically excluded from the survey. Those who chose not to participate were immediately directed to the survey’s end. All responses were collected anonymously and kept confidential. Participants could withdraw at any time without obligation to complete the survey. The online survey did not gather personally identifiable information such as names, phone numbers, or email addresses, ensuring respondent anonymity and confidentiality.

Results

The final analytic sample consisted of 112 certified APNs who met the inclusion criteria and provided complete responses. Among the respondents, 92.0% were women. The average age was 40.4 ± 7.1 years. Participants had an average of 17.5 ± 8.1 years of nursing experience. Regarding SDM experience, 34.8% had practiced SDM for 2–5 years. Most (85.7%) attended SDM training courses. Additionally, 52.7% reported partial involvement in SDM implementation (Table 1).

SDM attitude

The total SDM attitude scores( Table 3) ranged from 29 to 55, with a mean of 49.81 ± 5.64. The overall item-level mean was 4.53 ± 0.51, with the highest individual item score being 5.0 and the lowest 2.6. Overall, respondents showed moderately high self-perceived attitudes toward SDM. The mean scores were 4.58 ± 0.49 for the empathetic communication subscale and 4.48 ± 0.56 for the mastery learning subscale. The highest-rated item was “Eager to respect and empathies with patients’ preferences and values ”(4.61 ± 0.54). In contrast, the lowest-rated items were “Eager to assist and guide patients in discussing their conditions and making medical decisions step by step” (4.38 ± 0.70) and“Eager to participate in SDM training courses organized by medical institutions.”(4.38 ± 0.76).

Discussion

SDM knowledge, attitude, and behavior

This study examined the knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors of APNs in Taiwan regarding shared decision-making (SDM). The average SDM knowledge score was 6.4 ± 1.05, reflecting a moderate to slightly above-moderate knowledge level. The question with the fewest correct responses was about communicating risk: “When communicating information about risk, it is best to use relative risk.” This aligns with findings from other studies on nurse practitioners. Research suggests that APNs tend to prefer using natural frequency rather than relative risk to communicate risks [13]. Experts highlight that effective risk communication should involve explaining the nature of a risk, its origins, and management strategies. Healthcare professionals should also clearly communicate the likelihood of risk and consider patients’emotional reactions to perceived risks [20]. However, the identified gap in risk communication knowledge represents a potential break in the KAP chain. Even with favorable attitudes, a deficiency in specific technical knowledge can limit the precision of SDM implementation. This finding highlight that for APNs to successfully fulfill their roles under national policy requirements, targeted education in risk communication is essential to bridge the gap between intention and high-quality practice. Consequently, future SDM training should include lessons on risk communication techniques to enhance APNs’ ability to effectively convey risk information in different ways. Improving this skill is critical for boosting patients’ understanding and active participation in SDM.

Regarding SDM attitude, respondents rated higher in empathetic communication than mastery learning. The top-scoring item was “Eager to respect and empathies with patients’ preferences and values.,”aligning with a study involving oncology nurses [21]. These high scores in empathetic communication reflect the literature indicating that nurses use essential SDM skills during patient care, such as building effective physician–patient relationships, engaging in patient-centered communication, and evaluating patients’values and preferences. These skills underscore the role of empathy in SDM [15].

The lowest-scoring items in the mastery learning dimension were “Eager to assist and guide patients in discussing their conditions and making medical decisions step by step” and “Eager to guide patients step by step to discuss their condition and make medical decisions.” This dimension refers to the nursing staff’s use of research evidence, interpret findings, and provide recommendations to address patient needs and support personalized decision-making [15]. Factors influencing SDM learning include time constraints in educational programs, inadequate staffing, limited access to resources and research findings, the content of institutional training programs, and structural issues such as nursing workforce shortages [14]. Considering the current limitations in Taiwan’s clinical nursing setting, these factors probably led to the lower scores seen in this area. To enhance these attitudinal scores, institutions should focus not only on technical training but also on cultivating a supportive environment that encourages a positive attitude towards lifelong learning. Offering accessible decision aids and structured feedback can help healthcare systems energize APNs’ enthusiasm for functioning as evidence-based practitioners, thereby maintaining the SDM construct in line with its intended attitudinal framework.

Regarding SDM behavior, the factors associated with the level of effort focus on the individual and institutional factors that motivate APNs to initiate and commit energy to the SDM process. The item with the highest effort score was “I provide information and check patient understanding of treatment alternatives (which can include declining treatment), to support the patient in comparing alternatives.” Conversely, the lowest score was for " I encourage patients to share their preferences regarding the alternatives that have been presented.” This results in a subtle divergence from prior research on breast cancer care teams [22], which focused on disclosing decisional points and identified the most significant challenge as integrating elicited preferences into final decisions. In clinical practice, APNs’ facilitative behaviors in decision-making may be shaped by the views of individual physicians and recommendations from their multidisciplinary team. These dynamics can restrict the APNs to gather patient preferences during decision-making. In cancer care, the complexity of treatment may lead physicians to steer patients toward specific options based on concerns about patient vulnerability [22, 23]. Furthermore, some APNs may perceive that SDM-related responsibilities are outside the scope of their practice, which could negatively affect the level of effort they devote to SDM implementation.

Regarding SDM implementation quality, this section develops a deeper analysis of the qualitative aspects of SDM, such as empathetic communication and clinical precision, the highest-scoring item was “I told my patient that there are different options for treating his/her medical condition.” This result aligns with findings from other studies on nurses and may reflect their role in providing comprehensive and accurate information to ensure that patients understand available options and can make informed decisions [24]. The lowest-scoring item was “My patient and I selected a treatment option together.” In Taiwan’s clinical setting, patients tend to follow physicians’recommendations rather than actively share their own preferences. Although APNs play an important collaborative role in promoting SDM in clinical practice, their role mainly involves providing information and facilitating communication between patients and doctors, with limited direct decision-making responsibility. Additionally, the success of SDM implementation depends on several factors such as patient health literacy, illness severity, emotional condition, and the strength of the physician–patient relationship [25, 26]. While APNs do help support SDM by offering relevant information, their role in jointly selecting treatment options is still limited. Going forward, healthcare institutions should improve SDM quality and increase patient involvement by providing specialized SDM training programs and enhancing collaboration between professionals.

Based on the results of the risk assessment, the APN will engage in ongoing discussions regarding risk factors with the patient, reinforcing the education previously provided and addressing any patient concerns. Furthermore, the APN will guide the patient or their family members on when to seek follow-up care should any doubts or questions arise [43].

The implementation of Shared Decision-Making is determined not only by an APN’s individual professional autonomy, attitude, and competence but also by the willingness to invest effort within the constraints of the clinical environment. Specifically, factors such as time limitations, heavy workloads, and the degree of organizational support interact to influence the manifestation of SDM behaviors.

Consequently, the full realization of SDM cannot rely on individual traits alone; it requires an integrated, collaborative framework to mitigate environmental barriers. Within this framework, valuing patients’ past experiences and preferences can serve as a powerful catalyst for bolstering APNs’ intrinsic motivation and professional resilience. By centering on the patient’s narrative, APNs can transcend systemic limitations and ultimately achieve a substantive improvement in the quality of holistic care.

Relationship between SDM knowledge and behavior

Multivariate regression analysis was used to explore the link between APNs’ SDM knowledge and their behavior. The analysis showed no significant relationship between the two, aligning with findings from another study [27].

Although SDM knowledge is seen as vital for facilitating SDM and promoting positive attitudes and behaviors among health-care professionals [28], acquiring knowledge alone does not guarantee successful application in clinical practice. Factors like clinical experience, organizational support, and patient willingness can influence how SDM knowledge translates into practice. In complex settings, nurses need both professional expertise and practical skills to effectively participate in SDM and support patients in making informed decisions [14].

Long-term SDM training is crucial to ensure that the concept of SDM is fully understood and becomes an integral part of clinical practice. Through ongoing training, APNs can enhance their understanding of and dedication to the principles of SDM, thereby fostering the intention to implement SDM in practice [14, 28]. APNs who have both sufficient knowledge and positive attitudes toward SDM are better equipped to support patients in making decisions that match their individual needs. In clinical settings, this combination boosts nurses’ confidence and initiative, thereby increasing both the willingness and quality of patient participation in decision-making [29, 30].

To enhance the link between knowledge and behavior, SDM training for APNs should be further reinforced. This training must equip APNs with structured skills in risk communication and patient value assessment. Additionally, mechanisms for interprofessional collaboration should be developed to give nurses a more active role in decision-making [31, 32]. APNs must have thorough and precise knowledge of SDM to effectively help patients and their families make meaningful shared decisions.

Drawing on the COM-B model of behavior change, our findings suggest that knowledge serves only as a prerequisite capability. The lack of a significant correlation between knowledge and SDM behavior underscores that Capability alone is insufficient without Opportunity (structural and organizational support) and Motivation (the professional willingness to invest effort). [44] Implementation science frameworks suggest that for APNs to bridge the know-do gap, healthcare organizations must move beyond factual education and focus on enhancing organizational readiness and fostering an environment that authorizes and facilitates collaborative practice.

Relationship between SDM attitudes and behavior

SDM attitude was strongly linked to SDM behavior, aligning with existing research. A positive attitude continues to be a crucial factor in encouraging behavioral engagement in SDM [13, 33].

APNs possess unique clinical advantages, such as enhanced patient-clinician communication and the ability to foster trust, which are directly linked to improved patient outcomes and greater access to care. However, a critical gap remains despite these advantages: APNs operate within a hierarchical system in which their roles are often prioritized for clinical symptom management rather than structured decision-making [41].

Several factors influence SDM behavior. These include time limits, gaps between SDM practices and clinical guidelines, lack of teamwork, limited institutional support, staff exhaustion, and low patient willingness to engage [5, 34].

Changing attitudes necessitates ongoing education and self-reflection. To help APNs internalize positive attitudes and reliably apply them to shared decision-making (SDM) behaviors, clinical institutions should establish long-term training programs that include repeated practice opportunities. These programs should strengthen nurses’ positive orientation toward SDM and enhance their ability to apply SDM in practice. Furthermore, these programs should motivate nurses to incorporate research evidence into their clinical decision-making [11, 28, 35–37]. Only through ongoing learning and a strong system of clinical feedback can SDM be effectively integrated into routine care processes.

Predicators of SDM behavior

This study found a positive correlation between the attitude towards SDM and the willingness to invest effort in its implementation. Additionally, the level of respondent participation in SDM significantly predicted SDM behavior. These results are consistent with other research, which also highlights attitude as the key factor influencing behavioral execution [38]. In other words, both APNs’ attitude and their active involvement in SDM processes collaboratively influence their motivation and effort to advance SDM. As APNs become more familiar with and engaged in the SDM process, their willingness and ability to promote SDM are also increased.

Attitude emerged as the most influential predictor of SDM implementation quality, surpassing even effort levels, which highlights its significant role in determining behavioral quality in SDM. This aligns with other research emphasizing attitude as a key driver of behavior. To encourage APNs to adopt SDM practices, they must first understand and endorse the fundamental concepts and values of SDM [28]. When nurses are deeply committed to SDM ideals, show empathy by understanding patients’ perspectives, needs, and preferences, and are familiar with the decision-making process, they are more prone to start conversations, clarify patient values and preferences, and collaborate effectively with other professionals. These practices enable them to execute each step of SDM in a detailed and systematic manner [23, 34]. Repeated practice opportunities further improve these skills, supporting APNs in consistently engaging in SDM behavior [39]. Our study found that APNs in Taiwan demonstrate a highly positive attitude and significant empathy toward Shared Decision-Making (SDM). These results align with findings from research conducted in the United States and the Netherlands, which consistently show that APNs hold exceptionally favorable views of SDM [13, 22]. The cross-cultural consistency of these findings underscores the immense potential of APNs as a core driving force for SDM implementation. International research in Europe suggests that nursing professionals, often acting as case managers, play an indispensable role in facilitating ‘preference talk’ and ‘decision talk’ with patients. However, our findings reflect the unique challenges encountered by APNs within the Taiwanese cultural context. In traditional Asian cultures, patients often adopt a more reserved role, and medical decisions are frequently dominated by family members. Consequently, beyond clinical communication skills, Taiwanese APNs must invest significantly more effort into navigating cultural pressures and complex family dynamics to elicit patient preferences. Despite these cultural nuances, the obstacles faced by Taiwanese APNs align closely with the systemic dilemmas documented among international counterparts. Research from the Netherlands indicates that the primary barriers to SDM implementation include stringent time constraints and the persistent patient expectation for physicians to retain direct decision-making authority. Furthermore, within physician-led Multidisciplinary Teams (MDTs), APNs often encounter challenges such as role ambiguity or being constrained by the team’s predetermined recommendations, which can limit their autonomy in the SDM process [22, 28, 45].

In clinical practice, ensuring the substantive depth of the SDM process is an important consideration, particularly in multidisciplinary settings where APNs collaborate within physician-led decision-making frameworks. Under current healthcare systems, while ultimate medical decisions remain physician-led, the process can be influenced by clinical time pressures. Our findings suggest that the high level of professional effort and quality demonstrated by APNs provides a vital foundation for bridging this decisional gap.

By utilizing their professional competence to facilitate value expression and emotional support, APNs enhance the thoroughness of the SDM process. To further strengthen this collaborative effort, the use of systematic support tools, such as Patient Decision Aids (PDAs), is recommended. These tools provide a structured framework that empowers APNs to create a substantive feedback loop, ensuring that patient preferences are systematically integrated into the final decision. This team-based approach ensures that SDM remains a meaningful professional practice that truly reflects patient-centered values [22, 28].

Limitations

This study has methodological considerations regarding measurement and sampling. The SDM behavior measured in this study was operationalized as the self-reported effort and quality of execution, which may be subject to social desirability bias and lead to an overestimation of actual clinical actions. Furthermore, the online survey method may attract APNs with a more proactive stance toward SDM, potentially reflecting the perspectives of highly motivated professionals. Future research incorporating objective behavioral indicators and multi-site designs would further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study examined APNs’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding SDM in Taiwan. Attitude was the main predictor of SDM behavior, closely related to effort and the quality of implementation.

This study revealed gaps in APNs’ risk communication knowledge, particularly regarding the understanding of relative risk and natural frequency formats. In the future, nursing education should include SDM training that provides for risk communication strategies, reflective learning, simulations, practice, and structured feedback to deepen APNs’ understanding of SDM core concepts and foster consistent clinical application. Second, at the hospital system level, institutions should develop standardized SDM toolkits (e.g., PDAs) and integrate them into clinical pathways to reduce the cognitive load and time pressure on APNs. These tools empower APNs to provide a substantive feedback loop, systematically relaying patient values back to physicians to ensure the integrity of the decision-making process. Finally, at the policy level, establishing specialized billing codes would formally recognize the professional expertise APNs contribute to this team-based collaborative effort. By combining advanced training with structured clinical tools, APNs can effectively transition from being mere informants to becoming essential facilitators of substantive, patient-centered care.
