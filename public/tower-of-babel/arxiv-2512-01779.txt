A discrete approach to Dirichlet L-functions via spectral models: special values and zeros
Anders Karlsson, Dylan Müller

Abstract
We study Dirichlet L L -functions via discrete analogs L n L_{n} arising from the spectral theory of cyclic graphs as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty . Using a refined Euler-Maclaurin asymptotic expansion due to Sidi, together with an independent polynomiality property of these finite spectral sums at integers, we obtain exact special-value formulas, even starting at n = 1 n=1 . This yields new expressions for certain trigonometric sums of interest in physics, and recovers, by a different method, the striking formulas of Xie, Zhao, and Zhao. It also gives infinite families of recursion relations among special values of the Riemann zeta function and of Dirichlet L L -functions. Concerning zeros, we prove that, for odd primitive characters, a natural asymptotic functional equation for the discrete functions L n L_{n} is equivalent to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for the corresponding Dirichlet L L -function. We also provide some remarks about the non-existence of possible real zeros.



1
Introduction

Dirichlet
L
-functions, defined for characters
\chi
by the meromorphic continuation of

L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}},

play a central role in analytic number theory. Our perspective in this paper is to view these functions as spectral objects arising from the usual Laplacian, and to approximate them by quantities defined in a completely parallel way from the combinatorial Laplacian. This continues the approach set forth in
[
CJK10
,
FK17
,
F16
,
MV23
,
XZZ24
]
, and the focus of our paper is special values and zeros of
L(s,\chi)
, along with exact evaluations of certain trigonometric sums that appear in other areas.

From one particular spectral viewpoint, Riemann’s zeta function
\zeta(s)
and Dirichlet’s
L
-functions are associated with the circle
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Selberg’s zeta function
[
Se56
]
is similarly associated with surfaces
\mathbb{H}^{2}/\Gamma
. Here we study the zero-dimensional case
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. Ideas and speculations on how zeta functions in various dimensions interact can be found in
[
JL11
]
.

It should be said that the closest graph analog of Selberg’s zeta function is instead the Ihara zeta functions, for example since they both are Laplace transforms of heat kernels, see
[
CJK15
]
. The Ihara zeta function for general graphs was suggested by Serre in the 1970s
[
S02
, p. IX]
after reading Ihara’s work. It was developed by Sunada and others into an important chapter of spectral graph theory
[
Te10
]
. These zetas (and the
L
-functions of Stark-Terras
[
ST96
]
) have analogs with number theoretic zeta functions. In contrast, the functions associated to graph spectra discussed here have more direct connections. Selberg’s zeta function, with its Euler product and trace formula, has features analogous to classical number theory. But in addition, for the modular surface, the Riemann zeta function actually appears in the functional equation.

One fundamental graph spectral zeta function is the one associated to the discrete line
\mathbb{Z}
:

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}I_{0}(2t)t^{s}\frac{dt}{t}=\frac{1}{2^{2s}\pi^{1/2}}\frac{\Gamma(1/2-s)}{\Gamma(1-s)}=\binom{-2s}{-s}.

It has its share of analogs, for example a functional equation of the usual form
\xi_{\mathbb{Z}}(1-s)=\xi_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)

[
FK17
]
. Moreover, special values of
\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}
can be used to give the formula of the volumes of the round spheres in a form similar to the Minkowski-Siegel volume formulas for spaces like
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})

[
KP23
]
. But
\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)
also actually appears in the functional equation of Eisenstein series
[
Se56
, (3.7),(3.9)]
, and of Selberg’s zeta function
[
Se56
, p. 79]
and
[
Se54
, §10]
. Thus, in the case of the modular surface, both
\zeta(s)
and
\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)
appear in the fudge factors for the Selberg zeta function (
cf.

[
JL11
, p. 300]
). This may not be surprising since both
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}
can be thought related to the cusp. The graph zeta function
\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)
is also present in the Gindikin-Karpelevich formulas
[
K20
]
, and as Sarnak pointed out to us, it is suggestive that the special values at
s=-n
are exactly the integers exploited by Chebyshev in his work on primes.

An infinite sum, as the Dirichlet series displayed above, is by definition the limit of the partial sums. Here we take instead limits of finite sums with a much richer structure: they are defined entirely analogously as Mellin transforms of heat kernels, like in Riemann’s memoir or as emphasized by Jorgenson-Lang
[
JL11
]
. Thanks to this structure, these sums have independent interests in various physics and mathematics contexts, see
[
K20
,
JKS24
]
for references. The special value
\zeta^{\prime}(0)
is the negative of the logarithm of the determinant of the Laplacian, which counts the number of (rooted) spanning trees and therefore has a long history. It is a graph invariant with exceptionally wide relevance, for example to the study of water systems
[
BR18
]
.

The
spectral zeta function
of a graph or a space
X
is the Mellin transform of the trace of the heat kernel associated to a Laplacian on
X
:

\zeta_{X}(s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Tr^{*}(K_{X}(t))t^{s}\frac{dt}{t},

where
\Tr^{*}
is the usual trace but discarding the
0
eigenvalue of the Laplace operator when it is in the point spectrum, and where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. For infinite transitive graphs one can use
K_{X}(t,x,x)
instead of a trace.

Friedli
[
F16
]
studied the following discrete analog of Dirichlet
L
-functions:

L_{n}(s,\chi)=\sum_{j=1}^{qn-1}\frac{\chi(j)}{\left[4\sin^{2}(\frac{\pi j}{qn})\right]^{s}},

for even and non-principal
\chi
of modulus
q>1
, that is
\chi(-1)=1
. The denominators consist of the non-zero Laplace eigenvalues of the graph of
\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
. Note that for odd characters, the above function vanishes identically. Therefore, following Xie-Zhao-Zhao in
[
XZZ24
]
, we instead define the discrete
L
-function
L_{n}
for odd characters of modulus
q>1
to be

L_{n}(s,\chi)=\sum_{j=1}^{qn-1}\chi(j)\frac{\cot\left(\frac{\pi j}{qn}\right)}{\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi j}{qn}\right)\right]^{s}}.

Asymptotics.

Similarly to
[
FK17
]
in the case of the Riemann zeta function, see Theorem
3.1
below, and
[
F16
]
for even characters, see Theorem
3.2
below, we use
a modified Euler-Maclaurin formula that allows for endpoint singularities, developed by Sidi in
[
Sid04
]
and
[
Sid12
]
in the context of numerical analysis, to establish:

Theorem 1.1

(Asymptotics for odd characters)
.

Let
\chi
be an odd Dirichlet character of modulus
q>1
. Then, for any fixed
s\in\mathbb{C}
and any integer
m\geq 0
, the following asymptotic expansion holds as
n\to\infty
:

L_{n}(s,\chi)=2\sum_{k=0}^{m}b_{k}(s)L(1+2(s-k),\chi)\left(\frac{qn}{2\pi}\right)^{1+2(s-k)}+\mathcal{O}(n^{1+2(s-(m+1))}),

where the functions
b_{k}(s)
are polynomials of degree
k
, given by the Laurent expansion

\left(\frac{z/2}{\sin(z/2)}\right)^{2s}\cot(z/2)=\sum_{k\geq 0}b_{k}(s)z^{2k-1}.

The first coefficients are
b_{0}(s)=2
,
b_{1}(s)=(s-1)/6
and
b_{2}(s)=(5s^{2}-9s-2)/{720}
.

Special values.

The fact that limits of certain trigonometric sums provide a method to evaluate Euler’s special values for
\zeta(s)
has been observed in several places, for example by Apostol
[
Ap73
]
or Witten
[
Wi91
, p. 177-178]
. A remarkable discovery of Xie-Zhao-Zhao
[
XZZ24
]
is that there is in fact no need to take a limit. The finite cyclic graph approximates the circle, like in Archimedes, but in contrast for special values there is no need for any limits. In a precise sense, see below, already the graph on one vertex and one loop, or the graph on two vertices with a double edge, know
\pi^{2}/6
.

Moreover, the authors of
[
XZZ24
]
extends this to special values of Dirichlet
L
-functions, to obtain closed form expressions in terms of the discrete
L
-functions considered here. The special case of Riemann zeta functions actually already appears in a paper by Zagier
[
Za96
, Theorem 1.iii]
, although via different methods. It seems unlikely that Zagier thought of it as a graph zeta function, nor did he emphasize Riemann’s zeta function. Instead, he was concerned with Verlinde formulas, see
[
K20
]
for this connection to zeta functions.

In the present paper, our method appears flexible and conceptually attractive. It is the combination of a refined Euler–Maclaurin–Sidi asymptotics with the structural polynomiality of the spectral zeta functions. This gives that an asymptotic expansion that terminates because of trivial zeros must in fact coincide with the exact finite
n
identity. Curiously, this allows one to derive nontrivial closed formulas already at
n=1
from an argument initially designed for
n\rightarrow\infty
.

This asymptotic-to-exact mechanism seems applicable in other settings where spectral data on finite graphs approximates a continuous model, and may be of independent interest.

We prove the following theorem, and refer to
[
JKS24
]
for previous results and pointers to the extensive literature about such sums. In particular, they appear in several physics contexts.
First, let
s\mapsto a_{k}(s)
be the polynomial functions of degree
k
, given by the Taylor expansion

\left(\frac{z/2}{\sin(z/2)}\right)^{2s}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}(s)z^{2k}.

The first coefficients are
a_{0}(s)=1
,
a_{1}(s)=s/12
, and
a_{2}(s)=(5s^{2}+s)/1440
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
0<\theta<1
and integers
n,p\geq 1
, the following holds:

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sin^{-2p}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)=2^{2p}\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(k,\theta)n^{2k},

(1)

and

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\cot\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\left[\sin\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\right]^{-2p}=-\frac{2^{2p}}{2\pi p}\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)\,\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(k,\theta)n^{2k+1}.

(2)

The
\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}
is the spectral zeta function associated to a constant bundle over the circle depending on
\theta
. As such, it is a version of the Hurwitz zeta function, and the corresponding special values at integers involve Bernoulli polynomials and polygamma functions, see section
2.1
below. In particular, applying the theorem with
n=1
recovers part of a well-known reflection relation for the polygamma functions.

The formulas in Theorem
1.2
, and in Theorem
1.4
below, can be viewed as giving infinite families of recursion relations between special values. Here is the case of the Riemann zeta function:

Corollary 1.3
.

For any integers
n,p\geq 1
, the following holds:

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}(p)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(k)n^{2k}.

In particular for
n=1
,

\zeta(2p)=-\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}a_{p-k}(p)(2\pi)^{2p-2k}\zeta(2k),

and for
n=2
,

\zeta(2p)=2^{-2p-1}(2\pi)^{2p}-\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}a_{p-k}(p)2^{-2k}(2\pi)^{2p-2k}\zeta(2k).

These formulas generate infinite families of linear recursion relations among the values
\zeta(2p)
, extending earlier more isolated examples in the literature, see
[
Me17
]
for a recent study. In view of Euler’s evaluation, these formulas also translate into linear recursion relation between Bernoulli numbers. Shifting the viewpoint, they can also be viewed as Faulhaber-type formulas for trigonometric sums.

Furthermore, the theorem has the following corollary:

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\chi
be an even Dirichlet character of modulus
q
(not necessarily primitive). Then for any integers
n,p\geq 1
,

L_{n}(p,\chi)=2\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)L(2k,\chi)\left(\frac{qn}{2\pi}\right)^{2k}.

Let
\chi
be an odd Dirichlet character of modulus
q
(not necessarily primitive). Then for any integers
n,p\geq 1
,

L_{n}(p,\chi)=2\sum_{k=0}^{p}\frac{2k}{p}a_{p-k}(p)\,L(2k+1,\chi)\left(\frac{qn}{2\pi}\right)^{2k+1}.

In the case
n=1
these formulas were found by Xie, Zhao, and Zhao in
[
XZZ24
]
as mentioned above. They also displayed the corresponding formulas expressing the special values of the Dirichlet
L
-functions in terms of the discrete ones.

Zeros of Dirichlet
L
-functions.

The asymptotics in Theorem
1.1
lead to a statement about possible Siegel zeros:

Corollary 1.5

(of Theorem
1.1
)
.

Let
\chi
be a real, odd Dirichlet character. If, for each real
0<s<1
, there is a subsequence of
n\rightarrow\infty
such that
L_{n}(s,\chi)\geq 0
, then the associated Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
has no Siegel zero.

Note that in the asymptotics above, for
s
in the critical strip, the values of the Dirichlet
L
-function involved are all outside the critical strip. So in some sense, the existence of Siegel zeros is linked to certain values of
L
outside of the critical strip.

Not only is there a connection via the sign, or the vanishing, of
L_{n}(s,\chi)
, to zeros of
L(s,\chi)
, but there is a more surprising connection. Indeed, the Riemann Hypothesis (RH) in question is equivalent to a graph zeta- or
L
-function asymptotic functional equations. It has been established in the following cases:

•

\zeta(s)
in
[
FK17
]
,

•

L(s,\chi)
for primitive even Dirichlet character
\chi
in
[
F16
]

•

the Dedekind zeta function of the field of Gaussian rationals in
[
MV23
]
(requiring a
9
-point Laplacian).

In each case, the form and algebra of the proofs differ. Therefore it does not seem to be a one-time accident. In all cases, it relies on nontrivial monotonicity results of Matiyasevich-Saidak-Zvengrowski
[
MSZ14
]
. To this list we can now add the odd character version, which completes the picture in the
1
-dimensional case. Let

\xi_{n}(s,\chi)=2^{s}n^{-s}\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)L_{n}\left(\frac{s-1}{2},\chi\right).

Note that apart from the
2^{s}n^{-s}
, these fudge factors are what is needed to complete the corresponding Dirichlet
L
-function to have its functional equation in the symmetric form:

\xi(s,\chi)=w_{\chi}\xi(1-s,\overline{\chi}).

The factor
w_{\chi}
involves a Gauss sum, and has modulus
1
if
\chi
is primitive, see section
4
below.
We obtain:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\chi
be a primitive, odd Dirichlet character of conductor
q>1
. The following two statements are equivalent:

(i)

All zeros of the Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
in the strip
0<\real s<1
lie on the critical line
\real s=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

(ii)

For all
s
in the critical strip
0<\real s<1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{\xi_{n}(s,\chi)}{\xi_{n}(1-s,\overline{\chi})}\right|=1.

Remark:
In view of the standard factorization of the above-mentioned Dedekind zeta function, there are thus two graph reformulations of its RH: via cyclic graphs and via two-dimensional discrete tori.

Possible real zeros in the critical strip is a very important issue with numerous number theoretic consequences. Here is a variant of the GRH-reformulation restricted to real zeros:

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
\chi
be a primitive, odd, real Dirichlet character of conductor
q>1
. Take
0<s<1
,
s\neq 1/2
. Then
L(s,\chi)\neq 0
is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi_{n}(s,\chi)}{\xi_{n}(1-s,\overline{\chi})}=1.

We tried to use the discrete approach to rule out real zeros, but could only establish:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character, primitive of conductor
q>1
, real, even, and with the positive mean property. Then
\chi
is heat positive,
\tau(\chi)=\sqrt{q}
, and
L(s,\chi)>0
for
0<s<1
.

See section
5
for terminology. For these characters, the positivity of the Dirichlet
L
-function is already well-known from Chowla’s clever and simple proof in
[
Ch36
]
(corresponding to the case
m(\chi)=2
). We still stick to the notion that the discrete viewpoint, with one or another approach, could lead to new results on real zeros.

2
Bundle Laplacian and asymptotics

In this section we define a bundle Laplacian on the discrete circle
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
and its continuous counterpart
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. We then establish an asymptotic expansion for the spectral zeta function involved as the size of the discrete circle
n\to\infty
.

2.1
Hurwitz zeta functions

The
Hurwitz zeta function
is defined by the series

\zeta(s,\theta)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+\theta)^{-s}

for
0<\theta<1
and
\real(s)>1
. It can be analytically continued to the whole complex plane, with a simple pole at
s=1
of residue
1
. The special values of the Hurwitz zeta function at negative integers satisfy

\zeta(-n,\theta)=-\frac{B_{n+1}(\theta)}{n+1},

for
n\geq 0
, where
B_{n}(x)
are the Bernoulli polynomials defined by the generating series

\frac{te^{xt}}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}B_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

From this, we observe that

B_{n}(1-x)=(-1)^{n}B_{n}(x).

(3)

For positive integers
n
, the formulas are known to be related to the polygamma functions:

\zeta(n,\theta)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n-1)!}\psi^{(n-1)}(\theta),

where the digamma function
\psi(s)
is defined by
\psi(z)=\Gamma^{\prime}(z)/\Gamma(z)
.

2.2
Bundle Laplacian

The notion of a
vector bundle
over a finite graph is well explained in
[
Ke11
]
. To a finite graph
G
we can attach to each vertex a copy of a given vector space
V
together with the data of a
connection
, that is a map
\phi\colon E_{G}\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(V)
, where
E_{G}
is the set of edge of
G
and with the condition that
\phi(\overline{e})=(\phi(e))^{-1}
, where
\overline{e}
is the opposite edge of
e
. The bundle is said to be
unitary
if the connection takes values in
U(V)
. If
V
is one dimensional, we say that it is a
line bundle
. If
\gamma\colon\{1,\ldots,n\}\rightarrow E_{G}
is a closed path in
G
, the
monodromy
around
\gamma
is

m_{\phi}(\gamma)\coloneqq\phi(\gamma(n))\cdot\ldots\cdot\phi(\gamma(1))\in\mathrm{GL}(V).

To each
V
-bundle over
G
, we may associate a
bundle Laplacian
defined by

(\Delta_{\phi}f)(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}(f(x)-\phi_{(x,y)}f(y))

on functions
f\colon G\rightarrow V
.

2.2.1
A unitary line bundle over the discrete circle

Let
C_{n}
be the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
with generator
\{1\}
. For each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, we define a unitary line bundle over
C_{n}
associated with the constant connection
\phi_{\theta}((x,x+1))=e^{2\pi i\theta/n}
. It has monodromy
m_{\theta}(C_{n})=e^{2\pi i\theta}
. The bundle Laplacian is therefore equal to

(\Delta_{n,\theta}f)(x)=2f(x)-e^{2\pi i\theta/n}f(x+1)-e^{-2\pi i\theta/n}f(x-1),

for functions
f\colon C_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. In fact,
\Delta_{n,\theta}=(d_{\theta})^{*}d_{\theta}
for the difference operator
d_{\theta}\colon l^{2}(C_{n})\rightarrow l^{2}(E_{C_{n}})
defined by

(d_{\theta}f)(e)=e^{\pi i\theta/n}f(e^{+})-e^{-\pi i\theta/n}f(e^{-}),

where
e=(e^{-},e^{+})
is the edge that starts from
e^{-}
and ends at
e^{+}
. It is diagonalized by Fourier with spectrum

\sigma(\Delta_{n,\theta})=\left\{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\mid j=0,\ldots,n-1\right\}.

When
\theta\notin\mathbb{Z}
the spectral zeta function associated with
\Delta_{n,\theta}
is the entire function

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)=\zeta_{n}(s,\theta)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\right]^{-s}.

(4)

If
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}
the bundle Laplacian is unitary equivalent to the standard combinatorial Laplacian. Therefore its zeta function is the spectral zeta function on
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
,

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi j}{n}\right)\right]^{-s}.

We will also need the derivative with respect to
\theta
of this spectral function:

\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{n}(s,\theta)=-s\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\cot\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\right]^{-s}.

(5)

2.2.2
The continuous circle

In the same way, we define the bundle Laplacian on the circle associated with the unitary connection
\mathrm{d}+2\pi i\theta\mathrm{d}x
where we identify the circle with
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. We denote it by
\Delta_{\theta}
and it is simply

\Delta_{\theta}=-(\mathrm{d}+2\pi i\theta)^{2}.

It is again diagonalized by Fourier and has spectrum

\sigma(\Delta_{\theta})=\{4\pi^{2}(n+\theta)^{2}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

For
\theta\notin\mathbb{Z}
, the spectral zeta function associated to
\Delta_{\theta}
is therefore

\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2s}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|n+\theta|^{-2s}=(2\pi)^{-2s}\left[\zeta(2s,\theta)+\zeta(2s,1-\theta)\right].

The relation (
3
) implies that
\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)
vanishes at negative integers
n\leq 0
.
If
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}
, again
\Delta_{\theta}
is unitary equivalent to the usual Laplacian, its spectral zeta is therefore the spectral zeta function of the circle

\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s)=2(2\pi)^{-2s}\zeta(2s),

where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function. Observe that

\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s)=\lim_{\theta\to 0}\left(\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)-(2\pi\theta)^{-2s}\right).

(6)

Analogously to the discrete case, we compute its derivative with respect to
\theta
;

\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)=-2s(2\pi)^{-2s}\bigl[\zeta(1+2s,\theta)-\zeta(1+2s,1-\theta)\bigr].

Observe that for
0<\theta<1
, this is an entire function of
s
, with

-\frac{1}{2s}\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)=\psi(1-\theta)-\psi(\theta)+\mathcal{O}(s)=\pi\cot\pi\theta+\mathcal{O}(s).

2.3
Asymptotics

The main tool of this article is the following asymptotic expansion deduced from the Euler-MacLaurin formula adapted for singularities by Sidi in
[
Sid12
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
s\neq\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{5}{2},\ldots
, any
0<\theta<1
, and any
m\geq 0
, the following asymptotics hold as
n\to\infty
:

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)=n\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)+n^{2s}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}(s)\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s-k,\theta)n^{-2k}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2-2m})\right),

(7)

and without restriction on
s
,

\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}(s,\theta)=n^{2s}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}(s)\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s-k,\theta)n^{-2k}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2-2m})\right),

(8)

where the coefficients
a_{k}(s)
are defined in the introduction.

Remark 2.2
.

One might be tempted to deduce the second formula by differentiating the first with respect to
\theta
. We believe that this argument can be made rigorous, although we do not have control on the rest present in the asymptotic with respect to
\theta
. We therefore instead apply the Euler-MacLaurin-Sidi formula twice.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

It is a direct application of Theorem 2.1 of
[
Sid12
]
. Take any
0<\theta<1
, and
s\neq\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{5}{2},\ldots
and consider the function
f_{s}(z)=[4\sin^{2}(\pi z)]^{-s}
, which has the asymptotic for
z\to 0^{+}
, and by symmetry, as well for
z\to 1^{-}
:

f_{s}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}(s)(2\pi z)^{2(k-s)}.

Theorem 2.1 of
[
Sid12
]
implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f_{s}\left(\frac{j+\theta}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{1}f_{s}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}(s)(\zeta(2(s-k),\theta)+\zeta(2(s-k),1-\theta))\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)^{2(k-s)}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(n^{2(s-m-1)}).

The first asymptotics follow by recalling that

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\left[4\sin^{2}(\pi x)\right]^{-s}\mathrm{d}x,

and rearranging the terms.
To obtain the second asymptotics, we used again Theorem 2.1 in
[
Sid12
]
, but with the function
g_{s}(z)\coloneqq\cot(\pi z)f_{s}(z)
, which is odd around the line
\re z=1/2,
that is,
g_{s}(1-z)=-g_{s}(z)
, and therefore has asymptotic

g_{s}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}b_{k}(s)(2\pi z)^{2(k-s)-1},

for
z\to 0^{+}
and opposite asymptotic for
z\to 1^{-}
. By the Euler-MacLaurin-Sidi formula we obtain for any
s\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}g_{s}\left(\frac{j+\theta}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{1}g_{s}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{n}{2\pi}\delta_{s}(\psi(1-\theta)-\psi(\theta))

\displaystyle+\underset{k\neq s}{\sum_{0\leq k\leq m}}b_{k}(s)(\zeta(1+2(s-k),\theta)-\zeta(1+2(s-k),1-\theta))\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)^{2(k-s)-1}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(n^{1+2(s-m-1)}),

where
\delta_{s}=0
if
s\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and
\delta_{s}=b_{s}(s)
otherwise. Now observe first that since
g_{s}
is odd around
\re z=1/2
, the integral is identically
0
. We can incorporate the term with
\delta_{s}
in the main sum and rewrite the asymptotic as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}g_{s}\left(\frac{j+\theta}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2\pi s}\Bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{m}b_{k}(s)\frac{s}{2(s-k)}\partial_{\theta}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s-k,\theta)n^{2(s-k)}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(n^{2(s-m-1)})\Bigg],

which is the desired asymptotic when multiplied by
-\tfrac{2\pi s}{n}
, since

a_{k}(s)=b_{k}(s)\frac{s}{2(s-k)}.

∎

Theorem 3.2

(

Let
\chi

L_{n}(s,\chi)=2\left(\frac{2\pi}{qn}\right)^{-2s}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}(s)\left(\frac{2\pi}{qn}\right)^{2k}L(2(s-k),\chi)+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2-2m})\right],

Proof of Theorem
3.2

For any non-principal Dirichlet character
\chi

\chi

\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\chi(r)(\partial_{\theta}^{a}\zeta_{n})(s,r/q)=\left(-s\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)^{a}\cdot L_{n}(s,\chi).

and

\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\chi(r)(\partial_{\theta}^{a}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}})(s,r/q)=(-4s\pi)^{a}\cdot 2\left(\frac{q}{2\pi}\right)^{2s+a}L(2s+a,\chi).

Therefore, for any
\chi
non-principal, we obtain :

\displaystyle L_{n}(s,\chi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\chi(r)\partial_{\theta}^{a}\zeta_{n}(s,r/q)\left(\frac{n}{-2\pi s}\right)^{a}

\displaystyle=n^{2s}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}(s)\left(\frac{1}{-2\pi s}\right)^{a}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\chi(r)(\partial_{\theta}^{a}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}})(r/q,s-k)n^{a-2k}+\mathcal{O}(n^{a-2-2m})\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{qn}{2\pi}\right)^{2s}\left(a_{k}(s)\left(\frac{2(s-k)}{s}\right)^{a}L(a+2(s-k),\chi)\left(\frac{qn}{2\pi}\right)^{a-2k}+\mathcal{O}(n^{a-2-2m})\right)

Which concludes the proof of both Theorem

and Theorem

∎

Remark 3.3

\chi

4
Equivalence with GRH for odd character.

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.6
.
Recall that the following result is proven in
[
F16
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem 1.3 of
[
F16
]
)
.

Let
\chi
be a primitive and even character of conductor
q>1
. The completed
L
-function of
\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
is defined by

\xi_{n}(s,\chi)\coloneqq 2^{s}\,n^{-s}\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)L_{n}(\tfrac{s}{2},\chi),

for
0<\re s<1
. Then the following statements are equivalent

(i)

For all
0<\re s<1
such that
\im s\geq 8
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{\xi_{n}(s,\chi)}{\xi_{n}(1-s,\overline{\chi})}\right|=1.

(ii)

In the region
\im s\geq 8
, all the zeros of the
L
-function associated to
\chi
have real part
1/2
.

For
\chi
odd, primitive and of conductor
q>1
, the completed Dirichlet
L
-function is defined to be

\xi(s,\chi)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)L(s,\chi),

and it satisfy the functional equation

\xi(s,\chi)=w_{\chi}\xi(1-s,\overline{\chi}),

where

w_{\chi}=\frac{\tau(\chi)}{i\sqrt{q}},

with
\tau(\chi)
the Gauss sum

\tau(\chi)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\chi(j)e^{\frac{2\pi ij}{q}}.

It satisfies
|\tau(\chi)|=\sqrt{q}
and therefore
|w_{\chi}|=1
.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
that we present here is an adaptation of the proof of Theorem
4.1
presented in
[
F16
]
, with some modifications coming from
\chi
being odd. We start with a lemma, which is proven in
[
MSZ14
]

Lemma 4.2

(Corollary 2.6L in
[
MSZ14
]
)
.

Fix
t
real and write
s=\sigma+it
, then the function

\sigma\mapsto|\xi(s-2,\chi)|,

is strictly decreasing in the interval
0<\sigma<1
.

In fact, in Corollary 2.6L of
[
MSZ14
]
, they showed that

\sigma\mapsto|\xi(s,\chi)|

is strictly decreasing in the region
\sigma\leq 0
for any non-principal primitive character.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Recall that

\xi_{n}(s,\chi)\coloneqq 2^{s}n^{-s}\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)L_{n}\left(\frac{s-1}{2},\chi\right),

and therefore the asymptotic (
9
) gives

\xi_{n}(s,\chi)=4\xi(s,\chi)+\beta(s,\chi)n^{-2}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-4})

where

\beta(s,\chi)=\frac{\pi}{q}\frac{(s-1)(s-3)}{6}\xi(s-2,\chi).

First observe that if
\xi(s,\chi)\neq 0
then clearly we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi_{n}(s,\chi)}{\xi_{n}(1-s,\overline{\chi})}=w_{\chi}.

However, since
0<\re s<1
, we know that
\beta(s,\chi)\neq 0
and
\beta(1-s,\overline{\chi})\neq 0
, therefore if
\xi(s,\chi)=0
, we have that the following equality

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{\xi_{n}(s,\chi)}{\xi_{n}(1-s,\overline{\chi})}\right|=1

is equivalent to

|\beta(s,\chi)|=|\beta(1-s,\overline{\chi})|.

Now, the previous lemma implies directly that
|\beta(s,\chi)|
is strictly decreasing in the strip
0<\re s<1
, for any non-principal primitive character
\chi
. This shows that the equality
|\beta(s,\chi)|=|\beta(1-s,\overline{\chi})|
can happen at most once in the region
0<\re s<1
when the imaginary part is fixed. Moreover we know that equality holds at
\re s=1/2
, because for such a
s
,

\displaystyle\beta(1-s,\overline{\chi})

\displaystyle=\beta(\overline{s},\overline{\chi})

\displaystyle=\overline{\beta(s,\chi)}.

This shows the claimed equivalence.
∎

We now specialize the previous theorem when
\chi
is real, odd and for real zeroes of
L
. Recall that for such a
\chi
, Gauss himself proved that
w_{\chi}=1
. Therefore, as in the previous proof, for real
0<s<1
, if
\xi(s,\chi)\neq 0
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi_{n}(s,\chi)}{\xi_{n}(1-s,\overline{\chi})}=1.

Moreover, for
0<s<1
,
\beta(s,\chi)
is real and strictly decreasing. Therefore, in the case
\xi(s,\chi)=0
, then we obtain the equivalence

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi_{n}(s,\chi)}{\xi_{n}(1-s,\overline{\chi})}=1

\displaystyle\iff\beta(s,\chi)=\beta(1-s,\overline{\chi})

\displaystyle\iff s=1/2.

We therefore have proven Corollary
1.7
.

Remark 4.3
.

Using the exact same method as in the proof of Theorem
1.6
, one can reduce the lower bound of
8
in the imaginary part of Theorem
4.1
to less than

. However, we believe that one would need an improvement of this monotonicity argument to erase completely this lower bound.

5
Real zeros of Dirichlet
L
-functions

5.1
No real zero for Dirichlet
L
-functions with heat positive character

Let
\chi
be an even, real and primitive Dirichlet character modulo
q>1
. The starting point is the following asymptotics first established by Friedli and reproved in section
3
above, Theorem
3.2
:

For any fixed
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the following asymptotics, for
n\to\infty
, hold

L_{n}(s,\chi)=2\left(\frac{qn}{2\pi}\right)^{2s}\left(L(s,\chi)+\frac{s}{12}\left(\frac{qn}{2\pi}\right)^{-2}L(s-2,\chi)+\mathcal{O}(n^{-4})\right).

(10)

Now note that since
L(s,\chi)>0
for
s>1
real, the functional equation for even and primitive characters tells us that
L(s,\chi)
is negative for
-2<s<0
. Indeed, for
\chi
primitive of conductor
q>1
, real and even, we know that
L(s,\chi)
is entire and has the functional equation

\xi(s,\chi)=\frac{\tau(\chi)}{\sqrt{q}}\xi(1-s,\chi),

(11)

where
\xi(s,\chi)\coloneqq(\pi/q)^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)L(s,\chi)
and
\tau(\chi)
is the Gauss sum. It is well-known that for the characters we consider we have
\tau(\chi)=\sqrt{q}
, which shows that
\xi(s,\chi)
is even around
s=1/2
.

We say that
\chi
is
heat positive
if for some sequence
n_{i}\rightarrow\infty
, the following inequality,

\sum_{j=1}^{qn_{i}-1}\chi(j)e^{-t4\sin^{2}(\pi j/qn_{i})}\geq 0

(12)

holds for all
t\geq 0

We have the following corollary of the asymptotics:

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\chi
be a real, even, primitive Dirichlet character of conductor
q>1
and which is heat positive. Then
L(s,\chi)>0
for
0<s<1
.

Proof.

The Mellin transform of the sum in the hypothesis gives
L_{n}(s,\chi)\cdot\Gamma(s)
. The
\Gamma
factor is positive on
s>0
, and hence heat positivity implies that
L_{n_{i}}(s,\chi)>0
. Therefore, since
L(s-2,\chi)<0
for
0<s<1
as remarked above, the asymptotics (
10
) force
L(\chi,s)>0
.
∎

Which characters are heat positive? Characters with small
q
and with many partial sums of the character being positive. This points to Chowla and is closely linked to the classical theta function approach, see
[
LM13
]
. For this reason, at present time, this section does not give any new results on the positivity of
L(s,\chi)
on
0<s<1
. The question is whether the Mellin transform is always positive even when there is a window of
t
’s, moving with
n
, where the character is not heat positive.

5.2
First examples of heat positive characters

For illustration, we will prove a simple result:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\chi
be a real, even, primitive Dirichlet character, primitive of conductor
q>1
. Then the inequality (
12
) holds for all
t
and
n
such that
0<t<n^{2}/2\log 2
and
t>q^{2}n^{2}\log 2/4(16-\pi^{2})
.

Due to the appearance of
q
in one inequality and not the other, the proposition only implies that all characters with
q<7
heat positive (for all
n
). This can be significantly strengthened assuming some partial sums of
\chi
positive and using partial summation. One such case is treated in the next subsection.

The proof of Proposition
5.2
starts by writing
L_{n}(s,\chi)
as the Mellin transform of a solution of a particular discrete heat equation. Using Fourier transform and the heat kernel on the discrete circle
\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
, one gets two expressions for the heat solution.

Recall that the discrete Laplace operator
\Delta
on the discrete group
\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
is defined by

(\Delta f)(x)\coloneqq 2f(x)-f(x+1)-f(x-1),

(13)

acting on functions
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z})
. This operator is positive semi-definite and the eigenvalues are

\lambda_{j}\coloneqq 4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi j}{qn}\right)\text{, with }j\in\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}.

(14)

The discrete heat kernel associated is defined to be the one parameter family of operator

t\mapsto e^{-t\Delta},

and is realized by convolution against the function

K_{n}(t,x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{qn}\sum_{j=0}^{qn-1}e^{-t4\sin^{2}\left(\pi j/qn\right)}e^{2\pi ixj/qn}.

(15)

This function is the fundamental solution of the discrete heat equation

\begin{cases}(\Delta+\partial_{t})K_{n}=0\\
K_{n}\big|_{t=0}=\delta_{0}.\end{cases}

(16)

Let
\delta_{j}
be the Dirac function at
j\in\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
, that is
\delta_{j}(x)=1
if
x=j
and
\delta_{j}(x)=0
else.
Let us now consider the discrete heat equation with a special initial condition associated to the character
\chi
, namely

\begin{cases}(\Delta+\partial_{t})u=0\\
u\big|_{t=0}=\chi^{\flat},\end{cases}

(17)

with the initial condition being
\chi^{\flat}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\chi(j)\delta_{jn}
.

The heat kernel on
\mathbb{Z}
is the discrete Gaussian
K_{\mathbb{Z}}(t,n)=e^{-2t}I_{n}(2t)
, where
I
denotes the
I
-Bessel function, see
[
CJKS25
]
for a discussion on this. From usual consideration on covering of graphs, one obtains the formula

K_{n}(t,x)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{\mathbb{Z}}(t,x+jqn)=e^{-2t}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{x+jqn}(2t)

(18)

Proposition 5.3
.

The solution to (
17
) has the following two expressions

\frac{\tau(\chi)}{qn}\sum_{\omega=1}^{qn-1}\chi(\omega)e^{-t4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\omega}{qn}\right)}e^{2\pi i\frac{x\omega}{qn}}=\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\chi(j)K_{n}(t,x-jn).

In particular, for
x=0

\tau(\chi)\sum_{j=1}^{qn-1}\chi(j)e^{-t4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi j}{qn}\right)}=qn\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\chi(j)K_{n}(t,jn).

(19)

Proof.

First, taking the Fourier transform of the equation (
17
) leads to the equation

\begin{cases}(\Psi_{n}+\partial_{t})\widehat{u}=0\\
\widehat{u}\big|_{t=0}=\chi^{\sharp},\end{cases}

(20)

where
\chi^{\sharp}=\widehat{\chi^{\flat}}
is the Fourier transform of
\chi^{\flat}
and where
\Psi_{n}
is the diagonal operator of multiplication by the spectrum of
\Delta
, namely

(\Psi_{n}\rho)(\omega)=4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\omega}{qn}\right)\rho(\omega)

. One computes for
\omega\in\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\chi^{\sharp}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\chi(j)\widehat{\delta_{jn}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\chi(j)e^{-2\pi ij\omega/q}.

But it is well known that when
\chi
is primitive, this last sum is
\overline{\chi(-\omega)}\tau(\chi)
where
\tau(\chi)
is the Gauss sum. Therefore, since
\chi
is supposed to be real, even and primitive, one gets

\widehat{u}(t,\omega)=\tau(\chi)\chi(\omega)e^{-t4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\omega}{qn}\right)}.

Taking the inverse Fourier transform and discarding the term
\chi(0)=0
it gives

u(t,x)=\frac{\tau(\chi)}{qn}\sum_{\omega=1}^{qn-1}\chi(\omega)e^{-t4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\omega}{qn}\right)}e^{2\pi i\frac{x\omega}{qn}},

(21)

since we consider
\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
as discrete, the dual measure (with respect to Fourier) is therefore the compact one on
\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
. Taking the trace of this solution viewed as a operator acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z})
by convolution amounts to putting
x=0
and multiplying by
qn
, which gives

\Tr(u(t))=\tau(\chi)\sum_{j=1}^{qn-1}\chi(j)e^{-t4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi j}{qn}\right)}.

(22)

Now, as usual, the solution
u
is as well given by convolution of the heat kernel with the initial condition
\chi^{\flat}
,

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left(K_{n}(t)\ast\chi^{\flat}\right)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\chi(j)\cdot\left(K_{n}(t)\ast\delta_{jn}\right)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\chi(j)K_{n}(t,x-jn).

The symmetry of the heat kernel
K_{n}(t,-x)=K_{n}(t,x)
finishes the proof.
∎

Taking the Mellin transform of the left hand side of (
19
) gives for
\re s>0

\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Tr(u(t))t^{s}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}=\tau(\chi)L_{n}(s,\chi).

Note that the formula (
18
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\chi(j)K_{n}(t,jn)

\displaystyle=e^{-2t}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\chi(j)I_{n(j+lq)}(2t)

\displaystyle=e^{-2t}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\chi(m)I_{mn}(2t)

\displaystyle=2e^{-2t}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\chi(m)I_{mn}(2t).

The last inequality is true since
\chi
is even,
I_{-k}=I_{k}
, and since
\chi(0)=0
.
The idea is now that for any fixed
t>0
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
the whole expression tends to
0
, but with the
m=1
term dominating, giving something positive, since
\chi(1)=1
and the heat kernel is positive.

Since
\chi
takes values in
-1,0,1
and the
I
-Bessel function
is positive, we have in the worst case:

\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\chi(j)K_{n}(t,jn)>2e^{-2t}\left(I_{n}(2t)-\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}I_{mn}(2t)\right).

We proceed by comparing
I_{n}
with
I_{mn}
. In
[
Am74
]
one finds that

\frac{I_{k+1}(x)}{I_{k}(x)}\leq\frac{x}{k+\sqrt{k^{2}+x^{2}}}\leq\frac{x}{k+x}

for
k\leq 0
and
x>0
. This leads to

\frac{I_{(m+1)n}}{I_{mn}}\leq\frac{I_{mn+1}}{I_{mn}}\frac{I_{mn+2}}{I_{mn+1}}...\frac{I_{(m+1)n}}{I_{mn+n-1}}\leq\frac{x}{mn+x}\frac{x}{mn+1+x}...\frac{x}{mn+n-1+x}

\leq\left(\frac{x}{n+x}\right)^{n}.

Let
c_{n}(t)=\left(t/(n+t)\right)^{n}<1
. This means that

I_{n}(t)\geq I_{mn}(t)c_{n}(t)^{m-1}.

We apply this inequality to our expression:

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\chi(m)I_{mn}(2t)>I_{n}(2t)\left(1-\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}c_{n}(2t)^{m-1}\right).

So the question is now when do we have

\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}c(2t)^{m-1}=c_{n}(2t)/(1-c_{n}(2t))<1.

In other words
c_{n}(2t)<1/2
. This is equivalent to

(n+2t)^{n}>2^{1+n}t^{n}

and

n+2t>2^{1/n+1}t

n>2t(2^{1/n}-1)=2t(1+\frac{1}{n}\log 2+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\log 2}{n}\right)^{2}+...-1)>2t\frac{\log 2}{n}

In conclusion, whenever
n^{2}>2\log 2\cdot t
the heat solution at
x=0
, the right hand side of (
19
) is positive. Numerically, for all
0<t<0.72n^{2}
the expression is positive.

We now study large
t
using instead the spectral expression of the heat solution. In other words, let us consider the other expression of the identity (
19
), taking away
\tau(\chi)
.
So we are interested in

\sum_{j=1}^{qn-1}\chi(j)e^{-t4\sin^{2}(\pi j/qn)}

for all large
t
and fixed
n
. The idea is again that
j=1

and
j=qn-1
should dominate. Using that
\chi
is even, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{(qn-1)/2}\chi(j)e^{-t4\sin^{2}(\pi j/qn)}>\chi(1)e^{-4t\sin^{2}(\pi/qn)}-\sum_{j=2}^{(qn-1)/2}e^{-t4\sin^{2}(\pi j/qn)}=

=e^{-4t\sin^{2}(\pi/qn)}\left(1-e^{4t\sin^{2}(\pi/qn)}\sum_{j=2}^{(qn-1)/2}e^{-t4\sin^{2}(\pi j/qn)}\right).

Focusing on the sign of the parentheses, we now use the inequalities

x\frac{2}{\pi}\leq\sin(x)\leq x
in
0\leq x\leq\pi/2.

1-e^{4t\sin^{2}(\pi/qn)}\sum_{j=2}^{(qn-1)/2}e^{-t4\sin^{2}(\pi j/qn)}>1-e^{4t\pi^{2}/q^{2}n^{2}}\sum_{j=2}^{(qn-1)/2}e^{-4t\pi^{2}j^{2}/q^{2}n^{2}\cdot\left(4/\pi^{2}\right)}>

>1-e^{4t\pi^{2}/q^{2}n^{2}}e^{-16t4/q^{2}n^{2}}\frac{1}{1-e^{-16t5/q^{2}n^{2}}}.

So we want t such that

e^{4t\pi^{2}/q^{2}n^{2}}e^{-16t4/q^{2}n^{2}}<1-e^{-16t5/q^{2}n^{2}}

or equivalently

e^{4t\pi^{2}/q^{2}n^{2}}e^{-16t4/q^{2}n^{2}}+e^{-16t5/q^{2}n^{2}}<1.

It is valid if
t>q^{2}n^{2}\log 2/4(16-\pi^{2})\approx 0.028q^{2}n^{2}

or with better numeric
t>0.015q^{2}n^{2}
. Compare this to the above
0.72.

So we covered all of t as long as
q<7.

In conclusion, this shows that all even, real, primitive character with conductor less than or equal to
7
are heat positive, and their Dirichlet
L
-function has no real zeros in the unit interval.

5.3
Further examples of heat positive characters

With another argument, we show heat positivity without any a priori condition on
q
, but under a stronger hypothesis on the character.

Definition 5.4

(The positive mean property)
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character, primitive of conductor
q>1
, real and even. We say that
\chi
have the
positive mean property
if the following

\sum_{j=0}^{m}\chi(j)\geq 0,

holds for all integer
0\leq m\leq q/2
.

Note that in the literature, such positivity is usually assumed to hold for all
m<q
in the case of odd characters.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character, primitive of conductor
q>1
, real, even and with the positive mean property. Then
\chi
is heat positive and
L(s,\chi)>0
for
0<s<1
.

This was already proved by Chowla in
[
Ch36
]
in a quick way with a clever formula (corresponding to the case that
m(\chi)=2
).

Proof.

Look at the right hand side of (
19
), let
q^{\prime}\coloneqq\lfloor q/2\rfloor
and use symmetry plus Abel summation to get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\chi(j)K_{n}(t,jn)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=0}^{q^{\prime}}\chi(j)K_{n}(t,jn)

\displaystyle=2\left[\sum_{j=0}^{q^{\prime}}\chi(j)\right]K_{n}(t,q^{\prime}n)+

\displaystyle-2\sum_{j=0}^{q^{\prime}-1}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{j}\chi(k)\right](K_{n}(t,(j+1)n)-K_{n}(t,jn)).

The expressions in bracket
[.]
are positive by assumption on
\chi
, it remains only to show that the heat kernel is positive and decreasing with respect to the distance from
0
in the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}/qn\mathbb{Z}
with the generating set
\{\pm 1\}
.

Precisely, let
N\geq 3
, and
\Delta
be the discrete Laplace operator on
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
. Let also
K(t)=\exp(-t\Delta)\delta_{0}
the discrete heat kernel on
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
and define for
x\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
,
h(x)\coloneqq\min\{|x|,|N-x|\}
to be the distance of
x
from
0
in the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
.
The required monotonicity is established in Lemma
5.6
below.

Therefore we have proven that the trace
\Tr(u(t))
is positive for all
t\geq 0
, but since
\Gamma(s)>0
for all
s>0
, we conclude that the Mellin transform of
\Tr(u(t))
is also positive in the range
s>0
. This finishes the proof of Theorem
5.5
, in view of Corollary
5.1
. The positivity of
\tau(\chi)
follows from combining this with the positivity established in Theorem
5.2
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

With the notations as above, the heat kernel
K(t)
is positive and decreasing with respect to
h
. That is for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, we have

h(x)\leq h(y)\Longrightarrow K(t,y)\leq K(t,x).

Proof.

Consider the operator
S\coloneqq 3-\Delta
, defined by
(Sf)(x)\coloneqq f(x-1)+f(x)+f(x+1)
, which is the generator of the random walk on
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
allowing the walk to stand still. Let us also
\partial
be the forward difference operator
(\partial f)(x)\coloneqq f(x+1)-f(x)
. Then
K(t)=e^{-3t}\exp(tS)\delta_{0}
, and therefore we need only to analyze
\phi(t)\coloneqq\exp(tS)\delta_{0}
. But clearly,

\phi(t,x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}c_{n}(x),

where

c_{n}(x)=\#\{\text{paths from }0\text{ to }x\text{ of length }n\text{, allowing the path to stand still}\},

which shows that
\phi(t)
is positive, but most importantly, the function
c_{n}
satisfy the recurrence relation

c_{n+1}=Sc_{n}

for all
n\geq 0
. Therefore, for all
n\geq 0
, we have

\partial c_{n+1}=S\partial c_{n}.

Now, for
n=0
, we have
c_{0}=\delta_{0}
which indeed is decreasing with respect to the height
h
. By induction, suppose that
c_{n}
is decreasing with respect to the height
h
. It means exactly that
\partial c_{n}(x)\leq 0
for all
0\leq x<N^{\prime}
, where
N^{\prime}\coloneqq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, now look at

\displaystyle c_{n+1}(x+1)-c_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=(c_{n}(x)-c_{n}(x-1))

\displaystyle+(c_{n}(x+1)-c_{n}(x))

\displaystyle+(c_{n}(x+2)-c_{n}(x+1)).

If
0<x<N^{\prime}-1
, then every term on the right is negative by hypothesis on
c_{n}
, if
x=0
, then the right side is reduce to
c_{n}(2)-c_{n}(1)
which is negative and if
x=N^{\prime}-1
, then the right side is reduce to
c_{n}(N^{\prime}-1)-c_{n}(N^{\prime}-2)
which is also negative. We have proven that
\partial c_{n+1}(x)\leq 0
for all
0\leq x<N^{\prime}
, which conclude the induction and therefore the proof that
\phi(t)
is decreasing with respect to
h
, so is
K(t)
.
∎

6
Special values

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
, and by doing so we link special values of both discrete zeta functions and discrete
L
-functions with special values of the Riemann zeta function and Dirichlet
L
-functions, respectively. For this, we first use the Laplace transform of the discrete heat kernel on the
\theta
-bundle over
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
together with the characteristic polynomial of the bundle Laplacian
\Delta_{n,\theta}
(namely, the Chebyshev polynomials) to obtain a polynomial behavior of the discrete special value. Combining this polynomial behavior with the asymptotics of Theorem
2.1
, we obtain closed formulas for the zeta functions associated. By using standard formulas for
L
-functions and specializing
\theta
accordingly, we obtain the desired relation between discrete and continuous zeta and
L
-functions.

6.1
Formulas

In terms of discrete zeta function on the
\theta
-bundle, Theorem
1.2
says that for any
0<\theta<1
and integers
n,p\geq 1
, we have

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}(p,\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(k,\theta)n^{2k}.

(23)

As above, the maps
s\mapsto a_{k}(s)
are polynomial functions of degree
k
, given by the Taylor expansion

\left(\frac{z/2}{\sin z/2}\right)^{2s}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}(s)z^{2k}.

The Theorem
1.2
leads to the formulas in Corollary
1.4
, linking discrete and classical special values for zeta and Dirichlet
L
-functions. They were first obtained by Xie, Zhao, and Zhao in the case
n=1
.

Moreover, one can deduced the case of
\theta=0
as well, which has a long history and is connected to Verlinde formulas, see for example
[
Za96
, Theorem 1.iii]
. Here we put large emphasis on spectral zeta function involved in this formula. Namely,

Corollary 6.1
.

For any integers
n,p\geq 1
, the following holds:

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}(p)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(k)n^{2k}.

6.2
Characteristic polynomial of the bundle Laplacian.

Recall from
2.2.1
that
\Delta_{n,\theta}
is the bundle Laplacian on
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
associated to the parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
defined by

(\Delta_{n,\theta}f)(x)\coloneqq 2f(x)-e^{2\pi i\theta/n}f(x+1)-e^{-2\pi i\theta/n}f(x-1),

on functions
f\colon\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

The characteristic polynomial of
\Delta_{n,\theta}
is

P_{n,\theta}(x)=\det(\Delta_{n,\theta}-x)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)-x\right],

and in fact it can be expressed in terms of Chebyshev polynomials.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
T_{n}
be the
n
-th Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind. Then the characteristic polynomial of
\Delta_{n,\theta}
satisfies

T_{n}(1-2x)-\cos(2 \pi\theta)=\frac{1}{2}P_{n,\theta}(4x).

(24)

Proof.

Recall that
T_{n}
satisfies

T_{n}(\cos(x))=\cos(nx),

and therefore the polynomial

Q_{n,\theta}(x)\coloneqq T_{n}(1-2x)-\cos(2 \pi\theta)

vanishes at the numbers

\mu_{j}\coloneqq\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right).

If
\theta\notin\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
, then all the
\mu_{j}
are distinct and therefore

Q_{n,\theta}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)-4x\right],

since
T_{n}(x)
is of degree
n
and has leading coefficient
2^{n-1}
. By continuity of the map
\theta\mapsto Q_{n,\theta}(x)
, this last factorization of
Q_{n,\theta}
holds for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. This implies the desired result.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

The expansions of
T_{n}(z)
near
z=1
(see p. 779 of
[
AS64
]
) are well known and combined with Lemma
6.2
yield the equality

\det(x+\Delta_{n,\theta})=2(1-\cos(2\pi\theta))+2n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(n+k-1)!}{(n-k)!(2k)!}x^{k}.

(25)

First of all, this formula is of particular interest when
\theta=0
, since we recover the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of the discrete Laplacian
\Delta_{n}
, which in turn, by the famous Kirchhoff matrix tree theorem, are exactly the numbers of rooted
k
-spanning forests of the cyclic graph
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, where
k
stands for the number of components in the forest.

Secondly, and most importantly for us, it shows that if we write
T_{\theta}
for the map
T_{\theta}(n,x)\coloneqq P_{n,\theta}(x)
, then
T_{\theta}\in(\mathbb{C}[n])[[x]]
and is invertible in this ring if and only if
\theta\notin\mathbb{Z}
. This will be one of the main ingredients in our proof.

6.3
Laplace transform of the bundle heat kernel

The heat kernel of the bundle Laplacian
\Delta_{n,\theta}
on
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
is defined to be the one-parameter family of operators

K_{n,\theta}(t)\coloneqq e^{-t\Delta_{n,\theta}}.

One obtains its trace directly by spectral analysis:

\Tr[K_{n,\theta}(t)]=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e^{-t4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)},

and therefore its Laplace transform is given for
\re s<0
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\Tr[K_{n,\theta}]](s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{st}\Tr[K_{n,\theta}(t)]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-t(4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)-s)}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)-s}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\log[P_{n,\theta}(s)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{T_{n}^{\prime}(1-s/2)}{T_{n}(1-s/2)-\cos(2 \pi\theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{nU_{n-1}(1-s/2)}{T_{n}(1-s/2)-\cos(2 \pi\theta)}.

where we used that
T_{n}^{\prime}=nU_{n-1}
, with
U_{n}
the
n
-th Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind. If
\theta\notin\mathbb{Z}
, then

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)-s}=\sum_{p\geq 0}s^{p}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\right]^{-p-1}.

In fact, we have just shown:

Lemma 6.4
.

If
0<\theta<1
, the following equality holds:

\mathcal{L}[\Tr[K_{n,\theta}]](s)=\sum_{p\geq 0}s^{p}\zeta_{n}(p+1,\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{nU_{n-1}(1-s/2)}{T_{n}(1-s/2)-\cos(2 \pi\theta)},

(26)

in a non-trivial interval
-\varepsilon_{\theta}<s<0
, for some
\varepsilon_{\theta}>0
.

Corollary 6.5
.

For any
0<\theta<1
and for any integer
p\geq 1
, the map

n\mapsto\zeta_{n}(p,\theta)

is a polynomial function of
n
.

Proof.

It is a direct consequence of Lemma
6.4
, since the generating function of
\zeta_{n}(p,\theta)
satisfies

\sum_{p\geq 0}s^{p}\zeta_{n}(p+1,\theta)=\frac{nU_{n-1}(1-s/2)}{2(T_{n}(1-s/2)-\cos(2 \pi\theta))}.

By the equation (
25
), both the numerator and the denominator are in
(\mathbb{C}[n])[[s]]
, with the denominator invertible in this ring since
T_{n}(1)=1
.
∎

6.4
Proof of the theorems and their corollaries

First recall from the asymptotic of Theorem
2.1
that when
s\in\mathbb{C}
is not an half integer,
0<\theta<1
,
m\geq 0
, the following holds as
n\to\infty
;

\zeta_{n}(s,\theta)=n\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)+n^{2s}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}(s)\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s-k,\theta)n^{-2k}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2-2m})\right).

(27)

Where the
a_{k}(s)
are given in the introduction.

Since
\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}
vanishes at negative integers, one could expect that the asymptotic becomes an exact formula when
s
is a positive integer. Indeed, this is the formula (
23
). It will be a direct consequence of Corollary
6.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

When
s=p
is a positive integer, the left-hand side of the asymptotic of (
27
) is a polynomial in
n
. Since
m\geq 0
can be chosen arbitrarily, this implies that the asymptotic expansion terminates and becomes an exact formula. As
\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}
vanishes at positive integers, see
[
FK17
, Theorem 2.2]
, the formula (
23
) follows and therefore Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
6.1
.

Fix
n,p\geq 1
and let
0<\theta<1
. In the left hand side of formula (
23
), the term
j=0
can be expanded as

\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{n}\right)\right]^{-p}=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)\left(\frac{n}{2\pi\theta}\right)^{2k}+\mathcal{O}(\theta^{2}),

move it from the left hand side to the right hand side to obtain :

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(j+\theta)}{n}\right)\right]^{-p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(k,\theta)n^{2k}-\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{n}\right)\right]^{-p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{p-k}(p)(\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(2k,\theta)-(2\pi\theta)^{-2k})n^{2k}+\mathcal{O}(\theta^{2}).

Now one can take
\theta=0
and the desired result follows.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

One can either say that by the Corollary
6.5
the map
n\mapsto L_{n}(p,\chi)
is a polynomial and therefore the asymptotic in Theorem
3.2
and in Theorem
1.1
must stops and becomes exact formulas. One can also directly sum the formulas in Theorem
1.2
against the character
\chi
and by the standard formulas concerning

\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\chi(r)(\partial_{\theta}^{a}\zeta_{n})(s,r/q)=\left(-s\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)^{a}\cdot L_{n}(s,\chi).

and

\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\chi(r)(\partial_{\theta}^{a}\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}})(s,r/q)=(-4s\pi)^{a}\cdot 2\left(\frac{q}{2\pi}\right)^{2s+a}L(2s+a,\chi),

where

if

∎
