Unraveling the Molecular Reason of Opposing Effects of α‐Mangostin and Norfluoxetine on TREK‐2 at the Same Binding Site
Authors: Gangrae Kim, Nhung Thi Hong Van, Joo Hyun Nam, Wook Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTWIK‐related K+ channel (TREK)‐2, expressed in sensory neurons, is involved in setting membrane potential, and its modulations contributes to the generation of nociceptive signals. Although acute and chronic pain is a common symptom experienced by patients with various conditions, most existing analgesics exhibit low efficacy and are associated with adverse effects. For this reason, finding the novel modulator of TREK‐2 is of significance for the development of new analgesics. Recent studies have shown that α‐Mangostin (α‐MG) activates TREK‐2, facilitating analgesic effects, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain elusive. Intriguingly, even though norfluoxetine (NFx) is known to inhibit TREK‐2, α‐MG is also observed to share a same binding site with NFx, and this implies that TREK‐2 might be modulated in a highly complicated manner. Therefore, we examine the mechanism of how TREK‐2 is activated by α‐MG using computational methods and patch clamp experiments in the present study. Based on these results, we offer an explanation of how α‐MG and NFx exhibit opposing effects at the same binding site of TREK‐2. These findings will broaden our understanding of TREK‐2 modulation, providing clues for designing novel analgesic drugs.Keywords: Alpha-mangostin, Molecular dynamics, Ion channel, TREK-2, Modulation

Introduction

The Two‐pore domain potassium (K2P) channels, a member of the voltage‐gated ion channel (VGIC) superfamily, comprise a total of six subfamilies (TREK, TWIK, TRESK, THIK, TASK, TALK). [1] K2P channels are expressed in a wide array of cell types and tissues, including both the central and peripheral nervous systems and exhibit a notable abundance in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG).[ 2 , 3 ] These channels function as leak K+ channels, allowing a constant efflux of K+ ions from the cell, thereby contributing to the maintenance of a hyperpolarized resting membrane potential (RMP). [4] By setting RMP and shaping the duration and frequency of action potentials, K2P channels play a crucial role in regulating cellular excitability in neurons and other excitable cells found in the heart, brain, muscles, and sensory organs.[ 5 , 6 ] Previous studies have demonstrated the crucial involvement of K2P channels in various pathological conditions, including pain,[ 7 , 8 , 9 ] arrhythmia, [10] ischemia,[ 11 , 12 ] cardiac fibrosis, [13] depression, [14] and migraine.[ 15 , 16 ] These findings highlight the potential of K2P channels as therapeutic targets for treating a wide range of diseases and disorders.

The K2P channels form homodimers with each subunit containing two pore domains (PD1 and PD2) and a cap domain (Figure 1A). Each pore domain consists of two transmembrane helices (M1, M2 for PD1 and M3, M4 for PD2), a selectivity filter (SF1 for PD1 and SF2 for PD2), and a pore helix (P1 for PD1 and P2 for PD2). The combination of four SFs from two subunits create the potassium ion permeation pathway. [17] Unlike voltage‐gated potassium channels (Kv), K2P channels are known to regulate ion flux and selectively permit potassium ions to pass through the SF located at the center of the channel. [18] Among K2P channels, the TREK family lacks a lower gate that primarily regulates ion influx in the lower part of the channel, but hydrophobic residues surrounding the inner cavity beneath the pore form a hydrophobic barrier, thereby playing a role as a gate (Figure 1B). [19]

Architecture of TREK‐2. (A) TREK‐2 subunit diagram. (B) SF and inner cavity in TREK‐2. (C) Comparison of the structures between the down (left) and up state (right) of TREK‐2. Chain A is colored in green, chain B in yellow, and the M4 helix in blue.

The TREK family is characterized by distinct conformational states referred to as down and up states, which are governed by the movement of the M4 helix (Figure 1C).[ 20 , 21 ] In the down state, the transmembrane helices, including the M4 helix, extend toward the cytoplasm, resulting in a reduced size of the inner cavity. In this state, the fenestration site becomes exposed, allowing for the binding of a lipid acyl chain, thereby completely blocking the ion permeation pathway.[ 21 , 22 ] In contrast, in the up state, all transmembrane helices move toward extracellular direction, leading to an enlargement of the inner cavity, and this facilitates the entry of water molecules and potassium ions. Additionally, the structural changes in the up state lead to the collapse of the fenestration site, preventing the binding of the acyl chain and allowing ions to pass through unhindered.[ 20 , 21 ]

The TREK family of potassium channels, which includes TREK‐1, TREK‐2, and TRESK, is subject to regulation by various factors such as mechanical stretch, voltage, temperature, extracellular pH, and a variety of compounds.[ 18 , 23 ] Recently, these channels and their agonists have emerged as potential targets for pain treatment due to their role in modulating neuronal excitability. [24] α‐MG, a xanthone derived from the pericarp of mangosteen fruit, has attracted considerable interest due to its diverse biological effects on tumorigenesis, metastasis, inflammation, oxidation, bacterial growth, and metabolism. [25] Notably, several studies have reported that α‐MG exhibits significant analgesic effects in rodent models, suggesting its potential as a novel pain management agent.[ 26 , 27 ] A recent study discovered that α‐MG could manage pain by regulating several ion channels, including TREK‐2. The activation of TREK‐2 channels by α‐MG led to the stabilization of the resting membrane potential (RMP), thereby contributing to its analgesic effect. [28] Currently, first‐line medications for chronic pain management, such as non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and opioids, have limitations in terms of efficacy, particularly in neuropathic pain. Long‐term use of these medications is also associated with problems related to tolerance and opioid addiction. [24] Therefore, the development of novel analgesics with improved efficacy and reduced side effects is a pressing concern. Given the promising role of TREK‐2 channels in pain modulation and the analgesic effects of α‐MG mediated through TREK‐2 activation, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying this interaction could provide valuable insights for the design of novel analgesics.

In this study, we investigate the mechanism by which α‐MG activates the TREK‐2 channels using computational and experimental approaches. To this end, we first propose a plausible explanation for the leak current formation of TREK‐2 channels which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously addressed. Subsequently, we demonstrate that α‐MG, an activator of TREK‐2, likely binds to the same sites as ruthenium red (RuR) and norfluoxetine (NFx), well‐known inhibitors of TREK‐2.[ 21 , 29 ] This suggests a highly elaborate and complex modulation of TREK‐2 channels, and we offer potential interpretations for these opposing effects at the molecular level based on our MD simulation results and relevant experiments. We expect that the findings of this study will enhance our understanding of the modulation of TREK‐2 and provide valuable perceptions for developing novel medicine targeting the TREK‐2 channels.

Result and Discussion

Leak Current Formation in TREK‐2

In a previous study, it was shown that α‐MG can increase ion current by binding to the resting state of TREK‐2. [28] Therefore, prior to address the increased current induced by α‐MG, it was necessary to understand the leak current first which is known to occur in the resting state of TREK‐2. However, the exact molecular reason underlying the occurrence of leak current had been unclear, so we first examined the reason of leak current formation based on 1500 ns MD simulations started from the up and down state of apo TREK‐2. For each state, five replicas were created, and the size of the SF and inner cavity were monitored for last 500 ns (Figure 2A and B) of MD trajectories based on the assumption that the size change of the pore would lead to the alteration in the current.

(C) Overlay of the structures before (blue) and after (yellow) the occurrence of the size change of the inner cavity. (D) Schematic diagram illustrating the origin of leak current. TREK‐2 in the down state is colored in pink, and its up state is colored in green.

The MD simulation results from the five replicas for the down state showed that the inner cavity size didn't change significantly for three among the five replicas, while it is increased up to the size similar to that of the up state in two of them (Figure 2A). In the case of the former, when the last snapshot of MD trajectory was compared to the starting structure, the transmembrane helices appeared to stay in their original positions, indicating that the TREK‐2 remained in the down state. On the other hand, a clear upward movement of the transmembrane helices, including M4 helix, was observed for the latter, implying that a transition from the down to up state occurred during the simulation (Figure 2C). In the MD simulation results for the up state, no significant change in the inner cavity size was observed in any of the five replicas (Figure 2B). In addition, curves representing the pore radius of SF were observed to flatten out for some replicas in both up and down state (Figure 2A and B), and this is because some carbonyl groups forming SF were flipped (Figure S1). [30] However, this flip occurred regardless of the up and down state, so it seems to be an occasional event independent of the state.

According to the MD simulation results for the apo state, conformational changes from the down to up state was observed, but not vice versa. This implies that TREK‐2 in the up state is more stable than the down state in terms of free energy, and this further indicates that major population of TREK‐2 would exist as active up state rather than inactive down state in the resting state. However, considering the leak current being very weak current, TREK‐2 should primarily exist as inactive down state, and this cannot be compatible with our MD simulation results. Nevertheless, if we also consider the fact that the actual reason of TREK‐2 becoming inactive is due to the binding of an acyl chain, we can provide a possible explanation that can solve this inconsistency as follows. When an acyl chain binds to the fenestration sites of TREK‐2, they block the entrance of the SF, completely inactivating TREK‐2. Interestingly, this binding also obstructs the movement of transmembrane helices, including the M4 helix, thereby rendering the conformational shift from the down to up state impossible. This would make the down state more stable than the up state in terms of free energy and eventually induce equilibrium shift towards the down state (Figure 2D). We also conducted five replicas of a MD simulation for apo state in 1,2‐Dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DOPC) bilayer additionally to see whether this spontaneous conformational shift to the up state is POPC‐specific. However, we could observe the same conformational shift in one of the replicas with DOPC bilayer as well (Figure S2), indicating that this conformational shift is not attributed to the type of lipid bilayer.

Binding Sites and Poses of α‐MG

To examine the molecular mechanism behind the activation of TREK‐2 by α‐MG, we conducted molecular docking. Although the recent study has done the docking between α‐MG and TREK‐2 already, [28] they specified binding sites for their docking calculations based on the assumption that α‐MG would only bind to the known binding sites, so they potentially overlooked the possibility of α‐MG binding to other sites. However, here in this work, we included the whole TREK‐2 protein to the searching area for the docking of α‐MG and used Autodock Vina for these calculations. Autodock Vina is known to yield non‐reproducible outcomes due to its random selection of initial ligand positions and orientations, and we used this nature of Autodock Vina to identify statistically most probable binding site. [31] Namely, we repeated docking calculations for 10 times targeting at the whole TREK‐2 protein, obtaining 90 docking poses (9 docking results from each calculation), and we considered the most occurred binding site in the docking calculations as the most probable one. The binding probability computed from the occurrence frequency is 36.67 % for the keystone inhibitor site and 63.33 % for the fenestration site (Figure 3A). For each of these two binding sites, we carried out 5 additional docking calculations limiting the searching area to the corresponding binding site. In this way, we could finally identify the most stable pose for each binding site and used these poses for subsequent MD simulations.

(B−D) Three binding poses of α‐MG observed in five replicas. The snapshots were extracted from the last frame of MD trajectories. (E) Averaged pore radius over the last 500 ns MD trajectories of α‐MG and TREK‐2 complex.

The keystone inhibitor site is known as the binding site of the inhibitor RuR, and since this site is positioned at the top of the SF, ions exit through it. Therefore, upon binding to the keystone inhibitor site, RuR blocks ion efflux, thereby inhibiting TREK‐2 (Figure S3A). [29] According to our docking results, α‐MG can also bind to the keystone inhibitor site as RuR, and this implies a possibility that α‐MG could also block ion efflux, thus acting as an inhibitor. However, this cannot be acceptable given the role of α‐MG as an activator. To examine this inconsistency, five replicas of a 1500 ns MD simulation were carried out for the complex in which α‐MG binds to the keystone inhibitor site of TREK‐2. When the last snapshots from the five replicas are compared to the crystal structure of TREK‐2 and RuR complex, overall, α‐MG is found a bit further away from the passage of ions than RuR (Figure S3A–F). Moreover, since the positively charged amino groups of RuR are pointing at the exit of the SF when RuR is bound, potassium ions would be unable to proceed further from this point due to repulsion. However, there is no such repulsion when α‐MG is bound, so the binding of α‐MG to the keystone inhibitor site will not affect ion flow. In addition, almost no noticeable changes in the SF or inner cavity size across all replicas were observed (Figure S3G), indicating that the binding of α‐MG to the keystone inhibitor site is not able to induce any allosteric modulation of TREK‐2. Hence, α‐MG seems to have no effects on TREK‐2 when it binds to the keystone inhibitor site.

Like the keystone inhibitor site, the fenestration site is also known as the binding site of the inhibitor NFx. [21] Since our computational results for the binding to the keystone inhibitor site had not shown any sign of activating TREK‐2, the binding to the fenestration site was speculated to be a key to activating TREK‐2. To examine the effects of this binding, five replicas of MD simulations were conducted using the docking result of α‐MG bound to the fenestration site as an initial structure (Figure 3A). Three distinct binding poses (pose α, β, and γ) were observed from these five replicas. Pose α, observed in three out of the five replicas, lies across the M3 and M4 helix and is close to the inner cavity (Figure 3B). Another pose (pose β), appeared once out of the five replicas, is slightly further away from the inner cavity and is closer to the M3 helix compared to pose α (Figure 3C). The other pose (pose γ), also appeared once, lies in parallel with the M3 and M4 helix and is adjacent to the P2 helix. Unlike the other two poses, pose γ does not bind inside the fenestration site where an acyl chain is accommodated but to the entrance of the fenestration site (Figure 3D). In addition, all poses appear not to structurally obstruct the passage of ions. Interconversion between poses were not observed during the MD simulations, and the ligand RMSD plots also show that those binding poses are stable (Figure S4A).

The results monitoring the changes of the pore radius of the SF and the inner cavity size induced by each pose show that the inner cavity become remarkably narrowed in one of the three replicas that belong to pose α (pink line in Figure 3E). This is because the C‐terminal portion of the M4 helix is partially unfolded and then bent towards the inner cavity during the MD simulation (Figure S5). However, since this event was observed only once for all conducted MD simulations, and no other structural changes occurred except this C‐terminal, it seems to be a rare event originated from the flexibility of the C‐terminal rather than the allosteric modulation induced by α‐MG. Another noticeable change is that the inner cavity of pose γ is relatively widened (green line in Figure 3E), and this is probably associated with its binding pose distinct from others. The widening observed here is mainly attributed to the upward movement of M2 helix, and M4 helix moves a little bit downward rather than upward (Figure S6). Given that the fenestration site is not collapsed and a noticeable movement of the M4 helix is not observed, this state is still closer to the down state rather than the up state. To sum up, all poses of α‐MG bound to the fenestration site appear to impede the binding of an acyl chain but not ion flow and not to induce any allosteric modulation.

Binding Energy Calculations

From MD simulations on the binding of α‐MG to the fenestration site of TREK‐2, we obtained three possible binding poses of α‐MG. As a next step, we computed the binding interaction energies of these three poses to see which pose is most stable. Furthermore, we also decomposed these binding interaction energies into the contribution of each residue to identify the residues playing a pivotal role in the binding. To this end, we conducted molecular mechanics Poisson‐Boltzmann surface area (MM‐PBSA) calculations using the MD simulation trajectories of three different poses as input.

The computed binding free energies between α‐MG and TREK‐2 are −53.7 kcal/mol, −46.1 kcal/mol, and −43.9 kcal/mol for pose α, β, and γ, respectively. Pose α exhibits the lowest binding energy, and those of pose β and γ are comparable. Interestingly, a correlation between the computed binding free energies and the number of occurrences of each pose can be seen here. This also implies that α‐MG is most likely to exist as pose α when it binds to the fenestration site of TREK‐2.

Among many residues involved in the binding, we focused only on those with the binding energies below −2 kcal/mol. In pose α, the strong interactions are formed between α‐MG and Ile189, Pro193, and Phe311, and their binding energies are −2.10, −2.41, and −3.97 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 4A). In the case of pose β, the main interacting residues were identified as Ile189 and Phe311 with the binding energies of −3.50 and −2.56 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 4B). Therefore, pose β has two main interacting residues in common with pose α, and this is probably due to the similar orientation of two poses. Lastly, pose γ shows to have two main interacting residues, Leu274 and Phe311, with the binding energies of −2.26 and −2.95 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 4C). Since the orientation of pose γ remarkably differs from those of pose α and β, its overall binding interactions are also quite different from other two poses. However, it is noteworthy that Phe311 is always involved in strong binding interactions regardless of poses, and Pro193, Leu274, and Ile189 are rather pose‐specific. Additionally, no hydrogen bonds are observed in the binding interactions, and all residues, which mainly contributes to the binding interactions, have hydrophobic side chains, so hydrophobic interactions seem to be the main driving force for the binding between α‐MG and TREK‐2.

Patch Clamp Experiments on TREK‐2 Mutants

According to the MM‐PBSA results, Ile189, Pro193, Leu274 and Phe311 play a pivotal role in the binding between α‐MG and the fenestration site of TREK‐2. Therefore, to confirm whether the binding site predicted from our computations is correct, we performed site‐directed mutagenesis, replacing Ile189, Leu274, and Phe311 with alanine (A) and conducted patch clamp experiments for those mutants. Here, Pro193 was excluded from the experiments since its substitution to alanine could result in drastic structural changes of TREK‐2 due to the unique ring structure of proline. We assumed that if these three residues are crucial for the binding of α‐MG as predicted from MM‐PBSA calculations, their substitution with alanine would alter the binding affinity of α‐MG, consequently affecting the TREK‐2 current.

As a prerequisite, we verified that the introduced mutations did not disrupt the general functionality of the TREK‐2 channels using the selective activator ML335, which binds to K2P modulator pocket, that is different from α‐MG binding site on the TREK‐2 channels. [32] All mutants can be activated by ML335, which is a potent TREK‐2 activator with a different binding site from α‐MG, confirming their intact channel function (Figure S7A–D). Furthermore, there was no difference in the magnitude of TREK‐2 currents between WT and mutants at both baseline and treatment with 1 μM ML335 (Figure S7E). These findings indicated that genetic mutations did not change the configuration and operation of TREK‐2 channels and their current intensity.

Next, we evaluated the effect of α‐MG on TREK‐2 currents in wild‐type (WT) and mutant channels. In WT, 3 μM α‐MG enhanced TREK‐2 current by 2.91±0.36‐fold relative to the baseline. Similarly, α‐MG potentiated TREK‐2 currents in I189 A, L274 A, and F311 A mutants, with activation factors of 6.23±1.93, 3.20±1.12, and 3.72±1.57, respectively (Figure 5A). Interestingly, while L274 A and F311 A mutants showed no statistically significant differences compared to WT TREK‐2 (Figure S8A, C, D and 5A), the I189A mutant displayed a significantly higher activation by α‐MG (Figure S8B). Consistently, the magnitude of TREK‐2 currents in the I189A mutant was dramatically higher than in WT under the effect of α‐MG, but no difference was found when comparing L274 A and F311 A mutants to WT (Figure 5A). Importantly, the baseline current intensities of each mutant were not substantially different from WT (Figure 5A). These findings suggest that the Ile189 residue might play a role in modulating the binding affinity of α‐MG to TREK‐2.

(B−D) Three binding poses of α‐MG observed in the five replicas of I189A mutant. The snapshots were extracted from the last frame of MD trajectories. (F) Time constant (tau) of NFx inhibition in I189A mutant was significantly decreased compared to WT using least squares regression model. ns: nonsignificant, *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001.

Although I189A mutant exhibits increased current compared to WT, only one mutation solely could not be sufficient to evidence that α‐MG binds to the fenestration site. However, the rest of the binding residues except the three residues addressed above are expected to have minor contributions for the binding according to the MM‐PBSA results. Therefore, we additionally carried out new site‐directed mutagenesis experiments substituting with bulky tryptophan instead of alanine for other two most contributing residues beside Ile189 showing the binding energies below −2 kcal/mol, namely Leu274 and Phe311 (Figure S9). The results demonstrate that the L274 W mutant exhibits an enhanced relative current increase in response to α‐MG compared to WT. Specifically, while the baseline current of L274W is lower than that of WT, the fold‐increase in current upon α‐MG application is significantly larger for L274W. This altered response to α‐MG caused by another mutation within the fenestration site further supports our prediction that α‐MG binds to this region.

The Effect of I189A Mutation on Binding Affinity

The results from patch clamp experiments demonstrated that I189A mutant exhibits a significantly increased current than WT upon the binding of α‐MG. This is probably because more stable binding is possible when Ile189 is replaced with alanine. To substantiate this idea, the binding energy between α‐MG and I189A mutant was calculated with MM‐PBSA. To this end, α‐MG was docked to I189A mutant first, and five replicas of a MD simulation for I189A mutant complexed with α‐MG were performed prior to MM‐PBSA calculations. In three out of the five replicas, a binding pose (pose δ) which is highly comparable to pose α of WT was observed (Figure 3B and 5B). However, the remaining two binding poses are completely different from those observed in WT. One of these two, pose ϵ, is found in similar position as pose δ but with a flipped orientation (Figure 5C), while the other, pose ζ, is bound at the entrance of the SF (Figure 5D). All binding poses of α‐MG are stable (Figure S4B) and located near the fenestration site, and they seem to be capable of preventing an acyl chain from binding to the fenestration site but without impeding ion flow. Hence, all poses would be able to activate TREK‐2.

The binding energy of pose δ was computed to be −54.3 kcal/mol, which is the lowest among those of three poses. This energy is also almost identical to that of pose α, which is −53.7 kcal/mol, and these comparable binding energies of the two poses are likely due to high structural similarity between the two poses. In contrast, pose ϵ and ζ exhibit binding energies of −51.3 kcal/mol and −53.4 kcal/mol, respectively, which are noticeably lower than those of pose β (−46.1 kcal/mol) and pose γ (−43.9 kcal/mol). Therefore, the average of the binding free energies for all poses of α‐MG bound to I189A mutant is lower than that of WT, and this is in line with the results from patch clamp experiments. In addition, the increased current of I189A mutant observed in the experiments appears to be due to pose ϵ and ζ rather than pose δ because relatively lower binding free energies of pose ϵ and ζ than those of pose β and γ contributed mostly to lowering the average of binding free energies.

To further substantiate the binding of α‐MG at the fenestration site of TREK‐2, we also examined the effect of the I189A mutation on the inhibition of TREK‐2 by NFx. Since NFx is also known to bind to the fenestration site, NFx can probably exhibit stronger binding affinity to I189A mutant than WT as α‐MG, if NFx really shares the binding site with α‐MG. NFx inhibited the ML335‐induced TREK‐2 currents in both WT and I189A mutant channels. Intriguingly, the inhibitory effect of NFx was significantly more pronounced in the I189A mutant compared to WT. Following NFx application, the remaining currents were reduced to 16.99±6.85 % and 29.40±10.73 % in the I189A mutant and WT, respectively (Figure 5E). Moreover, the inhibition kinetics of NFx were significantly faster in the I189A mutant, with a time constant of 72.29 s (95 % confidence interval (CI), 63.42–82.81 s), compared to 183.5 s (95 % CI, 166.0–204.0 s) in WT (P‐value<0.001) (Figure 5F). These results provide further evidence for the involvement of the Ile189 residue in the interaction between TREK‐2 and its ligands, such as α‐MG and NFx.

Molecular Reason of TREK‐2 Activation by α‐MG

The patch clamp experiments for I189A mutant demonstrated that α‐MG shares a binding site with NFx, yet the underlying reason of their opposing effects on TREK‐2 is still unclear. To gain some clues on this issue, we compared the crystal structure of TREK‐2 complexed with acyl chains (PDB ID: 4XDJ), the crystal structure of TREK‐2 complexed with NFx (PDB ID: 4XDK), and the structure of TREK‐2 complexed with α‐MG obtained from our simulations. When an acyl chain binds to the fenestration site, it physically blocks the entrance of the SF, resulting in the complete inhibition of potassium ion flow (Figure 6A). In contrast, when NFx binds to the same site, it cannot reach the entrance of the SF as the acyl chain does because of its shorter length (Figure 6B), so the binding of NFx appears not to be able to block ion flow structurally. However, the ionized NH3+ group of NFx, facing towards the SF, brings about repulsion with potassium ions and eventually hinder ion entry into the SF, and this has been suggested as the reason of NFx acting as an inhibitor on TREK‐2. [33] Meanwhile, the binding structure of α‐MG on TREK‐2 obtained from our MD simulation results shows that α‐MG would be able to block the binding of an acyl chain but not ion flow structurally because of its short length like NFx. However, in contrast to NFx, α‐MG would facilitate ion flow rather than impeding it because α‐MG has no positively charged moiety but a hydroxyl group instead. The lone pairs of this hydroxyl group are oriented towards a potassium ion at the entrance of the SF and would stabilize the binding of the ion there as the lone pairs of the carbonyl groups forming the SF do, thus facilitating ion flow.

(C) Structure of α‐MG and TREK‐2 complex obtained from computations (pose α). Based on our MD simulation results of apo TREK‐2, we suggested that leak current originates from the small population of the up and down state where an acyl chain is not bound. In addition, we also showed that, when α‐MG binds to the fenestration site of TREK‐2, it blocks the binding of the acyl chain and suppresses the movement of M4 helix, thereby maintaining the down state of TREK‐2 but allowing ion flow. Since α‐MG competes with the acyl chain for the fenestration site in the down state, if the concentration of α‐MG increases, the down state TREK‐2 population bound with acyl chains will decrease due to the increased population bound with α‐MG. As a result, ion flow becomes possible in the significant amount of down state TREK‐2 population, and this is the reason why α‐MG can act as an activator of TREK‐2 (Figure 6D). Here, the competition between α‐MG and the acyl chain will be affected not only by the concentration of α‐MG but also the relative binding affinity of α‐MG compared to the acyl chain. Hence, finding α‐MG derivatives with stronger binding affinity to the fenestration site can lead to the development of more potent TREK‐2 activators.

Conclusions

While it has been reported that α‐MG activates TREK‐2, exhibiting analgesic effects, the detailed mechanism of this activation at the molecular level is not known. Given the low efficacy and side effects of currently available analgesics, there is an urgent need for the development of new analgesics. Therefore, we aimed at elucidating this mechanism in the present work through computational and experimental approaches. Prior to addressing this activation mechanism, we examined the underlying reason of leak current formation in the resting state first. Based on MD simulation results, we illustrated that the leak current arises from the small population of the up and down state in which acyl chains are not bound. Interestingly, subsequent molecular docking results demonstrated that α‐MG shares the fenestration site with well‐known inhibitor, NFx, for the binding, so we further examined how these two ligands trigger opposing effects at the same binding site. Through the comparison between our computational results and existing crystal structures, we showed that the binding of α‐MG to the fenestration site would allow ion flow but impede the binding of an acyl chain which completely blocks the passage of ions. In this way, α‐MG would act as an activator of TREK‐2. On the other hand, NFx would also compete with the acyl chain for the fenestration site like α‐MG, but NFx repels ions at the entrance of the SF because of its positively charged amino group and therefore would act as an inhibitor. Furthermore, we also validated the binding of α‐MG to the fenestration site based on patch clamp experiments for TREK‐2 mutants and MM‐PBSA calculations. We expect that the findings of the present study would broaden our knowledge on the modulation of TREK‐2 and provide valuable clues for designing new drugs targeting TREK‐2.

Experimental Section

Initial Structure Preparation

For both computer simulations and experiments, we used TREK‐2 (rTREK‐2) from rat in the present work. Since the crystal structure of rTREK‐2 is not available, we used homology modeling to create its structure. To this end, the amino acid sequence of rTREK‐2 was retrieved from the Uniprot database (www.uniprot.org).[34] Down (PDB ID : 4XDJ) and up state (PDB ID : 4BW5) structure of human TREK‐2 were downloaded from Protein Data Bank (www.pdb.org) [35] and missing residues in those crystal structures were added using MODELLER v10.4. [36] Employing these structures as templates, the down and up state of rTREK‐2 were modeled with MODELLER v10.4 using automodel class, and the model with lowest MODELLER DOPE score was selected for each state for further investigations.

Molecular Docking

Prior to conducting molecular docking, water molecules were deleted, and polar hydrogen atoms were added to the crystal structure using AutoDock Tools v.1.5.7. [37] Kollman charges were assigned for both ligand and protein, and AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 was used for all docking calculations. [31] When docking was conducted targeting at the whole protein, the exhaustiveness value was set to 8. After identifying most probable binding sites from the whole protein docking, 5 additional docking calculations were conducted for each identified binding site with the searching area limited to the corresponding site, and the exhaustiveness value was increased to 2048. Grid spacing was set to 1Å for all docking calculations, and the size of a grid box was set to 26×18×18Å and 20×14×20Å for the keystone inhibitor site and for the fenestration site, respectively. Structures of α‐MG and TREK2 complex were visualized using VMD. [38]

MD Simulation

The protonation state of titratable residues at pH 7.0 was determined on the basis of Propka‐online (www.ddl.unimi.it/vegaol/propka.htm) results. [39] The models were built into simulation systems using CHARMM‐GUI membrane builder. [40] The protein and complex structures were embedded in a 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐oleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (POPC) lipid bilayer with TIP3P waters,[ 41 , 42 ] and K+ ions were placed inside the selectivity filter. Subsequently, the solvated model was neutralized by adding counter ions, followed by the addition of K+ and Cl− ions to achieve a final salt concentration of 150 mM. FF14SB parameter set was employed for the proteins and General Amber Force Field2 (GAFF2) for α‐mangostin.[ 43 , 44 ] Each system was minimized and equilibrated according to the standard CHARMM‐GUI protocol, [45] and all productive MD runs were conducted for 1500 ns in the NPT ensemble. Temperatures was kept at 300 K using a Langevin dynamics with a collision frequency of 1.0, and a coupling constant of 1 ps. Pressure was maintained at 1 bar using Berendsen barostat. [46] Short range van der Waals and electrostatic interactions were cut‐off at 9.0 Å, and Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) summation was utilized to calculate long‐range electrostatic interactions. [47] The time step of 2 fs was used, and the SHAKE algorithm was used to restrain bonds including hydrogen atoms. [48] All MD simulations were carried out with periodic boundary conditions and in quintuplicate for each system using independent initial starting velocities. The pore radius of TREK‐2 along its z‐axis during MD simulations was computed using the HOLE program, [49] and all MD simulations in this study were conducted using pmemd module of AMBER 20. [50] The 500 snapshots from the last 500 ns of a MD trajectory were collected for the analysis, and the pore radii were averaged over those snapshots. The centroid of the alpha‐carbons of central Gly in four SF was set as 0 on the graphs.

MM‐PBSA

The mmpbsa.py script of AMBER 20 was utilized for Molecular Mechanics/Poisson‐Boltzmann and Surface Area (MM‐PBSA) calculations.[ 50 , 51 ] All conformations sampled at 5 ns interval from the last 500 ns of a MD trajectory were used to compute the molecular mechanics potential energies and solvation free energies in the MM‐PBSA procedure. The basic procedure of MM‐PBSA involves computing the binding free energy between the bound and unbound states in the gas phase and calculating their solvation energies with an implicit solvent model. This allows obtaining the binding free energy between protein and ligand in the solvent indirectly, bypassing a direct route in the thermodynamic cycle. The binding free energy in the gas phase was computed from the sum of the average potential energy in vacuum. Solvation energy was obtained as the sum of polar and nonpolar solvation energies. [52] The polar solvation energy was calculated using the Poisson‐Boltzmann (PB) equation, [53] while the nonpolar solvation energy was calculated using the solvent‐accessible surface area (SASA). [54] The PB equation was solved with internal (protein) and external (solvent) dielectric constants set to 20.0 and 80.0, respectively. Membrane environment was simulated with a membrane dielectric constant of 7 and a membrane thickness of 52 Å , and the ionic strength was set to 150 mM. For calculating nonpolar solvation energy, the surface tension was set to 0.0072 kcal/mol/Å2 , and the offset value was set to 0. In addition, to compute the contribution of each residue involved in the interaction between α‐MG and TREK‐2, pairwise per‐residue‐based PB energy decomposition was conducted with the idecomp value of 1.

HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (USA) and cultured in high glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Welgene, Korea) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Welgene, Korea) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Hyclone, USA). Electrophysiological Recordings

Whole‐cell patch‐clamp experiments were performed with rTREK‐2 wide‐type (WT) and mutants overexpressed in human embryonic kidney 293 T (HEK 293 T) cells. TREK‐2 currents were recorded by the system including an Axopatch 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), a Digidata 1440 A interface (Molecular Devices), and an inverted microscope (ECLIPSE Ti−S, Nikon). The 10 kHz sampling and 5 kHz low‐pass filtered frequencies were used. The bath solution contained 140 mM NaCl, 3.6 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM MgCl2, 1.3 mM CaCl2, 5 mM glucose, with pH adjusted to 7.4 using NaOH, and osmolarity adjusted to 310 mOsm using sorbitol. The pipette solution contained 135 mM KCl, 6 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 5 mM EGTA, 3 mM Mg‐ATP, pH was adjusted to 7.2 with KOH, and osmolarity was adjusted to 290 mOsm with sorbitol. The pipettes were polished to a resistance of 2–5 MΩ using Flaming/Brown Micropipette Puller (Butter Instrument). TREK‐2 currents were measured by applying a voltage ramp pulse from −100 mV to +20 mV in 200 ms every 20 s while cells were holding at −60 mV. All recordings were performed at 20–25 °C.

Transfected cells were transferred to a poly‐L‐lysine‐coated chamber for 15 min. Cells labeled with green fluorescence were selected to record TREK‐2 currents. The bath solution flowed through the chamber at a rate of 3 mL/min with or without the compounds α‐mangostin, ML335, and/or NFx. The bath solution including compounds was applied after the TREK‐2 currents maintained their stability for 1–2 min. The TREK‐2 currents at +20 mV were utilized in electrophysiology analysis.
