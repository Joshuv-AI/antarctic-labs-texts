Running water never stales: A new perspective on hydrodynamic-driven water self-purification
Authors: Bing Li, Hanbing Li, Xiaofeng Wu, Yuefei Huang, Shungui Zhou, Guangqian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water movement activates a hidden hydrogen-driven energy pathway that converts hydrodynamic forces into electrons and reactive oxygen species, directly powering microbial metabolism while simultaneously driving abiotic pollutant degradation. This hydro-electric mechanism reveals a previously overlooked engine of natural water purification that operates beyond sunlight or chemical inputs. Recognizing and harnessing this intrinsic energy flow is essential for protecting water quality at its source, reinforcing ecosystem resilience, and safeguarding human health—pointing toward a new frontier of nature-based sustainable environmental management strategy.

The missing link in water self-purification

The ancient adage “flowing water never stagnates” hints at a link between hydraulic movement and natural purification. Modern science formalizes this through the phototroph-chemoautotroph framework, where microbial life purifies water by harnessing light and chemical energy. A fundamental question, however, remains: how do microbial communities thrive and purify water in environments where conventional energy inputs (e.g., labile organic carbon or light) are severely limited and insufficient to sustain active microbial metabolism—such as deep ocean trenches, turbulent hyporheic zones, and lightless aquifers—devoid of both ample light and chemical energy? The answer may lie in converting ubiquitous hydrodynamic energy into electrons that power microbial metabolism and chemical redox reactions. Recent discoveries show microorganisms can harness electrons from piezoelectric mineral deformation and from sediment-water friction [1]. This hydro-electric coupling—defined here as the abiotic transduction of kinetic energy into interfacial electrical potential or electron flux—represents not merely an incremental addition to known pathways, but a fundamental, long-overlooked mode of energy acquisition and water purification that has operated throughout Earth’s history.

When water flows, electrobiotic purification emerges

Natural materials are rarely electrochemically inert. Non-centrosymmetric minerals like quartz and tourmaline, or biomolecules like chitin, develop electrical polarization under hydrodynamic stress, separating electron-hole pairs via the piezoelectric effect [2]. Similarly, common materials such as kaolinite, montmorillonite, and chitosan acquire surface charges through repeated contact-separation with water via triboelectricity. The former is primarily governed by intrinsic crystal asymmetry and mechanical deformation; the latter arises from interfacial charge transfer during dynamic contact processes. However, systematically disentangling and quantifying the relative contributions of piezo-vs. tribo-electric effects in heterogeneous natural flows remains a significant technical challenge. While negligible in static lab settings, these effects in dynamic natural environments (Fig. 1a)—with persistent waves, currents, and turbulence—transform mineral surfaces into continuous electron-hole generators at solid-water interfaces. The generated electrons become biologically critical as they are immediately available to electroactive bacteria, accumulating on material surfaces for direct capture—unlike the hard-to-liberate electrons in stable chemical bonds (Fig. 1b) [3]. The electrons transferred to the microorganisms are then used to drive metabolic and redox reactions, a process known as electrobiotic purification. Simultaneously, the resulting highly reactive holes can initiate oxidation processes at solid-water interfaces. In aqueous environments, these holes are rapidly converted into reactive oxygen species (e.g., ·OH) through reactions with H2O or OH. As a result, radical-mediated pathways likely dominate under natural conditions, while direct hole oxidation is largely limited to surface-bound contaminants (Fig. 1c) [4]. While excessive reactive oxygen species may impose oxidative stress on microorganisms, localized low concentrations of reactive oxygen species can break down recalcitrant organic matter into bioavailable substrates. Evidence from energy-limited environments

The significance of hydrodynamic-driven processes becomes clear from nature’s paradoxes. In deep-sea environments without hydrothermal vents or sunlight, conventional theory predicts severe energy limitation. Yet robust biogeochemical cycling, including nitrate reduction and methane oxidation [5], persists. Similarly, river hyporheic zones—shallow subsurface regions beneath streambeds—experience continuous turbulence that should inhibit biofilm formation [6]. Paradoxically, they function as biogeochemical hotspots with exceptionally high pollutant degradation rates [7,8].

Laboratory studies confirm these observations. Combining autotrophic denitrifying bacteria with piezoelectric materials under agitation drives efficient nitrate reduction [1,9]. Electroactive organisms cultured with piezoelectric nanoparticles and stirred without conventional electron donors exhibit carbon fixation, nitrogen assimilation, and biomass accumulation. This is driven by hydrodynamic-induced electron-hole pairs, with electrons utilized by electroactive microorganisms while holes or derived reactive species oxidize pollutants. The simultaneous electron supply and hole-mediated oxidation effectively couple otherwise unfavorable redox reactions, powered entirely by kinetic energy from agitation. More strikingly, mechanical energy alone can sustain microbial growth [5,10]. Natural piezoelectric materials generate current densities of approximately nA/cm2 to μA/cm2 under hydrodynamic stress, which is comparable to the electron fluxes required to sustain microbial maintenance metabolism (approximately 0.1–10 μA/cm2) [11,12]. Transcriptomics reveal upregulated electron transfer and energy metabolism genes, confirming cells actively utilize piezo-electrons, suggesting microbial metabolic diversity is far greater than previously recognized.

Rethinking water self-purification

These findings reshape how we understand water self-purification. Whereas traditional models relied on physical dilution, photochemical/abiotic reactions, and light/chemical-dependent biodegradation [13], hydrodynamics were considered only a physical dispersal aid. Mechanistically, dilution governs mass transport, photochemical processes rely on photon excitation, and biodegradation depends on chemical energy substrates, whereas the proposed pathway uniquely converts mechanical energy into interfacial electron flux that can directly drive redox reactions. The recognition that flowing water directly produces electrons and holes introduces a fourth, synergistic mechanism.

Flowing water performs multiple simultaneous functions by dispersing pollutants physically, generating reactive electrons and holes electrochemically at mineral surfaces, and delivering these charges to microorganisms biologically and pollutants chemically [14,15]. This creates a positive feedback loop where intensified hydrodynamic forces enhance electron-hole generation, accelerating microbial metabolism and pollutant transformation. The rapid electron consumption, in turn, further promotes charge separation efficiency (Fig. 2a). The implications extend beyond academic interest. If hydrodynamic energy contributes significantly to water self-purification capacity, then models predicting contaminant fate must incorporate flow regime characteristics such as velocity, turbulence intensity, and material composition as drivers of biochemical reactions rather than merely transport parameters. Water quality assessments in rivers, wetlands, and coastal zones may need recalibration to account for the electrochemical contribution of prevalent flow conditions. Restoration projects could potentially enhance purification by optimizing hydrodynamic patterns and introducing appropriate mineral substrates.

Bridging laboratory promise to environmental reality

Current hydrodynamic-driven evidence stems mainly from controlled laboratory systems using pure cultures and idealized materials under strong agitation (e.g., 400 rpm, corresponding to a linear velocity of 1 m/s) [16]. While this aligns with flow velocities in natural rivers and turbulent streams (0.1–3.0 m/s), natural environments are far more complex, featuring mixed microbial communities, diverse minerals, heterogeneous flows, and competing energy sources, raising critical questions (Fig. 2b). Laboratory studies show piezoelectric charging under intense stress, but natural flows are far gentler. Quantifying whether these moderate forces generate biologically meaningful electron fluxes requires sensitive field measurements, which remain technically challenging due to the low magnitude and transient nature of interfacial currents, as well as interference from background electrochemical processes. Besides, impurities and aggregates in natural materials may enhance or diminish electron generation versus pure compounds. In addition, not all water bodies experience equivalent hydrodynamic energy. Assessing the global impact thus requires mapping this energy across ecosystems and identifying the thresholds where its contribution becomes substantial.

Addressing these questions demands interdisciplinary integration of geochemistry, microbiology, and hydrodynamics. Essential advances include in situ measurement of transient interfacial currents, isotopic tracing of electron flow from minerals to cells, and high-resolution imaging of microbial-mineral interfaces under flow. Only such integration can quantify the full contribution of hydrodynamic-driven processes to biogeochemical cycling across spatial and temporal scales.

Beyond natural systems: Technological opportunities

These findings inspire novel water treatment by harnessing mechanical energy. Unlike conventional biological processes requiring external electron donors or acceptors, systems harvesting electrons from existing reactor agitation could reduce chemical inputs while maintaining performance. Preliminary demonstrations confirm that incorporating piezoelectric materials in aerated bioreactors enhances denitrification and organic removal [1].

With wastewater plants consuming 3%–4% of national electricity for aeration and mixing [17], reactor designs integrating piezoelectric or triboelectric components could convert this kinetic energy into redox potential, creating distributed electron sources throughout treatment volumes. These principles extend to constructed wetlands, aquaculture, and river restoration (Fig. 2b). Incorporating mineral substrates in high-flow zones may augment indigenous microbial purification without external energy, offering passive treatment solutions particularly advantageous for remote or resource-limited settings.

A paradigm awaiting validation

The hydro-electrobiotic hypothesis posits that hydrodynamic energy directly drives global biogeochemical cycles [18]. If validated, it would reshape ecosystem energy concepts, refresh Earth system models to include hydrodynamic inputs, and redefine the role of hydraulic engineering in water quality. Yet humility remains essential. Proponents must demonstrate, through rigorous measurement and modeling, that such processes contribute meaningfully to real-world purification beyond established mechanisms.

The discovery that microorganisms harness hydrodynamic energy fundamentally expands life’s known metabolic diversity. It reveals how evolution has enabled life to exploit Earthʼs pervasive kinetic energy, not just chemical or solar sources. Rather than functioning independently, such energy input is likely integrated within a broader network of coexisting energy sources in natural systems. A key unresolved challenge is to quantitatively disentangle the contributions and interactions among these energy pathways, including potential synergistic or inhibitory effects under different environmental conditions. Whether this pathway proves to be a dominant mechanism or a niche adaptation, it underscores life’s extraordinary resourcefulness. Furthermore, this electron-hole separation may have facilitated prebiotic synthesis and early life emergence, while also offering novel pathways for sustainable synthesis in green chemistry—connecting ancient natural purification to modern biochemical innovation.

The ancient wisdom that “flowing water never stagnates” contains a deeper truth than metaphorical insight. Rather than merely preventing stagnation through physical mixing, flowing water could continuously generate electrochemical energy that powers electron-hole pairs to drive nature’s invisible purification machinery.
