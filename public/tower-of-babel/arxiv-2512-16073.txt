Complete Decomposition of Anomalous Diffusion in Variable speed Generalized Lévy Walks Preprint: APS/123-QED
Abhijit Bera, Kevin E. Bassler

Abstract
Variable Speed Generalized Lévy Walks (VGLWs) are a class of spatio-temporally coupled stochastic processes that unify a broad range of previously studied models within a single parametrized framework. Their dynamics consist of discrete random steps, or flights, during which the walker’s speed varies deterministically with both the elapsed time and the total duration of the flight. We investigate the anomalous diffusive behavior of VGLWs and analyze it through decomposition into the three fundamental constitutive effects that capture violations of the Central Limit Theorem (CLT): the Joseph effect, reflecting long-range increment correlations, the Noah effect, arising from heavy-tailed step-size distributions with infinite variance, and the Moses effect, associated with statistical aging and non-stationarity. Our results show that anomalous diffusion in VGLWs is typically generated by a nontrivial combination of all three effects, rather than being attributable to a single mechanism. Strikingly, we find that within the VGLW framework the Noah exponent L L , which quantifies the strength of the Noah effect, is unbounded from above, revealing a richer and more extreme landscape of anomalous diffusion than in previously studied Lévy-walk-type models.



I
Introduction

Diffusive processes describe the spreading of particles over time as a result of the accumulation of many random steps or increments. If these increments are assumed to be independent, identically distributed, and possess finite variance, then the Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
33
;
24
applies. In this case, the distribution of particle displacements converges to a Gaussian distribution as the number of increments increases. Moreover, the variance of the distribution grows linearly with time. A diffusive process that satisfies the CLT is referred to as a normal diffusive process. A classical example is the Wiener process
17
.

However, numerous experimental systems exhibit anomalous diffusion, in which the mean squared displacement (MSD) of particles scales with time as

\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\propto t^{2H},\quad H\neq\tfrac{1}{2}

(1)

Various stochastic continuous-time random walks (CTRWs) models have been proposed to capture the essence of such anomalous dynamics
31
;
5
;
6
;
54
;
18
;
35
;
30
;
60
. In this paper, we focus on
Variable Speed Generalized Lévy Walks
(VGLWs)
7
;
8
. VGLWs provide a broad paradigm that encompasses many previously studied CTRW-type models. In the VGLW framework, particles take independent steps of random duration drawn from a power-law distribution. The distance traveled in each step scales as a power of the step duration, while the direction of motion is random. Importantly, during each step the particle’s velocity evolves as a deterministic function of both the step duration and the elapsed time.

Anomalous diffusion arises when the CLT is violated. It can be decomposed into three fundamental constitutive effects, each associated with a distinct mechanism of CLT violation
18
: correlations between increments, known as the
Joseph effect
; increment distributions with infinite variance, termed the
Noah effect
; and non-stationarity or statistical aging of the increments, referred to as the
Moses effect
. These three mechanisms—either independently or in combination—can generate anomalous diffusion. Each effect can be quantified by an independent scaling exponent that characterizes its relative strength. This decomposition provides a powerful framework for identifying the origin of anomalous behavior, even when the details of the underlying microscopic dynamics are inaccessible. In particular, it enables one to isolate the dominant mechanisms in experimental systems and to construct more accurate models of real-world processes.

The complete decomposition of anomalous diffusion was first carried out in
18
for a variety of stochastic processes. In
43
, the framework was extended to an aging deterministic system. It was further developed in
3
for Generalized Lévy Walks (GLWs) in the big-jump regime, where the distribution of jump times has an infinite mean. In
63
, the decomposition was applied to diverse real-world systems, ranging from the macroscopic motion of molecules to large-scale phenomena such as bird migration. Studies have employed this decomposition to examine different real-world processes, including finance and biological systems
61
;
45
;
11
;
66
;
53
;
52
.
In addition to analytical and empirical approaches, machine learning techniques have also been proposed to infer diffusion models directly from single-particle trajectories and to estimate all associated scaling exponents
44
;
47
;
10
;
25
;
36
.

In this paper, we analytically decompose the anomalous diffusion of VGLWs across their entire three-dimensional parameter space. While a portion of this parameter space has been analyzed previously
3
, our work provides a comprehensive treatment. We uncover a variety of dynamical phases, each characterized by a distinct combination of the three fundamental constitutive effects that collectively generate the anomalous diffusive behavior. A key finding is that within the VGLW framework there exists
no upper bound
to the magnitude of the Noah effect. In earlier studies, the maximum value of the Noah exponent
L
, which quantifies the Noah effect, was found to be less than or equal to unity. By contrast, our analysis shows that in VGLWs the exponent
L
can grow without bound, revealing a richer and more extreme landscape of anomalous diffusion than previously recognized.

II
Variable speed Generalized Lévy Walks (VGLWs)

Anomalous diffusion is observed across a wide range of real-world systems

;
40
;
41
;
48
;
51

18

12
;
55

crowded intracellular environments
51
, cold atoms in dissipative

21
, and blinking quantum dots

50
;
39

A wide variety of stochastic models have been developed to explain such behavior.
CTRWs
46
and their
extensions are among the most widely used. In CTRWs, Gaussian spatial increments occur at
random times drawn from a heavy-tailed (power-law decaying) waiting-time distribution . Lévy flights extend
this framework by allowing step lengths to follow a heavy-tailed distribution

56
;
37
. Lévy walks constitute a further
generalization, introducing a deterministic coupling between the displacement and the
duration of each step
58
;
32
;
65
.
In standard Lévy walks, particles move at constant velocity during each step, and the
step durations are heavy-tailed

67
;
68
. In GLWs

57
, the velocity is nonlinearly coupled to the step duration,
though it remains constant throughout each step.

VGLWs
7
;
16
are a more general space–time coupled CTRWs. The
process consists of independent steps whose durations follow a heavy-tailed distribution
that decays as
\tau^{-\gamma-1}
. In this work, we take the step-time distribution to be

\psi(\tau)=\gamma\tau_{0}^{\gamma}\tau^{-1-\gamma}\,\Theta(\tau\geq\tau_{0}),

(2)

where
\tau_{0}
is the minimal step duration. During each step, the walker moves in a
random direction with a deterministic time-dependent speed,

\mathrm{v}_{\nu,\eta}(\tau,t^{\prime})=\eta c\,\tau^{\nu-\eta}t^{\prime\,\eta-1},

(3)

where
\nu
,
\eta
,
\gamma
are positive definite parameters that affect scaling properties and
c
is a positive definite constant that does not affect the scaling properties. By tuning
\eta
, the
VGLW model interpolates between several well-known processes:

\eta=1
recovers GLWs
7
;
8
;

\eta=\nu
yields the Drude model
54
;
13
;
and the limits
\eta\to 0
and
\eta\to\infty
correspond to jump–wait–jump and
wait–jump–wait random walks, respectively.

The MSD of VGLWs was computed in
16
, and the displacement propagator
for the regime
\gamma<1
, where dynamics are dominated by the longest flight, was
derived in
62
. Owing to their intrinsic space–time coupling and
flexible parametrization, VGLWs provide a unified and versatile framework for modeling
anomalous transport across physical, biological, and financial systems.

III
Constitutive Exponents of Anomalous Diffusion in VGLWs

Diffusion in VGLWs is characterized by the mean squared displacement (MSD),

\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\equiv\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^{2}\rangle,

(4)

whose scaling behavior

\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle\sim t^{2H},

(5)

defines the
Hurst exponent

H

9
;
38
.
For processes obeying the Central Limit Theorem (CLT),
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
(normal diffusion).
When
H\neq\tfrac{1}{2}
, the CLT is violated and the process exhibits anomalous diffusion.

As discussed in the Introduction, violations of the CLT arise from three constitutive mechanisms:
(i) long-range correlations (Joseph effect),
(ii) heavy-tailed increment distributions (Noah effect), and
(iii) temporal non-stationarity or aging (Moses effect).
We now formalize these effects for VGLWs by defining the exponents
J
,
L
, and
M
and the statistical quantities used to extract them.

III.1
Joseph Exponent
J

The Joseph effect quantifies long-range correlations between increments.
It can be calculated from the scaling behaviour of time-averaged mean squared displacement (TAMSD),

\Big\langle\overline{x^{2}(t,\Delta)}\Big\rangle=\left\langle\frac{1}{t-\Delta}\int_{0}^{t-\Delta}\big[x(t_{0}+\Delta)-x(t_{0})\big]^{2}\,dt_{0}\right\rangle,

(6)

which scales as

\Big\langle\overline{x^{2}(t,\Delta)}\Big\rangle\sim t^{\,2L+2M-2}\,\Delta^{2J}.

(7)

The exponent satisfies
0\leq J\leq 1
, with

J=0
for fully anti-correlated increments and
J=1
for fully correlated ones.
Alternative estimations of
J
can be found with rescaled-range statistics (R/S)
29
, wavelet decomposition
2
, and DFA
49
.

III.2
Moses Exponent
M

The Moses effect captures temporal non-stationarity or statistical aging.
It is obtained from the first moment of the absolute velocity
3
:

\langle|v|\rangle\sim t^{\,M-\tfrac{1}{2}}.

(8)

Unlike
J
, the Moses exponent has no intrinsic upper or lower bound.

III.3
Noah Exponent
L

The Noah effect quantifies heavy-tailed increment statistics.
It is obtained from the second moment of the velocity
3
:

\langle v^{2}\rangle\sim t^{\,2L+2M-2}.

(9)

The Noah exponent satisfies
L\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
, with larger values corresponding to increasingly heavy-tailed, fluctuation-dominated dynamics.

III.4
Scaling Relation and Its Application to VGLWs

The complete decomposition of anomalous diffusion—linking
(J,L,M)
to the overall diffusion exponent
H
—was introduced in
18
.
Using the scaling Green–Kubo relation
42
,
3
showed that Generalized Lévy Walks satisfy

H=J+L+M-1.

(10)

The same derivation extends naturally to the VGLW framework, since the scaling form of the Green–Kubo relation remains valid in this process as well. Thus, once the exponents
J
,
L
, and
M
are determined from the velocity and TAMSD statistics, the Hurst exponent
H
follows immediately via Eq. (
10
).

In this work, we compute
L
and
M
from the velocity distribution of VGLWs, and determine
J
from the TAMSD using the scaling Green–Kubo approach
42
;
20
.
The Hurst exponent is taken from the analytical MSD scaling derived in
16
.
A key result of our analysis is that, unlike in previously studied Lévy-walk-type models, the Noah exponent
L
is
unbounded
.
This prediction is confirmed numerically (see sec.
7
).

IV
L
and
M
for VGLWs

According to Eqs.
8
and
9
, determining the exponents

requires evaluating the first and second moments of the absolute velocity.
In principle, these moments can be obtained by integrating the velocity propagator

p(\mathrm{v},t)
. However, the explicit form of this propagator depends on the parameter regime,
making a direct calculation cumbersome. A detailed discussion of
p(\mathrm{v},t)
is provided

A

A more compact and unified approach is to use the joint distribution of the total step
duration
\tau
and the elapsed time within the current step
t^{\prime}
at a fixed observation
time
t
, denoted by
p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})
. This representation allows all parameter regimes to be
treated within a single analytical framework.

The distribution
p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})
can be expressed as a sum over all possible step numbers,

p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}(t,\tau,t^{\prime}),

(11)

where
p_{n}(t,\tau,t^{\prime})
is the probability that the process is in its
n
-th step at time
t
,
the intended duration of that step is
\tau
, and the elapsed time within that step is
t^{\prime}
.
Conditioning on being in the
n
-th step gives

\displaystyle p_{n}(t,\tau,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\delta(\tau-t_{n})\,\delta\!\left[t^{\prime}-\Big(t-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}\Big)\right]

(12)

\displaystyle\times I\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}<t<\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\right)\Big\rangle,

where
t_{i}
is the duration of the
i
-th step, and
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function whose value is 1 if the argument is true otherwise 0.

Taking Laplace transforms with respect to the variables

t\to z
,
\tau\to s
, and
t^{\prime}\to u
, one obtains the closed-form expression

26
;
8

\widetilde{p}(z,s,u)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{p}_{n}(z,s,u)=\frac{1}{u+z}\,\frac{\widetilde{\psi}(s)-\widetilde{\psi}(s+u+z)}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(z)}.

(13)

Performing the inverse Laplace transforms step by step

8
, we obtain

\widetilde{p}(z,\tau,u)=\psi(\tau)\,\frac{1-e^{-(u+z)\tau}}{(u+z)\,[1-\widetilde{\psi}(z)]},

(14)

and subsequently

\widetilde{p}(z,\tau,t^{\prime})=\psi(\tau)\,\frac{e^{-zt^{\prime}}}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(z)}\,\Theta(\tau-t^{\prime}),

(15)

so that the full distribution is

\displaystyle p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\psi(\tau)\;\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(z^{\prime})}\right]\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\,\Theta(\tau-t^{\prime}),

(16)

where
z^{\prime}
is the Laplace variable corresponding to
t-t^{\prime}
.

The inverse Laplace term depends on whether the mean step duration is finite or infinite:

Case
\gamma<1
: divergent mean-step duration

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(z^{\prime})}\right]\approx\frac{(t-t^{\prime})^{\gamma-1}}{|\Gamma(1-\gamma)|\,\Gamma(\gamma)\,t_{0}^{\gamma}}.

(17)

Case
\gamma>1
: finite mean-step duration

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(z^{\prime})}\right]=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma\,t_{0}}.

(18)

Using

, the velocity moments follow as

\displaystyle\langle|\mathrm{v}|\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\int|\mathrm{v}|\,p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})\,d\tau\,dt^{\prime}

(19)

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\int|\eta c\,\tau^{\nu-\eta}t^{\prime\,\eta-1}|\;p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})\,d\tau\,dt^{\prime},

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{v}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\int\mathrm{v}^{2}\,p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})\,d\tau\,dt^{\prime}

(20)

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\int\eta^{2}c^{2}\,\tau^{2\nu-2\eta}t^{\prime\,2\eta-2}\;p(t,\tau,t^{\prime})\,d\tau\,dt^{\prime}.

V
J for VGLWs

As discussed in Section
III
, the Joseph exponent
J
is obtained from the
scaling of the TAMSD, defined in Eq.
6
.
For GLWs, this calculation was carried out in
8
.
Here we extend the analysis to VGLWs and show that
J
is identical to the GLW case;
in particular, it remains independent of the parameter
\eta

The TAMSD of VGLWs is related to the velocity autocorrelation function through the
scaling Green–Kubo relation
42
:

\left\langle\overline{x^{2}(t,\Delta)}\right\rangle\approx\frac{2}{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{0}\int_{0}^{\Delta}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{\Delta}dt_{2}\;\langle\mathrm{v}(t_{1}+t_{0})\,\mathrm{v}(t_{2}+t_{0})\rangle

(29)

valid in the limit
t\gg\Delta
.
The Joseph exponent
J
is extracted from the
\Delta
-dependence of the above
expression, according to the scaling form in Eq.
7
.

For VGLWs, the velocity autocorrelation function (VCF),

C(t,t+\Delta)=\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle
,
admits the scaling form provided by the generalized Green–Kubo framework

42
,

C(t,t+\Delta)\simeq C_{0}\,t^{q-2}\,\phi\!\left(\frac{\Delta}{t}\right),

(30)

where
C_{0}>0
is a constant,
q>1
is a scaling exponent, and

\phi(z)
is a universal scaling function that encodes the dependence on the ratio

\Delta/t
.
The scaling function satisfies the asymptotic bounds
42

\displaystyle\phi(z)<c_{1}\,z^{-\delta_{1}},

\displaystyle 2-q\leq\delta_{1}<1,\qquad z\to 0,

(31)

\displaystyle\phi(z)<c_{u}\,z^{-\delta_{u}},

\displaystyle\delta_{u}>1-q,\qquad z\to\infty,

(32)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{u}
are positive constants.
These conditions ensure convergence of Eq. (
29
) and allow the
scaling form of the TAMSD to be determined analytically.

Substituting the scaling Green–Kubo form of the VCF into Eq. (
29
)
and performing the resulting integrals yields a TAMSD that scales as

\Delta^{2J}
, consistent with Eq.
7

Thus, the Joseph exponent for VGLWs coincides with the GLWs result, demonstrating that

J
is independent of the velocity-shaping parameter
\eta

V.1
Velocity Correlation Function of VGLWs

To compute the velocity correlation function (VCF)
\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle
for the Variable-Speed Generalized Lévy Walk (VGLW), we follow the analytical framework developed by Godrèche and Luck
26
.
Let
C_{n}(t,\Delta)\equiv\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle_{n}
denote the contribution to the VCF from trajectories that undergo exactly
n
renewal events in the interval
(0,t)
.
Summing over all possible
n
yields the full VCF:

\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle=C(t,\Delta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}(t,\Delta).

(33)

A renewal during the interval
(t,\,t+\Delta)
destroys correlations between
\mathrm{v}(t)
and
\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)
.
Therefore, the only nonzero contribution to the VCF comes from trajectories for which no renewal occurs between
t
and
t+\Delta
, meaning both observation times lie within the same step.

A complete derivation of the VCF, including its asymptotic scaling form and dependence on the parameters
(\gamma,\nu,\eta)
, is provided in Appendix
C
.

V.2
TAMSD for VGLWs

A process observed from the beginning of its evolution (
t=0
) corresponds to a
non-aging
walk.
In contrast, when the system is initiated at

The Hurst exponent
H

4

The propagator

\displaystyle=r_{0}(t)-\frac{k^{2}}{2}\,r_{2}(t)+o(k^{2}),

IX
Discussion and Conclusion

VGLWs provide a unified framework that encompasses many previously studied
continuous-time random walk (CTRW) models
1
.
By tuning the parameter
\eta
, the model interpolates smoothly between a number of
important limiting cases.
For
\eta=1
, VGLWs reduce to GLWs
7
,
and for
\nu=\eta=1
, they further collapse to the standard Lévy walk
model
65
;
57
.
In the limits
\eta\to 0
and
\eta\to\infty

16
, the dynamics
correspond to the wait–jump and jump–wait Lévy flight processes, respectively.
When
\nu=\eta
, the model becomes equivalent to the Drude-type dynamics in which the
velocity depends only on the elapsed time of the step
54
;
13
.

In this work we analyze anomalous diffusion in VGLWs through its decomposition into the
Joseph, Noah, and Moses effects.
This decomposition reveals nine distinct dynamical phases, each governed by a different
combination of these three constitutive mechanisms.
Although the model contains three control parameters, the role of
\eta
is limited:
it affects only the position of the boundary of the non-scaling (infinite) regime, where
the standard diffusive scaling breaks down.

Earlier work
3
treated only the regime
\gamma<1
, in which the
mean step duration diverges and the dynamics are dominated by a single long step.
Here, we extend the decomposition to the regime
\gamma>1
, where the mean step
duration is finite and the statistics emerge from the collective contribution of many
steps.
For
\gamma<1
, anomalous diffusion is always generated jointly by the Joseph, Noah,
and Moses effects, producing five distinct dynamical phases.
In contrast, for
\gamma>1
the system may exhibit either anomalous or normal
diffusion.
Normal diffusion occurs when
\nu<\gamma/2
, where all exponents take the value

1/2
.
When
\gamma/2<\nu<\gamma/2+1/2
, anomalous diffusion arises solely from the
Joseph effect.
For
\gamma/2+1/2<\nu<\gamma
, anomalous diffusion results from the combined
action of the Noah and Joseph effects.
When
\gamma<\nu<\gamma/2+\eta
, all three effects act simultaneously.
Finally, for
\nu>\gamma/2+\eta/2
, the system enters a non-scaling (infinite)
regime in which the usual scaling laws no longer apply.

A striking feature of the VGLW framework is that, in the range

\gamma/2+\eta/2>\nu>\gamma/2+1/2\quad\text{with}\quad\eta>1,

the Noah exponent
L
can exceed unity.
This contrasts with earlier findings for Lévy-walk-type processes, where
L\leq 1

was believed to be the upper limit
18
;
3
.
Our results show that
L
can surpass
1
in the regime
\gamma>1
, demonstrating that
the Noah exponent has no intrinsic upper bound within the VGLW model.

This extension is particularly important because many real-world systems operate in the

\gamma>1
regime.
Examples include physical, biological, and geological transport processes such as
chaotic advection, groundwater flow, molecular motion, and animal foraging
dynamics
23
;
22
;
64
;
59
;
14
;
19
;
34
;
15
.
Our results enable the identification of which constitutive effect—Joseph, Noah, or
Moses—dominates the observed anomalous behavior in such systems.
The resulting framework therefore captures the essential physics of VGLW-type
transport and provides deeper insight into the mechanisms driving complex anomalous
diffusion phenomena.

Appendix A
The velocity propagator of VGLW

In this section, we derive the velocity propagator of VGLWs,

p(\mathrm{v},t)
, defined as the probability density of observing a
walker with velocity
\mathrm{v}
at time
t
.
Unlike GLWs, the velocity in a VGLW is not constant within a step; instead,
it depends deterministically on both the elapsed time
t^{\prime}
since the
beginning of the step and the total step duration
\tau
.

To compute
p(\mathrm{v},t)
, we first introduce the conditional propagator

p(\mathrm{v},t,t^{\prime})
, which gives the probability density of observing
velocity
\mathrm{v}
at time
t
, conditioned on the walker having spent
an elapsed time
t^{\prime}
in the current step. The full propagator is obtained by
integrating over all admissible
t^{\prime}
.

Using the step-duration distribution in
Eq.
2
and performing a change of variables
from
\tau
to
\mathrm{v}
, we obtain the conditional velocity distribution
at fixed elapsed time
t^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\chi(\mathrm{v},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\psi(\tau)\left|\frac{d\tau}{d\mathrm{v}}\right|

(50)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\tau_{0}^{\gamma}(\eta c)^{\gamma/(\nu-\eta)}}{\nu-\eta}\,t^{\prime\frac{(\eta-1)\gamma}{\nu-\eta}}\,|\mathrm{v}|^{-\frac{\gamma}{\nu-\eta}-1}.

For fixed elapsed time
t^{\prime}
, the joint distribution of velocity and step
duration for an incomplete step is

\phi(\mathrm{v},\tau,t^{\prime})=\chi(\mathrm{v},t^{\prime})\,\delta\!\left[\left(\frac{|\mathrm{v}|}{\eta ct^{\prime\eta-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu-\eta}}-\tau\right]\Theta(\tau-t^{\prime}).

(51)

Because the dynamics are symmetric with respect to direction,

\phi(\mathrm{v},\tau,t^{\prime})=\phi(-\mathrm{v},\tau,t^{\prime})
.

The escape probability
W(\mathrm{v},t,t^{\prime})
, defined as the probability that
a walker with elapsed time
t^{\prime}
continues the current step beyond time
t
,
is then

\displaystyle W(\mathrm{v},t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\infty}\phi(\mathrm{v},\tau,t^{\prime})\,d\tau

(52)

\displaystyle=\chi(\mathrm{v},t^{\prime})\,\Theta\!\left[\left(\frac{|\mathrm{v}|}{\eta ct^{\prime\eta-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu-\eta}}-t\right]\Theta(t-t^{\prime}).

The renewal density
R(t)
, representing the probability density for the
initiation of a new step at time
t
, is identical to that of GLWs since it
depends only on the step-duration distribution. Following
Ref.
4
,

R(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(z)}\right],

(53)

where
\widetilde{\psi}(z)
is the Laplace transform of
\psi(t)
.

The conditional propagator corresponds to a renewal at time
t-t^{\prime}
followed
by a step that survives at least until time
t
. It is therefore given by

\displaystyle p(\mathrm{v},t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=R(t-t^{\prime})\,W(\mathrm{v},t^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(54)

\displaystyle=R(t-t^{\prime})\,\chi(\mathrm{v},t^{\prime})\,\Theta\!\left[\left(\frac{|\mathrm{v}|}{\eta ct^{\prime\eta-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu-\eta}}-t^{\prime}\right]\Theta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\gamma\tau_{0}^{\gamma}(\eta c)^{\gamma/(\nu-\eta)}}{\nu-\eta}\,|\mathrm{v}|^{-\frac{\gamma}{\nu-\eta}-1}\,R(t-t^{\prime})\,t^{\prime\frac{(\eta-1)\gamma}{\nu-\eta}}

\displaystyle\times\Theta\!\left[\left(\frac{|\mathrm{v}|}{\eta ct^{\prime\eta-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu-\eta}}-t^{\prime}\right]\Theta(t-t^{\prime}).

The full velocity propagator is obtained by integrating over all elapsed
times,

p(\mathrm{v},t)=\int_{0}^{t}p(\mathrm{v},t,t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}.

(55)

The above construction is valid only when
\nu\neq\eta
.
In the special case
\nu=\eta
, the velocity depends solely on the elapsed
time,

\mathrm{v}_{\nu,\eta}=\pm\eta c\,t^{\prime\eta-1}.

(56)

Since the velocity is independent of the total step duration
\tau
, all
steps with duration exceeding
t^{\prime}
contribute equally.
The conditional propagator then reduces to

\displaystyle p(\mathrm{v},t,t^{\prime})=R(t-t^{\prime})\,\delta\!\left(|\mathrm{v}|-\eta ct^{\prime\eta-1}\right)\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\!\int_{t^{\prime}}^{\infty}\psi(\tau)\,d\tau.

(57)

The full propagator
p(\mathrm{v},t)
is again obtained by integration over

t^{\prime}
.

of
p(\mathrm{v},t)
resulting from these expressions. The detalied calculations are shown in appendix.

Appendix B

In this appendix, we present the detailed derivation of the velocity propagator

for Variable Speed Generalized Lévy Walks (VGLWs). Building on the renewal framework and joint time distributions introduced in
Appendix A, we derive the full velocity propagator by integrating over all
possible elapsed times within a step. We obtain explicit asymptotic forms of

p(\mathrm{v},t)
.

B.1
\nu\neq\eta

Eq.
54
contains the Heaviside step function
\Theta(\cdot)
, which evaluates to unity only when

Depending on the specific choices of the parameters
\nu
and
\eta
, four distinct cases arise, corresponding to different regions of parameter space. Each of these cases must be considered separately for both
\gamma<1
and
\gamma>1
.

B.1.1
\gamma<1

According to
4
, the Laplace transform of
R(t)
is given by
\widetilde{R}(s)=\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}
,
where
s
is the Laplace variable conjugate to
t
. The inverse Laplace transform

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\psi}(s)}\right]
is evaluated in
Eq.
17
for the case
\gamma<1
.
From that expression, the corresponding time-domain form of

is

\displaystyle R(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{(t-t^{\prime})^{\gamma-1}}{|\Gamma(1-\gamma)|\Gamma(\gamma)t_{0}^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Gamma(1-\gamma)|\Gamma(\gamma)t_{0}^{\gamma}}t^{\gamma-1}\left(1-\frac{t^{\prime}}{t}\right)^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Gamma(1-\gamma)|\Gamma(\gamma)t_{0}^{\gamma}}\left(t^{\gamma-1}\left(1-\frac{(\gamma-1)t^{\prime}}{t}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\frac{(\gamma-1)(\gamma-2)t^{\prime 2}}{2t^{2}}+O\left(\left(\frac{t^{\prime}}{t}\right)^{2}\right)\right)\right)

This expansion of

will be used in the subsequent equations.

Appendix C
Derivation of the velocity correlation function

VCF
\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle
can be written as,

\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle=C(t,\Delta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}(t,\Delta).

(111)

where
C_{n}(t,\Delta)=\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle_{n}
the contribution to the VCF from trajectories that undergo exactly
n
renewal events in the interval
(0,t)
.
C_{n}(s,u)
can be expressed as

C_{n}(s,u)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}dt\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u\Delta}d\Delta\langle\mathrm{v}(t)\mathrm{v}(t+\Delta)\rangle_{n}

(112)

Putting the constraint of
t_{n}<t<t+\Delta<t_{n+1}
in the above equation, where
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}
are the time of
n
and
n+1
th renewal process, we get

\displaystyle C_{n}(s,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{2}c^{2}\left\langle\tau_{n}^{2(\nu-\eta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}dt\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u\Delta}d\Delta\left(t-t_{n}\right)^{\eta-1}\left(t+\Delta-t_{n}\right)^{\eta-1}\right.\theta\left(t_{n}<t<t_{n+1}\right)

(113)

\displaystyle\left.\theta\left(t_{n}<t+\Delta<t_{n+1}\right)\right\rangle

Here
\theta(\cdots)=1
if the condition in the parenthesis is valid otherwise the
\theta
function is zero, so
\theta(\ldots)
is a square pulse function. The average is with respect to the time process. Switching integration variables according to
t-t_{n}=y
and using
t_{n+1}-t_{n}=\tau_{n}
we find

\displaystyle C_{n}(s,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{2}c^{2}\left\langle e^{-t_{n}s}\tau_{n}^{2(\nu-\eta)}\int_{0}^{{\tau}_{n}}dye^{-sy}y^{\eta-1}\int_{0}^{{\tau}_{n}-y}d\Delta e^{-u\Delta}(y+\Delta)^{\eta-1}\right\rangle

(114)

From renewal assumption the random variables
t_{n}
and
\tau_{n+1}
are independent. Further since
t_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\tau_{j}
and because the waiting times are also independent we have
\left\langle\exp\left(-st_{n}\right)\right\rangle=\psi^{n}(s)
, where
\psi(s)
is the Laplace
\tau\rightarrow s
transform of
\psi(\tau)
. The remaining average is with respect to
\tau_{n}
, which is a random variable drawn from
\psi(\tau)
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle C_{n}(s,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{2}c^{2}\psi^{n}(s)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\psi(\tau)\tau^{2(\nu-\eta)}\int_{0}^{\tau}dye^{-sy}y^{\eta-1}\int_{0}^{\tau-y}d\Delta e^{-u\Delta}(y+\Delta)^{\eta-1}

(115)

Let us denote

\displaystyle W(\tau)=\int_{\tau}^{\infty}d\tilde{\tau}\psi\left(\tilde{\tau}\right)\tilde{\tau}^{2(\nu-\eta)}

(116)

The above integral is convergent only if
2\nu\leq\gamma+2\eta
. Keeping that condition in mind, we can write

\displaystyle C_{n}(s,u)=\eta^{2}c^{2}\psi^{n}(s)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\left[-\frac{d}{d\tau}W(\tau)\right]\int_{0}^{\tau}dye^{-sy}y^{\eta-1}\int_{0}^{\tau-y}d\Delta e^{-u\Delta}(y+\Delta)^{\eta-1}

(117)

We now integrate by parts, and then the geometric series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{\psi}^{n}(s)=1/[1-\hat{\psi}(s)]
gives

\displaystyle C(s,u)=\frac{\eta^{2}c^{2}}{1-\psi(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau W(\tau)\tau^{\eta-1}e^{-u\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}dye^{-(s-u)y}y^{\eta-1}

(118)

The double laplace transform of the above function will give us the velocity correlation function,
C(t,\Delta)
. We can invert
u
to
\Delta
in the above function, resulting

Putting the value of
W(\tau)
,
