Killing Superalgebras in 2 Dimensions
Andrew D.K. BECKETT

Abstract
We provide some examples of Killing superalgebras on 2-dimensional pseudo-Riemannian manifolds within the theoretical framework established in [ SIGMA 21 (2025), 081, 61 pages]. We compute the Spencer cohomology group 𝖧 2 , 2 ​ ( 𝔰 − , 𝔰 ) \mathsf{H}^{2,2}(\mathfrak{s}_{-};\mathfrak{s}) and filtered deformations of the non-chiral flat model (Euclidean and Poincaré) superalgebra 𝔰 \mathfrak{s} for various Dirac currents and show these arise as Killing superalgebras for (imaginary) geometric Killing and skew-Killing spinors in both Riemannian and Lorentzian signature.

Introduction

In recent work
[
4
]
, we discussed a generalisation of the concept of Killing superalgebras, which arise as supersymmetry algebras of supersymmetric solutions in supergravity theories
[
11
]
, to a more general context. To this end, we introduced the concept of an
admissible connection

D
on a bundle of spinors
\underline{S}
over a pseudo-Riemannian manifold
M
of arbitrary signature equipped with a Dirac current
\kappa\colon\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}\underline{S}\to TM
or
\kappa\colon\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{\bigwedge}{\bigwedge}{\bigwedge}^{2}\underline{S}\to TM
. The Killing (super)algebra associated to this data was then defined as the vector space
\mathfrak{K}_{D}=\mathfrak{V}_{D}\oplus\mathfrak{S}_{D}
, where
\mathfrak{S}_{D}
is the space of
D
-parallel spinor fields (known here as
Killing spinors
) and
\mathfrak{V}_{D}
is the space of Killing vectors preserving the connection equipped with a bracket defined using the Dirac current
\kappa
and the (vectorial and spinorial) Lie derivative
[
4
, Definition 3.6]
.

We discussed the algebraic structure of these Killing superalgebras, showing
[
4
, Theorem 3.10]
that they were filtered subdeformations of
flat model
(super)algebras which are generalisations of the (
N
-extended) Poincaré superalgebra first systematically discussed in
[
23
]
. The classification of these flat models in general signature is given in
[
1
]
. We also discussed how the Spencer cohomology of the flat models can be used to study their filtered subdeformations with particular focus on the case of Lorentzian signature and symmetric, causal Dirac current, generalising many results from the previously-studied 11-dimensional supergravity case
[
13
]
to general dimension and
N
-extension.

While providing a number of theoretical results, our previous work
[
4
]
did not contain any concrete examples. Numerous examples are provided by existing work in Lorentzian signature, primarily in 11 dimensions
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
22
]
but also in 4, 5 and 6 dimensions
[
2
,
8
,
9
]
, while work in Riemannian signature
[
10
]
has shown that geometric Killing spinors on higher-dimensional spheres provide geometric realisations of the exceptional algebras
\mathfrak{f}_{4}
and
\mathfrak{e}_{8}
as well as
\mathfrak{so}(8)
triality as Killing algebras. However, the literature is lacking in some simpler examples which demonstrate the framework as well as the effect of different choices – for example, signature and Dirac current – on the definition of admissible connections and on the associated Killing (super)algebras. In this work, we provide these simple examples by considering admissible connections and Killing (super)algebras over 2-dimensional manifolds, and we also study the Spencer cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{2,2}
and filtered deformations of the relevant flat models.

This work is intended as a companion to
[
4
]
which illustrates its main theoretical ideas and is best read in parallel with that work; we use the same terminology and conventions in both works. We will consider only signatures
(0,2)
and
(1,1)
since the Clifford algebra is isomorphic to the real matrix algebra
\mathbb{R}(2)
in both cases, hence the situation is much simpler than the
(2,0)
case where the Clifford algebra is
\mathbb{H}
. Indeed, the former cases are so similar that we will be able to perform many of the calculations in a signature-agnostic way; we will however choose a sign convention (see equation (
2.1
)) which will allow us to work in practice with a positive-definite inner product in signature
(0,2)
. The quaternionic
(2,0)
case, as well as other generalisations, will be treated in future work.

2
Constructions, conventions and formulae for spinor modules

Throughout, we let
V=\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
or
\mathbb{R}^{0,2}
and note as mentioned in the introduction that in either case
\Cl(V)\cong\mathbb{R}(2)
. We will define explicit matrix representations of the Clifford algebras in either case below; for now we note that these will allow us to identify the real irreducible pinor module
\mathbb{S}
as
\mathbb{R}^{2}
under left multiplication by matrices.

2.1
Signature-agnostic Clifford algebra conventions

As discussed in
[
4
, Appendix A.1.2]
, there is a choice of sign convention in the Clifford algebra relation

v\cdot v=\pm\eta(v,v)\mathbbm{1},

(2.1)

where
v\in V
; we take the mathematical convention with
-
in defining
\Cl(p,q)=\Cl(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
but the more common convention in physics with
+
for explicit calculations, following some previous work on Killing superalgebras. We will speak of signature
(0,2)
to indicate that we are working with the Clifford algebra
\Cl(0,2)
, thus with the
+
convention above the inner product
\eta
must in fact be
positive-definite
. In choosing (ordered) orthonormal bases
\{e_{\mu}\}
for
V
, we take the “
mostly-positive
” convention
\eta_{00}=-\eta_{11}=-1
in signature
(1,1)
. We define the sign

\varsigma=\det[\eta]

which is
+1
in signature
(0,2)
and
-1
in signature
(1,1)
.

In an orthonormal basis
\{e_{\mu}\}
, we denote the matrix representing
e_{\mu}
under any specified isomorphism
\Cl(V)\to\mathbb{R}(2)
by
\Gamma_{\mu}
and also define
\Gamma_{\mu\nu}:=\Gamma_{[\mu}\Gamma_{\nu]}
. The matrix representing the canonical volume element
\vol\in\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{\bigwedge}{\bigwedge}{\bigwedge}^{2}V
is
\Gamma_{*}=\Gamma_{12}
or
\Gamma_{01}
depending on signature. Respectively to signature,
\{\mathbbm{1},\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{*}\}
or
\{\mathbbm{1},\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{*}\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{R}(2)
and we will refer to these as
\Gamma
-matrices
. The algebraic relations among these matrices are as follows:

\Gamma_{\mu}\Gamma_{\nu}=\Gamma_{\mu\nu}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\mathbbm{1}=\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\Gamma_{*}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\mathbbm{1},\qquad\Gamma_{\mu}\Gamma_{*}=-\Gamma_{*}\Gamma_{\mu}=\varsigma\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\Gamma^{\nu},\qquad(\Gamma_{*})^{2}=-\varsigma\mathbbm{1},

where
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}
is the Levi-Civita symbol defined so that
\varepsilon_{12}=-\varepsilon_{21}=+1
in definite signature and
\varepsilon_{01}=-\varepsilon_{10}=+1
in Lorentzian signature. Using the metric to raise and lower indices, we must define the symbols with raised indices so that
\varepsilon^{12}=-\varepsilon^{21}=+1
and
\varepsilon^{01}=-\varepsilon^{10}=-1
respectively. We have the following combinatorial identities for
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}
:

\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{\rho\sigma}=\varsigma\quantity(\eta_{\mu\rho}\eta_{\nu\sigma} - \eta_{\mu\sigma}\eta_{\nu\rho}),\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.14899pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.14899pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.53337pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.80954pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\nu}}}_{{{\rho}\mathchoice{\makebox[4.00928pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.00928pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.42052pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.72893pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}=\varsigma\eta_{\mu\rho},\qquad\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}=2\varsigma.

For the avoidance of confusion in calculations, it is useful to denote the top-rank gamma matrix with indices raised as
\Gamma^{*}=\Gamma^{12}
or
\Gamma^{01}
and note the following

\Gamma^{*}=\varsigma\Gamma_{*}
,

\Gamma^{\mu\nu}=\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\Gamma_{*}=\varsigma\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\Gamma^{*}
.
We also have the following traces of products

\Gamma^{\mu}\Gamma_{\mu}=2\mathbbm{1}
,
\Gamma^{\mu}\Gamma_{\nu}\Gamma_{\mu}=0
,
\Gamma^{\mu}\Gamma_{*}\Gamma_{\mu}=-2\Gamma_{*}
.
Finally, we note that the even subalgebra
\Cl_{\overline{0}}(V)
corresponds to the span
\mathbb{R}\{\mathbbm{1},\Gamma_{*}\}
and the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}(V)\cong\mathbb{R}}
to the span
\mathbb{R}\Gamma_{*}
.

2.2
Pinor and spinor modules, admissible bilinears and Dirac currents

We now describe the (s)pinor modules as well as their admissible bilinears and Dirac current explicitly. We will make extensive use of the Pauli matrices

\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\rm i}\\
{\rm i}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{3}
are real symmetric matrices and
\Omega={\rm i}\sigma_{2}
is the (real and skew-symmetric) standard symplectic matrix. The sets
\{\mathbbm{1},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
and
\{\mathbbm{1},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{3},\Omega={\rm i}\sigma_{2}\}
are both
\mathbb{C}
-bases for
\mathbb{C}(2)

\mathbb{R}
-basis for
\mathbb{R}(2)

\sigma_{i}^{\dagger}=\sigma_{i}
,

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\delta_{ij}\mathbbm{1}+{\rm i}\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}
,
where
†
denotes Hermitian adjoint (conjugate-transpose),
\varepsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita symbol with normalisation
\varepsilon_{123}=+1
, and we use the Einstein summation convention on repeated indices. The second identity is also useful to express in the form
{\commutator{\sigma_i}{\sigma_j}=2{\rm i}\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}}
,

\anticommutator{\sigma_i}{\sigma_j}=2\delta_{ij}\mathbbm{1}
,
where
\commutator{-}{-}
is the matrix commutator,
\anticommutator{-}{-}
is the anti-commutator.

We recall the following from
[
1
]
where the flat model (super)algebras are classified by classifying the possible Dirac currents, that is
\mathfrak{so}(V)
-equivariant maps
\kappa\colon\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}S\to V
where

is a (possibly
N
-extended) spinor module of
\mathfrak{so}(V)

\mathsf{S}

\kappa

B
(defined below) on
\mathsf{S}
via the formula

\eta(\kappa(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}),v)=B(\epsilon,v\cdot\epsilon^{\prime})

(2.2)

for all
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}
,

.

Definition 2.1

(admissible bilinear)
.

Let
(V,\eta)
be a (pseudo-)inner product space and let
\mathsf{S}
be a real irreducible module of
\Cl(V)
(a real pinor module). A real bilinear form
B
on
\mathsf{S}
is

(1)

is either symmetric or skew-symmetric

\varsigma_{B}=\pm 1

of

.

(2)

is either
B
-symmetric or
B
-skew-symmetric

for all
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}
, where
\tau_{B}=\pm 1
is called the
type
of
B
.

(3)

whenever
\mathsf{S}
is reducible as an
\mathfrak{so}(V)
-module,
\mathsf{S}=\mathsf{S}_{+}\oplus\mathsf{S}_{-}
, the submodules
\mathsf{S}_{\pm}
are either mutually

B

\iota_{B}
of

in the first case and
-1
in the second.

Furthermore, if
B
is an admissible bilinear on
\mathsf{S}

\kappa\colon\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}\mathsf{S}\to V
defined by equation (

) satisfies

\kappa(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})=\varsigma_{\kappa}\kappa(\epsilon^{\prime},\epsilon)

for all
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}
, where
\varsigma_{\kappa}=\varsigma_{B}\tau_{B}=\pm 1

of
\kappa
.

2.3
Adjoints and Fierz identity

The
Fierz identity
is a formula which allows us to rearrange products of (s)pinors involving bilinears. It is simplest to express using conjugate spinor notation; for
\epsilon\in\mathsf{S}
, we denote by
\overline{\epsilon}
the element of
\mathsf{S}^{*}
given by
\epsilon^{\prime}\mapsto\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon^{\prime}:=B(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})
(where we have fixed the bilinear
B
). Then as well as the inner product
\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon^{\prime}
, we have an
outer product
(or tensor product)
\epsilon\overline{\epsilon}^{\prime}:=\epsilon\otimes\overline{\epsilon}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}\otimes\mathsf{S}^{*}=\End\mathsf{S}
. If we choose an abstract basis for
\mathsf{S}
with indices
a,b,\ldots=1,2
in which
\epsilon
is represented by the vector
(\epsilon^{a})_{a=1,2}
, its adjoint
\overline{\epsilon}
by the covector
(\overline{\epsilon}_{a})_{a=1,2}
, a bilinear
B
by the matrix
(B_{ab})_{a,b=1,2}
, and an endomorphism
\Gamma
by the matrix
(\Gamma^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{b}}})_{a,b=1,2}
, we can write the following in Einstein notation:

\overline{\epsilon}_{b}=\epsilon^{a}B_{ab}
,

\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon=\overline{\epsilon}_{b}\epsilon^{b}=B_{ab}\epsilon^{a}\epsilon^{b}
,

(\epsilon\overline{\epsilon})^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{b}}}=\epsilon^{a}\overline{\epsilon}_{b}=\epsilon^{a}\epsilon^{c}B_{cb}
.

Proposition 2.2

(Fierz identity)
.

Let
\mathsf{S}
be the real irreducible pinor module in signature
(1,1)
or
(0,2)
and
B
either of the admissible bilinears from the appropriate table above. Then for
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}
, we have

\epsilon\overline{\epsilon}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity((\overline\epsilon'\epsilon)\1 + (\overline\epsilon'\Gamma_\mu\epsilon)\Gamma^\mu- \varsigma(\overline\epsilon'\Gamma_*\epsilon)\Gamma_*).

Proof.

Since
\epsilon\overline{\epsilon}^{\prime}
is an endomorphism of
S
, it is an
R
-linear combination of
\Gamma
-matrices; we have
{\epsilon\overline{\epsilon}^{\prime}=a\mathbbm{1}+b_{\mu}\Gamma^{\mu}+c\Gamma_{*}}

for some
a,b_{\mu},c\in\mathbb{R}
. We see from the explicit matrix representation given in Section
2.2
that
\Gamma_{\mu}
and
\Gamma_{*}
are traceless, while
\tr\mathbbm{1}=\dim\mathsf{S}=2
, whence we compute
{\tr(\epsilon\overline\epsilon')=2a}
,
\tr(\Gamma_\nu\epsilon\overline\epsilon')=2b_{\nu}
,
\tr(\Gamma_*\epsilon\overline\epsilon')=-\varsigma 2c
.
On the other hand, for any
\Gamma\in\End\mathsf{S}
,
\tr(\Gamma\epsilon\overline\epsilon')=\overline{\epsilon}^{\prime}\Gamma\epsilon
, which can be verified in the explicit representation or by noting that in Einstein notation, both expressions are equal to
B_{ab}\Gamma^{{{b}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.57375pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.57375pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1205pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.51463pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{c}}}\epsilon^{\prime a}\epsilon^{c}

∎

We can now render the definition (
2.2
) of the Dirac current
\kappa\colon\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}\mathsf{S}\to V
in the more convenient form

\kappa(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})^{\mu}=B(\epsilon,\Gamma^{\mu}\epsilon^{\prime})
.
Since
\kappa
is necessarily symmetric in either signature, for
\epsilon\in\mathsf{S}
we define
\kappa_{\epsilon}:=\kappa(\epsilon,\epsilon)\in V
which has components

\kappa_{\epsilon}^{\mu}=\overline{\epsilon}\Gamma^{\mu}\epsilon=B(\epsilon,\Gamma^{\mu}\epsilon)
.

The Fierz identity has consequences for the causal properties of the Dirac current.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\epsilon\in\mathsf{S}
. Then

\norm{\kappa_\epsilon}^{2}=(\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon)^{2}+\varsigma(\overline{\epsilon}\Gamma_{*}\epsilon)^{2}
.
In particular, in the Riemannian case
(\varsigma=+1)
,
\kappa_{\epsilon}=0
if and only if
\epsilon=0
; in the Lorentzian case
(\varsigma=-1)
,
\kappa
is null if and only if
\epsilon
is chiral, otherwise
\kappa
is spacelike for
\varsigma_{B}=+1
and timelike for
\varsigma_{B}=-1
.

Proof.

For the first claim, applying the Fierz identity to the expression
(\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon)^{2}
gives us

(\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon)^{2}=\overline{\epsilon}(\epsilon\overline{\epsilon})\epsilon=\frac{1}{2}\quantity((\overline\epsilon\epsilon)(\overline\epsilon\epsilon) + (\overline\epsilon\Gamma_\mu\epsilon)(\overline\epsilon\Gamma^\mu\epsilon) - \varsigma(\overline\epsilon\Gamma_*\epsilon)(\overline\epsilon\Gamma_*\epsilon)),

which can be rearranged to give the desired expression. Now, consider the two quadratic forms
\overline{\epsilon}\Gamma_{*}\epsilon
and
\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon
and note that
\overline{\epsilon}\Gamma_{*}\epsilon=0
if
\varsigma_{B}=+1
and
\overline{\epsilon}\epsilon=0
if
\varsigma_{B}=-1
. For
\varsigma=+1
, the expression which does not vanish identically vanishes if and only if
\epsilon=0
(check this in the explicit representation). For
\varsigma=-1
, since the isotropy
\iota_{B}=-1
for either admissible bilinear (see Table
1
) and
\Gamma_{*}
preserves the chiral subspaces, the expression which does not vanish identically vanishes if and only if
\epsilon\in\mathsf{S}_{\pm}
. This proves the second claim.
∎

3
Spencer cohomology and filtered subdeformations

We now consider flat model superalgebras
\mathfrak{s}
(as defined in
[
4
, Definition 2.1]
) associated to the inner product space
V=\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
or
\mathbb{R}^{0,2}
with odd part
S=\mathsf{S}
or
S=\mathsf{S}_{+}
(the latter only in the Lorentzian case) and the Dirac current
\kappa\colon\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}S\to V
being the one associated to one of the two admissible bilinears described in Table
1
or Table
2
respectively, or a restriction thereof (more details below). Note that only superalgebras are possible here since all Dirac currents are symmetric. In either signature, the two Dirac currents on
\mathsf{S}
can be distinguished by the invariant
\varsigma_{B}=\pm 1
. We will determine the Spencer cohomology group
\mathsf{H}^{2,2}(\mathfrak{s}_{-};\mathfrak{s})
for each such superalgebra as well as their maximally supersymmetric filtered subdeformations.

We recall that each superalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
is
\mathbb{Z}
-graded with
\mathfrak{s}_{-2}=V
,
\mathfrak{s}_{-1}=S
,
\mathfrak{s}_{0}=\mathfrak{so}(V)
and
\mathfrak{s}_{i}=0
otherwise, and its brackets are

\displaystyle\commutator{A}{B}=AB-BA=0,\qquad\commutator{A}{v}=Av,\qquad\commutator{v}{w}=0,

\displaystyle\commutator{A}{s}=A\cdot s=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{A}\cdot s,\qquad\commutator{v}{s}=0,\qquad\commutator{s}{s'}=\kappa(s,s^{\prime}),

(3.1)

for
A,B\in\mathfrak{s}_{0}
,
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}_{-1}
,
v,w\in\mathfrak{s}_{-2}
, and we will make use of depolarised equations involving symmetric combinations of spinors; for example the odd-odd bracket can be defined as
\commutator{s}{s}=\kappa_{s}:=\kappa(s,s)
for all
s\in S
. Note that since
\dim V=2
,
\dim\mathfrak{s}_{0}=\dim\mathfrak{so}(V)=1
, hence the first bracket must vanish.

In signature
(0,2)
, the pinor representation is irreducible under the action of the spin group, thus we take
S=\mathsf{S}_{1}=\mathsf{S}
and for either choice of Dirac current from Table
2
,
\mathfrak{s}
is a minimal flat model superalgebra. We note that
\End S\cong\Cl(V)
.

In the
(1,1)

3.1
Spencer cohomology

In either signature, let us fix
S=\mathsf{S}
and an admissible bilinear

(or

We recall that the Spencer complex

compatible with the differential

\mathsf{C}^{d,p}(\mathfrak{s}_{-};\mathfrak{s})=0

\mathfrak{s}
. We will not explicitly give the formula for

here; for a full description see
[
4

or the original work of Cheng and Kac on filtered deformations of graded superalgebras

. The natural action of
\mathfrak{s}
on the full cochain spaces

and coboundaries
\mathsf{B}^{d,p}(\mathfrak{s}_{-};\mathfrak{s})

\mathfrak{s}_{0}
on the graded cohomology groups
\mathsf{H}^{d,p}(\mathfrak{s}_{-};\mathfrak{s})=\mathsf{Z}^{d,p}(\mathfrak{s}_{-};\mathfrak{s})/\mathsf{B}^{d,p}(\mathfrak{s}_{-};\mathfrak{s})

Of most relevance here is the cohomology group

\displaystyle 0

3.2
Maximally supersymmetric filtered subdeformations

Let us now use the result above to describe the filtered subdeformations of
\mathfrak{s}
with odd part
{\mathfrak{s}_{-1}=S}
; i.e., the maximally supersymmetric deformations, in the terminology of
[
4
, Definition 4.10]
. Since we found that the Spencer cohomology in the chiral case (
S=\mathsf{S}_{+}
) in Lorentzian signature is trivial, we consider only the non-chiral case (
S=\mathsf{S}
) in either signature. Note that there is no sub-maximal highly supersymmetric case here since
\dim S=2
.

In
[
4
, Section 4.5.5]
, it is argued that odd-generated maximally supersymmetric filtered subdeformations are determined by normalised cocycles
\beta+\gamma\in\mathcal{H}^{2,2}
which are invariant under the action of the subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}_{(\beta+\gamma)}:=\gamma(\mathfrak{D})
of
\mathfrak{so}(V)
, where
\mathfrak{D}=\ker\kappa\subseteq\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}S
is the Dirac kernel, and which satisfy some integrability conditions. In the present case,
\dim\mathfrak{so}(V)=1
, so we must either have
\mathfrak{h}_{(\beta+\gamma)}=0
or
\mathfrak{h}_{(\beta+\gamma)}=\mathfrak{so}(V)
. Moreover, any element of
\mathcal{H}^{2,2}
is actually
\mathfrak{so}(V)
-invariant since
\mathcal{H}^{2,2}\cong\mathbb{R}
, the trivial
\mathfrak{so}(V)
-module. In particular, it suffices to study filtered deformations
\widetilde{\mathfrak{s}}
of the whole superalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
, since any deformation of the unique proper maximally supersymmetric subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}_{-}
can be extended to a deformation of
\mathfrak{s}
.

It remains only to check the integrability conditions, which were developed in
[
4
, Section 4.4.2]
. We adapt the definition of integrable cocycles
[
4
, Definition 4.23]
as follows. Fixing
{\beta+\gamma\in\mathcal{H}^{2,2}}

\Theta\colon V\otimes\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}S\to\mathfrak{so}(V)
be the map defined by

for
{v\in V}
,
s\in S
. We say that
\beta+\gamma
is

if the following hold.

(1)

The map
\Theta
annihilates the Dirac kernel
\mathfrak{D}
so factors through a map
\theta\colon V\otimes V\to\mathfrak{so}(V)
making the following diagram commute

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V\otimes\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigodot}}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}{\bigodot}^{2}S^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{so}(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V\otimes V.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta}

(2)

The map

It remains to check the third condition from the definition. We will first derive some results using the integrability condition

.

If
\epsilon

\nabla_{\kappa_{\epsilon}}b=0

Proof.

As already noted, conditions (1) and (2) of Definition

\mathscr{L}_{\kappa_{\epsilon}}\beta=0

\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}_{\kappa_{\epsilon}}\beta)(X)\zeta

4.2
Further insights from integrability

We can obtain yet further results on the geometries which support Killing superalgebras using the integrability equations. If we have some non-zero
\epsilon\in\mathfrak{S}_{D}
, then since
R^{D}(X,Y)\epsilon=0
the determinant of
R^{D}(X,Y)
(as a spinor endomorphism) must vanish everywhere. Contracting the curvature with a Levi-Civita symbol for convenience, we have the following in a local frame:

\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}R^{D}_{\mu\nu}=\begin{cases}\quantity(\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\varepsilon^{\sigma\tau}R_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau} + 2b^2\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu})\Gamma_{*}-2\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}b\Gamma_{\nu}\\
\qquad=\varsigma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}R + 4b^2)\Gamma_{*}-2\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}b\Gamma_{\nu}&\text{for }\varsigma_{B}=-1,\\
\quantity(\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\varepsilon^{\sigma\tau}R_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau} + \varsigma 2b^2\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu})\Gamma_{*}+\varsigma 2\nabla^{\mu}b\Gamma_{\mu}\\
\qquad=\quantity(\varsigma\frac{1}{2}R + 4b^2)\Gamma_{*}+\varsigma 2\nabla^{\mu}b\Gamma_{\mu}&\text{for }\varsigma_{B}=+1,\end{cases}

(4.2)

where in the second line of each case we have used the well-known fact that the Riemann tensor in 2 dimensions can be expressed in terms of the scalar curvature
R

R_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}=\tfrac{1}{2}R\quantity(\eta_{\mu\sigma}\eta_{\nu\tau}-\eta_{\mu\sigma}\eta_{\nu\tau})=\varsigma\tfrac{1}{2}R\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{\sigma\tau},

and a combinatorial identity for
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}
. Recalling our explicit descriptions of the Clifford algebras
\Cl(0,2)
and
\Cl(1,1)
in terms of the Pauli matrices, we can compute the determinant in the local frame using the formula

\det\quantity(a\1 + b\sigma_1 + c\sigma_2 + d\sigma_3)=a^{2}-b^{2}-c^{2}-d^{2}

for
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
, giving us (we multiply by a sign for a slight simplification)

\varsigma\det(\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}R^D_{\mu\nu})=\begin{cases}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}R + 4b^2)^{2}-4\norm{db}^{2}&\text{for }\varsigma_{B}=-1,\\
\quantity(\frac{1}{2}R + \varsigma 4b^2)^{2}-\varsigma 4\norm{db}^{2}&\text{for }\varsigma_{B}=+1.\end{cases}

(4.3)

We would now like to set the expression above to zero and examine the resulting equations in the scalar curvature
R
and the Killing number
b
. Let us first note that in Riemannian signature, the norm on differential forms is positive-definite, thus
\norm{db}^{2}\geq 0
with equality if and only if
b
is constant. A similar result holds in the Lorentzian case, but the statement is non-trivial since the norm is not positive-definite in this case.

Lemma 4.4
.

In Lorentzian signature, if
\mathfrak{S}_{D}\neq 0

\varsigma_{B}\norm{db}^{2}\leq 0
, and

is null at a point

Proof.

We will prove the
\varsigma_{B}=-1
case; the
\varsigma_{B}=+1
case is entirely analogous. By Lemma
4.2
,
(db)^{\sharp}
is orthogonal to
\kappa_{\epsilon}
for all
\epsilon\in\mathfrak{S}_{D}
. By Corollary
2.3
, for
\varsigma_{B}=-1
,
\kappa_{\epsilon}
is everywhere either timelike or null, and it is null if and only if
\epsilon
is chiral. Thus where
\kappa_{\epsilon}
is timelike,

must be either spacelike or zero, and where
\kappa_{\epsilon}
is null,

must be collinear with

\norm{db}^{2}\geq 0

(db)_{p}\neq 0

∎

In particular, when

R=\begin{cases}\pm 4\absolutevalue{db}-8b^{2}&\text{for }\varsigma_{B}=-1,\\
\pm 4\absolutevalue{db}-\varsigma 8b^{2}&\text{for }\varsigma_{B}=+1,\end{cases}

where

Constraints of the type we have just derived are well-known in the literature on geometric Killing spinors and their generalisations, but we have arrived at this expression in a slightly non-standard way. The usual method is to derive the integrability condition from the

\mp\not{\nabla}^{2}\epsilon+\Delta\epsilon=\frac{1}{4}R\epsilon

Finally, we recall that for

4.3
Maximally supersymmetric case

We call

\dim\mathfrak{S}_{D}=\dim S=2
