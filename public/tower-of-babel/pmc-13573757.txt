Civil society resistance to anti-rights mobilisation
Authors: Terry McGovern, Amy Meng, Maeve O’Rourke, Rima Athar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advocates must use history, evidence, and multilateral leverage to protect sexual and reproductive health from attempts to erode rights, write Terry McGovern and colleagues

Anti-rights forces—whether religious-conservative, nationalist, or far right—are increasingly gaining political power by challenging the multilateral system and the international human rights framework that safeguard gender equality, sexual and reproductive health, and LGBTQ+ rights. These political actors and alliances reject and distort settled international norms to obstruct inclusive development efforts, with disastrous effects on health outcomes. These trends erode the quality of patient care, restrict access to essential services, and hinder the implementation of evidence based care. Progressive civil societies are devising creative transnational and multilateral strategies to resist these forces. This paper, part of a collection of articles on the impacts of shifts in the multilateral system on sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR), spotlights jurisdiction specific examples of how anti-rights actors are targeting this arena and how SRHR advocates and progressive civil society can respond. The multilateral dimensions of this work are examined in depth elsewhere in the collection. 1

This landscape is complex. Anti-rights actors range from religious communities acting on religious beliefs to well funded transnational political networks (box 1). SRHR advocates also do not speak with one voice: civil society encompasses organisations with differing priorities, resources, and relationships to the communities they serve. Funding for such work is at an all-time low and is an ongoing problem. 1 Acknowledging this complexity is a precondition for strategies that are durable and context sensitive.

Anti-rights actors harm health

While anti-rights actors and alliances mobilise uniquely in their national contexts, there is a cross border effort to describe SRHR as “foreign imposition” or “woke ideology,” threatening “natural families,” “traditional values,” and “national culture”.2 3 These forces emerged in the late 1990s in reaction to a series of international agreements, including the 1994 International Consensus on Population Development and the 1995 Beijing Declaration, which acknowledged the rights of women to bodily and reproductive autonomy and created a blueprint to achieve global gender equality.3 They have built a transnational force with vast political and financial backing, driving poor health outcomes by pushing policies that block access to care.3

An estimated 20 anti-rights organisations operate transnationally with the specific aim of targeting global governance systems and attacking international normative frameworks such as the International Consensus on Population Development and Beijing Platform.4 These actors seek to initiate alternative norms, such as the 2020 Geneva Consensus Declaration, which undermine bodily autonomy.

The European Parliamentary Forum for Sexual and Reproductive Rights has identified $1.18bn (£0.9bn; €1bn) in funding spent between 2019 and 2023 in Europe and Russia for anti-rights purposes. US organisations, including media and litigation focused bodies, contributed 9% of these funds; 18% came from the Russian Federation. Oligarchs, aristocratic foundations, ultraconservative billionaires, and tech elites are joining forces with Catholic and other religious organisations, right wing politicians, and authoritarian governments to finance not only anti-rights rhetoric, restrictive policies, and harmful services, but also the undermining of democratic institutions.4 Far right think tanks pose as research institutions, relying on media platforms with algorithms designed to spread misinformation widely and manufacture moral panic.4

Having described how anti-rights actors are organised and supported, the following sections examine examples of their messaging and the resulting public health harms.

Paraguay—Comprehensive sexuality education

After a sustained anti-rights campaign in 2016 (eg, the Con Mis Hijos No Te Metas (Don’t mess with my kids), created by Peruvian anti-rights leader Christian Rosas), and Somos Muchos Muchos Màs (We are many many more) in 2017, led by known anti-rights figure Miguel Ortigoza, Paraguay became the first country to ban teaching about gender in schools.5 Part of a transnational conservative network that mobilised against comprehensive sexuality education and gender content in school curriculums in Latin America, they claimed that such education puts “the family in danger” and that the ban “protects Paraguayan sovereignty.”2 Inadequate sex education has been identified as a risk factor for adolescent pregnancy in Paraguay, which has one of the highest rates in the region at 72 births per 1000 girls aged 15-19.6

Poland—Abortion

In Poland, conservative Catholic actors framed abortion as “a threat to culture and nation,” targeting access to termination for “fatal fetal anomaly,” which had accounted for over 90% of all legal abortions.7 A 2020 constitutional ruling effectively banned this practice, forcing people to carry non-viable pregnancies to term.8 The clinical implications were immediate, with doctors discouraging women from perinatal testing owing to the lack of options if the pregnancy was unviable.9 During the first two months of 2021, there were 4000 fewer perinatal patients than during the same period in 2020.9 As a result of the restrictions, in 2021 Poland recorded a 9% increase in its infant mortality rate, reversing a long term decline, and the neonatal death rate rose by 19%.8

US—Gender affirming care

In the US, anti-rights alliances have mounted coordinated campaigns to enact state laws that block or severely limit established standards for adolescent gender affirming care for transgender and non-binary youth. Characterising such care as “child abuse” that threatens to subvert “the good of the American family,” these campaigners recast medical standards of care as ideological threats while invoking nationalistic sentiments to push a regressive moral agenda.10 Recent federal documents have also replaced gender affirming care with the term “sex rejecting procedures,” shifting the focus away from the patient’s health towards a judgment of the patient’s identity.11 Between 2021 and 2025, over 25 states introduced such laws, some of which created the threat of criminal proceedings (up to 10 years’ imprisonment) and professional jeopardy (licence revocation, malpractice insurance loss) for physicians.12 This forced many physicians to cease treating trans youth altogether, despite evidence that denying puberty blockers or hormone therapy to trans adolescents increases the rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidality.12 13 These bans deepen a public health crisis for an already marginalised population.

Uganda—LGBTQ+ rights

In Uganda, proponents of the Anti-Homosexuality Act heavily funded by anti-rights actors in the “West,” including Christian non-profit organisations from the US, spent over a decade framing homosexuality as a “western imposition” corrupting African culture.3 Following the act’s introduction in 2023, public health facilities were 31% less likely to offer HIV testing services specific to men-who-have-sex-with-men, and 39% of the key population were deterred from seeking care owing to the threat of criminalisation and punishment.14 Additionally, 39% of LGBTQ+ people reported discrimination at medical facilities and worsening mental health.14

In Paraguay, Poland, the US, and Uganda, the pattern is the same: anti-rights actors portray evidence based healthcare as foreign or immoral, use the language of culture and family to justify restrictive laws, and deploy legal threats and funding cuts to silence doctors and advocates. Effective strategies to fulfil the right to sexual and reproductive healthcare

To reclaim a resilient and rights based future, SRHR advocates should raise awareness of the positive health outcomes derived from domestic and international policies, laws, and multilateral architecture that promote and protect SRHR access. There is also an urgent need to counter the anti-rights movements’ distortion of culture and history. SRHR advocates must do the following.

Emphasise the historic universality of human rights

While anti-rights forces often frame sexual and reproductive health rights as a modern ideological imposition, the foundational principles underpinning SRHR—justice, dignity, autonomy, and social equity — are neither novel nor culturally specific. Though the specific language of “rights” is modern, the international human rights framework reflects concepts found across human societies throughout history.

The Manden Charter (Kurukan Fuga, 1236 CE), proclaimed under Sundiata Keita across West Africa, affirmed individual liberty and mandated women’s inclusion in governance, seven centuries before such protections were formalised in international law.15 The Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace (Kaianere’ko:wa) codified women’s rights to property, political agency, and divorce two centuries before equivalent rights existed in the US or Europe.16 Many indigenous communities have long recognised and respected gender beyond a binary: in Samoa, fa’afafine and fa’afatama move fluidly between male and female social worlds.17 Confucian thought articulated concepts of inherent human dignity in the 6th century BCE, which Chinese diplomat Peng Chun Chang drew on in drafting the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.18 Similarly, the 2008 Ecuadorian constitution honoured Andean indigenous principles of reciprocity and harmony with nature and community when establishing gender equality and SRHR as foundational principles of the state.19

In 2018, the Supreme Court of India in Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India struck down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, which criminalised “carnal intercourse against the order of nature.”20 The court sided with the petitioners, who argued that Section 377 was “a relic of colonial rule and of 19th century Victorian morality.”21 Their arguments drew on the abundance of precolonial representations of same sex love in Indian artwork, texts, and traditions.21

Contemporary rights frameworks have not invented new values. Rather, they codify principles of equal participation and protection that have long been recognised as foundational to collective wellbeing. To block access to SRHR on the grounds of cultural imposition is to misread history. Advocates should continue to highlight these distortions and their consequent harm.

Illuminate legal strategies and campaigns

Ireland

A turning point in the fight for abortion rights in Ireland occurred in 2012, when Savita Halappanavar died of sepsis after being denied abortion under existing law.22 The Abortion Rights Campaign and a larger movement used several strategies to activate public consciousness to support a referendum on legalising abortion.22 These included normalising abortion discourse, destigmatising abortions, and applying pressure on the Irish government by framing abortion as a human rights issue that was persistently violated by the country’s anti-abortion law.22 This strategy was reinforced internationally: two women, Siobhán Whelan and Amanda Mellet, were forced to leave the country for abortion because of fatal fetal impairment. The UN Human Rights Committee ruled that the anti-abortion law had violated their rights to privacy, equality, and freedom from inhumane treatment under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.22 In 2016, the Irish parliament (Oireachtas) responded to public pressure by establishing a citizens’ assembly, where randomly selected Irish citizens met to discuss pertinent legal and social issues.22 The citizens’ assembly heard testimonials detailing the barriers and health consequences faced by those seeking abortion, and in March 2017 it recommended a referendum to repeal the Eighth Amendment.22 In the vote on 25 May 2018, 66.4% supported ending the country’s ban on abortion, and the Health Act 2018 allowing free abortion services (within certain conditions) was signed into law in December.22Following this, all of Ireland’s public maternity hospitals now provide termination services, and the number of community providers has risen to 455 as of 2025.23

Colombia

In 2022, after a lawsuit filed by Causa Justa, a coalition of 120 civil society organisations and activists, Colombia fully decriminalised abortion up to 24 weeks of pregnancy.24 The campaign’s priorities included knowledge production around the need to decriminalise abortions; education to disseminate evidence based information to strategic audiences such as politicians, the community, the press, and decision makers; permanent monitoring and follow-up of Congress and pertinent health authorities’ actions; and acting on windows of opportunity to achieve successful litigation. Causa Justa brought in “amicus curiae” briefs from international bodies to argue that criminalising abortion constitutes “cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment” and a violation of standards set by the United Nations.25 Between 2006 and 2022, the denial of a voluntary abortion in Colombia increased a woman’s risk of death by 161% in the subsequent nine months, primarily due to complications of resorting to unsafe abortion practices.24 Since legalisation, the Ministry of Health and Social Protection has reported a 26% reduction in maternal mortality.26

Thailand

In 2016, Thailand became the first Asia-Pacific nation to eliminate mother-to-child HIV transmission through comprehensive, rights based integration of health services into its national infrastructure.27 The 2002 launch of Thailand’s universal health coverage, grounded in the Right to Health framework and the 1994 Cairo International Conference on Population and Development Programme of Action, shifted policy beyond disease control to a comprehensive maternal health model centred on upholding reproductive rights. HIV screening and antiretroviral treatment were provided free to all pregnant women, including undocumented migrants.28 By providing free prenatal HIV testing, antiretrovirals, and breast milk substitutes for HIV positive mothers, Thailand reduced vertical (mother-to-child) transmission from 30% to under 2%.27

Though HIV treatment was excluded from the universal health coverage owing to high costs, sustained advocacy by civil society and people living with HIV, alongside price reductions in medicines, led the government to pledge free treatment for all eligible people in 2001.27 The Thai Network of People Living with HIV/AIDS (TNP+), supported by UNAIDS and other partners, also pioneered a discrimination index that informed national “stigma-free” guidelines for healthcare facilities to align practice with international human rights standards.29

Ireland, Colombia, and Thailand show that successful advocacy, while context specific in its tactics, shared a similar underlying approach by building public and political support using evidence and personal stories, using international human rights frameworks to reinforce domestic campaigns, and measuring and communicating health outcomes to demonstrate the concrete benefits of expanded access. These are all transferable lessons.

Strengthen multilateral architecture

For decades, feminist activists have used innovative legal and cultural strategies to drive legislation rooted in international human rights standards. These successes demonstrate the vital synergy between domestic activism and multilateral human rights frameworks, and the need to safeguard them as a prerequisite for continuing to advance reproductive and sexual health globally (box 2).

As discussed elsewhere in this collection, civil society participation has been key to achieving multilateral agreements that guarantee access to sexual and reproductive health.30 Yet in the current United Nations reform process, progressive civil society is being sidelined.1

Conclusion

Whether the target is reproductive rights, comprehensive sexuality education, gender affirming healthcare, or LGBTQ+ rights, anti-rights actors employ the same tactics to undermine sexual and reproductive health rights: portraying evidence based health services as “woke” or “foreign” impositions, weaponising “traditional values” to justify legal bans, and relying on punitive mechanisms that silence providers and advocates and endanger patients and vulnerable populations. By reframing key treaty obligations like the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women and consensus guidelines from the World Health Organization and the United Nations sexual and reproductive health agency (UNFPA), as “foreign meddling,” anti-rights actors weaken global consensus and the multilateral architecture that safeguards gender equality and sexual minorities worldwide.

The way forward is clear. Progressive civil society must continue to monitor and raise awareness of how anti-rights actors work, while continuing to fill gaps and build evidence on the benefits of investing in SRHR. Enacting laws and health policies that adhere to international consensus is critical, as is the leadership of those directly harmed, in illuminating the importance of access to sexual and reproductive health for the wellbeing of all of society.
