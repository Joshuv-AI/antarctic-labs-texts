Experimental study on the damage and failure mechanism of freeze-thaw filled fractured sandstone under uniaxial compression
Authors: Jianxi Ren, Yabing Shen, Mengchen Yun, Ruixue Wang, Fengshuo Qin, Xiaoquan Huo, Jian Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to study the uniaxial compression damage and fracture mechanism of filled fractured sandstone under different freeze-thaw cycles, the uniaxial compression test of filling fractured sandstone under different freeze-thaw cycles, uniaxial compression tests of filled fractured sandstone under different freeze-thaw cycles were carried out. This was achieved by combining indoor macroscopic and microscopic tests with acoustic emission(AE), nuclear magnetic resonance(NMR) and CT scanning technology, and the macroscopic and microscopic fracture mechanisms were analyzed. The results show that with the increase of freeze-thaw cycles, the particle spalling at the edge of sandstone is intensified, the mass in a dry state increases first and then decreases, and the loss of longitudinal wave velocity is obvious. The uniaxial compression stress-strain curve is divided into five stages. The peak strength and elastic modulus show a linear decreasing trend with the increase of freeze-thaw cycles. The acoustic emission ringing count shows a change rule of ' low activity-local activity-advanced activity ‘, and the events are concentrated in the middle of the sample and around the crack. The T2 spectrum of nuclear magnetic resonance shifted to the right, the proportion of micropores gradually decreased, and macropores and ultra-macropores appeared, showing a ‘micropore shrinkage-micropore increase-macropore expansion ' chain damage process. The research results can provide scientific support for the evaluation of rock mass engineering stability in cold regions.Keywords: Freeze-thaw cycles, Filled fractured sandstone, Acoustic emission, Nuclear magnetic resonance, CT scanningSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Permafrost and seasonal frozen soils are widely distributed in China, and major infrastructure construction in seasonally frozen regions is expanding rapidly1. Under the repeated action of frost heaving forces, rock masses develop internal freeze-thaw damage, which severely compromises the long-term stability of engineering projects. Recent comprehensive reviews have systematically summarized the macroscopic and microscopic weathering mechanisms of rocks subjected to these complex freeze-thaw actions2. Furthermore, field-scale studies have highlighted the practical engineering implications by modeling the progressive thermo-mechanical destabilization of freestanding rock pillars in permafrost environments3. In recent years, scholars worldwide have conducted extensive research on the macro-meso mechanical characteristics and damage evolution mechanisms of rocks under freeze-thaw conditions. Wang et al.4 and Song et al.5 focused on freeze-thawed sandstone under different stress levels, analyzing the coupling effects of stress and freeze-thaw on mechanical properties and damage evolution. Ren et al.6 conducted macro-meso triaxial compression tests on freeze-thawed fractured sandstone, utilizing NMR to reveal the mechanical properties and mesoscopic pore evolution of single-fracture sandstone with varying inclination angles after 30 freeze-thaw cycles. In parallel, specific studies have detailed the degradation of physical and mechanical indicators, observing significant drops in uniaxial compressive strength and longitudinal wave velocity in sandstones across varying freeze-thaw cycles7. Similarly, comprehensive characterizations of rocks have confirmed the continuous deterioration of key physical and macroscopic mechanical properties with increasing freeze-thaw cycles8. To deeply understand this deterioration, multiscale investigations have linked macroscopic strength degradation to the internal accumulation of freeze-thaw-induced microscopic damage9. Song et al.10 investigated the physical and mechanical properties of limestone under freeze-thaw conditions, clarifying the exponential decay laws of compressive strength, elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio, and analyzed wave velocity changes in saturated and dry states.

Liu11 utilized CT technology to assist in freeze-thaw and uniaxial compression tests, demonstrating significant spatial heterogeneity in freeze-thaw damage. It was confirmed that frost heave cracks are mainly distributed at the specimen edges and macro-crack tips, a finding corroborated by Maji V et al.12, who used micro-CT to track the initiation and propagation of micro-cracks in sandstone/limestone; Building on this non-destructive approach, recent studies have utilized high-resolution µCT imaging to quantitatively evaluate the evolution of pore structures and complex fracture networks in rocks like andesite13. Researchers have also achieved dynamic, pore-scale monitoring of freeze-thaw induced fractures using X-ray micro-CT under ambient cycling14; moreover, classical 3D reconstructions via X-ray CT15, often combined with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), have intuitively confirmed microscopic structural changes caused by artificial freeze-thaw action, including particle shedding, increased porosity, and crack initiation16. Xie et al.17 deeply explored the localized damage effects at fracture tips, frost heave development, and fracture propagation paths through freeze-thaw tests on single-fracture sandstone-like materials; Advancing these visualization methods, in situ X-ray CT monitoring has recently been deployed to dynamically capture both macroscopic deformation and the spatial evolution of internal microcracks in sandstone during the complete loading sequence after freeze-thaw treatments18. While these methods are powerful, they come with specific benefits and constraints. CT scanning enables 3D visualization and precise spatial localization of internal meso-damage in sandstone. Quantitative parameters such as pore surface area and equivalent diameter can be extracted through 3D reconstruction, translating subjective visual descriptions into precise numerical values that robustly support damage mechanism analysis. However, Spatial resolution is limited for identifying micro-pores and micro-cracks below the micron level, making it difficult to characterize the initial stage of chain damage. Furthermore, threshold segmentation and noise determination involve some subjectivity, slightly affecting parameter calculation accuracy.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) technology plays a key role in mesoscopic damage research of rock masses in cold regions. Liu et al.19 used NMR to study the damage evolution of rock masses with pre-existing fissures and pre-compression damage, finding that initial damage accelerates the deterioration process. Zhang, Zeng, and Wang et al.20–22 applied NMR to sandstone under different freeze-thaw cycles, combining multifractal theory to analyze the evolution of multi-scale pore structures. Their research found that freeze-thaw action not only increases porosity but also alters pore distribution, thereby affecting mechanical performance. Su et al.23 measured the pore size distribution and unfrozen water content of red sandstone during freeze-thaw at different temperatures using NMR, establishing a damage evolution equation based on multi-factor coupling. For instance, NMR techniques utilizing transverse relaxation time (T2) distributions have effectively quantified microscopic pore expansion and cumulative damage severity in red sandstone24; By correlating these NMR-derived microscopic defect parameters with mechanical parameters, researchers can comprehensively evaluate the effects of cyclic freezing on macroscopic compressive strength25. Furthermore, advanced NMR testing has facilitated the development of sophisticated damage models that consider the variable frequency and duration of freeze-thaw actions26; Tai27 established PLSR and PCR prediction models for rock freeze-thaw damage mechanical parameters through laboratory tests and multivariate analysis. Furthermore, other scholars28,29 have used NMR to analyze the impact of pore parameter changes on rock damage, enriching the theory of freeze-thaw damage.

Acoustic Emission(AE) technology effectively reveals damage mechanisms and failure characteristics30,31. Studies utilizing AE to monitor the dynamic evolution of internal rock rupture have identified five characteristic stages in the stress-strain curve, where AE counts show a sharp rise followed by a rapid decline during the failure stage, closely related to brittle failure characteristics32. Barkat et al.33 used AE and energy evolution analysis to predict failure and study micro-crack evolution in freeze-thawed sandstone, marble, and granite. Based on Critical Slowing Down (CSD) theory, the timeliness and reliability of AE multi-parameter variance curves in predicting fractured rock mass failure have been verified34. Similarly, recent advancements have directly applied the critical slowing down theory to AE characteristics to successfully extract precursory signals and predict the failure of various freeze-thawed rocks7; Beyond failure prediction, AE parameters have also been effectively utilized to evaluate shear strength and distinguish between tensile and shear damage modes in jointed rock-like materials after freeze-thaw action35. Moreover, continuous AE monitoring during the actual freeze-thaw cycling phase itself has successfully recorded and identified dynamic micro-cracking events in materials like concrete, which provides a strong reference for rock deterioration studies36; Subsequent testing on treated samples further demonstrates that variations in AE event counts can accurately trace internal deformation stages and microcrack evolution when frozen-thawed rocks are subjected to uniaxial compression37.

The root cause of macroscopic rock failure typically lies in the propagation and proliferation of micro-cracks. Analyzing the evolution mechanism of micro-cracks during freeze-thaw damage is crucial for understanding macroscopic fracture characteristics. Early research primarily focused on hydrostatic pressure38 and capillary theories39. Although progress has been made, the complex correlations between internal pore evolution, mechanical parameter degradation, and crack propagation in filled fractured sandstone under freeze-thaw action remain to be fully elucidated. Specifically, the coupling mechanism of macro-meso damage under uniaxial compression requires further clarification. Therefore, this study employs a combination of macroscopic and mesoscopic tests to systematically analyze the fracture mechanism, aiming to provide a solid theoretical basis for stability evaluation and disaster prevention in cold region engineering.

Overview of uniaxial compression test on filled fractured sandstone

Preparation of fractured sandstone

The sandstone samples used in our test were collected from surface outcrops along the Qinghai-Tibet Railway in Qinghai Province, China, an area classified as a seasonally frozen region. Specimens containing pre-existing fractures were prepared in accordance with International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM) standards. Standard cylindrical cores (φ50 × 100 mm) were drilled vertically using a core drilling machine. Subsequently, parallel double fractures without filling were pre-fabricated using a water jet cutter. Based on previous research40, an inclination angle of 45° was selected as it represents the most unfavorable mechanical condition. To accurately characterize common rock mass fractures, the fracture thickness b was set to 2 mm, length to 20 mm, and the spacing between parallel double fractures to 40 mm. In seasonally frozen regions, open fractures are often filled with various materials due to moisture intrusion, frost heaving disintegration, and mineral precipitation, forming closed fractures. Different filling materials affect the strength and deformation of fractured sandstone differently. Soft clay fillers easily undergo plastic deformation under freeze-thaw cycles, reducing bearing capacity, whereas high-strength cement fillers can enhance rock integrity and stability to some extent. Therefore, we tested 3 typical cement mortar mix ratios for strength comparison and finally selected a water: cement: sand mass ratio of 1.0:1.5:2.0 as the filling material. As shown in Fig. 5, after 5 and 10 freeze-thaw cycles, the mass of both intact sandstone and filled fractured sandstone (d=40 mm) did not exhibit loss but rather a slight increase. The observed mass increase is actually due to an increase in total porosity and saturated water volume, rather than enhanced capillary retention. Our samples were vacuum-saturated before testing. In a fully saturated state, the total water capacity depends on the internal connected total pore volume (total porosity), including micro, small, meso, and macro-pores, as well as newly initiated micro-cracks. Although spectrum shows the relative proportion of micro-pores decreased, this is because frost heaving forces expanded and connected original micro-pores, turning them into larger pores and cracks. The sharp increase in the volume of larger pores “diluted” the relative percentage of micro-pores, but the absolute total volume available for water accommodation actually increased. However, after 15 cycles, particle spalling at the edges and end faces increased. The mass of internally stored water became less than the mass of the spalled material, resulting in an increased mass loss rate. Additionally, the mass loss of filled fractured sandstone was slightly higher than that of intact sandstone, primarily due to the loss of filling material at the fractures during the freeze-thaw process. Regarding wave velocity, the velocity of the rock specimens decreased significantly with increasing freeze-thaw cycles, with loss rates ranging from 12.08%~30.31%. This is because the water-ice phase transition occurs within the specimen during freezing and thawing. The core mechanism underlying the impact of the water-ice phase transition on the mechanical properties of rocks is the volume expansion effect generated during the phase transition. When water undergoes a freeze-thaw phase transition within the internal pores and fissures of the rock, volume expansion occurs, subsequently generating continuous and repetitive frost heaving forces. These frost heaving forces destroy the cementation structure between the internal rock particles, leading to the continuous expansion of primary pores and the initiation of new micro-pores and micro-cracks. Simultaneously, this triggers a chain evolution of the pore structure, causing an increase in the rock’s porosity, an uneven distribution of pore sizes, and a decrease in density, which prevents the effective transmission of stress. Ultimately, this results in the deterioration of mechanical parameters such as compressive strength and elastic modulus, along with a reduction in overall bearing capacity and structural integrity. Furthermore, wave propagation velocity varies with the medium; it is slower in water than in rock. When longitudinal waves pass through fractured rock, the velocity also attenuates due to refraction and other effects.

Mesoscopic nuclear magnetic resonance test results

To investigate the influence of freeze-thaw cycles on the pore structure evolution of filled fractured sandstone, NMR scans were performed on saturated sandstone after 0, 5, 10, and 15 freeze-thaw cycles, yielding spectrum distribution curves as shown in Fig. 6(a). The horizontal axis represents the relaxation time (ms) that is proportional to the pore structure size, while the vertical axis represents the amplitude that is proportional to the pore quantity, that is, the signal intensity corresponding to each transverse relaxation time . Analysis reveals that the spectra of both intact and 40 mm fractured sandstone exhibit a bimodal structure. The main peak is located between 1 and 100 ms, and the secondary peak ranges from 500 to 5000 ms. As the number of freeze-thaw cycles increases, the area under the spectrum curve expands, the amplitude increases, and the curve shifts noticeably to the right. This reflects that frost heaving forces induce the generation and evolution of internal fractures, leading to gradual pore development.

T2 spectrum and pore size distribution diagram of sandstone under different freeze-thaw cycles.

The evolution characteristics of pore structure play a critical role in the deterioration of the mechanical properties of filled fractured sandstone. Given the current lack of a unified standard for pore size classification, the sandstone pore classification adopted in the manuscript has been customized based on the specific experiments conducted. A five-level pore size distribution system was established: micropores(≤ 0.1 μm)、 small pores(0.1 ~ 1 μm)、 mesopores(1 ~ 10 μm)、 macropores(10 ~ 100 μm) and ultra-large pores(100 ~ 1000 μm), This system can accurately match the testing characteristics of the Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) T₂ spectrum, clearly characterize the progressive evolution of sandstone pores under freeze-thaw action, and analyze the pore structure reorganization and chain damage laws induced by freezing and thawing. This approach is highly suitable for studying the macroscopic and microscopic freeze-thaw damage mechanisms in filled fractured sandstone. As indicated by the pore size distribution in Fig. 6(b), micropores and mesopores gradually transition to larger pores, indicating increasing damage to the rock due to freeze-thaw cycles. In unfrozen sandstone, pores are primarily distributed as micropores, small pores, and mesopores, with small pores dominating. However, after freeze-thaw cycling, the pore structure undergoes significant reorganization; the proportion of micropores gradually decreases, while the proportions of small pores, mesopores, and macropores increase, accompanied by the appearance of ultra-large pores. The frost heaving force from the water-ice phase transition drives a chain damage process of “micropore shrinkage – small pore increase – macropore expansion,” leading to the decay of rock mechanical properties. This phenomenon suggests that the essence of freeze-thaw damage is a process of structural weakening triggered by changes in internal pore gradation. The synergistic evolution of micro- and meso-pores collectively leads to the deterioration of macroscopic mechanical properties by reducing bearing capacity and destroying the internal cementation of the rock specimen. Although the five-level pore classification system adopted in this study can effectively reveal the chain damage pattern of “micropore shrinkage, small pore increase, and macropore expansion” in fractured sandstone under freeze–thaw action, it has notable limitations: the classification is custom-defined for the experiment without standard specifications, the pore size boundaries are rigidly fixed and do not cover macro-fractures above the millimeter scale, and it depends on the measurement accuracy of NMR and CT techniques. Moreover, it is established only for sandstone under water-saturated freeze–thaw conditions. For other rock types and conditions, this system has some applicability to porous sedimentary rocks such as limestone and dolomite, but it is not directly applicable to crystalline rocks like granite and basalt, or to dry/unsaturated rocks. To enhance its cross-lithology and cross-condition scientific applicability, the pore size thresholds need to be adjusted according to lithology, pore structure characteristics, and environmental conditions

.

Analysis of mechanical properties under uniaxial compression after freeze-thaw

Analysis of stress-strain curve characteristics

To investigate the effect of freeze-thaw cycles on the mechanical properties of filled fractured sandstone, uniaxial compression tests were conducted on intact sandstone and sandstone with a fracture spacing of 40 mm subjected to different freeze-thaw cycles (0, 5, 10, and 15). The resulting stress-strain curves are plotted in Fig. 5.

As seen in Fig. 7, under uniaxial compression, the stress-strain curves of both intact and filled fractured sandstone exhibit distinct stages, which can be divided into five phases: fracture compaction, elastic deformation, stable crack growth, unstable crack growth, and residual strength. Before failure, the axial strain is greater than the radial strain. Upon entering the stable crack growth stage, the radial strain rapidly surpasses the axial strain, and there is almost no residual deformation after instability failure. With the increase in freeze-thaw cycles, the initial compaction stage of the sandstone becomes increasingly pronounced. Peak stress shows a decreasing trend, while axial strain shows an increasing trend. Furthermore, the freeze-thaw damage in sandstone with a 40 mm fracture spacing is more significant than in intact sandstone. This is because new cracks are easily generated at the tips of pre-existing fractures, reducing the degree of internal cementation and accelerating crack propagation and coalescence, leading to a sudden stress drop and instability failure. To further quantify this behavior, we have introduced a brittleness index () based on the stress-strain curve, defined by the post-peak modulus:

We analyzed the transition process of the sample response from brittleness to plasticity as the number of freeze-thaw cycles increased:0 freeze-thaw cycles: In the unfrozen state, the rock possesses high internal cementation strength. The stress-strain curve exhibits a very brief initial compaction stage and a steep elastic deformation phase. The brittleness index is at its highest level, indicating a dominant brittle failure phenomenon; 5 to 10 freeze-thaw cycles: As the number of cycles increases, the frost heaving force generated by the water-ice phase transition triggers a chain damage process. Macroscopically, the initial compaction stage becomes particularly pronounced, the elastic modulus decreases, and the axial strain required to reach the peak stress increases. After reaching the peak, the stress drop becomes significantly more gradual, resulting in a continuous decrease in the brittleness index and a corresponding increase in ductility;15 freeze-thaw cycles: The stress-strain curve flattens significantly. The rock undergoes substantial deformation before failure, and the post-peak curve displays an extended yielding phase with a minimal stress drop. The fractured sandstone has largely lost its original brittle characteristics, exhibiting pronounced plastic behavior.

Analysis of mechanical parameters

The relationship between peak strength, elastic modulus, and the number of freeze-thaw cycles for filled fractured sandstone is shown in Fig. 8.

The relationship between freeze-thaw cycle times and peak stress, elastic modulus.

Without freeze-thaw cycles, the peak strengths of intact sandstone and filled specimens with 40 mm fracture spacing were 31.96 MPa and 27.67 MPa, and their elastic moduli were 5.87 GPa and 4.94 GPa, respectively. After 5 freeze-thaw cycles, the peak strengths decreased to 27.98 MPa and 22.05 MPa, and the elastic moduli to 4.67 GPa and 4.01 GPa, respectively. The reductions in peak strength were 12.47% and 20.33%, with elastic moduli also declining to varying degrees. After 15 cycles, the reduction magnitudes further increased, with peak strengths dropping by 33.89% and 56.19%, and elastic moduli decreasing by 46.93%, 62.45%. Thus, it can be seen that the number of freeze-thaw cycles has a significant effect on the peak strength and elastic modulus of sandstone, and the internal cementation ability of the rock continuously weakens with increasing freeze-thaw cycles. Meanwhile, linear fitting of the peak strength and elastic modulus for intact and 40 mm spacing sandstone reveals that both parameters show a downward trend with increasing freeze-thaw cycles, linear correlation coefficient , exhibiting good linear correlation. Regarding physical sensitivity, both the intact sandstone and the filled fractured sandstone with a 40 mm spacing exhibit a consistent trend: a slight mass increase after 5 and 10 freeze-thaw cycles, followed by mass loss after 15 cycles. However, the mass loss rate of the filled fractured sandstone after 15 cycles is higher than that of the intact sandstone. This is primarily because the cement mortar at the filled fracture undergoes material loss during the freeze-thaw cycles, exacerbating the overall mass depletion. In terms of mechanical properties, prior to freeze-thaw conditioning, the peak strength of the intact sandstone is higher than that of the filled fractured sandstone. After 15 freeze-thaw cycles, the peak strength of the intact sandstone decreases by 33.89%, whereas the peak strength of the filled fractured sandstone drops by 56.19%. This significant disparity demonstrates that the peak strength of the filled fractured sandstone is more sensitive to freeze-thaw effects. Furthermore, regarding the sensitivity of the filling material, the cement mortar exhibits obvious deterioration characteristics during the cycles. As the number of freeze-thaw cycles increases, the loss of cement filler at the fracture becomes more severe, indicating that the bonding performance of the filling material is highly sensitive to the repeated action of frost heaving forces and freeze-thaw cycles.

Analysis of acoustic emission characteristics under different freeze-thaw cycles

Analysis of acoustic emission counts

To investigate the acoustic emission (AE) characteristics of filled fractured sandstone, tests were conducted on specimens with 40 mm fracture spacing under different freeze-thaw cycles (0, 5, 10, and 15). The relationship curves between stress and ringing counts were obtained, as shown in Fig. 9.

Acoustic emission stress and ring-down count diagram of sandstone with d=40mm in uniaxial compression test.

Analysis of the figure reveals that as freeze-thaw cycles increase, the ringing counts before the specimen reaches peak strength follow a pattern of “low activity – local activity – advanced activity.” The reason the “advanced outbreak (activity)” phenomenon occurs earlier is that the frost heaving force induces a chain damage process of “micropore shrinkage - small pore increase - macropore expansion” within the internal pores. This synergistic evolution of microscopic pores severely destroys the internal cementation of the rock. Consequently, during loading, the structure containing significant initial damage is highly susceptible to crack propagation and coalescence at lower stress levels. This triggers high-energy acoustic emission (AE) events, causing the energy release stage to shift forward overall. The cumulative ringing count curve is divided into a quiet period, an active period, and an outbreak period (initial loading has few ringing counts and a gentle curve; the active stage sees increased ringing counts and a steeper slope; the outbreak period approaches peak stress with a sharp increase in ringing counts and a nearly vertical curve). The data show variations in peak and cumulative ringing counts for sandstone with this spacing after different freeze-thaw cycles. After 5 cycles, the cumulative ringing count increased abnormally, which is related to the loading “step effect.” The “step effect” refers to the instantaneous, concentrated release of energy during the loading process. After 5 freeze-thaw cycles, the filled fractured sandstone has just begun to exhibit preliminary damage, and stress continuously accumulates at local defects. When the external force is sufficient to overcome the local structural strength, sudden macroscopic crack propagation occurs, accompanied by a violent release of energy. In AE monitoring, this manifests as a step-like jump in the counts. At 10 cycles, internal micro-cracks coalesced, causing energy release to shift forward and the cumulative value to decrease.

Three-dimensional localization characteristics of acoustic emission events

To investigate the damage and failure characteristics of filled fractured sandstone, uniaxial compression tests were conducted on sandstone with 40 mm fracture spacing under different freeze-thaw cycles, analyzed using AE localization technology. Figure 10 shows the 3D localization maps and the projections of 3D localization points on the Y-Z plane during uniaxial compression under different freeze-thaw conditions. The spheres in the figure represent recorded AE events, and the color scale legend indicates the ratio of the amplitude of each AE event to the maximum amplitude, ranging from 20% to 100%.

Three-dimensional distribution and Y-Z plane projection of localization points in d=40mm filled fracture sandstone.

The results show a clear increasing trend in the number of AE events for this sandstone spacing as freeze-thaw cycles increase. At 0 freeze-thaw cycles, the projection points on the Y-Z plane exhibit a random, sparse, and dispersed distribution across the entire field. High-amplitude events (with an amplitude ratio of 70%-80%) account for only 5% and are distributed in isolation without any obvious aggregation zones. This reflects that the internal structure of the rock sample is intact, with only low-energy damage resulting from the compaction of primary defects; After 5 freeze-thaw cycles, the projection points transition to a local aggregation pattern, and the aggregation zone precisely corresponds to the projected position of the fracture tip. The density of projection points in this area exceeds 60% of the total field. The proportion of high-amplitude events increases to 12%, all of which are concentrated in this aggregation zone. Freeze-thaw damage begins to develop locally from the fracture tip but has not yet propagated to the ends of the rock sample; At 10 freeze-thaw cycles, the number of projection points increases by 50% compared to that at 5 cycles. The main aggregation zone at the fracture tip further expands. Simultaneously, secondary aggregation zones form in the middle and lower parts of the rock sample along the Z-axis, as well as radially outward along the Y-axis, presenting a dispersed aggregation pattern where main and secondary aggregation zones coexist. The proportion of high-amplitude events continues to rise and they are densely distributed in the main aggregation zone. Micro-cracks experience initial coalescence and initiate secondary cracks in the vertical and radial directions. The damage transitions from localized to full-field, though it still has not reached the ends of the rock sample; After 15 freeze-thaw cycles, the number of projection points surges, displaying a dense, overlapping distribution across the entire field. The main and secondary aggregation zones fully merge, forming an ultra-dense aggregation band in the middle and lower parts of the rock sample at Z = 20–60 mm. The proportion of high-amplitude events suddenly jumps to 30% and spreads throughout the entire field, overlapping with medium- and low-amplitude points. Micro-cracks within the rock sample fully coalesce into macroscopic cracks, the cementation surfaces are completely destroyed, and cracks penetrate from the fracture tips to both ends of the sample. The rock sample is in an unstable failure stage. This indicates that freeze-thaw cycles greatly exacerbate internal damage in sandstone, and crack propagation tends to trigger high-energy AE, making the rock specimen more susceptible to failure under uniaxial compression.

Analysis of failure characteristics and CT scanning features of filled fractured sandstone

Failure characteristics of filled fractured sandstone under different freeze-thaw cycles

The propagation of new cracks at the tips of pre-existing fractures mainly exhibits two forms: wing cracks and secondary cracks. Wing cracks are generated at the tips of pre-existing macroscopic fractures under tensile stress and are classified as tensile cracks. Anti-wing cracks are reverse tensile cracks initiating from the crack tips at the ends of the rock sample. Secondary cracks are usually coplanar with the pre-existing fractures, formed under shear action, and are classified as shear cracks. The crack propagation modes are shown in Fig. 11.

Conclusions

To study the macro-meso characteristics and fracture mechanism of filled fractured sandstone under uniaxial compression with different freeze-thaw cycles, uniaxial compression tests were combined with acoustic emission, nuclear magnetic resonance, and CT scanning technologies. The mechanical properties, failure laws, and mesoscopic damage mechanisms of freeze-thawed filled fractured sandstone were analyzed, yielding the following conclusions:

Freeze-thaw cycles significantly affect the physical properties of the filled fractured sandstone. In terms of mass variation, the mass of the rock sample increases slightly from an initial 467.58 g to 467.72 g at 5 freeze-thaw cycles, reflecting an increase of 0.03%, and further increases to 468.02 g at 10 freeze-thaw cycles, reflecting an increase of 0.09%. At 15 freeze-thaw cycles, the mass drops to 464.38 g, representing a mass loss rate of 0.68%. Regarding the longitudinal wave velocity, it decreases from 2.778 km per second at 0 freeze-thaw cycles to 1.936 km per second at 15 freeze-thaw cycles, demonstrating a substantial wave velocity loss rate of 30.31%.

The macroscopic failure characteristics are significantly influenced by the number of freeze-thaw cycles. In the unfrozen state at 0 freeze-thaw cycles, the sandstone with a 40 mm spacing only forms one shear crack of approximately 15 mm in length at the tip of the filled fracture. After 5 freeze-thaw cycles, a wing crack about 25 mm long at an angle of approximately 30 degrees to the fracture and a shear crack about 20 mm long synchronously initiate at the fracture tip, with the wing crack penetrating to the end of the rock sample. After 10 freeze-thaw cycles, three non-penetrating cracks with a total length of approximately 60 mm are formed. Among them, two wing cracks and one anti-wing crack at the upper fracture tip have an average length of about 20 mm. After 15 freeze-thaw cycles, the crack length reaches 100 mm, which is consistent with the height of the rock sample.

The acoustic emission characteristics are closely related to the number of freeze-thaw cycles and crack evolution. As the number of freeze-thaw cycles increased to 15, the acoustic emission initial stress ratio decreased from 65% to 30%, achieving a significant advanced activity. Simultaneously, the duration of the significant acoustic emission active period extended from 150 s to 900 s, and the cumulative ring-down count increased 6.25-fold. Furthermore, three-dimensional localization demonstrated that the proportion of high-amplitude events jumped from 5% to 30%.

Mesoscopically, the total peak area of the nuclear magnetic resonance T2 spectrum for the sandstone with a 40 mm spacing increases from 8, 777.19 at 0 freeze-thaw cycles to 10,333.00 at 15 freeze-thaw cycles, indicating a growth rate of 17.73%. The main peak position of the T2 spectrum shifts to the right from 20 milliseconds at 0 freeze-thaw cycles to 50 milliseconds at 15 freeze-thaw cycles, corresponding to an increase in pore radius from 0.4 micrometers to 1.0 micrometer.

The observed chain damage of “micro-pore shrinkage - small-pore increase - macro-pore expansion” is a specific result based on the specific research object and test conditions of this study. The test conditions (freezing temperature − 20 °C, thawing temperature 20 °C, single freeze-thaw cycle of 8 h), water-saturated state, and uniaxial compression loading method jointly shaped the specific environment for pore evolution. If the test conditions change, the magnitude of the frost heaving force generated by the water-ice phase transition, the moisture migration path, and the stress-damage coupling effect will change, and the “chain” characteristics of the pore-level transition may be broken or reconstructed. Therefore, its applicability has clear limitations and cannot be directly regarded as a universal law for freeze-thaw damage in cold region rock masses.
