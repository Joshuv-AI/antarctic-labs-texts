Transcriptional profiling of anthocyanin biosynthesis in variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions of Impatiens uliginosa
Authors: Wenqi Zhang, Huanzhi Chi, Huangwei Yu, Lingjuan Zhang, Xiaoli Zhang, Xiaoyan Luo, Haiquan Huang, Meijuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPetal variegation is an important ornamental trait in flowering plants and has major implications for both evolutionary biology, because it can promote speciation and diversity, and horticulture, because it enhances aesthetic appeal and commercial value. Here, we used Impatiens uliginosa as a model system and combined morphological, anatomical, physiological, biochemical, transcriptomic, and molecular approaches to investigate the mechanisms underlying petal variegation.ResultsFreehand sectioning revealed that pigments were predominantly localized in the upper epidermal cells. Compared with the non-variegated dorsal petal regions, the variegated dorsal petal regions contained approximately three times as many pigment-accumulating cells; these cells were smaller, more densely arranged, and exhibited higher pigment density per unit area, collectively contributing to the darker coloration of the variegated regions. Pigment-content analysis showed that the variegated dorsal petal regions accumulated significantly higher levels of total carotenoids, anthocyanins, and flavonoids than the non-variegated dorsal petal regions. Semi-quantitative analysis further indicated that anthocyanin-related compounds, particularly delphinidin, pelargonidin, and peonidin derivatives, were enriched in the variegated dorsal petal regions.To investigate the molecular basis of this differential pigmentation, we performed comparative RNA-seq analysis of the variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions and identified a suite of candidate genes potentially involved in variegation formation. Functional validation of one candidate, IuMYB114, was carried out using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS). Silencing of IuMYB114 resulted in a significant reduction in both the size and color intensity of the variegated dorsal petal regions. qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that IuMYB114 expression in silenced plants was only 11% of that in wild-type plants. Moreover, the expression of several key anthocyanin-biosynthetic genes, including IuANS, IuUFGT, and IuF3′5'H, was significantly downregulated in the silenced lines.ConclusionsTaken together, these results suggest that IuMYB114 is likely associated with the positive regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis and may play an important role in the formation of variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions in I. uliginosa, potentially through the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthetic genes. The spatially restricted expression of IuMYB114 may therefore contribute to differential anthocyanin deposition and, consequently, to petal variegation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09116-2.

Introduction

Petal variegation, one of the most distinctive floral color patterns, is a highly prized ornamental trait in garden plants [1] and also plays important biological roles in pollination [2, 3]. Floral coloration is influenced by multiple factors. For instance, higher cytoplasmic pH values are associated with deeper coloration in chrysanthemum [4], and the spatial distribution of pigments directly affects the expression of variegation patterns [5]. Therefore, understanding petal epidermal cell morphology is fundamental to elucidating the mechanisms underlying variegation formation. Accumulating evidence indicates a close relationship between epidermal cell morphology and petal coloration in angiosperms. In Rosa hybrid cultivars, for example, pigments are predominantly concentrated in the upper epidermis, where the cells are conical, whereas the lower epidermal cells are flattened. This structural arrangement results in more intense coloration and lower brightness in the upper epidermis than in the lower epidermis [6]. Similarly, in the colored petals of Zantedeschia, flattened epidermal cells have been shown to influence petal coloration by altering light reflection [7].

Floral color diversity is primarily determined by four major classes of pigments: chlorophylls [8, 9], carotenoids [10, 11], flavonoids [12], and betalains [13]. Among these, anthocyanins, the major pigmented subclass of flavonoids, have one of the most thoroughly characterized biosynthetic pathways in plants [14]. To date, the genes governing anthocyanin biosynthesis have been well elucidated and can be broadly classified into two categories: structural genes and transcription factors. Structural genes encode enzymes essential for pigment biosynthesis, including chalcone synthase, chalcone isomerase, and flavanone 3-hydroxylase, among others [15]. In parallel, anthocyanin biosynthesis is tightly regulated by transcription factors that interact with cis-regulatory elements in the promoters of structural genes to modulate their expression. These regulators include members of the MYB family [16–18], bHLH family [19–21], and WD40 repeat protein family [22–24]. By fine-tuning the expression of anthocyanin biosynthetic genes in specific petal regions, these transcription factors orchestrate the spatial deposition of anthocyanins, ultimately giving rise to variegation patterns.

Among the transcription factors regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis, MYB transcription factors are the most extensively studied and play a central role in controlling pigmentation pattern formation [25, 26]. The C1 gene isolated from maize (Zea mays) was the first MYB gene identified in plants [27]. In apple, MdMYB1 and MdMYBA markedly enhance anthocyanin accumulation in the fruit skin by activating UFGT expression [28]. Yamagishi investigated the formation of splash spots on the tepals of Asiatic hybrid lilies (Lilium spp.) and showed that LhMYB12-Lat and MYB19Long determine spot formation on lily tepals [29]. Studies of anthocyanin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis have demonstrated that overexpression of MYB113 and MYB114 significantly increases anthocyanin accumulation [30]. Collectively, these findings indicate that variegation patterns are orchestrated by MYB transcription factors through tissue-specific positive or negative regulation of one or more structural genes, thereby modulating floral coloration and patterning.

Impatiens uliginosa is an annual or perennial herbaceous species in the family Balsaminaceae and the genus Impatiens, and it possesses abundant germplasm resources [31]. It exhibits distinctive floral morphology, including lip petals, wing petals, a dorsal petal, and a floral spur, all of which contribute to its high ornamental value. Its flower colors range from pink and white to deep red [32], and petal variegation represents one of its most important ornamental traits. To date, research on I. uliginosa has primarily focused on flower color [3], flower development [33], spur development [34, 35], and lignin biosynthesis [36]. However, the mechanisms underlying petal variegation in this species have received limited attention.

Preliminary observations by our research group identified a variegated phenotype in I. uliginosa, characterized by a distinct, regularly shaped pigmented patch on the dorsal petal that exhibits deeper coloration than the surrounding tissue. To date, studies on the mechanisms underlying petal variegation in Impatiens species remain scarce, underscoring the significance of investigating variegation formation in I. uliginosa. In this study, we employed an integrated approach combining colorimetric measurement, cell morphology observation, pigment content analysis, transcriptomic profiling, and virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) to elucidate the mechanisms underlying petal variegation. Our findings not only have important implications for novel cultivar breeding and germplasm innovation in Impatiens, but also provide technical support and a theoretical foundation for understanding the molecular regulatory mechanisms of petal variegation formation in herbaceous plants.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The plant material used in this study, variegated I. uliginosa (Fig. 1), was cultivated in the arboretum of Southwest Forestry University. Seeds of the variegated plants were originally collected from Qingshui Sea in Xundian County, Kunming, Yunnan Province, China. This lake is a plateau fault lake located at approximately 25°29′ N, 102°16′ E, at an elevation of about 2,180 m above sea level. All sampling procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Kunming Qingshuihai Protection Regulations and other applicable water-source protection laws and regulations. Measurement of chromaticity values

At full anthesis, the chromaticity parameters of the variegated (V) and non-variegated (nV) areas of the dorsal petals of variegated I. uliginosa flowers were measured using a portable spectrophotometer. Fresh petals were collected from 30 plants with uniform growth and no visible symptoms of pest infestation or disease. All measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 10:00 under standardized conditions. Colorimetric analysis was performed in the CIELAB color space using a portable spectrophotometer equipped with a standard CIE D65 illuminant and a CIE 10° standard observer. Each sample was measured in triplicate. In this color system, the lightness coordinate (L*) ranges from 0 (black) to 100 (white); the red-green coordinate (a*) indicates red (positive values) or green (negative values); and the yellow-blue coordinate (b*) indicates yellow (positive values) or blue (negative values). Chroma (C*), which reflects color saturation, was calculated as C* = (a*2 + b*2)^1/2.

Observation of cell morphology

Preparation and observation of free-hand sections

Freehand sections were prepared from the dorsal petals of variegated I. uliginosa to examine epidermal cell morphology and pigment distribution, and representative images were captured. For each tissue region (variegated and non-variegated), three fields of view were selected. Within each field, three areas of equal size were randomly chosen for cell counting. All images were uniformly magnified to 200 ×, with a field area of 200 × 200 μm. Only pigment-containing cells were counted. The variegated and non-variegated areas were designated V and nV, respectively.

Preparation and observation of paraffin sections for petal epidermal cell morphology

Dorsal petals of variegated I. uliginosa were fixed in FAA solution (90 mL of 50% ethanol, 5 mL of glacial acetic acid, and 5 mL of 37% formaldehyde). Conventional paraffin sectioning was performed according to the method described by Li et al. [37]. Sections were cut to a thickness of 10 μm using a rotary microtome (LEICA RM205) and stained with 1% (w/v) safranin solution. Observations and image acquisition were carried out using a light microscope (LEICA DMi 1).

Determination of pigment content

Total anthocyanin, total flavonoid, and total carotenoid contents in the variegated and non-variegated areas of I. uliginosa dorsal petals were quantified according to the method described by Wan et al. [38]. Subsequently, semi-quantitative analysis of pigment aglycones in both areas was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with a diode-array detector (HPLC–DAD).

Transcriptomic analysis

RNA sequencing and de novo assembly

Following the method described by Li et al. [34], tissue samples were collected from the variegated (V) and non-variegated (nV) areas of I. uliginosa dorsal petals at full anthesis. Petals were detached from the plants and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Each sample consisted of tissue pooled from more than three biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted from plant tissue using Plant RNA Purification Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (Takara). RNA-seq transcriptome libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). After quantification with a TBS380 fluorometer (Picogreen), the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten/NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were processed for adapter trimming and quality control using SeqPrep (https://github.com/jstjohn/SeqPrep) and Sickle (https://github.com/najoshi/sickle) with default parameters. De novo assembly of the clean reads was performed using Trinity (http://trinityrnaseq.sourceforge.net/) [39]. The assembled transcripts were clustered based on sequence homology, and the longest transcript within each cluster was designated as a unigene. Assembly quality was assessed and optimized using TransRate (http://hibberdlab.com/transrate/), followed by the removal of redundant and highly similar sequences using CD-HIT (http://weizhongli-lab.org/cd-hit/). The completeness of the transcriptome assembly was evaluated using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO, http://busco.ezlab.org).

Functional annotation

For functional annotation and categorization, unigene sequences were aligned against multiple public databases using BLASTX (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) with an E-value threshold of ≤ 1e-5. Annotation information was obtained by comparing the sequences against six major databases: Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Swiss-Prot, eggNOG, Pfam, and the Non-Redundant (NR) database.

Identification and clustering analysis of transcription factors

Transcription factor prediction and family classification were performed by aligning transcript sequences against the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB; http://planttfdb.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) using HMMER to identify homologous transcription factors. Hierarchical clustering analysis was subsequently performed on the differentially expressed transcription factors.

Screening and enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Gene and transcript expression levels were quantified using TPM (transcripts per million) and FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads), with abundance estimation performed using RSEM (http://deweylab.biostat.wisc.edu/rsem/) [40]. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 [41], DEGseq [42], or edgeR [43]. Genes meeting the criteria of Q-value ≤ 0.05 and |log2FC|> 1 (for DESeq2 or edgeR) or Q-value ≤ 0.001 (for DEGseq) were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. GO enrichment analysis was conducted using GOATOOLS (https://github.com/tanghaibao/GOatools), and KEGG pathway analysis was performed using KOBAS (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do) [44]. Statistical significance was assessed using Fisher's exact test, and P values were corrected using the Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) methods. Corrected P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Functional validation of IuMYB114 in regulating variegation formation in I. uliginosa

The VIGS silencing vector was constructed according to the method described by Chen et al. [12]. Specific primers were designed based on sequence information obtained from the NCBI database. The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of IuMYB114 was cloned into the pTRV2 vector using XbaI and BamHI restriction sites. After double digestion, ligation, and transformation into Escherichia coli DH5α, the recombinant plasmid was confirmed by colony PCR and sequencing. The verified construct was then introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 to generate the pTRV2-IuMYB114 recombinant vector. Plants infiltrated with the empty pTRV2 vector served as the positive control (PC), whereas those transformed with pTRV2-IuMYB114 were designated SP1.

Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration was carried out according to the method described by Gao et al. [45], with minor modifications. Positive clones harboring pTRV1, pTRV2, and pTRV2-IuMYB114 were cultured individually at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm until an OD600 of 0.5–0.8 was reached. Bacterial cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 10 min and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 μM acetosyringone, pH 5.6) to a final OD600 of 1.5. The three suspensions were mixed at a 1:1:1 ratio and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 3–4 h. Cuttings of variegated I. uliginosa at the bud stage were selected for infiltration. The bacterial mixture was injected into the stem and was also used to soak the flower buds via stem injection. A total of 30 plants were infiltrated. After infiltration, the plants were kept in the dark at 8 °C for the first two days, moved to 23 °C on day 3, and then transferred to normal light conditions. Phenotypic changes were monitored and documented every three days over a 15-day period.

Cellular morphology of variegated and non-variegated areas in I. uliginosa

Petal pigment distribution in variegated and non-variegated areas

Cell numbers in the variegated and non-variegated areas were assessed using both freehand sections (Fig. 2) and paraffin sections. At 200 × magnification within a 200 × 200 μm field of view, marked differences in cell number were observed between the two tissue types. In the freehand sections (Fig. 3A), approximately 24 cells were observed in the variegated areas, compared with about 8 cells in the non-variegated areas. In the paraffin sections (Fig. 3D), the variegated areas contained roughly 33 cells, whereas the non-variegated areas contained approximately 11 cells. Taken together, the results from both methods consistently showed that the number of pigment cells in the variegated areas was approximately three times that in the non-variegated areas, with strong agreement between the two datasets. Cell length (Fig. 2B) and width (Fig. 2C) were measured, and the results are shown in Figs. 3B and C. In the variegated areas, mean cell length was approximately 1.9 μm (corresponding to an actual length of about 17 μm), whereas in the non-variegated areas, mean cell length was approximately 2.0 μm (actual length about 18 μm). Mean cell width in the variegated areas was approximately 1.2 μm (actual width about 15 μm), compared with approximately 1.5 μm (actual width about 16 μm) in the non-variegated areas. Cells in the non-variegated areas were significantly longer than those in the variegated areas, suggesting that cells in the non-variegated regions may be at a more advanced developmental stage or may exhibit a distinct pattern of cell elongation. Although more numerous, cells in the variegated areas were shorter, which may be associated with active cell division and a lower degree of differentiation. Furthermore, cells in the non-variegated areas were significantly wider than those in the variegated areas, and the width difference (14%) was more pronounced than the length difference (8%). These findings indicate that cells in the non-variegated areas display a flattened and enlarged morphology, whereas those in the variegated areas appear short and compact.

Collectively, these findings suggest that pigment accumulation in the variegated areas (V) results from an increased number of pigment-accumulating cells rather than from cell expansion. Cells in the variegated regions are characterized by small size (short and narrow), high density, and elevated metabolic activity, making them well suited for rapid anthocyanin biosynthesis and accumulation. In contrast, cells in the non-variegated areas are larger (long and wide), less abundant, and presumably metabolically less active, suggesting that they primarily serve structural and protective roles with limited capacity for pigment production.

Petal epidermal cell morphology

As shown in Fig. 4, the upper epidermis and subepidermal layers of the variegated areas (V) contained abundant, plump, and nearly spherical pigment cells (Pc). These cells were densely arranged and exhibited high pigment content. In contrast, pigment cells in the non-variegated areas (nV) were sparse, flattened, and contained little pigment, representing the direct histological basis for the formation of purplish-red spots on the dorsal petals of I. uliginosa. Pigment accumulation occurred predominantly within the one to three cell layers immediately beneath the upper epidermis (Pc), consistent with an epidermal pigment-cell type of variegation mechanism. This pattern resembles that observed in ornamental flowers such as petunia and morning glory and is consistent with the vacuolar anthocyanin accumulation model. Cells in the variegated areas, including pigment cells (Pc), epidermal cells (Ec), and subepidermal cells (Sm), generally exhibited a plump, rounded morphology and dense arrangement, suggesting high metabolic activity, well-developed vacuoles, and efficient anthocyanin biosynthesis and storage capacity. Conversely, cells in the non-variegated areas appeared flattened and loosely arranged, indicative of lower metabolic activity. The histological observations, namely, a threefold higher number of pigment cells in the variegated areas than in the non-variegated areas, were fully consistent with the previously measured color parameters (L* = 41.13 in the variegated areas vs. 50.73 in the non-variegated areas, P < 0.01; a* = 33.75 in the variegated areas vs. 26.07 in the non-variegated areas). Pigment content analysis of variegated I. uliginosa

Pigment content in variegated and non-variegated areas

As shown in Fig. 5, the anthocyanin content in variegated areas (41.30 mg/g) was approximately threefold higher than that in non-variegated areas (13.87 mg/g). As water-soluble pigments accumulated in vacuoles, anthocyanins are primarily responsible for the high-saturation purplish-red coloration observed in the variegated regions. Flavonoid content was also elevated in variegated areas (65.38 mg/g) compared to non-variegated areas (45.05 mg/g), representing an approximately 1.5-fold increase, although the magnitude of this difference was smaller than that observed for anthocyanins. As byproducts of the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway and potential copigments, flavonoids may contribute to the stabilization of anthocyanin structures, enhance color stability (including resistance to light and heat), and provide ultraviolet protection. Carotenoid content, while substantially lower than that of anthocyanins and flavonoids, was approximately twofold higher in variegated areas (1.03 mg/g) than in non-variegated areas (0.51 mg/g). Semi-quantitative analysis of floral pigments in variegated I. uliginosa

As shown in Fig. 6, with the exception of quercetin, the contents of the remaining eight pigments were significantly higher in the variegated areas than in the non-variegated areas of I. uliginosa. Among these, the differences in delphinidin, pelargonidin, and peonidin were particularly pronounced. The elevated levels of these pigments in the variegated areas are likely major contributors to the darker and redder coloration observed, indicating that the pigmentation pattern results from the coordinated accumulation of multiple anthocyanin-related compounds rather than from the specific enrichment of a single pigment type. Delphinidin, pelargonidin, and peonidin therefore appear to be important contributors to coloration in the variegated areas. These findings reveal clear metabolic divergence between the variegated and non-variegated tissues: the variegated areas are characterized by stronger anthocyanin accumulation and deeper purplish-red coloration, whereas the non-variegated areas exhibit markedly lower anthocyanin content and a paler appearance, likely owing to the predominance of flavonols or other colorless or pale yellow compounds. Discussion

Floral variegation is a complex petal trait whose underlying mechanisms involve epidermal-cell microstructure, pigment distribution, and physiological and biochemical processes. Previous studies have shown that pigment accumulation in variegated petal areas is often associated with specific epidermal cell layers. For instance, in Phalaenopsis aphrodite, pigments accumulate predominantly in the upper epidermis of the variegated regions [46]. In Paeonia rockii, the number and morphology of pigment cells also play important roles in determining flower color [47]. In the present study, observations from both freehand and paraffin sections revealed that pigments in both the variegated (V) and non-variegated (nV) areas of I. uliginosa petals were primarily localized in the upper epidermis. Notably, the number of pigment cells in the variegated areas was significantly higher, approximately threefold, than that in the non-variegated areas. Cells in the variegated regions exhibited a plump, rounded, and densely arranged morphology, whereas those in the non-variegated regions were larger, irregularly shaped, and more loosely arranged. These findings suggest that differences in the number, size, shape, and packing density of pigment cells constitute an important structural basis for floral variegation.

Spatial differences in pigment composition and content form the biochemical basis of flower-color variegation. In this study, anthocyanin, flavonoid, and carotenoid contents in the variegated areas of I. uliginosa were significantly higher than those in the non-variegated areas, and pigment accumulation was positively correlated with color intensity. This pattern is consistent with pigment-enrichment patterns reported in Lilium brownii [48] and Raphanus sativus [49]. The marked differences in anthocyanin and flavonoid accumulation between the variegated and non-variegated regions suggest that these two classes of compounds are key metabolites driving color differentiation. Anthocyanins, as the primary chromogenic components of the flavonoid pathway, are widely recognized as critical determinants of flower-color diversity and variegated pattern formation [50]. In Nigella orientalis and Phalaenopsis aphrodite, the formation of variegated patterns depends on the high-level accumulation of anthocyanins specifically within the variegated areas [46–51].

The coloration conferred by anthocyanins is largely determined by their aglycone types. The most common anthocyanidin aglycones include cyanidin, pelargonidin, delphinidin, peonidin, petunidin, and malvidin [52]. Among these, pelargonidin and cyanidin are associated with red pigmentation, whereas peonidin is associated with purplish-red hues. Delphinidin, petunidin, and malvidin confer blue to blue-violet tones [53–55]. Previous studies have shown that specific variegated patterns often result from the combined accumulation of multiple aglycones. For example, the purple-red patch at the base of the passionflower corolla is rich in both delphinidin and cyanidin [56]. Consistent with this general pattern, the present study showed that several anthocyanin-related compounds, especially delphinidin-, pelargonidin-, and peonidin-related compounds, were more abundant in variegated than in non-variegated areas. Their enrichment in the variegated regions likely contributes to the darker and redder appearance of these tissues, indicating that coloration in the variegated areas arises from the coordinated accumulation of multiple pigments rather than from a single dominant compound. Furthermore, four pigment compounds, cyanidin-3-galactose-galactoside, dihydroquercetin, trimethoxyflavone, and chrysophanol-8-O-glucoside, were found to accumulate specifically in the variegated areas. These compounds may contribute to pigmentation and associated metabolic processes in the variegated regions.

Differences in gene expression within the anthocyanin and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways are an important molecular basis for flower-color variation. In Orychophragmus violaceus and Rhododendron rex, differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in the anthocyanin and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways [57, 58]. The functional enrichment results obtained in the present study are consistent with these findings, indicating that the variegated phenotype of I. uliginosa also arises from marked alterations in gene expression within these pathways. Although sampling at earlier developmental stages may provide more direct information for identifying the initial regulatory genes involved in the formation of variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions, precise sampling during early floral development in I. uliginosa is technically challenging because the spatial boundaries are not yet clearly established and the tissue volume is extremely limited. Therefore, samples at full anthesis were selected in this study to ensure accurate tissue separation and sampling reliability.

Transcriptome analysis identified a total of 20 transcription factor families, including MYB, AP2/ERF, and bHLH, among which the MYB family exhibited the most pronounced expression differences. MYB transcription factors, as key regulators of anthocyanin biosynthesis, have been functionally characterized in numerous plant species. The first MYB gene shown to regulate anthocyanins, ZmC1, was identified in Zea mays [27]. Subsequent studies, from the regulation of variegated patterns by PsMYB12 in Paeonia suffruticosa [59] to the induction of red flower coloration by MYB factors in Rhododendron simsii [60], have collectively underscored the pivotal role of MYB transcription factors in anthocyanin biosynthesis and patterning across diverse plant lineages.

In this study, qRT-PCR validation revealed that the expression pattern of IuMYB114 was especially prominent. The MYB114 subfamily, which is known to function as a positive regulator of anthocyanin biosynthesis, has been extensively characterized in Arabidopsis thaliana, pear, and apple [25, 61, 62]. To further assess its function, we silenced IuMYB114 using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) and observed a marked reduction in the size of the variegated areas in I. uliginosa petals, accompanied by a shift in flower color from deep pink to light pink. These phenotypic changes indicate that loss of IuMYB114 activity strongly affects pigmentation in the variegated regions. After silencing, the expression of key downstream structural genes in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, namely IuANS, IuUFGT, and IuF3′5'H, was downregulated to varying degrees, with IuF3′5'H showing the most pronounced reduction. This result is consistent with previous findings in alfalfa, where downregulation of F3′5'H affected flower-color formation [63], and supports the interpretation that IuMYB114 contributes to anthocyanin biosynthesis and spatial pigment distribution by positively regulating the expression of IuF3′5'H, IuANS, and IuUFGT. Collectively, these results indicate that IuMYB114 is an important positive regulator associated with both pigment intensity and the extent of the variegated areas in I. uliginosa flowers. However, the underlying regulatory network is likely more complex, and the precise molecular mechanisms governing variegated pattern formation warrant further investigation. In the present study, the TRV-based VIGS system was established based on our previous work in I. uliginosa, in which the empty TRV vector showed no obvious effects on plant growth, floral morphology, or developmental processes. Therefore, the same validated VIGS system was adopted in this study using the empty pTRV2 vector as the control. Although inclusion of unrelated-gene controls would further strengthen experimental rigor by excluding potential non-specific silencing effects, the current study primarily focused on preliminary candidate gene screening, and more comprehensive control strategies will be incorporated in future mechanistic studies.

The silencing results, combined with expression analysis, provide preliminary evidence supporting the involvement of IuMYB114 in anthocyanin regulation. However, the current data are insufficient to conclusively demonstrate direct transcriptional regulation of downstream structural genes by IuMYB114. Direct molecular interactions, including promoter binding activity and transcriptional activation, remain to be experimentally verified through yeast one-hybrid assays, electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA), dual-luciferase reporter assays, and other molecular approaches. Therefore, the regulatory relationship identified in this study should currently be considered as a potential rather than definitive transcriptional interaction.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the transcriptomic comparison was restricted to variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions at a single developmental stage, and therefore does not resolve temporal regulatory dynamics. Second, only one candidate regulator, IuMYB114, was functionally validated; additional MYB, bHLH, and structural-gene candidates, as well as direct promoter interactions, remain to be examined. Third, although the pigment analyses clearly distinguished the two tissue types, more comprehensive targeted metabolite profiling would further refine the contributions of individual anthocyanin derivatives. Accordingly, the regulatory model proposed here should be regarded as a supported working model that will benefit from further molecular and biochemical validation.

Overall, the present study provides preliminary evidence supporting the involvement of IuMYB114 and anthocyanin biosynthetic genes in the formation of variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions in Impatiens uliginosa.

The pigment analysis performed in this study mainly aimed to preliminarily identify the major pigment pathways associated with the formation of variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions. The observed consistency between pigment accumulation patterns and transcriptomic enrichment of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes provides strong support for the involvement of anthocyanin metabolism in regional pigmentation. However, semi-quantitative pigment analysis alone is insufficient to directly link IuUFGT expression with specific anthocyanin glycosides. More comprehensive targeted metabolomic analyses, such as LC–MS/MS-based profiling, will be performed in subsequent mechanistic studies. Importantly, the current work primarily focuses on candidate gene identification and initial functional screening rather than comprehensive mechanistic validation. Therefore, incorporation of unrelated-gene controls in VIGS experiments, direct molecular validation of transcriptional regulation, and targeted metabolomic analyses will be systematically addressed in our subsequent studies.

Conclusion

In this study, integrated cytological, physiological, pigment, transcriptomic, and VIGS analyses revealed significant differences between the variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions of I. uliginosa, indicating that differential anthocyanin accumulation is closely associated with floral variegation formation. Several structural genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis, including CHS, CHI, F3H, DFR, ANS, and UFGT, as well as multiple transcription factors, were identified as potential candidates associated with regional pigmentation. Among them, IuMYB114 showed a strong correlation with anthocyanin accumulation, and VIGS analysis provided preliminary evidence suggesting that IuMYB114 may participate in the transcriptional regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis in the variegated and non-variegated dorsal petal regions. These findings provide an important foundation for further elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying floral variegation formation in Impatiens species.
