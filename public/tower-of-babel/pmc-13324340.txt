Adaptive G-UKT: a unified probabilistic framework for knowledge tracing via adaptive graph topology learning and uncertainty-aware Gaussian embeddings
Authors: Nan Jia, Weitao Su, Junrui Xian, Shijia Zou, Yixue Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDynamic State Tracking (DST) is pivotal for personalized recommender systems and user modeling, aiming to estimate users’ evolving latent states from sequential interactions. However, existing deep sequence paradigms predominantly treat interaction entities, such as semantic tokens and items, as isolated deterministic vectors. This approach often overlooks latent structural dependencies among entities, including knowledge graph topologies, and remains limited in quantifying the epistemic uncertainty inherent in stochastic user behaviors caused by random interactions and aleatoric noise. To address these dual challenges, we propose Adaptive G-UKT (Adaptive Graph-Enhanced Uncertainty-aware Knowledge Tracing), a unified probabilistic framework for temporal sequence modeling. Unlike traditional point-estimation models, we map hidden user states into Gaussian distributions, enabling the simultaneous tracking of semantic activation levels and estimation confidence through diagonal covariance. To mitigate data sparsity, we design an Adaptive Graph Learner that autonomously infers latent semantic correlations from raw data, coupled with an Adaptive Gaussian-HGNN that propagates uncertainty information across the dynamically learned topology. Furthermore, we introduce a Wasserstein attention mechanism to perform distribution-aware sequence retrieval and an uncertainty-guided contrastive learning strategy to enhance model robustness against noisy interactions. Extensive experiments on four large-scale real-world sequential datasets, namely ASSISTments2009, Bridge2Algebra2006, Algebra2005, and NIPS34, demonstrate that Adaptive G-UKT achieves competitive performance against state-of-the-art baselines, showing particularly significant gains in sparse data regimes. Crucially, visualization analysis confirms the model’s capacity to autonomously uncover intrinsic structural topologies, bridging the critical gap between high-precision deep sequence learning and interpretable knowledge graph reasoning.Subject terms: Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

With the rapid proliferation of online learning platforms and adaptive inference systems, sequential latent state estimation over structured entity graphs has emerged as a central problem in representation learning1. At the core of this problem lies Knowledge Tracing (KT)—formally, the task of sequentially inferring latent competency state distributions of entities from historical observation streams2,3. By modeling how entities progressively update their latent states over structured concept graphs , downstream systems can perform confidence-aware inference and adaptive decision-making. Consequently, developing robust and accurate latent state estimation models is a key factor for reliable sequential inference pipelines.

In recent years, deep sequential encoders for KT, such as Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT)4 and Attentive Knowledge Tracing (AKT), have demonstrated remarkable potential by capturing complex temporal dependencies via recurrent and attention-based architectures. Recent advancements have further integrated forgetting mechanisms5 and adopted simple yet competitive deterministic baselines6 to refine predictive accuracy. However, despite their empirical success, existing methods still face two fundamental limitations that compromise their reliability in complex real-world sequential inference scenarios7.

First, the challenge of Structural Sparsity and Node Isolation. Existing encoders predominantly adopt an entity-isolated scheme, modeling each concept node independently and failing to exploit the latent relational structure of the underlying concept graph via graph-based message passing8,9. In practice, observation streams are frequently sparse: an entity may only interact with a small subset of nodes. Without propagating state information across topologically adjacent nodes, the model cannot infer latent states for unobserved entities. Recent attempts to incorporate structured relational priors10 or temporal extrapolation mechanisms11 have shown promise, yet addressing the cold-start problem under severe observation sparsity remains an open challenge.

Second, the limitation of Deterministic Point Estimation. From a Bayesian perspective, existing models collapse the posterior distribution to a Dirac delta function, discarding all epistemic uncertainty encoded in the covariance structure12. This renders them incapable of distinguishing high-confidence state estimates (low posterior variance ) from unreliable estimates corrupted by aleatoric noise—a critical failure mode for robust sequential inference. While some works have improved representational expressivity via question-centric encodings13,14 or retrieval-augmented mechanisms15, explicit posterior uncertainty quantification remains underexplored.

While several recent works have attempted to incorporate graph structures or probabilistic embeddings separately, a unified framework that simultaneously addresses structural sparsity and epistemic uncertainty quantification remains an open research question. Graph-based methods often rely on static, pre-defined adjacency matrices that cannot adapt to data-driven relational patterns, while existing contrastive learning approaches16–18 may not fully leverage uncertainty propagation across graph topology. Therefore, jointly modeling latent relational graph structure via adaptive topology learning and maintaining distributional state representations via Gaussian embeddings represents a critical avenue for advancing sequential latent state estimation.

More importantly, the novelty of our work does not lie in merely assembling adaptive graph learning, Gaussian embeddings, and contrastive learning as independent modules. Instead, Adaptive G-UKT couples these components within one probabilistic pipeline: the learned topology governs distributional message passing, Gaussian states provide a shared uncertainty-aware representation space, and Wasserstein attention together with uncertainty-guided contrastive regularization performs sequence retrieval and robustness enhancement directly in that space. This joint coupling allows the graph structure and posterior uncertainty to interact throughout state propagation, rather than being modeled in isolation as in prior uncertainty-aware KT or graph-based KT methods.

To address these challenges, we propose Adaptive G-UKT (Adaptive Graph-Enhanced Uncertainty-aware Knowledge Tracing), a unified probabilistic inference framework. Unlike traditional point-estimation encoders, we reformulate each entity’s latent state as a Gaussian Distribution , characterized by a posterior mean (state centroid) and a diagonal covariance (epistemic uncertainty bound). To address structural sparsity, we design an Adaptive Graph Topology Learner that infers latent relational dependencies end-to-end via differentiable sigmoid adjacency relaxation. Importantly, we propose an Adaptive Gaussian-HGNN that performs dual-channel distributional message passing across the learned topology, propagating both posterior means and diagonal variances via residual graph convolution, enabling inference of latent states for unobserved nodes through neighborhood aggregation. Furthermore, to enhance robustness against observation noise, we introduce an Uncertainty-Aware Contrastive Regularization strategy that leverages the learned covariance as a dynamic reliability filter for selective stochastic augmentation.

Related work

This section reviews the literature pertinent to our framework, categorizing prior research into three primary streams: Deep Sequential State Modeling, Graph-Structured Relational Learning, and Probabilistic Representation Learning with Contrastive Regularization.

Deep sequential state modeling

The transition from probabilistic graphical models, such as Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT), to deep representation learning paradigms has fundamentally reshaped sequential latent state estimation. Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) pioneered this shift by employing Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units to encode temporal observation sequences into latent state vectors, demonstrating superior capacity for modeling long-range dependencies compared to traditional HMM-based inference. Following this, Dynamic Key-Value Memory Networks (DKVMN)19 enhanced representational expressivity by decoupling static entity embeddings from dynamic latent state matrices via differentiable memory read/write operations. Recently, generative architectures coupling Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) with external memory mechanisms have further refined latent state disentanglement, enabling more accurate modeling of temporal forgetting and consolidation dynamics20.

To overcome the limitation of RNNs in capturing non-consecutive temporal dependencies, Transformer-based attention mechanisms have been widely adopted for sequential latent state modeling. Attentive Knowledge Tracing (AKT) introduces a monotonic attention kernel that weights historical observations by temporal relevance to the current query state, implementing an inductive bias for forgetting dynamics. Recent work has further expanded the representational capacity of sequential encoders by integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) to extract semantic feature representations from exercise content21.

Despite their empirical effectiveness, these sequential encoders share a fundamental limitation: they adopt an entity-isolated encoding scheme, treating each concept node independently. Consequently, they fail to exploit the rich latent relational structure—such as directed dependency edges and topological similarities—that inherently exists within the underlying concept graph .

Graph-structured relational learning for entity state estimation

To address structural isolation in sequential encoders, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been introduced to explicitly model relational dependencies between concept nodes . Early approaches constructed static adjacency matrices based on expert-defined prerequisite graphs or statistical co-occurrence probabilities. In these frameworks, latent state information propagates from observed nodes to topologically adjacent unobserved nodes via message passing, enabling inference over the full concept graph.

Recent years have seen a surge in dynamic and high-order graph representation learning. To capture temporal evolution of relational structure, DyGKT22 models observation histories as continuous-time dynamic graphs with time-stamped edge updates. To address entity heterogeneity, Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks have been employed to construct unified multi-relational graphs over entity types (exercises, concepts, responses), enabling cross-type feature fusion via heterogeneous message passing23,24. Furthermore, high-order relational dependencies have been explored via hypergraph-based architectures, modeling complex multi-entity “exercise-concept” hyperedges beyond simple pairwise connections25.

Recent graph-structured KT studies further extend this line by introducing hyperbolic hypergraph transformers with knowledge-state disentanglement and knowledge-associated memory-aware embeddings, demonstrating the value of richer relational inductive biases for concept-state modeling. Recent work has further introduced structural causal models into graph learning pipelines to disentangle spurious statistical correlations from true causal dependency edges26.

Beyond KT, recent GNN-related studies also highlight the importance of adaptive, personalized, incomplete, and heterophily-aware graph learning, including dynamic similarity fusion for recommendation27, grouping-enhanced personalization in federated recommendation28, class-imbalance and attribute-missing graph learning29, and broader incomplete graph learning surveys30. Related advances in adaptive graph meta-learning31, heterophilic graph learning under distribution shift32, flexible two-way flow message passing beyond homophily33, holistic hypergraph message passing34,35, temporal graph transformers for financial anomaly detection36, hybrid GCN-based intrusion detection37, and transformer-GGNN modeling for sarcasm detection38 further suggest that real-world relational structures are often noisy, partially observed, distribution-dependent, and not necessarily homophilic. These observations motivate data-adaptive graph construction rather than reliance on fixed topology.

However, a critical limitation shared by most prior graph-based methods is their heavy reliance on pre-defined or static adjacency matrices . In practice, such graph structures are often unavailable, incomplete, or corrupted by noise39, and fixed topology cannot adapt to the data-driven relational patterns emerging from observation streams. This underscores the need for end-to-end differentiable Adaptive Graph Topology Learning coupled with distributional uncertainty propagation mechanisms.

Probabilistic representation learning and contrastive regularization

Beyond deterministic point estimation, probabilistic representation learning recognizes that latent state distributions are characterized by inherent stochasticity arising from both observation noise and model uncertainty. Probabilistic extensions of sequential encoders have explored Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) coupled with Bayesian state-space models to decompose total predictive uncertainty into epistemic and aleatoric components12. Closer to our work, the Uncertainty-Aware Knowledge Tracing (UKT) 40 framework represents latent states as Gaussian distributions rather than deterministic point vectors, enabling explicit posterior variance estimation.

Simultaneously, InfoNCE-based Contrastive Learning (CL) has emerged as a powerful self-supervised regularization technique for improving latent representation robustness under sparse and noisy observations. Methods like CL4KT41 apply stochastic augmentation to construct positive pairs and maximize mutual information between augmented views via contrastive objectives. Recent strategies have evolved to address specific architectural challenges, such as integrating retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines to bootstrap structured relational graphs for multi-hop reasoning42. Others have introduced hard-negative mining strategies to improve discriminative capacity over semantically similar node representations. However, standard CL approaches treat all positive pairs uniformly, ignoring the heterogeneous reliability of samples induced by aleatoric observation noise. Our work advances this by leveraging estimated posterior variance as a dynamic reliability weight to perform uncertainty-gated selective augmentation. At a broader methodological level, this perspective also connects to uncertainty-aware learning beyond KT, including robust multimodal state modeling and fusion frameworks43–46 and Bayesian or federated uncertainty quantification for distributed sequential inference47–50. These studies collectively emphasize uncertainty as a structural signal that guides representation learning, information fusion, and robust decision-making under sparse or noisy observations, which helps position our framework within a wider uncertainty-aware learning landscape.

Research Gap and Our Approach Existing approaches typically address the above challenges in isolation. Sequential encoders lack relational inductive biases over ; graph-based methods rely on static, pre-defined adjacency matrices ; and probabilistic frameworks typically lack mechanisms for spatial uncertainty diffusion across graph topology. Adaptive G-UKT bridges these gaps by coupling an end-to-end differentiable Adaptive Graph Topology Learner with Gaussian Distributional State Representations, enabling robust sequential latent state estimation under both sparse observation regimes and high-noise environments. More specifically, compared with UKT-style uncertainty-aware models, Adaptive G-UKT introduces adaptive relational structure learning and graph-based uncertainty diffusion beyond standalone Gaussian state modeling. Compared with graph-based KT methods, our framework moves from deterministic node features to Gaussian latent states, and further performs sequence matching in the Wasserstein space. This distinction clarifies that our contribution is a unified probabilistic coupling of structure adaptation, uncertainty modeling, and sequence-level distribution matching, rather than a simple combination of existing ideas. In contrast to recent graph-centric KT models such as hyperbolic hypergraph transformers and memory-aware knowledge-associated embeddings51,52, which mainly strengthen deterministic relational representation learning, Adaptive G-UKT treats graph topology learning as part of a probabilistic inference pipeline: the learned topology guides Gaussian message passing, while uncertainty-aware Wasserstein retrieval operates on the propagated distributional states.

Methodology

While traditional sequential state estimation approaches predominantly rely on deterministic point encoders and static graph structures, they fail to account for the stochastic nature of latent state distributions and inter-node relational dependencies. To circumvent these limitations, we propose Adaptive G-UKT, a unified probabilistic inference framework that transitions from deterministic point estimates to full posterior Gaussian distributional representations . By explicitly quantifying epistemic uncertainty via diagonal covariance estimation and inferring adaptive graph topology end-to-end from observation data, our framework facilitates robust distributional state propagation under both sparse observation regimes and high-noise environments.

To further improve readability, we provide an intuitive summary of how the main components interact in the proposed framework. Gaussian state representation answers how certain the model is about the current latent state by explicitly encoding both semantic activation and uncertainty. Adaptive graph propagation answers where information should flow by transferring both posterior means and uncertainty signals across related concepts under the learned topology. Wasserstein-based sequential retrieval then answers which historical states are most relevant by matching past interactions in the same Gaussian distribution space. In this sense, uncertainty modeling and graph propagation are not independent add-ons: the graph controls how uncertainty-aware information is propagated, while the downstream retriever selects history according to both semantic similarity and confidence compatibility.

Probabilistic graph encoding via gaussian distributional message passing

A fundamental limitation of deterministic sequential encoders is the conflation of high-confidence state estimates with noisy, stochastic observations under a single point representation , collapsing the posterior to a Dirac delta function. This representation is geometrically inconsistent with the Riemannian manifold of probability distributions, and renders the model incapable of distinguishing reliable state estimates (low posterior variance) from unreliable ones corrupted by aleatoric noise. To address this limitation, we transition from deterministic point encoders to full distributional representations. Specifically, we project discrete observations into a continuous probabilistic embedding space, employing diagonal multivariate Gaussian distributions to explicitly disentangle the posterior mean (state centroid) from the diagonal covariance (epistemic uncertainty bound).

Distribution-aware sequential latent state modeling via Wasserstein attention

The theoretical motivation for introducing Wasserstein attention is that the latent states in Adaptive G-UKT are modeled as Gaussian distributions rather than deterministic point vectors. Under this representation, a similarity function should jointly reflect discrepancies in both semantic location and uncertainty structure. Standard dot-product attention is defined in Euclidean vector space and mainly compares first-order features, whereas the 2-Wasserstein distance provides a distribution-level metric that is geometrically compatible with Gaussian states. Compared with KL divergence, Wasserstein distance is also more suitable for our sequence retrieval setting because it is symmetric in measuring pairwise discrepancy and admits a closed-form expression for Gaussian distributions that explicitly decomposes similarity into mean and covariance discrepancies. This makes it better aligned with our uncertainty-aware retrieval objective and more convenient for stable end-to-end optimization. Importantly, the graph topology and Wasserstein retrieval are not assumed to share an identical metric form. Instead, they remain geometrically consistent because the adaptive graph only governs where information is propagated, while the downstream Wasserstein kernel measures similarity over the Gaussian states produced after this propagation. Since the dual-channel message passing updates both means and diagonal covariances within the same probabilistic latent space, the representations consumed by the sequence retriever remain distributionally well-formed, making topology-aware propagation and Wasserstein-based retrieval compatible parts of one unified Gaussian geometry.

Building upon the relational inductive biases captured by the adaptive graph topology, we employ a Transformer backbone to model the temporal evolution of distributional latent states . However, standard scaled dot-product attention is geometrically inconsistent with our probabilistic framework: it computes relevance scores based solely on mean vectors, completely discarding the epistemic uncertainty information encoded in covariance matrices . This induces a metric mismatch, as Euclidean inner products are ill-suited for measuring similarity on the Riemannian manifold of Gaussian distributions. Experimental settings

We implemented Adaptive G-UKT and all baseline models using the PyTorch framework, accelerated on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU. The model parameters were optimized via the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 64. The learning rate was initialized at and scheduled via a ReduceLROnPlateau strategy (decay factor 0.5, patience 5). To prevent overfitting and ensure robust convergence, we applied a dropout rate of 0.2 and utilized an early stopping mechanism with a patience of 10 epochs based on validation loss. Regarding model hyperparameters, the dimension of the Gaussian embeddings was fixed at to balance model expressiveness with computational efficiency. The Wasserstein balance coefficient was set to , the contrastive temperature to , and the contrastive loss weight to ; these settings are further examined through the hyperparameter sensitivity analysis reported in Table 8. To strictly assess predictive performance, we employ two standard metrics: Area Under the Curve (AUC) and Accuracy (ACC). Specifically, AUC evaluates the model’s discriminative capability between correct and incorrect responses, being less sensitive to class imbalance, whereas ACC measures the overall prediction correctness using a threshold of 0.5. To further evaluate the quality of uncertainty estimation, we additionally report Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) in the revised manuscript. NLL measures the likelihood quality of probabilistic predictions, while ECE evaluates the consistency between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy; lower values on both metrics indicate better uncertainty quality and calibration. For data partitioning, each dataset was randomly shuffled with a fixed random seed (1024) to ensure reproducibility, and subsequently split into training, validation, and test sets following an // ratio, with held out as the test set and the remaining further divided into for training and for validation. This partitioning strategy is applied consistently across all four datasets to ensure fair comparison. To ensure statistical reliability, all reported results represent the average of 5 independent runs with distinct random seeds.

The performance advantage of Adaptive G-UKT is most conspicuously observed on the AS2009 dataset, which represents a data-rich environment with long interaction sequences. As shown in Table 3, our model achieves a remarkable AUC of 90.62%, surpassing the strongest attention-based baseline, AKT (84.74%), by nearly 6.00%. This result offers a critical insight: while advanced attention mechanisms capture monotonic temporal dependencies, they remain fundamentally bounded by the point-estimation constraint—collapsing the posterior to a Dirac delta and discarding covariance information. In contrast, our Gaussian-Wasserstein mechanism leverages dense observation streams to perform geometrically consistent distribution-level similarity computation on the Wasserstein manifold, maximizing the utilization of high-density observation data for a substantial performance gain.

Beyond dense scenarios, our framework demonstrates exceptional precision on the fine-grained, step-level datasets (AL2005 and BD2006). Notably, on AL2005, Adaptive G-UKT achieves a commanding AUC of 93.37%. It is worth noting that while pure graph baselines like GKT (81.54%) perform decently, they struggle to match the precision of our model. This validates that static, expert-annotated adjacency matrices introduce irrelevant structural noise that degrades message passing quality. In contrast, our end-to-end differentiable Adaptive Graph Topology Learner dynamically filters spurious relational edges via sigmoid relaxation and hard thresholding, yielding consistent accuracy gains even over highly competitive baselines like AKT (92.94%). It is worth noting that on BD2006, our model’s margin over the probabilistic baseline UKT is narrow (AUC 81.82% vs. 81.78%), suggesting that uncertainty modeling alone already provides strong structural inductive bias on fine-grained step-level data. Nevertheless, the consistent improvements in both AUC and ACC across all datasets confirm that the integration of adaptive graph propagation and Wasserstein attention yields reliable gains beyond point-estimation and vanilla probabilistic baselines alike. For BD2006 in particular, we believe the marginal gain of the adaptive graph module is mainly due to two factors. First, this dataset already allows UKT to achieve a relatively strong baseline through uncertainty-aware sequential modeling alone, leaving less room for structural augmentation. Second, BD2006 contains fine-grained algebraic steps with highly local and repetitive response patterns, so much of the useful dependency signal is already captured by short-range sequential context and Gaussian uncertainty modeling. Under this condition, the adaptive graph learner still acts as a structural regularizer, but its contribution is more incremental than in datasets where longer-range or sparser relational cues play a larger role.

Furthermore, the robustness of our approach is rigorously tested under the data-scarce conditions of the NIPS34 dataset. Here, Adaptive G-UKT maintains a consistent lead with an AUC of 80.55%. A particularly interesting comparison is against RobustKT (78.54%) and ATKT (76.65%), which employ adversarial perturbations to handle data noise. The superiority of our probabilistic framework over these methods suggests that explicit posterior uncertainty quantification via diagonal Gaussian covariance is a more principled strategy for mitigating the cold-start problem under sparse observation regimes than implicit adversarial perturbation training. By utilizing the closed-form 2-Wasserstein metric to geometrically calibrate distributional similarity, Adaptive G-UKT achieves an optimal balance between representational stability and posterior plasticity, ensuring robust generalization under severely limited observation histories. Across all four datasets, Adaptive G-UKT consistently outperforms its most direct probabilistic competitor UKT (e.g., +4.99% AUC on AS2009, +0.17% on AL2005, +0.04% on BD2006, +0.20% on NIPS34), demonstrating that the proposed adaptive graph propagation and Wasserstein attention contribute complementary gains beyond uncertainty modeling alone.

At the same time, we acknowledge that UKT remains highly competitive on several datasets, especially AL2005 and BD2006 where the margins are relatively small. We attribute this phenomenon to the fact that UKT already captures a strong portion of the task benefit through Gaussian uncertainty modeling alone, particularly on fine-grained algebra datasets where local response patterns are informative and the baseline performance is already high. Under such settings, the adaptive graph learner mainly plays a regularizing and structural refinement role, so its gain is naturally more modest than in AS2009, where long interaction sequences allow Wasserstein-based retrieval and graph-enhanced uncertainty propagation to accumulate clearer benefits. Therefore, the comparison with UKT suggests that our contribution should be understood not as replacing uncertainty-aware KT, but as extending it with adaptive structural modeling that yields consistent, though dataset-dependent, improvements.

Ablation study

To rigorously verify the contribution of individual components within Adaptive G-UKT and dissect the interplay between probabilistic modeling and structural learning, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study. We selected two datasets with distinct characteristics—AS2009 (dense, long sequences) and NIPS34 (sparse, diagnostic)—to evaluate robustness across different data regimes. We compared the full framework against four variants: removing the uncertainty-guided regularization (w/o Contrastive Loss), disabling the GNN module (w/o Adaptive Graph), reverting to deterministic point estimates (w/o Gaussian), and retaining Gaussian embeddings but replacing the distance metric with standard dot-product attention (w/o Wasserstein Attn). On the dense AS2009 dataset, characterized by long observation sequences and high interaction density, the distribution-aware retrieval mechanism proves to be the dominant performance factor. The most severe degradation is observed in the w/o Wasserstein Attn variant (AUC 89.15%), confirming that Wasserstein distance is the dominant factor for data-rich sequential tasks. An intriguing observation is that the w/o Gaussian variant achieves unexpectedly high performance on AS2009 (AUC 90.28%). We attribute this to AS2009’s dense and long interaction histories, where a deterministic representation may still capture sufficient sequential patterns without full distributional modeling. This highlights a critical theoretical insight: probabilistic representations must be coupled with a mathematically aligned distance metric. When standard dot-product attention is applied to Gaussian embeddings, the model fails to effectively utilize the learned covariance (uncertainty) during retrieval. Consequently, the variance parameters devolve into redundant noise that interferes with the optimization process rather than serving as validity filters. This metric mismatch—applying Euclidean dot-product similarity on a non-Euclidean distributional manifold—prevents the model from distinguishing between high-confidence state estimates and high-variance stochastic observations, confirming that the 2-Wasserstein kernel is geometrically indispensable for unlocking the full potential of distributional uncertainty modeling in retrieval-heavy sequential inference scenarios.

Conversely, the performance dynamics shift significantly on the NIPS34 dataset, which features shorter and more diagnostic sequences. In this sparse regime, the w/o Gaussian variant suffers the most severe degradation among all variants (AUC 72.09%), which reveals a critical finding: on sparse diagnostic sequences, probabilistic embeddings are indispensable. Without Gaussian distributional representations, the model collapses to a deterministic point encoder paradigm, losing the ability to maintain posterior uncertainty bounds over limited observation histories and failing to distinguish high-confidence state estimates from noisy, aleatoric observations in short sequences. A further notable observation is that the w/o Wasserstein Attn variant also yields substantial degradation (AUC 77.29%, a drop of 3.26%), confirming that precise distribution-aware retrieval remains a critical bottleneck when interaction histories are limited. Furthermore, the w/o Contrastive Loss variant exhibits a sharper performance drop on NIPS34 (AUC drop of 1.42%) compared to AS2009 (AUC drop of 1.19%), validating the hypothesis that uncertainty-guided contrastive regularization is particularly vital for preventing overfitting when training data is limited.

In conclusion, the ablation study reveals that the components of Adaptive G-UKT are not merely additive but synergistic. The Gaussian embedding provides the fundamental uncertainty representation required for robust modeling in sparse contexts (NIPS34), while the Wasserstein attention ensures this information is correctly utilized for precise retrieval in dense contexts (AS2009). Together with the end-to-end differentiable graph topology learner and uncertainty-gated contrastive regularization, these components form a cohesive probabilistic inference framework capable of generalizing across diverse observation regimes ranging from dense to severely sparse sequential data distributions.

To further examine the design choice in Sect. 3.1.3, we additionally evaluate the sensitivity of the dual-channel Gaussian message passing module to HGNN layer depth. As reported in Table 7, a single graph propagation layer yields the best overall trade-off between relational aggregation and representation preservation. As the number of layers increases, AUC gradually decreases on both AS2009 and NIPS34, while NLL also shows no consistent improvement. This trend suggests that stacking deeper Gaussian message-passing layers tends to over-smooth concept representations and over-mix uncertainty information across the learned topology, thereby weakening the discriminability of knowledge states. Therefore, the single-layer design adopted in Adaptive G-UKT is not arbitrary, but is supported by both empirical performance and the need to avoid excessive uncertainty diffusion.

Discussion

The consistent empirical superiority of Adaptive G-UKT across diverse benchmarks provides strong empirical validation for transitioning sequential latent state estimation from deterministic point encoders to full posterior Gaussian distributional representations. By synthesizing our experimental findings, we observe that the framework’s success is not merely attributable to individual components, but rather to the synergistic interplay between relational structural inductive bias and epistemic uncertainty quantification. In sparse observation regimes such as NIPS34, the Adaptive Graph Topology Learner functions as a critical relational regularizer. This aligns with recent findings that explicit end-to-end graph structure learning effectively compensates for observation data scarcity by constraining the hypothesis space via relational inductive biases 40. By recovering latent relational dependencies—visualized as the sparse yet high-confidence edges in Fig. 2a—the model successfully propagates posterior state information from observed nodes to unobserved graph neighbors via message passing, effectively alleviating the cold-start problem under limited observation evidence. Crucially, this structural propagation is regulated by the posterior uncertainty quantification mechanism to prevent noise amplification along low-confidence edges. As evidenced by the modular block-diagonal cluster structure in Fig. 2b, the model successfully identifies latent node communities, through which the 2-Wasserstein attention kernel selectively propagates only robust, low-variance signals as a dynamic covariance-gated filter. This creates a self-correcting equilibrium: the learned graph topology provides the relational pathways for latent state diffusion, while the posterior uncertainty quantification regulates signal traffic, ensuring that only high-confidence distributional representations are propagated across the concept graph.

From a practical system deployment perspective, Adaptive G-UKT offers principled uncertainty quantification signals directly applicable to downstream adaptive inference pipelines. A primary contribution is the introduction of a confidence-aware decision boundary via explicit diagonal covariance estimation. Traditional deterministic encoders exhibit systematic over-confidence by outputting scalar point predictions without uncertainty bounds, leading to unreliable downstream decision-making. By explicitly estimating the posterior covariance , our framework enables downstream systems to distinguish between high-confidence state estimates (, low posterior variance) and unreliable, high-entropy observations (, high aleatoric noise), facilitating principled confidence-thresholded inference strategies. Furthermore, the framework demonstrates significant potential for automated relational graph mining. The modular block-diagonal cluster patterns autonomously recovered from fine-grained datasets (e.g., Fig. 2b) demonstrate that the Adaptive Graph Topology Learner can identify latent node communities and directed dependency hierarchies from observation data alone without structural annotation. Recent studies have validated that such data-driven graph topology mining can significantly reduce reliance on expensive manual graph annotation59, offering a scalable solution for dynamically evolving relational graph structures60. From this broader perspective, Adaptive G-UKT can be viewed as a task-specific instantiation of uncertainty-aware sequential modeling and probabilistic graph representation learning, echoing themes from robust multimodal reasoning and state assessment systems and Bayesian or federated learning frameworks that foreground uncertainty quantification for distributed inference. Relative to these directions, our contribution is to instantiate the same uncertainty-aware modeling principle within KT by jointly coupling adaptive graph discovery, Gaussian posterior state tracking, and Wasserstein-based sequence retrieval in one end-to-end framework.

Despite these contributions, we acknowledge certain limitations that motivate future research directions. Specifically, regarding the interpretability of the learned graph topology , while the adaptive adjacency matrix reveals strong statistical co-occurrence patterns between concept nodes, it does not inherently guarantee causal graph structure in the structural causal model sense. An edge implies statistical dependency between nodes and , but not necessarily a strict directed causal relationship (i.e., that high posterior mean at node causally induces high posterior mean at ). Looking ahead, we envision two promising technical directions. First, to address the cold-start problem under severely sparse observation regimes, future work could integrate pre-trained Large Language Models (LLMs) to provide semantic feature priors for Gaussian embedding initialization, thereby enriching posterior mean initialization with linguistically grounded semantic representations61,62. Second, to address the limitation of purely statistical relational learning, we propose incorporating Causal Discovery algorithms to prune the learned adjacency matrix . By moving beyond statistical co-occurrence to identifiable causal graph structures63, we aim to ensure the recovered topology reflects robust causal dependency paths rather than spurious statistical associations64.

In addition, the present work mainly provides geometric and modeling-level justification for Wasserstein attention, rather than a full formal treatment of convergence or uncertainty calibration guarantees; establishing such stronger theoretical results remains an important direction for future work.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed Adaptive G-UKT, a principled probabilistic inference framework that addresses the fundamental limitations of conventional deterministic point encoders in sequential latent state estimation. By coupling an end-to-end differentiable Adaptive Graph Topology Learner with a closed-form 2-Wasserstein attention kernel, our framework achieves geometric consistency between the similarity metric and the Riemannian manifold of Gaussian distributions, bridging the gap between predictive accuracy and distributional interpretability. We fundamentally shift the sequential modeling paradigm from static deterministic point vectors to dynamic posterior Gaussian distributions , ensuring that epistemic uncertainty—distinguishing high-confidence state estimates from aleatoric noise—is explicitly quantified, propagated, and calibrated throughout the inference pipeline.

Extensive empirical evaluations across diverse benchmarks (AS2009, AL2005, BD2006, NIPS34) demonstrate that Adaptive G-UKT consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across all evaluation metrics. Crucially, our results confirm that explicit posterior uncertainty quantification via diagonal Gaussian covariance acts as a robust distributional regularization mechanism, significantly mitigating performance degradation under noisy observation streams and cold-start sparse regimes. The framework exhibits superior cross-dataset stability and generalization, demonstrating that incorporating end-to-end differentiable relational graph topology learning into probabilistic sequential encoders is essential for robust latent state estimation over sparse, structured observation data.

Beyond predictive performance, Adaptive G-UKT provides a transparent “white-box” perspective on the learned latent relational manifold. Our work demonstrates that the model can autonomously recover the latent relational graph topology and distributional state representations without explicit structural supervision or manual graph annotation, identifying node community structures and directed dependency hierarchies purely from observation streams. This interpretable distributional representation provides downstream systems with trustworthy, granular posterior uncertainty estimates at the node level, paving the way for reliable confidence-aware inference pipelines in adaptive sequential decision-making systems.

Future Work. Building on these findings, future research will explore extending this probabilistic distributional inference framework to multimodal heterogeneous observation streams, integrating textual feature representations from pre-trained language models with behavioral sequential signals via cross-modal Gaussian fusion. Additionally, we plan to couple Adaptive G-UKT with Large Language Models (LLMs) to provide linguistically grounded semantic priors for posterior mean initialization , further enhancing cold-start robustness under sparse observation regimes and facilitating causal graph structure discovery via identifiable structural causal model inference.
