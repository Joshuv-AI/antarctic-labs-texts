Changes in the epidemiology of heart failure in the decade 2014–2023: insights from the AQUORE retrospective cohort study
Authors: Loreto Lancia, Angelo Dante, Valeria Caponnetto, Cristina Petrucci, Vittorio Masotta, Leondino Mammarella, Silvio Romano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHeart failure (HF) is a major public health issue due to its high morbidity, mortality and healthcare burden. This study aimed to provide estimates of HF incidence, survival rates, outcome changes and their predictive factors in a central Italian population over the decade 2014–2023.DesignPopulation-based retrospective cohort study.SettingHospital discharge records from all hospitals in L'Aquila Province of Italy were analysed.ParticipantsAll residents who experienced a HF index hospitalisation between 2014 and 2023 were included. HF index hospitalisation was defined as the first hospitalisation for HF in patients with no prior hospitalisation from the same diagnosis for at least 4 years.Primary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were age-standardised and sex-standardised incidence rates of HF index hospitalisations and all-cause mortality following index hospitalisation. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs), survival and their associated factors were analysed with negative binomial regression models, Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards models, respectively.ResultsA total of 6965 incident cases from 17 588 HF hospitalisations in the decade under study were analysed. The overall standardised incidence rate was 1.73 per 1000 person-years, with significant differences by age and sex. Incidence rates decreased by 17% from 2014–2018 to 2019–2023 (IRR: 0.834; 95% CI 0.743 to 0.936). Cumulative 1-year and 5-year survival were 70.05% and 36.38%, respectively, with a progressively higher mortality risk in older people. Compared with the 2014–2018 cohort, the 2019–2022 cohort showed improved survival at 30 and 90 days and at 1 year overall and by age groups, with <50 exception.ConclusionsHF incidence declined, particularly in older populations, and remained higher in men, while survival rates improved. Despite these trends, HF continues to represent a substantial clinical and public health burden.

Background

Despite significant advancements in disease therapy and prevention, heart failure (HF) remains a major health challenge worldwide, and a leading cause of mortality, recurrent hospitalisation and disability.1 2 Since HF is far more prevalent in older age groups, where it is often integrated into geriatric cardiovascular syndromes with their inherent burden of multimorbidity and frailty, the absolute number of patients living with HF is bound to grow because of ageing of the population, with an amplified personal and societal impact of the disease.3

The incidence of HF in European countries and the USA ranges widely from 1 to 9 cases per 1000 person-years (PYs) and strongly depends on the population studied and the diagnostic criteria used.4 In high-income countries, incidence rates stabilised between 1970 and 1990 and are now thought to be decreasing.4 The decline in HF incidence, particularly in people aged 60–84 years, suggests that HF prevention has improved, probably due to environmental changes, public health measures and improvements in clinical care.5 Despite this decline, the burden of HF remains significant and is similar to that of the four most common causes of cancer combined (breast, prostate, lung and bowel).5

Regarding mortality, very high 5-year mortality rates, up to 75%, have been found in observational studies reporting long-term data on HF patients.6 7 However, more recent nationwide data indicate declining mortality over time despite persistently high hospitalisation burden and suggest that among patients with a new HF diagnosis, considerable progress has been achieved in reducing mortality.8 9

The development of innovative healthcare models that can reduce hospitalisation frequency, improve prognosis and enhance patients’ quality of life requires an accurate understanding of the real dimension of the problem, especially when home-based approaches are not yet fully realised. In this regard, analytical and descriptive epidemiological studies based on precise information about actual populations, conducted in large and homogeneous geographic areas, are essential.

Although observational studies conducted in Italy, including analyses from large administrative databases such as the ARNO Observatory,10 have provided important information on clinical characteristics and hospital outcomes among patients with HF, population-based data on incidence and long-term survival trends across an entire resident population remain limited, reflecting the absence of a national HF registry.

The AQUORE study (Italian study name) addresses this gap by providing contemporary incidence and survival estimates in the complete resident population of a province in central Italy over a 10-year period. Secondary aims include investigating factors associated with these outcomes.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

A retrospective population-based cohort study was conducted from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2023 involving all residents of the province of L’Aquila who underwent an HF index hospitalisation. The province of L’Aquila consists of a populous area in central Italy of approximately 300 000 inhabitants distributed over 5035 km².

This observational study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. A completed STROBE checklist is provided as an online supplemental file.

For the purposes of this study, the index hospitalisation represents the basis for estimating HF incident cases and was defined as the first hospitalisation for HF in patients with no prior hospitalisation for the same diagnosis for at least 4 years. This wash-out period, which extends up to 13 years for patients with index hospitalisation on 31 December 2023, was adopted to reduce the risk of misclassifying prevalent HF cases as incident cases.

HF patients were identified using the primary discharge diagnosis recorded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM), which is the classification system currently used in Italy and was consistently applied throughout the study period.

Therefore, to identify cases with index hospitalisation occurring between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2023 (incident cases), patients hospitalised from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2013 were excluded. Included patients were retrospectively followed for a minimum of 1 year (patients admitted on 31 December 2022) to a maximum of 10 years (patients admitted on 1 January 2014). Patients with index hospitalisation occurring in 2023 were included for analysis of incident cases over time but excluded from survival analysis since they were not followed up for at least 1 year.

Variables, data sources and bias

Each patient’s demographic variables, including age, sex, marital status, length of stay (LOS) during index hospitalisation, were extracted from the hospital discharge administrative records of all public and private hospitals in the province of L’Aquila.

Diagnosis-related groups (DRG) classification was retrieved for descriptive purposes and to contextualise the burden of HF hospitalisations. Vital status was obtained from the national mortality register, and survival was assessed using all-cause mortality.

All data were anonymised prior to analysis.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed on all data. Categorical data were summarised as frequencies (N) and percentages (%), whereas medians and IQRs were used for continuous variables.

Cumulative, age-specific and sex-specific crude and standardised rates were calculated over 10 years (from 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2023). Crude rates reflect the observed burden of disease in the resident population, whereas standardised rates allow comparisons over time and across populations.

Standardised rates were age-adjusted and sex-adjusted to the 2013 European Standard Population.11 Using nine narrow and four broad age groups, crude and standardised rates were expressed per 1000 PYs and calculated by dividing the incident cases by the average resident populations of males and females computed on 1 January and 31 December of each year. A negative binomial regression model was used to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 95% CIs.

Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify variables significantly associated with survival, with their predictive power expressed as HRs and 95% CIs. The multivariable model included age, sex, marital status, year of index hospitalisation and LOS.

All data were analysed using IBM SPSS V.27.0 (IBM).

Results

Baseline characteristics

During the study period (2014–2023), a total of 6965 incident cases of HF were identified based on index hospitalisation and grouped into annual cohorts. These cases were ascertained from a total of 17 588 HF hospitalisations among resident citizens of the entire province of L’Aquila, after excluding hospitalisations occurring after the index events (duplicates) and hospitalisations that occurred between 2010 and 2013 (figure 1). Of these 6965 cases, 6461 (1 January 2014–31 December 2022) were followed from 1 month up to 9 years after the index event (median follow-up: 6.1 years) to perform survival analysis (figure 1).

The median age at index was 82 years (IQR 13) and higher in females than in males (84 vs 79 years, respectively). Over the 10-year study period, the sex and age distribution remained stable: overall, females represented 52.4% of HF index hospitalisations, while individuals aged over 70 years accounted for 84.5% of all cases. The most common diagnoses included unspecified congestive HF (428.0) (29.0%), left HF (428.1) (21.5%), unspecified HF (428.9) (11.5%) and benign hypertensive heart disease with HF (402.11) (13.2%). The median LOS was 7 days (table 1; detailed data in online supplemental table S1).

The overall crude and standardised incidence rates of index hospitalisations were 2.35 and 1.73 cases per 1000 PYs, respectively. In the multivariable regression model, significant differences were observed according to age, sex and cohort: the incidence rate for individuals aged 80–84 years was more than three times higher than that of those aged 70–74 years (IRR: 3.39; 95% CI 2.02 to 5.70) and more than 200 times higher than that of individuals under 50 years of age (IRR: 212.12; 95% CI 156.90 to 286.76); males had a 67% higher incidence rate than females (IRR: 1.67; 95% CI 1.48 to 1.88; figure 2A). Overall, incidence rates of index hospitalisations showed a declining trend from 2014 to 2023 (figure 2B), with no differences observed between sexes. The multivariable analysis confirmed a significant reduction in incidence by approximately 17% in the period 2019–2023 compared with 2014–2018 (IRR: 0.834; 95% CI 0.743 to 0.936). Adults aged 70 years and older drove this reduction (figure 2C), showing a difference between the 2019–2023 and 2014–2018 periods ranging from 36% in the 70–74 age group (IRR: 0.636; 95% CI 0.485 to 0.835) to 45% in the 90+ age group (IRR: 0.549; 95% CI 0.379 to 0.793). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the two periods in the younger age groups, with the standardised rates in the second period higher than in the first among individuals under 60 years (figure 2C).

Discussion

Main findings

In this population-based retrospective cohort study, we observed a significant decline over time in the incidence of index hospitalisations for HF, together with an overall improvement in survival, particularly among older individuals. These findings provide contemporary population-level evidence on temporal trends in HF burden and outcomes in an Italian province.

The advanced age of the study population (median 82 years) reflects the real-world epidemiology of HF in Italy and much of Europe, where patients requiring hospitalisation are predominantly elderly. Consequently, our incidence and survival estimates mainly apply to older patients and should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to younger populations or to individuals managed exclusively in outpatient care.

Measuring the burden of index hospitalisations for HF at a regional level can be a highly effective strategy for estimating disease incidence, especially in countries like Italy where community-based care has not yet been fully developed,12 the healthcare system is predominantly hospital-based, and there are no national registries since healthcare is managed at the regional level.13 In Italy, hospitalisations classified under DRG 127 (Heart Failure & Shock) represent the fourth leading cause of hospitalisation and the third leading cause of hospitalisation due to disease.14

As incidence in this study is operationally defined based on index hospitalisations, the reported rates primarily reflect trends in first HF admissions. Given the recurrent nature of HF, these estimates do not fully capture the overall burden of repeated hospitalisations and may also be influenced by changes in hospitalisation practices over time.

In our cohort, DRG 127 accounted for 84.3% of index hospitalisations identified using ICD-9-CM HF diagnosis codes (table 1), confirming that reliance on administrative DRG classification alone would underestimate the true burden of HF. Accordingly, HF was operationally defined using primary discharge diagnosis codes, capturing clinically overt HF requiring hospitalisation irrespective of underlying phenotype, an approach widely recognised as valid for HF case ascertainment.15

An accurate understanding of the epidemiological magnitude of HF, which is widely recognised as a priority public health issue in the Western world,16 is essential for implementing effective measures to address the needs of those affected, including patients and caregivers.

Interpretation of incidence trends

Similarly to other studies conducted at a regional level in Italy and Europe,17 18 the data from this study highlight an age-related increase in the incidence of index hospitalisations for both sexes, ranging from 0.07 to 22.89 per 1000 PYs for crude rates and from 0.03 to 0.35 per 1000 PYs for standardised rates, with higher values observed in males compared with females across all age groups.

In line with these overall findings, a more detailed analysis showed a decline over time in the incidence of index hospitalisations for HF.

In the last 5 years of the study period, compared with the first 5 years, this reduction was notably significant (−17%) and may reflect, at least in part, improvements in the management of conditions that increase the risk of progression to ventricular dysfunction and the broader implementation of evidence-based therapies in accordance with recent guidelines,19 20 although causal inferences cannot be drawn in the absence of individual-level treatment data.

However, this temporal decline should be interpreted with caution, as it may also partly reflect changes in admission thresholds and care pathways over time, in addition to true reductions in disease occurrence.

Our data are consistent with the decreasing trend in incidence rates over time observed in other European studies for older patients, but not for younger ones.5 21 22 Perhaps due to the inclusion criteria applied and the temporal asymmetry of the similar studies, our results show lower standardised incidence rates compared with other Italian and European studies.5 17 18

Comparisons with regions outside Europe further contextualise our findings. Recent US data23 report higher incidence and lower long-term survival compared with European cohorts, while global burden analyses indicate heterogeneous patterns across Asian regions.24 These differences highlight the influence of population structure and healthcare systems on HF epidemiology.

Survival trends and prognostic factors

Regarding survival, the results indicate a progressive decline over longer follow-up periods, particularly among older individuals.

Survival estimates in our cohort (70.1% at 1 year; 36.4% at 5 years; 17.7% at 9 years) are comparable with European data. In a large UK population-based study,25 overall survival after an HF diagnosis was 75.9% at 1 year, 45.5% at 5 years and 24.5% at 10 years; notably, among patients requiring hospital admission around diagnosis, survival was 68.8%, 36.7% and 17.8%, respectively.

Similar studies conducted at a regional level in Italy found a slightly higher 1-year survival rate,26 likely because they considered the start of observation from discharge rather than admission. As early mortality frequently occurs during the index hospitalisation, this methodological difference should be taken into account when interpreting survival estimates and comparing results across studies.

Comparisons across settings are further complicated by differences in case identification and diagnostic latency, which may vary according to healthcare system organisation.27

The study also revealed a significant percentage of deaths within the first 30 days of the index hospitalisation (11.6%), primarily due to deaths occurring during the index hospitalisation itself. Although there was a decline in deaths over the study period, these findings confirm the vulnerability of HF patients during the first 30 days, as previously observed in similar studies conducted in Italy.17 28 A trend of improving survival was also observed, confirming findings reported by other authors.8 27 However, as with incidence, this improvement did not extend to younger individuals in this study.

Regarding 5-year mortality, the multivariable model analysis outlines a risk profile that highlights not only clinical factors—such as LOS during the index hospitalisation (>7 days)—but also demographic factors, including age and sex.

Prolonged LOS is unlikely to be causative per se but rather reflects greater clinical severity and patient complexity and should be interpreted as a marker of vulnerability associated with poorer long-term outcomes.

The probability of surviving this 5-year period was up to nine times lower in patients aged ≥85 years compared with those aged ≤50 years. Male sex was independently associated with reduced 5-year survival (HR 1.24; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.36). Additionally, social factors play a role, with living alone emerging as a negative prognostic factor for survival. For this reason, older and frail patients generally deserve more care and attention, especially in the first months after discharge. In this context, technology-based strategies, such as telemonitoring, have proven to be effective in reducing both mortality and hospital readmissions in patients with HF.29

Limitations and implications

This study has limitations that should be considered. The retrospective design and the inclusion of a single-province population may further limit the generalisability of these findings to other healthcare settings or regions.

A further important limitation is the undercapture of incident HF cases that did not result in hospitalisation. Consequently, our incidence estimates reflect only clinically overt HF requiring admission and are likely to underestimate the true population burden.

However, the minimum 4-year look-back period may not have been sufficient to fully exclude patients with a prior HF diagnosis, potentially leading to some overestimation of index hospitalisations in the early years of the study.

This potential early overestimation should therefore be interpreted cautiously alongside the broader underestimation related to the exclusion of non-hospitalised incident cases.

Moreover, the use of administrative health records lacking detailed clinical information precluded stratification by HF subtype (HFrEF, HFpEF or other phenotypes), limiting the ability to explore differential epidemiological trends and underlying mechanisms across HF categories.

Regarding the observed association between social factors and survival, despite the use of multivariable Cox regression models, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly due to unmeasured comorbidities and socioeconomic factors not captured in administrative data. In addition, a relatively high proportion of patients had undeclared marital status, which may have influenced the assessment of associations between social factors and survival.

Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced hospitalisation patterns and short-term outcomes during part of the study period, and this potential impact should be considered when interpreting temporal trends.

To obtain a clearer understanding of the phenomenon, these data should be integrated with other studies analysing comorbidities and clinical conditions of patients.

Despite these limitations, our findings confirm a decreasing trend over time in HF incidence, particularly among older individuals, together with an overall improvement in survival.

Incidence was higher in older age groups and in men, mortality was strongly driven by age, with male sex independently associated with reduced 5-year survival. These results also highlight that the clinical and social burden of HF remains highly significant, especially in an ageing population.
