Sirolimus alleviated intractable diarrhea of IPEX syndrome: a case report and literature review
Authors: Lin Ye, Xue Song, Yun Cui, Shengnan Wu, Yizhong Wang, Ting Zhang, Wenhao Weng, Ting Ge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked (IPEX) syndrome is a rare single-gene X-linked immunodeficiency disease caused by mutations in the forkhead box protein 3 (FOXP3) gene. The typical clinical manifestations of IPEX mainly include severe atopic dermatitis, insulin-dependent type 1 diabetes mellitus, and intractable diarrhea.Case presentationHere, we report a boy with intractable diarrhea diagnosed with early-onset IPEX syndrome due to the c.434C > T (p.Ala145Val) mutation in exon 4 of the FOXP3 gene. The patient experienced intractable diarrhea and severe weight loss, and his clinical symptoms could not be alleviated by conventional supportive and anti-infection treatment. Sirolimus, an immunosuppressant, preferentially inhibits effector T cells while allowing the proliferation of Tregs and is used to treat IPEX patients and alleviate intractable diarrhea.ConclusionWe reviewed the literature on the use of sirolimus for the treatment of IPEX syndrome over the past two decades.

Background

Immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, and X-linked (IPEX) syndrome is a rare single-gene X-linked immunodeficiency disease affecting less than one in a million newborns. IPEX syndrome was first reported by Powell et al. in 1982 and is identified by mutations in the forkhead box protein 3 (FOXP3) gene [1, 2]. The FOXP3 gene is located on the human X chromosome (Xq11.3–q13.3) and is a key transcription factor for CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs)2. FOXP3 plays an important role in the functional stability and immunoplasticity of Tregs, and functional mutations in FOXP3 result in defects in Treg cell development, which impairs immune homeostasis and causes autoimmune disorders [3].

IPEX syndrome is a rare and generally fatal genetic disorder in which untreated affected individuals with typical clinical manifestations usually die within the first 2 years of life. The typical clinical manifestations of IPEX mainly include early onset (< 1 year old), severe atopic dermatitis, insulin-dependent type 1 diabetes mellitus, and intractable diarrhea [4]. Currently, the main treatments for IPEX syndrome include immunosuppressive drugs (IS) and bone hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), as well as nutrition-related supportive treatments [5, 6]. However, some patients are not sensitive to conventional immunosuppressive drugs, resulting in prolonged illness and reduced quality of life.

Sirolimus, a macrocyclic antibiotic produced by the actinomycete Streptomyces hygroscopicus, targets and inhibits a serine-threonine kinase called mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) [7]. By blocking mTOR, sirolimus potently arrestes lymphocytes in the late G1 or S phase [7]. Therefore, sirolimus has been used extensively for the prevention of rejection after renal transplants. Given that sirolimus selectively inhibits effector T cells while allowing the proliferation of Tregs, sirolimus has been used to treat IPEX patients and has achieved good therapeutic effects in previous reports [8–10].

Here, we report a patient with early-onset IPEX syndrome in China with a mutation, c.434C > T (p.Ala145Val), in exon 4 of the FOXP3 gene; this patient was diagnosed by genetic testing and treated with sirolimus to alleviate severe diarrhea. Moreover, we reviewed the literature on the use of sirolimus for the treatment of IPEX.

Case presentation

A one-month-and-fifteen-day-old baby boy with a history of intractable diarrhea was referred to our hospital. The characteristics of the diarrhea are loose, yellow–green, nonbloody, and ten times per day according to the Bristol Stool Scale VII [11]. The boy is the only male child that was born at term to unrelated Chinese parents. Before admission to our hospital, the patient experienced aggravated watery diarrhea for 22 days, 10 ~ 15 times daily, 4 days of fever and weight loss. The patient was treated with dexamethasone, piperacillin sodium and tazobactam sodium for intestinal infection. Four days later, the patient's temperature gradually stabilized, but the symptoms of diarrhea did not improve, so they were admitted to our hospital for further treatment. Laboratory tests suggested increased fecal calprotecin, normal IgA and IgM levels, increased IgE (78.80 IU/ml) and decreased IgG (1.47 g/L), complement C1q (73.57 mg/L) and C3 (0.51 mg/L) levels. Lymphocyte subset analysis revealed reduced proportions of CD3+ (40.78, reference range: 61.04–71.82%) and CD8+ T cells (6.29, reference range: 20.36–32.78%), incremental proportions of CD19+ B cells (41.97, reference range: 14.35–22.65%) and CD4+/CD8+ T cells (5.34, reference range: 0.94–1.86%). In addition, elevated levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 (51.26, reference range: ≤ 5.4 pg/mL), IL-8 (160.61, reference range: ≤ 20.6 pg/mL) and IL-17 (50.42, reference range: ≤ 21.4 pg/mL) were observed. The results for several autoimmune disease antibodies, including antinuclear antibodies and other antithyroglobulin and rheumatic immune-related antibodies, were negative (Tables 1 and 2). To relieve diarrhea and enhance nutritional status, the patient started receiving intravenous nutrition therapy. Red cell suspension and frozen plasma were given due to severe anaemia. Considering the possibility of intestinal inflammation (calprotectin = 87.6 µg/g) causing bloody stool, the patient was given anti-infection treatment. However, the patient still had up to 20 episodes of diarrhea per day, with a diarrhea volume of 252 ml/kg body weight. The stool was watery, bloody and mucus-containing. Finally, the baby boy was diagnosed with IPEX syndrome due to a c.434C > T (p.Ala145Val) hemizygous mutation in exon 4 of the FOXP3 gene through DNA Sanger sequencing, and the patient’s mother was a healthy carrier (Fig. 1). After the genetic diagnosis of IPEX, methylprednisolone (1 mg/kg per day) was given to the patient for anti-inflammatory treatment, and the immunosuppressor sirolimus (2 mg/m2 per day) was administered to regulate Treg cells. According to previous experience in patients with IPEX syndrome, the plasma level of sirolimus was maintained between 8 and 12 ng/mL [9, 10, 12–24] (Table 3). The patient was gradually weaned off methylprednisolone, and enteral and parenteral nutrition remained the primary supportive therapy. After 10 days on the regimen, the patient had significantly decreased stool volume and diarrhea (3 times per day) and started to gain weight. The stool was characterized as yellow‒green and mushy, with a small amount of mucus and no blood. After one month of sirolimus treatment, an increase in the number of double-negative T cells (DNTs) (CD3+/TCRαβ+//CD4−/CD8−) (3.23, reference range: 0.33–1.12%), CD4 central memory (CM) (CD3+/CD4+/CD45RA−/CD27+) (34.60, reference range: 10.11–28.2%) and CD4 effector memory cells (EMs) (CD3+CD4+/CD45RA−/CD27−) (3.00, reference range: 0.28–2.10%) and a decrease in the number of γδT (CD3+/TCRγδ+) (1.88, reference range: 3.32–7.40%) and naïve CD4 (CD3+/CD4+/CD45RA+/CD27+) (62.40, reference range: 69.15–88.10%) immune cells were detected. Discussion and conclusions

The main clinical manifestations of classical IPEX syndrome caused by mutations in FOXP3 include type I diabetes mellitus (TIDM), early-onset intractable diarrhea and eczema. Herein, we report a mutation site, c.434C > T, in exon 4 of the FOXP3 gene leading to IPEX syndrome characterized by refractory diarrhea onset in the neonatal period. The patient was admitted to the hospital due to recurrent watery diarrhea that could not be alleviated by conventional supportive and anti-infection therapy. Finally, the patient was diagnosed with IPEX syndrome based on whole exome sequencing (WES) and treated with sirolimus to alleviate severe diarrhea.

IPEX is a rare, potentially fatal immunodeficiency disease caused by mutations in the FOXP3 gene, which encodes a critical forkhead transcription factor essential for the maintenance of regulatory T (Treg) cells. The FOXP3 gene plays a significant role in the maintenance of self-tolerance, and Treg dysfunction could lead to multiorgan autoimmunity disorders in IPEX. The expression of FOXP3 varied in IPEX patients depending on the type of mutation, with low or no expression related to in-frame and frameshift deletion mutations, low expression in polyadenylation sites and splicing mutations, and diverse expression in patients with missense mutations. As IPEX awareness and characterization have increased, so has the identification of FOXP3 mutations, with at least 80 cases to date [25]. Thus, while FOXP3 is a unified gene, IPEX is a complicated and variable clinical continuum of disorders. In addition to the traditional clinical manifestations (i.e., severe enteropathic presentation, endocrinopathy, and skin manifestations), IPEX may encompass other distinct and diverse clinical presentations, such as hematologic abnormalities, infections, other immune-related complications, and renal involvement [25].

FOXP3 gene sequencing is crucial for confirming the diagnosis of IPEX, but it has demonstrated limited genotype‒phenotype correlation. The fact that identical mutations in different individuals may lead to different clinical manifestations indicates the presence of genetic modification factors and epigenetic and environmental variables, which together contribute to the onset and severity of the disease. For example, a case reported by Hou demonstrated that the main clinical manifestation of a mutation in exon 11 of the FOXP3 gene (c.1190G > A, p.R397Q) in a 22-day-old patient was hyperglycaemia and hypothyroidism [26]. However, the same mutation site was found in a 1-month-old infant who exhibited intractable diarrhea and chronic dermatitis with an eczematous and ichthyosiform appearance [27]. In addition, a 6-year-old boy with the same mutation had intractable diarrhea, abdominal pain, recurrent infections, and a poor quality of life [28]. The mutation site identified in our patient has been reported as a missense mutation by the University of Chicago and deposited in ClinVar in 2014 (NM_014009.4(FOXP3): c.434C > T (p. Ala145Val)), but no detailed case reports have been found. Therefore, a great deal of research is required to assist in devising efficient diagnostic and treatment strategies.

The clinical manifestation of IPEX is a direct result of an overactive immune system characterized by the generation of autoaggressive T and autoantibody–producing B cells. The inhibitory effect of FOXP3 may be achieved by competitively binding active T-cell nuclear factors to specific DNA-binding sites in the promoters of key immune regulatory genes (such as IL-2), thereby inhibiting activated T-cell activity [29]. To control the unregulated activity of effector T cells in this disorder, therapeutic drugs that inhibit T-cell function are recommended. Sirolimus, also known as rapamycin, is an inhibitor of mTOR. The phosphorylation induced by mTOR is a crucial step in promoting the production of mRNA transcripts into proteins necessary for the cell cycle process. Therefore, by blocking mTOR, sirolimus blocks lymphocyte proliferation in the late G1 or S-phase [30]. Sirolimus was approved in 2004 to prevent acute rejection of organ transplants. Since then, sirolimus has been increasingly used in many clinical conditions owing to its immunosuppressive and antiproliferative properties [16]. Autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS) is a disease characterized by defects in lymphocyte apoptosis. Sirolimus has been shown to induce lymphocyte apoptosis and reduce the lymphocyte survival rate, thereby improving the disease phenotype of ALPS [31]. In addition, off-label use of sirolimus in an Italian paediatric centre has shown that sirolimus can effectively alleviate various autoimmune diseases, such as ALPS, multicentric Castleman disease (mCD), activated PI3K delta kinase syndrome (APDS), and IPEX [16]. In 2005, sirolimus was first reported to be used successfully in a family of patients with IPEX syndrome, and the patients achieved long-term and sustained remission of clinical symptoms [9]. In addition, a 2008 report suggested that sirolimus is a clinically effective and safe therapeutic option for IPEX and IPEX-like patients. Sirolimus can effectively control the clinical gastrointestinal manifestations of IPEX patients, leading to decreased diarrhea, improved villous architecture and decreased inflammatory infiltration [10]. A recent study revealed that reduced T-cell inflammatory infiltration and increased regulatory-like T-cell infiltration in the gut were related to tissue healing and clinical improvement during rapamycin therapy [22].

In our case, we reported a mutation, c.434C > T, in exon 4 of the FOXP3 gene in an IPEX patient who had fatal watery diarrhea, which we tried to treat with sirolimus. The patient's diarrhea was well controlled, and the patient was gradually weaned off methylprednisolone. This study provides a good reference for clinicians in the treatment of IPEX. The parents of the patient were grateful that sirolimus effectively relieved refractory diarrhea and controlled the symptoms of infection.
