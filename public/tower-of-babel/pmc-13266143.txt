Spontaneous Hemorrhagic Rupture of a Pancreatic Mucinous Cystic Neoplasm: A Case Report and Literature Review of Cystic Pancreatic Lesions Presenting with Bleeding or Rupture
Authors: Ji Yeong Kim, Byoung Je Kim, Mu Sook Lee, Jung Hee Hong, Jae Hyuck Yi, Mi Jeong Kim, Hye Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCNs) are rare cystic tumors of the pancreas that account for approximately 1% of all pancreatic neoplasms. Because their thick fibrous capsule provides structural integrity and resistance to external stress, spontaneous rupture of MCNs is extremely rare. Herein, we report the case of a 46-year-old woman who presented to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain. CT and MRI initially suggested spontaneous rupture of a solid pseudopapillary tumor of the pancreas. The patient underwent distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy and partial gastrectomy, and the final pathological diagnosis confirmed a spontaneous rupture of an MCN. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of evaluating cystic pancreatic lesions with hemorrhagic complications. In addition, we reviewed the imaging and differentiating features of ruptured pancreatic cystic lesions to improve diagnostic accuracy.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic cystic lesions encompass a wide spectrum of entities ranging from benign inflammatory collections to cystic neoplasms with malignant potential. Hemorrhagic rupture of these lesions is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening event that can mimic other acute abdominal emergencies. Various cystic entities, such as pseudocysts, mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCNs), intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs), solid pseudopapillary tumors (SPTs), and serous cystic neoplasms (SCNs) may present with intra- or peri-lesional hemorrhage followed by rupture. As imaging findings often overlap between these entities, careful radiological and clinical correlations are essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. Herein, we describe a rare case of the spontaneous rupture of an MCN that presented as an acute abdomen, highlighting its clinical rarity and the importance of radiological differential diagnosis.

CASE REPORT

A 46-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset left back pain. The patient had a history of diabetes, but no history of pancreatitis, alcohol abuse, or trauma. Physical examination revealed mild tenderness of the left upper abdomen and above the left costovertebral angle. Laboratory tests revealed mildly elevated serum amylase and lipase levels (155 U/L and 260 U/L, respectively). All other laboratory findings, including serum carcinoembryonic antigen (1.6 ng/mL) and CA19-9 (9.3 U/mL), were within normal ranges.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a 64 × 40 mm cystic lesion in the pancreatic tail, containing an internal hemorrhage and associated hemoperitoneum. No enhancing solid components or calcifications were observed (Fig. 1A). MRI was performed. The lesion measured approximately 64 mm, showed no evidence of communication with the pancreatic duct, and was accompanied by internal septation. Contrast-enhanced and diffusion-weighted MRI revealed no evidence of contrast enhancement or restricted diffusion within the lesion (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the solid components of the mass were not prominent even in the subtraction images.

Based on these imaging findings, the initial consideration was spontaneous rupture of a SPT of the pancreas, while the possibility of rupture of an MCN or SCN of the pancreas should also be considered in the differential diagnosis. Considering the patient’s stable hemodynamic condition and mild symptoms, an elective surgery was planned. Ten days later, a distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy was performed. Intraoperatively, dense adhesions to the gastric fundus were noted, prompting a partial gastrectomy; however, preoperative imaging showed no evidence of gastric invasion. Mucin was not detected in the peritoneal cavity. Gross examination revealed a multiloculated cyst with a fibrotic wall containing hemorrhagic material without any prominent solid nodules (Fig. 1C). Microscopically, the cystic epithelium consisted of cuboidal mucinous cells without high-grade atypia underlain by ovarian-type stroma. Immunohistochemistry revealed MUC5AC expression in the epithelium and CD10 expression in the stromal components (Fig. 1D). These findings were consistent with those of a pancreatic MCN with low-grade dysplasia. No invasion of the surrounding pancreatic parenchyma was observed. Examination of the resected gastric wall revealed acute suppurative inflammation without any evidence of tumor invasion.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery, remained well at the 24-month follow-up without evidence of recurrence, and is under regular monitoring.

DISCUSSION

Hemorrhagic rupture of pancreatic cystic lesions is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening event that can mimic other acute abdominal emergencies. Various cystic entities, including pseudocysts, MCNs, IPMNs, SPTs, and rarely, SCNs, may present with intra- or peri-lesional hemorrhage followed by rupture. Because imaging findings often overlap, careful differentiation of these entities based on radiological and clinical findings is essential for appropriate management and surgical planning.

The mechanism of hemorrhagic rupture varies depending on the type of pancreatic cystic lesion. SPTs have a natural tendency for intratumoral hemorrhage because of their fragile vasculature and degenerative changes, and the rapid expansion caused by internal bleeding and increased intratumoral pressure can eventually lead to capsular rupture (1). MCNs are typically surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule that provides structural resistance; however, progressive enlargement with increasing intracystic pressure or localized infection may weaken the capsule, resulting in rupture (2). In IPMNs, rupture can occur because of ductal hypertension and mucin hypersecretion, sometimes accompanied by intraductal hemorrhage (3). SCNs rarely bleed; however, in large lesions, rupture may result from disruption of the highly vascular fibrous septa within the tumor (4). The rupture of pancreatic pseudocysts generally occurs secondary to enzymatic autodigestion or erosion of the adjacent vessels in pancreatitis (5).

Radiologically, the differentiation of ruptured pancreatic cystic lesions requires careful evaluation of cyst wall integrity, internal architecture, relationship with the pancreatic duct, and characteristics of the associated peripancreatic fluid or hemorrhage. An MCN typically appears as a well-encapsulated cystic mass that may be unilocular or multiloculated, with or without internal septations, and is usually located in the pancreatic body or tail without communication with the main pancreatic duct. When rupture occurs, which is an infrequent complication, imaging features may include perilesional fluid collection, ascites, or occasionally localized peritonitis, reflecting leakage of cystic contents into the peritoneal cavity following focal wall disruption (267). Hemorrhagic rupture of MCNs is even rarer and has only been described in a few reported cases (28). Because hemorrhagic cystic lesions are more commonly associated with SPT, the presence of hemorrhage in MCN can lead to diagnostic confusion. Therefore, the correlation between patient demographics and the absence of a solid component is critical for the distinction. Ruptured SPTs generally occur in young women and demonstrate mixed solid and cystic components, heterogeneous enhancement, and capsular disruption; most reported cases are accompanied by hemoperitoneal or retroperitoneal bleeding, reflecting their intrinsic tendency for intratumoral hemorrhage (9). Ruptured IPMNs often show ductal dilatation and continuity with the main pancreatic duct, sometimes with peripancreatic fluid representing mucin leakage into the peritoneal cavity, which may lead to pseudomyxoma peritonei. However, hemoperitoneum has been documented in only a single reported case, emphasizing its rarity (3). SCN rupture is exceedingly rare and typically occurs in large microcystic lesions, where bleeding arises from the hypervascular septa rather than from true capsular tearing (4). In contrast, pseudocyst rupture usually develops in the context of pancreatitis and demonstrates focal discontinuity of the fibrous wall with adjacent inflammatory changes and peripancreatic fluid (510). In the present case, a well-margined cystic lesion with hemorrhagic content and hemoperitoneum, without definite wall enhancement or enhancing nodules, was observed in the pancreatic tail of a middle-aged woman without pancreatitis. The imaging findings initially raised the possibility of SPT because of the presence of intracystic hemorrhage and peritoneal bleeding; however, the absence of a solid component and the overall cystic morphology were more compatible with a ruptured MCN.

Recognition of ruptured MCN is clinically important because, unlike benign cysts such as pseudocysts or SCNs, MCNs have malignant potential, and rupture can potentially lead to peritoneal dissemination. Although MCN rupture has occasionally been reported to be associated with malignant transformation, our case demonstrates that rupture can also occur in MCNs with low-grade dysplasia. Surgical excision remains the treatment of choice, and long-term postoperative imaging follow-up for at least 5 years is recommended to assess peritoneal recurrence in the form of ascites or development of nodularity (8). Similarly, ruptured IPMNs can present with peripancreatic fluid, representing mucin spillage into the peritoneal cavity, which may result in pseudomyxoma peritonei and warrants long-term follow-up (3). The rupture of SPTs, although rare, requires complete surgical resection and generally has an excellent prognosis (9). Pseudocyst ruptures can often be managed conservatively without the need for surgery. Recognizing these subtle imaging differences among ruptured pancreatic cystic lesions is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and guide timely and appropriate treatment.

In conclusion, although rare, hemorrhagic rupture of MCNs should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute abdomen with hemoperitoneum, particularly in middle-aged women with cystic pancreatic lesions. This case illustrates that even cystic neoplasms with thick fibrous capsules such as MCNs can rupture and mimic other hemorrhagic pancreatic lesions. Careful preoperative evaluation is essential for an accurate diagnosis and appropriate surgical management, and vigilant long-term follow-up after resection is recommended to detect potential recurrence or peritoneal dissemination.
