Abstract
Inspired by the embodied intelligence of biological creatures like the octopus, the soft robotic arm utilizes its highly flexible structure to perform various tasks in the complex environment. While the classic Cosserat rod theory investigates the bending, twisting, shearing, and stretching of the soft arm, it fails to capture the in-plane deformation that occurs during certain tasks, particularly those involving active lateral traction. This paper introduces an extended Cosserat rod theory addressing these limitations by incorporating an extra strain variable reflecting the in-plane inflation ratio. To accurately describe the viscoelasticity effect of the soft body in dynamics, the proposed model enhances the constitutive law by integrating the Saint-Venant Kirchhoff hyperelastic and Kelvin-Voigt viscous models. The active and environmental loads are accounted for the equations of motion, which are numerically solved by adapting the Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) approach to balance the accuracy and computational efficiency. Our contributions include the derivation of the extended Cosserat rod theory in dynamic context, and the development of a reduced-order numerical method that enables rapid and precise solutions. We demonstrate applications of the model in stiffness tuning of a soft robotic arm and the study of complex octopus’ arm motions. Keywords: Cosserat rod, Geometric Variable Strain, Soft Robotics, Embodied Intelligence

Introduction

Soft robotics are largely inspired by the
embodied intelligent
of biological creatures
[
1
,
2
]
: the physical body with compliant, elastic internal structure interacts continuously with the complex environment under the commands of nerve system. An example of embodied intelligence is the muscular hydrostats, a biological structure of muscle tissues with nearly infinite degrees of freedom, allowing the animal’s body to perform a wide range of motions like bending, shortening, elongating, reaching, and stiffness tuning
[
3
,
4
]
. A typical type of soft robot inspired by this principle takes the form of a slender beam, which includes designs such as the snake-like robot
[
5
]
, octupus-like manipulator
[
6

7
]
, and softworm
[

]
. Compared with the rigid robots, which are of closed-form in finite joint space
[
9
]
, the inherent dexterity and continuity of soft robots makes the governing partial differential equations (PDEs) highly non-linear and subjected to infinite degrees of freedom (DoFs), and often precludes analytical solutions
[
10
]
. Therefore, to describe the motion and deformation, and to exploit its embodied intelligence, a mathematical model that balances accuracy and numerical feasibility is required for further study of the robot
[
11
,
12
]
.

The most straightforward approach to model the dynamics of soft robots is through continuum 3D solid mechanics, which fully captures the complexity of soft materials
[
1
]
. This model, which relies on the 3D FEM approach, can accurately simulates deformations in soft bodies, regardless of their initial shape or material properties. However, the large DoFs within the soft-body makes it expensive computationally and unsuitable for real-time simulation and control. The reduced-order modeling works
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
evolving from the rigid robot dynamics address this problem by a specific geometry approximation on the multi segments of the soft link. Such an assumption is valid only if the soft body is constrained to some particular strains
[
10
]
, and thus, the geometrical model fails when there are complicated external loads applied to the soft link.

The demands of no restrictions imposed on displacement or rotation lead to the adoption of
Cosserat rod model
[
20
]
. The classic Cosserat theory models a soft-slender beam as a one-dimensional structure consisting of an infinite sequence of infinitesimal rigid cross-sections, which enables the beam to show deformations with all six degrees of freedom— stretching, shearing, bending, and twisting at the cross-sectional level. Previous study
[
21
]
and
[
22
]

apply the classic Cosserat theory to the soft robots subjected to complicated environment loads and thread-like actuation loads in statics and dynamics, respectively. In terms of the numerical solutions, the research
[
23
,
24
]
predicts the time derivatives of the system’s state variables based on the historic values of state variables and updates the rod’s position and orientation along the length at each time step. On the other hand, the
Geometric Variable Strain
(GVS) approach
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
projects the strain variables onto a finite joint space via a set of strain basis functions, and convert the original PDEs to a minimal set of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) in the form of Lagrangian mechanics. While previous works balance accuracy and computational cost, they do not fully capture the complexity of the soft body due to the rigid cross-sections assumption, where only deformations between consecutive infinitesimal cross-sections are considered, and thus the analysis of actuation loads are only limited to the axial or oblique actuators. If there are transversal actuators contracting the cross-section to stretch the soft rod-like
[
28
]
, or the soft rod itself is a hollow tube, the classic Cosserat theory fails to remain geometrically exact.
We also notice that transversal actuation is widely employed in a wide range of motions in creatures. For example, an octopus arm achieves elongation solely through the contraction of its transversal muscles and performs reaching motion by simultaneously releasing its oral longitudinal muscle while contracting the aboral and transversal ones
[
29
]
.

Some studies have already been conducted to extend the Cosserat theory to address the limitations cited above. For example, in
[
30
,
31
]
, radial contraction is modeled as an external force along the rod’s length. This method, while straightforward, does not provide a clear physical interpretation for this conversion and fails to reflect the cross-sectional deformation in computation. In contrast, research in
[
32
,
33
]
extend the classic Cosserat theory in
static
contexts by introducing in-plane deformation strain variables and developing a new constitutive law derived from hyperelastic material models. In computer graphics, a study by Angles
[
34
]
applies the extended Cosserat theory to simulate the dynamics of musculoskeletal structures. For computational convenience, they simplify the constitutive law to a quadratic function of strain variables. While this simplification is practical for computer graphic simulation, it is important to note that it deviates from the physical laws that govern real-world soft materials.

In this paper, we hope to create a model that is both efficient and robust enough for practical soft robots applications without sacrificing the detailed accuracy. In this work, for the first time, we derive an extended Cosserat theory in
dynamics
that
captures not only the deformation between infinitesimal cross-sections but also the in-plane inflation. This is done by introducing an extra strain variable reflecting the changes in magnitude of the body-fix director. To accurately describe the viscoelastic effect of the soft body in dynamics, we enhance the constitutive law by integrating the Saint-Venant Kirhhoff hyperelastic model and Kelvin-Voigt viscous model. We analyze all the external and internal loads applied to the soft link, including the environment loads, the longitudinal, oblique, and transversal actuation loads, and finally, obtain the equations of motion from momentum conservation law. We make the extended Cosserat theory computationally viable for soft robots application by parameterizing the extra strain variable in the GVS approach. The contributions of this work include:

•

Introduction of a new strain variable to capture the in-plane deformation of a soft rod, addressing the limitations of classic Cosserat theory.

•

Derivation of an extended Cosserat rod theory in
dynamics
context for viscoelastic materials from principles of three-dimensional continuum mechanics.

•

Development of a reduced-order numerical method that enables both rapid and precise solutions for the dynamics of the soft rod.

•

Implementation of our theory and numerical method in the study of creature motion and the actuation of soft robots.

Mathematical Model

2.1
Kinematics of Deformation

The Cosserat theory of rods describes the dynamical behavior of a spatial slender rod undergoing large deformations in space by stretching, torsion, bending, and shearing
[
35
]
. In the classic Cosserat Rod theory, the cross-section of the rod is assumed to remain unchanged. Let
\{\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\bm{e}_{3}\}
be the standard orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. For a rod with total length of
L
, the configurations
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{E}^{3}
with material arclength
s\in[0,L]\subset\mathbb{R}
consists of the centroid of the rod
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
and two orthonormal directors
\{\bm{d}_{1},\bm{d}_{2}\}\subset\mathbb{E}^{3}
which spans the cross section
\Omega
of the rod at the particular arclength
s
. Define the third director as
\bm{d}_{3}:=\bm{d}_{1}\times\bm{d}_{2}
and thus
\{\bm{d}_{1},\bm{d}_{2},\bm{d}_{3}\}
forms another right-handed orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. We call
\{\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\bm{e}_{3}\}
as global frame and
\{\bm{d}_{1},\bm{d}_{2},\bm{d}_{3}\}
as local frame. The rotation matrix from the global frame to the local frame is defined as
\bm{d}_{i}=\bm{R}\bm{e}_{i}
,
\bm{R}\in SO(3)
,
i=\{1,2,3\}
.

Let the strain variables
\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represent the linear strain and curvature in local frame along
s
, and thus they define the strain vector field of
\bm{r}
and
\bm{R}
:
\bm{r}^{\prime}:=\bm{R}\bm{\nu}
,
\bm{R}^{\prime}:=\bm{R}\widetilde{\bm{\kappa}}
,
where
\widetilde{(\cdot)}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathfrak{so}(3)
.
The terms
\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}
correspond to shear, and
\nu_{3}
represents stretch,
while
\{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\}
correspond to bending, and
\kappa_{3}
represents twisting.

Let
(X_{1},X_{2})
denotes the coordinates of the subspace formed by director
\{\bm{d}_{1},\bm{d}_{2}\}
. For any material point in
\mathcal{B}
, we express its referenced position coordinates by

\bar{\bm{p}}(X_{1},X_{2},s):\mathbb{E}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{3}
as

\bar{\bm{p}}(X_{1},X_{2},s)=\bar{\bm{r}}(s)+X_{\alpha}\bar{\bm{d}_{\alpha}}(s)

where
\bar{\bm{r}}
and
\bar{\bm{d}_{\alpha}}
represent the referenced centroid and cross-sectional directors, and the subscript
\alpha
denotes summation running from 1 to 2, respectively. Similarly, the position vector of the deformed material point
\bm{p}(X_{1},X_{2},s):\mathbb{E}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{3}
can be written as

\bm{p}(X_{1},X_{2},s)=\bm{r}(s)+X_{\alpha}\bm{d}_{\alpha}(s)
.

To further reflect the exact shape of the slender rod, especially the homogeneous in-plane deformation, we
introduce a new variable
\rho:s\in[0,L]\rightarrow(0,+\infty)
representing the cross-sectional inflation ratio of axisymmetric geometry. In this case, the position of any referenced material point is rewritten as

\bar{\bm{p}}(X_{1},X_{2},s)=\bar{\bm{r}}(s)+\rho(s)X_{\alpha}\bar{\bm{d}_{\alpha}}(s)
, and the position of the deformed material point is given by

\bm{p}(X_{1},X_{2},s)=\bm{r}(s)+\rho(s)X_{\alpha}\bm{d}_{\alpha}(s)=\bm{r}(s)+\rho(s)X_{\alpha}\bm{R}(s)\bm{e}_{\alpha}

Figure
1
illustrates the deformation of the extended Cosserat rod model. We assume no shear, stretch, bending, twisting, or inflation, i.e., stress-free in the referenced configuration in the following context so that

\bar{\bm{p}}(X_{1},X_{2},s)=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&X_{2}&s\end{pmatrix}^{T}
.
Since the strain field variables are invariant to the in-plane inflation ratio
\rho
, the definition of
\{\bm{\nu},\bm{\kappa}\}
described in classic Cosserat rod theory still holds.

Figure. 1:
The kinematics of deformation of the extended Cosserat rod model. The geometric of the cross section is required to be axisymmetric. For a circular cross section,
z
is the radius in referenced configuration

Now the deformation gradient tensor
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{E}^{3\times 3}
is calculated by the gradient of
\bm{p}
(define as
\nabla\bm{p}=\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\bm{e}_{j}\otimes\bm{e}_{i}
) and its pseudo-polar decomposition
[
36
]
, resulting in

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}=\nabla\bm{p}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\bm{R}\bm{e}_{1}&|&\rho\bm{R}\bm{e}_{2}&|&\bm{r}^{\prime}+X_{\alpha}(\rho\bm{R}^{\prime}+\rho^{\prime}\bm{R})\bm{e}_{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}

(1)

\displaystyle=\bm{R}(\bm{D}+\bm{I})=\bm{R}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\rho-1&0&a\\
0&\rho-1&b\\
0&0&c-1\end{pmatrix}+\bm{I}\right)

which is measured by the local displacement tensor
\bm{D}
connected to the moving frame
\bm{R}
, in terms of the strain variables
(\bm{\nu},\bm{\kappa})
with components

\displaystyle a(X_{1},X_{2})=\nu_{1}+X_{1}\rho^{\prime}-X_{2}\rho\kappa_{3}

\displaystyle b(X_{1},X_{2})=\nu_{2}+X_{1}\rho\kappa_{3}+X_{2}\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle c(X_{1},X_{2})=\nu_{3}-X_{1}\rho\kappa_{2}+X_{2}\rho\kappa_{1}

The Green-Lagrangian strain tensor
\bm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}-\bm{I}\right)
is used to describe large deformation in continuum mechanics
[
10
]
. Substituting Eqn.
1
into the formula results in the displacement expression of the strain tensor, we obtained

\bm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{D}^{T}+\bm{D}+\bm{D}^{T}\bm{D}\right)

. Even though the rod is undergoing large deformation, the infinitesimal strain is still small compared to any other dimensions, such as material point displacement. Therefore, it is reasonable to linearize both strain and stress tensor at the undeformed configuration by discarding the quadratic term
\bm{D}^{T}\bm{D}
, providing that
\left\|\bm{D}\right\|_{F}\ll 1
is assumed to hold for a small strain, where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm defined as
\left\|\bm{D}\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{\text{tr}(\bm{D}^{T}\bm{D})}
, such that

\bm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{D}^{T}+\bm{D}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho-1&0&\frac{1}{2}a\\
0&\rho-1&\frac{1}{2}b\\
\frac{1}{2}a&\frac{1}{2}b&c-1\end{pmatrix}

(2)

2.2
Constitutive Law with Kelvin-Voigt Viscous Damping

In 3D continuum mechanics, the elastic behavior can be specified in terms of a volume density of the strain energy function
W_{E}
[
37
]
. Generally, the strain energy density function for the hyperelastic material might be well approximated by the quadratic function
W_{E}\approx\frac{1}{2}\bm{E}:\mathbb{H}:\bm{E}
, where
\mathbb{H}
is the fourth order Hookean material tensor
[
38
]
. The corresponding stress tensor related to the strain and
W_{E}
is called the second Piola-Kirchhoff (2nd PK) stress tensor, defined as
\bm{S}_{E}=\partial W_{E}/\partial\bm{E}\approx\mathbb{H}:\bm{E}
. In the case of homogeneous and isotropic material,
\mathbb{H}
depends on only two constant elastic moduli: the first and second Lamé parameters
\lambda
and
\mu
, reducing the strain energy function to the Saint Venant-Kirchhoff (SVK) model as Eqn.
3
and
4
[
33
]
.

\displaystyle W_{E}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\tr^{2}(\bm{E})+\mu\bm{E}:\bm{E}

(3)

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{E}=\lambda\tr(\bm{E})\bm{I}+2\mu\bm{E}

(4)

If there is no shear, stretch, bending, twisting, and inflation in the referenced configuration, and the small strain assumption holds, then by substituting Eqn.
2
into Eqn.
3
and
4
, we obtain the explicit form of the strain energy function and the 2nd Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor:

\displaystyle W_{E}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(2(\rho-1)+(c-1)\right)^{2}+\mu\left(2(\rho-1)^{2}+(c-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}a^{2}+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}\right)

\displaystyle(\bm{S}_{E})_{ii}=2(\lambda+\mu)(\rho-1)+\lambda(c-1)

\displaystyle(\bm{S}_{E})_{ij}=0,\quad i\neq j

\displaystyle(\bm{S}_{E})_{i3}=(\bm{S}_{E})_{3i}=2\mu\bm{E}_{3i}

\displaystyle(\bm{S}_{E})_{33}=2\lambda(\rho-1)+(\lambda+2\mu)(c-1)

where
i,j\in\{1,2\}
.

For isotropic incompressible material, Poisson’s ratio
\nu^{0}\rightarrow 0.5
, leading
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
and infinite large strain density energy. This issue can be addressed by noting that in isotropic materials, the normal in-plane strain
\bm{E}_{ii}
and the normal out-of-plane strain
\bm{E}_{33}
are related by
\bm{E}_{ii}\equiv-\nu^{0}\bm{E}_{33}
. This indicates uniform in-plane inflation upon cross-section. Given that Poisson’s ratio is related to
(\lambda,\mu)
as
\nu^{0}=\frac{\lambda}{2(\lambda+\mu)}
, we obtain
2(\lambda+\mu)(\rho-1)+\lambda(c-1)\equiv 0
, which implies that
(\bm{S}_{E})_{ii}=0
, i.e. no lateral traction imposed. Integrating over the cross section,
2(\lambda+\mu)(\rho-1)+\lambda(\nu_{3}-1)\equiv 0
always hold. If we let the constraint regarding the zero lateral traction holds, the strain energy density function is reduced to

W_{E}=\frac{1}{2}\mu(a^{2}+b^{2})+\frac{1}{2}E(c-1)^{2}

(5)

where
E
is the Young’s modulus, defined as
E=\mu(3\lambda+2\mu)/(\lambda+\mu)
.

The integration of
W_{E}
over the cross-section domain
\Omega(s)
yields the line density of strain energy function, that is

\Phi_{E}(s)=\int_{\Omega(s)}W_{E}(\bm{E})d\Omega

\Phi_{E}(s)
can be further relaxed by imposing
\rho\approx 1
in
a
,
b
, and
c
so that only strain terms up to quadratic are retained. For a cross-section with axisymmetry, the simplest form of
\Phi_{E}
is

\displaystyle\Phi_{E}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mu A_{0}(\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}EA_{0}(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}EI_{\alpha\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu I_{33}\kappa_{3}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu I_{33}\rho^{\prime 2}

where
A_{0}
represents the referenced cross-section area and
I_{kk}
represents the second moment inertia of
\Omega(s)
about the director
\bm{d}_{k}
. The Young’s modulus
E
can also be rewritten as

E=\lambda+2\mu-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+\mu}

Substituting
E
into the second term of Eqn.
6
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}EA_{0}(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+\mu}(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+\mu}(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+\mu}(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}+2(\lambda+\mu)A_{0}(\rho-1)^{2}+2\lambda A_{0}(\rho-1)(\nu_{3}-1)

In this way, we obtain a more complex expression of the in-plane strain energy function:

\displaystyle\Phi_{E}=

\displaystyle 2(\lambda+\mu)A_{0}(\rho-1)^{2}+2\lambda A_{0}(\rho-1)(\nu_{3}-1)

(7)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mu A_{0}(\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)A_{0}(\nu_{3}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}EI_{\alpha\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu I_{33}\kappa_{3}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu I_{33}\rho^{\prime 2}

Although Eqn.
7
may appear ad hoc, it can be justified in the analysis of internal force in the following context. In computation, the infinite large
\lambda
for incompressible isotropic material is approximated by setting
\nu^{0}
closed to 0.5.

When it comes to viscous damping, the simplest model is the Kelvin-Voigt damping, which is a linear combination of the stress and strain rate tensor. The dissipation rate per unit volume is given by
\dot{D}=\dot{\bm{E}}:\mathbb{V}:\dot{\bm{E}}
, where
\mathbb{V}
is the fourth order viscosity tensor. If the material is homogeneous and isotropic, the viscosity tensor totally depends on two constant parameters
[
38
]

\zeta
and shear viscosity
\eta
, such that

is evaluated as

\dot{D}=(\zeta-\frac{2}{3}\eta)\tr^{2}(\dot{\bm{E}})+2\eta\dot{\bm{E}}:\dot{\bm{E}}

For incompressible material, constant volume indicates that
\tr^{2}(\dot{\bm{E}})=0
, so the first term vanishes. If we double integrate
\dot{D}
over the cross-section
\Omega(s)
and time and apply the same relaxing method, we obtain the quadratic line density of dissipation energy function in closed form for reflective symmetric cross-section:

\displaystyle\Phi_{D}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega(s)}\dot{D}d\Omega d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\Phi}_{D}d\tau

(8)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{t}\big(4\eta A_{0}\dot{\rho}^{2}+\eta A_{0}(\dot{\nu}_{1}^{2}+\dot{\nu}_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle+2\eta A_{0}\dot{\nu}_{3}^{2}+3\eta I_{\alpha\alpha}\dot{\bm{\kappa}}_{\alpha}^{2}

\displaystyle+\eta I_{33}\dot{\bm{\kappa}}_{3}^{2}+\eta I_{33}\dot{\rho}^{\prime 2}\big)d\tau

The total energy per unit length is
\Phi=\Phi_{E}+\Phi_{D}
. To derive the relationship between

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}

Internal Applied Forces and External Loads

The soft beam is driven by the internal applied force, such as the actuator’s loads. In an underwater scenario, the soft beam is also affected by external forces like gravity, buoyancy, flow dragging, etc. The actuators are usually divided into two types

\Omega(s)

\Omega(s)
; b) longitudinal or oblique actuator, which generates force with a decomposition along the length of the arm, and the resulting wrenches are distributed continuously at
s\in[x,y]\subset[0,L]
. Figure.

shows the important loads that might applied to the soft rod.

3.1
Transversal Actuation Load

We assume that the body force is homogeneous for all the material points in
\mathcal{B}
, then we find that term
\int_{\Omega}X_{\alpha}\rho_{0}\bm{b}d\Omega
of
r
vanishes. Therefore, the lateral traction is only given by the force applied to the normal of the boundary
C
of
\Omega(s)
. In the first term of

\bm{P}\cdot\bm{v}
corresponds to the traction acting on the undeformed infinitesimal surface
dS
, which is normal to the boundary vector
\bm{v}
, as shown in Figure.
4

dS
. If there is no shear pressure acting on the boundary of
\Omega(s)
, and the transversal actuators exert homogeneous pressure

which points inward to the origin of the deformed configuration in local frame, then we have
\bm{P}\cdot\bm{v}dC=f_{0}^{b}\bm{R}\bm{v}^{b}dC^{b}
, where
\bm{v}^{b}
is the outward normal vector to the deformed boundary
dC^{b}
. By multiplying the rotation matrix, we project the actual ”force” from local to global frame (noticed that
\bm{R}\bm{v}^{b}\neq\bm{v}
). Therefore, the lateral traction resultant is given by:

r=\bm{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{R}^{T}\left(\oint_{C}X_{\alpha}\bm{P}\cdot\bm{v}dC\right)=\bm{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\oint_{C}X_{\alpha}\rho f_{0}^{b}\bm{v}^{b}dC=2f_{0}^{b}\rho\pi z^{2}

where
z
is the radius of the cross-section in referenced configuration.

Alternatively, we can also derive the lateral traction resultant by computing the active Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
[
39
]
with pressure
f_{0}
defined in the reference configuration. In the reference configuration, the active stress
f_{0}
acts on the boundary
C
with homogeneous magnitude, by Eqn. 4.10 in
[
39
]
, we have the active Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor as:

\bm{P}_{a}=\frac{f_{0}}{\sqrt{I_{4}}}\mathbf{F}\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v}

where
I_{4}
is an invariant defined as
I_{4}=\mathbf{F}\bm{v}\cdot\mathbf{F}\bm{v}
, and the operator
\otimes
denotes the tensor product. By substituting
\bm{P}=\bm{P}_{a}
into the above equation, we have the lateral traction resultant as

r=2f_{0}\pi z^{2}

(29)

Longitudinal or Oblique Actuation Load

A longitudinal or oblique actuator acts on the soft rod
\mathcal{B}
by an applied tension

and a distributed moment along the actuator route inside
\mathcal{B}

[
26
]

\bm{r}_{c}(s)=\bm{r}(s)+\rho(s)\bm{R}(s)\bm{d}
,

, where
\bm{d}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},0)^{T}\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
denotes the coordinates of the actuator within the local frame. The resultant force will have the same magnitude as the tension, and point toward the tangent
\bm{t}
of the route
\bm{r}_{c}
, while the resultant distributed moment is proportional to
T(t)
and points toward the direction of
\bm{d}\times\bm{t}
. To show the explicit expression of the longitudinal and oblique actuation load, we take the derivative of
\bm{r}_{c}
w.r.t arclength as

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{c}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bm{r}^{\prime}+Y_{\alpha}\rho\bm{R}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{\alpha}+Y_{\alpha}^{\prime}\rho\bm{R}\bm{e}_{\alpha}+Y_{\alpha}\rho^{\prime}\bm{R}\bm{e}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\bm{R}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\rho&0&a(Y_{1},Y_{2})\\
0&\rho&b(Y_{1},Y_{2})\\
0&0&c(Y_{1},Y_{2})\\
\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{F}_{c}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}^{\prime}\\
Y_{2}^{\prime}\\

so that the unit tangent within the local frame can be written as
\bm{t}=\bm{R}^{T}\bm{r}_{c}^{\prime}/\left\|\bm{R}^{T}\bm{r}_{c}^{\prime}\right\|=\mathbf{F}_{c}\bm{d}_{0}^{\prime}/\left\|\mathbf{F}_{c}\bm{d}_{0}^{\prime}\right\|
. With regards to the resultant force and moment, they are given by

\bm{n}_{a}=T(t)\bm{t},\quad\bm{m}_{a}=T(t)\bm{d}\times\bm{t}

(30)

3.3
External Loads

If the rod interacts with water, the soft rod deforms due to hydrodynamic forces, which in turn is influenced by the state of the soft rod. To describe these forces, we integrate the simplified hydrodynamic model proposed by
[
40
]
. This model allows for the decoupling of the interactions between the soft rod and the water. The overall external forces and moments per unit length are expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{e}=\bm{f}_{b}+\bm{f}_{a}+\bm{f}_{d}+\bm{f}_{p}

(31)

\displaystyle\bm{l}_{e}=\bm{l}_{p}

(32)

where the subscripts
b
,
a
,
d
, and
p
represent the contributions from gravity and buoyancy, added mass from the water, drag, and point forces or moments, respectively.

Assuming that the density of the water is
\rho_{a}
and a homogeneous material density in the cross-section, then we have the gravity and buoyancy per unit length as

\bm{f}_{b}=(\rho_{0}-\rho_{a})A\bm{G}

where
A
is the cross-sectional area,
\bm{G}
represents the gravity acceleration in inertial frame, which equals
(-9.81,0,0)^{T}
m/s
2
. Since gravity and buoyancy are homogeneous on
\Omega(s)
, there are no extra moments produced w.r.t. the centroid.

The acceleration of the surrounding water produces the inertial forces interacting with the soft rod. Such added mass load is obtained as

\bm{f}_{a}=-\bm{M}_{a}\dot{\bm{u}}=-\pi z^{2}\rho_{a}\text{diag}(B_{1},B_{2},0)\dot{\bm{u}}

(33)

on a circular cross section, where
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
are constant added-mass coefficients in the
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
direction, respectively. The added-mass loads act normal to the surface of the rod, and when there is geometrical symmetry on the cross-section, the added-mass moments will cancel out.

The dragging and lifting forces act opposite to the linear velocity of the cross-section and are proportional to its magnitude and the circular cross-sectional radius, given by

\bm{f}_{d}=-\mathcal{D}\left\|\bm{u}\right\|\bm{u}=-z\rho_{a}\begin{pmatrix}C_{D}&-C_{L}&0\\
C_{L}&C_{D}&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\left\|\bm{u}\right\|\bm{u}

(34)

where
C_{L}
and
C_{D}
are lifting coefficient along the
\bm{d}_{3}
direction, and dragging coefficient acting on the
\bm{d}_{1}
and
\bm{d}_{2}
direction, respectively. The dragging load produces no moments for the geometrical symmetric cross-section.

When the rod is subjected to the point force
\bm{F}_{p}
and moment
\bm{L}_{p}
applied on
\bar{s}
, we need to determine how the localized loads affect the rod. We assume that both
\bm{F}_{p}
and
\bm{L}_{p}
concentrate on the origin of the cross-section
\Omega(s)
, then we can compute the distributed force
\bm{f}_{p}
and moment
\bm{l}_{p}
per unit length as

\bm{F}_{p}(s)=\int_{0}^{L}\delta(s-\bar{s})\bm{f}_{p},\quad\bm{L}_{p}(s)=\int_{0}^{L}\delta(s-\bar{s})\bm{l}_{p}

(35)

respectively, where
\delta(s)
is the dirac distribution. To be simplified, we take the value of
\bm{f}_{p}
and
\bm{l}_{p}
the same as
\bm{F}_{p}
and
\bm{L}_{p}
, except that the unit changes to N/m.

4
Strain Parameterization

Fast computation requires reducing Eqn.
24
,
25
, and
28
from continuous to a finite domain by parameterization. An effective approach is to extend the Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) model
[
26
]
by parameterizing the extra strain variable
\rho
, such that Eqn.
24
,
25
and
28
are projected to the general coordinates on the strain field.
The extended GVS model framework allows the efficient estimation of geometric Jacobian, statics, and dynamics of the system. The estimations are completed by a recursive computation of the forward kinematics, velocities, and acceleration.

4.1
Recursive Kinematics

The configuration of the soft rod
\mathcal{B}
w.r.t the inertial frame can be expressed using the space curve

\bm{g}(\cdot):s\in[0,L]\mapsto\bm{g}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{R}&\bm{r}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\textit{SE}(3)

(36)

and the inflation ratio of the cross-section
\rho
. By taking the derivatives of Eqn.
36
w.r.t
s
and time, we obtain

\bm{g}^{\prime}(s)=\bm{g}(s)\widehat{\bm{\xi}}(s),\quad\dot{\bm{g}}(s)=\bm{g}(s)\widehat{\bm{\eta}}(s)

(37)

where

\widehat{\bm{\xi}}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{\kappa}}&\bm{\nu}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{se}(3),\quad\bm{\xi}=(\bm{\kappa}^{T},\bm{\nu}^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}

(38)

represents the strain twist in the body frame, while

\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}&\bm{u}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{se}(3),\quad\bm{\eta}=(\bm{\omega}^{T},\bm{u}^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}

(39)

represents the screw velocity in the body frame. Given the strain twists
\bm{\xi}
at
s
, we obtain the centroid’s configuration as an exponential map
[
41
,
42
]

\bm{g}(s)=\text{exp}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}(s)\right),\quad\text{with }\bm{g}(0)=\bm{I}_{4}

(40)

where
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}
is the Magnus expansion of
\bm{\xi}
at
s
. The fourth-order Zannah quadrature approximation, which balances the accuracy and computation cost, is provided in
Appendix C
. By integrating the compatibility law given by Eqn.
21
along the length of the rod, we obtain

\bm{\eta}=\text{Ad}^{-1}_{\bm{g}}\int_{0}^{s}\text{Ad}_{\bm{g}}\dot{\bm{\xi}}ds

(41)

Next, we parameterized
\bm{\xi}
using a polynomial basis and the general coordinates
\bm{q}_{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
of dimension
n_{\xi}
as:

\bm{\xi}(s,\bm{q}_{\xi})=\bm{\Phi}_{\xi}(s)\bm{q}_{\xi}+\bm{\xi}^{*}(s)

(42)

The basis
\bm{\Phi}_{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n_{\xi}}
is independent of
\bm{q}_{\xi}
and time
t
, and subjected to the constrained strains such as the boundary conditions.
\bm{\xi}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
is the reference strain twist which models the unstressed configuration.

For the variable strain case, the Magnus expansion
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}(h)
is approximated through small intervals
h
of
s
, and the kinematic terms are computed recursively along the length of the rod.

\displaystyle\bm{g}(s+h)

\displaystyle=\bm{g}(s)\text{exp}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}(h)\right)

(43)

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}(s+h)

\displaystyle=\text{Ad}^{-1}_{\text{exp}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\right)}\left(\bm{\eta}(s)+T_{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\Omega}}\dot{\bm{q}}_{\xi}\right)

(44)

where
T_{\bm{\Omega}}
is the tangent operator of the exponential map and
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\Omega}}
is the Magnus expansion of the basis
[
8
]
. The geometric Jacobian
\bm{J}(\bm{q}_{\xi},s)\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n_{\xi}}
maps ”joint” velocities to the body velocities according to

\bm{\eta}=\bm{J}\dot{\bm{q}}_{\xi}

(45)

Substituting
\bm{J}
into Eqn.
44
, we obtain

\bm{J}(\bm{q}_{\xi},s+h)=\text{Ad}^{-1}_{\text{exp}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\right)}\left(\bm{J}(\bm{q}_{\xi},s)+T_{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\Omega}}\right)

(46)

The acceleration twist is derived as

\dot{\bm{\eta}}(s,\bm{q}_{\xi},\dot{\bm{q}}_{\xi},\ddot{\bm{q}}_{\xi})=\bm{J}(s,\bm{q}_{\xi})\ddot{\bm{q}}_{\xi}+\dot{\bm{J}}(s,\bm{q}_{\xi},\dot{\bm{q}}_{\xi})\dot{\bm{q}}_{\xi}

(47)

The detailed steps to perform recursive computation are listed in
Appendix C
.

Similarly, the inflation ratio
\rho
is parameterized as

\rho(s,\bm{q}_{\rho})=\bm{\Phi}_{\rho}(s)\bm{q}_{\rho}+\rho^{*}(s)

(48)

where the basis
\bm{\Phi}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n_{\rho}}
is independent of the general coordinate
\bm{q}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\rho}}
and is subjected to constraints of
\rho
.
\rho^{*}
is the reference inflation ratio under the unstressed configuration. Since we have
\dot{\rho}=\bm{\Phi}_{\rho}\dot{\bm{q}}_{\rho}
, we notice that the basis
\bm{\Phi}_{\rho}
is itself the Jacobian matrix for
\bm{q}_{\rho}
and
\rho
. The acceleration of
\rho
is computed as
\ddot{\rho}=\bm{\Phi}_{\rho}\ddot{\bm{q}}_{\rho}
.

5
Applications

In this section, we provide three simulation examples of cable-driven bio-inspired soft manipulators to show the capabilities of our model. The soft manipulator can deform to complex shapes by applying both longitudinal and transversal tension to the actuator inside the soft body. In the first example, we focus on how the soft body tunes its stiffness by modulating longitudinal and transversal loads to resist both compression, unlike the classic model, which only allows for tension. In the remaining examples, we investigate the planar reaching and the 3D fetching motion of an octopus-like soft robotic arm, respectively. By comparing our model with the classic Cosserat model, we find that our model is more consistent with the biological profiles of a real octopus. This enhanced performance highlights the potential of our model in developing more efficient soft robotic systems.

6
Conclusion

In this paper,we develop the extended Cosserat rod model by introducing an extra strain variable, the inflation ratio
\rho
, and derive the constitutive law involving both elasticity and damping from the first principle. We then obtain the equations of motion from the momentum balances and analyze all the possible applied forces on the soft rod. To solve the extended model efficiently, we project the equations of motion from the Euclidean space to the manifold of the parameterized strain configurations using the GVS approach. The paper also includes five appendices, which provide details about the constitutive law, momentum balances, and computation procedures. We also provide open-source
MATLAB
codes to guide the reader to implement the extended Cosserat rod model on their applications.

To show the capabilities of the extended Cosserat rod model, we study three applications: the axial stiffness tuning and the reaching and fetching motion of an octopus-arm-like manipulator. In the first application, we demonstrate that the contraction of the inflation ratio
\rho
can be used to resist axial compression, which is not possible to model in the classical Cosserat rod model. We compare the simulated results with analytical solutions and show that the extended Cosserat rod model can predict the axial stiffness accurately. In the second application, we further validate the stiffening wave hypothesis of the octopus arm reaching by co-contraction of all the LMs and TM. We study the biological profiles of the reaching motion and find them consistent with those of the real octopus. We compare the extended model with transversal actuation, the extended model without transversal actuation, and the classic Cosserat rod model and show the significance of the transversal actuators of the soft manipulator in the complex motions. In the last example, we demonstrate the fetching motion of the octopus arm, which is achieved by the actuation of all types of muscles. The oblique muscles are responsible for the bending and twisting, avoiding collision when the bending curvature is large.

Despite the fact that the in-plane deformation together with other strains originally considered in the classic Cosserat rod model are effectively simulated, the extended Cosserat rod model is still a simplified model of the soft rod. We do not model the
anistropic
deformation, i.e., the cross-section is not reflective symmetric, or there exists out-of-plane deformation. Even if we roughly impose the incompressibility constraints by relating the diagonal elements of the strain tensor, the model still does not fully preserve volume due to the lack of evaluation of the global volume. The model can be further improved by considering a more general constitutive law, such as the Neo-Hookean hyperelastic model, and incorporating a penalty term for the constant volume in the strain energy function
[
37
]
. For applications, we can further study the control and optimization problems of the soft manipulator, such as the impedance control and motion planning, since our energy-based constitutive law is naturally suitable for creating Lyapunov functions for closed-loop control. Finally, we hope to see more physical soft robotics applications using the extended Cosserat rod model in the future.
