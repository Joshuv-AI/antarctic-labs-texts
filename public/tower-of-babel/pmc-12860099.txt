Impact mechanisms of grandparental care on social isolation among older migrants: evidence from a megacity in China
Authors: Xinru Li, Jianan Han, Yanan Wang, Chichen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAmid population aging and migration, social isolation among older migrants in megacities has increasingly become a public health challenge. This study explores how grandparental childcare affects social isolation via social participation, and the moderating role of settlement intention in this path, offering a new perspective on older migrants’ social integration.MethodsUsing survey data of older migrants in Shenzhen, a Chinese megacity, this study applies PROCESS macro Model 4 to test the mediating role of social participation in the association between grandparental childcare and social isolation, and moderated mediation Model 14 to analyze the moderating role of settlement intention in the ‘social participation → social isolation’ association.ResultsGrandparental childcare frequency is significantly associated with higher LSNS-6 scores (indicating lower social isolation) (β = 2.841, t = 3.018, P < 0.01). Social participation partially mediates the association between grandparental childcare and social isolation, accounting for 34.53% of the total effect. Settlement intention significantly moderates the association in the second half of the mediating pathway (β=−1.016, t=−2.675, P < 0.01). Specifically, the strength of the association between social participation and LSNS-6 scores (indicating lower social isolation) was stronger among older migrants without settlement intention (B = 2.222, t = 7.072, P < 0.01) than among those with settlement intention (B = 1.206, t = 5.376, P < 0.01).ConclusionsThis study shows grandparental childcare alleviates older migrants’ social isolation (as reflected by higher LSNS-6 scores) by enhancing social participation, with settlement intention moderating the link between social participation and social isolation. Notably, temporary residence mentality may strengthen social participation’s positive effect, offering new evidence for explaining migrants’ “cultural barriers.” Future research should examine mediating variables like language ability and cultural identity to inform differentiated social integration policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06867-9.Keywords: Older migrants, Social isolation, Grandparental childcare, Social participation, Settlement intention

Introduction

Unprecedented population mobility in China has profoundly reshaped the population distribution pattern, making “mobility” a prominent feature of contemporary society. The Seventh National Census shows that in 2020, the total number of migrant populations in China reached 376 million. Among them, the size of older migrant populations reached 33.27 million, an increase of 20.23 million compared with 2015 [1, 2]. Against the backdrop of the intersection of global urbanization and population aging, the issue of social integration of older migrants in megacities has become a major public health concern.

This study focuses on Shenzhen, a typical megacity of immigration in China. As a frontier of China’s reform and opening-up, Shenzhen has a distinct population structure: by the end of 2023, its permanent population reached 17.7901 million, with non-registered population accounting for 65.9% [3]. This makes it one of the megacities in China with the highest proportion of migrant populations and the strongest population mobility. Such a unique population structure, coupled with the high dependence of many migrant families on grandparental childcare, makes Shenzhen an ideal research site to explore the mechanism linking grandparental childcare and social isolation among older migrants. Megacities like Shenzhen, as core hubs of population mobility, host a large number of older migrants who move with their families. The risk of social isolation they face urgently requires in-depth attention.

Older migrants are a structural product of the interaction between the rapid aging process and socio-economic transformation [4]. Unlike young and middle-aged migrant groups dominated by economic opportunities, the migration of older migrants is mostly “passive”. Their main motives are grandparental childcare or family reunion [5]. China Migrant Population Development Report 2016 points out that in 2015, 43% of older migrants migrated to provide childcare for their grandchildren [6]. However, this “passive migration” model, dominated by family responsibilities, is often accompanied by dual pressures: the breakdown of original social ties and difficulties in rebuilding social networks in the destination [7]. This leads to prominent risks of social isolation for this group.

Social isolation refers to the objective state of individuals having insufficient social networks at the interpersonal, group, and community levels [8, 9]. It poses a serious threat to the health of older adults. Existing studies have confirmed that social isolation significantly increases the risk of depression, cognitive decline, impairment in activities of daily living, and psychological distress among older adults [10, 11]. Although grandparental childcare is a major motive for older people’s migration, there are still theoretical controversies about its impact mechanism on social isolation. The role strain hypothesis emphasizes that high-intensity childcare limits social participation through time compression [12]. The role accumulation perspective, however, holds that childcare activities may strengthen family bonds and promote community interaction [13]. This theoretical divergence highlights the need to explore in depth grandparental childcare behavior itself and its contextual moderating factors.

To sum up, although existing studies have made progress in exploring the relationship between grandparental childcare behavior and social isolation among older migrants, key knowledge gaps remain significant. First, social participation as a potential mediating mechanism through which grandparental childcare affects social isolation has not been systematically tested. Second, the potential moderating effect of settlement intention—a key contextual factor affecting the social integration strategies of older migrants—has not been fully explored. This study aims to fill the above research gaps by constructing an analytical framework that integrates grandparental childcare, social participation, social isolation, and settlement intention. The research findings can not only deepen the theoretical understanding of the social integration mechanism of older migrants but also provide a scientific basis for megacities to formulate more targeted health intervention policies for older migrants.

Conceptual definition and hypotheses

Concept definition and differentiation

Social isolation and social participation

Due to differences in cultural backgrounds and individual characteristics, there is no unified standard for defining social isolation [14]. Academic consensus holds that social isolation is a continuum of the abundance of social relationship networks [15]. A social network refers to a relatively stable relationship system formed through interactions among individual members of society. It focuses on the interactions and connections between people, and further how these interactions influence people’s social behaviors [16, 17]. Its core lies in describing the degree of lack of social connections, rather than a simple dichotomous division (i.e., the presence or absence of social isolation).

As a key intervenable domain, social participation refers to the specific ways and practical processes through which individuals participate in social activities and interpersonal interactions. It exerts positive impacts on the physical and mental health of older adults by providing social support, alleviating loneliness, and improving life satisfaction [18].

Social isolation and social participation form a unity of opposites in the core dimension of “social connection”: the former reflects the state of disconnected connections, while the latter represents the process of realizing connections. Together, they constitute the basic aspects of the relationship between individuals and society. However, in real contexts, they are not absolutely opposite binary existences. Instead, they present a continuous transitional form from “complete isolation” to “in-depth participation”. They may also show mixed states in different social fields (e.g., high participation in some fields and high isolation in others). This continuum perspective not only helps grasp the essential relationship between them (e.g., social participation can be an important way to alleviate social isolation) but also avoids simplistic binary thinking (e.g., “limited social interaction” does not equal “social isolation”).

In this study, social isolation is conceptualized as an outcome state and subjective perception. It reflects the sense of loneliness and relational deprivation experienced by individuals due to insufficient size and quality of their social networks, focusing on the structure of relationships and emotional experiences. Social participation is conceptualized as a behavioral process and the performance of social roles, referring to individuals’ active engagement in formal or informal social activities (e.g., visiting friends, participating in community clubs), with a focus on the frequency and occurrence of behaviors. The operational definition of social participation focuses on the frequency and type diversity of older adults’ participation in various social activities. Social isolation, on the other hand, focuses on the structural characteristics of the social networks of older migrants, including indicators of relationship strength such as the number of friends and the frequency of contact with relatives and friends. The clear distinction between the two in terms of measurement dimensions provides an empirical basis for exploring the core question of “whether grandparental childcare affects social isolation through social participation” — the direction and intensity of this association are exactly what this study attempts to clarify.

Grandparental childcare and settlement intention

Grandparental childcare refers to the set of behaviors in which grandparents provide daily care for their grandchildren [19]. As an important supplementary form of parental care [20], it has become the main motivation for older adults’ cross-regional mobility in China’s megacities [21].

Settlement intention is the subjective decision-making tendency formed by migrants after weighing the benefits and costs of migration. Its distribution characteristics directly affect urban population structure and mobility patterns [22]. As a subjective attitude, settlement intention influences specific choices in residence decisions by shaping individuals’ long-term behavioral expectations [23], and thus has important theoretical value for understanding the diverse differentiation of population mobility in China.

For older adults who migrate for grandparental childcare, childcare behavior constitutes the initial motivation for their migration, but there is no inevitable connection between this starting point and the formation of settlement intention. The theoretical basis for settlement intention as a moderator variable is that differences in its intensity may change the impact of social participation on social isolation. For example, a strong settlement intention may encourage older adults to more actively convert social participation into network capital, thereby alleviating isolation more effectively. The existence and intensity of this moderating mechanism are the core research objectives for setting this variable in this study.

Research hypotheses

Direct impact of grandparental childcare on social isolation

As a core form of intergenerational support, grandparental childcare plays an important role in promoting intergenerational emotional bonds and sustaining family functions among older migrant populations. Existing studies have shown that the effectiveness of intergenerational support networks directly affects the level of social integration of older adults. Based on an empirical analysis of older adults in Shanxi Province, China, Ren Wenzhuo (2023) found that the level of intergenerational support has a significantly negative predictive effect on the risk of social isolation. Specifically, older adults with higher intergenerational support have a lower incidence of social isolation [24]. This provides an important basis for understanding the protective function of grandparental childcare.

From the perspective of social network theory, grandparental childcare behavior exerts a dual empowering effect by reshaping the social relationship structure of older adults. On the one hand, older adults may expand their social networks through grandparental childcare. For example, when taking grandchildren to public settings such as parks, older adults can meet other peer caregivers, thereby expanding social connections. Such networks not only enhance individuals’ psychological capital to cope with stress but also expand their access to social resources through continuous information exchange mechanisms [25]. On the other hand, the “family bond” effect derived from grandparental childcare significantly increases the frequency of intergenerational interactions. While maintaining parent-child relationships, older adults form new weak-tie networks with other grandparental caregivers through childcare practices in community public spaces [26]. This multi-dimensional social participation effectively buffers the risk of relationship breakdown during migration.

Although some scholars have pointed out the restrictive impacts of potential role conflicts (such as conflicts in time resource allocation and differences in intergenerational expectations) [27], in the specific context of migrant populations, grandparental childcare essentially constitutes a practical form of intergenerational solidarity. Especially against the backdrop of the household registration system and family separation, childcare responsibilities provide older migrants with an institutionalized family role positioning, enabling them to obtain continuous social interaction scenarios in the destination. Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes:

Hypothesis 1: Grandparental childcare behavior among older migrants is significantly negatively associated with their level of social isolation.

Mediating role of social participation

Existing studies have shown that grandparental childcare may indirectly affect the level of social isolation of older migrants by activating social participation pathways. Empirical research by Li Yanchu and Gao Jingjing (2024) found that older adults providing grandparental childcare exhibit a significant incremental effect in daily social practices. Specifically, this is reflected in the systematic improvement of three types of social participation behaviors: neighborhood visits, peer interactions, and community activities [28]. This finding suggests that grandparental childcare may restructure older adults’ social action frameworks through role embedding mechanisms. Childcare responsibilities not only create scenarios for intergenerational interactions but also may form new social network relationships through community childcare practices (such as picking up/dropping off grandchildren and participating in school activities).

It is worth noting that the limited social participation of older migrants is often related to their difficulties in role adaptation. High-intensity childcare needs may squeeze their resources for social participation [12], but grandparental childcare itself may also become an opportunity to break through this dilemma. For example, research by Xu Wei (2021) revealed that older migrants’ participation in collective social activities such as community sports not only improves their cultural adaptation ability and interaction frequency but also strengthens their sense of social belonging at the psychological identity level [29]. This multi-dimensional mechanism of improving social integration provides a key perspective for understanding the functional transformation of grandparental childcare.

Although existing literature has separately confirmed the promoting effect of grandparental childcare on social participation [28] and the mitigating effect of social participation on social isolation [30, 31], the transmission mechanism between the two remains a theoretical gap in the context of older migrant populations. Based on this, this paper proposes:

Hypothesis 2a: Social participation plays a mediating role between grandparental childcare and social isolation among older migrants.

Hypothesis 2b: Higher frequency of grandparental childcare among older migrants is associated with a higher level of social participation, which in turn is associated with lower social isolation.

Moderating role of settlement intention

The settlement intention of older migrants essentially reflects their level of social identity with the destination, and this psychological tendency rooted in their settlement intention may further affect the scope, frequency, and depth of their social participation. Some studies have pointed out that the short-term migration model characterized by “maximizing family welfare” generally shapes the feature of low settlement intention among older migrants [32]. This “task-oriented” mobility leads to functional contraction in their social interactions: on the one hand, geographical identity with the place of origin strengthens the inertia of maintaining cross-regional social relationships; on the other hand, the temporary nature of migration motives causes them to actively avoid deep social embeddedness in the destination [33]. In this context, although grandparental childcare may trigger limited social participation, the accumulation of their social capital is often constrained by the psychological identity of being a “temporary resident“ [34].

Empirical evidence further reveals the complex mechanism of settlement intention on social isolation. Research by Liu Chengbin and Gong Naxin (2020) shows that the willingness of older migrants whose core task is caring for grandchildren to reside in cities is significantly lower than that of other migration types [33]. This inhibitory effect may stem from the dual constraints of intergenerational role tension and family power structure. National survey data by Ren Yuan et al. (2020) confirms the particularity of this group: among older migrant caregivers, the superimposed characteristics of younger age (82% under 70) and inter-provincial migration (50%) [35] suggest that they are more likely to fall into the integration dilemma of “physical presence but psychological absence”. However, findings by Yang Ni (2018) and other scholars provide a reverse explanatory pathway: active social participation can strengthen older migrants’ willingness to settle locally [36], indicating that settlement intention and social participation may form a dynamic mutual construction relationship.

More critically, differences in settlement intention may moderate the effectiveness of social participation in combating social isolation. In-depth interviews by Ding Manxin (2021) revealed that older migrants with strong settlement intention tend to adopt more proactive social interaction strategies. In contrast, although passively migrating older adults are physically present, their “migratory bird mentality” limits social participation to an instrumental level [34]. This difference in psychological positioning may lead to two consequences: first, those with strong settlement intention are more likely to convert social participation into sustainable social capital; second, social interactions of those with weak settlement intention are mostly limited to functional scenarios, making it difficult to form in-depth social support networks. Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes:

Hypothesis 3a: Settlement intention has a moderating effect between social participation and social isolation.

Hypothesis 3a: Settlement intention has a moderating effect between social participation and social isolation.

Hypothesis 3b: Compared with older migrants without settlement intention, the association between social participation and lower social isolation is more pronounced among those with settlement intention.

Materials and methods

Data sources

This study adopted a multi-stage random sampling method. From July to August 2023, one district (LH District) was randomly selected in Shenzhen, followed by random selection of 6 communities within this district. Older adults aged 60 years and above were then randomly sampled from each community. Face-to-face questionnaires were administered by investigators who received standardized training. After the survey [37], two quality supervisors reviewed the questionnaires for quality control based on the back-to-back principle, including screening for invalid values and conducting logical checks. A questionnaire was deemed invalid if missing indicators accounted for 30% or more of all indicators.

Based on the sample size formula for observational studies: n = 400*(1-P)/P (where P is the incidence of the target event) [38], this study used the reported social isolation rate of 34.9% among Chinese older adults as the parameter estimate [39]. The calculated basic theoretical sample size was 747. To ensure research efficiency, considering factors such as invalid questionnaires and response rates, the theoretical sample size was adjusted by increasing it by 30%, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 972. A total of 1,200 questionnaires were distributed, with 1,039 recovered. Among these, 1,036 were valid, giving a valid recovery rate of 86.33%. After excluding cases with missing values for key variables, 1,032 samples were initially retained (including non-migrant older adults).

Measurements

Dependent variables

The dependent variable in this study was social isolation, measured using the Lubben Social Network Scale-Short Form (LSNS-6). It is important to note that a higher score on the LSNS-6 indicates a richer social network and thus a lower level of social isolation. This is a standardized tool for assessing individual social network characteristics, with its core function being to quantitatively measure the quality of interaction between participants and their core family members and close friends [40]. The scale has a two-dimensional structure, including two subscales: the family network dimension (3 items) and the friend network dimension (3 items). Each item is scored using a Likert 6-point scale (0 = completely inconsistent, 5 = completely consistent), with a theoretical total score ranging from 0 to 30. According to internationally accepted criteria, a total score ≥ 12 indicates that an individual has an adequate social support network, while a total score < 12 suggests a risk of social isolation (a state of significantly insufficient social network resources). This scale has been well-validated in the Chinese older adult population [10]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.904, the KMO value was 0.835, and the Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ2 = 3434.605, P < 0.001) indicated good reliability and validity of the scale.

Independent variables

The independent variable in this study is Grandparental Childcare, measured by asking older adults: “Do you need to spend time and energy caring for your grandchildren?” The original options were: 1 = Always need, 2 = Often need, 3 = Sometimes need, 4 = Never need. Referring to previous studies [41, 42], we performed reverse coding and linear transformation on this variable. Specifically, the original values were converted into continuous scores ranging from 0.25 to 1.00 (i.e., “Always need” = 1.00, “Often need” = 0.75, “Sometimes need” = 0.50, “Never need” = 0.25). This was to construct a continuous “care intensity” variable with a consistent direction (higher values represent greater care intensity) that is easy to interpret in linear regression models. Furthermore, methodological studies have shown that converting categorical data from Likert scales for scoring is consistent with traditional scoring methods in key indicators such as reliability and factor structure [43], which supports the feasibility of the current processing method.

Mediating variable

The mediating variable in this study is Social Participation. Drawing on the commonly used method for constructing formative indicators in social epidemiology [44, 45], social participation is defined as a composite concept consisting of various activities. Therefore, we constructed this variable by summing the frequency of older adults’ participation in various social activities, rather than using the Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient based on internal consistency. The reason is that different activities are not necessarily highly correlated; instead, they are more likely to collectively reflect an individual’s overall participation level at the formative level. The specific item for social participation is: “In the past month, have you participated in the following social activities? (Multiple choices allowed)”. The options include 11 types of activities. We simply summed the number of activity types selected by the respondents, with higher total scores indicating higher levels of social participation among older migrants. To maintain research transparency, all specific activity items have been fully listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Moderating variable

The moderating variable was settlement intention, measured by asking older migrants about their plans to settle down. This variable was dichotomous: a value of 0 indicates that older migrants have no intention to settle down, while a value of 1 indicates they have the intention to settle down.

Control variables

Referring to previous studies [46, 47], the following variables were included as control variables in the analysis: gender, age, marital status, relationship with children, per capita monthly household income, disability status, chronic pain, educational level, number of chronic diseases, mental health, and self-rated health. The complete table of measurement methods for the control variables related to this study is provided in Supplementary Table 2 for details.

Analysis strategies

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. First, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was used to assess the internal consistency of the scale. Then, since key variables did not meet the conditions of normal distribution, Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to test the correlations between variables. Harman’s single-factor analysis was used to test for common method bias in this study.

Mediating effects were tested using Model 4 in the PROCESS macro program developed by Hayes. For testing the moderating effect of settlement intention on the relationship between grandparental childcare intensity and social isolation through the number of social activities, Model 14 was used in the latter half of the pathway. We used the Bootstrap sampling method with 5000 repeated samplings and constructed 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. If the confidence interval does not include 0, the effect is considered significant.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Flow characteristics

This study initially surveyed 1,032 older adults, among whom older migrants accounted for 80.33% (829 people). As illustrated in Figure 2 in terms of the geographical distribution of older migrants, the sample showed a prominent southern dominance: 80.46% (667 people) held household registrations in southern China, while only 19.54% (162 people) were from northern China. Inter-provincial mobility was particularly prominent, with 74.55% (618 people) being inter-provincial migrants and 25.45% (211 people) being intra-provincial migrants. Regarding settlement intention, the proportion of older adults with long-term settlement intentions (474 people, 57.18%) was slightly higher than those without (355 people, 42.82%). Notably, the sample data showed that caring for grandchildren was the primary driver of migration among older adults (432 people, 52.11%).

Sample characteristics and group differences

After excluding non-migrants, 829 migrant elders remained (381 males, 448 females; mean age = 69.59 ± 4.94 years). Social isolation prevalence was 26.78% (222 cases). Common method bias test

This study used Harman’s single-factor test to examine potential common method variance. Exploratory factor analysis showed 4 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor explained 26.596% of the variance. When the variance explained by the first unrotated factor does not exceed 40% of the total variance, we can conclude that there is no significant common method bias. Thus, this study had no serious common method bias.

Correlation analysis of primary variables

Spearman’s tests (Table 2) revealed positive correlations between grandparental childcare frequency and social participation (r = 0.188, P < 0.01) and between childcare frequency and LSNS-6 scores (r = 0.125, P < 0.01). Social participation also positively correlated with LSNS-6 scores (reflecting a negative association with social isolation) (r = 0.344, P < 0.01).

Examination of the mediating effects of social participation between grandparental childcare and social isolation

This study used Model 4 in the SPSS-PROCESS macro to analyze mediating effects, aiming to explore how the frequency of grandparental childcare among older migrants affects social isolation through the number of social activities. We controlled for variables such as gender, age, marital status, relationship with children, per capita monthly household income, disability status, chronic pain, educational level, number of chronic diseases, mental health, and self-rated health. We then constructed a mediation model with the frequency of grandparental childcare as the predictor, number of social activities as the mediator, and degree of social isolation as the outcome.

We used the Bootstrap method with 5000 resamplings to determine significance, based on whether the 95% confidence interval (CI) included 0. The results (Table 3) showed the following: The frequency of grandparental childcare was directly and positively associated with LSNS-6 scores for older migrants (β = 2.841, t = 3.018, P < 0.01), indicating that more frequent grandparental childcare helped reduce their social isolation. When including the number of social activities as a mediator, the direct effect of grandparental childcare remained significant (β = 1.860, t = 2.040, P < 0.05), and it is also significantly and positively associated with the number of social activities (β = 0.636, t = 3.720, P < 0.01). The number of social activities is significantly and positively associated with LSNS-6 scores (β = 1.544, t = 8.333, P < 0.01). Bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap tests showed the mediating effect of social participation level was 0.981 (95%CI [0.453, 1.576]), accounting for 34.53% of the total effect. This confirms that social participation plays a partial mediating role between grandparental childcare and social isolation.

Test of moderated mediation model

This study used Model 14 in the SPSS-PROCESS macro to test whether settlement intention moderates the latter half of the mediation pathway (number of social activities → degree of social isolation). We included control variables such as gender, age, marital status, relationship with children, per capita monthly household income, disability status, chronic pain, educational level, number of chronic diseases, mental health, and self-rated health. We then constructed a moderated mediation model with the frequency of grandparental childcare as the independent variable, number of social activities as the mediator, degree of social isolation as the dependent variable, and settlement intention as the moderator.

We used the Bootstrap method with 5000 resamplings to test significance, based on whether the 95% CI included 0. Results from Model 2 in Table 4 showed the interaction term between social participation level and settlement intention is significantly and negatively associated with scores on the social network scale (β=−1.016, t=−2.675, P < 0.01). This confirms that settlement intention moderates the association between the number of social activities and the degree of social isolation. Specifically, the strength of social activities’ effect in alleviating social isolation varied significantly with individuals’ settlement intention. We used simple slope analysis to explain the moderating effect of settlement intention. The results (Fig.3) showed the following: For those without settlement intention, the number of social activities is significantly and positively associated with LSNS-6 scores (i.e., associated with lower social isolation) (B = 2.222, t = 7.072, P < 0.01). For those with settlement intention, this protective effect of social participation was significantly weaker (B = 1.206, t = 5.376, P < 0.01).

Sensitivity analyses

Changing estimation methods

To assess the robustness of model estimation, we re-estimated the benchmark model using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). Prior to regression analysis, we first conducted a multicollinearity test. The maximum Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) of all variables was 1.09, which is far below the empirical critical value of 10 [48], indicating no severe multicollinearity among variables. Subsequently, we used the Breusch-Pagan/Cook-Weisberg test to examine heteroskedasticity, with a result of P = 0.076, suggesting no significant heteroskedasticity in the model.

Controlling for community-level cluster effects

Given that this study adopted a multistage random sampling method, the data may have a cluster effect at the community level. To address this, we incorporated cluster-robust standard errors at the community level into the models to correct for potential standard error bias. In addition, we conducted a robustness test by removing control variables. The results (see Supplementary Table 3, Model 3) show that after accounting for the cluster effect and changing the set of control variables, the estimated results of the core variables remain stable, which further supports the reliability of the main conclusions.

Alternative measurement of core variables

To further test the robustness of the models, we adopted alternative measurement and estimation methods for the core variables.

First, we converted the Grandparental Childcare variable into ordered dummy variables and re-estimated the models. The results (see Supplementary Table 4) show that the direction, magnitude, and significance of the variable’s impact on Social Isolation have not undergone substantial changes, indicating that the model estimation results have good robustness.

Second, we adopted the quantile regression method to examine the impact of the LSNS-6 scale across different quantiles. The results (see Supplementary Table 5) show that at the 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 0.90 quantiles (corresponding to groups with moderate-high to extremely low social isolation), the positive effect of grandparental childcare on reducing social isolation remains significant and stable. This echoes the core conclusions of the main analysis, strongly verifying the robustness of the results. Notably, at the 0.1 quantile (corresponding to the group with the highest level of social isolation), this effect did not reach statistical significance. This finding not only does not undermine the main conclusion but also provides valuable supplementary information for understanding the boundary conditions of grandparental childcare’s role.

Discussion

Based on a sample of older migrants in Shenzhen, a megacity in China, this study constructed a moderated mediation model to systematically examine the association mechanism between grandparental childcare and social isolation. It aimed to test the association between grandparental childcare and social isolation among older migrants, as well as the roles of social participation and settlement intention in this process. The findings are as follows: (1) Higher frequency of childcare is significantly associated with lower social isolation among older migrants. (2) Higher frequency of grandparental childcare is associated with a higher level of social participation among older migrants, which in turn is associated with lower social isolation. (3) Compared with older migrants who have settlement intentions, higher social participation is more strongly associated with lower social isolation among those without settlement intentions.

Existing studies generally confirm that grandparental childcare is positively associated with the physical and mental health of older adults [49–51], especially the positive correlation between non-coresident grandparental childcare and subjective well-being [52]. However, other studies have found that childcare is associated with adverse outcomes in the context of guardianship care lacking financial support from adult children [53]. This study further found that the frequency of grandparental childcare is negatively associated with social isolation among older migrants, which is consistent with the conclusion of Tang et al. (2020) that intergenerational support is associated with the expansion of older adults’ social networks [54]. Although some scholars worry that grandparental childcare may take up social time [12], for older migrants, the support of internet technology for cross-regional social interaction and the strengthening effect of “family happiness” in traditional Chinese family ethics on family social networks can, to a certain extent, be associated with lower social isolation related to geographical mobility. Therefore, this study believes that the accumulation of family social capital brought by such intergenerational interaction provides a unique social support system for older migrants.

The mediating effect of social participation verifies the spillover mechanism of the social functions of intergenerational childcare. This study found that higher frequency of grandparental childcare is indirectly associated with lower social isolation through its association with higher social participation. Unfortunately, few existing studies directly explore the mechanism of social participation between grandparental childcare and social isolation. However, existing research provides reliable support for this finding. Yan Yu et al. (2022) found that providing grandparental childcare is significantly associated with a higher likelihood of social participation among older adults [46]. In addition, Zhang and Li (2022) found that higher social participation among older adults is associated with expanded social networks [55]. Specifically, in the process of childcare, older migrants gain opportunities to establish social connections with peers through accompanying grandchildren in outdoor activities. This expansion of social interaction based on childcare scenarios breaks the closed nature of traditional housework, forming a unique transformation path from intergenerational childcare to social participation.

The moderating effect of settlement intention shows a “double-edged sword” characteristic, forming a theoretical dialogue with existing studies. Yang Ni et al. (2018) found that older migrants with more active social participation are more likely to have stronger settlement intentions [36]. However, interestingly, our results contradict Hypothesis 3b. We found that the association between social participation and lower social isolation is stronger among the group without settlement intentions. This paradox can be explained by the regional characteristics of the Shenzhen sample (74.55% are migrants from other provinces). The amplifying effect of cultural barriers makes those without settlement intentions form a “sojourner mentality”: when moving to a new place, the original support networks of older migrants may collapse, and it is difficult to form new social support networks in a short time [56]. In contrast, those with strong settlement intentions face structural constraints such as language barriers [57] and customs differences in in-depth social interactions, may lead to a social dilemma of ‘high participation - low identification’. This finding highlights the complexity of social integration among older migrants, suggesting that subsequent studies should pay attention to the impact of regional cultural differences.

Besides, the sensitivity analysis results indicate that the association between the independent variable and social isolation revealed by the main analysis is not limited to the mean level but remains stable across the middle-high to middle-low ranges of social isolation. The social networks of groups with moderate-high to extremely low isolation are still modifiable, and the mechanisms through which the independent variable acts (such as expanding social connections and increasing interaction frequency) are consistent. In contrast, the particularity of the group with extremely high isolation (e.g., dominated by structural barriers or irreversible individual characteristics) excludes them from the general law of the main effect. This finding does not negate the validity of the main conclusion; instead, through heterogeneity analysis at the quantile level, it further clarifies the applicable scope of the independent variable’s effect, enhancing the reliability of the research conclusions and the accuracy of practical guidance.

The results of this study have important policy implications. The construction of age-friendly communities should be accelerated, focusing on the following aspects: (1) Embed intergenerational interaction spaces in community aging-friendly renovations to promote the transformation of childcare scenarios into social scenarios; (2) Establish cross-cultural adaptation support systems to help older migrants break social barriers through activities such as language training and folk culture popularization; (3) Organize inclusive community activities during traditional festivals to strengthen older migrants’ sense of participation efficacy. These measures can effectively connect with the existing framework for building age-friendly communities [58], achieving the dual goals of supporting intergenerational childcare and promoting social integration.

It should be acknowledged that this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design makes it difficult to establish causal relationships between variables. Although the theoretical framework and DAG (Fig. 1) set Grandparental Childcare as the antecedent variable and Social Isolation as the outcome variable, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be excluded. For example, older adults with higher levels of Social Isolation may be more inclined to return to family roles, thereby increasing their participation in Grandparental Childcare and further restricting their social interactions. Future studies should adopt longitudinal data or natural experiment methods to more robustly verify the “double-edged sword” effect and causal pathways of variables such as Settlement Intention on Social Isolation. Second, there are certain limitations in variable measurement. Grandparental Childcare was measured only in a four-category manner, which may lead to the loss of some information. Future research should conduct a comprehensive investigation from dimensions such as care frequency, duration, and broader intergenerational support. Meanwhile, although the Social Participation variable is count data, we mainly used linear models for analysis to maintain model consistency and interpretation convenience. Future studies could attempt to use models more suitable for count data (e.g., Poisson regression or negative binomial regression) for verification. In addition, although Social Participation (behavior) and Social Isolation (state) are conceptually distinct, there is some overlap in the content of the scales used (e.g., LSNS-6), which may lead to overestimation of the mediating effect. Future research should use measurement tools with higher discriminant validity for testing. Third, the sample has geographical bias, with southern samples accounting for 80.46%, which may weaken the explanatory power of regional differences. Future studies need to introduce more nationally representative samples to improve the generalizability of the conclusions. Finally, although we controlled for multiple demographic and socioeconomic covariates and adopted a moderated mediation model to enhance internal validity, residual confounding may still exist. For example, unobserved variables such as personality traits, social competence before migration, and intergenerational relationship quality may simultaneously affect Grandparental Childcare behavior and Social Isolation risk, which deserve in-depth exploration in subsequent studies.

Conclusion

This study reveals the association mechanism linking grandparental childcare to lower social isolation among older migrants, with social participation acting as a mediator, and finds that settlement intention has a unique moderating effect on the relationship between social participation and social isolation. Compared with those with clear settlement intentions, the ‘temporary residence mentality’ is associated with a stronger positive association between social participation and lower social isolation. This finding highlights the key role of cultural adaptation in the social integration of older migrants. Subsequent studies should deepen the research on the path of “cultural barriers”, focusing on mediating mechanisms such as language ability, customs, and social capital reconstruction, to provide theoretical support for formulating targeted social integration policies and improve the health and well-being of older migrants.
