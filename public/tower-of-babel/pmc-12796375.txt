Foundation model-guided multi-view semi-supervised CT segmentation of liver tumors in resource-constrained settings
Authors: Yuchuan Jiang, Yao Du, Kai Xiong, Kuiyuan Huang, Tengzheng Li, Zhangyun Li, Morang Zhang, Xiaoning Gan, Qiang Li, Junjie Liang, Mingrong Cao, Jian Sun, Junfu Wang, Jinzhong Duanmu, Xueqin Li, Zhili Wen, Qunguang Jiang, Xin Yu, Shangxiang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a label-efficient pipeline for CT auto-segmentation in resource-constrained settings. The framework couples a semi-supervised segmentation backbone with a foundation model-guided regularizer to strengthen the learning from scarce annotations. To better exploit volumetric context, we introduce a multi-view collaborative learning procedure that performs view-specific inference to form a unified supervision signal that suppresses view-dependent noise and improves mask fidelity. We evaluate on a public CT benchmark with varying numbers of labeled scans. In the highly label-limited regime, the approach yields strong accuracy with average Dice 83.79% for the liver and 60.08% for the tumor using 20 labeled cases, outperforms existing segmentation methods. By reducing contouring from hours to seconds, improving small-structure recovery and boundary fidelity, and requiring no interactive prompts, the method offers a plug-and-play path to deployment and a reliable basis for downstream radiomics and longitudinal monitoring.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

The liver serves as the link between metabolic homeostasis and digestive function, and is an extremely important organ in human physiology. Its critical role is profoundly challenged by hepatic malignancies, which constitute a major source of cancer-related morbidity and mortality globally, posing a formidable threat to public health1. The oncological landscape of the liver is notably complex, encompassing not only primary neoplasms like hepatocellular carcinoma but also a high frequency of metastatic deposits from distant malignancies2. The radiological assessment of this complex pathology is paramount, and among the array of imaging modalities, multi-phase contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) has emerged as the clinical standard, affording unparalleled spatial and contrast resolution for the detailed interrogation of hepatic parenchyma, its associated vasculature, and focal lesions3,4. The translation of these rich imaging data into actionable clinical intelligence is contingent upon the accurate and precise segmentation of the liver and its intrahepatic tumors5,6. This process of delineation provides the quantitative foundation for modern oncology. Volumetric and morphometric analyses derived from segmentation serve as indispensable biomarkers for differential diagnosis, prognostication, and disease stratification. This quantitative imperative is epitomized by standardized frameworks such as the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors, which mandate objective measurements to supersede subjective radiological impressions in the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy7. Furthermore, the clinical utility of precise segmentation is profound and far-reaching: it is instrumental in defining surgical resection margins, delineating target volumes for ablative therapies like stereotactic radiation and radiofrequency ablation, and quantifying tumor burden to guide and monitor locoregional treatments8.

Despite its foundational importance, the conventional practice of manual contouring is a significant impediment to clinical workflow efficiency and scientific rigor. This method is not only laborious but is also plagued by substantial inter- and intra-observer variability, which compromises the reproducibility and reliability of clinical data9. In clinical practice, the manual segmentation of liver tumors is a time-consuming and subjective task, demanding considerable effort and expertise from radiologists. This inefficiency creates a bottleneck in clinical workflow, delaying critical analyses for diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up. The inherent variability in manual contours also introduces significant challenges for quantitative analysis and reproducible research. In response, automated segmentation methods powered by deep learning have emerged, demonstrating great promise10. However, the clinical adoption of these deep learning models, such as the widely used 3D U-Net11, has been limited by specific, persistent challenges in liver tumor segmentation. The key difficulties of this task include the indistinct boundaries between tumors and the surrounding liver parenchyma, especially in non-contrast or poorly enhanced scans. Meanwhile, the lesions exhibit extreme heterogeneity, varying dramatically in size, shape, number, location, and appearance, which can confound even sophisticated algorithms12. In addition, these deep learning models require large-scale annotated data for training, while the liver and tumor of CT images need to be annotated by domain experts, making it often difficult to obtain them in large quantities13.

More recently, the advent of Foundation Models14 has signaled a paradigm shift, offering a potential solution to the data scarcity problem in medical imaging. These powerful models are pre-trained on massive, diverse datasets, allowing them to learn a broad range of features and representations that are highly transferable. This capability is a game-changer, as it circumvents the need for large, meticulously annotated datasets for every new task, which has long been a bottleneck in medical AI development. Foundation models offer transformative potential for a wide range of clinical applications, including disease detection15,16, where they can identify subtle pathological patterns with unprecedented accuracy; report generation17,18, where they can automatically generate comprehensive and structured radiology reports from imaging data; and image understanding19,20, where they can interpret and contextualize complex anatomical structures and their relationships. Their ability to generalize across different imaging modalities and X-ray, and various anatomical structures makes them highly effective for most medical imaging analysis tasks21. A key advantage of foundation models is their capacity to leverage transfer learning, applying knowledge gained from one domain to another, which enhances performance even when labeled data is limited. This is particularly beneficial in the medical field, where high-quality annotated datasets are often scarce due to privacy concerns and the specialized expertise required for accurate labeling22. The application of foundation models in medical imaging is still in its early stages, but the results to date have been highly promising, suggesting they are poised to become a cornerstone of future medical imaging analysis tools, driving significant improvements in diagnostic accuracy and clinical workflow efficiency23.

For medical image segmentation, MedSAM24 is a medical-specific foundation model that supports universal medical image segmentation. It is developed on a large-scale medical imaging dataset and is based on the Transformer architecture of the Segment Anything Model (SAM)25. MedSAM is a semi-automatic medical segmentation model that requires users to manually draw bounding boxes as prompts to guide the segmentation process. Although MedSAM performs exceptionally well in most medical imaging segmentation tasks, it still has certain limitations. Their native 2D architectural design inherently disregards the volumetric context and inter-slice spatial coherence crucial for anatomical fidelity, often yielding discontinuous and erroneous results. Despite subsequent 3D segmentation foundation models have been proposed for 3D medical images26,27 like SAM-Med3D28, they still necessitate manual initialization via user-provided prompts like points or boxes. This perpetuates a semi-automated workflow, thereby failing to fully alleviate the operational bottleneck and human-induced variability they were intended to solve.

Therefore, despite significant progress, a critical gap persists. There is an urgent need for a solution that transcends the current limitations and delivers on the promise of a fully automatic, accurate, and label-efficient liver tumor segmentation method. Leveraging the pre-trained knowledge from foundation models can be used to facilitate efficient learning with limited labeled data. However, these large models are relatively computationally expensive and require manual prompting for each training stage. Instead, we aim to utilize foundation models to assist in training a dedicated, task-specific segmentation model. This approach effectively harnesses the power of foundation models while avoiding their significant deployment and interaction costs. Besides, for 3D CT images, utilizing information from various perspectives, including axial, coronal, and sagittal views, is efficient and crucial to improve the overall 3D segmentation performance29. These views provide complementary information about the volumetric relationships and morphological characteristics of anatomical structures. Fusing this data leads to a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying anatomy, which is essential for accurate segmentation30,31.

In this paper, we propose a foundation model-driven multi-view collaborative learning framework for label-efficient liver tumor segmentation from CT images. The framework utilizes a pre-trained 3D foundation model to assist in the training of a task-specific liver tumor segmentation model with limited annotations. By capturing anatomical structures from various perspectives for the fusion of different features to enhance the learning process, the framework enhances the quality of pseudo-labels to improve the overall performance of the segmentation model. The framework can be used for efficient adaptation of foundation models to the downstream liver tumor segmentation task with only one or a few annotated cases, assisting radiologists by providing accurate and automated liver tumor segmentation results, indicating potential for real-world usage as a promising image-level assistant tool. Besides, the model is efficient enough, which has fewer trainable parameters and faster inference speed compared with segmentation foundation models like SAM-Med3D, which is highly suitable for deployment scenarios.

Results

Study design

The overview of this work is illustrated in Fig. 1. In this study, we use the Liver Tumor Segmentation Benchmark (LiTS)6 for training and evaluation of our method, which is a CT dataset focused on the segmentation of livers and their tumors. This dataset collects data from 7 different medical centers, including 131 cases in the training set. All the scans are resampled to the same resolution with intensity normalized to zero mean and unit variance. In our experiments, we meticulously designed a robust evaluation protocol to assess the performance of our models under varying conditions of data scarcity. We began by partitioning our dataset, allocating 100 scans for the training phase and 33 scans for validation. The validation set remained constant throughout all experiments, providing a stable evaluation on the same data for performance comparison. A core component of our experimental design was the exploration of a semi-supervised learning paradigm. Within the training set of 100 scans, we conducted a series of experiments by systematically varying the amount of labeled data. Specifically, we used subsets of 1, 3, 5, 10, and 20 scans as our labeled data, with the remaining training scans serving as unlabeled data. This approach allowed us to simulate real-world scenarios where expert annotations are limited and expensive to acquire. To quantitatively evaluate the segmentation results, we use four complementary evaluation metrics. Dice similarity coefficient (Dice) and Jaccard Index (Jaccard), two region-based metrics, are used to measure the region mismatch. Average surface distance (ASD) and 95% Hausdorff Distance (95HD), two boundary-based metrics, are used to evaluate the boundary errors between the segmentation results and the ground truth.

Accurate segmentation with limited annotation

Our proposed method achieved superior performance in accurate and automatic liver tumor segmentation. To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, we compared our approach against several representative segmentation frameworks, including state-of-the-art fully-supervised (FS) segmentation methods like 3D U-Net11, as well as a representative semi-supervised segmentation framework, Mean teacher32. We present the comparison of using the same annotated samples for training to ensure a fair comparison. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of DSC scores for the three methods. Overall, the fully-supervised 3D U-Net demonstrates the lowest performance across all evaluation metrics, as the model is developed using only the annotated cases. For example, it achieved a Dice score of only 42.82% with one annotated case and reached 69.78% with 20 annotated cases. While the semi-supervised Mean teacher model shows a slight improvement over the fully-supervised 3D U-Net, their performance gains were marginal when the annotated cases are limited. Specifically, with only one annotated case, the Dice performance of Mean Teacher was 52.56%, and it improved to 74.97% with 20 annotated cases. In contrast, our proposed method achieved a significant increase in segmentation accuracy under the same data conditions. With just one annotated case, our method’s Dice score was 71.63%, a substantial improvement of 19.07% over the Mean Teacher model. As the number of annotated cases increased to 20, our model’s Dice score reached 79.46%, maintaining its lead. This significant performance leap is attributed to our collaborative learning mechanism, which effectively leverages the pre-trained knowledge from the segmentation foundation model to supervise and optimize a task-specific segmentation model. By using the foundation model’s output as a supervisory signal, our approach refines the segmentation and prompt generation capabilities of the task-specific model without requiring any manual intervention. This collaborative process allows our model to overcome the dependency on prompts, enabling truly automated segmentation and demonstrating the power of leveraging generalist model knowledge for enhanced accuracy in specific medical imaging tasks like liver tumor segmentation. Efficiency of multi-view fusion

To evaluate the contribution of our proposed multi-view fusion strategy, we conducted an ablation study. We compared the performance of our model, which incorporates collaborative learning from axial, coronal, and sagittal views, against three variants of Single-View Models, which use only one specific view for segmentation, mirroring the approach of traditional segmentation methods.

Our results qualitatively demonstrate the significant benefits of the multi-view collaborative approach. Single-view models often struggled with volumetric consistency, especially in regions where tumor boundaries were indistinct in the axial plane. It also failed to accurately capture the full extent of tumors that were small or poorly contrasted in a single view. The fusion of multiple views shows some improvement, as the simple combination of results helped to smooth out some of the inter-slice inconsistencies. In contrast to single-view models, our framework produced segmentation masks that were smooth and anatomically consistent in 3D. The model successfully reconciled ambiguous boundaries by leveraging complementary information from all views simultaneously. For example, a tumor that was difficult to delineate in the axial view might have a clearer boundary in the sagittal or coronal view, and our model effectively used this information to refine the final 3D segmentation. The resulting tumor volumes were more coherent and accurate, closely matching the ground truth, particularly for small and irregularly shaped lesions.

Low computational overhead for automatic inference

In addition to high segmentation accuracy, our framework is also engineered for exceptional efficiency, addressing critical limitations of existing approaches. For general-purpose foundation models, when applied directly, they demand extensive manual prompting for liver and tumor from each case, creating a bottleneck that prevents true automatic segmentation. This process is not only time-consuming but also requires specialized anatomical knowledge, making it impractical for large-scale clinical use. Meanwhile, while foundation model-based adaptation methods offer some level of automation, they still fundamentally rely on invoking the large, computationally heavy foundation model during inference. This dependency not only inflates computational costs, leading to high latency and power consumption, but also hinders deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as on-premise hospital servers or mobile diagnostic units, due to their lack of a lightweight design. The sheer size and computational demand of these models make real-time analysis difficult, posing a significant barrier to their widespread adoption in routine clinical workflows.

In stark contrast, our framework eliminates these drawbacks by decoupling the training and inference stages. During the inference phase, it exclusively leverages a compact, task-specific model, obviating the need for either manual prompting or calls to large foundation models. This design choice is pivotal for efficiency. With a parameter count comparable to a standard 3D U-Net, our model drastically reduces computational burdens, allowing for rapid processing of medical scans. This makes it ideal for real-time or near-real-time applications, where timely results are critical for patient care. Moreover, the framework’s flexible architecture supports adaptable backbone models, enabling researchers and clinicians to fine-tune the model’s complexity to match available resources without compromising performance. This modularity further boosts training efficiency by allowing for the use of pre-existing, optimized architectures. This synergy of high accuracy, full automation, and unparalleled efficiency positions our method as a pragmatic and powerful solution for clinical settings where computational resources are often limited, enabling seamless and swift integration into real-world workflows. This combination of accuracy and efficiency makes our method a practical solution for clinical applications where computational resources may be limited.

Discussion

Liver tumor segmentation plays a pivotal role in modern medical imaging diagnosis and treatment planning. However, traditional manual segmentation methods suffer from inefficiency and inconsistent results, significantly hindering clinical workflows. The foundation model-driven multi-view collaborative learning framework proposed in this study integrates pre-trained 3D medical segmentation foundation models with lightweight task-specific models, achieving high-precision automatic segmentation with limited annotated data. This innovative approach not only markedly improves segmentation accuracy and consistency but, more importantly, addresses two long-standing challenges in medical image analysis: data scarcity and computational resource constraints.

From a clinical application perspective, the value of this technology is primarily reflected in three aspects. First, the fully automated segmentation process reduces the time required for manual delineation from hours to seconds, significantly enhancing radiologists’ workflow efficiency and allowing them to focus more on diagnostic decision-making rather than tedious image processing33. This shift not only enhances workflow efficiency but also reduces the risk of human error and inter-observer variability inherent in manual segmentation. The consistency provided by an automated model ensures that segmentation results are reproducible and standardized across different practitioners and clinical sites. Second, the multi-view feature fusion strategy effectively captures the complex and often irregular morphological characteristics of liver tumors in three-dimensional space34. This provides a far more reliable and detailed quantitative reference than visual inspection alone, which is crucial for determining precise surgical resection margins and ensuring complete tumor removal35. The accurate 3D volumetric data aids surgeons in pre-operative planning, enabling them to visualize the tumor’s exact location, size, and relationship to critical blood vessels and other organs. This allows for safer and more effective surgical interventions. Furthermore, these high-fidelity 3D models are invaluable for delineating accurate target volumes for stereotactic radiotherapy and planning precise ablation therapy36. Particularly in tumor response assessment, the objective volumetric measurements provided by this method are more sensitive and reproducible than traditional RECIST criteria-based manual measurements. This quantitative objectivity facilitates more precise monitoring of treatment efficacy, enabling clinicians to make data-driven adjustments to therapy and providing an early detection mechanism for tumor recurrence37.

Methodologically, the design of this framework makes it particularly suitable for deployment in real-world clinical settings. By leveraging the vast prior knowledge of foundation models to guide the training of lightweight models, our system maintains high performance while significantly reducing the reliance on a large volume of annotated data38. This is a critical advantage for hospitals and research institutions with limited access to expert-annotated datasets. It democratizes access to advanced AI tools, as it enables even resource-limited facilities to rapidly and cost-effectively establish usable segmentation models without a large-scale data collection effort. This is particularly relevant for rare tumor types where large datasets are simply unavailable. Furthermore, the framework’s elegant two-stage design is a key to its practicality. During the inference phase, only the parameter-efficient task-specific model needs to be executed, completely eliminating the need to invoke the computationally intensive foundation model. This design choice ensures smooth and rapid operation on conventional medical workstations, which typically lack the high-end GPUs required to run massive foundation models. By removing this technical barrier, our framework guarantees low latency and high throughput, making it feasible for real-time analysis and daily clinical practice39. The low computational footprint and high processing speed make it a plug-and-play solution that can be seamlessly integrated into existing radiology reporting systems. Evaluation results prove the robustness and consistency of segmentation results across different center data. This indicates that our model has not over-relied on center-specific imaging features but has instead learned generalizable characteristics for segmentation.

Despite its promising performance and clinical utility, our proposed framework has several limitations that warrant discussion. For example, while our model excels with common liver tumor types, its generalization to rare or highly heterogeneous tumors may be suboptimal due to limited representation in our training data. Clinical AI requires broad generalization across diverse populations, which needs further validation. Besides, CT acquisition protocol variations directly impact image resolution, tissue contrast, and noise characteristics. These differences can challenge model generalization, as features learned from one protocol may not transfer reliably to others. Future work will evaluate our method’s robustness across such variations and explore protocol-adaptive strategies to enhance stability. Looking ahead, this research opens several new avenues in the field of medical image analysis. On one hand, the framework’s core principle of knowledge transfer from a generalist model to a specialized one is highly extensible. Integrating active learning-driven high-impact case selection into a federated learning setup would be a valuable extension. Local sites could first identify uncertain or high-priority unlabeled cases via the framework, then collectively prioritize these cases for centralized annotation, ensuring annotation resources are allocated to the most beneficial samples across decentralized datasets40. The framework can be adapted and fine-tuned for multi-modal segmentation, integrating information from different imaging modalities such as CT, MRI, and PET scans to enhance diagnostic capabilities for complex and heterogeneous cases41. For instance, combining CT’s anatomical detail with PET’s metabolic information could lead to more accurate tumor boundary delineation and viability assessment. On the other hand, the high-precision segmentation results provide a foundation for extracting rich quantitative radiomic features from both the liver and tumor tissues. These features hold immense promise as novel prognostic biomarkers that can be correlated with patient outcomes, treatment response, and genetic information42. The proposed automatic liver tumor segmentation has the potential for application in clinical workflows. From a clinical perspective, the proposed automatic liver and tumor segmentation framework holds significant translational potential across multiple interventional and therapeutic workflows. In surgical planning, precise 3D segmentation enables accurate delineation of tumor boundaries and adjacent vascular structures, supporting surgeons in defining optimal resection margins while minimizing the risk of damaging critical anatomy43. For interventional radiology applications such as Y-90 radioembolization, radiotherapy, and transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), the volumetric segmentation facilitates accurate dose calculation, catheter trajectory optimization, and individualized treatment planning44,45. Similarly, for thermal or microwave ablation procedures, the system provides a reliable basis for defining ablation safety margins and verifying complete tumor coverage post-procedure. These advancements will facilitate the transition of liver tumor diagnosis and treatment from an empirical, qualitative approach to data-driven precision medicine, ultimately enabling the development of personalized therapeutic strategies and improving long-term patient outcomes. In essence, this study not only provides an efficient technical solution but also represents an innovative paradigm for integrating cutting-edge artificial intelligence with clinical needs, offering valuable insights for the future development of intelligent healthcare.

Methods

Backbone segmentation framework

The network architecture employed is a 3D U-Net, a fully convolutional neural network meticulously crafted for the task of volumetric image segmentation. This model distinguishes itself with a symmetric encoder–decoder structure and incorporates crucial skip connections, enabling it to effectively fuse high-level semantic context with fine-grained spatial information. This design is particularly advantageous for medical imaging, where precise localization and context-aware segmentation are paramount.

The encoder path, or the contracting path, systematically downsamples the input volume to extract hierarchical features. It consists of a series of convolutional blocks, each containing two 3 × 3 × 3 convolutions. These are followed by batch normalization to stabilize training and a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function to introduce non-linearity. To halve the spatial dimensions and increase the receptive field, a 2 × 2 × 2 max-pooling operation with a stride of two is applied after each block. Concurrently, the number of feature channels is doubled, allowing the network to capture increasingly abstract and compact representations of the input data at each deeper layer.

The decoder path, or the expansive path, reconstructs the segmentation mask by upsampling the feature maps back to the original input resolution. At each stage, the feature map is upsampled, often through a transposed convolution or trilinear interpolation, and then concatenated with the corresponding high-resolution feature map from the encoder path via a skip connection. This is a pivotal step, as the skip connection injects essential spatial information, helping the network recover details lost during the downsampling process and refine the segmentation boundaries. The combined features are then processed by another sequence of convolutional blocks.

The final layer is a 1 × 1 × 1 convolution that maps the feature channels to the desired number of output classes, producing a pixel-wise segmentation map for the 3D volume. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, a dropout layer is strategically incorporated at the bottleneck of the U-Net and in the final upsampling stage. This robust and efficient architecture is exceptionally well-suited for complex medical image analysis tasks, such as tumor or organ segmentation, where both global context and local details are critical for accurate results.

Task definition of label-efficient liver tumor segmentation

The core goal of label-efficient segmentation is to build a model capable of accurately segmenting liver and tumor from CT images, making full use of limited labeled data. One direct and practical method is semi-supervised learning by training with limited labeled data and a large amount of unlabeled data.

In the task of semi-supervised segmentation, we work with two types of data: labeled images and unlabeled images. Labeled images contain detailed annotations for specific structures, which serve as the core basis for model training. However, due to the time-consuming nature of their generation and the reliance on professional expertise, their quantity is often limited. In contrast, unlabeled images, though lacking such annotations, contain abundant potential information that can provide significant support for the optimization of segmentation models. This approach aims to integrate the strengths of both types of data: using labeled data to lay the foundation for the model’s recognition of target structures, and using unlabeled data to supplement information on data distribution to enhance the model’s generalization ability.

We define the dataset D for training into a small subset with M cases as DL={xli,yi}i=1M based on the available budget, while the remaining unlabeled set with N unlabeled cases as DU={xui}i=1N, where xl and xu denote the labeled and unlabeled input images, and y denotes the corresponding ground truth of labeled data. Generally, DL is a relatively small subset of the entire dataset D, which means M ≪ N. For segmentation task aims to build a data-efficient deep learning model to learn with the combination of densely labeled data DL and unlabeled data DU for training. Recent advances in semi-supervised medical image segmentation have largely centered on integrating this unlabeled data into the training process through an unsupervised regularization mechanism. Consequently, the model’s optimization is achieved by minimizing a combined loss function, comprising a supervised segmentation loss Lsup and an unsupervised regularization loss Lunsup. Foundation model for 3D medical image segmentation

A plethora of foundation models have been released for various computer vision tasks46,47, but most of these models rely on interactions between images and text. The SAM25, a foundation model for image segmentation, addresses this limitation by performing general segmentation tasks without the need for additional text guidance. However, its 2D architecture has inherent limitations when processing 3D CT images. To overcome this, we choose SAM-Med3D28 as the foundation model used in our framework.

SAM-Med3D employs a fully 3D learnable architecture designed to directly capture 3D spatial information from volumetric medical images. As shown in Fig. 5, its architecture is built upon three core components: a 3D Image Encoder, a 3D Prompt Encoder, and a 3D Mask Decoder. The 3D Image Encoder begins by embedding patches of the input image using a 3D convolution with a kernel size of (16, 16, 16). These embeddings are then combined with a learnable 3D absolute positional encoding (PE), which is a natural extension of SAM’s 2D positional encoding with an additional dimension. The patch embeddings are then fed into a 3D attention block. This module integrates 3D relative positional encoding into SAM’s multi-head self-attention (MHSA) to directly capture fine-grained 3D spatial details. In the 3D Prompt Encoder, sparse prompts are represented with 3D positional encoding (PE) to capture subtle 3D spatial differences, while dense prompts are processed using 3D convolutions. The 3D Mask Decoder integrates a 3D upsampling process utilizing 3D transposed convolutions. This process allows the decoder to fuse image embeddings, prompt features, and output tokens to ultimately generate the segmentation probability maps.

Overview of the network architecture of the 3D segmentation foundation model SAM-Med3D28 used in our study.

The model comprises a 3D image encoder with patch embedding and 3D positional encoding, a 3D prompt encoder handling sparse and dense prompts, and a 3D mask decoder with transposed convolutions to fuse image, prompt, and token features; this fully 3D design captures volumetric context and supplies robust pseudo-labels from prompts to regularize the task-specific learner.

This pure 3D design enables SAM-Med3D to better capture 3D spatial information and significantly reduces the number of prompts required. It can accurately segment a variety of anatomical structures and lesions across different modalities with only a few prompt points.

Overview of the semi-supervised segmentation framework

To utilize unlabeled data for learning, the Mean Teacher framework32 is a widely adopted semi-supervised learning approach that leverages both labeled and unlabeled data to enhance model performance. Despite their efficiency in utilizing unlabeled data, these semi-supervised medical image segmentation methods may struggle to achieve significant performance gains when labeled data are extremely limited. The core issue is that with very little annotated data, the initial knowledge base is too small for the teacher model to generate high-quality pseudo-labels. This inherent lack of knowledge means the pseudo-labels, which are intended to provide additional supervision, may be noisy or inaccurate, failing to guide the student model effectively toward learning robust and accurate representations, making it difficult to bridge the performance gap with fully-supervised methods.

Foundation model-driven multi-view collaborative learning

Given the three-dimensional nature of anatomical structures in CT images, we introduce a multi-view collaborative learning framework to effectively leverage volumetric information for the segmentation. This framework enhances the segmentation process by integrating knowledge from different perspectives of the 3D volume. The core of our approach involves a multi-stage process. Initially, we employ the SemiSAM+ framework to process each view of the 3D volume independently. This means that for each perspective (e.g., axial, sagittal, coronal), a separate inference procedure is executed. During this stage, view-specific prompts are generated to guide the segmentation process, allowing the model to capture the unique anatomical details and contextual information present in each individual view. This parallel processing ensures that the model is exposed to a wide variety of features from all angles. After obtaining segmentation results for each view, a crucial step of multi-view fusion is performed. Instead of relying on a single view’s output, the predictions from all views are ensembled to form a unified, comprehensive segmentation map. This fusion process is a collaborative procedure designed to consolidate the strengths of each view while mitigating its individual weaknesses. By combining outputs from different perspectives, we can effectively suppress noise and artifacts that might be prominent in one view but less so in others. This collaborative integration helps to create a more robust and accurate segmentation result, which in turn provides a more reliable and complete learning signal. The ensembled result from the multi-view fusion is then used to guide the overall learning procedure of the Mean Teacher framework. This integrated feedback loop ensures that the student model learns from a more stable and accurate source of supervision, benefiting from the collective intelligence of all views. A key limitation of existing approaches is that, while they leverage the strong prior knowledge of foundation models to generate initial pseudo-labels for unlabeled data, this process is inherently restricted to a single, fixed view. Consequently, the generated pseudo-labels may appear plausible within the specific view but often exhibit poor generalization. Our multi-view collaborative learning framework addresses this limitation by introducing promptable segmentation from diverse perspectives. By fusing the predictions from these multiple views, we generate higher-quality pseudo-labels, which are significantly more reliable and accurate than those generated from a single view. Therefore, our method ensures a more reliable supervision signal for training as the reliable ensemble labels across multiple views are utilized. Ultimately, this multi-view collaborative learning framework significantly enhances segmentation performance, making the results more reliable for clinical applications where accurate and consistent anatomical segmentation is paramount. The ability to aggregate information across views makes the model more resilient to data inconsistencies and more capable of producing high-fidelity segmentation masks for complex 3D structures.

Training and implementation details

In our study, all of our experiments are implemented in Python with PyTorch, using an NVIDIA A100 GPU. For the task-specific segmentation, the 3D U-Net11 is used as our backbone network, which is a choice widely adopted in many semi-supervised approaches49,50. We use SAM-Med3D28 designed for general-purpose 3D medical image segmentation, as the foundation model in our work. In our training procedure, we use the Stochastic Gradient Descent optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.01, a weight decay of 1e-4, and a momentum of 0.9 to update the network parameters. The maximum training iterations are set to 30,000. We randomly crop 128 × 128 × 128 sub-volumes as the network input, and the final segmentation results are obtained using a sliding window strategy. We use the standard data augmentation techniques on-the-fly to avoid overfitting during the training procedure, including randomly flipping and rotating with 90, 180, and 270° along the axial plane51.

To quantitatively evaluate segmentation results, we employ a set of complementary metrics that assess different aspects of model performance. These metrics can be broadly categorized into two groups: region-based metrics and boundary-based metrics.

Region-Based Metrics: these metrics measure the overlap between the predicted segmentation and the ground truth, providing a high-level assessment of the region mismatch.

The first evaluation metric is Dice Similarity Coefficient (Dice), which is a widely used metric that measures the similarity between two regions. It’s calculated as twice the area of the intersection divided by the sum of the areas of both regions. The Dice score ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect overlap.
