Simulation-based modeling of aortic valve neocuspidization with oversized pericardial leaflets
Authors: Jalal Cherkaoui, Ines A Martínez, Yassine Toufique, James D St Louis, Mohammed Cherkaoui, Ignacio Lugones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAortic valve replacement in children is challenging. The lack of accommodation for somatic growth negatively impacts long-term outcomes. Data on the use of symmetrical oversized leaflets in neocuspidization are limited. We present a computer-based simulation of patient growth after a replacement with an oversized aortic valve leaflet.MethodsA new aortic valve design was modeled in silico. Three computer-simulated symmetrical leaflets, each 18 mm in nominal width, were constructed for 4 simulated aortic roots with different diameters (12, 14, 16, and 18 mm). Pressure was applied to the aortic root side to simulate diastolic pressure and valve closure. The morphology of the closed valve was evaluated across different settings to examine morphologic changes during somatic growth. Three parameters of oversizing, previously described in our in vitro and in vivo studies, were assessed across the simulations: windmill shape deviation, coaptation length, and billow below the annular plane distance.ResultsA decrease in oversizing parameters was observed as the diameter of the aortic root increased. The average deviation from the windmill shape, the coaptation length, and the distance from the annular plane to the leaflet billow all decreased consistently with growth, indicating morphological adaptation of the valve geometry. These correlated changes suggest that the valve design adapts to anatomical growth by altering its functional configuration without compromising coaptation.ConclusionsAchieving optimal outcomes in aortic valve replacement in children remains challenging. Neocuspidization with oversized aortic valve leaflets addresses important limitations of current valve replacement techniques. Larger leaflets support growth by maintaining proper coaptation.Key Words: aortic valve, valve replacement, neocuspidization, in silico

Geometric adaptation of an oversized AVaTAR valve preserves competence during growth.

Central Message

The AVaTAR neocuspidization approach uses intentional leaflet oversizing to provide a geometric mechanism that allows the new aortic valve to maintain competence throughout pediatric somatic growth.

Perspective

Pediatric aortic valve replacement is limited by the inability of current techniques to accommodate growth. Our findings show how intentional leaflet oversizing in AVaTAR neocuspidization approach preserves valve competence during somatic growth by geometric adaptation, supporting a rational surgical strategy for durable pediatric valve reconstruction.

Semilunar valve disease in the pediatric population presents unique challenges distinct from those of adult-acquired disease. Current valve replacement and repair are subject to both short- and long-term failures. The ideal valve replacement in the pediatric population would be competent and nonstenotic with excellent long-term durability and the ability to accommodate somatic growth.1 It would also be composed of autologous tissue, either from the patient's own tissue or bioengineered from native cells. Aortic valve disease may be either congenital or acquired. In the aortic position, bicuspid morphology predominates as a cause of stenosis and/or regurgitation in children and young adults, whereas in adults, degenerative calcific stenosis is the dominant form.2 Institutional review board/ethics review board approval was not applicable and was not required for this work. Patient informed written consent also was not required, because no human participants or identifiable patient information were involved in this study.

Surgical options for semilunar valve disease include valvuloplasty, valve replacement (mechanical or bioprosthetic), the Ross procedure, and leaflet neocuspidization techniques.3,4 In the aortic position, compared with mechanical or stented bioprosthetic replacement, neocuspidization techniques such as the Ozaki procedure avoid lifelong anticoagulation and preserve or increase effective/geometric orifice area by eliminating sewing rings, delivering favorable hemodynamics with low gradients.5,6 However, in children, any reconstructive or replacement strategy must contend with ongoing somatic growth, which can progressively alter valve-root relationships and compromise long-term success. In addition, current neocuspidization techniques have several significant limitations that affect long-term durability. The procedure has a steep learning curve, with unpredictable technical outcomes and limited reproducibility. The material currently used has unproven long-term durability as a result of tissue classification and inflammatory response.

Our group previously reported a new method for semilunar valve replacement, called aortic valve trileaflet autologous reconstruction (AVaTAR). This neocuspidization technique creates a completely symmetrical trileaflet valve, independent of native valve morphology, and intentionally oversized in pediatric candidates to preserve competence as anatomic dimensions enlarge over time.

Previous in vitro and acute in vivo studies from our group showed that the oversized AVaTAR valve is competent and nonstenotic, exhibiting 3 distinctive morphologic features: increased coaptation length, leaflet billowing below the annular plane, and a characteristic windmill shape.7,8 These features were clearly emphasized as pronounced oversizing during in vitro testing and mild oversizing during in vivo evaluation, which conceptually represent the configuration at the time of implantation and the late configuration after the patient approaches definitive size. However, the intermediate states—and the geometric pathway by which an AVaTAR valve adapts as the outflow tract enlarges—remain poorly understood.

In the present study, we address this gap using an in silico model of somatic growth, in which the aorta is represented as a cylindrical root and growth is modeled as a progressive increase in diameter. Using finite element simulations of diastolic closure, we evaluate how the closed-valve morphology evolves across growth conditions using the same 3 previously described indices (windmill shape deviation, coaptation length, and billow distance below the annular plane).

Methods

Building of the Aortic Root and Valve Model

A computational model of the aortic root and valve was developed to simulate the behavior of an oversized trileaflet aortic valve during somatic growth. The valve geometry was based on the AVaTAR design previously described and validated in both in vitro and acute in vivo studies. The same leaflet geometries used in those experimental settings were used in the present simulations to ensure continuity and comparability across platforms.

Three identical symmetric leaflets with a nominal width of 18 mm were constructed using linear interpolation techniques implemented in MATLAB (MathWorks). The valve model was then exported to ABAQUS/CAE (Dassault Systèmes) for finite element analysis.

The valves were modeled in Abaqus/CAE 2024 as shell elements, their thickness (0.5 mm) being negligible with respect to their length. Element S4R was used to mesh the valves. The valves were modeled as hyperelastic Ogden material of order 2 as found in literature for body tissues.9 The coefficients were fine tuned to reproduce the morphology observed in vitro studies.

The aortic root was idealized as a cylindrical structure. This simplification was intentionally chosen to isolate the effects of progressive root enlargement on valve morphology, avoiding confounding geometric variables related to sinus asymmetry.

Stitching Process and Valve Implantation

Valve implantation was simulated by constraining leaflet attachment to a predefined aortic attachment line using a discrete set of stitches. For each leaflet, 17 stitch locations were defined, and their placement and spacing were determined by an experienced congenital heart surgeon (I.L.) to reproduce operative constraints and achieve the intended 3-dimensional leaflet configuration.

Stitch spacing on the leaflet was intentionally nonuniform. Along the most basal segment of the leaflet attachment, corresponding to the nadir region and its adjacent low-curvature segment, stitch locations on the leaflet were placed 3.8 mm apart (Figure 1). In contrast, in the commissural regions and slightly below the commissures, stitch locations on the leaflet were placed 3 mm apart. On the aortic wall, the stitch locations defining the aortic attachment line were placed at a constant spacing of 3 mm throughout.

Geometry of the leaflet viewed at a 30° oblique angle, where each reference point (yellow isolated dots) represents a stitching point that will be displaced to the aortic attachment line.

This controlled discrepancy in spacing between the leaflet and the aortic attachment line was used to accommodate the fact that the leaflet attachment line is longer than the corresponding anchoring line on the aortic wall. Concentrating the excess leaflet length within the basal curved segment allows that region to absorb the length mismatch, promoting a 3-dimensional configuration in which the leaflet attachment becomes relatively concave toward the aortic wall rather than remaining planar.

The aortic attachment line was defined geometrically as the intersection between the cylindrical model representing the aorta and a hemispherical surface representing the sinus of Valsalva and its corresponding leaflet. Each leaflet stitch location was mapped to its corresponding location on the aortic attachment line, and the resulting displacements were imposed as boundary conditions within the finite element framework.

The stitching process was first performed for a single leaflet model. After the stitched configuration of 1 leaflet was established, it was duplicated and rotated by 120° and 240° about the central axis of the cylindrical root to generate the complete symmetric trileaflet valve configuration.

Growth Simulation and Physiological Loading Conditions

The initial model was constructed by implanting the nominal 18-mm valve within a cylindrical aortic root of 18 mm diameter. After this baseline configuration was established, additional models were generated by preserving the same leaflet geometry and stitched trileaflet configuration while changing only the diameter of the cylindrical root to 12, 14, and 16 mm. Together with the baseline 18-mm model, this produced a complete set of growth-stage models with root diameters of 12, 14, 16, and 18 mm.

This framework represents sequential stages of somatic growth in which the valve is implanted early in life within a smaller root (eg, 12 mm), and the outflow tract subsequently enlarges over time.

Physiologic diastolic pressures were assigned to each growth stage to replicate age-appropriate loading conditions during valve closure. The rationale was to apply a diastolic pressure representative of normal systemic blood pressure for the age corresponding to each aortic root diameter, more specifically, at the sinotubular junction level. Blood pressure tables from the European Society of Hypertension for male patients were used in order to apply the greatest diastolic pressure within the normal range.10 For each age, the diastolic blood pressure corresponding to the 99th percentile at the 50th percentile of height was selected.

Sinotubular junction dimensions were obtained using the Boston Children's Hospital z-score reference system and used as the diameter of the simulated cylinders. For each age, body weight and height at the 50th percentile were derived from World Health Organization growth charts for male patients,11 and together with age and male sex were entered into the z-score calculator. The sinotubular junction diameter expressed in integer millimeters closest to a z score of 0 was selected as representative for that age.

Using this approach, a physiologically consistent diastolic blood pressure value was assigned to each aortic root diameter in the simulations. A correspondence table linking age, diastolic blood pressure, and aortic root diameter (grouped in 1-mm increments) was constructed (Table 1).

Simulation of Diastolic Valve Closure and Morphologic Analysis

Valve closure was simulated by applying a constant diastolic pressure load to the aortic side of the leaflets. For each growth stage, the pressure magnitude corresponded to the age-specific value defined previously. Simulations were run until a stable closed-valve configuration was achieved.

Valve morphology was analyzed with specific focus on the 3 characteristic features of leaflet oversizing previously described: windmill shape, leaflet billow below the annular plane, and coaptation length. The analysis was performed using measurement strategies designed to mimic clinical echocardiographic assessment, including long-axis and short-axis views, complemented by 3-dimensional visualization of the closed valve. For standalone clarity, each oversizing parameter is explicitly defined herein.

Coaptation length was assessed on long-axis sectional views of the closed valve. In these views, the coaptation region is seen as the area of apposition between 2 adjacent leaflets. Coaptation length was measured as the linear extent of leaflet-to-leaflet contact along the coaptation zone and was obtained for each leaflet pair (1-2, 2-3, and 3-1).

Leaflet billow below the annular plane was also assessed on long-axis views. First, an annular reference plane was defined as the plane passing through the three nadirs of the valve (one nadir per leaflet). With the valve closed, the lowest point of each leaflet belly (the point of maximal billow) was identified. The billow below the annular plane was defined as the distance from the nadir-defined annular plane to this lowest leaflet point, measured per leaflet in the long-axis sectional plane.

Windmill shape was quantified on a short-axis en face view of the valve in the fully closed configuration. For each commissure, a reference line was defined from the commissure to the central meeting point of the three leaflets. Along each interleaflet coaptation region, the deviation of the coaptation line relative to this commissure-to-center reference line was measured as a set of distances within the short-axis plane. These distances were then averaged to obtain a mean deviation value for each interleaflet region, and an overall mean deviation was calculated for the valve. In addition to these planar measurements, the closed valve was also evaluated morphologically using a 3-dimensional reconstruction to provide a view analogous to 3-dimensional echocardiographic assessment.

Results

Morphologic Evaluation of Valve Closure During Growth

When physiological diastolic pressures were applied to the open valve across the different simulated stages of aortic root growth, effective valve closure was achieved in all cases. Visual inspection from both long-axis and short-axis views demonstrated complete leaflet coaptation without gaps or areas of incomplete closure.

From the short-axis (aortic) view, the closed valves consistently exhibited a symmetric configuration with preserved central coaptation. Three-dimensional visualization further confirmed uniform leaflet apposition throughout the coaptation zone, with no evidence of commissural leakage or asymmetric distortion (Video 1).

Across all growth stages, the valves displayed the three previously described morphological features associated with leaflet oversizing: a windmill configuration in the closed state, leaflet billow extending below the annular plane, and increased coaptation length. These features were most pronounced at smaller aortic root diameters and became progressively attenuated as root diameter increased (Video 2).

Quantitative Evaluation of Oversizing Features

Quantitative analysis demonstrated a progressive reduction in all 3 oversizing-related parameters with increasing aortic root diameter (Table 2). Total mean deviation from the central center-commissure line in the windmill shape decreased steadily as the simulated aortic root enlarged. The distance between the nadir-defined annular plane and the lowest point of leaflet billow was highest in the smallest aortic root and decreased with increasing root diameter. Coaptation length measurements decreased with increasing aortic root diameter. Figure 2 summarizes the evolution of all these features.

Discussion

The surgical treatment of aortic valve disease in children remains one of the most unresolved challenges in congenital heart surgery. As emphasized by Buratto and Konstantinov, the ideal aortic valve intervention in the pediatric population should restore competence without obstruction, preserve physiological root dynamics, use autologous tissue whenever possible, and—most importantly—accommodate somatic growth over time.1 Despite decades of innovation, no currently available valve substitute fully meets these criteria.

This in silico study provides novel mechanistic insight into how a reconstructed trileaflet oversized semilunar valve, the AVaTAR valve, can maintain competence across progressive stages of aortic root growth through geometric adaptation alone, without assuming valvular tissue growth. This finding represents a critical conceptual advance in pediatric valve reconstruction. Our previous pulsatile in vitro testing and acute in vivo porcine study established feasibility and acute physiological performance of the valve at the initial stage and the late configuration but left unanswered the fundamental question of how the valve evolves geometrically as the patient grows.7,8

Addressing this question experimentally would require long-term chronic animal models, which are costly, ethically questionable, and limited in their ability to replicate pediatric growth trajectories. At the same time, computational modeling and in silico clinical trials are increasingly being adopted across the medical device lifecycle because they can accelerate design optimization, inform bench and animal testing strategies, and in some contexts reduce or partially replace animal and even human participants—provided that model credibility is addressed through structured verification, validation, and adequacy assessments.12 In this context, an in silico approach represents an ideal intermediate model, allowing controlled evaluation of valve behavior under progressive changes in root dimensions and physiological loading conditions.

In the present work, we modeled a single nominal 18-mm trileaflet valve implanted within progressively larger aortic root diameters, simulating different stages of somatic growth. Importantly, the leaflet geometry, material properties, and stitching configuration were kept constant, isolating the effect of root enlargement on valve geometry and closure mechanics. The close morphological correspondence between the in silico results and previously reported in vitro and in vivo echocardiographic findings strongly supports the validity of this modeling strategy (Figure 3).

A key finding of this study is that valve competence was preserved at all simulated stages of growth, despite progressive reduction in oversizing. At physiologically scaled diastolic pressures, all models demonstrated complete leaflet coaptation without gaps or single-leaflet prolapse, as confirmed by morphologic assessment that mimicked standard 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional echocardiographic views. Quantitative analysis showed that the three defining features of oversizing decreased progressively with increasing aortic root diameter. Importantly, this reduction occurred in a controlled and proportional manner. These observations support the central hypothesis that the AVaTAR valve achieves growth accommodation through progressive geometric redistribution of excess leaflet tissue.

A clinically intuitive way to frame this behavior is to consider a pediatric patient whose aortic root measures approximately 14 mm (around 4 years of age in our sizing correlation) and receives a nominal 18-mm AVaTAR valve. Under this paradigm, the valve is expected to function with a higher degree of oversizing early after implantation and then progressively spend that geometric reserve as the root enlarges toward 18 mm (around 10 years of age in the same correlation), while remaining competent throughout the transition. This conceptual example provides a simple rationale for how geometric adaptability can bridge multiple years of growth.

Growth-accommodating strategies have also been explored in right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction, where surgically implanted size-adjustable pulmonary valves aim to preserve competence as children grow.13 These approaches share the goal of delaying reintervention in growing patients, although they differ from AVaTAR in valve position, loading conditions, and mechanism of adaptation. Our strategy is also different from conventional neocuspidization approaches that size leaflets to the patient's anatomy at the time of surgery. For example, the Ozaki neocuspidization technique creates a valve sized to the patient's anatomy at the time of surgery.14 Depending on the underlying native valve morphology and on intraoperative sizing, the reconstructed valve may end up having leaflets with different sizes, particularly when the native valve is inherently asymmetric. In pediatric candidates, AVaTAR intentionally incorporates leaflet oversizing and symmetry to preserve competence as the root enlarges.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted considering several limitations inherent to the modeling framework. First, the aortic root was idealized as a rigid cylindrical geometry, and the sinus portion was simplified. Therefore, native aortic root asymmetries and dynamic root deformation throughout the cardiac cycle were not captured. Somatic growth was modeled exclusively as radial enlargement of the aortic root; longitudinal growth of the sinotubular junction and commissural height was not incorporated and could influence leaflet tension and coaptation geometry. In addition, detailed sinus anatomy and coronary ostia were not modeled, so potential sinus crowding, commissural distortion, or coronary impingement by oversized leaflet billow could not be evaluated. Second, valve closure was evaluated under a static diastolic pressure load. Systolic opening of the valve and fluid-structure interactions were beyond the scope of this work. Third, leaflet material behavior was represented using a fitted hyperelastic constitutive model; although coefficients were tuned to reproduce experimentally observed morphology, time-dependent changes such as fibrotic retraction, asymmetric shrinkage, altered compliance, fatigue, calcification, and remodeling were not modeled and could influence the rate at which geometric reserve is consumed.

In addition, the implantation simulated an interrupted suture technique instead of the combination of running and u-stitches technique used in clinical setting. Therefore, local stress concentrations and stitch-tissue interaction were not directly assessed.

Finally, this in silico growth model intentionally assumes that leaflet tissue does not grow, focusing instead on geometric adaptation of an oversized design as the root enlarges. Although this assumption is consistent with the mechanistic objective of the present study, future work should incorporate more anatomically realistic root geometry, 3-dimensional growth, cyclic loading, and other clinically relevant variables to further strengthen predictive capacity and enable patient-specific translation. Besides this, it is important to note that the present simulations were limited to root diameters of 12 to 18 mm; whether the same geometric strategy is applicable to annuli smaller than 12 mm remains uncertain. In very small annuli, excess leaflet bulk could theoretically reduce effective orifice area or create relative stenosis during systole. Because the present model evaluates diastolic competence only, systolic opening dynamics and transvalvular gradients were not assessed. Future fluid-structure interaction simulations and pulsatile testing under systemic conditions will be needed to define this balance.

Conclusions

In this in silico model of somatic growth, a trileaflet oversized AVaTAR valve maintained complete diastolic competence across progressive enlargement of the aortic root diameter from 12 to 18 mm under physiologically scaled diastolic pressures. Morphologic evaluation mimicking echocardiographic long-axis, short-axis, and 3-dimensional views demonstrated consistent coaptation without gaps across all growth stages. Quantitative measurement of the 3 specific features of oversizing (windmill shape, billow below the annular plane, and coaptation length) showed a progressive decrease with increasing root diameter, supporting a mechanism of growth accommodation through geometric adaptation. These findings bridge previous in vitro and acute in vivo feasibility studies by providing a mechanistic description of the geometric pathway through which an intentionally oversized AVaTAR valve can remain competent as the outflow tract enlarges (Figure 4).
