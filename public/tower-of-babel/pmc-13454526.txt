Fatal Catheter-Related Hematogenous Disseminated MSSA Sepsis in an Immunocompromised Lung Cancer Patient: A Case Report
Authors: Yuanfei Li, Fei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe pathogenicity of Staphylococcus aureus is well-recognized, stemming from its potent toxins and invasive capabilities. However, disseminated infections caused by methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) are prone to being overlooked clinically. This study aims to report a case of disseminated invasive infection caused by MSSA in an immunocompromised patient, highlighting the clinical challenges in diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes.Case PresentationIn October 2022, the patient was found to have a pulmonary nodule (65 × 53 mm); in April 2023, a biopsy confirmed a diagnosis of lung cancer; on November 21, 2024, a PICC line was inserted to administer chemotherapy. After completing chemotherapy, the patient was discharged home, where he gradually developed diarrhea. On December 3, 2024 (12 days after chemotherapy), the patient began experiencing shortness of breath and coughing. The patient presented with septic shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and acute renal failure. Imaging studies revealed bilateral pulmonary infiltrates; a chest X-ray showed a “white lung” sign, and a CT scan demonstrated multiple nodules and cysts consistent with hematogenous dissemination. Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) was detected via tNGS, blood culture, and bone marrow culture. Despite a series of antimicrobial treatments, the infection spread to the bone marrow and spleen. Although the treatment regimen was ultimately adjusted to cefoxitin combined with clindamycin and the symptoms of the infection were brought under control, the persistence of the previous infection and a secondary Enterococcus faecalis infection, combined with the progression of lung cancer, ultimately led to multiple organ failure, and the patient died on day 28.ConclusionMSSA can cause rapidly progressive disseminated infection in immunocompromised patients, even with susceptible strains. Early diagnosis using tNGS and targeted antibiotic therapy are critical. Strict catheter care and infection control measures are essential to prevent such life-threatening complications.Keywords: methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, MSSA, disseminated infection, catheter-related bloodstream infection, ceftobiprole

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a major pathogen contributing to global infection-related morbidity and mortality, causing millions of severe invasive infections annually.1,2 It can lead to pneumonia, surgical site infections, prosthetic joint infections, endocarditis, and hospital-acquired bacteremia,3 with a predilection for immunocompromised individuals, including patients undergoing intravenous chemotherapy or with indwelling venous catheters.

Its pathogenicity primarily relies on virulence factors, such as toxins, immune modulators, exoenzymes, that mediate adhesion, colonization, nutrient acquisition, and host immune evasion.4–6 Toxins are the key protective factors against host clearance.7 Additionally, S. aureus can form biofilms, thereby causing persistent in vivo colonization, exacerbating the risk of fatal infections.8

Although methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) infections are well-documented to cause higher mortality, morbidity, and longer hospital stays,9 a meta-analysis included a total of 41 studies (64 meta-analyses) were selected by the inclusion criteria. The summary meta-analysis revealed global prevalence rates for S. aureus [24.8%, 95% CI 0.248 (0.204–0.293), I2 = 93%], methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) [5.8%, 95% CI 0.058 (0.044–0.073), I2 = 92%],10 methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) can also cause disseminated multi-organ infections owing to its virulence and pathogenicity. Emerging evidences have identified certain MSSA lineages (like, sequence type ST398) with high virulence characteristics that can lead to fatal infections,11,12 suggesting the need to reassess the clinical threat posed by MSSA, particularly in immunocompromised hosts.

Case Presentation

A 61-year-old male patient with lung cancer developed diarrhea, shortness of breath, and fever 14 days after Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter (PICC) catheter placement of chemotherapy. He was initially admitted to the oncology department and later transferred to the respiratory intensive care unit (RICU) because of worsening respiratory failure and renal insufficiency. Bedside chest radiography revealed diffuse patchy high-density shadows (“white lung” changes) in both the lungs (Figure 1). After RICU admission he was diagnosed with septic shock, severe pneumonia, acute renal failure, and thrombocytopenia. Invasive mechanical ventilation via tracheal intubation was initiated and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected through bronchoscopy for targeted gene sequencing. Considering the renal insufficiency, hemodialysis catheter placement and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) were performed simultaneously. Empirical treatment with meropenem (0.5 g every 6 h, total 2 g/24 h) and vancomycin (0.5 g every 12 h, total 1 g/24 h) was initiated.

During the initial vancomycin therapy, the patient showed persistent elevation of blood leukocytes (peak white blood cell count 48.02×109/L) accompanied by progressive deterioration and thrombocytopenia, which raised suspicion of leukemoid reaction. Bone marrow aspiration and culture (positive for S. aureus at 44.5 h) confirmed bone marrow dissemination. Drug sensitivity testing identified MSSA, prompting an adjustment to cefoxitin combined with clindamycin. However, the patient continued to have persistent fever, slow leukocyte decline, and worsening thrombocytopenia (< 10 × 109/L), which indicated persistent Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia. Repeat CT showed progression of the pulmonary infection with possible splenic dissemination leading to a switch to ceftobiprole (500 mg every 8 h, total 1.5 g/24 h). White blood cells returned to normal on day 8 after ceftobiprole administration, blood cultures were negative on day 10, and partial regression of lung lesions was observed on CT (Figure 2C).

Although ceftobiprole managed to control the symptoms of infection, prior invasive and persistent Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia resulted in immune dysfunction and susceptibility to opportunistic infections, with decreased total lymphocyte counts and helper T cells (CD4) causing irreversible systemic damage. The patient ultimately succumbed to multiorgan failure on day 28 of admission due to deteriorating mental status from lung cancer brain metastasis, impaired airway clearance, worsening respiratory failure (requiring reintubation), progressive renal and circulatory dysfunction and Enterococcus faecium-caused secondary infection.

Discussion

Unlike previously reported MSSA-associated infections such as prostatic abscess, osteomyelitis and post-lung-transplant infection, this case occurred in a patient with advanced lung cancer undergoing chemotherapy via PICC. Chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression and intravascular catheterization created a unique high-risk condition for hematogenous MSSA dissemination. Notably, the pathogen was rapidly identified through combined detection of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and peripheral blood using mNGS. In addition, the patient presented with typical imaging characteristics of hematogenous staphylococcal pneumonia on chest CT. This combination of special underlying disease, dual mNGS diagnostic mode and distinctive imaging manifestations distinguishes our case from existing literature and provides practical reference for the diagnosis and management of similar critically ill patients.

Rapid and Precise Diagnosis of Hematogenous Disseminated Staphylococcus aureus Infection

The patient with disseminated invasive S.aureu infection initially presented with respiratory failure as diffuse “white lung” changes (Figure 1), marked leukocytosis, and septic shock. Detection of S. aureus in BALF (mp-tNGS) and blood (hc-tNGS) suggests a hematogenous infection. Confirmation of hematogenous dissemination requires correlation with the CT findings.

Hematogenous S. aureus pneumonia arises from bacterial seeding of the lungs via bloodstream spread from distant foci. Its CT features differ significantly from aspiration pneumonia; early-stage lesions appear as multiple ill-defined patchy/nodular opacities in the peripheral lung fields (predominantly lower/middle zones, related to rich peripheral pulmonary vasculature and bacterial emboli entrapment). As the disease progresses, thin-walled pneumatoceles (often coexisting with nodules) and multiple small abscesses (round/oval, uneven wall thickness with air-fluid levels) develop. These lesions are more widespread and smaller than abscesses in aspiration pneumonia. Differentiation from metastatic lung cancer is critical; metastatic nodules are typically well-defined, homogeneous, and lack pneumatoceles or abscesses, as opposed to that in hematogenous S. aureus pneumonia. In this patient, the CT findings (Figure 2) aligned with these features, and moreover the diagnosis of hematogenous S. aureus pneumonia was supported by the acute onset, severe systemic toxicity, PICC catheter history, and positive catheter/peripheral blood cultures.

Mortality-Reduction Strategies: Empirical vs Targeted Therapy for MSSA and MRSA Infections

Patients with MRSA bacteremia has worse clinical outcomes and is associated with significantly higher mortality rate than compared to those with MSSA bacteremia. However, studies have compared virulence genes and pathogenicity between MRSA and MSSA. MSSA has a quicker killing potential compared with MRSA, suggesting that the carriage of virulence determinants does not solely determine pathogenicity in S. aureus.13 MSSA strains have a more diverse and abundant carriage of virulence genes compared to MRSA strains, suggesting they may be potentially more pathogenic.14 A study showed that MSSA isolates carry more virulence genes than MRSA isolates; the sea gene was detected in 20% of MRSA isolates, compared with 13% of MSSA isolates. With regard to other virulence genes, MSSA isolates exhibited higher virulence than MRSA isolates, and sensitivity to cefoxitin was significantly associated with the presence of the hla, hld, crtN, and cap8H genes (P < 0.05). Notably, the cap8H gene was detected only in the MSSA group.15 The Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL)-encoding genes have also been identified in MSSA and were similar between MRSA and MSSA strains. The S. aureus strains with this gene are generally associated with a higher mortality rates. Invasive MSSA infections are a substantial public health problem with an incidence rate 1.8 times higher than MRSA, based on surveillance data from 8 counties in the US.16 Disseminated MSSA infections can cause severe, life-threatening multi-organ disease comparable to MRSA, affecting immunocompromised and healthy patients alike. While plenty of cases demonstrate MSSA’s virulent potential, the evidence base lacks large cohort studies quantifying incidence or outcomes of disseminated disease specifically. A study of ICU patients found that patients infected with MSSA actually had a higher 7-day mortality rate (with a relative risk approximately 2.6 times that of MRSA).17

In this case, empirical therapy with meropenem and vancomycin was initiated because of an unknown etiology and critical illness (invasive ventilation + CRRT). Although other antimicrobial regimens were tried in the interim, the bacteria were never eradicated, leading to persistent SAB; treatment was ultimately switched to ceftobiprole. Evidence from a retrospective cohort study and a matched case-control study involving 294 patients suggests that vancomycin is inferior to β-lactam antibiotics in the treatment of MSSA bacteremia, with higher mortality rates (37% vs 18%).18 Guidelines from the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) outline treatment strategies for S. aureus infections.19 For disseminated MSSA infections, β-lactamase-resistant penicillin (such as oxacillin, cloxacillin) are recommended as first-line therapy, with cephalosporins as alternatives for patients allergic to penicillin. Individualized regimens should consider infection site, disease severity, and patient-specific factors. A 2025 meta-analysis indicated that β-lactam antibiotics are more effective than vancomycin in treating Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bloodstream infections. Although the two groups were similar in terms of clinical outcomes such as mortality and duration of treatment, a faster clinical response was observed with β-lactam antibiotics, supporting the preferential use of this class of drugs in cases of MSSA infection.20 Ceftobiprole also exhibits rapid bactericidal activity, Early treatment with ceftobiprole may improve patient outcomes. Demonstrating excellent early improvement in the SAB Phase 3 clinical trial. In patients with MSSA infection, bloodstream clearance occurred at a median of 3 days.21

Analysis of mortality causes includes: i) Disseminated Multi-Organ Infection and Sustained Bacterial Load: the patient was confirmed diagnoses included catheter-related bloodstream S. aureus infection, S. aureus pneumonia, and osteomyelitis. Herein, CT suggested possible splenic dissemination, and lung cancer brain metastasis (unconfirmed meningitis due to thrombocytopenia < 20 × 109/L precluding lumbar puncture). Despite cefoxitin plus clindamycin treatment had been taken as soon as the comfirm of MSSA in blood and BALF sample, the leukocyte count declined slowly, and thrombocytopenia persisted (refractory to thrombopoietic therapy). Blood cultures normalized after switching to ceftobiprole; however, bone marrow findings indicated infection-related thrombocytopenia, reflecting the severity and persistence of dissemination; ii) Persistent Altered Mental Status: this can be directly linked to lung cancer, brain metastasis, and blood-brain barrier disruption, with a potential contribution from the dissemination of S. aureus and persistent Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia. iii) Deterioration of Malignancy-Related Baseline Status: lung cancer cachexia, post-chemotherapy immunosuppression, renal insufficiency, and multiple invasive procedures exacerbate the risk of secondary infections, creating a vicious cycle of infection and immunosuppression.

Limitations of the study: (1) This is a retrospective single-case study; the patient’s unique immune status limits the generalizability of the conclusions; (2) The patient died, so there are no long-term follow-up data; (3) It is not possible to establish a causal relationship between drug efficacy and prognosis based on a single case. Conclusion

This case highlights that MSSA infections complicating PICC placement during chemotherapy, despite sensitivity to methicillin, resulting in rapid dissemination, widespread invasion, and sustained tissue damage. Therefore, clinicians should recognize the invasive potential of MSSA infections as well as the perniciousness of persistent SAB. For disseminated and persistent MSSA infections, antibiotic selection and dose should be individualized based on disease progression, infection site distribution, and culture sensitivity results. Novel antimicrobial agents like ceftobiprole may serve as empirical treatment choice for S. aureus infections than vancomycin due to its excellent efficacy against both MSSA and MRSA infections. Early treatment with ceftobiprole may improve patient outcomes. However, core prevention strategies require the strengthening of hospital infection control systems. Additionally, strict adherence to PICC maintenance protocols is essential; suspected catheter-related infections warrant prompt catheter removal to eliminate the source of infection.
