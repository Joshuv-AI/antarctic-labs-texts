Uncommon aortic prosthesis vegetation due to Histoplasma capsulatum: clinical and laboratory aspects
Authors: Reginaldo Gonçalves de Lima-Neto, Moacir Batista Jucá, Tomaz Christiano de Albuquerque Gomes, Karla Roberta Soares Rodrigues, Cícero Pinheiro Inácio, Rejane Pereira Neves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We describe a rare case of Histoplasma capsulatum prosthetic aortic valve endocarditis in an immunocompetent patient with previous histoplasmosis. Diagnosis was established by direct microscopy, culture, and molecular identification from excised valve tissue. Antifungal susceptibility testing showed low MICs to amphotericin B and azoles, with limited echinocandin activity. Thus, after surgical valve replacement and liposomal amphotericin B therapy, the patient was discharged with a good outcome.

Introduction

Histoplasma capsulatum is the etiological agent of histoplasmosis, an endemic mycosis in the Americas, Asia, and Africa, with sporadic cases reported worldwide [1]. Treatment depends on disease severity and host-related risk factors. Mild to moderate infections are usually treated with azoles, particularly itraconazole, whereas amphotericin B is recommended for severe disease [2]. Although antifungal therapy is generally effective, refractory cases and relapses have been reported [3]. Fewer than 1% of infected individuals develop symptomatic disease, most commonly with pulmonary involvement [2]. Endocarditis represents a rare manifestation of disseminated histoplasmosis, with fewer than 50 cases described, affecting both native and prosthetic valves [[4], [5], [6]]. Diagnosis is frequently delayed, as more than 80% of cases present with negative blood cultures, and the absence of timely diagnosis and appropriate antifungal therapy is associated with high mortality [4]. Without treatment, histoplasma endocarditis is uniformly fatal.

Case

Herein, we describe the long-term treatment of histoplasmosis in an immunocompetent patient. In 2024, a 68-year-old man had a prior diagnosis of splenic histoplasmosis, for which he underwent splenectomy and was treated with itraconazole (200 mg). A cholecystectomy was performed concurrently due to complications related to the disease. At baseline, the patient completed antifungal therapy with itraconazole following splenectomy. Three months later (Month 3), he presented with fever, abdominal discomfort, and loss of appetite. At this time, transesophageal echocardiography revealed vegetation on the aortic prosthetic leaflet measuring 1.2 × 0.6 cm (Fig. 1A). Empirical antibiotic therapy (ceftriaxone, oxacillin, and rifampicin) was initiated and maintained for 8 days. Due to lack of clinical improvement, liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day) was started, and repeat aortic valve replacement surgery (third procedure) was performed. During surgery, two vegetations and rupture of the left coronary leaflet were identified. Postoperatively, the patient experienced significant clinical deterioration. Subsequently, microbiological and molecular diagnostic tests confirmed Histoplasma capsulatum infection. Antifungal therapy was continued, and after clinical stabilization, the patient was discharged on oral itraconazole. His medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, and the presence of an aortic prosthesis implanted more than 36 years earlier. Due to persistent symptoms and worsening aortic insufficiency, empirical antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone, oxacillin, and rifampicin was initiated and maintained for 8 days. In the absence of clinical improvement, liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day) was started, and surgical replacement of the aortic valve (third valve replacement) was recommended. Following surgery, the patient's condition deteriorated significantly. Intraoperative findings revealed two vegetations on the ventricular aspect of the aortic prosthesis and rupture of the left coronary leaflet. Serological testing for histoplasmosis by immunodiffusion (IMMY, Norman, OK, USA) showed undetectable H bands and weakly positive M bands. Microbiological analyses were performed at the Laboratory of Diagnosis in Tropical Diseases, Federal University of Pernambuco, Brazil. Direct microscopic examination of the vegetation, clarified with 20% KOH, revealed hyaline, globose yeast forms (Fig. 1B). Culture examination demonstrated globose macroconidia with characteristic tuberculate ornamentation (Fig. 1C), consistent with Histoplasma capsulatum. Molecular identification was confirmed by ITS1/4 DNA sequencing, with partial sequences deposited in GenBank under accession number OM443075. Antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) of both yeast and mycelial forms was performed according to CLSI protocols M27-A3 and M38-A2, respectively, with 48-h incubation. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs/MECs) for quality control strains were within CLSI-recommended ranges, and results were reproducible across independent experiments. All isolates demonstrated susceptibility to amphotericin B, itraconazole, fluconazole, and voriconazole. In contrast, echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin) showed low antifungal activity (Table 1). Treatment with liposomal amphotericin B (AmBisome®) was initiated. Follow-up imaging showed no significant changes. However, laboratory findings revealed leukocytosis (25,500/mm3), worsening renal function (creatinine 2.3 mg/dL; urea 73 mg/dL), anemia (hemoglobin 6.5 g/dL; hematocrit 26.7%), and hypokalemia (potassium 2.9 mEq/L). Supportive therapy included potassium chloride, dipyrone, pantoprazole (40 mg every 12 h), and bisoprolol (5 mg). Discussion

This case describes an uncommon and severe presentation of Histoplasma capsulatum infection involving an aortic prosthetic valve in an immunocompetent patient, highlighting the complexity of diagnosing and managing fungal prosthetic valve endocarditis. Although histoplasmosis is relatively frequent in endemic areas, cardiac involvement remains rare, particularly in the form of prosthetic valve infection, and is often associated with delayed recognition and poor outcomes [6,7]. Recent literature emphasizes that even immunocompetent individuals may develop severe or atypical forms of endemic mycoses, particularly in the presence of prosthetic devices, reinforcing the need for heightened clinical suspicion [8,9].

The patient had a previous history of splenic histoplasmosis treated with itraconazole (200 mg/day) for 2 months, and presented with recurrent systemic symptoms three months after completing antifungal therapy. Current international guidelines recommend itraconazole as first-line therapy for mild-to-moderate histoplasmosis, typically for at least 6–12 weeks, with longer durations in complicated or disseminated cases [4,9,10]. This temporal relationship raises concern for relapse or persistent infection, which has been increasingly recognized even in patients without overt immunosuppression [4,5,9,10]. The presence of a long-standing aortic prosthesis likely represented a major predisposing factor, providing a surface for fungal adherence and possible biofilm formation [7].

Diagnosis in this case was particularly challenging. Serological testing showed only weak positivity for the M band and absence of the H band, findings consistent with the known limitations of immunodiffusion assays, especially in localized or chronic infections [5,6,10,11]. In endemic regions, diagnostic performance may also be affected by variability in laboratory infrastructure [11]. In addition, cross-reactivity with other fungal pathogens has been reported in antigen-based assays, potentially complicating interpretation [12]. In contrast, direct microscopic examination and culture of the excised vegetation were decisive, followed by molecular confirmation, reinforcing the importance of tissue-based and molecular diagnostic approaches in suspected fungal endocarditis.

Antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) was performed according to standardized CLSI methodologies [1,2]. The isolate showed low MICs for amphotericin B and azoles such as itraconazole and voriconazole, consistent with previous reports [4]. Fluconazole demonstrated higher MIC values, supporting its limited role in severe disease [3,4]. Echinocandins showed low antifungal activity, consistent with their limited efficacy against H. capsulatum [4].

Management of histoplasma endocarditis is not well standardized due to its rarity; however, current recommendations support combined surgical and antifungal therapy, typically with liposomal amphotericin B followed by prolonged azole therapy [4,9,10]. In the present case, treatment with liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day) was initiated after lack of response to antibacterial therapy, followed by surgical valve replacement. Despite these measures, the patient experienced significant clinical deterioration, including renal dysfunction and hematological abnormalities, which may reflect both disease severity and drug-related toxicity.

Alternative antifungal options may be considered in selected cases. Posaconazole (particularly delayed-release tablets) has shown activity against H. capsulatum and may represent an alternative in cases of intolerance or refractory disease. Isavuconazole has also emerged as a potential option, although clinical data remain limited [9,10]. In addition, novel antifungal agents currently under investigation may expand future therapeutic options for endemic mycoses.

Overall, this case highlights the aggressive nature of H. capsulatum prosthetic valve endocarditis and underscores the importance of early diagnosis, adherence to guideline-based therapy, and individualized management strategies. It also reinforces the need for prolonged follow-up after antifungal treatment, particularly in patients with prosthetic material, given the risk of relapse or persistent infection. By documenting this rare presentation, this report contributes to the limited literature on histoplasma endocarditis and emphasizes the importance of multidisciplinary care in complex fungal infections [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]].
