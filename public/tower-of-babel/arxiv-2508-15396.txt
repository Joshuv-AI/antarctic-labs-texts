Attribution, Citation, and Quotation: A Survey of Evidence-based Text Generation with Large Language Models
Tobias Schreieder, Tim Schopf, Michael Färber

Abstract
The increasing adoption of llm has been accompanied by growing concerns regarding their reliability and trustworthiness. As a result, a growing body of research focuses on evidence-based text generation with llm , aiming to link model outputs to supporting evidence to ensure traceability and verifiability. However, the field is fragmented due to inconsistent terminology, isolated evaluation practices, and a lack of unified benchmarks. To bridge this gap, we systematically analyze 134 papers, introduce a unified taxonomy of evidence-based text generation with llm , and investigate 300 evaluation metrics across seven key dimensions. Thereby, we focus on approaches that use citations, attribution, or quotations for evidence-based text generation. Building on this, we examine the distinctive characteristics and representative methods in the field. Finally, we highlight open challenges and outline promising directions for future work.

5.1
Attribution Approach

Huang and Chang 2024
categorize attribution into parametric and non-parametric approaches, distinguished primarily by the nature of knowledge used during text generation.
Parametric approaches
rely solely on knowledge implicitly encoded within the model’s parameters, acquired during training. In contrast,
non-parametric approaches
incorporate external sources at inference time. We follow this categorization but expand it to include approaches that did not fit the original framework. As shown in Figure
5
, 126 non-parametric and 25 parametric attribution approaches were identified across 134 papers, with post-retrieval being the most common (73 papers).

Figure 5:
Number of studies per attribution approach.

5.1.1
Parametric Attribution

Previous work treats parametric attribution as a single category
Huang and Chang 2024
;
Li et al. 2023
, while our review identifies three types.

Pure
llm
rely on the inherent attribution capabilities of existing
llm
without altering their architecture, training procedures, or underlying data. Studies in this category focus on evaluating the attribution behavior of
llm
, for example, by assessing the presence and quality of citations generated by the model
Byun et al. 2024
;
Malaviya et al. 2024
;
Zuccon et al. 2023
. While pure
llm
are typically prompted to provide citations,
Moayeri et al. 2024
observed that GPT models occasionally generate citations spontaneously.

Model-Centric
approaches aim to improve attribution by modifying the
llm
architecture or training objectives. In our review, we found that two papers annotated as "approach" exemplify this strategy. First,
Chu et al. 2025
propose FARD, which trains a student model to imitate citation-grounded rationales distilled from a teacher model, replacing standard chain-of-thought reasoning to improve causal-aware attribution. Second,
Khalifa et al. 2024
introduce source-aware training, where the
llm
learns to associate knowledge with document identifiers during pretraining, followed by instruction tuning that encourages explicit citation of sources.

Data-Centric
attribution curates, augments, or synthetically generates data without changing
llm
architectures or internal behavior.
Li et al. 2024a
introduce an automatic preference optimization framework that models attribution as a preference learning task using both curated and automatically synthesized citation preference data.
Huang et al. 2024b
propose FRONT, a training approach that supervises
llm
with fine-grained supporting quotes to guide and improve citation generation.
Wang et al. 2024
frame attribution as a watermarking task, enabling
llm
to embed source-identifying signals into the generated text.

5.1.2
Non-Parametric Attribution

In contrast to parametric attribution, non-parametric approaches rely on the integration of external evidence sources.
Li et al. 2023
distinguish between
post-retrieval
and
post-generation
approaches. We extend this categorization with the two classes
in-generation
and
in-context
, which we derive from recent work and an extended analysis of tasks.

Post-Retrieval
attribution, also known as
pre-hoc

Huang and Chang 2024
, refers to a class of methods which retrieve relevant external information before text generation
Li et al. 2023
. The retrieved content is incorporated as additional context to condition the
llm
’s response. A prominent architecture in this category is
rag
, wherein the
llm
is instructed to base its output solely on the retrieved documents. However, standard
rag
does not inherently support attribution, and must be extended with mechanisms for providing explicit references to the retrieved source documents. Some notable examples are GopherCite
Menick et al. 2022
, PURR
Chen et al. 2023
,
Huang et al. 2024a
, OpenScholar
Asai et al. 2024a
and MIRAGE
Qi et al. 2024
.

Post-Generation
attribution approaches, which are also known as
post-hoc

Huang and Chang 2024
, first generate a response and subsequently retrieve relevant evidence based on the
llm
output
Li et al. 2023
. These approaches resemble classical citation recommendation
Färber and Jatowt 2020
by retrieving sources for each claim, leveraging evidence retrieval on both the user input (e.g., a question) and the
llm
output to attribute each generated claim.
Gao et al. 2023a
introduce a revision step in their system, RARR, which performs posterior verification by assessing the correctness of the
llm
response with reference to the retrieved documents and making adjustments when necessary. Other post-generation approaches include CEG
Li et al. 2024c
, START
Huang et al. 2024c
,
Patel et al. 2024
and ARE
Yan et al. 2024
.

In-Generation
attribution is an emerging
llm
paradigm, where the model dynamically determines the need for additional evidence during inference and retrieves it live during generation. This contrasts with traditional post-retrieval or post-generation methods by tightly integrating retrieval decisions into the generation itself. A prominent example is Self-
rag

Asai et al. 2024b
, which introduces self-reflection tokens enabling the
llm
to adaptively retrieve relevant evidence and iteratively critique and revise its outputs during inference. Other notable approaches include AGREE
Ye et al. 2024
, which trains
llm
to retrieve and cite supporting evidence dynamically during generation, and Think&Cite
Li and Ng 2025
, which employs self-guided tree search combined with reward modeling to progressively enhance attribution through iterative evidence gathering. Additionally, NEST
Li et al. 2024b
performs token-level retrieval at each generation step, incorporating relevant spans directly into the output to improve attribution.

In-Context
attribution differs significantly from previous approaches, as it does not require retrieval. Instead, the
llm
user explicitly provides relevant evidence as part of the prompt, embedding it directly into the context. This setting has been adopted in a variety of scenarios. For instance, several studies use in-context attribution to evaluate their attribution approaches by supplying a curated set of source documents, rather than relying on retrieval systems
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Cohen-Wang et al. 2024
;
Slobodkin et al. 2024
. Furthermore, some tasks inherently require in-context attribution. A typical example is document-level summarization, where multiple documents are given to the
llm
for summarization with attribution. Another example is citation text generation, where the
llm
is prompted to generate a citation-worthy text that integrates the content of a given scientific paper into a surrounding context, such as a related work section
Anand et al. 2023a
;
Gu and Hahnloser 2024
.

5.2
LLM Integration

This section outlines how
llm
are integrated into the evidence-based text generation. We found two complementary strategies:
training
-based approaches and
prompting
-based approaches, both of which primarily optimize attribution, citation, and correctness (see Figure
7

5.2.1
Training

We labeled each paper for the use of pre-training and fine-tuning. Only four papers employed
pre-training
, with half focused on parametric attribution, in which the authors consider pre-training a necessary component
Khalifa et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
. Others include Fanar, an
llm
pre-trained for multi-modality with post-retrieval attribution
Abbas et al. 2025

Patel et al. 2024

Notably, only 45% of the studies perform
llm

fine-tuning
. Among these,
supervised fine-tuning
is the most common approach (42%), primarily used to enhance generation and attribution performance
Li et al. 2024a
;
Ye et al. 2024
. In contrast,
self-supervised fine-tuning
(3%)
Huang et al. 2024c
;
Chen et al. 2023
and
reinforcement learning
(7%)
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Huang et al. 2024b

5.2.2
Prompting

Prompting
llm
constitutes a dominant strategy in evidence-based text generation, with 78% of studies employing some form of prompting-based approach to guide model outputs.
Zero-shot
prompting is the most common approach, used in 58% of studies, reflecting a strong preference for leveraging pre-trained model capabilities without additional examples
Tahaei et al. 2024
;
Laban et al. 2024
.
Few-shot
prompting appears in 34% of studies, often used to provide in-context demonstrations tailored to the task
Ateia and Kruschwitz 2025
;
Xia et al. 2025
.
Chain-of-thought
prompting is adopted in 11% of cases, typically to elicit intermediate reasoning steps that support stronger evidence attribution
Li et al. 2024c
;
Li et al. 2024e

Shaier et al. 2024
;
Muller et al. 2023

Beyond state-of-the-art prompting approaches, a small number of papers introduce strategies specifically designed to improve citation generation. These include
chain-of-citation
and
chain-of-quote

Li et al. 2024e
, which prompt models to explicitly align reasoning steps with cited sources and quoted evidence, as well as
conflict-aware
prompting

active-oriented
prompting
Li et al. 2024e
,
role play
prompting
Şahinuç et al. 2024
, and
self-consistency
prompting
Shen et al. 2024
;
Dani et al. 2024

5.3
Citation Characteristics

Another important aspect is the nature of the evidence provided to the user and how it is presented. In this section, we discuss five key citation characteristics:

,

5.3.1
Citation Modality

Citation modality addresses the question of what type of evidence is cited. Although evidence-based text generation requires the
llm
to produce textual output, the modality of the underlying evidence can differ. We identify four citation modalities. The most prevalent is
texts
, used in 96% of studies, which encompasses all forms of unstructured textual data, such as Wikipedia articles
Gao et al. 2023b
, web pages
Malaviya et al. 2024
, and scientific publications
Kang et al. 2023
. Less frequently, studies cite
graphs
as structured representations of entities and relations
He et al. 2025b
;
Dehghan et al. 2024
,
tables
as data organized in rows and columns
Saha Roy et al. 2025
;
Mathur et al. 2024
, and
visuals
as images
Ma et al. 2025
;
Suri et al. 2025
.

5.3.2
Evidence Level

Each citation modality can be cited at different levels of granularity, which we refer to as the evidence level. For
texts
, the cited evidence can be a full
document
like a scientific article
Anand et al. 2023b
, a
paragraph
for example a retrieved chunk
Gao et al. 2023b
, a single
sentence

Xu et al. 2025a
, or even individual
tokens

Phukan et al. 2024
. Among all studies analyzed, document-level evidence was cited in 43%, paragraph-level in 40%, sentence-level in 12%, and token-level in 2%. For
graphs
, the evidence level corresponds to a
triple

Li et al. 2024d
, while no approach cites a full knowledge graph. The
tables
modality includes the evidence levels
table

Suri et al. 2025
and
table cell

Mathur et al. 2024
, and the
visuals
modality includes
image

Suri et al. 2025
and
bounding box

Ma et al. 2025
. Due to the sparsity of studies for non-textual citation modalities, differences in evidence level for these modalities are not significant.

5.3.3
Citation Style

We identified six citation styles, each differing in how references to underlying evidence are presented to a user. Figure
6
illustrates the distribution of these styles across all papers. The most common approach, used in over 60% of the methods, is the use of
in-line citations
, where references are inserted directly after citation-worthy claims (e.g., [1][2])
Huang et al. 2024a
. This enables users to trace individual statements back to their supporting sources. Another frequently used method is the
citation report
, which provides a separate list of references with the
llm
-generated output
Bohnet et al. 2023
. Several approaches opt to display only the
passage
retrieved or used during generation, especially in attribution settings

. This style reflects the specific text segments that the
llm
indirectly quoted or relied upon.
Few methods use
narrative citations
, which incorporate references into the natural flow of texts (e.g., "Author et al. argue that…"), enhancing contextual clarity

.
The
highlight gradient
approach visually marks the influence of cited content by coloring relevant tokens or sentences both in the output and the source text, providing a visual form of traceability

5.3.4
Citation Visibility

While most approaches generate citations, our findings show not all aim for user-facing traceability or verification. We identified two forms of citation visibility. While most papers include citations in the
final response
, enabling users to trace claims to their original sources and verify the content
Gao et al. 2023b
, six approaches generate citations only in an
intermediate text
, used internally by the
llm
to support verification or revision during generation
Fang et al. 2024
.

5.3.5
Citation Frequency

Finally, citation frequency captures how many citations are provided per
llm
-generated claim. This reflects a trade-off between coverage and simplicity: multiple citations may strengthen evidential support, whereas a single citation can enhance clarity and reduce cognitive load. Notably, more than two-thirds of approaches generate
multiple
citations per claim
Shaier et al. 2024
, while others provide only a
single
citation
Khalifa et al. 2024
. We observe that parametric approaches are often unable to produce multiple citations due to architectural constraints, whereas non-parametric approaches can support multiple citations per claim through retrieval.

6.1
Evaluation Methods

The evaluation methods characterize the underlying strategies used to compute the evaluation metric scores. Our initial categorization was derived from
Dziri et al. 2022
, which we iteratively refined and extended during the data annotation process to obtain the final set of evaluation methods presented in this study.
Human evaluation
metrics use human judges to evaluate different aspects of
llm
-generated texts, typically on numerical scales of 1-5 or 1-10.
Most studies define custom evaluation criteria, which limits standardization, comparability, and reusability across approaches.

Inference-based
metrics use
nli
(
nli
) models to automatically classify whether an
llm
-generated text is entailed by a reference text. This evaluation method is commonly used to determine whether a grounded
llm
-generated text can be attributed to its source and to assess the factual correctness of an
llm
-generated response based on entailment with a ground truth reference.
Lexical overlap
metrics evaluate the degree of lexical overlap between an
llm
-generated text and a reference (ground truth).

llm
-as-a-judge
metrics use
llm
to automatically evaluate the quality of texts generated by other
llm
, thereby eliminating the need for human judges. Typically, the
llm
acting as a judge produces numerical scores, often normalized to a 0–1 scale.
Retrieval-based
metrics quantitatively evaluate how effectively an approach incorporates relevant evidence by comparing the retrieved or integrated evidence with a ground truth. This method is commonly used to assess whether the correct sources are accurately cited in
llm
-generated texts, as well as to determine whether appropriate sources can be retrieved and attributed to
llm
-generated texts post-hoc.
Semantic similarity-based
metrics assess the degree of semantic similarity between an
llm
-generated text and a reference (ground truth) text, typically by computing the cosine similarity between their dense vector embeddings.

6.2
Evaluation Dimensions

Evaluation dimensions describe specific aspects of
llm
-generated outputs that are being measured.

Attribution.
These metrics evaluate whether an
llm
-generated output can be attributed to specific sources of evidence, without requiring explicit citations or considering factual correctness. This dimension is among the most critical and widely studied in the literature, and is typically assessed using inference-based methods. The
Citation
nli
metric cluster, proposed by
Gao et al. 2023b
, is the most commonly adopted approach in this space. It uses
nli
-based models to determine whether an
llm
-generated output can be attributed to its cited reference, and computes corresponding
precision
,
recall
, and
F_{1}
scores. The
ROUGE
cluster, including
ROUGE-L
and
ROUGE-N

Lin 2004
, measures lexical overlap between
llm
-generated texts and referenced sources. In contrast,
BERTScore

Zhang et al. 2020
calculates the cosine similarity between dense embeddings of
llm
-generated texts and references.
Auto-AIS-sentence

Gao et al. 2023a
employs an
nli
model to estimate the percentage of sentences in an
llm
-generated text that are fully attributable to some evidence. Finally,
FActScore

Min et al. 2023
evaluates attribution by decomposing an
llm
-generated text into atomic facts and verifying whether each fact is supported by a referenced source, either via human annotation or using an
nli
model.

Citation.
Metrics in this dimension assess whether an
llm
-generated output properly references the correct evidence sources. Unlike attribution metrics, which evaluate whether content can be traced back to evidence, citation metrics emphasize the correctness and completeness of citations. These typically use retrieval-based evaluation methods. The
Citation Retrieval
metric cluster is the most commonly adopted within this dimension. Derived from
Liu et al. 2023a
, citation retrieval metrics use a gold standard to assess whether sources cited in
llm
-generated outputs correctly support the generated content, and whether relevant sources are cited appropriately. Evaluation results are typically reported using
precision
,
recall
, and
F_{1}
scores. Separately,
Citation Accuracy

Shaier et al. 2024
measures whether citation strings in
llm
-generated outputs correspond to the correct sources by matching them against gold standard citation strings.

Correctness.
This dimension evaluates if an
llm
-generated text is semantically accurate, such as providing correct answers without hallucinations or generating summaries without omitting key content.
The majority of correctness metrics rely on human evaluation, highlighting the limitations of current automated methods in capturing nuanced semantic accuracy. Due to their heterogeneous implementation, such metrics are not among the most frequently reported in Figure
7
.
Among automated metrics, lexical overlap–based methods are widely adopted. The
Exact Match
cluster, including
recall
,
precision
,
F_{1}
, and
accuracy
, is frequently applied
Gao et al. 2023b
;
Buchmann et al. 2024
, especially for factoid-style
qa
, though less suited for long-form generation with diverse wording. Another lexical metric,
BLEU-N
, assesses n-gram overlap to measure the similarity with ground-truth texts
Papineni et al. 2002
. In multiple-choice
qa
settings, the lexical
Accuracy QA
metric is commonly used
Chu et al. 2025
. To evaluate correctness of long-form text, inference-based metrics, human evaluation, and
llm
-as-a-judge approaches are used. Examples of inference-based metrics include
Claim Recall
, which employs
nli
models to verify if outputs entail gold claims
Gao et al. 2023b
, and
Accuracy NLI
, measuring factual consistency between texts
Malaviya et al. 2024
.

Linguistic Quality.
These metrics primarily capture aspects such as fluency, referring to the naturalness and grammatical correctness of text, and coherence, which reflects the logical flow and consistency of ideas. Most extracted metrics rely on human evaluation or adopt the
llm
-as-a-judge paradigm, as in
G-Eval Fluency
and
G-Eval Coherence

Liu et al. 2023b
. However, the most commonly used metric is
MAUVE
, which employs semantic similarity to assess whether generated text is fluent and coherent
Pillutla et al. 2021
. The inference-based metric
Perplexity
measures an
llm
’s uncertainty in predicting the next word in a sequence, with lower values indicating more probable and often more fluent text under the model’s learned distribution
Liu et al. 2021
.

Preservation.

Gao et al. 2023a
introduced the evaluation dimension of preservation in the context of post-retrieval attribution. This setup often includes a revision step, where the
llm
-generated text is updated after retrieving relevant evidence. Preservation measures how much the revised text
y
retains from the original output
x
. We extracted two frequently used metrics for evaluating preservation.
Preservation Levenshtein
, a lexical overlap–based metric, penalizes unnecessary changes by computing the character-level Levenshtein edit distance
Gao et al. 2023a
.
F1-AP
represents the harmonic mean of attribution and preservation. It can be derived from the AIS-sentence metric, Preservation Levenshtein, and the human-annotated Preservation Intent
Gao et al. 2023a
.

Relevance.
This dimension evaluates how well an
llm
-generated text aligns with the user query or task. This dimension is often associated with notions of utility or helpfulness, particularly in
qa
scenarios.
G-Eval Relevance
uses an
llm
-as-a-judge approach to assess how relevant the generated response is to the original question
Liu et al. 2023b
. Within the RAGAS evaluation framework
Es et al. 2024
, several relevance-focused metrics have been proposed.
RAGAS Answer-Relevancy
is a semantic similarity-based metric that computes the average similarity between the input question and a set of questions automatically generated from the answer.
RAGAS Context-Recall
is a retrieval-based metric that measures how well the retrieved context reflects the ground-truth answer, focusing on whether key information has been successfully retrieved and incorporated.

Retrieval.
The performance of retrieval is mainly evaluated for non-parametric attribution, where it serves as a partial measure to assess whether the retrieval system provides relevant evidence for the
llm
. As expected, most metrics in this dimension rely on retrieval-based evaluation methods. The most common metrics include
Precision@k
and
Recall@k
, which evaluate the proportion of relevant documents retrieved among the top-k results and the proportion of all relevant documents retrieved, respectively
Ramu et al. 2024
.
Mean Reciprocal Rank
is also frequently used to measure how early the first relevant item appears in the ranked list
Xiao et al. 2025
. Additionally, the
RAGAS Context-Entity-Recall
metric captures entity-level recall by computing the fraction of entities from the ground-truth answer appearing in the retrieved context
Es et al. 2024
.

6.4
Frameworks and Benchmarks

We define evaluation frameworks as standardized procedures that systematically apply a predefined set of metrics over specific tasks in order to assess methodological performance for at least one evaluation dimension. In contrast, we define benchmarks as curated combinations of datasets that facilitate reproducible and comparative evaluation.
We identified 17 evaluation frameworks and 11 benchmarks, of which only two from each group are reused across multiple studies. This limited reuse highlights a persistent lack of consensus and standardization in current evaluation practices.

Frameworks.
Figure
7
maps the two most frequently used frameworks to the evaluation dimensions they cover. Fully applied in 12 studies,
ALCE

Gao et al. 2023b
is the most commonly used framework, which covers attribution, correctness, and linguistic quality. Several other studies use individual ALCE metrics, such as Citation Precision NLI and Citation Recall NLI, without applying the full framework.
G-Eval

Liu et al. 2023b
is used in two studies and provides a broader evaluation of natural language generation. It follows an
llm
-as-a-judge approach to assess attribution, linguistic quality, and relevance. Since evidence-based text generation with
llm
is a rapidly evolving task, several frameworks have been published starting in 2024 and have not yet been adopted in recent research. Table
5
in the appendix provides a complete overview of these evaluation frameworks, detailing the evaluation dimensions and methods for each.

Benchmarks.
Similar to the evaluation frameworks, we identified only two benchmarks that were used more than once.
ALCE

Gao et al. 2023b
functions both as a framework and benchmark, and has been used in nine studies. It supports multiple evaluation dimensions and is built on datasets focused on information-seeking tasks.
RAG-RewardBench

Jin et al. 2025
, designed to evaluate reward models for preference alignment in
rag
, spans 18 datasets and targets evaluation scenarios such as multi-hop reasoning, fine-grained citation, appropriate abstention, and conflict robustness. We provide the full list of evaluation benchmarks in Table
6
in the appendix, including their associated task and data domain.

7
Discussion

As shown, evidence-based text generation with
llm
is rapidly evolving, with 86% of papers emerging after 2023 and continued growth projected. While the limitations outlined by
Huang and Chang 2024
remain relevant, we emphasize and extend them based on our findings, as the field still faces gaps requiring further investigation.

Parametric Attribution.
Our analysis identified only seven studies that specifically explore novel parametric attribution approaches, extending beyond simply evaluating the citation capabilities of pure
llm
. While non-parametric attribution remains valuable, it primarily relies on synthesizing externally retrieved evidence, which is constrained by the performance of the retrieval system and does not fully exploit the knowledge encoded within the
llm
itself. Advancing towards more trustworthy
llm
will require parametric attribution methods that allow outputs to be traceable to the model’s training data, enabling users to better assess the correctness of generated content. Therefore, future research should explore hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of both parametric and non-parametric attribution.

Evaluation Standards.
With over 300 evaluation metrics identified but only two frameworks and benchmarks in common use, we emphasize the urgent need for standardized evaluation frameworks to enable fair and consistent comparison across methods. Future work should ensure these frameworks are adaptable to the diverse tasks within evidence-based text generation and comprehensively cover all evaluation dimensions. Additionally, the wide variation in human evaluation, with many studies introducing unique and non-standardized metrics, underscores the need for automated and scalable evaluation approaches.

Explainable Citations.
While a variety of approaches have been published in recent years, the citation behavior of
llm
remains largely underexplored. These models may exhibit citation-related biases similar to those seen in human authorship. To improve explainability of
llm
-generated texts, users should be able to understand why
llm
select a particular source from multiple candidates. Transparent citation reasoning is a prerequisite to identify such biases and increase trust. Future work should systematically analyze the citation behavior of current approaches and improve the explainability of citation reasoning.

8
Conclusion

We surveyed 134 papers on evidence
-
based text generation with
llm
, introduced a novel taxonomy, categorized 300 evaluation metrics into seven dimensions and six method and extracted 231 unique datasets. The field is still fragmented with varied terminology and evaluation standards. Our synthesis offers a clear reference point for advancing the reliability and verifiability of
llm
.
