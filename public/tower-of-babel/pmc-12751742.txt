Management for prolactinomas of postmenopausal female patients: a retrospective single-center study
Authors: Xiaoxue Chen, Yixin Lu, Jiayu Liu, Xiaoan Ke, Hui Miao, Lian Duan, Fengying Gong, Hongbo Yang, Hui Pan, Linjie Wang, Huijuan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate outcomes of prolactinoma management in postmenopausal women, focusing on treatment withdrawal strategies in premenopausal-diagnosed patients and treatment response in postmenopausal-diagnosed patients.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed postmenopausal patients with prolactinomas treated at Peking Union Medical College Hospital from 2014 to 2024. Patients were stratified by diagnosis timing (premenopausal vs. postmenopausal), with premenopausal-diagnosed cases further divided by treatment status.ResultsAmong premenopausal-diagnosed patients (n = 53), 35 attempted to discontinue the medication, and 16 of them experienced recurrence, with median time to recurrence of six months (IQR 4.25–9.75). All recurrent cases regained remission upon retreatment. History of unguided withdrawal attempts before menopause were identified in over half (n = 9, 56.25%) of recurrent cases. Subsequent prolactin measurement revealed statistically higher in the recurrence group than in the remission group in last follow-up before withdrawal, 1–6 months (p = 0.000136) and 6–12 months (p = 0.006717) after withdrawal. The postmenopausal-diagnosed subgroup (n = 6) consisted primarily of microadenomas (n = 5) and most showed excellent dopamine agonist response.ConclusionsThis study represents the largest retrospective analysis to date to explore the management of prolactinoma in postmenopausal women. The current evidence indicates that dopamine agonist withdrawal represents a promising therapeutic strategy for postmenopausal patients, and further research is warranted to confirm.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Prolactinoma, Menopause, Dopamine agonists, Withdrawal

Introduction

Prolactinomas are the most common type of pituitary adenoma and predominantly affect women aged 25–44 years, with a female-to-male ratio ranging from 5:1 to 10:1, though this ratio tends to equalize in postmenopausal women and age-matched men [1].

In premenopausal women, the most common clinical manifestations include amenorrhea or oligomenorrhea, infertility, and galactorrhea [1]. Dopamine agonists (DA) such as cabergoline and bromocriptine serve as the primary treatment [2]. Due to the varying responses of patients after drug withdrawal and the possibility of recurrence, patients might require long-term dopamine agonist therapy and continuous monitoring of the hormone and imaging results [3]. Previous retrospective studies have suggested that menopause may positively influence disease course, and some patients could maintain remission after withdrawal [4–8]. A recent multicenter retrospective study of 99 patients, including 56 who attempted discontinuation, has reported a 66% remission rate [8]. However, only five relevant studies were reported to date. In addition, these studies predominantly involved cabergoline-treated patients, with limited bromocriptine outcomes. Further research into postmenopausal drug withdrawal strategies is therefore warranted.

The precise prevalence of prolactinomas that develop after natural menopause remains difficult to determine, as most cases were incidentally detected during imaging examinations [9–11]. To date, few studies have specifically focused on his patient population. Current understanding of the pathogenesis, clinical features, and treatment of these patients remains limited. The true prevalence of prolactinomas in the postmenopausal population remains uncertain. This is largely because tumors that do not cause mass effects or overt symptoms often go unrecognized, leading to underdiagnosis.

Postmenopausal health management remains a significant research priority [12]. For patients with prolactinoma, developing standardized, effective, safe, and well-studied strategies to smoothly navigate the menopausal transition and subsequent postmenopausal management is urgently needed.

Our study aims to retrospectively analyze a cohort of postmenopausal patients with prolactinomas to evaluate DA withdrawal outcomes in women diagnosed before menopause, assess prolactin and tumor changes in patients continuing therapy, and characterize the clinical features and treatment responses in postmenopausal-diagnosed cases. To our knowledge, this represents the largest Chinese cohort investigating bromocriptine withdrawal in postmenopausal women. As bromocriptine is the primary treatment at our center, this study may offer further evidence to guide withdrawal decisions in similar clinical settings.

Subjects and methods

Patients

This study included 104 female patients with prolactinoma treated at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Beijing, China, between 2014 and 2024, clinically confirmed to be postmenopausal at final follow-up. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Ethical audit number: I-24PJ1285). The informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Menopause was defined as spontaneous amenorrhea lasting ≥ 12 months with elevated serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, excluding pregnancy, iatrogenic menopause, and other pathological causes [13]. The diagnosis of prolactinoma was established based on typical clinical manifestations of hyperprolactinemia, serum prolactin (PRL) levels >25 ng/mL on at least two separate measurements by serial sampling and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-confirmed sellar mass lesions, with exclusion of macroprolactinemia, non-functioning sellar lesions which lead to elevated prolactin levels through a stalk effect, and other secondary causes of hyperprolactinemia [1, 14].

The study categorized patients into two groups based on the timing of prolactinoma diagnosis relative to menopause, the premenopausal-diagnosed group and the postmenopausal-diagnosed group. The premenopausal-diagnosed group comprised patients diagnosed before their final menstrual period. These patients were additionally required to maintain DA therapy through the menopausal transition. The postmenopausal-diagnosed group consisted of patients diagnosed after their final menstrual period.

Exclusions included multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome (n = 1) and premature menopause from idiopathic/iatrogenic causes (n = 10). The premenopausal-diagnosed group further excluded patients who discontinued DA therapy before menopausal transition with achieved biochemical remission (n = 10), those who underwent pituitary surgery/radiotherapy and no longer required medical therapy (n = 13) and cases where DA withdrawal directly induced amenorrhea (n = 11).

Premenopausal-diagnosed patients were further divided into two groups based on whether they stopped treatment or not. Biochemical remission was defined as sustained serum PRL levels < 25 ng/mL for ≥ 24 months [1]. Disease recurrence was characterized by confirmed serum PRL ≥ 25 ng/mL on two consecutive measurements. Clinical assessment

Serum levels of PRL and sex hormones were measured using Beckman chemiluminescent immunoassays (Brea, CA, USA). MRI scans were evaluated by neuroradiologists to determine the maximal tumor diameter. Based on the maximum diameter of the tumor, prolactinomas were classified into microadenomas (< 10 mm) and macroadenomas (≥ 10 mm). Data on hormone levels, MRI findings, medication regimens, and clinical outcomes were collected during follow-up. Medication withdrawal candidates included patients with biochemical remission confirmed by physicians, as well as those with sustained stability in hormone levels and tumor size who opted for discontinuation after medical discussion.

Statistical analysis

Data were extracted from our institutional medical records and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed the distribution of quantitative variables. Normally distributed parametric variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-parametric variables are expressed as median with interquartile range [25th, 75th]. For normally distributed data, Student’s t-test was used for comparisons. Otherwise, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value < 0.05. Figures were designed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0).

Results

Clinical characteristics and treatment of patients with premenopausal diagnosis

A total of 53 patients diagnosed with prolactinoma before menopause were enrolled and followed for at least 12 months after menopause. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean age at onset was 39.83 ± 8.69 years, and the median diagnostic age was 43.00 years (IQR 35.0-47.5). Presenting symptoms included menstrual irregularity (88%), galactorrhea (62%), headache (24%), and visual impairment (14%). Only one case was incidentally detected by imaging. At diagnosis, 35 patients had microadenomas and 14 patients had macroadenomas, with a median tumor diameter of 8.5 mm (IQR 5.00-12.25). All patients received long-term DA treatment, including one patient who did not achieve remission after the operation and then switched to drug treatment. In the DA first-line treatment group, cabergoline was the initial choice for only one patient, bromocriptine was switched to cabergoline in one patient, and the remaining 50 patients maintained bromocriptine monotherapy.

The mean age at menopause was 50.30 ± 3.24 years. At the last visit before menopause, the median PRL level was 16.03 ng/ml (IQR 7.90–33.6), with a mean tumor diameter of 7.68 ± 2.43 mm. At menopause, the mean luteinizing hormone (LH) level was 30.75 ± 17.09 IU/L, and the median FSH level was 68.28 IU/L (IQR 34.77–97.16).

During menopausal transition period, treatment was discontinued in 35 patients and continued in 18 patients. The decision to stop medication was made jointly by the doctor and the patient. Some patients who achieved biochemical remission still chose to continue taking the medicine even after the doctor’s advice, while some patients, although their hormones were not normalized but remained stable for a long time, voluntarily requested to stop after menopause for observation. At the last visit before menopause, the biochemical remission rate of PRL was 68.00% in DA withdrawal group (hormone records available for 25 patients) and 66.67% in continuing medication group (hormone records available for 12 patients). The median follow-up duration was 176.00 months (IQR 106.00-258.00) in the discontinuation group and 116.50 months (IQR 84.00-181.25) in the continuation group. The median treatment duration was 100.00 months (IQR 61.00-186.00) in the former group and 116.50 months (IQR 84.00-181.25) in the latter group. At the last follow-up, the biochemical remission rate was 71.42% in the discontinuation group (35 patients) and 66.67% in the continuation group (18 patients).

Remission and recurrence in DA withdrawal patients

Among the patients who discontinued DA therapy, 16 (45.71%) experienced recurrence during follow-up, with a median time of 6 months (IQR: 4.25–9.75). Recurrence occurred in 13 patients within the first year after treatment withdrawal and in 3 patients thereafter. Symptomatic recurrence occurred in four patients, including dizziness (n = 1), headache (n = 1), and triggered galactorrhea (n = 1).

The PRL level changes in both the remission group and the recurrence group without restarting medication are shown in Fig. 2. Notably, one patient in the remission group had a PRL level of 38.59 ng/mL at last visit before DA withdrawal but achieved biochemical remission (PRL: 18.23 ng/mL) at the first follow-up after stopping medication. Of the patients who experienced recurrence, 11 were managed with observation alone, whereas 5 restarted dopamine agonist therapy. Successful PRL control was achieved in all cases after restarting treatment (Fig. 3).

Follow-up of patients continuing medication

Eighteen patients maintained bromocriptine treatment after menopause. Of note, two patients (aged 49 and 53 years) initially presumed natural menopause presented with abnormal postmenopausal bleeding. Hormonal evaluation revealed markedly elevated PRL with non-elevated FSH levels. Following treatment, both achieved normalized PRL levels and resumed menstruation, thus meeting criteria for premenopausal-diagnosed group.

At the last visit before menopause, biochemical remission was achieved in eight patients. Six patients continued their original treatment, with daily bromocriptine doses ranging from 1.25 mg to 6.25 mg. Among these, three maintained PRL levels below 25 ng/mL, while one failed to achieve biochemical control (Fig. 4). Two patients who didn’t achieve remission before menopause demonstrated persistently decreasing prolactin levels during follow-up.

Three patients were incidentally diagnosed through routine prolactin testing, two via sellar masses detected by MRI, and only one presented with clinical galactorrhea. Detailed reevaluation revealed that incidentally diagnosed patients actually exhibited symptoms including visual field defects, galactorrhea, headache, dizziness, vision loss, and fatigue.

At diagnosis, median PRL was 139.8 ng/mL (IQR 60.61-194.14) with median tumor size of 8 mm (IQR 7.4–15.5). Five patients initiated DA therapy (median treatment duration: 25 months [IQR 4–48]), while one opted for observation. During median follow-up of 35 months (IQR 10.5–50), three treated patients achieved biochemical remission, one showed significant PRL reduction without full remission, one patient represented a tendency of drug resistance, and the observed patient maintained stable hyperprolactinemia.

Discussion

This retrospective study analyzed the postmenopausal management of patients with prolactinomas at our center, including 53 women with premenopausal diagnosis who transitioned to menopause and 6 with postmenopausal diagnosis. The study focused on remission outcomes after treatment withdrawal in women who initially presented during reproductive years, and clinical characteristics and treatment responses in postmenopausal-diagnosed cases. The findings aim to explore management strategies for patients with prolactinomas in postmenopausal status.

The strict withdrawal criteria for premenopausal patients required PRL normalization, effective treatment with low-dose DA for ≥ 2 years, and >50% tumor size reduction ([15]– [16]). Three prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses summarized withdrawal outcomes in reproductive-aged women [17–19]. A 2018 meta-analysis of 24 studies included more than 1100 patients treated with bromocriptine or cabergoline, which reported an overall remission rate of 37% after withdrawal, ranging from 15.1% in bromocriptine-treated macroadenomas to 40.8% in cabergoline-treated microadenomas [17]. Our cohort included both patients meeting strict criteria and those with incompletely normalized PRL levels. Notably, two patients whose PRL was over 25 ng/mL before withdrawal achieved sustained remission during follow-ups, suggesting a potential beneficial effect of menopause on prolactinoma management.

The 2023 Pituitary Society international Consensus Statement strongly recommends a trial of dopamine agonist withdrawal for well-controlled microprolactinomas in women entering menopause [1]. For premenopausal-diagnosed patients, menopause appears to provide additional benefits for disease control, likely mediated by postmenopausal hypoestrogenism. It was demonstrated in previous basic studies that estrogen promoted lactotroph proliferation via estrogen receptors both in vivo and in vitro, while blockade of estrogen-related pathways suppressed the proliferation [20–24].

In our study, 54.3% of postmenopausal patients achieved sustained remission after treatment withdrawal. Previous studies reported remission rates of 46%-73%, with the largest menopausal withdrawal study (n = 99) demonstrating 66% remission [4–8]. It was significantly higher than rates observed in women before menopause. Unlike most previous studies where cabergoline was preferred, our center primarily used bromocriptine yet achieved comparable remission rates. The lower remission rate in our study might be reasonably attributed to the predominant use of bromocriptine, which has been shown to be less effective than cabergoline in inducing long-term remission after withdrawal. This finding underscores the treatment landscape in our clinical setting and provides valuable real-world data on bromocriptine outcomes. It provides the largest dataset on bromocriptine withdrawal outcomes in women after menopause, which offer new insights for clinical decision-making.

It was reported that PRL levels before treatment withdrawal and in the immediate early period after withdrawal could predict remission outcomes [4–8]. Our data strengthen these observations, demonstrating the utility of serial PRL monitoring prior to and following drug withdrawal. Notably, patients who maintained remission exhibited significantly lower PRL levels before withdrawal than those who relapsed, which suggested that prolactin could serve as a biomarker for identifying optimal candidates for treatment withdrawal. Moreover, dynamic changes in PRL at 6 and 12 months after withdrawal warrant close attention. These results highlighted the critical need for vigilant monitoring during the first year after treatment discontinuation to facilitate early detection of recurrence and timely therapeutic reintervention. Another instructive finding was that patients with a history of self-directed treatment interruptions before menopause faced higher recurrence risks. It is suggested that clinicians should be particularly cautious when considering treatment withdrawal in women with documented poor compliance or previous unsuccessful discontinuation attempts.

Our study also included patients with postmenopausal-diagnosed disease. To ensure diagnostic clarity, we excluded cases where menopause resulted from hysterectomy rather than natural ovarian decline, as well as those with amenorrhea that might have masked earlier diagnosis. Several patients showed visual field defects, headache, and vision loss. Notably, these symptoms occurred in patients whose tumors, although classified as microadenomas, were large in size (approaching 1.0 cm in diameter, see Table 3). Among the six qualifying patients in our cohort, the majority were identified incidentally through routine hormone testing or imaging findings. Limited awareness frequently contributed to delayed diagnosis as some patients actually had symptoms. Current evidence on this patient subgroup remains scarce, with only two published case series available for comparison. Shimon et al. described a small group of 14 patients. Five were completely asymptomatic at diagnosis, while the remaining patients presented with headaches, visual disturbances, double vision, or galactorrhea [10]. Notably, 10 patients in that series had macroadenomas, with three meeting criteria for giant adenomas. Another study followed 17 patients and found that headaches and visual symptoms prompted diagnosis in nearly half the cases [11]. That cohort included just one microadenoma, with the remaining 16 patients having macroadenomas that frequently showed extrasellar extension. While existing literature highlighted macroadenomas in this population, microadenomas represented the majority in our cohort (83.3%, 5/6), with only one macroadenoma observed.

Among patients who received DA therapy in our study, 3/5 achieved PRL levels < 25 ng/mL, with the remaining case showing significant reduction, demonstrating generally favorable treatment responses. Following treatment, a marked improvement in symptoms was observed in all but one patient. The decline in hormone levels was accompanied by the resolution of galactorrhea. In contrast, visual field defects and impaired vision showed limited improvement. In Shimon’s cohort, 12/14 patients received medical therapy, and 9 ultimately maintained controlled PRL levels [10]. Santharam et al. reported 15/16 (94%) macroadenoma patients achieving normalized PRL with DA therapy, while one untreated microadenoma case exhibited progressive PRL elevation [11].

Notably, therapeutic intervention restored regular menstruation in two patients who had been clinically diagnosed with natural menopause before treatment initiation. Previous reports included a case of a 54-year-old woman with a 40-mm adenoma whose amenorrhea began at age 40 [10]. Such cases illustrated the diagnostic difficulty in distinguishing prolactinoma-related hypogonadism from natural menopause in women beyond 40 years of age, and it often results in diagnostic delays.

Metabolic abnormalities and related comorbidities were frequently observed in postmenopausal women with prolactinoma, regardless of whether disease diagnosis occurred before or after menopause. One consideration favoring treatment continuation after menopause appears to be the medication’s metabolic benefits. Mazziotti et al. conducted a comparative study of bone health parameters in pre- and postmenopausal patients with prolactinomas, revealing significantly higher vertebral fracture prevalence in postmenopausal women [25]. Untreated patients showed greater fracture incidence than those receiving cabergoline therapy. Previous investigations on amenorrhea women with hyperprolactinemia demonstrated that DA therapy improves bone mass in those with osteopenia ([26]– [27]). Epidemiological analyses of the association between prolactin and cardiovascular diseases demonstrated that elevated serum PRL levels positively correlated with increased arterial blood pressure in postmenopausal women [28]. Despite these findings, research specifically addressing metabolic outcomes in postmenopausal patients with prolactinomas remained limited and unclear. Therefore, treatment discontinuation remains a clinically viable option for postmenopausal patients, provided that individualized risk-benefit assessments are conducted.

This study has certain limitations, including variability in withdrawal criteria for premenopausal-diagnosed patients and occasional missing follow-up data. Routine MRI surveillance was not consistently performed, leading to incomplete radiological data. In addition, as this is a retrospective study, radiological data were not available for all patients. The limited sample size and non-continuous monitoring preclude a formal comparison of the tumor sizes before and after treatment discontinuation which were presented in Table 2. In addition, selection bias is a key limitation, resulting from the real-world, retrospective design of our study. Specifically, the decision to withdraw treatment was not strictly protocolized but was influenced by clinical judgment and patient preference. As such, some patients who might have been eligible for withdrawal did not attempt it. The potential for misclassification bias must be acknowledged, particularly due to the relatively low prolactin cutoff used to define hyperprolactinemia. While this threshold was selected to maximize clinical sensitivity and avoid underdiagnosis, it may have increased the risk of misclassification.

Nevertheless, our study provides novel and critical insights beyond prior reports. While the existing literature on withdrawal is indeed largely based on cabergoline cohorts, our work represents the largest dedicated cohort investigating bromocriptine withdrawal in postmenopausal women. Its primary value lies in providing robust evidence for clinicians in regions where drug accessibility and cost make bromocriptine, not cabergoline, the first-line therapy. Furthermore, as a substantial Chinese cohort, it adds important geographic diversity to the literature. Most significantly, our analysis moves beyond confirming known factors to identify a prior failed withdrawal attempt as a novel risk factor for postmenopausal recurrence—a key, previously unreported clinical insight for stratifying management. Our approach provides methodological advantages through strict menopausal status verification that excludes surgical or unexplained premature menopause, and comprehensive long-term follow-up. These observations underscore the necessity for prospective, multicenter trials to refine cessation protocols and further elucidate the natural history of postmenopausal-diagnosed prolactinomas. Larger studies involving multiple centers remain necessary to further investigate optimal treatment withdrawal strategies and better characterize postmenopausal-diagnosed prolactinoma cases.

In conclusion, this study represents a large retrospective analysis to date to explore the management of prolactinoma in postmenopausal women. It not only systematically evaluates treatment withdrawal outcomes in premenopausal-diagnosed prolactinomas but also describes the special clinical characteristics of postmenopausal-diagnosed disease. The current evidence indicates that dopamine agonist withdrawal represents a promising therapeutic strategy for postmenopausal patients, and further research is warranted to confirm.
