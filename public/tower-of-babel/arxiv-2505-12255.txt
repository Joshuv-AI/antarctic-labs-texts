Anisotropic Calderón Problem for a Non-Local Second Order Elliptic Operator
Susovan Pramanik

Abstract
. This paper investigates the anisotropic Calderón problem for a non-local elliptic operator of order 2, on closed Riemannian manifolds. We demonstrate that using the Cauchy data set, we can recover the geometry of a closed Riemannian manifold up to standard gauge.



1.
Introduction

The anisotropic Calderón problem is geometric in nature (cf.
[
39
,
47
]
). Let us state the well-known open problem in the context of a closed, connected Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with
\dim(M)>2
. Let
\mathcal{O}\subset M
be a non-empty open set. The Calderón problem examines whether knowledge of the Cauchy data set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{O}}_{M,g}=\{(u|_{\mathcal{O}},(-\Delta_{g})u|_{\mathcal{O}})\mid u\in C^{\infty}(M),\,-\Delta_{g}u=0\,\text{in}\,\,M\setminus\overline{\mathcal{O}}\}

determines the isometry class of the manifold
(M,g)
.
(-\Delta_{g})
represents the positive Laplace-Beltrami operator on
(M,g)
.

When
\dim(M)=2
, the problem is solved using an additional gauge due to conformal invariance of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. See some of the references
[
42
,
4
,
7
,
29
]
. The problem for real-analytic manifolds in dimensions three and above has been addressed (see the references
[
39
,
38
,
37
]
), but it is still open for smooth manifolds. Positive results have been observed in transversally anisotropic geometries, see
[
16
,
17
]
. Starting from these celebrated works
[
48
,
35
]
, for current research on the Calderón problem, we refer to the survey article
[
51
]
.

The purpose of this work is to explore the anisotropic Calderón problem for a second order elliptic psedodifferential operator
((-\Delta_{g})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{\frac{1}{2}}
for some fixed
m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, on smooth, closed, connected Riemannian manifolds.

Let
(M,g)
be a closed, connected Riemannian manifold with dimension
\dim(M)\geq 2
. We define
-\Delta_{g}
as the positive definite, self-adjoint Laplace-Beltrami operator on
L^{2}(M)
with domain
\mathcal{D}(-\Delta_{g})=H^{2}(M);

[
50
, pp. 25]
.

The spectrum of
(-\Delta_{g})
over
(M,g)
consists of a discrete sequence of eigenvalues

0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\to+\infty,

where each eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
has a finite multiplicity
d_{k}
.

Define
\pi_{k}:L^{2}(M)\to\ker(-\Delta_{g}-\lambda_{k})
as the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{k}
. Then, for any function
f\in L^{2}(M)
,

\pi_{k}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\langle f,\phi_{k_{j}}\rangle_{L^{2}(M)}\phi_{k_{j}},

where,
\{\phi_{k_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{d_{k}}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\ker(-\Delta_{g}-\lambda_{k})
.

Set
m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. We consider the positively perturbed bi-Laplace operator on
(M,g)
as:

\mathcal{L}_{g}:=(-\Delta_{g})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I}.

This is an unbounded operator on
L^{2}(M)
, with the domain of definition
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{g})=H^{4}(M)
, see
[
49
]
.

We now define the operator of our interest, the
\frac{1}{2}
-fractional power of
\mathcal{L}_{g}
, through its action over its domain of definition
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}})=H^{2}(M)
(cf.
[
49
]
), as

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}u

\displaystyle=\left((-\Delta_{g})^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}u,\quad u\in H^{2}(M)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\lambda_{k}^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\pi_{k}(u).

We note that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is an elliptic pseudodifferential with order
2
. For related discussion, see Section
2.2
.

Because all eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{g}
are strictly positive, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is invertible over the range space
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{L}_{g})=L^{2}(M)
, and its inverse is given by

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f

\displaystyle=\left((-\Delta_{g})^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}f,\quad f\in L^{2}(M)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\lambda_{k}^{2}+m^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\pi_{k}(f).

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset M
be a non-empty open subset of
M
. For each
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
, the equation

(3)

\mathcal{L}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{g}u=f\quad\mbox{in }M

has a unique solution
u=u_{f}\in C^{\infty}(M)\cap L^{2}(M)
. See
[
20
]
. The solution is formally expressed as
u=\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f
(cf. (
2
)).

Now we state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M_{j},g_{j})
be two smooth, closed, connected Riemannian manifolds, with
M_{1}\cap M_{2}\neq\emptyset
. Consider
\mathcal{O}_{j}\subset M_{j},\,j=1,2
to be two non-empty open sets such that
(\mathcal{O}_{1},g_{1})=(\mathcal{O}_{2},g_{2})=:(\mathcal{O},g)
. Assume that

\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f|_{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f|_{\mathcal{O}},\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{O}).

Then, there exists a diffeomorphism
\Phi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
such that
\Phi^{\ast}g_{2}=g_{1}
.

Remark 1.3
.

Let
m\in(0,\infty)
, and we introduce another operator of interest,

\mathcal{A}_{g}:=\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}-m\mathbb{I}.

The definition is:

\mathcal{A}_{g}u=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left((\lambda_{k}^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}-m\right)\pi_{k}u,

where
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{g})=H^{2}(M)
, and
\{(\lambda_{k},\pi_{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
as previously.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}(M)\cap L^{2}(M)
be the unique solution of the equation

\mathcal{A}_{g}u=0\mbox{ in }M

with satisfying
\langle u,1\rangle_{L^{2}(M)}=0
for
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
. See
[

.

Next, we define the corresponding Cauchy data set:

\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{O}}_{M,g}=\{(u|_{\mathcal{O}},\mathcal{A}_{g}u|_{\mathcal{O}})\mid u\in C^{\infty}(M),\,\,\,\mathcal{A}_{g}u=0\,\text{in}\,M\setminus\overline{\mathcal{O}}\}.

This dataset contains solutions to the equation
\mathcal{A}_{g}u=0
and serves as the basis on the anisotropic Calderón problem for the operator
\mathcal{A}_{g}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(M_{i},g_{i}),\,i=1,2
be two closed, connected Riemannian manifolds with
\dim(M_{i})\geq 2
. Assume that
\mathcal{O}\subset M_{1}\cap M_{2}
is a non-empty open subset where the metrics agree, i.e.,
(\mathcal{O},g_{1})=(\mathcal{O},g_{2}):=(\mathcal{O},g)
. The equality of the Cauchy data set over
(\mathcal{O},g)
, i.e.
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{O}}_{M_{1},g_{1}}=\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{O}}_{M_{2},g_{2}}
, implies the existence of a diffeomorphism
\Phi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
such that
\Phi^{\ast}g_{2}=g_{1}
.

The proof is quite similar to Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminary understandings.

2.1.
Heat semigroup approach in defining the fractional power of
\mathcal{L}_{g}

Here, we define the fractional power of
\mathcal{L}_{g}=((-\Delta_{g})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})
using the semigroup technique, which is equivalent to the prior definition.

Let
e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}
denote the associated heat semigroup on
L^{2}(M)
. Let
v\in L^{2}(M)
, we define

(4)

e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}v(x)=\int_{M}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g}}(t,x,y)v(y)\,dV_{g}(y),

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g}}(t,x,y)
is the heat kernel for the heat semigroup
e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}
, which is defined as

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g}}(t,x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-t(\lambda_{k}^{2}+m^{2})}\,\phi_{k}(x)\,\phi_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=e^{-m^{2}t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-t\lambda_{k}^{2}}\,\phi_{k}(x)\,\phi_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=e^{-m^{2}t}\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g}^{2}}(t,x,y),

where,
K_{\Delta^{2}_{g}}(t,x,y)
be the heat kernel for the heat semigroup
e^{-t\Delta^{2}_{g}}
.

Fei He
[
32
]
provides a pointwise estimate of the heat kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g}^{2}}(t,x,y)
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
32
]
)
.

Let
x,y
be two points on an arbitrary smooth connected compact Riemannian manifold
M
, and let
t\in(0,\infty)
. Then

(6)

\left|\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g}^{2}}(t,x,y)\right|\leq\frac{C}{t^{n/4}}e^{-\frac{c\,d_{g}(x,y)^{\frac{4}{3}}}{t^{\frac{1}{3}}}},

where
C>0
, and
d_{g}(x,y)
is the Riemannian distance between
x
and
y
.

According to
[
32
]
,
\int_{M}\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g}^{2}}(t,x,y)\,\,dV_{g}=1
, yields
\int_{M}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g}}(t,x,y)\,\,dV_{g}\leq 1
, showing that
\{e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a submarkovian semigroup. Using Theorem
2.1
, we obtain the following estimate:

(7)

\left|\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g}}(t,x,y)\right|\leq\frac{C}{t^{n/4}}\,e^{-m^{2}t}\,e^{-\frac{c\,d_{g}(x,y)^{\frac{4}{3}}}{t^{\frac{1}{3}}}},\quad\forall(t,x,y)\in(0,\infty)\times M\times M.

Euclidean case in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},e)
:

The heat kernel for the bi-Laplace heat equation has the self-similar form:

\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{e}^{2}}(t,x,y)=\frac{1}{t^{\frac{n}{4}}}\,e^{-c_{n}\frac{|x-y|^{\frac{4}{3}}}{t^{\frac{1}{3}}}},\quad\forall(t,x,y)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
c_{n}>0
is some constant.

So for the operator
(-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I}
in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},e)
, we have

e^{-t((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})}v(x)=e^{-m^{2}t}\,\frac{1}{t^{\frac{n}{4}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-c\frac{|x-y|^{\frac{4}{3}}}{t^{\frac{1}{3}}}}v(y)\,dy,

for
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

This can be seen that,

\displaystyle e^{-t((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})}v(x)

\displaystyle=e^{-m^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,\frac{1}{t^{\frac{n}{4}}}R\left(\frac{x-y}{t^{\frac{1}{4}}}\right)v(y)\,dy,\quad\text{where, }R(x)=e^{-c|x|^{\frac{4}{3}}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\left|e^{-t((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})}v(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-m^{2}t}\,\Big|\Big|\frac{1}{t^{\frac{n}{4}}}R\left(\frac{x-y}{t^{\frac{1}{4}}}\right)\Big|\Big|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{y}^{n})}\,\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{y})}

\displaystyle\quad=e^{-m^{2}t}\,\|R\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Next, we define the fractional power of
((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})
in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},e)
. Consider
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We first define

((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-\alpha}v=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})}v\,dt.

This is pointwise well-defined, since

\displaystyle\left|((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-\alpha}v(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-t((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})}v(x)\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)}\|R\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-m^{2}t}\frac{dt}{t^{1-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)}\|R\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)}{|m|^{2\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad=\,\frac{\|R\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{|m|^{2\alpha}}<\infty,\quad m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

In other words, we have the mapping property

((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-\alpha}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mapsto L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\mbox{with }\|((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-\alpha}\|_{L^{2}\mapsto L^{\infty}}\lesssim\frac{1}{|m|^{2\alpha}}.

Further one defines, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
u\in H^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{\alpha}u

\displaystyle=((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})\circ((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-(1-\alpha)}u

\displaystyle=((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{-(1-\alpha)}\circ((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\alpha}e^{-t((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})}(((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})u)\,dt.

However,
H^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{D}\left(((-\Delta_{e})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{\alpha}\right)=H^{4\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, can be seen easily through the Fourier transform definition. We refer
[
25
]
for this approach and the related mapping properties.

In
(M,g)
:

We would like to do the same exercise for the closed manifold
(M,g)
that we are interested in. From (
4
) and (
7
), we have the pointwise bound on
e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}v
as

\displaystyle|e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}v(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{M}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g}}(t,x,y)v(y)\,dV_{g}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-m^{2}t}\,\int_{M}\frac{1}{t^{n/4}}R\left(\frac{d(x,y)}{t^{1/4}}\right)|v(y)|\,dV_{g}(y),

(8)

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-m^{2}t}\,\|R\|_{L^{2}(M)}\,\|v\|_{L^{2}(M)}

for
v\in L^{2}(M)
.

Now we define the fractional power of
\mathcal{L}_{g}
. Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We define
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}
over
L^{2}(M)
as

(9)

\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}v=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}v\,dt.

for
v\in L^{2}(M)

Using (
8
), as before it can be seen that

\left|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}v(x)\right|\leq C\,\frac{\|R\|_{L^{2}(M)}\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}}{|m|^{2\alpha}}<\infty,\quad m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},

or, the mapping property follows as

\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}:L^{2}(M)\mapsto L^{\infty}(M),\quad\|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}\|_{L^{2}\mapsto L^{\infty}}\lesssim\frac{1}{|m|^{2\alpha}}.

Let us recall (
4
) here, since all the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{g}
are positive, and
0<(\lambda_{1}+m^{2})\leq(\lambda_{2}+m^{2})\leq\cdots\leq(\lambda_{k}+m^{2})\to\infty
, then
\exists\,\,\beta>0
such that

\|e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}\,v(x)\|_{L^{2}(M)}\leq e^{-\beta t}\,\|v(x)\|_{L^{2}(M)},

for
v\in L^{2}(M)
.

Now by using Minkowski’s integral inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}v\|_{L^{2}(M)}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{M}\left|\frac{1}{\Gamma({\alpha})}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-(1-\alpha)}e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}v(x)\,dt\right|^{2}dV_{g}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma({\alpha})}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-(1-\alpha)}\left(\int_{M}|e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}v(x)|^{2}\,dV_{g}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\Gamma({\alpha})}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-(1-\alpha)}\|e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}v\|_{L^{2}(M)}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}(M)}}{\Gamma({\alpha})}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-(1-\alpha)}e^{-\beta t}\,dt

(10)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}(M)}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\,\frac{1}{\beta^{\alpha}}=\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}(M)}}{\beta^{\alpha}}.

Hence, the mapping property

\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}:L^{2}(M)\mapsto L^{2}(M),\quad\mbox{with }\|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-\alpha}\|_{L^{2}\mapsto L^{2}}\leq\,\frac{1}{\beta^{\alpha}}.

Next, we define
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\alpha}
over
H^{4}(M)
as

\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\alpha}u=\mathcal{L}_{g}\circ\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-(1-\alpha)}u=\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-(1-\alpha)}\circ\mathcal{L}_{g}u=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\alpha}e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g}}(\mathcal{L}_{g}u)\,dt,

for
u\in H^{4}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\alpha})
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. See
[
10
]
.

2.2.
Symbol and order

Now, let us discuss the symbol and order of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}
defined over
(M,g)
. Up to the level of chart, let us summarise our understanding of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}:=((-\Delta_{g})^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I})^{\frac{1}{2}}
over
(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)
. We begin with the following result

Theorem 2.2

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\beta>0
and
g
be a
C^{\infty}
Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, which agrees with the Euclidean metric outside of a compact set. Then the operator
(-\Delta_{g})^{\beta}\in\Psi^{2\beta}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and is a classical elliptic pseudodifferential operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with principal symbol

\left(\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}g^{jk}(x)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\right)^{\beta},\quad(x,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

Remark 2.3
.

In the preceding finding, we understand
(-\Delta_{g})^{\beta}
as follows. Let
\beta=m+\alpha
, where
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
(-\Delta_{g})^{\beta}=(-\Delta_{g})^{m}\circ(-\Delta_{g})^{\alpha}
, where the operator
(-\Delta_{g})^{\alpha}
is defined via the functional calculus as follows.

Consider the operator
(-\Delta_{g})
in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},g)
. Let
e^{t\Delta_{g}}
denote the associated heat semigroup on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We define

(-\Delta_{g})^{-\alpha}v(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\,e^{t\Delta_{g}}v(x)\,dt.

The operator
(-\Delta_{g})^{\alpha}
over
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
is defined as

(-\Delta_{g})^{\alpha}u(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\alpha}\,e^{t\Delta_{g}}((-\Delta_{g})u(x))\,dt.

However,
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{D}\left((-\Delta_{g})^{\alpha}\right)=H^{2\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We refer to
[
20
]
for further details.

Next, we review the symbol class definitions.

Assume
m,\rho,\delta\in\mathbb{R}
and
0\leq\rho,\delta\leq 1
. We define the symbol class
S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
to consist of all functions
p\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that, for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all multi-indices
\alpha,\beta
, there exists a constant
C_{K,\alpha,\beta}>0
such that

|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}p(x,\xi)|\leq C_{K,\alpha,\beta}(1+|\xi|)^{m-\rho|\beta|+\delta|\alpha|},\quad\text{for all }x\in K,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In particular, the subclass
S^{m}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
consists of symbols
p(x,\xi)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}))
for which the estimates

|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}p(x,\xi)|\leq C_{K,\alpha,\beta}(1+|\xi|)^{m-|\beta|}

hold uniformly for all
x\in K
, where
K
is any compact set.

The space
\Psi^{m}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of pseudodifferential operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

associated with the symbol class
S^{m}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})

consisting of operators of the form

Au(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i(x-y)\cdot\theta}\,a(x,y,\theta)\,u(y)\,dy\,d\theta,\quad u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where the amplitude
a(x,y,\theta)\in S^{m}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For more details, we refer to
[
28
]
.

Symbol class for
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}

We have

\mathcal{L}_{g}=\left(-\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}g^{jk}(x)\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\right)^{2}+m^{2}\mathbb{I},\quad m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

The symbol for
\mathcal{L}_{g}
is

p(x,\xi)=\left(\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}g^{jk}(x)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\right)^{2}+m^{2}.

This is a classical example of a symbol in
S^{2}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, since it fulfills the symbol estimates:

|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}p(x,\xi)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}(1+|\xi|)^{2-|\beta|},

for all multi-indices
\alpha,\beta
.

Thus,

\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\Psi^{2}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

with its symbol

\left(\left(\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}g^{jk}(x)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\right)^{2}+m^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad(x,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

3.
Proof of Main Result

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we divide the argument into multiple propositions. Consider
\omega_{1}\subset\subset\mathcal{O}
as a non-empty open set. Then, there exists another non-empty open set
\omega_{2}\subset\subset\mathcal{O}
, such that

\overline{\omega_{1}}\cap\overline{\omega_{2}}=\emptyset.

Proposition 3.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, for any
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\omega_{1})
and for all
k=0,1,2,\dots
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,s^{k}\,ds=0,

where

(11)

\phi(s)=\frac{\big(e^{-\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}}\big)f(x)}{s^{\frac{1}{2}}},\quad x\in\omega_{2}.

Proof.

The proof follows the steps outlined in
[
20
, Proof of Theorem 1.1]
.

Since
g_{1}=g_{2}=g
on
\omega_{1}
, and
C_{0}^{\infty}(\omega_{1})
is an invariant subspace under
\Delta_{g}
, we obtain, for any
k=0,1,2,\dots
,

\Delta_{g_{1}}^{2}f=\Delta_{g_{2}}^{2}f=\Delta_{g}^{2}f\,\,\implies\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}^{k}f=\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}^{k}f=\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f\quad\text{on }\omega_{1}.

From our assumption, this leads to

\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f|_{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f|_{\mathcal{O}},\quad\forall f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\omega_{1}),\quad k=0,1,2,\dots.

Thus from (
9
) for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
there and for
x\in\mathcal{O}
, we have

(12)

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\left((e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})\,\,\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f\right)(x)\,dt=0,

or, by using the equation
\partial_{t}^{k}(e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{i}}}v)=e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{i}}}(\mathcal{L}_{g_{i}}^{k}v)
in
(M_{i},g_{i})
for any
v\in C^{\infty}_{0}(M_{i})
and
x\in\mathcal{O}
,

(13)

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\left(\partial_{t}^{k}(e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})f\right)(x)\,dt=0.

Now, for
l=0,1,\dots,k-1
, and for any
x\in\omega_{2}
and
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\omega_{1})
, we express the heat kernel difference as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{l}((e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})f)(x)

\displaystyle=((e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})\,\,\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f)(x)

(14)

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega_{1}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}(t,x,y)-\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}}(t,x,y))\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f(y)\,dV_{g}(y).

Thus, for
t>0
,
x\in\omega_{2}
, and
k=0,1,2,\dots
, we obtain the bound

(15)

\left|\partial_{t}^{l}((e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})f)(x)\right|\leq\|\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\omega_{1}\times\omega_{2})}\,\,\|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f\|_{L^{1}(\omega_{1})}.

For
t\in(0,1)
, using (
7
) and (
15
), we obtain for
l=0,1,2,\dots

(16)

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{t}^{l}((e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})f)(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-m^{2}t}e^{-c_{1}/t^{\frac{1}{3}}}\|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f\|_{L^{2}(\omega_{1})},

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\text{as }t\to 0,

where,
c_{1}>0
depends on
d_{g}(\overline{\omega_{1}},\overline{\omega_{2}})
.

According to
[
52
, Theorem 1]
,

(17)

\displaystyle\|e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{i}}}\mathcal{L}_{g}^{2l}f\|\leq\frac{C}{t^{\frac{n}{2}}}\,\,\|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f\|_{L^{1}(\omega_{1})}\quad\text{for}\,\,t>0,

which implies that for
t\in[1,\infty)
and
l=0,1,2,\dots,k-1
, applying to (
14
), yields

(18)

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{t}^{l}((e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})f)(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{t^{\frac{n}{2}}}\,\,\|\mathcal{L}_{g}^{k}f\|_{L^{1}(\omega_{1})},

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

Now, using integration by parts in (
13
) along with the decay estimates (
16
) and (
18
), we obtain

(19)

\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})f\,\,t^{-(k+\frac{1}{2})}\,dt=0\quad\text{for }k=1,2,\dots

Rewriting the above for
k=0,1,2,\dots
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}})f\,\,t^{-(k+1+\frac{1}{2})}\,dt=0.

Finally, by changing the variable
s=\frac{1}{t}
, we get at

\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,s^{k}\,ds=0,

where,
\phi(s)=\frac{\big(e^{-\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}-e^{-\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}}\big)f}{s^{\frac{1}{2}}}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Here
\phi(s)\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
, and the function defined by

(20)

f(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,e^{2\pi iz\,s}

is holomorphic on

\mathbb{C}^{+}:=\{x+iy:y>0\}
.

Proof.

Using (
16
) and (
18
), for
l=0
there, we get

|\phi(s)|\leq Cs^{\frac{n}{2}}\quad\text{for}\,\,s\in(0,1]\quad\text{and}\,\,\,|\phi(s)|\leq Ce^{-c_{1}s^{\frac{1}{3}}}\text{for}\,\,s\in(1,\infty).

Observed that,

\int_{0}^{1}|\phi(s)|^{2}ds\leq C^{2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{n}ds<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}|\phi(s)|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2c_{1}s^{1/3}}ds

\displaystyle=3C^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2c_{1}s}\,s^{2}ds<\infty.

Since both integrals are finite, it follows that

(21)

\phi\in L^{2}(0,\infty).

Let
f
is given as in (
20
). We estimate
f(z)
as

\displaystyle\left|f(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}|\phi(s)|e^{-2\pi\,y\,s}ds,\quad z=x+\mathrm{i}y,\mbox{ with }y>0,

\displaystyle\leq C\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}.

Thus, it is well defined pointwise on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and let
\{z_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
such that
z_{n}\to z
. Then, one finds

\displaystyle\left|f(z_{n})-f(z)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|\phi(s)||e^{2\pi iz_{n}s}-e^{2\pi izs}|ds\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\phi(s)||e^{2\pi iz_{n}s}-e^{2\pi izs}|^{2}ds.

For both
\operatorname{Im}(z)>\delta>0
, and
\operatorname{Im}(z_{n})>\delta
, gives

|e^{2\pi iz_{n}s}-e^{2\pi izs}|\leq 4e^{-2\delta s}.

Therefore, using the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we conclude that
f
is continuous at
z
.

Next, let
T
be any closed triangle in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Then, one sees

\displaystyle\int_{T}f(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)e^{2\pi izs}dsdz

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)ds\int_{T}e^{2\pi izs}dz=0.

Thanks to Morera’s theorem
[
11
]
, we conclude that
f
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

f(z)
defined in (
20
) is identically zero over
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

Proof.

Let

(22)

I=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi i\,z\,s)^{k}}{k!}\phi(s)\,ds.

Then, we estimate
|I|
as follows:

\displaystyle|I|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi\,|z|\,s)^{k}}{k!}|\phi(s)|\,ds

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2\pi|z|s}|\phi(s)|\,ds

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi|z|s}|\phi(s)|\,ds+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{2\pi|z|s}|\phi(s)|\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{e^{2\pi|z|s}s^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}_{h_{z}(s)}ds\,\,+\int_{1}^{\infty}\underbrace{e^{2\pi|z|s}e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{s}}s^{\frac{n-2}{4}}e^{-c_{1}s^{\frac{1}{3}}}}_{g_{z}(s)}\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad=C\int_{0}^{1}h_{z}(s)\,ds+\int_{1}^{\infty}g_{z}(s)\,ds.

Here, we see that
\left|\int_{0}^{1}h_{z}(s)\,ds\right|<\infty
.
For the following integral, define
c_{2}(z)=-c_{1}+2\pi|z|
, where
c_{1}
is the constant from (
16
), and let

D=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}:c_{2}(z)<0\},\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad|z|<\frac{c_{1}}{2\pi}.

Then, given
z\in D
, we estimate the second integral (using (
18
)):

\displaystyle\left|\int_{1}^{\infty}g_{z}(s)\,ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{2\pi|z|s}s^{\frac{n-2}{4}}e^{-c_{1}s^{\frac{1}{3}}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{(-c_{1}+2\pi|z|)s^{\frac{1}{3}}}s^{\frac{n-2}{4}}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{c_{2}(z)s^{\frac{1}{3}}}s^{\frac{n-2}{4}}\,ds.

Since
c_{2}(z)<0
for
z\in D
, the integral converges, meaning that
I<\infty\quad\forall z\in D.
Using Fubini’s theorem, we can interchange summation and integration on (
22
) to obtain:

\,\,\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi i\,z\,s)^{k}}{k!}\phi(s)\,ds=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi i\,z\,s)^{k}}{k!}\phi(s),\,ds,\quad\forall z\in D.

Let
\Omega\subset\subset D
be a non-empty, simply connected open set. For each
a\in\Omega
, the Cauchy integral formula yields

\displaystyle f(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{z-a}\,dz\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,e^{2\pi izs}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{z-a}\,dz\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi izs)^{k}}{k!}\phi(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{(2\pi iz)^{k}}{(z-a)k!}\,dz\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)s^{k}\,ds=0.

As
f(z)
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, the identity theorem implies that

f(z)=0\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

\phi
, defined in (
11
), is
0
over
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Let’s recall (
20
)

(23)

f(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,e^{2\pi iz\,s},

where
\phi\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
thanks to (
21
). According to Proposition
3.2
,
f
is a holomorphic function over
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

At this point, we define the so called Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
, that is the space of holomorphic functions in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, such that

(24)

\|f\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})}:=\sup_{y>0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x+iy)|^{2}dx\,<\infty.

By the classical Paley–Wiener theorem
[
43
]
, given any
\phi\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
, defining
f
as in (
23
), we have that
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
, and

(25)

\|f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}.

As we have already demonstrated that
f(z)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
(cf. Proposition
3.3
), it follows from (
25
) that
\phi(s)\equiv 0
for all
s>0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

This leads to our final finding:

Proposition 3.5
.

\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g_{1}}}(t,x,y)=\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g_{2}}}(t,x,y)\quad\text{$\forall t>0$ and $x,y\in O$},

where
\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g_{i}}}(t,x,y)
is the heat kernel of the heat semigroup
e^{t\Delta_{g_{i}}}
in
(0,\infty)\times(M_{i},g_{i})
for
i=1,2
respectively.

Proof.

Using Propositions
3.1
-
3.4
, we have that, for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\omega_{1})
,

e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{1}}}f(x)=e^{-t\mathcal{L}_{g_{2}}}f(x),\quad\forall\,\,t>0,\mbox{ and }x\in\omega_{2}.

This implies, for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\omega_{1})
,

(26)

e^{-t\Delta^{2}_{g_{1}}}f(x)=e^{-t\Delta^{2}_{g_{2}}}f(x),\quad\forall\,\,t>0,\mbox{ and }x\in\omega_{2}.

Next, we use the transmutation formula of Kannai (cf.
[
34
]
), which says, for all
v\in C^{\infty}(M_{i})
,

(27)

e^{-t\Delta^{2}_{g_{i}}}v=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}t^{\frac{1}{3}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\tau}{4t}}\frac{\sin(\sqrt{\tau}\Delta_{g_{i}})}{\Delta_{g_{i}}}v\,d\tau,\quad\forall t>0,

where
i=1,2
.

Let
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\omega_{1})
. Then from (
26
) and (
26
), we have

(28)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\tau}{4t}}\left(\frac{\sin(\sqrt{\tau}\Delta_{g_{1}})}{\Delta_{g_{1}}}f\right)(x)\,d\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\tau}{4t}}\left(\frac{\sin(\sqrt{\tau}\Delta_{g_{2}})}{\Delta_{g_{2}}}f\right)(x)\,d\tau,

for all
t>0
, and
x\in\omega_{2}
.

Then by applying the inverse Laplace transform, we obtain

(29)

\left(\frac{\sin(\sigma\Delta_{g_{1}})}{\Delta_{g_{1}}}f\right)(x)=\left(\frac{\sin(\sigma\Delta_{g_{2}})}{\Delta_{g_{2}}}f\right)(x),\quad\forall\sigma>0,\,\,\mbox{and }x\in\omega_{2}.

Since on
\mathcal{O}\supset\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
, we have
g_{1}=g_{2}=g
, it follows that

(30)

(\sin(\sigma\Delta_{g_{1}})f)(x)=(\sin(\sigma\Delta_{g_{2}})f)(x),\quad\forall\sigma>0,\,\,\mbox{and }x\in\omega_{2}.

Differentiating (
29
) with respect to
\sigma
yields

(31)

(\cos(\sigma\Delta_{g_{1}})f)(x)=(\cos(\sigma\Delta_{g_{2}})f)(x),\quad\forall\sigma>0,\,\,\mbox{and }x\in\omega_{2}.

By extending the sine function oddly and the cosine function evenly, we find that for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\omega_{2}
,

(32)

\displaystyle(\sin(\sigma\Delta_{g_{1}})f)(x)=(\sin(\sigma\Delta_{g_{2}})f)(x),\quad\mbox{and}\quad(\cos(\sigma\Delta_{g_{1}})f)(x)=(\cos(\sigma\Delta_{g_{2}})f)(x);

which gives

(33)

(e^{i\sigma\Delta_{g_{1}}}f)(x)=(e^{i\sigma\Delta_{g_{2}}}f)(x),\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\mbox{and }x\in\omega_{2}.

Now for any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, we can express

(\phi(\Delta_{g_{i}})f)(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{i\sigma\Delta_{g_{i}}}f)(x)\widehat{\phi}(\sigma)\,d\sigma.

Let us take
\psi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. then there exists a sequence
{\phi_{j}}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\phi_{j}\to\psi
uniformly. In particular, by choosing
\psi(\sigma)=\frac{\sin{t\sqrt{|\sigma|}}}{\sqrt{|\sigma|}}
, we obtain

(34)

\left(\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{-\Delta_{g_{1}}})}{\sqrt{-\Delta_{g_{1}}}}\right)f(x)=\left(\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{-\Delta_{g_{2}}})}{\sqrt{-\Delta_{g_{2}}}}\right)f(x),\quad\forall t>0,\,\,\mbox{and }x\in\omega_{2}.

Next, we seek Kannai’s transmutation formula (see
[
34
]
) again, to write for every
v\in C^{\infty}(M_{i})

(35)

e^{t\Delta_{g_{i}}}v=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}t^{\frac{1}{3}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\tau}{4t}}\frac{\sin(\sqrt{\tau}\sqrt{-\Delta_{g_{i}}})}{\sqrt{-\Delta_{g_{i}}}}v\,d\tau,\quad\forall t>0,

where
i=1,2
.

Thus using (
34
) and (
35
), we can deduce that for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\omega_{1})
,

e^{t\Delta_{g_{1}}}f(x)=e^{t\Delta_{g_{2}}}f(x),\quad\forall t>0,\,\,\mbox{and }x\in\omega_{2}.

Since the function
(e^{t\Delta_{g_{1}}}-e^{t\Delta_{g_{2}}})f
restricted to
(0,\infty)\times\overline{\omega_{2}}
is smooth,

\mbox{i.e.}\,\,(e^{t\Delta_{g_{1}}}-e^{t\Delta_{g_{2}}})f\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\overline{\omega_{2}}).

And it satisfies the following heat equation

(\partial_{t}-\Delta_{g})(e^{t\Delta_{g_{1}}}-e^{t\Delta_{g_{2}}})f=0\quad\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{O},

thanks to
g_{1}=g_{2}=g
in
\mathcal{O}
.

Therefore, by the unique continuation property of the heat equation (cf.
[
41
]
), we conclude that, for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\omega_{1})
,

(e^{t\Delta_{g_{1}}}-e^{t\Delta_{g_{2}}})f=0\quad\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{O}.

Since
\omega_{1}\subset\mathcal{O}
is arbitrary, it follows that,

e^{t\Delta_{g_{1}}}f=e^{t\Delta_{g_{2}}}f\quad\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{O},

for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{O})
.

This finally gives the equality of heat kernels:

\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g_{1}}}(t,x,y)=\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g_{2}}}(t,x,y),\quad\text{$\forall t>0,$ and $x,y\in O$}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we use the following result from
[
20
, Theorem 1.5]
to finish the proof of our Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 3.6

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
(N_{1},g_{1})
and
(N_{2},g_{2})
be smooth connected
complete Riemannian manifolds of dimension
n\geq 2
without boundary. Let
\mathcal{O}_{j}\subset N_{j}
,
j=1,2,
be open nonempty sets. Assume that

\mathcal{O}_{1}=\mathcal{O}_{2}:=\mathcal{O}
. Assume furthermore that

\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g_{1}}}(t,x,y)=\mathcal{K}_{\Delta_{g_{2}}}(t,x,y),\quad\forall t>0,\,\,\mbox{and }x,y\in\mathcal{O}.

Then there exists a diffeomorphism
\varphi:N_{1}\mapsto N_{2}
such that
\varphi^{\ast}g_{2}=g_{1}
on
N_{1}
.

This concludes the discussion on the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎
