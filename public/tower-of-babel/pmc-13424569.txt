Estimating temporal treatment-effect patterns of radiotherapy and chemotherapy in lower-grade gliomas using causal machine learning
Authors: Everest Yang, Sparsh Agrawal, Connor J Kinslow, Simon K Cheng, Lillian Yang, Eric Wang, Tony J Wang, Lisa A Kachnic, David J Brenner, Igor Shuryak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment decisions for lower-grade gliomas (WHO grades 2–3) rest on trial averages, which lack temporal resolution. We applied Causal Analysis of Survival Trajectories (CAST), a causal-machine-learning method that builds treatment-effect trajectories from horizon-specific estimates, to 776 adults from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, n = 512) and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, n = 264) across six radiotherapy and alkylating-chemotherapy scenarios on overall (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Elastic-net propensity scores with overlap weighting (target: average treatment effect on the overlap population, ATO) balanced age, sex, grade, IDH, 1p/19q, and extent of resection. Chemotherapy showed adjusted survival-probability gains peaking at 0.34 (95% CI -0.32 to 1.00) at 84 months (TCGA OS) and 0.48 (0.04 to 0.92) at 108 months (CGGA OS); E-values of 5.1–27.6 indicate robustness to unmeasured confounding. Radiotherapy estimates were mixed (E-values 1.1–5.1) and are reported as adjusted associations sensitive to residual confounding from missing extent-of-resection and performance-status data, not as evidence of treatment-induced effect. Introduction

Lower-grade gliomas (WHO grades 2–3) are slow-growing but biologically diverse tumors1,2. The recent advent of molecular classification has reshaped how they are defined and treated3,4. Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations, present in approximately 80% of lower-grade gliomas, are associated with better outcomes, and 1p/19q codeletion in oligodendrogliomas confers especially favorable survival5,6. The 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) framework prioritizes IDH status and recognizes that certain IDH-wildtype lower-grade gliomas with molecular features such as Telomerase Reverse Transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutation, Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) amplification, or chromosome 7/10 imbalance may behave as the more aggressive glioblastomas despite lower histological grade4,7. Standard therapy for lower-grade gliomas begins with maximal safe resection, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy depending on residual disease and risk factors8,9.

Landmark randomized controlled trials have established the efficacy of combined modality treatment in this population. RTOG 9802 demonstrated that adjuvant procarbazine, lomustine, and vincristine (PCV) after radiotherapy improves overall survival in high-risk lower-grade gliomas10. RTOG 9402 and EORTC 26,951 showed long-term benefits of PCV-based chemoradiation in anaplastic oligodendrogliomas11,12. The CATNON trial established the benefit of adjuvant temozolomide in 1p/19q non-codeleted anaplastic gliomas13. Although some retrospective data suggest that PCV combined with radiotherapy may be superior to temozolomide-based regimens14,15, no head-to-head comparisons have been published; the ongoing CODEL trial16 and recently presented ECOG-ACRIN E3F05data17 continue to refine comparative effectiveness. These studies confirm that radiotherapy and chemotherapy confer survival benefits, but their temporal dynamics - when effects emerge, peak, and potentially attenuate - remain poorly understood.

The challenge is compounded by the observational nature of most real-world glioma data, where treatment assignment depends on patient characteristics, institutional practices, and evolving standards of care. While randomized controlled trials remain the gold standard, they are limited by narrow inclusion criteria, ethical constraints on withholding effective treatments, and high costs that restrict long-term follow-up18. Observational datasets offer broader representation and longer follow-up, but require sophisticated methods to account for confounding and censoring when estimating causal treatment effects. A major problem in estimating treatment effects from observational data is confounding by indication; patients receiving more aggressive treatment often have more aggressive disease, creating systematic bias18. Standard survival analysis methods such as the Cox proportional hazards model assume constant hazard ratios, precluding assessment of temporal effect dynamics. Causal survival forests, an extension of the generalized random forests framework19, enable nonparametric estimation of heterogeneous treatment effects on survival outcomes without imposing proportional hazards assumptions20,21. These methods produce point estimates at individual time horizons but do not by themselves yield temporal trajectories with proper uncertainty quantification.

We apply the Causal Analysis of Survival Trajectories (CAST) framework22, a recently developed extension of causal survival forests. CAST was originally introduced and validated in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma22. The method synthesizes horizon-specific treatment effect estimates into smooth temporal curves by estimating the between-horizon covariance structure via bootstrapping and applying Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage23 to regularize the resulting covariance matrix. This enables generalized least squares fitting of parametric models alongside nonparametric smoothing splines with bootstrap confidence bands, providing both interpretable summary parameters (peak timing, peak magnitude, half-life) and flexible trajectory characterization. We apply CAST to estimate time-varying effects of radiotherapy and alkylating chemotherapy on overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in two independent cohorts, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA), across six treatment-outcome scenarios. By incorporating clinical and molecular covariates (age, sex, WHO grade, IDH mutation status, 1p/19q codeletion, extent of resection) with elastic net propensity score modeling and overlap weighting24, and performing subgroup analyses by age, sex, grade, and IDH status, we characterize both average and subgroup-specific temporal treatment dynamics. Treatment effect estimates are validated using three refutation tests and E-value sensitivity analysis25. This approach complements prior trial evidence by characterizing temporal patterns of estimated adjusted associations across multiple horizons, and illustrates how a covariance-aware estimator can synthesize horizon-specific causal forest output into coherent temporal trajectories.

Methods

Study populations

We used the CAST (Causal Analysis of Survival Trajectories) framework22 to estimate time-varying effects of radiotherapy and alkylating chemotherapy on survival in adults with WHO grade 2–3 diffuse gliomas. Two publicly available datasets were analyzed independently. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) included adult diffuse glioma patients with clinical, molecular, and treatment data6. For radiotherapy analyses, the full TCGA dataset (512 patients across multiple institutions) was used. For chemotherapy analyses, a 291-patient subset with detailed chemotherapy annotation was used, where chemotherapy status codes were binarized (no chemotherapy vs. any chemotherapy). The Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) included 264 adult diffuse glioma patients from two institutional datasets (CGGA-325 and CGGA-693 batches combined)26. Only OS outcomes were available in CGGA. Both cohorts included patients with WHO grade 2–3 diffuse gliomas. Separate analysis of the two cohorts avoided dataset-specific biases, ensured consistent covariate availability, and enabled replication across distinct populations The chemotherapy field in both TCGA and CGGA does not distinguish PCV from temozolomide; the chemotherapy exposure is binary (any alkylating chemotherapy vs. none). This binary specification is supported by meta-analytic evidence that PCV-based and temozolomide-based alkylating regimens produce comparable hazard ratios for progression and overall survival across WHO grades and IDH/1p/19q subgroups27. Radiotherapy dose, fractionation, and treatment technique are similarly not captured in either harmonized cohort, so the radiotherapy exposure is binary as well. These archive-level limitations are part of the unobserved confounder set discussed in the Limitations.

Causal framework, target estimand, and covariate selection

We adopt the potential-outcomes framework. For each scenario the target estimand is the average treatment effect on the overlap population (ATO): the population whose covariate distribution is the harmonic mean of the treated- and untreated-group covariate distributions24. The ATO is appropriate when, as in our setting, parts of the covariate space contain little treated or little untreated mass and the standard ATE on the unrestricted population would be heavily extrapolated. We do not target the ATE on the entire grade 2–3 lower-grade-glioma population.

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed using the dagitty framework28 to formalize assumptions about the causal structure and verify that the adjustment set satisfies the backdoor criterion. A DAG is a diagram in which each node is a variable and each directed edge represents an assumed causal influence (for example, treatment → tumor response → survival; performance status → treatment choice and performance status → survival). Evaluating the DAG identifies which variables to adjust for (true confounders), which to avoid (colliders or mediators), and which assumptions remain unverifiable from data alone. The full DAG used for our analyses, showing observed adjustment-set confounders alongside unobserved confounders (Karnofsky performance status [KPS], tumor location, MGMT-promoter methylation, radiotherapy dose and fractionation, and chemotherapy regimen), is shown as Fig. 1. The unobserved confounders violate the backdoor criterion in both cohorts; the resulting residual-confounding risk motivates the E-value sensitivity analysis and refutation testing described below.

Each unobserved confounder is assumed to influence both treatment decisions and outcomes; their presence motivates the E-value sensitivity analysis and refutation tests.

KPS is unmeasured in both TCGA and CGGA and is the dominant unmeasured confounder for radiotherapy in this disease setting: KPS strongly determines radiotherapy referral and is itself a strong prognostic factor in lower-grade gliomas9,29. Published associations of KPS < 80 with all-cause mortality in glioma cohorts are typically in the relative-risk range 2–3, with comparably strong associations with reduced radiotherapy receipt. We therefore cannot rule out, on these data alone, the possibility that the observed radiotherapy-OS associations are partially or fully driven by unmeasured KPS imbalance between treated and untreated arms.

Covariates considered for adjustment included age at diagnosis (continuous), sex (binary), WHO grade (2 or 3), IDH mutation status (mutant vs. wildtype), 1p/19q codeletion status, extent of resection (TCGA only, categorical), and the alternative treatment modality (e.g., chemotherapy status when analyzing radiotherapy effects). EOR was unavailable in CGGA and was excluded from CGGA analyses. Variables with > 25% missingness were excluded prior to imputation; in particular, chemotherapy status had 43.3% missingness in the TCGA radiotherapy analyses and was therefore excluded from those propensity and forest models. The final adjustment set used in each scenario is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Data preprocessing

A six-month landmark analysis was applied to mitigate immortal time bias30, excluding patients who died or were censored within six months and adjusting survival times accordingly. Missing values were imputed using a cascaded approach: missForest31(random forest-based imputation excluding outcome and treatment variables) was attempted first, with fallback to multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE; m = 20 imputations combined using simplified Rubin’s rules)32, and finally median/mode imputation. Data were split into training (75%) and test (25%) sets using stratified sampling to preserve the treatment-outcome distribution.

Propensity score estimation and overlap weighting

Treatment propensity scores were estimated using elastic net logistic regression33 with the mixing parameter alpha selected via cross-validation over [0.01, 0.99] and lambda chosen to minimize cross-validated deviance. Rather than trimming patients with extreme propensity scores, we employed overlap weighting24: treated patients received weight (1 - e(X)), untreated patients received weight e(X), where e(X) is the estimated propensity score. Overlap weights target the average treatment effect in the overlap population, upweighting patients in the region of clinical equipoise where treated and untreated groups are most comparable. Balance was assessed using standardized mean differences and variance ratios.

Causal survival forests

Causal survival forests from the grf package19,20 estimated conditional average treatment effects (CATEs) on two survival metrics at each of 10 time horizons (12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108, 120 months post-diagnosis): survival probability (SP) differences and restricted mean survival time (RMST) differences. Each forest used 5,000 trees with propensity scores supplied and overlap weights via the sample.weights parameter. Following the gradient-based splitting framework of generalized random forests19,20, the default causal_survival_forest splitting rule was used; the forest searches splits that maximize heterogeneity in the influence-function pseudo-outcome. The RMST treatment effect at horizon is computed by grf as the difference between integrated survival curves in the treated and untreated groups, evaluated under the overlap weights. The present analysis treats death (or, for PFS, the composite of death and progression) as a single survival event and does not implement competing-risks methodology; this is appropriate for the OS endpoint and is a simplification for the PFS endpoint. The ATO at each horizon was estimated by augmented inverse-probability weighting (AIPW) on the training set using overlap weights, and corresponding test-set predictions were produced for the held-out 25% test split. Hyperparameters were tuned automatically via the grf tune.parameters="all” option. Variable importance was extracted via the grf variable_importance() function and aggregated across horizons.

E-values were computed following VanderWeele and Ding25, applied to the test-set predicted ATOs to provide an out-of-sample assessment of robustness. For the SP estimand at horizon t, we converted the SP ATO τ_SP(t) to a risk ratio for the event via RR_SP = (1 − S₀(t) − τ_SP(t))/(1 − S₀(t)), where S₀(t) is the baseline (untreated-group) survival probability estimated from the held-out test set by Kaplan-Meier. For the RMST estimand we used RR_RMST = (t − µ₀(t) − τ_RMST(t))/(t − µ₀(t)), with µ₀(t) approximated by the test-set sample mean of min(T, t) on the test set; this is a conservative simple-mean approximation rather than a Kaplan-Meier-integrated baseline RMST and tends to overestimate, slightly attenuating the resulting risk ratio and the E-value. The E-value is then E = RR + √(RR·(RR − 1)) for RR > 1 and the analogous expression for RR < 1 (applying the formula to 1/RR).

CAST curve methodology

To synthesize horizon-specific ATO estimates into temporal trajectories, we applied the CAST framework22, which was originally developed for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and presented at three NeurIPS 2025 workshops (AI4Science, CauScien, TS4H). ATO estimates at neighboring horizons are correlated because they arise from the same patients. Peak timing is t* = −β₁/(2β₂) when β₂ < 0, and the peak effect is β₀ + β₁ t* + β₂ (t*)². Effect half-life is reported as the additional time after t* at which the parametric trajectory drops to half the peak value. When β₂ ≥ 0 the parametric quadratic has no interior maximum within the observed horizon range; in such cases (e.g., the CGGA chemotherapy and TCGA radiotherapy OS scenarios in this analysis) Supplementary Table S2 reports the parametric trajectory evaluated at the upper boundary of the observed horizons (120 months) as the “peak”, and the half-life is omitted. A nonparametric smoothing spline weighted by inverse standard errors, with 95% confidence bands from parametric bootstrap (200 replicates), captured flexible treatment trajectories without assuming a specific functional form.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup-specific CAST curves were generated for age groups (above/below median), sex, WHO grade, and IDH mutation status. For each subgroup, patients were filtered to the relevant subgroup and weighted averages of individual CATEs were computed using overlap weights. Subgroup CAST curves with 95% confidence intervals were generated to visualize treatment effect variation across subgroups and over time.

Refutation and validation tests

Three classes of refutation tests assessed the robustness of causal effect estimates. Placebo tests randomly permuted the treatment vector (20 replicates per horizon) to verify null ATOs under no treatment-outcome association. Unobserved confounder sensitivity tests introduced a synthetic confounder correlated with age at strengths gamma = 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5, modifying both treatment assignment and outcomes, then refitting the forest without the confounder and measuring ATO distortion (20 replicates per strength). Negative control tests verified null ATOs under random treatment assignment and stability of ATOs when five Gaussian noise covariates were added alongside the real treatment.

Statistics and reproducibility

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1. Key packages included grf (v2.5.0) for causal survival forests, glmnet (v4.1.10) for elastic net propensity models, survival (v3.8.3) and survminer for Kaplan-Meier analyses, missForest and mice for imputation, and dagitty and ggdag for DAG evaluation. All statistical tests were two-sided. Sample sizes were determined by the available data in each publicly accessible dataset; no a priori power calculations were performed. Results were replicated independently in TCGA and CGGA cohorts to assess robustness across populations.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The TCGA cohort comprised up to 512 patients for radiotherapy analyses and 291 patients with detailed chemotherapy annotation for chemotherapy analyses (Supplementary Table S3). The CGGA cohort comprised 264 patients analyzed for both radiotherapy and chemotherapy effects on OS. After six-month landmark analysis to mitigate immortal time bias, sample sizes ranged from 228 to 442 across the six scenarios (Table 1). The number of patients excluded by the landmark stratified by treatment arm is reported in Supplementary Table S4. All four TCGA scenarios show differential 6-month landmark exclusion across treatment arms, driven primarily by early censoring rather than early death: the radiotherapy analyses excluded a larger fraction of untreated than treated patients (18.6% vs. 4.4% in OS; 20.8% vs. 9.5% in PFS), whereas the chemotherapy analyses show the opposite pattern (19.8% vs. 6.8% in OS; 23.3% vs. 13.6% in PFS). CGGA differential exclusions were small (< 3% points). Implications for selection bias are discussed in the Limitations. Kaplan-Meier survival curves by treatment group illustrate the confounding by indication challenge: in several scenarios, treated patients have worse unadjusted survival due to selection of higher-risk patients for treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1). Elastic net propensity score distributions and overlap weight performance are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2; pre- versus post-overlap-weighting standardized mean differences for every covariate in every scenario are visualized as Love plots in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Time-varying treatment effects: chemotherapy

Chemotherapy was associated with positive adjusted survival-probability effects on OS that built progressively over time (Fig. 2a, b). In TCGA, the training-set ATO on SP rose from near zero at 12 months (ATO = −0.02) to a peak at 84 months (ATO = 0.34, 95% CI −0.32 to 1.00), before declining at later horizons (ATO = 0.16 at 120 months). Test-set predictions tracked training estimates (test ATO = 0.32 at 84 months). RMST effects increased monotonically, reaching 19.6 months at 10 years (training; test: 17.5 months). E-values peaked at 5.1 (SP at 84 months) and 2.3 (RMST at 120 months), indicating strong robustness to unmeasured confounding for the chemotherapy SP estimand. Chemotherapy effects on PFS were larger than on OS (Fig. 2c, d), with training-set SP ATOs reaching 0.45 at 48 months (test: 0.33) and remaining above 0.40 through 72 months. The late decline in the parametric SP trajectory at horizons beyond 72 months (Fig. 2c) reflects increased right-censoring and reduced effective sample size rather than a biological reversal of effect, consistent with the wide 95% CIs that persist through late horizons. RMST effects increased monotonically to 30.0 months at 10 years (training; test: 24.4 months). E-values reached 10.5 (SP at 72 months), indicating strong robustness to unmeasured confounding for the PFS endpoint. The larger PFS effects compared to OS are consistent with chemotherapy delaying disease progression as the primary mechanism underlying the OS benefit, aligning with the established efficacy of chemotherapy in lower-grade gliomas from RTOG 9802, EORTC 26,951, and CATNON10–13.

In CGGA, chemotherapy was associated with delayed positive adjusted effects on OS (Fig. 2e, f). SP ATOs were near zero at 12–36 months, then rose to 0.18 at 60 months, 0.28 at 84 months, and peaked at 0.48 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.92) at 108 months before declining to 0.44 at 120 months. Test-set predictions tracked training estimates (test ATO = 0.46 at 108 months). RMST effects were initially negative but turned positive by 60 months, reaching 16.8 months at 10 years (training; test: 15.1 months). E-values were exceptionally large at later horizons (27.6 at 108 months for SP), indicating strong robustness to unmeasured confounding for the late CGGA chemotherapy estimate. The delayed emergence of benefit in CGGA relative to TCGA (108 vs. 72–84 months for peak SP effect) may reflect differences in patient populations, molecular subtype distributions, chemotherapy regimens, or healthcare systems between Western and Chinese glioma cohorts34,35.

Time-varying treatment effects: radiotherapy

Radiotherapy estimates are framed throughout as adjusted associations sensitive to residual confounding rather than as estimates of true individual-patient causal effects (E-values 1.1–5.1). In TCGA OS, the adjusted SP association exhibited a transition from negative at early horizons to modestly positive at later horizons (Fig. 3a, b). Training SP ATOs were negative from 12 to 48 months (training ATO = −0.05 at 24 months, 0.06 at 48 months), became positive at 60 months (ATO = 0.24), and peaked at 96 months (ATO = 0.21). Test-set predictions showed a similar transition with attenuated magnitudes. RMST effects followed the same pattern, turning positive at 72 months and reaching + 10.5 months at 120 months (training; test: +6.0 months). The early negative values are most parsimoniously explained by confounding by indication driven by missing KPS and extent-of-resection data; the late positive estimates are compatible with a genuine treatment benefit on PFS that is partially masked by indication confounding for OS, but should not be read as definitive late OS benefit.

Radiotherapy adjusted SP associations on PFS were more favorable than on OS, with positive values throughout most of the follow-up period (Fig. 3c, d). SP ATOs built from 0.10 at 12 months to a peak of 0.32 at 36 months (training), before declining and reversing at 108–120 months. RMST effects were monotonically increasing through 108 months, reaching + 30.3 months at 108 months (training; test: +27.6 months). The more favorable PFS profile compared to OS is compatible with a genuine effect of radiotherapy on local progression that is partially obscured in OS analyses by residual confounding, but is not presented here as a definitive causal effect.

In CGGA, the adjusted radiotherapy SP association on OS was negative across all horizons (Fig. 3e, f). SP ATOs worsened progressively from − 0.02 at 12 months to −0.37 at 84 months and − 0.75 at 120 months (training). The persistently negative values cannot, on their face, distinguish between a true absence of OS benefit and unresolved indication confounding driven by the absence of extent-of-resection data, a known confounder of both treatment decisions and outcomes36. We do not interpret these negative estimates as evidence of treatment-induced harm.

For TCGA chemotherapy on OS (Fig. 4a, b), older patients (age at or above the median) showed larger adjusted associations than younger patients at most horizons (RMST ATO at 84 months: 14.2 vs. 8.3 months), consistent with the dominant role of age in the variable importance analysis. Male patients showed modestly larger associations than females (12.1 vs. 9.6 months at 84 months). Grade 3 patients showed larger associations than grade 2 patients (11.5 vs. 10.3 months at 84 months). IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype subgroups showed similar magnitudes (10.6 vs. 11.6 months at 84 months), suggesting that while IDH status is strongly prognostic, it is not a major driver of chemotherapy treatment-effect heterogeneity in this analysis.

For TCGA radiotherapy on OS (Fig. 4c, d), older patients again showed larger positive adjusted associations at later horizons, while all subgroups exhibited the early negative-to-late positive transition compatible with residual confounding. CGGA chemotherapy subgroup effects followed similar patterns but with the delayed temporal profile characteristic of the CGGA cohort: older patients showed larger effects at later horizons, and IDH-mutant patients showed stronger late effects (RMST ATO at 120 months: 15.9 vs. 12.4 months for IDH-wildtype). CGGA radiotherapy showed consistently negative effects across all subgroups, with older patients and IDH-wildtype patients exhibiting the most negative effects.

Feature importance and cross-cohort comparison

Variable importance analysis from the causal survival forests identified age at diagnosis as the dominant driver of treatment effect heterogeneity across all six scenarios, accounting for 46–52% of splits (Supplementary Table S5). In TCGA, extent of resection was the second most important feature (13–17%), followed by sex (13–18%) and WHO grade (6–12%). In CGGA, which lacks EOR data, grade (15–16%) and 1p/19q codeletion (13%) emerged as the second and third most important features, likely absorbing variance that EOR captures in TCGA. IDH mutation status showed zero importance across all scenarios despite its well-known prognostic significance, reflecting the distinction between prognostic and predictive biomarkers: after including 1p/19q codeletion and grade, IDH provides no additional information for partitioning treatment effect heterogeneity.

CAST curve parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Chemotherapy effects were consistently positive in both cohorts, with TCGA showing earlier benefit emergence (24–36 months) and CGGA showing delayed but comparable magnitudes. Radiotherapy effects were more negative in CGGA than in TCGA, likely reflecting stronger residual confounding due to the absence of extent of resection data.

Validation and refutation tests

Three classes of refutation tests assessed the robustness of the adjusted-association estimates (Supplementary Figs. S6-S7). Placebo (treatment permutation) tests produced ATO estimates centered around zero across all horizons and metrics, confirming that the causal forest does not generate spurious associations when the treatment-outcome relationship is destroyed. Mean absolute ATOs under permutation were consistently below 0.05 for SP and below 1.0 month for RMST across all 20 replicates per scenario. Unobserved-confounder sensitivity tests introduced synthetic confounders at three strength levels (gamma = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5). At low and moderate strengths, perturbations generally remained within standard-error bounds; at high strength, meaningful shifts were observed at longer horizons where censoring reduces effective sample size. Negative-control tests verified null ATOs under random treatment assignment and stability when five Gaussian-noise covariates were added alongside the real treatment; per-scenario negative-control panels are provided in Supplementary Fig. S8.

Discussion

The temporal trajectories reported here describe estimated adjusted associations in the overlap population of these two retrospective cohorts; they are not direct estimates of true individual-patient causal effects, and their strength of evidence is calibrated by E-value sensitivity analysis. Our analysis of 776 patients from TCGA and CGGA reveals distinct temporal patterns with implications for precision oncology. Applied here to lower-grade gliomas, CAST yields temporal trajectories of horizon-specific overlap-weighted causal-survival-forest estimates with bootstrap-based uncertainty bands. The method does not impose proportional hazards or a fixed parametric form on the effect over time.

The central finding is the durable positive adjusted chemotherapy association across both cohorts and across both OS and PFS endpoints, supported by E-values of 5.1–27.6. In TCGA, chemotherapy ATOs on survival probability peaked at 0.34 (95% CI −0.32 to 1.00) at 84 months for OS and 0.45 at 48 months for PFS, with RMST gains of 19.6 and 30.0 months at 10 years, respectively. In CGGA, chemotherapy showed delayed positive associations on OS, peaking at 0.48 (0.04 to 0.92) at 108 months with RMST gains of 16.8 months at 10 years. These estimates are directionally consistent with landmark trials (RTOG 9802, RTOG 9402/EORTC 26951, CATNON) while revealing temporal dynamics obscured by fixed-interval reporting10–13. The larger PFS than OS effects in TCGA are biologically plausible, suggesting that chemotherapy delays disease progression as the primary mechanism underlying the OS benefit. The delayed emergence of chemotherapy benefit in CGGA relative to TCGA (108 vs. 84 months for peak SP) may reflect differences in patient populations, molecular-subtype distributions, chemotherapy regimens, or healthcare systems between Western and Chinese glioma cohorts26,34,35. With appropriate caveats, the adjusted-association direction for chemotherapy reproduces across these two distinct populations.

Radiotherapy estimates are framed throughout as adjusted associations sensitive to residual confounding rather than as estimates of true individual-patient causal effects (E-values 1.1–5.1). In TCGA, the adjusted RT-OS estimate transitioned from negative at early horizons to modestly positive at later horizons, and the RT-PFS estimate was generally positive. In CGGA, the adjusted RT-OS estimate was consistently negative across all horizons. Several factors likely explain this pattern. First, confounding by indication is expected to be stronger for radiotherapy than for chemotherapy; radiotherapy is more commonly administered to patients with aggressive disease features (higher grade, less complete resection, IDH-wildtype status). Second, the absence of extent-of-resection data in CGGA amplifies residual confounding, since surgical extent influences both RT decisions and outcomes36. Third, KPS is unmeasured in either dataset and is a strong prognostic factor and major treatment selection criterion in lower-grade gliomas. The positive TCGA RT-PFS estimates, combined with the late positive RT-OS estimates, are compatible with a genuine treatment benefit on PFS that is partially masked by indication confounding for OS, consistent with the established efficacy of RT in lower-grade gliomas (particularly on PFS) from randomized trials10,37,38. The persistently negative CGGA RT-OS estimates cannot, on their face, distinguish between a true absence of OS benefit and unresolved indication confounding driven by the missing extent-of-resection variable; we therefore do not interpret these negative estimates as evidence of treatment-induced harm.

The CAST framework provides a principled approach for visualizing temporal treatment effect dynamics. Causal survival forests estimate separate treatment effects at each time horizon, but these estimates are correlated because they arise from the same patients. CAST addresses this by estimating the between-horizon covariance structure via bootstrapping and applying Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage23 for regularization. The combination of parametric and nonparametric CAST models offers complementary perspectives: the quadratic parametric model provides interpretable summary statistics (peak timing, peak magnitude, half-life) while the smoothing spline captures complex trajectory shapes without parametric assumptions. This covariance-aware approach22 accounts for patient-level dependencies that are ignored by naive time-point-by-time-point analyses.

Variable importance analysis consistently identified age at diagnosis as the dominant treatment effect heterogeneity driver (46–52% of splits), aligning with clinical knowledge that treatment response varies with age39. The prominence of extent of resection in TCGA (13–17%) is consistent with the interaction between surgical completeness and adjuvant treatment efficacy36. In CGGA, 1p/19q codeletion status emerged as a key heterogeneity driver (13%), likely compensating for the absence of EOR data. The zero importance of IDH mutation status reflects its high correlation with 1p/19q codeletion and grade rather than a lack of biological relevance, highlighting the important distinction between prognostic and predictive biomarkers.

Limitations

This retrospective analysis has several limitations. Residual confounding remains possible because important prognostic variables were unavailable in TCGA and CGGA, including performance status, MGMT promoter methylation, chemotherapy regimen (temozolomide vs. PCV), radiotherapy dose and fractionation, and tumor size; the DAG identifies KPS and extent of resection (EOR) as the principal unmeasured confounders, with the EOR gap concentrated in radiotherapy analyses where confounding by indication is strongest. An EOR-stratified sensitivity analysis within TCGA radiotherapy-OS (Supplementary Table S6 and Supplementary Fig. S9) shows that the temporal pattern of estimated associations differs across surgical-extent strata, consistent with the EOR variable-importance ranks in Supplementary Table S5. For the TCGA chemotherapy-OS scenario the cross-validated elastic-net propensity model collapsed to an intercept-only solution under small-sample (n = 181) and treatment-imbalance (137:44) conditions, so the resulting overlap weights are uniform and estimates rely on the causal-survival-forest model’s internal treatment-heterogeneity estimation rather than on explicit AIPW overlap weighting (Supplementary Table S7). The TCGA chemotherapy analyses are further restricted to a 291-patient annotated subset that excludes EOR-level-1 patients (Supplementary Table S8); the chemotherapy estimates therefore apply to a documented-surgical-extent-2–5 subpopulation and reflect receipt of any alkylating regimen rather than regimen-specific effects. CGGA lacks progression-free survival outcomes and EOR data, limiting direct quantitative cross-cohort comparison of radiotherapy estimates. Long-horizon estimates (96–120 months) have wide confidence intervals because of censoring and reduced sample sizes; subgroup-heterogeneity analyses are exploratory rather than evidence for individualized treatment selection; multiple comparisons across time horizons were not adjusted; and follow-up beyond 10 years was not available, so very late treatment effects or toxicities may not be captured.

Conclusions

Our results describe when adjusted treatment-effect estimates emerge and attenuate over a 10-year follow-up in two independent lower-grade glioma cohorts. We apply the CAST framework, an extension of causal survival forests that synthesizes horizon-specific estimates into smooth temporal trajectories while accounting for between-horizon covariances. Applied to lower-grade gliomas, CAST curves show that chemotherapy is associated with durable positive adjusted estimates across both TCGA and CGGA cohorts, with SP gains of 0.34–0.48 and RMST gains of 16.8–30.0 months, directionally consistent with landmark RCT evidence and supported by E-values of 5.1–27.6. Radiotherapy estimates are mixed (E-values 1.1–5.1) and are reported as adjusted associations sensitive to residual confounding rather than as evidence of treatment-induced harm or definitive late benefit. Future work should validate these temporal patterns in prospective cohorts with standardized molecular characterization, performance-status capture, and longer follow-up.
