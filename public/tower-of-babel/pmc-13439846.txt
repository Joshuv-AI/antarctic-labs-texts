Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on reported malaria incidence in children under five years of age in Cameroon: an interrupted time series analysis with regional heterogeneity (2017–2022)
Authors: Léa Melataguia Mekontchou, Georges Nguefack-Tsague, Alice Verdiane Koudjou Sonegueng, Paul Yvan Deffo, Emtom Sepde, Sékou Samadoulougou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a major cause of childhood morbidity and mortality in Cameroon. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted access to healthcare and the continuity of malaria control programmes; however, its impact on reported incidence among children under 5 years of age, and its regional heterogeneity, remain insufficiently studied. We aimed to estimate the impact of the COVID-19-related interruption on reported malaria incidence among children under five years of age in Cameroon, nationally and by region, using routine DHIS2 surveillance data from 2017 to 2022.MethodsWe conducted an interrupted time series (ITS) study using monthly DHIS2 data from January 2017 to December 2022. Reported malaria incidence among children under 5 years of age was modelled using negative binomial regression (NB2) with a population offset. Two interruption points were defined a priori: March 2020 and January 2022. The model controlled for monthly fixed effects, long-lasting insecticidal net (LLIN) distribution activity, seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) activity, the paediatric consultation rate, and one-month lagged rainfall. Inference relied on Newey–West HAC-robust standard errors (lag = 12). Regional ITS models, counterfactual analysis, and sensitivity analyses complemented the primary analysis.ResultsIn March 2020, reported incidence showed an immediate 36.9% decline relative to the pre-pandemic trend (D1: IRR = 0.631; 95% CI 0.539–0.738; p < 0.001), followed by a positive slope change of 3.1% per month (P1: IRR = 1.031; 95% CI 1.017–1.045; p < 0.001). The second interruption point was not associated with any significant change in level or slope. The LLIN distribution indicator was inversely associated with reported incidence (IRR = 0.978; 95% CI 0.960–0.997; p = 0.024). Counterfactual analysis estimated a deficit of 69,708 notifications over 2020–2021, followed by an excess of 74,831 in 2022. Marked regional heterogeneity was observed, with particularly severe initial declines in the Centre (IRR = 0.467) and East (IRR = 0.544) regions.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a substantial initial disruption in reported childhood malaria incidence in Cameroon, followed by a progressive recovery with marked territorial disparities. These findings support strengthening the continuity of malaria services during crises and maintaining high-quality surveillance systems to enable regionally tailored programmatic decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05958-1.

Background

Malaria remains a major global public health challenge. In 2022, approximately 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths were recorded across 85 endemic countries, a level exceeding pre-pandemic estimates and reflecting a partial reversal of the gains accumulated since the early 2000 s [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) Africa Region accounts for 94% of cases and 95% of deaths. Children under five years of age remain the most exposed to severe morbidity and mortality [2]. Despite progress driven by the scale-up of the use of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), and seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC), these gains remain vulnerable to exogenous shocks.

The Coronavirus 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic crystallised this fragility. Mobility restrictions, restructuring of healthcare pathways, pressures on diagnostic and therapeutic supplies, and the redeployment of resources towards the COVID-19 response may have altered reported incidence independently of actual biological transmission [3]. As early as April 2020, WHO warned that, without mitigation measures, severe service disruptions could lead to a near-doubling of annual malaria deaths in sub-Saharan Africa [4]. In several African countries, this configuration was associated with a contraction in notifications and consultations, consistent with under-detection that may have obscured true epidemiological dynamics [5]. These effects were not, however, uniform: WHO pulse surveys on the continuity of essential services reported substantial heterogeneity both between and within countries, reflecting differences in organisational resilience, geographical access, and adaptive capacity [3].

In Cameroon, malaria is one of the leading causes of childhood morbidity and mortality. The 2022 Malaria Indicator Survey estimated a parasite prevalence of 26% among children aged 6 to 59 months, with marked disparities between settings: approximately 16% in urban areas compared with 34% in rural areas and across regions [6]. This geography of risk is embedded within a well-established eco-epidemiological gradient: perennial transmission in the southern and coastal regions, and seasonal transmission concentrated in the northern zones [7]. This gradient, combined with inequalities in access to healthcare, renders national averages difficult to interpret in the face of a systemic shock. The Cameroonian government response (March 2020) was based on non-pharmaceutical measures likely to constrain mobility and service demand, while also imposing logistical constraints on malaria campaigns [8].

Despite the growing body of African studies on COVID-19-related disruptions, existing work often relies on aggregated estimates and insufficiently documented subnational heterogeneity, even though the effects of a systemic shock may differ considerably according to transmission profile, urbanisation, geographical accessibility, and local adaptive capacity [5, 9, 10]. Routine data collected by the National Health Information System-District Health Information Software second version (DHIS2) offer an opportunity to observe the temporal dynamics of reported incidence with monthly resolution and complete national coverage [11].

The present study assesses the association between the COVID-19 pandemic and monthly reported malaria incidence in children under five years of age in Cameroon over the period January 2017–December 2022, using DHIS2 data analysed by an interrupted time series (ITS) method. It aims to quantify, at national and regional levels, the level changes and trend changes associated with the pandemic, to identify vulnerability and resilience profiles capable of informing the management of malaria programmes during crisis situations [12].

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a quasi-experimental interrupted time series (ITS) study to quantify level and trend changes in monthly reported malaria incidence among children under five years of age associated with the COVID-19-related interruption [13]. This approach uses the pre-interruption trend to estimate the counterfactual trajectory that would have been expected in the absence of the COVID-19-related interruption and compares this expected trajectory with the observed post-interruption pattern [12].

The monthly time series covered January 2017 to December 2022, yielding 72 monthly time points before lagging. For the ITS analysis, the series was structured around two predefined interruption points and three analytical segments: the pre-interruption period (January 2017–February 2020; 38 monthly time points), the first post-interruption period corresponding to the acute COVID-19 disruption phase (March 2020–December 2021; 22 monthly time points), and the second post-interruption period corresponding to the post-acute transition phase (January–December 2022; 12 monthly time points). The interruption points themselves were March 2020 and January 2022. The acute pandemic phase (March 2020–December 2021) begins with the notification of Cameroon's first official COVID-19 case and the implementation of the first government restriction measures [8]. The post-acute transition phase (January–December 2022) corresponds to the progressive lifting of the main public health restrictions, including the reopening of borders and the resumption of normal social activities.

The study covers the entire territory of Cameroon, a country characterised by marked geographical and climatic diversity that generates a latitudinal eco-epidemiological gradient shaping malaria transmission dynamics. In the northern regions with a Sudano-Sahelian climate (Far North and North), transmission is highly seasonal and concentrated during the short rainy season [14]. In the southern and coastal regions with an equatorial climate (Centre, South, Littoral, East, South-West, and North-West), transmission is more perennial, with sustained parasite pressure throughout the year [7]. The Adamawa region, characterised by a Sudanese savanna climate, presents an intermediate seasonal transmission profile between the perennial south and the highly seasonal north. The West region, with its highland topography, also shows distinct transmission patterns. Study population

The target population comprised all children under five years of age residing in Cameroon between January 2017 and December 2022. This age group accounts for most of the malaria-related hospital morbidity and mortality in the country. In 2022, it was estimated at approximately 5 million individuals, representing 18% of the total population [15].

Data sources

Data were extracted from the DHIS2 platform, which centralises routine monthly reports from public, private, and faith-based health facilities [16]. Only series that had undergone institutional validation by the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) were used, ensuring internal consistency and reducing data entry errors. The extraction covers the ten administrative regions over 72 months, constituting a longitudinal panel of 720 monthly observations. Environmental data, namely monthly mean rainfall by region, were obtained from the NASA POWER satellite database and spatially and temporally matched to the health panel [17].

Definition of study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable was the monthly number of notified confirmed uncomplicated malaria cases among children under 5 years of age. Biological confirmation was based on either a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) or microscopy, in accordance with national guidelines [18]. The under-five population was included as a logarithmic offset term, allowing the results to be expressed as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) comparable across geographic strata.

Definition of study periods

The study period was divided into three analytical segments: a pre-interruption period from January 2017 to February 2020, a first post-interruption period from March 2020 to December 2021, and a second post-interruption period from January to December 2022. March 2020 was selected as the first interruption point, corresponding to the onset of the COVID-19 epidemic in Cameroon and the implementation of the first response measures [19]. January 2022 was selected as the second interruption point, corresponding to a phase of relaxation of the main restrictive measures and a transition in the functioning of health services. In this study, the post-acute transition period refers to this third analytical segment and does not imply the complete end of COVID-19 or of its effects on the health system.

ITS modelling variables

In accordance with the two-interruption-point ITS framework, five time variables were constructed. Variable T represents the number of months elapsed since January 2017 (T = 1 to T = 72) and captures the secular trend. The indicator variables D₁ and D₂ mark March 2020 and January 2022, respectively, and estimate immediate level changes. The slope variables P₁ and P₂ count the months elapsed after each interruption point and assess gradual trend changes during and after the pandemic phase.

Adjustment covariates

The model was adjusted for three domains of potential confounding.

Monthly rainfall (lag-1): Regional monthly rainfall was included with a one-month lag, reflecting the biological delay between rainfall, vector proliferation, and the clinical occurrence of malaria cases. At the national level, this variable corresponded to the population-weighted mean across the ten regions, using the under-five population as weights.

Reported malaria prevention activities: Two indicators derived from DHIS2 were included. (i) The monthly LLIN distribution indicator, expressed as a percentage of the population of children under 5 years of age and reported monthly by health facilities through DHIS2. (ii) The monthly seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) activity indicator, expressed as a percentage of the population of children under 5 years of age and reported monthly by health facilities through DHIS2. These indicators reflect programme activities reported through the routine information system rather than coverage estimates derived from population-based surveys. They were therefore treated as proxies for the intensity of preventive interventions as captured by routine surveillance. For the national analysis, both indicators were aggregated as weighted means across regions, using the under-five population as weights.

Monthly paediatric consultation rate: The consultation rate among children under 5 years of age (per 1,000 children) was used as a proxy for healthcare-seeking behaviour and access to health services, both of which may influence notification volume independently of underlying transmission.

Statistical analysis

Given the observed overdispersion, we used a type-2 negative binomial regression model (NB2) with a log link and the under-five population as an offset. Effects were reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs; IRR = exp(β)) with 95% confidence intervals.

Seasonality was controlled by a combined strategy: inclusion of one-month lagged rainfall and the addition of monthly fixed effects (month indicators) to capture residual annual cycles. Two specifications were compared with and without monthly fixed effects and selected based on AIC/BIC criteria and the plausibility of seasonal adjustment.

The validity of the retained model was assessed by inspection of Pearson residuals (ACF/PACF functions) and by the Ljung–Box test at lags 12 and 24; influential observations were identified by Cook's distance (threshold: 4/n). As residual serial dependence was detected (Ljung–Box, p < 0.001), standard errors were recalculated using Newey–West HAC estimators (Bartlett kernel, lag = 12), adjusting the variance–covariance matrix without modifying the point estimates. Negative binomial models with an autoregressive component were explored; the absence of convergence under the full specification led to their not being reported. The inference of the primary model therefore rests on HAC-robust standard errors [20].

The same ITS specification was replicated in each of the ten regions to obtain region-specific IRRs (D₁, P₁, D₂, P₂), synthesised by forest plots. Three sensitivity analyses were conducted: comparison of specifications with and without monthly fixed effects, a placebo interruption point test, and exclusion of influential months. The introduction of the lag-1 term for rainfall renders the first month undefined, so estimation is based on N = 71 months. All analyses were performed in R (v4.2.1; packages MASS, sandwich, lmtest, tidyverse, dplyr, readr).

Between 2017 and 2022, the mean national reported malaria incidence among children under 5 years of age was 11.00 (2.80) cases per 1,000 children. Descriptive means increased across periods, from 9.94 (2.18) before the pandemic to 11.25 (3.19) during the COVID period, and then to 13.89 (1.46) after January 2022. The consultation rate among children under 5 years of age also increased over time, from 160.8 (63.0) to 179.5 (59.4) and then 182.8 (58.6) per 1,000 children. Mean monthly rainfall remained broadly stable across periods. Finally, the aggregated prevention indicators showed a gradual increase, with higher values after 2021 for both the LLIN indicator and the SMC indicator.

The national monthly time series (Fig. 2) shows regular seasonality, with recurrent peaks toward the end of the year (September–November). Between 2017 and mid-2019, incidence fluctuated around a moderate level; late 2019 was characterised by a more marked increase. From March 2020 onward, the curve showed a clear dip during the first months, followed by a gradual rise throughout 2020 and 2021. By late 2021, incidence reached the highest levels observed over the entire series (approximately 18 cases per 1,000). In 2022, incidence remained generally high, with a narrower range of monthly fluctuations.

Table 2 presents the comparison between the unadjusted and adjusted NB2 models (with monthly fixed effects and covariates) under classical inference. Adjustment led to only limited changes in the ITS estimates. In particular, the immediate level decline in March 2020 (D1) shifted from an IRR of 0.621 in the unadjusted model to 0.631 in the adjusted model, while the D2 effect at January 2022 attenuated from 0.860 to 0.805 and the P2 slope from 0.968 to 0.984.

Table 3 presents the results of the primary model using HAC-robust inference. The adjusted negative binomial ITS model identified an immediate level change at the onset of the pandemic period. In March 2020, reported incidence was 36.9% below its expected level based on the pre-pandemic trend (D1: IRR = 0.631; 95% CI 0.539–0.738; p < 0.001). This initial decline was followed by a positive slope change corresponding to a mean relative increase of 3.1% per month beyond the pre-existing trend (P1: IRR = 1.031; 95% CI 1.017–1.045; p < 0.001).

The second interruption point, set at January 2022, was not associated with any statistically supported change in either level (D2: IRR = 0.805; 95% CI 0.607–1.067; p = 0.131) or slope (P2: IRR = 0.984; 95% CI 0.960–1.009; p = 0.203), findings consistent with a continuation of notification dynamics without a clear inflection at the start of 2022. The underlying monthly trend during the pre-COVID-19 period was weakly but significantly positive after accounting for serial dependence (T: IRR = 1.006; 95% CI 1.000–1.013; p = 0.038), indicating an average relative increase of approximately 0.6% per month before the pandemic.

Among the covariates, the LLIN distribution indicator was inversely associated with reported incidence (IRR = 0.978; 95% CI 0.960–0.997; p = 0.024), consistent with a potential protective association of reported distribution activities as captured by the routine information system. The paediatric consultation rate showed a positive association of small magnitude, at the margin of statistical significance (IRR = 1.001; 95% CI 1.000–1.003; p = 0.086), whereas SMC activity was not statistically associated with reported incidence (IRR = 0.989; 95% CI 0.955–1.024; p = 0.525). One-month lagged rainfall was not statistically significantly associated with reported malaria incidence (IRR = 1.064; 95% CI 0.991–1.143; p = 0.089), a result that may reflect the national aggregation of this indicator, which smooths the inter-regional climatic variability that is a key determinant of malaria transmission in Cameroon.

A visual summary of the ITS analytical structure and national estimates from the unadjusted, adjusted, and HAC-robust models is provided in Supplementary Figure S2 to facilitate interpretation of the interruption points and the corresponding level and slope changes.

Diagnostics and robustness of inference (Figure S1)

Diagnostics of the adjusted negative binomial model with monthly fixed effects showed persistent residual autocorrelation, with significant Ljung–Box tests at lags 12 (p = 6.37 × 10⁻⁶) and 24 (p = 8.00 × 10⁻5). Inference for the primary model therefore relied on Newey–West HAC-robust standard errors (lag = 12), while the point estimates of the IRRs remained unchanged (Table S3). The estimated effects associated with entry into the pandemic period remained robust under HAC inference, whereas the immediate effect in January 2022 was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.131 vs. p = 0.050 under classical inference). Influence analysis identified 11 influential months according to Cook’s distance, with December 2017 as the most influential observation. Full graphical diagnostics are provided in the Supplementary Material (Figure S1).

The region-specific adjusted ITS models indicated marked subnational heterogeneity in the effects associated with the pandemic. The immediate level change in March 2020 (D₁) was negative in several regions, with the lowest estimates in the Centre (IRR = 0.467), East (0.544), South-West (0.560), Littoral (0.596), and Adamawa (0.608). A more moderate decline was observed in the Far North (0.683) and North-West (0.805). By contrast, in the North, South, and West, the confidence intervals for D₁ included unity, indicating no clear immediate level change in these regions.

After March 2020, most regions showed a positive slope (P₁), with the largest relative monthly increases in the South-West (IRR = 1.068), Adamawa (1.047), East and Far North (1.040), and then the Centre (1.032). More modest increases were also observed in the South, North-West, and Littoral. In the North, a marginal but statistically significant positive slope was observed (P₁ = 1.015; p < 0.05). By contrast, in the West region, P₁ was below 1 (IRR = 0.983), indicating a declining slope after March 2020.

The second interruption point, set at January 2022, was not associated with an immediate level change in most regions, with D₂ estimates generally remaining compatible with unity. The South-West was the main exception, with an immediate decline in January 2022 (IRR = 0.653). Finally, post-January 2022 trends (P₂) were below 1 in several regions, notably in the South-West (0.935), Adamawa (0.949), Far North (0.959), East (0.962), and Centre (0.976), whereas the other regions remained broadly compatible with relative slope stability after January 2022.

Sensitivity analyses (Tables S1A and S1B)

Adding monthly fixed effects improved the fit of the adjusted national model (AIC 1443 with MOIS_F versus 1457 without MOIS_F) (Table S1A). Estimates for the first interruption remained similar across the two specifications, with an immediate decline in March 2020 (D₁ = 0.654, 95% CI 0.560–0.762 without MOIS_F; 0.642, 95% CI 0.566–0.728 with MOIS_F) and a positive post-March 2020 slope (P₁ = 1.028, 95% CI 1.016–1.039; 1.031, 95% CI 1.021–1.040, respectively). By contrast, the evidence of an immediate decline in January 2022 observed without explicit control for seasonality (D₂ = 0.760, 95% CI 0.600–0.964; p = 0.024) attenuated after the introduction of monthly fixed effects (D₂ = 0.823, 95% CI 0.664–1.020; p = 0.076). No post-January 2022 slope change was statistically supported in either specification.

The interruption point displacement analysis showed that a negative immediate change and a positive post-interruption slope were observed for all three candidate dates, but that March 2020 provided the best overall model fit (AIC 1443), ahead of January 2020 (AIC 1444) and May 2020 (AIC 1459) (Table S1B). Shifting the interruption point to May 2020 was associated with an attenuation of the immediate effect (D₁ = 0.735, 95% CI 0.637–0.847), supporting the choice of March 2020 as the primary date for the first interruption.

Discussion

Our findings, based on a 72-month national series of routine surveillance data, show an immediate and substantial disruption in reported malaria incidence among children under 5 years of age in Cameroon from March 2020 onwards, followed by a progressive recovery whose magnitude varied considerably across regions. These observations fit within a broader pattern of disruption to essential health services in sub-Saharan Africa, while providing a territorialised perspective that aggregated analyses cannot capture.

The immediate contraction in notifications in March 2020 is consistent with the disruptions in health service utilisation documented in several African countries during the first phase of the pandemic [19, 21]. In the Cameroonian context, movement restrictions and the reallocation of health resources likely affected both care-seeking and case detection capacity. The positive association, of small magnitude and at the margin of statistical significance, observed between the paediatric consultation rate and reported incidence supports this interpretation: fluctuations in health facility attendance appear to have contributed to notification dynamics, although routine data alone do not allow disentangling reduced care-seeking, reduced testing, or impaired reporting completeness [9, 12].

It is important to maintain a rigorous distinction between reported incidence and true incidence. The counterfactual analysis estimated a deficit of approximately 70,000 notifications over 2020–2021, a signal compatible with disruption of the symptom-consultation-testing-treatment-reporting continuum, each component of which may be affected in a crisis context [12]. These missing cases may correspond to patients who did not seek care and were therefore untreated, to cases that were diagnosed but not recorded, or to a combination of both. As for the progressive increase in the reported post-March 2020 slope (P1 = 1.031) and the excess notifications observed in 2022 relative to the counterfactual, several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms may account for these findings: (i) a progressive restoration of services and surveillance, in line with international evidence [3, 22]; (ii) catch-up in previously deferred demand during the initial phase; (iii) a genuine increase in transmission, related to the relaxation of non-pharmaceutical measures or to disruptions in preventive campaigns; and (iv) improved DHIS2 completeness in response to health system strengthening efforts. On the basis of routine data alone, these hypotheses cannot be ranked without triangulation with independent sources, such as coverage surveys and parasitological data.

The most specific contribution of this study lies in the demonstration of marked regional heterogeneity in the initial disruptions and subsequent trajectories. The most severe shocks were observed in several regions, particularly the Centre, East, South-West, Littoral, and Adamawa, a pattern compatible with more intense disruptions in health service utilisation in these settings, as documented in ITS analyses of routine data from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Ethiopia [9, 21]. In these areas, reduced care-seeking, changes in care pathways, and operational constraints may have jointly affected case detection, treatment, and reporting completeness [3].

By contrast, the relative stability of notifications in some northern regions at the time of the initial shock may partly reflect the role of SMC in maintaining regular contact between populations and health services, although this interpretation must remain cautious given the partial and geographically restricted nature of this indicator in the national analysis, and would need to be tested using independent coverage data [10]. The inverse association observed between the LLIN distribution indicator and reported incidence is compatible with a protective effect of the interventions, while also potentially reflecting structural differences across settings and periods that were not fully captured by the available covariates [1, 10].

The non-significant association observed for aggregated national rainfall (IRR = 1.064; p = 0.089) may be explained by the fact that a population-weighted national mean smooths substantial inter-regional climatic heterogeneity. A single indicator cannot adequately reflect the diversity of Cameroon’s rainfall regimes and may also share part of the seasonal information already captured by the monthly fixed effects. In this context, the absence of a statistically supported association in the national model remains plausible.

The particularly marked recovery observed in the South-West (+ 6.8% per month) should be interpreted with caution. In this region, the overlap between the health crisis and documented security constraints may have durably affected health service utilisation [23]. A rapid rebound in notifications may therefore reflect either a genuine catch-up in care-seeking or instability in the quality of routine data. The additional signal observed in January 2022 further suggests that resilience remained fragile in the face of repeated shocks and persistent structural constraints.

All these regional contrasts must be interpreted in light of the possible heterogeneity in DHIS2 data quality and completeness, which may have been amplified during the crisis period. Stratified ITS provides a territorialised perspective that is useful for programme steering, but it calls for triangulation with independent sources, such as coverage surveys and parasitological data, to distinguish fluctuations in notification from real changes in disease burden [3].

From a programmatic perspective, the heterogeneity of regional trajectories argues for differentiated responses rather than uniform national steering. Protecting the diagnostic and therapeutic continuum appears to be a priority in the regions most sensitive to the shock. Strengthening community-based strategies could help maintain access to interventions when health facilities are under strain, particularly in settings where security fragility overlaps with limited accessibility [24].

Strengths and limitations

This study is based on a long monthly series (72 months) and uses an ITS framework well suited to quantifying disruptions associated with a population-level shock. The use of negative binomial regression with a population offset, adjustment for seasonality through monthly fixed effects, and inclusion of relevant covariates enhance the internal plausibility of the estimates [12]. The use of HAC standard errors (Newey–West, lag = 12) explicitly addressed residual autocorrelation and improved the robustness of inference without altering the point estimates. Counterfactual quantification translated the ITS parameters into notification differentials directly relevant for programme steering. Region-stratified ITS further highlighted substantial territorial heterogeneity, providing an added contribution relative to aggregated African analyses.

The use of DHIS2 data exposes the analysis to variation in completeness, reporting delays, and diagnostic practices, all of which may have been amplified during the crisis period. Adjustment for consultation activity partially reduced care-seeking bias, but some programmatic dimensions such as RDT/ACT stock disruptions and the actual intensity of community-based activities were not available monthly and may therefore have introduced residual confounding. It should also be noted that the LLIN distribution and SMC activity indicators used here were provider-reported variables captured through DHIS2; they do not constitute estimates of population coverage and may underestimate activities not recorded in the routine information system. As with any uncontrolled ITS, this approach cannot distinguish reduced care-seeking or notification from a true change in transmission, and the absence of a formal control group limits the ability to rule out unmeasured concurrent events. In addition, the imposition of a single and uniform interruption point date across all regions does not account for possible temporal differences in the implementation and impact of restrictive measures across territories. These limitations require clear distinction between the observed statistical disruptions and the mechanistic hypotheses that may underlie them, which in turn call for triangulation with independent sources, including MIS survey data.

Conclusion

Our findings, based on routine surveillance data, show that the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a marked disruption in reported malaria incidence among children under 5 years of age in Cameroon, followed by a progressive recovery whose magnitude varied substantially across regions. The distinction between variation in notification and variation in true transmission remains an inherent limitation of routine data and requires these results to be interpreted primarily as reflecting a shock affecting care-seeking, case detection, reporting, and service functioning, without excluding a contribution from changes in actual morbidity.

The territorial heterogeneity identified constitutes a clear programmatic signal: surveillance systems and malaria services do not respond uniformly to crises. Strengthening their resilience requires both continuity of preventive and diagnostic-therapeutic interventions and the availability of high-quality data that enable a regionalised and responsive interpretation of evolving patterns. In a context where future disruptions remain likely, this local adaptive capacity represents a key lever for preserving gains in child health in Cameroon and, more broadly, in sub-Saharan Africa.
