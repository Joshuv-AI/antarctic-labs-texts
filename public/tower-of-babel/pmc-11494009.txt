In the arm-in-cage test, topical repellents activate mosquitoes to disengage upon contact instead of repelling them at distance
Authors: Mathurin Fatou, Pie Müller
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Topical repellents provide protection against mosquito bites and their efficacy is often assessed using the arm-in-cage test. The arm-in-cage test estimates the repellent’s protection time by exposing a repellent-treated forearm to host-seeking mosquitoes inside a cage at regular intervals until the first confirmed mosquito bite. However, the test does not reveal the repellents’ behavioural mode of action. To understand how mosquitoes interact with topical repellents in the arm-in-cage test, we used a 3D infrared video camera system to track individual Aedes aegypti and Anopheles stephensi females during exposure to either a repellent-treated or an untreated forearm. The repellents tested were 20% (m/m) ethanolic solutions of N, N-diethyl-meta-toluamide, p-menthane-3,8-diol, icaridin and ethyl butylacetylaminopropionate. All four repellents substantially reduced the number of bites compared to an untreated forearm, while the flight trajectories indicate that the repellents do not prevent skin contact as the mosquitoes made multiple brief contacts with the treated forearm. We conclude that, in the context of the arm-in-cage test, topical repellents activate mosquitoes to disengage from the forearm with undirected displacements upon contact rather than being repelled at distance by volatile odorants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74518-x.

Introduction

Biting mosquitoes (order Diptera; family Culicidae) are vectors of medically important infectious diseases caused by parasites and viruses, including the two most prevalent vector-borne diseases, malaria and dengue1–3. For most mosquito-borne diseases, neither specific treatments nor vaccines are available; therefore, mosquito control at the community level and personal protection are key in preventing disease transmission. In addition to community-based approaches, such as indoor residual spraying or insecticide-treated bed nets, the application of topical repellents to bare skin may provide additional personal protection from vector-borne diseases4.

Although topical repellents have been widely used for a long time5, their modes of action, and how they correlate with behaviour, are largely unknown. An exception is diethyltoluamide (DEET), the most widely used insect repellent, though even for DEET, there is still some debate about its mode of action and the mechanism is complex. DEET likely has multiple modes of action and may act at a distance, upon contact or both6,7. It has been found that DEET may activate individual or multiple receptor families located on specific sensory appendages in mosquitoes, such as the antennae, maxillary palps, labella or tarsi8–17. Other studies suggest that DEET impacts chemoreceptor activity in response to host odours, acting as a ‘confusant’13,14,18–20. Finally, it has also been hypothesised that DEET ‘masks’ host odours by altering their volatility, thus reducing the number of particles reaching and activating the mosquito odour receptors10,21. While DEET’s mode of action has been broadly discussed, the situation for other actives is far less clear. Recent neurological evidence suggests that the synthetic repellents butylacetylaminopropionate (EBAAP) and icaridin – like DEET – mask human odours, while the natural repellent lemon eucalyptus oil actually repels mosquitoes by activating their odour receptors6.

The efficacy of topical repellents, defined as complete protection time, is often assessed under laboratory conditions using the so-called arm-in-cage test22–24. In the arm-in-cage test, the repellent is applied to the bare forearm between the wrist and the elbow of a study participant. Upon application, the study participant exposes the repellent-treated forearm to host-seeking mosquitoes in a cage at regular intervals (i.e. at every 30–60 min) for a few minutes (e.g. 3 min) while the numbers of mosquito landings, bites or both are recorded. The procedure is repeated until the repellent fails to provide complete protection, usually defined by the time elapsed from the application of the repellent until the first confirmed bite. A confirmed bite is a bite followed by a second bite within the same or two consecutive exposure intervals. The complete protection time for a topical repellent is then estimated as the average across all participants in a study.

Results from arm-in-cage laboratory studies may differ considerably from field studies measuring the same endpoints of the identical repellent formulations25–27. Repellents also seem to last much longer when they are tested in a walk-in chamber28. One plausible explanation for these discrepancies are differences in basic biting rates related to species-specific behaviours and mosquito density. As a remedy, Moreno-Gómez et al.29 suggested calibrating biting rates in laboratory assays to rates reported from the field by adjusting the number of mosquitoes released into the experimental space. However, Barnard et al.30 found that the efficacy of DEET against Aedes aegypti was inversely proportional to the size of the experimental cage but barely affected by mosquito density, suggesting that mosquito biting behaviour changes with the dimension of the experimental set-up. These observations highlight that mosquito behaviour changes in relation to the environmental context, while our understanding of how mosquitoes interact with topical repellents remains limited.

While being practical and easy to implement, a limitation of the arm-in-cage test is that it does not provide any information on a repellent’s behavioural mode of action (i.e. it does not explain why a mosquito does not land or bite). Indeed, scoring the numbers of landings and bites does, for example, not distinguish between a mosquito being actually repelled by the odour of the chemical leading to oriented movements away from the stimulus, being dispersed upon contact with the chemical on the skin, or being confused or ‘blinded’ due to the repellent’s masking effect. In fact, we do not know what the arm-in-cage test is actually measuring and to what extend it mimics the real-use scenario.

Measuring and quantifying mosquito behaviour beyond simple endpoints, such as landing and biting rates, has been technically extremely challenging. Thanks to the latest developments, affordable and versatile video camera systems are now available, capable of recording and analysing the behaviour of free-flying mosquitoes31–33. This technology has expanded our understanding of mosquito biology, aiding in the improvement of vector control tools like insecticide-treated bed nets and traps34–36. To our knowledge, video tracking has not yet been used to study mosquito behaviour in the context of topical repellents. Employing video tracking in this area could provide valuable insights into how repellents affect mosquito behaviour. This, in turn, could lead to the design of more effective repellent molecules and formulations, or result in improved bioassays that mirror the natural context for host-seeking mosquitoes in a more realistic way. Such bioassays would provide stronger predictive evidence of efficacy for the real-use scenario during the early laboratory stage of product development.

The aim of the present study was to record mosquito flight trajectories of host-seeking Ae. aegypti and Anopheles stephensi females in 3D as they interacted with a repellent-treated or an untreated forearm in the arm-in-cage test in order to understand the behavioural mode of actions of topical repellents. The repellents tested were 20% (m/m) ethanolic solutions of either DEET, EBAAP, icaridin or p-menthane-3,8-diol (PMD). We identified six distinct behaviours, including ’touch and go’, ‘bouncing’, ‘landing’, ‘probing’, ‘flying’ and ‘resting’. While all four repellents reduced the number of landings and bites, the flight trajectories indicate that the repellents do not prevent contact with the skin as the mosquitoes made multiple brief contacts with the treated forearm.

Methods

Experimental animals

We used 5- to 10-day-old females of two mosquito species, Ae. aegypti and An. stephensi. Both species are recommended model organisms for testing topical repellents24. We maintained both mosquito colonies at a mean temperature of 26.9 °C (ranging between 25.8 °C and 27.7 °C), a mean relative humidity of 75.1% (ranging from 68.3% to 83.9%) and a 12 h day-night-cycle starting at 02:00 local time. The detailed rearing protocol is described in Owusu et al.37. For the experiments, we removed female mosquitoes from a stock cage where females and males were allowed to mate with access to water and a 10% sucrose solution ad libitum. Upon removal, we starved the female mosquitoes of the sucrose solution for 14 h.

Repellents

We included four active ingredients commonly present in commercially available topical repellent formulations in the study: N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide (DEET), p-menthane-3,8-diol (PMD), icaridin and ethyl butylacetylaminopropionate (EBAAP). The formulations were prepared by Labor Hartmann GmbH (Uznach, Switzerland) as 20% (m/m) ethanolic solutions. DEET was selected as it is a highly effective and widely used mosquito repellent and, hence, regarded as the ‘gold standard’38. Icaridin is an alternative synthetic active ingredient that demonstrates repellent properties comparable to DEET8,39. PMD is a naturally derived compound that also shows similar efficacy to DEET27,40,41. EBAAP is a synthetic molecule which has shown an efficacy similar to that of DEET42, except against Anopheles gambiae mosquitoes43.

Experimental set-up

We performed the experiments in a specialised, climate-controlled room at the Swiss Tropical and Public Health Institute (Swiss TPH) in Basel, Switzerland. The design and dimensions of the experimental cage were based on our standard test cage used for routine evaluation of topical repellents27. The experimental cage was built according to the recommendations of the World Heatlh Organization (WHO)24, measuring 40 cm x 40 cm x 40 cm, with slight modifications, allowing for the infrared video tracking system to follow mosquitoes inside the cage (Fig. 1). The front, bottom, side and top panels were made of acrylic glass with the top panel and one side panel being opaque white to produce a uniformly illuminated background against which the mosquitoes were tracked as a dark object. The front panel had a round opening and a sleeve made of netting fabric through which a forearm can be inserted and exposed to mosquitoes. The back panel consisted of plastic mesh to permit airflow. In addition, one panel was fitted with a cylindrical handle bar that helped stabilise the arm during the exposure and keeping it in the same position in each experiment.

In order to record the mosquitoes’ flight trajectories, we used the software Trackit3D Fly suite 2.0 (Scitracks, Bertschikon, Switzerland), installed on a desktop computer, in combination with two acA2040-90umNIR USB 3 digital cameras (Basler AG, Ahrensburg, Germany) and three custom-made near-infrared (NIR) light emitting diode (LED) lamps (Ingenieurbüro Warnke, Wietzendorf, Germany). The video cameras, with a resolution of 4 MP, acquired images at a rate of 50 frames per second (fps). We equipped the cameras with Fujinon DV3.4 × 3.8SA-SA1 lenses (Fujifilm Holdings K.K., Tokyo, Japan) and Midopt BP850 NIR band-pass filters (Midwest Optical Systems, Palatine, IL, USA) that only pass light at a wavelength above 850 nm. The NIR LED floodlights emitted light at a wavelength of 850 nm with a power of 100 W. The dimensions of the LED plate of 15 cm x 17 cm (width x height) and the 30° beam angle provided good illumination of the entire experimental arena. This combination of cameras, lenses, filters and lamps enabled us to track mosquitoes in both bright light and darkness as mosquitoes are not sensitive to light in the NIR range44.

The Trackit3D software determines the mosquitoes’ position every 20 ms based on stereo image pairs of dark objects against a bright background by triangulation45, while the positional precision is improved through a Kalman filter loop46. We calibrated the system with the software’s built-in calibration function while setting the tracking parameters for exposure time, minimum object area, gain and threshold to 4,500 µs, 20 pixel (px), 0 (no unit) and 9 (no unit), respectively. In order to prevent the video system from tracking the forearm as a mosquito, we set the threshold for the size of objects to be tracked at above 1,000 px in length and height.

Since the output of Trackit3D are position data and no video files are stored, we used Open Broadcaster Software version 22.0.2 (Philadelphia Works, Philadelphia, USA) to record videos of the computer screen showing the views of the cameras while Trackit3D was running. This allowed us to later playback the images from both cameras and validate the reconstructed flight trajectories from the position data.

Experimental procedure

The experimental procedure was an adaptation of the protocol for the arm-in-cage test described in the WHO guidelines for efficacy testing of mosquito repellents for human skin24. While the dimensions and the design of the experimental cage followed the guidelines, we introduced and tracked only one female mosquito at a time, instead of releasing 200 mosquitoes into the cage as recommended by the WHO protocol. Tracking single mosquitoes allowed to unambiguously assigning all behavioural measurements to the same individual and to exclude any potential interactions between mosquitoes.

We performed the experiments with both mosquito strains under artificial light at an intensity of 640 lx. The room had a mean temperature of 24.6 °C (ranging from 22.7 °C to 28.0 °C), a mean relative humidity of 65.5% (ranging from 38.5% to 78.3%) and a mean CO2 concentration of 613 ppm (ranging from 489 ppm to 878 ppm). Ambient temperature, relative humidity and CO2 levels were continuously monitored with calibrated Tinytag data loggers (Gemini Data Loggers Ltd, Chichester, UK).

The study included no study participants, instead all exposures were done by the first author, henceforth called the ‘experimenter’. In preparation of an experiment, the experimenter shaved his forearms to prevent the video tracking system from incidentally tracking the skin hairs as mosquitoes. The treatment of the forearms took place in the morning at 08:30 h. Before treatment, the forearms were washed with neutral soap and dried with a towel, then swabbed with 70% ethanol (v/v) and a paper tissue, and finally let air dry. We applied the ethanolic repellent solutions evenly on the bare forearm between the wrist and the elbow at an application rate of 1 ml per 600 cm2 as recommended in the WHO guidelines for testing topical repellents24. Accordingly, we adjusted the application volume by estimating the surface area of the forearm, calculated as the distance between the elbow and the wrist, multiplied by the mean of the circumferences of the elbow, the wrist and the position between the elbow and the wrist. While one forearm was left untreated, we treated the other forearm with one of the four repellents, DEET, EBAAP, icaridin or PMD. The allocation of repellent, the left or right forearm and experimental day sequence was randomised using the R function ‘sample’47,48 in R version 4.0.249.

In the experiment, the experimenter exposed either the untreated or a repellent-treated forearm into the cage at three exposure intervals post application: 0.5, 3.5 and 6.5 h (i.e. at 09:00, 12:00 and 15:00 h, respectively), with each exposure lasting 3 min. We selected these time intervals based on prior experience with the arm-in-cage test, corresponding to the points when the repellents were typically still active (0.5 h), beginning to fail (3.5 h) or mostly ineffective (6.5 h).

For each exposure, we introduced a single female mosquito into the experimental cage and immediately started the video tracking. Once the forearm was inside the cage, the experimenter exhaled twice into the cage to provide a CO2 stimulus, triggering the mosquito to initiate host seeking31. At the end of the 3-min exposure, we stopped the video tracking and removed the mosquito from the cage before repeating the procedure with the other forearm.

In addition to the automated video tracking, the experimenter recorded visually how often and where the mosquitoes landed and probed on the forearm by counting the numbers of events on the forearm’s ‘dorsal’, ‘ventral’, ‘ulnar’ and ‘radial’ side. We defined a ‘landing’ as contact with the forearm that lasted longer than 1 s, while we excluded shorter contacts from the visual analysis, since they could not be reliably recorded by eye. We defined probing as insertion of the mosquito’s mouthparts into the skin.

For each combination of mosquito strain, repellent treatment and exposure interval we tracked 10 mosquitoes, resulting in 240 trajectories. Back-to-back with each test condition and exposure interval, we ran a negative control with the untreated arm, which resulted in another 60 trajectories (i.e. 30 per mosquito strain). In addition, we recorded the trajectories of 30 Ae. aegypti and 30 An. stephensi mosquitoes released into an empty cage as an additional control.

Data analysis

The readouts of the 3D video tracking system were Cartesian x-, y- and z-coordinates that were recorded every 20 ms and were already filtered for outliers and missing observations by the Trackit3D software. We imported the data from the Trackit3D CSV files into R version 4.0.249. In R, we also captured the visual counts of landings and probings on the forearm. We performed all statistical analysis in R and generated the graphs using the ‘rgl’50 and ‘ggplot’51 packages. For all statistical analysis, we set the level of significance at α = 0.05.

We first investigated the number of mosquitoes that probed the forearm and how often they had contact with it as a function of treatment and mosquito species. Our hypothesis was that repellents would reduce the number of probings, and that repellents with strong spatial effects would decrease the frequency of contacts with the forearm. To test for differences in the proportions of probings and contacts with the forearm between treatments and mosquito species, we used the ‘glm’ function in R’s ‘stats’ package49 to fit a generalised linear model with a logit link function and a binomial error distribution. We defined a contact as any physical interaction with the forearm other than probing, including landings and any shorter interaction.

We also investigated whether mosquitoes landed and probed equally across the dorsal, ventral, ulnar and radial side of the forearm. To test for differences in the number of landings and probings across the anatomical sides, we used the χ2 test.

If a topical repellent exerts a spatial effect (e.g. being repellent at a distance), we expected that mosquitoes would move into different areas of the cage upon exposure; hence, the space would be occupied differently between treatments. To assess whether a treatment altered the mosquitoes’ use of space, we split the experimental cage into a virtual upper and lower half of equal volume and counted the number of positional data points in each half when mosquitoes were in mid-air, defined as moving at a speed above 0.015 ms-1. For the analysis, we included the trajectories recorded during the first exposure of the forearm with each repellent and the untreated forearm, and data from video tracking of mosquitoes flying in an empty cage (n = 10 for each condition and mosquito species). Based on the total counts in the upper and lower half, we assigned a ‘1’ or a ‘0’ to each mosquito depending on whether it spent more time (i.e. higher count) in the upper or lower half of the cage, respectively. To assess the effect of mosquito species and condition on the binary outcome, we used a generalised linear model with a logit link function and a binomial error distribution.

We hypothesised that repellents would also affect the mosquito flight trajectories. To quantify these trajectories – and since the data points correspond to a discrete time series with one measurement every 20 ms – we applied the finite helix fit (FHF) approach of Crenshaw et al.52, which has previously been used to analyse flight trajectories of tachinid flies53. The FHF decomposes a 3D trajectory into its translational velocity V, with speed s, its curvature κ and its torsion τ as a function of time. Generalised linear models using the ‘glm’ function allowed to compare the spatial flight patterns in terms of FHF parameters between species and treatments. While for speed we could assume a Gaussian distribution, we transformed the individual values for curvature and torsion before including them in the statistical models since they were not normally distributed. For the transformation we used the Box-Cox method implemented in the function ‘boxcox’ of the R package MASS54. We picked the λ value that maximised the log-likelihood based on the plot produced by the boxcox function and then back-transformed the data for their presentation in the main text and figures.

Based on the FHF parameters, visual inspection of plotted trajectories and viewing the actual videos recorded from the computer screen, we split the trajectories into sequences of distinct behavioural categories and quantified them in terms of their relative frequency and intensity (i.e. duration) as a function of treatment and mosquito species. To investigate whether these behaviours varied between treatments, we grouped related behaviours and plotted the course of their intensity over each 3-min exposure interval. We then calculated the area under the curves using the ‘auc’ function from the R package ‘MESS’55 and tested for statistical significance between the different conditions using the Student’s t-test.

Ethics declaration

This study did not require ethical approval as the only human participant was the experimenter himself while it follows the general ethical principles for research in humans.

Results

We recorded flight trajectories from 360 mosquitoes. In theory, each trajectory consists of 180 s of xyz positional data points at a frequency of 50 Hz, resulting in 9,000 data points per trajectory and in total 3.24 million data points. However, the video tracking system occasionaly lost track of the mosquitoes when they flew out of the cameras’ fields of view, primarily when they were obscured by the forearm or near the cage opening where the forearm was inserted. Additionally, we excluded the first 8 s of each trajectory, since this was the time lag required to initiate the tracking system, insert the forearm into the cage, and allow the system to ‘learn’ the background. Once learned, the system was able to ignore the background, such as the exposed forearm, while tracking the mosquitoes. The percentage of tracking was similar across repellent treatments, ranging from 53% to 64% in Ae. aegypti and from 63% to 73% in An. stephensi. However, tracking improved when an untreated arm was inserted with 88% and 92% for Ae. aegypti and An. stephensi, respectively. This increase occured since, for most of the mosquitoes exposed to an untreated forearm, the 3-minute exposure consisted of a more direct trajectory towards the forearm, followed by probing.

Probing and other contacts with the exposed forearm

On the untreated forearm, 28 out of the 30 mosquitoes in both species engaged in probing, while the odds of a mosquito probing were greatly reduced by the application of topical repellents. Across all exposure intervals, DEET and icaridin provided the greatest protection, both with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.02 (95% confidence interval, CI: 0.07–0.6), followed by PMD with an OR of 0.043 (95% CI: 0.016–0.112) and EBAAP with an OR of 0.061 (95% CI: 0.024–0.155). Introducing a term for species did not improve the generalised linear model as the term ‘species’ was statistically not significant.

When exposed to an untreated forearm, a single contact generally led to probing, and no further contacts occurred. In contrast, mosquitoes made multiple contacts with a treated forearm without probing. It is important to note that we treated contacts and probings as mutually exclusive events. Compared to the untreated arm, the frequency of mosquito contacts with the forearm increased by 3.1-fold (95% CI: 1.4–6.9) for DEET, 4.5-fold (95% CI: 2.1–9.7) for icaridin, 4.9-fold (95% CI: 2.3–10.4) for PMD and 4.5-fold (95% CI: 2.1–9.7) for EBAAP. As for the negative controls, introducing a term for ‘species’ did not improve the generalised linear model for the number of contacts.

We also investigated where on the forearm the mosquitoes landed and probed excluding other contacts as the experimenter could not count them reliably by eye. On the untreated forearm, both mosquito species landed more frequently on the dorsal side (Fig. 2), although the difference was statistically significant only for Ae. aegypti (χ2 = 9.39, df = 3, p = 0.024). When the forearm was treated, the number of contacts were too low to test for statistical significance, but they occurred across all sides of the forearm.

Spatial variation in flight activity

Similar to our analysis of where mosquitoes landed and probed on the forearm, we investigated whether the topical repellents affected how host-seeking mosquitoes used the space inside the experimental cage. While in flight, we measured the proportion of time mosquitoes spent in the upper and lower halves of the cage. Generally, Ae. aegypti spent more time in the upper half of the cage, whereas An. stephensi preferred the lower half of the cage (Fig. 3, OR = 0.14, 95% CI = 0.05–0.36). However, introducing an arm into the cage evoked An. stephensi to spend more time in the upper half (OR = 14.0, 95% CI = 1.54–127.2). Upon exposure to DEET, they spent more time in the lower half compared to the other treatments (OR = 0.07, 95% CI = 0.01–0.65). Otherwise, the spatial patterns across treatments were similar within each species.

Characterisation of flight trajectories

Overall, An. stephensi mosquitoes flew at an average flight speed of 0.21 m·s-1 (95% CI: 0.2–0.22 m·s-1), which was 0.02 m·s-1 (95% CI: 0–0.03 m·s-1) faster than Ae. aegypti (t = 2.662, p = 0.008), irrespective of whether the arm was treated with a repellent. Within species, average flight speeds were similar in the empty cage and when exposed to an untreated arm. However, both species reduced their speed upon exposure to a repellent-treated forearm during the first exposure interval to 0.18 m·s-1 (95% CI: 0.17–0.18 m·s-1) and 0.20 m·s-1 (95% CI 0.19–0.21 m·s-1) for Ae. aegypti and An. stephensi, respectively. This reduction was statistically significant only for Ae. aegypti (t = -2.352, p = 0.025). Intriguingly, the effect appears to be reversible in both species, since the average flight speeds returned to normal in subsequent exposure intervals the repellent effect had waned (Fig. 4).

The average curvature in An. stephensi was 331.9 rad·m-1 (95% CI: 227.3–436.5 rad·m-1), which was similar to the average curvature in Ae. aegypti (332.4 rad·m-1, 95% CI: 261.1–403.7 rad·m-1). The pattern of curvature shows an opposite trend of what is observed for speed and closely mirrors the pattern of torsion (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S1). Curvature and torsion are highly correlated (r = 0.99, p < 0.001) and inversely correlated with speed (curvature vs speed: r = -0.69, p < 0.001; torsion vs speed: r = -0.66, p < 0.001).

Frequency of individual contact behaviours with exposed forearm

As mentioned above, almost all mosquitoes landed on the untreated forearm and started probing. In contrast, all four repellents reduced the number of mosquitoes probing in both mosquito species, though the protection waned over time (Fig. 6). However, even when the forearm was treated with a topical repellent, mosquitoes still made multiple, brief contacts with the forearm by either bouncing along the surface (‘bouncing’), immediately turning away upon contact (‘touch and go’), or even alighted on the forearm (‘landing’) and stayed over 1 s before taking off again.

Duration and course of behaviours

In addition to examining the frequency of behaviours, we also investigated their duration – or ‘intensity’ – and their course across treatments within a single 3-min exposure interval. We grouped the behavioural categories into three groups: ‘active and flying’, ‘stationary but active’ and ‘resting’. The ‘active and flying’ group includes the categories ‘bouncing’, ‘flying’ and ‘touch and go’, while the ‘stationary but active’ group includes ‘landing’ and ‘probing’.

When comparing the areas under the curves, both Ae. aegypti and An. stephensi mosquitoes spent more time ‘resting’ than ‘active and flying’, irrespective of the condition (Ae. aegypti: t = 11.3, p < 0.001; An. stephensi: t = 12.1, p < 0.001). The course of the behaviours remained relatively stable across the exposure interval, except when exposed to an untreated forearm (Fig. 7). In this case, landing and probing increased, while the other behaviours diminished over the course of exposure. The time spent probing on the untreated forearm was statistically significantly longer than with any of the topical repellents tested (Ae. aegypti: t = 7.5, p < 0.001; An. stephensi: t = 6.2, p < 0.001). On average, it took mosquitoes 36.2 s (95% CI: 21.8–50.7 s) for Ae. aegypti and 39.7 s (95% CI: 29.2–50.2 s) for An. stephensi to locate and land on the untreated forearm. Most host-seeking Ae. aegypti and An. stephensi mosquitoes landed and probed within the 3-min exposure interval.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study that rigorously measured mosquito behaviour in response to topical repellents beyond recording simple endpoints (i.e. bites and landings) using a 3D video tracking system to record and quantify mosquito flight trajectories. The trajectories reveal that host-seeking Ae. aegypti and An. stephensi mosquitoes show distinct patterns. Notably, An. stephensi spent more time near the bottom of the experimental cage. In both species, we identified six distinct behaviours, including ‘touch and go’, ‘bouncing’, ‘landing’, ‘probing’, ‘flying’ and ‘resting’ when a forearm was exposed into the experimental cage. While all four repellents – DEET, EBAAP, icaridin and PMD – reduced the number of bites compared to an untreated forearm, the recorded flight trajectories revealed that the mosquitoes still made multiple contacts with the forearm, including ‘bouncing’, ‘landing’ and ‘touch and go’.

Both species slowed down when the forearm was treated, regardless of the repellent applied. The effect appeared to be reversible, since their speed gradually increased back to similar levels as in the control. Similarly, mosquitoes exposed to a treated forearm reduced their flight activity towards the end of the exposure interval. The mechanism behind this effect is unclear, but it could be a result from cumulative exposure to the repellent, either through repeated skin contact, volatile odorants from the repellent or both.

Aedes aegypti is an extremely anthropophilic species56,57, while An. stephensi is a more opportunistic feeder, with a variable preference for humans or animals depending on host species availability58. The difference in spatial activity between these two species likely reflects alternative host-seeking strategies mediated by sensory cues, including olfaction, vision, heat and moisture57,59. For example, Aedes and Culex species express odorant receptors that respond to DEET, whereas Anopheles species lack these receptors60 Previous video tracking studies have shown that Ae. aegypti and Anopheles coluzzii orient towards visual objects when exposed to CO2 and human body odours44,59. These biological differences may also affect how each mosquito species interacts with the experimental set-up. Consequently, the results such as those from the arm-in-cage test may not depend solely on the chemistry of the topical repellent but also on the context in which it is presented to the host-seeking mosquito, an aspect largely ignored.

A limitation of our camera set-up was that it did not track mosquitoes flying behind or directly in front of the exposed forearm. Mosquitoes behind the forearm were obscured from the cameras’ field of view, while those in front of the forearm where not recognised because the forearm was masked to prevent the system from tracking the forearm instead of the mosquito. Consequently, both the forearm and the mosquito appeared as a black area against the bright background. However, the experimenter manually recorded where on the forearm the mosquitoes landed and probed, revealing that they mostly landed on either the dorsal or ventral side of the forearm, where the system could track them. Nonetheless, future studies should consider extending the tracking system with more than one pair of cameras to eliminate blind spots. Another aspect for consideration is standardising the attractive host stimuli. Another area for improvement is the standardisation of host stimuli. Instead of the experimenter exhaling air into the cage, a more controlled release of CO2 and other kairomones would be desirable.

When exposed to an untreated forearm in the arm-in-cage test, mosquitoes quickly landed and immediately began probing and feeding. In contrast, when the forearm was treated with a topical repellent, the number of probings was substantially reduced. However, the mosquitoes continued to make multiple brief contacts with the forearm, either through a single touch (‘touch and go’) or by bounce along the arm’s surface (‘bouncing’) before flying away. In the medical entomology literature, the behaviour frequently referred to ‘excito-repellency’. Excito-repellency combinationes the terms ‘excitation’ and ‘repellency’ and was first mentioned in relation to exposure to sub-lethal doses of DDT61, describing an avoidance behaviour exhibited by mosquitoes upon brief exposure with a chemical. However, the term is misleading as it implies a directed movement away from the source. In this study, the mosquitoes’ flight trajectories suggest undirected displacements, making the behaviour more accurately described as ‘contact disengagement’ from the repellent-treated forearm, mediated by a contact locomotor stimulus as suggested by Miller et al.62.

Contact disengagement has also been described by Dennis et al.17 for Ae. aegypti, showing that mosquito legs are important contact sensors of DEET. Similarly, tracking of individual body parts in An. coluzzii revealed that they, too, respond to DEET upon contact with the legs63. In contrast, Afify et al.6 found that natural repellents, including lemongrass oil and eugenol, elicit a strong response of the olfactory receptor neurons in An. coluzzii, while DEET, EBAAP and icaridin reduce the amount of volatile odorants reaching these neurons, leading to a masking effect of human odour. Since mosquitoes still reached the forearm in our experiments, our results do not appear to confirm their findings at the behavioural level. One possible explanation for this is that the collective attractive stimuli, including cues other than chemical cues, such as vision, heat or moisture have outweighed the potential effects of the repellents’ volatile odorants at a distance. Most intriguingly, our data suggest that topical repellents other than DEET also mediate a contact-based stimulus, since we did not see a clear difference in the observed behaviours between DEET and the other three repellent formulations – EBAAP, icaridin and PMD.

A key constraint of the arm-in-cage test, that has received little attention, is that the set-up confines the mosquitoes to a small space close to the repellent-treated forearm; a situation being far different from the conditions a host-seeking mosquito would normally encounter in nature. In this confined context, cues such as vision, heat and humidity may become equally or even more important for host location than odorants, and these cues might mask repellent properties of volatile odorants that act over longer distances. Therefore, it would be valuable to conduct similar experiments using 3D video tracking while increasing the experimental arena, similar to the taxis boxes used to measure spatial effects of repellents64,65.

In conclusion, all four repellents substantially reduced the number of bites compared to the untreated forearm. However, the flight trajectories indicate that the repellents do not prevent contact with the skin, as mosquitoes made multiple brief contacts with the treated forearm. Therefore, at least in the context of the arm-in-cage test, an important behavioural mode of action of topical repellents is to activate mosquitoes to disengage from the repellent-treated forearm and fly away upon contact, rather than being repelled or influenced by volatile odorants at a distance.
