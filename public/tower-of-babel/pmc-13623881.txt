Probing Bethe strings in an attractive one-dimensional Bose gas
Authors: Milena Horvath, Alvise Bastianello, Sudipta Dhar, Rebekka Koch, Yanliang Guo, Jean-Sébastien Caux, Manuele Landini, Hanns-Christoph Nägerl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bethe strings are bound states of constituent particles in a variety of interacting many-body one-dimensional (1D) integrable quantum models relevant to magnetism, nanophysics, cold atoms and beyond. They are the quantum analogue to bright solitons. As emergent fundamental excitations, they are predicted to collectively reshape observable equilibrium and dynamical properties. Small individual Bethe strings have recently been observed in quantum magnets and superconducting qubits. However, creating states featuring intermixtures of many, including large, strings remains an outstanding experimental challenge. Here, using nearly integrable ultracold Bose gases, we realize such intermixtures of Bethe strings out of equilibrium, by dynamically tuning interactions from repulsive to attractive. We measure the average binding energy of the strings, revealing the presence of bound states of more than six particles. We find further evidence for them in the momentum distribution and in Tan’s contact, connected to the correlated density. Our data quantitatively agree with predictions from generalized hydrodynamics (GHD). Manipulating intermixtures of Bethe strings opens new avenues for understanding quantum coherence, nonlinear dynamics and thermalization in strongly-interacting 1D systems.

Introduction

Strong correlations present modern physics with both challenges and opportunities. In elementary cases, interactions merely renormalize bare excitations1 or generate emergent collective hydrodynamic modes2,3. A further (and far more exciting) possibility is for a non-perturbative overhaul of microscopic degrees of freedom to occur, invalidating any description of the many-body state from a non-interacting basis. The formation of bound states is a simple manifestation of this paradigm: Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) pairs illustrate how bound states give rise to superconductivity4, while quark confinement leads to the hadrons of high-energy physics5. Bosonic systems are potentially far richer than fermionic ones, since they accommodate large compounds of tightly-bound and thus strongly-correlated emergent particles. Realizing long-lived ensembles of attractive bosons, however, poses major practical challenges in analogy with the vacuum instability of attractive QED6. In bosonic systems, the absence of Pauli exclusion permits the formation of large complexes, which in turn collapse due to short-ranged molecular processes. Attractively interacting bosons have thus been observed only at weak interactions for a limited number of particles 7–9, or in one dimension for strong attractive interactions10–14 where bound states are suppressed due to energetics. The realization of a system of strongly attractive bosons displaying hierarchies of large bound states has remained elusive due to these difficulties.

In 1D integrable quantum systems15, attractive interactions do not necessarily induce collapse, but instead give rise to Bethe strings, multi-particle bound states predicted nearly a century ago16. They emerge as stable excitations in spin chains15, cold atomic gases17, and quantum magnets18,19. Their stability stems from the characteristic absence of diffractive collisions in integrable systems15, which prevents bound states from decaying into lower-lying states. Recent studies show that large Bethe strings determine anomalous transport properties of integrable spin chains and drive the emergence of the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class20–27. Beyond traditional condensed matter systems, they are also relevant in high-energy physics and string theory28. A proper understanding of this wealth of manifestations requires the ability to create and manipulate dense intermixtures of Bethe strings of different sizes.

Bethe strings are expected to exist in the cold-atom Lieb-Liniger (LL) setting29,30. In the semiclassical limit, Bethe strings converge to the bright solitons of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation 31. Soliton-trains, stable only at weak interactions, have been realized in attractive bosons near the semiclassical regime9. Soliton formation is understood in this context as modulation instability of the non-linear Schrödinger equation and its generalizations32. Here, using ultracold attractively-interacting Cs atoms in 1D, we create a dense intermixture of strings by sweeping the interaction from repulsive to attractive, passing through the non-interacting point, as proposed in Ref. 33. We operate in the quantum regime beyond the applicability regime of the non-linear Schrödinger equation, and a fast collapse of the gas is hindered by the approximate integrability29,30 of the system. Using a finite-rate sweep, we create a far-from-equilibrium state that exhibits multiple, including large, Bethe strings. Their typical size is set by the initial conditions, primarily the temperature and atomic cloud density. We detect their signature by comparing release measurements in 1D and 3D and by analyzing the momentum distribution and performing a measurement of Tan’s contact34. Our data agrees well with the predictions33,35 from generalized hydrodynamics36,37.

Experimental protocol

Our experimental sequence starts with a 3D Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) of Cs in a crossed-beam optical dipole trap, levitated against gravity by a magnetic force44. We control the atom number in the BEC within the range of 2 × 104 to 6 × 104, with an uncertainty of 10%, and the temperature between 10 nK to 35 nK, with a precision of 1 nK. A broad Feshbach resonance allows us to tune the 3D s-wave scattering length a3D via an offset magnetic field Bz. Initially, Bz is set to 20.8(1) G, at which a3D = 209(4) a0. The BEC is in the Thomas-Fermi regime. It is adiabatically loaded into two lattice beams that cross at 90∘ in the horizontal x − y plane (see Fig. 1b). This results in an array of isolated 1D tubes with trapping frequencies of ω⊥/2π = 10.5(1) kHz in the transversal and ωz/2π = 29.3(1) Hz in the longitudinal direction for a lattice depth of 25 Er, where, Er = π2ℏ2/(2md2) is the recoil energy and d = 532.2 nm is the lattice spacing set by the wavelength of the lattice light. This gives longitudinal and transversal harmonic oscillator lengths of aho = 1.6(2) μm and a⊥ = 83(4) nm, respectively. We tune the average number of atoms per tube N between 7 and 30 by varying the density of the initial BEC, resulting in 3000 to 5000 populated tubes.

In our 1D setting, the strength of the interaction is controlled via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{c}\simeq 4{a}_{{{{\rm{3D}}}}}/{a}_{\perp }^{2}$$\end{document}c~≃4a3D/a⊥245. After lattice loading, we set the interaction strength to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{c}=3.4(1)$$\end{document}c~=3.4(1) μm−1, putting our systems in the moderately interacting TG regime. The 1D temperature T1D is set to values between 3 nK and 35 nK with an uncertainty of 20% (see “Methods”). In this range of parameters, our system is in the quantum regime46. There, the system cannot be described within the Gross-Pitaevskii approximation47, and GHD appears as the only viable theoretical approach. We ramp the interaction parameter to lower and to negative values, crossing the non-interacting point, with a nearly constant rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial \tilde{c}/\partial t\approx -0.038$$\end{document}∂c~/∂t≈−0.038 μm−1ms−1. We expect that this procedure maps our system onto a distribution of Bethe strings as the interactions become attractive33. For our experiment, for an interaction strength of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{c}=-1$$\end{document}c~=−1 μm−1, a string of size n = 4 has a bond length of about 0.67 μm. The binding energy of this string then is h × 190 H ≈ kB × 9 nK. Note that Bethe strings are only well defined when their bond length Δzn is much larger than a⊥. For short distances, loss through short-range molecular processes is expected. Such a loss is not captured by the LL model. The absence of transverse mode excitations in time-of-flight (TOF) measurements confirms that the system remains deeply in the 1D regime (see “Methods”).

We employ the rapidity measurement protocol13,48–51, previously used in the absence of bound states, to probe our system. This protocol consists of a two-step expansion sequence: first, an expansion within the tubes for a time t1D, followed by a free expansion into 3D space for time t3D (see Fig. 1c). We ensure that t1D is large enough so that the system enters a dilute regime, where the interparticle interactions become negligible (see “Methods”). In the repulsive regime, a subsequent absorption image yields the rapidity distribution of the gas. However, in the attractive regime, the connection between the rapidity distribution and the absorption image becomes more intricate, as we explain below. Experimentally, the expansion in the tubes is achieved by simultaneously switching off the dipole traps and removing the residual longitudinal confinement created by the lattice beams by applying a 808-nm blue-detuned anti-trapping beam that flattens out the potential52. For the second expansion step, the expansion into 3D, we simply shut off the transverse confinement and simultaneously set a3D ≃ 0 to avoid any interaction-induced broadening of the density distribution of the expanding cloud.

Discussion

In summary, we have used 1D attractive Bose gases to realize novel non-equilibrium states of quantum matter featuring dense intermixtures of Bethe strings. In our experiment, approximate integrability stabilizes these bound states and bypasses the rapid collapse normally associated with attractive bosons7–9. We have experimentally characterized these bound states through measurements of the binding energy, Tan’s contact, and the momentum distribution following 1D expansion. Our results agree with predictions from GHD, showcasing the latter’s validity beyond the previously-considered repulsive46,49,50,59–61 and sTG regimes13. Our results show that the presence of Bethe strings (somewhat similar to Efimov physics62) in correlated gases greatly enriches the landscape of achievable non-equilibrium states in the vicinity of integrability. Since Bethe strings display remarkable resilience to integrability breaking63, they can alter transport properties64, give rise to new interesting pre-thermal phases65, and affect the emergent hydrodynamics and thermalization of nearly-integrable systems60,66–68. Going further, it would be interesting to study their participation in the emergence of KPZ universality69. The fate of these Bethe strings in the dimensional crossover70 and the connection to and the onset of Efimov physics71 are also interesting avenues for future research.

Atom number estimation in tubes

Since the formation of Bethe strings in our protocol is a non-linear function of the density, having a good quantitative estimation of the distribution of atoms across the tubes is of key importance. Before the creation of the 1D tubes, we assume the gas is at thermal equilibrium, and it remains so until the dimensional crossover. When the transverse trapping is strong enough, the tubes cannot exchange particles any longer and are effectively decoupled. Just before the tubes decouple, we approximate the gas as a collection of 1D systems at thermal equilibrium, characterized by a unique temperature and a local chemical potential according to the shallow three-dimensional trap72. We also approximate the 1D interactions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{c}$$\end{document}c~ to be the same as the one at the end of the cross-over in one dimension. Below, we summarize in a table the relevant parameters for the tubes’ loading.

Under these assumptions, we can obtain the distribution Nx,y of the atomic sample across the array of tubes at position (x, y) using thermodynamic Bethe ansatz (see Supplementary Information), while keeping Tcross-over as a tunable parameter. Notice that during the dimensional crossover, the trapping frequency along the z− direction smoothly changes from the loading frequency w1 to the final 1D value. The thermodynamics of the 1D tubes at the dimensional crossover is computed using the loading frequency as the frequency of the 1D trap. An example of how different choices of Tcross-over affect the tubes’ population Nx,y is provided in Fig. 5. In this figure, the number of tubes corresponds to the tubes where Nx,y > 1. A working code solving the thermodynamics of the 1D tubes is provided on Zenodo73. From the dimensional crossover to the true 1D regime, the transverse trap is further increased, but the number of atoms in each tube remains constant. As the system enters deeper into the 1D regime, it approaches the integrable limit, hindering thermalization. We assume that at the end of the lattice loading protocol, each tube is still well-approximated by a thermal ensemble with a new temperature T1D, which is uniform across all the tubes. This approximation is currently the best modeling we can provide, but one could expect fluctuations in the 1D temperature across the tubes. Even more fundamentally, the approximation of considering a thermal Gibbs ensemble for the initial 1D tubes may not be exact, since in 1D, thermalization is suppressed. However, a quantitative modeling of these effects is beyond our current theoretical understanding; we assume the initial tubes are at thermal equilibrium with a uniform temperature T1D. The validity of this assumption is a posteriori supported by the good agreement with experimental data, which is particularly accurate at low temperatures with increasing corrections on hotter ensembles, see also Fig. 5. The parameters T1D and Tcross-over are estimated by matching the experimental data in the weakly repulsive regime (see Supplementary Information). Determining the release energies via the second moment

We check that we remain in 1D throughout the interaction ramp protocol by fitting the transverse direction of the momentum distribution with a Gaussian function. Within experimental error, we observe a constant transverse width, indicating no transverse modes are populated throughout our protocol. A representative dataset for t1D = 8 ms and t3D = 24.5 ms is shown in Fig. 6. Atom loss and lifetime measurements

In our experiment, short-range molecular processes lead to atom loss. In Fig. 8a, we show the atom losses during the interaction ramp corresponding to the measurements given in Fig. 3b. In the repulsive regime \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{c} > 0$$\end{document}c~>0, the atom number remains stable; we see enhanced losses for interactions below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{c}=0$$\end{document}c~=0. We attribute this atom loss primarily to the formation of bound states, which enhance inelastic scattering processes not taken into account by the 1D Hamiltonian Eq. (1). Since we use Cs atoms in the lowest hyperfine ground state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert F,{m}_{F}\right\rangle=\left\vert 3,3\right\rangle$$\end{document}F,mF=3,3, two-body inelastic processes are suppressed. Therefore, these losses in our experiment primarily originate from the three-body inelastic scattering. The stability of the gas depends both on the interaction strength and on the initial conditions of the protocol. The stronger the attraction, the tighter the bound states, with more frequent inelastic scattering. For a fixed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{c}$$\end{document}c~, the atom loss decreases at higher temperatures, as the probability of forming larger strings decreases at higher temperatures. While for T1D = 20 and 35 nK, the losses are less than 20%, for the coldest T1D = = 15 nK, we observe losses around 40% for the strongest attractive interaction. Even with such high losses, the agreement between experimental data for the momentum distribution after 1D expansion (normalized with atom number) and GHD results, as shown in Fig. 3a, remains very good. Next, we measure the lifetime of the gas for different interaction strengths. Here, the density of the gas increases, resulting in higher three-body losses. We fit the data with a phenomenological model for three-body losses, enhanced by the increased density due to string formation. Assuming the strings are dilute, losses are dominated by the process involving three particles belonging to the same string. To simplify the calculation, we will assume that, if a loss event occurs inside a string, the whole string is lost. This overestimates the losses, since it does not consider the possibility that long strings would break into shorter ones after the loss event.
