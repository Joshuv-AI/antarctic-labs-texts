Restored wetlands: interplay between environmental variables, aboveground and belowground plant traits, and carbon cycling
Authors: Marta Baumane, Johan Emil Kjær, Filippa Fredriksson, Hans Henrik Bruun, Kaj Sand‐Jensen, Søren Jessen, Chiara Raineri, Josefine Kock Jørgensen, Lars Baastrup‐Spohr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Wetlands are crucial for biodiversity and climate change mitigation. Vast wetland areas have been lost through land‐use intensification. Thus, restoration efforts have gained attention globally.We assessed relationships between environmental conditions, aboveground and belowground plant traits and carbon cycling in restored wetlands in Denmark, both grazed and ungrazed, compared to a baseline in near‐natural wetlands.Wetland plant species strategy varied from acquisitive to conservative. More conservative species tended to have larger root diameters and higher percentages of root aerenchyma. However, at the community level, we found no evidence for differences in the suite of traits expressed in restored and near‐natural wetlands.Environmental and carbon cycling variables were not consistently different between restoration treatments. Average summer carbon fluxes were highly variable, but net ecosystem exchange was negative, indicating sequestration of CO2. Groundwater level was the main driver of CO2 exchange in summer, with relatively dry sites tending to fix more carbon. Relationships between plant traits and carbon cycling were weak. Our results suggest that, for carbon cycling, plant community trait composition is less important than the abiotic environment, in particular, the water table depth. This shows that ecosystem functions can be restored, even in cases where plant biodiversity has not recovered. Keywords: agricultural landscape, carbon cycling, ecological filters, plant functional traits, wetland restoration

Introduction

Globally, vast wetland areas have been severely degraded or lost through land‐use change, that is, when wetlands have been converted into either agricultural lands or urban areas (IPCC, 2022). Wetland loss has been pronounced in Europe, where only one quarter of the wetland area is in a pristine or near‐natural state, compared to three centuries ago (Fluet‐Chouinard et al., 2023). Apart from being vital for biodiversity, wetlands also provide a wide range of ecosystem services (Bobbink et al., 2006) and are particularly crucial for climate change mitigation (Mitsch et al., 2013). By contrast, wetlands that have been converted to arable land are sources of carbon emissions to the atmosphere due to greatly accelerated decomposition (Kayranli et al., 2010; Moor et al., 2017; Laine et al., 2019). However, by restoring degraded wetlands, the emissions may be reversed (Temmink et al., 2022). In the past 25 yr, more than 150 Danish wetland areas have been restored under the Action Plans for the Aquatic Environment (APAE; Baumane et al., 2025a). The main aim of these efforts was to reduce nitrogen and phosphorus losses from agriculture to streams and downstream aquatic ecosystems, while increased biodiversity was stated as a subaim. Recently, the main focus for wetland restoration has shifted to carbon sequestration and storage, with nitrogen removal and increased biodiversity as subaims, but the restoration methods used that follow the guidelines from The Ministry of Environment of Denmark (2024) are largely the same. The APAE wetlands, that is, the wetlands restored under the APAE, have now existed for two to three decades and can therefore provide important knowledge on the long‐term effects of restoration on both biodiversity, carbon cycling and their interactions. Better understanding of these effects should lead to novel and important recommendations for current and future actions.

Assembly of local communities, in both natural and restored areas, is a process where species from the local species pool are sorted into the communities by ecological filters, that is, only species with suitable traits are allowed to establish and persist, thus determining community membership (Keddy & Laughlin, 2021). Typically, APAE wetlands are located in agriculture‐dominated landscapes, where the pool of potentially colonising species has been reduced to the point of depletion affecting the propagule rain into restored sites (Baattrup‐Pedersen et al., 2013; Baumane et al., 2021). Thus, restored wetlands often only harbour regionally common wetland species (Riis et al., 2014; Baumane et al., 2021, 2025a). In wetlands, the main ecological filters that impact plant traits are hydrology, edaphic conditions and disturbance regime (Nygaard & Ejrnæs, 2009; Pan et al., 2020a). Grazing by large herbivores is a major part of the disturbance regime and an important ecological process in riparian wetlands and typically has a positive effect on plant species richness (Fløjgaard et al., 2022; Baumane et al., 2025a). Overall, grazing counteracts dominance by acquisitive, clonal plant species and creates bare soil for regeneration and increases light availability at the surface (Grime, 1973). Following restoration, in the absence of grazing, tall forbs and graminoids often gain strong dominance in riparian wetlands (Baumane et al., 2021, 2025a; Kreyling et al., 2021). When managed, Danish APAE wetlands are rarely grazed, but rather mown either annually or irregularly in drier years, typically without removing the cut thatch, to receive per‐hectare agricultural subsidies.

Plant functional traits can be categorised into response traits, associated with the responses to environmental conditions (e.g. stress and disturbance), and/or effect traits, which determine the effects of plants on ecosystem function (e.g. carbon cycling; Lavorel & Garnier, 2002), although classification can be challenging. However, traits related to gas exchange, water and nutrient uptake will likely be both (de Bello et al., 2021). Most aboveground plant traits vary along two main axes: whole‐plant size and leaf economics (leaf construction costs vs growth potential; Díaz et al., 2016). The leaf economics spectrum ranges from acquisitive to conservative and is also known as the fast‐slow spectrum (Grime, 1979; Wright et al., 2004; Reich, 2014), where acquisitive species are expected to have comparatively high specific leaf area (SLA), leaf nitrogen (Leaf‐N) and phosphorus (Leaf‐P) content and lower leaf dry matter content (LDMC). The fine root economics spectrum is multi‐dimensional, with some traits aligning with the leaf economics spectrum, whereas others do not (Kramer‐Walter et al., 2016). Moreover, the choice of sampling method strongly affects the root trait measurements (Freschet & Roumet, 2017). Nevertheless, there is evidence that some traits, such as root diameter (Root‐D), are correlated to root lifespan (McCormack et al., 2012; Chen & Brassard, 2013). Furthermore, wetland plants must find a way to provide oxygen to roots that grow in an anoxic environment. One of the main adaptive traits to anoxia is aerenchyma (Root‐Aer), that is, root cortex gas spaces that allow oxygen diffusion from shoots to roots (Baastrup‐Spohr et al., 2015). Additionally, Root‐Aer formation also reduces the root respiration (Yamauchi et al., 2021).

Even though the recognition of trait‐based ecology has increased in recent years, most research is done on terrestrial ecosystems, which differ from wetlands in terms of abiotic conditions (e.g. water saturation, soil and water chemistry), plant adaptations (e.g. aerenchyma) and ecosystem processes (denitrification, carbon cycling; Sosnová et al., 2010; Moor et al., 2017). Carbon cycling in particular is expected to be determined by plant traits and soil conditions (Moor et al., 2017). ‘Fast’ traits are linked to faster primary production and a rapid litter decomposition, whereas ‘slow’ traits are related to slower ecosystem process rates (Cornwell et al., 2008; Reich, 2014). Furthermore, high nutrient availability, which is expected in restored wetlands on former agricultural soils, tends to promote ‘fast’ traits, whereas plants with ‘slow’ traits are out‐competed (Reich, 2014). Thus, in restored sites, plants with ‘fast’ traits, which would tend to enhance gross primary productivity (GPP), would be expected to be more common than in near‐natural wetlands. High nutrient availability is also related to accelerated decomposition caused by more degradable litter and more favourable conditions for the microbial decomposers (Fennessy et al., 2008; Moor et al., 2017). The generally oxygen‐depleted conditions in saturated wetland soils, typically associated natural wetlands, further limit the decomposition rate and thereby enhance accumulation of organic material (Mitsch et al., 2013; Moor et al., 2017). In restored wetlands, natural hydrology is often not fully restored, that is, restored sites tend to have lower mean groundwater levels and a higher annual amplitude compared to near‐natural wetlands (Kreyling et al., 2021; Kjær et al., 2025). Therefore, plants in restored sites might be nutrient‐demanding species less adapted to anoxic environments, which in interaction with environmental conditions potentially causes faster ecosystem respiration rates (ER).

To determine how aboveground and belowground traits in interaction with environmental conditions affect carbon cycling in restored wetlands, we set the following overall objectives: (1) to assess ecological filters and their effect on plant communities in restored riparian wetlands; and (2) to quantify how aboveground and belowground traits affect carbon cycling and to compare these findings with a baseline in near‐natural wetlands. We expected that the main drivers of carbon cycling were groundwater and nutrient levels in interplay with plant functional traits. Specifically, we hypothesised that: (1) wetter conditions lead to a higher Root‐Aer; and (2) sites with higher nutrient availability should be dominated by species with acquisitive traits (high SLA, Leaf‐N, Leaf‐P, species vegetative height, low LDMC and Root‐D). Further, we hypothesised that (3) in wetland vegetation dominated by species with a more acquisitive nutrient‐use strategy, carbon cycling and litter decomposition should be faster than in wetlands with a high abundance of species with a conservative strategy.

Materials and Methods

Study design

To study environmental variables, plant functional traits and carbon cycling in restored and near‐natural wetlands, we conducted an investigation along three river valleys in medium‐sized agriculture‐dominated catchments: Omme (OM), Odense (OD) and Tryggevælde (TR). We analysed three riparian wetland types: restored grazed (RG), restored ungrazed (RU) and near‐natural grazed (NG). In the TR river valley, the wetland type RU was omitted due to unfavourable sampling conditions and missing carbon flux measurements because the chamber‐based method did not capture the primary production of shrubs and trees that were dominant in these plots (Supporting Information Table S1). For the same reason, that is, missing carbon flux measurements, we also excluded all near‐natural ungrazed sites from all three river valleys. This led to a combined total of eight sites (Fig. 1a–d, Table S1). We refer to individual sites by their letter combinations, for example, OMRG for the restored grazed site in the Omme river valley. In this paper, the term near‐natural is used for sites which had vegetation characteristic of natural wetlands, but which could not be classified as completely pristine. For example, because of shallow drainage ditches that slightly altered site hydrology (TRNG) or nearby agricultural fields (OMNG) and urban areas (ODNG). At each site, six replicate plots were placed to cover local variations in soil moisture and vegetation structure (48 plots in total, Ø 60.8 cm, area 0.29 m2). River valleys were selected based on dominant catchment geology (Table S2).

Environmental variables

To evaluate hydrological conditions, we installed shallow groundwater wells next to each plot. Each well consisted of 1 m long polyethylene 59.2/50 mm OD/ID tubes (KIWA HDPE, Rotek A/S, Sdr. Felding, Denmark) with a 30 cm screen placed at 0.5–0.8 m belowground level. The wells were installed with a 01.12.SA hand bailer auger set (Eijkelkamp, Giesbeek, the Netherlands) with casing (Ø 10 cm). The screens were gravel packed (0.9–1.6 mm quartz sand) and sealed from the top of the screen to the surface with a mix of cement‐bentonite (w/w 83/17%, BC‐2, Rotek A/S, Sdr. Felding, Denmark) and water (0.86 l kg−1 dry cement‐bentonite mix). The groundwater level (GW‐L, cm below terrain) in the wells was measured once a month from June to September 2022 with a water level meter (Solinst 101 P2, Georgetown, Canada) from the top of the well and corrected for the distance to terrain. GW‐L data were missing from three wells (OMRG, ODNG and TRRG, one in each site; Table S1).

For groundwater chemistry analyses, samples were collected and analysed twice: in January–February and April–May 2023 (Methods S1). Nitrate (NO3 −) and ammonium (NH4 +) were combined to inorganic nitrogen (IN), and average pore water concentrations of phosphate (PO4 3−), total iron (TFe) and IN (mg l−1) were calculated for each well. Some of the installed shallow wells were either dry or flooded during the sampling, and water chemistry data were entirely missing from one well at OMRG, three wells at ODNG, and TRRG (Table S1).

In June 2022 and July 2023, a sediment core was collected next to each plot (except one at OMNG; Table S1) with a hand‐operated borer set with round plastic liners (Ø 5 cm, length 30 cm). This soil profile was used because peat soils in Denmark are defined as areas where the 30‐cm topsoil organic carbon concentration is > 6% (Beucher et al., 2023). The sediment cores were then divided into three depth sections (0–10, 10–20, 20–30 cm) and a mixed subsample from each depth section was dried at 105°C for c. 24 h. Soil organic matter content (SOM, %) was thereafter determined as the percentage mass loss from the dried sample after 4 h at 550°C (Santisteban et al., 2004). SOM values were calculated as an average of the three sections of each core.

Plant traits

Aboveground traits

We measured SLA (mm2 mg−1), leaf dry matter content (LDMC, mg g−1), Leaf‐N (mg g−1) and leaf phosphorus (Leaf‐P, mg g−1) concentrations of the 42 most abundant vascular plant species with the aim to cover at least 80% of the total relative abundance in each plot (Pakeman & Quested, 2007). For plant abundance calculations, we used pin‐point data, collected in June and July 2021 (41 plots) and 2022 (7 plots), by placing a circular pin‐point frame (Ø 60.8 cm, area 0.29 m2) with 21 points over the plot, inserting a stick (Ø 2 mm) vertically at each point (i.e. the point‐intercept method), and recording intercepts per species. Additional vascular plant species that were present, but not touched, were recorded and assigned a standard low cover value of 0.01.

In August and September 2022, we collected three individuals (i.e. replicates) of each species from the same plot (exceptions shown in Table S3). For each species, the specific plot was selected based on the highest average soil moisture, measured at four points within each plot (N, S, E, W directions, SM150‐kit, Delta‐T Devices Ltd, Cambridge, UK) in July 2022. This was done to explore wetland plant species specialization to assess ecological filters. Leaves were then placed in plastic bags with moist paper towels, transported in a cooling box to the laboratory and kept at 5°C. Fresh leaf scanning and weighing were done within 24 h of collection. For larger‐leaved species (e.g. Carex acuta L.), a replicate consisted of a single leaf, while for smaller‐leaved species (e.g. Galium palustre L.), batches of up to 10 leaves per plant were collected. We used ImageJ to assess leaf area (including petiole). The samples were then dried at 70°C for 48 h, and the dry mass measured (scale precision 0.0001 g). Dried leaves were cut in smaller pieces and ground with mortar and pestle for Leaf‐N and Leaf‐P measurements (Methods S2). Then we calculated the mean of all available replicates per species. In cases where we were not able to collect the species in the field, our own data were supplemented with median values from the TRY plant trait database for species where ≥ 3 measurements were available (Kattge et al., 2020; Table S3). To indicate if communities were dominated by shorter or taller plant species, we did not measure plant height for each species in the field due to differences in grazing pressure at the sites where plants were collected. Instead, we used median plant vegetative height from the TRY plant trait database for 41 species, that is, potential species height (Kattge et al., 2020; Table S3). As Carex cespitosa L. only had one measurement available from TRY, we used the maximum value from Schou (2018).

Belowground traits

We measured root diameter (Root‐D, mm) and proportion of root aerenchyma (Root‐Aer, %) of 37 species selected by the same criteria as for aboveground traits (Table S3). Plants were collected in August and September 2022, at their closest occurrence to the plots, and across the soil moisture gradient based on measurements done in July 2022. Plants were dug up with surrounding soil and taken to the laboratory. Within 24 h, roots were carefully washed out, preserved in 3% glutaraldehyde, and refrigerated before further analysis. When possible, we collected three representative fine roots (diameter < 2 mm) from different individuals from each plot. Cross‐sections were prepared using a microtome (Leica VT1200S; Leica Biosystems Nussloch GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) and analysed by light microscopy (BX60; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Root‐D and the proportion of Root‐Aer were quantified with ImageJ.

Carbon cycling

CO2 flux

We measured the CO2 exchange with a portable transparent chamber in each of the 48 plots approximately once a month during the growing season from June to September 2022 (outer Ø 63 cm, height 80 cm), representing the period of peak annual carbon exchange. The frame of the chamber was made of metal, walls of fluorinated ethylene propylene foil and the lid of acrylic sheet (Fig. 1e). To ensure an airtight seal for the chamber, we installed metal collars (outer Ø 63 cm, heights 5 and 10 cm) in the summer and autumn 2021. In a few cases, where the installation process was difficult due to variable microtopography (e.g. tussocks), we sealed exposed edges of the collar with sand that was subsequently overgrown by the plant community. The order of the measured sites was changed each month to cover different times of the day. The measurements were done during daytime (sunny weather conditions, no or light wind) with a K30 CO2 sensor (Senseair, Delsbo, Sweden), which recorded the CO2 concentration every second for at least 180 s for each measurement, starting when the chamber was entirely placed on the collar. First, we measured net ecosystem exchange (NEE) in the light, then the chamber was ventilated for at least 60 s and covered with an opaque polyester fabric to measure ER. To ensure that the air in the chamber was well mixed, a continuously running ventilator was installed.

We used the R package FluxBandit (Martinsen, 2024) to aid in visualisation and evaluate a representative part of the measurements. We discarded the first 10–20 s of each measurement to avoid artefacts from the chamber placement. To ensure good data quality, we removed fluxes if R 2 was below 0.7 if the flux was above |0.01| μmol m−2 s−1. Fluxes close to 0 μmol m−2 s−1 always had a low R 2 due to the higher noise‐to‐signal ratio and were therefore better assessed through visual inspection for irregularities in the data. To calculate GPP, we subtracted ER (positive sign) from NEE (negative sign) and used a positive sign for GPP. We discarded 44 out of 599 raw CO2 fluxes because of highly fluctuating CO2 concentrations based on a visual inspection or low R 2. Although GPP by definition was positive, we kept fluxes with slightly negative values due to the measurement uncertainty of the sensor but removed 39 measurements where GPP was < −0.3 μmol m−2 s−1 (based on detection limits).

Vegetation height

Vegetation height was measured in June and July 2021 (41 plots) and 2022 (7 plots) using the visual obstruction method (Jackson & Paine, 2006) following the Danish National Monitoring and Assessment Program for the Aquatic and Terrestrial Environments NOVANA standard (Aarhus University, 2022).

Litter decomposition

We collected litter centred at four points, placed at 1 m in the N, S, E, W directions from the edge of each of the 48 collars in September and October 2022 with the aim of collecting litter representing each plot but evading disturbance inside plots. The collected litter could therefore differ slightly in species composition, cover and chemical quality compared to the vegetation within the collar. If the designated spot was trampled, the distance from the collar edge was increased up to 2 m. At each point, we placed a 20 × 20 or 40 × 40 cm2 frame, depending on the amount of available litter. Vegetation inside each frame was cut to c. 5 cm long pieces. Fresh leaves, woody stems and soil particles were discarded. Litter samples were processed as fast as possible and were kept at 5°C until processing. The four subsamples from each plot were mixed, dried at 70°C for 48 h, and a 3 g sample of litter was placed in nylon mesh bags (10 × 10 cm, mesh size 0.25 mm, Sintab AB, Oxie, Sweden). In November 2022, the litter bags were replaced at the plots at 8 cm soil depth and 1.2 m in the N direction from the edge of each collar. After 3 months, the bags were retrieved, rinsed with demineralised water and dried at 70°C for 48 h. The litter was carefully separated from the bag and weighed to calculate litter mass loss (LM, %). Five litter bags were lost and not retrieved due to winter flooding (2 bags in OMNG, 1 in ODRU, 2 in ODNG; Table S1).

Statistical analyses

To calculate aboveground and belowground trait community‐weighted means per plot (48 plots in total), we combined each species' relative abundance, based on the pin‐point recordings, with trait values. In calculations of community‐weighted means, we only used species with recorded trait values, which in most cases composed > 80% of the total relative abundance. In four to seven plots, depending on traits (one plot at OMRU and TRRG, three at OMNG and TRNG), the 80% level could not be reached as communities contained a larger fraction of species where traits were not measured due to logistical constraints. However, these plots were kept in the analyses. To compare plant traits, environmental‐ and carbon‐cycling variables across the three treatments (RG, RU and NG), we fitted linear mixed effect models, tested the overall significance of treatment with analysis of variance (ANOVA) and performed estimated marginal means (EMMs) pairwise comparisons if there was an overall treatment effect. For further analyses, the dataset was reduced to 36 plots due to missing data on hydrology and lost litter bags (Table S1). First, we applied PCA to summarise the environmental, community‐weighted mean plant trait data and carbon‐cycling variables as well as trait data at the measured species level (36 in total).

To model the hypothesised direct and indirect influences of environmental variables on trait variables and subsequently on carbon‐cycling variables (Table S4; Figs S1, S2), we fitted piecewise structural equation models (pSEM), based on linear mixed effect models, where sites were included as a random effect. The direct effect could come from an environmental variable itself, for example, GW‐L on GPP, whereas the indirect effect could be caused by, for example, an effect of GW‐L on SLA that then affected GPP rate. Model selection was performed by comparing conditional Akaike information criterion values. We validated each submodel by inspecting residuals. The global models were validated by using Fisher's C and P‐value; a nonsignificant P‐value indicated that the model reproduced the data well. Before modelling, we inspected intercorrelation for the explanatory variables by using Pearson correlation and removed variables with strong correlations (r ≥ ±0.6), while we retained the variables we assessed most likely to have an ecological effect. To meet the normality assumptions, we performed the following transformations: log‐transformed SOM, Root‐Aer and potential plant vegetative height, added 0.01 and log‐transformed IN, and PO4 3−. All explanatory variables were then standardised by subtracting the mean and dividing by the SD. LDMC was added to the initial models but did not have a significant effect in any of the final models and was therefore removed from pSEMs.

Data analysis was done using R v.4.2.1, packages emmeans v.1.10.0. (Lenth et al., 2024), lme4 v.1.1.33 (Bates et al., 2015), mumin v.1.48.11 (Bartoń, 2023), piecewisesem v.2.3.0 (Lefcheck, 2016) and vegan v.2.6.4 (Oksanen et al., 2022).

Results

Environmental variables

Average GW‐L in the summer ranged from −61.0 ± 19.4 cm (mean ± 95% confidence interval) in TRRG to 1.1 ± 15.1 cm in OMNG (Fig. 2a). Average IN was below 7 mg l−1 in most wetlands, except in OMNG (19.1 ± 23.8 mg l−1) and TRNG (17.1 ± 36.3 mg l−1). In these sites, nitrate comprised most of the IN (Fig. 2b). Average PO4 3− and TFe values were higher in restored sites along the Omme and Odense river valleys, but not in the Tryggevælde river valley, compared to near‐natural sites (Fig. 2c, d). The highest average SOM was measured in TRNG (65.1 ± 3.8%), ODRG (34.2 ± 17.1%) and ODRU (21.5 ± 4.4%), while the remaining sites had average SOM of c. 10% (Fig. 2e). Treatment did not have a significant effect on any of the environmental variables (Table S5).

A PCA of the environmental conditions showed some clustering of plots by site, but no clear clustering of treatment (e.g. for near‐natural grazed plots). PC1 had high loadings of PO4 3−and TFe, PC2 of IN and SOM, whereas PC3 had a high loading of GW‐L. The three axes accounted for 39%, 24% and 20% of the total variance (Fig. S3; Table S6).

Plant traits

PCA for aboveground and belowground traits

In a species‐based PCA of plant traits, the PC1 represented the fast‐slow plant economics spectrum with high loadings of SLA, Leaf‐N, Leaf‐P and LDMC and accounted for 43% of the total variance. Furthermore, species with high LDMC tended to have a higher Root‐Aer and Root‐D (e.g. Carex and Juncus spp.). The PC2 accounted for 16% of the total variance and it was mainly related to LDMC, Root‐Aer, Root‐D, whereas the PC3 represented potential plant vegetative height and accounted for 13% of the total variance (Fig. 3, Table S7).

Community‐weighted plant traits

We found no significant difference between the community‐weighted aboveground traits across the three treatments (Table S5). In most cases in restored wetlands in Omme and Odense river valleys, community‐weighted SLA, Leaf‐N and Leaf‐P were higher and LDMC lower than in near‐natural grazed wetlands. However, in TRRG and TRNG plots, SLA, Leaf‐N and Leaf‐P values were similar, whereas LDMC was higher in the restored grazed site (Fig. 4a–d). Community‐weighted potential plant height was lowest in the restored grazed sites in Omme and Odense river valleys, while this pattern was reversed in Tryggevælde (Fig. 4e). Regarding belowground traits, Root‐D was not significantly different across the treatments (pseudo‐R 2 = 0.34/0.19, Table S5, Fig. 4f). While there was a significant effect of treatment on Root‐Aer (pseudo‐R 2 = 0.48/0.40, Table S5, Fig. 4g) and the contrast model showed significant difference between RG and NG sites (EMMs, P = 0.02), RU and NG sites were only marginally different (EMMs, P = 0.08; Table S8).

Testing if individual treatments had different trait combinations showed some clustering. PC1 (SLA, Leaf‐N, Leaf‐P, Root‐D) and PC2 (LDMC, Root‐Aer, potential species height) accounted for 59% and 14% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. S4, Table S9).

Carbon cycling

Average summer GPP, ER and NEE did not significantly differ across the three treatments (Table S5). GPP ranged from 5.87 ± 1.45 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 in OMRU to 13.20 ± 1.93 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 in ODNG (Fig. 5a), whereas ER was highly variable across the sites and ranged from 2.48 ± 1.08 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 in OMRG to 5.84 ± 1.35 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 in ODRG (Fig. 5b). NEE (range from −8.91 ± 1.10 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 in ODNG to −1.56 ± 3.78 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 in ODRG) was negative and thus indicated more carbon sequestration in the near‐natural sites in the Omme and Odense river valleys, while the pattern was opposite in Tryggevælde river valley (Fig. 5c). Additionally, we did not find a significant difference in average vegetation height (range from 0.18 ± 0.12 m in ODRG to 0.87 ± 0.35 m in ODRU) across the three treatments (pseudo‐R 2 = 0.60/0.26, P = 0.18; Table S5; Fig. 5d). Furthermore, no difference was shown in winter litter decomposition (LM, range from 11.3 ± 10.6% in ODNG to 38.1 ± 10.8% in ODRG) among the three treatments (pseudo‐R 2 = 0.42/0.17, P = 0.24; Table S5; Fig. 5e).

Considering all carbon cycling variables together, the PCA showed some clustering. PC1 (NEE, GPP, ER and LM), PC2 (GPP, vegetation height, LM) and PC3 (vegetation height, ER) explained 42%, 32% and 17% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. S5, Table S10).

Relationship between environmental factors, traits and carbon cycling

None of the environmental variables, included in the pSEMs, had a significant effect on community‐weighted aboveground or below‐ground traits, except for the positive effect of GW‐L and the negative effect of IN on potential species height (Fig. 6). GW‐L also had a significant negative effect on summer GPP (i.e. higher water level decreased primary production) along with negative effects of SLA and vegetation height, while potential species height had an indirect effect on GPP through vegetation height. Additionally, GW‐L had a significant negative effect on LM and a significant positive effect on SOM. SOM in turn had a significant positive effect on summer ER, suggesting a further indirect effect of GW‐L. As the SOM model had a low marginal R 2 (0.14), and since SOM is produced on centennial time scales, the indirect effect of GW‐L through SOM should be interpreted with caution. GW‐L also had a significant positive effect on NEE (i.e. higher water level reduced the uptake of CO2) along with effects on SLA, vegetation height and LM (Fig. 7). Global goodness of fit for the global models was good (ER, GPP Fisher's C = 62.9, P = 0.31, NEE Fisher's C = 15.0, P = 0.67).

Discussion

Globally, wetland plants tend to represent the more acquisitive part of the fast‐slow leaf economics spectrum and tend to show weaker trait relationships than fully terrestrial plants (Moor et al., 2017; Pan et al., 2020b). The main drivers of the spectrum are nutrients, light and water availability (Reich, 2014), and wetlands often represent extremes of the gradients, for example, flooded conditions, saturated soil and nutrient scarcity or the exact opposite – increased nutrient availability (Moor et al., 2017).

In the studied wetlands, we found species that represent both acquisitive nutrient‐use strategies (e.g. Agrostis stolonifera L. and Alopecurus geniculatus L.) and the more conservative part of the spectrum (e.g. several Carex and Juncus species, location along axis 1 in Fig. 3). Furthermore, plant potential vegetative height was one of the three major dimensions in the analysed trait‐space, which aligns with previous studies (Díaz et al., 2016). We also found that root diameter and percentage of root aerenchyma tended to correlate positively with leaf dry matter content, which suggests that species with conservative traits tend to develop aerenchyma to adapt to anoxia. Previous research shows that obligate wetland plants tend to develop greater root aerenchyma volume (Jackson & Armstrong, 1999; Baastrup‐Spohr et al., 2015). Furthermore, plants with higher LDMC also tend to be taller, which might increase the probability of survival during flooding in the growing season (Garssen et al., 2015).

At the community level, we found a significant difference between restored grazed and near‐natural grazed sites, and a marginal difference between restored ungrazed and near‐natural grazed sites in terms of root aerenchyma. Comparison of species in restored wetlands, both grazed and ungrazed, represented the more acquisitive side of the fast‐slow leaf economics spectrum in two of the analysed river valleys (Odense and Omme), and generally were less adapted to waterlogged conditions than in near‐natural sites. The limited trait differences in the Tryggevælde river valley could have been influenced by the low trait coverage in the NG sites (three of six plots were < 80%, depending on the trait). The group of species with missing trait values was primarily more conservative species (e.g. Carex panicea). The tendency towards more acquisitive species in restored areas potentially reflects colonization of restored sites by generalist species common in the agricultural dominated landscape. Although most plant traits did not differ across the treatments, a previous study showed that taxonomic composition was significantly different (Baumane et al., 2025a).

Apart from aerenchyma formation, wetland plants may have a range of different responses to soil inundation, including clonal growth and increased shoot height (Moor et al., 2017). Most of the observed species had stolons and rhizomes either aboveground or belowground (based on data from CLO‐PLA; Klimešová & Klimeš, 2019), which agrees with findings in Sosnová et al. (2010). Both community‐weighted potential plant height and measured vegetation height tended to be higher in restored ungrazed wetlands, thus indicating a filtering effect of grazing. When comparing the grazed sites, restored sites had lower vegetation than near‐natural wetlands, except in the Tryggevælde river valley.

The original aim of re‐establishment of these wetlands was to reduce nutrient loading to waterways from surrounding agricultural lands, and restored sites could therefore be expected to be more nutrient‐rich than their near‐natural counterparts (Hoffmann & Baattrup‐Pedersen, 2007). However, we did not find a significant effect of treatment on any of the studied abiotic factors, though, soil water phosphate and total iron tended to be highest in restored wetlands, while the average soil water IN values were similar across almost all sites. Previous research shows that legacy of the land‐use practice before restoration and continuous high nutrient loads from surrounding arable land after restoration, hamper successful wetland habitat restoration (Baumane et al., 2021; Kreyling et al., 2021). Additionally, summer groundwater level tended to be lower in restored sites in the Omme and Tryggevælde river valleys, than in the near‐natural wetlands, which might be due to incompletely restored hydrological conditions. Furthermore, the studied restored sites showed long‐term changes in both soil properties and hydrology, for example, phosphorus accumulation in the topsoil, and increased bulk density and annual water level amplitude, compared to near‐natural sites (Kjær et al., 2025; Fredriksson et al., 2026, under peer review). This is also corroborated by previous reporting showing that restored and near‐natural wetlands differ significantly in terms of hydrology, that is, near‐natural systems have a relatively stable water level, whereas there is a much higher variance in restored wetlands (Kreyling et al., 2021).

Plant traits tend to have a strong response to environmental filters, such as soil fertility and climate on large spatial scales (Wright et al., 2005; Ordoñez et al., 2009). Similar responses can be found at the local scale, for example, in steep gradients in areas with the same management and history (Baastrup‐Spohr et al., 2015). However, the response of various aboveground and belowground traits on a community level in Dutch ecosystems, revealed rather weak relationships between wetland plant traits and water stressors, while disturbance and nutrient availability had a much stronger effect (Douma et al., 2012). We did not find significant support for hypotheses i and ii. This might be because we analysed a rather narrow environmental gradient, that is, there were small differences between restored and near‐natural wetlands regarding nutrient and hydrological conditions compared to the overall difference found in the regional context (Wright & Sutton‐Grier, 2012). Although the environmental filtering appeared to have been weak, the dispersal filter seems to play a stronger role for community assembly in the restored wetlands as these typically have low plant diversity compared to near‐natural sites (Baumane et al., 2025a). The dispersal limitation is likely caused by surrounding landscapes being dominated by intense agriculture and depauperate in natural areas needed to supply the propagules for restored sites (Baumane et al., 2025a).

Carbon fluxes are difficult to measure due to their complex and dynamic nature (Bansal et al., 2023). It is not always obvious whether or not a restored wetland continues being a carbon source, or if it starts storing more carbon than it emits. A number of factors can influence the carbon balance in restored wetlands, for example, wetland restoration age, location, land management, environmental conditions and climate (Kayranli et al., 2010; Bansal et al., 2023). We found no significant difference in winter litter mass loss or summer respiration between restored and near‐natural sites. Instead, litter mass loss seemed to be driven primarily by groundwater level and not directly explained by the functional composition of the plant communities, although litter traits (e.g. nutrient and lignin content) have been shown to be key drivers of decomposition (Moor et al., 2017; Stoler & Relyea, 2020). The strong effect of groundwater level could be overriding litter trait effects while slight differences between the functional composition of litter and vegetation, caused by litter being collected outside each plot, could also play a role. Like litter mass loss, summer carbon fluxes were not affected by restoration treatment. Among traits, SLA had a positive effect on net ecosystem exchange, whereas GPP was negatively affected by SLA, contradicting hypothesis iii that in wetland vegetation dominated by species with a more acquisitive nutrient‐use strategy, carbon cycling should be faster. However, carbon fluxes appeared to be structured by local hydrology rather than by plant functional traits. Gross primary productivity exceeded ecosystem respiration at all sites resulting in a negative net ecosystem exchange during summer, that is, both restored and near‐natural wetlands provided atmospheric CO2 sequestration (Mitsch et al., 2013). The key environmental factor for the net ecosystem exchange was the groundwater level: drier systems tended to incorporate more carbon in summer months due to higher GPP (Peng et al., 2024). Further, grazed sites with low vegetation height tended to have higher primary production and net CO2 uptake during summer corroborating with findings from northern grass‐ and heathlands (Klopsch et al., 2026). Annual CO2 flux measurements from the same sites showed that both groundwater level and soil temperature significantly affected both ecosystem respiration and GPP, that is higher groundwater level decreased both ecosystem respiration and GPP, while soil temperature had the opposite effect (Kjær et al., 2025). Most of the plant traits studied did not have a significant effect on carbon cycling in the wetlands, which might be due to the narrow variation range. Hence, despite clear differences in the plant taxonomic composition (Baumane et al., 2025a), the plant community trait composition and ecosystem functioning in terms of carbon cycling did not differ consistently between restored and near‐natural wetlands. Thus, for carbon cycling, plant community trait composition appears less important than environmental variables, which suggests that ecosystem functions may be restored, even in cases where biodiversity and natural hydrology is not.
