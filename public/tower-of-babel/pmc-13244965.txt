From scat to seedling: seasonal variation in seed deposition and emergence by chacma baboons (Papio ursinus) in a semi-arid South African savanna
Authors: Mpoko Belly Malatji, Sellina Ennie Nkosi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seed dispersal by primates is a key ecological process, yet research remains comparatively limited in semi-arid savannas. This study investigates the seasonal variation in scat-derived seed deposition and emergence patterns of chacma baboons (Papio ursinus) and their potential role as seed dispersers in Suikerbosrand Nature Reserve, South Africa. Across wet and dry seasons, 240 scats (120 per season) were collected over a 12-month period (May 2012-April 2013). A total of 23,132 seeds, representing 22 plant seed taxa, were recovered from faecal samples. Seed count per scat and taxon representation were higher in the dry season, indicating seasonal differences in ingested resources. Fruits dominated the diet in both seasons, while leaves, grasses, pods and invertebrates contributed more in the wet season. Greenhouse trials recorded seedling emergence from seven taxa in the dry season and five in the wet season. Emergence was dominated by herbaceous forbs, particularly Oxalis corniculata and Chamaesyce prostrata. These results indicate potential for emergence rather than direct germination success or gut-passage effects. By linking seed deposition and emergence, this study provides a process-based assessment of baboon-mediated seed dispersal. Results are interpreted as patterns of seed deposition rather than resource selection. These findings highlight baboons as ecologically relevant seed dispersers and support their role in savanna regeneration processes.

Introduction

Chacma baboons (Papio ursinus) are widely distributed and ecologically important primates in African savannas, partly due to their behavioural flexibility and omnivorous diet [1, 2]. They consume a wide range of food resources, including fruits, seeds, grasses, invertebrates, and occasionally small mammals. Their diets shift seasonally in response to the availability of food resources [3–6]. This adaptability enables them to meet their nutritional needs while influencing ecological processes, such as seed dispersal. Seed dispersal by baboons has been documented across southern Africa [2, 7–10]. Seeds are often swallowed whole and later excreted in dung, frequently in locations that favour germination, and several studies have demonstrated that primate-mediated seed dispersal can enhance plant recruitment through effective seed deposition and improved germination conditions [9, 11, 12]. Passage through the digestive system can enhance seed germination by removing inhibitory coatings or through physical scarification [7, 9, 13], with gut passage times in chacma baboons typically ranging from approximately 24 to 48 h, allowing seeds to be transported over considerable distances while undergoing digestive processing that may influence germination outcomes [9, 10, 14]. In this way, baboons may act as agents of vegetation regeneration, especially in open savanna systems where recruitment sites are patchy [11, 12, 15, 16].

They also influence vegetation through their foraging behaviour. Chacma baboons dig for tubers, strip bark and break branches, causing structural changes in plant communities [2, 17, 18]. Although these activities are sometimes perceived as destructive, they can create microsites that facilitate germination and increase spatial heterogeneity in vegetation structure [6, 19]. In addition, the deposition of faecal matter enriches soils with organic nutrients, enhancing nutrient cycling processes and potentially supporting plant growth and establishment [1]. Collectively, these foraging-related disturbances and nutrient inputs highlight the role of baboons as ecological modifiers that can influence vegetation dynamics beyond seed dispersal alone.

Climate variability plays a critical role in shaping resource availability in savanna ecosystems by influencing plant phenology, particularly the timing of fruiting and leaf production, as well as overall productivity. Variations in rainfall and temperature regimes can therefore alter the timing, abundance and spatial distribution of key food resources, driving seasonal shifts in primate foraging behaviour [12, 20–22]. Similar climate-driven shifts in activity patterns and resource use have been documented across chacma baboon populations in southern Africa, reinforcing their responsiveness to environmental variability [6]. In chacma baboons, such environmental fluctuations are closely linked to dietary flexibility, with individuals adjusting their feeding strategies in response to changing resource conditions [3, 20, 23]. These seasonal dietary shifts not only support troop survival and nutritional balance but also have important ecological implications, particularly by influencing the timing, composition and spatial patterns of seed deposition across the landscape [11, 12]. As a result, the effectiveness of baboon-mediated seed dispersal and subsequent plant recruitment may vary across seasons and environmental contexts [13, 24].

Seed dispersal is one ecological process through which baboons can influence vegetation dynamics and regeneration pathways by redistributing potentially viable seeds across the landscape, thereby shaping patterns of seed recruitment and plant community reassembly [9, 13]. Comparable seed-dispersal-recruitment linkages have been demonstrated in other primate systems globally, where frugivory directly influences seedling establishment and community structure [25]. Notably, not all ingested seeds remain viable following gut passage [11, 24]. In chacma baboons, seed dispersal occurs primarily through the ingestion of fleshy fruits, with seeds later defecated either intact or partially digested, depending on seed characteristics and gut processing [1, 9, 17]. Studies [9, 24] have shown that baboons can transport seeds across varying distances before deposition, often in dung that provides a nutrient-rich microsite, although the viability and germination success of these seeds remain highly species-dependent. Furthermore, the effectiveness of baboon-mediated seed dispersal is influenced by post-dispersal processes, including seed predation, environmental conditions and seasonal variability in resource availability, which collectively determine recruitment outcomes in savanna ecosystems [1, 21]. In chacma baboons, scat-derived seed records have been widely used to assess the composition of dispersed taxa and to infer their role in seed dispersal processes within open ecosystems [7, 9]. Recent work has also demonstrated that baboons can disperse both native and invasive plant species, further emphasising their role in shaping vegetation dynamics across landscapes [2]. These approaches provide important insights into the types of seeds dispersed, although germination outcomes remain species-dependent and influenced by seed traits and post-dispersal conditions [24].

Despite growing interest in primate-mediated seed dispersal beyond tropical forest systems, evidence from semi-arid and temperate savanna mosaics remains comparatively limited, particularly regarding post-dispersal germination outcomes. This gap is especially important in savanna and grassland ecosystems, where vegetation dynamics are highly sensitive to seed availability, recruitment processes and disturbance interactions, making them particularly responsive to animal-mediated dispersal mechanisms [15, 26]. In such systems, seed dispersal processes that influence the timing, composition and spatial distribution of propagules may have disproportionate effects on post-disturbance regeneration. Existing studies on chacma baboons [3, 7, 9, 24, 27] have frequently emphasised diet composition and seasonal foraging ecology, but fewer have explicitly linked scat-derived seed loads with greenhouse or controlled germination assessments to evaluate the emergence potential of dispersed seeds. This represents a critical gap in understanding the functional role of baboons in shaping vegetation recovery pathways. Moreover, there is limited clarity on whether baboon-mediated seed dispersal primarily contributes to herbaceous recruitment, which underpins savanna resilience, or to woody plant establishment, which may be constrained by dormancy mechanisms not easily overcome by gut passage [28, 29]. Addressing this gap is therefore essential for improving ecological understanding of animal-plant interactions in savanna systems and for informing conservation and restoration strategies in disturbance-driven, multi-use landscapes. Although this study is conducted within a grassland biome, increasing woody encroachment and structural heterogeneity have resulted in savanna-like characteristics, making this system particularly suitable for examining seed dispersal and recruitment dynamics across biome transitions [2, 30, 31].

This study examined the seasonal variation in chacma baboon scat contents and evaluated their potential role in seed dispersal. Specifically, the study addressed the following questions: (i) how does diet composition vary between wet and dry seasons, (ii) how does this variation influence the quantity and taxonomic composition of seeds dispersed, and (iii) to what extent do seeds recovered from baboon scats retain germination potential under controlled conditions? We hypothesised that seasonal shifts in resource availability would drive changes in diet composition, leading to differences in seed load, composition and germination success between seasons. Key drivers influencing these processes likely include seasonal variation in resource availability, which shapes plant phenology and fruit production; seed traits (e.g., size, dormancy mechanisms); and gut passage effects that may enhance or inhibit germination.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Suikerbosrand Nature Reserve (SBRNR), a protected area approximately 50 kilometres southeast of Johannesburg in Gauteng Province, South Africa, at 26°28’58.33” S and 28°14’05.49” E (Fig. 1). The reserve spans approximately 18,000 hectares and is situated within the Grassland biome, characterised by mesic grassland systems interspersed with woodland patches and mountainous terrain, reflecting heterogenous landscape influenced by fire and grazing dynamics. Elevation ranges from 1,550 to 1,917 m above sea level, creating habitat variation across altitudinal gradients. The climate is temperate with clearly defined wet (October – March) and dry (April – September) seasons. Average rainfall ranges between 650 and 700 mm per annum, significantly influencing vegetation phenology and the availability of food resources for wildlife [20, 22, 32]. The reserve experiences warm summers with temperatures ranging from 15 °C to 28 °C, and cold winters with temperatures dropping to − 2 °C in some areas [32]. Frost is common during winter, affecting plant growth cycles and favouring frost-tolerant species. The reserve is bordered by agricultural fields and expanding residential developments. Chacma baboons within the reserve are regularly observed navigating between core habitats and edge zones near human-modified areas.

The dominant vegetation type is Sourveld, specifically the Carletonville Dolomite Grassland, classified under the Mesic Highveld Grassland Group [32]. This grassland is characterised by species such as Themeda triandra (red grass) and Eragrostis curvula (weeping lovegrass), with scattered shrubs such as Searsia leptodictya (mountain karee) and geophytes such as Hypoxis hemerocallidea (African potato). Soils are derived from dolomitic parent material, supporting flora adapted to fire and grazing regimes typical of mesic grassland systems [15, 32]. Ecologically, these vegetation communities are significant for biodiversity and also contribute to key ecosystem services such as soil stabilisation and water regulation. The reserve forms part of the Vaal River Catchment and contributes to the headwaters of the Suikerbosrand River.

Study species and sampling overview

As previously noted, chacma baboons exhibit considerable dietary flexibility and contribute importantly to seed dispersal processes within savanna ecosystems [4, 33]. A single baboon troop was selected for this study based on accessibility and a stable home range within the study area. The troop comprised 38 individuals at the start of the study (7 adult males, 12 adult females, 5 subadult males, 8 subadult females, 6 juveniles) and increased to 46 individuals by the end due to births (infants) [34]. To minimise the impact of observers on natural behaviour, the troop underwent habituation (over a period of 5 months) prior to data collection. This involved gradual, repeated exposure to researchers at fixed distances, following accepted protocols for primate field studies [35, 36]. This process ensured that natural foraging and movement behaviours were maintained throughout the study period.

Data collection took place over a 12-month period from May 2012 to April 2013, encompassing both wet and dry seasons, to capture potential seasonal variation in food availability and associated differences in scat-derived diet remains and seed deposition [20, 22, 36, 37]. The study adopted a mixed-methods approach combining faecal sample collection and analysis with greenhouse germination (seedling emergence) trials. Scat-based approaches are widely used in primate ecological studies as a non-invasive method of assessing diet composition and for quantifying the seed component of primate diets [27].

Seasonal comparisons in this study focused on differences in scat-derived dietary remains and deposited seed loads, as well as on the emergence outcomes of seeds defecated during the wet and dry seasons. This design allows for direct comparison of seasonal variation in diet composition, seed deposition and germination potential using a consistent sampling framework. By combining scat analysis with controlled germination trials, the approach enables assessment of both the composition of dispersed seeds and their potential to contribute to recruitment, thereby providing a functional evaluation of seed dispersal processes within the study system.

Data collection

Scat collection

Observations began early in the morning (~ 06:00), when the troop left their sleeping sites, and continued until they returned in the evening (~ 18:00). The study troop was followed on foot for two full observation days per month, with sampling dates distributed across each month to provide balanced temporal coverage and minimise potential sampling bias. A single chacma baboon troop was followed on foot throughout the study period, allowing for the location and sampling of fresh scat deposits [38]. The same troop was consistently monitored throughout the study period and across seasons, ensuring comparability in dietary composition, seed dispersal patterns, and germination outcomes. On each sampling day, ten (10) fresh scats were collected shortly after defecation, resulting in 20 scats per month and a total of 240 scats collected over the study period (dry season: n = 120; wet season: n = 120). This sampling design ensured consistent temporal coverage across seasons, and the cumulative sample size is comparable to those used in similar scat-based primate dietary and seed dispersal studies [7, 9, 24], providing sufficient representation to assess seasonal variation in diet composition, seed loads and germination outcomes. To reduce pseudo-replication [39], scats were selected from spatially separated deposits (≥ 10 m apart) and, where possible, from observed defecation events by different individuals. This approach was used as a practical field-based measure to reduce the likelihood of repeatedly sampling from the same defecation event within a troop exhibiting overlapping ranging behaviours [7, 9]. Similarly, in this study, individual identity was not always ascertainable because multiple individuals may defecate within short time intervals; therefore, sampling was designed to ensure broad representation of the troop rather than targeting specific age-sex classes.

Once collected, the scats were placed in labelled brown paper bags and air-dried in the shade on corrugated iron sheets until hardened to preserve seed and plant material, following established protocols in primate diet studies [40–43]. The contents were then manually disaggregated by gently breaking dried faecal samples apart with gloved hands in a clean tray, while preserving seed integrity. The disaggregated material was then washed and passed through a series of nested sieves of decreasing size to separate seeds, plant fragments and invertebrate remains [7, 9]. To minimise potential loss or underrepresentation of small seeds, fine mesh screens were included in the sifting sequence, and all retained material was carefully inspected under laboratory lighting, with residues re-examined to ensure that smaller seeds were not overlooked or miscounted. Dietary components were classified into the following categories: fruits/seeds, leaves, grasses, stems, pods, and invertebrates. Although the study primarily focuses on fruit and seed components relevant to dispersal and recruitment, the inclusion of broader dietary categories allowed for assessment of seasonal variation in overall diet composition, which is a key driver of seed ingestion, dispersal patterns and potential recruitment outcomes [1, 12, 20]. This was based on visual identification of scat contents, and their occurrence was recorded to describe the seasonal diet composition [44]. Seeds were sorted, counted and identified to the lowest possible taxonomic level using a reference collection developed during preliminary fieldwork. The reference collection consisted of seeds obtained from plant species occurring within the study area, collected and catalogued to facilitate comparison with recovered material. Seed identification was conducted based on morphological characteristics, including size, shape, colour and surface texture, and where necessary, comparisons were made with published seed identification guides and regional floras [45]. In cases of uncertainty, plant fragments associated with the seeds were also examined to support identification. Additional quantification included counting and weighing seeds per scat to estimate their relative dietary importance and dispersal potential [7, 46]. The methodology aligns with global approaches to evaluating primate roles in seed dispersal under experimentally controlled conditions [8, 21].

Following separation during scat processing, intact seeds were selected for greenhouse trials to assess the seedling emergence potential of defecated seeds. Seed taxa abundance data used for seasonal seed assemblage diversity analysis were derived from scat processing. In total, seeds representing 22 taxa were extracted across seasons; seeds retrieved from dry- and wet-season scats were sown separately. Seeds from wet- and dry-season scats were kept separate to enable seasonal comparisons in emergence outcomes. Similar approaches combining scat-derived seed recovery with controlled germination assessment have been widely applied to evaluate seed dispersal effectiveness beyond deposition alone [11, 12, 24]. Four sterilised seedling trays (1 × 1 m2) were used to plant the seeds (two for the wet and another for the dry season). Trays were sterilised prior to use using hot water and detergent, followed by thorough rinsing with distilled water and air drying. Each tray was then filled with nutrient-rich, sterile soil to reduce contamination [29]. Trays were placed and maintained under greenhouse conditions and watered at regular intervals to maintain consistent soil moisture suitable for germination, while avoiding waterlogging [16, 47]. This standardised irrigation regime was applied to minimise moisture variation among trays, and the temperature was kept between 15 °C and 25 °C to support emergence across taxa [11, 48, 49]. These controlled conditions also ensured that differences in germination could be attributed primarily to seed origin, rather than environmental variability [9, 50]. Seeds recovered from the scats were not pre-counted, categorised or treated prior to sowing but were instead sown as mixed assemblages to reflect natural seed deposition patterns. This approach was intended to simulate realistic dispersal conditions in which multiple species are deposited together within faecal material. The mixed seed material was randomly distributed across the trays containing sterile soil, without prior selection by species or treatment. As a result, the study focused on overall germination emergence rather than species-specific germination rates or proportional success. Trays were monitored over a six-month period (October 2015 to March 2016); germination was recorded as the emergence of seedlings. Newly emerged seedlings were identified to species level using regional floras and field guides to southern African savannas [45, 51, 52]. Weekly counts were taken to track germination, emergence timing and total germination success across plant types. During greenhouse monitoring, seedling emergence was recorded for each taxon and season. Emergence observations were treated as occurrence-based records, reflecting which seed taxa produced seedlings during the trial period. These emergence records were subsequently summarised using proportional occurrence metrics to describe the relative contribution of each taxon to the overall emergence composition within each season.

Data analysis

The scat data were analysed to compare dietary variation, focusing on frequency (number of scats containing a given species; occurrence), abundance (seed counts) and biomass (total mass of recovered seeds). These metrics provided an overview of seed abundance and the relative contribution of different species across seasons. Analyses were performed separately for dry (April – September) and wet (October – March) seasons. Seasonal differences in scat mass were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Prior to analysis, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, which indicated significant deviation from normality for both dry (W = 0.885, p < 0.001) and wet (W = 0.915, p < 0.001) season datasets, thereby justifying the use of a non-parametric approach. The Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric test for comparing two independent samples) was used to determine whether scat mass differed significantly between the dry and wet seasons, with individual scat samples treated as independent observations [53]. Results are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR).

A frequency distribution of plant species was developed for each season to assess dominant and minor seed taxa based on occurrence and abundance patterns [54]. Baboon Scat Equivalent (BSE) mass, seed counts per BSE, species richness per BSE and seasonal seed numbers per species were calculated to evaluate variation in seed dispersal patterns between seasons. Scats were manually disaggregated into identifiable categories (seeds/pods, fruits, leaves, grasses, bulbs/roots, invertebrates) and analysed. For each diet item, frequency of occurrence (FO) was calculated as the number of scats containing that item, and percentage of occurrence (%FO) was calculated as FO divided by the total number of observations for the season × 100. These occurrence-based measures are commonly used in scat-based dietary studies because they provide a robust, standardised description of diet composition based on presence/absence data [9, 55, 56]. Germinated species were grouped into functional categories (grasses, forbs, or woody plants) and compared. Because non-ingested control seeds were not included in the greenhouse experiment, the emergence trials were used to assess the emergence potential of defecated seeds rather than the effect of gut passage relative to untreated seeds [11, 24]. Germination outcomes from greenhouse trials were summarised as seedling emergence counts per species and per season, providing a controlled assessment of emergence potential under standardised environmental conditions. While this approach does not capture field-based variability in germination processes, it allows for the isolation of emergence potential under controlled conditions, thereby providing baseline insights into the viability of seeds dispersed through baboon scats. Given that seeds were not pre-counted, categorised prior to sowing, or replicated at the species level, the data structure did not support model-based approaches such as Generalised Linear Models (GLMs). Instead, differences in emergence outcomes between wet and dry seasons were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test based on taxon-level percentage emergence occurrence values (i.e., the proportional contribution of each taxon to the total seedling emergence records per season). This approach allowed for comparison of seasonal patterns in emergence composition across taxa. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05. All analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel.

Plant species per season (dry and wet)

Seasonal differences were also evident in the plant species whose seeds were ingested and subsequently defecated by chacma baboons. During the dry season, baboons consumed 22 seed taxa, whereas in the wet season, 20 species were recorded. In the dry season, the five most frequently recorded diet items accounted for 61.3% of total occurrence records (Table 3), namely Searsia leptodictya (15.0%), Celtis africana (13.8%), Olea africana (11.4%), grasses (10.9%) and Grewia occidentalis (10.3%). Taxa absent from a given season were not recorded in scat-derived seed assemblages during that sampling period.

Discussion

Seasonal foraging flexibility and scat-derived evidence of dietary change

This study provides evidence that chacma baboons function as ecologically relevant consumers and potential seed dispersers in the southern African savanna-grassland mosaic, with scat-derived diet signals showing clear seasonal variation. Seasonal differences in scat mass and seed deposition align with the well-established trophic flexibility of baboons and their ability to adjust diet composition as resource availability fluctuates [3, 17]. Such flexibility is a core feature of generalist primates and enables persistence across environments characterised by strong seasonality and patchy resources [20, 60]. The significantly higher scat mass in the dry season (Table 2) is consistent with seasonal shifts in intake quantity and/or dietary composition observed in other baboon populations experiencing resource constraints and temporal variation in food supply [6, 19, 20].

The increased seed counts and higher seed-related metrics in the dry season (Table 1) indicate greater representation of seed-bearing taxa in the dry-season scat assemblages. This pattern may reflect seasonal shifts in the types of foods consumed, consistent with previous studies showing that primate diets often vary with changes in resource availability and plant phenology, particularly fruiting patterns associated with rainfall and temperature regimes [22]. In chacma baboons, climatic variability and rainfall seasonality have been linked to changes in foraging behaviour, time budgets and diet composition [19, 20]. However, because this study did not quantify plant abundance, fruit availability or dietary preferences ratios, these results are interpreted as evidence of seasonal variation in scat-derived seed deposition rather than direct evidence of resource selection or preference.

Seasonal composition of ingested seed taxa and phenological interpretation

The scat-derived seed assemblages indicate that certain taxa contribute consistently across seasons (e.g., Olea africana and Celtis africana), while others show seasonal prominence (e.g., Searsia leptodictya in the dry season and Diospyros lycioides in the wet season; Tables 3 and 4). This pattern is consistent with the primate feeding ecology literature, which shows that a subset of plant taxa often forms a relatively stable dietary core, while other taxa fluctuate seasonally in their contribution to the diet in response to changes in availability and phenology [61, 62]. These seasonal differences are interpreted in terms of variation in the composition of scat-derived seed assemblages rather than in direct measures of plant availability or selection. Seasonal prominence is possibly explained by fruiting phenology and the accessibility of seed-bearing foods across seasons, particularly in systems where rainfall governs productivity pulses [15, 63]. Importantly, the absence of taxa in a given season, particularly for this study (Tables 3 and 4), is interpreted as “not detected in the scat-derived assemblages” rather than evidence of active avoidance, which is appropriate when using faecal methods [27, 55, 64, 65].

The increased representation of grasses and other secondary food categories in the wet season (Fig. 2; Table 4) is consistent with the wet-season expansion of herbaceous biomass and green forage availability in savanna ecosystems [17, 63, 66]. Similarly, invertebrate remains recorded in the wet season likely reflect opportunistic consumption when animal prey is more detectable or abundant. Opportunistic inclusion of animal matter is consistent with baboons’ omnivorous feeding strategy [33], and animal-source foods can provide concentrated protein and micronutrients even when consumed in relatively small quantities [67]. Within primate nutritional ecology, seasonal or contextual supplementation with animal matter is often interpreted as a strategy to meet nutritional targets when plant foods vary in nutrient content or when specific nutrients are limiting [68, 69].

Seasonal assemblage diversity

The seasonal Shannon-Wiener diversity values reported in this study (Table 5) add an important compositional dimension to the seed deposition results, because diversity reflects not only the number of taxa present, but also their relative abundances [70]. Notably, the wet season showed higher Shannon diversity (H’) despite a lower total number of seeds compared with the dry season. This indicates that wet-season seed assemblages were more evenly distributed among taxa, whereas dry-season assemblages were more strongly dominated by a smaller subset of taxa, resulting in lower evenness. This pattern is consistent with ecological scenarios in which seasonal shifts influence the relative contribution of taxa within assemblages [71]. Importantly, these diversity patterns reflect variation in scat-derived seed assemblages rather than changes in dietary preference per se.

At the same time, it is essential to emphasise what this diversity metric represents in this study: Shannon diversity here quantifies the diversity of seed taxa recovered from scats (i.e., the deposited seed assemblage), not the total diversity of baboon diet items. Because scat-based methods differ in their ability to detect or preserve diet components, particularly soft-bodied items that may be rapidly digested, the diversity values in this study describe the relative diversity and evenness of scat-derived seed assemblages, rather than a complete reconstruction of dietary breadth [27, 65, 72].

Greenhouse emergence patterns

A central contribution of this study is the pairing of scat-derived seed recovery with greenhouse emergence trials to document which seed taxa recovered from baboon scats produced seedlings under controlled conditions. Emergence was dominated by herbaceous taxa (forbs), with relatively low emergence occurrence for woody taxa and limited representation of grasses in the germination dataset (Table 6; Fig. 3). This is broadly consistent with ecological expectations that fast-growing, early successional forbs often exhibit rapid emergence and low dormancy requirements, particularly in disturbed or open habitats [18, 73–75]. Comparable patterns have been documented in other contexts where gut-passed seeds of pioneer or early successional species show comparatively higher germination or emergence under controlled conditions [24].

Greenhouse emergence outcomes provide a useful indication of the potential for baboon-dispersed seeds to contribute to early recruitment stages. In this study, emergence is interpreted as evidence of emergence potential rather than germination success or gut-passage effects, given the absence of control seeds and pre-counted samples. Conceptually, this aligns with seed dispersal effectiveness frameworks that emphasise dispersal as a multi-stage process, spanning deposition, seed survival, germination and recruitment, where success depends on interactions among disperser behaviour, seed traits and post-dispersal environmental conditions [11].

The seasonal differences in the emerging taxa (Table 6) suggest that seasonal foraging influences which taxa enter the emergence phase. However, the absence of a statistically significant difference between seasons (Mann-Whitney test) indicates that these compositional differences did not translate into differences in overall emergence distribution. This aligns with the idea that dispersers can vary the composition of dispersed seeds seasonally while maintaining broadly comparable emergence outcomes across seasons when assessed at the level of the emergence dataset [76, 77]. A further possible explanation for the limited emergence of woody taxa is that many savanna trees and shrubs exhibit dormancy mechanisms requiring temperature variation, smoke, heat, microbial interactions or mechanical scarification cues that are not captured by uniform greenhouse watering regimes [15, 78, 79].

Ecological implications for regeneration in savanna-grassland mosaics

Taken together, the diet composition, seed deposition, and emergence outcomes suggest that chacma baboons may contribute to vegetation regeneration primarily by redistributing seeds and potentially supporting recruitment of early-successional herbaceous taxa in this system. The combination of high seed deposition and demonstrated emergence potential indicates that baboon-mediated dispersal can contribute to early recruitment pathways, particularly for herbaceous taxa. In mosaic landscapes, where recruitment sites may be patchy, and regeneration is shaped by disturbance regimes, animal-mediated seed movement can contribute to spatial connectivity and post-disturbance recovery [7, 9]. Even where gut passage does not enhance germination relative to controls (which remains untested here), dispersal away from parent plants can still reduce density-dependent mortality and competition and alter seed shadows, a core consideration in seed dispersal effectiveness frameworks [11]. Deposition in faecal material may also provide nutrient enrichment that could support early establishment under favourable moisture conditions [12], though field-based testing would be needed to confirm this effect in the study area.

The underrepresentation of woody taxa in emergence outcomes is not interpreted as evidence that baboons are unimportant to woody recruitment. Rather, it more conservatively indicates that, under the greenhouse conditions and monitoring period used here, woody taxa were less likely to produce seedlings. In savannas, woody recruitment is strongly contingent on post-dispersal microhabitat conditions, soil moisture, herbivory pressure and disturbance regimes, including fire [15, 26, 80]. Thus, the ecological outcome of baboon-mediated deposition is likely to be expressed through complex field interactions rather than solely through greenhouse emergence.

Study limitations and future research directions

While this study provides important insights into seasonal diet composition, seed deposition patterns and emergence potential of scat-derived seeds, several limitations should be acknowledged, particularly regarding inference beyond controlled experimental conditions. The greenhouse trials provided a structured way to assess whether seeds recovered from baboon scats could produce seedlings under consistent conditions; however, germination and recruitment in semi-arid savanna systems are strongly shaped by environmental heterogeneity. Factors such as fluctuating soil moisture, diurnal temperature variation, microbial interactions and episodic rainfall events can influence germination timing and early seedling survival, and these cues are often difficult to replicate under standardised greenhouse regimes [15, 78, 79]. In addition, many woody taxa possess complex dormancy strategies that require specific triggers, such as mechanical scarification, heat shock, or smoke-associated cues, which are common in fire-prone ecosystems but absent in most greenhouse contexts [15, 78, 81]. These limitations highlight the importance of interpreting greenhouse emergence results as indicative rather than definitive measures of recruitment potential under natural conditions.

A further limitation relates to the absence of paired non-ingested control seeds for the same taxa, which limits the extent to which gut passage effects on emergence can be directly evaluated. Gut passage outcomes are known to vary substantially across taxa, depending on fruit traits, seed coat hardness, dormancy physiology and plant functional group [24]. Future studies that incorporate non-ingested control seeds alongside scat-derived seeds for each taxon would allow a more explicit assessment of whether gut passage enhances, reduces or has no effect on emergence probability. Such improvements would strengthen inference about seed dispersal effectiveness beyond mere demonstration of emergence potential.

Beyond germination, dispersal effectiveness is best understood as a multi-stage process involving deposition location, post-dispersal seed survival, germination under ambient conditions and longer-term recruitment into the plant community [11]. Accordingly, field-based seed fate studies represent a critical next step. These could quantify deposition microsites, exposure to predation or desiccation, and the persistence of seedlings in relation to microhabitat structure, disturbance regimes and herbivory pressure. Linking deposition to seedling establishment would allow a more ecologically integrated interpretation of baboon-mediated dispersal within savanna mosaics.

Additionally, the present study did not incorporate spatial tracking of baboon movements or defecation patterns, which limits inference regarding dispersal distances, seed shadows and habitat targeting. Movement ecology tools, such as Global Positioning System (GPS) telemetry and landscape analysis, could be used to quantify dispersal kernels, identify deposition hotspots, and assess the potential contribution of baboons to vegetation connectivity across heterogeneous terrain [82]. Scat-based approaches may also under-detect soft-bodied or highly digestible dietary items [27, 55, 65], and complementary molecular methods such as DNA metabarcoding could enhance taxonomic resolution and improve the detectability of cryptic plant taxa, thereby refining seasonal comparisons of diet composition [72]. Integrating these approaches would strengthen the linkage between seed deposition patterns, emergence potential, and realised recruitment, which remains a key applied need in seed dispersal ecology [11, 83, 84].

Conservation and management implications

Despite these constraints, the findings are relevant for understanding ecosystem functioning and regeneration processes in protected savanna-grassland mosaics. These findings reinforce the importance of recognising animal-mediated seed dispersal as a functional ecological process in savanna-grassland systems. Chacma baboons are often framed primarily as consumers [2, 3, 9] and, in some landscapes, as conflict-prone species [85]; however, the evidence presented here highlights an additional ecological dimension: their role as agents of seed redistribution through endozoochory [7]. By depositing viable seed across seasons, baboons may contribute to spatial seed movement and potentially facilitate early-stage regeneration processes, particularly for herbaceous taxa associated with disturbed or open habitats. Such processes may be especially relevant in systems where recruitment sites are patchy, and regeneration is strongly shaped by fire, herbivory, drought and episodic rainfall events [15, 26, 86].

In this context, maintaining habitat heterogeneity and access to diverse food resources across seasons becomes important not only for sustaining baboon populations but also for supporting ecological processes associated with their foraging and movement. The consistent contribution of fruit-derived dietary items across seasons suggests that fruiting taxa remain nutritionally central to baboons across the annual cycle, reinforcing the importance of conserving fruit-bearing shrubs and trees within the reserve landscape [36]. Habitat changes that reduce fruiting plant availability or simplify vegetation structure may therefore have cascading effects on diet composition, spatial movement and the diversity of deposited seed assemblages.

At broader scales, seed dispersal services provided by animals are increasingly threatened in many ecosystems, with implications for vegetation recovery, recruitment dynamics and long-term resilience. When dispersal declines or ecological interactions are disrupted, regeneration outcomes can be altered, potentially leading to reduced recruitment success and simplified plant assemblages [12]. Recognising the functional role of baboons within these ecological networks strengthens the rationale for incorporating faunal processes into restoration planning, particularly where passive restoration approaches are considered. Conservation planning should thus account for trophic interactions and movement ecology alongside habitat protections, ensuring that animals capable of transporting propagules remain integrated within landscape-level restoration and management strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights the ecological relevance of chacma baboons in shaping vegetation dynamics through scat-mediated seed dispersal within a semi-arid savanna landscape. Clear seasonal variation in scat mass, dietary composition and deposited seed assemblages reflects high foraging flexibility, enabling baboons to persist across contrasting environmental conditions while continuously redistributing seeds across the landscape. The recovery of large numbers of seeds representing multiple plant taxa across both wet and dry seasons provides evidence that baboons contribute to endozoochorous seed movement throughout the year.

Greenhouse emergence trials further demonstrate that a subset of scat-derived seeds can successfully produce seedlings under controlled conditions, with emergence dominated by herbaceous taxa, particularly forbs. Although these outcomes are best interpreted as evidence of emergence potential rather than direct measures of gut passage effects (given the absence of paired non-ingested controls), they nonetheless suggest that baboon-mediated seed deposition can support early-stage recruitment pathways for pioneer taxa. Such herbaceous pioneers are ecologically important in disturbed or open habitats, where they may contribute to soil stabilisation, nutrient cycling, and the development of microsites that facilitate subsequent successional processes in systems shaped by fire, grazing, and anthropogenic disturbances. In this sense, baboons may contribute to passive regeneration processes within savanna mosaics by redistributing viable propagules to spatially heterogeneous microsites. Importantly, integrating scat-based seed recovery with emergence trials provides a process-based understanding of seed dispersal that links consumption, deposition, and potential recruitment, thereby strengthening inference beyond descriptive dietary analyses.

Beyond its site-level implications, this work contributes to broader recognition of animal-plant interactions in dryland ecosystems, which remain underrepresented in global primate seed dispersal research. By focusing on a southern African protected area, the study complements the dominant forest-based literature and expands understanding of primate-mediated dispersal functions across biomes. This study, therefore, advances empirical evidence for the functional role of generalist primates in savanna systems, where dispersal processes are often assumed but rarely quantified. Future research that integrates field-based seed-fate monitoring, spatial movement ecology, and control-inclusive germination experiments will be essential to quantify realised dispersal effectiveness, recruitment success, and potential long-distance dispersal pathways. Such integrative approaches will enable a more complete evaluation of the ecological significance of baboon-mediated dispersal under natural conditions and across heterogenous landscape. Overall, the findings emphasise that chacma baboons’ function not only as consumers but as active agents of seed redistribution with measurable implications for vegetation structure and regeneration processes. Recognising and conserving these ecological functions will be critical for maintaining ecosystem resilience and supporting passive restoration pathways in savanna-grassland mosaics under increasing environmental change.
