Longitudinal TCR repertoire profiling predicts refractory cytomegalovirus reactivation after haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Hailong Yuan, Lijuan Liu, Gang Chen, Jianli Xu, Kaile Zhang, Ruixue Yang, Chunxia Han, Jia Hou, Ming Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation is a major cause of morbidity and mortality after haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (Haplo-HSCT). While T cells are crucial for controlling CMV, the dynamic characteristics of the T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire and their predictive value for refractory CMV reactivation remain poorly characterized. This study aimed to longitudinally monitor the TCR repertoire in Haplo-HSCT patients to identify features associated with CMV reactivation and to explore predictive biomarkers for refractory and recurrent CMV disease.MethodsWe enrolled 164 Haplo-HSCT patients, of which a subset of 28 was prospectively monitored for TCR repertoire dynamics via multiplex PCR and high-throughput sequencing. Patients were stratified into control (no reactivation, n = 10), acute phase (first reactivation, n = 18), and resolution phase (after clearance, n = 18) groups. CMV-specific TCRs were identified by database matching.ResultsThe incidence of CMV reactivation was 48.17% (79/164). This skewed architecture persisted even after viral clearance, suggesting delayed CMV-specific immune reconstitution. Consistent with this, patients with CMV reactivation had significantly lower CD4+ T cell counts compared to those without reactivation (P = 0.003). Longitudinal analysis revealed that impaired CD4+ T cell recovery preceded CMV reactivation. Background

Cytomegalovirus (CMV), a member of the Betaherpesvirinae subfamily, is a widespread infectious agent with a global seroprevalence approaching 100% in developing countries [1]. Following primary infection, CMV can establish lifelong latency. While it typically causes mild or subclinical symptoms in immunocompetent adults, it may lead to opportunistic infections and reactivation in individuals with compromised, immature, or suppressed immune systems, such as hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients [2]. CMV reactivation represents a major challenge in the setting of haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (Haplo-HSCT), an established curative modality for hematological malignancies and bone marrow failure syndromes [3]. Despite the therapeutic efficacy of Haplo-HSCT, CMV-related disease remains a prevalent and serious complication [4], potentially causing life-threatening conditions such as graft failure and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) [5]. This risk is particularly pronounced as Haplo-HSCT itself has been identified as an independent risk factor for CMV reactivation [6, 7]. Therefore, identifying protective factors against CMV reactivation in Haplo-HSCT patients is essential for reducing non-relapse mortality and improving overall survival.

T cells play a critical role in controlling CMV reactivation in transplant recipients [8]. Their ability to recognize and respond to CMV is mediated by the T-cell receptor (TCR), which achieves its immense diversity through V(D)J recombination of the complementary determining region 3 (CDR3). This diversity is fundamental to the generation of CMV-specific T cells, which form the basis of protective immunity against viral reactivation [9]. Studies have suggested that the diversity of TRBV gene usage and TRBV-BJ pairings may be linked to CMV reactivation and can shape the clonality of the TCR repertoire [10, 11]. However, the precise dynamics of the TCR repertoire and the role of CDR3 in CMV reactivation following Haplo-HSCT remain poorly characterized. Further investigation into TCR repertoire changes before and after CMV reactivation in Haplo-HSCT patients may help elucidate the mechanisms of CMV reactivation and provide valuable insights for future CMV treatment and immune monitoring.

In this study, we monitored peripheral blood TCR repertoire dynamics in Haplo-HSCT patients and analyzed these changes in relation to CMV reactivation events. We aimed to characterize the TCR repertoire features in patients with CMV reactivation and identify sensitive predictive indicators for CMV reactivation, thereby enabling early warning of CMV reactivation and associated complications.

Methods

Study design and patient cohort

This single-center, observational study consecutively enrolled 164 patients who underwent Haplo-HSCT at the Hematology Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between January 2022 and December 2024, with a mandatory follow-up period of > 6 months. From this cohort, a subset of 28 patients transplanted in 2024 was prospectively selected for longitudinal TCR repertoire monitoring at predefined time points. Inclusion criteria: (1) patients who had an underlying hematological malignancy or bone marrow failure syndrome with an indication for transplantation and underwent Haplo-HSCT. (2) patients who were seropositive for CMV-IgG (CMV-seropositive) before transplant. (3) patients who survived for at least six months post-transplantation. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients who had primary graft failure. (2) patients with severe concurrent dysfunction of the heart, lungs, liver, or kidneys. (3) patients who survived for less than six months post-transplant. (4) patients with the presence of any uncontrolled active infection at the time of enrollment. (5) patients who were recipients of a second or subsequent allogeneic HSCT. (6) patients who had CMV-seronegative status (CMV-IgG negative) before transplant. (7) patients who declined to participate in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians before transplantation and study inclusion.

CMV serostatus and HLA matching assessment

CMV serostatus (CMV-IgG) of the 28 patients in the TCR-sequenced cohort and their donors was determined from pre-transplant peripheral blood samples using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. High-resolution HLA typing for loci A, B, C, DRB1, DQB1, and DPB1 was performed for all donor-recipient pairs, and the number of matched alleles out of 12 possible loci was recorded.

Conditioning regimens

Patients received one of two standard conditioning regimens. For myeloablative conditioning, an antithymocyte globulin (ATG)-based regimen consisting of cytarabine (Ara-C) + busulfan (Bu)/cyclophosphamide (Cy) was used. Ara-C (2–4 g/m2) was administered intravenously on days − 9 to − 8; Bu (3.2 mg/kg/day) was given in four divided doses intravenously on days − 7 to − 5; Cy (1.8 g/m2) was administered intravenously on days − 3 to − 2; and ATG (2.5 mg/kg/day) was given on days − 4 to − 1. For reduced-intensity conditioning, the fludarabine + Ara-C + Bu regimen was employed: fludarabine (30 mg/m2) on days − 9 to − 5; Ara-C (2 g/m2) on days − 9 to − 5; Bu (3.2 mg/kg/day) on days − 4 to − 3; and ATG (2.5 mg/kg) on days − 4 to − 1.

GVHD prophylaxis

The prophylaxis regimen was based on cyclosporine A or tacrolimus + short-course methotrexate + mycophenolate mofetil + glucocorticoids. Cyclosporine A was administered from day − 5 to day + 100, starting at 2–2.5 mg/kg/day intravenously, then switching to 4–5 mg/kg/day orally. Alternatively, tacrolimus was used from day − 5 to day + 100, starting at 0.02 mg/kg/day intravenously, then switching to 0.05 mg/kg/day orally in two divided doses. Mycophenolate mofetil (0.5 g twice daily) was given orally from day − 1 to day + 100. Methotrexate was administered at 15 mg/m2 on day + 1, and 10 mg/m2 on days + 3, +6, and + 11 intravenously. Anti-CD25 monoclonal antibody (12 mg/m2; 20 mg per dose) was given intravenously once daily on days + 1 and + 2. For Haplo-HSCT patients, dexamethasone (2.5 mg) was administered intravenously every 12 h from day + 1 to day + 15, then gradually switched to oral prednisone and tapered off.

Treatment of acute GVHD

Diagnosis and grading of acute GVHD followed the most recent guidelines [12]. First-line therapy with methylprednisolone (1–2 mg/kg/day) was initiated upon the onset of acute GVHD symptoms. If the response was inadequate, second-line agents (e.g., mycophenolate mofetil) or third-line monoclonal antibodies were employed. Cyclosporine A concentrations were closely monitored, and doses were adjusted accordingly.

Viral prophylaxis and preemptive therapy

A standardized antiviral prophylaxis regimen was administered according to the clinical era. Prior to the clinical availability of letermovir, patients received intravenous ganciclovir (250 mg twice daily) from day − 7 to − 1, switched to acyclovir (250 mg twice daily) from day + 1 until neutrophil engraftment (absolute neutrophil count > 0.5 × 109/L), followed by a second two-week course of ganciclovir commencing on day + 15. After letermovir became clinically available, patients received letermovir for primary CMV prophylaxis within 28 days post-transplant, depending on gastrointestinal tolerance (e.g., absence of diarrhea). The standard dose was 480 mg orally once daily, with the treatment course typically extended to day + 100 post-transplant. For patients who did not receive letermovir for primary prophylaxis, letermovir was selected for secondary prophylaxis following successful control of CMV reactivation with ganciclovir, to prevent subsequent re-reactivation.

Lymphocyte subset analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all patients at protocol-defined time points. For patients with CMV reactivation, samples were collected pre-reactivation, during active reactivation, and post-resolution. For patients without CMV reactivation, samples were collected at matched time points post-transplant. Absolute counts of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes were determined by flow cytometry. Briefly, whole blood was stained with fluorescence-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD3, CD4, and CD8 (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). After red blood cell lysis, samples were acquired on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, and absolute cell counts were calculated using BD Trucount tubes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were analyzed using FACSDiva software (version 8.0).

CMV diagnosis and definitions

CMV reactivation was defined as two consecutive positive tests for plasma CMV-DNA using quantitative real-time PCR, with the first positive date marking the onset. CMV disease was defined per the latest guidelines for CMV infection management [13]. All patients underwent weekly surveillance for CMV-DNA via quantitative real-time PCR for at least the first six months post-transplant. The duration of CMV infection (clinical course) for the first reactivation episode was defined as the period from the initial reactivation time until the first of two consecutive negative CMV-DNA tests.

TCR repertoire sequencing and analysis

TCR β-chain sequencing and primary data analysis were outsourced to Wuhan Kindstar Biotech Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Briefly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were collected, and genomic DNA was extracted from isolated T cells. The TCR β-chain CDR3 was amplified using a multiplex PCR system targeting V and J genes. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform with a minimum of 50,000 reads per sample. Sequencing data processing was performed as follows: Raw reads were demultiplexed, and CDR3 regions were assembled and annotated using the International ImMunoGeneTics information system database as the reference. Somatic hypermutation and PCR errors were corrected using unique molecular identifiers and synthetic spike-in templates. The analyzed metrics included: (1) Clonality: A normalized measure (0–1) of the dominance of particular clones, where 1 indicates a monoclonal population; (2) Richness: The total number of unique clonotypes detected; (3) Shannon Diversity Index: A measure of the overall diversity and evenness of the TCR repertoire; and (4) V-J Gene Usage: The frequency of specific TRBV-TRBJ pairings. CMV-specific TCR clonotypes were identified via bioinformatic matching of the obtained CDR3 amino acid sequences against a publicly available curated database of experimentally validated CMV-epitope-specific TCR sequences. A volcano plot was generated to visualize the relationship between the statistical significance (-log10(P-value)) and the magnitude of change (log2(Fold Change)) in V-J pairing frequencies, allowing for the identification of significantly differentially utilized gene pairs.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square. Continuous variables were expressed as median (range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons across multiple groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-parametric comparisons of TCR metrics. The association between CMV-specific TCR abundance and the duration of CMV infection was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient and visualized with a scatter plot. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 25.0.

Results

Patient characteristics and CMV reactivation incidence

A total of 164 patients who underwent Haplo-HSCT and completed at least six months of follow-up were included in the final analysis. Among them, 79 patients (48.17%) developed CMV reactivation within six months post-transplantation. The demographic and clinical characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 43 years (range: 7–66) in the reactivation group and 36 years (range: 7–68) in the non-reactivation group, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.103). However, a significant gender disparity was observed: CMV reactivation occurred in 45.57% (36/79) of male patients and 54.43% (43/79) of female patients in the reactivation group, whereas in the non-reactivation group, 64.71% (55/85) were male and 35.29% (30/85) were female (P = 0.014). The distribution of underlying primary diseases—including acute myeloid leukemia, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, and aplastic anemia—was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.870).

T lymphocyte counts and other infections in CMV reactivators

To investigate the impact of CMV reactivation on overall immune reconstitution, we compared absolute CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts between patients with and without CMV reactivation in the overall cohort of 164 patients. As shown in Fig. 1A, patients with CMV reactivation (n = 79) had significantly lower CD4+ T cell counts compared to those without reactivation (n = 85) [median 82.08 (38.95–211.70) cells/µL vs. 188.32 (105.02-270.66) cells/µL, P = 0.003]. However, CD8+ T cell counts were comparable between the two groups [716.00 (312.12-1553.20) cells/µL vs. 744.05 (415.14-1371.50) cells/µL, P = 0.881] (Fig. 1B).

To further investigate the temporal relationship between T cell recovery and CMV reactivation, we longitudinally analyzed CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts in patients with CMV reactivation at three time points: pre-reactivation, during active reactivation, and post-resolution. As shown in Fig. 1C, CD4+ T cell counts at the pre-reactivation time point [median 4.86 (0.88–33.02) cells/µL] were significantly lower compared to during active reactivation [82.08 (38.95–211.70) cells/µL] (P < 0.001) and post-resolution [136.00 (70.68–243.10) cells/µL] (P < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between active reactivation and post-resolution (P = 0.477). As shown in Fig. 1D, CD8+ T cell counts also increased significantly from pre-reactivation [13.91 (2.58–53.28) cells/µL] to active reactivation [716.00 (312.12-1553.20) cells/µL] (P < 0.001) and to post-resolution [821.42 (483.30–1308.00) cells/µL] (P < 0.001), with no significant difference between active reactivation and post-resolution (P = 0.829). These findings indicate that impaired CD4+ T cell recovery precedes CMV reactivation.

As an additional observation, among the 79 patients with CMV reactivation, 34 (43.0%) had Epstein-Barr virus co-reactivation and 4 (5.1%) developed fungal infections.

Patient characteristics of the TCR-sequenced cohort

Among the 164 patients in the overall cohort, 28 patients were prospectively enrolled for longitudinal TCR repertoire monitoring, including 10 patients without CMV reactivation (control group) and 18 patients with CMV reactivation. The baseline characteristics of this TCR-sequenced cohort were as follows: median age was 46 years (range 23–66); 13 patients (46.43%) were male and 15 (53.57%) were female. The distribution of primary diseases included acute myeloid leukemia (n = 15, 53.57%), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (n = 11, 39.29%), myelodysplastic syndrome (n = 2, 7.14%).

All 28 recipients and their corresponding donors were CMV-seropositive. High-resolution HLA typing for 12 loci (HLA-A, B, C, DRB1, DQB1, DPB1) revealed that 21 patients had 6 out of 12 matched loci and 7 patients had 7 out of 12 matched loci, consistent with haploidentical related donor transplantation. The number of HLA mismatches was comparable between patients who later experienced a single reactivation (n = 12) and those who developed re-reactivation (n = 6), indicating that the degree of HLA disparity did not significantly influence CMV reactivation risk in this cohort.

Regarding steroid exposure at the time of TCR sampling, all samples were collected after the completion of the routine dexamethasone prophylaxis regimen (day + 1 to + 15). At the time of sampling, two patients had active acute GVHD. One patient with grade I skin acute GVHD was managed with close observation without steroid treatment. The other patient with grade II skin acute GVHD was receiving oral methylprednisolone (0.5 mg/kg/day). The remaining 26 patients (92.9%) had no evidence of active GVHD and were not receiving any GVHD-specific therapy at the time of TCR sampling. Combined with the grade I acute GVHD patient who did not receive steroids, a total of 27 patients (96.4%) were not on active steroid therapy at the time of TCR analysis.

Dynamic changes in TCR repertoire following CMV reactivation and recovery

To delineate the impact of CMV reactivation on T-cell immunity, we compared TCR repertoire metrics across three timepoints: control (1-month post-HSCT), acute phase (first CMV reactivation), and resolution phase (after viral clearance). Comparative analysis across these timepoints revealed no significant differences in the mean CDR3 amino acid sequence length (P = 0.846) (Fig. 2A), overall diversity (P = 0.607) (Fig. 2B), or the Shannon index of V-J gene usage (P = 0.846) (Fig. 2C). In contrast, key metrics of clonal architecture were significantly altered. Specifically, CDR3 amino acid sequence clonality (P = 0.011) (Fig. 2D), clonotype distribution evenness (P = 0.011) (Fig. 2E), and the number of unique Clonotypes (P = 0.009) (Fig. 2F) all showed significant differences. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed that these parameters differed significantly between the control and acute phases, as well as between the control and resolution phases (P < 0.05). These findings indicate that CMV reactivation induces a state of oligoclonal expansion within the TCR repertoire. Notably, this skewed clonality persists even after successful antiviral therapy when CMV-DNA levels fall below 500 copies/mL, suggesting a delayed CMV-specific immune reconstitution compared to patients without CMV reactivation.

Association between V-J gene pairings and CMV re-reactivation

We further investigated potential mechanisms underlying CMV re-reactivation by stratifying the 18 patients with reactivation into two groups: single reactivation (n = 12) and re-reactivation (n = 6). Analysis at the first acute episode showed no significant differences in any TCR metrics between the two groups (P > 0.05; data not shown). However, pronounced differences emerged during the treatment resolution phase (Fig. 3). While most metrics (Fig. 3A and E), including Clonotypes, were comparable, patients with re-reactivation exhibited a significantly reduced Shannon index for V-J gene usage (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3F), indicating a loss of diversity in V-J gene pairings.

To identify specific gene pairs associated with this loss of diversity, we performed a comparative analysis of V-J combination frequencies, presented as a volcano plot (Fig. 4). This analysis revealed a significant downregulation of the TRBV28/TRBJ1-1 and TRBV6-1/TRBJ2-7 gene pairs in the re-reactivation group. This suggests a biased clonal expansion and impaired diversity generation during TCR rearrangement, reflecting a reduced breadth of the CMV-specific T-cell response and potentially contributing to an increased risk of recurrent viral reactivation.

CMV-specific TCR abundance as a predictor of refractory CMV infection

We next evaluated the clinical utility of CMV-specific TCR measurements as predictive biomarkers. Compared to patients with only a single reactivation (n = 12), patients who later experienced re-reactivation (n = 6) had significantly lower abundance of CMV-specific TCRs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A) and a lower total frequency of CMV-specific TCRs in peripheral blood (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B).

Given the strong association with re-reactivation, we explored the relationship between CMV-specific TCR abundance and disease course. Patients were divided into low-abundance (< 1%) (n = 6) and high-abundance (≥ 1%) groups (n = 12) based on CMV-specific TCR frequency. The clinical course of the first CMV episode was significantly longer in the low-abundance group (Fig. 6A). A scatter plot illustrating the correlation between CMV-specific TCR abundance and the duration of the first CMV episode showed a trend toward an inverse relationship, albeit with a modest fit (R2 = 17.86%), suggesting that lower abundance is associated with a more prolonged infection (Fig. 6B).

Notably, 4 of the 6 re-reactivation patients experienced their first reactivation within 2 months post-transplant. Despite secondary prophylaxis with letermovir (480 mg daily) following the initial clearance, all 4 patients developed CMV re-reactivation within two weeks. Strikingly, none of these 4 patients had detectable CMV-specific TCR sequences within their top 100 most abundant CDR3 sequences at the first episode, indicating a profound lack of CMV-specific T cells that may underlie the failure of pharmacologic prophylaxis.

Discussion

This study, through longitudinal monitoring of the TCR repertoire, delineates the dynamic characteristics of T-cell immunity in patients with CMV reactivation following Haplo-HSCT. Our principal findings are that CMV reactivation drives oligoclonal expansion within the TCR repertoire, marked by increased CDR3 clonality and delayed CMV-specific immune reconstitution; CMV re-reactivation is associated with a loss of diversity and significant downregulation of specific TRBV-BJ gene pairings, such as TRBV28/TRBJ1-1 and TRBV6-1/TRBJ2-7; and a low abundance of CMV-specific TCRs in peripheral blood serves as a potential sensitive indicator for predicting prolonged CMV infection and the risk of re-reactivation.

CMV reactivation drives oligoclonal expansion and delays immune reconstitution

CMV reactivation is a frequent and serious complication after Haplo-HSCT, with reported incidence rates varying from 18% to 85% [6, 7]. Our observed rate of 48.17% falls within this range and underscores the clinical significance of this complication, which can progress from viremia to end-organ disease with high mortality [14]. T cells are central to controlling CMV, and their function is dictated by the TCR, a highly diverse molecule generated through V(D)J recombination [15].

Our data confirm that CMV reactivation induces a state of oligoclonal expansion, characterized by a significant increase in CDR3 sequence clonality that persists even after virological clearance, suggesting delayed CMV-specific immune reconstitution. Consistent with the observed oligoclonal TCR expansion, the reduced CD4+ T cell count in CMV reactivation patients indicates specific barriers to CD4+ T cell reconstitution. This finding aligns with reports that CMV reactivation and specific T-cell clones shape the clonality of the post-transplant TCR repertoire [16]. Longitudinal analysis revealed that impaired CD4+ T cell recovery predated CMV reactivation.

The expansion of virus-specific T cells from a memory population rather than de novo generation post-transplant has been previously demonstrated [17]. In our cohort, the observed oligoclonal expansion upon CMV reactivation can be attributed to the immunological background, in which all patients with TCR data were recipient CMV-seropositive and donor CMV-seropositive. In this setting, the CMV-specific T cells responsible for viral control are likely derived from donor-derived memory T cells transferred with the graft. The increased clonality, reduced evenness, and restricted V-J gene usage observed in our study are consistent with the expected behavior of oligoclonal, immunodominant T cells responding to viral rechallenge. However, this conclusion is based on inference from the transplant setting rather than direct experimental lineage tracing. Future studies incorporating such approaches could provide more definitive evidence.

While the mean CDR3 amino acid length did not differ significantly between groups, we observed skewness in its distribution, potentially attributable to the combined effects of immune reconstitution and CMV reactivation, as noted by others [9]. Previous work has identified preferential CDR3 lengths for CMV-specific cytotoxic T cells [18], which differ from those targeting other viruses [19], and altered CDR3 length distributions are a feature of certain disease states [20], highlighting the importance of the CDR3 region in antiviral immunity.

Consistent with previous findings highlighting the importance of HLA disparity in CMV control [21], all patients in our cohort underwent haploidentical transplantation with substantial HLA mismatches. Interestingly, within this uniformly haploidentical setting, the degree of mismatch did not significantly influence CMV reactivation risk, suggesting that CMV-specific TCR abundance may serve as a more sensitive predictor of outcomes once the threshold of significant HLA disparity is met.

Impaired V-J gene diversity and specific pairings underlie re-reactivation

The mechanisms predisposing patients to recurrent CMV reactivation remain poorly understood. We found that patients experiencing re-reactivation exhibited a significant reduction in the diversity of V-J gene combinations (Shannon index) during the resolution phase, alongside specific downregulation of TRBV28/TRBJ1-1 and TRBV6-1/TRBJ2-7 pairings. This indicates an impaired diversity mechanism during TCR gene rearrangement. V-J pairing is fundamental to generating CDR3 diversity and an effective T-cell response against pathogens [9]. Our findings extend previous observations of altered TRBV-BJ usage in CMV reactivation [9, 22] by specifically linking the loss of diversity and particular V-J pairs to the clinical phenotype of re-reactivation. This suggests that the depth of the T-cell response, not just its presence, is critical for durable CMV control.

CMV-specific TCR abundance as a biomarker and implications for therapy

The quantitative assessment of virus-specific immunity offers profound clinical insights. We found that low CMV-specific TCR abundance was significantly associated with a longer CMV infection duration and a higher risk of re-reactivation. Importantly, this association remained significant despite uniform donor CMV-seropositive status, comparable HLA mismatch distribution, and minimal steroid exposure in our cohort, suggesting that CMV-specific TCR abundance is an independent predictor of re-reactivation risk beyond these potential confounders. This is consistent with the previous study linking low CMV-specific T-cell counts to higher recurrence rates [23]. A pivotal observation was that four patients developed early re-reactivation despite letermovir prophylaxis, and these individuals notably lacked CMV-specific TCRs within their most abundant clones. This suggests that pharmacological prophylaxis alone may be insufficient in hosts lacking a robust CMV-specific T-cell response, a concept supported by the association between recurrent reactivation and T-cell senescence [24, 25]. Consequently, monitoring CMV-specific TCR abundance could help identify high-risk patients for preemptive intervention. For these patients, adoptive T-cell therapies represent a promising strategy. Donor-derived CMV-specific T-cell therapy has shown high efficacy (overall response rate: 89.5%) in treating refractory CMV in pediatric allo-HSCT patients [6], and CMV-TCR-T cell therapy, which overcomes donor serostatus limitations, has demonstrated success in achieving complete and sustained viral clearance in clinical trials [26]. Thus, for patients with deficient CMV-specific T-cell immunity, early adoptive T-cell therapies may be fundamental beyond conventional antivirals. We also acknowledge that the use of ganciclovir and letermovir prophylaxis itself may have influenced CMV-specific immune reconstitution in our cohort, as previous studies have suggested that letermovir prophylaxis may be associated with delayed CMV-specific T-cell reconstitution, possibly related to decreased viral antigen exposure [27, 28]. However, the substantial inter-individual variation in CMV-specific TCR abundance despite uniform drug exposure highlights that host immune factors remain critical determinants of outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we infer that the CMV-specific T cells are donor-derived based on the recipient and donor CMV-seropositive status and the T-cell-replete transplant setting, we did not perform direct lineage tracing experiments (e.g., using sex-mismatched pairs or STR analysis of sorted CMV-specific T cells) to definitively confirm their origin. Future studies incorporating such approaches could provide more definitive evidence. Second, the sample size for the TCR sequencing cohort was relatively small (n = 28), which may limit the generalizability of our findings and precluded more detailed subgroup analyses. In particular, the limited number of re-reactivation events (n = 6) prevented a robust multivariable analysis to adjust for potential confounders, and the results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Larger prospective cohorts are warranted to validate the independent predictive value of CMV-specific TCR abundance. Third, this was a single-center study, and validation in larger, multicenter cohorts would strengthen the clinical applicability of CMV-specific TCR abundance as a predictive biomarker. Fourth, the identification of CMV-specific TCRs in this study was based on bioinformatic matching to a database of experimentally validated sequences, which does not provide direct functional confirmation of T-cell activity against CMV. Future work incorporating functional assays such as intracellular cytokine staining or ELISpot will be important to validate the cytotoxic capacity and cytokine profiles of the identified CMV-specific clones. Fifth, although we collected data on steroid use at the time of sampling, the number of patients on active steroid therapy was too small to perform statistical adjustment, and the potential confounding effect of immunosuppressive agents on TCR repertoire dynamics cannot be fully excluded. Finally, while we observed a relatively high rate of Epstein-Barr virus co-reactivation in CMV reactivators, the impact of such coinfection on immune reconstitution, TCR repertoire evolution, and long-term outcomes warrants further investigation in larger prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that early TCR repertoire dynamics are closely associated with CMV reactivation outcomes after Haplo-HSCT. The analysis of the TCR immune repertoire, particularly the quantification of CMV-specific TCRs, enables risk stratification for recurrent and refractory CMV disease. These insights provide a critical foundation for developing personalized monitoring and treatment strategies, ultimately aiming to improve patient survival and quality of life post-transplant.
