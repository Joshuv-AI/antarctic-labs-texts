Ocular facial myositis and sialadenitis presenting with new onset ulcerative colitis
Authors: Nicole Du, Nicole Mendez, Anil Darbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtra‐intestinal manifestations of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) can involve most organ systems, although the immunologic underpinnings are not well understood. Most patients who present with an extra‐intestinal manifestation have a single site of extra‐intestinal involvement; however, a small cohort presents with multiple different sites of involvement. Ocular and facial muscle and salivary gland involvements are rarely seen in IBD, especially in children with ulcerative colitis (UC). In this study, we describe a case of ocular facial myositis and sialadenitis in a child with new‐onset UC.

INTRODUCTION

The extra‐intestinal manifestations (EIMs) of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) may involve many organ systems. Most patients who present with an EIM have a single site of extra‐intestinal involvement; however, an extremely small cohort presents with multiple different sites of involvement. 1 We present a case of a child who presented with multiple, rare EIMs, who was diagnosed with new onset ulcerative colitis (UC).

CASE REPORT

A 13‐year‐old Spanish‐speaking girl was admitted to the intensive care unit with 3 days of headaches and 1 day of right‐sided facial and periorbital swelling. She had a history of anemia for which she was taking iron supplements. A computed tomography (CT) scan showed edema of the right periorbital area, muscles of mastication, parotid gland, buccal space, and with lack of enhancement of the right cavernous sinus, a concern for cavernous sinus thrombosis (Figure 1A). An ophthalmologic exam showed edema of the right extraocular muscles, but no ophthalmoplegia/optic nerve head edema. Physical examination was otherwise non‐focal, although limited by significant right facial swelling. Initial laboratory studies were significant for a low hemoglobin of 5.8 g/dL, low mean corpuscular volume of 78, high erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 80 mm/h, low albumin of 2.1 g/dL, and a high C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 4.55 mg/dL.

Radiological studies: (A) computerized tomography of the head with contrast demonstrating right periorbital edema. (B) Magnetic resonance imaging with and without contrast of the head demonstrating edema of the right parotid gland, muscles of mastication, and buccal space.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) obtained on the following day revealed continued extensive edema of the right parotid gland, muscles of mastication, and buccal space (Figure 1B). The intra‐orbital edema and proptosis resolved, and cavernous sinus thrombosis was excluded. Multiple rheumatologic, immunologic, and infectious studies were sent, including, but not limited to, evaluations for hereditary angioedema, systemic lupus erythematosus, Epstein–Barr virus, Sjogren's syndrome, myositis, tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, and vasculitis. Given the constellation of inflammatory symptoms, a paraneoplastic process and/or a primary oncologic process was also considered.

Investigations for iron‐deficiency revealed a positive fecal occult blood and a fecal calprotectin of 3730 mcg/g. By the second day of admission, she was reported to have frankly bloody stools, leading to gastroenterology consultation. On admission, she had reported mild fecal urgency, but no diarrhea, blood in stools, vomiting or blood in vomit, ulcers, or rashes. Her mother had UC, reported to be in remission. Further history revealed a small three‐pound weight loss in the 2 weeks leading up to presentation. Stool studies were negative for infectious etiology, including Clostridium difficile. An endoscopy and colonoscopy were performed with concomitant salivary gland biopsy. Colonoscopy showed normal terminal ileum, but diffuse colitis. The colonic biopsies demonstrated chronic active colitis with cryptitis, distortion of glandular architecture, and crypt abscesses (Figures 2 and 3). Patient subsequently confirmed a history of intermittent bloody stools in the 2 weeks preceding admission.

Intraoperative colonoscopy image of the transverse colon demonstrating diffuse colitis.

Colonic biopsy specimen demonstrating chronic architectural changes and crypt abscess with gland destruction.

The salivary gland biopsy was negative for inflammatory and neoplastic processes, and the patient was transferred to their primary healthcare facility. She was started on oral prednisolone with plan for biologics to be started by their primary facility. A 1‐year follow‐up from outside facility revealed that the family refused treatment with biologics but agreed to mesalamine 3.6 g orally daily. The EIM remained completely resolved. Latest lab studies indicate hemoglobin of 8.8 g/dL, ESR 34 mm/h, CRP < 3 mg/dL, and fecal calprotectin 99 mcg/g. Normal upper endoscopy and biopsy, and focal colitis in the sigmoid colon, but otherwise normal biopsies from the terminal ileum and the rest of the colon from colonoscopy.

DISCUSSION

The immunologic mechanisms underlying the EIM of UC are not well understood. Many EIMs correlate with disease severity, which would suggest a systemic immunologic reaction to a common antigen. 2 This may be a cross‐reactive peptide that is found in the colon, biliary tract, skin, ciliary epithelial cells of the eye, and joints or alterations in transmembrane glycoproteins in the gut and polarization of cluster of differentiation type 4 T cell lymphocytes. 3 , 4 While the EIMs of UC are found in multiple organ systems, it is unusual for a combination to occur in the pediatric age group with new onset UC as the primary clinical presentation.

Ocular pathologies secondary to UC are rare, occurring in less than 10% of all cases of all IBD. 5 In addition, ocular EIMs of IBD are more associated with Crohn's disease than with UC. 5 This child presented with myositis of extraocular and facial muscles seen on imaging. While myositis can be seen accompanying IBD, there are few case reports, mostly with Crohn's disease, 6 , 7 , 8 and none in the pediatric population.

The unilateral parotid gland inflammation seen in this child is a very rare EIM of UC. Only three previous case reports have noted sialadenitis with UC, and the salivary glands are not commonly thought of as a site of involvement. 9 , 10 , 11 Interestingly, this child only had clinical and imaging findings consistent with parotitis, and minor salivary gland biopsy demonstrated normal pathology (her primary site in the right parotid gland was not biopsied). Given sialadenitis is also seen with Type II autoimmune pancreatitis in addition to UC, this may be an avenue of further investigation into the cellular mechanisms underlying EIM of IBD. 9 , 10 The ocular facial myositis presenting as EIM responded well to mesalamine and prednisone, supporting the link between active bowel inflammation and the EIM.

CONCLUSION

Our case emphasizes that UC may present with symptoms in almost all organ systems. Therefore, when presented with a child with unexplained inflammation, it is important to keep IBD on the differential even in the absence of clear/obvious gastrointestinal symptoms.
