Genome-wide characterization, evolutionary and expression pattern analysis of the sHSP family genes in orchardgrass and functional characterization under abiotic stress
Authors: Dan Luo, Jie Zhu, Yuchen Yang, Bingchao Wu, Huan Zhang, Xinyi Wan, Haidong Yan, Chunli Mao, Xiaoshan Wang, Guangyan Feng, Linkai Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrchardgrass is a vital forage crop worldwide, yet its growth and yield are highly susceptible to environmental stress. Small Heat Shock Proteins (sHSPs) family is a wide protein family crucial for enhancing plant stress tolerance. Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of the sHSP family in orchardgrass is lacking, particularly concerning its response to abiotic challenges. In the present study, we identified 47 sHSP family members in orchardgrass and provided a comprehensive analysis of their characteristics. Syntenic analysis revealed that the expansion of DgsHSP is primarily driven by tandem duplication events. Multiple abiotic challenges were shown to stimulate certain DgsHSP genes, suggesting they play a key regulatory role in how orchardgrass responds to these stresses. Heterologous expression in yeast demonstrated that DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 improve tolerance to drought and salt stress. Additionally, physiological indicators and phenotypes under stress in Arabidopsis mutants also support this viewpoint. These findings lay the groundwork for understanding the functional role of the DgsHSP family in orchardgrass under environmental stress, offering potential applications for enhancing forages and crops through breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07977-7.Keywords: SHSP family, Orchardgrass, Dactylis glomerata, Expression patterns, Abiotic stress

Introduction

Plants are unable to move, thereby they must deal with abiotic challenges including drought, salt, and extreme temperatures. These stresses severely limit plant spread, affect growth and development, and impair productivity in agriculture [1–3]. Improving crop yield and tolerance to abiotic stress is crucial for addressing food security challenges. Grasses are not only integral to the planet’s ecosystems but also offer an invaluable genetic foundation for crop development, which is instrumental in bolstering global food security and reducing poverty [4–6]. Moreover, grasses provide critical ecosystem services, including enhancing soil health, improving water retention, sequestering carbon, and mitigating soil erosion and nitrogen leaching [7]. However, as they are frequently grown in challenging conditions subject to various abiotic stresses [8, 9], a significant knowledge gap exists regarding the molecular basis of their stress tolerance.

Small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) are widely found in archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes. Plants, like other species, sHSPs can be upregulated under stress and function as molecular chaperones [10–12]. The induction of sHSPs is a general stress response, triggered not only by high temperatures but also by various other abiotic stresses, including salinity, drought, cold, and heavy metal stress [13–16], implying a function in plant response to a wide range of abiotic stresses. Moreover, their contribution to various aspects of plant development has also been reported [17, 18]. For instance, the cytosolic class II HSP17.2 enhances cold and salt stress resistance in transgenic Arabidopsis [15]. Moreover, in rice, OsMSR3 is a member of the class I sHSP family. The expression of the OsMSR3 gene could substantially improve the adaptability of Arabidopsis to cadmium and Cu stressors [19, 20]. Although the sHSP gene family is well-characterized in model species and primary crops under various abiotic stresses, its functional significance in orchardgrass (Dactylis glomerata L.) remains poorly understood.

Orchardgrass is a globally significant cool-season forage grass and a member of the Pooideae subfamily (Poaceae) [21]. Originally native to Eurasia and northern Africa, it has become naturalized on practically all continents, serving primarily as pasture or hay [22, 23]. Orchardgrass is among the four most commercially significant perennial forage grass globally, vital for producing forage-based meat and dairy in temperate areas due to its high biomass yield, shade tolerance, and adaptation to abiotic stress [24–26]. Currently, numerous valuable genes linked to orchardgrass development and responses to stress have been discovered [27–30]. While sHSP genes are known to play crucial roles in both plant development and stress responses, their functions in orchardgrass have been largely overlooked. Therefore, a systematic identification and functional characterization of the DgsHSP family is a critical first step towards the genetic improvement of this vital forage crop, particularly for enhancing its stress resilience.

This research aimed to understand the role of sHSPs in the adaptability of orchardgrass by identifying 47 sHSP genes in its genome. Detailed analyses were conducted on gene structures, motif patterns, duplication events, and phylogeny, and expression profiles were examined across different tissues and abiotic conditions. Moreover, to further comprehend the functions of DgsHSP genes, their expression patterns were analyzed under abiotic stresses. Furthermore, functional assays were conducted to validate the roles of candidate genes in conferring tolerance to salt and drought stresses. These findings will enhance our understanding of DgsHSP gene functions under abiotic stress, with potential applications in breeding programs for forages and crops.

Materials and methods

Identification of DgsHSP gene family

The Hidden Markov model (HMM) file for the sHSP domain (PF000110) was obtained from Pfam database to identify DgsHSP gene family members, while orchardgrass genome data were retrieved from orchardgrass database [21]. To ascertain the reliability of the sHSP sequences, CDD Search and the Pfam database were utilized to confirm if they included conserved domains. The ExPASy Server was applied to estimate the isoelectric points (pI) and molecular weights (Mw) of sHSP proteins.

Phylogenetic analysis and categorization of the DgsHSP gene family

Ensembl provided the sHSP protein sequences for Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. (At), Oryza sativa L. (Os), and Glycine max. The phylogenetic tree was created by MEGA (version 7.0) and the maximum likelihood (ML) approach, utilizing sHSP proteins from orchardgrass, Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, and Glycine max. The analysis was performed with the Poisson model and 1000 bootstrap replicates for statistical reliability.

Gene structure and motif analysis of DgsHSP genes

The exon–intron structures of DgsHSP genes were studied using the gene structure display server [31]. MEME was applied to find conserved motifs in DgsHSP proteins, with a motif count of 15 and other default parameters [32]. DgsHSP gene function motifs were visualized using the TBtools software.

Identification of cis-acting elements in promoters

The 1500 bp upstream sequence (excluding the ATG sequence) of the sHSP genes from orchardgrass was extracted and uploaded to the PlantCARE online tool for the prediction of cis-regulatory elements. Finally, stress-related elements were screened and visualized using the software TBtools.

Chromosomal mapping, gene duplication events, and syntenic comparisons with different plant species

The DgsHSP genes were mapped to their appropriate chromosomes by MapGene2Chrome [33]. MCScanX (with default settings) was employed to detect duplication events of sHSP genes in orchardgrass. Syntenic relationships between orchardgrass and ten other species (Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. (At), Glycine max (L.) Merr. (Gm), Medicago truncatula Gaertn. (Mt), Brassica rapa L. (Br), Brachypodium distachyon (L.) P. Beauv. (Bd), Hordeum vulgare L. (Hv), Oryza sativa L. (Os), Triticum aestivum L. (Ta), Zea mays L. (Zm), and Setaria viridis (L.) P. Beauv. (Sv)) were also analyzed by TBtools [34]. The websites and their links used in the above analysis were shown in Table S1.

Plant growth and stress conditions

The selection of stress treatment materials is the orchardgrasss variety ‘Baoxing’. The seeds of ‘Baoxing’ were grown in pots containing sterilized quartz and ddH2O under constant conditions in a growth chamber (22/20 °C, 14/10 h, day/night) for seven days before being grown in Hoagland's solution. PEG-6000, as a penetration mimic agent, simulated arid environments very well [35], so we used PEG to simulate arid environments in the experiment. When orchardgrass seedlings grow to 3–4 leaves, seedlings with consistent growth are selected to be subjected to drought stress (root soaking with 20% PEG-6000 solution), salt stress (root soaking with 250 mmol/L NaCl solution), and heat stress (40℃/35℃, day and night). Samples were collected from each treatment at different periods (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h), using 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution as the control. For each treatment, three biological replicates were frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C. The seeds of 'Baoxing' were not collected from the wild and are only stored as experimental materials at the College of Grassland Science and Technology, Sichuan Agricultural University.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and analysis of gene expression

Total RNA was extracted with the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, China). First-strand cDNA was generated using PrimeScript RTase (TaKaRa, China). qRT-PCR was conducted on a BioRAD CFX Connect using SYBR Green Real-time PCR Master Mix (Monad, China). The qRT-PCR reaction volume was listed in Table S2. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the reference gene, and relative expression levels were determined using the 2−△△Ct method [36]. Three technical replicates were conducted for each biological replicate. Previously published RNA-seq data for diverse orchardgrass tissues (SRR5341102 and SRP131899) [21], as well as those affected by heat (SRP049315) [30] and drought (SRP158919) [29], were obtained.

Functional analysis of DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae

MonClone™ Hi-Fusion Cloning Mix (Monad, China) was used to insert the full-length coding sequences of DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 into the pYES2 vector at HindIII and XbaI restriction sites. The INVSc1 yeast strain (Weidi, China), designed for recombinant protein expression, was transformed with pYES2-DgsHSP30, pYES2-DgsHSP31, and empty pYES2 plasmids according to the provided protocols. Transformed yeast cells were cultured on selective SD/-Ura medium at 28 °C for 48 h. Individual colonies were then selected, and the correct insertion of the target gene was verified by colony PCR. To assess the ability of the DgsHSP genes to confer tolerance to osmotic (sorbitol) and salt (NaCl) stress, confirmed transformants were grown in liquid SD/-Ura medium at 28 °C until the cultures reached OD600 ≈ 1.2. The yeast culture was then serially diluted (10−1, 10−2, 10−3, and 10−4) with medium, and 3 μL of each dilution was applied to SD-Ura plates with either 2.0 mol/L sorbitol or 1.0 mol/L NaCl. Then incubated them at 28 °C for five days before being photographed. Table S3 listed all the primers utilized in this research.

The performance of Arabidopsis mutants under stress treatments

Functional verification was performed using Arabidopsis T-DNA mutants. For NaCl and PEG treatments, sterilized wild-type (WT) and T-DNA mutant seedlings germinated in 1/2 Murashige and Skoog medium for 10 days before being transferred to sterilized soil. Three weeks after transplanting, WT and mutants with consistent growth were chosen and divided into three groups: a control group that was watered with normal 1/2 Hoagland's nutrient solution, the drought stress group, and the salt stress group were watered with 1/2 Hoagland's nutrient solution containing 20% PEG-6000 and 150 mM NaCl, respectively. The phenotypes were observed, photographed, and sampled for physiological index measurements. 0.1 g of entire seedlings were taken from the control, salt stress, and drought stress groups in three biological replicates each. After treatment, samples are taken and frozen in liquid nitrogen for physiological index determination. Arabidopsis mutant seeds (At5g59720: SALK_019176C and At1g53540: SALK_056782C, respectively) purchased from Arashare (www.arashare.cn).

Measurement of the relative electrical conductivity (REC)

Leaves (0.1 g) were wrapped in gauze and put in a tube containing 20 mL of water. The initial electrical conductivity (S1) was measured after 12 h. The tube was then boiled for 15 min, cooled to room temperature, and the second electrical conductivity (S2) was measured. The percentage (%) of S1 to S2 was calculated.

Measurement of malondialdehyde content (MDA)

The thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method [37] was applied to determine MDA content. Briefly, the crude enzyme solution was prepared using roughly 0.1 g of leaves. A 0.5 mL aliquot of the enzyme solution was combined with 1 mL of 20% trichloroacetic acid containing 0.5% TBA, and then boiled at 95 ℃ for 15 min. Following quick cooling, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The MDA content was determined by using a spectrophotometer to measure absorbance at 532 and 600 nm. The protein concentration in the crude enzyme solution was determined using a protein standard curve. MDA content was reported as unit mg−1 protein.

Measurement of the relative water content (RWC)

RWC (%) was measured using the saturation weighing method [38]. Fresh leaf weight was recorded, and leaves were submerged in water for 12 h. By removing surface moisture, the saturated fresh weight was measured. Then packed the leaves in kraft paper bags, dried at 105 °C for 30 min, and subsequently dried at 65 °C until a constant weight was reached.

Results

Genome-wide identification of sHSP family members in orchardgrass

Identification analysis was conducted using HMMER 3.0 and SMART software, filtering out sequences without a typical ACD domain. Eventually, 47 DgsHSP proteins were found in orchardgrass and designated after their chromosomal positions. The amino acid (aa) length of DgsHSP proteins varies significantly, ranging from 128 (DgsHSP18) to 791 (DgsHSP47) amino acids. The Mw ranges from 14.4 (DgsHSP18) to 89.2 kDa (DgsHSP47), and the predicted theoretical pI range from 4.01 (DgsHSP20) to 10.48 (DgsHSP44). Data on the gene names, gene IDs, chromosomal positions, number of exons, amino acid count, Mw, and theoretical pI of DgsHSP proteins were listed in Table S4.

Phylogenetic investigation and classifying of the DgsHSPs

To evaluate the evolutionary connections among DgsHSP genes, sHSP genes from Glycine max, Oryza sativa, orchardgrass, and Arabidopsis were utilized to create a phylogenetic tree using MEGA 7.0 software (Fig. 1). The 47 sHSP gene family members in orchardgrass were divided into 10 groups and an orphan gene DgsHSP20. Among them, 31 orchardgrass sHSP proteins were clustered into 5 cytoplasmic groups (CI to CVI), which may indicate that most orchardgrass sHSP proteins play their functions in the cytoplasm. It is worth noting that orchardgrass lacks members within the CIV and CVII group. And previous studies have reported that the cytoplasmic CIV group sHSP proteins are only present in dicotyledonous plants, which is consistent with our study and may indicate that the cytoplasmic CIV group sHSP protein is unique to dicotyledonous plants.

The evolutionary relationship of sHSP derived from orchardgrass, rice, soybean, and Arabidopsis

Conserved motif and gene structural studies of DgsHSP genes

To determine the diversity and structural similarity of DgsHSP proteins, the MEME program was utilized to find 15 distinct conserved motifs. Motif 3, motif 6, and motif 8 were often combined and distributed in the CI group, motif 11 and motif 12 were only distributed in the P group, motif 9 and motif 15 were only distributed in the CV group, motif 13 was only distributed in the CⅡ group, and motif 14 was only distributed in the CⅢ group (Figs. 1 and 2). Members of the same group had the same or similar motif composition, and the distribution of motifs was roughly the same, indicating their high conservatism. The exon–intron compositions of the sHSP family in orchardgrass were examined to comprehend their gene-structural changes. Based on the number of introns, 47 sHSP genes could be divided into three categories: no intron genes, 1–2 intron genes, and multiple intron genes (Fig. 2). Among them, there were a total of 29 genes without introns, accounting for more than half (61.70%) of all gene family members. The sHSP genes with introns were mainly distributed in the CⅠ and P groups, with 4 genes (8.51%) having only one intron and 14 genes (29.79%) having two introns (Figs. 1 and 2). This structural pattern of genes may be related to evolutionary trends and indirectly reflects the conservatism of the orchardgrass sHSP gene family evolution. Analyzing the distribution of introns in different groups of orchardgrass, it was found that members of the same group have similar exon–intron structures, which resembled the motif distribution pattern.

Analysis of cis-acting elements of the DgsHSP genes

The promoter sequences upstream 1500 bp from the transcription start sites of the 47 DgsHSP gene family members were evaluated by PlantCARE (Fig. 3). According to their functions, the cis-acting elements of the DgsHSP genes could be roughly divided into three categories: light-responsive elements, plant growth and development related elements, and stress-responsive elements. Notably, stress-responsive cis-acting elements appeared frequently, including those related to defense and stress, dehydration, low-temp, and salt stress, low-temperature stress, drought stress, abscisic acid, and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) response elements. All DgsHSP genes had at least one stress-related cis-acting element, demonstrating that the DgsHSP gene family is significant for orchardgrass response to abiotic stress.

Chromosomal distribution and duplication events analysis

To determine the chromosomal distribution of the DgsHSP genes, we used MG2C to map them into the orchardgrass genome. Among the predicted 47 DgsHSP genes, 45 were mapped to seven chromosomes, while 2 genes were located on scaffolds. Most DgsHSP genes are primarily located on four chromosomes: chromosome 2 (14 genes), chromosome 5 (9 genes), chromosome 6 (7 genes), and chromosome 4 (6 genes). Meanwhile, chromosome 7 had the least DgsHSP genes (Fig. 4).

The chromosomal locations of orchardgrass of DgsHSP. The orange shadows indicate tandem duplications of 6 DgsHSP gene pairs

Duplication events were identified to more fully comprehend the expansion and evolution of DgsHSP genes. Six pairs of DgsHSP genes revealed tandem duplicates; three pairs on chromosome 5, two on chromosome 6, and one on chromosome 2 (Fig. 4, Table S5). In addition, three pairs of segmental duplicated genes were identified in the DgsHSP family (Fig. 5, Table S5). The results suggested that DgsHSP genes likely expanded through tandem and segmental duplication events, with tandem duplication serving as the predominant driving force.

Schematic depictions illustrate the interchromosomal relationships of DgsHSP genes. Red lines highlight segmental duplications of DgsHSP gene pairs

Syntenic comparison of sHSP genes in orchardgrass and ten representative plants

To clarify the possible evolutionary insights of DgsHSP genes, comparative syntenic maps were created for ten representative species (Fig. 6), including four dicots and six monocots. The study identified 73 homologous pairs between orchardgrass and Triticum aestivum, followed by Setaria viridis (30), Brachypodium distachyon (27), Zea mays (32), Oryza sativa (28), and Hordeum vulgare (20). In contrast, fewer than ten syntenic pairs were identified between orchardgrass and dicots, specifically 8, 4, 3, and 2 pairs for Glycine max, Medicago truncatula, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Brassica rapa, respectively. Compared to dicots, monocots have more syntenic pairs with orchardgrass, suggesting a closer evolutionary relationship with monocots.

Tissue-specific expression patterns of DgsHSP genes

RNA-seq data from various tissues were used to evaluate the tissue-specific expression of DgsHSP genes and their possible contributions to growth and development (Fig. 7). Among the 47 DgsHSP genes, most DgsHSP genes exhibited tissue-specific differential expression, which may indicate that DgsHSP genes play various roles and have differentiated functions during the growth and development of orchardgrass. All DgsHSP genes were expressed in at least one tissue, with 33.34% of the genes showing high expression in at least one tissue. The expression levels of 21.28% of the DgsHSP genes were significantly higher in root organs than in other tissues, and 17.02% had higher expression levels in spike organs compared to other tissues, with only the DgsHSP3 gene showing higher expression in leaves compared to other tissues. These findings indicate that sHSP genes may play a crucial role in tissue formation.

Expression patterns of DgsHSP genes in different tissues

DgsHSP genes expression are induced by multiple abiotic stresses

To examine the probable role of DgsHSPs under diverse conditions, including NaCl, drought, and heat stresses, sHSP gene expression patterns were identified. Transcript abundance under drought and heat conditions was examined using previously published RNA-seq datasets, while qRT-PCR was used for other treatments. The findings indicated differential expression of DgsHSP genes during heat and drought stresses (Fig. 8). After heat treatment for 10 days, the heat-resistant material ‘Baoxing’ had 13 sHSP genes with upregulated expression, and three genes with downregulated expression. After 26 days of heat treatment, except for DgsHSP28, the previously upregulated twelve DgsHSP genes continued to show upregulated expression, and four genes were downregulated. The heat-sensitive material ‘01998’, after 10 and 26 days of heat treatment, consistently had nine sHSP genes upregulated and four downregulated. Additionally, the DgsHSP30 gene, which had no detectable expression before heat stress, showed increased expression levels after 10 and 26 days of heat treatment. Notably, the DgsHSP13 and DgsHSP35 genes were significantly upregulated after heat stress treatment (Fig. 8a). Under drought stress for 18 days, the expression profiles of DgsHSPs in the leaves and roots of orchardgrass are shown in Fig. 8b. After the drought stress treatment, 15 DgsHSP genes were downregulated and 5 DgsHSP genes were upregulated in the leaves. While in the roots, 10 DgsHSP genes were downregulated, and 12 DgsHSP genes were upregulated. It is worth mentioning that before the drought stress treatment, the DgsHSP31 gene had the highest expression in the roots, but after stress treatment, it was almost not expressed in both tissues.

qRT-PCR expression analysis of DgsHSP genes

To investigate the role of DgsHSP genes in stress responses, eight genes were chosen for expression analysis during heat, drought, and salt stress at different time points using qRT-PCR (Fig. 9). The results showed that under stress conditions, the transcription levels of each gene vary at different treatments or time points, and qRT-PCR analysis showed that most genes mainly responded in the early stages of stress. DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 were highly expressed under salt stress for 12 h and significantly induced expression under drought stress for 24 h. Moreover, these two genes showed upregulation at least one time point under three abiotic stresses. DgsHSP8 was significantly downregulated at various stages after drought and salt stress treatment. DgsHSP13 and DgsHSP35 were significantly upregulated after heat treatment and were increased considerably in the early stages of stress (2–12 h). The expression level of DgsHSP3 was 126.2-fold higher than that of CK treatment after 24 h of heat treatment, and it was significantly upregulated in the late stage of salt stress.

Heterologous expression assays in INVScI

Given that DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 show rapid and robust upregulation in leaves during the early stages of drought and salt stress. Therefore, they were selected for heterologous expression detection in yeast to explore their potential role in stress tolerance. Following sorbitol or NaCl treatment in the SD-Ura medium, DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 overexpression strains showed better growth compared to the empty vector (pYES2). Our research showed that overexpression of DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 significantly improved the resistance of INVScI yeast to sorbitol and NaCl (Fig. 10). These results indicated that DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 improved the tolerance of yeast cell to osmotic and salt stresses.

Function verification on recombinant yeasts

DgsHSP genes reduced the resistance of Arabidopsis mutants to abiotic stress

Three-week-old WT plants and mutants were transferred to nutrient solutions containing NaCl and PEG-6000 for stress treatments. Compared to wild-type plants, the athsp30 and athsp31 mutants exhibited greater sensitivity to stress. Over the treatment period, they developed more severe leaf wrinkling and displayed more extensive chlorosis (leaf yellowing). The growth condition of each line in the non-stressed control group remained normal (Fig. 11). In addition, under PEG and NaCl treatment, the REC and MDA of athsp30 and atshsp31 increased with treatment time, reaching their highest values at the last time point and consistently higher than WT. While RWC decreased with treatment time, and the mutants showed the most significant decrease (P < 0.001) compared to WT after 12 days of salt stress. It was worth noting that a significant reduction (P < 0.001) in RWC of the mutants were observed after 6 days of drought treatment (Fig. 12). The comprehensive phenotype and physiological indicators indicate that DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 may play a positive role in regulating resistance to PEG-induced drought and NaCl stress.

Discussion

Orchardgrass is an important perennial forage grass globally and is widely used in developing grass-based agriculture on marginal land [39]. Enhancing its stress tolerance is therefore crucial. The sHSP gene family, which contributes to plant growth, development, and environmental stress, might be a valuable resource for improving yield through variety enhancement [40–42]. The sHSPs have been discovered in many species, such as Arabidopsis [43], barely [44], rice [45], wheat [46], switchgrass [47], and soybean [48]. Nonetheless, little is known about the sHSP gene family in orchardgrass.

The genome-wide analysis identified 47 sHSP genes in orchardgrass, which were divided into five groups. Gene structure, motif, and alignment analysis revealed both conservation and variety throughout evolution. Tandem duplication, and whole genome duplication events have played critical roles in expanding the gene family and diversifying gene functions, enabling better adaptation to environmental changes [49]. Notably, six pairs of DgsHSP genes were found to be tandem duplicates and three pairs as segmental duplications, indicating that tandem duplication events primarily contributed to the expansion and diversity of the DgsHSP family. This pattern suggests that the DgsHSP gene family has undergone adaptive evolution under strong environmental pressure. The retention of these tandem clusters likely provides a specific evolutionary advantage, allowing orchardgrass to rapidly generate high levels of sHSP chaperones to prevent protein aggregation under harsh conditions. Changes in gene structure and organization have significance for the development and evolution of a gene family [50], so we looked into their gene structure further. It was found that 43 (91.49%) of the sHSP genes in orchardgrass had only 0–1 introns, which is like previous studies. The absence of introns or the presence of very few introns is an important feature of the sHSP gene. Research has shown that 97.14% and 83.33% of the sHSP genes in pepper [51] and tomato [52], respectively, have no or only one short intron, while 64% of sHSP genes in soybean [53], and 74% of sHSP genes in rice all have no introns [54]. Although previous studies on the relationship between the number of introns and gene expression levels are uncertain [55], there are still some studies suggesting that the number of introns negatively regulates gene expression, and genes with lower intron density may respond quickly to stress [56–58]. Therefore, in the long-term evolution of plants, genes with lower intron density may be more inclined to be selected by stressful environments [59]. This may be the reason why sHSP family members maintain protein homeostasis, respond quickly to adverse conditions such as high temperature and salt stress, and cause protein aggregation to form the first line of defense [60]. Several cis-acting elements were discovered concurrently, with light-responsive elements constituting the most prevalent. The presence of particular elements in DgsHSP family members highlights their specialized functions. Notably, the promoter region of each DgsHSP had at least one stress-responsive element, such as those related to defense, dehydration, low-temperature, salt stress, and abscisic acid response, suggesting that the DgsHSP gene family may play a significant role in abiotic stress response. In brief, the investigations of the DgsHSP family revealed the participation of sHSP genes in multiple regulatory processes, along with their conservation and diversity in orchardgrass.

Gene expression levels could offer valuable insights into gene function research. RNA-seq results revealed tissue-specific expression profiles, most DgsHSP genes exhibiting low transcript abundance in leaves. Our study showed that a considerable proportion (21.28%) of DgsHSP genes, such as DgsHSP31, were highly expressed in roots. We hypothesize that this high basal expression level is crucial for maintaining protein proteostasis, supporting the correct folding and stability of proteins essential for root cell division, differentiation, and transport functions under normal growth conditions. This is consistent with previous findings where overexpression of AtsHSP22 promotes increased lateral root number [61]. This may indicate that specific DgsHSP genes play a part in orchardgrass growth and development, each playing distinct roles and mechanisms, leading to functional diversity. Growing evidence suggests that sHSP genes have a role in abiotic stress responses, as seen in rice [62], soybean [63], grape [64], and wheat [65]. Overexpression of maize (Zea mays) HSP16.9 in tobacco enhances heat and oxidative resistance [66]. Similarly, overexpression of HSP20s in Arabidopsis increased antioxidant enzyme levels, leading to higher tolerance to heat, salt, osmotic, and oxidative stress [67–70]. Interestingly, we observed a divergence in the expression pattern of DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 between short-term and long-term stress conditions. qRT-PCR analysis showed a rapid and robust upregulation of these two genes during the early stages of stress (e.g., within the first few hours), whereas RNA-seq data from long-term stressed tissues indicated a suppression of expression. This pattern suggests that DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 functions primarily as a transient, early-response chaperone. Orchardgrass is more tolerant to abiotic stress compared to other perennial forage grasses. Nevertheless, the role of DgsHSP genes under abiotic stress is not fully understood. In the present investigation, most DgsHSP gene expression patterns showed variable responses to diverse abiotic stimuli at the transcriptional level, showing the functional variety of DgsHSP proteins.

Emerging evidence indicates that small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) are not confined to the thermotolerance response but are regulators of plant adaptation to various environmental cues. For instance, overexpression of PtHSP17.8 in Arabidopsis was shown to enhance tolerance to both heat and salt stress by maintaining ROS homeostasis and coordinating the expression of other stress-related genes [71]. In addition, the constitutive expression of GhHSP24.7 in cotton was found to impair drought and heat tolerance by interfering with stomatal closure, a process linked to its interaction with mitochondrial ROS and cellular ATP levels [72]. Furthermore, our study provides compelling evidence for the crucial roles of DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 in abiotic stress responses. Initially, heterologous overexpression of these genes in yeast conferred enhanced tolerance to simulated drought and salt conditions. More definitive insights were obtained from the characterization of athsp30/athsp31. Under both salt and drought stress, the mutants exhibited significantly higher REC compared to the WT, indicative of compromised membrane integrity (Figs. 11 and 12). This was accompanied by elevated levels of MDA, a marker for lipid peroxidation, and a drastically reduced RWC. These physiological data collectively demonstrate that the absence of DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 disrupts membrane protein homeostasis under stress, leading to severe membrane damage and uncontrolled water loss, which culminates in hypersensitivity to both drought and salt. Therefore, our findings establish DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 as positive regulators of drought and salt tolerance.

Conclusion

Briefly, 47 sHSP genes in orchardgrass were discovered and studied using bioinformatics, demonstrating their conservation and diversity. Phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal locations, duplication events, conserved motifs, gene structures, and cis-acting elements of DgsHSP genes were examined. Furthermore, expression patterns of DgsHSP genes showed that some were regulated by stressors such as drought and salt, indicating that DgsHSP genes have significance for orchardgrass stress tolerance. Due to early induction of DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 expression by drought and salt treatments, their functions were evaluated through overexpression in yeast. Furthermore, the Arabidopsis mutants of these two genes were also used to verify their functions. Finally, DgsHSP30 and DgsHSP31 were found to positively regulate responses to drought and salt stresses. In conclusion, this research provides a solid foundation for further investigation into the evolution and biological functions of the sHSP family in orchardgrass.
