MRI cytometry imaging for cervical cancer differential diagnosis: a preliminary study
Authors: Zhi-Lin Yuan, Di-Wei Shi, Hui Guan, Fan Liu, Zong-Shu Wang, Shang-Ying Yang, Xin Gao, Thorsten Feiweier, Jin-Xia Zhu, Zheng-Yu Jin, Jun-Zhong Xu, Yuan Li, Hua-Dan Xue, Yong-Lan He, Hua Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrecise noninvasive detection and differentiation of pathological subtypes in cervical cancer remains challenging. Diffusion MRI (dMRI)-based cytometry, an imaging technique quantifying tumor microenvironments, shows diagnostic potential but requires clinical validation.Methods74 patients with cervical cancer and 44 healthy volunteers underwent diffusion-weighted imaging using oscillating gradient spin-echo (OGSE) and pulsed gradient spin-echo (PGSE) sequences at 3T. Three radiologists independently scored image quality on a 5-point scale. Time-dependent apparent diffusion coefficients (ADCs) as well as information from ‘imaging microstructural parameters using limited spectrally edited diffusion’ (IMPULSED) alone or incorporating transcytolemmal water exchange (JOINT) were used to distinguish cancerous from normal tissues and identify tumor subtypes. The microstructural parameters included intracellular volume fraction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{in}$$\end{document}), cell diameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document}), extracellular diffusivity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{ex}$$\end{document}), and water exchange rate constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{in}$$\end{document}). Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks fourth in both prevalence and mortality among women worldwide; an estimated 661,021 new diagnoses and 348,189 deaths from this disease were reported globally in 2022 [1, 2]. Treatment decisions are primarily based on the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics staging (FIGO); surgery is often recommended for early-stage patients while radiotherapy and chemotherapy are used for those in advanced stages. Pathological type is an important prognostic factor of cervical cancer patients. The differential lymph node metastasis rates, combined with their established disparities in response to chemoradiotherapy, collectively indicate distinct disease biology and prognosis between squamous and non-squamous cervical carcinomas [3–5]. Therefore, the identification of pathological subtypes is crucial to select the proper therapeutic strategy [6]. Current guidelines recommend pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for cervical cancer diagnosis, staging, pre-treatment assessment, and follow-up [7]. However, the identification of pathological subtypes still requires invasive pathological biopsies, as traditional imaging tools showed limited sensitivity [8]. Diffusion MRI (dMRI), which can reflect microenvironment within tumors by measuring water diffusion, has been commonly used in cervical cancer diagnosis; the dMRI-derived metric, apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), also plays a key role in clinical imaging studies [9, 10].

However, the ADC value represents the average diffusivity within the imaging voxel, which can be influenced by multiple pathological and microstructural features, such as cell density, intra- and extra-cellular diffusion coefficients, water exchange between different compartments, as well as edema and necrosis. Some of such features exhibit opposing effects on ADC measurements [11], resulting in the insensitivity of this average diffusivity to some pathological and physiological changes in tumors. Therefore, the conventional ADC metrics demonstrate only limited reliability in terms of distinguishing cervical cancer from normal tissues and distinguishing tumor pathological subtypes [12, 13]. To address this limitation, some quantitative imaging methods have been developed to incorporate different diffusion times (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{diff}$$\end{document}) and diffusion weightings (b-value) into signal acquisitions, allowing the decoupling extraction of microstructural parameters, like intracellular volume fraction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{in}$$\end{document}), cell diameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document}), and extracellular diffusivity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{ex}$$\end{document}), from the biophysical models. These imaging metrics have clear physical significance, and they can directly reflect specific changes in microstructures, potentially providing enhanced diagnostic efficacy in clinics. Such dMRI-based microstructural imaging technology, i.e., MRI cytometry, including IMPULSED (imaging microstructural parameters using limited spectrally edited diffusion) [14, 15], VERDICT (vascular, extracellular and restricted diffusion for cytometry in tumors) [16], and JOINT (a ‘joint’ method where the transcytolemmal water exchange is incorporated into IMPULSED), has shown promising clinical potential in solid tumor studies [14], including prostate [17], breast [18–20], pediatric glioma [21], thyroid [22], and endometrial cancers [23, 24]. The involved clinical tasks include preoperative tumor diagnosis [25], risk stratification [23], and evaluation of treatment efficacy [26, 27]. However, the clinical value of MRI cytometry remains unclear in cervical cancer studies, i.e., whether it can improve the diagnosis or predict prognosis, thereby supporting clinical decision-making.

Therefore, this study is the first to investigate the diagnostic efficacy of MRI cytometry in cervical cancer. We hypothesize that MRI cytometry provides quantitative parameters that effectively distinguish cervical cancer from normal tissue and further differentiate its pathological subtypes. To this end, we quantified its tumor detection capability and evaluated its discernment for pathological subtyping. The derived microstructural parameters were used for these analyses.

Materials and methods

Patients

This prospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and/or regulations including the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) availability of preoperative pelvic MRI and complete clinicopathological data, (ii) no prior treatment, and (iii) a visible tumor on MRI. Patients were excluded if there was an obvious mismatch in the positions of the diffusion-weighted sequences, i.e., obvious misalignment between slices of different diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences that resulted in inaccurate model fitting. The priori sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2; Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two-tailed), a statistical power (1-β) of 0.8, using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, the analysis indicated that a total of 42 participants were required. Ultimately, this study enrolled 74 consecutive women with pathologically confirmed cervical cancer at our hospital who were treated between December 2023 and January 2025; 44 healthy volunteers were also enrolled. The exclusion criteria of the healthy volunteers were as follows: absence of malignant cervical lesions. A flowchart of participants is shown on Fig. 1. Comprehensive clinical characteristics and pathological information, including age and pathological type, were extracted from the electronic medical records at our institution and thoroughly reviewed. Patients were categorized into squamous cell and non-squamous cell carcinoma groups based on biopsy data; the latter included those with adenocarcinoma and adenosquamous carcinoma.

MRI acquisition

All scans were performed with the patients in the supine position using a 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with whole-body gradients and a phased-array body coil. The acquisition protocol was similar to that of a previous study that used both OGSE and PGSE sequences [15]. The OGSE data were acquired at oscillating frequencies of 40 Hz and 20 Hz. The positioning parameters were consistently maintained across all three sequences to ensure their proper registration. A detailed overview of the MRI parameters is shown in Table 1; additionally, the repetition time/echo time was 5100/127 ms, field of view was 300 × 224 mm2, in-plane resolution was 2 × 2 mm2, slice thickness was 4 mm, number of slices was 20, and acquisition time was ~ 8 min.

Prior to DWI, T1- and T2-weighted images were obtained to identify the most suitable cross-section of the cervical tumor on an axial plane. Additional standard MRI sequences encompassed sagittal and fat-suppressed coronal T2-weighted imaging.

Data preprocessing

Alignment between the three acquired diffusion-weighted sequences (PGSE, OGSE-20 Hz, and OGSE-40 Hz) was performed using the built-in “imregtform” and “imwarp” functions of the MATLAB R2022b software. Images obtained from OGSE-20 Hz were selected as the alignment template because the three sequences were implemented in the order of OGSE-40 Hz, OGSE-20 Hz, and PGSE; hence, using images from the intermediate sequence as the template would provide the optimal matching result. Moreover, four-dimensional MRI denoising (length, width, height, and b-value) was performed using Marchenko-Pastur principal component analysis by exploiting data redundancy in the principal component analysis domain using the universal properties of the eigenspectrum of random covariance matrices [28]. Furthermore, for tumor imaging, the intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) effect induced by blood flow may introduce bias in the final model fitting, we preemptively remove this effect by recalculating the non-diffusion-weighted signals (b = 0 s/mm2) based the diffusion-weighted signals of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:50<b<1000$$\end{document} s/mm2 and log-linear fitting.

Data analysis

Two MRI cytometry methods, IMPULSED and JOINT, were used to analyze the dMRI signals acquired at different diffusion times. IMPULSED models tumor cells as impermeable spheres and ignores water exchange between the intra- and extracellular compartments. Three free microstructural parameters are measured: intracellular volume fraction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{in}$$\end{document}, cell diameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document}, and extracellular diffusivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{ex}$$\end{document}. In contrast, JOINT incorporates transytolemmal water exchange into the biophysical modeling and provides an additional free parameter, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{in}$$\end{document}, that describes the water exchange rate between intra- and extracellular compartments. Detailed information on these two quantitative methods is provided in the Supplement Materials. The microstructural parameters obtained via IMPULSED and JOINT were solved using the data-fitting MATI platform [29].

Qualitative image analysis

Three experienced gynecological radiologists (Y.H., Z.Y., and S.Y.; referred to as readers 1, 2, and 3, respectively) independently reviewed all images while blinded to all patient clinical and pathologic data.

The scoring criteria included image clarity, absence of artifacts, contour sharpness, and lesion visibility. Specifically, accurate lesion identification with high-quality OGSE images featuring clear tumor delineation and minimal artifacts (i.e. those that did not affect lesion observation), with no need for conventional MRI (e.g., PGSE and T2-weighted imaging), received 5 points. Lesions that were accurately identified on OGSE images with slightly visible artifacts and/or for which the region of interest (ROI) required minimal evaluation using conventional images received 4 points. Tumors that were recognizable on OGSE images but with less distinct contours and more noticeable artifacts, wherein referring to conventional imaging was often required, received 3 points. Tumors that were faintly recognizable on OGSE images with significant artifacts and contours that could only be distinguished via conventional imaging received 2 points. A lack of identifiable lesions or incorrect identifications on OGSE images earned 1 point.

Quantitative metrics analysis

ROIs: Images were exported into the ITK-SNAP software (Version 4.0, www.itksnap.org) for manual delineation of the ROI. Tumor ROIs obtained via DWI were carefully outlined by a seasoned gynecological radiologist (Z.Y.) using tumor contours from PGSE images (b = 1400 s/mm2) across all slices. Areas of degeneration, hemorrhage, and necrosis were maximally avoided. All ROIs were reviewed and modified by a senior gynecological radiologist (Y.H.). ROIs from DWI performed on healthy volunteers corresponded to the full cervical slices in the PGSE images with b = 0 s/mm2.

Quantitative microstructural information: The model-fitted parameters were calculated within each voxel and averaged over the entire ROI. The time-dependent ADC metrics were calculated.

Three months later, 30 randomly selected participant images (from healthy and cervical cancer patients) were re-contoured to assess repeatability and inter-observer agreement.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, version 26.0.0.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of continuous variables. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or the median and its 95% confidence interval.

For qualitative image assessment, Kendall’s W test was used to evaluate the interobserver agreement. The Kendall’s W coefficient of concordance was categorized into ‘positive but poor’ (≤ 0.4), ‘good’ (0.41–0.75), and ’excellent’ (> 0.75) agreement.

For quantitative analysis, the ROI-averaged microstructural parameters and ADC values for cervical cancer and normal cervices were compared using the Mann-Whitney U-Test. We also compared quantitative metrics across different pathological subtypes; receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantitatively evaluate the discriminatory effect of each metric. To further improve the diagnostic efficacy of differentiating pathological subtypes, we combined different microstructural parameters or ADC metrics and calculated the AUC values for the combined regression models. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess the measurement repeatability. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative image analysis

All 74 patients with visible cervical cancer underwent image quality evaluation; the detailed scores are shown in Table 3. Kendall’s W statistics (W = 0.819, P < 0.0001) indicated excellent inter-reader agreement for the OGSE images. The readers’ mean image quality scores were 4.49 ± 0.763, 4.55 ± 0.761, and 4.45 ± 0.779 (P = 0.538), indicating high-quality images; the scores mostly ranged from 4 to 5, demonstrating a consensus on their superior quality. In two cases, however, DWI images were deemed suboptimal by all readers and scored 2 points. One of them showed partially unclear boundaries owing to complex tissue composition and mixed signals that rendered manual delineation challenging. The other showed a poorly defined tumor with insufficient signal intensity, leading to a lack of contrast relative to the surrounding tissues as well as a blurred image.

These results indicate that the parameters obtained by MRI cytometry outperformed the traditional ADC metrics in terms of differentiating cervical cancer from normal tissues and that JOINT, which incorporates transcytolemmal water exchange, can provide better diagnostic efficacy than IMPULSED.

Figure 5b shows the performance of the microstructural parameters and ADC metrics when classifying the different subgroups; this clinical task is apparently more challenging than differentiating cervical cancer from normal cervical tissue. Furthermore, combining the model-fitted microstructural parameters with ADC metrics achieved a higher AUC. Discussion

This preliminary study demonstrates the clinical potential of MRI cytometry in the diagnosis of cervical cancer. In particular, the model-fitted microstructural parameters showed better performance in distinguishing cervical cancers from normal tissues than traditional ADC metrics. Combining MRI cytometry-derived parameters with ADC can more effectively identify cervical cancer subtypes with a high AUC of 0.853. Our findings show that this emerging technology has broad applicability for the noninvasive preoperative assessment of cervical cancer.

Currently, dMRI techniques and related data post-processing methods play crucial roles in the clinical management of cervical cancer owing to their ability to obtain rich microenvironmental information [30]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that ADC metrics can reliably differentiate between cervical cancer and the normal cervix, with tumors typically exhibiting lower ADC values [31]. Our study also found that the ADC values of cervical cancer were significantly lower than those of the normal cervix when using both the PGSE and OGSE sequences. While published data on cervical pathological subtype classification remain inconsistent [10, 32, 33], this study found no significant difference in the ADC metric of the commonly used PGSE between the squamous and non-squamous carcinoma subgroups; Crucially, the differences in the ADC obtained using the OGSE sequences were significant. This result attests to the advantage of the emerging OGSE sequences, which are more sensitive to microstructural differences owing to shorter diffusion times. By combining multiple \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{diff}$$\end{document} values, time-dependent dMRI achieves comprehensive multi-scales tissue characterization, thereby supporting more broad and accurate clinical applications– as evidenced by prior OGSE applications in tumor grading [34].

In addition to the conventional ADC mapping and quantitative microstructural parameters used in this study, several advanced dMRI-based imaging metrics have been imvestigated in the previous study of cervical cancer to provide more accurate and comprehensive assessments of the tissue microenvironment. The intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM)-related parameters, which reflect water diffusion in the extravascular space and perfusion-related pseudo diffusion, were implemented in some cervical cancer studies [35, 36]. Similarly, The quantitative parameters obtained from dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE), assessing microvascular perfusion, permeability, and extracellular extravascular space volume, were used for characterizing cervical cancer tissue hemodynamics [37, 38]. Furthermore, amide proton transfer -weighted imaging (APTWI) and diffusion kurtosis imaging can also probe microstructural properties, including cellular protein content and tissue heterogeneity, their metrics can differentiate different pathological subtypes of cervical cancer [39]. While these imaging techniques indirectly reflect tumor microstructure through quantitative but relatively vague parameters, MRI cytometry methods can provide several imaging indicators with clear biophysical significance, such as cell diameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document}), enabling direct quantification of cervical cancer microstructure.

IMPULSED has become a widely used method in clinical tumor microstructure imaging studies [17, 18, 21]; JOINT incorporates water exchange between the intra- and extracellular compartments into the IMPULSED framework, improving its underestimation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{in}$$\end{document} and cell density caused by neglecting exchange effects [40]. Our results show that incorporating water exchange not only provided an additional valuable parameter when differentiating cervical cancer from normal cervical tissue (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{in}$$\end{document}) with an AUC of 0.920, but that it also improved the clinical significance of other fitted parameters: the AUCs for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{in}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document} increased from 0.668 to 0.832 and from 0.618 to 0.687, respectively, while remaining comparable for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{ex}$$\end{document} (0.967 vs. 0.962). This demonstrates the clinical value of more accurate and comprehensive biophysical modeling in MRI cytometry, implying that the emerging JOINT method may have greater clinical potential. This ability to directly measure tissue microstructure provides a pathway to address other unmet clinical needs, including the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy [41].

The cervical cancer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document} values obtained from IMPULSED and JOINT were both significantly higher than those of the normal cervix, reflecting the enlarged cellular size of malignant tissue [42, 43]. The significant elevation in\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{in}$$\end{document} provides a direct quantitative measure of this expanded intracellular space resulting from cellular enlargement, while the reduced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{ex}$$\end{document} reflects the consequent physical compression and reduction of the extracellular compartment. In particular, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{in}$$\end{document} obtained from JOINT as well as the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{ex}$$\end{document} derived using both techniques showed excellent diagnostic performance with AUCs >0.9, quantitatively reflecting altered water molecular kinetics during tumor proliferation; this surpassed any ADC metric. The most reliable imaging metric to classify cervical cancer subtypes was model fitted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document}, with an AUC of 0.743 (IMPULSED) or 0.777 (JOINT). Squamous cell carcinoma exhibited a significantly smaller mean cell diameter than non-squamous carcinoma, potentially due to densely packed cells compressing cellular dimensions [44]. Similarly, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d$$\end{document} showed the best diagnostic performance when differentiating high-grade serous ovarian cancer from serous borderline ovarian tumors in a previous study [45]. The efficacy of diagnosing pathological subtypes could be further improved by combining MRI cytometry-derived parameters with ADC metrics, with the highest AUC >0.85. This suggests that the integration of microstructural parameters with traditional ADC metrics can improve the efficacy of regression models and provide more precise diagnostic data in clinical practice. Discovering the clinical value of various metrics will be a focus of future tumor microstructural imaging studies.

This prospective study had some limitations. First, the data was collected at a single center; therefore, sampling bias was inevitable. To mitigate this, future multicenter studies should be conducted to ensure a more diverse and representative patient population. Second, the sample size, particularly for the non-squamous carcinoma subgroup (e.g., adenosquamous carcinoma), was relatively small. As this was a preliminary investigation, we will expand the sample size in subsequent studies. Third, the current results lack a correlation analysis between pathological characteristics and microstructural parameters; therefore, we plan to conduct a detailed comparative analysis of the pathological microstructure using fitted imaging parameters in a future study. Fourth, the current technique is primarily applicable to patients with MRI-visible lesions, and its efficacy in detecting MRI-occult early-stage disease requires further investigation. Such quantitative comparisons will provide a deeper understanding of the relationship between tissue morphology and microstructural imaging, contributing to a more comprehensive analysis of imaging results.

Conclusion

Our results showed that the MRI-cytometry-derived microstructural parameters were able to differentiate cervical cancer from normal cervical tissues and further identify pathological subtypes combing with ADC metrics. This approach not only facilitates cervical cancer identification but also enables noninvasive discrimination of pathological subtypes, which is of critical significance in guiding therapeutic decision-making for patients. Based on the derived quantitative microstructural information at the cellular level, we will further explore their clinical value for more accurate diagnosis, efficacy assessment, and prognosis prediction in cervical cancer patients.
