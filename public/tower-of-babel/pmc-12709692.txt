Impact of delayed sternal closure and negative pressure therapy after complex cardiovascular surgery
Authors: Katheryne Chaparro Mendoza, María Paula Cortés Salas, Sergio Alzate-Ricaurte, Natalia Plata-Ayala, Mabel Soraya Moreno, Akemi Arango Sakamoto, Juan Manuel Maldonado, Jossman Javier Carvajal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the safety of delayed sternal closure with negative pressure therapy in complex cardiovascular surgeries, with a focus on infectious complications, compared to primary sternal closure.MethodsThis retrospective observational study analyzed 285 adult patients undergoing complex cardiovascular surgeries between January 2019 and February 2021 at a quaternary care hospital. Patients were divided into delayed closure (N = 91) and primary closure (N = 194) groups. Propensity score matching was applied to adjust for baseline differences, resulting in 168 matched patients for outcome comparison.ResultsPatients from the delayed closure group more frequently required emergency surgery (37% vs. 4.6%, p < 0.001), experienced longer extracorporeal circulation times (median 140 min vs. 84 min, p < 0.001), and needed more intraoperative transfusions (98.9% vs. 68%, p < 0.001). Postoperative bleeding in the first 12 h was higher in the delayed closure group (median 500 ml vs. 250 ml, p < 0.001). Reoperation rates due to bleeding were higher in the delayed closure group in the unmatched cohort (19.8% vs. 8.8%, p = 0.014), but not in the matched cohort (p = 0.078). In-hospital mortality (13.2% vs. 7.2%, p = 0.2) and postoperative infection rates (3.6% vs. 3.3%, p > 0.9) were similar between groups. Patients who underwent delayed closure had longer ICU and hospital stays.ConclusionDelayed sternal closure with negative pressure therapy appears to be a safe management strategy for high-risk patients in complex cardiovascular surgeries. Despite increased transfusions and postoperative bleeding, delayed closure with negative pressure therapy did not result in higher mortality or infection rates compared to primary sternal closure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03729-8.Keywords: Complex cardiovascular surgery, Negative pressure therapy, Primary sternal closure, Delayed sternal closure

Introduction

Delayed sternal closure is a valuable surgical strategy for managing severe complications following adult and pediatric cardiac surgery. This approach is employed in approximately 1–4% of patients undergoing open-heart surgery [1–4] and has been shown to prevent cardiac and pulmonary compression in cases of significant myocardial or pulmonary edema, thereby improving hemodynamic and respiratory stability [5]. Despite the absence of standardized criteria for its use in clinical practice guidelines, its application is largely guided by the surgeon’s clinical judgment. Delayed sternal closure has demonstrated benefits in patients with low cardiac output, arrhythmias, myocardial edema, significant postoperative bleeding, or the need for ventricular assist devices [2, 3, 6–11].

The relationship between delayed closure and an increased risk of sternal wound infection remains inconclusive. Several risk factors have been identified as contributors to postoperative infections, including advanced age, diabetes mellitus, obesity, heart failure, left ventricular dysfunction, smoking, emergency or urgent procedures, repeat cardiac surgery, and reoperations [12, 13]. The use of prophylactic antibiotic therapy has demonstrated clear benefits in reducing the risk of postoperative infections and is recommended for all patients undergoing cardiac surgery [14]. However, its administration varies significantly, ranging from standard prophylactic regimens for the first 48 h to prolonged courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics extending several days after sternal closure [4, 13, 14].

An alternative strategy involves the use of negative pressure therapy via vacuum-assisted closure systems for open chest wounds following complex cardiovascular surgeries. In a randomized trial, Bakaeen and colleagues reported a reduction in sternal wound infection rates with the application of a negative pressure dressing to an open sternum, with infection rates of 5% in the control group and 2% in the intervention group, similar to those observed in patients undergoing primary sternal closure [9]. Nevertheless, the impact of negative pressure therapy has been constrained by small sample sizes and a lack of robust comparative data against primary sternal closure procedures, with many studies focusing on its use once infection or dehiscence has been established [15–18].

This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the safety of delayed sternal closure with negative pressure therapy. We hypothesize that this approach does not increase the risk of infectious complications or mortality compared to primary closure.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective, analytic observational study of adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent complex cardiovascular surgeries, defined as two or more procedures during a single operation, between January 2019 and February 2021 at our institution. Eligible procedures included elective, urgent, and emergency cases.

We excluded patients with known coagulation disorders, pregnancy or postpartum status (within 2 months), minimally invasive approaches, isolated coronary artery bypass grafting, intraoperative death, perioperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and patients treated with negative pressure therapy for mediastinitis rather than as part of a planned delayed closure strategy.

Patients were divided into two groups based on the sternal management approach: primary closure and delayed closure, which included the use of negative pressure therapy. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and included sociodemographic characteristics, preoperative status, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes. The primary outcome was infectious complications. Infectious complications were defined as superficial or deep sternal wound infection, mediastinitis, or endocarditis diagnosed within 30 or 90 day surveillance period, according to National Healthcare Safety Network/Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria. Secondary outcomes included reoperations, in-hospital mortality, intraoperative parameters, postoperative bleeding, need for transfusions, acute kidney injury (defined as an increase in serum creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dl within 48 h, an increase ≥1.5 times baseline, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 h) and length of ICU and hospital stay. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as all eligible patients during the study period were included.

All patients received preoperative prophylaxis with cefazolin and vancomycin, followed postoperatively by ceftriaxone ± vancomycin. Antibiotics were continued for 48 h after surgery in the primary closure group and for 48 h after definitive sternal closure in the delayed closure group. Prolonged courses were administered in selected cases as determined by the infectious disease and intensive care teams. Moreover, closure techniques were consistent between groups. After removal of negative pressure therapy, the sternum was closed conventionally with three to four crossed wires, and titanium ligaclips were used when required for hemostasis control. The fascia and subcutaneous tissue were closed in layers with PDS 1–0, and the skin was closed with a 3–0 Prolene intradermal suture.

Statistical analysis

To assess baseline differences and ensure appropriate group comparisons, we performed descriptive and bivariate analyses. The normality of numerical variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, supplemented by visual assessment of distribution symmetry. Parametric variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD), and non-parametric variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons were conducted using Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and either Student’s t test or Welch’s t test for continuous parametric variables after testing for variance homogeneity. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to non-parametric continuous variables. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To account for potential confounders and the non-randomized design, we performed a propensity score-matched analysis using logistic regression. Variables included in the model were selected based on a univariate p value threshold <0.25 when comparing patients who underwent delayed closure versus primary closure. The final model included age, preoperative endocarditis, heart transplantation, and prior cardiac surgery. Matching was performed using the nearest-neighbor method without replacement. We calculated the false discovery rate to adjust for multiple comparisons.

All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 2022.12.0 + 353. The study was approved by our Institutional Review Board, with a waiver of informed consent due to its retrospective nature and compliance with national regulatory standards. All procedures conformed to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Between January 2019 and February 2021, a total of 709 adult patients underwent complex cardiovascular surgeries at our institution. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 285 patients met inclusion criteria, of whom 168 were included in the matched analysis (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

The median age of the overall cohort was 61 years (IQR 50–70), and 66.3% of patients were male. Obesity was present in 16.8% of patients, and 7% reported active tobacco use. Common comorbidities included arterial hypertension (59.6%), heart failure (58.9%), pulmonary hypertension (22.8%), chronic kidney disease (17.2%), diabetes mellitus (13.2%), and liver disease (3.2%). Approximately 28.8% of patients were on antiplatelet therapy and 20.4% on anticoagulants. Most patients (67%) had a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (≥50%), and 7.7% had endocarditis. These characteristics were generally balanced between the primary and delayed closure groups, both before and after propensity matching (Table 1).

Surgical characteristics

Most procedures were urgent (63.9%), followed by elective (21.1%) and emergency cases (15.1%). Emergency surgeries were significantly more frequent in the delayed closure group (37.4%) compared to the primary closure group (4.6%, p < 0.001), and this difference remained significant after matching. Delayed closure was more commonly used in patients undergoing heart transplantation (17.6% vs. 4.6%), ascending aorta replacement with or without valve replacement (20.9% and 30.8% vs. 3.1% and 2.6%, respectively), Bentall procedures (26.4% vs. 7.7%), and reoperations (34.1% vs. 13.4%) (p < 0.001). After matching, differences in heart transplantation and reoperations were no longer statistically significant.

Patients in the delayed closure group more frequently underwent deep hypothermia and had longer ischemic times (median 94 min, IQR 74–112) and durations on extracorporeal circulation (median 140.5 min, IQR 110–167.8) compared to the primary closure group (p < 0.001 for both). Intraoperative transfusions were significantly more common in the delayed closure group (98.9% vs. 68%, p < 0.001), including higher volumes of red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitates (Table 2).

Postoperative outcomes

Bleeding in the first 12 postoperative hours was substantially higher in the delayed closure group (median 500 ml, IQR 350–975) compared to the primary closure group (250 ml, IQR 162.5–480; p < 0.001). A similar difference was observed between 12 and 24 h postoperatively. Reoperation due to bleeding was more frequent in the delayed closure group in the unmatched analysis (p = 0.014), though the difference was not significant after matching (p = 0.078).

Postoperative transfusions were required in 57.8% of the total cohort. Patients in the delayed closure group more often received multiple blood components, particularly platelet concentrates (63.2%), fresh frozen plasma (58.8%), and cryoprecipitates (57.9%) (p < 0.001 for all). Importantly, no complications were directly attributed to the delayed closure technique.

The incidence of infectious complications was low (1.8%) and similar between groups. Acute kidney injury was more frequent in the delayed closure group in the unmatched cohort (p = 0.008), but not after matching (p = 0.2). Patients with delayed closure also had longer ICU and hospital stays, as well as more days on mechanical ventilation (p < 0.001 for all). In-hospital mortality was 9.1% in both groups, with no significant difference (Table 3).

Discussion

This study evaluated whether delayed sternal closure with negative pressure therapy affects postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing complex cardiovascular surgery. As delayed closure was reserved for patients with higher surgical complexity and clinical acuity, propensity score matching was applied to ensure comparability. Our results demonstrate that delayed closure does not significantly increase the risk of surgical site infections, reoperation, or in-hospital mortality compared to primary sternal closure.

Before matching, the delayed closure group had longer ischemia and extracorporeal circulation times, more frequent use of deep hypothermia, and higher intraoperative transfusion requirements, indicators of greater procedural complexity. These differences are consistent with prior studies, which have shown that delayed closure is often used in patients undergoing aortic procedures, reoperations, or heart transplantation [3, 9, 19]. However, after accounting for these baseline differences, the incidence of major outcomes such as infection, mortality, and reoperation due to bleeding did not differ significantly between groups.

Reported infection rates typically range from 2–5% for delayed closure compared to 1–2% for primary sternal closure [3, 4, 20–23]. However, consistent with our findings, multiple studies have not identified significant differences in infection risk between the two techniques [4, 22, 24]. Notably, Bakaeen et al. reported a reduction in wound infections using prophylactic negative pressure therapy, although their results may have been influenced by patient selection [9]. While concerns exist regarding prolonged open sternum time as a potential risk factor for infection, our protocol ensured closure within 48 h in all cases, which may have contributed to the low incidence of surgical site infections. Additionally, our results support previous evidence suggesting that negative pressure therapy may help reduce infection risk by enhancing tissue perfusion and maintaining a sealed environment [9, 11].

In-hospital mortality was also similar between groups. This finding contrasts with earlier studies that reported higher mortality associated with delayed closure, often attributed to the critical condition of patients requiring delayed closure, rather than the technique itself [19, 21–23]. Our matched analysis suggests that when controlling for clinical severity, delayed closure does not independently increase the risk of death. Similarly, while patients with delayed closure had longer durations of mechanical ventilation, ICU and hospital stay, these differences reflect the underlying severity of illness and were expected.

Our study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the safety of delayed sternal closure with negative pressure therapy in high-risk patients. The matched analysis strengthens the internal validity of our findings by account for confounding related to baseline risk factors. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. This was a retrospective, single-center study, which may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias. Although propensity score matching controlled for observable confounders, unmeasured variables, such as intraoperative decision-making, tissue characteristics, or subtle hemodynamic changes, could still influence outcomes. The absence of STS or EuroSCORE II data precluded incorporation of validated risk scores, and important variables such as diabetes, BMI, or chronic steroid use were not included in the matching process. Our relatively modest sample size limits the power to detect small differences in rare complications and precludes subgroup analysis. Logistic regression to explore predictors was also unfeasible due to the limited number of outcome events, representing an area for future research. Further studies should aim to develop predictive models to identify patients most likely to benefit from delayed sternal closure, given the absence of standardized selection criteria. Finally, our analysis was restricted to in-hospital outcomes and did not evaluate long-term complications, cost-effectiveness, or patient-reported outcomes.

Conclusion

Delayed sternal closure with negative pressure therapy is a safe and effective option for selected patients undergoing complex cardiovascular surgery. In this propensity-matched analysis, delayed closure was not associated with increased risk of infection, reoperation, or in-hospital mortality. These findings support its judicious use in high-risk settings and reinforce the importance of timely sternal closure within established safety windows. Future prospective studies should aim to define standardized protocols and explore long-term clinical and patient-centered outcomes.
