Assessing family quality of life in families of people with brain injuries: a Delphi study
Authors: José Luis Castillo, Alba Aza, María Fernández, Antonio Manuel Amor, Miguel Ángel Verdugo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcquired brain injury (ABI) has a profound impact on the lives of survivors and their loved ones. However, there is a paucity of research on the quality of life of the whole family. Also, there are no instruments for assessing family quality of life (FQoL) from a holistic and systemic perspective after ABI in the Spanish context. The goal of this study was to identify a set of indicators and items of FQoL for this population in order to develop a preliminary version of such an assessment tool. A modified Delphi study was conducted with twelve social and healthcare professionals (70% women; age: M = 42.52, SD = 6.39) from ABI rehabilitation services and organizations (four psychologists, three neuropsychologists, three social workers, one nurse and one physiotherapist), who were asked to assess a pool of items throughout three rounds according to two criteria (importance and sensitivity). Descriptive analysis and percentage of agreement were employed to select the best items, as well as coefficients of concordance for the final pool of items. As a result, a pool of 71 items for five domains—Individual well-being, Family relationships, Family resources, Community relationships, and Community resources—was obtained. The findings of this study provided content validity evidence for a field test version of a FQoL tool, which may help to inform assessment and family-centered support planning for people with ABI and their families.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02530-y.Keywords: Acquired brain injury, ABI, Family quality of life, FQoL, Delphi, Assessment

Introduction

Acquired brain injury (ABI) refers to a sudden injury that interrupts normal brain functioning, encompassing a wide range of brain damages, such as cerebrovascular accidents or traumatic brain injuries [1]. Brain injuries are one of the leading causes of adult disability in Europe [2]. According to the 2020 Spanish Disability, Personal Autonomy and Dependency Situations Survey, the population with ABI was estimated at 414,680 individuals in Spain [3].

As a result, individuals may experience long-lasting physical, behavioral, and psychosocial impairments post-injury, which may lead to a decrease in quality of life (QoL, hereinafter) and significant life changes for survivors and their loved ones [4, 5]. In this sense, several studies have addressed either the individual QoL of survivors or that of their relatives within rehabilitation settings in order to improve their well-being [4, 6]. Despite its importance for building family interventions, the well-being of the ABI family as a whole system has received relatively little attention in the literature [7].

By contrast, the interest in the well-being of families of individuals with other conditions linked to limitations in human functioning (e.g., intellectual and developmental disabilities, hereinafter IDD) led to significant efforts to conceptualize and assess Family Quality of Life (FQoL) as a systemic construct during the late 1990s and early 2000s [8–10]. In this sense, FQoL has been understood as “a dynamic sense of family well-being, collectively and subjectively defined, and informed by its members, in which individual and family-level needs interact” (p. 262) [11]. It encompasses the extent to which family needs are met, the enjoyment of family life, and opportunities to engage in valued activities [12]. The FQoL construct is the ultimate outcome of family-centered approaches, which is shaped by factors across four levels: (a) individual family members, (b) the family system, (c) supports, practices, and services, and (d) programs, policies, and systems [11, 13]. This is a useful framework for studying and improving the impact of support systems on the family well-being of different groups at risk of social disadvantage [14].

FQoL is a multidimensional construct with objective and subjective indicators, which can be assessed from the perspectives of the different family members [15]. Hu et al. [16] highlighted the importance of ensuring that the family, rather than the individual members, remains as the unit of analysis. Therefore, FQoL assessment should incorporate indicators that capture both the individual elements of the family system and the system as a whole.

There have been several efforts to operationalize the core domains and indicators of FQoL, which resulted in two major assessment approaches beyond ABI populations [15, 17]. The Beach Center on Disability group developed a measurement scale based on focus groups, pilot testing and validation studies in the USA. Such a strategy is optimal with groups at risk of social disadvantage as it empowers them by defining their desired living conditions [18, 19]. This scale captures family members’ perception of FQoL across five domains: Family interaction, Parenting, Emotional well-being, Physical and material well-being, and Disability-related support [20, 21]. By contrast, the Family Quality of Life Survey (FQoLS-2006) [22, 23] of the International Family Quality of Life Project is the result of extensive work with a panel of experts, pilot testing, and validation of a FQoL survey across different countries [23–25]. The FQoLS-2006 combines qualitative and quantitative approaches to describe FQoL according to nine domains—i.e., Health of the family, Financial well-being, Family relationships, Support from other people, Support from disability-related services, Influence of values, Careers and preparing for careers, Leisure and recreation, and Community interaction [22].

Both approaches have been applied to multiple populations, such as families of individuals with deafness, blindness, dementia or severe physical illnesses [26–30]. However, FQoL has barely been studied in ABI populations and, mostly from the perspective of primary caregivers [31, 32], which fail to represent the experience of the whole family system. In Spain, Climent et al. [33] used a non-validated version of the Beach Center on Disability’s FQoL Scale to assess importance and satisfaction with FQoL from ABI caregivers’ perspective.

Recently, a qualitative study on FQoL has been conducted with ABI survivors, primary caregivers, and non-primary caregivers in Spain [34]. This study explored the perspectives of family members on FQoL and identified a set of indicators according to five domains, namely Individual well-being, Family relationships, Family resources, Community relationships, and Community resources (see Fig. 1). This approach is based on the conceptual underpinnings of the FQoL theory [11] and partly overlaps with those other previously described, sharing key indicators such as having time for oneself (i.e., Individual well-being) with the Beach Center on Disability’s Emotional well-being domain [20, 21] or family involvement in community activities (i.e., Community relationships) with the Community Interaction domain of FQoLS-2006 [24]. However, differences emerge as a result of the characteristics of the target population. In the original study, participants reflected on mutual support within families, including survivors. This differs from the family roles captured by the Parenting domain of the Beach Center model, which examines families’ ability to provide support to children with disabilities [10]. These discrepancies mainly stem from the fact that the Spanish study targeted families of adult survivors. Differences between models are also evident in the importance of pre-onset relationships (i.e., Community relationships), since the loss of significant bonds and social isolation are common in this population [35].

Given these discrepancies, it becomes necessary to develop assessment tools tailored to the experiences of the targeted population, since individual and family QoL indicators are sensitive to cultural and contextual factors [36]. Thus, generic QoL and family assessment tools may fail to adequately capture the needs of families of individuals with ABI and may require adaptations, potentially compromising the validity and reliability of the assessment methods. Currently, there are no FQoL assessment tools tailored to the whole family system of people with ABI, since most available instruments have been developed for children with IDD [15, 37].

This paper is part of a project whose main goal is to integrate the strengths of both research strategies (i.e., Beach Center on Disability and International Family Quality of Life Project), in order to develop a FQoL assessment tool for ABI families in the Spanish context. A set of items for assessing this population was developed based on the identified domains and indicators [34], as well as on the existing literature on FQoL and ABI families. Specifically, this study reports the evaluation of these items by a panel of national experts to obtain a sufficiently comprehensive and appropriate pool of items for developing the preliminary version of a FQoL scale in Spain for people with ABI and their families.

Methods

To achieve this aim, an expert consensus methodology called the Delphi method was used. The Delphi method is a set of structured group communication techniques that seek to obtain information regarding a complex problem from individuals considered to be experts in a field [38]. It has been widely used across different disciplines, such as health, nutrition, education, psychology or family studies [39–43], for different purposes, including the development of QoL and ABI rehabilitation assessment tools [44–47]. According to Landeta & Lertxundi [48], Delphi studies are characterized by an iterative consultation process in which a panel of experts is requested to anonymously provide their opinion. The responses are then statistically analyzed, and structured feedback on the group results is systematically presented. This method has proved to be useful to capture information from a heterogeneous sample of experts [49]. Thus, this method may be highly valuable for identifying key indicators relevant to families, which can be addressed accordingly by rehabilitation professionals.

For this study, we chose a modified version of Delphi method. The decision to use this approach was based on the need to integrate the ABI family members’ perspective—captured in a previous phase of the project through focus groups, described in detail in the 2.2. instrument subsection—and the professionals perspective. The use of such a consensus method enabled us to structure expert consensus efficiently, facilitating the prioritization of items and ensuring that the resulting questionnaire not only faithfully represented the construct as defined by the study population, but was also practical and useful in professional settings.

Methodological rigor of this Delphi study has been established by following the Conducting and Reporting Delphi Studies’ recommendations (CREDES) [50]. See Additional File 1 for the reporting checklist.

Participants

There is no agreement on the number of participants that should be included in Delphi studies [51, 52]. For efficiency purposes, it is recommended that researchers include only the minimum number required to achieve a representative set of judgments [46, 53]. In this study, 12 professionals of ABI rehabilitation services were offered to participate in the study. Although there are no clear criteria to define expertise either [54], professionals from rehabilitation and support services for people with ABI were considered since they would provide important insights for developing a useful scale for assessing and supporting families within services. Thus, criteria were established in collaboration with the Spanish Federation of Brain Injuries (Daño Cerebral Estatal, in Spanish). Participants were: (a) professionals of rehabilitation and support services for people with ABI, (b) they had to have prior work experience of at least 6 years within this field, and (c) part of their work experience should be in direct support for families of people with ABI.

Participation was offered by e-mail through the Spanish Federation of Brain Injuries and other organizations for people with ABI. In order to participate, professionals were required to complete an online survey providing sociodemographic information. Thirty professionals answered the survey to participate. Many of them belonged to the same centers/organizations. Finally, 12 were selected to ensure representation across different disciplines and organizations, thereby maintaining anonymity. The sample consisted of nine women (n = 9) and three men (n = 3), with ages ranging between 30 and 51 (M = 43.36; SD = 6.25). Most of them were professionals of non-governmental organizations for people with ABI (n = 9) in different regions of Spain: Andalusia (n = 3), Galicia (n=3), Community of Madrid (n = 4), and Valencian Community (n = 2). Additionally, two professionals were relatives of people with ABI. Table 1 displays participants’ characteristics.

Instrument: family quality of life survey for ABI

The Family Quality of Life for ABI Families instrument was developed according to an inductive-deductive strategy within a sequential and exploratory mixed-methods approach [55]. A preliminary pool of 346 items was generated through a series of preparatory steps. First, a systematic review of the experiences of family members of people with ABI was conducted, which allowed us to describe the changes within the family system post-ABI. Also, assessment tools as well as main aspects of family well-being were identified. Then, a qualitative study of focus groups with ABI family members was carried out in order to determine the core domains and indicators of FQoL for this population [34]. As a result, a set of indicators of FQoL was identified and organized across the five cited domains—i.e., Individual well-being, Family relationships, Family resources, Community relationships, and Community resources—. These steps contributed to the definition of the construct of FQoL and the development of a population-specific pool of items to assess it.

Later, a group of researchers on disability, brain injuries, QoL and family reviewed the preliminary pool of items. They focused on refining the wording, eliminating redundancies, and ensuring content relevance. This process resulted in a pool of 204 items for the current study. Although the original items were mostly drawn from the focus groups (88.9%), some of them correspond to previous instruments and studies on QoL [21, 22] and family post-injury [56]. Procedure

The Delphi method was used to assess the pool of 204 items. This process consisted of three rounds carried out anonymously and asynchronously throughout a Moodle platform (Modular Object-Oriented Dynamic Learning Environment), a free and open-source software for managing online learning activities. The virtual environment was structured by five modules. The first module included information about the study’s aims, how the pool of items was generated, and procedural instructions. The following three sections were each related to the three rounds (see Fig. 2), which included instructions and necessary materials to carry out tasks. Also, a report on the results of the previous rounds was provided before starting a new round. This included summary statistics by criteria for each round, the number of kept and rejected items by rounds and domains, and the rationale for the research team’s decisions based on participants’ quantitative and qualitative feedback.

Flow diagram of Delphi procedure

Participants were instructed to assess item content according to: (i) importance (i.e., the perceived relevance of that item to assess FQoL), and (ii) sensitivity (i.e., the degree to which an item can be modified by contextual interventions), ranging from 1 (i.e., absence of the attribute) to 4 (i.e., maximum presence of the attribute). Participants were also invited to offer any additional comments on the items, if desired. All items were randomly presented to experts. In round 1, they were asked to assess the initial pool of items, providing rationale for those rated as not important/sensitive (i.e., rated as 1 or 2), and propose a maximum of five new items by domain if necessary. Round 2 focused on new items and those modified from round 1. After the first two rounds, it was noted that Family relationships and Community relationships were the domains with the highest number of discarded items. Therefore, in round 3, a re-assessment was carried out on previously discarded items that didn’t achieve the required levels of agreement but met the rest of the criteria.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Salamanca (Protocol No. 2022/742). All procedures described complied with the principles of the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. Participants could withdraw from the study at any moment without consequences. Also, anonymity was ensured by using pseudonyms, thus complying with the data protection regulations in force both in Spain and the European Union (Regulation EU 2016/679).

Data analysis

For data analysis, each round involved a series of tasks that required a specific data analysis approach, combining both quantitative and qualitative strategies.

In round 1, a quantitative analysis was used for tasks related to items’ importance and sensitivity assessment. Mean, standard deviation and percentage of agreement by items and domains were calculated. Additionally, qualitative deductive content analysis has been used to analyze experts’ comments about specific items. A codebook was developed based on previous experience with Delphi studies. First, comments provided by each participant were individually coded. Later, these comments were reviewed and summarized for each item in order to integrate the different perspectives of experts. Finally, a decision was reached on whether to accept or reject each comment. Comments were coded by JLC in the participants’ native language.

The data collected in round 2 were analyzed according to the percentage of acceptance of proposed/new items and comments about the reasons to accept or reject items were once again coded by JLC with the same procedure as round 1. Later, items were analyzed by ratings of importance and sensitivity (i.e., M, SD, percentage of agreement).

In round 3, experts were asked to re-assess some items from domains with high percentages of rejection according to importance and sensitivity. Once again, data were analyzed in terms of mean, standard deviation and percentage of agreement, as well as coding comments on items for re-assessment.

Criteria used to discard items throughout the rounds were similar to those used in previous studies [44, 45]: (1) have a mean score of less than 3.00, (2) a standard deviation of more than 1.00, and (3) a percentage of agreement of less than 80%. A fourth criterion was used in the case of qualitative information by which items with negative comments were discarded. Negative comments were defined as participants’ comments which meant whether rejection or reformulation (e.g., conceptual disagreement, rationale about criteria, or wording issues). Item retrieval implied that items had to meet quantitative criteria for both importance and sensitivity. However, the presence of negative comments meant that they were discarded. For example, although i.166 “My family members can count on people who are also going through the same situation as we are” met quantitative criteria, it was discarded due to a negative expert’s comment. Any negative comment was sufficient to discard an item. However, in cases where both negative and positive comments were given, disagreements among experts’ comments were solved by authors.

Finally, for the two criteria involved (i.e., importance and sensitivity) and the five domains, agreement between experts was calculated using Bangidwala’s weighted statistic for ordinal data (BWN). This statistic allows calculating the agreement level among judges when categorizing the items for each criterion studied and expresses this agreement on a scale from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating the absence of agreement and 1 the strongest agreement possible. Agreement strength can be poor (0.000 to 2.00), weak (0.201 to 0.400), moderate (0.401 to 0.600), and very good (0.801 to 1). Unlike the initial agreement (BN), which provides information about the strict agreement between experts (i.e., when the experts give the same score to the item for a given criterion, for example, a score of 3 for sensitivity criterion regarding the Community resources domain), the BWN also includes partial agreements (i.e., when the experts give more or less similar scores for the criterion) [57].

Discussion

This study aimed to obtain a valid and useful set of items as the basis for developing a FQoL assessment tool for families of adults with ABI in the Spanish context. Through a modified Delphi method, the item pool has been evaluated by a group of experts in rehabilitation and support services to people with ABI and their families to provide content validity evidence [58]. As a result of the study, a final pool of 71 valid items has been generated with high consensus among participants. Validity evidence regarding the content of the items ensures that these items adequately represent the world of aspirations and needs relevant to measure FQoL in the context and group of interest [44].

The selected criteria used for assessing items were similar to those used in previous studies in QoL assessment [44–46]. Specifically, importance and sensitivity were used to retrieve items that were (1) representative of the FQoL domains and (2) useful for assessing and supporting families to enhance their well-being, respectively. In this study, importance ratings were higher than sensitivity and showed greater agreement. This is consistent with the above-mentioned studies where sensitivity tends to be the lowest rated criterion [44–46]. However, this study differs from the previous ones since the criteria that produced the most discarded items was importance. This can be partially explained by the large number of items to be assessed and the inclusion of more conservative retrieval criteria by including the percentage of agreement, which has been previously recommended [52, 59].

Regarding the analysis of the final pool, the retrieved items corresponded to identified needs for families of people with ABI, as well as other FQoL measures and domains (see Additional File 4). While the Individual well-being domain captures information about important aspects of individual QoL for ABI survivors and their relatives (e.g., mental health issues or life satisfaction), other domains, such as Family relationships or resources, address issues related to family day-to-day issues (e.g., adaptation to loss, communication, care distribution or transport), which has been described in previous studies [60–62]. In the case of Community relationships and resources, experts’ assessment resulted in the inclusion of a majority of items regarding support received, including both from people within the community and services. This highlights the importance of empowering families by helping them develop protective support resources after ABI. However, this is not exclusive of other indicators such as valued relationships, activities or general community resources (e.g., parks, libraries, pools) and their usage. In sum, the selection of such items by experts reflects the complex theoretical relationships at different ecological levels between individual, family and social well-being environments [11, 63–65].

According to the final ranks, we found that the items from the Individual well-being domain were the ones valued as most important, along with Family and Community resources. These items capture indicators consistently documented within the FQoL literature: the well-being of each family member, which is reflected on domains such as Emotional well-being [20, 21] or Health of the family [22]. In a recent Delphi study on critical items for screening caregiver needs, two of the final pool items were identified as the most important rated indicators (i.e., depression and stress) [66].

Regarding the Resources domains, these items align with those others from the Physical/Material well-being and Disability-related Support domains of the Beach Center on Disability’s FQoL Scale [21], which were identified as the most important aspects of FQoL for ABI caregivers in Spain [33]. In fact, some items from the Family resources domain included in the final pool (i.e., i42-44) have been highlighted as key research priorities for promoting family well-being by caregivers in other Delphi studies [67]. In this line, a previous study on FQoL in dementia reported that caregivers of people with dementia in Spain tended to perceive lower levels of attainment and satisfaction with aspects related to resources, as well as the Family and Community relationships domains, in the Family Quality of Life Survey [30]. These findings correspond to the domains whose items were ranked as the least sensitive in our study.

The lower levels of consensus regarding perceived sensitivity observed in the Family relationships and Community relationships domains may reflect the inherently contextual and dynamic nature of relational well-being. Unlike domains more closely linked to service provision or individual functioning, relational domains are strongly shaped by cultural norms, family structures, socio-economic conditions, and regional variations in community support systems. These domains also tend to capture processes—such as communication patterns, emotional climate, or community inclusion—that fluctuate over time and are less directly influenced by formal rehabilitation or social services. As a result, experts may find it more difficult to agree on which indicators best represent these constructs or to judge how sensitive they are across different family environments.

However, these findings should be interpreted considering certain methodological choices shaped the study. The project followed an inductive-deductive approach which strengthened the theoretical coherence of the resulting item pool. This constitutes a sound strategy that has previously been used in scale development processes [51]. The inductive phase (systematic review and focus groups with ABI families) as a set of preparatory steps for the current study ensured that the pool captured needs and experiences directly expressed by the target population, while the deductive phase (expert consultation and literature-based refinement) aligned these indicators with existing conceptualizations of FQoL and QoL [11, 17]. This iterative integration allowed us to: (a) situate emergent, family-derived categories within established theoretical domains; (b) avoid conceptual redundancy by refining items with overlapping content; and, (c) identify gaps that would not have been visible from either perspectives alone, such as the need to strengthen items related to community resources and services access. As a result, the final items reflect both ecological validity and adherence to theoretical frameworks, enhancing the future utility and interpretability of the scale. On the other hand, a sample composed by professionals from rehabilitation and support services for people with ABI and their families was selected to assess the pool of items for the current study. Participants selection was also influenced by the definition of expertise, which is thought to shape the results within Delphi studies [68, 69]. Expertise was framed by participants’ membership of ABI rehabilitation services and organizations as professionals, their disciplines, years of clinical experience, and role within services/organizations by providing direct support and care to people with ABI and their families. The described criteria were stated in collaboration with the Spanish Federation on Brain Injuries, which ensured the representation of important knowledge and experience for the study’s aim. Furthermore, the panel included two professionals who were family members of people with ABI as well. This may have affected the item assessment and the resulting pool. However, the inclusion of the lived experience in the item selection is considered a methodological strength since it ensures technical knowledge while also guaranteeing the representation of desired outcomes and needs for the target population, which is congruent with the QoL and FQoL theory [11, 70].

Besides the definition of expertise, the number of participants within experts’ panels also warrants further reflection. According to Belton et al. [52], there is no current agreement about the number of experts needed, ranging from fewer than 10 to more than 60. Yet, Delphi studies’ sample size has been suggested to be dependent on the homogeneity of the expert panel, so that a larger number of participants are needed for heterogeneous panels in order to reach consensus [69, 71]. This study was conducted with a heterogeneous sample of twelve professionals from different social and healthcare disciplines. The requirement to preserve anonymity has restrained the number of participants by excluding professionals from the same centers/organizations. Also, two participants dropped out of the study in round 1 because of the high workload within organizations. While both aspects further limited the sample size and may have caused difficulties in reaching consensus, especially for some domains (i.e., Family and Community Relationships), it should be noted that high levels of participants’ agreement were finally observed in the final pool as expressed by Bangidwala’s method [57].

Finally, the findings of this study and the resulting pool of items should be considered with caution since the item pool has been developed for families in which the person with ABI is an adult. Thus, selected indicators and items should not be generalizable to families of children with disabilities. Also, the development process was conducted within the Spanish rehabilitation context. In this country, different ABI rehabilitation regulations and services coexist due to the decentralized organization of the autonomous communities, which results in significant territorial disparities. Although the regions represented in the panel of experts are among those with the most developed service provision systems for survivors in the chronic phase, family support during this phase tends to be scarce and is often provided by NGO’s [72]. This may have contributed to the lower level of consensus observed for sensitivity regarding the Community resources domain. Hence, cross-cultural validation of the item pool remains essential, since the abovementioned diverse cultural norms regarding family dynamics, caregiving expectations, social participation, and social and healthcare systems may impact how indicators, and items are interpreted or prioritized. Further studies should examine conceptual and linguistic equivalence. To this end, it is considered highly relevant to acknowledge such cultural assumptions and contextual problems that may influence family well-being across context.

Overall, the current research is one of the first efforts to address FQoL evaluation in families of people with ABI. By integrating the perspectives of families and the experience of social and healthcare professionals, the study resulted in a comprehensive pool of 71 items deemed representative and useful for measuring FQoL. Yet, this is a first step towards the development of a FQoL assessment tool for this population. Such a process should carefully consider the response format and who should be involved in the assessment process. This is a particularly important issue in family assessment, where the unit of analysis is the whole family system, although many existing tools rely solely on the perspectives of main caregivers, which may pose potential threats to the validity of results [16]. Likewise, further steps should examine how these items work by pilot testing the assessment tool and the validity of the theoretical model proposed in previous studies [34].

Beyond the relevance of the retrieved items for operationally defining this construct and developing an assessment tool, the study findings have potential implications for rehabilitation and family-support professionals at micro-, meso-, and macro-system levels. At microsystem level, the identified items and, consequently, its related indicators provide a framework for supporting individual ABI families which can be used across disciplines. For nursing staff, items related to the Individual well-being and Family resources domains provide concrete indicators that can be integrated into clinical routines during admission and discharge planning, such as identifying emotional strain or care-management demands within families. Rehabilitation teams (e.g., physiotherapists, neuropsychologists, occupational therapists) may use items from the Community and Family resources domains to prioritize interventions that enhance participation, autonomy, and accessibility, particularly those aspects rated by experts as highly sensitive. Likewise, social workers can benefit from the explicit identification of indicators related to financial stress and support, community services, facilitating more accurate referrals, navigation of services and, long-term planning with families. At meso-system level, this model doesn’t only provide indicators for assessing family needs within organizations to support the planning and delivery of family-specific programs, but also encourages organizational changes towards family-centered support models by providing a framework for family-centered outcomes-based evaluation [73, 74]. This idea can be also applied at macro-system levels where the pool of items and their related indicators can be used to guide the identification of valued family outcomes for public policy implementation and evaluation aimed at promoting the wellbeing of ABI families [75].
