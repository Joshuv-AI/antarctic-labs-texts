Neutrophil‐Macrophage Interactions Shape Inflammatory Macrophage Remodeling in Gastric Cancer During Chemo‐Immunotherapy

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe tumor microenvironment (TME) in gastric cancer (GC) is a dynamic ecosystem where immune, stromal, and malignant cells influence therapeutic outcomes. Although macrophages and neutrophils are key regulators of the TME, their coordinated responses to distinct treatments remain unclear. In this study, we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing on GC biopsy specimens from treatment‐naïve (TN) patients, as well as patients treated with chemotherapy (C), chemotherapy plus nivolumab (CN), or chemotherapy plus trastuzumab (CT). Comparative analyses revealed marked remodeling of the myeloid compartment, particularly involving the CN group. Neutrophils in the CN group exhibited transcriptional reprogramming, characterized by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II expression, inflammatory activation, and enrichment of maturation‐related signatures. Pseudotime and ligand–receptor analyses identified neutrophils as a prominent sender of signals communicating with macrophages via chemokine and cytokine signaling. These neutrophil‐derived signals promoted macrophage polarization toward a CXCL10+ M1‐like phenotype and activated inflammatory signaling pathways. In vivo validation using a syngeneic CT26 mouse model confirmed that neutrophil depletion abolished the antitumor efficacy of combined oxaliplatin and PD‐1 blockade, underscoring the functional importance of this crosstalk. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that PD‐1 blockade combined with chemotherapy reprograms the GC TME by enhancing neutrophil–macrophage interactions, which amplify inflammatory signaling and strengthen antitumor immunity. These results highlight the myeloid‐centered axis as a potential determinant of immunotherapy responses in GC.

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a highly complex and dynamic ecosystem comprising diverse cellular populations, including malignant, immune, and stromal cells [1, 2]. Cellular and molecular interactions between TME components collectively determine tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic responsiveness [3]. In the era of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), the spatial and functional profiles of tumor‐infiltrating T cells play a pivotal role in ICI responsiveness [4]. Among immune cell populations beyond T cells, macrophages and neutrophils have emerged as critical modulators of tumor‐immune ecosystems [5, 6]. Macrophages display remarkable plasticity, polarizing into pro‐inflammatory (M1‐like) or immunosuppressive (M2‐like) states that differentially modulate tumor immunity [7]. Neutrophils, previously considered short‐lived effector cells, are now recognized for their diverse phenotypes and context‐dependent pro‐ and antitumor functions within the TME [8]. Together, these myeloid cells orchestrate the immune landscape of the TME, influencing tumor progression and therapeutic efficacy. However, the dynamic crosstalk among these myeloid populations in the TME remains poorly understood, particularly under therapeutic interventions.

Gastric cancer (GC) is among the most common and lethal malignancies worldwide, with particularly high incidence in East Asian populations [9, 10]. In advanced stages, fluoropyrimidine‐ and platinum‐based chemotherapy has historically served as the primary treatment modality. However, patient outcomes remain poor due to frequent relapse and widespread metastasis [11, 12]. The advent of targeted therapies such as trastuzumab, an anti‐HER2 monoclonal antibody, has expanded treatment options and demonstrated survival benefits when combined with conventional chemotherapy [13]. More recently, ICIs such as nivolumab have provided durable responses in a subset of patients with GC, particularly when administered in combination with chemotherapy [14]. Despite these advances, clinical responses are highly heterogeneous and the determinants of treatment sensitivity and resistance remain largely unresolved.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) has emerged as a powerful tool to unravel cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communication within the TME with unprecedented resolution [15]. Recent studies applying scRNA‐seq to various cancers have revealed the complexity of myeloid cell states and their modulation by therapeutic agents [16]. Nevertheless, comprehensive single‐cell profiling of the TME ecosystem in patients undergoing chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy remains largely unexplored. Understanding these TME ecosystems is essential for revealing how cellular dynamics differ across therapies and whether such variability correlates with treatment sensitivity.

In this study, we performed scRNA‐seq analysis of GC biopsy samples from treatment‐naïve patients and patients treated with chemotherapy, chemotherapy plus nivolumab, or trastuzumab [17, 18]. This analysis revealed significant reprogramming of neutrophil and macrophage populations, particularly following chemotherapy combined with PD‐1 blockade. We identified a mature, activated neutrophil subset expressing major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules, which was associated with enhanced neutrophil–macrophage communication through multiple signaling pathways. These interactions were linked to macrophage polarization toward a pro‐inflammatory, antitumor M1 phenotype [19], suggesting a potential role for neutrophil–macrophage interactions in governing the immune dynamics of GC TMEs.

Materials and Methods

Cell Line

CT26 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

Ethics Statement

We enrolled patients with GC who underwent upper esophagogastroduodenoscopy at the National Cancer Center (NCC) Hospital East between September 2021 and November 2022 and provided written informed consent for study participation. This study was approved by our institutional review board (registration number 2021–019) and performed in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical information was obtained from electronic medical records. Clinical staging was performed according to the 8th edition of the TNM classification [20].

Tissue Processing

All sample fractions (~3 mm2) were acquired using endoscopic biopsy forceps from two to three GC tumor sites (patient details are provided in Figure S1A). Freshly obtained tissues were collected in 3 mL of saline solution on ice. Within 30 min after collection, tissue fractions were dissociated into single cells using a human tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany, Cat# 130–095‐929) and gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) at 37°C for 30 min. Dissociated samples were filtered through 70 and 30 μm MACS SmartStrainers (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130–098‐462 and 130–098‐458) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The pellets were then incubated with 1 mL of Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130–094‐183) for 2 min, followed by suspension in 10 mL DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After spinning down the cell pellets at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, the cells were re‐suspended in 1 mL of PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% FBS. The cell suspension was adjusted to 200–1500 cells/μl for single‐cell sequencing.

Single‐Cell Library Construction and Sequencing

Single‐cell libraries were constructed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3 GEM Library and Gel Bead Kit v.3.1 (10× Genomics, Cat# PN‐1000121) according to the manufacturer's instructions. One or two libraries were generated simultaneously for each sample. After gel bead‐in‐emulsion (GEM) generation, cDNA amplification and 3′ gene expression library construction were performed. Libraries underwent sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system.

Antitumor Efficacy Study

Antitumor efficacy studies were performed using the CT26 syngeneic mouse model at the National Cancer Center (NCC) in Japan. Female BALB/c mice (CLEA Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) were inoculated subcutaneously with 2 × 105 CT26 cells each (in 0.1 mL PBS) in the right flank. The mice were randomized 14 days after tumor inoculation and subsequently received intraperitoneal administration of test compounds (listed in Table S1) on Days 0 and 3. Tumor growth was monitored with Vernier calipers twice each week, and mean tumor volumes were calculated using the formula (0.5 × [length × width [2]]).

The protocol and any amendments or procedures involving the care and use of animals in this study were reviewed and approved by the National Cancer Center of Japan (approval number: K24014) before initiation of the study. Animal care and use were conducted in accordance with the regulations of the Association for the Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC).

Immune Cell Analysis in Tumors by Flow Cytometry

Tumor tissues were collected from mice on Day 7 and suspended using a Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Suspended cells were stained using an antibody cocktail (listed in Table S2) and incubated at 4°C for 1 h in the dark. Cells were analyzed using FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences), and the obtained data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v.10.8.1, BD Biosciences).

Results

ScRNA‐Seq Uncovers Dynamic Remodeling of the Tumor Microenvironment in GC Across Distinct Drug Treatments

To investigate the transcriptional dynamics of the TME under different drug treatments, we performed scRNA‐seq on endoscopically obtained GC biopsy samples [17, 18]. The samples were categorized into four groups based on the drugs: treatment‐naïve (TN; 8343 cells, n = 3), chemotherapy alone (C; 23,904 cells, n = 7), combined chemotherapy and nivolumab (CN; 11,986 cells, n = 5), and combined chemotherapy and trastuzumab (CT; 8973 cells, n = 3) (Figure 1A and Figure S1A [18]). Regarding clinical responses, the C group included four partial responses (PRs) and three progressive diseases (PDs), the CN cohort included two PRs and three stable diseases (SDs), and the CT group included three PDs.

High‐resolution clustering of the TME components in GC endoscopic biopsies was performed using a previously optimized scRNA‐seq platform [21]. The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) technique identified 40 distinct clusters, of which 14 were annotated by the scType package with cell type‐specific markers for TME cellular components: malignant cells (MC; 5.3%), classical monocytes (cMo; 7.4%), endothelial cells (EC; 4.9%), erythroid cells (0.056%), fibroblasts (FB; 1.4%), interferon‐stimulated gene (ISG) expressing immune cells (ISG; 1.8%), macrophages (Mφ; 8.0%), mast cells (Mal; 1.8%), dendritic cells (DC; 2.1%), naïve B cells (Bn; 2.5%), neutrophils (Neu; 14%), plasma B cells (B/P; 11%), T cells (T; 30%), and unclassified cells (UKN; 10%) (Figure 1B,C, Figure S1B,C). Accuracy for macrophage annotation was evaluated by canonical macrophage markers (C1QA, C1QB, C1QC, and APOE) in the whole‐cluster UMAP (Figure S1D). These markers were highly expressed in macrophage clusters but showed minimal expression in neutrophil clusters, supporting accurate segregation of macrophages with less contamination between macrophage and neutrophil subsets.

In UMAP, heterogeneity in TME composition across treatment groups was also observed (Figure 1D). In the C group, neutrophils, classical monocytes, and endothelial cells were preferentially enriched, whereas T cell levels were reduced, indicating a myeloid‐rich and immunosuppressive TME (Figure 1D,E). In contrast, in the CN group, macrophages were preferentially enriched, accompanied by a reduction in neutrophils, suggesting a T cell‐rich and immunologically active TME (Figure 1D,E). Of these, myeloid‐lineage immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages were markedly altered in abundance within the CN TME, although statistical significance was not reached because of the limited number of samples (Figure 1F). In contrast, the abundance of T cells and other cells changed minimally. (Figure 1E,F; Figure S1E).

To assess the effects of CN treatment on the transcriptomic profiles of cellular components of the GC TME, we performed differential gene expression (DEG) analysis between the CN and TN for the indicated cellular components. Most components exhibited extensive gene upregulation following CN treatment (Figure 1G and Figure S2A). Among these cellular components, myeloid lineage immune cells exhibited a particularly high number of upregulated genes. Importantly, a substantial proportion of the upregulated genes corresponded to activation markers such as CD74 in neutrophils, S100A8 and S100A9 in macrophages, and CD80 and CD86 in DCs. Cytokines and chemokines, including members of the CCL and TNF families, and MHC‐related molecules indicated activation of myeloid‐lineage immune cells in CN TMEs (Figure 1G and Figure S2A,B). In contrast, comparing DEGs in neutrophils between treatment groups revealed that a large number of downregulated DEGs were detected in the C and CT groups relative to TN (Figure S3A). Sample quality control (QC) metrics (nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, percent.mt) were assessed and no significant differences in QC metrics were detected between the CN group and the other three groups (Figure S3B). Taken together, these findings suggest that CN treatment drives the transcriptional reprogramming of myeloid cells within the TME, thereby contributing to TME remodeling.

Functional and Maturation Profiles of Neutrophils in Chemo+Nivo Treatment

Given that neutrophils underwent the most dramatic transcriptional remodeling in response to CN treatment (Figure 1 and Figure S1), we analyzed their transcriptional profiles across treatment groups. Neutrophils display functional heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment, exerting either pro‐ or antitumorigenic effects, depending on the TME context [22]. Despite technical challenges in single‐cell profiling of neutrophils due to their short half‐life and low RNA content [23], we successfully characterized transcriptionally distinct neutrophil subsets at high resolution.

Subcluster analysis of neutrophils revealed five transcriptionally distinct subclusters, each of which was characterized by unique marker genes: Neut1 (PADI4 + CPPED1+), Neut2 (MTRNR2L1 + CD177+), Neut3 (CXCL8 + CD69+), Neut4 (HLA‐DRA + CD74+), and Neut5 (SIGLEC15 + TGFBR1+) (Figure 2A,B). Notably, Neut1 expressed PADI4 and CPPED1, which are mediators of neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and involved in metastasis and immune evasion [24, 25]. Neut2 is defined by CD177 expression, a marker of poor prognosis in lung cancer [26], whereas Neut3 is characterized by the enrichment of CXCL8, a cytokine that drives tumor progression [27]. In contrast, Neut5 expresses SIGLEC15 and TGFBR1, molecules linked to immune suppression [28, 29], whereas Neut4 expresses HLA‐DRA and CD74, indicating its antigen presentation capacity and potential antitumor activity [30]. To exclude potential QC‐related artifacts, we also evaluated QC metrics of neutrophil subclusters (Figure S3C). Median mitochondrial gene percentages were consistently low across subclusters (1.4%–3.1%), with overlapping interquartile ranges, indicating that clustering was not driven by differences in cell viability. Although modest variations were observed in nFeature_RNA and nCount_RNA, no subcluster exhibited values suggestive of low‐quality or stressed cells. To further exclude potential macrophage contamination within neutrophil subclusters, we assessed the expression of canonical macrophage markers alongside neutrophil markers (S100A8 and S100A9) (Figure S3D). Canonical macrophage markers were minimally expressed across all neutrophil subclusters, whereas neutrophil markers were consistently and robustly detected, supporting the accurate annotation of neutrophil identities and arguing against substantial macrophage admixture. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed that the Neut4 exhibited significantly upregulated inflammation‐related hallmarks (Figure 2C and Figure S4A). We also performed pseudo‐time analysis to delineate transcriptional dynamics of these neutrophil subclusters. In pseudotime analysis, the transcriptional trajectory revealed a neutrophil transition from Neut2 to Neut5, Neut3, and Neut1, ultimately converting to Neut4 (Figure 2D and Figure S4B). This trajectory was also validated by quantitative pseudotime analysis, indicating that the Neut4 represents a more mature neutrophil state (Figure 2E). In this study, Neut2 was used as a starting point for pseudotime analyses; Neut2 appeared to occupy a relatively less differentiated or less polarized position (e.g., CXCR4 low expression) while Neut4 displayed features of a terminal activation phenotype (e.g., CXCR4 high expression) and was enriched in the CN treatment (Figure S4C).

Next, we analyzed the distribution of neutrophil subclusters across treatment groups. Neut1 was enriched in the TN, Neut2 and Neut5 were predominant in the C, whereas Neut4 was almost exclusively enriched in the CN (Figures 2F–H). Neutrophils in the CN group exhibited profound and statistically significant enrichment of inflammation‐related hallmarks, whereas neutrophils in group C tended to downregulate these hallmarks (Figure 2I and Figure S4D). More specifically, neutrophils in the CN exhibited higher expression of chemokines involved in immune recruitment, cytokine receptors associated with inflammatory responses (Figure 2J), and MHC class II genes involved in antitumor immunity, with a favorable prognosis [30] (Figure 2K). In addition, activation markers were also markedly upregulated (Figure S4E), suggesting that neutrophils in the CN were not only activated but also possessed enhanced inflammatory and antitumor properties. Furthermore, neutrophils in the CN expressed high CXCR4 and low CXCR2, a pattern consistent with the mature phenotype [31]. In contrast, neutrophils in the C exhibited low expression of both receptors, indicative of an immature state (Figure 2L), suggesting that the accumulation of mature Neut4 in the CN underlies their enhanced inflammatory and antitumor capacities. Given that the CN group included both clinical responders (PR) and non‐responders (SD), we evaluated whether the neutrophil transcriptional landscape was influenced by clinical outcomes. Remarkably, neutrophils in the CN exhibited conserved upregulation of inflammatory mediators, MHC class II–related genes, maturation‐associated markers, and activation signatures, independent of the clinical response (Figure S4F–I). Consistently, GSEA confirmed enhanced inflammatory signaling pathways in these neutrophils, irrespective of clinical outcome (Figure S4J). These results suggest that the transcriptional profiles uniquely observed in neutrophils in the CN were predominantly driven by the therapeutic intervention. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that CN treatment fostered the emergence of a transcriptionally distinct neutrophil population, distinguished by a mature, inflammatory, and potentially antitumor phenotype.

CN Treatment Enhances Neutrophil‐Macrophage Interactions and Modulates the TME

Given that the transcriptional profiles of neutrophils exhibited dynamic changes following drug treatment, we next investigated the involvement of neutrophils in TME ecosystems across different drug treatments. We used the CellChat package to visualize cell‐to‐cell interactions (CCIs) in TME ecosystems (Figure 3A). In TN TMEs, neutrophils engage in intensive communication with endothelial cells and themselves, while interacting moderately with macrophages, ISG‐expressing immune cells, plasma/B cells, and classical monocytes. In group C, neutrophil‐mediated communication declined sharply. Conversely, the combination of CN amplified neutrophil interactions beyond baseline levels, with particularly strengthened crosstalk with macrophages. In contrast, when we analyzed macrophages as primary senders, we found that their interactions with neutrophils were only modestly altered by CN treatment (Figure S5A). This finding highlights neutrophils as a prominent sender of signals to macrophages within the TME, suggesting that the neutrophil‐to‐macrophage signaling axis is a key target for remodeling by therapeutic intervention, particularly by CN treatment.

To investigate the neutrophil‐centered ecosystem in the TME in more detail, we analyzed neutrophil‐mediated interactions by focusing on the cytokine and chemokine ligand–receptor (LR) pairs across treatment regimens. Enrichment analyses revealed that cytokine and chemokine LR pairs, which are mediated by neutrophils as key senders, were significantly increased in the CN compared to those in the other cohorts (Figure 3B and Figure S5B). In a focused interaction between neutrophils and macrophages, 1, 2, 11, and 3 LR pairs were significantly enriched in these respective groups (p < 0.01), indicating that cytokine and/or chemokine interactions between neutrophils and macrophages were markedly increased in CN TMEs. More specifically, the LR pairs regarding the CCL signaling were profoundly enriched in the CN group, suggesting enhanced recruitment and differentiation of macrophages [23, 32]. In contrast, the LR pair regarding the VEGF, TNF and IL1A/B signaling was uniquely enriched in the CN group, suggesting that the inflammatory and angiogenic signals provided by neutrophils drive macrophages toward an inflammatory‐associated phenotype within the CN TMEs. Violin plots also confirmed that the number of key ligands of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines was significantly elevated in neutrophils within the CN TMEs (Figure S6A). These findings were corroborated by LR analyses using the CrossTalkR package, which showed that chemokine‐mediated signaling was markedly enhanced in CN TMEs (Figure 3C and Figure S5C). In the network centrality analysis of the inferred CCL signaling, neutrophils in the CN emerged as key senders of CCL signaling (marked with a red box, Figure S6B).

In addition to their active role as key senders, neutrophils also emerged as potential receivers and assimilated diverse signals from other cellular components in the CN TMEs (Figure 3D and Figure S6C). More specifically, macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) signaling pathways, which promote neutrophil survival [33], are upregulated in CN TMEs originating from various cell types. Conversely, ANXA1 signaling pathways, which function to suppress neutrophil‐driven inflammatory responses [34], were notably absent from neutrophil‐receiving enriched pathways in the CN TMEs (Figure 3E and Figure S7A). In gene expression analysis, CD74 and CD44, both of which are key mediators of the MIF signaling pathway, were considerably upregulated in neutrophils within the CN TMEs (Figure 3F). Network centrality analysis of the inferred MIF signaling pathways revealed that neutrophils in the CN emerged as central receivers, underscoring their active role as the receiving hub for MIF signaling within TMEs, which is known to promote neutrophil survival (marked with a red box, Figure S7B). In summary, neutrophils in CN function as communication partners with macrophages via specific chemokine and cytokine signaling pathways, exhibiting enhanced survival and activation, and collectively contributing to antitumor responses.

CN Treatment Induces Pro‐Inflammatory Macrophage Polarization

Given the intercellular interactions between neutrophils and macrophages in the CN, we also investigated the transcriptomic profiles of macrophage clusters in these TMEs. Subclustering analysis revealed four transcriptionally distinct macrophage subclusters characterized by molecular marker genes: MφC1 (SPP1 + CLEC5A + ), MφC2 (CCR2 + CX3CR1 + ), MφC3 (CXCL10 + , M1‐like), and MφC4 (MRC1 + , M2‐like) (Figure 4A–C). MφC1 expressed SPP1 and CLEC5A, which are preferential markers for immunosuppressive and inflammatory TMEs, contributing to tumor progression and therapy resistance [35]. MφC2 expressed CCR2 and CX3CR1, which are receptors involved in monocyte recruitment and macrophage retention, suggesting that MφC2 is associated with poor prognosis [36]. MφC3 expressed CXCL10 and TNF, which are characteristics of M1‐like macrophages, highlighting potential antitumor activity [37, 38], while MφC4 expressed MRC1 and CSF1R, which are canonical markers of M2‐like macrophages, indicating a role in pro‐tumor immune modulation [39, 40]. GSEA further confirmed that the MφC3 robustly and significantly upregulated inflammation‐related gene signatures (Figure 4D and Figure S8A). Furthermore, gene expression analysis also demonstrated substantial upregulation of inflammation‐related genes in both the MφC3 and MφC1, which included numbers of cytokines, chemokines and transcripts related to TNF signaling pathways (Figure 4E). In MHC‐related gene expression, the MφC3 robustly upregulated MHC class I genes, while the MφC4 upregulated MHC class II molecules (Figure 4F). These results indicate that enhanced MHC‐I expression in MφC3 could promote cross‐presentation and subsequent activation of CD8+ T cells, thereby potentiating antitumor immune responses. In contrast, upregulation of MHC‐II–related molecules in the MφC4 may facilitate tumor progression by enhancing interactions with immunosuppressive CD4+ T‐cell subsets such as regulatory or Th2‐T cells, thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive TME.

Next, we examined the distribution of these macrophage sub‐clusters across treatment groups (Figure 4G and Figure S8B). In the TN, the distributions of MφC1, MφC2, and MφC4 were comparable, whereas the distribution of the MφC3 was markedly diminished. In the C, the distribution of MφC1 was reduced, while MφC4 was expanded relative to the TN. In the CN, by contrast, the distributions of MφC1 and MφC3 were substantially increased, whereas those of MφC2 and MφC4 were correspondingly reduced. Strikingly, the MφC3 was predominantly in the CN, highlighting the potential role of the MφC3 as a key immunological determinant in the CN TMEs.

Consistently, in the GSEA compared between the treatment groups, macrophages in the CN significantly upregulated inflammation‐related gene signatures (Figure 4H and Figure S8C). Gene expression analyses further revealed that macrophages in the CN robustly upregulated a broad set of inflammation‐related genes, including those involved in immune cell recruitment and cytokine signaling (Figure 4I). In parallel, multiple MHC class I genes were markedly elevated in these macrophages, consistent with their enhanced capacity for antigen presentation and promotion of antitumor immunity (Figure 4J).

Given that the CN included both PR and SD, we evaluated whether the macrophage transcriptional landscape was influenced by clinical outcomes. In GSEA, macrophages in the CN tended to exhibit conserved upregulation of inflammation‐related gene signatures which were independent of the clinical response (Figure S8D). Moreover, the distribution of subclusters across treatment groups exhibited similar trends, independent of the therapeutic response (Figure S8E). These results suggest that the transcriptional profiles uniquely observed in macrophages in the CN were predominantly driven by the therapeutic intervention. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CN treatment promoted the emergence of a transcriptionally distinct macrophage population distinguished by an inflammatory and potentially antitumor phenotype.

Neutrophils Contribute to the Antitumor Efficacy of Chemotherapy Combined With PD‐1 Blockade in a Syngeneic CT26 Tumor Model

To validate the functional contribution of neutrophils to the TME, we performed in vivo preclinical studies using a CT26‐allografted syngeneic mouse model. Since immunocompetent GC models have been reported to show limited responses to PD‐1 blockade [20, 41], we selected CT26 for the functional studies in combination with PD‐1 blockade, which is well‐established as PD‐1‐responsive models with an immune‐active microenvironment [42]. The allografted mice were treated with vehicle control (Veh), oxaliplatin (Oxia; a representative chemotherapeutic agent), mouse anti‐PD‐1 antibody (mPD‐1), or their combination (Combo) according to the indicated regimens. Neutrophil depletion was achieved by administering an anti‐Ly6G antibody to the combination treatment group (Figure 5A). While we did not have anti–Ly6G monotherapy control data in this study, previous reports indicate that neutrophil depletion alone, in the absence of checkpoint blockade, exerts minimal impact on tumor growth, with tumor progression comparable to vehicle‐treated controls in the CT‐26 model [43]. In vivo studies demonstrated that both single‐agent mPD‐1 and combination treatment exerted significant antitumor activity. In contrast, neutrophil depletion markedly attenuated the antitumor efficacy of the combination therapy, resulting in tumor volumes comparable to those observed in the Veh and Oxia groups (Figure 5B–E).

Next, we analyzed tumor‐infiltrating immune cells to delineate the dynamic changes in intra‐tumoral macrophages in response to drug treatment. Using the indicated molecular markers, we isolated each myeloid cell population (Figure 5F and Figure S9A). Flow cytometric analyses demonstrated that the anti‐Ly6G antibody treatment effectively depleted neutrophils (Figure 5G and Figure S9B). Although total neutrophil numbers were slightly reduced in the mPD‐1 and Combo groups, the proportion of MHC‐II+ neutrophils was increased compared with Veh or Oxia cohorts (Figure 5H,I). In addition, M1 macrophages were enriched in mPD‐1 and Combo groups, whereas M2 macrophages displayed the opposite trend. Importantly, neutrophil depletion selectively reduced M1 macrophages in the mPD‐1 and Combo allografts (Figure 5J and Figure S9C). The overall proportion of macrophages remained unchanged across all treatment cohorts (Figure 5K and Figure S9D). Together with the scRNA‐seq data in primary gastric TMEs (Figure 2), these preclinical results demonstrate that combination chemotherapy with PD‐1 blockade promotes the emergence of immune‐active MHC‐II+ neutrophils which may contribute to macrophage polarization toward a pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype.

Discussion

In this study, by performing high‐resolution scRNA‐seq profiling in clinical GC specimens, we demonstrated that neutrophil–macrophage crosstalk constitutes a key feature of the drug‐perturbed GC TME, driving inflammatory activation and reshaping immune cell communication. In agreement with our clinical observations, preclinical models have also demonstrated that neutrophils orchestrate macrophage activation in the TME, particularly under combination chemotherapy and ICI regimens. These findings highlight the potential role of neutrophil–macrophage interactions in governing immune dynamics of the GC TME (Figure 6).

Schematic representation of neutrophil‐macrophage interactions shaping inflammatory macrophage remodeling during chemo‐immunotherapy in gastric cancer.PD‐1 blockade combined with chemotherapy induces MHC‐II+ inflammatory neutrophils in gastric cancer, which engage in dynamic crosstalk with macrophages to remodel the tumor microenvironment toward a pro‐inflammatory state, thereby amplifying antitumor immune responses.

Distinct TME ecosystems across treatment groups were identified using endoscopically obtained GC microbiopsies. Drug‐exposed tumor samples are rarely accessible and their molecular profiling is technically challenging. Our approach, using freshly processed microbiopsies, enabled high‐quality scRNA‐seq profiling even in treatment‐phase samples. Notably, we identified that neutrophil–macrophage crosstalk was markedly reinforced within the CN TMEs, suggesting ICI‐induced remodeling of the myeloid compartment in GC TMEs. More specifically, neutrophils in the CN exhibited a mature and activated phenotype, which considerably upregulated inflammatory‐related genes and antigen presentation molecules of MHC class II, showing enrichment of “aged neutrophils” to suggest immune modulation and antitumor activity. Trajectory analysis also demonstrated neutrophil maturation with advanced differentiation in the CN TMEs.

In CN treatment, neutrophils demonstrated 1. upregulation of IFN‐, TNF‐, IL‐, and MHC‐II gene signatures, 2. acquisition of CXCR4HIGH; CXCR2LOW mature phenotype, and 3. enrichment of HLA‐DRA+; CD74+ populations in the terminal region of the pseudo‐time trajectory. In ligand–receptor analysis, neutrophils were identified as principal recipients of MIF signaling. Collectively, these data indicate that a cytokine‐ and IFN‐enriched TME under CN treatment promotes differentiation toward an antigen‐presenting mature neutrophil phenotype, while MIF‐dependent signaling sustains neutrophil activation and survival. In parallel, neutrophil‐to‐macrophage CCL3/4/5 signaling reinforces a positive‐feedback inflammatory circuit in the TME. In addition, we analyzed the expression of costimulatory (CD80 and CD86) and costimulatory‐like molecule (ICAM1) in neutrophils (Figure 2B). Although CD80 and CD86 were not detectably expressed, ICAM1 expression was profoundly upregulated in HLA‐DRA+CD74+ neutrophil in the CN. It has been reported that binding of ICAM1 with LFA‐1 promotes stable interactions between neutrophils and T cells to enhance T‐cell activation and its effector function [44], despite neutrophils not being considered professional antigen‐presenting cells. ICAM1 expression in neutrophils has also been reported to be upregulated in murine models of inflammation. These findings suggest that CN therapy may enhance the capacity of tumor‐infiltrating neutrophils to support T‐cell activation within the TME as well.

Polymorphonuclear myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (PMN‐MDSCs) are generally defined as immature or pathologically activated neutrophil‐like cells with immunosuppressive functions, typically characterized by the expression of suppressive mediators such as OLR1 and ARG1. In our dataset, OLR1 expression was detected in three neutrophil subclusters (Neu1, Neu3, and Neu4), which are expected to be immune‐active/matured neutrophils based on their DEG profiles (Figure 2A,B). On the contrary, classical suppressive mediators such as ARG1 were not detected in these clusters. These findings suggest that these OLR‐positive neutrophil subpopulations may partially overlap with reported PMN‐MDSC phenotypes but lack definitive suppressive signatures.

In terms of neutrophil‐mediated networks in TME ecosystems, interactions between neutrophils and macrophages through chemokine and cytokine signaling were profoundly and selectively enriched in CN TMEs. In alignment with the involvement of neutrophil‐macrophage crosstalk in the survival and inflammatory polarization of macrophages [45], macrophages in CN TMEs also exhibited immune‐active features linked to robust antitumor immunity: upregulation of inflammatory mediators, enrichment of TNF‐related hallmarks, and conversion to an M1‐like phenotype. In preclinical studies of syngeneic mouse models, neutrophil depletion experiments demonstrated reduced M1‐like macrophage polarization, supporting the role of neutrophils in shaping the pro‐inflammatory macrophage phenotype within the TME. Together, these findings highlight an underappreciated mechanism by which ICIs reprogram the TME, not solely via T‐cell reinvigoration, as traditionally emphasized, but also through myeloid‐centered remodeling, which promotes innate immune activation and intercellular coordination.

Our study also underscores the translational impact of the neutrophil–macrophage axis on GC TMEs. First, the maturation and/or activation states of neutrophils may serve as predictive biomarkers for chemo‐immunotherapy regimens. Recent clinical studies have indicated that an elevated neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio or an increase in circulating immature neutrophils is associated with poorer responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors and worse overall survival across several cancers [46]. However, these bulk blood metrics provide limited insights into cell state diversity. Therefore, our single‐cell transcriptome profiling of GC offers a more nuanced view of intratumoral neutrophil heterogeneity, suggesting that specific maturation or activation states, rather than total neutrophil counts, may be stronger predictors of chemo‐immunotherapeutic outcomes. Second, the chemokine and cytokine signaling pathways identified in this study may represent potential therapeutic targets for enhancing macrophage‐mediated antitumor immunity. Third, pharmacological modulation of the neutrophil‐macrophage axis may promote M1 polarization, offering a therapeutic strategy to synergize with ICIs. Neutrophil‐derived CC chemokines are potent activators of macrophages through CCR1‐CCR5 signaling, leading to NF‐κB activation and expression of proinflammatory mediators such as TNF and IL‐12. Previous studies have shown that stimulation of these pathways promotes M1 polarization and enhances antigen presentation and cytotoxic T‐cell priming [47, 48]. The elevated expression of these chemokines in CN suggests that combination therapy may exploit neutrophil–macrophage communication to reinforce innate immune activation and antitumor immunity.

While this study provides novel insights into neutrophil‐macrophages, several experimental limitations should be considered. First, the relatively small number of clinical samples may limit statistical power and generalizability. Second, pre‐ and post‐comparisons were performed based on independent, non‐paired samples from different patients and the sampling time points were not precisely controlled. Therefore, although this hypothesis was tested in well‐controlled murine studies, we cannot completely rule out the potential influence of interpatient tumor heterogeneity on the results. Consistent with these limitations, clinical response analyses should be interpreted with caution. As shown in Figure 1A, PR was observed in 4 of 7 patients in Group C (3/7 PD) and in 2 of 5 patients in Group CN (3/5 SD). Although Group CN showed a favorable trend toward a higher disease control rate (100% versus 57.1%), a definitive clinical benefit in terms of PR rate could not be established. We speculate that this discrepancy primarily reflects the limited cohort size, which was underpowered for formal statistical comparison. Larger prospective studies will be required to validate these associations. Third, the absence of spatial transcriptomic information prevents the direct mapping of cellular interactions in situ, and incorporating spatially resolved approaches in future studies will be essential to validate and extend the observed neutrophil–macrophage crosstalk.

In conclusion, we demonstrated the potential role of neutrophil–macrophage interactions in remodeling of the immunological architecture of GC TMEs, which underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting this myeloid axis to optimize immunotherapy responsiveness. These findings offer translational frameworks for identifying biomarkers, developing novel therapeutic strategies, and ultimately improving the clinical outcomes of patients with GC.
