Efficacy of oral and rectal administration of human faecal microbiota transplant (FMT) in human microbiota-associated mouse models
Authors: Petra Hanzely, Kristian Holm, Jørgen V. Bjørnholt, Espen Melum, Johannes R. Hov, Henrik Rasmussen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiome has gained significant interest due to its association with immune dysregulation, allergies, autoimmune conditions, metabolic disorders, and inflammation-associated malignancies. Understanding underlying mechanisms requires appropriate in vivo models, such as human microbiota-associated mouse models to study the microbiota–host interactions. This study compared the efficacy of oral and rectal administration of human faecal microbiota transplant (FMT) from a single donor in C57BL/6J germ-free mice as these methods are often used interchangeably. Using 16S rRNA sequencing, we quantified colonisation efficacy in luminal and tissue samples from orally- (n = 6) and rectally- (n = 6) colonised mice. We detected 84 genera in the FMT sample, 17 of which were not transferred at all, while additional 7 genera were found exclusively in rectally-colonised mice. A significantly higher proportion of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (33% vs. 26%; P < 0.05) and genera (32% vs. 25%; P < 0.05) were absent in orally- compared to rectally-colonised group. Some taxa showed different relative abundances in human vs. mouse samples (e.g. Alistipes and Bacteroides relatively more abundant in mice while Faecalibacterium considerably decreased). Beta diversity analysis revealed greater similarity between FMT and tissue samples irrespective of the administration route, with distinct separation of the tissue and luminal samples. Overall, rectal administration yielded more overlapping genera and ASVs with FMT, highlighting that it could have advantages compared with oral administration for microbiota establishment studies.

Introduction

The human gut microbiota plays a crucial role in health and disease. Many chronic inflammatory diseases such as autoimmune conditions and metabolic disorders have been associated with changes in microbial composition1–3, but the underlying mechanisms are in general not understood well4. Therefore, appropriate mechanistic models for studying the complex microbiota-host interactions are warranted.

Gnotobiotic mouse models are important tools to study the effect of individual microbes or defined complex bacterial mixtures in mice. Of particular interest, and of relevance to human health, are models where a complete human microbiota is transferred to mice to observe the effects, often referred to as human microbiota-associated (HMA) murine models. These models offer the unique advantage of studying host-microbiota interactions in vivo, and several disease phenotypes have been reported to be transmissible by the gut microbiota only5,6. However, engraftment efficacy of the human faecal microbiota into mice has been reported to vary between 44 to 88% due to anatomical, genetic, behavioural, and physiological differences between the donor and the recipient7–9. Moreover, the preparation of faecal microbiota transplant (FMT) can play a crucial role in microbiota recovery, and hence it is not surprising that various protocols for FMT preparation and administration exist in both research and clinical practice10,11.

The International Human Microbiome Standards (IHMS) project was established to coordinate procedure and protocol standardization within Human Microbiome research programs and to develop standard operating procedures (SOPs) for faecal sample collection, identification, and processing. SOPs for self‐collection, and subsequent laboratory analysis within 4 to 24 h emphasise high standards for clean/sterile sampling and processing, and generation of anaerobic condition for sample protection12.

Another consideration in HMA mouse models is the route of administration. Co-housing, oral-gastric gavage and rectal administration are in general the three routes used for administration in mice, out of which only the latter two are suitable for human-mouse transfer. Oral-gastric gavage is a common and preferred method but its downsides for animals are potential pharyngeal/oesophageal/gastric irritation or injury with stricture formation, aspiration pneumonia, and oesophageal and gastric rupture13. Additionally, there is less enzymatic activity in colon/rectum compared to more proximal parts of the gut and by rectal administration FMT is directly placed in the colon where anaerobic conditions are first established in germ-free (GF) animals. Downsides of this route are the risk of colonic puncture and potential leakage from the rectum14.

The present study aimed to assess and compare the feasibility and engraftment efficacy of human FMT in mice after oral-gastric and rectal administration of a cryopreserved human stool sample.

Results

The effect of colonisation by human FMT on general health

Twelve mice allocated to two groups (equal sex distribution): orally-colonised and rectally-colonised mice were used in the present study (Fig. 1a). Prior to the FMT administration, the germ-free status of the mice was verified by anaerobic and aerobic culturing of faecal samples. None of the mice exhibited signs of distress or discomfort following dosing and all were gaining weight during entire experimental period (Fig. 1b). Colonisation efficacy following human FMT

To evaluate colonisation, efficacy analysis of individual bacteria was performed at both ASV level (i.e. unique sequence) to define exact efficacy, and binned into different taxonomic levels (e.g. genus) to study transmission patterns. Bacteria were categorised into three groups; i) detected both in the FMT and in the mouse samples; ii) detected in the FMT but not in the mouse samples, i.e. no colonisation or present at an abundance below the detection limit of the 16S rRNA sequencing, and iii) detected in the mouse samples but not in the FMT, i.e. likely present at low abundance in the human sample but expanding in mice.

In total, we detected 175 ASVs and 84 genera present in the FMT sample, while in the orally-colonised mice we detected 262 ASVs and 87 genera, and in the rectally-colonised mice 301 ASVs and 95 genera. Out of all detected bacteria, the proportion present in both the human FMT sample and the mice was higher in the rectally-colonised than the orally-colonised group (mean value of 54% vs. 49% on genus level, P = 0.074; mean value of 36% vs. 32%, when evaluated at ASV level, P = 0.048) (Fig. 2a,b), with 67 genera and 138 ASVs detected in rectally-colonised group compared with 60 genera and 113 ASVs in orally-colonised group. In line with this, a higher proportion of bacteria unable to colonise mice was observed in the orally-colonised compared with the rectally-colonised mice (genera: mean value of 32% vs. 25%, P = 0.039; ASVs: mean value of 33% vs. 26%, P = 0.027) (Fig. 2a,b). Overall, 24 genera and 62 ASVs were not detected in orally-colonised mice, whereas 17 genera and 37 ASVs were absent in rectally-colonised mice. As already said, 17 genera were not detected in any mice despite their presence in the FMT. Out of them, two belong to Actinobacteriota, namely Bifidobacterium and Collinsella, which had relative abundances in the human sample of 10% and 2%, respectively. All others belonged to Firmicutes (i.e. Bacillota in the most updated nomenclature), with the majority categorised as part of the Lachnospiraceae family (Table 1). Additionally, seven other genera were not detected in any orally-colonised mice while being detected in the rectally-colonised group (Table 2). No genera were found exclusively in orally-colonised mice, while being absent in rectally-colonised mice, suggesting better engraftment of FMT via rectal route. On average, 35% of the ASVs not present in the faecal microbiota transplant were detected in the orally-colonised group and 39% in the rectally-colonised group (P = 0.018) (Fig. 2b). At the genus level, this corresponded to on average 19% and 20% in the orally and rectally-colonised group, respectively (P = 0.096) (Fig. 2a).

Compositional differences between human and mouse samples were also apparent when looking at the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio, a parameter widely used as a marker of dysbiosis15,16. While no overall difference was observed between rectally and orally-colonised groups, all samples showed a lower F/B ratio than the original FMT sample. Notably, in luminal samples, the ratio dropped below 1, indicating an overrepresentation of Bacteroidota—opposite to the FMT sample, where phylum Firmicutes was more abundant (Fig. 2c).

When comparing relative abundances of bacteria at phylum level between the FMT sample and the experimental groups, a drop in Actinobacteriota (Bifidobacterium, Collinsella) was observed in the experimental groups (Fig. 2d). Differences between mouse tissue (ileum, caecum, and colon) and luminal samples (faeces and caecal content) were also obvious, with higher relative abundances of phylum Bacteroidota in luminal samples (Fig. 2d). There was also a very high relative abundance of Alistipes in luminal samples (mean in different samples of 5–22%), despite a low relative abundance in the FMT sample (0.3%) (Fig. 2e). Considering the relative abundances of the top 10 genera in the global dataset, we can see the major drop mostly in Faecalibacterium as well as increase in Bacteroides in the mice compared to the FMT sample (Fig. 2e).

Global assessments of microbiota composition

Finally, we considered the colonisation efficacy of the global composition. The intra-individual (alpha) diversity of the mouse samples was consistently lower compared to FMT when using a measure of richness, i.e. observed features while no significant differences were observed between the oral and rectal groups (Fig. 3a). In the next step, we analysed global composition using the beta diversity metrics Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and weighted UniFrac. There was clear separation of the tissue and luminal samples (Permanova, P = 0.001), with the FMT overlapping with the mouse tissue samples when evaluated by Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) of the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (Fig. 4a). The taxa driving differences between luminal and tissue microbiota were the genera belonging to Lachnospiraceae family, which were enriched in mucosal samples, while genera enriched in luminal samples belonged to Rikenellaceae, Barnesiellaceae and Bacteroidaceae (some examples are Alistipes, Barnesiella and Bacteroides) (Fig. 4b). However, the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity indicated a relatively high dissimilarity (values closer to 1) between the mouse and FMT samples with no difference between experimental groups (Fig. 4c). Discussion

In the present study we investigated the colonisation efficacy of human FMT in germ-free mice comparing oral and rectal administration. We found that neither route resulted in adverse effects, with the rectal route demonstrating higher colonisation rates. We observed significantly greater overlap of detected ASVs between the FMT and the rectally-colonised mice compared to the orally-colonised mice. A similar trend of overlapping detection in the FMT and the rectally-colonised mice was evident at the genus level. Additionally, significantly more ASVs and genera from the FMT sample were not detected in the orally-colonised mice compared to the rectally-colonised mice. In total, seven genera detected in the rectally-colonised mice were not observed in any orally-colonised mouse. Overall, this suggests a slightly better outcome of the rectal administration route.

The engraftment efficacy of human FMT seemed very comparable to similar studies showing range from 44 to 88%7–9,17,18, while generally higher establishment efficacy around 90% can be observed in the case of allogenic FMT19. We detected 17 genera that could not be recovered in any mice, including Bifidobacterium, which was not established irrespective of the administration route despite its quite high relative abundance (10%) in the human FMT. These results are in line with a study by Lundberg et al. (2020) which also showed that Bifidobacterium was unable to establish in the mouse gut17. Interestingly, the bifidobacterial strain B. bifidum S17 initially isolated from a breast-fed infant was shown to colonise GF mice in mono-colonisation settings, whereas its transfer into SPF mice did not result in stable colonisation20. A possible explanation is that Bifidobacterium cannot compete against other bacteria present in the full human FMT and are consequently lost in HMA mouse models. Moreover, transferring complex human microbiota into mice resulted in different relative abundances of some important inhabitants of human gastrointestinal tract, such as Faecalibacterium and Bacteroides. Faecalibacterium dominated the human donor sample, with relative abundance of more than 25%. Its relative abundance in mice was clearly lowered, which is in line with a previous study in C57BL/6NTac mice17. On the other hand, relative abundance of Bacteroides was below 10% in the human donor sample, whereas its relative abundance was around 25% in the mouse mucosal tissue samples while reaching 60% in the luminal samples. Other studies using human inoculum to colonise GF mice have also showed Bacteroides being dramatically increased in HMA mice despite a low abundance in the human donor sample8,17.

When examining the overall composition at both the phylum and genus levels, we found that the tissue samples more closely resembled the human FMT sample compared to the luminal samples. Moreover, this was also shown by separation of luminal and tissue samples visualised on PCoA plot, while tissue samples clustered together with FMT. However, when analysing tissue and luminal samples of the orally- and rectally-colonised group, the numbers of detected and absent FMT-derived genera and ASVs were quite similar. We could detect many enriched genera in luminal samples such as Alistipes, Barnesiella and Bacteroides, while having low abundance in human FMT sample. On the other hand, examples of bacterial genera enriched in mucosal samples are Hungatella and Lachnoclostridium. Futhermore, the luminal samples showed an overrepresentation of the phylum Bacteroidota, as indicated by the F/B ratio. Overall, the data are not sufficient to definitely claim whether the engrafted microbiota in tissue was more similar to the FMT than the luminal microbiota.

In human microbiota studies, stool samples are typically used due to their non-invasiveness, affordability and convenience21,22. Rodent models, however, offer the unique advantage, as different sample types are available. While majority of the research studies are focused on luminal samples, such as faeces or different intestinal contents, a key strength of this study is the inclusion of mucosal samples. These, as shown, provide extra level of complexity and should be considered in future studies. Another important strength is the way how FMT is prepared. It has been shown that processing of FMT in ambient air results in significant decrease of bacterial viability23. Therefore, oxygen exposure should be avoided to the highest possible rate. We used a protocol adapted from International Microbiome Standards12 with emphasis on handling, hence we used anaerobic collection, and the human sample was processed in nitrogen-rich condition and in the presence of L-cysteine to preserve oxygen-sensitive bacteria. Lastly, when performing rectal administration, the use of chloral hydrate as a mild sedative has been suggested14,24. However, to avoid potential confounding effects of anaesthesia, and to minimize stress from sedation and recovery, we performed rectal administration without anaesthesia. Instead, brief physical restraint was used, and the gavage tube was lubricated with the FMT solution itself. This approach reduced the risk of contamination without increase in severity and consistent animal welfare conditions between administration routes.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. It needs to be emphasised that a pelleted irradiated rodent diet has been used in the present study. Since gut microbiota composition is highly affected by the dietary substrate7,25 the lack of the mouse and human donor diet matching is one of the limitations of the study that could influence bacterial engraftment. However, this is unlikely to have an impact on differences between the oral and rectal routes. Additionally, this study was designed as a proof-of-concept comparison study to test if the route of delivery can affect the colonisation outcomes, therefore one human FMT sample was used. Further studies would be needed to determine how broadly these findings apply. It also has to be emphasised that the relatively small sample size used may limit statistical power. The observation of certain bacteria in mice but not in the FMT sample suggests they may have been present below detection limit, highlighting the value of deeper sequencing approaches in future studies. It is also difficult to fully exclude that contamination in the experimental phase was the cause of genera detectable in mice only, although it is unlikely that this would appear as complex bacterial mixtures across multiple cages. Lastly, reliance on 16S rRNA sequencing, while informative for taxonomic profiling, may fail to detect low-abundant taxa or provide functional resolution.

It should be emphasised that both administration routes have specific advantages and disadvantages. Gastric acids and aerobic conditions during small intestinal transit may affect colonisation efficacy, particularly for anaerobic bacteria in orally-colonised mice. By rectal administration of human FMT directly into the colon, the microbiota will be located in the intestinal segment where anaerobic conditions are first established in GF animals. This should be taken into consideration especially when colonising mice with single anaerobic bacteria, when oral administration can lead to unsuccessful transfer.

There is currently no consensus on the optimal FMT dosing. While single dosing is the most commonly used in GF mice7,9,18,26, several doses are typically applied in case of antibiotic-treated or conventional mice27,28. In the present study we chose to administer FMT twice with one day gap to increase colonisation efficacy29,30. However, further studies are needed to determine whether alternative protocols such as repeated administration in later time points (e.g., two weeks post-FMT) may influence engraftment outcomes. Given the variability of engraftment overall using only one donor and one mouse strain it is also important to emphasise that e.g. different donors or different mouse strains could influence the impact of administration route on FMT efficacy. A detailed assessment of this would require a more complex design and should be considered a priority in further studies.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that rectal administration yielded similar, yet slightly better, outcomes compared to oral gavage. Taken together with no risk of aspiration during dosing and deposition in the intestinal niche most suited for anaerobic bacteria, this could represent an argument for rectal administration.

Methods

Mice

Germ-free C57BL/6J mice were bred and housed in open cages (Eurostandard type II, 1144B, Tecniplast, Buguggiate, Italy) in a sterile flexible film isolator at the Department of Comparative Medicine at Oslo University Hospital (Rikshospitalet, Oslo, Norway). Mice were kept in a humidity (55 ± 10%) and temperature (21 ± 1 °C) -controlled environment on 12 h dark/12 h light cycle during entire period. The mice had free access to sterilised tap water and standard autoclaved chow Lab diet 5021 (Lab Diet, IPS Products Supplies, Alfreton, Great Britain). At seven weeks of age, the mice (6 males and 6 females) were exported into autoclaved individually ventilated cages (IVCs, Green Line GM500, Tecniplast, Buguggiate, Italy) with sterile bedding and nesting material and enrichment in groups of three. First week served as physiological acclimatisation to a new housing condition and potentially cage groups. After the export, the mice were fed the irradiated diet NP021-740 (Ssniff, Soest, Germany). The germ-free status was verified by repeated culturing of faecal samples before the animals were included in the study and at the day of FMT administration. Briefly, faecal samples were seeded in brain heart infusion broth and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 5 days, followed by incubation on chocolate agar for 18–24 h. Additionally, the samples were plated onto human milk oligosaccharide agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h in an anaerobic atmosphere generated by ANOXOMAT Mark II AN2CTS (Mart Microbiology, Lichtenvoorde, the Netherlands) (85% N2, 10% H2 and 5% CO2) or anaerobic generator Oxoid AnaeroGen (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

All procedures were performed under sterile conditions at the same time during the day from 14.00–15.00 by two operators (P.H. and H.R.)31 and to minimise the effect of potential confounders, the animal cages were kept in the same rack and the same row during experiment. The animal experiment was approved by the Norwegian Food Safety Authority (ID25770) and was conducted according to the European Directive 2010/63/EU, ARRIVE guidelines and the Norwegian Animal research legislation.

Preparation of faecal microbiota transplant

A healthy donor (31 years old Caucasian female; body mass index within the healthy range (18.5–24.9 kg/m2); non-vegetarian and no history of antibiotic and probiotic treatment for at least three months before stool submission) was recruited to the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and provided written informed consent prior to their involvement in the study. The use of human material was approved by the Regional Ethical Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics South-Eastern Norway (project number 605276). Since the donor was recruited to research only, the stool has not been administered in clinical settings. A stool sample was collected according to International Microbiome Standards12. Briefly, fresh stool sample was collected at home directly in a sterile blender bag (VWR International, Radnor, PA, USA) placed in a sterile sample container (VWR International) under anaerobic conditions using the anaerobic atmosphere generation bags (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Louis, MO, USA) and delivered to the laboratory the same day. The stool sample was then further processed under nitrogen-rich conditions. Ten grams of stool sample was resuspended in sterile PBS containing 0.1% L-cysteine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich) as reducing agent (w/v 1:5), homogenised, filtered through sterile gauze and 100 µm cell strainer, and centrifuged (4000 g/10 min. at 20 °C without brake). The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS containing 10% glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich) to a volume of half of original volume. The faecal microbiota transplant was aliquoted in 0.7 to 1 ml aliquots, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until required for use.

Experimental design

Eight-week-old GF mice were inoculated with 200 μl of human FMT twice with 1 day gap either by oral-gastric gavage (hereafter referred to as oral) (n = 6) or rectal administration (n = 6) using 18G stainless gavage feeding tube during brief physical restraint. The mice were followed for 4 weeks (Fig. 1a). Fresh faeces were collected from each mouse after three and four weeks of colonisation. The samples were stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. At the end of the experiment, the animals were euthanised with CO2 and mucosal tissue samples from distal ileum, caput caecum and proximal colon and caecal content samples were collected from each mouse and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. Oesophagus and colon tissue were harvested and fixed in 10% buffered paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin blocks, sectioned (3 μm), and stained with H&E. The colon tissue was prepared by the swiss-roll technique before fixation. Oesophageal tissue samples from the rectally-colonised group served as control tissues for the orally-colonised group, and vice versa. The slides were scanned with the slide scanner Olympus VS200 (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) in bright field at 10 × magnification.

DNA extraction, library preparation and 16S rRNA sequencing

Donor FMT sample and mouse luminal (faecal and caecal) samples were homogenised by bead-beating (1 min) (MiniBeadBeater-16, BioSpec Products, OK, USA) and DNA was extracted using the genesig Easy RNA/DNA kit following manufacturer’s instructions (PrimerDesign, Eastleigh, UK). DNA from tissue samples was extracted using optimised protocol 332 and QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

DNA libraries were generated from PCR amplicons targeting V3-V4 regions of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene according to Fadrosh et al.33. Universal primers 319F (forward) and 806R (reverse) and Phusion High‐Fidelity PCR Master mix with HF buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for amplification. The SequalPrep Normalization Plate Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for cleaning and normalisation of PCR products. Quality control and quantification of pooled libraries were performed using Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and the Kapa Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosystems, London, UK). Sequencing was performed at the Norwegian Sequencing Centre (Oslo, Norway), applying the Illumina MiSeq platform and v3 kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA), allowing for 300‐bp paired‐end reads.

Sequence processing and bioinformatics

Paired-end reads containing Illumina Universal Adapters or PhiX were discarded using bbduk version 38.90 (BBTools; https://jgi.doe.gov/data-and-tools/bbtools/) (parameters adaptor filter: k = 23 hdist = 1 tbo cf = TRUE ftm = 5; parameters phix filter: k = 31 hdist = 1) and the remaining reads were demultiplexed using cutadapt version 3.534 (parameters: –errors 1 –no-indels –overlap 12 –discard-untrimmed –action none). Trimming of indexes, heterogeneity spacers and primers was also done with cutadapt (parameters: –error-rate 0.1 –overlap 20 –discard-untrimmed –minimum-length 250) and the paired-end reads were subsequently quality trimmed and merged using bbmerge version 38.9035 (parameters: qtrim = r trimq = 15 maxlength = 500 mininsert = 300). The merged contigs were truncated to 400 bp and denoised to amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) with deblur36 in Qiime2 version 2023.537. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was done according to RESCRIPt38 in Qiime2 using a naïve Bayes classifier39 trained on the V3-V4 region of a preclustered version (99% sequence similarity) of the Silva database version 13840, which was also the source of nomenclature/taxonomy. ASVs from mitochondria, chloroplast or lacking taxonomic annotation at order level were removed and a de-novo phylogenetic tree was built in Qiime2 based on the remaining ASVs.

To reduce the effect of uneven sequencing depths, samples were rarefied (subsampled without replacement) to an even level of 1972 counts per sample and all diversity analyses were performed on the rarefied dataset (the average read depth prior to rarefying was 5600).

Statistical analysis

Differences between groups were analysed using unpaired t test for parametric data or Mann–Whitney U test for nonparametric data using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.0 (Boston, Massachusetts, USA) and the statistical programming language R version 4.4. Graphs of relative abundances of microbiota at phylum level, relative abundance of top 10 genera, and PCoA plot were plotted in R. Other graphs were plotted in GraphPad Prism.
