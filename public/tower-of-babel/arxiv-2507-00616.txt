Geometric Gaussian Approximations of Probability Distributions
Nathaël Da Costa, Bálint Mucsányi, Philipp Hennig

Abstract
Approximating complex probability distributions, such as Bayesian posterior distributions, is of central interest in many applications. We study the expressivity of geometric Gaussian approximations. These consist of approximations by Gaussian pushforwards through diffeomorphisms or Riemannian exponential maps. We first review these two different kinds of geometric Gaussian approximations. Then we explore their relationship to one another. We further provide a constructive proof that such geometric Gaussian approximations are universal, in that they can capture any probability distribution. Finally, we discuss whether, given a family of probability distributions, a common diffeomorphism can be found to obtain uniformly high-quality geometric Gaussian approximations for that family.



1
Introduction

Approximating probability distributions is a central task of interest in statistics and machine learning. For example, density estimation aims to learn an underlying probability distribution given data sampled from that distribution. In Bayesian statistics, posterior distributions—that is, probability distributions over the parameters conditioned on data—can often not be computed in closed form and must instead be approximated.

Such applications thus require approximation schemes for probability distributions. For example, in Bayesian statistics, one may use a Gaussian to approximate a distribution with the help of a Laplace approximation or variational inference. Such Gaussian approximations are clearly limited in their expressivity, thus requiring more expressive approximating families. Instead, one may approximate the distribution of interest through a non-linear pushforward of a Gaussian distribution. For example, deep neural networks such as normalising flows or diffusion models are used to learn an approximate mapping from a Gaussian distribution to the distribution of interest
[
7
,
11
]
.

In this paper, we study reparametrised and Riemannian Gaussian approximations. The former consists of approximations to probability distributions by diffeomorphism pushforwards of Gaussians
[
8
,
6
,
3
]
, while the latter consists of approximations by exponential map pushforwards of Gaussians in a tangent space under some Riemannian metric
[
2
,
12
]
.

Our first result (
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
) shows that there is a one-to-one correspondence between reparametrised Gaussian approximations and Riemannian Gaussian approximations under flat metrics.

Our second result (
Theorem

4.1
) then shows that, under mild regularity assumptions, even reparametrised Gaussian approximations can approximate any probability distribution. This makes both reparametrised and Riemannian Gaussian approximations
universal
.

Finally, we discuss whether, given a family of probability distributions, there exists a diffeomorphism such that every member of the family may be well approximated by a Gaussian pushforward through this diffeomorphism. An information-geometric argument shows that it is generally not possible to do this exactly. This leaves several open questions as to the form of the optimal such diffeomorphism.

2
Geometric Gaussian Approximations

Definition 1

A Gaussian Approximation is an approximation to a probability measure
\mathrm{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
.

For example, given some dataset
\mathcal{D}
and a parametric statistical model, Bayesian statistics is faced with the problem of computing the posterior distribution over the parameters

p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D})=\frac{p(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta})p(\bm{\theta})}{p(\mathcal{D})}

(1)

where
p(\bm{\theta})
is a prior distribution over the parameters,
p(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta})
is a likelihood, and
p(\mathcal{D})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta})p(\bm{\theta})\;d\bm{\theta}
is the evidence. This last integral is usually intractable; thus, the posterior
Eq.

1
is often approximated. The classical Laplace approximation to a posterior with probability density function
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D})
assumed to be twice continuously differentiable in
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a Gaussian approximation computed by taking a second-order Taylor expansion of the negative logarithm of the unnormalised posterior density

-\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta})-\log p(\bm{\theta})\approx\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*})^{\top}\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*})+\text{const}

(2)

where
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is a maximum a posteriori estimator and
H_{ij}:=\frac{\partial^{2}(-\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta})-\log p(\bm{\theta}))}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}
. The posterior distribution is then approximated by
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{H}^{-1})
. Note that when the posterior is Gaussian, this approximation is exact.

Clearly, such Gaussian approximations are limited in their expressivity and fail to capture complex probability distributions faithfully. For this reason, more flexible classes of approximations have recently been proposed (
Fig.

1
).

Definition 2

A Reparametrised Gaussian Approximation (ReparamGA) is an approximation to a probability measure
\mathrm{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a pushforward
\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
of a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
, where
\bm{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is some diffeomorphism.

A Gaussian approximation to a distribution (specifically a Laplace approximation) and a reparametrised Gaussian approximation (specifically a reparametrised Laplace approximation).

Definition 3

A Riemannian Gaussian Approximation (RiemannGA) is an approximation to a probability measure
\mathrm{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a pushforward
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\mu}*}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
where
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
is a Gaussian distribution defined on a tangent space
T_{\bm{\mu}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
at some
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\mu}}\colon T_{\bm{\mu}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the exponential map under some Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3
Relationship Between the Approximations

We now show that a ReparamGA is a special case of a RiemannGA.

Theorem 3.1

Any given ReparamGA
\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
coincides with the RiemannGA
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})*}\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\varphi}\bm{\Sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\varphi})^{\top}\right)
under the Riemannian metric
\bm{\varphi}^{-1*}g_{e}
, with
g_{e}
, the standard Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof

\bm{\varphi}
is an isometry from
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{e})
to
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\varphi}^{-1*}g_{e})
. Let
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\colon T_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the exponential map with respect to the metric
\bm{\varphi}^{-1*}g_{e}
.

g_{e}
is a flat metric, hence so is
\bm{\varphi}^{-1*}g_{e}
. Thus, we deduce from
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
that if we let
\tilde{g}_{e}
be the Euclidean metric on
T_{p}\mathbb{R}^{d}
induced by the inner product on this tangent space,
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}
is an isometry from
(T_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\mathbb{R}^{d},\tilde{g}_{e})
to
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\varphi}^{-1*}g_{e})
. Consequently, we have the commutative diagram of isometries

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(T_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\mathbb{R}^{d},\tilde{g}_{e})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{e})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\varphi}^{-1*}g_{e})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\mu)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}^{-1}\circ\bm{\varphi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{\varphi}}

\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}^{-1}\circ\bm{\varphi}
is thus an isometry from
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{e})
to
(T_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\mathbb{R}^{d},\tilde{g}_{e})
. This is an isometry between Euclidean spaces which maps
\bm{\mu}
to
\bm{0}
, so it sends the Gaussian distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at
\bm{\mu}
to the Gaussian distributions on
T_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at
\bm{0}
. Specifically, it sends
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
to

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\left(\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}^{-1}\circ\bm{\varphi}\right)\bm{\Sigma}\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\left(\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}^{-1}\circ\bm{\varphi}\right)^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 45.00006pt=\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}^{-1}\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\varphi}\bm{\Sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\varphi})^{\top}\left(\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\varphi}(\bm{\mu})}^{-1}\right)^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 45.00006pt=\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\varphi}\bm{\Sigma}(\mathrm{D}_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\varphi})^{\top}\right)

where the first equality holds by the chain rule and the second by the fact that the derivative of the exponential map at the origin is the identity. Here,
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}}f
denotes the matrix representation of the derivative of
f
at
\bm{x}
with respect to the standard Euclidean coordinates, not the abstract linear map between tangent spaces.

Gaussianity plays an important role in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, where it is used that Gaussian distributions are closed under affine transformations and thus isometries of Euclidean spaces.

In the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, we see that when a Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is flat, the exponential map
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\mu}}
is a diffeomorphism from
T_{\bm{\mu}}\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus we get the partial converse to
Theorem

3.1
:

Theorem 3.2

A RiemannGA
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\mu}*}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
under a flat Riemannian metric
g
is a ReparamGA
\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
with
\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\mu}}
.

Theorem

3.2
can only be a partial converse, as when
g
is not flat,
\bm{\exp}_{\bm{\mu}}
is not a diffeomorphism.

Thus, it may seem at this point that RiemannGA is more expressive than ReparamGA. The following section shows that this is incorrect, as ReparamGA can already express virtually any distribution.

4
Universality of Reparametrised Gaussian Approximations

In this section, we explicitly construct a diffeomorphism from the unit Gaussian distribution to any (sufficiently regular) probability distribution. The inverse of this transformation is known in the literature as the Rosenblatt transformation
[
5
]
. Therefore this construction is not novel, but we include it here for completeness.

We first prove a crucial lemma.

Lemma 1

Let
\mathrm{p}^{1}
,
\mathrm{p}^{2}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with non-vanishing smooth probability density functions
f^{1}
,
f^{2}
respectively. Then there exists a diffeomorphism
\bm{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathrm{p}^{1}=\mathrm{p}^{2}
.

Proof

We show that there exist diffeomorphisms
\bm{\varphi}^{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,1)^{d}
such that
\bm{\varphi}^{i}_{*}\mathrm{p}^{i}
are both the uniform measure on
(0,1)^{d}
for
i=1,2
. Then the result follows by taking
\bm{\varphi}:=(\bm{\varphi}^{2})^{-1}\circ\bm{\varphi}^{1}
.

Let
\bm{X}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
valued random variable with law
\mu^{1}
. Let
F_{X_{1}}
be the c.d.f. of the first component of
\bm{X}
.
f^{1}
being smooth and non-vanishing implies that
F_{X_{1}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
is a diffeomorphism. Define
\varphi^{1}_{1}(\bm{x}):=F_{X_{1}}(x_{1})
. For
i>1
, let
F_{X_{i}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{i-1}=x_{i-1}}
be the conditional c.d.f. of the
i
th
component of
\bm{X}
, i.e.,

F_{X_{i}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{i-1}=x_{i-1}}(x_{i}):=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{x_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-i}}f^{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},y_{i},\dots,y_{d})\;dy_{d}\cdots dy_{i}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-i+1}}f^{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},y_{i},\dots,y_{d})\;dy_{d}\cdots dy_{i}},

which is a diffeomorphism
\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
. Define
\varphi^{1}_{i}(\bm{x}):=F_{X_{i}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{i-1}=x_{i-1}}(x_{i})
.

Now for
u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle(\varphi_{1}^{1}(\bm{X})\leq u_{1},\dots,\varphi_{d}^{1}(\bm{X})\leq u_{d})

\displaystyle=P\left(X_{1}\leq F^{-1}_{X_{1}}(u_{1}),\dots,X_{d}\leq F^{-1}_{X_{d}|X_{1},\dots,X_{d-1}}(u_{d})\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}_{X_{1}}(u_{1})}\dots\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}_{X_{d}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{d-1}=x_{d-1}}(u_{d})}f^{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\;dx_{d}\cdots dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}_{X_{1}}(u_{1})}\dots\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}_{X_{d-1}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{d-2}=x_{d-2}}(u_{d})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\;dx_{d}

\displaystyle\quad F_{X_{d}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{d-1}=x_{d-1}}\left(F^{-1}_{X_{d}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{d-1}=x_{d-1}}(u_{d})\right)\;dx_{d-1}\cdots dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}_{X_{1}}(u_{1})}\dots\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}_{X_{d-1}|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{d-2}=x_{d-2}}(u_{d})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\;dx_{d}\cdots dx_{1}\cdot u_{d}

\displaystyle=\dots

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{F_{X_{1}}^{-1}(u_{1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}f^{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\;dx_{d}\cdots dx_{1}\cdot u_{2}\cdots u_{d}

\displaystyle=F_{X_{1}}\left(F_{X_{1}}^{-1}(u_{1})\right)u_{2}\cdots u_{d}

\displaystyle=u_{1}\cdots u_{d}

which shows that
\bm{\varphi}^{1}_{*}\mu^{1}
is the uniform measure on
(0,1)^{d}
.

From
Lemma

1
, we directly obtain the universality of ReparamGA for probability measures with non-vanishing densities:

Theorem 4.1

For
\mathrm{p}
a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a non-vanishing smooth probability density function, there exists a diffeomorphism
\bm{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathrm{p}=\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
. That is, there is a ReparamGA which is exact. As a consequence, there is a RiemannGA which is exact.

Proof

Take
\mathrm{p}_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
,
\mathrm{p}_{2}=\mathrm{p}
in
Lemma

1
. The existence of an exact RiemannGA then follows from
Theorem

3.1
.

Note that, from the proof of
Lemma

1
, if we only require the probability density function to be
C^{k}
, then we still get
Lemma

1
and hence
Theorem

4.1
with a
C^{k+1}
diffeomorphism
\bm{\varphi}
.

The transformation
\bm{\varphi}
of
Theorem

4.1
is not unique, as pre-composing
\bm{\varphi}
with any orthogonal transformation will still yield a mapping from
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
to
\mathrm{p}
.

Finally, note that, unlike in
Theorem

3.1
,
Theorem

4.1
does not make use of Gaussianity, and Gaussians could have been replaced with other approximating probability distributions.

5
Families of Reparametrised Gaussian Approximations

Now suppose that instead of approximating one target probability measure
\mathrm{p}
and choosing a
\bm{\varphi}
to perform a good (or exact) ReparamGA, we are tasked with choosing a fixed
\bm{\varphi}
that creates uniformly good ReparamGAs over some family of probability measures
\{\mathrm{p}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
. This is useful if we know the distribution of interest belongs to such a family
[
8
,
9
]
.

One may wonder if a similarly strong result as
Theorem

4.1
can be obtained in this more general case. That is, can we find a diffeomorphism
\bm{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathrm{p}_{\alpha}=\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\alpha},\bm{\Sigma}_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha
with some
\bm{\mu}_{\alpha}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\alpha}
?

This is generally not possible. Indeed, if we assume
\{\mathrm{p}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
is a statistical manifold
\mathcal{M}
, and write
\mathcal{G}
for the statistical manifold of Gaussian distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then, by Chentsov’s theorem
[
1
, Section 5.1.4]
, any diffeomorphism
\bm{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
preserves the Fisher information metric on
\mathcal{G}
. That is,
\bm{\varphi}
is an isometry from
(\mathcal{G},g_{F})
to
(\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathcal{G},g_{F})
where
g_{F}
denotes the respective Fisher information metrics. Thus,
\mathcal{G}
can be mapped surjectively onto
\mathcal{M}
only if
(\mathcal{G},g_{F})
is isometric to a submanifold of
(\mathcal{M},g_{F})
. This is clearly restrictive.

When this is not possible, one may ask how to choose a
\bm{\varphi}
such that
\bm{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{G}
is ‘close’ to
\mathcal{M}
, such that probability measures in
\mathcal{M}
may be well approximated by Gaussians. Defining a good notion of ‘closeness’, and a corresponding good
\bm{\varphi}
, is an interesting direction of future research.

One option proposed in
[
9
, Appendix D1]
is to choose probability measures
\mathrm{p}_{1}\in\mathcal{G}
,
\mathrm{p}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
and to use
Theorem

4.1
to construct a
\bm{\varphi}
such that
\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathrm{p}_{1}=\mathrm{p}_{2}
. One would hope that such a
\bm{\varphi}
would make
\bm{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{M}
‘close’. More generally, given a (second-order) probability distribution
Q
on
\mathcal{M}
and some statistical divergence
\mathrm{D}
, one could attempt to find a diffeomorphism
\bm{\varphi}
that minimises the expected divergence
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}_{2}\sim Q}[\inf_{\mathrm{p}_{1}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{p}_{2},\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathrm{p}_{1})]
. The suggestion of
\bm{\varphi}
that makes
\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{M}
intersect at a select point
\bm{\varphi}_{*}\mathrm{p}_{1}=\mathrm{p}_{2}
comes from choosing
Q
to be a Dirac measure at some
\mathrm{p}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
.

6
Conclusion

We reviewed two different types of approximations from the literature under a common umbrella of geometric Gaussian approximations, clarifying their exact relationship to one another. We then presented the construction of the Rosenblatt transformation, which proves that such geometric Gaussian approximations are universal. Future work could develop a theory for addressing the questions raised in
Section

5
about the approximate mapping of statistical manifolds to one another through diffeomorphisms of the base measure space. These appear to touch on some deep questions in information geometry.

A third type of geometric Gaussian approximation, proposed in
[
10
]
and based on Riemannian diffusions, remains to be included in this framework.
