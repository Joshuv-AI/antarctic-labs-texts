Incident Risk Prediction for Global Maritime Networks Under a Changing Climate
Authors: Alireza Azadnia, Elise Miller‐Hooks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMaritime risk is a fundamental component of operational cost and decision‐making in global container shipping, yet quantitative, route‐comparable risk estimates, especially in monetary terms and under future climate conditions, remain limited. This study develops a climate‐driven Bayesian network (BN)‐based Global Maritime Incident Risk Assessment and Prediction tool to quantify how projected future climate conditions may alter maritime incident risks at the grid‐cell, link, and route levels. Global open waters are partitioned into 2° × 2° grid cells, and route risk is computed by aggregating cell‐level risk along a path. The framework combines: (1) an estimate of incident occurrence probability derived from historical traffic and incident patterns, (2) two tree augmented BN models that predict incident type and then severity using discretized climate and bathymetry variables, and (3) monetary consequence values to express risk in real units ($ per voyage). Using monthly climate projections for 2025–2069, the approach is demonstrated on container routes in the 2M alliance network. In addition to grid cell, link and route‐based incident probabilities and their projections, a key contribution is interpretable, global monetary risk values and predictions that enable direct comparison of competing routes.Keywords: incident probabilities, maritime incident risks, shipping consequences, weather‐related incidents

Introduction

Maritime vessels are vulnerable to a range of threats while at sea, including accidents, natural disasters, piracy, and cyberattacks. These risks arise from both natural and anthropogenic causes and are influenced by a variety of contributing factors. Maritime incidents are characterized by low probability but high‐impact losses, including cargo loss, vessel or infrastructure damage, environmental harm, increased insurance premiums, legal liability, and damage to reputation for the involved shipping company. Early in the 20th century, the probability of vessel loss on Europe–Asia routes was roughly 1%, and this rate has declined to approximately 0.1% in recent decades (Allianz 2012). Despite significant improvements in maritime safety, climate‐sensitive maritime incidents continue to occur and may evolve under changing future environmental conditions. Due to the potential for substantial consequence, it is crucial to consider their likelihood in making maritime shipping activity decisions.

The need to consider accident risks in maritime transport has long been recognized in such areas as the Arctic, where sea ice poses significant challenges and affects the needed vessel class, speed, and other operational choices. POLARIS and other similar tools have been developed to support maritime operations in these settings. A growing body of academic research (discussed in the next section) has also focused on developing quantitative models to assess the influencing factors, probabilities, and consequences of maritime incidents over more commonly navigated global routes.

In developed quantitative models, risk is defined in multiple ways, but the most accepted formulation of risk characterizes it as the expected value of loss, which is computed as the product of an event's probability and the magnitude of its consequences. Following the general risk formulation, risk assessment requires estimating both the likelihood of occurrence and the severity of potential consequences.

In maritime shipping in specific regions, such as the Arctic, Taiwan, Australia, or Korea, the probability of occurrence (e.g., Zhou 2024; Rawson, Sabeur, et al. 2022; Dobbins and Jenkins 2011) or level of consequence (e.g., Ma et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2013) has been widely studied. Together, these can be used to assess risk, but global values remain unpublished.

Environmental and operational factors have been widely studied as predictors of incident occurrence and their outcomes. Climate variables have been a particular focus of incident risk, as they are statistically significant with well‐understood causal links to incident likelihood and consequences (Brandt et al. 2024). With a changing climate and related weather patterns, there is a need to characterize risks to ensure the future safety of maritime operations. Moreover, recent advancements in data collection technologies and growing accessibility of long‐term, high‐fidelity climate projections have made it increasingly feasible to pursue this objective.

This study develops a climate‐driven Bayesian network (BN)‐based Global Maritime Incident Risk Assessment and Prediction tool for evaluating future climate change impacts on maritime incidents. The tool is designed to predict the probability of four key climate‐related incident types—collision, grounding, foundering, and equipment failure—and their severities within latitudes −70° to 70° (i.e., within global open waters). The method projects the outcomes onto the global container shipping network, along with key shipping routes.

The BN model was trained using climate‐related maritime incident records from 2004 to 2024 sourced from the International Maritime Organization (IMO)’s Global Integrated Shipping Information System (GISIS) database, along with geospatial and environmental variables. Climate projections obtained from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, Phase 6 (Copernicus Climate Change Service 2021), enable probabilistic forecasting of future conditions that affect incident risks. An axiom‐based sensitivity analysis was applied to test for model consistency as applied in prior works (e.g., Jiang et al. 2024).

Literature Review

Works in an extensive body of literature have examined maritime incident risk on the basis of past incident occurrences using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. A common limitation of qualitative approaches is their reliance on expert judgment, which is inherently subjective. To overcome this subjectivity, most works in risk assessment and prediction are now quantitative in nature and exploit empirical data using probabilistic models.

In maritime applications, frequently used quantitative methods as studied in Jiang et al. (2024) include fuzzy logic (FL), evidential reasoning (ER), fault tree analysis (FTA), event tree analysis (ETA), and BNs. Jiang et al. found that in contrast to FL and ER, BN provides a more holistic framework for capturing causal relationships among influencing factors. Moreover, compared to FTA, BN can simultaneously represent multiple state variables and outputs. Machine learning techniques, including neural networks (Maceiras et al. 2024; Medda et al. 2024; Choe et al. 2025), gradient‐boosted trees, such as XGBoost, random forest, and decision trees (Rawson et al. 2021; Adland et al. 2021; Rawson, Brito, et al. 2022; Rawson, Sabeur, et al. 2022; Kim and Lim 2022; Kandel and Baroud 2024; Knapp and van de Velden 2024; Zhou 2024; Chen et al. 2025; Yi et al. 2025), and logistic regression (Knapp et al. 2011; Vander Hoorn and Knapp 2015; Bye and Aalberg 2018; Rawson et al. 2021; Medda et al. 2024), have also been adopted. Each such modeling approach has distinct advantages and limitations that depend on its structure and implementation.

Some models in the literature incorporate weather and environmental conditions as influencing risk factors, whereas others rely primarily on operational factors. Designed for polar navigation, the IMO also proposed a framework for risk assessment known as POLARIS. POLARIS estimates shipping risk under ice conditions, primarily based on ice thickness and concentration. It was designed to support operational decisions, such as safe speeds, for a real‐time context.

Given their robustness in handling variable data and performing both predictive and diagnostic evaluations, BN‐based models have become one of, if not, the most often adopted quantitative maritime incident risk assessment approach (Jiang et al. 2024). Yang et al. (2023) built a data‐driven framework to predict Arctic ship‐navigation accident occurrence as a binary class. They compared performance of Tree‐Augmented Naive Bayes (TAN), K2 BN, Random Forest, and SVM and found that TAN Bayes performed best among the tested methods. TAN takes advantage of dependencies between features in its methodology. By aggregating wind and wave variables into an adaptive spatial grid, Choe et al. (2025) trained a BN to estimate accident probability around the Republic of Korea and validated it against an artificial neural network (ANN). They found that the BN outperformed ANN on most performance metrics in prediction of accident occurrence in grid‐time cell versus nonoccurrence.

The various quantitative maritime incident assessment and prediction studies of relevance have pursued various objectives. Some focus on forecasting the likelihood of either general incident occurrence (Wu et al. 2009; Rezaee et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2023; Zhou 2024; Medda et al. 2024; Choe et al. 2025) or a specific type of incident, such as a collision (Li et al. 2024), grounding (Rawson, Brito, et al. 2022) or weather‐related incident (Knapp et al. 2011; Rawson et al. 2021). Some attempt to identify the key factors affecting the type of incident and developed models for incident prediction (Baksh et al. 2018; Fan et al. 2020, 2022; Li et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2024), and others aimed to predict the severity of incidents (Jiang et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021; Kim and Lim 2022; Ma et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024; Chang et al. 2024; Fan et al. 2025).

Risk is computed from not only the likelihood of an incident, but also the incident's potential consequences. Thus, a comprehensive risk assessment requires the simultaneous estimation of incident probability, type, and severity. Whereas many existing studies focus exclusively on predicting either the type or severity of maritime incidents, a smaller subset integrates both by modeling incident type as an influencing variable for severity within the BN framework. In such models, the probabilities of different incident types can also be inferred, effectively allowing for joint prediction of type and severity. A limited number of studies consider all elements needed for such risk computation (Jiang et al. 2020; Liao et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024; Chang et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025), though, only Li et al. (2025) developed risk predictions as is the purpose herein, but their work focuses on a specific location: the Arctic.

Jiang et al. (2020) built a BN to analyze and predict maritime incident risk along the main route of the Maritime Silk Road. They compiled 413 incident records (2010–2017) from IMO reports and Lloyd's Register Fairplay. With the addition of vessel, environment, and traffic factors, their approach uses an improved K2 search approach to learn the BN structure. They identified incident type as the most significant risk influential factor, followed by location, ship‐related and operational factors, and weather factors.

A TAN is built in both Liao et al. (2023) and Ma et al. (2024) for assessing the importance of various features in incident severity prediction. The first built a probabilistic scenario‐evolution model for maritime incidents using a TAN trained on 5660 global accidents (2005–2020) from the IMO GISIS database. Their model included incident time period and vessel type and characteristics. No climate variable is included in their model. The second built a TAN to analyze bulk‐carrier incident consequence severity in Australian waters based on manually extracted factors from the Australian Transport Safety Bureau incident reports (2000–2022). They found incident type, emergency handling, navigational condition, ship speed, and visibility as the top drivers of severity.

A BN was constructed in Zhou et al. (2024) to predict the severity of maritime incidents worldwide. Suspecting that feature importance changes over time, they compared results from five models produced from 1 to 5 years over the span of 2017–2021 using incident records compiled from IMO's GISIS and vessel details from Lloyd's Register. They found that the BN structure changed with the time span on which it was trained. Generally, though, they identified incident type, vessel operation, vessel type, and voyage segment as the most significant predictors for severity, whereas weather variables were found to be of lower significance.

Chang et al. (2024) built a BN for Taiwanese waters based on 431 incidents (2014–2022) to predict incident severity. They clustered wind, wave, and current into a five‐state weather clusters and used K2 algorithm and expert adjustments to learn the BN. They found accident type, ship type, gross tonnage, weather cluster, location, and ship age as the most significant predictors, respectively.

Moreover focused on a specific region and using the K2 algorithm with expert knowledge, Li et al. (2025) developed a data‐driven, expert knowledge‐based BN on incidents compiled in the Compendium of Arctic Ship Accidents (CASA) database from Protection of the Arctic Marine Environment (PAME). The CASA data spans from 2005 to 2017. Their method was used to estimate the probability of four incident categories (collision, grounding, foundering, and equipment failure) and their severities over Arctic subregions and key Arctic routes. Their inputs included surface air temperature, wind speed, significant wave height, sea‐ice concentration and thickness, and water depth. They used their model with future climate projections to produce decadal forecasts through 2069 along the Arctic routes.

There are several gaps in this literature. Although several studies have considered climate variables as influencing risk factors and examined either maritime incident types or their consequences, very few have jointly modeled both components, as is required to measure risk (Jiang et al. 2020; Chang et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025). Among those, only Zhou et al. (2024) examined the probabilities of specific incident types given an incident occurs at a global level, whereas others focused on a regional level. Moreover, only Li et al. (2025) utilized predictive modeling capabilities for future prediction; other techniques were not specifically designed for future risk prediction, and their utility in doing so may be limited, where there are limitations in the long‐term predictability of their models’ needed inputs. Consequently, the absence of a predictive global path‐based risk assessment capability that incorporates climate conditions along shipping paths remains a significant gap in the literature. By overlaying predicted risks onto specific shipping paths, these outcomes also enable future path‐based risk forecasts, which are provided herein.

To estimate risk along a given path, the global open waters were partitioned into 2° × 2° grid cells, with a risk value computed for each cell. At present, there is no universally accepted rule for determining the appropriate grid‐cell size. The choice of grid resolution is important, because excessively large cells may produce an overly coarse representation of the original dataset, whereas excessively small cells may generate a large number of incident‐free cells. Previous spatial analyses of maritime accidents have employed grid‐cell sizes ranging from 0.1° to 2° or have partitioned the global map into 10 × 20 fishnets (Zhang et al. 2021). In general, the appropriate cell size depends on both the characteristics of the data and the objective of the analysis. In this study, a grid cell size of 2° × 2° degrees was selected for two reasons. First, projecting the historical incident records onto the global map indicated that this was the smallest cell size that limited excessive sparsity and the presence of many empty cells. Second, some of the future climate input data used in this study were only available at a spatial resolution of approximately 2.5° × 1.9°, making a 2° × 2° grid an appropriate and compatible choice for this work. The total risk along a path can be calculated by identifying the route's constituent grid cells and taking a sequential survival weighted aggregation over all the grid cells.

Equation (1) incorporates four components within a localized geographic grid cell: (1) probability of an incident occurring, (2) conditional probability distribution over incident types given an incident occurs, (3) conditional probability distribution over severity levels given a specific incident type, and (4) monetary valuation of the incident consequences based on the incident type and its severity. As a path traverses multiple grid cells, each with potentially different levels of exposure, the total risk associated with the path is obtained by aggregating the risk contributions of all intersected areas as given in Equation (2).

The first component of the risk estimate from Equation (1), the probability of incident occurrence in a grid cell, is derived from historical incident frequencies and vessel traffic. This derivation is detailed in Section 3.2. The conditional probabilities of incident occurrence and its severity are obtained through the analysis of climatic variables using BN models trained on historical data. This is described in Section 3.3. The last, required component of this equation, the monetary valuation of the incident consequence, is discussed in Section 3.4.

Data Sources and Preprocessing

To support the analysis in this study, maritime incident data covering the period from 2004 to 2024 were sourced from the IMO's GISIS. Records prior to 2004 were excluded due to lack of geographic coordinates. Within the selected timeframe, GISIS contains 7383 incident reports, initially classified under more than 530 distinct primary incident types, mostly due to a non‐standardized data recording system. For analytical clarity, these types were consolidated into six broader categories: collision, foundering, grounding, equipment failure, human loss/injury, and others. Of these, four key categories (collision, foundering, grounding, and equipment failure) were selected for analysis. After filtering out inconsistent records and retaining only those within the selected classes, a total of 3870 incident records remained. Figure 2 shows the geospatial distribution of the historical incidents.

Geospatial distribution of maritime incident records from 2004 to 2024 (IMO).

Each incident was further classified by severity into one of three IMO‐defined categories: very serious marine casualty, marine casualty, and marine incident. A very serious marine casualty refers to a marine incident that results in the total loss of a vessel, fatalities, or significant environmental damage. A marine casualty involves more general consequences, such as loss, death or serious injury to a person, loss or abandonment of a ship, major damage to a vessel or marine infrastructure, or environmental harm. In contrast, a marine incident is a less severe event linked to ship operations that either endangered or could have endangered the safety of the vessel, its crew, others, or the environment if left unaddressed.

To enhance the consistency of the BN models for estimating the probabilities of incident type and severity, a subset of incidents attributed to adverse weather conditions was identified through a detailed review of summary of events in the dataset. A total of 963 such cases were extracted and used to train the models described in Section 3.3. Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of incident types and their corresponding severity levels for the selected incident types in the developed dataset.

Frequency of selected incident types and severity levels in the GISIS (2004–2024) climate‐related maritime incident dataset.

This study utilizes high‐quality, global‐scale environmental datasets to characterize wind speed, near‐surface air temperature, wave significant height, and bathymetric conditions on the time and location of the incident records. Wind and temperature projections were sourced from CMIP6, which includes daily grid‐based data from numerous global climate models. The data are provided on a 2.5° × 1.9° grid scales, with vertical coverage from 1 to 1000 hPa.

Wave height data were derived from the significant height of combined wind waves and swell in ERA5 reanalysis as produced by the European Centre for Medium‐Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). ERA5 integrates global observations and numerical models using data assimilation, delivering consistent, hourly estimates of atmospheric, oceanic, and land‐surface parameters from 1940 to present. The reanalysis is provided on a regular latitude–longitude grid with a horizontal resolution of 0.5° × 0.5° for ocean wave fields.

Bathymetric information is obtained from the GEBCO 2024 Grid, a global digital elevation model produced by the general bathymetric chart of the oceans project. The GEBCO 2024 Grid offers high‐resolution global terrain data for both oceanic and terrestrial surfaces, featuring a spatial resolution of approximately 500 m between data points.

Generally, these historical data elements are recorded on an hourly basis. Given the temporal and spatial accuracy and coverage of the datasets, weather conditions at the closest hour and specific location to the recorded incidents were extracted and used in analysis and modeling.

Vessel‐generated tracking signals transmitted at regular intervals, reporting key navigational and identification information, such as position, speed, and time of observation, were obtained from AIS message data for 2022. Data for 2022 were chosen as it exhibits fewer missing periods across the available annual records and is sufficiently distant from the COVID‐19 period to avoid pandemic‐related impacts on global shipping activity.

Probability of Incident Occurrence Estimation

To calculate the probability of incident occurrence in each grid cell, the number of historical incidents recorded within that cell is divided by the volume of maritime traffic that passed through it during the analysis period. The AIS data were used to estimate vessel traffic counts per grid cell. For each grid cell, the number of AIS signals from moving vessels was counted to represent traffic intensity. To avoid duplication, signals were filtered so that only one record per unique maritime mobile service identity (MMSI) was considered within each grid cell. As preliminary investigation showed that global shipping annual traffic patterns remained relatively stable over the study period, traffic data were extrapolated from a single year, specifically 2022.

An average annual traffic growth rate of 2.87% in seaborne trade volume between 2004 and 2023 (Statista.com) was applied to account for traffic growth over time using year 2022 as a reference. Due to the high geographical dispersion of recorded incidents, many grid cells exhibit zero estimated probability of occurrence, even when adjacent to cells with high incident probabilities; an outcome that may not reflect actual risk levels. To address this inconsistency, the spatial probability values required normalization and outlier removal. To improve the reliability of the analysis, grid cells with only a single recorded incident were excluded and treated as having no incidents. Subsequently, spatial outliers were identified and given a minimum incident occurrence probability using the Local Moran's I method (Moran 1948). In the end, the spatial distribution of incident probabilities was normalized using diffusion smoothing to produce a more realistic and continuous risk surface.

Local Moran's I outlier analysis evaluates each grid cell's value relative to its immediate neighbors. Using inverse‐distance spatial weights between cells, the method flags outlier cells, where a value contrasts with its surroundings. A detailed explanation of this method can be found in Wang et al. (2022).

To reduce noise and unrealistic local fluctuations in incident probabilities within any of the grid cells, as might arise as a consequence of data sparsity, a Laplacian (diffusion‐based) smoothing approach was employed assuming the standard assumption of positive spatial autocorrelation. Positive spatial autocorrelation posits that neighboring cells tend to exhibit similar risk levels. The formulation of this smoothing function is given in Equation (4). Training the Data‐Driven BN Model

A BN is a probabilistic graphical model defined on a directed acyclic graph S=(X,E), where each node Xi∈X denotes a random variable and each directed edge in E shows a direct conditional dependence summarized by a conditional probability table (CPT). The class variable, C∈X, represented in the graph as the root node, has no assumed parent, whereas, based on the structure building approach, predictor variables, A∈X, may have single or multiple parents. BN structures can be specified either subjectively, by eliciting causal links from expert knowledge or learned from data‐driven algorithms that search for dependency patterns. The latter approach has become common as larger, cleaner datasets reduce subjectivity inherent in expert knowledge.

Three widely used data‐driven approaches for building the BN structure are naïve Bayesian networks (NBNs), augmented Bayesian networks (ABNs) and tree augmented Bayesian networks (TANs). An NBN assumes conditional independence of predictors. This means there are no edges between the variables in the constructed model. This simplicity yields robustness and low variance but can be unrealistic when predictors interact. ABNs fully relax this assumption by allowing arbitrary arcs among predictors; they can capture rich dependencies, but at the cost of denser graphs, higher training complexity, and reduced interpretability. TANs strike a middle ground, where every predictor maintains a class variable C, but at most one additional predictor as parent. Without incurring the complexity of ABNs, the TAN's design keeps inference simple and explanations transparent while modeling the strongest inter‐predictor relationships omitted from NBNs. To balance model simplicity with predictive strength, this study adopts the TAN approach. Figure 5 shows a simple example of these approaches for building the BN structure.

The outcomes of the BN methodology, herein, are CPTs for incident types and incident severity level arising from predicted climate conditions of wind speed, maximum wave height, near‐surface temperature and bathymetry.

In a CPT derived from a model, where incident severity is treated as the class variable (i.e., the root node) and incident type serves as a predictor, it is theoretically possible to infer the distribution of incident types under varying climatic conditions. However, within the TAN framework, each predictor variable is permitted to have at most one parent node. As a result, the influence of multiple climatic variables on incident type becomes constrained once incident severity occupies the root position, thereby limiting the model's ability to capture the true dependence of incident type on environmental conditions. To address this limitation, two separate BN models were developed in this study. The first model predicted incident type solely as a function of climatic variables, and the second model estimated incident severity based on both climatic conditions and the predicted incident type. Incident type was identified in several previous studies as one of the most important risk‐influencing factors (RIFs) affecting incident severity; see, for example, Jiang et al. (2020), Ma et al. (2024), Zhou et al. (2024), Chang et al. (2024), and Li et al. (2025).

In probability theory, MI measures the amount of information gained about one variable through the observation of another and can be interpreted as a reduction in entropy when the value of one variable is known (Fan et al. 2020). A higher MI value between two variables indicates a stronger association between the variables. In this study, MI was used as a criterion for pruning the initially learned network structure. Specifically, variable dependencies with MI values below the average MI across all edges in the initial network were considered relatively insignificant and were removed from the model. This pruning step helps reduce model complexity while preserving the most informative relationships. The final network structure for the models is given in Figure 6.

Model Assessment

Various approaches have been proposed for validating BN models, including conventional classification metrics commonly used in machine learning (e.g., precision, recall, sensitivity, and Cohen's kappa), and assessing whether specific axioms are satisfied during sensitivity analysis. The first group is primarily applied in deterministic evaluation settings, because these metrics require a single predicted class label (typically defined as the class with the highest predicted probability) to be compared with the observed outcome. In contrast, the axiom‐based checks are well suited to probabilistic BN models, as they assess the consistency of posterior probability responses under controlled perturbations of the inputs. Accordingly, this study adopts the second validation approach. Model evaluation was performed using 10‐fold cross‐validation, with 70% of the data used for training and the remaining 30% reserved for testing.

True Risk Influence (TRI)

To assess how strongly each predictor affects the target node in the BN, the TRI sensitivity measure proposed by Alyami et al. (2019) was applied. Given a predictor A, and the target class C, with discrete states a∈A and c∈C, respectively, the TRI of A with respect to C is derived by identifying the predictor states that produce the maximum and minimum changes in the probabilities of the target classes, thereby summarizing the strongest and weakest state‐level impacts of A on C. The baseline probability of each target class was first computed with no evidence, P0(C=c). Then, to measure how much A can shift this baseline, the posterior probability P(C=c|A=a) for each state a∈A was evaluated by setting that state to certainty and running inference. Model Consistency Validation

To evaluate the internal behavioral consistency of the BN models, axiom‐based validation tests that have been employed in prior studies (e.g., Jiang et al. 2024) were utilized. That is, the following axioms must be fulfilled during the sensitivity analysis of predictor impacts on target variables:

A small increase (or decrease) in the prior probability of each predictor should produce a corresponding increase (or decrease) in the posterior probability of the target node.

The overall impact of simultaneously varying probabilities across a larger set of parameters a should be at least as large as the impact obtained from varying a subset y⊂a of those predictors.

The first axiom expresses a monotonicity requirement; if a predictor state is identified as the most supportive state for a given target class, then gradually increasing the prior probability of that state should not reduce the target‐class probability. The second axiom evaluates a cumulative (joint) effect, when multiple predictors are moved toward their individually supportive states for a given target class, the resulting posterior change should not diminish as additional predictors are included. The strength of the predictors’ states was assessed by their TRI values.

Determining Global Trade Route Risks

Input Details

The proposed route‐based risk estimation framework was applied to real‐world maritime containership routes from the 2M Alliance network operated by Maersk and Mediterranean Shipping Company as obtained in Li et al. (2021). It comprises 32 routes connecting 77 ports, in Europe, North America and Asia as depicted in Figure 7.

Routes trajectories for the 2M Alliance network (data obtained from Li et al. 2021).

The analysis groups links based on their origin and destination ports and routes based on their regional coverage. For this purpose, the network's ports are classified into three main regions: (1) East and South Asia (EA/SA), (2) Middle East, Mediterranean, and Europe (EU/ME/MD), and (3) North America (AM). Routes are categorized according to their connections across Asia, Europe, and the Americas. Full details of the classification for specific ports and routes are provided in Supporting Information Appendix C.

Forecasts of all climatic variables were extracted from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) and Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organization (CSIRO) databases on a monthly scale from 2025 to 2069 to support the risk assessment framework. Future projections of near‐surface air temperatures and near‐surface wind speeds were obtained from the EC‐Earth3‐CC model under the SSP2‐4.5 scenario using the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). SSP2‐4.5 represents a moderate socioeconomic pathway, characterized by intermediate population and economic growth, along with moderate advancements in technology and energy efficiency (O'Neill et al. 2014). Wave height projections were derived from the results of analysis of the same model, but under the SSP1‐2.6 scenario. Among the available datasets, this scenario most closely aligns with SSP2‐4.5. Though, the SSP1‐2.6 scenario reflects slightly lower future warming and emissions compared to SSP2‐4.5. All datasets employed are publicly accessible through online repositories.

These monthly climate variables corresponding to each grid cell were utilized to predict the monthly probabilities of different incident types with their severity levels. The decadal risk values by grid cells were obtained by averaging monthly risk levels over all the included months. Future decadal risk values along links and routes were estimated for two semiannual periods: spring (January to June) and fall (July to December).

Conclusions

This study addresses the need for global, forward‐looking maritime incident probability and risk estimates. For this purpose, the study developed a climate‐driven BN‐based Global Maritime Incident Risk Assessment and Prediction tool that can support path‐ and route‐level decision‐making under evolving climate conditions. Building on the idea that comprehensive risk assessment requires estimating incident probability and consequence severity, this article modeled climate‐related incident risk by partitioning global open waters into 2° × 2° grid cells and aggregating cell‐level risk contributions along maritime links and routes. The framework combines: (1) a traffic‐based estimate of incident occurrence probability per cell, (2) two TAN BN models to predict incident type and severity from discretized climatic and bathymetric variables, and (3) monetary estimates of consequences, enabling route‐level risk comparisons. The trained models were driven by monthly climate projections (2025–2069) and applied to real‐world container routes from the 2M Alliance network to create the incident and severity probabilities, as well as risks, by grid cell, link and route for routes belonging to a major maritime consortium.

Current and predicted route‐level incident probabilities and risk values given in monetary terms ($/voyage) provide directly interpretable values along entire routes that support comparison among competing paths. For 2025–2029, most links longer than 3000 nautical miles fall between $100,000 and $200,000 per voyage (with shorter links showing greater variability and risk levels up to approximately $350,000), whereas route‐level risk forecasts range from approximately $250,000 to $1.4 million. The Asia–Europe routes, in 2025–2029, exhibit the highest overall risk at approximately $1.2 million ($58/nautical mile [nm]). This is followed by Asia–America routes with the overall risk of approximately $485,000 ($28/nm) and Europe–America routes with the overall risk of $310,000 ($31/nm). These values in open water routes are not expected to rise significantly by 2060–2069. In a secondary finding, projected changes in risk values are generally small through 2069 (typically below 3% for links, but some as high as 8%, and up to 2% for routes). This is consistent with results found in Li et al. (2025), where the average overall risk along Arctic routes computed with a 3% inflation rate is estimated to triple by 2060–2069. Excluding inflation from their calculations results in an estimated increase of approximately 10% in total risk for 2060–2069, which would be due solely to the effects of climate change. The results, thus, are consistent as greater change in risk in coming decades is expected for the Arctic as compared to other portions of the globe.

These risk forecasts provide valuable input for strategic planning, freight pricing, and cargo operations in the maritime shipping industry, supporting both short‐ and long‐term decision‐making under evolving climate conditions. For example, from a carrier's perspective, risk can be incorporated alongside conventional metrics, such as financial costs and travel times, in selecting a best path, reconfiguring existing routes, allocating vessels across routes, and selecting less risky legs along routes for cargo transport. An example of the integration of risk into global freight transport optimization is provided in Li and Miller‐Hooks (2025), where a risk‐constrained maritime cargo flow optimization model is proposed for predicting global cargo traffic flows with the opening of the Arctic passageways in future decades. World‐wide risk estimates are critical inputs to this model as high‐risk routes will have less flow due to insurance premiums or even an unwillingness to insure. Changes in risk along logistical transport paths can also have significant impact on supply chain structure (Ghayoomi and Miller‐Hooks 2025). Additionally, shipping lines can employ incident predictions in planning future routes, route capacities and even vessel inventories.

There were several limitations of this study, and they must be recognized. One key limitation is that incident occurrence probabilities were estimated from historical traffic and incident patterns, whereas future traffic distributions are uncertain and may shift substantially. Such changes could alter route exposure and incident likelihood beyond the climate‐driven effects captured in this study.

The analysis used average monthly values of key environmental variables to represent future conditions. Although this approach captures long‐term average trends, it does not directly consider changes in variability or the presence of extreme events, which may continue to grow in significance in the future. Consequently, future risk levels, while perhaps stationary on average, may have large short‐term fluctuations that are not captured in this study. Future studies may incorporate such variability and event forecasts for a more complete estimation of climate‐related maritime risk. Last, these predictions do not consider major changes that could arise in vessel technologies or, more generally, how goods are transported, that could affect future values.
