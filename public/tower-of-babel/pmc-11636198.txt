Investigating Major Sources of Methane Emissions at US Landfills
Authors: Tia R Scarpelli, Daniel H Cusworth, Riley M Duren, Jinsol Kim, Joseph Heckler, Gregory P Asner, Eben Thoma, Max J Krause, Daniel Heins, Susan Thorneloe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAirborne remote sensing observations were collected at 217 landfills across 17 states in the US in 2023. We used these observations to attribute emissions to major sources, including the landfill work face, where new waste is placed at the landfill and gas-control infrastructure. Methane emissions from the work face appeared to be more prevalent than gas-control infrastructure emissions, with 52 landfills exhibiting work face emissions out of the 115 observed landfills shown to be emitting in 2023. Landfills with work face emissions were often the highest emitters, especially sites with associated renewable natural gas facilities, and the total average site emissions from these landfills accounted for 79% of the observed emissions, indicating inefficient gas capture at these sites. Landfills with work face emissions also displayed the greatest disparity between observed emission rates and hourly emission rates that we estimated using annual emissions reported to the US EPA’s Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program. Work face emissions present a major opportunity for methane mitigation: Observed emissions from work face emitting-landfills in this study were equivalent to 15% of US methane emissions from municipal solid waste landfills in 2022, as reported in the 2024 Greenhouse Gas Inventory, though these landfills accounted for only 4% of open sites in the US. As the 217 landfills in this study cover only 17% of open landfills in the US, the total mitigation potential is likely greater. Using remote sensing, we find that the largest contributor to observed methane emissions at US landfills is the landfill work face, an area of the landfill often left out of the required monitoring and traditional emissions accounting methods.Keywords: landfill, methane, renewable natural gas, flares, remote sensing

Introduction

The recent report by The International Panel on Climate Change recognizes the immediate need for deep reductions of global methane emissions to limit the effects of human-caused climate change.1 Despite the urgency of this need, methane emissions have not only continued to rise but have increased pace compared to previous decades with recent increases attributed to anthropogenic activities in the fossil fuel, waste, and agricultural sectors.2 The waste sector, which contributes 20% of global anthropogenic methane emissions, has high mitigation potential with 30–60% of abatement measures expected to be negative (quick payback of cost) or low-cost.2

In the US, landfills accounted for 17% of 2022 methane emissions with a majority of those emissions from municipal solid waste (MSW) landfills.3 These landfills present an opportunity to reduce emissions using known mitigation strategies such as the early expansion of landfill gas capture systems, more frequent and effective surface emission monitoring, use of high-oxidizing surface covers, and minimization of the landfill work face.4 The US EPA has identified reducing methane emissions at MSW landfills as a top regulatory enforcement priority for 2024–2027.5 However, some of the technical solutions to achieve methane reductions at landfills apply to areas of the landfill where waste has been in place for less than 5 years and is not subject to emissions monitoring.6

Landfills are dynamic engineered systems, so there can be multiple emissions sources, and those sources may manifest in different ways (e.g., a concentrated emission from a piece of infrastructure versus more diffuse area emissions across the landfill surface). Recent studies have observed large emissions from US landfills.7−9 These large emissions can be concentrated in an area, exhibiting point-source behavior, and may come from a variety of processes or infrastructure at the landfill, including cracks or gaps in the surface cover, the work face, gas collection wells, and flares or other equipment at associated gas facilities. To prioritize mitigation efforts and track progress toward abatement targets, there is a need to attribute these large emissions to the underlying activities and infrastructure at the landfill.

Additionally, studies have shown significant differences between the annual average emissions reported to the Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (GHGRP)10 and emissions estimated based on remote sensing observations,9,11 though to do this comparison, these studies have to make some assumptions about the diurnal and seasonal variability of emissions. The GHGRP collects and reports GHG emissions from different source categories including landfills, with GHGRP reporting required for landfills estimated to emit more than 25,000 t of CO2 equivalents. As the GHGRP is not intended to serve as an inventory of methane emissions, it does not capture some emission sources, including emissions related to cover integrity issues (i.e., cracks, gaps, or poor maintenance), insufficient vacuum, turning a portion of the wellfield offline, construction or drilling, and the work face. Despite these gaps, the GHGRP-reported emissions are incorporated into the EPA national inventory,3 which is used to set emissions reduction targets and report progress, including through submission under the Paris Agreement and United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. By identifying the underlying processes leading to persistent large emissions, the observational data can be used to inform and improve traditional accounting methods, especially for sources not captured in current reporting.

A recent report by the US EPA attributes 58% of US MSW landfill methane emissions to food waste sent to the landfill.12 As new food waste and other organic materials are placed in the landfill, the deposited material begins to decompose and generate methane. Daily cover (typically an earthen material) is placed over new waste, but this is primarily for odor and vector (e.g., rodents) control. The work face is often not required to have active gas capture as waste is less than 5 years old.6 In addition, existing gas collection wells are sometimes taken offline when waste is being deposited on top of existing waste.13 Wells near recent work face activities may need to be operated at reduced collection efficacy to avoid excessive oxygen introduction to the waste mass.14 For these reasons, methane generated from the newly placed waste or from older waste layers below may have a higher potential to escape into the atmosphere. The landfill work face has been recognized as a source of methane emissions in past studies,8,15−18 though many of these studies focus on only a handful of landfills.

Methane emissions from the landfill work face typically are not monitored with current manual surface emission measurement approaches19 due to safety concerns. The work face may also be difficult to monitor with emerging ground-based methods due to operator traffic and the potential for the work face location to shift day-to-day. Airborne and satellite remote sensing technologies and emerging unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) approaches are designed to survey a broad geographic area, allowing identification of any high-emission point source across the face of the landfill, including the landfill work face. Airborne remote sensing data have been used to notify operators of large emissions and, in some cases, have guided operator follow-up verification and mitigation.13,20 The high spatial resolution provided by airborne remote sensing (∼3–5 m9) allows the attribution of observed methane plumes to specific regions of the landfill.

Here, we used airborne remote sensing observations at 217 landfills across 17 US states to attribute emissions to the landfill work face and gas-control infrastructure. In doing so, we investigated the prevalence of work face and gas-control infrastructure emissions. We investigated the patterns of emissions at landfills that either flare landfill gas or recover the methane for electricity generation on-site or for treatment and injection into a nearby natural gas pipeline (referred to as “renewable natural gas” projects). We show that the attribution of emissions to landfill processes at this level of categorical and spatial detail can inform best management practices for achieving methane mitigation and help evaluate discrepancies between observed emissions and traditional emissions accounting methods used to track progress toward emission reduction targets.

Materials and Methods

This study focused on airborne observations at US landfills occurring between April 2023 and September 2023 with each geographic region flown for 1–2 weeks, allowing revisiting of individual landfills (within the same day and on different days) to assess source variability, though we did not investigate seasonal variability due to the limited time in each region. In general, each landfill was observed on multiple days within each 1–2 week period, although the number of revisits per site varied due to weather. These regions and landfills were chosen based on a variety of factors, including collaborations with specific stakeholders, the colocation of desirable targets (e.g., oil/gas and landfills), and a desire to observe new geographic regions and landfills not covered in Cusworth et al.9

For methane plume detection and quantification, we followed the methods described by Cusworth et al.9 and Duren et al.7 We used the imaging spectrometer on the Global Airborne Observatory (GAO),21 operated by Arizona State University. The instrument observes solar backscattered radiation between 385 and 2510 nm across a wide field of view (∼3 km swath width) and fine spatial resolution (3–5 m) and is sensitive to methane absorption signals at shortwave infrared (2200–2400 nm) wavelengths. These absorption signals were translated to column-averaged methane enhancement mixing ratios (the methane concentration above the background) using a linearized matched filter retrieval algorithm.7,22 We can detect large methane emissions, typically 10–100 kg/h or larger, from a localized area, typically on the order of 1–10 m2 in size. This is traditionally referred to as a point source, though at a landfill, this may manifest as a single point source (e.g., unlit flares) or as a grouping of multiple point sources. The instrument’s field of view allows us to identify any of these large, spatially concentrated sources across a landfill and/or the associated gas facilities. We may not be able to detect or quantify emissions from all possible sources at a landfill, including (1) methane sources that have emission rates below the detection limit of the instrument, which is scene-dependent (i.e., can vary due to environmental conditions), and (2) diffuse methane sources–often referred to as area sources.

The methane concentrations resulting from the matched filter then went through plume detection, delineation, and quantification steps. Plume detection was done manually by a data analyst, while delineation and quantification were automated. During plume detection, methane plumes were identified and assigned a sector (e.g., solid waste, etc.). To build confidence in the detection, the analyst considered any evidence that points to potential false positives: for example, we looked for ground features that may cause false enhancements and whether these features were consistent across multiple observations.23 The analyst also considered evidence in support of a true detection such as past observations of methane plumes from the source and similarities with nearby plumes (i.e., nearby plumes may have similar shapes due to the prevailing wind). Once the analysts were confident of a true methane plume, they attributed the origin of the emission (latitude and longitude) based on information such as the methane concentrations within the plume, wind direction, and visible imagery of the source on the ground. During plume delineation, the enhancements of methane concentration around the chosen source location were used to define the boundary of the methane plume or plume mask; this involved thresholding the concentration and applying filters to remove noise.7 All data sets are available publicly, including full-scene matched filter concentration imagery, and segmented plume boundaries and origins (https://data.carbonmapper.org/).

For plume quantification, we used the methane concentrations within the delineated methane plume to get the integrated mass enhancement (IME) of methane and then combined this IME with wind speed from the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) model’s24 hourly reanalysis to determine an emission rate.7 We determined the emission rate uncertainties by combining the wind speed and IME uncertainties in quadrature.9 We averaged plume emission rates by day and then averaged across all observed days, accounting for persistence (number of days a landfill is emitting compared to total days observed), to estimate an annual average hourly emission rate, as described by Cusworth et al.9 Additional quality control measures were implemented to identify cases where the quantification algorithms lose accuracy (e.g., extreme wind speeds or false enhancements due to artifacts that overlap the methane plume),9 and in these cases, we did not publish an emission rate, though the detection was still used in our calculation of persistence.

For the 2023 landfill observations, we took an additional step to identify the source generating the landfill emissions. We focused on a subset of landfill emission sources: the work face, where new waste is added at open landfills, and gas-control infrastructure. Here, we considered only gas-control infrastructure that is separate from the landfill area containing waste, including control stacks, which we define here as flares and thermal oxidizers, and any infrastructure used for gas control or treatment at a gas facility next to the landfill. We limited ourselves to the listed sources because their location could be identified in visible imagery, whereas attributing emissions to individual gas wells on the landfill surface required geospatial site information (e.g., individual well locations) that was often not in the public domain. For some observations, the source was clearly identifiable but did not fall into our two categories (work face or gas-control infrastructure); we labeled these as “other sources”. In other cases, we were unable to confidently ascribe emissions to the work face due to uncertainty in the source location, but the methane source was clearly associated with the landfill surface, in which case we also labeled these as “other sources”.

Because the locations of new waste-filling operations change day-to-day, we used contemporaneous visible imagery of the landfill surface, provided by the spectrometer, to identify the location of the active daily work face, as shown in Figure 1. The definition of work face area employed here includes the active daily work face and a surrounding spatial area of ∼100 m in all directions (see Figure 1). In this spatially liberal definition of work face area used here, we are assuming that operational practices relating to gas management are influenced by proximity to active filling operations and that construction activities affecting emissions have a higher propensity to occur near the active daily work face.

The work face or gas-control infrastructure was considered the source when the estimated origin of the methane plume overlapped the location of the work face (as defined in this study) or gas-control infrastructure, respectively. For gas-control infrastructure sources, we labeled the individual methane plumes that originate from gas-control infrastructure and categorized landfills as “with gas-control infrastructure emissions” if they had at least one methane plume from a gas-control infrastructure source.

Because the work face plumes may be more complex in shape, we used the plume shape, methane concentration gradient, and wind information (speed and direction) to estimate the location of the plume origin for each landfill. We categorized landfills as “with evidence of work face emissions” if they had at least one plume that originated from the work face. In some cases, we used information from multiple overpasses to determine plume origins, as shown in Figure 1.

We used additional data sets to investigate the variability of emissions between landfills, including information from the Landfill Methane Outreach Program (LMOP)26 as of March, 2023, and data reported to the GHGRP27 for reporting year 2022. The GHGRP data included reported annual waste quantity received, landfill surface area, average waste depth, gas flow to collection system, gas collection system capacity, average methane concentration in collected landfill gas, estimated methane generation, and number of wells (note that the number of wells is reported under GHGRP but not used in emissions calculations). Landfill surface area and waste depth were used both as totals and separated by cover type and collection system status (see Table HH-3 in Subpart HH28).

Figure 3 further breaks down the number of emitting, open landfills by emission source with aggregate percentages for each source type shown in Figure S1. We saw methane emissions from both the landfill work face and the gas-control infrastructure (i.e., control stacks or other component leaks at gas facilities). Work face emissions were observed in most states and typically occurred at multiple landfills in each state. We observed gas-control infrastructure emissions less often, except for Texas where 8 landfills had gas-control infrastructure emissions. Of the 114 open landfills with detected point sources, we attributed plumes to the work face (n = 52) or gas-control infrastructure (n = 12) at 47% (n = 54) of landfills, with most of the landfills with gas-control infrastructure emissions also having work face emissions. The remaining 53% (n = 60) of open landfills had plumes from other sources at the landfill or plumes that we could not confidently attribute to a source but were clearly coming from the landfill (see Figure S1). Despite only 46% of open, emitting landfills having work face emissions (52 out of 114 landfills), these landfills account for a majority of the observed emissions in most states, as shown in Figure 3. Work face emitting landfills in some states can account for over 90% of observed state emissions (4 states) with work face emitting landfills overall accounting for 79% of total observed emissions.

Methane Emissions from Gas-Control Infrastructure

There were 13 landfills where we attributed methane plumes to gas-control infrastructure, including 1 closed landfill and 12 open landfills. Gas-control infrastructure and gas facilities were identified using satellite imagery and verified with the LMOP database. The gas facilities at these landfills are designed for the beneficial use of landfill gas, including renewable natural gas (RNG) and gas-to-electricity (GTE) facilities. We will hereafter refer to these as landfill gas-to-energy (LFGTE) projects. The RNG projects typically need control stacks for the destruction of tail gas (leftover gas from the purification process),29 and they may also use flares for the destruction of raw landfill gas or treated gas during equipment downtime.14 For 9 of these landfills, we identified that the emission source was likely a control stack (e.g., flare or thermal oxidizer), including the 1 closed landfill with detected emissions, and for 1 landfill, we identified the source as an engine. For the remaining landfills, we could identify that the source was gas-control infrastructure at an LFGTE project, although we were unable to identify the specific component given the density of the infrastructure. Figure 4 shows methane emission rates averaged by day for four open landfills with RNG facilities, where we observed methane emissions from gas-control infrastructure. These landfills were chosen as they provide examples of different types of emissions from gas-control infrastructure. Landfills 1–3 had emissions from control stacks, while landfill 4 had a gas component leak at the RNG facility (images of landfills shown in Figure S3). Each site was observed on multiple days over a 1–2 week period. In addition to showing the days on which emissions were detected, separated by source type, Figure 4 also shows the days on which a heat signal was detected with GAO, indicating active destruction at the control stack (see the SI for heat signal detection methods).

Landfills 1 and 4 had high emissions from the landfill surface (work face or other sources with total emissions, averaged by day, of >1000 kg/h) and active flaring detected for every overpass of the landfill, implying that the control stacks were operational, but methane capture efficiency was nonoptimal, leading to persistent emissions from the landfill surface. The methane emissions from the gas-control infrastructure were very different for the two landfills: landfill 1 had persistent emissions from the control stack, likely indicating the incomplete combustion of the landfill gas, whereas landfill 4 had no control stack emissions and only showed a short-duration emission event at the RNG facility on April 16.

Landfills 2 and 3 showed variable control stack activity with a sudden spike in emissions at the control stack toward the end of the observation period. For both landfills, the control stack emission persisted across multiple days and was larger in magnitude than the landfill surface emission. For landfill 2, surface imagery showed two control stacks, with a heat signal detected at the eastward control stack on May 5. The eastward control stack appeared inactive upon revisiting on May 10, and instead, we saw methane emitted from the westward control stack that persisted to the next day (see Figure S3). No heat signals were seen from the westward control stack. For landfill 3, there were two control stacks, and both continued to operate for the entire observation period (starting June 11 and ending June 24), but, similar to landfill 2, there was a sudden spike in emissions from the control stacks on June 22 with emissions persisting upon revisit 2 days later. For landfills 2 and 3, the observed emissions may have been related to a multiday equipment malfunction. For a properly functioning gas-control system, there should not be persistent control stack venting from downtime or malfunction, and instead, the gas should be collected and routed to a flare or at least collection should be stopped.

Though landfills 1–4 all had detected heat signals, implying active control stacks, and all had associated RNG facilities, the sources of methane and source persistence varied widely between the landfills. The landfills exhibited varying degrees of methane collection and destruction efficiencies. We compared estimated hourly gas collection rates for 2022 (based on annual gas collection reported to the GHGRP) to our observed emissions and estimated the leakage rate from gas-control infrastructure at the time of our observations. For the 3 sites with quantified emissions from gas-control infrastructure in Figure 4 (landfills 1–3), the instantaneous observed emissions could account for 13–36% of estimated hourly gas collection at the facilities.

Methane Emissions from the Work Face

As we show in Figure 3, the landfills with evidence of work face emissions accounted for less than half of emitting sites but 79% of total emissions, implying higher emissions from these landfills compared to landfills with other sources. Figure 5 shows a map of our annual average hourly emission rates across the US for landfills observed in 2023 and highlights those landfills where we found evidence of work face emissions. The landfills with work face emissions were generally responsible for the highest emissions in each geographic region surveyed, as shown by the density plots inset in Figure 5 (emissions distribution for all sites shown in Figure S4). The highest average landfill emissions (>3000 kg/h) are for four Texas landfills, all with evidence of work face emissions and three with additional gas-control infrastructure emissions.

Average methane emission rates for landfills that were observed during airborne campaigns in 2023. Landfills with evidence of work face emissions are shown in orange. “Other sources” are shown in green and represent landfills with emissions from nonwork face sources or landfills where we were not able to attribute emissions to a specific source, but emissions were clearly coming from the landfill surface. Inset plots show the kernel density estimates separated by source type for each major geographic region.

In general, we saw higher observed emissions compared to estimated hourly emissions derived from the annual emissions reported to the GHGRP (R2 = 0.06), similar to the findings of Cusworth et al.,9 although we showed this for an additional 121 landfills not included in their study (Figure S5). Other studies have found similar discrepancies.11,17,30 The landfills with evidence of work face emissions accounted for most of this discrepancy between the GHGRP and observations. This misalignment is also evident when compared to waste-in-place, where a subset of landfills with evidence of work face emissions had greater observed emissions relative to waste-in-place compared to the GHGRP hourly emissions that we estimated (Figure S5). When comparing our observations, which are limited to daytime hours, to the GHGRP, we did not account for potential diurnal variability of emissions (e.g., due to daily cover placement), which is an ongoing subject of research. However, the analysis of Nesser et al.11 found similar results and should account for diurnal variability.

All work face emission landfills have gas capture systems, and a majority have LFGTE projects. Of emitting landfills, those with evidence of work face emissions tended to be slightly larger and get more precipitation (Figure S2), but in general, when considering the other landfill characteristics reported to the GHGRP27 (e.g., waste quantity received, surface area by cover type, estimated methane generation, number of wells), there were no characteristics that clearly distinguished landfills with work face emissions from other emitting landfills. We also found no relationships between the magnitude of work face emissions and these landfill metrics except a slight positive correlation with average annual methane concentration in landfill gas collected at the landfill (R2 = 0.17) and typical precipitation rates31 for the geographic region (R2 = 0.22). We note that for the precipitation relationship, we saw a similar result for all emitting landfills (R2 = 0.22) whereas the methane concentration relationship was weaker when all emitting landfills are considered (R2 = 0.13). It is possible that there are errors in the landfill data reported to the GHGRP by operators, but it is unlikely that this can explain the lack of correlation, given that we analyze data from different landfills reported by varying operators.

The lack of clear evidence for why some landfills show large work face emissions may indicate that these emissions are driven by site-specific operational practices. Model-based studies have recognized the dependence of work face emissions on site-specific practices related to cover, gas capture, and the placement of new waste on top of older waste,17 while flux chamber studies have found a lack of seasonal variability in emissions from fresh waste.15 To further investigate these site-specific practices, we looked at summary reports for airborne campaigns in California13 (2020–2021) and Pennsylvania20 (2021), where we found evidence of support for the prevalence of work face emissions. During the campaigns, operators were notified of large methane emission sources through collaboration with the California Air Resources Board (CARB) and the Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection (DEP). In California, there were over 50 incidents shared with landfill operators, with approximately half of the operators reporting mitigation. The largest emission events were attributed by operators to construction and the practice of taking wells offline near the work face where waste was being deposited on top of existing waste.13 In Pennsylvania, there were 10 discrete methane plumes detected at landfills, with 8 of those plumes physically located or addressed by the operator. Operators identified and mitigated sources near the work face that included the erosion of a temporary liner in the active area and down or malfunctioning wells where active waste placement was occurring (see Figure S6 for plume images).20 These summary reports showed evidence of specific management practices, like putting wells offline during filling operations, that may be contributing to the observed work face emissions.

Additionally, we considered information from inspection reports for nine landfills in Washington, Oregon, Texas, and Illinois where we saw evidence of work face emissions and were included in a recent summary report32 by Industrious Labs. The landfill inspections occurred prior to our observations (2021–2023) and did not include a survey of the work face, but the reports provide context for site-specific practices and typical methane sources. The summary report documents multiple methane exceedances (surface methane concentrations above 500 ppm6) at these landfills with common sources of methane including gas wells, leachate risers, areas of exposed waste, erosion, and other cover integrity issues (e.g., cracks).32 Some of these documented sources of methane align with our observed emissions sources at the same landfills, years later, including venting flares and leakage from edges of tarp covers.32,33 These inspection reports document widespread methane leakage from nonwork face regions of the landfill at the same landfills where we observed work face emissions, implying that the existing gas capture systems and management practices at these landfills may have been insufficient for capturing the large amounts of methane generated and thereby allowing fugitive methane to escape through various routes including cover penetrations and the landfill work face. An enforcement alert issued by the EPA cited recurring Clean Air Act compliance issues leading to a significant release of methane and other air pollutants at US landfills.34 The alert notes that inspections found failure to properly design, install, operate, and maintain the gas collection and control system and failure to maintain cover integrity due to leaks and cracks found during inspections.

Methane Emissions at Landfills with LFGTE Projects

For the airborne campaign in 2023, we observed 86 open landfills with LFGTE projects (Table 1). For these 86 landfills with LFGTE projects, we found that 81% had detectable point source emissions whereas only 44% of observed open landfills without LFGTE projects had detectable point source emissions. The percentage rose to 92% when we considered only landfills with RNG projects, although the sample size was small (24 RNG landfills were observed in 2023; see Table 1). This high detection rate at LFGTE landfills may, in part, be due to the tendency of LFGTE projects to be associated with larger landfills that typically have a higher methane generation potential and gas collection volumes. When accounting for this size dependence, we found that the mean and median emission rates for large, emitting landfills with LFGTE projects (mean: 1057 kg/h, median: 614 kg/h) were over 50% larger than for large emitting landfills without LFGTE projects (Figure S4). Again, we define large as landfills with a median waste-in-place greater than 9.8 million metric tons. The subset of LFGTE landfills that had RNG had the highest emissions (mean: 1774 kg/h; median: 1167 kg/h; see Table 1) with all but one of the 22 emitting sites considered large landfills by our definition.

We used our attribution of emissions to sources and further information in the inspection reports to understand the underlying reasons for our higher observed emissions at landfills with RNG. The RNG landfills with work face emissions had the highest emissions of RNG landfills, accounting for 59% of emitting RNG landfills by number, but for 86% of methane emissions. The inspection reports documented numerous exceedances (surface methane concentrations above 500 ppm6) at landfills with associated RNG with one report noting that wells may be tuned to avoid air infiltration and ensure a 50% or higher methane content for the RNG plant.32,35 In practice, there is a balance between avoiding air infiltration (lower vacuum) and maximizing gas capture efficiency to avoid odors and surface emissions (higher vacuum).14 If well tuning for gas quality is occurring at landfills with operational RNG, either by reducing vacuum across the wellfield or selectively adjusting the vacuum to prioritize high-methane content wells, it is possible that this practice could lead to unintended emissions at the landfill. If this practice is leading to emissions, it could explain why we saw higher observed emissions at landfills with RNG and why we saw the positive correlation between the methane concentration in collected landfill gas and emission rates at landfills with work face emissions.

We looked for supporting evidence for well tuning toward higher methane concentrations (i.e., avoiding air infiltration) at landfills with RNG by comparing average annual methane concentrations in collected gas, as reported to the GHGRP. We focused on Texas as this is where we observed the highest emission at landfills with RNG and where most of the RNG landfills we observed were located. For open landfills in Texas (59 landfills), reported methane concentrations for 2022 were higher when gas was sent to an operational RNG facility compared to GTE or when there was no LFGTE project, as shown in Figure S7. It is possible that the landfills with RNG simply had higher methane content in their gas regardless of well-tuning practices compared to landfills without RNG. To account for this, we only considered the top-emitting landfills in Texas (>1500 kg/h) that also reported methane concentrations in years with and without operational RNG at the landfill for the 2010–2022 period. For the five landfills meeting this description, we found that reported methane concentrations were higher after the RNG facility had been installed compared to before the RNG facility was installed at the same landfill (see Figures S7 and S8), implying that gas collection practices may have been adjusted to increase methane content (i.e., by reducing the nitrogen content in the landfill gas to meet inlet nitrogen specifications) when RNG facilities were receiving the gas, though it is also possible that other practices (e.g., upgrading cover materials) could have led to improved gas quality.14 We also see that the trend in annual methane collected (adjusted for waste-in-place) before and after RNG is variable between these 5 landfills, as shown in Figure S8. Some facilities show an increase in methane collected after RNG, while others show a decrease or little change, despite an accompanying increase in methane concentration, implying that less landfill gas is being collected per waste disposed. The variability between sites, our small sample size, and a lack of site-specific operational information make it difficult to fully assess the impact of RNG on methane collection efficiency. Further research is needed to determine whether there is widespread evidence across the US of well tuning for gas quality at landfills with RNG and the impacts this practice may have on the magnitude and nature (e.g., point source versus diffuse) of fugitive methane emissions at the landfill.

Potential Impacts and Future Work

Using airborne remote sensing, we found evidence of large work face emissions at just under a quarter (n = 52) of the 217 MSW landfills surveyed across 17 US states in 2023. The landfills with work face emissions by number accounted for only 46% of emitting sites, but in terms of methane emissions, they accounted for 79% of observed emissions, reflecting the high emissions observed at these landfills, especially those with RNG facilities. We also found large emissions related to gas-control infrastructure, though these emissions were less prevalent than the emissions from the landfill work face. These observed work face emissions present a significant opportunity for methane mitigation in that they are large and persistent methane sources. For example, we observed emissions of 15 MMT CO2 equivalent per year for the 52 landfills with work face emissions which could account for 15% of the methane emissions reported by the US for MSW landfills in 2022, though these sites accounted for only 4% of the total number of open landfills in the US.3,26

Operationally, control of work face emissions on larger sites that handle daily waste loads is logistically more challenging compared to lower waste volume sites, though there are best practices that can reduce emissions including early expansion of landfill gas capture systems and minimization of the work face area.4 Summary reports from previous airborne surveys in California and Pennsylvania identified specific management practices at the work face, such as placing wells offline during filling, that may account for methane emissions at the work face. Additionally, inspection reports from 2021 to 2023 at work face emission landfills showed a high number of methane exceedances (surface methane concentrations above 500 ppm6) in areas of the landfill where no active filling is occurring, implying that there may be broad inefficiencies in methane capture across the landfill leading to fugitive methane loss from various points, including the landfill work face and through penetrations in surface cover. Current regulations do not require landfill gas management for new waste areas at the landfill,6 meaning that without updated regulatory best practices, mitigation of large work face emissions will rely primarily on voluntary action by industry, as was shown by the California and Pennsylvania examples.

Similar to previous studies,9,11 we found higher observed emissions compared to hourly emission rates that we estimated based on annual reporting to the GHGRP. This is not surprising given that some of the major sources of emissions discussed here are not regulatorily required under GHGRP. Most landfills with underestimated emissions compared to observations had work face emissions, indicating that existing methodologies might not be accurately capturing emissions from these landfills due to the lack of inclusion of this source in GHGRP methods. The identification of high methane concentrations near wells and cover penetrations at these same landfills, as identified in the inspection reports, suggests that an overestimate of gas collection efficiencies might also contribute to the discrepancies between observations and GHGRP estimates, similar to the findings of other studies.11 We find little correlation between existing widely available site parameters and work face emissions, and there is a need for wider availability of site-specific geospatial and time-resolved information (e.g., locations of gas collection wells and time-series of well downtime) to identify the methodological changes needed to better account for work face emissions in current emissions models.

We saw the highest emissions and largest disagreement with the GHGRP for landfills with both the work face and gas-control infrastructure emissions. This disagreement may, in part, be explained by the assumptions related to gas-control infrastructure on- and off-site of the landfill: GHGRP reporting through Subpart HH28 assumes that gas-control devices on-site of the landfill have 99% destruction efficiency (or a manufacturer’s specified destruction efficiency) while control devices at off-site energy projects, like control stacks at RNG facilities, are assumed to be outside reporting requirements. Even without the inclusion of gas-control infrastructure emissions, the large discrepancies between observations and reporting remain, and there is a need for a review of GHGRP methodologies to account for these large emissions related to the work face.

The dynamic nature of the landfills and the limitations of existing ground-based survey methods (e.g., inability to access the work face) highlight the need for more comprehensive methane monitoring to identify and quickly mitigate high-emission sources. This need was recognized in a report by the Greenhouse Gas Monitoring and Measurement Interagency Working Group (GHG IWG) that called out the need for a combination of observing approaches to understand landfill emissions and to detect and quantify super-emitters in the sector.36 The wide field of view and high spatial resolution of imaging spectrometers make them ideal for identifying large localized methane sources across the landfill. The observations provide both source locations, which can guide follow-up mitigation action, and emissions characterization (e.g., magnitude of emission and extent of methane plume), which can be used to prioritize the largest sources for mitigation and improve best practices. Coordinated studies with state governments have shown that operators may take voluntary action to mitigate work face emission sources when guided by these observations, but these observations have been limited to a handful of states, and much broader action is needed to take advantage of this opportunity for methane emissions reductions. Satellites that can detect these large emission sources at high spatial resolution, such as those being deployed by the Carbon Mapper Coalition (∼30 m resolution), can play a key role in the operational monitoring of landfills, as they can identify the largest emission sources at facilities across the US, enabling informed decision making by operators. Regular, comprehensive monitoring will be especially important for enabling mitigation at landfills, as we have shown in this study that large methane emissions are associated with one of the most dynamic areas of a landfill: the work face.
