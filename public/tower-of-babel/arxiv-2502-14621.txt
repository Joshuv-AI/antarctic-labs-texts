Asymptotic Analysis and Practical Evaluation of Jump Rate Estimators in Piecewise-Deterministic Markov Processes
Romain Azaïs, Solune Denis

Abstract




Abstract

Piecewise-deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs) offer a powerful stochastic modeling framework that combines deterministic trajectories with random perturbations at random times. Estimating their local characteristics (particularly the jump rate) is an important yet challenging task. In recent years, non-parametric methods for jump rate inference have been developed, but these approaches often rely on distinct theoretical frameworks, complicating direct comparisons.
In this paper, we propose a unified framework to standardize and consolidate state-of-the-art approaches. We establish new results on consistency and asymptotic normality within this framework, enabling rigorous theoretical comparisons of convergence rates and asymptotic variances. Notably, we demonstrate that no single method uniformly outperforms the others, even within the same model. These theoretical insights are validated through numerical simulations using a representative PDMP application: the TCP model. Furthermore, we extend the comparison to real-world data, focusing on cell growth and division dynamics in
Escherichia coli
.
This work enhances the theoretical understanding of PDMP inference while offering practical insights into the relative strengths and limitations of existing methods.

Keywords

Piecewise-deterministic Markov process; Growth-fragmentation model; Jump rate; Non-parametric estimation; Consistency; Central limit theorem; Vector-valued martingale

Introduction

1.1
Piecewise-deterministic Markov processes

Piecewise-deterministic Markov processes (commonly abbreviated as PDMPs) were introduced by Davis in
[
18
,
19
]
as a broad category of continuous-time stochastic models that exclude diffusion. These processes are well-suited for modeling deterministic dynamics where randomness manifests through discrete events. The motion of a PDMP
X_{t}
(with jump times
T_{n}
) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(endowed with the Borel algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) is defined from its local characteristics:

•

\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the deterministic flow, which satisfies the semi-group property,

\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~\forall\,t,\,s\in\mathbb{R},~\Phi(t+s|x)=\Phi(s|\Phi(t|x)).

In some contexts,
\Phi(\cdot|x)
will also be denoted
\Phi_{x}
.

•

\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the jump rate, related to the flow by the following condition,

\forall\,x\in E,~\exists\,\varepsilon>0,~\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\lambda(\Phi(t|x))\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

•

Q:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
is the transition kernel such that,

\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~Q(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{x\}|x)=1.

The jump mechanism, that is, the discrete part of the dynamics, can be expressed as follows. Starting from
X_{0}
at
T_{0}=0
, for any integer
n
, for any bounded measurable test function
\varphi
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n+1}-T_{n}>t\,|\,X_{T_{n}},T_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda\left(\Phi(s|X_{T_{n}})\right)\mathrm{d}s\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{T_{n+1}})\,|\,\Phi(T_{n+1}-T_{n}|X_{T_{n}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(z)\,Q\left(\mathrm{d}z|\Phi(T_{n+1}-T_{n}|X_{T_{n}})\right).

These equations iteratively define the jump times
T_{n}
and the post-jump locations
Z_{n}=X_{T_{n}}
. Between two consecutive jump times
T_{n}
and
T_{n+1}
, the motion is expressed as,

\forall\,T_{n}\leq t<T_{n+1},~X_{t}=\Phi(t-T_{n}|X_{T_{n}}).

Beyond jump times and post-jump locations, one may also consider the inter-jumping times
S_{n}=T_{n}-T_{n-1}
and the pre-jump locations
Z_{n}^{-}=\Phi(S_{n}|Z_{n-1})
. An illustrative PDMP trajectory is depicted in Figure
1

Z_{n}
,
Z_{n}^{-}
,
S_{n}
, and
T_{n}
. For example, when the flow is known, it is sufficient to know the pair
(Z_{n},S_{n+1})
(or
(Z_{n},Z_{n+1}^{-})
), which notably form Markov chains, to reconstruct the entire trajectory.

1.3
Goal: non-parametric estimation of the jump rate

Given the wide range of applications for the class of PDMPs (in reliability, biology, insurance, see for instance
[
16
,
20
,
29
]
and references therein), the question of estimating local characteristics is crucial. In particular, the jump rate is a key quantity of interest in this type of model. Depending on the context, it may govern the onset of congestion (in the TCP model
[
12
]
), a cell division (in a growth–fragmentation model
[
22
,
32
,
36
]
), a change in the propagation regime (in reliability
[
2
,
20
]
), or even a relapse (in a cancer model
[
15
]
). Several statistical frameworks can be considered for this purpose. In this paper, we adopt a particularly general framework, aiming not only to develop statistical methods that can be applied to a large variety of problems, but also to better understand what distinguishes PDMPs. Specifically, we focus on the non-parametric estimation of the jump rate
\lambda
.

Various observation schemes for this type of process can be considered, the most challenging probably being noisy observations on a time grid, which generally do not allow direct measurement of the jumps
[
2
,
15
]
. In this article, we instead assume that the embedded chain is perfectly observed over a long time horizon, i.e. the first
n
jumps of a single trajectory are observed. Assuming that the flow
\Phi
is known, this scheme, notably adopted in
[
7
,
5
,
6
,
25
,
31
,
30
]
, is equivalent to fully observing the continuous-time trajectory. As shown in recent literature
[
5
,
6
,
25
,
31
,
30
]
, even under this ideal observation framework, the non-parametric estimation of the jump rate remains a non-trivial problem.

We consider general processes without imposing specific assumptions on the flow, the transition kernel, or the jump rate. However, to ensure convergence properties of the estimators (as
n
tends to infinity), it is typically required to assume or state an ergodicity condition on the embedded chain
Z_{n}
. Interestingly, there is an equivalence on stability, recurrence and ergodicity between the continuous-time process
X_{t}
and a discrete-time embedded Markov chain, which is fully defined from the local characteristics
[
17
]
. In this paper, the PDMP of interest
X_{t}
is supposed to admit a unique invariant measure
\mu^{\text{\tiny CT}}
. Additionally,
\mu
represents the invariant distribution of post-jump locations
Z_{n}
, while
\mu^{-}
denotes the invariant distribution of pre-jump locations
Z_{n}^{-}
.

To capture the jump rate of interest, one approach is to observe that the jump intensity of the process starting from
\xi
is in fact the composition
\lambda(\Phi(t|\xi))
, which can be written as the ratio of the conditional density function
f(t|\xi)
of
S_{n+1}
given
Z_{n}=\xi
over the related conditional survival function
G(t|\xi)
, with

\displaystyle f(t|\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda(\Phi(t|\xi))\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(\Phi(s|\xi))\mathrm{d}s\right),

\displaystyle G(t|\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(\Phi(s|\xi))\mathrm{d}s\right).

(2)

Multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the invariant measure
\mu
of
Z_{n}
, one obtains the formula

\lambda(\Phi(t|\xi))=\frac{\mu(\xi)f(t|\xi)}{\mu(\xi)G(t|\xi)},

(3)

where the numerator can be interpreted as the invariant density of
(Z_{n},S_{n+1})
at
(\xi,t)
and the denominator can be seen as the measure of
(Z_{n},\mathbbm{1}_{\{S_{n+1}>t\}})
at
(\xi,1)
under the invariant distribution. By estimating these two invariant measures using a non-parametric method, one obtains a quotient estimator of
\lambda(\Phi(t|\xi))
in any dimension. Any pair
(\xi,t)
such that
\Phi(t|\xi)=x
then provides an estimator of
\lambda(\Phi(t|\xi))=\lambda(x)
.

In dimension
1
, if the flow defines an increasing change of variable, it is straightforward to see from (
3
) that

\lambda(x)=\frac{\Delta(x)\mu^{-}(x)}{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}\right)},

(4)

where
\Delta(x)=\partial_{t}\Phi(0|x)
. This provides another strategy for estimating the jump rate of interest: both the numerator and the denominator are related to the invariant measure of the embedded chain, which makes them amenable to non-parametric estimation.

Consequently, at this stage we have two quotient-type formulas for non-parametrically estimating the jump rate of interest in dimension
1
. In a sense, they differ according to when the flow is used as a change of variable: either before or after the estimation step. The main objective of this paper is to compare these two approaches for one-dimensional PDMPs when the invariant distributions are estimated using non-recursive kernel methods. It should be noted that the aforementioned applications
[
2
,
12
,
15
,
16
,
20
,
22
,
29
,
32
,
36
]
involve one-dimensional models, which justify the development and study of estimation techniques tailored to this case.

1.4
A commented state of the art

We review here, in chronological order, the non-parametric methods developed in the literature and their link with formulas (
3
) and (
4
) above. Some connections and similarities in the construction of the estimators, highlighted in Remarks
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
, do not appear to have been reported in the existing bibliography.

Fujii (2013)

The method designed in
[
25
]
to estimate the jump rate of a PDMP is based on Rice’s formula derived for one-dimensional processes in
[
11
]
, which highlights the link between local time and stationary distribution. The author assumes that the flow of the PDMP under consideration
X_{t}
is of the form,

\Phi(t|x)=x+\int_{0}^{t}\Delta(\Phi(s|x))\mathrm{d}s,

(5)

where
\Delta
is a real-valued function such that
\inf\Delta>0
. By definition,
\Delta
coincides with the time-derivative of the flow appearing in (
4
). The local time of
X_{t}
is then defined as

r(x)=\frac{\#\{t\in[0,T]\,:\,X_{t}=x\}}{\Delta(x)}.

After noting that the normalized local time
r(x)/T
estimates the invariant density (of the continuous-time process)
\mu^{\text{\tiny CT}}(x)
, the author turns to the estimation of the jump rate and proposes to estimate
\lambda(x)
by
\widehat{\lambda}_{T}(x)=TA(x)/r(x)
, with

A(x)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}K_{h_{T}}\left(X_{t^{-}}-x\right)\mathrm{d}\chi(t,z),

where
K_{h_{T}}
is a kernel with bandwidth
h_{T}
and
\chi
is the counting measure defined by

\chi(t,A)=\#\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,T_{n}\leq t,\,Z_{n}\in A\}.

Under the assumption that jumps are only additive and downwards, i.e.
Z_{n}=Z_{n}^{-}-J_{n}
with
J_{n}>0
almost surely, the uniform convergence (when
T
goes to infinity) in probability of this estimate is notably established. It should be noted that the observation scheme deviates from the one presented earlier in this paper: the observation window is in the time of the process
X_{t}
and not in the discrete time of the embedded chain.

Remark 1.1
.

Denoting
n_{T}
the (random) number of jumps before
T
and using the monotonicity of the flow, we rewrite Fujii’s estimator in the more conventional form,

\widehat{\lambda}_{T}(x)=\Delta(x)\times\frac{\frac{1}{n_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{T}}K_{h_{T}}(Z_{i}^{-}-x)}{\frac{1}{n_{T}}\sum_{i=0}^{n_{T}-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{i}\leq x<Z_{i+1}^{-}\}}}.

(6)

Interestingly, the numerator is a kernel estimator of the invariant distribution
\mu^{-}
while the denominator is the empirical version of
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-})
. Therefore, Fujii’s estimator implicitly uses (
4
) to capture the jump rate of interest.

Azaïs, Dufour and Gégout-Petit (2014)

The approach developed in
[
5
]
does not rely on (
3
) or (
4
) to capture the jump rate but on the multiplicative intensity model developed by Aalen
[
1
]
. The multiplicative intensity model assumes the observation of a continuous-time counting process
N(t)
whose stochastic intensity is of the form

\lambda(t)Y(t)
for some predictable process
Y(t)
. Equivalently, the process
M(t)=N(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)Y(s)\mathrm{d}s
is a continuous-time martingale. Within this framework, the Nelson–Aalen estimator provides a consistent estimator of the cumulative jump rate
\Lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s
. Kernel-based methods can then be developed to estimate directly the quantity of interest
\lambda(t)

[
35
]
.

The multiplicative intensity assumption holds in a wide range of applications
[
3
]
, notably for non-homogeneous marked renewal processes whose dynamics resemble those of PDMPs
[
4
]
. However, the jump mechanism of PDMPs differs from that of marked renewal processes, so that the multiplicative intensity model is generally not satisfied. The approach proposed in
[
5
]
circumvents this difficulty by identifying a suitable transformation of the process of interest that satisfies the multiplicative intensity assumption.

The return to the jump rate of interest is also studied, leading to an estimate of the conditional density
f
of inter-jumping times.
The main drawback of this technique is that, in order to return to the quantity of interest, it requires integration over the whole space, even for local estimation. Nevertheless, convergence in probability, uniform on any compact set, of the conditional density estimator is demonstrated for processes on very general spaces (more general than
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) and involving forced jumps at the boundary.

Azaïs and Muller-Gueudin (2016)

PDMPs investigated in
[
6
]
are defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, may involve forced jumps at the boundary, and possess a transition kernel that is continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. The key is the quotient formula (
3
) for
\lambda(\Phi(t|\xi))
, where both the numerator and the denominator are directly related to the invariant distribution of the embedded chain. Both are estimated using recursive kernel methods with
h_{n}^{\textrm{\tiny s}}
and
h_{n}^{\textrm{\tiny t}}
denoting respectively space and time bandwidths. Under mainly an ergodicity assumption and a Lipschitz mixing condition, pointwise almost sure convergence and asymptotic normality with expected rate
\sqrt{n(h_{n}^{\textrm{\tiny s}})^{d}h_{n}^{\textrm{\tiny t}}}
of this estimator have been established in
[
6
]
. As aforementioned, the authors notice that any couple
(\xi,\tau_{x}(\xi))
such that
\Phi(\tau_{x}(\xi)|\xi)=x
provides a consistent estimator of
\lambda(x)
. Indeed,

\widehat{\lambda\circ\Phi}_{n}(\tau_{x}(\xi)|\xi)\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\lambda(\Phi(\tau_{x}(\xi)|\xi))=\lambda(x).

It is then proposed to select the one with minimal asymptotic variance, which yields to maximize
\mu(\cdot)G(\tau_{x}(\cdot)|\cdot)
on
\mathcal{C}_{x}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\exists\,t\geq 0,\,x=\Phi(t|\xi)\}
. Of course, this quantity is unknown but well-estimated by the denominator of
\widehat{\lambda\circ\Phi}_{n}(\tau_{x}(\xi)|\xi)
. The estimator obtained with optimal argument selection from the estimated criterion is not theoretically investigated but numerical simulations show its good properties.

Krell (2016)

PDMPs under consideration in
[
31
]
are one-dimensional and with a specific transition kernel of the form
Q(\cdot|x)=\delta_{\{h(x)\}}
, i.e. jumps are deterministic with
Z_{n}=h(Z_{n}^{-})
. The statistical approach relies on the assumption that both the flow
\Phi(\cdot|x)
(which will also be denoted
\Phi_{x}
to shorten some equations) and the fragmentation function
h
define a change of variable on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which makes this strategy specific to dimension
1
. In that context, it is shown that the targeted jump rate can be written as
\lambda(x)=\mu(h(x))/D^{\text{\tiny K}}(x)
, with

D^{\text{\tiny K}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[g_{Z_{0}}(h(x))\mathbbm{1}_{\{h(Z_{0})\leq h(x)\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{1}\geq h(x)\}}\right],

(7)

where

g_{x}(y)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{(h\circ\Phi_{x})^{\prime}((h\circ\Phi_{x})^{-1}(y))}.

The numerator is evaluated as a kernel estimator of the invariant distribution
\mu
composed with
h
, while the denominator is estimated by its empirical version. The main result of
[
31
]
states the convergence in
\mathbb{L}^{1}
-norm to
0
of the square error with a rate depending on the regularity of
\lambda
. In contrast with previous approaches
[
5
,
6
,
25
]
, this paper does not assume the ergodicity of the process; rather, it establishes it as an intermediate outcome derived from regularity assumptions on its local characteristics. It should be noted that this estimator requires the knowledge of the transition kernel through the fragmentation function
h
. However, this part does not involve any randomness, which makes it easy to be estimated.

Remark 1.2
.

In fact,
g_{x}(y)
does not depend on x. Indeed, using the flow property, a direct calculus shows that

g_{x}(y)=\frac{(h^{-1})^{\prime}(y)}{\Delta(h^{-1}(y))},

where
\Delta(x)=\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(0)
appears in (
4
) and (
5
). This allows us to simplify the formula (
7
) of
D^{\text{\tiny K}}(x)
,

\displaystyle D^{\text{\tiny K}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(Z_{0}\leq x,Z_{1}\geq h(x)\right)}{h^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x)},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}\right)}{h^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x)},

showing

\lambda(x)=h^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x)\frac{\mu(h(x))}{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}\right)}.

(8)

Together with
\mu^{-}(x)=\mu(h(x))h^{\prime}(x)
, this makes it possible to point out that Krell’s approach is actually based on (
4
) to capture the jump rate of interest, but tackles the numerator via
\mu
(and not
\mu^{-}
) using the knowledge on the deterministic transition kernel
Q
.

Krell and Schmisser (2021)

The last paper
[
30
]
of this review generalizes the approach of
[
31
]
still to one-dimensional processes but with general transition kernel (in particular not assumed to be continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure as in
[
6
]
). Assuming again that the flow defines a change of variable on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, it is shown that

\lambda(x)=\mu^{-}(x)/D^{\text{\tiny KS}}(x)
, with

D^{\text{\tiny KS}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[(\Phi_{Z_{0}}^{-1})^{\prime}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}\}}\right].

As in
[
31
]
, the denominator is estimated by its empirical version, while the numerator is evaluated by an adaptive projection estimator of
\mu^{-}
. The main result of
[
30
]
states that, under regularity assumptions, the estimator under consideration is nearly minimax (up to a
\log^{2}n
factor) in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
-norm. In contrast with
[
5
,
6
,
25
]
and as in
[
31
]
, the ergodicity of the embedded chain is not assumed but is instead established on the basis of the properties of the process’s local characteristics.

Remark 1.3
.

Reusing the rationale of Remark
1.2
, it is easy to see that

D^{\text{\tiny KS}}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}\right)}{\Delta(x)},

proving again (
4
). This highlights the fact that, like Fujii’s approach, the procedure of Krell and Schmisser estimates the jump rate of interest via the quotient (
4
). Besides the observation window (in the time of the PDMP or in the time of the embedded chain), the main difference in terms of estimator construction between the two approaches lies in the technique used to handle the numerator: kernel estimator in Fujii’s paper
[
25
]
vs. adaptive projection estimator in Krell and Schmisser’s article
[
30
]
. It is important to point out, however, that the theoretical results demonstrated in these papers are different and complementary: uniform convergence in probability in
[
25
]
vs. minimax rate in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
-norm in
[
30
]
.

1.5
Kernel estimators of the jump rate

The first conclusion to be drawn from this review of the literature is that the results obtained, albeit within the same framework, i.e. general PDMPs observed over a long period of time, are difficult to compare, notably:

•

[
25
]
focuses on the asymptotics in the (continuous) true time of the PDMP, while the other publications deal with the number of jumps observed.

•

[
5
]
does not estimate the jump rate but the associated conditional density.

•

[
6
]
implements a recursive kernel method,
[
5
,
25
,
31
]
assume a non-recursive kernel estimator, while
[
30
]
uses an adaptive projection estimator.

•

[
25
,
31
,
30
]
deal with one-dimensional processes, while the state space at hand in
[
6
]
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
[
5
]
considers general metric spaces.

•

Transitions at jump times are deterministic in
[
31
]
, continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure in
[
6
]
, and of a general type in
[
5
,
25
,
30
]
.

•

The convergence results obtained do not involve the same measures of estimation error.

•

Several strategies are employed to capture the jump rate of interest:
[
25
,
30
]
rely on the representation (
4
),
[
31
]
uses (
8
), while
[
6
]
is based on (
3
).

The main goal of this paper is to make the first rigorous, both theoretical and numerical, comparison of the strategies used to capture the jump rate of interest. To achieve this, we assume a unique statistical framework and apply the same estimation method to the different strategies. That is why, in the present paper, we only consider one-dimensional processes observed via their embedded Markov chain, which entails asymptotic analyses in the number of jumps observed. In addition, in order to work with estimators built on a single method, we restrict ourselves to non-recursive kernel estimators.

K^{d}

, i.e. a density on

. To shorten equations,
K_{h}^{d}=K^{d}(\cdot/h)/h^{d}

. In addition, we omit the exponent when

, i.e.

. The estimators selected for the rest of the study are as follows:

•

\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)=h^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x)\times\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}K_{h_{n}}(Z_{i}-h(x))}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{i}\leq x\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{i+1}\geq h(x)\}}},

•

\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}(x)

Remark 2.1
.

In light of Theorem
2.2
, estimators
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)

\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}(x)

converge at the same rate
\sqrt{nh_{n}}
. Furthermore, in the case of deterministic fragmentation

, the asymptotic variances are identical up to the multiplicative factor

. In the particular case of linear fragmentation,
h(x)=\kappa x
with
0<\kappa<1
, estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
always has a better variance than
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
. However, variances can not generally be ordered: for instance, if
h(x)=x/(1+\exp(-x))
, which satisfies
0\leq h(x)\leq x
, we observe that
h^{\prime}(1)<1
and
h^{\prime}(2)>1
. This occurs even though
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
uses additional information (the form of the transition kernel) compared to
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

As stated in Theorem
1.1
, estimator
\mathring{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}(x)
converges at the rate
\sqrt{nh_{n}^{\textrm{\tiny s}}h_{n}^{\textrm{\tiny t}}}
, whereas we have just shown in Theorem
2.2
that estimators
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}(x)
converge at the rate
\sqrt{nh_{n}}
. This difference is due to the presence of temporal smoothing in the former. Consequently, if we assume that the spatial windows of the three estimators should be equivalent, then estimator
\mathring{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}(x)
is slower and, asymptotically, disqualified. Conversely, if we assume

, the comparison of asymptotic variances
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}^{2}(x)
,
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}^{2}(x)
and
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}^{2}(x)
becomes meaningful and determines which estimator is asymptotically superior (at least based on the variance). In practice, we generally work with a large but finite dataset. The variances of the estimators are therefore on the order of the asymptotic variance in the central limit theorem normalized by the convergence rate. For not-too-large values of
n
, it is possible that a slower estimator achieves a lower variance than a faster one (because the asymptotic variance in the central limit theorem for the former is significantly smaller than that of the latter). The theoretical comparison of variances (normalized by the convergence rate or not), is inherently complex given the generality of our problem. Therefore, this analysis will be undertaken using the example of the TCP model in Section
3
.

\widehat{D}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n\Delta(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\leq x\leq Z_{k}^{-}\}}.

Estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
given in (
9
) is basically the quotient of
\widehat{\mu}_{n}(h(x))
over
\widehat{D}_{n}(x)/h^{\prime}(x)
while
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}(x)
given in (
10
) is the quotient of
\widehat{\mu}^{-}_{n}(x)
over
\widehat{D}_{n}(x)
. In order to establish the asymptotic properties of
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}(x)
, we shall study the two-dimensional vectors
(\widehat{\mu}_{n}(h(x)),\widehat{D}_{n}(x)/h^{\prime}(x))^{\top}
and
(\widehat{\mu}^{-}_{n}(x),\widehat{D}_{n}(x))^{\top}
. Our main goal is to show that they tend towards their deterministic counterparts,
(\mu(h(x)),D(x)/h^{\prime}(x))^{\top}
and
(\mu^{-}(x),D(x))^{\top}
, and catch the rates of convergence.

To this end, we introduce the following pieces of notation. First,
F_{n}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n}
, while
G_{n}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by

Z_{0},Z_{1}^{-},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}^{-},Z_{n}
. We also introduce
M_{n}=(M_{n}^{(1)},M_{n}^{(2)})^{\top}
and
\widetilde{M}_{n}=(\widetilde{M}_{n}^{(1)},\widetilde{M}_{n}^{(2)})^{\top}
, the vector martingales (adapted to
F_{n}
and
G_{n}
, respectively) defined, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, by

\displaystyle M_{n}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(A_{k}^{(i)}-B_{k}^{(i)}\right),

(15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{n}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\widetilde{A}_{k}^{(i)}-\widetilde{B}_{k}^{(i)}\right),

where

\displaystyle A_{k}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\;K_{h_{n}}\!\left(Z_{k}-h(x)\right),

\displaystyle\qquad\widetilde{A}_{k}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\;K_{h_{n}}\!\left(Z_{k}^{-}-x\right),

\displaystyle{A_{k}^{(2)}}

\displaystyle=\;{\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\leq x\leq Z_{k}^{-}\}}}{h^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x)}},

\displaystyle\qquad\widetilde{A}_{k}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\;{\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\leq x\leq Z_{k}^{-}\}}}{\Delta(x)}},

\displaystyle B_{k}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[A_{k}^{(i)}\mid F_{k-1}\right],

\displaystyle\qquad\widetilde{B}_{k}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widetilde{A}_{k}^{(i)}\mid G_{k-1}\right].

It is worth noting that in the case of
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
, because the jumps are deterministic, the numerator of
A_{k}^{(2)}
can be rewritten as a function of
Z_{k}
and
Z_{k-1}
only, with
\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\leq x\leq Z_{k}^{-}\}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\leq x,Z_{k}\geq h(x)\}}
.

We also consider
R_{n}=(R_{n}^{(1)},R_{n}^{(2)})^{\top}
and

\widetilde{R}_{n}=(\widetilde{R}_{n}^{(1)},\widetilde{R}_{n}^{(2)})^{\top}
defined by

\displaystyle R_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}^{(1)}-\mu(h(x)),

\displaystyle\qquad\widetilde{R}_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{B}_{k}^{(1)}-\mu^{-}(x),

\displaystyle R_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}^{(2)}-\frac{D(x)}{h^{\prime}(x)},

\displaystyle\qquad\widetilde{R}_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{B}_{k}^{(2)}-D(x).

Then a direct calculus shows that the two-dimensional estimation errors are given by

\left(\begin{matrix}\widehat{\mu}_{n}(h(x))\\
\widehat{D}_{n}(x)/h^{\prime}(x)\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mu(h(x))\\
D(x)/h^{\prime}(x)\end{matrix}\right)=\frac{M_{n}}{n}+R_{n}

(16)

and

\left(\begin{matrix}\widehat{\mu}^{-}_{n}(x)\\
\widehat{D}_{n}(x)\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mu^{-}(x)\\
D(x)\end{matrix}\right)=\frac{\widetilde{M}_{n}}{n}+\widetilde{R}_{n}.

These two decompositions are at the core of our demonstration. In order to study
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
, we establish that
M_{n}/n
almost surely goes to
0
by virtue of the law of large numbers for vector martingales (see Appendix
A.2
), and state that the remainder term
R_{n}
also vanishes, establishing the consistency of the estimator given in Theorem
2.1
. For the asymptotic normality, we first state that
n^{(1-\gamma)/2}R_{n}
goes to
0
in probability (see Appendix
A.3
). The convergence of the estimation error is therefore carried by the martingale term, which we study in light of the central limit theorem for vector martingales. This proves the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
given in Theorem
2.2
. The investigations for
\widetilde{M}_{n}
and
\widetilde{R}_{n}
are not presented in this paper but follow the same steps and yield similar convergence properties for
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}(x)
.

3.2
Bandwidth selection

Kernel methods are highly sensitive to the smoothing parameter, which must therefore be chosen carefully. The estimators under consideration rely on estimating different invariant measures. For instance,
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
involves estimating
\mu^{-}
, whereas
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
relies on estimating
\mu
. Consequently, there is no reason for them to share the same bandwidth. Moreover, there is no a priori justification for selecting the bandwidth independently for the kernel component of the jump rate estimator; instead, it should be based on the overall formula of the estimator. Additionally, developing a method for adjusting the smoothing bandwidth is a challenging and interesting question that goes far beyond the scope of this paper. Here, we propose a bandwidth selection procedure for each of the estimators based on our knowledge of the ground truth, which, therefore, can not be applied in practice to real-world data.

For the estimator
\widehat{\lambda}
, the smoothing parameter selected in this simulation study is the minimizer of the integrated square error

\int_{0.5}^{2.5}\left[\widehat{\lambda}(x)-\lambda(x)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}x,

where the integral is evaluated with a step of
0.05
and the numerical minimization is performed with a step of
0.025
for
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
and a step of
0.01
in space and of
0.05
in time for
\mathring{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
. Indeed, it should be noted that the latter two estimators, given in (
11

\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
on the left side of the state space, the theoretical predictions are generally supported by the numerical simulations, especially from trajectories of size
n=10\,000
: for
0.7\leq x\leq 1.1
,
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}(x)
performs better than
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}(x)
, while for
x\geq 1.3
, the situation reverses.

While the previously mentioned limitations of applying theoretical results in this context remain valid (and are particularly evident when comparing the four estimators in detail), they nonetheless allow for a reasonably reliable comparison of the two main estimators studied. Moreover, the numerical analysis confirms, using the TCP model as an example, that none of the methods is uniformly better across the state space.

The estimator constructed in

To this end, we propose to compare estimators

4
Real data analysis

4.1
Context

The life cycle of a cell alternates phases of growth and division. This is a typical application of piecewise-deterministic models in dimension 1 (see for instance
[
16
,
22
,
32
]
and references therein): growth is considered exponential and divisions, assumed to be quasi-instantaneous, occur at random times. In addition, the division mechanism appears to be linked to cell size
[
36
]
, making the idea of a jump rate as a function of cell size relevant. In this context, the process under consideration models the size of a cell (and its progeny) over time: its flow is of the form
(x,t)\mapsto x\exp(\theta t)
,
\theta>0
, and its transition kernel can be written as
\delta_{\{x/2\}}
(or more generally as any distribution with support
[0,x]
to take into account both the intrinsic variability and an eventual bias in the division process). The jump rate, which is very difficult to parameterize in this kind of application, is the typical function of interest.

In this section, we propose to use single-cell data from
Escherichia coli

[
37
]
to implement and compare, within the framework of the probabilistic model just described, the jump rate estimation strategies studied in this paper. The data in question are measurements of cell size, obtained by microscopy, under different temperature conditions (25°C, 27°C, or 37°C). For each, multiple independent data sets from different mother cells are available.

In Subsection
4.2
, we describe the available data and explain how a piecewise-deterministic model is fitted to them. Summary statistics are provided in the main document only for the 37°C temperature condition; Appendix
B
gathers corresponding figures for the other temperatures. The results of the jump rate estimation for the three temperature conditions are presented in Subsection
4.3
, with a numerical validation of the model developed in Subsection
4.4
.

In addition, a division indicator is available which, in a piecewise-deterministic model, precisely indicates the jump times. 4.3
Jump rate estimation

Now that the model has been fitted, we can calculate the estimators of the jump rate. Estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
only requires the evaluation of

[
6

15

\tau=52.4

\lambda(x)

The final step is to select the smoothing parameters. In the absence of ground truth, these are chosen by hand to avoid over-fitting (resulting in excessive oscillations) and under-fitting (no apparent variation). 4.4
A posteriori validation

The aim of this work is the comparison of estimation strategies of the jump rate in PDMPs. In the real data context of this section, we can not compare them on any theoretical criterion because there is no ground truth: the mechanism triggering cell division is unknown. To overcome this difficulty, we propose to compare our estimation results with the empirical data based on their distribution in continuous time rather than on the jump mechanism. In other words, we propose to return to the continuous-time invariant distribution of the model, computed using the estimated jump rates, which is directly comparable to the distribution of the raw data. It should be noted that the latter does not depend on the model fitting and can therefore play the role of theoretical reference.

For each temperature condition, we consider the PDMPs with the following local characteristics:

•

The flow
\Phi
is defined by
\Phi(t|x)=x+\theta t
where the slope
\theta
depends on the temperature condition and was estimated in Subsection
4.2
.

•

The fragmentations are defined by
Z_{i}=K_{i}Z_{i}^{-}
where the
K_{i}
’s are independent and Gaussian with mean and variance estimated from division ratios under each of the temperature conditions (see Figures
10
and
14
).

•

The jump rate is one of the estimates under consideration in Subsection
4.3
.

The continuous-time invariant distribution is difficult to evaluate exactly. We therefore propose to estimate it from simulated data generated according to the local characteristics that we have just specified.
To this end, for each temperature condition and for each jump rate estimate, we simulate the first
1\,000
jumps of the process starting from
X_{0}=1.5
on a minute-based discrete time grid (like in the real data sets). We then extract from the resulting trajectory the
10\,000
last positions, which approximately follow the invariant distribution. We finally estimate this distribution with a classical kernel estimator. The comparison with raw data is given in Figure

5
Concluding remarks

Non-parametric estimation of the jump rate of PDMPs requires the development of specific methods. In dimension 1, the state of the art is limited to five main approaches
[
5
,
6
,
25
,
31
,
30
]
that address the function of interest in different ways. In the first part of the article, we highlighted a connection between three of them
[
25
,
31
,
30
]
that has not been noticed in the literature: they implicitly capture the jump rate from formula (
4
). It is important to note that the statistical techniques and subsequent results in these papers are nevertheless different and complementary.

The objective of the present paper is not to compare the statistical approaches developed in the literature, but rather to compare the expressions of the jump rate that underlie these methods. To this end, we introduced two new kernel estimators of the jump rate
\lambda
. The estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
is based on formula (
8
) (in the setting of deterministic fragmentations), similarly to
[
31
]
, whereas
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
is based on formula (
4
), in the same spirit as the estimators proposed in
[
25
,
30
]
. These estimators are to be compared with the kernel estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
and its oracle version
\mathring{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
, both based on formula (
3
) as in
[
6
]
. While all these methods yield consistent estimators of the jump rate, the aim of the paper is to compare them at a higher order, through their convergence rate and their asymptotic variance. The approach based on the multiplicative intensity model
[
5
]
was set aside, primarily because it does not provide a direct estimator of the jump rate. However, we believe that this method warrants further investigation and should be considered in a broader comparative analysis.

We have shown new results of consistency and asymptotic normality for
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
, making them comparable with
\mathring{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
. The proof involves the study of vector martingales constructed along the embedded chain of the PDMP. The theoretical results prove that the three estimators can not in general be ordered based on their asymptotic variance, even within the same model. This result is surprising for two main reasons: (i)
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
is the only estimator that leverages the deterministic nature of the transitions, which does not guarantee that it achieves the lowest variance everywhere; (ii) the technique developed to construct
\mathring{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
in
[
6
]
does not exploit the one-dimensional nature of the state space (as it was developed to operate in any dimension), which does not prevent it from achieving the lowest variance in certain regions of the state space.

We have demonstrated that these theoretical results align with numerical simulations in the context of the TCP model, a representative application of PDMPs in dimension 1. However, the numerical experiments also highlight the greater sensitivity of the estimators
\mathring{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5664,1}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}\text{\footnotesize$\spadesuit$}}}
to the choice of smoothing parameters. Unlike the other two methods
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
(which involve smoothing only in space), this approach requires smoothing in both space and time, as well as an optimal argument selection, making it the most complex technique to implement. The application to real data confirms this trend, underscoring the practical advantages of
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
. Furthermore, implementing the adaptive projection-based versions of
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
, which behave in a very similar way to their non-adaptive kernel versions, suggests that the technique used to estimate the jump rate matters less than the formula capturing it. As a conclusion, given the current state of knowledge, the estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\text{\footnotesize$\vardiamondsuit$}}}
(or its variant) is likely the most suitable choice in dimension 1, despite exhibiting higher variance in certain cases.

The most promising solution is likely to involve aggregating the estimators studied in the present paper to leverage the strengths of each approach. For example, a compelling direction suggested by our analysis would be to select, at each point in the state space, the estimator with the lowest asymptotic variance.

Extending the approach developed in the present paper to multidimensional PDMPs would be relevant in many settings, given the variety of such models (see, for instance,
[
14
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
and references therein).
The estimation strategies resulting from formulas (
4
) and (
8
) are both based on a time-space change of variable, which is naturally specific to dimension 1. In the literature in statistics, the only general nonparametric approach linking the jump rate of interest to the invariant distribution of multidimensional PDMPs relates to (
3
). However, it might be possible to exploit the structure of some particular multidimensional models to establish a tractable formula for the jump rate, as it is done in one-dimensional growth-fragmentation processes
[
31
,
30
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, in the aforementioned multidimensional applications of PDMPs, no closed form of the invariant distribution is available in a general setting (with nonparametric jump rate). This is not surprising since the study of the invariant distribution in multidimensional models is in general a difficult problem. For instance, establishing the uniqueness of the invariant distribution or understanding its qualitative properties appear to be challenging questions, even in specific frameworks (see
[
8
,
10
]
and references therein).

Nevertheless, one-dimensional PDMPs might help to better understand some particular multidimensional processes. As an illustration, consider the class of two-dimensional PDMPs where the flow is exponential, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\dot{x}}=\alpha\boldsymbol{x}
with
\alpha>0
, the jump rate depends only on the Euclidean distance to the origin, i.e.
\lambda(x)=l(\lVert x\rVert)
, and the jump mechanism is characterized by an instantaneous contraction of the distance to the origin and a new random direction. The polar form of the process is also a PDMP with independent coordinates which radial component is a growth-fragmentation process. Using one-dimensional formulas (
4
) and (
8
) on the radial component, one can derive two estimation formulas for the jump rate of interest
\lambda
. Even though this is a hypothetical example, it reinforces the interest of studying one-dimensional processes, in addition to their numerous applications in biology (cell growth-division models developed in
[
22
,
32
,
36
]
or the cancer model studied in
[
15
]
), in insurance (see for instance
[
29
]
) or in reliability theory (see
[
2
,
20
]
and references therein).

The predictable square variation process of

Lemma A.1
.

When

goes to infinity, the following equality holds almost surely,

\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}=\left[\begin{matrix}n^{1+\gamma}\omega_{1}(1+o(1))&O(n)\\
O(n)&n\omega_{2}(1+o(1))\end{matrix}\right],

where
\omega_{1}>0
and
\omega_{2}\geq 0
.

Proof.

We analyze the coefficients of the matrix separately.

\bullet
Study of
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,1)}

A direct calculus shows that

\displaystyle\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}\textrm{ar}(M_{k}^{(1)}|F_{k-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}\textrm{ar}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}(A_{j}^{(1)}-B_{j}^{(1)})\Big|F_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(A_{k}^{(1)})^{2}\big|F_{k-1}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[A_{k}^{(1)}\big|F_{k-1}\right]^{2}\right),

where the terms
A_{k}^{(i)}
and
B_{k}^{(i)}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
and
k\leq n
, are defined in Subsection
2.3
.
Recalling that
P
is the conditional density of
Z_{k}
given
Z_{k-1}
, with a change of variable, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(A_{k}^{(1)})^{2}\big|F_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[K^{2}\left(\frac{Z_{k}-h(y)}{h_{n}}\right)|Z_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}\left(\frac{z-h(y)}{h_{n}}\right)P(z\lvert Z_{k-1})\ \mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}\left(u\right)P(h_{n}u+h(y)\lvert Z_{k-1})\ \mathrm{d}u,

and analogously

\mathbb{E}\left[A_{k}^{(1)}\big|F_{k-1}\right]^{2}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}K\left(u\right)P(h_{n}u+h(y)|Z_{k-1})\mathrm{d}u\right)^{2}.

This yields

\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,1)}=nT_{n}^{(1)}-T_{n}^{(2)},

(17)

where

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}\left(u\right)P(h_{n}u+h(y)|Z_{k-1})\mathrm{d}u,

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}K\left(u\right)P(h_{n}u+h(y)|Z_{k-1})\mathrm{d}u\right)^{2}.

We shall investigate the limit behavior of
T_{n}^{(1)}
and
T_{n}^{(2)}
. To this end, we define

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{2}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}P(h(x)|Z_{k-1}),

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}\left(u\right)P(h_{n}u+h(x)|Z_{k-1})\mathrm{d}u,

where
\tau^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}(x)\mathrm{d}x
. Using the fact that
P
is Lipschitz as supposed in Assumptions
2.3
and
K
has compact support by Assumptions
2.5
, one has

\displaystyle|\hat{T}_{n}^{(1)}-\tilde{T}_{n}^{(1)}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}(u)\left|P(h_{n}u+h(x)|Z_{k-1})-P(h(x)|Z_{k-1})\right|\mathrm{d}u

(18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{[P]_{\text{\tiny Lip}}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}(u)|h_{n}u|\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h_{n}[P]_{\text{\tiny Lip}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{2}(u)|u|\mathrm{d}u~\to 0,

when
n
goes to infinity, because
h_{n}=n^{-\gamma}
. Furthermore, by virtue of the almost sure ergodic theorem
[
34
, Theorem 17.1.7]
(which we can apply by Assumption
2.2
) and recalling that
\mu
is the invariant measure of
P
,

\hat{T}_{n}^{(1)}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\tau^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[P(h(x)|Z_{0})]=\tau^{2}\mu(h(x)).

Since
\hat{T}_{n}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{T}_{n}^{(1)}
have the same limit by (
18
), and writing
T_{n}^{(1)}=\tilde{T}_{n}^{(1)}/h_{n}
, one gets the following almost sure equivalent when
n
goes to infinity,

nT_{n}^{(1)}\sim n^{1+\gamma}\tau^{2}\mu(h(x)).

(19)

In addition, since
P
is bounded according to Assumptions
2.3
,

T_{n}^{(2)}\leq\|P\|_{\infty}^{2}n.

Together with (
17
) and (
19
), one gets

\frac{\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,1)}}{n^{1+\gamma}}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\tau^{2}\mu(h(x)),

which, together with
\mu(h(x))=\mu^{-}(x)/h^{\prime}(x)
where
\mu^{-}(x)>0
and
h^{\prime}(x)>0
(
h
is assumed to be strictly increasing), establishes the result for coefficient
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,1)}
.

\bullet

Study of
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(2,2)}

One has

\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(2,2)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}\textrm{ar}(A_{k}^{(2)}|Z_{k-1}).

Each term,
\mathbb{V}\textrm{ar}(A_{k}^{(2)}|Z_{k-1})
, of the sum is a function of
Z_{k-1}
and is independent of
n
. This contrasts with its counterpart,
\mathbb{V}\textrm{ar}(A_{k}^{(1)}|Z_{k-1})
, in
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,1)}
, which exhibits an explicit dependence on
n
. In that context, one can directly apply the almost sure ergodic theorem
[
34
, Theorem 17.1.7]
and get

\frac{\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(2,2)}}{n}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{h^{\prime}(x)^{2}\Delta(x)^{2}}\mathbb{V}\textrm{ar}_{\mu}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}\}}\right),

where the limit can be rewritten using

\mathbb{V}\textrm{ar}_{\mu}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}\}}\right)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{h^{\prime}(x)^{2}\Delta(x)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p(y)(1-p(y))\mu(\mathrm{d}y),

with
p(y)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}\leq x<Z_{1}^{-}|Z_{0}=y)
,
stating the expected result for
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(2,2)}
.

\bullet

Study of
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,2)}=\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(2,1)}

With a change of variable, it is easy to see that both

\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{(1)}A_{k}^{(2)}|Z_{k-1}]

and

\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{(1)}|Z_{k-1}]\,\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{(2)}|Z_{k-1}]

are bounded by
\|P\|_{\infty}/(h^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x))
. Together with

\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{(1,2)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{(1)}A_{k}^{(2)}|Z_{k-1}]-\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{(1)}|Z_{k-1}]\,\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{(2)}|Z_{k-1}],

this shows that the non-diagonal coefficients are in
O(n)
, as expected.
∎

A.2
Consistency

If the martingale term
M_{n}/n
and the remainder term
R_{n}=\left(R_{n}^{(1)},R_{n}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}
, defined in Subsection
2.3
, go to
0
, then, in light of (
16
),
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
is consistent. We tackle the two terms separately.

A.2.1
Remainder term

With a change of variable,

R_{n}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K\left(u\right)P\left(h_{n}u+h(x)|Z_{k-1}\right)\mathrm{d}u-\mu(h(x)).

Then, using
\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(u)\mathrm{d}u=1
(see Assumptions
2.5
),

R_{n}^{(1)}=R_{n}^{(1,1)}+R_{n}^{(1,2)},

(20)

where

\displaystyle R_{n}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K\left(u\right)\left[P\left(h_{n}u+h(x)|Z_{k-1}\right)-P\left(h(x)|Z_{k-1}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}u,

\displaystyle R_{n}^{(1,2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}P\left(h(x)|Z_{k-1}\right)-\mu(h(x)).

(21)

Since
P
is Lipschitz according to Assumptions
2.3
,

|R_{n}^{(1,1)}|\leq h_{n}[P]_{\text{\tiny Lip}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(u)|u|\mathrm{d}u~\to 0.

(22)

In addition,
R_{n}^{(1,2)}
almost surely vanishes by direct application of the almost sure ergodic theorem
[
34
, Theorem 17.1.7]
. This proves that
R_{n}^{(1)}
tends to
0
. Furthermore,

R_{n}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{nh^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\geq Z_{k-1}\}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k}\geq h(x)\}}|Z_{k-1}]-\frac{D(x)}{h^{\prime}(x)},

which directly goes to
0
, in light of the almost sure ergodic theorem again, and by definition (
14
) of
D(x)
.

A.2.2
Martingale term

The submultiplicativity of the Frobenius norm, together with the asymptotic behavior of
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}
stated in Lemma
A.1
, imply that

\displaystyle\frac{\|M_{n}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{1/2}\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{-1/2}M_{n}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{-1/2}M_{n}\|^{2}n^{\gamma-1}\max(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

By virtue of the law of large numbers for vector martingales
[
23
, Theorem 1.3.15]
, for any
\eta>0
, when
n
goes to infinity,

\|\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}^{-1/2}M_{n}\|^{2}=o\left(\frac{\log(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{1+\eta}}{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\right),

where
\mathcal{E}_{n}
(
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, respectively) denotes the smallest eigenvalue (the trace, respectively) of
\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}
. By Lemma
A.1
, it is clear that
\mathcal{T}_{n}=O(n^{1+\gamma})
and
\mathcal{E}_{n}\sim\omega_{2}n
almost surely. All together, this establishes that
M_{n}/n
almost surely vanishes, which ends the proof of the consistency of
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
.

A.3
Asymptotic normality

Using (
16
), we derive a central limit theorem for
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)
by proving that the martingale term converges to a Gaussian distribution as the remainder term vanishes.

A.3.1
Remainder term

We begin with
R_{n}^{(1)}
written as the sum (
20
) of
R_{n}^{(1,1)}
and
R_{n}^{(2,2)}
. With (
22
),

n^{(1-\gamma)/2}R_{n}^{(1,1)}\leq n^{(1-3\gamma)/2}[P]_{\text{\tiny Lip}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(u)|u|\mathrm{d}u,

which goes to
0
as long as
1/3<\gamma<1
. In addition, by virtue of
[
23
, Theorem 6.3.17]
applied to functional
P
(which satisfies Assumptions
2.4
) along Markov chain
Z_{n}
, one has almost surely

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(P(h(x)|Z_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[P(h(x)|Z_{0})\right]\right)\right|=o\left((n\ln(n)^{1+\gamma})^{\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{a_{1}}}\right).

With
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[P(h(x)|Z_{0})\right]=\mu(h(x))
, one gets

\left|\frac{1}{n^{\frac{1+\gamma}{2}}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(P(h(x)|Z_{k})-\mu(h(x))\right)\right|=o\left(n^{\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{a_{1}}-\frac{1+\gamma}{2}}\ln(n)^{(1+\gamma)\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{a_{1}}}\right).

Moreover
(r_{1}+r_{2})/a_{1}-(\gamma+1)/2\leq-\gamma/2<0
because
(r_{1}+r_{2})/a_{1}\leq 1/2
in light of Assumptions
2.4
. Together with the definition (
21
) of
R_{n}^{(1,2)}
, this shows that
n^{(1-\gamma)/2}R_{n}^{(1,2)}
almost surely vanishes. As a conclusion,
n^{(1-\gamma)/2}R_{n}^{(1)}
almost surely goes to
0
.

On the other hand, the central limit theorem for Markov chains
[
34
, Theorem 17.5.3]
proves that
\sqrt{n}R_{n}^{(2)}
weakly converges to a Gaussian distribution with mean
0
and finite variance. Consequently,
n^{(1-\gamma)/2}R_{n}^{(2)}
tends to
0
, and

n^{(1-\gamma)/2}R_{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(23)

A.3.2
Martingale term

To establish the asymptotic normality of the martingale term
M_{n}/n
with rate
n^{(1-\gamma)/2}
, we rely on the central limit theorem for vector martingales
[
23
, Corollary 2.1.10]
. To this end, we need to check its conditions of applicability. First, in light of Lemma
A.1
, it is clear that

\frac{\left\langle M\right\rangle_{n}}{n^{1+\gamma}}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\left[\begin{matrix}\tau^{2}\mu(h(x))&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right].

(24)

Second, we have to check Lindeberg’s condition. To this end, we fix
k\leq n
. Using a change of variable and the definition (
15
) of
M_{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|M_{k}^{(1)}-M_{k-1}^{(1)}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|A_{k}^{(1)}-B_{k}^{(1)}\right|~\leq~n^{\gamma}\|K\|_{\infty}+\|P\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\left|M_{k}^{(2)}-M_{k-1}^{(2)}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|A_{k}^{(2)}-B_{k}^{(2)}\right|~\leq~\frac{1}{h^{\prime}(x)\Delta(x)}.

Therefore,
\|M_{k}-M_{k-1}\|^{2}=O(n^{2\gamma})
. In particular, with
\gamma<1
, one has

\|M_{k}-M_{k-1}\|=o(n^{(1+\gamma)/2}).

For any
\varepsilon>0
, for
n
large enough, the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|M_{k}-M_{k-1}\|\geq\varepsilon n^{(1+\gamma)/2}\}}
is thus almost surely null, which proves that

\frac{1}{n^{1+\gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|M_{k}-M_{k-1}\|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|M_{k}-M_{k-1}\|\geq\varepsilon n^{(1+\gamma)/2}\}}\big|F_{k-1}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

As a consequence, the central limit theorem for vector martingales yields

\frac{M_{n}}{n^{(1+\gamma)/2}}\overset{\textrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma(x)),

(25)

where
\Sigma(x)
denotes the limit in (
24
), which states the asymptotic normality of the martingale term.

Gathering (
16
), (
23
), (
24
) and (
25
) together, and by virtue of Slutsky’s theorem, we obtain

n^{(1-\gamma)/2}\left[\widehat{\lambda}_{n}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8672,0.6289,0.8672}\text{\footnotesize$\clubsuit$}}}(x)-\lambda(x)\right]\overset{\textrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\tau^{2}(h^{\prime}(x))^{2}\mu(h(x))}{D(x)^{2}}\right),

which proves the expected result. The formula of the variance is obtained from (
8
).
