Association of an inter‐arm systolic blood pressure difference with all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality: A meta‐analysis of cohort studies
Authors: Min Li, Fangfang Fan, Lin Qiu, Wei Ma, Yan Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis meta‐analysis evaluated the potential association of a simultaneously measured inter‐arm systolic blood pressure difference (IASBPD) and all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. The Medline, Cochrane Library, Embase, and PubMed databases were searched through to April 14, 2023 for relevant literature. The outcomes were the associations of IASBPD with all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. Finally, 10 cohort studies that included 15 320 individuals were included. An IASBPD of ≥15 mm Hg was associated with increased all‐cause mortality (pooled hazard ratio [HR] 1.28, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.02–1.61) but an IASBPD of ≥10 mm Hg was not (pooled HR 1.28, 95% CI 0.89–1.85). The pooled HR for cardiovascular mortality was 1.88 (95% CI 1.31–2.71) for an IASBPD of ≥10 mm Hg and 1.93 (95% CI 1.24–2.99) for an IASBPD of ≥15 mm Hg. Subgroup analysis showed that younger patients (HR 9.03, 95% CI 2.00–40.82, p = .004) with an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg were at higher risk of cardiovascular mortality than older patients (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.06–2.64, p = .03); the difference between groups was statistically significant (p = .04). In conclusions, our findings show that a simultaneously measured IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg predicts increased all‐cause mortality and an IASBPD of ≥15 mm Hg or ≥10 mm Hg predicts increased cardiovascular mortality. An IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg appears to be more correlated with cardiovascular mortality in younger patients than in older patients.Keywords: all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, inter‐arm systolic blood pressure difference, simultaneous measurements

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is an important public health challenge in both economically developing and developed countries 1 and is associated with multiple cardiovascular and renal outcomes. 2 Despite several initiatives, the prevalence of raised blood pressure (BP) is increasing globally. 3 , 4 Numerous guidelines for the management of hypertension stress that BP should initially be measured in both upper arms, and if there is a consistent difference of >10 mm Hg in repeated measurements between arms, the higher BP should be used. 5 , 6 Therefore, the inter‐arm systolic BP difference (IASBPD) has attracted increasing attention from clinicians. A difference in BP measurements between arms has been reported in cohorts with hypertension, 7 diabetes, 8 and peripheral arterial disease. 9 A meta‐analysis showed that the prevalence of IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg was 11.2% in hypertension, 7.4% in diabetes, and 3.6% for a general adult population. 10 Clark and coworkers demonstrated an independent association of IASBPD with cardiovascular events and mortality. 11 Other meta‐analyses reported by Cao and coworkers 12 and Zhou and coworkers 13 showed that IASBPD of ≥10 mm Hg and ≥15 mm Hg were associated with increased all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. However, most studies have not distinguished further between the different BP measurement methods.

Simultaneous measurements resulted in fewer patients with an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg as compared with sequential measurements. 10 One reason for the higher IASBPD with sequential measurements could be the white‐coat effect, whereby the first measurement is higher than the one that follows. 14 Furthermore, BP is a variable hemodynamic phenomenon that fluctuates over time, making sequential measurements difficult to compare. 15 Although a subgroup analysis by Cao and coworkers 12 showed that an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg when measured simultaneously was associated with increased all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, several cohort studies of simultaneous BP measurements have been published in the last 8 years. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Therefore, it was possible for us to perform an updated meta‐analysis to assess the predictive value of an IASBPD for all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in cohort studies.

METHODS

Literature search and study selection

A systematic review of the published literature was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and registered on PROSPERO as CRD42023423694. 22 We performed a comprehensive online search of the published literature through to April 14, 2023 using the Medline, Cochrane Library, Embase, and PubMed databases to identify all published clinical studies that evaluated IASBPD. The search strategy used relevant keywords and medical subject heading terms, including (“inter‐arm blood pressure difference” or “subclavian stenosis”), and (“mortality” or “prognosis” or “survival”). Further potentially eligible publications were identified by hand‐searching the bibliographies of the included articles and reviews.

Eligibility criteria

Studies qualified for inclusion if they included sufficient information on key points. The inclusion criteria were as follows: a prospective or retrospective cohort study design; outcomes data on all‐cause mortality and/or cardiovascular mortality; simultaneous measurement of BP in both arms; and comparison of an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg versus an IASBPD < 10 mm Hg and/or an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg versus an IASBPD < 15 mm Hg. Abstracts, case reports, conference presentations, editorials, and expert opinions were excluded.

Extraction of data and critical appraisal

Data were extracted by two authors working independently using a predefined standardized protocol and data collection instrument. Information on study design, number of participants, duration of follow‐up, mean age at baseline, sex, prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and smoking, and cutoff values for inter‐arm BP differences were recorded. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was used to evaluate the quality of the included studies. 23 Scores of 0−3, 4−6, and 7−9 were considered poor, fair, and good, respectively. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus between the authors.

Outcome measures

Given that the objective of this review was to investigate the association of IASBPD with cardiovascular risk and outcomes, we prespecified all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality as outcome measures. The outcome measures were not further categorized into primary or secondary outcomes because they were thought to be equally important indicators of global cardiovascular risk.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as the mean ± standard deviation and numerical data as the number (percentage). In order to combine the results of the included studies, we chose the hazard ratio (HR) estimates calculated by multivariable models with the most complete adjustment for potential confounding factors. The pooled HR and 95% confidence interval [CI] (participants with IASBPD above the cutoff versus those with IASBPD below the cutoff) were shown in forest plots to measure the association between IASBPD and risk of mortality. Considering that the IASBPD cutoff value is usually defined as 10 mm Hg or 15 mm Hg, we analyzed the associations of interest separately for these values. Pooled estimates were calculated using fixed‐effect models (I2 < 50%), whereas random‐effect models were applied in the event of heterogeneity across studies (I2 ≥ 50%). We checked for publication bias by visual examination of funnel plots and assessment of asymmetry using Egger's regression test. To test the stability of our meta‐analysis further, we performed multiple subgroup analyses based on mean age at baseline, overall health status, sample size, and follow‐up duration.

All statistical analyses were performed using the RevMan software package (Review Manager, version 5.1; The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and Stata software 12.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). All p‐values were two‐tailed and considered statistically significant at <.05.

RESULTS

Study selection and characteristics

The search yielded 1230 potentially relevant records, 1108 of which were excluded after screening of the title and abstract. We carefully read the full‐text versions of the remaining articles and further excluded papers that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Eight articles were excluded because they used sequential BP measurements. A study by Aboyans and coworkers 9 combined data from three cohorts, so we used the results of the individual cohorts rather than those for the total population, two of which (A and C) measured BP simultaneously in both arms and reported mortality data. Finally, 10 cohort studies (in 11 cohorts) were included in the meta‐analysis (Figure 1).

Flow diagram showing the process used to select the study participants.

Tables 1 and S1 summarize the characteristics of the included studies. The total number of participants was 15 320, and the number of participants in the individual studies ranged from 200 to 4790. The median follow‐up duration ranged from 342 days to 16.4 years. Six studies had a prospective cohort design 16 , 18 , 20 , 21 , 24 , 25 and the remainder were retrospective cohort studies. 9 , 17 , 19 , 26 All the study populations contained a proportion of patients with hypertension or diabetes. Assessment of study quality yielded an average score of 7.1. Nine studies had a score ≥7.

All‐cause mortality

Six studies reported mortality data for groups defined by an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg and four cohorts reported mortality data at an IASBPD cutoff of 15 mm Hg (Figure 2). The pooled HR for an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg was 1.28 (95% CI 0.89−1.85); there was statistically significant heterogeneity across the studies (p for heterogeneity, .01; I2 = 65%). The pooled HR for an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg was 1.28 (95% CI 1.02−1.61), and there was no statistically significant heterogeneity across studies (p for heterogeneity, .23; I2 = 30%).

Pooled hazard ratios for all‐cause mortality at an IASBPD of ≥10 mm Hg (A) or ≥15 mm Hg (B). CI, confidence interval; IASBPD, inter‐arm systolic blood pressure difference; IV, inverse variance; SE, standard error.

Cardiovascular mortality

The overall results for the association between IASBPD and the risk of cardiovascular mortality are presented as a forest plot in Figure 3. Five cohorts reported on the association of an IASBPD cutoff of 10 mm Hg and the risk of cardiovascular mortality. Compared with participants who had an IASBPD < 10 mm Hg, the pooled HR for cardiovascular mortality was 1.88 (95% CI 1.31−2.71) in those with an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg, with no statistically significant heterogeneity found across studies (p for heterogeneity, .27; I2 = 23.0%). Four cohorts reported on an IASBPD with a cutoff of 15 mm Hg and risk of cardiovascular mortality. The pooled HR for cardiovascular mortality in participants with an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg versus those with an IASBPD < 15 mm Hg was 1.93 (95% CI 1.24−2.99), with no statistically significant heterogeneity across studies (p for heterogeneity, .15; I2 = 43%).

Pooled hazard ratios for cardiovascular mortality at an IASBPD of ≥10 mm Hg (A) or ≥15 mm Hg (B). CI, confidence interval; IASBPD, inter‐arm systolic blood pressure difference; IV, inverse variance; SE, standard error.

Subgroup analysis

The results of the stratified analyses are presented in Tables 2 and 3. In the cohorts with a cutoff of 15 mm Hg, we found a stronger correlation with cardiovascular mortality in younger patients (HR 9.03, 95% CI 2.00−40.82, p = .004) than in older patients (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.06−2.64, p = .03); with statistical differences between groups (p = .04). Other subgroups did not show statistically significant differences in terms of the relationship between IASBPD and cardiovascular mortality.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of funnel plots for all studies showed partial symmetry for the associations of IASBPD with all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality (Figures S1 and S2). The results of the Egger's test suggested no evidence of publication bias in the analysis of the relationship between IASBPD and cardiovascular mortality (p = .589 for a cutoff of 10 mm Hg and p = .066 for a cutoff of 15 mm Hg). Similarly, there were no evidence of publication bias in the analysis of the relationship between IASBPD with a cutoff of 10 mm Hg and the risk of all‐cause mortality (p = .654). However, Egger's test indicated potential publication bias in the association of the IASBPD with a cutoff of 15 mm Hg (p = .043). Therefore, we used the trim‐and‐fill method. We found no indications of publication bias using this method (with no new studies added), indicating that our findings are stable.

DISCUSSION

Our meta‐analysis demonstrated that a simultaneously measured IASBPD of ≥15 mm Hg was a predictor of increased all‐cause mortality but an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg was not and that both an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg and an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg predicted an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality based on 10 studies that included 11 cohorts. An IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg appeared to be more correlated with cardiovascular mortality in younger patients than in older patients

A meta‐analysis by Cao and coworkers 12 found that a simultaneously measured IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg was a strong predictor of all‐cause mortality while an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg was not. This finding conflicts with the accepted opinion. After incorporating more cohorts, we found that a simultaneously measured IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg was a predictor of all‐cause mortality and an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg was not. In an angiographic study, an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg was highly specific and moderately sensitive for subclavian stenosis but an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg was more sensitive but less specific. 27 Of note, an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg was associated with carotid or aortic artery disease and vascular disease caused by high BP. 28 In summary, an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg seemed to have a greater predictive value for adverse outcomes. A meta‐analysis by Clark and coworkers 29 also found that an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg was associated with an increase in total mortality (HR 1.3, 95% CI 1.10–4.50; p = .03) but that the pooled HR for an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg was not, although none of the studies in their meta‐analysis used a simultaneous measurement method. In our meta‐analysis, we used simultaneous IASBPD measurements and obtained consistent results. The lack of a clear description of the preferred measurement method has led to wide variation in assessment and interpretation of IASBPD, with undesirable consequences. 15 Another study showed that the inter‐arm difference was smaller for simultaneously measured IASBPD than for sequentially measured IASBPD and that the number of patients with clinically relevant inter‐arm BP differences with similar reproducibility was markedly reduced. 30 Sequential measurement also reflected within‐visit BP variability (BPV), which has been identified as an important risk factor for mortality 31 and stroke, 32 thereby expanding the effect of BP difference. For a reliable estimation of the inter‐arm BP difference, simultaneous measurement should be performed at a patient's initial visit, which may give the opportunity to obtain the most accurate double‐arm measurements. 6

However, there is still some controversy regarding the simultaneously measured BP difference and mortality. In a study by Clark and coworkers, 514 patients with diabetes who were followed up for 52.4 months showed no clear evidence of higher all‐cause mortality whether the IASBPD was ≥10 mm Hg or ≥15 mm Hg. 25 Another prospective study in which BP was measured simultaneously in both arms in 3133 community‐dwelling older persons found a significant correlation of both IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg and IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg with total mortality over a period of 4 years. The differences in the included population may be the reason for this finding. Given the above‐mentioned inconsistency in results caused by inclusion of different populations, we performed a subgroup analysis of all‐cause deaths. We observed that compared with patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), patients without ACS have a certain predictive value of IASBPD with a cut‐off of 10 mm Hg (p = .03 vs. p = .5). Similarly, IASBPD seemed to have better predictive value in a community‐dwelling population than in a hospital cohort (p < .01 vs. p = .81), although there was no statistically significant between‐group difference. Another study showed that IASBPD was associated with a higher risk of future vascular events only in patients without clinical manifestations of vascular disease. 33 Therefore, specific threshold values need to be established for different populations.

Our meta‐analysis also indicated that the risk of cardiovascular mortality was increased by 88% in patients with an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg and by 93% in those with an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg. Previous studies reached similar conclusions. 12 , 13 There are several potential explanations for this significant positive association of an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg and an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg with the risk of cardiovascular mortality. Some studies have demonstrated a relationship between high IASBPD and atherosclerosis. 34 , 35 IASBPD was reported to increase vascular stiffness, 36 and an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg was found to be related to carotid intima‐media thickness. 27 Therefore, atherosclerosis may be an important mechanism; however, some non‐atherosclerotic mechanisms might also be responsible for the correlation between the significant IASBPD and cardiovascular mortality. Hwang and coworkers found that patients with an IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg had slower recovery of heart rate, 37 and a slower heart rate has been reported to be associated with impaired fibrinolysis expressed as elevated plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 activity, tissue plasminogen activator antigen and fibrinogen to promote atherothrombosis. 38 besides, an impaired autonomous system might another cause. 39 Further studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms of this relationship.

In cohorts with an IASBPD cutoff of 15 mm Hg, we found that the correlation with cardiovascular mortality was stronger in younger patients (aged < 65 years) than in older counterparts (aged ≥65 years) and that the difference between groups was statistically significant (p = .04). A study found no association of IASBPD with mortality in a cohort of hospitalized elderly individuals with a mean age of 85 ± 5 years. 16 A large prospective study in middle‐aged men and women without cardiovascular disease showed that an interarm systolic BP difference is common and associated with a significantly increased risk of future cardiovascular events. 40 We also observed that an IASBPD with a cutoff of 10 mm Hg had better predictive value for cardiovascular death in younger patients without ACS than in older patients with ACS (p < .01 vs. p = .38), although the between‐group difference was not statistically significant (p = .14). Because the prevalence of traditional risk factors was high in elderly patients, these risk factors may have “neutralized” the influence of IASBPD. Furthermore, a large proportion of the older patients were well controlled on medication, thereby eliminating potential vascular injury resulting from the atherosclerotic process. 16 The difference in distribution of atherosclerosis according to age may be another reason. Therefore, IASBPD might be valuable as an additional risk factor for cardiovascular mortality in populations with a low risk of cardiovascular disease.

The follow‐up duration seemed to affect the relationship between IASBPD with cardiovascular mortality with a cutoff of 15 mm Hg, studies within 5 years but not after 5 years showed predictive value, although there was no statistically significant difference between these two groups. This phenomenon may be related to BPV. BP is a variable factor, and patient compliance and adjustment of treatment plans may also affect the prognosis. Another study found that the rate of detection of IASBPD decreased as the systolic BP level decreased in hypertensive patients on antihypertensive therapy. 41 Therefore, long‐term dynamic monitoring of BP is necessary.

This meta‐analysis has several limitations. First, there was some moderate heterogeneity in all‐cause comparisons at an IASBPD cutoff of 10 mm Hg. The results of the sensitivity analysis suggested that the study by Kilic and coworkers 20 was the source of this heterogeneity. Their study included patients with ACS and excluded stroke, a history of subclavian artery stenosis, and patients who needed inotropic therapy, all of which can be associated with mortality. Removal of that study changed our final result (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.01−2.03; p = .04; I2 = 57%). This is consistent with the possible differences in predictive value among different populations we mentioned earlier. Second, different studies adjusted for different covariates. Some studies adjusted for relatively similar covariates, but others had a very different mix of covariates. An entirely different result may have been obtained if all studies had adjusted for the same covariates. Third, we did not search specifically for pulse‐wave velocity studies but are aware that recent technology can generate bilateral BP measurement data, which means that we could have missed relevant publications.

CONCLUSIONS

An IASBPD ≥10 mm Hg or ≥15 mm Hg could be a useful predictor of increased cardiovascular mortality in patient populations, and an IASBPD ≥15 mm Hg might help to predict all‐cause mortality. Therefore, both a cut‐off of 10 and 15 mm Hg may have clinical significance.

Due to the influence of sample sizes, patient population, associated comorbidities, age and methods of BP assessment, there may be differences in the threshold values. Further prospective research is needed in the future to determine the optimal cutoff values for different populations.
