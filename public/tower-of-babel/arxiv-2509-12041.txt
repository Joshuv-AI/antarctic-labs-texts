Leading order asymptotics for non-local energies and the Read-Shockley law
Peter J. Grabner, Florian Theil

Abstract
. We study an energy minimization problem ∑ i ≠ j W ⁡ ( z i − z j ) \sum_{i\neq j}W(z_{i}-z_{j}) for N N points { z 1 , … , z N } \left\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\right\} with applications in dislocation theory. The N N points lie in the two-dimensional domain ℝ × [ − π , π ] \mathbb{R}\times[-\pi,\pi] , where the kernel W W is derived from the Volterra potential V ⁡ ( x , y ) = x 2 x 2 + y 2 − 1 2 ​ log ⁡ ( x 2 + y 2 ) V(x,y)=\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\log(x^{2}+y^{2}) . We prove that the minimum energy is given by − N ​ log ⁡ N + 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ) -N\log{N}+\mathcal{O}(N) . This lower bound recovers the leading order term of the Read-Shockley law characterizing the energy of small angle grain boundaries in polycrystals.



1.
Introduction

The classical Read-Shockely formula

\gamma=-\gamma_{0}\,\theta\log\theta+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta),\quad 0\leq\theta\ll 1

expresses the energy density
\gamma
of small angle grain boundaries in terms of the tilt angle
\theta
and the constant
\gamma_{0}=\frac{\mu b}{4\pi(1-\nu)}
where
b
is the length of the Burgers vector,
\nu\in[0,1)
is the Poisson ratio and

\mu>0
is the shear modulus.
In
[
13
]
this formula is obtained as the leading order term for a straight equi-spaced dislocation array in a two-dimensional isotropic linearly elastic medium. This derivation does not address the question whether such configurations are energetically optimal.

Recently there has been renewed interest in detailed mathematical modeling of grain boundaries, e.g.
[
12
]
. The analysis of phenomena like grain boundary motion involves careful use of grain boundary energy models, see
[
5
]
. Therefore, it is desirable to derive the Read-Shockley formula from variational principles.

The first step in this direction has been taken by
Luckhaus and Lauteri; in
[
8
]
they analyzed a non-linear variational continuum model

{\mathcal{F}}_{\epsilon}(A,S)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\int_{\Omega\setminus S_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{dist}^{2}(A,\mathrm{SO}(2))\,\mathrm{d}x+|S_{\epsilon}|\right),

where
\Omega=[-1,1]^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a square,
S\subset\Omega
,
S_{\epsilon}=S+B_{\epsilon}(0)
,
A\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
and the pair
(A,S)
satisfies admissibility conditions. Their main result is matching upper and lower bounds for the infimum of
\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}
which are uniform in
\epsilon
and consistent with the Read-Shockley scaling. More precisely, it is shown that

(1)

\displaystyle C_{1}\leq\frac{\inf\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(A,S)}{\theta\log\theta}\leq C_{2}

if

A
satisfies the boundary condition

A|_{x=\pm 1}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\pm\sin\theta\\
\mp\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

It is worth mentioning that in
[
3
]
it is shown that
\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}
has a
\Gamma
-limit for
\epsilon\to 0
which can be written in the form of a grain boundary energy density.

Currently, it is not known, if it is possible to choose
C_{1}=C_{2}
in (
1
) as
\epsilon,\theta\to 0
. To address this problem we revisit the quadratic setting where the Read-Shockley formula has been derived. Our main result is a rigorous proof that the formula holds even if the dislocations are not necessarily equispaced, or on a straight line (Theorem
2.1
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall the dislocation model introduced by Read and Shockley
[
13
]
and present it in a variational formulation, which allows then to put it in the context of discrete energy minimization where one seeks to minimize finite sums of the form

{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z,z^{\prime}\in\omega\\
z\neq z^{\prime}\end{subarray}}K(z-z^{\prime}).

Here
\omega
is a finite subset of the underlying space (
\Omega=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
) and
K:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a kernel function; we set
N=|\omega|
. The kernel function
K
is singular at
\{0\}
which prevents the use of Bochner’s theorem. The key step in our approach is the construction of a positive definite regularization
K_{t}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
so that
K-K_{t}\geq o(1)
for
0<t\ll 1
.

Taking a sequence of point sets
\omega
with
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\omega}\delta_{z}\rightharpoonup\mu
and rescaling with
N^{-2}
the corresponding discrete energies converge in the sense of
\Gamma
-limits to the continuous energy

E_{K}(\mu)=\iint\limits_{\Omega\times\Omega}K(z-z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z^{\prime}),

where the measure
\mu
ranges through all Borel probability measures on
\Omega
, cf.
[
10
]
.
Especially, the empirical measures of point configurations
\omega
minimizing the discrete energy converge weakly to a minimizer of the continuous energy (see also
[
1
, Chapter 4]
).

Continuous energies are the object of study of classical potential theory (e.g.
[
7
]
). An important general result is the fact that under the condition of strict positive definiteness of the kernel
K
and compactness of the space
\Omega
the empirical measures given by the discrete minimizers weakly tend to the unique continuous minimizer. In our case we neither have strict positive definiteness nor compactness of the space, which forces us to use a different methodology.

In Section
3
we study the continuous energy problem for the Read-Shockley kernel and observe that without an additional confining external potential the continuous minimizer of the energy is not unique; we are still able to characterize all minimizers.
In particular, it turns out that minimizers are not necessarily concentrated on a one-dimensional set (Proposition
3.1
). On the other hand, the concavity of the Read-Shockley energy with respect to
\theta
promotes grain-boundaries with a one-dimensional support. This shows that the discrete model measures genuinely finer properties than the continuous
\Gamma
-limit.

We also study the continuous energy in the presence of an external potential proportional to
|x|
(the
x
-coordinate of the point) and determine a phase transition between non-existence and uniqueness of the minimizer depending on the strength of the external field.

2.
The Read-Shockley formula

2.1.
Derivation of the variational model

We work in the setting of two-dimensional, linear elasticity.
The elastic and strain fields
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}
and
\beta:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
corresponding to a collection of
edge dislocations with Burgers vector
{\bf b}=(b,0)
and cores located in
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfy the equilibrium equations

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\sigma&={\bf 0},\\
\mathrm{curl}(\beta)&=\sum\limits_{z\in\omega}\delta_{z}{\bf b},\end{cases}

in the sense of distributions. See
[
11
,
4
]
for a discussion of the model and the properties of solutions of (
2
).

Analytic expressions for
\sigma
and
\beta
are well known in the case of isotropic elasticity where
\sigma
and
\beta
satisfy the constitutive relation

\sigma=\frac{2\mu\nu}{1-2\nu}\mathrm{tr}(\beta)\mathrm{Id}+\mu(\beta+\beta^{T})
.
Define

\displaystyle u_{0}=

\displaystyle\frac{-b}{8\pi(1-\nu)}\begin{pmatrix}4(1-\nu)\arctan(x,y)-\frac{2xy}{r^{2}}\\
(1-2\nu)\log(x^{2}+y^{2})+\frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{r^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}=

\displaystyle\frac{2\gamma_{0}}{r^{4}}\begin{pmatrix}-y(3x^{2}+y^{2})&x(x^{2}-y^{2})\\
x(x^{2}-y^{2})&y(x^{2}-y^{2})\end{pmatrix},

where
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
and
\arctan(x,y)\in(-\pi,\pi]
denotes the argument of
x+iy
with a branch cut along
\mathcal{C}=\{(0,y)\;:\;y<0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, cf.

[
6
, eqns. (3.43), (3.45), (3.46)]
.
Then
\beta=\sum_{z\in\omega}\nabla u_{0}(\cdot-z)
,
\sigma=\sum_{z\in\omega}\sigma_{0}(\cdot-z)
satisfy (
2
). Since
u_{0}
has a jump discontinuity along
\mathcal{C}
we need to clarify that
\nabla u_{0}
is defined to be the regular part of the distributional derivative, i.e. it ignores the jump.

The elastic energy density associated with
\sigma
and
\beta
is given by

f(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\beta(\zeta):\sigma(\zeta),

where
\beta:\sigma=\mathrm{trace}(\beta^{T}\sigma)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}\beta_{ij}\sigma_{ij}
.
Note that
f
is singular on
\omega
;
the total elastic energy stored in a compact domain
\Omega
such that
\Omega\cap\omega=\emptyset
is given by

\mathcal{E}=\int_{\Omega}f(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Since
f
is a bilinear function in
\beta
and
\sigma
we can decompose it into pair-interactions

\displaystyle f(\zeta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{z\in\omega}\beta_{0}(\zeta-z)\right):\left(\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\omega}\sigma_{0}(\zeta-z^{\prime})\right)=\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in\omega}q_{z,z^{\prime}}(\zeta),

where

q_{z,z^{\prime}}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\beta_{0}(\zeta-z):\sigma_{0}(\zeta-z^{\prime}).

Now we consider the energy density created by periodic configurations
\widehat{\omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying
\widehat{\omega}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(\omega+2\pi nhe_{2})
, where
h>0
,
e_{2}=(0,1)
and
\omega\subset\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}\times[-\pi h,\pi h)
.
Let

q^{\mathrm{per}}_{z,z^{\prime}}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta(\zeta-z-2\pi nh\,e_{2}):\sigma(\zeta-z^{\prime}-2\pi n^{\prime}h\,e_{2}),

then

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in\widehat{\omega}}\int_{\Omega}q_{z,z^{\prime}}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in\omega}\int_{\Omega}q_{z,z^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

as long as
\Omega
is compact and
\omega\cap\Omega=\emptyset
. With this notation the normalized total energy stored in
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}\times[-\pi h,\pi h]
admits the representation

(4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)=\sum_{z\in\omega}U_{\mathrm{self}}(z)+\sum_{z\neq z^{\prime}\in\omega}U_{\mathrm{int}}(z-z^{\prime})

where

(5)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{self}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi h}\int_{\Omega_{h}\setminus B_{\rho}(z)}q^{\mathrm{per}}_{z,z}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta,

(6)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{int}}(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi h}\int_{\Omega_{h}}q^{\mathrm{per}}_{z,z^{\prime}}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

To avoid the complication that
q_{z,z}\not\in L^{1}(\Omega_{h})

we have removed
B_{\rho}(z)
from the domain of integration in
U_{\mathrm{self}}
, see
[
11
]
for a discussion of this step. The radius
\rho
is sometimes referred to as ‘core radius’ in the literature, its purpose is to account for atomistic effects. The precise value only affects the infimum of
\mathcal{E}
at non-leading order when
N=o(h)
,
h\gg 1
. For the sake of notational simplicity we will choose
\rho=b
.

2.2.
Main results

Now we are in a position to provide a variational derivation of the leading order constant in the Read-Shockley formula.
The key property in grain boundary modeling is the density of dislocations per unit-length. The tilt
\theta
angle between two adjacent grains satisfies the relation

\sin\theta=\frac{b}{D},

where
D
is the average spacing between dislocations. In our notation
D=\frac{2\pi h}{N}
, so for small angle grain boundaries where
N={\mathcal{O}}(h)
one obtains the approximation

\theta=\frac{Nb}{2\pi h}\text{ for }0<\theta\ll 1.

Theorem 2.1

(Read-Shockley formula)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be given by (
4
). Then

(7)

\displaystyle\min_{|\omega_{N}|=N}{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)=-\gamma_{0}\,\theta\log\theta+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta),\quad 0<\theta\ll 1,

where
\gamma_{0}=\frac{\mu b}{4\pi(1-\nu)}
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
and other results in the article crucially rely on an analytic representation of
U_{\mathrm{int}}
. This result is well known, we include it here for the convenience of readers.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

(8)

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\alpha\,x\sinh x}{\cosh x-\cos y}-\log(2(\cosh x-\cos y))+(1-\alpha)|x|\right].

The functions
U_{\mathrm{self}}
and
U_{\mathrm{int}}
admit the representation

(9)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{self}}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h}(\log h+{\mathcal{O}}(1)),\quad h\gg 1,

(10)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{int}}(z,z^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h}\,W_{1}((z-z^{\prime})/h).

Our main result is a lower bound for the interaction energy.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
C=1-\log 2<0.31
.
For each
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}\times(-\pi,\pi]
with the property that
|\omega|=N
the inequality

(11)

\sum_{z\neq z^{\prime}\in\omega}W_{\alpha}(z-z^{\prime})\geq-N(\log N+C)

holds.

Conjecture 2.4
.

Proposition
2.3
is true for
C=0
. This would imply that the minimum energy is attained for equally spaced points on a circle
x=x_{0}
. Numerical experiments support this conjecture.

With these results we are in a position to justify the Read-Shockley formula.

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
2.1
and Propositions
2.2
and
2.3

Proof of Thm.
2.1
.

We will establish lower and upper bounds which match at leading order.

Lower bound

If
\omega\subset\Omega_{h}
has the property that
|\omega|=N
then Prop.
2.2
implies that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h}\left(\sum_{z\in\omega}(\log h+{\mathcal{O}}(1))+\sum_{z\neq z^{\prime}\in\omega}W_{1}((z-z^{\prime})/h)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h}\left(N(\log h+{\mathcal{O}}(1))+\sum_{z\neq z^{\prime}\in\omega}W_{1}((z-z^{\prime})/h)\right).

After rescaling
\omega
the second term reads
\sum_{z\neq z^{\prime}\in\omega}W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})
and we can apply Prop.
2.3
. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)\geq\gamma_{0}\frac{Nb}{2\pi h}\left(\log\left(\frac{2\pi h}{b}\right)-\log\left(\frac{2\pi}{b}\right)-\log N-C\right).

Recalling that
\theta=\frac{Nb}{2\pi h}
we obtain the desired lower bound

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)\geq-\gamma_{0}\,\theta\left((\log\theta+C+\log(2\pi/b)\right).

Upper bound

We will demonstrate that

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{z\neq z^{\prime}\in\omega_{N}}W_{\alpha}(z-z^{\prime})=-N\log N

if
\omega_{N}
is an equispaced vertical configuration, i.e.

\omega_{N}=\{(x_{0},2\pi j/N)\;:\;j=0\ldots N-1\},

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is arbitrary.
This result can also be found in
[
14
]
. For the convenience of the reader we include a simple, self-contained proof here.

Observe that the first term in
W_{\alpha}
vanishes if
x=0
therefore we only have to analyze the logarithmic term.

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}{\bf 1}_{\ell\neq j}\left[\log(1-\cos(2\pi(\ell-j)/N))+\log 2\right]=\frac{N}{2}\log(F_{N})

where

\displaystyle F_{N}=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}[2(1-\cos(2j\pi/N))]=\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}\left[4\sin(\pi j/N)^{2}\right]=\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}(2\sin(\pi j/N))\right]^{2}

The sine-product formula

(14)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\sin(x+\pi j/N)=2^{1-N}\sin(Nx)

implies that

F_{N}=\left(\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}(2\sin(x+\pi j/N))}{2\sin(x)}\right)^{2}=\left(\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin(Nx)}{\sin(x)}\right)^{2}=N^{2}

and therefore the right hand side of (
13
) equals
-N\log N
which is (
12
).

∎

Proof of Prop.
2.2
.

We prove the representation of the periodized interaction kernel.

Step 1. Periodization identities.

To simplify the task the individual terms are periodized separately. We will show that the following identities hold.

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{x^{2}+(2\pi n+y)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2x}\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x-\cos y},

(16)

\displaystyle\log(x^{2}+y^{2})+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\log\left[\frac{x^{2}+(2\pi n+y)^{2}\big)}{(2\pi n)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\log\left(2(\cosh(x)-\cos(y))\right).

To show (
15
) we start with the classical expansion

\pi\cot\left(\frac{y\pm ix}{2}\right)=\frac{2\pi(y\mp ix)}{x^{2}+y^{2}}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{2\pi}{(y-2\pi n)\pm ix}-\frac{1}{n}\right).

Subtracting the two equations with the two choices of the sign gives

\pi\cot\left(\frac{y-ix}{2}\right)-\pi\cot\left(\frac{y+ix}{2}\right)=\frac{2\pi i\sinh(x)}{\cosh(x)-\cos(y)}=4\pi i\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+(y-2\pi n)^{2}},

which gives (
15
).

For the proof of (
16
) we start with the classical product expansion for the sine function again evaluated at
\frac{y\pm ix}{2\pi}

\displaystyle 2(\cosh(x)-\cos(y))

\displaystyle=4\sin\left(\frac{y+ix}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{y-ix}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(x^{2}+y^{2})\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{(y+ix)^{2}}{(2\pi n)^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{(y-ix)^{2}}{(2\pi n)^{2}}\right).

Regrouping the factors in the product gives

2(\cosh(x)-\cos(y))=(x^{2}+y^{2})\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x^{2}+(y+2\pi n)^{2})(x^{2}+(y-2\pi n)^{2})}{(2\pi n)^{4}}.

Taking the logarithm now gives (
16
).

Step 2. Periodized stress.

Define the Volterra potentials

(17)

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}(x,y)=\alpha\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\log(x^{2}+y^{2}).

Differentiation shows that
\sigma
admits the representation

\displaystyle\sigma=2\gamma_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y}V_{1}&-\partial_{x}V_{1},\\
-\partial_{x}V_{1}&\partial_{y}V_{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Equations (
15
) and (
16
) imply that for
(x,y)\in\Omega_{h}

(18)

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}(x,y)+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\left[V_{\alpha}(x,y+2\pi hn)-\log(2\pi n)\right]=

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}(x/h,y/h).

Formula (
18
) implies that

(19)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sigma(x,y+2\pi nh)=\frac{2\gamma_{0}}{h}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y}W_{1}&-\partial_{x}W_{1}\\
-\partial_{x}W_{1}&\partial_{y}W_{-1}\end{pmatrix}(z/h).

Uniform convergence away from the singular set justifies differentiation
under the sum.
The periodized strain
\beta_{\mathrm{per}}
is defined in a similar fashion.

Step 3. Reduction to a line integral.

Recall that
\beta=\sum_{z\in\omega}\nabla u_{0}(\cdot-z)
where
u_{0}
has a jump discontinuity along the branch cut
\mathcal{C}
. Since
\nabla\!\cdot\!\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}=0
(cf (
2
)) we can reduce the the two-dimensional integrals in (
5
) and (
6
) to a one-dimensional integrals along
\mathcal{C}
using partial integration.

If
u^{\pm}
denotes the limiting values of
u
when
\zeta
approaches
\mathcal{C}
from above (+) or below (-) then
u^{-}-u^{+}=(b,0)
, this follows immediately from the requirement that
\mathrm{curl}\nabla u_{0}=\delta_{0}{\bf b}
(eqn. (
2
)).
Integrating the right-hand side of (
6
) by parts implies

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{int}}(z,z^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi h}\int_{\Omega_{h}\setminus{\mathcal{C}}}\beta_{\mathrm{per}}(\zeta-z):\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}(\zeta-z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi h}\int_{0}^{\infty}e_{2}\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}(z-z^{\prime}-(0,x))\underbrace{[u_{\mathrm{per}}^{-}-u_{\mathrm{per}}^{+}]}_{=(b,0)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq.sigmaper}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{x}W_{1}(z-z^{\prime}-(x,0))\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h}W_{1}(z-z^{\prime}),

which is (
10
).

Step 4. Self-energy.

The integration domain in (
5
) is slightly more involved because we have to remove the non-integrable singularity.

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{self}}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi h}\int_{\Omega\setminus({\mathcal{C}}\cup B_{\rho})}\beta_{\mathrm{per}}:\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi h}\biggl[\int_{\mathcal{C}\setminus B_{\rho}}e_{2}\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}{\bf b}\,\mathrm{d}x-\rho\int_{S^{1}}\xi\cdot[\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}u_{\mathrm{per}}](\rho\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h^{2}}\int_{\rho}^{\infty}\partial_{x}W_{\alpha}(-x/h,0)\,\mathrm{d}x-O\left(\frac{\rho b}{h}\log(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{4\pi h}\,\biggl[\underbrace{\frac{\rho}{h}\tanh\left(\rho/h\right)}_{={\mathcal{O}}((\frac{\rho}{h})^{2})}-\log(\underbrace{\cosh(\rho/h)-1}_{=\frac{1}{2}(\rho/h)^{2}\,(1+{\mathcal{O}}((\rho/h)^{2})})-\log 2)+O\left(-\frac{\rho}{b}\log\rho\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}b}{2\pi h}\left[\log(h/\rho)+O\left((\rho/h)^{2}-\frac{\rho}{b}\log\rho\right)\right].

This is eqn. (
9
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

The kernel
W_{\alpha}
has an analytical Fourier representation

W_{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{k_{2}\neq 0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\alpha)k_{1}^{2}+(1+\alpha)k_{2}^{2}}{(k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2})^{2}}e^{i(k_{1}x+k_{2}y)}\,dk_{1}.

Notice that

-\frac{|x|}{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(k_{1}x)-1}{k_{1}^{2}}\,dk_{1},

which can be interpreted as the summand for
k_{2}=0
. The Fourier transform is non-negative for
-1\leq\alpha\leq 1
; for
\alpha=1
the Fourier transform simplifies to
{\widehat{W}}_{1}(k)=\frac{k_{2}^{2}}{\|k\|^{4}}
.

Furthermore, we have the expression

W_{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{\infty}(1+\alpha k_{2}|x|)\frac{e^{-k_{2}|x|}}{k_{2}}\cos(k_{2}y),

which will be used in the sequel.

For
-1\leq\alpha\leq 1
we consider the function

(20)

W_{\alpha,t}(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}(1+\alpha n|x|)e^{-n|x|}\cos(ny)e^{-nt}.

Then
W_{\alpha,t}
is positive definite for all
t\geq 0
and
W_{\alpha,0}(x,y)=W_{\alpha}(x,y)
. Since (
20
) is similar to the geometric series it is possible to derive an analytical form

\displaystyle W_{\alpha,t}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha|x|\sinh(|x|+t)}{2(\cosh(|x|+t)-\cos(y))}-\frac{1}{2}\log(2(\cosh(|x|+t)-\cos(y)))

\displaystyle+\frac{t}{2}+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}|x|.

We will show that

(21)

W_{\alpha}(x,y)\geq W_{\alpha,t}(x,y)-\frac{t}{2}.

by considering the function
W_{\alpha,t}(x,y)-\frac{t}{2}
.
We show that this function is monotonically decreasing in
t
for all
(x,y)
. The derivative with respect to
t
equals

\frac{1}{2(\cosh(|x|+t)-\cos(y))^{2}}\\
\times\left(\alpha|x|(1-\cos(y)\cosh(|x|+t))-\sinh(|x|+t)(\cosh(|x|+t)-\cos(y))\right).

We have to show that the numerator is non-positive. We observe that the dependence on
\cos(y)
is linear, so it suffices to show non-positivity for the two extremal values
\cos(y)=\pm 1
. This gives the expressions

\displaystyle(\alpha|x|-\sinh(|x|+t))(1+\cosh(|x|+t))

\displaystyle(\alpha|x|+\sinh(|x|+t))(1-\cosh(|x|+t))

which are both non-positive (for the range of
\alpha
), which proves (
21
).

Then we argue

\sum_{i\neq j}W_{\alpha}(z_{i}-z_{j})\geq\sum_{i,j}W_{\alpha,t}(z_{i}-z_{j})-\frac{t}{2}N(N-1)-NW_{\alpha,t}(\mathbf{0}).

We have

\displaystyle W_{\alpha,t}(\mathbf{0})=-\log\left(2\sinh\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{1}{t}\right).

Inserting
t=\frac{2}{N}
into the above inequality and using the positive definiteness of
W_{\alpha,t}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}W_{\alpha}(z_{i}-z_{j})

\displaystyle\geq-(N-1)-N\log(N)+N\log 2

\displaystyle\geq-N\log N-(1-\log 2)N.

This is (
11
).
∎

Remark 2.5
.

The singularity of the kernel
W_{\alpha}
at
\mathbf{0}
necessitates the exclusion of the diagonal terms from the sum (
11
). This makes arguments using positive definiteness inapplicable directly. A classical method to remedy this problem is the use of a suitable smoothing operation; in our case the ”Poisson-type” smoothing in (
20
) turned out to be most suitable. After smoothing, positive definiteness can be applied. On the other hand the smoothing is responsible for the
\mathcal{O}(N)
error term, which is conjectured to vanish.

3.
Continuous energies

In this section we study continuous energies, namely expressions of the form

E_{\alpha}(\mu)=\iint\limits_{(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T})^{2}}W_{\alpha}(z-z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z^{\prime})

for all Borel probability measures
\mu
. The continuous energy is
\Gamma
-limit of the rescaled discrete energy for
N\to\infty
, this can be proved by following the argument in
[
9
]
(Theorem 3.3) where a related energy is derived from a semi-discrete strain energy model. Since its study turns out to be technically simpler than for the discrete case, it can also be seen as an idealisation. As reference for the interplay between the discrete and the continuous problem we refer to
[
1
]
, see
[
7
]
for a classical reference on potential theory.

In
[
2
]
it is shown that for
|\alpha|\leq 1
and

V_{\alpha}
defined in (
17
)
the minimizing probability measure of the energy

J_{\alpha}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}V_{\alpha}(z-z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z^{\prime})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

is given by

\mu_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\pi}\chi_{\Omega(\sqrt{1-\alpha},\sqrt{1+\alpha})},

where

\Omega(\sqrt{1-\alpha},\sqrt{1+\alpha})=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;:\;\frac{x^{2}}{1-\alpha}+\frac{y^{2}}{1+\alpha}<1\right\}

is the characteristic function of an ellipse.
Theorem 1.1 in
[
2
]
provides the formula

J_{\alpha}(\mu_{\alpha})=C_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\alpha}(z),

where

C_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}-\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{2}\right)+\frac{\alpha\,\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{1+\alpha}}.

An easy calculation yields that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{2}
, this implies that
J_{\alpha}(\mu_{\alpha})=J^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{min}}
, where
J^{\alpha}_{\min}=C_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}
.

As opposed to the study of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as underlying space in
[
2
]
we will investigate the periodized model on the space
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
. This space turns out to share different features due to its nature as a Cartesian product of a non-compact and a compact space.

3.1.
Energy without external fields

For a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
we define the Fourier transform

\widehat{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})=\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}}e^{-i(k_{1}x+k_{2}y)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x,y)

for
(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}
.
The energy
E_{\alpha}(\mu)
can then be expressed as

E_{\alpha}(\mu)=\sum_{k_{2}\neq 0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\alpha)k_{1}^{2}+(1+\alpha)k_{2}^{2}}{(k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2})^{2}}|\widehat{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}k_{1},

which is non-negative and vanishes exactly, if

\widehat{\mu}=0\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\times(\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})

which is equivalent to

\widehat{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})=\widehat{\mu}(k_{1},0)\widehat{\lambda}(k_{2}),

where
\lambda
denotes the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Thus
E_{\alpha}(\mu)
is exactly minimized for all measures of the form

\mu=\nu\otimes\lambda
, where
\nu
is any probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

In order to achieve a unique minimizer we can modify the energy in two ways. The first one is to modify the kernel by adding additional repulsion:

\widetilde{W}_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=W_{\alpha}(x,y)+\beta|x|

for
\beta>0
. The corresponding energy

(22)

E_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu)=\iint\limits_{(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T})^{2}}\widetilde{W}_{\alpha,\beta}(z-z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z^{\prime})

is then minimized for all measures of the form
\mu=\delta_{x_{0}}\otimes\lambda
for fixed
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
(
\delta_{x_{0}}
denotes the point mass concentrated in
x_{0}
). This can be seen from the discussion above and the simple fact that

\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}|x_{1}-x_{2}|\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x_{2})

is minimized exactly for point masses
\delta_{x_{0}}
.

Summing up, we have proved the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
-1\leq\alpha\leq 1
.
For
\beta>0
the continuous energy (
22
) is uniquely minimized by measures
\mu=\delta_{x_{0}}\otimes\lambda
(
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
) amongst all Borel probability measures. For
\beta=0
the energy is minimized for all measures
\mu=\nu\otimes\lambda
, where
\nu
is any Borel probability measure in
\mathbb{R}
. For
\beta<0
the energy takes arbitrarily large negative values and thus does not attain a minimum.

The second possibility is to add an external field, which will be the subject of the next subsection.

3.2.
Energy with external fields

We consider the energy

E_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mu)=\iint\limits_{(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T})^{2}}\left(W_{\alpha}(z-z^{\prime})+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\left(|x|+|x^{\prime}|\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z^{\prime})

with
z=(x,y)
for
-1\leq\alpha<1
. Then we have

E_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mu)=E_{\alpha}(\mu)+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(-|x-x^{\prime}|+|x|+|x^{\prime}|\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime}),

where
\nu(A)=\mu(A\times\mathbb{T})
.

We study the two terms individually. The first term is the energy
E_{\alpha}(\mu)
studied in Section
3.1
.
This is minimized with vanishing energy for all measures
\mu=\nu\otimes\lambda
.
The second term is

\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(-|x-x^{\prime}|+|x|+|x^{\prime}|\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime})\geq 0;

the positivity follows by the triangle inequality. Equality holds for
\nu=\delta_{0}
. In order to
prove that this is the unique minimizer, we assume that there exists
x_{0}\neq 0

such that
\forall\epsilon>0:\nu((x_{0}-\epsilon,x_{0}+\epsilon))>0
. We choose

\epsilon<\frac{|x_{0}|}{2}
. Then we have

\iint_{(x_{0}-\epsilon,x_{0}+\epsilon)^{2}}\left(-|x-x^{\prime}|+|x|+|x^{\prime}|\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime})\\
\geq\iint_{(x_{0}-\epsilon,x_{0}+\epsilon)^{2}}\left(-2\epsilon+2(|x_{0}|-\epsilon)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime})\\
=(2|x_{0}|-4\epsilon)\nu((x_{0}-\epsilon,x_{0}+\epsilon))>0,

which shows that for any measure
\nu
with
0\neq x_{0}\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
we
have strict inequality. Thus
\nu=\delta_{0}
is the unique minimizer for the
second term.

Altogether, this shows that for
-1\leq\alpha<1
,
E_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mu)
is uniquely
minimized by
\mu=\delta_{0}\otimes\lambda
amongst all Borel probability
measures.

More generally, we study

(23)

E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mu)=\iint\limits_{(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T})^{2}}\left(W_{\alpha}(z-z^{\prime})+\beta\left(|x|+|x^{\prime}|\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z^{\prime})

with
\beta>0
and
-1\leq\alpha\leq 1
. First, we notice that for
\beta>\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
,
E_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu)
is uniquely minimized by
\mu=\delta_{0}\otimes\lambda
by the discussion above. For
0<\beta<\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
and
\alpha<1
the energy
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mu)
attains arbitrarily large negative values. This can be seen by computing

\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(-|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\frac{2\beta}{1-\alpha}(|x|+|x^{\prime}|)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime})

for
\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\frac{1+a}{2A^{a+1}}|x|^{a}\mathbbm{1}_{[-A,A]}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, which gives

(1-\alpha)A\frac{a+1}{a+2}\left(-\frac{a+2}{2a+3}+\frac{2\beta}{1-\alpha}\right).

Since
\frac{a+2}{2a+3}
approaches
1
from below as
a\to-1+
, the term in
parenthesis can be made negative by choosing
a
close enough to
-1
. Then the value can be made arbitrarily negative by increasing
A
.

Summing up, we have proved the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
-1\leq\alpha\leq 1
. Then for
\beta>\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
the energy (
23
) is uniquely minimized by
\mu=\delta_{0}\otimes\lambda
. The same holds for
\alpha<1
and
\beta=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
. For
\beta<\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
the energy attains arbitrarily large negative values and thus does not attain a minimum.
