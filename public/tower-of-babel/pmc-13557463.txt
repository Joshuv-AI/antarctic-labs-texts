Smartphone-based Detection of Group A Streptococcal Pharyngitis in Ugandan Children
Authors: Joselyn Rwebembera, Emma Ndagire, Gloria Kaudha, Jafesi Pulle, Juliet Alepere, Jane-Liz Nambogo, Henry Kajumbula, Rachel Sarnacki, Miriam Nakitto, Raymond T. Ng, Udit Gupta, Steve Rebiffe, David Talan, Peter Whitehead, Emmy Okello, Ndate Fall, David Bell, Andrea Beaton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prompt diagnosis of group A streptococcal pharyngitis is essential for primary prevention of acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease, yet affordable point-of-care diagnostics remain limited in low-resource settings. We conducted a prospective study among Ugandan children 5–15 years of age presenting with sore throat, comparing smartphone-acquired oropharyngeal videos analyzed using an artificial intelligence–based image recognition model—trained and validated using combined data from Ugandan and US-based cohorts—with microbiologic culture as the reference standard. Among 82 children evaluated with the optimized model, 12 (15%) had culture-confirmed group A streptococcal pharyngitis. The smartphone-based approach demonstrated 100% sensitivity (95% confidence interval [CI]: 73.5%–100%), 95.7% specificity (95% CI: 88.0%–99.1%) and an overall accuracy of 96.6% (95% CI: 90.0%–99.3%), with an area under the receiver operating curve of 0.93 (95% CI 0.83–1.00). These findings support the potential of smartphone-based artificial intelligence as a rapid, noninvasive diagnostic tool for primary prevention in rheumatic heart disease-endemic settings.

Without treatment, up to 3% of children with group A streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis in high-risk or endemic settings develop acute rheumatic fever,1 an immune reaction that can damage heart valves and cause chronic rheumatic heart disease (RHD). In low-incidence settings, the risk is substantially lower. Primary prevention—prompt GAS diagnosis and antibiotic treatment—can prevent up to 80% of RHD cases.2 However, adoption of primary prevention remains low in most low-income countries,3 where over 90% of the 55 million individuals with RHD live.3 A major barrier to scale-up of primary prevention is the lack of affordable, practical diagnostics.2,3 Improved point-of-care diagnostics could enhance the implementation of primary prevention and reduce the global RHD burden.4 At the same time, indiscriminate antibiotic use for sore throat—most of which is viral—can contribute to antimicrobial resistance and unnecessary adverse effects, further underscoring the need for accurate, point-of-care diagnostics.

Recently, a noninvasive smartphone-based technology was developed (proprietary to Light AI, Vancouver, BC, Canada) using a light-based pattern-recognition algorithm and artificial intelligence to diagnose GAS pharyngitis (Fig. 1).5 In this study, we contributed the first images from African children to refine the algorithm and evaluated its performance against microbiologic culture in an RHD-endemic population.

METHODS

Samples and images were obtained from a prospective study of childhood sore throat in Eastern Uganda between September 2022 and January 2023.6 Children 5–15 years old presenting to public health centers with sore throat or difficulty swallowing, without any exclusions, were invited to participate. Research staff used a smartphone to capture at least one short video showing a clear, unobstructed view of the oropharynx. A pharyngeal swab for microbiologic culture was then obtained using standard best practices. Videos were analyzed using a supervised machine-learning convolutional neural network optimized for smartphone-based image classification implemented in PyTorch (The Linux Foundation, San Francisco, CA, USA). The model used a lightweight architecture (MobileNetV2) designed for efficient performance on mobile devices, enabling accurate image classification with reduced computational requirements. This approach supports real-time analysis and is well suited for point-of-care use in low-resource settings.

To support model development, imaging and culture data from this Ugandan cohort were combined with an independent dataset collected in the United States using similar image acquisition methods and microbiologic reference standards. From the Ugandan dataset, a randomly selected subset of imaging/culture pairs was included in the combined dataset for model training and validation. The pooled dataset was then randomly divided into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets, maintaining representation of both Ugandan and US samples. The remaining Ugandan samples, not included in the training or validation sets, were reserved as an independent test set to evaluate model performance. All test set analyses were conducted with the model blinded to microbiologic culture results. Sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of the convolutional neural network model were calculated according to standard methods.

Written assent and consent were obtained from participants ≥8 years old and all parents, respectively. The study was approved by the Makerere University School of Medicine Research Ethics Committee, the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology, Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board and Advarra Institutional Review Board for US training samples.

RESULTS

Of 425 Ugandan children enrolled in the study, 145 had complete imaging and culture data (Fig. 2). To support model development, 43% of these imaging/culture pairs (n = 63) were randomly selected and combined with 187 imaging/culture pairs from an independent US-based dataset, yielding a total of 250 pairs (158 GAS-negative and 92 GAS-positive). Of these, 80% (n = 200) were used for model training and 20% (n = 50) for validation. The remaining 57% of Ugandan samples (n = 82), independent of the training and validation datasets and blinded to culture results, were used as a test set to evaluate the performance of the optimized model.

Among the 82 Ugandan participants in the independent test set, 12 (15%) had culture-confirmed GAS pharyngitis. The smartphone-based model demonstrated an overall accuracy of 96.6% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 90.0%–99.3%), with sensitivity of 100% (95% CI: 73.5%–100%) and specificity of 95.7% (95% CI: 88.0%–99.1%). The positive predictive value was 85.4% (95% CI: 65.9%–94.6%) and the negative predictive value was 100%. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.83–1.00).

DISCUSSION

Improving diagnostics for RHD primary prevention has been identified as a high priority by the National Institutes of Health4 and the World Health Organization.7 In this study, a smartphone-based diagnostic performed well in distinguishing African children with GAS pharyngitis from those with other causes of sore throat. This distinction is clinically important. Most childhood sore throats are viral and do not require antibiotics. However, for those with GAS pharyngitis, prompt antibiotic treatment is critical to reduce the risk of rheumatic fever and severe cardiac outcomes.2 Many clinical decision rules for GAS pharyngitis have been developed, but these show only modest discrimination between viral and GAS sore throat.8 Rapid antigen detection tests are widely used but demonstrate variable sensitivity, typically ranging from approximately 70%–90%, with high specificity, and their performance may depend on pretest probability and operator technique.9 In contrast, point-of-care molecular assays, including polymerase chain reaction–based tests, offer higher sensitivity and specificity, often exceeding 95%,10 but remain cost-prohibitive and less accessible in many low-resource settings. In this context, the smartphone-based approach demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in our study, with the potential advantage of being noninvasive, rapid and scalable in resource-limited environments. Previous deep learning studies have demonstrated the feasibility of smartphone-based pharyngitis detection,11–13 but our work uniquely validates this approach prospectively against microbiologic culture in an RHD-endemic African population.

This AI-based image recognition technology, which is noninvasive, has the potential to provide near-instant results.5 The relatively small number of culture-confirmed GAS cases is a limitation and warrants validation in larger prospective cohorts. In addition, inclusion of data from a US-based cohort in model development may affect generalizability to similar high-burden, low-resource settings and further validation using exclusively locally derived data is warranted. While further testing, including implementation metrics, costing at scale, direct comparison of this approach with existing diagnostic strategies such as Rapid Antigen Detection Tests, and understanding how this technology can be integrated into clinical decision-making pathways is needed, smartphone-based GAS diagnosis has the potential to transform primary prevention in the lowest-resource settings and reduce the global burden of RHD.
