Performance of sheep and goats in a delay of gratification task
Authors: Laurie Castro, Raymond Nowak, Valérie Dufour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-control, the ability to resist an immediate reward for a more beneficial delayed outcome, is an adaptive skill underlying decision-making. While widely studied in non-human primates and birds, its evolution remains debated. Factors such as brain size, metabolic rate, longevity, social complexity, or foraging ecology may shape self-control across species. To investigate further the link between these factors and self-control, we compared two domesticated ungulates, sheep (Ovis aries, n = 7) and goats (Capra hircus, n = 7), in a delay of gratification task. These species mostly differ on their level of social cohesion and foraging ecology but share a relatively similar evolutionary and domestication history. In our study, sheep waited for 54.3 ± 15.1 s while goats waited for 62.9 ± 13.8 s (group level mean ± SD). Some individuals from both species delayed gratification for up to 80 s. The moderate higher success rate of goats may relate to their feeding ecology as dispersed foragers or to their social flexibility compared to sheep. The two species adapted their decision according to different parameters, such as the duration of the upcoming delay or the relative values of the rewards and engaged in distraction behaviours to increase their success rate in waiting, indicating that they share common cognitive strategies. These findings suggest that factors such as social complexity or foraging ecology may shape self-control in these domesticated species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10071-026-02071-3.Keywords: Self-control, Anticipation, Delayed gratification, Quantity discrimination, Ungulates

Introduction

The ability to inhibit impulsive behaviours is crucial for individuals to refrain from prepotent responses to achieve future goals and optimise decisions. In humans, self-control abilities, i.e. tolerate a delay or invest an effort to achieve a more valuable outcome compared to a present one, are often associated with general intelligence. Studies have demonstrated a link between self-control abilities in children and academic success in later life (Converse et al. 2014; Mischel 2014; Mischel et al. 1989; Tangney et al. 2018). Delay of gratification tasks are commonly used to assess and compare self-control abilities, such as the Marshmallow test, developed for children by (Mischel and Ebbesen 1970). These tasks require individuals to choose between a smaller (or lesser), immediate reward and a larger (or better), delayed reward. The subject must decide whether to wait (delay-choice task) and, in some cases, maintain their choice to wait (delay-maintenance task), for a more valuable reward. Note that other paradigms, such as accumulation tasks (i.e. food rewards accumulate within reach of the subject until it decides to reach for (and eat) them) or exchange tasks (i.e. the subject has to keep a small reward intact for a given duration after which it can be exchanged for a larger one) have also been used to assess an individual’s ability to delay gratification (Pelé et al. 2011). Many studies have been conducted in non-human animals, providing a valuable framework for investigating the cognitive processes underlying self-control and their evolution.

Self-control may be more cognitively demanding than motor self-regulation, which involves regulating impulsive motor actions. Indeed, self-control contains a decision component and includes strategic efforts, such as redirecting attention (Beran 2015; Miller et al. 2019). It involves the control of emotions, behaviour, and cognition, for example by using working memory (Beran 2015; Beran et al. 2016a, b; Miller et al. 2019; Nigg 2017). Studies reveal that most species often fail for relatively low delays in these waiting tasks. For example, tamarins or marmosets fail to wait for more than 15 s in standard delay of gratification tasks (Stevens et al. 2005). Other species show intermediary performances (capuchins: Beran et al. 2016a, b; Bramlett et al. 2012; Perdue et al. 2015; Ramseyer et al. 2006; Tonkean macaques (Macaca tonkeana) and brown capuchin monkeys (Cebus apella): Pelé et al. 2011; parrots: Auersperg et al. 2013; Brucks et al. 2022a, b; rats (Rattus norvegicus): Richards et al. 1997; cuttlefish (Sepia officinalis): Schnell et al. 2021), compared to chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes) for example (Beran et al. 1999; Beran and Evans 2006; Dufour et al. 2007) which can wait for longer than a minute. Note that dogs (Canis familiaris) and corvids were able to wait several minutes in exchange paradigms (Hillemann et al. 2014; Leonardi et al. 2012). Studies reveal great inter-specific variations but also inter-individual variations (long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis): Pelé et al. 2009; rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta): Evans and Beran 2007b; wrasse: Aellen et al. 2021; wolves (Canis lupus): Range et al. 2020). Individual characteristics such as sex, age and/or social status (i.e., position in the dominance hierarchy and in the social network) are among the factors likely to affect individual self-control abilities (Addessi et al. 2011; Anderson et al. 2000; Drobetz et al. 2012; Hillemann et al. 2014). Various coping behaviours, such as looking away from the rewards or moving around a lot, help subjects to wait for longer delays by distracting their attention (such as in chimpanzees (Dufour et al. 2007; Evans and Beran 2007a), parrots (Brucks et al. 2022a, b), dogs (Brucks et al. 2017; Leonardi et al. 2012; Range et al. 2020) or horses (Brucks et al. 2022a, b)). Still, a common finding is that most species take into account the relative value of the delayed reward compared to the immediate one (Aellen et al. 2021; Dufour et al. 2007; Leonardi et al. 2012; Pelé et al. 2009, 2011; Ramseyer et al. 2006). In delay maintenance tasks, a majority of subjects were also able to decide early on whether to wait or not (monkeys: Pelé et al. 2009, 2011; corvids: Dufour et al. 2012; children: Steelandt et al. 2012; fish: Aellen et al. 2021; horses: Brucks et al., 2022a; parrots: Brucks et al. 2022b) showing that they can assess the costs and benefits associated with the amount of food while taking the delay into account.

Various hypotheses have been proposed to explain the evolution of self-control abilities. Some of these hypotheses rely on physiological traits, such as body size, metabolic rate, or longevity (MacLean et al. 2014; Stevens 2014). Smaller animals, for instance, may have faster metabolic rates and therefore be able to wait for shorter delays than bigger animals. Likewise, animals with shorter life-expectancies may not live long enough to receive a more valuable but delayed reward. Therefore, natural selection would have favoured individuals making the decision to take the immediate reward (Stevens 2014). On the other hand, self-control abilities may relate to general cognitive abilities (Beran 2015). Brain size/volume, which is a proxy of general intelligence, could then be a good predictor of waiting times (Stevens 2014). In line with the ecological and social intelligence hypotheses, self-control may also have evolved due to variations in feeding ecology and social complexity. Animals may have developed advanced levels of self-control in situations where they rely on unpredictable and challenging resources which would explain the differences in self-control between cottontop tamarins (Saguinus oedipus) and common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus; Stevens et al. 2005). Indeed, when tested on the same delay of gratification task, marmosets, which rely more on gum (a food product acquired by waiting for exudate to flow from trees) outperform tamarins which feed on insects (a food requiring impulsive action). In addition, species that live in large groups with complex relationships should employ inhibitory strategies more often to opt to engage or not in social events, or enable coordination behaviours (Miller et al. 2019). Levels of fission-fusion in primate species have been shown to be positively associated with enhanced inhibitory skills (Amici et al. 2008a, b). Accordingly, individuals living in more cohesive groups may demonstrate less behavioural inhibition, which can compromise their performance in self-control tasks (Pelé et al. 2011).

Social ungulates also live in groups of varying degrees of cohesion, providing new opportunities to investigate the link between self-control abilities, and factors such as brain size, sociality or foraging ecology. Due to the many challenges arising from testing wild species, a first step is to turn to domestic ungulates. Note that in domestic species, the process of domestication may have contrasting effects on self-control, both reducing it through relaxed environmental demands, or enhancing it via selection for tamer individuals for example (Brucks et al., 2022a; Ferreira et al. 2023; Hare and Tomasello 2005). However, the evolution of self-control abilities in domestic species may be driven by the same factors listed above for wild species. Sheep (Ovis aries) and goats (Capra hircus) have relatively the same history of domestication by humans (~ 10,000 years; Alberto et al. 2018), are relatively similar in size and physiology, and are herbivores, which may require little self-control during foraging (Hayden 2018). Compared to horses (Brucks et al., 2022a), they are expected to wait for smaller durations due to their smaller body-sizes and shorter life-expectancies in a delay of gratification task. They also live in large gregarious groups living in fission-fusion societies and have many interactions with conspecifics that includes dominance hierarchies (Stanley and Dunbar 2013). Interestingly, sheep and goats show differences in independence from conspecifics presumably related to their dietary preferences (Dumont and Boissy 2000; Dunbar et al. 1990; Hosoi et al. 1995; Sibbald and Hooper 2004). Indeed, sheep (grazers) value proximity to their conspecifics more than patches of high-quality food (Michelena et al. 2009). On the contrary, goats (browsers) value high-quality resources and can spread over different patches leading to less cohesion when foraging. We know that sheep and goats already succeeded in different tasks evaluating their behavioural inhibition: they inhibit prepotent responses in the cylinder task, demonstrating equivalent motor self-regulation to other mammals or bird species tested in the same task (goats: Langbein 2018; sheep: Knolle et al. 2019). Goats were also successful in the A-not-B task (which assesses self-control abilities; Dunbar and Shultz 2025), where only a few individuals continued to search for a reward at a first learned location after it was moved to a second one (Nawroth et al. 2015). When tested in a spatial detour task, an alternative test of inhibitory control, both species were able to choose the correct side following a change of gap position, although goats recovered from perseveration errors faster than sheep (Raoult et al. 2021). Sheep and goats are therefore good models to study under what conditions sociality (different levels of cohesion) and feeding ecology (different levels of food selectivity) are related to self-control abilities (Amici et al. 2008b; Miller et al. 2019; Stevens 2014; Stevens et al. 2005).

To investigate this question, we compared sheep and goats in the same delay of gratification task. In this task, subjects can choose between an immediate low-quantity reward and a delayed high-quantity reward. To obtain the larger reward they must continuously refrain from taking the small reward throughout the trial. If they change their choice during the trial, they receive the smaller reward and the trial ends. We assessed the ability to delay gratification by quantifying the length of time the subject refrained from an impulsive response in a given trial (Pelé et al. 2009). In addition, we compared the maximum delay reached for each species, and investigated the weight of individual factors (sex, age, and social status). As pointed out by Bramlett et al. ( 2012), it is very important to use a paradigm that favours a full understanding of the nature of the task by subjects. In their study, they used a rotating tray task to provide both a temporal and spatial marker of the delay. In our task, the delayed reward, once chosen, was gradually brought closer to the subject, so that its movement could be tracked, and the subject could anticipate the amount of time left before delivery. In addition, to ensure that the choice of the large (delayed) reward did not simply reflect the subject’s initial preference for the larger reward (regardless of the delay), the subjects were free to reverse their choice at any time during the process. Moreover, we investigated whether sheep and goats adjusted their decision according to the value of the delayed reward relative to the immediately available reward. To do so, we first tested the capacity of these species to discriminate between different food quantities (1 vs. 4, 1 vs. 8, and 4 vs. 8), and then we assessed whether they could wait longer for larger compared to smaller food rewards. We also checked whether sheep and goats exhibited distraction behaviours that could potentially facilitate their waiting success. Finally, following Dufour et al. (2007), we analysed the latency to stop waiting during a trial (hereafter called “giving-up times”) – the point at which the subject renounces waiting. This measure can provide an index of anticipation, as subjects are expected to abandon trials earlier when they anticipate longer delays. Accordingly, we tested whether subjects renounced earlier for longer delays and less desirable rewards.

As domestic herbivores that rely on evenly distributed food, both sheep and goats may show very little self-control. They may mostly refuse to wait or fail to take the ratio of reward into account when waiting. If body size and life-expectancy have a major impact on self-control abilities, we also expect sheep and goats to perform relatively poorly compared to other domestic ungulates tested on the same type of task (to our knowledge, solely horses, although with a different design; Brucks et al., 2022a). However, they may show between-species differences in their performances, which would suggest an impact of feeding ecology and/or sociality on their self-control abilities. In that case, we expect goats to perform better than sheep, due to their higher selectivity for food that can sometimes be dispatched away from other conspecifics.

Material & methods

Subjects

We tested 19 subjects (11 goats: 7 females and 4 males, and 8 sheep: 3 females and 5 males; all the male subjects were neutered; see Table S2 in Supplementary Material for subjects’ details) from 20 February to 30 June 2023. They were housed in the Friedel Park in Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France. Sheep and goats lived together with ponies, alpacas, pigs, chickens, cows, and donkeys in a two-hectare area that was open to the public during weekends and school holidays. Water was available ad libitum. Both sheep and goats shared the same daily routine: they had access to the same barn for sleeping and fed altogether in the morning for approximately three hours in the same enclosed barn with hay, straw, and wheat flour. They had access to food enrichment including fresh fruit, vegetables, and twigs at various times of the day. The animals participated in the experiment based solely on their motivation and were rewarded with treats. Some individuals had previously participated in cognitive tests examining inferential reasoning abilities using exclusion tasks (Duffrene et al. 2022). Before starting the experiment, we familiarised the sheep and goats with the equipment and procedure (see below). Each animal was invited to enter the test area alone with the experimenter. It could then move around and explore the setup for a few minutes before being released. If, after three days of this habituation, animals showed any signs of distress, such as bleating, urinating, defecating or restlessness, they were then excluded from further training (one sheep). Before conducting the delay of gratification task, it was necessary to investigate if all subjects could discriminate between different quantities of food. Only those individuals who were successful in this discrimination task were then tested in the delay of gratification experiment (see Table S11 for further details on the different phases and the associated criteria).

Social structure analysis

We conducted analyses on the social structure of the herd to characterise the groups of sheep and goats in terms of social cohesion and dominance hierarchy, and to determine the social status of every subject (see details and results in Supplementary Material). First, we established the dominance hierarchy of the group in order to have information the ranking of each individual. We used a method of sampling all occurrences of social agonistic behaviours (Altmann 1974) to record interactions between individuals. Observations occurred on five mornings between 15 November 2022 and 25 November 2022 while animals were locked in their main resting barn for feeding, as part of their daily routine (see Table S9 for the ethogram of recorded agonistic interactions). Second, we built the social network of the group by relying on the social proximity between individuals (Whitehead 2008). Spatial distances between individuals were collected thanks to 82 scans, from 20 April 2023 to 20 June 2023. We then used the softwares SOCPROG (Whitehead 2009) to build an association matrix and Gephi (Bastian et al. 2009) to plot the social network of the group and to compute individual metrics (e.g. eigenvector centrality).

Quantity discrimination task

Equipment and procedure

The aim of the experiment was to evaluate whether sheep and goats spontaneously choose the larger quantity of food items among two different quantities. The experimenter (LC) sat behind a table covered from both sides by grids to prevent access by the subject (Fig. 1). Food rewards (small pieces of carrots) were placed on two grey, round plates, spaced approximately 40 cm apart, providing clear visibility for the animals. The subjects could insert their snouts through the grid bars when facing a choice.

From left to right: Baiting out of sight of the subject; Goat selecting a reward by indicating one side; Carrot pieces used as rewards

Training methods

Two preliminary training periods were conducted prior to the task, even for animals who had previously experienced exclusion tasks during the previous study, which was two years ago (Duffrene et al. 2022). The first training period had two objectives: firstly, to train individuals to position their snout at a food reward placed on the table and secondly, to teach that food rewards could be either on the left or on the right side of the table. During a session, the reward was a slice of carrot (approximately 3 mm thick). The experimenter initially placed it four times in the centre of the table, to encourage the subject to position themselves correctly in front of the grid and insert their snout to indicate the carrot. Once in position, the subject received the reward. For the 20 remaining trials, the subject first faced the middle of the table, and a carrot slice was then placed either to the right or to the left of the table, 10 times each in a semi-randomised order. Once the subject moved to the correct side and maintained its position, it received the reward. A subject could not consume more than 24 carrot slices during a session. Each individual underwent as many sessions as necessary to learn to point unambiguously to the reward, i.e., to insert their snout through the grid and maintain the position for a few seconds without exhibiting “unwanted behaviours” such as attempting to go under or to the sides of the table, switching positions multiple times, or kicking or head-butting the table. Once these criteria met, the individual was considered ready to move up to the second training period. Note that some individuals added signals to their “pointing” such as licking towards the food (that they could not reach), scratching the floor with their leg, gently bobbing their head towards the rewards.

The second training period focused on familiarizing the animals with the task material and training them to distinguish between two distinct locations on the table, where only one was baited. The experimenter placed a single grey round plate on either the left or right side of the table and the opposite side had an empty plate. The two plates were subsequently covered with opaque over-cups. The subjects were encouraged to position themselves in the centre of the table by a few quick taps with the finger (most already did so spontaneously from the first training period). Then, the experimenter simultaneously removed over-cups, revealing the two options from which the subjects had to choose. Each option was pseudo-randomly baited, ensuring that the food reward was placed 10 times on each side during one session. The subject received the reward as soon as they correctly pointed to the baited side. If the subject pointed to the non-baited side, the experimenter shifted the plate ahead of them to demonstrate its emptiness. The experimenter then redirected his/her attention to the baited side by tapping the table showing the rewards but without giving them to the subject. After pointing at the reward with at least 80% accuracy for two consecutive sessions (16 out of 20 correct trials), the subject had completed the training phase.

Seven individuals (four goats and three sheep) who had already taken part in a previous study (Duffrene et al. 2022) needed only three training sessions: one during phase 1 and two during phase 2; The other naïve individuals required between 4 and 13 training sessions.

Testing methods

The testing general procedure was similar to the procedure used in the second training period, except that a piece of cardboard prevented the subject to look at the baiting. In step 1, we investigated whether goats and sheep could distinguish between one and four carrot pieces. The side position (left or right) of the largest amount was counterbalanced in a session and was never presented more than three times in a row on the same side. There were eight sessions, each consisting of 12 choices. The experimenter made no attempt to arrange the food items into a consistent pattern. For the testing, all carrot pieces were uniform, as sliced into small pieces measuring 1 × 1 × 2 cm each (Fig. 1). If at the end of step 1, some participants exhibited a side bias (n = 8; Table S2 in Supplementary Material), they entered a correction procedure (see below) before resuming testing. In step 2, we investigated if sheep and goats could discriminate between a larger and a smaller amount of food when presented with the following three ratio: 1 carrot piece versus 4; 1 versus 8 and 4 versus 8. The study comprised eight sessions, with each individual having to make 12 decisions per session (4 trials for each ratio). For each ratio, the larger quantity was presented as many times on the right as on the left, but never more than three times in a row on the same side.

Side bias correction procedure

In step one or two, if a subject selected the reward (small or large) more than 10 times (out of 12) on the same side, in three consecutive sessions, we considered that they exhibited a side bias, leading us to move them to corrective sessions. Side bias correction involved two phases: first, we conducted two sessions of six choices in the same manner as the second training period, i.e., the subject had to choose between four carrot pieces and nothing, but the reward was positioned on the opposite side of their bias. Subsequently, the individual had to relearn to indicate the non-preferred side. Second, we carried out four sessions consisting of 12 choices between the low-quantity and the high-quantity reward, with the high-quantity reward consistently placed on the opposite side of the bias. The subject could return to testing once they had either achieved two consecutive successful sessions, i.e., more than 80% correct trials, or had successfully retrieved the reward every time during one session (100% success). Most individuals exhibited a side bias during the first session of step 1, except for two (Igor and Tyrex), who exhibited bias during the second and third session of step 1, respectively. Only two individuals (Hector and Satine) failed to correct their side bias during the corrective sessions and were excluded from the study. Tagada was the only subject to exhibit a side bias in step 2 of testing, and she did not correct it.

A single experimenter conducted all sessions of this study – both training and testing – and recorded them with a JVC digital video camera. Animals were tested individually.

Subjects

These subjects were selected on their success in the discrimination task for different quantities (step 2, Table S4 in Supplementary Material).

Methods

We conducted the experiment under the same conditions and with the same material as for the step 2 of the quantity discrimination task. A session comprised twelve trials, and each trial consisted in presenting the subject with a high-quantity and a low-quantity rewards, whereby the highest quantity of reward could be obtained after a specific delay duration. If the subject selected the low-quantity reward, it received it directly from the experimenter through the bars of the grid. Otherwise, if the subject selected the high-quantity reward, the experimenter gradually brought it closer within a given delay. The low-quantity reward was always visible for the subject. At any point, the subject could modify its decision by pointing at the low-quantity reward, indicating a willingness to give up waiting. In this event, the experimenter promptly removed the high-quantity reward from the table and gave the low-quantity reward. The experimenter recorded the quantity of food eaten by each subject (i.e., low or high-quantity), along with the giving-up time, and waited for 20 s after consumption of the reward before proceeding to the next trial.

As in Pelé et al. (2011), each subject was tested in a succession of stages, each stage corresponding to a delay, and involved four sessions of twelve trials each with this delay. The delay increased at each stage, starting from 2 s, increasing to 5 s, 10 s, 20 s, and increasing in 20 s increments thereafter (40 s, 60 s, etc.). The ratios of food quantity used were the same as those used in the quantity discrimination task (1 vs. 4; 1 vs. 8; 4 vs. 8). Each ratio and side of the largest amount within a ratio were presented equally often within a session. If the subject failed to choose and/or wait for the delayed reward in every trial over four sessions (failing at 48 out of 48 trials at a given delay stage), it was not tested further in the next stage.

Behavioural coding

To ensure the quality of annotations while keeping the analysis feasible, a representative subset (30%) of the videos from the delay of gratification task were analysed using Boris (v.7.13.9; Friard and Gamba 2016), which represented 1,500 scored trials, with a total of 22,000 behaviours annotated. For the 5 s and 10 s delays, we analysed the fourth session for all individuals. For the 20 s, 40 s, and 60 s delays, two sessions were randomly selected for each individual. The analysis focused on the waiting period, defined as the time interval between the subject making its choice and receiving the reward. Behaviours were categorised into two distinct groups: (1) frequency of behaviours directed towards the apparatus (such as head-butting or pushing the table) that could be related to frustration (Carbonaro et al. 1992; Chapagain et al. 2014) (2) frequency of behaviours more likely to serve as coping behaviours, not directed towards the apparatus (such as self-scratching or sniffing the floor; see Table 2 for detailed descriptions). A single experimenter (LC) conducted all phases of this study (training and testing), and the different behaviours exhibited during the waiting period could be classified without ambiguity. Interobserver reliability tests were, therefore, unnecessary.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed in RStudio (v.2023.06). Given the heterogeneity of the study population and the small sample size, our aim was to use the most robust analytical strategy, allowing for repeated measures (e.g. mixed models where applicable), while acknowledging that no formal sample size calculation had been conducted.

Quantity discrimination task

We ran two Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM1 for step 1 and GLMM2 for step 2) in order to analyse the most relevant variables that could explain success at selecting the high-quantity reward), such as the Species (goat or sheep), the Number of sessions and the Ratio (for step 2). We also performed binomial tests to assess if they chose the high-quantity reward among both amounts of food, on a global and individual basis. Full details are reported in the supplementary material.

Maximum delays

First, we wanted to assess whether the maximum delay reached by individual (i.e., the longest delay at which the individual succeeded at least once) depended on species (sheep or goat) or individual characteristics (age, sex, eigenvector centrality and dominance ranking). Details about the social structure of the group can be found in the Supplementary Material. We pooled the maximum delay sustained by each individual (n = 14) and performed non-parametric tests.

Percentage of success

Subsequently, we aimed to compare the task performances of both species. We defined success as “waiting the entire delay to obtain the maximal amount of food”. Failures were trials wherein subjects decided to select the lowest quantity or gave up before the end of the delay. To analyse performance differences, we coded success as a 0/1 variable (n = 4656) and developed a model to identify which of the following variables could account for the differences: Species (goat or sheep), Delay stage as a factor (in seconds), Ratio of reward quantity (1:4, 1:8, 4:8), and Side of the high-quantity reward (left or right). The full model also included the potential effects of the interaction between Delay and Species (to detect the potential differences of success between species at specific delays), the interaction between Species and Ratio (to test the possibility of species choosing different food amounts depending on ratios) and the interaction between Ratio and Delay (to evaluate whether the success of waiting at specific delays could differ depending on the relative value of offered food quantities). The model also included individuals as a random factor, with session number (ranging from 1 to 4) nested within individuals.

We fitted a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a binomial family and a logit link function with the Laplace approximation, using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). We first ran a GLMM model with a model selection procedure based on Akaike’s second-order information criterion to identify variables that contribute to the best model, using the MuMIn package (Bartoń 2023). We found that the best model was the full model based on lowest AICc. Model assumptions were evaluated by first computing the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values of the model using the car package. All VIF values were below 5, indicating the absence of excessive multicollinearity in the model (Daoud 2017; Fox & Weisberg, 2019). The DHARMa package (Hartig 2022) was used to analyse the simulated residuals. The residuals demonstrated uniform distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test: D = 0.008, p = 0.9), with no under- or over-dispersion (Dispersion test: dispersion = 1.01, p = 0.8) nor outlier (Outlier test: p = 0.9).

Finally, we used a decision tree analysis for each species to analyse the hierarchical decision pathway. This approach is particularly suited to identifying potential hierarchical relationships and interactions among predictors, which may be difficult to detect using more traditional statistical models in small datasets. Following Romain et al. (2021), we used the glmertree package (Fokkema et al. 2018). This algorithm identifies subgroups, distinguished by different success rates. Observations in the dataset are partitioned based on defined splitting variables, as in typical tree algorithms. In particular, GLMM trees consider the clustered structure of the dataset by incorporating a cluster random effect, which accounts for pseudo-replication of the same individual. To pinpoint subgroups that differ in success rates, we specified a node-specific model with a single intercept (mean success rate per subgroup). At each node, the algorithm assumes at least one splitting variable, that, at an optimal splitting point, divides observations into subsets for more stable intercept coefficients (success rate values). For each species, we specified the splitting variables “Delay”, “Ratio” and “Side bias” (i.e., yes if the chosen side was the same for at least 9 out of 12 choices during one session; no otherwise) as potential partitioning variables. We set the identity of individuals as a random intercept to account for individual differences, and specified family as binomial because the response was a binary variable. We obtained the intercept coefficients (µ) and the success probabilities as percentages and reported them in the decision trees (Figs. 4 and 5).

Behaviours

To analyse how the different behaviours during the waiting period affected the success, we fitted a second logistic GLMM. Among the coded sessions, we kept the trials in which the individual chose the highest quantity (n = 895). Success (the response variable) was defined as “waiting the entire delay”, while failures were trials in which the subject gave up before the end of the delay. We included the Delay, the Species, and the frequency of Apparatus-directed behaviours and Other behaviours as fixed effects. We included any interactions between these variables, as well as individual as a random factor, with session number nested within individual. As before, we selected the best model based on Akaike’s second-order information criterion and found that the best model included the four main variables and the interaction between Apparatus-directed behaviours and Species. Model assumptions were assessed by first calculating the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values of the model using the car package (Fox & Weisberg, 2019). All VIF values were less than 3, indicating the absence of excessive multicollinearity in the model (Daoud 2017). The DHARMa package (Hartig 2022) was used to analyse the simulated residuals. The residuals were uniformly distributed (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test: D = 0.04, p = 0.2), with no under- or over-dispersion (Dispersion test: dispersion = 1.0, p = 0.9) or outliers (Outlier test: p = 0.09).

Giving-up times

Following Dufour et al. (2007), we aimed to analyse the giving-up times – the point at which the subject decides to quit waiting during a trial. Our objective was to determine whether sheep and goats could anticipate the delay at the beginning of the trial, and whether they could give up early if they estimated that the delay was going to be too long. We compared the distribution of observed giving-up times with the distribution of times expected under the null hypothesis of a constant chance of giving-up. Kaplan-Meier time analysis was used to calculate the probability for each individual to wait longer than the already elapsed time for each trial. We included both failures times (when the subject chose the low-quantity reward) and successful trials – with successful time representing the delay corresponding to the current session (as censored data). This observed survival distribution was compared to an expected exponential distribution chance of giving-up using an adjusted Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, as described in Haccou and Meelis (1992). A significant difference between the two distributions indicated that the subject gave up earlier than predicted by the model. Initially, this analysis was executed individually for every delay duration from 10 s-delay by merging all ratios of rewards (n = 48 events). In the subsequent phase, distinct ratios were evaluated for each individual regarding each delay duration, again from 10 s-delay. The analysis then proceeded with a smaller number of events (n = 16 events).

Results

Quantity discrimination task

In step 1, a binomial test indicated that the success was significant across all eight sessions (p < 0.001) when discriminating between one and four carrot pieces. However, GLMM1 revealed that subjects consistently showed improvement across sessions (Table S1 and Fig. S1 in Supplementary Material). Therefore, we performed binomial tests on an individual basis for the final session of step 1, which was indicative of their task performance. Twelve subjects (seven goats and five sheep) reliably chose the four carrot pieces over one during their last session (Table S2 in Supplementary Material for detailed results).

When introducing various reward ratios in step 2 (1:4, 1:8 and 4:8), the overall success remained significant (Binomial test, p < 0.001), and this was also the case for each ratio (1:4: p < 0.001; 1:8: p < 0.001; 4:8: p < 0.001; Supplementary Material). The performances of both sheep and goats were similar, with no impact of the species. In contrast to the previous findings, there was no effect of the session, revealing consistent performances across all eight sessions. The success rate was slightly lower for the 4:8 ratio than for the other two for both species (GLMM2, Table S3 and Fig. S2 in Supplementary Material). To account for individual disparities and select the most successful subjects for the delay of gratification task, we conducted binomial tests on an individual basis. Only individuals who consistently opted for the high-quantity reward in all trials (n = 15/17 individuals) were eligible to participate in the delay task (Table S4 in Supplementary Material).

Maximum delays

Goats were able to wait 62.9 ± 13.8 s (mean ± SD; median = 60 s) while sheep waited 54.3 ± 15.1 s (mean ± SD; median = 60 s), although the difference was not significant (Mann-Whitney test: n1 = 7, n2 = 7, U = 32.5, p = 0.30). At the individual level, two goats (Ella and Pépette) and one sheep (Popeye) still waited at least once at the 80 s-delay. Most of the remaining goats completely failed waiting at 80 s (four out of five), and one individual (Tyrex) failed waiting at 60 s. Three sheep failed waiting at 80 s while the other three failed waiting at 60 s. There was no correlation between maximum delay and sex (Mann-Whitney test: n1 = 8, n2 = 6, U = 27, p = 0.7), nor age (Spearman’s correlation test: r = 0.26, p = 0.4). Eigenvector centrality and dominance rank were highly correlated with species (centrality: Mann-Whitney test: n1 = 7, n2 = 7, U = 1, p = 0.001; rank: Mann-Whitney test: n1 = 7, n2 = 7, U = 7, p = 0.03), with goats being more dominant and less central than sheep, so it was not possible to test for these effects.

Percentage of success

We found that the percentage of success was 69.3% ± 46.1 including all trials. It was significantly affected by the Delay (χ2 = 317.2, df = 7, p < 0.001), the Ratio (χ2 = 91, df = 2, p < 0.001) and the Side (χ2 = 11.6, df = 1, p < 0.001). The interactions of the variables Delay*Species (χ2 = 43.5, df = 7, p < 0.001), Ratio*Species (χ2 = 14.6, df = 2, p < 0.001) and Delay*Ratio (χ2 = 96.6, df = 14, p < 0.001) also influenced the number of successful trials. The variable Species (χ2 = 1.8, df = 1, p = 0.2) was not significant.

Post-hoc analysis of contrasts revealed that the percentage of success decreased significantly for both species after the 20 s-delay compared to the reference level of 5 s (contrast 5–20 s for goats: estimate = 1.7, SE = 0.3, z.ratio = 5.7, p < 0.001; for sheep: estimate = 1.0, SE = 0.3, z.ratio = 4.0, p = 0.002). In addition, the success rate of sheep was significantly lower than that of goats for delays of 40, 60, and 80 s (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the performance of both species varied according to the ratio and delay. Subjects consistently chose the high-quantity reward more often when it was eight times larger than the small reward, compared to the 4:8 ratio. They also chose to wait more often for an 8-fold reward than for a 4-fold reward at 20 s and 40 s delays (Fig. 3). However, as the delay increased, they stopped waiting for the high-quantity reward, irrespective of the ratio. For example, at the 80 s-delay, the small success rate did not vary whether the high-quantity reward was two, four or eight times greater than the low-quantity reward (Fig. 3). This trend was consistent across both species (the Species*Delay*Ratio interaction was not significant and had therefore been excluded from the model). Moreover, the significant effect of the Side revealed that a greater amount of reward located on the left side was associated with more successful outcomes. It demonstrated a bias towards one side that emerged among the subjects during the experiment: as the delay increased, most individuals began to favour one side over the other (Fig. S4 in Supplementary Material).

The key determinant in the decision tree analysis for both sheep and goats was the delay, as shown in Figs. 4 and 5. The hierarchical decision pathway differed slightly between the two species. Goats primarily distinguished between delays below and above 40 s whereas sheep differentiated between delays below and above 20 s, thus confirming the analysis of the GLMM. For longer delays (60 and 80 s for goats; 40, 60 and 80 s for sheep), only the delay significantly influenced the percentage of success of sheep (node 13, 14 and 15). In cases of shorter delays (≤ 40 s for goats, ≤ 20 s for sheep), the ratio served as a crucial splitting variable. Both species exhibited more difficulties succeeding for the 4:8 ratio compared to the other ones. Lastly, side biases also limited the success to wait. The decision tree of goats is more complex, with the interaction of the three variables (delay, ratio, and side bias) leading to more ramifications of the tree than in sheep. For example, for delays of 20 and 40 s, goats that did not exhibit a side bias were more successful for 1:8 ratio than for 1:4 ratio. The difference between these two ratios was not observed in sheep.

Behaviours

Behaviours exhibited during the waiting period were analysed for 30% of the trials (Table 3). For this subset of data, the GLMM confirmed a significant effect of the delay, with longer delays resulting in more failed trials (χ2 = 96.5, df = 4, p < 0.001), and goats appeared moderately more successful than sheep, although the effect was not significant (χ2 = 3.7, df = 1, p = 0.06). Subjects that exhibited more apparatus-directed behaviours were more successful (χ2 = 4.1, df = 1, p = 0.04). Conversely, individuals that did not direct their attention towards the apparatus showed a slight tendency to give up waiting more frequently (χ2 = 2.6, df = 1, p = 0.1). Finally, the interaction Species*Apparatus-directed behaviours was not significant (χ2 = 0.1, df = 1, p = 0.7).

Giving-up times

We found that all subjects gave up waiting earlier than expected under a null hypothesis of a steady chance of quitting. The ratio between low- and high-quantity rewards influenced the duration of waiting in individuals, as well as the delay. In goats, Tyrex and Petit-Oscar gave up earlier than expected by the model from the 10 s-delay; Eden and Ella from the 20 s-delay; Tartine and Pépette from the 40 s-delay. Raspoutine only gave up earlier from the 60 s-delay, while he was still successful in about 30% of the trials. In sheep, all but one (Popeye) began to give up prior to the estimates in the model below the 20 s-delay: Igor, Soquette, Nanook and Praline at 10 s; Schyne and Neige at 20 s. Popeye stopped waiting later than the model’s prediction at 60 s (Table 4). Concerning ratios, most individuals opted to consume the low-quantity reward earlier. This was particularly evident when the high-quantity was only twice the low-quantity, with six goats and five sheep out of seven starting to give up waiting earlier for the 4:8 ratio than for the 1:8 ratio. Few differences in giving-up times were observed between 1:4 and 1:8 ratios (Tables S7 and S8 in Supplementary Material). Finally, waiting times in relation to trials (Fig. S3 in Supplementary Material) showed that all subjects did not gradually stop waiting, but instead stopped waiting suddenly as the delay extended. Some subjects (n = 7) were able to wait for long delays despite several previous consecutive failures.

Discussion

We found that sheep and goats were able to wait similar maximum delays (up to 80 s) in the same delay of gratification task, although goats were more successful than sheep with increasing delays. Both species anticipated the upcoming delay and adjusted their decision according to the value of the delayed reward relative to the immediately available reward, showing similarities in the cognitive mechanisms at work in the decision-making process.

Our first objective was to evaluate sheep and goats in a delay-maintenance task, and to contextualise their performance in comparison with other wild and domestic species. Domestication may first have had major effects on their self-control abilities. Indeed, for selection purposes, domesticated species show altered levels of metabolic rates (Rauw et al. 2017). Domestication is also known to affect the general level of cognitive competence and/or of emotional control (Boissy and Erhard 2014). Furthermore, good self-control skills could be advantageous in the wild, for example in foraging or mate choice, but domesticated species do not experience the same challenges in their simpler and more predictable environment (Brucks et al., 2022a; Ferreira et al. 2023). Alternatively, the process of domestication has selected for less aggressive and tamer individuals (“emotional reactivity hypothesis”; Hare and Tomasello 2005b), which could enhance inhibitory abilities. For example, it has been shown that more aggressive rats waited for less longer than less aggressive rats (Van den Bergh et al. 2006). Accordingly, the close relationship with humans could also positively modify waiting skills. Dogs, for example, have been selected to cooperate with humans, which requires the ability to forgo immediate benefits and temporal discounting (Hare et al. 2002; Leonardi et al. 2012). Dogs also tend to exhibit submissive behaviour towards humans to be rewarded, which could explain why they tolerate higher delays than other domesticated species and their wild relatives (Brucks et al. 2017; Leonardi et al. 2012; Range et al. 2020). In our study, mean maximum delays were similar between the two species (54.3 s for the group of sheep and 62.9 s for the group of goats) with the majority of individuals waited up to 60 s. But some individual sheep and goats were able to delay gratification for up to 80 s to obtain the larger reward, with this inter-individual variation appearing unrelated to age or sex. The average success was only slightly lower than most of monkeys and apes tested on the same delays (Table 4 of Susini et al. 2020). Although studies on domestic animals were conducted using a diversity of experimental conditions and designs, the success of sheep and goats was quite unexpected when considering the maximum delays reached by domesticated animals (Table 5). Indeed, sheep and goats outperformed chickens (maximum individual delay reached 22 s; Abeyesinghe et al. 2005) and pigs (maximum individual delay reached: 20 s in Krause et al. 2021; see also Zebunke et al. 2018). They showed better performances than horses, whose maximum individual delay reached 60 s (by about 40% of the subjects) in a quality paradigm, and a mean maximum delay of 15 s at group scale in a quantity paradigm more similar to ours (Brucks et al., 2022a). Moreover, our delay-maintenance task required them to show continuous self-control throughout the trial, which was not the case in horses testing procedures. Thus, despite a lower life expectancy compared to horses they perform marginally better. Despite subtle differences of feeding ecology, sheep and goats, like horses, eat food evenly distributed compared to other taxa. According to the idea that feeding ecology is related to self-control (Addessi et al. 2011; MacLean et al. 2014; Stevens et al. 2005), herbivores should exhibit poor self-control abilities, compared to species that rely on more scarce resources. However, satiation could influence self-control abilities, with more satiated animals having better self-control (Miller et al. 2010 for dogs; Mayack and Naug 2015 for bees). In Brucks et al. (2022a), horses fed hay ad libitum achieved higher maximum delays than horses fed hay on a restricted basis. In addition to the fact that goats and sheep had access to water and hay ad libitum in our study, they were also free to roam in a two-hectare area in an enriched environment. This could have resulted in better performances in comparison to horses (Zentall 2021; Zobel and Nawroth 2020). It is finally unlikely that the presence of the observer affected the sheep and goats. We had taken great care to minimise any potential effects, such as avoiding looking at the animals and remaining impassive during the experiment. Although these results could contradict the idea that domestication explains the low waiting times of farm animals, testing the wild ancestors of domestic species (such as mouflons and ibexes) would be necessary.

A second objective was to compare the performances of both species. We found slight differences in performance between sheep and goats in our delay of gratification paradigm. At the longest delays (40, 60, and 80 s), goats were more successful than sheep. This result is in accordance with our predictions. Indeed, if goats prefer high quality patches, they need to refrain from staying on low quality ones, which can make self-control an important feature of their foraging ecology. We observed that sheep were more cohesive than goats in our population (see Supplementary Material), which supports the flexibility hypothesis suggested by Amici et al. (2008a,b) stating that animals living in more cohesive and stable groups would exhibit less self-control compared to individuals living in fission-fusion societies. In addition, the dominance relationships between individuals could play a role in the expression of self-control abilities. In fact, in groups with strong dominance hierarchies, subordinate individuals have to refrain from eating where/when the dominant is present (Barta and Giraldeau 1998; Genty and Roeder 2006; Miller et al. 2019; Stears et al. 2014; Thierry 2013). In our population, we found that the hierarchy was stronger and more linear in the goat group than in the sheep group, which may explain why goats seem to show more self-control than sheep in our task. However, at an individual level, most of the sheep were lower-ranking than the goats, but they did not wait longer than goats. Further studies are needed to experimentally control for the presence of dominant or stronger conspecifics to assess their impact on performance in self-control tasks.

One of the aims of our study was to investigate the cognitive mechanisms involved in decision-making in our delay-maintenance task. We observed that sheep and goats exhibit similarities: the main determinant of their decision was the delay and the quantity of food, in line with previous findings in other mammals, birds and fish (Aellen et al. 2021; Brucks et al. 2022a, b; Pelé et al. 2011; Schnell et al. 2021). Note that, to our knowledge, no study has previously been conducted on quantity discrimination in sheep and goats. We observed that prior to training, sheep and goats did not clearly discriminate between the quantities 1 vs. 4, but gradually learned to do so, discriminating also new quantities (1:8 and 4:8 ratios). Their relatively high percentage of success for 1:8 and 1:4 ratios up to 60 s-delay, makes it comparable to the performance of Tonkean macaques and brown capuchins tested in a delay-maintenance task for the same relative values (Pelé 2010; Pelé et al. 2011). This ability to adjust decisions based on the relative size of the rewards has not been found in marmosets, tamarins, rats, or pigeons (Green et al. 2004; Richards et al. 1997; Stevens et al. 2005). As suggested in Brucks et al. 2022a, this may be related to the ability of herbivores to attend to different food values in quantities when grazing. By adapting their decision to wait to the expected food amount, sheep and goats may have shown temporal discounting, evaluating the benefit of the future reward according to the delay needed to obtain it (Frederick et al. 2002; Mazur 1987).

The decision whether a reward is worth waiting for was made early in the trial. The majority of individuals (Fig. S3 in Supplementary Material) were able to wait the entire delay after several consecutive failures, which confirm that failure was not due to a progressive extinction of the waiting response. This ability has also been found in dogs (Leonardi et al. 2012), parrots (Auersperg et al. 2013; Brucks et al. 2022a, b), primates (chimpanzees: Dufour et al. 2007; Tonkean macaques and brown capuchins: Pelé et al. 2011), cleaner fish (Aellen et al. 2021) and horses (Brucks, Härterich, et al., 2022), but not in 3-years old children (Steelandt et al. 2012) nor noncleaner wrasse (Aellen et al. 2021) for example. These non-random giving-up times indicate that they had some understanding of the upcoming delay (Dufour et al. 2007). This complements well previous studies on anticipatory behaviours in various contexts (food anticipation tasks in sheep: Reefmann et al. 2009; in goats: Baciadonna et al. 2019). Although performance in this task does not demonstrate the ability to plan for future needs, anticipation in ungulates could increase predictability and control over the environment, enabling them to make optimal decisions. Knowing this could help design welfare interventions, such as the need for temporary consistency in daily routines (e.g., feeding time) and the reduction of cues to stressful events (De Vries et al. 2011; Destrez et al. 2013; Ross et al. 2023).

Some behaviours were correlated to improved waiting performances, especially those directed towards the apparatus (like hitting, threatening, scratching). It is likely that these behaviours helped the animals to divert their attention away from the food (and to report it on the apparatus itself), although it could also simply be the expression of some frustration. Similar patterns have already been documented in other species (Brucks et al. 2017; Brucks et al. 2022a, b; Dufour et al. 2007; Evans and Beran 2007a; Leonardi et al. 2012; Range et al. 2020), suggesting a common strategy to control impulsivity in delay tasks.

Finally, we found that sheep and goats exhibited a bias towards choosing the option placed on one side of the table. Side preferences are commonly observed in cognitive tasks in several species (in self-control tasks: squirrel monkeys (Saimiri sciureus): Anderson et al. 2000; capuchins (Cebus apella): Ventricelli et al. 2013). Usually, side bias can occur in cognitive tasks in subjects that do not understand the contingencies of the task and prefer to use simpler strategies (Killeen et al. 2018; Treviño et al. 2020), i.e., always choosing the option placed on the same side, in order to get the reward half of the time. Here, however, the side bias only appeared with longer delays (from 20 s-delay; Fig. S4 in Supplementary Material). It is possible that the side bias occurred because the subjects had reached an indifference point and did not care whether they received the high- or low-quantity reward (Albiach-Serrano et al. 2007). In addition, picking up a side that turned out to be the one associated with the long delay was not too costly as individuals could still change their decision later in the trial. Besides, the hierarchical decision analysis revealed that animals considered multiple factors simultaneously in order to make their decision. Note that three individuals failed to enter testing due to side bias; it would be interesting to investigate further these side biases, as they may be connected to the performances (e.g. subjects that did not correct their side bias may have less cognitive flexibility). This first experiment with a delay-maintenance task in a domesticated species shows that they use the same cognitive mechanisms as wild species, such as macaques or capuchin monkeys, tested under similar conditions.

In conclusion, we showed that sheep and goats can inhibit impulsive behaviour and consider different information when making a decision. They were able to adapt their decision according to different parameters, associating the information of the relative value of rewards with the delay, but applying simpler heuristics (side bias) when it became more costly. Both species reached similar maximum delays (with a median of 60 s), although slight differences were observed. Despite our sample size (that could be increased in the future) and differences in the experimental designs, our results indicate comparable performance to some monkeys and better than other domesticated species (Table 5). They performed better than horses, although it would be pertinent to compare the species with the same exact experimental setup in the same living conditions. Their physiological traits (lower brain size, longevity, and higher metabolic rate compared to horses) can’t explain their performances, and they are also less likely to have been selected for their cooperation with humans than horses or dogs. We have thus no evidence that these factors impacted sheep and goats’ self-control. Instead, our findings suggest that factors that shaped self-control in wild species such as social complexity, or foraging ecology were still influent despite the domestication process. Further studies with wild ungulates are needed to clarify the role of domestication per se in the evolution of self-control and the underlying cognitive and emotional competences.
