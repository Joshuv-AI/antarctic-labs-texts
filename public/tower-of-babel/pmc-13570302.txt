Periodontitis Exacerbates Insulin Resistance via Gut Barrier Dysfunction and TLR4/NF-κB Activation
Authors: Lanlan Jiang, Yuxiao Huang, Shimin Zhang, Xuelian Li, Feifei Mo, Chunyan Huang, Renchuan Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and AimsPeriodontitis and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are interrelated, potentially linked through intestinal barrier dysfunction. Yet, whether their comorbidity is associated with worsened barrier integrity and how this relates to insulin resistance remain unclear. This study examined associations between periodontitis, gut barrier disruption, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) activation, and metabolic parameters in a T2DM mouse model.MethodsTwenty-four C57BL/6 mice were randomised into 4 groups (n = 6): healthy control, chronic periodontitis, T2DM without periodontitis (DNP), and T2DM with periodontitis (DP). Glucose homeostasis was assessed by measuring fasting blood glucose and the area under the curve of oral glucose tolerance tests and insulin tolerance tests. The colonic barrier’s biological, chemical, physical, and immune properties were evaluated using 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing, haematoxylin and eosin staining, transmission electron microscopy, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, Western blotting, and flow cytometry. Furthermore, serum insulin and inflammatory cytokines were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.ResultsThe composition of both the periodontal and faecal microbiota in the DP group was markedly different from that in the DNP group. In the DP group, transmission electron microscopy revealed severe damage to colonic mucosal ultrastructure; messenger RNA and protein expression levels of MUC2 and tight junctions (including occludin, claudin-1, and ZO-1) were significantly decreased; the proportion of regulatory T cells diminished, while Th17 cells increased, resulting in an imbalanced regulatory T-cell/Th17 ratio. Proinflammatory cytokines in intestinal tissues and serum were raised, whereas anti-inflammatory cytokines were decreased. Moreover, in the DP group, activation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway was observed in intestinal tissue. Notably, levels of fasting blood glucose, serum insulin, and area under the curve for oral glucose tolerance tests and insulin tolerance tests were all higher in the DP group than in the DNP group.ConclusionsPeriodontitis is related to intestinal barrier disruption, TLR4/NF-κB signalling activation, and worsened metabolic parameters in T2DM mice.Clinical RelevanceThese findings may establish periodontitis as a potent driver of gut barrier dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and insulin resistance, highlighting its profound impact on metabolic health.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis, a prevalent chronic oral infectious disease, is initiated by pathogenic microorganisms in the oral cavity.1 Patients with periodontitis may swallow 1012 to 1013 bacteria in saliva every day.2 Among them, periodontal pathogens possess an array of virulence factors.3 Periodontitis has a major effect on various systemic diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and coronary heart disease.4 The intrinsic mechanisms involve distant spread and ectopic infection by periodontopathogens, distal end migration of oral inflammation–activated Th17 cells, and metabolic and immune disorders.5 Studies indicate that T2DM increases the risk of periodontitis,6 while severe periodontitis raises the risk of T2DM by 53%,7 negatively affecting glycaemic control and patient outcomes.8 In addition, clinical evidence shows that periodontal treatment improves blood glucose control and reduces systemic inflammation.9

Periodontal pathogens are known to exacerbate insulin resistance through modulating adaptive immunity.10 A proposed mechanism involves the swallowing of saliva, which introduces oral microbiota into the gastrointestinal tract. As a primary digestive and immune organ, the intestine is essential for nutrient absorption and the maintenance of microbial symbiosis. This balance is safeguarded by the intestinal barrier—a multilayered system consisting of the microbiota, mucus, epithelial cells, and immune networks—which provides critical microbial, chemical, physical, and immune defences.11 The disruption of this barrier represents a common pathologic mechanism underlying various diseases.12 It was reported that periodontal pathogens may compromise the intestinal barrier, increasing the risk or severity of systemic diseases associated with low-grade inflammation.13, 14, 15, 16 In animal models, Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) exposure disrupts intestinal microbiota, reduces important intestinal physical barrier tight junctions (TJs) such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) expression, and increases mucosal permeability, leading to bacterial leakage and endotoxemia.17 Meanwhile, Pg aggravates intestinal inflammation and Th17/regulatory T-cell (Treg) imbalance in a gut microbiota-dependent manner.5 Additionally, diabetic states are associated with reduced gastric acid secretion.18 Patients with T2DM often exhibit intestinal microbiota imbalances, inflammation, and barrier damage, including reduced butyrate-producing bacteria, which lowers anti-inflammatory butyrate production.19 Moreover, periodontal microorganisms such as Streptococcus and Veillonella have been detected in the intestines of patients with T2DM.20 In addition, increased lipopolysaccharide (LPS) resulting from gut dysbiosis exacerbates intestinal inflammation by activating the LPS/Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway.21 Specifically, LPS translocates into the circulation, binds to TLR4, and, via MyD88, activates NF-κB. This leads to the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-β, which aggravate inflammation and damage the intestinal barrier.22

The intestinal barrier may link periodontitis and T2DM, but whether its impairment worsens in individuals with both conditions and how this relates to insulin resistance remain unclear. This study establishes a T2DM periodontitis mouse model to assess glucose metabolism, analyse microbiota differences, evaluate intestinal barrier damage, and investigate insulin resistance. The findings characterise gut barrier dysfunction in T2DM periodontitis and provide correlational evidence to guide future mechanistic investigations.

Materials and methods

Prior to experimentation, 8-week-old wild-type male C57BL/6 mice were acclimatised and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. According to sample size calculation in animal studies using the resource equation approach, 24 mice were included in this study. The animals were then randomly allocated into 4 experimental groups (n = 6 per group): healthy control (HC), chronic periodontitis (CP), T2DM without periodontitis (DNP), and T2DM with periodontitis (DP). Samples were collected at the end of the 12th week after the experiment began. Glucose metabolism was evaluated by measuring fasting blood glucose (FBG), and area under the curve levels of an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and an insulin tolerance test (ITT). Micro–computed tomography (CT) analysis and haematoxylin and eosin staining were used to verify the successful construction of the experimental periodontitis model, and FBG ≥11.1 mmol /L was the successful criterion for T2DM. Full-length 16S ribosomal RNA gene amplicon sequencing, transmission electron microscopy, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, Western blot, and flow cytometry were applied to detect biological, chemical, physical, and immune barriers in the colon. Serum insulin and inflammatory factors were detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Th17 and Treg cells were defined by flow cytometry. The detailed materials and methods are described in the Supplemental Materials. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (approval number: 202109025).

Results

Glucose metabolism was assessed using FBG, OGTT, ITT, fasting insulin, Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance Index (HOMA-IR), and body weight. At the endpoint, the FBG level of the CP group was significantly higher than that in the HC group, with the level in the DP group higher than that in the DNP group (P < .05; Figure 1E). HOMA-IR analysis showed comparable levels between the CP and HC groups (P > .05), whereas the DP group had significantly higher levels than the DNP group (P < .05; Figure 1K).

Impaired colon barrier in mice with T2DM and experimental periodontitis

Alterations of gut microbiota

Microbial composition in both the oral cavity and the gut

Analysis of oral and gut microbiomes in T2DM mice revealed significant group differences (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). In the oral microbiome, the group of periodontal microflora in periodontitis (PP) exhibited higher abundances of Bifidobacterium animalis, Streptococcus hyointestinalis A, Rodentibacter heylii, Ligilactobacillus murinus, Enterococcus faecalis, Allobaculum stercoricanis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae compared with the group of periodontal microflora in healthy control (PHC) (P < .05). In contrast, Sediminibacterium magnilacihabitans, Streptococcus parasanguinis C, Methylovirgula ligni, and S parasanguinis B were significantly lower in the PP group compared to the PHC group (P < .05). The group of periodontal microbiota in DP had higher levels of B animalis, Escherichia coli, Escherichia flexneri, E faecalis, K pneumoniae, Dubosiella sp000403415, and S magnilacihabitans but lower abundances of Sediminibacterium sp000515315, S magnilacihabitans, Bradyrhizobium sp000426785, and S parasanguinis compared to the group of periodontal microflora of T2DM without periodontitis (P < .05; Figure 2A). In the gut microbiome, the group of gut microbiota in DP (GDP) showed higher levels of A stercoricanis, E flexneri, B animalis, Escherichia dysenteriae, and so on but lower abundances of Faecalibaculum rodentium, Ileibacterium valens, and Olsenella umbonata compared to the group of gut microbiota in DNP (P < .05). The group of gut microbiota in CP had higher levels of A stercoricanis, Dubosiella sp000403415, Desulfovibrio sp011039085, Prevotellamassilia sp002933955, B animalis, Desulfovibrio sp011039135, Muribaculum sp001701195, and Lactobacillus intestinalis but lower levels of A stercoricanis, Lacrimispora sp000526575, Ligilactobacillus animalis, and so on compared to the group of gut microbiota of HC (P < .05; Figure 2B). Compromised physical barrier integrity in mice with T2DM and periodontitis

Ultrastructure analysis of colon mucosa

Transmission electron microscopy analysis of colonic mucosa (scale 1.0 μm) revealed intact, dense, and continuous TJs in the HC group. Meanwhile, a certain degree of looseness in tight junctions was observed in the CP and DNP groups, with a more obvious loose structure in the DP group (Figure 3D).

Disrupted tight junctions in the colon

Immunohistochemical analysis (intercellular brown staining) revealed positive expression of tight junction proteins (claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1) in the colon tissue of mice (Figure 3E). mRNA/protein expression levels showed that claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1 were highest in the HC group and lowest in the DP group (P < .05; Figure 3F, G, H, J). In particular, mRNA expression of claudin-1 and occludin in the DP group was significantly lower than in the DNP group (P < .05; Figure 3F and G). Additionally, the protein expression levels of occludin and ZO-1 in the DP group were lower than those in the DNP group, similar to the result in the CP group compared with the HC group (P < .05; Figure 3K and L).

Impaired immune barrier in the colon

The percentage of Treg cells in the DP group was the lowest compared to both HC and DNP groups (P < .05; Figure 3N). Conversely, the proportion of Th17 cells was highest in the DP group compared with the HC, CP, and DNP groups (P < .05; Figure 3O). Additionally, the Th17/Treg ratio in the DP group was elevated relative to the other 3 groups (P < .05; Figure 3P).

Redundancy analysis

At the species level, it showed that B animalis in the periodontal microbiota of the DP group strongly correlated with HOMA-IR, while E flexneri correlated with FBG (Figure 6C). In the gut microbiota, Dubosiella sp000403415 most positively correlated with HOMA-IR and B animalis with FBG (Figure 6D). Dubosiella sp000403415 and A stercoricanis strongly correlated with IL-10 mRNA, while E faecalis correlated with IL-6 mRNA (Figure 6F). Discussion

Periodontitis has a high prevalence and endangers the entire body. In the present clinical study, it was reported that periodontitis may induce gut microbiota dysbiosis and intestinal inflammation by the translocation of salivary microbes. In addition, periodontitis and diabetes have a 2-way relationship, where each can exacerbate the other. T2DM is also prone to impaired intestinal mucosal barrier function, which in turn exacerbates insulin resistance and accelerates the progression of diabetes. We found that periodontitis may be related to poor glycaemic control. In our study, the CP group exhibited higher FBG levels than the HC group, while the DP group showed higher levels than the DNP group. These findings align with prior studies indicating that periodontitis can influence FBG levels in C57BL/6J mice23 and exacerbate diabetes severity in humans.24 Periodontal pathogenic bacteria can aggravate insulin resistance by regulating adaptive immunity in diabetic mice.25 Our results further demonstrate that the DP group had significantly higher OGTT and ITT levels compared with the DNP group, indicating more severe glucose metabolism dysfunction in T2DM with periodontitis.

Periodontitis played a pivotal role in T2DM development by impairing the intestinal barrier (eg, microbial composition, intercellular integrity, and immune homeostasis).26 In our study, periodontitis might have significantly altered microbial compositions in the oral cavity and gut of T2DM mice. The GDP group showed reduced gut microbial diversity, with a notably lower abundance of F rodentium compared to the gut microbiota in DNP group. F rodentium promotes epithelial proliferation via an RA–eosinophil–IFN-γ–dependent pathway,27 and its growth is stimulated by sugars and ILC3, displacing Th17-inducing microbiota and reducing Th17 cells.28 Conversely, A stercoricanis, E flexneri, and E dysenteriae in the GDP group were the most abundant among groups. Escherichia species, known to cause bacillary dysentery, invade and multiply in colonic epithelial cells.29 Periodontal inflammation promoted the growth of Enterobacteriaceae, including K pneumoniae and E flexneri, which can ectopically colonise the gut and exacerbate intestinal inflammation.30 These bacteria not only thrive in inflammatory environments but are also uniquely adapted to survive translocation from the mouth through the harsh, acidic stomach. Thus, periodontal inflammation not only promoted the growth of oral pathobionts but also correlated with gut dysbiosis and inflammation.

Interestingly, as one of the pathogenic bacteria of periodontitis, K pneumoniae can be isolated and detected in the specimens of patients with stage II and III periodontitis.31 In this experiment, K pneumoniae was also one of the major periodontal pathogens in the mouse model of experimental periodontitis; compared with the DNP group, the abundance level of K pneumoniae in the DP group was significantly increased. Five of the top 22 bacterial species (Dubosiella sp 000403415, E faecalis, R heylii, E flexneri, L johnsonii) were detected at high abundance levels in both the periodontal and intestinal areas, while none of these species were found in the DNP group. This indicates that these 5 bacterial communities are significantly different and closely related to the development of periodontitis and suggests that periodontitis induced more oral pathogenic bacteria to ectopically enter the gut of T2DM mice and affected gut microbiota. Among the microbial factors, K pneumoniae may act as the microbial bridge between periodontitis and other diseases.32 This might be attributed to the directly connected anatomy between oral and intestinal microbiota, which communicate with each other through the oral-gut microbiome axis and may also be indirectly involved in the regulation of systemic health and disease through the disturbed gut.33 Periodontal therapy was associated with a significant change in both oral and gut microbiota, with posttreatment microbial profiles resembling those of healthy volunteers.34 Additionally, there is evidence suggesting that periodontitis may lead to gut microbiota dysbiosis due to the influx of salivary microbe.35

The intestinal mucus barrier, or chemical barrier, comprises mucin 2 (MUC2), antimicrobial peptides, and lysozymes, separating bacteria in the lumen from epithelial cells and protecting them from damage.36 Defects in mucin production or processing can compromise this barrier, allowing bacteria to reach the epithelium and disrupt intestinal integrity. In this study, MUC2 expression levels were lower in the DNP group compared with the HC group and even further reduced in the DP group, indicating that periodontitis may exacerbate intestinal mucus barrier damage in T2DM mice. MUC2-knockout mice are known to develop spontaneous inflammation, highlighting the importance of MUC2 in maintaining intestinal homeostasis.37 A compromised chemical barrier allows harmful substances and bacteria to directly contact and damage the intestinal epithelium, which may be associated with subsequent dysregulation.

The intestinal physical barrier, primarily formed by intestinal epithelial cells and tight junctions (TJs), confines intestinal luminal antigens and microorganisms. TJs consist of transmembrane proteins (eg, claudin, occludin) that regulate paracellular permeability and cytoplasmic proteins (eg, ZO-1) that link them to the cytoskeleton, forming a stable network and mediating cell signalling.36,38,39 The disruption of this barrier and the consequent increase in intestinal permeability are common pathophysiological mechanisms in numerous diseases.40,41 Transmission electron microscopy revealed reduced microvilli and loosened TJs in the DNP and DP groups, with the DP group showing more severe damage. Expression levels of occludin, claudin-1, and ZO-1 mRNA, as well as occludin and claudin-1 protein, were reduced in the CP, DNP, and DP groups, with the DP group exhibiting the lowest levels. These findings indicate that periodontitis is associated with more severe physical barrier damage in the colonic mucosa of T2DM mice.

Intestinal immune homeostasis depends on the balance between proinflammatory cells (Th1, Th2, Th17) and anti-inflammatory Foxp3+ Tregs.42 During homeostasis, CD4+ Foxp3+ Tregs are abundant in the intestinal mucosa, supporting immune balance locally and systemically.43 The gut microbiota plays a key role in regulating the Th17/Treg ratio,44 and disruptions in gut flora can lead to a Th17/Treg imbalance.45 In this study, the most severe dysbiosis was observed in the DP group, reflecting the combined impact of periodontitis and T2DM on gut microbial composition. Notably, oral pathobionts such as K pneumoniae can activate Th17 cells, which may migrate from the oral cavity to the gut, exacerbating intestinal inflammation along the oral-gut axis. Furthermore, CD4+ T cells deficient in IL-17/RORγT can induce T2DM.46 Th17 cells, activated by oral pathogens such as K pneumoniae, can migrate from the oral cavity to the intestine, exacerbating colitis and promoting inflammation along the oral-gut axis. The pronounced Th17/Treg imbalance in the DP group indicates compromised immune regulation, in which K pneumoniae appears to contribute significantly to intestinal inflammation and immune dysfunction. Together, these findings may underscore the far-reaching effects of periodontitis and T2DM on the chemical, physical, and immune barriers of the gut.

TLR2 and TLR4 are key Toll-like receptors in innate immunity and inflammation. Notably, TLR4 specifically recognises LPS from gram-negative bacteria and its metabolite lipid A,47 giving it a functional advantage over TLR2 in LPS sensing.48,49 Upon binding LPS, TLR4 activates the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, upregulating NF-κB and triggering a proinflammatory signalling cascade.50 In this study, TLR4 and NF-κB mRNA expression in colonic tissues was elevated in the DNP group after 12 weeks of a high-fat diet compared with the HC group and was further increased in the DP group, indicating pathway activation. These findings highlight the role of gut microbiota disturbances and intestinal barrier dysfunction in T2DM, with LPS-TLR4 pathway activation accelerating disease progression.51 In this study, after 12 weeks of high-fat feeding, TLR4 and NF-κB mRNA expression in colonic tissues was higher in the DNP group than in the HC group and further elevated in the DP group, indicating TLR4/NF-κB pathway activation. This finding aligns with reports that Pg-LPS and Escherichia coli‌ (E.Coli)-LPS enhance TLR2/4 and NF-κB/STAT3 signalling to promote the release of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-23.52 Long-term TLR4 activation is linked to insulin resistance and impaired insulin sensitivity.53 These findings highlight the role of gut microbiota disturbances and intestinal barrier dysfunction in T2DM, with LPS-TLR4 pathway activation accelerating disease progression.54 This study further supports the upregulation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway in T2DM with periodontitis, which may contribute to heightened colonic inflammation and intestinal barrier damage.

Serum endotoxin levels were higher in the CP group than in the HC group due to oral flora imbalances associated with periodontitis. Gram-negative bacteria in periodontitis can release virulence factors into circulation from ruptured periodontal pockets, causing low-level endotoxemia.55 The DP group showed significantly higher serum endotoxin levels than the CP and DNP groups, which may suggest additional endotoxin sources. TJ structure in distal intestinal epithelia can be specifically impaired by High-Fat Diet (HFD) intake in T2DM mice,56 which can increase colonic permeability by inhibiting the mRNA expression of TJ proteins in intestinal epithelial cells57 and increasing serum endotoxin levels,58 as seen in this study. Experimental periodontitis can also alter the gut microbiome23 and composition, increasing intestinal permeability and downregulating the expression of TJs (ZO-1 and occludin).59 Thus, the more severely impaired intestinal barrier in the DP group may facilitate endotoxin leakage, which may contribute to elevated serum endotoxin levels.

Periodontitis exacerbated systemic chronic inflammation in patients with T2DM.60 In this study, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels were higher in the CP and DNP groups compared to the HC group, with the highest levels observed in the DP group. This suggests that periodontitis may amplify the inflammatory responses associated with T2DM.61 Furthermore, serum endotoxin levels, strongly linked to insulin resistance, serve as an independent risk factor for reduced insulin sensitivity. Additionally, the interplay among glycometabolism, the microbiome, immune responses, proinflammatory cytokines, and TLR4/NF-κB pathway activation provides a foundation for future mechanistic studies of their interaction.

In this study, the intestinal barrier damage in T2DM mice with periodontitis and the activation changes of inflammatory pathways have been detected, but the specific mechanism of periodontitis-induced intestinal microbial changes and how metabolites or signalling molecules affect the intestinal barrier has not been further explored. More comprehensive data and validations using in vitro and in vivo assays in future research are necessary.

Conclusions

In conclusion, based on oral-gut interactions, periodontitis was associated with T2DM progression, intestinal barrier dysfunction, upregulation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, and impaired glucose metabolism. These findings highlight the critical role of intestinal barrier integrity in linking periodontitis to T2DM and provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the influence of periodontitis on systemic diseases.
