Learning to Answer from Correct Demonstrations
Nirmit Joshi, Gene Li, Siddharth Bhandari, Shiva Prasad Kasiviswanathan, Cong Ma, Nathan Srebro

Abstract
We study the problem of learning to generate an answer (or completion) to a question (or prompt), where there could be multiple correct answers, any one of which is acceptable at test time. Learning is based on demonstrations of some correct answer to each training question, as in Supervised Fine Tuning (SFT). We formalize the problem as offline imitation learning in contextual bandits, with demonstrations from some optimal policy, without explicitly observed rewards. Prior work assumes that the demonstrator belongs to a low-complexity policy class, which motivates maximum likelihood estimation (i.e., log-loss minimization). In contrast, we propose relying only on the reward model (specifying which answers are correct) being in a low-cardinality class, which we argue is a weaker assumption. We show that likelihood maximization methods can fail in this case, and instead devise an alternative novel approach that learns with sample complexity logarithmic in the cardinality of the reward class. Our work motivates looking beyond likelihood maximization when learning from correct demonstrations.

Introduction

Many real-world problems involve generating an answer to a question, where there may be many
equally good

not
to reproduce all correct responses, but to generate a

.

The same principle underlies the deployment of modern LLMs, where success is judged by whether the model generates a single high-utility answer to the user’s question

OpenAI 2023

Anthropic 2024

DeepMind 2023

Policy Class Assumption and Likelihood Maximization.

A common approach when learning from demonstrations is to assume that the demonstrator
\pi_{*}
belongs to a low-capacity policy class
\Pi

However, for this guarantee to be meaningful,

This requires us to have a small policy class for how precisely demonstrators behave—e.g., modeling the generative process of all optimal demonstrators, such as all possible students producing solutions to math problems, rather than just modeling what it means for a solution to be correct.

Setting and Problem Definition

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{Y}
respectively be the sets of all possible contexts and actions. A
policy

\pi
is specified by a mapping
x\mapsto\pi(\cdot\mid x)
from contexts to distributions over actions. We also overload notation and refer to deterministic policies as mappings
\pi:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Y}
, where
\pi(\cdot\mid x)=\delta_{\pi(x)}
, formally mapping each context
x
to a distribution placing all its mass on a single action
\pi(x)
. As described in the Introduction, our goal is to find a policy with small loss
L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\pi)
, defined in Eq. (
1
), where the loss is specified by a context distribution
{\mathcal{D}}\in\Delta({\mathcal{X}})
and a ground-truth support function
\sigma_{*}:{\mathcal{X}}\to(2^{\mathcal{Y}}\setminus\emptyset)
. We use
(x,y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}\times\pi
to denote a joint distribution where
x\sim{\mathcal{D}}
and
y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)
. Learning is based on an i.i.d. samples from
{\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*}
, for some optimal (i.e., zero loss) demonstrator policy
\pi_{*}
.

Crucial to our work is the distinction between two types of hypothesis classes and corresponding realizability assumptions:

•

Model Class Assumption.
The unknown support function
\sigma_{*}
is in a known hypothesis class of support functions,
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
. The demonstrator
\pi_{*}
is optimal, i.e.,
\pi_{*}(\cdot\mid x)
is supported on
\sigma_{*}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. But the support function
\sigma_{*}
does not uniquely determine
\pi_{*}
as there are many optimal policies.

•

Policy Class Assumption.
The unknown demonstrator
\pi_{*}
is in a known policy class
\pi_{*}\in\Pi\subseteq(\Delta(\mathcal{Y}))^{\mathcal{X}}
and is optimal
4
4

4

The view of
Foster et al. 2024
is slightly different in an important way: they do not assume
\pi_{*}\in\Pi
is optimal, and instead, their goal is to compete with the (possibly suboptimal)
\pi_{*}
. While we argue that the Model Class Assumption is strictly weaker (i.e., better, more general) than the Policy Class Assumption as we define it (i.e., requiring the demonstrator is optimal), this statement
does not
extend to the weakened assumption without requiring optimality. See
Section

6
for a discussion on relaxing the optimality assumption.
for the unknown
\sigma_{*}
.

The two assumptions can be related to each other as follows: The Model Class Assumption
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, combined with the optimality of
\pi_{*}
w.r.t.
\sigma_{*}
, implies that
\pi_{*}
is in the corresponding policy class:

\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}:=\bigcup_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}}\Pi_{\sigma}\,\,,\text{ where }\,\,\Pi_{\sigma}:=\left\{\pi:{\mathcal{X}}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\,\,\middle|\,\,\forall_{x}\,{\rm supp}\,\pi(\cdot\mid x)\subseteq\sigma(x)\right\}\,.

(2)

And so the Model Class Assumption with
{\mathcal{S}}
implies the Policy Class Assumption with
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
. However, once
|\sigma(x)|>1
, the class
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
is much larger than
{\mathcal{S}}
. Even if
{\mathcal{S}}
has finite cardinality, the class
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
is continuous and infinite. Even if we discretize this probability space,
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
can still be much larger, since we are introducing
(|\sigma(x)|-1)
parameters
per instance
to choose the distribution over correct answers, so overall a number of parameters that scales with the domain size
and
the number of correct answers. For example, even just restricting to policies that are uniform on a subset of
\sigma(x)
, there are
\prod_{x}(2^{|\sigma(x)|}-1)
optimal policies for each
\sigma
.

On the other hand, each policy class
\Pi
can be associated with a model class

\mathcal{S}_{\Pi}:=\left\{\sigma_{\pi}:x\mapsto{\rm supp}\,\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\,\middle|\,\,\pi\in\Pi\right\}\,,

(3)

where
|\mathcal{S}_{\Pi}|\leq|\Pi|
. Strictly speaking, the Policy Class Assumption does not imply the Model Class Assumption, since
\sigma_{*}
could be a superset of
\sigma_{\pi_{*}}
, i.e.,
\sigma_{\pi_{*}}(x)\subsetneq\sigma_{*}(x)
. However, the loss
L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\pi)
of any policy
\pi
w.r.t. such
\sigma_{*}
is bounded by the loss w.r.t.
\sigma_{\pi_{*}}
(i.e.,
L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\pi)\leq L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{\pi_{*}}}(\pi)
). Thus, a loss guarantee under the Model Class Assumption on
\mathcal{S}_{\Pi}
implies a loss guarantee under the Policy Class Assumption for any
\Pi
. And so, even if we start with a Policy Class Assumption with some low-cardinality
\Pi
, we can instead use a Model Class Assumption with the class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\Pi}
that is not any larger.

We are now ready to formalize our learning goal, which is captured by the following definition:

Definition 1

(Learning from Correct Demonstrations)
.

We say that the reward model class
\mathcal{S}
is
learnable from correct demonstrations
by a learning rule
{\mathcal{A}}:({\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{Y})^{*}\to(\Delta(\mathcal{Y}))^{\mathcal{X}}
with sample complexity
m_{{\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}}}(\epsilon,\delta)
, if for any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, and any sample size
m\geq m_{{\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}}}(\varepsilon,\delta)
, for any
{\mathcal{D}}
, and
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, and any
\pi_{*}\in\Pi_{\sigma_{*}}
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}}\left[L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}({\mathcal{A}}(S))\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\delta\,.

Why MLE is Good for Low-Cardinality
\Pi
but Fails for
{\mathcal{S}}
?

Given a policy class
\Pi\subseteq(\Delta(\mathcal{Y}))^{\mathcal{X}}
, the (conditional) Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) (or the log-loss minimizer) is defined as

\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi}(S)=\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\pi(y_{i}\mid x_{i})=\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\pi(y_{i}\mid x_{i})\right)\,.

(MLE)

Low-cardinality
\Pi
.

To contrast with our work, we first consider learning under the stronger Policy Class Assumption, with a low cardinality policy class
\Pi
. In this case, MLE is
minimax optimal
with respect to
|\Pi|
, ensuring the best possible (worst case) loss of
O(\tfrac{\log|\Pi|}{m})
, as captured by the following proposition (in similar spirit to
Cohen et al. 2024
;
Foster et al. 2024
, and proved for completeness in Appendix
C.1
):

Proposition 1
.

For any finite
\Pi\subseteq(\Delta(\mathcal{Y}))^{\mathcal{X}}
, for any
{\mathcal{D}}
, any
\sigma_{*}
and any
\pi_{*}\in\Pi
that is optimal for
\sigma_{*}
(i.e.,
\pi_{*}\in\Pi_{\sigma_{*}}
), with probability at least
1-\delta
over
S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}
, any
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi}(S)
enjoys the following guarantee:

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}})\leq L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{\pi_{*}}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}})\leq D_{{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}({\mathcal{D}}\times\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}},{\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})\leq\frac{6\log(2|\Pi|/\delta)}{m}\,.

In the above,
D_{{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}):=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}(\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(z)}-\sqrt{\mathbb{Q}(z)})^{2}
is the squared Hellinger distance between distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
over a discrete domain
\mathcal{Z}
. The proof uses ideas from
Foster et al. 2024
; one can first use the standard guarantees in density estimation to establish convergence in squared Hellinger distance
D_{{\mathsf{H}}}^{2}({\mathcal{D}}\times\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}},{\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})
. The loss
L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}})
can then be bounded in terms of this squared Hellinger distance. However, this guarantee is only meaningful when
\log|\Pi|
is small. Note that if MLE succeeds thanks to
Proposition

1
and the cardinality
|\Pi|
being small, we would not only have low loss, but
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}
would also match the distribution of
\pi_{*}
.

Low-Cardinality
{\mathcal{S}}
: Simple Failure of MLE over Natural Policy Classes.

We now ask what can be said about the performance of the Maximum Likelihood Estimator based only on the model class
{\mathcal{S}}
being finite. First, since our problem is only specified in terms of the model class
{\mathcal{S}}
, we need to specify which policy class
\Pi
we want to maximize the likelihood over.

The most natural choice is to consider the class
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
from Eq. (
2
) of all policies supported on
{\mathcal{S}}
, since as discussed, the Model Class Assumption implies
\pi_{*}\in\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
. The problem is that even if
{\mathcal{S}}
is small, the class
|\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}|
may be infinite, making the guarantee in
Proposition

1
vacuous. Indeed, we will see that
\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}}
can fail to learn with any sample size, even when
|{\mathcal{S}}|=2
.

Let us think of how
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}}(S)
would behave. For any
x
observed in the training set, any distribution over correct answers is allowed, and only correct answers are observed, so
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}(\cdot\mid x)
will match the empirical distribution
\pi_{S}(\cdot|x)
of observed
y_{i}
’s for
x_{i}=x
. However, on unseen contexts
x
, it may choose any distribution over actions
y\in\sigma(x)
for some
\sigma
that is consistent with the data, i.e., in:

\textsc{cons}_{\mathcal{S}}(S)=\left\{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}\mid\forall_{(x,y)\in S}\,\,\,y\in\sigma(x)\right\}.

(4)

And the MLE predictor can be written as:

\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}(\cdot|x)=\begin{cases}\pi_{S}(\cdot|x)\,,&\text{if $x$ is observed};\\
\text{any dist over $\sigma(x)$}\,,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\quad\quad\text{for some $\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{\mathcal{S}}(S)$}.

(5)

Since the loss on observed contexts is zero, the loss of the MLE predictor
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}
is exactly the same as just choosing any
\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{\mathcal{S}}(S)
consistent with the data, and predicting actions
y\in\sigma(x)
on any unseen context
x
. Maximizing the likelihood over a policy class does not buy us anything for unseen contexts over just ensuring consistency. While relying on just consistency is fine, even optimal, in (realizable, finite cardinality) supervised learning, we will see that it fails for our problem, and so does MLE which is equivalent to it.

Consider
{\mathcal{S}}=\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{01}\}
, where
\sigma_{0}(x)=\{0\}
and
\sigma_{01}(x)=\{0,1\}
for all
x
. If the true hypothesis is
\sigma_{*}=\sigma_{0}
, then all observed labels are
0
. However,
\sigma_{01}
also remains consistent, and thus
\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}}(S)
may output
1
at test time—
failing to generalize and go beyond memorization
. Thus, for an input distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
such that the
missing mass
(i.e., unobserved contexts) is arbitrarily close to 1, we get the following failure.

Theorem 1

(Failure of MLE over
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
)
.

There exists
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
with
|{\mathcal{S}}|=|\mathcal{Y}|=2
and
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathbb{N}}
and a choice of
(\sigma_{*},\pi_{*})
such that for every sample size
m
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exists a marginal distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
such that almost surely for
S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}
, some
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}}(S)
has the following guarantee:

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}})\geq 1-\gamma\,.

The failure of
\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}}
can be attributed to the fact that the induced policy class
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
is too large, with infinitely many hypothesis, even though
{\mathcal{S}}
is small. We might instead consider using Maximum Likelihood Estimation, but over a smaller policy class whose size matches that of
{\mathcal{S}}
. A natural choice for such a policy class is:

\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}:=\{\pi_{\text{unif},\sigma}:\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}\}\,\,\text{ where }\,\,\pi_{\text{unif},\sigma}(\cdot\mid x)={\rm Unif}(\sigma(x)),

where for simplicity we consider
\sigma(x)
to be finite here. The advantage is that we now have
|\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}|=|{\mathcal{S}}|
. However, this policy class is
misspecified
in that the actual demonstrator
\pi_{*}
need not be uniform on all correct answers and so
\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}
might not contain
\pi_{*}
. We again cannot rely on
Proposition

1
.

Interestingly, despite the misspecification, with enough samples from any optimal demonstrator
\pi_{*}
(even if
\pi_{*}\not\in\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}
), we can at least ensure that
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}}(S)
achieves an overlap with the true correct answers
\sigma_{*}
. More specifically,
Theorem

8
in Appendix
C.3
ensures that for
S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}
and any
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}}(S)
,

\mathbb{P}_{S}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\!\left[\;\exists_{y\in\sigma_{*}(x)}\,\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}(y|x)>0\;\right]\;\geq\;1-\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,,\quad\text{ for any }\quad m\geq\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\big(\log|{\mathcal{S}}|+\log(1/\delta)\big).

However, such an overlap is not our goal and it is not very helpful in general: The set
\sigma(x)
, and thus the support of
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}(\cdot|x)
, can be huge, and the error of
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}
can be as high as
1-1/|\sigma(x)|
. One can show this is indeed the case—with large
\sigma(x)
, the loss can be arbitrarily high even as the sample size increases, as formalized below and proved in Appendix
C.2
:

Theorem 2

(Failure of MLE over
\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}
)
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exists
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
with
|{\mathcal{S}}|=2,|{\mathcal{X}}|=1,|\mathcal{Y}|=2\lceil 1/\gamma\rceil
, such that for some choice of
{\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*}
for some optimal
\pi_{*}
supported on
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, for every sample size
m
, and almost surely for
S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}
, there is a unique
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}}(S)
and it suffers from the following loss:

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}})\geq 1-\gamma\,.

4
Learning Algorithms

In this section, we present our main result: a learning rule that can learn from correct demonstrations (as in
Definition

1
) with sample complexity
O(\log|{\mathcal{S}}|)
. We begin with a simple rule that does ensure learning, but with high (super) linear sample complexity. We then amend it to obtain the desired logarithmic sample complexity.

4.1
Warm-up: A Sample Inefficient Learner

Our first rule is simple: output according to a majority vote among all the consistent hypotheses.

\textsc{Majority}(S):\quad\text{Return }\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{Maj}}(x)=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}|\{\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{\mathcal{S}}(S):y\in\sigma(x)\}|\,\,.

(6)

The returned policy
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{Maj}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Y}
is deterministic. Note that the rule
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{Maj}}(x)
always outputs from the common intersection
\bigcap_{\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{\mathcal{S}}(S)}\sigma(x)
whenever this common intersection is non-empty. In fact, Appendix
D.2
we prove the following Theorem, which shows that just outputting from this common intersection (whenever it is non-empty) is sufficient for learning:

Theorem 3
.

Any finite
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
is learnable from correct demonstrations (cf.
Definition

1
) using the rule
Majority
(or any rule that outputs from
\bigcap_{\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{\mathcal{S}}(S)}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\hskip-8.53581pt\sum_{y_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t})\text{ and }\widehat{y}_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})}\hskip-17.07164pt2w^{(t)}(\sigma)+\sum_{y_{t},\widehat{y}_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t})}\hskip-2.84526ptw^{(t)}(\sigma)=\hskip-8.53581pt\sum_{y_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t})\text{ and }\widehat{y}_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})}\hskip-17.07164ptw^{(t)}(\sigma)+\sum_{y_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t})}\hskip-2.84526ptw^{(t)}(\sigma)

5.1
Learning General Bounded Reward Classes from Optimal Demonstrations

We first extend our setting and results from binary to real valued rewards. Instead of a reward model class specified by a set
{\mathcal{S}}
of support functions, it is now specified by a set
\mathcal{R}
of bounded reward functions

5.2
\mathsf{pass@}k
Error Minimization

As an extension to our setup, we also consider a
\mathsf{pass@}k
objective. Here, for each context
x
, the policy
\mu
is allowed to output
k
responses
(y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(k)})
, and this is considered correct (i.e., not an error) if at least one of the responses is correct, i.e.,
\exists_{i\in[k]}\,\,y^{(i)}\in\sigma_{*}(x)
. In terms of a general reward, we receive the maximal reward among all candidate responses, i.e.,
\max_{1\leq i\leq k}{r_{*}}(x,y^{(i)})
. Such an objective is often used in benchmarks (e.g.,
Chen et al. 2021
;
Orlanski et al. 2022
;
Dalal et al. 2025
) and makes sense in settings where it is sensible to output multiple completions and let the user choose (e.g., in writing assistance tasks), or when verifying an answer is easier than finding one (e.g., when the answer is a Lean proof, or a Python program that can be unit tested, or perhaps using another LLM as a verifier). Importantly, at training time, the learner still receives only a single correct answer
y_{t}
for each training question
x_{t}
.

Formally, the learner outputs a policy
\mu:{\mathcal{X}}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{k})
that maps each context
x
to a
distribution
over
k
actions (recall that the policy, even in the
k=1
case, is allowed to be stochastic). The policy
\mu
is allowed to output any joint distribution over the set of
k
actions, with arbitrary dependencies, e.g., encouraging diversity or coverage. This need not be a product distribution corresponding to repeated independent sampling from a single-response policy
\pi:{\mathcal{X}}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
. Training is still based on demonstrations from an optimal single-response policy
\pi_{*}
supported on an unknown
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.

The
\mathsf{pass@}k
loss and value of such a policy are defined as

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}=({{y}^{(1)}},\dots,{{y}^{(k)}})\sim{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{y}^{(i)}\notin\sigma_{*}(x);\forall i\in[k]\}\right],

(17)

V_{{\mathcal{D}},{r_{*}}}(\mu):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}=({{y}^{(1)}},\dots,{{y}^{(k)}})\sim{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq k}{r_{*}}(x,{{y}^{(i)}})\}\right]\,,

(18)

Our methods and analysis can be extended to the
\mathsf{pass@}k

Algorithm

1

3

k

k

\sigma

(from uncovered
\sigma

w^{(t+1)}(\sigma)\leftarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(k+1)\,w^{(t)}(\sigma)}

Remark 2

When the demonstrator is suboptimal, controlling the loss as in

The situation is more subtle for general non-binary reward model classes

. Here the demonstrator may be arbitrarily close in value to the optimal policy, yet have a large loss
L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{{r_{*}}}}(\pi_{*})
—potentially as large as 1. Thus, controlling loss suboptimality w.r.t. the class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{R}}
, cf. Eq.(
16
), does not translate into anything for value suboptimality in this setting. On the other hand,
Foster et al. 2024
can achieve
\varepsilon
-value suboptimality w.r.t. any bounded reward even when
\pi_{*}
is not necessarily optimal (by matching its distribution), under the stronger assumption that
\log|\Pi|
is small. We leave it as an interesting and important direction for future work whether we can achieve
\varepsilon
-value suboptimality for general bounded reward model classes even if the demonstrator is suboptimal.

7
Reward Maximization vs Distribution Matching

In our paper, we view
reward maximization
or
utility
as our goal when learning from demonstrations. That is, we are not measuring success by how well we match the demonstrator policy, but only by the reward our policy obtains. This is in line with other work on imitation learning
(e.g.,
Pomerleau 1988
;
Ng et al. 2000
;
Rajaraman et al. 2020
;
Rashidinejad et al. 2021
;
Foster et al. 2024
, etc.)
, which also view the demonstrator as a resource, but the ultimate goal is to obtain good reward.

One approach to obtaining good reward based on demonstrations is to match the distribution of the demonstrator (a form of
cloning
), as we have seen in
Proposition

2
. Indeed, the approach of
Foster et al. 2024
ensures high reward (in more general settings) by the virtue of cloning the demonstrator under the low-cardinality Policy Class Assumption.

Our work shows that matching the distribution of the demonstrator is not necessary if our goal is reward maximization, and that it may be more effective to focus directly on the reward by rethinking how we learn from demonstrations. Specifically, we show how to ensure good reward under our proposed low-cardinality Model Class Assumption. In fact, it is easy to construct an example of a trivial model class, so reward maximization is straightforward, but for which cloning the demonstrator is impossible.

Observation 1

(Informal, formalized in
Proposition

3
)
.

There exists a simple class
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
with
|{\mathcal{S}}|=1
so reward maximization is possible without any samples, but matching the distribution (under
TV, Hellinger, KL, or reverse KL) is impossible.

We further argue that this departure from cloning also entails looking beyond maximum likelihood estimation (i.e., log-loss minimization). The likelihood maximization is inherently associated with, and typically analyzed through, cloning. Indeed, in
Section

3
we showed that, in our setting, maximum likelihood estimation fails at reward maximization—and therefore also at cloning—necessitating new approaches that do not rely on cloning.

Question-Answering with Language Models.

For question-answering or prompt-completion with LLMs, the emphasis on reward maximization translates to a focus on producing good answers (i.e., correct or high-utility ones). We do not view matching the distribution of answers as a goal. This aligns with how LLM-based AI agents are used and evaluated on benchmarks
(
OpenAI 2023
;
DeepSeekAI 2025
;
Anthropic 2024
;
DeepMind 2023
)
. Reward maximization is also the view taken in post-training of LLMs. Even in limited cases where the answer distribution might matter—such as when there is no clear consensus—the model should arguably produce a more comprehensive response that reflects its uncertainty
(
Kirchhof et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, rather than attempt to match the distribution of some specific training “population”.

Our reward-based view also matches that of
Kleinberg and Mullainathan 2024
, who also view the goal of language learning as generating correct responses based on correct demonstrations. In fact,
Kleinberg and Mullainathan 2024
’s setup is a special case of our online process in
Figure

1
, except that all prompts
x_{t}
are empty.
6
6

6

To capture
Kleinberg and Mullainathan 2024
’s unprompted setting, we would have a singleton context space
{\mathcal{X}}=\{\epsilon\}
(i.e., a multi-armed, non-contextual bandit setting),
\sigma(\epsilon)
would be the language being learned, and
{\mathcal{S}}
would then represent the language family. In the context of our online process,
Kleinberg and Mullainathan 2024
’s requirement is that we make only a finite number of mistakes.
In our case, the problem would become trivial without a prompt, as we could always output a good response we already observed.
Kleinberg and Mullainathan 2024
avoid this degeneracy by requiring outputting an infinite number of distinct outputs. However, outputting distinct outputs is still very different from distribution matching, as
Kleinberg and Mullainathan 2024
’s generator is still allowed to output from a vanishingly small fraction of the demonstrator distribution. We argue for a purer focus on utility, requiring only correctness, and not infinite generation: if we are asked a million times ‘‘what is a prime number’’ (or a Google top question ‘‘how many ounces in a cup’’), it is perfectly fine to give the same (correct) answer repeatedly. Instead of infinite generation, we emphasize generalization to different prompts.
7
7

7

Kleinberg and Mullainathan 2024
also studied a prompted version of their main setting as an extension, but this differs more significantly from ours. Their demonstrator provides demonstrations unrelated to the prompt, whereas we require it. Even in their prompted setup, to obtain generation in the limit from “standard” demonstrations, they still emphasize infiniteness, requiring each prompt to have infinitely many completions, whereas we do not. A further distinction is that our focus lies in obtaining finite-sample, PAC-style learning guarantees in the offline statistical setting, with an emphasis on optimal sample complexity. In contrast, they focus only on eventually stopping to make mistakes (i.e., finite but unbounded number of mistakes) under minimal assumptions.

Having established reward maximization as the goal for LLMs, the next important question is: what methods should we adopt when learning from demonstrations available during SFT? The predominant approach is log-loss minimization (i.e., Maximum Likelihood), which as discussed earlier, succeeds through distribution matching under the small Policy Class Assumption. Since supervised fine-tuned models are not always satisfactory
(
Ji et al. 2023
;
OpenAI 2023
, e.g.,)
, it may be wise to question this assumption and approach. Perhaps in these SFT problems, distribution matching is impossible (with the policy class used and the amount of data), but a direct reward-based approach might be possible and successful.

Our work shows that cloning is not necessary, and might not be the right approach to reward maximization.

Kalai and Vempala 2024
and
Kalai et al. 2025
go even further and argue that distribution matching and reward maximization (i.e., accuracy) might even be in tension with each other, and that insisting on distribution matching could hurt the reward. As discussed, even in situations where matching the distribution could be important, the model should indicate its uncertainty. Thus the focus should perhaps be more directly and even exclusively on
utility
or
reward
, rather than attempting to solve the more difficult task of matching the distribution—a sentiment well-captured in a quote by Vladimir Vapnik:

“When solving a problem of interest,

do not solve a more general problem as an intermediate step.”

8
Summary and Open Issues

We study the problem of learning to answer from correct demonstrations, i.e., imitation learning in contextual bandits from an optimal demonstrator.
Departing from prior work, we assume only a low-cardinality reward Model Class Assumption. We show that natural approaches based on likelihood maximization fail under this assumption, necessitating going beyond them. We design a new learner that achieves sample complexity logarithmic in the model class size. Our results motivate looking beyond likelihood maximization and distribution matching, and developing methods based on direct assumptions (or inductive biases) about the reward model class, i.e., the notion of “correctness”, and that can succeed even when the harder problem of distribution matching is not achievable.

Relationship with Contextual Bandits, Imitation Learning and Inverse Reinforcement Learning.

The standard online contextual bandit setting
(
Langford and Zhang 2007
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
;
Agarwal et al. 2014
)
differs from our online process (
Figure

1
) in that the learner receives bandit feedback (rewards for chosen actions), whereas in our case no such feedback is observed. This distinction is crucial: in the standard setting, the learner can actively explore by choosing actions and observing the resulting rewards, enabling policy improvement through exploration–exploitation tradeoffs. In contrast, our online learner (
Algorithm

1
) is entirely passive and learns solely from demonstrations.

The absence of interactive feedback brings our setting closer to imitation learning
(
Pomerleau 1988
;
Ross et al. 2011
;
Rajaraman et al. 2020
;
Rashidinejad et al. 2021
)
or offline contextual bandits
(
Rashidinejad et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2022
)
, both of which rely on demonstrations from a high-valued expert, possibly without observing the rewards. In our case, we do not directly observe rewards, but we know that the demonstrator is optimal (so for binary rewards, we know it is always 1). Imitation learning has been studied either with a finite context space
{\mathcal{X}}
(or, more generally, a finite MDP state space), where per-context (or per-state) memorization is possible
(
Rajaraman et al. 2020
;
Rashidinejad et al. 2021
)
, or for a general MDP under the small Policy Class Assumption
(
Ross et al. 2011
;
Foster et al. 2024
)
, which in our setting leads to a guarantee as in
Proposition

1
. While in a simpler contextual bandit setup, our main distinction is the low-cardinality reward Model Class Assumption, which motivates new algorithmic ideas. It remains interesting to see how this distinction plays out for general MDPs, and what algorithms are possible.

Finally, our work is conceptually related to inverse reinforcement learning (IRL)
(
Ng et al. 2000
)
. In classical IRL
(
Abbeel and Ng 2004
;
Ratliff et al. 2006
;
Ziebart et al. 2008
)
, only demonstrations from an expert policy are available without direct access to rewards like our setting, however, it seeks to recover the underlying reward function that rationalizes the expert’s behavior (in our case,
\widehat{\sigma}
). This, however, is again a more difficult task than directly learning a good policy that minimizes the error of taking an incorrect action.

Continuous Classes.

Our analysis is for arbitrary abstract, but finite, model classes
{\mathcal{S}}
. What class
{\mathcal{S}}
should be used and provide good inductive bias? In practice, we typically use continuous parametric function classes, making it natural to ask whether our results extend to such continuous settings. Given a parameterized function class
{\mathcal{F}}=\{f_{w}:({\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbb{R}\mid w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
of real valued “score functions”, we can consider the associated support function class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{F}}=\{\sigma_{w}:x\mapsto\{y:f_{w}(x,y)\geq 0\}\mid w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. Natural choices for
{\mathcal{F}}
include linear (or kernel) classes with
f_{w}(x,y)=\langle w,\phi(x,y)\rangle
(for some specified feature map
\phi(x,y)
), low-rank bilinear scores, or even transformer models where
f_{w}(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}
is the scalar output of a transformer with weights
w
taking the sequences
x
and
y
as input. What is the sample complexity with such infinite-cardinality continuous classes? Can we characterize the sample complexity of learning
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{F}}
from correct demonstrations in terms of properties of
{\mathcal{F}}
(e.g., Pollard subgraph dimension, or other notions of dimensionality of complexity)? Or for specific parametric classes of interest? And how does learning based on a transformer policy class compare with learning based on a transformer model class?

Tractable Implementation.

Another limitation of our work is that while the sample complexity is logarithmic in the model class size, a naive implementation of our methods requires runtime and memory linear in the cardinality. This is particularly unappealing for continuous parametric classes like those described above: we might be able to get sample complexity linear in the dimensionality by discretizing the parameter space, but our method would require keeping track of each cell in the discretization and thus runtime exponential in the number of parameters. Can we obtain computationally tractable, or at least practically implementable, methods for learning from correct demonstrations for the classes described above?

Randomization and Properness.

Our main learning rule,
Algorithm

2
, returns a
stochastic
and
improper
(i.e.,
\widehat{\pi}\notin\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}
) policy. Are stochasticity and improperness necessary? The
Majority
rule of
Section

4.1
returns a deterministic policy, and although it is not proper, in Appendix
D.2
we discuss an implementation of the common intersection rule that is deterministic and proper (always returns a policy in
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}
, i.e., supported on some
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}
).
Theorem

3
then establishes learnability from correct demonstrations using a deterministic and proper learning rule, but with a suboptimal (super)linear sample complexity. Is it possible to achieve the optimal
O(\log|{\mathcal{S}}|)
sample complexity with proper or deterministic policies?

\mathbb{P}_{S}\big(\exists\text{ bad }\pi\in\Pi\text{ that survives}\big)\;\leq\;|\Pi|\,e^{-\varepsilon m}.

The proposition follows by choosing

Proof for any general
\Pi

C.2
Simple MLE Failures for Low-Cardinality
{\mathcal{S}}

We first show that there is a simple instance of a support class, where some MLE over the entire class
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}:=\bigcup_{\sigma}\Pi_{\sigma}
fails.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Fix any
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Let
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
and
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbb{N}
. Define a support class
{\mathcal{S}}=\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{01}\}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{{\mathcal{X}}}
by

\sigma_{0}(x)=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{01}(x)=\{0,1\}\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}}.

Choose the ground-truth support
\sigma_{*}=\sigma_{0}
and let the data-generating conditional be the point mass

\pi_{*}(\cdot\mid x)=\delta_{0}(\cdot)
for all
x
; thus every observed label equals
0
.
Let
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
be the class of all policies supported on
{\mathcal{S}}
(see (
2
)). Now fix a sample size
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Set

q\;:=\;\Big\lceil\frac{m}{\gamma}\Big\rceil,

and define the marginal
{\mathcal{D}}
to be the uniform distribution on
[q]=\{1,2,\dots,q\}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{D}}(\{x\})=1/q
for
x\in[q]
and
0
otherwise.

For any dataset
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}
, write
S_{\mathrm{dis}}:=\{x_{i}:i\in[m]\}
for the set of distinct unlabeled inputs in
S
(so
S_{\mathrm{dis}}\leq m
). Consider the predictor
\widehat{\pi}
defined by

\widehat{\pi}(\cdot\mid x)=\begin{cases}\delta_{0}(\cdot),&x\in S_{\mathrm{dis}},\\[2.0pt]
\delta_{1}(\cdot),&x\notin S_{\mathrm{dis}}.\end{cases}

(25)

We claim that
\widehat{\pi}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}}(S)
. Indeed, the log-likelihood is

\ell_{\log}(\pi;S)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\pi(0\mid x_{i})=\sum_{x\in S_{\mathrm{dis}}}N_{x}(S)\,\log\pi(0\mid x),

where
N_{x}(S):=|\{i:x_{i}=x\}|
. This expression depends only on the values
\pi(0\mid x)
for
x\in S_{\mathrm{dis}}
and is maximized by setting
\pi(0\mid x)=1
for every
x\in S_{\mathrm{dis}}
. For
x\notin S_{\mathrm{dis}}
the likelihood does not constrain
\pi(\cdot\mid x)
, so any choice (for tie-braking) is a valid maximizer; in particular, (
25
) yields a valid MLE in
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}}
.

We next evaluate its population loss against the support
\sigma_{*}
. Since
\sigma_{*}(x)=\{0\}
for all
x
,

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi})=\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}},\,\widehat{y}\sim\widehat{\pi}(\cdot\mid x)}\big(\widehat{y}\notin\sigma_{*}(x)\big)=\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\big(x\notin S_{\mathrm{dis}}\big)=1-\frac{|S_{\mathrm{dis}}|}{q}.

Using
|S_{\mathrm{dis}}|\leq m
and
q\geq m/\gamma
,

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi})\;\geq\;1-\frac{m}{q}\;\geq\;1-\gamma.

The bound holds deterministically for every sample
S
, hence

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}}\!\left(L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi})\geq 1-\gamma\right)=1\,.

∎

We now show that the attempt to restrict the capacity of the class via another natural choice of considering
\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}}\{\pi_{\text{unif},\sigma}\}
also does not work, when the expert demonstrations
\pi_{*}
does not necessarily follow the distribution
\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{*}}
while still showing optimal demonstrations.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
and let
s:=\lceil 1/\gamma\rceil
. Take
{\mathcal{X}}=\{x\}
and

\mathcal{Y}=\{y^{\star}\}\;\cup\;\{a_{1},\dots,a_{s-1}\}\;\cup\;\{b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\},

so
|\mathcal{Y}|=1+(s-1)+s=2s=2\lceil 1/\gamma\rceil
. Define
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
by

\sigma_{1}(x)=\{y^{\star},a_{1},\dots,a_{s-1}\}\quad(\text{size }s),\qquad\sigma_{2}(x)=\{y^{\star},b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\}\quad(\text{size }s{+}1),

so
\sigma_{1}(x)\cap\sigma_{2}(x)=\{y^{\star}\}
and they are otherwise disjoint. Let
{\mathcal{S}}=\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}
and

\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}:=\{\pi_{\text{unif},\sigma}:\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}\},\qquad\pi_{\text{unif},\sigma}(y\mid x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|\sigma(x)|},&y\in\sigma(x),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Set
{\mathcal{D}}
to be the point mass at
x
and choose the ground-truth support
\sigma_{*}=\sigma_{2}
with data-generating conditional
\pi_{*}=\delta_{y^{\star}}
(always emit
y^{\star}
). For any
m
, every dataset
S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}
equals
\{(x,y^{\star})\}^{m}
.

It is simple to see that
\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{1}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}}(S)
is the unique maximum likelihood estimator. This is because

\prod_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{1}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})=\Big(\tfrac{1}{s}\Big)^{m},\qquad\prod_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{2}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})=\Big(\tfrac{1}{s+1}\Big)^{m}.

However, with
\sigma_{*}(x)=\sigma_{2}(x)
, the estimator
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}=\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{1}}
has the error

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}})=\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{y}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{1}}(\cdot\mid x)}\big(\widehat{y}\notin\sigma_{2}(x)\big)=1-\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{1}}(y^{\star}\mid x)=1-\frac{1}{s}\;=\;1-\frac{1}{\lceil 1/\gamma\rceil}\;\geq\;1-\gamma.

All bounds are deterministic given
S
, hence

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}}\!\left(L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}})\geq 1-\gamma\right)=1\,,

for every
m

∎

C.3
Overlap of MLE

We now show that MLE over the restricted capacity class
\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}
attains a
non-trivial overlap
at the statistical limit of
Theorem

5
, though its failure to directly optimize the objective of interest (cf.
Section

3
). Note that it is trivial to achieve overlap, by just using a policy which puts mass on one good response from each support function class. However, this would produce the responses outside the support with overwhelming probability. Thus, for the purpose for overlap to be meaningful, we call it a
non-trivial
one if the output policy is only supported on one of
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}
(some notion
properness
). The MLE over the class
\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}}\{\pi_{\text{unif},\sigma}\}
will be of this form and achieves a non-trivial overlap.

Theorem 8
.

For any finite class
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
and an unknown joint realizable distribution
{\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*}
, where
\pi_{*}
is supported on some
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, for any estimator
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}}(S)
, we have the following guarantee: for any sample size
m\geq\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\log|{\mathcal{S}}|+\log(1/\delta)\right)\,,

we have

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\;\exists_{y\in\sigma_{*}(x)}\,\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}(y|x)>0\;\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,.

Proof of
Theorem

8
.

The proof is simple. First note that
\pi_{\mathrm{unif},\sigma_{*}}
has non-zero likelihood. Therefore, any policy in the set
\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}}(S)
must have non-zero likelihood. Thus, for any
\sigma
for which
\pi_{\text{unif},\sigma}\in\mathrm{MLE}_{\Pi_{\textnormal{unif},\mathcal{S}}}(S)
, we must have that
\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S)
.
Therefore, in order to establish

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\;\exists_{y\in\sigma_{*}(x)}\,\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{mle}}(y|x)>0\;\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,,

it suffices to establish

\mathbb{P}_{S}\left(\forall\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S):\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\sigma(x)\cap\sigma_{*}(x)\neq\emptyset\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,.

(26)

This is equivalent to:

\mathbb{P}_{S}\left(\forall\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S):\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\sigma(x)\cap\sigma_{*}(x)=\emptyset\right)\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,.

(27)

Consider any bad
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}(\sigma(x)\cap\sigma_{*}(x)=\emptyset)>\varepsilon
. With each draw
(x_{i},y_{i})\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})
, we have that
\sigma
becomes inconsistent with probability at least
\varepsilon
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}_{(x_{i},y_{i})\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})}(y_{i}\notin\sigma(x_{i}))>\varepsilon
.
Therefore, for any fixed
\sigma
, after sample
S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})^{m}

\mathbb{P}_{S}(\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S))\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{m}\leq e^{-\varepsilon m}.

Therefore, by a standard union bound,

\mathbb{P}_{S}\big(\exists\text{ bad }\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S)\text{ that survives}\big)\;\leq\;|{\mathcal{S}}|\,e^{-\varepsilon m}\leq|{\mathcal{S}}|\,2^{-\varepsilon m}.

The theorem follows by noting that the
|{\mathcal{S}}|\,2^{-\varepsilon m}\leq\delta
for any
m\geq\frac{\log|{\mathcal{S}}|+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}.

∎

Remark 3

(Comparison with multi-class classification)
.

Note that for multiclass classification when
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
(i.e., all
|\sigma(x)|=1
, the guarantee captured in (
27
) is enough to ensure learnability by just outputting a single predictor from
\widehat{\sigma}\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S)
(i.e., consistent / ERM). This happens because the overlap implies that that labels are the same and so no error. However, for our problem despite this overlap, it is unclear how to output a single label so that it belongs to the support of
\sigma_{*}
. What would be sufficient for our problem is the following guarantee, where the quantifier
\forall\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S)
is taken inside the randomness of test point sampling
x\sim{\mathcal{D}}
:

\mathbb{P}_{S}\left(\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\forall\sigma\in\textsc{cons}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(S):\sigma(x)\cap\sigma_{*}(x)=\emptyset\right)\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,.

However, we know that this provably requires the sample size where there is
\Omega(|{\mathcal{S}}|)
dependence on cardinality—see the lower bound for
Common-Intersection
estimator (
Theorem

11
).

It may be possible to turn this into a predictor that directly starts to produce good responses, depending on the overlap among hypotheses and other types of feedback available in post-training (e.g., whether a generated response is good or not). This overlap can be captured by a parameter that reflects the need for repeated sampling and the number of feedback that must be queried, which in turn allows for a more quantitative understanding of how many feedbacks are required to guarantee performance in terms of this parameter. For example, this parameter would be maximum in the case of multi-class classification (
Remark

3
) and no additional feedback is required. However, we leave it open to formulate an interesting setup that enables a study of both types of feedbacks together for our problem, and we do not attempt to investigate this any further.

The model class

consists of set-valued functions

Analysis.

We now start by analyzing the

Theorem 9

(Online Guarantee for

.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{S}\left(V_{K+1}\neq\emptyset\cap\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}(A_{K+1})>\varepsilon\right)

E.1
Regret Analysis of Online Learner with Softer Update

Our goal is to bound the
regret
against the best hypothesis in hindsight in a certain sense. Let’s define two types of mistakes over
T
rounds for any given hypothesis
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}
:

•

Algorithm’s Mistakes relative to
\sigma
:
This is the number of times our algorithm’s prediction
\widehat{y}_{t}
was not in
\sigma
’s valid set absolutely, irrespective of what
y_{t}
was.

M_{T}^{\text{alg}}(\sigma):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[\widehat{y}_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})]

•

Hypothesis
\sigma
’s Mistakes:
This is the number of times the true label
y_{t}
was not in
\sigma
’s valid set.

M_{T}(\sigma):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[y_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})]

The total number of mistakes made by our algorithm with respect to
\sigma
is
M_{T}^{\text{alg}}(\sigma)
, and the number of mistakes made by
\sigma
itself is
M_{T}(\sigma)
. Note that in some rounds, both types of mistake could occur. We now analyze the algorithm with the first simple lemma that the total weight of the hypotheses in the system is again non-increasing under a minor condition on
(\alpha,\beta)
.

Lemma 4

(Non-increasing Total Weight)
.

In
Algorithm

1
, as long as
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
satisfies
\alpha\leq 2-\beta
and
\alpha\beta\leq 1
, the total weight
\{W_{t}\}_{t}
in the system is non-increasing.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Let

W_{t}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}w^{(t)}(\sigma)

be the total weight at the start of round
t
. Define the following disjoint sets of hypotheses:

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\mid y_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t}),\;\widehat{y}_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t})\},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\mid y_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t}),\;\widehat{y}_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})\},

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\mid y_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t}),\;\widehat{y}_{t}\in\sigma(x_{t})\},

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\mid y_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t}),\;\widehat{y}_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})\}.

By the update rule

w^{(t+1)}(\sigma)=w^{(t)}(\sigma)\,\alpha^{\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{y}_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})\}}\,\beta^{\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}\notin\sigma(x_{t})\}},

we have:

w^{(t+1)}(\sigma)=w^{(t)}(\sigma)\begin{cases}1,&\sigma\in S_{1},\\
\alpha,&\sigma\in S_{2},\\
\beta,&\sigma\in S_{3},\\
\alpha\beta,&\sigma\in S_{4}.\end{cases}

Therefore:

W_{t+1}=W_{t}(S_{1})+\alpha W_{t}(S_{2})+\beta\,W_{t}(S_{3})+\alpha\beta\,W_{t}(S_{4})\,.

The change in total weight is:

W_{t}-W_{t+1}=(1-\alpha)W_{t}(S_{2})+\left(1-\beta\right)W_{t}(S_{3})+\left(1-\alpha\beta\right)W_{t}(S_{4}).

By the prediction rule since
\widehat{y}_{t}
is the weight-maximizer, we have

W_{t}(S_{2})\leq W_{t}(S_{3}).

Thus:

W_{t}-W_{t+1}\geq(1-\alpha)W_{t}(S_{2})+\left(1-\beta\right)W_{t}(S_{2})+\left(1-\alpha\beta\right)W_{t}(S_{4}).

A sufficient condition for
W_{t+1}\leq W_{t}
is:

1-\alpha+1-\beta\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad 1-\alpha\beta\geq 0,

which simplifies to:

\alpha\leq 2-\beta\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha\beta\leq 1.

∎

Final Regret Bound

We are now ready to bound a certain type of regret of the algorithm.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
be any finite model class, and let
\{w^{(t)}(\sigma)\}
be the weights generated by
Algorithm

1

satisfying
\alpha\leq 2-\beta
and

Proof.

Let
W_{t}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}w^{(t)}(\sigma)
be the total weight at round
t
. By the update rule of
Algorithm

1
, we have for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}
:

Since
\alpha,\beta\geq 0

and
\alpha\beta\leq 1

4
ensures that

is non-increasing, hence:

i
labels greedily as:

a_{i}\coloneqq w^{(t)}\!\Big(A_{y_{t}}\setminus\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{i}}A_{z}\Big)\quad\text{so that}\quad a_{0}=w^{(t)}(A_{y_{t}}),\;\;a_{k}=w^{(t)}(A_{y_{t}}\setminus U),

and
a_{0}\geq a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{k}
.
Correspondingly, define

s_{i}\coloneqq a_{i-1}-a_{i}\;=\;w^{(t)}\!\Big(\big(A_{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}}\cap A_{y_{t}}\big)\setminus\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}}A_{z}\Big).

In addition, we define the uncovered weight for which
\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}
got picked as

m_{i}\coloneqq w^{(t)}\!\Big(A_{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}}\setminus\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}}A_{z}\Big).

By the design of the greedy selections, we have

m_{i}\;\geq\;a_{i-1}\qquad\text{for all }i\in[k].

(30)

With these definitions and relations in place, we are ready to prove the desired inequality.
Basic set inclusion / exclusion tells us that

\displaystyle w^{(t)}(U\setminus A_{y_{t}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w^{(t)}(A_{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}}\setminus A_{y_{t}}\cup A_{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(1)}}\dots\cup A_{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i-1)}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\{w^{(t)}(A_{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}}\setminus\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}}A_{z})-w^{(t)}\!\Big(\big(A_{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}}\cap A_{y_{t}}\big)\setminus\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}}A_{z}\Big)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(m_{i}-s_{i}),

where the last equality uses the definitions of
m_{i}
and
s_{i}
.
Using (
30
), we obtain

m_{i}-s_{i}\;\geq\;a_{i-1}-(a_{i-1}-a_{i})\;=\;a_{i},

which allows us to further lower bound
w^{(t)}(U\setminus A)
as

w^{(t)}(U\setminus A_{y_{t}})\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\;\geq\;k\,a_{k}.

Here we use the fact that
(a_{i})_{i}
is non-increasing. This proves the claim.

F.1
Statistical Upper Bound

Algorithm 4

Statistical Learner via Online-to-Batch Conversion for
\mathsf{pass@}k
Error

Input:
Sample
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i\in[m]\}
and a finite model class
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{{\mathcal{X}}}
.

•

Run
Algorithm

3
once over
S
, recording
w^{(t)}
at the
start
of each round
t\in[m]
.

•

Find the deterministic predictor
\widehat{\mu}_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Y}^{k}
used by the online algorithm from the snapshot. I.e., for any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and any
t\in[m]
, define slices (with respect to
V_{t}
)

A_{y}^{t}(x)\;\coloneqq\;\{\sigma\in V_{t}:\ y\in\sigma(x)\}\,.

And greedily pick top
k
labels according to the rule described in
Algorithm

3
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}_{0}(x)=\emptyset
. For
i=1,\ldots,k
set

\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}(x)\;\in\;\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}(x)}w^{(t)}\!\Big(A_{y}^{t}(x)\setminus\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}(x)}A^{t}_{z}(x)\Big)

\mathcal{Y}_{i}(x)\leftarrow\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}(x)\cup\{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}(x)\},

breaking ties by arbitrary fixed rule.

•

Then the deterministic predictor
\widehat{\mu}_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Y}^{k}
is given by:

\widehat{\mu}_{t}(x)\;\coloneqq\;(\widehat{y}_{t}^{(1)}(x),\ldots,\widehat{y}_{t}^{(k)}(x)).

•

Final batch predictor.
Output a randomized predictor policy as follows:

\widehat{\mu}_{\textnormal{o2b}}(\cdot\mid x)={\rm Unif}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(x),\dots,\widehat{\mu}_{t}(x)\right):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\widehat{\mu}_{t}(\cdot\mid x).

(31)

Equivalently, on every test context
x
, draw
I\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,m\}
and output
\widehat{\mu}_{I}(x)
.

We do the same online-to-batch conversion based on a random stopping time of the online rule in
Algorithm

3
. This is given for completeness in
Algorithm

4
. Below is the statistical guarantee for this estimator in similar spirit to
Theorem

5
.

Theorem 14

(Statistical Guarantee for
\mathsf{pass@}k
)
.

For any finite model class
{\mathcal{S}}
, the estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{o2b}}
in Eq. (
31
) achieves the following guarantee for any joint distribution
({\mathcal{D}}\times\pi_{*})
supported on
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
:

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{m}}\!\left[L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}\!\big(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{o2b}}\big)\right]\;\leq\;\frac{\log_{k+1}|{\mathcal{S}}|}{m}\,,

and, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}\!\big(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{o2b}}\big)\;\leq\;\frac{1+2\log_{k+1}|{\mathcal{S}}|+12\log\!\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right)}{m}\;.

Proof of
Theorem

14
.

The proof is exactly similar to that of
Theorem

5
and given for completeness.
Let
\ell_{t}=\mathbbm{1}\{\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}(x_{t})\notin\sigma_{*}(x_{t}),\forall\,\,\widehat{y}^{(i)}_{t}(x_{t})\in\widehat{\mu}_{t}(x_{t})\}
.
Because
\widehat{\mu}_{t}
is a deterministic function of
S_{<t}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i<t\}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}\mid S_{<t}]=L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\,.

Hence

\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{o2b}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\ell_{t}\right]\leq\frac{\log_{k+1}|{\mathcal{S}}|}{m}\,,

where in the last inequality we used
Theorem

13
, which guarantees
\sum_{t=1}^{m}\ell_{t}\leq\log_{k+1}|{\mathcal{S}}|
.

For the high-probability statement, define the martingale differences

Z_{t}:=L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})-\ell_{t},\qquad\text{where }|Z_{t}|\leq 1\text{ almost surely.}

Then
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid S_{<t}]=0
, and

\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}^{2}\mid S_{<t}]=\mathbb{E}\!\big[(L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})-\ell_{t})^{2}\mid S_{<t}\big]={\rm Var}(\ell_{t}\mid S_{<t})=L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\bigl(1-L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\bigr)\;\leq\;L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\,.

Taking
W_{m}=\sum_{t=1}^{m}L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})
and
\sigma^{2}=m
suffices; thus, using
Lemma

3
with
n=\log m
gives, with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{m}Z_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{8\left(1+\sum_{t=1}^{m}L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\right)\log\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{4}{3}\log\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sum_{t=1}^{m}L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\right)+4\log\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right)+\frac{4}{3}\log\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right)

(GM
\,\leq
AM)

Substituting
Z_{t}
and rearranging terms,

\sum_{t=1}^{m}L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})\;\leq\;1+2\sum_{t=1}^{m}\ell_{t}+\frac{32}{3}\log\!\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right).

Finally, noting that
L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{o2b}})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{t})
and that
\sum_{t=1}^{m}\ell_{t}\leq\log_{k+1}|{\mathcal{S}}|
(by
Theorem

13
), we obtain that with probability
1-\delta
,

L_{{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{*}}(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{o2b}})\leq\frac{1+2\log_{k+1}|{\mathcal{S}}|+12\log\!\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right)}{m}.\qed

F.2
Lower Bounds for Online and Statistical Settings for
k
-pass Error

We next provide a lower bound that, information-theoretically, this dependence cannot be improved and we only gain a factor of
1/\log k
in sample complexity as well as mistake bound in the worst-case.

Theorem 15

(Online
\Omega(\log_{k+1}|{\mathcal{S}}|)

\mathsf{pass@}k
mistake bound even for multiclass classification)
.

Fix integers
k\geq 1
and
d\geq 2
. There exists a problem instance
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
with
|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq d,|\mathcal{Y}|=k+1,|{\mathcal{X}}|=\lfloor\log_{k+1}d\rfloor
such that for any deterministic online learning algorithm that outputs at most
k
labels, there exists a sequence
(x_{t},y_{t})_{t\in[|{\mathcal{X}}|]}
realizable by some
\sigma_{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that it makes mistake on every round.

Note that our instance is an instance of multiclass classification problem
\sigma:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Y}
. This is isomorphic to an instance
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
, where
|\sigma(x)|=1
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}},\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof of
Theorem

15
.

Let
m:=\lfloor\log_{k+1}d\rfloor
and take
{\mathcal{X}}=\{1,\dots,m\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,k{+}1\}
.
Consider the full product class
{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{Y}^{{\mathcal{X}}}
, which has size
(k{+}1)^{m}\leq d
. First of all, observe that in any round in which
y_{t}
does not belong to the list of
(\widehat{y}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{y}_{t}^{(k)})
, the mistake is made because we are in the multiclass classification setting.

For rounds
t\in[m]
, present a fresh coordinate
x_{t}=t
.
Since
|\mathcal{Y}|=k{+}1
, there exists a label
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
that the learner failed to output in the set;
y_{t}\neq\widehat{y}_{t}^{(i)}
for all
i\in[k]
. Reveal this
y_{t}
.
This forces a mistake on every round. Moreover, this sequence is realizable since
{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
contains all functions from
{\mathcal{X}}
to
\mathcal{Y}
.
∎

Theorem 16

(Statistical lower bound of
\Omega(\log_{k}|{\mathcal{S}}|)
)
.

Fix integers
k\geq 1
and
q\geq 1
. Let
{\mathcal{X}}=\{1,\dots,q\}
,
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,2k\}
, and take the hypothesis class

{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{Y}^{{\mathcal{X}}}
(all multiclass functions), so its cardinality is
d:=|{\mathcal{S}}|=(2k)^{q}
.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be the uniform distribution on
{\mathcal{X}}
.
Then for any estimators
\widehat{\mu}:({\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{Y})^{*}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{k})^{\mathcal{X}}

\inf_{\widehat{\mu}}\,\sup_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}}\,\ \mathbb{E}_{S\sim({\mathcal{D}}\times\sigma)^{m}}\,\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\sim\widehat{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[\,\sigma(x)\notin\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\,\big]\;\;\geq\;\;\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{q}\Big)^{m}.

In particular, to ensure expected error at most
0<\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}
for all
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}
, one needs

m\;\geq\;\frac{\ln(1/(2\varepsilon))}{-\ln(1-1/q)}\;\;\geq\;\;q\,\ln\!\Big(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big).

Proof.

Fix any (possibly randomized) estimator
\widehat{\mu}
.
Let
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be the training sample drawn i.i.d. from
({\mathcal{D}}\times\sigma)
for
\sigma\sim{\rm Unif}({\mathcal{S}})
, and let

U_{S}=\{x_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
be the set of distinct inputs seen in
S
.
Draw
x\sim{\mathcal{D}}
independently of
S
and then
\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\sim\widehat{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)
.

On any
x\notin U_{S}
, under the prior where
\sigma
is uniform over
{\mathcal{S}}
, for any (possibly randomized)
k
-list
\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\sim\widehat{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\big[\sigma(x)\in\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\mid S,\,x\notin U_{S}\big]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\#\text{ of distinct labels in }\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\ \middle|\ S,\,x\notin U_{S}\right]\leq\frac{k}{2k}=\frac{1}{2},

so
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}[\sigma(x)\notin\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\mid S,\,x\notin U_{S}]\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
(Allowing duplicates in
\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)
cannot decrease this probability.)

If
x\in U_{S}
, the learner can always include the observed label and incur zero error on that
x
.
Therefore, for any estimator
\widehat{\mu}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,x,\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\sim\widehat{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\sigma(x)\notin\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\mid S\big]\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}[x\notin U_{S}].

Taking expectation over
S
and using
{\mathcal{D}}=\mathrm{Unif}({\mathcal{X}})
yields

\mathbb{E}_{S}\,\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,x,\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\sim\widehat{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\sigma(x)\notin\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\big]\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{S}\big[1-|U_{S}|/q\big]\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{q}\Big)^{m},

since
\mathbb{E}[|U_{S}|]=q\big(1-(1-\tfrac{1}{q})^{m}\big)
.
Finally, by minimax principle

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mu}}\ \sup_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}}\ \mathbb{E}_{S}\,\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\sim\widehat{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[\,\sigma(x)\notin\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\,\big]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\widehat{\mu}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim{\rm Unif}({\mathcal{S}})}\ \mathbb{E}_{S}\,\mathbb{P}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\sim\widehat{\mu}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[\,\sigma(x)\notin\widehat{{\bm{y}}}(x)\,\big]

\displaystyle\geq\;\;\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{q}\Big)^{m}.

For the sample-complexity bound, solve
\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\tfrac{1}{q})^{m}\leq\varepsilon
for
m
and use
-\ln(1-1/q)\leq 1/q
.
∎

Because
d=(2k)^{q}
, we have
q=\log_{2k}d
, so the bound implies
m=\Omega\!\big(\log_{k}d\big)
under
{\mathcal{D}}=\mathrm{Unif}({\mathcal{X}})
.

Remark 5
.

Both online (
Theorem

15
) and statistical lower bounds (
Theorem

15
) for
\mathsf{pass@}k
essentially demonstrate that one cannot do better than memorization below
\Omega(\log_{k}d)
barrier in the worst-case, even for the special case of the problem of multiclass classification
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
which is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{\mathcal{X}}
with
|\sigma(x)|=1
.
