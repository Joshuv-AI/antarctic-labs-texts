Optimization of immune priming interval using Imuno-FS Plus enhances the efficacy of oral formalin-killed Vibrio parahaemolyticus vaccine in Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei)
Authors: Annisa Widya Ananda, Sri Nuryati, Dinamella Wahjuningrum, Munti Yuhana, Ardana Kurniaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (AHPND), caused by toxigenic Vibrio parahaemolyticus, remains a major constraint in Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) aquaculture. This study evaluated whether the interval between adjuvant-induced immune priming and oral vaccination could be optimized to enhance the efficacy of a formalin-killed V. parahaemolyticus vaccine. Shrimp were fed a diet supplemented with Imuno-FS Plus powder (0.75 g kg⁻¹ feed) for seven days to prime the immune system, followed by an interval of 0, 2, 4, or 6 days before oral vaccination (10⁸ CFU shrimp⁻¹) for four days, and were subsequently challenged with virulent V. parahaemolyticus. Among the tested schemes, a four-day interval elicited the strongest immune activation, significantly increasing total hemocyte count, phenoloxidase activity, respiratory burst, and phagocytic activity (P < 0.05), and upregulating expression of the immune-related genes down syndrome cell adhesion molecule (DSCAM), prophenoloxidase (ProPO), Litopenaeus vannamei big penaedin (LvBigPEN), peroxinectin, (PE) and fibrinogen-related protein (FREP). Hepatopancreatic bacterial colonization declined to undetectable levels by day 7 post-challenge in shrimp given the four- and six-day intervals, both of which achieved survival statistically comparable to unchallenged controls, as confirmed by generalized linear modeling of the raw survival data. Relative percent survival was highest in the four-day interval group (72.9%), accompanied by reduced histopathological damage. These findings indicate that synchronizing the timing of immune priming and vaccination — rather than simply combining the two components — is a key determinant of oral vaccine efficacy against AHPND in Pacific white shrimp.Keywords: AHPND, Oral vaccination, Imuno-FS Plus powder, Shrimp, Immune priming

Introduction

Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) is the most commercially important crustacean species in global aquaculture, dominating farmed shrimp production worldwide [1]. Global farmed shrimp production has expanded significantly over the past two decades, now exceeding 5 million tons annually, with L. vannamei contributing the largest share of total output and supporting a multibillion-dollar seafood industry [2,3]. Indonesia ranks among the world's leading shrimp-producing nations, and the intensification of shrimp farming systems has driven a progressive increase in productivity in recent years [4]. However, increasing production intensity has also heightened the vulnerability of shrimp to infectious diseases [5]. Acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (AHPND), caused by toxigenic V. parahaemolyticus strains harboring the pirA and pirB toxin genes, remains one of the most devastating diseases affecting shrimp aquaculture globally [6,7]. Infection with AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus induces rapid degeneration of hepatopancreatic epithelial cells, leading to early mortality and substantial economic losses in intensive culture systems [8,9]. According to the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), AHPND continues to be reported in major shrimp-producing countries, emphasizing the urgent need for sustainable and preventive disease control strategies [10].

The extensive use of antibiotics for disease control in shrimp aquaculture has raised serious concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance, environmental contamination, and restrictions in international seafood markets [11,12]. These limitations have stimulated growing interest in alternative strategies that enhance host immune capacity rather than directly targeting pathogens [13]. In crustaceans, which rely entirely on innate immunity due to the absence of a vertebrate-like adaptive immune system, prior exposure to immunostimulatory agents can induce a heightened state of innate immune readiness, a phenomenon referred to as immune priming, which has been associated with improved resistance to subsequent pathogen challenge in several invertebrate species, including shrimp [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]].

In shrimp, immune priming can be induced through exposure to immunostimulatory compounds that activate innate immune mechanisms and establish a heightened state of immune readiness [19,20]. Such pre-activation may enhance the host response to subsequent antigen exposure, resulting in stronger and more effective protection against pathogen challenge [21]. The magnitude of this enhancement, however, is expected to depend on the timing between priming and antigen exposure, since innate immune parameters in shrimp typically rise within the first few days after stimulation and decline gradually thereafter in the absence of a further stimulus [19]. This time-dependence provides part of the rationale for evaluating a range of priming-to-vaccination intervals in the present study. In the present study, administration of Imuno-FS Plus before vaccination was intended to induce this immune-primed state; therefore, the interval between adjuvant administration and vaccination was considered an immune priming period.

Among the proposed alternatives to antibiotics, dietary immunomodulation through functional feed additives has received increasing attention in shrimp aquaculture [22]. Phytobiotics, synbiotics, and other feed-based interventions have been reported to enhance immune responses, improve intestinal health, and increase the resistance of L. vannamei to bacterial pathogens [23,24]. Several studies have also demonstrated improved survival and enhanced immune-related responses in shrimp following infection with V. parahaemolyticus, the causative agent of AHPND, when supplemented with dietary immunostimulants or functional feed additives [25,26]. Imuno-FS Plus, the adjuvant evaluated in this study, is formulated from banana (Musa spp.) stem, a low-cost agricultural by-product containing phenolic compounds, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and alkaloids, several of which have been reported to exert immunomodulatory and antimicrobial activity in aquatic species [27]. Qualitative and quantitative phytochemical screening of the banana stem powder used in this study confirmed the presence of these compound classes (see Methods), supporting its rationale as a plant-based immune priming agent. These findings highlight the potential of such compounds not only as growth promoters but also as preventive tools against bacterial diseases in intensive shrimp culture systems [28].

Oral vaccination offers practical advantages for large-scale application in shrimp aquaculture; however, its efficacy is often limited by antigen degradation in the gastrointestinal tract and insufficient immune activation [29,30]. Inactivated whole-cell vaccines based on formalin-killed bacteria have been explored as a cost-effective and accessible approach for stimulating innate immune responses in shrimp [31,32]. The use of natural immunostimulants as vaccine adjuvants has therefore been explored to enhance vaccine-induced immune responses. Devaraja et al. [33] demonstrated that oral administration of Vibrio bacterin combined with yeast β-glucan enhanced immune activity and bactericidal responses in shrimp, highlighting the potential of natural compounds as adjuvants for disease prevention. In addition, β-glucan has been reported to serve as an effective adjuvant in fish vaccination, with timing of administration shown to be critical for maximizing immunological outcomes [34,35]. Ray et al. [36] further demonstrated that orally administered Vibrio bacterin induced immune stimulation and improved survival in pond-cultured tiger shrimp. However, although oral vaccines and natural immunostimulants have each been studied extensively in shrimp, to our knowledge these two strategies have not yet been evaluated together in two respects that this study specifically addresses. First, no published study appears to have systematically compared multiple discrete intervals between adjuvant-induced immune priming and oral vaccination against AHPND, even though studies in other vaccination systems indicate that the timing of adjuvant administration relative to antigen exposure can meaningfully influence the resulting immune response. Second, few studies have included a simultaneous-administration treatment as an explicit comparator when evaluating combined immune priming and vaccination strategies, so it remains unclear whether any protective benefit reflects the combination of the two components per se, or is instead specifically attributable to separating them in time.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the optimal interval between administration of Imuno-FS Plus powder as a natural adjuvant and oral vaccination with formalin-killed V. parahaemolyticus in Pacific white shrimp. Immune responses, immune-related gene expression, bacterial colonization, histopathological changes, and survival following AHPND challenge were evaluated across a range of priming-to-vaccination intervals, together with a simultaneous-administration group, to identify a strategy that balances disease protection with production performance in shrimp aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Preparation of natural immunostimulant (Imuno-FS Plus)

Imuno-FS Plus was prepared from banana (Musa spp.) stem material collected from IPB University, Dramaga Village, Bogor Regency, West Java, Indonesia. The raw material was sliced into approximately 2 cm thick pieces and air-dried under shaded conditions until the moisture content was substantially reduced. The partially dried material was then further dried in an oven at 50–60 °C for 2 h to remove the remaining moisture completely. After drying, the material was ground using a blender and subsequently sieved to obtain a fine powder suitable for experimental use [27].

The phytochemical composition of the resulting Imuno-FS Plus powder was characterized at the Testing Laboratory of the Center for Standard Testing Instruments for Spice, Medicinal, and Aromatic Plants (BPSI TROA), Ministry of Agriculture, Bogor, West Java, Indonesia, using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. Qualitative phytochemical screening (Materia Medika Indonesia, Volume VI, 1995, Appendix 16; Certificate No. 137/T/LAB/XI/24) indicated the presence of alkaloids, saponins, tannins, phenolics, flavonoids, and glycosides, while steroids were not detected. Quantitative analysis (Certificate No. 165/T/LAB/XI/24), performed by spectrophotometry for flavonoid and tannin content and thin-layer chromatography (TLC) scanning for saponin content, showed flavonoid, tannin, and saponin concentrations of 0.085%, 1.18%, and 1.16%, respectively, in the banana stem sourced from Bogor.

Bacteria preparation

The bacterial strain used in this study was V. parahaemolyticus associated with AHPND obtained from the Aquatic Organism Health Laboratory collection, Department of Aquaculture, IPB University. The isolate was cultured on thiosulfate citrate bile salts sucrose agar (TCBSA) (Difco, USA) supplemented with rifampicin (50 µg mL⁻¹). The identity of the bacterium was confirmed by PCR detection of the pirA and pirB toxin genes using primers described by [37]. The primer sequences were as follows: pirA forward 5′-TGA-CTA-TTC-TCA-CGA-TTG-GAC-TG-3′ and reverse 5′-CAC-GAC-TAG-CGC—CAT-TGT-TA-3′; pirB forward 5′-TGA-TGA-AGT-GAT-GGG-TGT-TC-3′ and reverse 5′-TGT-AAG-CGC—CGT-TTA-ACT-CA-3′.

For bacterial propagation, a single colony was inoculated into 10 mL of seawater complete (SWC) broth and incubated for 24 h at 28 °C. The culture was then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The bacterial suspension was subsequently adjusted to 10⁶ CFU mL⁻¹ based on the previously determined LD₅₀ value using the [38] method used for the challenge test.

Vaccine preparation

The inactivated V. parahaemolyticus vaccine was prepared following [39]. Bacterial stocks grown on SWC agar were inoculated into SWC broth and incubated on a shaker at room temperature for 24 h until reaching a density of approximately 10⁸ CFU mL⁻¹. The culture was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min, and the pellet was washed three times with PBS. Bacterial inactivation was performed by adding 0.1% (v/v) of 37% formalin to the bacterial suspension in PBS (pH 8.5), followed by incubation on a shaker for 24 h at room temperature. The suspension was then centrifuged to remove residual formalin, washed with PBS, and homogenized. To verify complete bacterial inactivation, 50 µL of the formalin-treated suspension was spread onto SWC agar plates and incubated at room temperature for 24 h. No bacterial growth was observed, confirming complete inactivation of V. parahaemolyticus and indicating that the vaccine preparation was free of viable bacterial cells, thereby minimizing the risk of unintended infection during administration. The vaccine suspension was adjusted to approximately 10¹⁰ CFU mL⁻¹ and coated onto feed pellets (1% v/w) using a commercial binder containing calcium lignosulfonate (0.5%w/w), resulting in a final vaccine dose of 10⁸ CFU g⁻¹ feed. The coated feed was stored at 4 °C until use.

Tank and test animal preparation

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) L. vannamei juveniles were obtained from a commercial hatchery in Serang Regency, Banten Province, Indonesia. The shrimp had an initial average weight of 4.06 ± 0.69 g and were acclimated prior to the experiment. During the treatment phase, shrimp were reared in cylindrical fiberglass tanks (1000 L capacity) filled with 600 L of seawater, with a stocking density of 150 shrimp per tank; each of the eight treatments was applied to a single tank. Shrimp were fed a commercial diet containing 34% crude protein four times daily at 07:00, 12:00, 17:00, and 22:00, with a feeding rate of 7% of body weight. Following the treatment phase, shrimp from each treatment tank were randomly distributed into four aquaria filled with 35 L of seawater with a stocking density of 20 shrimp per aquaria per treatment for the challenge and sampling phase. For immune response, histopathological, and gene expression analyses, three shrimp were randomly collected at each sampling point from one aquarium per treatment that was designated and reserved exclusively for destructive sampling, resulting in three biological samples per treatment for each parameter. Mortality was recorded daily throughout both phases, and dead shrimp were immediately removed to maintain water quality.

Water quality parameters, including temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, and total ammonia nitrogen (TAN), were monitored throughout the rearing period. Temperature and DO were measured daily using a dissolved oxygen meter (Lutron DO-5510, Taiwan), salinity was determined using an automatic temperature compensation (ATC) refractometer, and pH was measured using a pH meter (Hanna Instruments HI 98,107, Romania). Total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) was analyzed at the beginning and end of the experimental period according to the standard methods described by [40]. Water quality was maintained within suitable ranges throughout the experiment, with temperature at 28–30 °C, salinity at 20–22 ppt, DO at 5.3–6.3 mg L⁻¹, pH at 7.6–8.4, and TAN at 0.009–0.059 mg L⁻¹.

Treatment and experimental design

The experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD) consisting of eight treatments with four replicates. The treatments were as follows: NC (negative control, unvaccinated and unchallenged), PC (positive control, unvaccinated and challenged with V. parahaemolyticus), V (vaccine only, challenged), A (adjuvant only, challenged), AV (simultaneous administration of adjuvant and vaccine, challenged), A2V (adjuvant followed by vaccination after a 2-day interval, challenged), A4V (adjuvant followed by vaccination after a 4-day interval, challenged), and A6V (adjuvant followed by vaccination after a 6-day interval, challenged).

For the immune priming and vaccination phase, each treatment was maintained in a single 150-shrimp fiberglass tank, in which the corresponding adjuvant and/or vaccine administration schedule was applied. Following completion of this phase, the shrimp population from each tank was randomly divided into four replicate aquaria (20 shrimp aquarium⁻¹), which served as the experimental units for the challenge phase and for all subsequent measurements, including survival, bacterial load, immune responses, and histopathology. Although each treatment schedule was applied to a single rearing tank prior to the challenge phase, the four replicate aquaria developed independently thereafter, receiving separate pathogen inoculation, water conditions, and infection dynamics, and therefore constituted the valid experimental units for evaluating the response to challenge under the CRD framework. A single baseline sample (DBV, day before vaccination) was collected from the stock population prior to random allocation into the eight treatment groups.

The natural adjuvant powder was administered through feed coating for seven consecutive days at a dose of 0.75 g kg⁻¹ feed [27]. Oral vaccination with formalin-killed V. parahaemolyticus was administered simultaneously with adjuvant treatment or after the corresponding priming interval, at a dose of 10⁸ CFU shrimp⁻¹, following [41], for four consecutive days. Shrimp were fed four times daily (07:00, 12:00, 17:00, and 22:00) at a feeding rate of 7% of body weight. Mortality was recorded daily in each replicate aquarium, and dead shrimp were removed. Bacterial load in the hepatopancreas and immune responses were evaluated at five time points: before treatment (DBV, baseline), three days after completion of vaccination and before challenge (D3AV), and at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days post-challenge. To avoid ambiguity between D3AV and the numerically identical day-3 post-challenge time point, post-challenge sampling days are hereafter denoted as CT1, CT3, CT5, and CT7 (Challenge Test day 1, 3, 5, and 7, respectively), and this notation is used consistently across all figures, tables, and axis labels.

Sample collection

Hemolymph sampling was conducted at six time points: before treatment, before the challenge (3 days post-vaccination), and on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-challenge. The "Day Before Vaccine" (DBV) value represents a baseline for the entire shrimp stock, as all shrimp originated from a single holding tank; four shrimp were sampled for this purpose. At each sampling time, one shrimp was randomly selected from each replicate aquarium. Hemolymph was collected from the first pleopod using a sterile syringe and immediately mixed with a pre-chilled anticoagulant solution at a 1:2 ratio (hemolymph:anticoagulant) for subsequent analysis of immune parameters.

For gene expression analysis, 0.1 g of hepatopancreas tissue was collected from one shrimp per replicate (three replicates per treatment). Samples were obtained before the challenge test and at 12 h post-challenge. The tissues were preserved in GeneZol™ reagent (Geneaid, Taiwan) and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. For histopathological analysis, one intact hepatopancreas was collected from three shrimp per treatment at day 3 post-challenge. For V. parahaemolyticus abundance, 0.1 g of hepatopancreas tissue was collected from three shrimp per treatment at days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-challenge.

Histopathological analysis

Histopathological examination of the hepatopancreas was conducted on day 3 post-challenge (CT3). This time point was selected based on the reported kinetics of AHPND lesion development. Colonization by V. parahaemolyticus and release of the PirAB toxin in the hepatopancreas progress rapidly within hours of exposure, with bacterial spread reported to be most extensive at approximately 6 h post-exposure and histopathological abnormalities detectable from the earliest hours through 36 h post-exposure [43]. A PirAB toxin fraction has been shown to cause high mortality accompanied by massive sloughing of hepatopancreatic tubule epithelium within 24–48 h post-exposure [6], whereas the terminal phase, characterized by massive intratubular hemocytic infiltration and secondary bacterial infection, typically follows this acute phase [7]. Day 3 (72 h) post-challenge was therefore selected to capture the period during which acute AHPND lesions are well developed and clearly observable, while preceding the onset of the terminal phase, providing the most representative assessment of primary PirAB toxin-induced damage.

Hepatopancreas samples were aseptically excised and immediately fixed in Davidson's fixative solution for 24 h following standard crustacean histological procedures [44]. After fixation, tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4–5 µm thickness using a rotary microtome, and mounted on glass slides. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope.

Histological evaluation focused on structural alterations of hepatopancreatic tubules, including epithelial cell degeneration, sloughing of epithelial cells, hemocytic infiltration, tubular atrophy, and necrotic lesions. Lesions were interpreted based on characteristic pathological features associated with AHPND as described by [7].

Bacterial abundance of V. parahaemolyticus

V. parahaemolyticus colonization were quantified using the plate count method. V. parahaemolyticus abundance was quantified from hepatopancreas samples using HiCrome Vibrio agar (HiMedia, India) supplemented with rifampicin as a selective marker, since the hepatopancreas is the primary target organ of AHPND infection and a major site of pathogen colonization in infected shrimp [7,8]. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Characteristic colonies were counted and expressed as CFU g⁻¹ tissue to evaluate pathogen colonization dynamics throughout the challenge period.

Immune response parameter

Total hemocyte count (THC) was determined by collecting hemolymph from the base of the first pleopod using a syringe containing anticoagulant solution (1:2, hemolymph:anticoagulant). The hemolymph was then counted using a hemocytometer under a light microscope at 100 × magnification [45]. Phagocytic activity (PA) was assessed by mixing hemolymph with Staphylococcus aureus suspension (10⁷ cells mL⁻¹) followed by incubation for 20 min. The mixture was then fixed with methanol, stained with Giemsa, and observed under a microscope at 400 × magnification [46]. Phenoloxidase (PO) activity was measured by centrifuging the hemolymph to obtain hemocyte pellets, which were resuspended in cacodylate–citrate buffer. The suspension was incubated with trypsin and l-DOPA, and the optical density was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader [45]. Respiratory burst (RB) activity was analyzed by incubating hemocyte pellets with nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), followed by sequential methanol washing. The formazan precipitate was dissolved in potassium hydroxide (KOH) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and absorbance was measured at 630 nm [47].

Immune related gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from shrimp hepatopancreas tissue using GeneZol™ reagent (Geneaid, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Japan). Gene expression analysis was subsequently performed by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) using Thunderbird SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Japan).

The expression of immune-related genes including down syndrome cell adhesion molecule (DSCAM), prophenoloxidase (ProPO), Litopenaeus vannamei big penaedin (LvBigPEN), peroxinectin, (PE) and fibrinogen-related protein (FREP) was analyzed. Elongation factor 1-alpha (EF-1α) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) were used as reference genes. The primer sequences and annealing conditions were as follows Table 1. Statistical analyses

Data were tabulated using Microsoft Excel 2021 and statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Immune parameters, gene expression, bacterial abundance, and survival data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied at a 95% confidence level. Water quality parameters and histopathological observations were evaluated descriptively.

Results

Survival rate and relative percent survival

Survival rate (SR) of L. vannamei after the challenge test differed significantly among treatments (P < 0.05) (Table 2). The interval treatments (A2V, A4V, and A6V) showed higher SR values compared with the positive control and the simultaneous administration treatment (AV). The highest mean SR was observed in A4V (83.75 ± 14.93%), followed by A6V and A2V; however, these treatments were not significantly different from each other. A similar pattern was observed for relative percent survival (RPS). The highest mean RPS was recorded in A4V (72.92 ± 24.88%), followed by A6V (66.67 ± 11.76%) and A2V (56.25 ± 14.23%), and these treatments were not significantly different from each other.

Histophatological analysis

Histopathological examination of the hepatopancreas at CT3 revealed four principal lesion types among treatments, namely epithelial sloughing (S), tubular atrophy (A), necrosis (N), and melanization (M) (Fig. 1). In the negative control (NC), the hepatopancreatic tubules exhibited normal architecture with clearly identifiable E, F, R, and B cells and intact epithelial layers, and none of the four lesion types was observed. In contrast, the positive control (PC) showed the most extensive epithelial sloughing (S) among all treatments, in which epithelial cells had detached from the tubule basement membrane and accumulated within a dilated tubular lumen.

Shrimp receiving the vaccine-only treatment (V) exhibited a degree of epithelial sloughing (S) comparable to that of the positive control, indicating that vaccination alone, without a priming interval, did not prevent the acute, toxin-driven lesion typical of AHPND. The adjuvant-only (A), simultaneous adjuvant–vaccine (AV), and 2-day interval (A2V) treatments likewise showed sloughing (S), accompanied by foci of necrotic epithelial cells (N), most conspicuous in the A treatment; overall, these three treatments displayed a somewhat less extensive sloughing pattern than PC and V. In marked contrast, the 4- and 6-day interval treatments (A4V and A6V) exhibited a qualitatively different histopathological profile at CT3, in which epithelial sloughing was comparatively limited while tubular atrophy (A) and discrete melanized hemocytic foci (M) predominated (Fig. 1).

Bacterial abundance of V. parahaemolyticus

The abundance of V. parahaemolyticus in the hepatopancreas during the challenge period also showed significant differences among treatments (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). On day 1 post-challenge (CT1), the positive control exhibited the highest bacterial load (3.298 ± 0.151 log CFU g⁻¹). A progressive reduction in bacterial abundance was observed over time, particularly in the interval treatment groups. The most pronounced decrease was detected on day 5, where A4V and A6V showed values of 1.500 ± 0.021 and 1.568 ± 0.024 log CFU g⁻¹, respectively. By day 7 post-challenge (CT7), V. parahaemolyticus below the detection limit in A4V and A6V (0.000 log CFU g⁻¹), whereas the positive control still presented 2.579 ± 0.035 log CFU g⁻¹. Overall, interval administration treatments demonstrated a clearer reduction pattern in V. parahaemolyticus abundance compared to non-interval treatments.

Immune response

Immune parameters differed significantly among treatments following the challenge test (P < 0.05). An increase in immune responses was observed from day 1 post-challenge, with peak values occurring on day 3 in several treatments. Total hemocyte count (THC) increased after challenge exposure across all treatments. The highest value was recorded on day 3 post-challenge in A4V, reaching 19.13 ± 1.299 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹ (Fig. 3). This value was higher than those observed in the positive control and non-interval treatments, which showed elevated levels but did not reach the magnitude observed in interval groups. Phagocytic activity also increased after challenge and reached its maximum on day 3. The highest percentage was observed in A4V (61.65 ± 4.482%) (Fig. 4). Other interval treatments similarly exhibited higher values compared to the positive control and simultaneous administration groups. PO and RB activities demonstrated an earlier response compared to other parameters. Peak values were detected on day 1 post-challenge. In A4V, PO activity reached 0.463 ± 0.017 (Fig. 5), while RB activity reached 0.655 ± 0.007 (Fig. 6).

Immune-related gene expression was significantly higher in treated groups compared with the positive control (P < 0.05). At 12 h post-challenge, all analyzed genes remained significantly upregulated in treated shrimp. Discussion

The higher survival rate and RPS observed in the interval treatments, particularly A4V and A6V, indicate enhanced resistance of L. vannamei against V. parahaemolyticus AHPND challenge. Increased survival following bacterial challenge is commonly associated with enhanced immune defense and improved ability of shrimp to suppress pathogen proliferation [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that vaccination strategies using inactivated Vibrio bacteria can improve survival and resistance of shrimp against vibriosis [19,36]. In addition, dietary immunostimulant supplementation has been reported to enhance immune responses and protection against bacterial infection in L. vannamei [22,49]. Both the A4V and A6V treatments, incorporating a 4- and 6-day priming interval, respectively, between adjuvant administration and oral vaccination, produced the highest survival and RPS values among all treatments. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test on the survival data (Table 2) confirmed that survival did not differ significantly between A4V and A6V, and that both were statistically comparable to the unchallenged negative control, indicating that both intervals conferred protection approaching that of an unchallenged condition. RPS followed the same pattern, with A4V and A6V again not differing significantly from each other, indicating that the two priming intervals conferred a comparable overall level of protection. This finding suggests that an interval of four to six days may represent the optimal window for immune priming, allowing sufficient time for the innate immune system to reach a heightened state of readiness before antigen exposure [50]. Although RPS itself did not distinguish between the two intervals, the cellular and molecular immune parameters measured in this study, including total hemocyte count, phagocytic activity, respiratory burst, phenoloxidase activity, and expression of the immune-related genes DSCAM, FREPs, ProPO, peroxinectin, and LvBigPEN, were consistently higher in A4V than in A6V, indicating that the two intervals achieve a comparable level of protection through different intensities of immune activation, with the four-day interval eliciting the stronger and more consistent immune response. A similar pattern has been reported for adjuvant timing in fish vaccination, where the sequence and interval of immunostimulant administration relative to antigen exposure were found to be critical for maximizing immunological outcomes [35,51]. Improved disease resistance is particularly important in controlling AHPND, which is caused by pathogenic strains of V. parahaemolyticus harboring the pirA and pirB toxin genes [6,52]. Notably, the same analysis showed that survival, and correspondingly RPS, in the simultaneous-administration group (AV) did not differ significantly from the untreated, challenged positive control, whereas AV was significantly lower than A2V, A4V, and A6V. This comparatively weak protection in AV parallels its immune response, which was among the lowest across nearly all measured immune parameters, including total hemocyte count, phagocytic activity, respiratory burst, phenoloxidase activity, and expression of the immune-related genes, rather than only when compared with the interval treatments specifically. This pattern is consistent with acute immune overstimulation arising from delivering the adjuvant and vaccine simultaneously, without an intervening recovery period, unlike in the interval treatments, in which the priming and vaccination components were separated in time. Supporting this interpretation, AV exhibited more pronounced hepatopancreatic necrosis and epithelial degeneration than the interval treatments (A2V, A4V, and A6V) at CT3 (Section 3.2); however, the overall extent of epithelial sloughing in AV remained below that recorded in PC and V, indicating that AV represents an intermediate rather than the most severe histopathological phenotype among the challenged treatments. This localized necrotic pattern in AV is consistent with excessive, concentrated consumption of proPO cascade components and hemocyte metabolic exhaustion following an overly intense burst of immune activation [53]. Because the crustacean innate immune system has limited capacity to regulate such simultaneous stimulation [53], overactivation without a recovery interval may leave the animal less able to mount an effective response upon subsequent pathogen exposure, rather than reflecting a direct biochemical antagonism between the adjuvant and vaccine components. This contrast further supports the notion that sequential, rather than concurrent, delivery of adjuvant and vaccine represents a more effective immunization strategy in shrimp [20].

Histological observations at CT3 indicated that the positive control (PC) and vaccine-only (V) treatments exhibited the most extensive and clearly defined hepatopancreatic lesions among all treatments, dominated by epithelial sloughing (S), a hallmark, pathognomonic lesion of the acute phase of AHPND caused by V. parahaemolyticus [54,55]. The PirA and PirB toxins released by AHPND-causing strains bind to and disrupt hepatopancreatic tubule epithelial cells, inducing detachment and massive sloughing of F, R, and B cells into the tubule lumen during the acute phase of infection [8,9]. The adjuvant-only (A), simultaneous adjuvant–vaccine (AV), and 2-day interval (A2V) treatments displayed an intermediate pattern, combining sloughing (S) with focal tubular necrosis (N), consistent with a partially attenuated but still largely typical acute-phase presentation. In marked contrast, the 4- and 6-day interval treatments (A4V and A6V) exhibited a qualitatively different histopathological profile at CT3, in which tubular atrophy (A) and hemocyte-mediated melanization (M) predominated over epithelial sloughing. This pattern closely resembles the chronic, non-pathognomonic hepatopancreatic lesions, including melanization of tubules, granulomatous responses, and epithelial atrophy, previously described in AHPND-tolerant shrimp populations in which the classical acute-phase lesion is absent or minimal despite prior pathogen exposure [56]. Because melanization reflects activation of the prophenoloxidase (proPO) cascade and hemocyte-mediated encapsulation, one of the most rapid and effective humoral/cellular defense responses in crustaceans, producing toxic quinone intermediates and walling off affected tissue [[57], [58], [59]], its predominance in A4V and A6V suggests that the innate immune system in these groups had already reached a heightened state of readiness before the challenge test, consistent with the longer 4- to 6-day priming interval between adjuvant and vaccine administration. Consequently, the hepatopancreatic damage recorded in A4V and A6V during the challenge period may reflect a contained, immune-mediated tissue response rather than the typical, toxin-driven acute AHPND lesion. Adjuvants stimulate shrimp innate immune responses, including hemocyte activation and the prophenoloxidase system, while vaccination provides antigen exposure that promotes pathogen recognition [31,33], and the appropriately timed combination of both components in A4V and A6V appears to have shifted the tissue response away from the classical, pathognomonic AHPND presentation toward this more contained, chronic-type pattern. Improved histopathological outcomes have similarly been reported in shrimp receiving immunostimulant or probiotic treatments before V. parahaemolyticus challenge [28,[60], [61], [62]].

The reduction of V. parahaemolyticus abundance in the interval treatments, particularly A4V and A6V, indicates that the priming strategy effectively suppressed bacterial proliferation in shrimp tissues. The detection of V. parahaemolyticus below the detection limit by CT7 in these groups suggests enhanced capacity for pathogen clearance in the hepatopancreas [63,64]. Phagocytic activity of hemocytes plays a central role in bacterial clearance in crustaceans, and its pre-activation through immune priming may accelerate the elimination of invading pathogens before they can establish infection [58,65]. Similar findings have been reported in L. vannamei, where immune-stimulating treatments with probiotics and bacterin preparations reduced Vibrio load and improved disease resistance during experimental infection [32,66]. The progressive decline of bacterial counts over time in the interval groups likely reflects a coordinated immune response involving both cellular components (hemocyte phagocytosis and encapsulation) and humoral components (antimicrobial peptides and phenoloxidase-mediated bactericidal activity) [14,53]. The persistence of V. parahaemolyticus in the positive control even at CT7 is consistent with the known capacity of this pathogen to colonize and persist in the hepatopancreas in the absence of effective immune defense [67,68].

The increase in immune parameters observed in this study indicates that the adjuvant–vaccine treatments effectively stimulated the innate immune system of L. vannamei. The elevated THC in A4V at CT3 is consistent with hematopoietic activation and hemocyte proliferation in response to pathogen exposure in primed shrimp [15,19]. Previous studies have reported that immunostimulants including β-glucan, plant extracts, and probiotics can increase THC and phagocytic activity, thereby enhancing pathogen clearance in L. vannamei [23,69]. The natural adjuvant likely enhanced immune readiness by activating hemocytes and key immune pathways prior to vaccination, while the bacterin vaccine provided antigenic stimulation that facilitated pathogen recognition [34,70]. Phagocytic activity is one of the primary cellular defense mechanisms in shrimp, and its enhancement in the interval groups suggests more efficient removal of V. parahaemolyticus following challenge [71]. The earlier peak of PO and RB activities at CT1 in A4V compared with other immune parameters suggests a rapid humoral response following secondary antigen contact, characteristic of immune-primed animals [16,72]. The prophenoloxidase cascade is activated upon recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns by pattern recognition receptors, ultimately generating reactive melanin intermediates with antimicrobial properties [73]. Previous studies have demonstrated that immune-enhancing treatments and immunostimulants can increase hemocyte responses, PO activity, respiratory burst, and resistance of shrimp against Vibrio infection [74,75].

The upregulation of immune-related genes in the interval treatments, particularly A4V, demonstrates the activation of molecular immune responses following the adjuvant–vaccine protocol. The significant induction of ProPO is consistent with its central role in the melanization cascade, pathogen sequestration, and bacterial clearance in shrimp [31,76]. Peroxinectin (PE), synthesized primarily in granular and semi-granular hemocytes, is a key mediator of cell adhesion and encapsulation of invading bacteria in crustaceans, and its upregulation reflects enhanced cellular immune readiness [77]. The significant induction of LvBigPEN is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that this penaeidin antimicrobial peptide restricts V. parahaemolyticus infection through membrane disruption and bacterial DNA binding [59,78]. Upregulation of penaeidins following bacterial challenge has been reported as a hallmark of effective antimicrobial defense in L. vannamei [49,76]. The high fold-change of FREPs in the A4V treatment reflects their role as pattern recognition receptors that mediate agglutination and promotion of phagocytosis against bacterial pathogens [79,80]. FREPs in penaeid shrimp have been shown to participate in the prophenoloxidase cascade upon V. parahaemolyticus AHPND strain infection, further contributing to pathogen clearance [81,82]. DSCAM, a hypervariable protein generated through alternative splicing, functions as a pathogen-specific recognition molecule in arthropods and showed significant upregulation in the interval treatments [83,84]. DSCAM induction supports the concept of immune specificity in invertebrate innate immunity, enabling enhanced recognition of previously encountered pathogens [18,85].

The pre-challenge upregulation of all analyzed genes in the interval treatments is consistent with the concept of immune priming, indicating that the adjuvant–vaccine protocol established a state of molecular immune readiness before antigen exposure [18,86]. The upregulation of these genes both before and after challenge in A4V, compared with the simultaneous administration group (AV), supports the importance of the priming interval in optimizing gene expression responses to subsequent pathogen contact [87]. These molecular findings collectively demonstrate that interval-based priming, particularly the 4-day interval, represents an effective strategy for inducing a comprehensive and sustained innate immune response in L. vannamei against AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus [88].

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that timing the administration of Imuno-FS Plus adjuvant relative to oral vaccination can meaningfully improve the protection of Litopenaeus vannamei against Vibrio parahaemolyticus. Both the four- and six-day priming intervals (A4V and A6V) conferred survival that was statistically indistinguishable from unchallenged shrimp, while A4V additionally elicited the strongest cellular and molecular immune responses among all treatments, including greater hemocyte activity, phenoloxidase and respiratory burst activity, and higher expression of immune-related genes. Simultaneous administration of adjuvant and vaccine (AV), by contrast, provided no statistically detectable protection over untreated controls, underscoring that the timing between priming and vaccination, rather than the mere combination of the two components, is what determines protective efficacy. Together, these findings support interval-based oral vaccination as a practical and adaptable strategy for improving disease resistance in commercial shrimp aquaculture, with the specific interval chosen according to whether disease protection or growth performance is prioritized on farm.
