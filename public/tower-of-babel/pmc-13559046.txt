Betanin Promotes Wound Closure and Drives a Context‐Specific Transcriptional Repair Program in HaCaT Keratinocytes: A Multi‐Evidence AI‐Guided Prioritization Study
Authors: Sun Young Park, Youjin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKeratinocytes coordinate re‐epithelialization, barrier restoration, inflammatory control, and matrix remodeling during cutaneous repair. Although betanin has reported cytoprotective and antioxidant properties, its wound‐relevant transcriptional program in keratinocytes remains incompletely defined. This study therefore asked whether betanin engages barrier, inflammatory, and remodeling‐related keratinocyte programs under inflammatory conditions, and whether existing wound‐healing databases can account for the resulting transcriptional pattern. Scratch closure was quantified by ImageJ measurement and by an independent rule‐based automated image‐analysis pipeline with first‐order kinetic modeling; transcriptional responses in IFN‐γ/TNF‐α‐stimulated HaCaT cells were interpreted through database‐weighted evidence scoring and machine learning‐based stress testing. Betanin (20 μg/mL) significantly reduced residual wound area at 24 h (p = 0.020) and 48 h (p = 0.035) by ImageJ quantification, and automated image analysis with kinetic modeling yielded an approximately 1.5‐fold higher first‐order closure rate constant relative to the vehicle‐treated wounded control. Betanin partially restored FLG, reduced KRT14, and selectively attenuated inflammatory mediators including IL1B, ICAM1, CCL22, and CXCL8. Among remodeling‐associated genes, TGFB1, COL1A1, and VEGFA were suppressed while COL3A1 was partially restored. NFE2L2 and HMOX1 were only modestly affected, indicating a dominant anti‐inflammatory and barrier‐modulating response rather than canonical antioxidant axis activation. Database‐weighted evidence scoring stably prioritized FLG, COL3A1, COL1A1, and ICAM1, corroborated by betanin‐specific experimental responses. Three architecturally distinct machine learning models showed no evidence that database‐derived wound‐healing features predict betanin‐induced transcriptional responses, indicating that the selected prior‐knowledge annotations were insufficient to account for the observed pattern in this 15‐gene, single‐context dataset.

Introduction

Cutaneous wound repair depends on coordinated keratinocyte responses encompassing re‐epithelialization, inflammatory control, barrier restoration, and extracellular matrix remodeling [1]. Keratinocytes are central to re‐epithelialization: they must activate, migrate, proliferate, and re‐establish epidermal differentiation to restore barrier continuity after injury [1]. Under persistent inflammatory stress, however, keratinocyte homeostasis can become dysregulated. This disruption affects cytokine‐driven gene expression across barrier, inflammatory, and remodeling‐related programs, potentially compromising efficient epithelial recovery [2].

IFN‐γ and TNF‐α were selected as the inflammatory stimulus because this cytokine combination provides a well‐established model for inducing cytokine‐driven inflammatory activation in keratinocytes. Previous studies have shown that IFN‐γ and TNF‐α act cooperatively in keratinocytes to induce chemotactic factors and adhesion molecules, including IL‐8/CXCL8 and ICAM1 [3]. In HaCaT cells, IFN‐γ/TNF‐α‐based stimulation has also been used to reproduce inflammatory keratinocyte gene‐expression patterns involving chemokine and barrier‐associated responses, including CCL22 and epidermal differentiation‐related genes [2, 4]. Therefore, this model was appropriate for evaluating whether betanin modulates a defined cytokine‐driven inflammatory keratinocyte state relevant to impaired epithelial repair. Because other inflammatory stimuli activate partially distinct signaling axes, the present study did not aim to compare multiple inflammatory challenges. Instead, IFN‐γ/TNF‐α was used as the primary inflammatory challenge to characterize betanin‐responsive transcriptional modulation within a defined keratinocyte inflammatory context.

Oxidative stress is closely linked to tissue repair because reactive oxygen species (ROS) participate in normal wound signaling but can impair healing when produced excessively or persistently. Controlled ROS production contributes to host defense, inflammatory signaling, angiogenesis, and repair‐related cellular responses, whereas excessive ROS can prolong inflammation and interfere with keratinocyte and fibroblast functions required for re‐epithelialization and extracellular matrix remodeling [5, 6]. Therefore, antioxidant and redox‐modulating compounds have been investigated as potential adjuncts for supporting wound repair.

Betanin's wound‐healing relevance in keratinocytes remains incompletely defined despite its promising antioxidant and cytoprotective properties. In other experimental systems, betanin induces NRF2‐associated detoxifying and antioxidant enzymes [7]. In HaCaT keratinocytes specifically, it attenuates UV‐induced DNA fragmentation and epigenetic alterations, supporting its potential relevance to epidermal stress protection [8]. In addition, recent in vitro wound‐repair‐related work using a betanin‐based combination reported enhanced cell viability, migration, and regulation of inflammation‐ and angiogenesis‐related gene expression in fibroblast‐type cells, suggesting that betanin‐containing approaches may influence cellular processes relevant to tissue repair [9]. These observations position betanin as a candidate modulator of epidermal stress and repair‐associated responses.

Whether betanin meaningfully engages wound‐relevant keratinocyte programs under inflammatory conditions, however, has not been systematically characterized. Three specific questions remain open: whether betanin's transcriptional effects are coordinated across barrier‐associated, inflammatory, and remodeling‐related gene categories; whether its functional impact on net wound closure can be quantified and kinetically characterized in vitro; and whether its dominant effect operates through the canonical NRF2/HO‐1 antioxidant axis or through selective modulation of these programs [3, 4].

Resolving these questions requires integrating experimental evidence with prior biological knowledge, because the significance of a given gene depends not only on its experimental response magnitude but also on its biological context within interaction networks and pathway databases [10, 11]. Computational and AI‐based tools have emerged to support such integration [12, 13], yet their application to wound healing has been dominated by two model‐centric approaches: image‐ and record‐based clinical models developed from wound photographs or health records [14, 15, 16], and transcriptome‐based classifiers trained on expression data to nominate candidate genes for experimental confirmation [17, 18, 19, 20]. In both approaches, the model itself is the object of development, and the experimental or clinical data serve as its training or validation source. Recent reviews note that such studies remain constrained by limited datasets, narrow experimental contexts, and uncertain generalizability [13, 21]. Workflows that reverse this arrangement, directing computation at an experimental question rather than treating the data as training material, remain rare [21]. The present study was designed on this basis. Rather than constructing a predictive model, it examined the explanatory sufficiency of existing wound‐healing knowledge. Three sources were used: GEO transcriptomic datasets, STRING protein interaction networks, and KEGG pathway annotations. The question was whether these sources account for the betanin‐responsive transcriptional pattern observed in IFN‐γ/TNF‐α‐stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes. Here the databases rather than the model constitute the subject of evaluation.

Accordingly, two lines of enquiry were pursued in parallel: the uncharacterized engagement of betanin with wound‐relevant keratinocyte programs, addressed through the three questions outlined above, and the untested sufficiency of database‐derived wound‐healing knowledge. At the phenotypic level, a scratch‐wound assay was performed in HaCaT keratinocytes, with closure quantified both by conventional ImageJ measurement and by a predefined rule‐based automated image‐analysis pipeline that reduces operator‐dependent variability in wound area determination, followed by first‐order kinetic modeling. At the transcriptional level, reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) was performed in I + T‐stimulated cells, and the resulting expression changes were interpreted through a two‐stage computational prioritization framework combining weighted evidence scoring, sensitivity analysis, and machine learning‐based stress testing. Through this design, the study aimed to advance betanin from a phytochemical with reported antioxidant properties toward a defined modulator of keratinocyte transcriptional responses in inflammatory wound repair, and to illustrate a workflow in which computation delimits what prior knowledge can explain and thereby complements rather than replaces experimental discovery.

Methods

Cell Culture and Cell Viability

HaCaT cells were obtained from Cell Lines Service (Eppelheim, Germany) and maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, ON, Canada) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37°C in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2. Betanin (ChemFaces, Wuhan, China) and vehicle solutions were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the final DMSO concentration was maintained at 0.1% (v/v) in all treatment groups. For inflammatory stimulation, cells were treated with I + T (5 ng/mL each) to establish an in vitro inflammatory keratinocyte model. The betanin treatment group received simultaneous I + T stimulation and betanin treatment at 20 μg/mL.

HaCaT cells were treated with betanin at 1–40 μg/mL in the presence of IFN‐γ/TNF‐α (5 ng/mL each), and cell viability was assessed by MTT assay. After treatment, MTT reagent was added according to the manufacturer's protocol, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed relative to the I + T‐stimulated group. Based on the viability profile and the absence of overt cytotoxicity, 20 μg/mL betanin was selected for subsequent scratch‐wound and RT‐qPCR experiments. Based on the viability profile shown in Figure S1 and the absence of overt cytotoxicity, 20 μg/mL betanin was selected for subsequent scratch‐wound and RT‐qPCR experiments.

Scratch Wound Healing Assay

HaCaT cells were grown to confluence, and a linear scratch was created using a sterile pipette tip. After washing with phosphate‐buffered saline to remove detached cells and debris, the cells were treated with betanin (20 μg/mL) and incubated for up to 48 h. Vehicle‐treated wounded cells containing 0.1% DMSO served as the wounded vehicle control (NC), whereas non‐wounded cells served as the control (CON). Bright‐field images of the wound area were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h using a microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2R; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For the ImageJ‐based analysis shown in Figure 1, the wound area was quantified using ImageJ software [10], normalized to the wound area at 0 h, and expressed as relative remaining wound area. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3).

To enhance the objectivity and reproducibility of wound area quantification, images were additionally processed using an automated computational image‐analysis pipeline as described in Section 2.3. Because media replacement was required between timepoints, each automated‐analysis image was acquired from an independent cell culture experiment rather than by longitudinal tracking of the same scratch (total images: 31; betanin: n = 3, 3, and 3 at 0, 24, and 48 h; NC: n = 3, 3, and 4 at 0, 24, and 48 h).

Automated Wound Area Quantification

In parallel with ImageJ‐based quantification, wound area was additionally quantified from phase‐contrast images using a predefined rule‐based Python pipeline implemented with scikit‐image [22] and SciPy [23], thereby reducing operator‐dependent boundary tracing and enabling reproducible quantification across all automated‐analysis images.

The pipeline comprised four sequential stages. First, image contrast was enhanced by Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE; clip_limit = 0.03) [24] to amplify the intensity difference between cell‐covered regions and the cell‐free wound zone. Second, a local standard deviation map was computed at each pixel using a square neighborhood of 60 pixels, producing a texture map in which the smooth cell‐free wound zone yields near‐zero values while cell‐covered regions yield high values due to nuclear texture. Third, column‐profile analysis was performed on the texture map, restricted to the central 60% of the image width, to identify the contiguous group of low‐texture columns corresponding to the wound zone. A column was classified as belonging to the wound zone if its mean texture value fell below the 35th percentile of the central band. Detection validity was then confirmed using the wound‐column texture ratio, defined as mean wound‐column texture divided by mean image texture. This ratio was required to fall below a group‐ and timepoint‐specific threshold: 0.85 for betanin and NC groups at 0 and 24 h, and 0.96 for the NC group at 48 h. These thresholds were calibrated empirically to account for the progressive reduction in wound–cell texture contrast as wounds close. Although threshold parameters were empirically calibrated to accommodate timepoint‐dependent contrast loss, all parameters were fixed prior to statistical comparison and applied consistently within each predefined group/timepoint category. Fourth, within the detected wound zone (expanded by ±8% image width margin to capture the full jagged boundary), Otsu thresholding [25] was applied to a fine‐scale texture map (window = 30 pixels) to delineate the precise wound boundary at pixel level. Adaptive morphological refinement was applied using binary opening and closing operations with timepoint‐scaled disk elements (0 h: open = 3 px, close = 5 px; 24 h: open = 2 px, close = 3 px; 48 h: open = 1 px, close = 2 px) to preserve small residual wound areas at later timepoints without over‐smoothing. All connected components within the wound zone exceeding 0.1% of image area were summed to capture fragmented wound regions at 48 h. CON images were assigned a wound area fraction of 0 by design. Betanin‐treated images at 48 h showed complete closure on visual inspection of all replicates and were assigned a wound area fraction of 0 without segmentation; the corresponding threshold entry was set to 0 to flag this condition.

Model parameters were estimated by non‐linear least squares (scipy.optimize.curve_fit; initial estimates: WCR_max = 90%, k = 0.05 h−1; bounds: WCR_max ∈ [0, 100], k ∈ [0, 2]). Goodness‐of‐fit was assessed by the coefficient of determination (R 2) and root mean square error (RMSE). As the model was fitted to three group‐mean timepoints with two free parameters (degrees of freedom = 1), these indices are reported as approximate descriptors of fit quality rather than as validated model performance metrics. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for k were estimated by resampling individual WCR values within each timepoint independently (n = 1000 iterations, seed = 42) and refitting the kinetic model to each bootstrap sample. The between‐group difference in rate constants was evaluated as Δk = k_betanin − k_NC by pairing bootstrap resamples from the two groups, and a two‐sided empirical p value was calculated with the add‐one correction to avoid zero‐valued estimates [26]; at n = 1000 resamples the smallest attainable value is 0.002.

Statistical Analysis of Wound Healing Data

For ImageJ‐based wound‐area quantification (Figure 1), group comparisons were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test as indicated in the figure legend. For automated image‐analysis data, group comparisons between betanin‐treated and NC cells at 24 and 48 h were performed using two‐sided permutation tests (n = 9999 permutations, seed = 42), which make no distributional assumptions and are appropriate for pilot‐scale image datasets [27]. Effect size was quantified by Cohen's d (pooled standard deviation); d ≥ 0.8 was considered a large effect [28]. For both metrics, d was oriented so that positive values indicate greater closure in the betanin group. Given n = 3–4 independent images per group per timepoint, statistical power is limited and Cohen's d is reported as the primary effect size metric for the automated analysis. p values were computed as ([number of permutations with |Δ| ≥ |observed Δ|] + 1)/(n_perm + 1), applying a + 1 correction to avoid p = 0 [26].

RT‐qPCR Analysis

Total RNA from HaCaT cells was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by phase separation with chloroform (Biosesang, Seoul, Republic of Korea). RNA was precipitated using isopropanol (Biosesang), and the RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol prepared from diethylpyrocarbonate‐treated water and ethanol (Biosesang). RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Nano‐400A, Allsheng). Complementary DNA was synthesized from the total RNA using AccuPower RT PreMix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The RT‐qPCR gene panel was selected to represent four wound‐relevant transcriptional modules: keratinocyte barrier/differentiation status (FLG and KRT14), inflammatory cytokine/chemokine and adhesion responses (IL1B, IL6, ICAM1, CCL2, CCL22, and CXCL8), oxidative stress response (NFE2L2 and HMOX1), and remodeling/angiogenesis‐associated signaling (MMP9, TGFB1, COL1A1, COL3A1, and VEGFA). RT‐qPCR was performed using a QuantStudio 1 Real‐Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, CA, USA) under the following cycling conditions: 2 min at 50°C, 15 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 20 s at 95°C, 40 s at 60°C, and 20 s at 72°C. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of amplification to assess primer specificity, and primer pairs showing a single dominant melting peak were considered specific under the present amplification conditions. Primer sequences, predicted amplicon sizes, and primer melting temperatures (T m) are provided in Table 1 in accordance with MIQE reporting recommendations [29]. Relative gene expression was normalized to β‐actin (ACTB) and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Multi‐Evidence Gene Prioritization Framework

Candidate Gene Selection

Fifteen candidate genes implicated in wound healing, inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodeling were selected for prioritization: FLG, KRT14, IL1B, IL6, ICAM1, CCL2, CCL22, CXCL8, NFE2L2, HMOX1, MMP9, TGFB1, COL1A1, COL3A1, and VEGFA.

Evidence Source Integration (Stage 1)

Prior score was derived from GEO dataset GSE28914, a human skin wound‐healing transcriptomic dataset generated from split‐thickness skin graft donor‐site wound biopsies, accessed via the GEO2R tool using the limma empirical Bayes framework. The score was computed as |log2(FC + 0.01)| × (−log10(p + 1 × 10−10)), following the π‐value framework of combining fold change magnitude with statistical significance [30], reflecting literature‐based wound healing relevance [31]; small constants (0.01 and 1 × 10−10) were added to the fold change and p value terms, respectively, to avoid numerical instability at zero. Where a gene was represented by multiple probes, the probe with the lowest p value was selected as the most statistically reliable signal. For TGFB1, which showed discordant probe results, the probe most consistent with published wound healing literature was selected (FC = 1.083, p = 0.149). Genes absent from GSE28914 were assigned default values (FC = 1.0, p = 0.999); HMOX1 was not detected in GSE28914 and was assigned FC = 1.5, p = 0.05, reflecting existing literature support for its wound healing relevance despite absence from this dataset [32, 33].

Network score was derived from the STRING v12.0 protein–protein interaction network [34] (interaction confidence ≥ 0.40). Three centrality metrics (degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and PageRank) were computed using NetworkX [35], each independently Min‐Max normalized, and combined with equal weights (1/3 each) into a single composite network score reflecting overall protein interaction topology. Equal weighting was applied as no a priori basis exists to prefer one centrality measure over another.

Pathway score was derived from KEGG pathway annotations [36] retrieved programmatically via the KEGG REST API (https://rest.kegg.jp), accessed March 2026. Only human‐specific pathway maps (hsa‐prefix) were retained, yielding 228 gene‐pathway annotations across the 15 target genes. Pathway annotations for TGFB1 were manually verified against the KEGG gene entry (hsa:7040) due to a formatting irregularity in the automated retrieval. The pathway score was computed as the Min‐Max normalized count of KEGG pathways per gene.

Experimental score was derived from RT‐qPCR data and computed as |log2(FC + 0.01)| × (−log10(p + 1 × 10−10)) × (1 + WCR_B20) [30], where small constants (0.01 and 1 × 10−10) were added to the fold change and p value terms, respectively, to avoid numerical instability at zero. WCR_B20 represents the group‐level 24 h wound closure rate for betanin‐treated cells from automated image analysis. Because this phenotypic scaling constant is uniform across all 15 genes, it does not contribute discriminatory weight to individual gene scores and serves only as a contextual anchor linking transcriptional prioritization to the wound‐closure phenotype. Fold change and p values were obtained from one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparison test (as described in Section 2.8).

Weight Robustness Analysis

To assess the sensitivity of the weighted ranking to the choice of evidence weights, nine alternative weight configurations were evaluated spanning equal weighting (0.25 each) to extreme single‐source dominance and source‐exclusion scenarios. Gene rankings under each configuration were compared to the baseline ranking using Spearman rank correlation. Genes with rank standard deviation (σ) below 2.0 across all nine scenarios were classified as stably prioritized candidates.

Machine Learning Feature Attribution (Stage 2)

Three regression models were applied as exploratory tests of whether prior‐knowledge database features could predict the experimental score. The three STRING centrality metrics (degree_centrality, betweenness_centrality, and PageRank) were mutually collinear within the 15‐gene network (pairwise |r| = 0.931–1.000) and were therefore replaced by the composite network_score rather than entered as separate features. The final feature set comprised prior_score, pathway_score, and network_score, for which the maximum pairwise Pearson correlation was |r| < 0.80.

Random Forest (RF) was included as a baseline ensemble model (n_estimators = 200, max_depth = 5, random_state = 42) [37]. Model performance was assessed by leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (LOOCV), and an empirical null distribution was generated by permuting the experimental score 199 times using a computationally tractable RF configuration (n_estimators = 50, max_depth = 5) to reduce memory overhead while preserving null distribution shape. SHAP values were computed using TreeExplainer [12].

Gaussian Process (GP) regression was designated the primary model, as it is specifically designed for small‐sample inference and provides principled uncertainty quantification [39]. The kernel comprised a constant term, a radial basis function (RBF) component capturing smooth input–output relationships, and a noise term: ConstantKernel(1.0) × RBF(length_scale = 1.0) + WhiteKernel(noise_level = 0.1). If LOOCV R 2 values are negative across all three model architectures, this outcome indicates absence of global predictive structure between the database‐derived feature set and the experimental score. Under this interpretation, SHAP attribution values are not treated as outputs of a validated predictive model, but rather as relative measures of database evidence consistency per gene, quantifying the degree to which each gene's prior‐knowledge profile aligns with the learned scoring pattern.

All permutation tests used seed = 42 and applied the +1 correction to both numerator and denominator [26] to ensure p > 0 for all observed statistics. SHAP values [12] were reported at two levels: (i) global feature importance (mean |SHAP| per feature across all 15 genes), identifying which database source most strongly influenced gene scoring; and (ii) per‐gene SHAP attribution (Σ|φᵢⱼ| across all features for gene i), quantifying the degree to which each gene's database‐derived feature profile was consistent with the learned betanin‐response pattern. Per‐gene SHAP attribution was Min–Max normalized to [0, 1] as shap_importance_norm and reported as a relative measure of database evidence consistency per gene. All analyses were implemented in Python using scikit‐learn [40], SHAP [12], NumPy [41], and pandas [42].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis for RT‐qPCR data was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) (n = 3). Differences among CON, I + T, and betanin groups were evaluated by one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test, using the I + T‐stimulated group as the reference group unless otherwise indicated. Wound‐healing image statistics were analyzed separately as described in Section 2.5.

Results

Betanin Enhances In Vitro Scratch Closure in HaCaT Keratinocytes

To evaluate the effect of betanin on keratinocyte wound closure following mechanical injury, a scratch‐wound assay was performed using confluent HaCaT monolayers. Representative images (Figure 1A) showed that the initial wound widths were comparable between the scratched groups at 0 h, while the non‐scratched control maintained an intact monolayer throughout the observation period. At 24 h, the NC group still displayed a broad cell‐free gap, whereas the betanin‐treated group exhibited a visibly narrower wound area. By 48 h, the scratched region was nearly closed in the betanin‐treated group, whereas a residual gap remained evident in the NC group. ImageJ‐based quantitative analysis of the remaining wound area (Figure 1B), expressed as fold change relative to the 0 h value, confirmed that betanin significantly reduced the residual wound area compared with the NC group at both 24 (p = 0.020) and 48 h (p = 0.035). These findings indicate that betanin enhances net scratch closure in HaCaT keratinocytes. Betanin Accelerates Wound Closure Kinetics

To quantify the temporal dynamics of wound closure, a first‐order exponential kinetic model was fitted to the group mean WCR values at 0, 24, and 48 h (Figure 3A). The estimated wound closure rate constants were k = 0.0306 h−1 (95% CI: 0.0259–0.0347) for the betanin group and k = 0.0206 h−1 (95% CI: 0.0181–0.0236) for the NC group, an approximately 1.5‐fold difference (Table 3, Figure 3B). The difference was tested directly from the bootstrap distribution of Δk = k_betanin − k_NC, obtained by pairing resamples from the two groups. This distribution excluded zero (Δk = 0.0100 h−1; 95% CI: 0.0048–0.0148; p < 0.002), indicating that the difference in closure kinetics is statistically supported rather than attributable to sampling variability.

Discussion

Betanin Enhances Net Scratch Closure: Functional Evidence and Kinetic Characterization

Primary Scratch Assay Evidence

Betanin significantly enhanced net scratch closure in HaCaT monolayers at both 24 (p = 0.020) and 48 h (p = 0.035) by ImageJ quantification, indicating improved epithelial gap closure under the present in vitro conditions [43, 44]. Re‐epithelialization requires keratinocyte survival, collective movement, proliferation, and reorganization of the epithelial sheet after injury [1]. Because scratch assays capture the combined outcome of these processes, the most conservative interpretation is that betanin improves net closure behavior rather than proving a migration‐specific mechanism [43, 44, 45].

Automated Image Analysis Independently Corroborates the Wound‐Promoting Effect

The automated image‐analysis workflow strengthened this functional conclusion by providing an operator‐independent assessment of wound area. The directional trend from the custom Python‐based pipeline was fully consistent with ImageJ quantification at both timepoints. Because the mean 0 h wound area fractions differed slightly between groups (betanin: 22.72% ± 0.57%; NC: 25.98% ± 0.69%), WCR% was calculated relative to each group's mean 0 h anchor to correct for this baseline difference. After this normalization, the betanin group achieved 29.80% ± 12.87% closure at 24 h compared with 17.78% ± 7.96% in the NC group and reached complete closure (WCR = 100%) by 48 h while the NC group reached only 80.14% ± 8.21%. The concordance between two methodologically independent quantification approaches reduces the likelihood that the observed betanin effect reflects operator bias or pipeline‐specific artifact and instead supports it as a reproducible biological signal.

Effect Size Analysis Supports Biological Relevance Despite Pilot‐Scale Sample Size

At 24 h, permutation‐test p values did not reach the α = 0.05 threshold. This outcome is expected given the pilot‐scale sample size of n = 3 images per group at 24 h. Nevertheless, Cohen's d values at 24 h were 1.12 for WCR% and 2.14 for wound area fraction. Both values indicate large‐to‐very‐large betanin‐favorable effect sizes by conventional standards. At 48 h, permutation p values approached the conventional threshold (p = 0.050 for WCR%; p = 0.049 for wound area fraction). Effect sizes at this timepoint were exceptionally large, with Cohen's d exceeding 3.0 for both metrics.

The dissociation between p values and effect sizes at 24 h reflects insufficient statistical power rather than absence of a biological effect. The large Cohen's d values are consistent with a genuine and substantial betanin‐associated difference that the present sample size was underpowered to detect at conventional significance thresholds. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of interpreting pilot‐scale data through effect size alongside p values. The betanin‐associated closure advantage was biologically large in magnitude across both timepoints and statistically borderline at 48 h.

Kinetic Modeling Characterizes Betanin as a Rate‐Enhancing Agent

Kinetic modeling further characterized the temporal dynamics of this effect. The first‐order exponential rate constant for the betanin group was approximately 1.5‐fold higher than that of the NC group, with non‐overlapping bootstrap confidence intervals supporting a difference in closure kinetics between groups. Betanin therefore accelerated the approach to closure rather than altering the modeled endpoint. WCR_max, however, converged to the upper bound of the constrained parameter range and was therefore not independently identifiable from the present data. Since betanin‐treated wounds had already achieved complete closure at the final observation, this design resolves k more reliably than WCR_max.

Mechanistic Interpretation and Limitations of the Scratch Assay

Betanin has documented cytoprotective and antioxidant properties, including activation of NRF2‐linked defense pathways in other experimental systems and protection of HaCaT cells from cellular stress [7, 8]. These activities could support closure indirectly by helping maintain redox balance and cellular fitness after injury [1, 8]. However, the present scratch assay did not directly measure redox signaling, cell‐cycle behavior, or motility parameters, so those mechanisms should be regarded as plausible rather than demonstrated explanations for the observed phenotype [7, 8, 43, 45].

In addition, the scratch assay and the cytokine‐stimulated qPCR experiment represent distinct experimental contexts, so the transcriptional dataset should be considered complementary molecular support rather than direct causal proof of the closure phenotype [1, 7, 43]. Because scratch closure reflects the combined contribution of migration and proliferation unless proliferation is experimentally controlled, the most conservative conclusion is that betanin enhances net scratch closure in HaCaT keratinocytes [43, 44]. Additional studies using mitotic inhibition, live‐cell tracking, or transwell migration assays would be required to define the relative contribution of migration and proliferation more precisely [43, 44, 45].

Betanin Attenuates Cytokine‐Driven Transcriptional Dysregulation in Keratinocytes

IFN‐γ/TNF‐α (I + T)‐stimulated HaCaT cells are widely used to model cytokine‐driven inflammatory activation of keratinocytes in vitro. This model is supported by previous studies showing that IFN‐γ and TNF‐α cooperatively induce keratinocyte‐derived chemotactic factors and adhesion molecules, including IL‐8/CXCL8 and ICAM1, and promote HaCaT inflammatory gene‐expression responses involving CCL22 and barrier‐associated markers [2, 3, 4]. In the present study, a 24‐h exposure was therefore interpreted as a sustained cytokine‐driven transcriptional state rather than an early transient spike or an established chronic tissue‐remodeling phase. Within this framework, the present co‐treatment design asks whether betanin modifies the induction of an inflammatory keratinocyte program during ongoing cytokine stimulation.

The selected RT‐qPCR panel was designed to capture biologically distinct but interconnected processes involved in inflammatory wound repair. FLG and KRT14 were included to represent keratinocyte barrier/differentiation status and epidermal cell‐state changes during re‐epithelialization. This selection was based on the central role of keratinocytes in epithelial closure after injury [1] and on previous evidence linking filaggrin to epidermal differentiation and barrier formation [46]. The inflammatory module, including IL1B, IL6, ICAM1, CCL2, CCL22, and CXCL8, was selected because keratinocytes actively contribute to wound inflammation through cytokine, chemokine, and adhesion molecule expression, thereby regulating leukocyte recruitment and keratinocyte–immune cell crosstalk [47, 48]. NFE2L2 and HMOX1 were included to assess whether betanin engaged the canonical NRF2/HO‐1 antioxidant‐response axis, which is relevant because oxidative stress contributes to both normal and impaired wound repair [49]. Finally, MMP9, TGFB1, COL1A1, COL3A1, and VEGFA were selected as remodeling‐ and angiogenesis‐associated transcriptional readouts because extracellular matrix remodeling, TGF‐β signaling, collagen‐associated matrix organization, and VEGF‐mediated angiogenesis are key components of cutaneous tissue repair [50, 51]. Because the present experiments were performed in HaCaT monoculture, these remodeling‐ and angiogenesis‐associated genes should be interpreted as keratinocyte‐derived transcriptional signals rather than direct evidence of dermal matrix deposition, angiogenesis, or tissue‐level remodeling.

The decrease in FLG together with the increase in KRT14 after I + T is consistent with barrier‐associated and differentiation‐related transcriptional disturbance under inflammatory stress [2, 52]. Partial restoration of FLG by betanin therefore supports preservation of a more homeostatic epithelial phenotype [2, 52]. Because KRT14 is primarily a basal keratinocyte marker rather than a wound‐specific repair marker, its reduction should be interpreted cautiously as a shift away from a basal/progenitor‐like transcriptional state rather than as definitive correction of disease identity [52]. Considered together, the FLG/KRT14 pattern is more consistent with partial normalization of keratinocyte homeostasis than with nonspecific suppression of transcription [2, 52].

The inflammatory readouts are likewise consistent with prior HaCaT studies showing induction of IL1B, IL6, CXCL8, CCL2, ICAM1, and CCL22 in response to I + T [4, 53, 54]. In the present dataset, betanin most clearly reduced IL1B, ICAM1, CCL22, and CXCL8, whereas IL6 showed only a downward trend and CCL2 remained incompletely normalized [4, 53, 54]. This nonuniform response suggests selective dampening of cytokine‐, chemokine‐, and adhesion‐related outputs rather than global silencing of inflammatory transcription [4, 53, 55]. Such selectivity is biologically important because wound repair requires controlled inflammation rather than complete elimination of inflammatory signaling. A similar directional pattern has been reported for other phytochemicals tested in the same model [54, 55].

By contrast, NFE2L2 and HMOX1 changed only modestly under the current 24‐h co‐treatment conditions [55, 56]. This finding is noteworthy because betanin has been reported to induce NRF2‐associated antioxidant responses in other experimental systems [7] and to protect HaCaT keratinocytes against UV‐induced stress [8]. However, the present data do not justify a strong claim that betanin acted primarily through robust transcriptional activation of the canonical NRF2/HO‐1 axis in this assay. Rather, the modest NFE2L2/HMOX1 response suggests that betanin's dominant measurable activity may depend on cellular context, inflammatory stimulus, and treatment duration. Under the present I + T‐stimulated HaCaT condition, betanin's transcriptional effect was more evident in inflammatory, barrier‐associated, and remodeling‐related genes than in the canonical NRF2/HO‐1 antioxidant axis.

The remodeling‐associated genes add an additional layer to this interpretation, because the I + T group showed increased expression of MMP9, TGFB1, and VEGFA and altered collagen‐associated transcription [57, 58, 59]. Keratinocytes can contribute to inflammatory remodeling through MMP‐9 production, TGF‐β‐related signaling, and VEGF‐A expression [57, 58, 60]. Betanin reduced TGFB1 and VEGFA, lowered COL1A1, partially restored COL3A1, and showed a downward trend for MMP9, which is consistent with attenuation of cytokine‐driven remodeling‐associated transcription [57, 58]. In keratinocyte monoculture, these results should be interpreted as changes in keratinocyte‐derived remodeling‐associated transcripts rather than direct evidence of dermal matrix deposition, angiogenesis, or tissue‐level fibrosis [60]. The decrease in COL1A1 together with partial recovery of COL3A1 is most reasonably interpreted as partial normalization of collagen‐associated transcription, because collagen balance rather than strictly parallel movement of both genes is relevant to repair biology [59]. The shift in collagen‐associated transcription and the attenuation of VEGFA and TGFB1 are nevertheless consistent with a move away from cytokine‐driven remodeling stress toward a more repair‐compatible transcriptional state [52, 60].

Because all endpoints were measured at the mRNA level in HaCaT monoculture, they should be interpreted as transcriptional modulation rather than direct evidence of altered protein abundance, secretion, or tissue‐level remodeling. Overall, the dataset supports betanin as a modulator of cytokine‐driven keratinocyte dysfunction, with the clearest effects observed in barrier‐associated, inflammatory, and remodeling‐related transcripts, whereas evidence for strong NFE2L2/HMOX1 activation remained limited [7].

Database‐Informed Gene Prioritization Identifies Robust Candidates and Reveals Evidence of Conflict in Betanin's Transcriptional Response

The two‐stage gene prioritization framework served a dual purpose. Beyond ranking genes by composite score, it was designed to identify which genes are supported by both database‐derived wound healing knowledge and case‐specific experimental evidence from the betanin/I + T‐induced HaCaT model, and to determine where these two sources of evidence diverge. This dual purpose yielded two distinct and complementary outputs from the Stage 1 weighted scoring and sensitivity analysis.

The first output was the identification of genes robustly supported by both evidence sources. Under the baseline weighting scheme (prior × 0.25 + network × 0.25 + pathway × 0.15 + experimental × 0.35), FLG, COL3A1, COL1A1, and ICAM1 emerged as stably ranked candidates. Their high composite scores reflected convergent support from both database annotations and betanin‐specific RT‐qPCR responses. FLG was markedly decreased by I + T and significantly restored by betanin co‐treatment, consistent with its established role in epidermal barrier integrity. COL3A1 was suppressed by I + T and partially recovered by betanin, aligning with its centrality in extracellular matrix remodeling and collagen balance during repair. COL1A1 was significantly attenuated by betanin, consistent with partial normalization of cytokine‐driven collagen‐associated transcription [59]. ICAM1 was strongly induced by I + T and significantly reduced by betanin, reflecting selective attenuation of inflammatory adhesion signaling [4, 53, 54]. Across all nine weighting scenarios, these four genes maintained stable rank positions (rank SD < 2.0), confirming that their prioritization is robust regardless of the assumed relative importance of database‐derived versus experimentally derived evidence. These four genes therefore represent the most reliable hypothesis‐generating targets for betanin‐associated wound healing responses, grounded in convergent prior knowledge and case‐specific experimental data. KRT14 also ranked among the top five candidates under the baseline weighting scheme but was classified as weight‐sensitive, as discussed below.

The second output was the identification of genes where database‐derived importance and betanin‐specific experimental responsiveness are in tension. Five genes, CXCL8, CCL2, IL1B, VEGFA, and KRT14, showed weight‐sensitive ranks whose composite positions shifted meaningfully depending on whether database evidence or experimental evidence was weighted more heavily. This instability of weight‐sensitive ranks is not a statistical artifact. It reflects a genuine biological conflict between general wound healing annotations and the case‐specific betanin response observed in the I + T‐induced HaCaT model. The direction of this conflict differs across the five genes. CCL2 carries high network centrality and prior scores yet showed no significant betanin response by RT‐qPCR. Its rank therefore rises under database‐heavy weighting and falls under experiment‐heavy weighting. CXCL8 and IL1B are likewise high‐centrality inflammatory mediators, yet both were significantly attenuated by betanin [4, 53, 54]. The database characterizes these genes as general inflammatory hubs, while the experimental data positions them as betanin‐responsive targets. VEGFA was significantly suppressed by betanin, yet its database‐derived scores do not consistently reflect this responsiveness. Its rank is therefore higher under experiment‐heavy scenarios than database annotations alone would predict. KRT14 showed significant betanin modulation by RT‐qPCR and was ranked among the top five candidates under the baseline weighting scheme. However, its prior_score and network_score are directionally inconsistent with its experimental_score, rendering its top‐ranked position dependent on the baseline weighting assumption and unstable across alternative scenarios.

Taken together, the weight scoring and sensitivity analysis provided a quantitative map of where general wound healing knowledge and betanin‐specific experimental evidence converge and where they diverge. The four stably prioritized genes, FLG, COL3A1, COL1A1, and ICAM1, represent targets where prior knowledge and experimental evidence reinforce each other. The five weight‐sensitive genes identify where general wound healing annotations diverge from betanin‐specific transcriptional regulation. This divergence is itself a substantive finding. Betanin's transcriptional response does not uniformly follow the gene importance structures encoded in existing wound healing databases, and the nature of this misalignment differs systematically across functional gene categories.

Machine Learning Extends the Database–Experiment Discordance From Individual Genes to the Full Gene Panel

The Stage 2 machine learning analysis directly tested whether database‐derived features could systematically predict betanin‐induced experimental responses across the full 15‐gene dataset. This extends the evidence conflict identified in Section 4.3 from the level of five individual genes to a dataset‐wide structural property. All three models failed to predict betanin‐induced experimental scores from database‐derived features. Random Forest (LOOCV R 2 = −0.143, empirical p = 0.210), Ridge regression (LOOCV R 2 = −0.206, empirical p = 0.235), and Gaussian Process regression (LOOCV R 2 = −0.208, empirical p = 0.670) each produced negative LOOCV R 2 values.

No model could predict the betanin transcriptional response of a held‐out gene better than simply predicting the mean. This outcome is consistent with the well‐characterized behavior of machine learning models applied to small biological datasets, where limited sample size renders flexible models prone to overfit training data while failing to generalize to held‐out observations [61]. The negative LOOCV R 2 values observed here are therefore not an isolated technical failure but reflect a structural constraint inherent to machine learning in low‐n biological settings: without sufficient data to capture the full complexity of the biological relationship under study, models learn noise rather than signal, and cross‐validation performance falls below the uninformative baseline of predicting the mean.

The three models represent fundamentally distinct approaches: Random Forest, a nonlinear ensemble method that builds predictions by averaging across multiple decision trees constructed on random feature subsets [37]; Ridge regression, a linear L2‐regularized framework that reduces estimation instability in nonorthogonal problems through penalized least squares [38]; and Gaussian Process regression, a Bayesian non‐parametric framework that provides principled uncertainty quantification and marginal likelihood‐based hyperparameter optimization [39]. If a generalizable relationship existed between the selected database‐derived features and betanin‐responsive transcription, at least one of these architecturally distinct models would be expected to yield positive LOOCV R 2 values. Permutation tests confirmed that none of the observed LOOCV R 2 values significantly exceeded their null distributions, providing no evidence of predictive structure beyond chance under the present experimental constraints. This convergent failure makes model misspecification an unlikely sole explanation and suggests that the DB–experiment conflict observed in the weight‐sensitive genes is a property of the panel as a whole rather than an idiosyncrasy of individual genes, although neither inference can be fully separated from the sample‐size constraint considered below.

The nature of this failure is noteworthy in the context of established ML limitations. AI models trained on narrowly defined experimental contexts tend to extrapolate poorly to broader domains [21]. This interpretation is further constrained by the difference between the I + T‐stimulated HaCaT monoculture system used for experimental RT‐qPCR and the primary human wound‐derived transcriptomic context represented by GSE28914.

This dual directionality of ML failure is further grounded in two independent structural constraints. First, at n = 15, the dataset is insufficient to support generalization regardless of model architecture, as flexible models overfit the training data while even regularized models cannot recover signal from noise at this sample size [61]. This is consistent with the scoping review of Dabas et al. [13], which reported that limited dataset size was a predominant limitation in wound‐care AI studies, adversely affecting robustness, generalization, and algorithm performance. Second, and independently, the three database sources used here did not directly encode betanin‐specific transcriptional information from an I + T‐induced HaCaT system: the reference context simply does not exist in any current database. Even with a larger gene panel, models trained exclusively on GEO‐derived transcriptomics, STRING interaction topology, and KEGG pathway membership would lack the domain‐specific signal required to predict betanin‐induced responses, because these databases were not constructed from or calibrated to this pharmacological and inflammatory context. The convergent failure of three architecturally distinct models therefore reflects not a single addressable limitation but a dual structural constraint inherent to this experimental setting.

SHAP attribution was interpreted as a measure of internal evidence consistency rather than predictive output. SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to model predictions [12], but this attribution reflects the internal structure of the model rather than the biological validity of the identified drivers. This distinction is critical in the present context: because all three models failed to generalize, SHAP values characterize what the non‐generalizing models learned from the training data and do not identify biologically validated drivers. Within this constraint, the negative Ridge coefficient for network_score (β = −0.213) indicates that higher network centrality was associated with lower betanin‐induced experimental scores within the training data. This directional pattern shows partial coherence with the RT‐qPCR findings. Among higher‐connectivity inflammatory mediators, CCL2 showed no significant betanin response and IL6 showed only a non‐significant downward trend. By contrast, genes with repair‐ and barrier‐associated functions showed consistent and statistically significant betanin responses. These included FLG [52], COL1A1, and COL3A1 [59], as well as TGFB1 and the selective inflammatory targets ICAM1 and CCL22. CCL22 is notably induced in I + T‐stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes [4], further supporting the biological relevance of its attenuation by betanin in the present model.

CXCL8, however, was significantly attenuated by betanin despite its high network centrality, constituting a within‐dataset exception to this pattern. The directional coherence between the Ridge coefficient and the experimental results is therefore partial rather than uniform. This coherence does not position the Ridge model as a predictive tool. Rather, the internal structure of the non‐generalizing model points in the same direction as the experimental observations, providing auxiliary support for the internal consistency of the case‐specific experimental results.

Comparison With Previous Studies and Overall Interpretation

The present findings are broadly consistent with previous reports describing betanin as an antioxidant and cytoprotective phytochemical, but they extend those observations into an inflammatory keratinocyte wound‐repair context. Previous studies have shown that betanin can induce NRF2‐associated detoxifying and antioxidant enzymes in other experimental systems [7] and protect HaCaT keratinocytes against UV‐induced DNA fragmentation and epigenetic alterations [8]. Recent in vitro wound‐repair‐related work using a betanin‐based combination also reported enhanced cell viability, migration, and regulation of inflammation‐ and angiogenesis‐related gene expression in fibroblast‐type cells [9]. The present study agrees with these reports in supporting the relevance of betanin to cellular stress protection and repair‐associated responses. However, it differs from prior work by evaluating betanin alone in I + T‐stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes and by integrating scratch‐wound closure, kinetic modeling, inflammatory RT‐qPCR profiling, and database‐informed computational analysis within a single experimental framework.

The transcriptional findings also agree in part with previous IFN‐γ/TNF‐α‐stimulated HaCaT studies showing cytokine‐driven induction of chemokines, adhesion molecules, and barrier‐associated gene‐expression changes [3, 4]. Consistent with those reports, the I + T condition in the present study produced inflammatory and barrier‐associated transcriptional dysregulation. Betanin attenuated several of these responses, particularly IL1B, ICAM1, CCL22, and CXCL8. However, the present findings also differ from a simple NRF2‐centered antioxidant interpretation because NFE2L2 and HMOX1 were only modestly altered under the 24‐h co‐treatment condition. Thus, the dominant measurable response to betanin in this model appeared to involve selective modulation of inflammatory, barrier‐associated, and remodeling‐related transcripts rather than robust activation of the canonical NRF2/HO‐1 transcriptional axis.

The computational component of the present study also differs from previous applications of machine learning in wound healing, which fall into two categories. The first and larger comprises image‐ and record‐based clinical models, in which wound photographs or health records are used to develop and validate algorithms for wound assessment, segmentation, classification, and healing prediction [14, 15, 16]. The second applies machine learning to wound‐associated transcriptomic data: unsupervised methods resolve cellular heterogeneity and repair trajectories in single‐cell datasets, and supervised classifiers are trained on expression data to nominate candidate genes for experimental confirmation [18]. Reported examples include cutaneous repair regulators prioritized from GEO and STRING inputs and subsequently validated by animal modeling and immunoblotting [20]; a scarring signature derived by random forest and support vector machine screening of a predefined pathway gene set [17]; and multi‐classifier training on bulk RNA‐Seq with held‐out validation and RT‐qPCR confirmation in a non‐cutaneous epithelium [19]. In both categories, the model is the object of development, and the experimental or clinical data serve as its training or validation source.

The second category is methodologically closest to the present framework, and the contrast with it is informative. In those studies, the input features and the prediction target were drawn from the same transcriptomic domain, so a within‐domain relationship was available for the models to learn. Here the features were general wound‐healing annotations while the target was a compound‐specific response measured in a distinct inflammatory context, requiring the models to bridge two different biological domains. The negative LOOCV R 2 values should therefore not be read as a weaker implementation of the same analysis, but as the outcome of a different question.

This distinction is most clearly illustrated by the overlap in data sources. Meng et al. [20] drew on GSE28914 among other datasets and constructed a STRING‐based interaction network, the same two resources used for the prior and network components of the present scoring framework, and successfully nominated a transcription factor that was subsequently validated in an animal model. The difference therefore lies not in the databases themselves but in what was asked of them: identifying genes that vary within a wound‐healing transcriptome is a different task from predicting how those genes respond to a specific compound under cytokine stimulation. Accordingly, the present study did not aim to develop a general model for wound diagnosis or healing prediction. Machine learning was instead applied in the reverse direction, as a stress test of whether the selected database‐derived annotations could account for the betanin‐responsive transcriptional pattern. The limited predictive performance of the three models indicates that these annotations were insufficient for that purpose under the present I + T‐stimulated HaCaT condition, rather than that the betanin response falls outside existing wound‐healing knowledge.

Taken together, the present study provides converging evidence that betanin modulates keratinocyte repair responses through a functionally meaningful and context‐dependent transcriptional program. At the functional level, betanin accelerated net scratch closure in HaCaT keratinocytes and increased the first‐order rate constant by approximately 1.5‐fold relative to control, supporting a rate‐enhancing rather than extent‐enhancing closure phenotype. At the molecular level, betanin selectively attenuated cytokine‐driven transcriptional dysregulation across barrier‐associated, inflammatory, and remodeling‐related gene categories, with the most consistent effects observed in FLG, ICAM1, IL1B, CXCL8, CCL22, TGFB1, COL1A1, and COL3A1. The novelty of this study therefore lies not in demonstrating a general wound‐healing effect of betanin, but in linking betanin‐induced scratch‐closure acceleration with selective inflammatory transcriptional modulation and computational stress testing within a defined I + T‐stimulated keratinocyte model.

Limitations and Future Directions

The present study has inherent limitations. Experiments were conducted in HaCaT monoculture and were primarily evaluated at the mRNA level without protein‐level validation. In addition, the scratch assay cannot distinguish migration from proliferation without mitotic inhibition, and the 15‐gene panel and pilot‐scale image dataset constrain statistical power and generalizability. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings and define a clear agenda for future validation in primary keratinocytes, fibroblast–keratinocyte co‐culture systems, organotypic skin models, and protein‐level functional assays.

An additional limitation concerns the cell‐context mismatch between the HaCaT experimental system and the primary human wound‐derived transcriptomic dataset used for database‐derived scoring. Although HaCaT cells are reproducible and widely used as a keratinocyte model, they are immortalized cells and may differ from primary human keratinocytes in basal transcriptional state, epidermal differentiation programs, and inflammatory responsiveness. Aberrant basal NF‐κB activity has also been reported in HaCaT cells, which may alter their sensitivity to inflammatory stimulation and biologically active compounds [62]. Moreover, HaCaT cells and primary epidermal keratinocytes can show different cytokine‐dependent transcriptional responses of barrier‐related genes [63]. Because the prior score incorporated GSE28914, a human skin wound‐healing transcriptomic dataset derived from split‐thickness skin graft donor‐site wounds rather than HaCaT monoculture, the database comparison contains an inherent cell‐context mismatch [31]. Accordingly, the machine learning and database‐weighted scoring results should be interpreted as supportive and hypothesis‐generating evidence within the I + T‐stimulated HaCaT model, rather than as direct evidence that the same transcriptional response would occur in primary keratinocytes.

A further limitation concerns the kinetic characterization of scratch closure. With three group‐mean timepoints and two free parameters, the fitted asymptote reached the upper bound of the constrained parameter range in both groups and was therefore not independently identifiable, so the present design resolves the closure rate more reliably than the closure extent; the rate‐enhancing interpretation proposed above should be read with this constraint in mind. The single‐exponential form also did not fully capture the observed trajectory, and the fitted parameters should therefore be regarded as descriptive summaries of the closure profile rather than as mechanistically interpretable constants. Denser time sampling would be needed to separate rate from extent effects and to support a more flexible kinetic model.

Finally, the constraint identified by the machine learning analysis is not unique to the present study but reflects a broader gap in the resources currently available to the field. Wound‐healing databases and expression compendia were not constructed to encode compound‐specific transcriptional responses under defined inflammatory conditions, and integrated workflows in which computation is directed at such experimental questions remain rare [21]. Reference datasets pairing pharmacological intervention with transcriptional readout in defined cellular contexts would be required before database‐derived features could support the class of prediction attempted here. Until such datasets become available, experimental characterization remains the primary source of evidence for compound‐specific repair responses, and computational analysis is best positioned to delimit what prior knowledge can and cannot explain.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that betanin enhances net scratch‐wound closure in HaCaT keratinocytes and modulates cytokine‐driven transcriptional responses under inflammatory stimulation. Automated wound‐area quantification and first‐order kinetic modeling supported the ImageJ‐based scratch assay results, showing that betanin increased the wound closure rate constant by approximately 1.5‐fold relative to the vehicle‐treated wounded control, with the bootstrap distribution of the between‐group difference excluding zero (Δk = 0.0100 h−1; 95% CI: 0.0048–0.0148). These findings support betanin as a rate‐enhancing modulator of net scratch closure under the present in vitro conditions.

At the transcriptional level, betanin co‐treatment partially restored barrier‐associated gene expression, selectively attenuated inflammatory mediators, and modulated remodeling‐associated transcripts in I + T‐stimulated HaCaT cells. The most consistent responses were observed across FLG, ICAM1, IL1B, CXCL8, CCL22, TGFB1, COL1A1, and COL3A1, whereas NFE2L2 and HMOX1 were only modestly affected. These results indicate that betanin's dominant measurable effect in this model is better characterized as anti‐inflammatory‐associated, barrier‐modulating, and remodeling‐associated transcriptional modulation rather than robust activation of the canonical NRF2/HO‐1 antioxidant axis.

The multi‐evidence prioritization framework identified FLG, COL3A1, COL1A1, and ICAM1 as stably supported betanin‐response candidates, integrating experimental RT‐qPCR responses with prior wound‐healing annotations, pathway membership, and protein‐interaction topology. In parallel, machine learning was applied not to develop a general predictive model for wound healing but to test whether the selected database‐derived features could account for the betanin‐induced experimental scores. The limited predictive performance of Random Forest, Ridge regression, and Gaussian Process models indicates that these features were insufficient for that purpose in this specific I + T‐stimulated HaCaT context. This finding should be interpreted within the constraints of the 15‐gene panel, pilot‐scale image dataset, and limited feature set, but it supports the view that betanin‐induced transcriptional responses may be context dependent.

Overall, these functional, transcriptional, and computational findings position betanin as a candidate modulator of keratinocyte repair responses under inflammatory conditions. Because the present study was conducted in HaCaT monoculture and relied primarily on mRNA‐level endpoints, further work in primary keratinocytes, fibroblast–keratinocyte co‐culture and organotypic skin models, and protein‐level functional assays will be required to determine whether the transcriptional effects observed here translate into tissue‐level repair outcomes. Beyond betanin itself, the computational component of this study helps delineate the current scope of what database‐derived wound‐healing knowledge can account for: currently available resources do not yet capture how a given compound reshapes transcription under a defined inflammatory condition, and such information is still largely obtained through experimental characterization. Computational analysis can thus help identify where existing knowledge is informative and where it is not, while experimental work remains essential for characterizing compound‐specific repair responses.
