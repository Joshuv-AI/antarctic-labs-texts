Increasing patient access to faecal microbiota transplantation with remote delivery: a cost analysis of outpatient versus home-based treatment
Authors: Renée Hangaard Olesen, Emma Bendix Larsen, Tone Rubak, Simon Mark Dahl Baunwall, Sara Ellegaard Paaske, Merete Gregersen, Christian Erikstrup, Katrin Olsen, Jens Frederik Dahlerup, Thea Kirkegaard Kjaer, Camilla Birch Krogh, Lars Holger Ehlers, Christian Lodberg Hvas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFaecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is used in less than 10% of the patients in Europe who meet the indication. Encapsulated FMT allows increased use for CDI because capsules can be safely shipped to local healthcare facilities and administered orally to patients without use of e.g. colonoscopy. The aim of this study was to calculate and compare the costs of alternative ways of delivering encapsulated FMT to patients with CDI in Denmark, including outpatient treatment at a specialised FMT centre, outpatient treatment at local hospitals including remote delivery to the Faroe Islands, and home-based care.MethodsApplying a healthcare perspective, we used an activity-based costing approach, combining data from a pragmatic clinical randomised trial with the best available literature and expert input. Only relevant costs were included. The main outcome was the average cost of delivering outpatient, encapsulated FMT treatment at an FMT centre, at a local hospital, or in the patient’s home, only including additional costs related to delivery. An 8-week time horizon was applied for the analysis. Probabilistic and deterministic sensitivity analyses were applied to evaluate decision uncertainty and the robustness of the results.ResultsIn a pragmatic randomised trial including 217 patients with CDI, 135 patients received FMT. Analysing relevant costs in a base case analysis, local outpatient treatment (€145) was cost-saving compared with both treatment at the FMT centre (€209) and home-based treatment (€353). These differences remained robust across sensitivity analyses, including those accounting for patients’ time costs. In the Faroe Islands case, shipment to remote locations added an average cost of €54 per FMT to the cost for the local outpatient treatment.ConclusionCapsule-based FMT treatment has made it clinically and economically feasible to administer FMT in contextual settings outside of specialised hospitals. Our findings highlight the importance of local hospital-based treatment for increasing patient access to FMT, the ability to ship FMT to remote locations lacking a FMT centre, and the option of home-based treatment for a selected group of patients who may not tolerate transport to hospital.Keywords: Home-care services, hospital-based; Fecal microbiota transplantation; Costs and cost analysis; Clostridioides difficile

Introduction

Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT - transfer from a healthy person of minimally processed faeces to another individual to treat disease by correcting a disturbed intestinal microbiome) is a safe and cost-effective treatment option for Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI), embedded in international clinical guidelines [1, 2].

Barriers in its dissemination limit a widespread adoption of FMT. A Europe-wide survey of clinical FMT activity documented that the current use of FMT only covers approximately 10% of patients with recurrent CDI who are eligible for the procedure [3]. This indicates a significant underutilisation that emphasises a need for clinical awareness and to increase the European FMT activity by approximately 10-fold.

The expected growth in the future demand for FMT calls for more knowledge on how to overcome barriers in dissemination and patient access to FMT. Capsule-based FMT has been shown to be clinically effective [4]. For patients, it provides a convenient and non-invasive method of administration, while for clinicians, it requires a lower level of specialisation, making it easier to implement. Relevant for an economic perspective, encapsulated FMT has the potential to increase dissemination because it can safely be shipped to local health care facilities. Treatment can be provided to patients orally at local facilities without investments in capacity for colonoscopy, rectal enema, or upper gastrointestinal tract tube insertion.

In 2019, colonoscopy remained the preferred method of FMT delivery among most FMT centres in Europe, with capsule administration available at only 6 (19%) of 31 centres [3]. Transitioning FMT to other treatment modalities with an increased ease of use such as encapsulated FMT may help improve capacity. The regulation for substances of human origin (SoHO), recently issued under the European Union, provides a framework to support the expansion of FMT, outlining how SoHO establishments can collaborate with SoHO entities for the clinical application and follow-up of patients who receive FMT [5].

In this study, we explore the potential for a wider dissemination of FMT by comparing the cost of three alternative ways of organising the delivery of encapsulated FMT to patients with CDI currently used in Denmark. First, the FMT capsules are produced at specialised FMT centres, which also function as centralised facilities for treatment. Second, the FMT centres collaborate with local hospitals and remote locations, where FMT components are shipped from the FMT centre and treatments given orally to patients at local healthcare facilities. An example of a remote location is the National Hospital in Tórshavn; the Faroe Islands being a self-governing territory within the Kingdom of Denmark, located approximately 1,160 km from the mainland. As the Faroe Islands does not have a FMT centre, shipment from a distant FMT centre is necessary to allow patient access to treatment. Third, home-based treatment is also currently used and is particularly relevant, as many individuals with CDI over 60 years old are frail and unable to tolerate transportation to outpatient treatment [6]. These FMT treatments are performed as outgoing services from the FMT centre with transport of clinical personnel to the patients’ home. Hospital at home treatment models to older frail patients have generally proven to be a cost-effective alternative to inpatient hospitalisation [7–9].

Clarifying the resources and expenses required for different ways of organising outpatient treatments and home-based treatment may offer valuable insights for decision-makers. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the costs of these three different ways of providing encapsulated FMT treatment: outpatient treatment at FMT centre, outpatient treatment at local hospital and home-based treatment, for patients with CDI. Particularly, we included FMT treatment in remote locations with the Faroe Islands as an example.

Methods

Study design

This study was a cost analysis, conducted according to international and Danish guidelines for health economic evaluation, comparing three treatment organisations with encapsulated FMT [10–12]. Only relevant costs were included, i.e., costs that differ between the alternative ways of organising FMT. The costs of the FMT components itself, approximately €881 on average per treatment, were not included [13]. The focus of the analysis was the comparison of incremental costs of transportation, FMT handling, patient assessment and FMT administration associated with the treatment at the different locations (Fig. 1). A treatment with encapsulated FMT consists of 15 to 30 capsules originated from 45 to 50 g of stool and the capsules are swallowed within 15–30 min [4]. The setting of the analysis was the Central Denmark Region, with one FMT centre serving a population of approximately 1.3 million residents.

The analysis applied an activity-based costing approach following the methods suggested by Dehlholm-Lambertsen et al. in 2019 [14, 15]. The time horizon for the cost analysis was the follow-up period of 8 weeks following FMT administration reflecting a single treatment cycle. This study was mainly based on published data from clinical experience in Denmark and a pragmatic randomised trial (CLODIfrail) [16, 17]. CLODIfrail included 217 patients aged 70 years or more and with CDI, from the Central Denmark Region, who were randomised to receive either an intervention combining hospital-at-home and FMT, or the current standard treatment. An expert panel consisting of specialists and nurses from the Department of Hepatology and Gastroenterology and the Department of Geriatrics at AUH with extensive experience with FMT or home-based treatment was established to help estimate resource usage where no published evidence was available.

For analysis of remote delivery, we included the recently adopted collaboration with Department of Internal Medicine at the Country Hospital in Tórshavn, the Faroe Islands. Patients from the Faroe Islands were not included in CLODIfrail, but the Faroe Islands contributed data related to logistics and shipment. Shipment costs were estimated together with the accounting department at Aarhus University Hospital (AUH).

The base case analysis adopted a public health care perspective. All costs were estimated in Danish Kroner (DKK) and subsequently converted to EUR 2023-level prices using an exchange rate of €100 = DKK 745. The cost-analysis was developed in TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2023 (TreeAge Software, Williamstown, MA, USA).

Costing methods

For each activity, costs were allocated based on estimated resource usage, measured in physical units, with corresponding unit costs assigned [14, 15]. The unit cost for a nurse and specialist were derived from the national average data on gross yearly salary assuming 1,122 effective working hours annually [12, 18]. The unit cost for patient time was derived from the standard hourly wage reported by Statistics Denmark [12]. Data regarding amount of patient assessments, shipments to local hospitals, distance in kilometres (personnel transport and patient transport) and time (transportation time and patient time) were derived from CLODIfrail [17]. Based on data from CLODIfrail we further assumed that 34% of patients required a patient assessment because 66% were diagnosed with CDI and received a patient assessment during a hospital admission (Fig. 1).

Unit costs and resource use per patient assessment and FMT administration for outpatient and home-based FMT are listed in Table 1. Time for personnel transport and transition between treatments were aggregated to ‘additional time’ and included the time spend on transportation to and from the patients’ homes. The transition time referred to when the personnel left their vehicle until the assessment/administration began, and from the end of assessment/administration until they returned to their vehicle. Cost of transportation of personnel from the FMT centre to home-based treatment was estimated as the hospitals transport expenses for regional outgoing services where the personnel use their own vehicle [12]. Transportation costs were included for all patients and correspond to the Danish Flextrafik system, an on-demand paratransit service [19]. The cost of shipping FMT components applied to the local hospital were based on financial figures from AUH and covered packaging, transportation, distribution and overhead. The shipment cost was only applied for 75% as 25% had the FMT centre as their local hospital. For simplicity, overhead costs were estimated as a 40% add-on to the average personnel wage [12].

Sensitivity analysis

To access the overall decision uncertainty in the mean delivery cost per patient, we performed a probabilistic sensitivity analysis using a cost distribution for each activity, illustrated with 1,000 random samples. We derived 95% credibility intervals for the total cost of each treatment organisation to represent the range within which the true total cost is expected to fall with 95% probability, given model assumptions [20]. Gamma distributions were applied to variables such as patient transport, personnel transportation costs, patient time, and additional time, based on confidence intervals from the CLODIfrail study. For the remaining variables, distributions were assumed with a mean uncertainty of +/- 25%. One-way sensitivity analyses were performed on all variables to access the robustness of the analysis (data not shown). As a scenario analysis, we adopted a limited societal perspective recommended by the Danish Medicines Council [11]. In this analysis, relevant cost of patient time consisted of transportation time to and from the hospital and an average waiting time of 2.9 min per outpatient visit [22].

Results

The study was based on data of 217 patients diagnosed with CDI. A total of 102 patients in the intervention group and 33 patients in the standard-of-care group received FMT during the trial. In total, 135 (62%) of the 217 patients received FMT as part of their treatment.

In the base case analysis, the local outpatient treatment was the cheapest (Fig. 2), with an estimated mean delivery cost per patient of €145 for local hospital compared to €209 for FMT centre and €353 for home-based treatment (Table 2). The estimated credibility interval for the total costs was €178–208 for local hospital, €283–319 for FMT centre and €334–371 for home-based treatment (Table 2). The credibility intervals showed no overlap between treatment organisations suggesting a discernible difference in total costs.

Comparing local outpatient treatment with FMT centre treatment, the higher cost at the FMT centre was primarily driven by patient transport costs, reflecting the distance to the FMT centre. In contrast, local hospital outpatient FMT incurred additional costs due to the shipment of FMT components from the FMT centre. The cut-even distance is 73 km, meaning that for distances over 73 km, sending FMT to local hospitals is the least expensive option. However, for distances under 73 km, the FMT centre becomes the least expensive option. Home-based treatment was the costliest option, largely attributed to additional time and cost of transportation of personnel required to reach patients’ homes. The additional time related to transportation time and transition time accounted for 119 min of personnel time per roundtrip and the cost of transportation accounted for 118 km per roundtrip for each patient assessment and FMT treatment.

Adopting a limited societal perspective increased the costs of FMT for outpatient treatment at both the FMT centre and the local hospital due to the inclusion of patient time cost, while the cost of home-based treatment remained unchanged. The mean estimated cost was €300 for the FMT centre and €191 for the local hospital. The cost of home-based treatment of €353 remained the most expensive form of dissemination (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Shipment of FMT to remote locations such as the Faroe Islands incurred an extra shipment cost per FMT treatment of €54 to the cost of outpatient local hospital. This cost should be viewed in relation to other expenses, such as the cost of outpatient treatment, FMT components and antibiotics. The shipment cost came in addition to establishing a remote treatment facility at the local gastroenterology department, including access to clinical microbiology, outpatient treatment facilities, and multidisciplinary team conference structure.

Discussion

Capsule-based FMT treatment enables the use of FMT in contextual settings outside of specialised hospitals. This includes treatment at local facilities and home-based care, potentially allowing a shift in therapy and care to municipalities or general practitioners. In the present study, comparing centralised, local and home-based FMT, we found local hospital outpatient treatment to be the least costly option. Importantly, our findings adhere to the framework described in the recently issued SoHO regulation under the European Union, describing how SoHO establishments (FMT centres) can collaborate with SoHO entities (local hospitals), carrying out clinical application of FMT and patient follow-up [5].

Shifting treatments away from the specialised FMT centre to local hospitals yields savings of €64–109 per treatment, depending on the sector perspective. Treatment organisations closer to the patients could also reduce time consumption related to transportation for both patients and personnel depending on the treatment organisation. To facilitate FMT treatment at local hospitals including remote locations, it is essential to establish a treatment facility within the local gastroenterology department. This requires access to clinical microbiology, outpatient treatment facilities, and a well-structured multidisciplinary team conference setup and access to specialised counselling. The latter could be facilitated by video conferences. The implementation of local hospital-based encapsulated FMT enables the widespread dissemination of FMT treatment, which is crucial for increasing access to care. This is illustrated by the increased use of FMT in the Centre for Faecal Microbiota Transplantation at Aarhus University Hospital during the past decade, mainly attributed to starting external treatment sites [4].

We calculate the costs associated with transitioning outpatient treatment into treatment in the patients’ homes. Home-based treatment is costly, but it improves patients’ accessibility to treatment, which is crucial for those who cannot tolerate transportation to the hospital or are otherwise unable to attend outpatient treatment. We previously found that hospital-at-home is cost-effective for older patients with CDI [23]. Home-based FMT allows patients who might otherwise decline treatment to access care, potentially generating savings by ensuring access to FMT and reducing hospital costs. On the other hand, home-based treatment limits personnel time available for other tasks, and the ‘true’ personnel cost of home-based treatment may be higher than estimated in this study. This residual cost could be reduced by grouping patient visits, optimising transport schedules and implementation of FMT evaluation via tele-health and self-administration of FMT capsules, when possible. Overall, while home-based FMT is more costly and time-intensive for healthcare personnel, it remains a crucial treatment option for ensuring equal access to care and remains cost-effective in case of FMT because it prevents acute readmissions [17].

Previous research demonstrated cost savings associated with providing FMT for patients with recurrent CDI, highlighting the importance of a broader adoption of FMT [15]. FMT treatment led to a 42% reduction in hospital costs, resulting in average savings of €23,599 per patient after one year as the primary cost driver was the reduction in hospital admission days. This is supported by several studies that have shown that FMT is cost saving from a healthcare perspective [24, 25]. None of the existing published health economic evaluations of FMT include the value of encapsulated FMT to break down treatment barriers [24–29]. Increasing dissemination of FMT could enhance its accessibility and utilisation, further contributing to cost savings. These strategies optimise care for patients with CDI while addressing the projected shortage of healthcare capacity and workforce highlighted by the World Health Organization [30], emphasising the need for treatments that minimise healthcare resource use. We believe that our study illustrates the potential of health economic evaluations of implementation methods to support dissemination of cost-effective treatments.

Shipment of FMT to remote locations is a key factor in ensuring access to FMT. In the current study, we established delivery to the Faroe Islands, which lack an FMT centre. Without this option, remote locations would not have access to FMT, potentially resulting in higher patient mortality and morbidity and, ultimately, higher healthcare costs. The costs of shipment must be weighed against cost savings from providing FMT in remote locations. These costs could be further minimised by establishing a system with the capacity to store larger quantities of components at remote locations, allowing for consolidated shipments, such as annual deliveries.

Our study has several limitations. First, a costing analysis is a simplification of reality and since the available data did not cover all aspects of the analysis, several assumptions were made leading to a degree of uncertainty. The analysis was conducted using an average patient profile, with the understanding that no patient is truly average. Second, there is a potential underestimation of personnel costs. The scarcity of nurses and physicians may result in a higher wage, particularly since home-based treatment often requires additional working hours, potentially leading to increased overtime expenses. Furthermore, both salaries for health professionals and overhead costs at university hospitals may be even higher than accounted for in this study. Third, this study did not account for how alternative ways of organising treatment delivery, such as home-based care, might lead to indirect cost effects through changes in access to care and potential impacts on health inequalities. Fourth, it may be challenging to generalise all organisation forms and costs to other countries due to differences in healthcare systems, which may impact the feasibility and applicability. Fifth, mean costs were calculated for an eight-week period, not accounting for the entire treatment course in cases requiring multiple FMT administrations.

Future research should determine the total cost per treatment course until CDI resolution and/or death. More research is needed to find ways of increasing patients’ access to FMT in Europe. Furthermore, it is essential to clarify which patients should be eligible for referral to home-based treatment.

Conclusion

Comparing outpatient treatment at an FMT centre, outpatient treatment at local hospitals and home-based treatment, our study identified outpatient FMT treatment at local hospitals as the least costly option, while home-based FMT was the costliest. The cost differences were marginal. With a modest increase in expenditure, home-based FMT could enhance accessibility and ensure equitable access to care for frail patient groups. Our findings highlight the importance of developing local hospital-based treatment, the ability to ship FMT to remote locations that lack an FMT centre, and the option of home-based treatment for a selected group of patients.
