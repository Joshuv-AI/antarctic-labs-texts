The regulation of pyroptosis by post-translational modifications: molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets
Authors: Yi-Rao Zhou, Jun-Jie Dang, Qi-Chao Yang, Zhi-Jun Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryPyroptosis, a type of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin family proteins, releases a large amount of immune stimulatory substances, which further contribute to inflammation and elicit an adaptive immune response against tumours and pathogens. And it occurs through multiple pathways that involve the activation of specific caspases and the cleavage of gasdermins. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) could influence the chemical properties of the modified residues and neighbouring regions, ultimately affecting the activity, stability, and functions of proteins to regulate pyroptosis. Many studies have been conducted to explore the influence of PTMs on the regulation of pyroptosis. In this review, we provide a comprehensive summary of different types of PTMs that influence pyroptosis, along with their corresponding modifying enzymes. Moreover, it elaborates on the specific contributions of different PTMs to pyroptosis and delves into how the regulation of these modifications can be leveraged for therapeutic interventions in cancer and inflammatory diseases.

Due to the constraints on the number of references permitted for this review, a considerable portion of the primary literature could not be included. This review primarily focused on literature published within the past 5 years.

Introduction

Pyroptosis is a type of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin family proteins, which typically relies on caspase activity.1 Initially classified as apoptosis due to shared characteristics like caspase-driven activation, DNA harm, and nuclear condensation, pyroptosis was officially labelled in 2001 to identify it as a pro-inflammatory programmed cell death. Pyroptosis achieves this by disrupting the protective intracellular environment of pathogens causing cell breakdown and subsequent discharge of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), as well as inflammatory elements and cytokines. The released substances aid in heightened recruitment of adaptive immune cells, improved antigen presentation, and widespread immune activation.2 Consequently, significant research is aimed at promoting pyroptosis in tumour cells to stimulate adaptive antitumour immunity, thereby amplifying the antitumour impact of cancer immunotherapy. However, excessive or uncontrolled pyroptosis can also lead to unchecked and damaging inflammatory responses, resulting in tissue dysfunction and harm.3 Above all, maintaining a balance between the protective and detrimental effects of pyroptosis is crucial in the treatment of various diseases, including tumours and inflammatory diseases.4 While numerous mechanisms have been discovered to regulate pyroptosis, the regulation of pyroptosis by post-translational modifications (PTMs) lacks a comprehensive and extensive summary.

PTMs are known to exist in mammalian cells and significantly impacts proteins in various ways.5 They target specific amino acids in regulatory domains of proteins, involving the covalent addition of groups like phosphate, acetyl, ubiquitin, and others.6 These modifications impact the chemical properties of modified residues and adjacent regions, ultimately influencing protein charge, structure, binding properties, and function.7 Furthermore, it's noteworthy that the majority of PTMs are reversible and dynamic, allowing their addition or removal from polypeptides by specific enzymes.7,8 Recent reports have identified multiple PTMs in pyroptosis, such as phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitylation, SUMOylation, palmitoylation, prenylation, succination, lactylation and ADR-ribosylation, which can finely modulate the activation or inhibition of pyroptosis by regulating the gasdermins-dependent pathways.9 These studies not only provide new regulatory mechanisms and research ideas for the regulation of pyroptosis, but also offer new drug targets for treating diseases related to pyroptosis or enhancing the therapeutic effect of tumours by harnessing pyroptosis. Furthermore, enzymes that directly or indirectly participate in PTMs, including modifying enzymes, de-modifying enzymes, as well as metabolites and metabolic enzymes that influence the occurrence of PTMs, can be utilized as potential drug targets.10

While many studies have discussed PTM-mediated pyroptosis regulation, a comprehensive overview detailing PTM types and the roles of modifying enzymes in pyroptosis regulation and disease treatment is lacking. The review offers insights into PTM types and their impact on pyroptosis-related proteins, along with discussing the potential of regulating these modifications to treat cancer and inflammatory conditions. Additionally, we propose future research directions and practical applications for PTMs regulation in pyroptosis.

Overview of pyroptosis

Pyroptosis can be classified into five distinct pathways, based on the specific gasdermin proteins that are involved. Gasdermin D (GSDMD)-dependent pyroptosis is a canonical pathway initiated by inflammasome activation, composed mainly of leucine-rich repeat proteins such as NOD-like receptors (NLRs), ASC, and pro-caspase-1. The NLRs entail leucine-rich repeats (LRR), nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NACHT), and CARD/pyrin domain (PYD).11,12 Non-NLR proteins like absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) can also recruit pro-caspase-1 and ASC.13 The inflammasome can assemble and activate in response to various PAMPs and DAMPs.14,15 Upon activation, caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD at Asp275, forming GSDMD-N, leading to pyroptosis and the discharge of interleukin-1β (IL-1β)/interleukin-18 (IL-18) through these pores.16 Caspase-4/5/11 can also induce GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis by interacting with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and processing GSDMD into GSDMD-N.17 Additionally, K+ efflux from pyroptosis can activate NLRP3 and caspase-1 as well, causing IL-1β/IL-18 secretion.18

In the gasdermin E (GSDME) pathway, granzyme B (GZMB) from NK cells or CTL can directly cleave GSDME to GSDME-N, leading to membrane permeabilization and pyroptosis. And GZMB can also indirectly trigger pyroptosis by activating caspase-3.19 Gasdermin A (GSDMA)-dependent pyroptosis is induced by the cleavage of GSDMA at Gln246 by streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxin B (SpeB).20 In gasdermin B (GSDMB)-dependent pyroptosis, granzyme A (GZMA) cleaves GSDMB at Lys229/Lys244, unleashing its pore-forming activity which triggers pyroptosis.21 Finally, in the gasdermin C (GSDMC)-dependent pathway, caspase-8 activated by TNF-α can cleave GSDMC, leading to pyroptosis.21 Recent research have also identified two forms of cleavage-independent GSDM for pyroptosis. In Trichoplax adhaerens, TrichoGSDM, sharing about 24% sequence identity with the GSDM-N domains of human GSDME, demonstrates pore-forming ability in its monomeric form. Additionally, regulator of cell death-1 (RCD-1)-1 and RCD-1-2 in Neurospora crassa, could create pores and trigger pyroptosis when encountering each other.22

Pyroptosis in cancer and inflammatory diseases

Pyroptosis has multifaceted roles in both physiological and pathological processes. In normal physiology, it is a critical component of the innate immune response, contributing to host defence against pathogens.23 However, excessive or dysregulated pyroptosis may impact the progression of tumours and inflammatory diseases.24 It exhibits a dualistic impact on tumour development and management. Sustained pyroptosis of cancer cells, triggered by the tumour microenvironment (TME), tends to support cancer progression.25 Conversely, acute and intense activation of pyroptosis can prompt an adaptive antitumour immune response to inhibit tumour growth. The released DAMPs and inflammatory cytokines, can enhance immune cell recruitment, antigen presentation, and extensive immune activation. Besides, IL-18 is pivotal in attracting and activating natural killer NK cells and promoting Th1 response. Overall, the regulation of pyroptosis in cancer cells holds promise as a therapeutic avenue for cancer treatment and immunotherapy enhancement.4

In addition, excessive or dysregulated pyroptosis can contribute to the development of inflammatory diseases across multiple organ systems. In cardiovascular diseases, pyroptosis has been implicated in atherosclerosis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and myocardial infarction.26,27 Cholesterol crystals can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, initiating caspase-1 activation and the subsequent cleavage of pro-IL-18 and pro-IL-1β, inducing inflammation and fostering the development of atherosclerotic plaques.28 GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis plays an important role in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.29,30 Furthermore, liver cell pyroptosis can drive hepatic fibrosis.31 Emerging evidence indicate the potential role of pyroptosis in the development of neurological conditions like ischemic stroke, Parkinson's disease (PD), Alzheimer's disease (AD), and musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs).26

Overview of post-translational modifications

PTMs have been identified in mammalian cells and play a fundamental role in various cellular progresses. These modifications take place on particular amino acids within regulatory regions of target proteins, involving the addition or removal of functional groups (such as phosphate, acetate, or ubiquitin), collectively enhancing the diversity and complexity of the protein.6 PTMs generate slightly modified forms of original peptides, proteins, and lipoproteins, thereby profoundly impacting protein properties in multiple aspects such as activity, stability, function, structure, localization, trafficking, signalling transduction, and interactions with partner biomolecules.32 The main forms of PTMs consist of ubiquitylation, phosphorylation, acetylation, glycosylation, SUMOylation, palmitoylation, prenylation, succination, and ADR-ribosylation. Interestingly, the function of PTM can vary differently depending on the specific protein and cellular context. It's crucial to highlight that most PTMs are reversible and dynamic, mediated by specific enzymes; however, some PTMs can occur non-enzymatically through spontaneous chemical reactions.7,8,33, 34, 35

PTMs are associated with the onset and progression of various diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic diseases, by modulating the involved proteins. Furthermore, PTMs are crucial regulators of immunity, impacting the activation, survival, proliferation, differentiation, and migration of immune cells.6 Significant advances in cancer immunotherapy have revealed the potential of PTMs to directly or indirectly impact the efficacy of immunotherapy, by regulating immune checkpoints or reshaping the TME.32 This complex interaction of PTMs acts as an intricate fine-tuning mechanism, enabling cells to finely adjust their responses to even the tiniest environmental changes.36 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Involved molecules and functions of PTMs.

The regulation of GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis by PTMs

GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis represents a canonical pathway initiated by inflammasome activation.37 Following activation, mature caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD at Asp275, generating GSDMD-N, which forms membrane pores, leading to pyroptosis and the liberation of lL-1β/lL-18 via these pores.16 Moreover, caspase-4/5/11 can prompt GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis, by binding to LPS and cleaving GSDMD into GSDMD-N, inducing pyroptosis.17 Importantly, PTMs hold the potential to modulate the onset and treatment of diseases by influencing the structure and function of key proteins within the GSDMD-dependent signalling pathway.

PTMs of NLRP3 inflammasome

The NLRP3 inflammasome is an intracellular sensor, an essential part of the innate immune system involved in danger recognition. It consists of three main components: NLRP3, ASC, and pro-caspase-1. After being activated by PAMPs, NLRP3 undergoes conformational changes and induces the activation of pro-caspase-1. Similar to DAMPs, PAMPs are molecular structures present on the surface of pathogenic microorganisms, which can be recognised by cytosolic pattern recognition receptors. Subsequently, activated caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD to promote cell pyroptosis and catalyse the processing and release of IL-1β and IL-18.38 Multiple studies have shown that various PTMs can affect Ubiquitylation

Ubiquitination is a critical PTM process characterised by covalent bonding between the 76-amino acid ubiquitin protein and target proteins. This process involves enzymatic stages with ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2), and ubiquitin E3 ligase.92 Ubiquitination primarily inhibits pyroptosis by facilitating protein degradation, notably through E3 ubiquitin ligase. Specifically, tripartite motif (TRIM)-containing proteins act as crucial E3 ligases that negatively regulate NLRP3 inflammasome activation. TRIM24-induced ubiquitination inhibits NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β-mediated pyroptosis.39 Different E3 ligases may interact; for instance, RNF125 triggers K63-linked ubiquitination in the LRR domain of NLRP3, recruiting Cbl-b to promote K48-linked ubiquitination at Lys496 in NLRP3.40 Polyubiquitination typically restrains inflammasome activation, hence preventing endotoxemia linked with NLRP3 inflammasome. Overexpression of PARK2 in BEAS-2B airway epithelial cells exposed to house dust mite, impedes NLRP3 inflammasome activation, IL-1β/IL-18 secretion and pyroptosis.41 Varying E3 ligases can exert contrasting impacts on NLRP3 based on ubiquitylation specificity. For example, Pellino2 activates NLRP3 through K63-linked ubiquitination during priming, while HUWE1A1 targets NLRP3, NLRC4, and AIM2 with K27-linked polyubiquitination, both boosting inflammasome assembly and pyroptosis.42, 43, 44

Deubiquitylation is also crucial in inflammasome activation, with deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) like BRCC36 stimulating NLRP3 inflammasome activity and pyroptosis by reversing ubiquitination.45 USPs, a major DUB family regulating NLRP3 inflammasomes, such as USP9X, USP14, USP7, and USP47, promote NLRP3 activity, cytokine release, and pyroptosis through cleaving ubiquitin chains from NLRP3.46, 47, 48

Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation, the addition of phosphate groups to proteins, is one of the most widely studied PTMs. The specific impacts of these events can vary based on the protein targets, cellular context, and upstream signalling pathways involved. Phosphorylation at Thr659 in NLRP3, mediated by P21-activated kinase class 1 (PAK1), is necessary for NLRP3-NEK7 interaction and inflammasome activation, thereby further promoting pyroptosis.49 The dual-specificity phosphatase PTEN can interact with NLRP3 at Tyr32 (human)/Tyr30 (mouse) for dephosphorylation, thereby promoting NLRP3-ASC interaction and activation.50 Phosphatase 2A dephosphorylates the PYD domain to initiate inflammasome assembly and promote its activation.51

SUMOylation

SUMOylation, a PTM, dynamically binds small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) molecules to specific amino acid residues of target proteins, regulating cell pyroptosis.93 SUMO-conjugating enzyme (UBC9) interacts with NLRP3 and promotes SUMO1 to catalyse SUMOylation at Lys204 in NLRP3, thereby promoting NLRP3 activation and cell pyroptosis. Conversely, SUMO-specific protease 3 (SENP3) catalyses deSUMOylation of NLRP3, reducing ASC recruitment and speck formation, ultimately inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as well as IL-1β cleavage and secretion.52 In contrast, SUMOylation mediated by SUMO E3 ligase MAPL at K689 in NLRP3 inhibits inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. DeSUMOylation mediated by SENP6 and SENP7 at the same site promotes NLRP3-dependent ASC oligomerization, caspase-1 activation, and pyroptosis.53 Additionally, SUMOylation can interact with other PTMs involved in pyroptosis. TRIM28 binds to NLRP3 and promotes the SUMO1, SUMO2 and SUMO3 modifications of NLRP3, which inhibits its ubiquitination and proteasome degradation, thereby promoting inflammasome activation.54

Acetylation

Acetylation was first discovered by Vincent Allfrey in 1964 on histone 1, as a conserved PTM by n-acetyltransferase.94 Acetylation can neutralize the positive charge of lysine, thereby influencing various aspects of protein function, including stability, enzyme activity, and subcellular localization, ultimately regulating pyroptosis.95 NLRP3 is acetylated in macrophages and then deacetylated by the deacetylase sirtuin 2 (SIRT2). Acetylation of NLRP3 at K21 (human)/K22 (mouse) enhances the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, while SIRT2-mediated deacetylation inhibits this process.55

Alkylation

NLRP3 alkylation has been found to impair the ATPase activity of NLRP3, thereby hindering NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Meanwhile, its alkylation which can be modified by benzoxathiole derivative, BOT-4-one, also increases the ubiquitination level of NLRP3.56

Citrullination

Citrullination is the process of converting arginine residues in protein peptide chains into citrulline residues, catalysed by peptidyl arginine deiminases (PADs). Calcium-dependent enzymes PAD2/4 can catalyse citrullination of NLRP3, leading to NLRP3 assembly in mouse macrophages and subsequent pyroptosis.57

Palmitoylation

Palmitoylation, a highly conserved PTM of proteins, is widely found in eukaryotes. It is classified as S-palmitoylation, N-palmitoylation, or O-palmitoylation depending on the mode of connection. Many proteins must undergo S-palmitoylation to perform their normal physiological functions. Notably, this change is reversible, unlike other lipid modifications.96

Impaired palmitoylation of NLRP3 is associated with pathological conditions characterized by excessive inflammation. Wang et al. demonstrated that the zinc-finger DHHC (Asp-His-His-Cys)-type-containing 12 (ZDHHC12), an NLRP3 palmitoylation S-acyltransferase, modified its palmitoylation at C844, promoting its degradation through the chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) pathway and thereby inhibiting pyroptosis.58

S-nitrosylation

S-nitrosylation is a selective and reversible PTM of protein sulfhydryl groups by the bioactive signalling molecule nitric oxide (NO). NLRP3 S-nitrosylation has been found to suppress IL-1β secretion and pyroptosis by inhibiting mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and NLRP3 assembly in mouse and human myeloid cells. Additionally, NO is identified as a key negative regulator of abnormal NLRP3 inflammasome activation.97

PTMs of other inflammasomes

PTMs play crucial roles in regulating the activity of inflammasomes, including NLRC4 and other members. For instance, NLRC4, unlike NLRP3, contains a CARD at its N-terminal, functioning as a nucleotide-binding protein and capable of activating caspase-1, thereby inducing pyroposis.98 As mentioned above, HUWE1A1 targets NLRC4 with K27-linked polyubiquitination, enhancing pyroptosis. In addition to ubiquitination, phosphorylation is also pivotal for NLRC4 activation. The NLRC4 kinase PRKCD (PKCδ) phosphorylates NLRC4 at S533 during S.typhimurium infection, promoting inflammasome and caspase-1 activation, leading to pyroptosis.59 Additionally, SlRT3 deacetylates NLRC4 at specific lysine residues, such as Lys71 or Lys272, promoting its activation by inducing ASC speck formation and oligomerization, thereby inducing pyroptosis both in vitro and in vivo. It's worth noting that SIRT3 is also essential for the activation of other inflammasomes such as NLRP3 and AIM2.60 In the context of other inflammasomes, Interferon-gamma-inducing protein 16 (IFI16), a member of the PYHIN family, assembles the inflammasome and initiates pyroptosis. In the case of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, it has been reported that HPV can recruit the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 to induce the ubiquitylation and proteasomal degradation of the IFI16 inflammasome, ultimately leading to the suppression of pyroptosis and immune surveillance. These examples demonstrate the diverse and intricate roles of PTMs in regulating the activity and function of inflammasomes.61

PTMs of caspase-1/4/5/11

Caspases are a family of evolutionarily conserved cysteine proteases that play significant roles in programmed cell death and inflammation. Among the caspases, caspase-1, caspase-4, caspase-5, and caspase-11 belong to the inflammatory caspase family, which can be activated by the inflammasome and initiate immune responses. Upon activation, they can induce GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis and prompt the activation and secretion of DAMPs to promote inflammation.99

Ubiquitylation

The ubiquitylation of caspases always promotes their proteasomal degradation to inhibit cell pyroptosis. The E3 ubiquitin ligase, NEDD4 induces the K48-linked polyubiquitylation of caspase-4/11 (human/mouse) in multiple residues and promotes their degradation, resulting in pyroptosis inhibition.62 Besides, the ubiquitination of caspase-1/4/11 mediated by the Brucella effector protein TcpB also exerts an inhibitory effect on pyroptosis, as it degrades caspases and mitigates LPS-induced inflammasome activation.63 The role of ubiquitylation in pyroptosis regulation depends on the environment. The linear ubiquitin assembly complex (LUBAC), an important component of the immune system, adds the linear polyubiquitin to the CARD domain of caspase-1, activating caspase-1 and promoting pyroptosis.64

Phosphorylation

Caspase-1 was found to be phosphorylated at Ser376 by PAK1, which promotes caspase-1 activation, contributing to IL-1β maturation and cell pyroptosis.65

ADP-riboxanation

Li et al. found that Shigella can evade caspase-4/11-mediated pyroptosis using a type III secretion system (T3SS) effector, OspC3. Further research revealed that OspC3 induces ADP-ribosylation at Arg314 and Arg310 in caspase-4 and caspase-11, respectively, preventing their auto-processing, recognition, and GSDMD cleavage, ultimately inhibiting pyroptosis. This modification was named ADP-2′-imino-nucleofuran– [1′,2’:4, 5] oxazolidylation modification by Shao's team.66

PTMs of GSDMD

GSDMD is a pore-forming protein that consists of a 31 kDa GSDMD-N and a 22 kDa GSDMD-C connected by a peptide linker. When activated, GSDMD can lead to the formation of transmembrane pores by GSDMD-N, resulting in the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 from the cell and pyroptosis.2 Compared to other proteins, PTMs of GSDMD directly impact the occurrence of cell pyroptosis.

Ubiquitylation

The E3 ubiquitin ligase, synoviolin (SYVN1), mediates the K27-linked polyubiquitination of GSDMD at K203 and K204, promoting GSDMD-induced pyroptosis.67 Shigella ubiquitin ligase IpaH7.8 ubiquitinates human GSDMD and targets it for proteasomal degradation. By utilizing IpaH7.8 to suppress pyroptosis, Shigella contributes to severe gastroenteritis in human.68

Phosphorylation

GSDMD is phosphorylated at Ser46 by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which hinders its membrane translocation and oligomerization, inhibiting its pore-forming ability and pyroptosis.69

Palmitoylation

Palmitoylation has been identified as a key regulatory mechanism controlling the membrane localization and activation of GSDMD.70 Upon activation by inflammasome, palmitoyl transferases ZDHHC5/9 can induce S-palmitoylation at Cys191 (human)/Cys192 (mouse) on GSDMD, generating palmitoylated GSDMD-N which effectively triggers pyroptosis. Interestingly, full-length palmitoylated GSDMD can also form pores to induce pyroptosis, although less efficiently. Furthermore, Elevated ROS levels further enhance ZDHHC5/9 activity, enhancing the palmitoylation of GSDMD.100 Moreover, another research reported that the palmitoyl acyltransferase DHHC7 mediates GSDMD palmitoylation, directing its cleavage by caspase-1. Subsequently, DHHC7 also palmitoylates GSDMD-N to facilitate its membrane translocation, whereas acyl protein thioesterases (APT2) depalmitoylates GSDMD-N to unmask the C192 residue, promoting its oligomerization and pyroptosis.71

Succination

Succination is a PTM that occurs when fumarate reacts with cysteine residues in proteins, resulting in the formation of succinated cysteine. This modification has been associated with cellular metabolic alterations and conditions such as cancer. Succination of GSDMD at Cys191 (human)/Cys192 (mouse) hinders its interaction with caspases, thereby restricting its capacity to process, oligomerize, and induce cell death.72

PTMs of proteins indirectly-involved in GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis

PTMs play various roles in regulating cellular processes and proteins involved in inflammatory responses. For instance, pro-IL-1β undergoes cleavage by caspase-1 or in some cases, caspase-8, resulting in the processing of pro-IL-1β into the active cytokine IL-1β, a pivotal regulator of immune responses. UBE2L3, an atypical ubiquitin E2 conjugase, triggers K48-linked ubiquitylation of pro-IL-1β, leading to its degradation via the proteasome and suppressing mature IL-1β production, which ultimately inhibits pyroptosis.73 Additionally, PTMs of other proteins contribute to the modulation of inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. For example, the linear ubiquitylation of liver kinase B1 (LKB1) promotes its activation, leading to subsequent activation of AMPK, which in turn inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis.74 Moreover, the acetylation of the ETS-related gene (ERG) suppresses TNF-α-induced pyroptosis by inhibiting the cleavage of GSDMD and GSDME. Conversely, histone deacetylase 11 (HDAC11) forms a complex with ERG and reduces its acetylation, exhibiting the reverse effect.75 Furthermore, in response to Yersinia infection, receptor-interacting serine–threonine protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) and caspase-8 are recruited to the lysosomal Rag–Regulator complex, where RIPK1 is phosphorylated, promoting caspase-8 activation. This activated caspase-8 then cleaves GSDMD and GSDME, contributing to cell pyroptosis.76 Notably, glycosylation, a crucial PTM, has been implicated in modulating protein stability and function. Glycosylation of heat shock cognate 71 kDa protein (HSC70) has been linked to caspase-1 activation by enhancing its stability. Additionally, it has been reported that the peptide RPS4XL, derived from long non-coding RNAs, suppresses pulmonary hypertension-induced pyroptosis by binding to HSC70 and inhibiting its glycosylation.77 Furthermore, lactate has recently been found to modify lysine residues on histones, and this particular PTM is referred as lactylation. As mentioned above, NEDD4 reduces caspase-11 protein levels through enhancing its ubiquitination, thereby inhibiting pyroptosis. While lactate can directly regulate NEDD4 K33 lactylation by lysine acetylase p300, hindering the NEDD4-caspase-11 interaction, thereby up-grading caspase-11 and promoting cell pyroptosis.78 SIRT1, known as a delactatese, leads to the removal of the canopy FGF signalling regulator (CNPY3) lysine lactylation, affecting its cellular localization and thereby resulting in the destruction of lysosomal proteins function and stru lactate cture. This process then activates pyroptosis through the caspase 1/GSDMD pathway.79

The regulation of GSDME-dependent pyroptosis by PTMs

In 2017, Wang et al. uncovered that chemotherapeutic drugs can induce the cleavage of GSDME at Asp270 by activating caspase-3 in cells with high GSDME levels, contributing to pyroptosis in tumour cells.101 Furthermore, GZMB derived from NK cells, CTLs or IL2Cs has the capability to directly cleave GSDME to GSDME-N, which is responsible for permeating the cell membrane and triggering pyroptosis.19 Additionally, GZMB can indirectly induce pyroptosis by activating caspase-3. PTMs of STAT3

Studies have shown that STAT3, as a member of the signal transduction activators of transcription (STAT) family, significantly reduces the production of mitochondrial ROS, thereby inhibiting the pyroptosis of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells. Conversely, tetra arsenic hexoxide can inhibit STAT3 phosphorylation, resulting in the activation of caspase-3 and GSDME-dependent pyroptosis, offering a potential novel therapeutic approach to impede TNBC progression.80

PTMs of caspase-3/8

When GSDME is overexpressed, activated caspase-3 can cleave it and release its N-terminal domain to form pores in the cell membrane, contributing to pyroptosis. Chromobacterium outer protein C (CopC) is an enzyme that can catalyse PTM of caspase-3 involving ADP ribosylation at Arg207 and deamination mediated cyclization, abbreviated as “ADPR-deacylization ". It blocks the protease activity of caspase-3 towards GSDME and thus inhibits pyroptosis. This mechanism of CopC represents a pattern of bacteria against host defences and defines a novel PTM which was unappreciated.81

While caspase-8 is primarily known as an apoptotic caspase, it also exhibits inflammatory caspase functions that induce pyroptosis by directly regulating cleavage and activation of gasdermin proteins.102 Caspase-1 and caspase-8 share the same cleavage site on GSDMD, but the processing capacity of GSDMD by caspase-8 is considerably lower compared to caspase-1.103 Furthermore, it has been discovered that the serine/threonine protein kinase polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) directly phosphorylates pro-caspase-8 at Ser305, thereby inhibiting its processing and caspase-3 activation under Fas stimulation, which suppresses GSDME formation and pyroptosis.82

PTMs of GSDME

GSDME, located on chromosome 7 (7p15), plays a role in regulating cell pyroptosis. When activated directly by caspase 3, the N-terminal domain of GSDME can form membrane pores to execute cell pyroptosis.104 At the same time, it can also be activated by GZMB, which is released by cytotoxic lymphocytes. Upon cleaved by GZMB, GSDME-N can induce tumour cell pyroptosis.19 Multiple studies have demonstrated that various PTMs can affect pyroptosis by regulating GSMDE.

Ubiquitylation

Analysis of multiple prostate cancer cohorts suggests that CDC20, the most significantly overexpressed E3 ligase, acts as a negative regulator in pyroptotic cell death by targeting GSDME for proteolysis mediated by ubiquitylation.83 In addition, ovarian tumour family deubiquitylation enzyme 4 (OTUD4) can deubiquitinate GSDME, enhancing its stability and GSDME-dependent pyroptosis, consequently improving radiosensitivity in nasopharyngeal carcinoma.84

Phosphorylation

Research demonstrates that mannose, either alone or in combination with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazine/iron, significantly enhances the phosphorylation of AMPK at Thr172, leading to AMPK activation. Subsequently, activated AMPK phosphorylates GSDME at Thr6, blocking caspase-3-induced GSDME cleavage and thereby inhibiting pyroptosis. These findings reveal a novel mechanism by which mannose antagonizes GSDME-mediated pyroptosis through AMPK activation and suggest the potential benefits of mannose supplementation in reducing chemotherapy-induced side effects in cancer patients during clinical practice.85

Succination

The succination of GSDME at Cys45 has a similar effect to the succination of GSDMD at Cys191 (human) or Cys192 (mouse), leading to the inhibition of GSDME-dependent pyroptosis.72

Palmitoylation

Several ZDHHC proteins, including ZDHHC-2, 7, 11, and 15, have been found to facilitate the dissociation of the GSDME-C and GSDME-N by inducing its palmitoylation at C407 and C408, resulting in pyroptosis induced by chemotherapy drugs.86

PARylation

Intense ultraviolet-C irradiation triggers the hyperactivation of nuclear PARP1, leading to the extensive formation of poly (ADP-ribose) (PAR) polymers. These PAR polymers are then released into the cytoplasm, where they activate PARP5 to promote GSDME PARylation, triggering a conformational change in GSDME and reducing autoinhibition. Additionally, ultraviolet-C irradiation stimulates cytochrome c-catalysed cardiolipin peroxidation, increasing lipid ROS levels. These ROS are detected by PARylated GSDME, prompting oxidative oligomerization and directing full-length GSDME to the plasma membrane for perforation, ultimately inducing pyroptosis. Both PARylation and ROS-mediated oxidation are necessary for full-length GSDME-driven pyroptosis.87

The regulation of GSDMA/B-dependent pyroptosis by PTMs

Like other gasdermins, GSDMA plays a crucial role in inducing pyroptosis by forming oligomers and inserting them into cellular or mitochondrial membranes, thus creating pores. Notably, GSDMA is a major gasdermin in the skin and is vital for immune defence against aggressive skin infections.20 Furthermore, the serine–threonine kinase PLK1 mediates the phosphorylation of GSDMA at Thr8, thereby hindering its oligomerization and pore formation, ultimately inhibiting pyroptosis.88,89 Concerning GSDMB, it exists in six splicing variants in humans, each displaying distinct expression profiles and varied subcellular localization in different cell types. GSDMB-mediated pyroptosis operates independently of the classical caspase pathway, involving protein cleavage mediated by perforin and granzyme, triggered by cytotoxic particles.105 Moreover, PTMs influence GSDMB-mediated pyroptosis. Notably, USP24 acts as a deubiquitinase for GSDMB in cancer, stabilizing GSDMB to activate STAT3 signalling, thereby amplifying GSDMB protein levels in bladder cancer cells, promoting pyroptosis and glycolysis, ultimately contributing to enhanced growth and invasion of bladder cancer cells.90 Furthermore, IpaH7.8, a virulence effector secreted by Shigella, ubiquitinates GSDMB at specific sites, inhibiting its pore-forming activity and leading to GSDMB proteasomal degradation. This hampers GSDMB's pyroptotic activity, enabling Shigella to effectively evade attacks from cytotoxic lymphocytes and natural killer cells during infection, thereby promoting bacterial survival in the host ecosystem.91 (Fig. 3).

Targeting PTMs to regulate pyroptosis for diseases treatment

The impact of PTMs on pyroptosis presents intriguing avenues for the development of targeted drugs to treat related diseases. Currently, numerous drugs have been discovered to modulate the PTMs of proteins involved in pyroptosis to either promote or inhibit the process.

Positive effects on pyroptosis

Research has investigated the potential anti-tumour effect of tetraarsenic hexoxide in invasive TNBC cells, revealing its ability to efficiently arrest tumor growth through the induction of cell pyroptosis. Mechanismly, tetraarsenic hexoxide exerts a pronounced inhibitory effect on the phosphorylation of mitochondrial STAT3, leading to a significant elevation in the generation of ROS. This in turn, induces a caspase-3-dependent cleavage of GSDME, ultimately leading to cell pyroptosis.80

Negative effects on pyroptosis

While enhancing the anti-tumour efficacy, chemotherapy-induced pyroptosis unfortunately also incurs damage to normal tissues, resulting in undesirable toxic and side effects. Recently, a study found that for patients undergoing chemotherapy, co-administration of mannose could reduce diarrhea and fecal white blood cells. Furthermore, in mouse models, the administration of mannose alleviated chemotherapy-induced injury in the small intestine and kidney, while not dampening the antitumor potency of platinum-based chemotherapeutic drugs (cisplatin and oxaliplatin). Mechanically, mannose metabolism increases the level of the metabolite N-acetylglucosamine 6-phosphate (GlcNAc-6P), which then binds to AMPK, promoting its phosphorylation. In turn, activated AMPK phosphates GSDME to inhibit cell pyroptosis. These results indicate that mannose supplementation is a simple, safe, and selective combined treatment approach to alleviate the toxic side effects induced by chemotherapy in cancer patients.85 Moreover, the benzoxathiole derivative BOT-4-one inhibits NLRP3 activation by alkylating NLRP3, effectively restraining downstream pyroptosis through two key mechanical changes: impairment of ATPase activity and enhancement of ubiquitylation level.56 In addition, the inhibitor 2-bromopalmitate can impede the palmitoylation of GSDME-C, thereby inhibiting the release of GSDME-C after GSDME-N cleavage and consequently suppressing chemotherapy-induced pyroptosis.86 Furthermore, the specific BRCC36 inhibitor G5 can inhibit proteasome deubiquitylation, thereby decreasing cell pyroptosis. These substances hold potential as targets for the treatment of inflammatory diseases.45

In a model of early brain injury after subarachnoid haemorrhage in rats, hypothermia treatment upregulates AMPK phosphorylation, downregulates the protein expression of NLRP3, ASC, cleaved caspase-1, GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18, and inhibits cell pyroptosis through an AMPK-dependent pathway, thereby alleviating brain oedema, neuronal damage, and inflammatory response.106 Furthermore, colchicine inhibit STAT3 phosphorylation by suppressing NLRP3 promoter acetylation via the STAT3/EP300 complex, thereby alleviating sepsis-induced acute lung injury.107 The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a critical role in the progression of inflammatory bowel disease and colitis-associated cancer (CAC). Caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE) enhances the binding of NLRP3 with ubiquitin molecules, promoting NLRP3 ubiquitination, thereby exerting anti-tumour effects.108 Tranilast effectively enhances NLRP3 ubiquitination, inhibits the assemble and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby suppressing pyroptosis, and improving vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis in low-density lipoprotein receptor and apolipoprotein E-deficient mice.109 Wedelolactone inhibits inflammasome activation and pyroptosis by enhancing PKA signalling to promote the Ser/Thr phosphorylation of NLRP3, thereby establishing its potential application in treating MSU-induced peritonitis and gouty arthritis.110 Overall, there is significant ongoing development of clinical drugs that target PTMs, offering promising prospects for future therapies in diseases related to pyroptosis (Table 2).Table 2Targeting pyroptosis-associated PTMs for disease treatment.InhibitorTarget spotPTMPyroptosis (types)FunctionTetraarsenic hexoxideSTAT3PhosphorylationGSDMEInhibit cancer progression80MannoseAMPKPhosphorylationGSDMEReduce chemotherapy induced side effect85BOT-4-oneNLRP3AlkylationGSDMDInhibit inflammation562-bromopalmitateGSDME-CPalmitoylationGSDMEInhibit chemotherapy-induced pyroptosis86BRCC36 inhibitor G5ProteasomeDeubiquitylationGSDMDInhibit inflammation45Mild hypothermiaAMPKPhosphorylationGSDMDInhibit inflammation106ColchicineSTAT3PhosphorylationGSDMDInhibit sepsis-induced acute lung injury107Caffeic acid phenethyl esterNLRP3UbiquitinationGSDMDInhibit inflammation108TranilastNLRP3UbiquitinationGSDMDReduces vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis109WedelolactoneNLRP3PhosphorylationGSDMDInhibit inflammation110

Conclusion and outstanding questions

Following the discovery by Shao et al. that the gasdermin family of proteins serves as the executioners of pyroptosis and elucidated its mechanisms, increasing evidence has shown that pyroptosis plays a critical role in the occurrence, progression, and treatment of various diseases.2 Additionally, the PTMs of pyroptosis-related proteins also play diverse regulatory roles in the occurrence of pyroptosis.111 This review provides a comprehensive overview of various PTMs affecting pyroptosis and their associated modifying enzymes. It also outlines the specific functions of these PTMs in pyroptosis and examines the regulation of PTMs involved in pyroptosis for potential therapeutic potential in cancer and inflammatory diseases. However, as an emerging field, there are still several intriguing and important aspects of this field that remain elusive and represent important topics for further investigation.

Uncovering the molecular mechanism of PTMs on pyroptosis

While some progress has been made in researching the impact of PTMs on pyroptosis, there are still mechanistic issues that require further investigation. PTMs not only independently affect protein functions, but more importantly, they often exhibit complex interactions and synergistic effects among them. Delving into the interaction networks among these PTMs and how they collectively influence specific cell death pathways is essential for a comprehensive understanding of the complexity and dynamism of this process. In different types of tumours or cells, there may be variations in the mechanisms of PTMs regulating pyroptosis, necessitating further exploration. While certain PTMs have been observed to impact cell pyroptosis, the associated modifying enzymes have yet to be identified, thereby impeding the study of targeting PTMs in the regulation of pyroptosis-related diseases. Moreover, it remains unclear whether additional forms of PTMs could exist and possess potential pyroptosis-regulating function. And, PTMs are frequently mediated by metabolites, and further research is needed to explore whether the PTMs involved in pyroptosis can be influenced by regulating metabolism.

The detection and diagnostic value of PTMs

Several types of PTMs have been linked to pyroptosis. However, the involvement of other forms of PTMs, such as succinylation, and malonylation, as well as undiscovered PTMs, in regulating pyroptosis, remains poorly understood. Advanced technological platforms, capable of comprehensive high-resolution mass spectrometric measurement and analysis, are crucial for the specific and sensitive detection of new protein alterations, enabling a deeper understanding of cellular mechanisms.112 The development of protein–protein interaction omics and flow omics may also contribute to the discovery of PTMs that play important roles in the occurrence of pyroptosis. Furthermore, the development of site-specific antibodies for detecting pyroptosis-related PTMs is also greatly needed and urgent.113 Further research is necessary to determine whether the PTMs levels of proteins can serve as indicators of patient prognosis and whether the PTMs of certain specific proteins can be used as diagnostic markers for cancers.114

Targeting PTMs to regulate pyroptosis for cancer immunotherapy

Cancer cell pyroptosis has been identified as a pro-inflammatory cell death, which could ignite adaptive antitumour immunity to enhance antitumour effect of cancer immunotherapy.19 Targeted therapy and chemotherapy can induce tumour cells pyroptosis, releasing a large amount of antigens and inflammatory substances, promoting the intratumoural infiltration of T cells, altering the “cold” tumour immune microenvironment to a “hot” one, thus overcoming tumour immune escape mechanisms and enhancing the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy.19,115 In addition, studies have found that immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy can induce GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in tumour cells, which is beneficial for activating adaptive antitumour immunity and thus enhancing the efficacy of ICB therapy.116 Exploring the molecular mechanisms of PTM regulation of pyroptosis and identifying potential therapeutic targets are crucial for enhancing drug-induced pyroptosis in tumours, which presents new avenues and strategies to enhance the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy.

Targeting PTMs to regulate pyroptosis for the treatment of inflammatory diseases

Pyroptosis is a pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death, which plays a significant role in the occurrence and progression of various inflammatory diseases including cardiovascular diseases, non-alcoholic fatty liver, PD, AD and ischemic stroke.4 Recently, multiple studies have confirmed that PTMs can indirectly regulate the occurrence and progression of inflammatory diseases by modulating the structure and function of proteins associated with the pyroptosis signalling pathway. This has deepened the understanding of the underlying mechanisms of inflammatory diseases and provided new ideas and therapeutic targets for their treatment. Typically modified proteins include apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) and NLRP3.10 For example, Actinidia arguta has been shown to regulate NLRP3 ubiquitination and ASC oligomerization, thus inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated IL-1β secretion. Additionally, acacetin can enhance gp78-mediated NLRP3 ubiquitination, thereby suppressing inflammation and improving depression-related dry eye syndrome.117 These key biochemical alterations regulating pyroptosis provide new pathways and strategies to enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapy for inflammatory diseases.

Developing strategies for targeting PTMs to regulate pyroptosis

Although some progress has been achieved in the study of PTMs influencing pyroptosis, the research on targeting PTMs for the treatment of related diseases still encounters certain difficulties and challenges including off-target effects, dosing issues, and potential resistance mechanisms. Due to the widespread nature of PTMs, the same type of PTM occurring on diverse proteins may yield varying effects on pyroptosis. Furthermore, the same type of PTMs may serve distinct roles in various cell types. Therefore, developing targeted strategies to specifically regulate pyroptosis-related PTMs is crucial for treating related diseases.118 Nanomedicine can be categorized into organic types (liposomes, polymeric micelles, albumin, etc.) and inorganic types (gold nanoparticles, quantum dots, etc.), providing effective and precise drug delivery systems for disease treatment.119,120 The future trend should focus on employing nanotechnology to deliver PTMs-related drugs, thereby reprogramming protein structure and function and impacting cell death, including pyroptosis.

Translating basic research to clinical application

By identifying novel therapeutic targets and biomarkers, these discoveries pave the way for the development of precision medicines, gene therapies, and immunotherapies in cancer and inflammatory diseases. Translating fundamental research on PTMs that regulate pyroptosis into clinical applications requires a comprehensive and multifaceted approach. This process includes the validation of crucial PTM regulators and modulators in preclinical models, as well as the design and execution of rigorous clinical trials to evaluate the safety and efficacy of potential therapies. By bridging the gap between bench and bedside, we can harness the power of PTM-based insights to develop innovative therapies that effectively target and modulate pyroptosis, ultimately advancing the treatment of pyroptosis-related diseases and improving patient outcomes.
