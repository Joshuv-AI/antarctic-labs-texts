A high-order matrix-free adaptive solver for the shallow water equations with irregular bathymetry
Luca Arpaia, Giuseppe Orlando, Christian Ferrarin, Luca Bonaventura

Abstract
We present the first step in the development of an Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) solver for coastal engineering applications, based on a high-order Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method as implemented in the deal.II library. This environment provides efficient and native parallelization techniques and automatically handles non-conforming meshes to implement both static and dynamic AMR approaches. The proposed method is automatically well-balanced, allows the use of realistic bathymetry data without any regularity assumption, and includes a consistent conservative discretization for transported chemical species. Numerical experiments on idealized benchmarks validate the proposed approach, while results obtained on realistic bathymetries and complex domains show its potential for accurate and efficient adaptive simulations of coastal flows.

1
Introduction

The shallow water equations are a simplified model of hydrostatic flows at small depth-to-width aspect ratio, which is widely used in river hydraulics and coastal engineering. A rigorous derivation of this model from the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations can be found, e.g., in
[
69
,
76
]
. A great number of different approximation techniques for their numerical solution has been proposed in the past, often tailored to address adequately the specific difficulties posed by different flow regimes. For low Froude number regimes, which will be the focus of this work, efficient semi-implicit finite difference methods were proposed, e.g., in the seminal papers
[
73
,
75
]
. These methods are extremely popular in the coastal engineering community and they have been extended to high-order finite elements, e.g., in
[
71
,
78
,
114
]
. The purpose of this work is to present the first step in the development of an adaptive solver for the shallow water equations for coastal engineering applications, which also inherits the efficiency and robustness properties of the methods introduced in
[
73
,
75
]
.

The proposed spatial discretization is based on a high order Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) approach. The application of the DG method for the numerical solution of the shallow water equations has been performed by many authors since the early 2000s, on Cartesian
[
112
,
118
]
and on unstructured meshes, see
[
83
,
94
]
among many others. We have used the DG implementation of the
deal.II
library
[
60
,
65
]
. This modelling framework has proven to be extremely useful for the implementation of numerical models for environmental flows, see, e.g.,
[
102
,
103
,
104
]
. The
deal.II
library provides efficient and native parallelization techniques and automatically handles non-conforming meshes to implement both static and dynamic Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) approaches with excellent scalability results, as shown in the previously cited papers. A numerical method for the shallow water equations based on
deal.II
was also recently proposed in
[
87
]
. However, the approach described in this work differs from that of
[
87
]
in terms of model equations, numerical discretization, and implementation, as we will briefly discuss in the following.

Our longer term goal is to implement efficient Implicit-Explicit Runge-Kutta (IMEX-RK) time discretization schemes with an implicit treatment of the pressure gradient terms, as already done in
[
102
,
103
]
for atmospheric models, thus guaranteeing time step restrictions based on velocity rather than celerity values. However, in this work we will restrict our attention to IMEX-RK time discretization methods that treat the pressure gradient terms explicitly, while applying the implicit scheme to the frictional terms, that can become very stiff when realistic friction models are employed on strongly varying bathymetries.

In this high-order framework, we focus on the treatment of the bathymetry, nowadays available with higher resolution than the mesh, in coastal areas. This poses a series of challenges for higher order methods that work on coarse meshes: the mesh may not be aligned to large bathymetric gradients and the finite element method should be able to handle such gradients within an element or along an edge. We propose a robust and accurate approach that uses the realistic bathymetry data without any regularity assumption. Similarly to the formulation considered in
[
74
]
, we choose as prognostic variable the free-surface elevation
\zeta
that, in subcritical regimes, is a smooth variable and thus suited for a finite element representation. The bathymetry
z_{b}
at the quadrature node is directed evaluated from the reference data without any modification. With respect to the conservation of mass, if the water depth is strictly positive, evolving the water depth or the free-surface elevation is equivalent. This is no longer true in presence of wetting and drying regions, because of the nonlinear relationship
h=\max\left(\zeta+z_{b},0\right)
. The choice of evolving the water depth assures mass-conservation straightforwardly and, for this reason, it is the preferred choice for flooding applications, for DG see
[
70
,
80
,
116
]
. However, a mass-conserving formulation that still keeps the free-surface elevation as prognostic variable was proposed in
[
74
]
and will be the basis for our treatment of wetting and drying in future work. For the purposes of this paper, we assume that
h>0
throughout the considered time interval and spatial domain. As in
[
74
]
we consider the hydrostatic pressure gradient in a non-conservative form. This choice is motivated by the fact that, as it will be shown, the hydrostatic well-balancing, known in the literature as the C-property
[
117
]
, follows automatically also in a high-order finite element context with this formulation. This is fully justified in the low-Froude regimes that are the target applications of the present work. Two non-conservative formulations of the pressure gradient are considered, introduced in
[
100
,
113
]
. Their relationship to the standard conservative formulation is discussed in detail, showing that they are equivalent for smooth bathymetries.

These choices yield a finite element discretization different with respect to well-balanced
[
107
,
117
,
118
]
or pre-balanced formulations
[
95
,
109
]
, for which the same polynomial representation of the water depth and bathymetry is employed. Hence, in the case of higher order polynomials and irregular bathymetry, smoothing or TVD limiting of the bathymetry is then needed to avoid or cure the well-known Gibbs phenomenon.

Following the analysis in
[
86
,
99
]
, we take special care to guarantee that the conservative discretizations of the continuity and tracer concentration equations are consistent. Failure of this consistency with continuity (CWC) property was shown in
[
86
]
to cause macroscopic violations of monotonicity even for completely monotonic methods. Indeed, we show that, for a non-polynomial bathymetry, a naif conservative update of the depth-integrated concentrations will fail to guarantee such a consistency. On the other hand, a consistent discretization that guarantees this property also for arbitrary bathymetries is proposed.

We test for the first time static and dynamic AMR based on the non-conforming meshes handled by
deal.II
for coastal engineering purposes. Well known applications of the AMR approach to the shallow water equations have been presented, among many others, in
[
97
,
106
]
and, with DG, in
[
81
,
91
]
. As discussed, e.g., in
[
62
,
77
,
98
,
106
]
, when performing dynamic adaptation with non-conforming meshes, guaranteeing simultaneously well-balancing and mass conservation is not straightforward. The discretization proposed here guarantees automatically well-balancing also in the case of dynamical AMR, while mass conservation is violated in that case. This is consistent with our focus on demonstrating the potential efficiency gain of dynamic AMR approaches for short term simulations, for which mass conservation is less relevant.

Numerical results obtained on idealized benchmarks validate the robustness and accuracy of the proposed method in presence of irregular bathymetry also with under-resolved features at
the grid scale. Simulations of coastal flows using realistic and complex domain geometries and bathymetries demonstrate the effectiveness of the resulting discretization as compared to state of the art coastal flow models and its good parallel performance.

The outline of the paper is the following. In Section
2
, the model equations are discussed. Their numerical approximation is introduced in Section
3
and the validation of the
deal.II
implementation of these methods, based on a number of relevant benchmarks of increasing complexity, is presented in Section
4
. Moreover, we consider a realistic bathymetry and a complex geometry in Section
5
, where we report results of semi-realistic simulations of the tidal circulations in the Venice Lagoon. A parallel efficiency analysis of the implementation, including dynamic AMR, is presented in Section
6
. Some conclusions and perspectives for the future developments mentioned before are reported in Section
7
, while a more detailed description of the time discretization schemes is reported in Appendix
A
.

2
Model equations

We consider the shallow water equations in the domain
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
,
t\in(0,T],
which can be formulated as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\zeta}}{\partial{t}}+\dive\mathbf{q}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathbf{q}}}{\partial{t}}+\dive\left(\mathbf{q}\otimes\frac{\mathbf{q}}{h}\right)=-gh\grad\zeta-\gamma\left(q,h\right)\mathbf{q}

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{hc_{k}}}{\partial{t}}+\dive\left(\mathbf{q}c_{k}\right)=h\phi_{k}\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{M}\right),\quad k=1,\dots,M,

endowed with suitable initial and boundary conditions. Here
T
is the final time,
\zeta
denotes the free-surface elevation, whereas
z_{b}
denotes the bathymetry depth, both computed with respect to a fixed reference height, so that
h=\zeta+z_{b}
is the total fluid depth. We denote by
\mathbf{q}=(hu,hv)^{\top}
the mass flux or discharge per unit area, while
\vel=(u,v)^{\top}=\mathbf{q}/h
denotes the vector of vertically averaged velocities and
\gamma
denotes a non-negative friction coefficient, which will depend on the absolute value of the discharge
q=|\mathbf{q}|
and on the fluid depth
h
. More specifically, we will consider the friction model

\gamma\left(q,h\right)=\frac{gn^{2}q}{h^{\frac{7}{3}}},

(2)

where
n
denotes the so-called Manning friction coefficient.
The quantities
c_{k},k=1,\dots,M
represent the concentrations of
M
chemical or biological species with reaction rates
\phi_{k}
, whose motion is determined by that of the fluid. Finally,
g=$9.81\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-2}$
is the gravitational acceleration. Notice that no assumption is made on the regularity of the bathymetry
z_{b}
that can be discontinuous.

Similarly to the formulation considered in
[
74
]
, system (
2
) has two main differences with respect to well-balanced
[
87
,
107
,
117
]
or pre-balanced formulations
[
95
,
109
]
. A first main difference is the use of the free-surface elevation
\zeta
as prognostic variable. As already remarked, in subcritical regimes,
\zeta
is a smooth variable and thus suited for a finite element representation. A second key difference with respect to other standard formulations is that system (
2
) considers the hydrostatic pressure gradient in a non-conservative form. As it will be shown, hydrostatic well balancing follows automatically also in a high-order finite element context with this formulation.

3
Numerical discretization

In this Section, we outline the numerical methods employed for the spatial and temporal discretization of system (
2
)
and we analyze in detail the treatment of the non conservative free-surface gradients and the CWC property of the resulting scheme.

3.1
Time discretization

For the time discretization, an IMplicit-EXplicit (IMEX) Runge-Kutta (RK) method
[
89
]
will be used. These methods are useful for time dependent problems that can be formulated as
\mathbf{y}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{f}_{S}(\mathbf{y},t)+\mathbf{f}_{NS}(\mathbf{y},t)
, where the
S
and
NS
subscripts denote the stiff and non-stiff components of the system, respectively, to which the implicit and explicit companion methods are applied. IMEX-RK methods are represented compactly by the two Butcher tableaux
[
72
]
:

\mathbf{c}

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{b}^{\top}

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}

\tilde{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}

with
\mathbf{A}=\left\{a_{lm}\right\},\mathbf{b}=\left\{b_{l}\right\},\mathbf{c}=\left\{c_{l}\right\},\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\left\{\tilde{a}_{lm}\right\},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\left\{\tilde{b}_{l}\right\}
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}=\left\{\tilde{c}_{l}\right\}
. Coefficients
a_{lm},b_{l}
, and
c_{l}
denote the coefficients of the explicit companion method, while
\tilde{a}_{lm},\tilde{b}_{l}
, and
\tilde{c}_{l}
denote the coefficients of the implicit companion method. The coefficients are determined so that the method is consistent of a given order. In particular, in addition to the order conditions specific to each sub-method, the coefficients should respect specific coupling conditions, as discussed in detail in
[
105
]
, to which we refer for a complete presentation (see also the discussion in Appendix
A
). We consider Diagonal Implicitly Runge-Kutta (DIRK) methods for the implicit companion scheme, so that
\tilde{a}_{lm}=0
for
l<m
. If
\mathbf{v}^{n}\approx\mathbf{y}(t^{n})
, the generic
l-
stage IMEX-RK method can be defined therefore as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{v}^{(n,l)}=\mathbf{v}^{n}&+&\Delta t\sum\limits_{m=1}^{l-1}\left[a_{lm}\mathbf{f}_{NS}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(n,m)},t+c_{m}\Delta t\right)+\tilde{a}_{lm}\mathbf{f}_{S}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(n,m)},t+\tilde{c}_{m}\Delta t\right)\right]\\
&+&\Delta t\,\tilde{a}_{ll}\,\mathbf{f}_{S}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(n,l)},t+\tilde{c}_{l}\Delta t\right),\end{array}

where
l=1,\dots,s
. After the computation of the intermediate stages,
\mathbf{v}^{n+1}
is computed as

\mathbf{v}^{n+1}=\mathbf{v}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{l=1}^{s}\left[b_{l}\mathbf{f}_{NS}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(n,l)},t+c_{l}\Delta t\right)+\tilde{b}_{l}\mathbf{f}_{S}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(n,l)},t+\tilde{c}_{l}\Delta t\right)\right].

The generic semi-discrete IMEX-RK stage for system (
2
) with implicit treatment of the friction term then reads as follows:

\displaystyle\zeta^{(n,l)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{n}-\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}\dive\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}

\displaystyle h^{(n,l)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{(n,l)}+z_{b}

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}^{(n,l)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}^{n}-\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}\dive\left(\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}\otimes\frac{\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}}{h^{(n,m)}}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}gh^{(n,m)}\grad\zeta^{(n,m)}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}\tilde{a}_{lm}\gamma\left(q^{(n,m)},h^{(n,m)}\right)\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}-\Delta t\tilde{a}_{ll}\gamma\left(q^{(n,l-1)},h^{(n,l-1)}\right)\mathbf{q}^{(n,l)}

\displaystyle h^{(n,l)}c_{k}^{(n,l)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h^{n}c_{k}^{n}-\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}\dive\left(\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}c_{k}^{(n,m)}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}h^{(n,m)}\phi_{k}\left(c_{1}^{(n,m)},\dots,c_{M}^{(n,m)}\right),\qquad k=1\dots M.

Notice that the source terms representing biogeochemical reactions have been discretized in time with the explicit part of the IMEX method. In most commonly used biogeochemical models, these terms are non-stiff and often simply discretized by the forward Euler method in practical applications. However, inclusion of stiff chemical reactions discretized by the implicit part of the IMEX scheme would be possible along the same lines, resulting in a set of decoupled
M\times M
nonlinear systems for each discrete degree of freedom. The full discretization of these terms will not be considered further in this paper, but it can be included without changes in the general structure of the proposed method and will be taken into account in more realistic simulations to be carried out in future work.

The nonlinear dependence of the friction term on the discharge and fluid depth values is linearized in time, so as to avoid the solution of a nonlinear system at each time step. Since the stability restrictions based on celerity do not allow the use of very large time steps for this approach and considering also the uncertainties associated with the empirical friction formulae usually employed to define
\gamma
, this simplification does not imply a significant loss of accuracy. Furthermore, similar simplifications were performed in
[
113
]
in the context of large time step, high-order DG methods without degrading the accuracy of the results. All the details of the Butcher tableaux used for the time discretization are reported in Appendix
A
.

We stress the fact that we solve for the concentration and not for the depth-integrated concentration, as it would be natural for a conservative formulation. As explained later in this Section, this allows us to obtain a discretization that is consistent with that of the continuity equation. This choice also simplifies the implementation of the initial and boundary conditions, as well as the output of the concentration variables.

3.2
Spatial discretization

For the spatial discretization, we employ a standard nodal DG method
[
82
]
, which is implemented in the framework of the
deal.II
library
[
60
,
65
]
. We consider a decomposition of the domain
\Omega
into a family of quadrilaterals
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}
and denote each element by
K
with mesh size
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. The skeleton
\mathcal{E}
denotes the set of all element faces and
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}^{I}\cup\mathcal{E}^{B}
, where
\mathcal{E}^{I}
is the subset of interior faces and
\mathcal{E}^{B}
is the subset of boundary faces. Then, suitable jump and average operators have to be defined for discontinuous finite element discretizations. A face
\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{I}
shares two elements that we denote by
K^{+}
with outward unit normal
\mathbf{n}^{+}
and
K^{-}
with outward unit normal
\mathbf{n}^{-}
, whereas for a face
\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{B}
we denote by
\mathbf{n}
the outward unit normal. For a scalar function
\varphi
the jump is defined as

\left[\!\left[\varphi\right]\!\right]=\varphi^{+}\mathbf{n}^{+}+\varphi^{-}\mathbf{n}^{-}\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{I}\qquad\left[\!\left[\varphi\right]\!\right]=\varphi\mathbf{n}\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{B}.

The average is defined as

\left\{\!\!\left\{\varphi\right\}\!\!\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varphi^{+}+\varphi^{-}\right)\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{I}\qquad\left\{\!\!\left\{\varphi\right\}\!\!\right\}=\varphi\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{B}.

Similar definitions apply for a vector function
\bm{\varphi}
:

\displaystyle\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}\right]\!\right]=\bm{\varphi}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}+\bm{\varphi}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{-}\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{I}\qquad\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}\right]\!\right]=\bm{\varphi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{B}

\displaystyle\left\{\!\!\left\{\bm{\varphi}\right\}\!\!\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\varphi}^{+}+\bm{\varphi}^{-}\right)\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{I}\qquad\left\{\!\!\left\{\bm{\varphi}\right\}\!\!\right\}=\bm{\varphi}\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{B}.

For vector functions, it is also useful to define a tensor jump as:

\left<\!\left<\bm{\varphi}\right>\!\right>=\bm{\varphi}^{+}\otimes\mathbf{n}^{+}+\bm{\varphi}^{-}\otimes\mathbf{n}^{-}\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{I}\qquad\left<\!\left<\bm{\varphi}\right>\!\right>=\bm{\varphi}\otimes\mathbf{n}\quad\text{if }\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}^{B}.

We also introduce the following finite element spaces

Q_{r}=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):v\rvert_{K}\in\mathbb{Q}_{r}\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}\right\}\qquad\mathbf{V}_{r}=\left[Q_{r}\right]^{d},

where
\mathbb{Q}_{r}
is the space of polynomials of degree
r
in each coordinate direction. We then denote by
\bm{\varphi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})
the basis functions for the space
\mathbf{V}_{r}
and by
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})
the basis functions for the space
Q_{r}
, the finite element spaces chosen for the discretization of the discharge and of the free-surface height (as well as the concentration of the chemical species), respectively, so that

\mathbf{q}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{d\left|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}\right|(r+1)^{d}}q_{j}(t)\bm{\varphi}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\qquad\zeta\approx\sum_{j=1}^{\left|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}\right|(r+1)^{d}}\zeta_{j}(t)\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x}).

The shape functions correspond to the products of Lagrange interpolation polynomials for the support points of
\left(r+1\right)
-order Gauss-Lobatto quadrature rule in each coordinate direction. Notice that, while equal polynomial degrees have been used for the discretization of the free-surface and discharge variables, the implementation framework would easily allow to use a higher polynomial degree for the discharge if required by specific applications.

Furthermore, it is important to remark that the total fluid depth
h
is not expanded in terms of the basis functions, but rather computed only at the quadrature nodes using the second equation in (
3.1
). This allows us to use non-polynomial representations of the bathymetry.
We will further discuss this point in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
.

Given these definitions, the algebraic system for the continuity equation (
3.1
) at each stage reads as follows:

\mathbf{M}\bm{\zeta}^{(n,l)}=\mathbf{M}\bm{\zeta}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}\mathbf{F}^{(n,m)}.

(4)

Here,
\mathbf{M}
denotes the mass matrix,
\bm{\zeta}^{(n,l)}
denotes the vector of the degrees of freedom associated to the free-surface height, and
\mathbf{F}^{(n,l)}
represents the DG approximation of the right-hand side of the continuity equation at previous stages according to (
3.1
). More specifically, we have set

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\psi_{j}\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(5a)

\displaystyle F_{i}^{(n,m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}\cdot\grad\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(5b)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\left\{\!\!\left\{\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}\right\}\!\!\right\}-\frac{\lambda^{(n,m)}}{2}\left[\!\left[\zeta^{(n,m)}\right]\!\right]\right)\cdot\left[\!\left[\psi_{i}\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma.

Notice that, for discontinuous finite elements, the mass matrix is block diagonal, since the support of the test functions associated to a given element
K
is restricted to that element. The integral at the interface between two elements is computed with a Rusanov flux
[
111
]
, so that:

\displaystyle\lambda^{(n,m)}=\max\left(\left|\vel^{+,(n,m)}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}\right|+\sqrt{gh^{+,(n,m)}},\left|\vel^{-,(n,m)}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{-}\right|+\sqrt{gh^{-,(n,m)}}\right)

(6)

Analogously, the algebraic system for the discharge equation at each stage reads as follows:

\mathbf{A}^{(n,l)}\mathbf{Q}^{(n,l)}=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}\mathbf{G}^{(n,m)}+\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}\tilde{a}_{lm}\mathbf{L}^{(n,m)},

(7)

\mathbf{Q}^{(n,l)}
denotes the vector of the degrees of freedom associated to the discharge field,
\mathbf{A}^{(n,m)}
denotes a modified mass-matrix,
\mathbf{G}^{(n,l)}
and
\mathbf{L}^{(n,l)}
represents the DG approximation of the non-stiff and stiff components of right-hand side of the discharge equation according to (
3.1
) and evaluated at the previous stages. Here we have therefore set

\displaystyle A_{ij}^{(n,m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\left[1+\tilde{a}_{ll}\Delta t\gamma\left(q^{(n,l-1)},h^{(n,l-1)}\right)\right]\bm{\varphi}_{j}\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(8a)

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\mathbf{q}^{n}\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(8b)

\displaystyle G_{i}^{(n,m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}\otimes\frac{\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}}{h^{(n,m)}}:\grad\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(8c)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\left\{\!\!\left\{\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}\otimes\frac{\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}}{h^{(n,m)}}\right\}\!\!\right\}+\frac{\lambda^{(n,m)}}{2}\left<\!\left<\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}\right>\!\right>\right):\left<\!\left<\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right>\!\right>\mathrm{d}\Sigma

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}gh^{(n,m)}\grad\zeta^{(n,m)}\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}\left(g\left\{\!\!\left\{h^{(n,m)}\right\}\!\!\right\}\left\{\!\!\left\{\zeta^{(n,m)}\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]-g\left\{\!\!\left\{h^{(n,m)}\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\zeta^{(n,m)}\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]\right)\mathrm{d}\Sigma

\displaystyle L_{i}^{(n,m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\gamma\left(q^{(n,m)},h^{(n,m)}\right)\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(8d)

The algebraic formulation for the equations of the concentrations at each stage can be written in a similar manner

\mathbf{H}^{(n,l)}\mathbf{C}^{(n,l)}=\tilde{\mathbf{C}}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}\mathbf{R}^{(n,m)},

(9)

where now the reaction source terms have been omitted. Here,
\mathbf{C}^{(n,l)}
denotes the vector of the degrees of freedom associated to the concentration
c
of the generic species.
\mathbf{H}^{(n,m)}
denotes a modified mass matrix where we have condensed the water depth and
\mathbf{R}^{(n,l)}
represents the DG approximation of the right-hand side of the concentration equation at previous stages according to (
3.1
). More precisely,

\displaystyle H^{(n,l)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}h^{(n,l)}\psi_{j}\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(10a)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}h^{n}c^{n}\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(10b)

\displaystyle R_{i}^{(n,m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}c^{(n,m)}\cdot\grad\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(10c)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\left\{\!\!\left\{\mathbf{q}^{(n,m)}c^{(n,m)}\right\}\!\!\right\}-\frac{\lambda^{(n,m)}}{2}\left[\!\left[\zeta^{(n,m)}c^{(n,m)}\right]\!\right]\right)\cdot\left[\!\left[\psi_{i}\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma.

The previously described discretization can be extended seamlessly to non-conforming, locally refined meshes, thanks to the flexible implementation of such meshes available in
deal.II
. These capabilities were already successfully exploited in
[
100
,
103
,
104
]
for the discretization of the Euler equations. Here, we follow the same approach as in these works. For the adaptive simulations, we denote the case-dependent refinement indicator on each element by
\eta_{K}>0
. We refine and coarsen an element based on the following criteria:

if

\displaystyle\eta_{K}>\theta_{r}\,\eta_{max}\textrm{ and }n_{K}<n_{max}\textrm{ and }\mathcal{H}_{K}>\mathcal{H}_{min}

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{refine }K

if

\displaystyle\eta_{K}<\theta_{c}\,\eta_{max}

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{coarsen }K

where

\eta_{max}=\max_{\mathcal{K\in T_{H}}}\eta_{K},

whereas
\theta_{r}
and
\theta_{c}
are the refinement and coarsening thresholds, respectively. We denote by
n_{K}
the mesh level of the element, ranging from the coarsest level of the initial mesh,
n_{K}=1
, to the finest level
n_{K}=n_{max}
. For initial meshes with a variable mesh size, we also set a minimum target mesh size
\mathcal{H}_{min}
, below which a cell cannot be refined, even if
n_{K}<n_{max}
. Notice that, as discussed, e.g., in
[
62
,
77
,
98
,
106
]
, performing
dynamical mesh adaptation with non conforming meshes may lead to violations of either the C-property or mass conservation. The approach proposed here does not guarantee mass conservation in the dynamical refinement case, while its well-balancing and C-property remain unaffected, due to the choice of
\zeta
as a prognostic variable. On the other hand, the method is both conservative and well balanced on statically adaptive meshes. In Section
5
we control the relative mass conservation error for a realistic adaptive coastal application.

Equations (
2
) do not include physical viscosity nor diffusion beyond the friction term and all numerical
experiments in Sections
4
and
5
have been carried out without adding numerical viscosity or diffusion. However, for the realistic tests in Section
6
, a small numerical diffusion term was added to the concentration equation, as a simple monotonization mechanism. This is necessary to control spurious oscillations that arise in regions with large tracer gradients. This numerical dissipation was included adding to (
10c
) the term:

-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\nu_{c}h^{(n,m)}\grad c^{(n,m)}\cdot\grad\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(11)

where
\nu_{c}
is a constant eddy diffusivity scaled by the polynomial degree
r
and by the level of refinement, i.e.
\nu_{c}=C\left(2^{(n_{max}-1)}r\right)^{-2}
, and the constant
C
is to be
chosen empirically for each case.

3.3
Properties of the spatial discretization

Although most of the DG approximation outlined above is relatively standard, we discuss here in detail some of its specific and appealing properties for simulations of coastal flows in realistic domains. Firstly, the treatment of the non-conservative pressure term is based on a double integration by parts, as originally proposed in
[
66
]
. More specifically, as in
[
113
]
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}ghD_{h}\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}gh\grad\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}g\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left\{\!\!\left\{\zeta\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma-\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}g\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\zeta\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}gh\grad\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}d\Omega-\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}g\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left\{\!\!\left\{\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right\}\!\!\right\}\cdot\left[\!\left[\zeta\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma,

where in the last relation we have employed the so-called
magic formula

[
61
]
:

\left[\!\left[\zeta\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]=\left\{\!\!\left\{\zeta\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]+\left[\!\left[\zeta\right]\!\right]\cdot\left\{\!\!\left\{\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right\}\!\!\right\}.

(13)

For a continuous bathymetry, formulation (
12
) can be reinterpreted as a DG approximation of the pressure term written in flux form under the so-called strong form, which simply stands for a double integration by parts
[
63
,
68
]
. More specifically, starting from the conservative formulation of the pressure gradient and considering a centered flux for the pressure gradient, after a double integration by parts we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\left(D_{h}\left(P\mathbf{I}\right)+ghD_{h}z_{b}\right)\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}gh\grad\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(14)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}\left\{\!\!\left\{P\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]d\Sigma-\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}\left[\!\left[P\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}gh\grad\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega-\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}\left[\!\left[P\right]\!\right]\cdot\left\{\!\!\left\{\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right\}\!\!\right\}\mathrm{d}\Sigma,

with
P=\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}
denoting the shallow water pressure. Thanks to (
13
), in the case of continuous bathymetry, we obtain

\left[\!\left[P\right]\!\right]=g\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[h\right]\!\right]=g\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\zeta\right]\!\right],

so as to recover relation (
12
). Formulation (
12
) is similar, but not identical to that later proposed in
[
101
]
, which reads as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}ghD_{h}\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}gh\grad\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(15)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}g\left\{\!\!\left\{h\zeta\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma-\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}g\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left[\!\left[\zeta\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]\mathrm{d}\Sigma.

After some algebraic manipulations, one obtains

\left\{\!\!\left\{h\zeta\right\}\!\!\right\}=\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left\{\!\!\left\{\zeta\right\}\!\!\right\}+\frac{1}{4}\left[\!\left[h\right]\!\right]\cdot\left[\!\left[\zeta\right]\!\right],

(16)

so that (
15
) reduces to

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}ghD_{h}\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}gh\grad\zeta\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(17)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\Gamma}g\left(\frac{1}{4}\left[\!\left[h\right]\!\right]\left[\!\left[\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right]\!\right]-\left\{\!\!\left\{h\right\}\!\!\right\}\left\{\!\!\left\{\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right\}\!\!\right\}\right)\cdot\left[\!\left[\zeta\right]\!\right]d\Sigma.

We have inspected numerically the difference between the two formulations in the idealized tests, and no noticeable differences have been found. One can easily notice that both relation (
12
) and relation (
17
) satisfy the C-property exactly, without any constraint either on the quadrature formula or on the regularity of the bathymetry. Indeed, independently of
z_{b}
, one has
\grad\zeta=0
in
K
and
\left[\!\left[\zeta\right]\!\right]=0
on
\Gamma
if
\zeta=const
.

The choice of the numerical quadrature formula is related to the need to integrate accurately the nonlinear advection term, which consist of non-polynomial terms. For integrals over the inter-element boundaries
\Gamma
we use a Gauss-Legendre formula with
\textrm{floor}(\frac{3}{2}r)+1
quadrature points, whose degree of exactness is then
3r
. The same formula is applied to integrals over elements
K
by considering tensor products of the quadrature points over the reference quadrilateral. Only for the mass matrix integrals we use a Gauss-Legendre quadrature formula with
r+1
points along each coordinate direction. Notice that, since the entries of the mass matrix (
5a
) consist of the product of two basis functions, no aliasing error is introduced. The use of the same number of quadrature points and degrees of freedom along each coordinate direction yields, after a change of basis, diagonal blocks in the algebraic structure of the mass matrix
[
92
]
. More specifically, as explained in
[
92
,
93
]
, the mass matrix is inverted as
\mathbf{M}^{-1}=\mathbf{S}^{-T}\mathbf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{S}^{-1}
. Here
\mathbf{J}
is a diagonal matrix, whose entries are equal to the determinant of the Jacobian times the quadrature weight, while
\mathbf{S}
is a square matrix with basis functions in the row and quadrature points in columns, i.e.
S_{ij}=\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
. The matrix
\mathbf{S}
is constructed as the Kronecker product (tensor product) of small one-dimensional matrices that can be inverted efficiently at each stage. The same approach is applied to the modified mass matrices (
8a
) and (
10a
). Notice that, if friction is present and for a general bathymetry, the entries can be no longer represented in general as the product of polynomials.

Another important theoretical property that is easy to verify for the proposed discretization is the consistency of the discrete forms of the concentration equation and continuity equation. As remarked in
[
86
]
, this property is essential to guarantee monotonic solution for tracer transport coupled to free-surface flow, whenever flux form discretizations are employed. Inserting a constant concentration, into the concentration equation
9
and comparing (
5b
) and (
10c
), one obtains

R^{(n,m)}_{i}=F^{(n,m)}_{i}.

Furthermore, one has

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}h^{(n,l)}\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}h^{n}\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega+\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}F^{(n,m)}_{i}.

(18)

Using the definition of the water depth, one obtains

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\left(\zeta^{(n,l)}+z_{b}\right)\psi_{i}d\Omega=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\left(\zeta^{n}+z_{b}\right)\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega+\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}F^{(n,m)}_{i}.

(19)

The integrals in (
19
) are computed by numerical integration, in which only the nodal values of the (in general) non-polynomial function
z_{b}
are involved. These terms appear in an identical fashion on the left and right-hand side, so after cancellation and expansion of the polynomial expression of the free-surface in terms of basis functions we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{\left|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}\right|(r+1)^{d}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\psi_{j}\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega\,\zeta^{(n,l)}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\left|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}\right|(r+1)^{d}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\psi_{j}\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\Omega\,\zeta^{n}_{j}+\Delta t\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}a_{lm}F^{(n,m)}_{i},

(20)

which is identical to the discrete continuity equation (
4
). If instead the vertically integrated concentration
m=ch
had been chosen as prognostic variable to be expanded in terms of the local polynomial basis, this would have entailed the need to compute, in
R^{(n,m)}_{i}
, the quotients
m/h
to recover the concentration variables. Due to the non-polynomial nature of
h
in our formulation, this would lead to the impossibility to guarantee theoretically the consistency with the fully discrete formula (
4
), resulting in spurious monotonicity violations.

4
Idealized numerical experiments

The numerical method outlined in Section
3
is now validated in a number of relevant idealized benchmarks. In the numerical experiments without friction, we test different fully explicit time discretizations beyond the three-stage second-order Runge-Kutta (RK32), which is the explicit companion method of the IMEX-RK method (see Appendix
A
). More specifically, we employ the third order Strong Stability Preserving schemes
[
85
]
, denoted as SSP33, and the classical fourth order Runge-Kutta scheme (RK44), see, e.g.,
[
96
]
. The time discretization is chosen to match the formal order of accuracy of the spatial discretization.

If an exact solution is available, accuracy is measured by the
L^{2}
error norm on the free-surface and of the discharge, which we compute as

\left|\left|\zeta-\zeta_{ex}\right|\right|^{2}_{2}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\left(\zeta-\zeta_{ex}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega\qquad\left|\left|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}_{ex}\right|\right|^{2}_{2}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int_{K}\left|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}_{ex}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(21)

4.2
Preservation of hydrostatic equilibrium

In order to check numerically the preservation of the hydrostatic equilibrium, or C-property, that was discussed in Section
3
, we consider the classical lake-at-rest benchmark. A rectangular basin is considered with horizontal dimensions
$2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$\times$1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
and constant free-surface height value of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
. The bathymetry profile is given by the non-smooth function

z_{b}(x,y)=-z_{b0}\exp\left(\psi(x,y)\right),

with

z_{b0}=$0.65\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$,\quad\psi=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\left(x-0.9\right)^{2}+\left(x-0.5\right)^{2}},&\text{if }\mathbf{x}\in\left(0.9,1.1\right)\times\left(0.3,0.7\right)\\
-5\left(x-0.9\right)^{2}-50\left(x-0.5\right)^{2},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where all the length values given above are in meters. The mesh has
40\times 20
elements arranged in a Cartesian pattern. The elements have randomly distorted edges in correspondence of the discontinuity (Figure
3
). As a result, the jump in the bathymetry is not aligned with the edges, but it crosses the elements and the edges themselves. In Table
2
we report the errors at
T=$48\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
, which corresponds to 10000-45000 time steps, depending on the polynomial order. It can be observed that the hydrostatic balance is preserved up to machine precision without any need of special treatment of the fluxes or the bathymetry.

Next, we consider a small amplitude perturbation of the lake-at-rest free-surface. The perturbation is confined in a narrow band:

\zeta(\mathbf{x},0)=1+\begin{cases}$0.01\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$,&\text{if }$0.05\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$\leq x\leq$0.15\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The perturbation generates two small waves. The left-propagating wave leaves the domain from the left boundary, thanks to a non-reflecting boundary condition, while the right-propagating one travels over the irregular bathymetry and is deformed as a consequence. We test the ability of the numerical scheme to handle robustly the irregular bathymetry by observing that small amplitude wave patterns are not polluted by spurious oscillations generated at the bathymetry jump. A reference solution is computed on a very fine regular mesh composed of
2000\times 1000=2000000
points with a robust, oscillation-free Finite Volume scheme. The FV scheme scheme is obtained from the numerical method of Section
3
with the choices of piecewise constant polynomials (
r=0
) and forward Euler time stepping.

Parallel efficiency

Finally, we have assessed the parallel performance of our adaptive model in a strong scaling test. The test is based on the travelling vortex benchmark described in Section
4.1
. We keep constant the size of the problem, or the total number of degrees of freedom, and increase the number of processors used for the computation. We have pushed the level of refinement to
n_{max}=7
for the static simulation and
n_{max}=8
for the adaptive simulation. With a polynomial of degree
r=3
, this corresponds to 157,286,400 degrees of freedom for the static simulation and 61,160,640 for the adaptive simulation. All the other parameters are the same as in Section
4.1
, included the final time. Figure
19
shows the measured speed-up on a bilogarithmic scale, which is computed assuming that the wall time for 1 processor is 48 times slower than the wall time for 48 processors. The tests were performed on the Galileo100 machine at the CINECA computing centre. The nodes used for the computations are x86 Intel Xeon Platinum 8276/L 2.4GHz bi-processors with 384 GB RAM. Each node consists of 48 processors. The measured scalability is very satisfying, with an efficiency of 82.8% and 87.6% for the static and the adaptive run, respectively, up to 1536 processors.

7
Conclusions

We have proposed and validated a high-order Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) solver for coastal engineering applications, based on the
deal.II
library. The library capabilities have been exploited to handle automatically non-conforming meshes and implement both static and dynamic AMR approaches. In this work, only discretization methods have been employed that treat the free-surface gradients in an explicit way, as a first step towards the application of advanced and high-order IMEX techniques. Thanks to the use of the free surface as prognostic variable, which is well justified for the foreseen application regimes, the proposed method is automatically well-balanced, allows the use of realistic bathymetry data without any regularity assumption, and includes a consistent conservative discretization for transported chemical species. More specifically, since the bathymetry values are only evaluated at the quadrature points, the method allows to employ realistic bathymetric datasets without need of any smoothing or pre-processing.

We have performed a wide range of numerical experiments on idealized benchmarks, showing the effectiveness of the AMR approaches in maintaining a high level of accuracy while reducing the computational cost. Results obtained on realistic bathymetries and complex domains show the potential of the method for accurate and efficient adaptive simulations of coastal flows. The good parallel performance of the AMR implementation has also been demonstrated in a strong scaling test.

In future work, we aim to extend the present approach by a mass-conserving treatment of wetting and drying based on the proposal in
[
74
]
, also addressing the mass conservation issues for dynamically adapted meshes. We then plan to implement efficient Implicit-Explicit Runge-Kutta (IMEX-RK) time discretization schemes with an implicit treatment of the pressure gradient terms, as already done in
[
102
,
103
]
for atmospheric models, thus guaranteeing time step restrictions based on velocity rather than celerity values. Finally, we plan to include basic eddy viscosity turbulence models and to proceed to validate the resulting adaptive coastal flow model on completely realistic benchmarks.
