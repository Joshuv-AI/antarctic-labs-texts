Brain imaging in girls with central precocious puberty onset between the ages of six and eight: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Sin-ting Tiffany Lai, Chi-hung Patrick Cheung, Kwok-leung Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain imaging is recommended for girls with central precocious puberty (CPP) that begins before the age of six; however, its necessity remains controversial for those with onset between the ages of six and eight. We aim to evaluate the role of brain imaging in girls experiencing the onset of puberty within this age range.MethodsThis retrospective observational study evaluated 162 girls diagnosed with CPP in Hong Kong from January 2010 to December 2020. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings for 139 of these girls were reviewed. Clinical findings of 125 girls who entered puberty between the ages of six and eight were analyzed.Results15.8% (22 of 139) of girls with CPP had abnormal MRI findings. The incidence was lower in girls who began puberty between six and eight years of age (13.6%, 17 of 125 girls) compared to those who started puberty before the age of six (35.7%, 5 of 14 girls), with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.047). Abnormal MRI findings in the 17 girls who started puberty between six and eight years of age included: a RCC (n = 6), a pituitary microadenoma (n = 4), a pituitary macroadenoma (n = 1), either a RCC or a pituitary microadenoma (n = 2), a pineal cyst (n = 1), an arachnoid cyst in the right posterior fossa with a RCC (n = 1), pituitary hyperplasia along with a RCC and an arachnoid cyst in the right middle fossa (n = 1), and a duplicated posterior pituitary gland (n = 1). None of the girls had neurological concerns or required neurosurgical interventions during the follow-up period of 4.6 ± 1.9 years. Among the 125 girls who began puberty between the ages of six and eight, no significant differences in clinical findings were observed between those with normal and abnormal MRIs.ConclusionsThe likelihood of identifying clinically significant intracranial pathologies through routine brain imaging in girls who started puberty between the ages of six and eight is low. In the absence of neurological concerns, watchful observation may be appropriate, provided that parents are fully informed of the minimal risk of overlooking underlying intracranial pathology.

Introduction

Central precocious puberty (CPP) results from the early activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, which may lead to impaired final height and negative psychological effects [1, 2]. Additionally, CPP may indicate a potential intracranial pathology. While the prevalence of brain abnormalities varies from 40% to 75% among boys with CPP, it is up to 10% in girls. Most cases of CPP in girls are idiopathic [3–5]. A consensus statement published in 2009 and updated in 2019 recommends brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for all boys with CPP and for girls who begin puberty before the age of six; however, this recommendation remains controversial for girls who start puberty between the ages of six and eight [6, 7]. Consequently, many tertiary centers routinely perform brain MRIs on all girls with CPP. In 2015, the US Food and Drug Administration reported unclear health implications associated with brain deposits resulting from contrast agents commonly used in MRI procedures [8, 9]. Routine brain MRI not only imposes financial burdens and increases parental anxiety but also introduces risks associated with sedation and the application of contrast.

Based on epidemiologic studies from the 1950s, which indicated that 95% of girls experienced the onset of breast development between the ages of 8.5 and 13 years [4, 10], the age limit for diagnosing precocious puberty in girls is commonly accepted as before the age of eight [4, 11]. Since the mid-20th century, a downward secular trend in pubertal timing has been observed in the US, Europe [12, 13], and Asia [13–18]. Nonetheless, lowering the age limit for sexual precocity increases the risk of overlooking true rapidly progressive precocious puberty and potential underlying intracranial pathologies. As a result, sexual precocity, defined as the onset of puberty before the age of eight in girls, remains recognized in clinical practice.

With a downward secular trend in the timing of puberty worldwide, an increasing number of girls are expected to experience the onset of puberty between the ages of six and eight. We present the brain MRI findings in girls with CPP, as well as the clinical findings of those who began puberty between the ages of six and eight, to clarify recommendations for brain imaging in this patient group.

Methods

This retrospective observational study evaluated data on girls diagnosed with CPP managed at the Department of Pediatrics and Adolescent Medicine at the United Christian Hospital between January 2010 and December 2020. The study protocol was approved by the Kowloon East and Kowloon Central Cluster Research Ethics Committee under the Hong Kong Hospital Authority (KC/KE-21-0027/ER-2).

Females with the diagnostic code (ICD-9) for ‘precocious puberty’ or ‘precocious sexual development and puberty’ during the specified period were identified using the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System of the Hospital Authority. Electronic records were reviewed. Exclusion criteria included CPP not confirmed by history, physical examination, and biochemical testing; failure to undergo contrast brain MRI; or the presence of pre-existing brain pathologies.

Precocious sexual development was defined as the onset of breast development before eight years of age, accompanied by clinical, biochemical, and radiological signs of pubertal activation [3–5, 10, 19, 20]. CPP was diagnosed based on a basal luteinizing hormone (LH) level greater than 0.3 IU/L, a luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) stimulated peak LH greater than 5 IU/L, or a stimulated LH to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) ratio greater than 0.66 [21—24]. An LHRH stimulation test was conducted following a standard protocol [21—24]. LHRH 2.5 micrograms/kg (maximum of 100 micrograms) was administered intravenously at 0 min. LH, FSH, and estradiol levels were measured at 0 min, and LH and FSH levels were measured at 30 and 60 min. Bone age assessment was conducted using a radiograph of the left hand and wrist, reported according to the Greulich and Pyle standard system. A transabdominal pelvic ultrasound was performed to detect pubertal changes in the ovaries and uterus [19].

All girls with CPP were offered a contrast brain MRI. The MRI findings were classified as either normal or abnormal. Based on their causal association with CPP, as determined by consensus in the literature, abnormal MRIs were further categorized into (1) pathological findings with an established causal association, (2) abnormal hypothalamic-pituitary findings with a questionable association, and (3) abnormal non-hypothalamic-pituitary findings that were likely incidental, to better inform their clinical significance [25–28].

Clinical information regarding the timing of pubertal onset and progression was collected. Results for basal LH, FSH, and estradiol, as well as stimulated LH and FSH, along with findings from bone age assessments and pelvic ultrasounds, were obtained. Additional clinical details, to include neurological concerns (such as headaches, visual disturbances, vomiting, and seizures), past medical history, body weight (BW), body height (BH), body mass index (BMI), and sexual maturity rating at the time of diagnosis, were collected. Standard deviation scores (SDS) for BW, BH, and BMI were calculated using the Hong Kong Chinese reference [29]. The treatment received and the follow-up periods were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize general and demographic data. Parametric continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-parametric continuous data were presented as median (interquartile range). To compare differences between two groups of continuous data, the independent-sample t-test was applied for parametric data, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-parametric data. Categorical data were expressed as absolute proportions N (%). The Pearson Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact tests were performed, depending on sample size, to compare two categorical data groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with significant differences in univariate analysis were further analyzed using a multivariate regression model.

Results

Study population

A total of 162 girls diagnosed with CPP were identified. Twenty girls opted not to undergo brain MRIs. Additionally, three girls were excluded due to pre-existing brain conditions, specifically two with encephalomalacia from perinatal injury and one with a hypothalamic hamartoma, resulting in a cohort of 139 girls (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the cohort are presented in Table 1. Among the 139 girls with CPP who underwent brain MRI, 14 (10.1%) started puberty before the age of six, while 125 (89.9%) began puberty between the ages of six and eight.

Brain imaging findings in girls with CPP

Most girls with CPP (117 out of 139, 84.2%) had normal brain MRIs. Twenty-two girls (15.8% of 139) had abnormal MRI findings: 17 (out of 125 girls; 13.6%) began puberty between the ages of six and eight, and 5 (out of 14 girls; 35.7%) started puberty before the age of six (p = 0.047) (Fig. 1).

The abnormal MRI findings in the five girls who began puberty before age six included a hypothalamic hamartoma (n = 1), a RCC (n = 1), a pituitary microadenoma (n = 1), a left frontal arachnoid cyst (n = 1), and pituitary hyperplasia (n = 1). Among the 17 girls who began puberty between the ages of six and eight, the findings included: a RCC (n = 6), a pituitary microadenoma (n = 4), a non-functioning pituitary macroadenoma (n = 1), either a RCC or a pituitary microadenoma (n = 2), a pineal cyst (n = 1), an arachnoid cyst in the right posterior fossa with a RCC (n = 1), pituitary hyperplasia along with a RCC and an arachnoid cyst in the right middle fossa (n = 1), and a duplicated posterior pituitary gland (n = 1) (Table 2). One girl (out of 139 girls with CPP; 0.7%) who started puberty before the age of six had a hypothalamic hamartoma, which was the only pathological finding in the cohort. The remaining 21 girls (out of 139 with CPP; 15.1%) had either abnormal hypothalamic-pituitary findings (17 girls; 12.2%), abnormal non-hypothalamic pituitary findings (2 girls; 1.4%), or both (2 girls; 1.4%). The most common findings were RCC (n = 7) and microadenoma (n = 6). None of the girls with abnormal MRIs had neurological concerns or required neurosurgical intervention during a mean (± SD) follow-up period of 4.6 (± 1.9) years.

Discussion

Our study found that 15.8% of girls with CPP had abnormal MRI findings, which was significantly lower in those who began puberty between six and eight years of age (13.6%) compared to those who started puberty before the age of six (35.7%) (p = 0.047). A systematic review and meta-analysis from 2018 reported that the pooled prevalence of abnormal MRI findings in girls with CPP was 9% (of 1853 girls), higher in those who began puberty before the age of six (25% of 173 girls) compared to those between the ages of six and eight (3% of 514 girls) [30]. Subsequent studies examining brain imaging in girls with CPP revealed abnormal MRI findings in 8.2% to 58% of cases [26—28, 31—33]. A study in Italy, which reported the highest rate at 58%, attributed this to improved technical detection and a strict selection criterion of rapidly progressive puberty [26]. Nonetheless, our findings were consistent with the majority of existing literature, indicating that 8.2% to 21.5% of girls with CPP had abnormal MRI findings, which were more common in those who began puberty before the age of six compared to those who started puberty between the ages of six and eight [11, 30–33].

Only one girl in our cohort (out of 139 girls with CPP; 0.7%) had a pathological finding of a hypothalamic hamartoma, which had an established causal association with CPP [11, 34]. She started puberty at the age of 4.5 years and presented at 5.1 years with Sexual Maturity Rating breast development stage 3. LHRH stimulation test conducted at the age of 5.4 years confirmed the diagnosis of CPP. Brain MRI performed at the age of 5.5 years revealed a T1- and T2-isointense, non-enhancing tuber cinereum hamartoma measuring 0.9 × 0.7 × 1 cm. She was commenced on gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogue therapy. Follow-up contrast MRI scans at the ages of six and eight demonstrated stable lesions. She had no neurological symptoms and did not require neurosurgical intervention. Another girl, who was excluded from the current cohort, had a pre-existing hypothalamic hamartoma (measuring 2.1 × 1.3 × 1.3 cm), which initially presented with gelastic seizures at the age of three. Surgical excision was performed at the age of 4.9 years due to intractable epilepsy. She developed CPP at the age of five and was commenced on GnRH analogue therapy. Follow-up MRI showed a stable residual tumor measuring 1.7 × 1 × 1 cm.

Within our cohort, all girls who began puberty between the ages of six and eight exhibited abnormal MRI findings that were either of questionable causal association with CPP or incidental. Additionally, none of the girls with abnormal MRI had neurological symptoms or required neurosurgical intervention. Similar findings have been observed in the existing literature. A study in Helsinki with 41 girls experiencing puberty onset between the ages of six and eight identified a craniopharyngioma [31]. In another study in Korea involving 317 girls with CPP who underwent MRI, although the exact age of puberty onset was not specified, one case of choroid plexus papilloma was observed [32]. The most common findings were pituitary hyperplasia and RCC, and none required neurosurgical intervention [32]. Another study in Korea involving 55 girls with puberty starting between the ages of six and eight found one girl with a hypothalamic hamartoma, which was considered pathological [27]. The most common findings in this study were RCC, pineal cyst, pituitary microadenoma, and partial empty sella, all of which were regarded as either of questionable relevance to CPP or incidental [27]. Similarly, a Taiwan study involving 251 girls with CPP identified one girl with a hypothalamic hamartoma who started puberty before the age of six [33]. The most frequent lesions in this group were pituitary microadenomas and pituitary pars intermedia cysts/RCC, none of which required neurosurgical intervention [33]. The Italian study, which reported the highest rate of abnormal MRI findings at 58% among 112 girls with CPP, found that the most common findings were inter-hypothalamic adhesions, pituitary pars intermedia cysts/RCC, and nonspecific brain T2 hyperintensities, and none required neurosurgical intervention [26]. However, 11 brain tumors were identified, including seven girls who experienced puberty onset between the ages of six and eight [26]. Notably, another Turkish study involving 654 girls who experienced puberty onset between the ages of six and eight identified two girls with brain tumors [28]. The absence of pathological MRI findings in our cohort of girls who started puberty between the ages of six and eight could be due to a small sample size, which limited its statistical power.

The majority of previous studies compared girls with CPP began before the age of eight with normal and abnormal MRI. A study in Italy reported that girls with hypothalamic hamartoma were younger than six years of age and had significantly higher mean baseline and stimulated LH values, LH-to-FSH ratios, serum 17β-estradiol levels, and uterine lengths [8]. Another study in Copenhagen demonstrated a statistically higher height SDS, basal LH, and peak LH following the LHRH stimulation test in girls with intracranial pathologies [25]. Conversely, two studies in Helsinki and Korea revealed no significant differences in clinical findings between girls with and without intracranial pathologies [31, 32]. In contrast to previous studies, the current study focused on comparing the findings of girls who experienced puberty onset between six and eight years of age, with normal and abnormal MRIs; however, we did not observe any significant difference in clinical findings between the two groups.

Overall, although no predictive factors were identified, the likelihood of detecting clinically significant intracranial pathologies through routine brain imaging in girls who began puberty between the ages of six and eight remained low. Based on the observation that the majority of abnormal MRI findings were either of questionable causal association with CPP or incidental and did not require neurosurgical intervention, in the absence of neurological concerns, watchful observation without immediate brain imaging may be appropriate, provided that parents are fully informed of the minimal risk of overlooking underlying intracranial pathologies. Parents should be provided with comprehensive information regarding the risks and benefits of brain imaging to facilitate an informed decision.

The present study has several limitations. It is a retrospective, single-center study conducted among the Chinese population in Hong Kong, which may limit its generalizability to other populations because pubertal timing varies across ethnic subgroups. Additionally, the study had a limited sample size, which reduced its statistical power, particularly in subgroup analyses. Furthermore, not all girls diagnosed with CPP underwent brain imaging in our study. However, the proportion of girls who received brain MRIs (142 out of 162; 87.7%) was relatively high compared to previous studies. Conducting a larger-scale prospective study would be instrumental in validating these findings and providing more definitive guidance regarding brain imaging in this demographic.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that girls who began puberty between the ages of six and eight were significantly less likely to have abnormal MRI findings compared to those who started puberty before the age of six. Most of the abnormal MRI findings were of questionable causal association with CPP or incidental. We questioned the necessity of routine brain MRI in girls who started puberty within this age range without neurological concerns, proposing that watchful observation might be adequate, provided that parents were well-informed about the small risk of overlooking underlying brain pathology.
