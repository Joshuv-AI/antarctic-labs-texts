Monitoring weekly δ13C variations along the cambium–xylem continuum in the Canadian eastern boreal forest
Authors: Sepideh Namvar, Étienne Boucher, Annie Deslauriers, Hubert Morin, Martine M Savard, Frida Piper
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntra-annual variations of carbon stable isotope ratios (δ13C) in different tree compartments could represent valuable indicators of plant carbon source–sink dynamics, at weekly time scale. Despite this significance, the absence of a methodological framework for tracking δ13C values in tree rings persists due to the complexity of tree ring development. To fill this knowledge gap, we developed a method to monitor weekly variability of δ13C in the cambium–xylem continuum of black spruce species [Picea mariana (Mill.) BSP.] during the growing season. We collected and isolated the weekly incremental growth of the cambial region and the developing tree ring from five mature spruce trees over three consecutive growing seasons (2019–21) in Simoncouche and two growing seasons (2020–21) in Bernatchez, both located in the boreal forest of Quebec, Canada. Our method allowed for the creation of intra-annual δ13C series for both the growing cambium (δ13Ccam) and developing xylem cellulose (δ13Cxc) in these two sites. Strong positive correlations were observed between δ13Ccam and δ13Cxc series in almost all study years. These findings suggest that a constant supply of fresh assimilates to the cambium–xylem continuum may be the dominant process feeding secondary growth in the two study sites. On the other hand, rates of carbon isotopic fractionation appeared to be poorly affected by climate variability, at an inter-weekly time scale. Hence, increasing δ13Ccam and δ13Cxc trends highlighted here possibly indicate shifts in carbon allocation strategies, likely fostering frost resistance and reducing water uptake in the late growth season. Additionally, these trends may be related to the black spruce trees' responses to the seasonal decrease in photosynthetically active radiation. Our findings provide new insights into the seasonal carbon dynamics and growth constraints of black spruce in boreal forest ecosystems, offering a novel methodological approach for studying carbon allocation at fine temporal scales.Keywords: cambium, xylem, tree ring, carbon isotope composition, nonstructural carbohydrates, boreal

Introduction

Carbohydrate source–sink dynamics are fundamental aspects of tree growth and can modulate the response to environmental changes (Dietze et al. 2014). Nonstructural carbohydrates (NSCs) are the predominant products of photosynthesis occurring in the leaves (Carbone et al. 2013). Once produced, NSCs may be allocated to build structural components including cell walls in tree rings (e.g., cellulose), or can be stored in the form of starch for future utilization (Hartmann and Trumbore 2016). A comprehensive body of knowledge on NSC dynamics has been generated from studies investigating carbohydrate concentrations in various tree sinks over time (Richardson et al. 2013, Fajstavr et al. 2018, Piper 2020, Rademacher et al. 2022, Rathgeber et al. 2022). However, there are still uncertainties regarding the processes and pathways related to NSCs allocation in mature living trees (Wiley and Helliker 2012).

In the stem, the cambium–xylem growing continuum is a critical site where carbohydrate dynamics can be tackled (Deslauriers et al. 2014). The cambial region (i.e., cambium and differentiating phloem and xylem cells), an important sink for NSCs synthesized in leaves, is at the origin of wood production (Giovannelli et al. 2011, Rossi et al. 2013, Simard et al. 2013). Cambium’s NSC source is mostly limited to leaf-originating photosynthates unloaded from the phloem (Gessler et al. 2014). However, cambium-derived sinks such as earlywood or latewood are possibly fed synergistically from two different sources: cambial NSCs and NSCs stored in ray parenchyma cells (starches and soluble sugars) (Giovannelli et al. 2011, Dietze et al. 2014, Deslauriers et al. 2016). These variable source–sink dynamics may introduce significant time lags between the synthesis and fixation of photosynthates in xylem tissue, impeding our capacity to model and predict growth responses to a changing environment. The impact of storage on stem growth during the initial stages of wood formation has been proven for deciduous trees (Kagawa et al. 2006, Simard et al. 2013), where there is a larger number of wood parenchyma cells compared with conifers (Schweingruber et al. 2007). However, there are exceptions to this pattern, as some research on Mediterranean Pinus pinea L. demonstrated that both current and stored carbon sources are significant for development of tree rings in this conifer species (Castagneri et al. 2018).

Stable carbon isotope ratios in tree ring cellulose (δ13C) may greatly help trace different NSCs' origin, translocation and utilization by various plant growth sinks (Gessler et al. 2009). Photosynthetic discrimination of 13CO2 against 12CO2 in C3 plants is associated with the ratio of leaf internal to external CO2 partial pressure (ci/ca) which, itself, is regulated by stomatal conductance (gs) and the rate of CO2 assimilation (Farquhar et al. 1982). Hence, the δ13C value of current NSCs should, in theory, reflect the interplay between such controlling factors at the time of synthesis in leaves. By contrast, the δ13C values of stored NSCs could diverge from that of current assimilates, particularly if storage includes carbohydrates in the form of 13C-enriched starch (MacNeill et al. 2017), that were synthesized at different times of the year, and potentially during previous years. In boreal conifers for instance, a high amount of starch reserves is built during spring, especially in the needles, prior to growth activation and remobilized for both primary and secondary growth later during the growing season (Deslauriers et al. 2019, Tixier et al. 2019, Cartenì et al. 2023). If this holds true, then δ13C values in the cambial region (δ13Ccam, mainly derived from current photosynthates) and the xylem cellulose (δ13Cxc, derived from a mixture of current + stored NSCs) should diverge in a manner that is somewhat proportional to the amount of stored assimilates involved in growth during xylogenesis, either coming from leaves or other woody parts of the tree. While more information is available on the chemical and isotopic properties of different wood constituents (Helle et al. 2022 and literature therein), few studies have examined the variations in sugars and chemical compounds in the cambium zone (Giovannelli et al. 2011, Simard et al. 2013, Deslauriers et al. 2016). Consequently, δ13Ccam values remain unknown. Therefore, differences in the δ13C values of NSCs from source (leaves-cambium) to sink (xylem) provide an ideal opportunity to track down the utilization and remobilization of NSCs by trees.

One of the most important obstacles that prevents tracking δ13C values from source to sink is the absence of a methodological framework enabling the construction of intra-annual δ13C chronologies along the growing cambium–xylem continuum. Recent improvements in dendro-isotopic analysis, such as dividing wood segments or cores by cutting tangential slices manually or using the microtome (Verheyden et al. 2004, Ogée et al. 2009, Roden et al. 2009, Pons and Helle 2011, Fu et al. 2017, Belmecheri et al. 2018, Barçante Ladvocat Cintra et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2022) or by laser ablation techniques (Schollaen et al. 2014, Loader et al. 2017, Saurer et al. 2023) have greatly enhanced the possibility to study isotopic composition in tree rings at intra-annual resolution (Schollaen et al. 2014). In these studies, each ring sector is assumed to be associated with a specific time period based on its position within the previously developed ring. However, despite their seemingly straightforward structure, new anatomical studies suggest that tree rings cannot be reduced to a sequence of cell rows formed during distinct and regular time intervals (Cuny et al. 2015, Buttò et al. 2019). Instead, a given cell row within a tree ring results from multiple, largely successive, developmental processes (e.g., cell enlargement, cell wall thickening, etc., Figure 1) most of which overlap in timing, rate and duration (Ziaco et al. 2016, Fonti et al. 2018). Knowing this, an alternative option for tracking intra-annual variations would be the live δ13C monitoring in both cambium and xylem, during the growing season. The method would be intrinsically cumulative (i.e., sampling during wood formation in current tree ring), rather than sequential (i.e., sampling after ring formation is complete). In other words, it would measure incremental changes in fractionation with a precise dating. A procedure developed by Giovannelli et al. (2011) overcame the challenge of separating the cambial region from other tissues, including phloem and xylem, for NSC determination (Deslauriers et al. 2014, 2016). The ‘scraping method’ employed in this research offered the opportunity to collect samples from the cambial region throughout the year, enabling the examination of metabolic alterations during the process of tree-ring formation. However, to this date, no conductive method has been developed to analyze the intra-annual δ13C variations in the cambial region where the secondary growth occurs.

Here, we developed a method to investigate, at a weekly resolution, the co-evolution of δ13C values in the cambium and forming-mature xylem region of mature black spruce [Picea mariana (Mill.) BSP.] trees, in the eastern Canadian boreal forest. This study aims to provide insights into how δ13C values fluctuate in the cambium–xylem continuum over the entire growing season and how each tissue responds to the carbon demand for stem growth. We hypothesize that δ13Cxc and δ13Ccam either do not correlate or vary with some temporal lag. This is because xylem cellulose has access not only to newly formed assimilates but also to stored carbohydrates with relatively high δ13C values.

Materials and methods

Study area

The research was carried out in the Saguenay-Lac-Saint-Jean region, located in the boreal forest of Quebec, Canada (Figure 2). Two specific sites of Simoncouche (SIM, 338 m a.s.l.) and Bernatchez (BER, 611 m a.s.l.) with mature black spruce stands were selected. Black spruce is one of the most prevalent species in boreal forests of Northern America. This slow-growing conifer typically establishes after forest fires and has a shallow root system, primarily located within the upper 20 cm of organic soil (Belien et al. 2012). Monitoring was performed between May and October in 2019–21, and in 2020–21 for SIM and BER, respectively (Table 1). Meteorological stations were installed in each site, consisting of a 30-m tower equipped with an automatic data acquisition system to which several measuring devices are attached. Various climatic variables including maximum temperature, vapor pressure deficit (VPD), photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), relative humidity (RH), precipitation and soil water content were recorded on an hourly basis (Table 2). The climatic data indicated a mean annual air temperature of 3.2 °C in SIM (2019–21) and 0.6 °C in BER (2019–21). During the growing season (May–Oct) mean temperatures decreased by ~2.5 °C, dropping from 12.8 °C in SIM to 10.3 °C in BER. This change reflects a colder and shorter growing season in BER (days with minimum daily temperature above 5 °C on average: SIM 170 ± 20 days, BER 139 ± 15 days, mean ± standard deviation) (Rossi et al. 2011, Buttò et al. 2019). Annual mean precipitation was higher in SIM compared with BER with 640.6 and 570.1 mm, respectively (2019–21). However, the mean precipitation during the growing season was similar with 484 mm of rain in both sites (Table 1).

Sample preparation

In both sites, five mature black spruce trees were chosen for each growing season in 2019 (SIM only), 2020 and 2021 (see Table S1 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). The selected trees were healthy, with over 50% of branches alive in the crown. To avoid reaction wood, the tree samples were grown on flat surfaces with upright stems. To estimate the average age of the black spruce stands at each site, we counted the tree rings in cores extracted from the trunk of each selected tree sample using an increment borer (Grissino-Mayer 2003). The mean tree age was estimated to be 78 ± 7.7 years in SIM and 133 ± 4 years in BER with a mean stem diameter of 24.8 ± 3.8 cm in SIM and 23.6 ± 1.8 cm in BER (Table 1). The diameter of the selected trees was large enough to support the sampling of 13 to 22 wood strips during the growing season (Table S2 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). To provide high-quality weekly samples and sufficient mass for IRMS analysis, the strips were collected on the selected trunks starting at the beginning of the growing seasons (late April) up to the end of the growing seasons in October. To reduce possible damage to the tree’s cambium layer and its growth, sampling was done on different parts of the stem, preferably at breast height (1.35 m), in accordance with the micro-core collection methodology outlined in previous studies (Rossi et al. 2011, Buttò et al. 2019).

Rectangle wood strips of ~ 3 × 6 cm, including the bark (phloem) and wooden part (xylem), were separated from the trunk using a wood chisel and a mallet. In the laboratory, the samples were divided in two parts along the tangential plane in the cambial region, either manually or with a surgical scalpel (Figure 1) (Giovannelli et al. 2011). The two separated parts (Figure 3a) were plunged into liquid nitrogen for 1 min to stop any enzymatic activity followed by lyophilization for 5 days at a constant temperature of −50 °C under vacuum (0.05 mbar or less). The wood strip separation between phloem and xylem was more problematic at the growing season's beginning (May) and ending (October), because of the absence of dividing cambium and elastic tissues linked with enlarging phloem and xylem cells. For these samples, separation after freeze-drying was more efficient. The loss of intra and intercellular water in the cambial region during the drying process caused the shrinkage of the developing elastic tissues, leading to an easier separation of the two parts (Giovannelli et al. 2011).

Sampling the cambial region

The cambial region, probably including a few enlargement cells (Deslauriers et al. 2016), was collected by gently scraping the bark's inner part (Figure 3b) and the xylem's outermost part, according to Giovannelli et al. (2011). The cambial region was clearly identifiable as a thin dark brown layer on the phloem (light brown area) and as a thin transparent layer on the outermost part of the xylem sample. To reduce the costs of stable isotope analysis, pooling of the cambial region material was done for each sampling date including all five trees (Table S2 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). To maximize homogenization, the pooled samples were ground using a two-bladed mixer mill (Retsch MM 400, Haan, Germany) at 30 Hz for 30 s.

Sampling the developing xylem (α-cellulose)

The separation of the currently forming ring was done from the last date of the growing season (mid-October) when the tree ring was formed completely. To do so, the transverse section was sanded and moisturized, and the forming ring was cut using a razor blade under a binocular microscope with 10× magnifying lenses and LED ring light (Figure 3c). For narrower rings, at the beginning of the growing season, cutting from the transverse section was not possible because of a very low number of cells. Therefore, the tangential area was moisturized, and the cells were scraped (in the form of fiber) with a surgical scalpel (Figure 3d) down to the previous year’s tree ring. The difference in color and brightness between the previous year’s tree ring (light brown and bright) and newly produced cells (white and opaque) facilitated the scraping process and its intensity.

The collected xylem samples for each date of sampling were ground into fine particles (≤0.1 mm) in the mixer mill at 30 Hz frequency each for 3 min. Pooling was done for a few of the xylem samples from the beginning dates of the growing season when there was not enough mass to perform the cellulose extraction separately (Table S2 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). The samples weighing <5 mg, acquired from the beginning dates of wood formation (early June), were excluded due to the small amount of cellulose extraction material.

Cellulose was extracted from xylem samples following a modification of the method described by Epstein et al. (1976). The extraction involved three consecutive steps to eliminate soluble organic compounds, lignin and hemicellulose, respectively. Between 15 and 20 mg of ground sample was loaded in Teflon/polyester filter bags (ANKOM® XT4) and heat-sealed for cellulose extraction. The filter bags were placed into a 1:1 solution of toluene: ethanol (to extract lipids and resins) and placed into the ultrasonic bath for 90 min, followed by a period of 80 min in acetone. The bags were boiled for 1 h to extract soluble organic compounds. A sodium chlorite/acetic acid solution was used to ‘bleach’ the samples over a 3-h extraction in heated (80 °C) demineralized water to remove lignin. To obtain pure α-cellulose, the samples were placed in a strong (17% w/v) NaOH solution followed by an acetic acid solution (10%) to neutralize the pH, with each step followed by extensive rinsing with double-distilled water. The α-cellulose was dried under the hood at room temperature for 24 h.

Analysis of carbon stable isotope ratios

All isotopic measurements were performed at the light-stable isotope geochemistry laboratory at the University of Quebec (Geotop, Montreal, Canada). Between 0.5 and 0.8 mg of cellulose (for xylem samples, δ13Cxc) or pooled bulk material (for the cambial samples, δ13Ccam) were weighed into tin capsules to obtain the same amount of CO2 for all samples and reference materials. The samples were then analyzed with a Micromass model Isoprime 100 isotope ratio mass spectrometer coupled to an Elementar Vario MicroCube elemental analyzer in continuous flow mode. The overall analytical uncertainty was better than ±0.1‰. This uncertainty was based on the propagation of uncertainties of the normalization of the internal reference materials and the samples, but did not include the homogeneity nor the representativity of the sample (Hélie and Hillaire-Marcel 2021).

Climate response and δ13Ccam and δ13Cxc variations

In order to compare our weekly values with meteorological variability, the δ13Ccam and δ13Cxc values were first corrected to account for changes in atmospheric δ13C (δ13Catm). This has two benefits. First, it corrects for the Suess effect, which is the progressive decline in δ13Catm, due to fossil emissions, since 1850 (Pre-Industrial reference, δ13Catm_PI = −6.61‰) (McCarroll and Loader 2004, Belmecheri and Lavergne 2020). Second, it accounts for the seasonal variability in δ13Catm, attributable to the preferential sequestration of 12C during photosynthesis, leaving a progressively enriched atmosphere as photosynthesis increases (Ballantyne et al. 2010). Unfortunately, on site measurements of δ13Catm were unavailable. In order to perform these corrections, we used the δ13Catm data collected from the NOAA-ESRL tall tower at Argyle, ME, USA (AMT, 45.03°N, 68.68°W, δ13Catm_AMT, https://gml.noaa.gov/dv/iadv/) in conformity with our sampling dates. The Argyle site is the closest δ13Catm measurement station to our study region. The tower is located in a mixed (deciduous and evergreen) forest in rural central Maine, north of Bangor, at 50 m above sea level. The Argyle site is the closest site, but it is quite a different ecosystem from the one studied here. We therefore also performed a similar correction using comparable, although remotely located, boreal forest δ13Catm data. Indeed, we performed the same calculations, this time using the δ13Catm Lac La Biche, Alberta, Canada (LLB, 54.95°N, 112.47°W, δ13Catm_LLB). This site is located at slightly the same altitude as our study region (540 m above sea level) and is dominated by P. mariana and Larix laricina, two species found locally in our study region. However, the δ13Catm data for this site were only available between 2008 and 2013 (https://gml.noaa.gov/dv/iadv/), so we only considered the average δ13Catm seasonal cycle for the LLB site and applied it uniformly to all years (Table S3 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online).

Pearson correlations were employed to assess the relationships between δ13Ccam_cor and δ13Cxc_cor values corrected using the AMT dataset and the different climatic parameters. The weekly daytime (08:00 to 18:00 h) values of six different climate parameters: maximum temperature, VPD, PAR, RH, precipitation and soil volumetric water content (VWC), were averaged on time periods (day of year) matching those of sampling intervals for each year and site (Table 2). The climate data were obtained from in situ meteorological stations at each site.

The cumulative sampling method developed here may induce time discrepancies when weekly δ13Cxc_cor values are compared with weekly climatic variables. This is because values measured using our method are intrinsically additive rather than ‘instantaneous’. Specifically, the δ13Cxc_cor values measured at week t represents the fractionation during week t which adds up to the fractionation of the preceding weeks. To minimize these discrepancies and remove the associated autocorrelation in δ13Cxc_cor measurements, we weighted and isolated the fractionation at week t and subtracted it from that of previous weeks (δ13Cxc_seq; Method S2 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). Pearson correlations were then used to analyze the relationships between the detrended series of δ13Cxc_seq and climatic variables at tree level and site level.

Results

Our method facilitated the acquisition of varying quantities of materials from the cambial region and developing tree ring depending on the date of sampling and the size of the strips, both in SIM and BER. At the beginning (late April–May) and end (Sep–Oct) of the growing season, 5–10 mg of scraped tissues from the cambial region were obtained only from the inner side of the bark. However, during the xylem differentiation phase (June–August), the amounts increased to 15–30 mg in each tree sample. Moreover, we obtained sufficient masses of xylem cells to perform weekly δ13Сxc analysis on individual trees in both sites. At the beginning of wood formation (June to early July), 10–20 mg of materials were obtained using the scraping technique. The cutting of the widening ring (July–Oct) yielded masses over 20 mg per tree. Pooling of xylem cells was only necessary in SIM 2020 and BER 2020 at the earliest dates of wood formation (mid-June), at times when xylogenesis was beginning (Figure 4b and d).

Intra-annual δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc variability

During the full growing season, all δ13Сcam profiles showed an increasing trend, with a slight decrease observed at the very end of the growing season in most profiles (Figure 4). SIM and BER sites showed comparable increasing δ13Сcam trends (F = 1.0, ρ = 0.3 for 2020, F = 0.5, ρ = 0.5 for 2021) although with different intercepts (F = 71.4, ρ < 0.0001 for 2020, F = 14.3, ρ < 0.001 for 2021) (Figure 5a and b). Compared with BER, the δ13Сcam mean in SIM was significantly higher by +0.8 and +0.2 ‰, in 2020 and 2021, respectively (ρ < 0.001) (Table 3).

A consistent seasonal pattern of increasing δ13Сxc was observed in most replicates from the first sampling date to the last for all sites and years investigated (see Figure S1 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). In SIM, 9 out of 15 trees presented a significant positive δ13Сxc trend (average slope parameter, β = 0.008, ρ < 0.05). In BER, the δ13Сxc series depicted similar patterns: 7 out of 10 trees also presented significant positive trends (β = 0.009, ρ < 0.05) (Table S4 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online).

Inter-tree δ13Сxc correlations were relatively high in each study year ranging between −0.4 to +0.9 in SIM 2019–21 (Figure 6a–c) and +0.1 to +0.8 in BER 2020–21 (Figure 6d and e). In SIM, Rbar varied between 0.1 and 0.5 over the three growing seasons (Table 3). The inter-tree statistics were improved once tree no.1 in SIM 2019 and tree no.5 in SIM 2021 were removed. These trees showed negative correlations with some of other individuals (Figure 6a and c). This removal boosted the Rbar to 0.6 (2019) and 0.3 (2021). The EPSw also increased to 0.9 and 0.7 once these trees were removed. In SIM 2020, the Rbar and EPSw were 0.5 and 0.8, respectively. In BER, Rbar was 0.4 and EPSw was 0.8 both in 2020 and 2021. Despite the high variability in inter-tree correlation, inter-tree δ13C variability remained quite low in both sites (standard deviations of averages for trees were lower than 0.5‰, Table 3).

Inter-annual δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc variability

During each of the investigated growing seasons, δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc covaried in both sites (Figure 4): positive correlations in SIM for 2019 (r = +0.8, ρ < 0.01), 2020 (r = +0.9, ρ < 0.001) and 2021 (r = +0.4, ρ < 0.2), and in BER for 2020 (r = +0.8, ρ < 0.01) and 2021 (r = +0.8, ρ < 0.01) (Table 3). Increasing trends in δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc are comparable for all study years and both sites (F = 1.1, ρ = 0.5), except for SIM 2021 (F = 8.8, ρ < 0.05), but intercepts for these slopes differed (F = 136.6, ρ < 0.0001) (Figure 4). Indeed, the mean δ13Сxc was systematically higher than the mean value of δ13Сcam in SIM (+0.8, +1.3 and +1‰ differences in 2019–2021) and in BER (+1 and +0.8‰ in 2020–2021, ρ < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

The linkages between cambial merismatic activity and stem growth are essential for an accurate understanding of source-sink dynamics of NSCs in plants (Simard et al. 2013). Although high-resolution studies using manual cutting or laser-based systems are becoming more frequent to analyze intra-ring isotopic composition profiles (Verheyden et al. 2004, Ogée et al. 2009, Roden et al. 2009, Pons and Helle 2011, Fu et al. 2017, Belmecheri et al. 2018, Barçante Ladvocat Cintra et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2022, Saurer et al. 2023), a specific technical procedure is still required for sampling and analyzing both the cambial region and the developing tree ring. This procedure is necessary to produce precisely dated samples that could link tree-ring formation and, at the same time, isotopic values of both tissues as they are forming. In this paper, we presented a suitable and non-lethal method to collect these two tissues from mature black spruce trees. This developed method can be used to monitor the weekly δ13С evolution of the cambium–xylem continuum.

Our strategy for collecting the cambial cells and developing tree ring was primarily based on the plant water status and stage of tree-ring formation at the sampling date (Giovannelli et al. 2011). During the dormant phases and at the beginning or ending of the growing season, smaller quantities of scraped tissues were obtained in the cambial region (i.e., cambium initial cells) because of narrow cell population in this zone during those periods. On the contrary, during the xylem differentiation phase, the quantity of scraped tissues (i.e., initial cambial cells with differentiating xylem and phloem cells on both sides) increased notably in each tree samples. The higher availability of scrapped tissue coincided with an enhanced hydration and a lack of lignification in the developing area of the cambial region (Giovannelli et al. 2011, Deslauriers et al. 2016). Our method consistently provided sufficient masses of tissue (mg) for δ13C analysis on a weekly basis throughout the growing seasons. Both the scraping of the narrower developing ring at the onset of wood formation and the cutting of the widening ring, yielded ample masses per tree. However, black spruce, being a slow-growing species, exhibits less total cell production during the growing season. In contrast, a study conducted on fast-growing poplar yielded greater quantities of material both in cambium and xylem tissues (Deslauriers et al. 2009). To ensure that enough mass is sampled, cutting bigger strips (>3 × 6 cm) from the tree trunk during earlier phases of secondary growth (June) could be considered in future monitoring studies.

In both sites, our method generated isotopically lighter δ13Сcam (by ~ 1‰) compared with δ13Сxc (Figure 4, Table 3). This most likely reflects the difference in isotopic values of the material analyzed along the continuum (Gessler et al. 2014). Intra-annual δ13Сxc profiles were measured on α-cellulose isolated from the developing tree ring. In contrast, δ13Сcam measurements were performed on raw material, without cellulose extraction because of the lower mass of collected tissue. In conifers, different types of NSCs along with other compounds including polyols and the raffinose family of oligosaccharides are transport from phloem to the cambial region (Simard et al. 2013, Dominguez and Niittylä 2022). The presence of isotopically less enriched substances such as pinitol, and soluble sugars like fructose in the cambial region of black spruce (Simard et al. 2013, Rinne et al. 2015) may contribute to the observed more negative δ13Сcam values. Indeed, extracting α-cellulose on cambial cells would otherwise have resulted in drastic mass losses on these already extra light samples, which would be incompatible with IRMS analysis. Indeed, hemi- and α-cellulose are polymers only present in low amounts in the cambial region. Thus, the multiple source (i.e., different substances and sugars in the cambial region) versus single sink (α-cellulose in tree rings) approach we used here may not fully unveil the subtle source–sink interactions that impact δ13С ratios in these two tissues. To achieve a deeper understanding of those interactions, comparing isotopic signature of δ13С in the cambial region with δ13С in whole wood may provide further insights into δ13С ratios within the growing cambium-xylem continuum (Helle et al. 2022, Rathgeber et al. 2022).

However, regardless of the chemical composition of the tissues analyzed, our study highlighted a notable coherence both in the seasonal trends and weekly variations of δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc mean profiles (Figure 4, Table 3). This suggests that cambium and xylem share carbohydrates that originate from similar sources and that were synthesized at the same time during the growing season. These sources can be either: (i) newly assimilated NSCs unloaded from the phloem alone (Vaganov et al. 2009), or (ii) newly assimilated NSCs unloaded from the phloem along with carbohydrates stored in ray cells (Soudant et al. 2016).

Evidence from studies focalizing on NSC concentration in the cambial zone and xylem indicate that the first scenario (i) is more credible (Simard et al. 2013, Deslauriers et al. 2016), especially for conifer species of boreal zones. In these studies, mobile sugars in the cambial zone were shown to originate predominantly from recently fixed NSCs unloaded from the phloem. These sugars then supply the xylem sink during the growing season (Deslauriers et al. 2016), amplifying the coherence between the δ13С values of both tissues. Complementarily, low variations in 13C-enriched starch were observed in the cambium of larch and spruce trees during the growing season, suggesting that a constant supply of fresh assimilates to the cambium–xylem continuum may be the dominant process feeding secondary growth (Simard et al. 2013, Rinne et al. 2015). Thus, the high level of correspondence between δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc revealed in our study does not support our initial hypothesis. We initially assumed that, if stored NSCs were to be used by xylem and not by cambium, divergences in δ13С variations between the two tissues would necessarily emerge, reflecting a mixture of NSC sources synthesized at different time periods.

Our novel method helps supporting the fact that conifers are less prompt than deciduous broadleaves species to exhibit a sharp transition in NSC source from stored NSCs to current assimilates during the growing season (Klein et al. 2005, Sarris et al. 2013, Schollaen et al. 2014). On the contrary, our study provides additional isotopic evidence that the secondary growth of evergreens relies primarily on current assimilates (Hansen and Beck 1990, Ogée et al. 2009, Klein and Hoch 2015, Rinne et al. 2015). Indeed, a recent study on black spruce showed very strong correlations in the δ13C ratios of latewood and whole rings over 70 years, suggesting that both earlywood and latewood were constructed from current-year photosynthates (Alvarez et al. 2018). These results are compatible with the coherence of intra-annual δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc profiles shown in our study. The isotopic signature of starch, a major stored carbohydrate during spring in conifers’ needles, is influenced by metabolic processes during its synthesis in the chloroplast. During daylight, starch is produced and accumulates in the chloroplasts of leaves, originating from fructose-6-phosphate via fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. The aldolase enzyme, acting during the regeneration phase of the Calvin Cycle, favors 13C during production of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, leading to a relative 13C enrichment of starch as an end product (Gleixner and Schmidt 1997). Therefore, if 13C-enriched, starch-derived NSCs would contribute significantly to earlywood and less to latewood formation, then, a negative slope contrary to the one observed here would be expected.

Intra-annual increasing trends in δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc in our sites may, on the contrary, partially reflect the δ13C signature of the local atmosphere. Indeed, photosynthesis is well known to use lighter 12CO2 preferentially, resulting in an atmosphere that becomes progressively enriched in 13CO2 as the growing season comes to an end (Ballantyne et al. 2010). However, accounting for seasonal δ13Catm values at AMT and LLB, does not entirely remove the trends in δ13Сcam (Figures S3 and S5 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online) and δ13Сxc series (Figures S4 and S6 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). We observed that the increasing pattern in the two profiles remained even after the correction for seasonal δ13Catm variations in almost all intra-annual series. While recognizing that the trends in δ13Catm might be both smoothed and conservative, one could argue that the residual trends in δ13Cxc and δ13Ccam may reflect either climate-induced stress responses, changes in carbon allocation strategies or a combination of both.

Our analysis reveals that the increasing δ13C trends in the cambium–xylem continuum are unlikely to be entirely driven by climatic conditions. A vast body of knowledge in dendroclimatology exists, which suggests that increasing moisture stress toward the end of the growing season may result in higher δ13С values in the tree ring (Barbour et al. 2002, Sarris et al. 2013, Castagneri et al. 2018, Xu et al. 2022). For instance, a decline in precipitation and soil water content, excessively high temperatures and a lower RH may all contribute to the limitation of gs in the needles of evergreen conifers. In turn, this could lead to increasing δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc trends. However, our detailed micro-meteorological analysis suggests that all these variables (Tmax, VPD, RH, P, VWC) are not indicative of trees growing in conditions that become increasingly cold and wet toward the end of the growing season (Figures S7 and S8 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online), neither at the tree level (Figure S9 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online), nor at the site level (Tables 4 and 5). In this context, it is unlikely that the increase in δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc reflects a seasonally integrated gs response to water stress.

In absence of detrimental climatic effects, we speculate on three alternative causes that may individually or jointly explain the residual increasing trends in δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc. These upward trends remain evident even after detrending the δ13Сxc_cor series (δ13Сxc_seq) (Figure S2 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online). First, as the growing season advances, trees may shift allocation strategies, from a secondary growth priority (in the early to mid-season) to a frost resistance priority (in the late season). As this shift operates, trees would then allocate less enriched NSCs (e.g., pinitol and lipids) for frost resistance purposes (Rinne et al. 2015, Sleptsov et al. 2023). This strategy is commonly adopted by trees that need to maintain tissue functional integrity despite prolonged freezing winters (Lipavská et al. 2000). Consequently, the unreacted, 13C-enriched residual sugars like sucrose (Rinne et al. 2015) may be loaded into the phloem and ultimately used for wood formation, mostly in the form of latewood. Trees like spruce may be particularly susceptible to frost damage due to their thin bark (Simard et al. 2013). Thus, they probably need an effective resource allocation strategy to resist frost damages specifically in the cambial region (Gurskaya and Shiyatov 2006). The observed slight decrease in δ13Ccam values toward the season’s end (Figure 4) could be attributed to an increase in the concentration of 13C-depleted sugars, possibly raffinose (Simard et al. 2013, Deslauriers et al. 2016), unloaded from phloem into the cambial region (Simard et al. 2013), as a response to the dropping temperatures in the study region. A second cause could be that, in early autumn, trees gradually reduce water content and increase residual evaporation through the needles to prevent frost damages due to ice crystal formation (Bozonnet et al. 2024, Charrier and Améglio 2024). Although this is not a response to a typical atmospherically induced drought stress, less water uptake could result in a lower gs which, in turn, could yield NSCs that are systematically enriched in 13C as the growing season comes to its end. The third cause could be related to the reduced PAR as the growing season ends (Figures S7d and S8d available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online), leading to a gradual decline in the photosynthesis rate. This decline forces a proportional reduction in gs, leading to the observed increasing trends in the δ13Ccam and δ13Cxc profiles (Farquhar and Sharkey 1982, Siegwolf et al. 2023). In this context, a dual-isotope analysis of weekly variations in carbon and oxygen isotopes can provide deeper insights into the physiological processes affecting tree growth on a seasonal scale.

While the sampling approach proposed here enables to capture similarities between δ13Сcam and δ13Сxc profiles, it also underlines several differences at the inter-site scale. For example, we found that the mean δ13С levels in SIM, for both the cambial zone and the xylem, are systematically more enriched by about 0.6‰ compared with BER. Longer growing seasons along with higher soil temperatures in SIM (Balducci et al. 2021) may contribute to an increase in the rate of carboxylation at the leaf level at this site. This leads to a decrease in ci/ca, resulting in less negative δ13C values at this site compared with the colder site of BER (Siegwolf et al. 2023). This difference cannot be explained by other confounding factors such as stand age or altitude. Indeed, stand age usually has the opposite effect: δ13C is known to increase with tree age (Fonti et al. 2018, Helama et al. 2018, Marchand et al. 2020). Here, BER presents the oldest individuals (Table 1), but reveals the lowest δ13C values. Altitude could also have a positive effect on δ13С composition in tree rings (Hultine and Marshall 2000, Li et al. 2009, Helama et al. 2018). Warren et al. (2001) indicated that the δ13С values of conifer stem wood increased by +2.5‰ per km of rising altitude. Applying this rate to our δ13C data for the two study sites of SIM and BER with a 273 m altitudinal difference (Table 1) could result in a potential increase of +0.7‰, while we observed a decrease in δ13Сxc and δ13Сcam values in BER, suggesting that altitude does not act as a direct single factor in the region of study (Warren et al. 2001).

The method we developed also underlines significant differences in δ13Сxc profiles at the intra-site scale. In about all sites and years, most individual trees share a common increasing pattern (Figure S1 available as Supplementary data at Tree Physiology Online), but that there is also a notable amount on inter-tree weekly variability, reflected in EPSw that remain quite low (Table 3). High inter-individual variability in δ13C profiles has also been observed, at inter-annual time scales, for black spruce (Bégin et al. 2015) and various other deciduous and coniferous species (Raffalli-Delerce et al. 2004, Roden et al. 2011). This may suggest that, just like for inter-annual time scales, micro-environmental conditions surrounding each sampled tree may have a large influence on the weekly carbon isotopic fractionation (Bégin et al. 2015). Genetic factors and nutritional stress from inter-tree competition may also contribute to the variability in δ13C series among tree samples (McCarroll and Loader 2004, Bégin et al. 2015). Furthermore, our findings align with previous research indicating that sampling from different parts of the stem contributes to the variability in δ13Сxc values, due to incoherent intra-ring δ13C variability (i.e., circumferential variability) (Tans and Mook 1980, Leavitt 2010). One way to improve the current method, for black spruce at the very least, would be to increase sampling depth to about 10 individuals per site, so to converge toward EPSw > 0.85.

Conclusions

Overall, our study unveils the first in situ monitoring of carbon isotope fractionation in trees, at a weekly time scale. Its main contribution lies in the ability to perform δ13С analysis on newly formed cambium and xylem cells, separately. Performing δ13С analysis on these two tissues has the potential to reduce knowledge gaps that persist on the timing of NSC synthesis in leaves, their subsequent transport/remobilization through phloem/cambium, and ultimately, their incorporation in tree-ring cellulose. In the present case, our method has clearly demonstrated that the weekly construction of black spruce’s cambium and xylem cells are formed by shared NSC pools, originating predominantly from current photo-assimilates. While this method has proven effective for monitoring the weekly δ13C evolution of the cambium–xylem continuum in black spruce, further research is recommended to evaluate its applicability to species with different growth characteristics.

However, to better ascertain the variations in the rate of production of such assimilates, the eco-physiological processes involved, and how they relate to external (e.g., climate-related) or internal drivers (e.g., sink dynamics) additional data are required. Fortunately, the method is most likely also suitable for oxygen stable isotopes (δ18O), opening the way for multi-isotope analysis at a weekly resolution. Coupling such isotopic analysis to high precision xylogenesis monitoring, anatomical features and phenological changes, for example, would represent a means to refine current state-of-the-art eco-physiological models (Guiot et al. 2014) and better predict the short-term response of boreal forests to environmental changes.

Additionally, a study comparing weekly variations of δ13C series in cellulose and whole wood within developing tree ring could provide further insights into the use of whole wood versus α-cellulose for weekly monitoring of carbon stable isotopes and help optimize sample collection and analysis. Lastly, while our multiple-source (δ13Сcam), single-sink (δ13Сxc) analysis approach provided insights into carbon dynamics in black spruce, a compound-specific monitoring of carbon stable isotopes in the cambium–xylem continuum (e.g., δ13С in different sugars found in the two tissues) could offer further information on seasonal carbon allocation strategies in boreal trees.
