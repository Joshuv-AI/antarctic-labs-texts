MitoQ supplementation does not impact redox responses to acute exercise in skeletal muscle of older individuals
Authors: Sophie C Broome, Jamie Whitfield, Kristel Janssens, John A Hawley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgeing is associated with attenuated exercise responses in skeletal muscle, which may be related to a failure of muscle redox signalling. The attenuation of redox responses to exercise in aged muscle has been linked to perturbations in redox homeostasis induced by age-related increases in mitochondrial oxidative stress. Accordingly, we investigated the effects of supplementation with the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoQ on mitochondrial bioenergetics and H2O2 emission as well as acute exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle of older individuals. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel design, 10 males and 12 females aged 65–80 years were randomised to receive either MitoQ (20 mg/day) or a placebo for 12 weeks before completing a single bout of exercise. Vastus lateralis muscle biopsies were collected before supplementation and before, immediately post- and 4 h post-exercise. MitoQ supplementation reduced mitochondrial H2O2 emission capacity in skeletal muscle but did not impact mitochondrial respiration, H2O2 emission in the presence of ADP, or the sensitivity for ADP to stimulate respiration (apparent Km) and attenuate H2O2 emission (apparent IC50). Acute exercise-induced peroxiredoxin oxidation in skeletal muscle was not altered by MitoQ supplementation. Similarly, MitoQ had no effect on the phosphorylation of several redox-sensitive protein kinases (AMPK, p38 MAPK, and ERK1/2) or the upregulation of mitochondrial and antioxidant genes following exercise. Collectively, these findings indicate that MitoQ supplementation did not influence the basal myocellular redox state or redox responses to exercise in skeletal muscle of older individuals.Keywords: Ageing, Mitochondria, Reactive oxygen species, Antioxidant

Introduction

Population ageing is one of the greatest challenges facing healthcare systems worldwide, with a major threat to healthy ageing being the progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass and function that occurs with advancing age. Given the global number of people aged over 65 years is projected to more than double by 2050 [1], there is an urgent need to identify and optimize interventions that support the maintenance of muscle mass and function in older individuals. In this regard, exercise training (EXT) induces a wide range of beneficial adaptations in skeletal muscle that are crucial for maintaining and improving muscle mass, quality and function during ageing [2]. Studies investigating the mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects of exercise on skeletal muscle have identified several key exercise-sensitive signalling pathways [3]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), specifically hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), are key factors that stimulate adaptive responses in contracting skeletal muscle, including mitochondrial biogenesis, increased antioxidant capacity and muscle hypertrophy, via transient oxidation of cysteines on target proteins in exercise-sensitive signalling cascades [4]. However, several ROS-stimulated exercise responses are attenuated in aged muscle, including stress responses [5,6], mitochondrial biogenesis [7,8] and anabolic signalling [9], which may partly explain the attenuation of exercise adaptations in aged skeletal muscle [10,11]. Consequently, the ability of EXT to increase muscle mass and function is reduced in older individuals and interventions that restore redox responses to exercise may help preserve skeletal muscle health during ageing.

Ageing is associated with an increase in mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) emission in skeletal muscle, which is driven by a combination of factors, including the loss of motor neuron integrity – leading to repeated cycles of denervation and reinnervation [12] – along with declines in mitochondrial bioenergetic function, such as reduced ADP sensitivity [13]. Age-related increases in mtROS emission have been linked to the failure of exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle [14]. Specifically, mitochondrial oxidative stress may induce an adaptive upregulation of cytoprotective antioxidant proteins through retrograde signalling, thereby shifting the redox state of cysteines on key ROS-sensitive signalling proteins toward a more reduced state, making them less sensitive to oxidation/activation by exercise-induced ROS. Aging is associated with increased cytosolic antioxidant protein content in skeletal muscle [15]. Furthermore, the basal oxidation state of several proteins is lower in skeletal muscle of older individuals [16,17]. Consistent with the age-related disruption of exercise-induced redox signalling, contraction-induced peroxiredoxin (PRDX) 2 oxidation is attenuated in aged muscle [17]. Given that PRDX2 plays a role in mediating adaptive responses to exercise-induced ROS [18], this impaired oxidation may contribute to the attenuated adaptive responses observed in muscle of older individuals. Collectively, these data implicate elevated mtROS emission in the age-related failure of exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle. Therefore, targeted reduction of mtROS may restore redox homeostasis and exercise-induced redox responses in muscle of older individuals.

The mitochondria-targeted antioxidant mitoquinone mesylate (MitoQ) consists of a ubiquinone moiety linked to a lipophilic cation, which enables it to pass through the plasma membrane and accumulate in mitochondria driven by the plasma and mitochondrial membrane potential, respectively [[19], [20], [21]]. Inside the mitochondrion, MitoQ is absorbed to the mitochondrial inner membrane where it acts as an antioxidant, primarily by preventing lipid peroxidation [19]. Skeletal muscle mitochondrial H2O2 emission is reduced following MitoQ supplementation in middle-aged men [22]. Furthermore, MitoQ supplementation has been shown to augment acute exercise-induced transcriptional responses and training-induced improvements in physical performance, highlighting the potential for MitoQ to promote exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle [23]. In this study, we tested the hypothesis that MitoQ supplementation would decrease mitochondrial H2O2 emission and augment acute exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle of older adults.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-four healthy older men and women were recruited and provided written informed consent to participate in the study. Two participants withdrew from the study before completion, and results are presented for the 22 participants who completed the study. Participant characteristics are provided in Table 1. All participants reported as non-smokers, were free from injury and chronic illness, including cardiovascular and metabolic disease, had not taken antioxidant supplements or medications that may impact exercise responses for >6 weeks prior to starting the study, and completed <2.5 h of physical activity per week. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at Australian Catholic University (2023-2907HC), was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ACTRN12623000687695) on June 26, 2023, and was conducted according to the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

The study was a double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel design (Fig. 1). Participants were randomly assigned to consume MitoQ or a placebo for 12 weeks before completing an exercise trial. One week before the exercise trial, participants completed an incremental cycling test on an electronically braked cycle ergometer (Lode, Groningen, Netherlands) with open-circuit spirometry (TrueOne 2400; Parvo Medics, Sandy, UT, United States) to determine peak aerobic capacity (V˙ O2peak). After a 5-min warm-up at 30 W, the incremental test protocol consisted of 1-min stages increasing 25 W until volitional exhaustion. Continuous 12-lead electrocardiography was performed during the test to assess for ischemia or arrhythmia suggestive of underlying cardiac pathology. V˙ O2peak was determined as the highest 30-s average value recorded, and maximal aerobic power output (MAP, W) was calculated as Wfinal + (t/60∗25), where t is the time in seconds of any incomplete stage. Results from the incremental test were used to determine submaximal power outputs for the exercise trial. On the day of the exercise trial, participants arrived at the laboratory following a 12-h overnight fast. Participants completed a 20-min bout of high-intensity interval exercise involving 5 x 2-min cycling intervals at 80 % MAP followed by 2 min at 40 % MAP. Participants cycled at a self-selected cadence (≥70 RPM). High-intensity interval exercise was chosen as it may confer superior adaptations and health benefits compared to moderate-intensity continuous exercise in older individuals [24]; however, redox responses to high-intensity interval exercise are attenuated in skeletal muscle of older individuals [5].

Supplementation protocol

Participants were randomised to receive MitoQ (20 mg/day mitoquinone mesylate, Alaron; Nelson, New Zealand) or an identical placebo (Alaron; Nelson, New Zealand [tapioca powder, precipitated silica and microcrystalline cellulose 101]). This dose was selected as it has previously been shown to suppress skeletal muscle mitochondrial H2O2 emission after six weeks of supplementation in middle-aged men [25]. A computer-generated allocation sequence was created by an independent researcher using block randomisation with a 1:1 allocation ratio to ensure equal distribution between the two study arms. To ensure balance across prognostic variables, the randomisation was stratified by age (≤70 vs > 70 years) and sex. The allocation sequence was uploaded into the randomisation module within Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) and was not accessible to study personnel involved in participant enrolment. Group assignments were automatically revealed by the REDCap system only after a participant's eligibility was confirmed and baseline data, including stratification variables, had been entered. Tablets were dispensed into unmarked bottles by an independent researcher and these bottles were given to the researcher conducting the trial for distribution to participants. Participants were instructed to consume one tablet per day, 30 min before breakfast, for 12 weeks. During this period, participants received a daily survey via text message, which asked them to confirm they had taken their supplement and report whether they were experiencing any adverse effects. Adherence to the supplementation protocol was also monitored by providing participants with an excess supply of tablets and counting the number of tablets returned at the final study visit. The participant's data was included in the analysis if they had a supplementation protocol adherence rate of >70 %.

Muscle sampling

Percutaneous skeletal muscle biopsies were collected from the vastus lateralis under local anaesthesia using a suction-modified Bergstrom needle before supplementation and before, immediately post- and 4 h-post exercise (Fig. 1). Muscle samples were either placed in ice-cold biopsy preservation solution (BIOPS, in mM: 50 K-MES, 30 sucrose, 20 taurine, 20 imidazole, 15 PCr, 7.23 K2EGTA, 2.77 CaK2EGTA, 6.56 MgCl2, 5.7 ATP, and 0.5 dithiothreitol, pH 7.1 at 0 °C) for the assessment of mitochondrial respiration and H2O2 emission or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. For the assessment of PRDX oxidation state, approximately 20 mg muscle was placed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 100 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) for 5 min before being snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Muscle tissue was stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Mitochondrial bioenergetics

Mitochondrial respiration and H2O2 emission were measured in fresh skeletal muscle biopsies collected before and after supplementation as previously described [13]. Skeletal muscle was separated under a microscope into bundles using fine-tipped forceps, treated with 50 μg/mL saponin for 30 min at 4 °C, and then washed for 15 min in buffer Z (105 mM K-MES, 30 mM KCl, 1 mM EGTA, 10 mM K2HPO4, 5 mM MgCl2·H2O with 5 mg/mL fatty acid-free BSA [pH 7.4]). Oxygen consumption and H2O2 emission were determined in buffer Z supplemented with 20 μM Amplex Red (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 5 U/mL horseradish peroxidase, 5 U/mL superoxide dismutase, and 25 μM blebbistatin, using an Oxygraph-2 K with a fluorometry modular attachment (Oroboros Instruments, Innsbruck, Austria). Experiments were conducted at 37 °C and commenced with mild hyper-oxygenation to prevent re-oxygenation during an experiment (250 μM O2), in the presence of various substrates, including 10 μM cytochrome c to ensure outer mitochondrial membrane integrity. The rate of H2O2 emission was calculated from a standard curve established with the same reaction conditions using the DatLab software (Oroboros Instruments) after subtracting the fiber background. All fibers were weighed in buffer Z before the start of an experiment to normalize respiration to muscle bundle weight.

Immunoblotting

Muscle samples were homogenised in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, U.S.) with cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, United States). For samples used to assess PRDX oxidation state, 100 mM NEM was added to the homogenization buffer. Samples were centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations from collected supernatants were determined via bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (Pierce, Rockform, IL, United States). Proteins (15 μg protein/well) were separated by either reducing or non-reducing SDS-PAGE on 4–15 % precast stain-free gels (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, United States) before being transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, 1704156) and incubated in blocking solution and primary antibody [Total OXPHOS Antibody Cocktail (Abcam ab110413, 1:1000), Catalase (Cell Signaling Technology 14097, 1:1000), PRDX2 (Cell Signaling Technology 46855, 1:1000), PRDX3 (Abcam ab73349, 1:1000), TRX1 (Cell Signaling Technology 2298, 1:1000), TRX2 (Cell Signalling Technology 14907, 1:1000), SOD1 (Cell Signaling Technology 2770, 1:1000), SOD2 (Cell Signaling Technology 13194, 1:1000), 4HNE (Abcam ab46545, 1:500), phospho-AMPKαThr172 (Cell Signaling Technology 2535, 1:1000), AMPKα (Cell Signaling Technology 5831, 1:1000), phospho-p38 MAPKThr180/Tyr182 (Cell Signaling Technology 4511, 1:1000), p38 MAPK (Cell Signaling Technology 8690, 1:1000), phospho-ERK1/2Thr202/Tyr204 (Cell Signaling Technology 9101, 1:1000), ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology 9102, 1:1000). After washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Membrane proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence and the Chemidoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad). The volume density of each target band was quantified using Bio-Rad Image Lab and normalized to total protein in each lane using stain-free imaging technology using Image Lab software (version 6.1, Bio-Rad).

Gene expression analysis

RT-qPCR was performed to quantify gene expression in muscle. RNA was extracted from muscle using the AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quantity and purity of extracted RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). In a 20 μL reaction, 1000 ng RNA was reverse-transcribed using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, United States) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 10 μL reaction was prepared containing 5 μL SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, United States), 500 nM forward and reverse primers, nuclease-free H2O, and 3 ng template. Quantification of genes of interest was performed using a CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, United States) using the following thermocycling conditions; 3 min at 95 °C followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C. Genes of interest were normalized using the geometric mean of three reference genes (GAPDH, B2M, and ACTB) and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method. Melt curve analysis verified that a single PCR product was amplified. Primer sequences are shown in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10. The normality of distribution was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (AVOVA) [group (placebo, MitoQ) x time (pre-supplementation, post-supplementation)] was used to evaluate the effect of MitoQ supplementation on skeletal muscle mitochondrial respiration and H2O2 emission, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and antioxidant protein content, PRDX2 and 3 oxidation state, and 4-hydroxynonenal (4HNE) protein adduct content. A two-way ANOVA [group (placebo, MitoQ) x time (pre-, post- and 4 h post-exercise)] was used to evaluate the effect of MitoQ supplementation and exercise on skeletal muscle PRDX2 and 3 oxidation state, 4HNE protein adduct content, protein kinase phosphorylation, and mRNA expression. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Sidak test where appropriate. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± SD.

Results

Supplementation protocol adherence

Adherence to the supplementation protocol was similar between groups (placebo: 88 ± 10 %, MitoQ: 95 ± 7 %, p = 0.12) and no adverse effects of supplementation were reported.

Skeletal muscle mitochondrial bioenergetics

To assess the effect of MitoQ on skeletal muscle mitochondrial bioenergetics in older individuals, we measured mitochondrial respiration and H2O2 emission in permeabilised skeletal muscle fibers collected before and after 12 weeks of supplementation. There was no difference in maximal succinate (p = 0.10) and succinate + pyruvate/malate (p = 0.17) H2O2 emission rates between groups before supplementation (Fig. 2A). A main effect of time was observed for maximal succinate (p = 0.004) and succinate + pyruvate/malate (p = 0.008) H2O2 emission rates (Fig. 2A). Post-hoc analysis indicated that succinate (p = 0.01) and succinate + pyruvate (p = 0.02) H2O2 emission rates were lower following MitoQ supplementation. MitoQ supplementation did not impact the apparent sensitivity for ADP to attenuate H2O2 emission, as indicated by the apparent IC50 (Fig. 2B and C). Similarly, absolute H2O2 emission and the percent attenuation of H2O2 emission following the addition of 25 and 175 μM ADP were not affected by MitoQ supplementation (Fig. 2D and E). There was also no effect of MitoQ supplementation on the fraction of electron leak to H2O2 in the absence and presence of ADP (Fig. 2F–H).

There was little effect of MitoQ supplementation on mitochondrial respiration in the presence (state III) or absence (state II) of ADP (Fig. 3A), maximal oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 3A), or the respiratory control ratio (RCR) (Fig. 3B). Titrating ADP revealed the expected Michaelis-Menten kinetic curve (Fig. 3C), which was used to estimate the apparent sensitivity for ADP to stimulate respiration (apparent Km). There was no effect of MitoQ supplementation on the apparent ADP Km (Fig. 3D). A group × time interaction effect was observed for absolute respiration following the addition of 25 μM ADP (p = 0.03, Fig. 3E) and post hoc analysis indicated that respiration was lower following MitoQ supplementation (p = 0.03). However, MitoQ supplementation did not impact the stimulation of respiration following the addition of 25 μM ADP (Fig. 3F). A group × time interaction effect was observed for the percent stimulation of respiration following the addition of 175 μM ADP (p = 0.05). However, post hoc analysis did not reveal any differences between groups or timepoints (Fig. 3F). MitoQ did not impact the expression of OXPHOS protein complexes in skeletal muscle (Fig. 3G).

Skeletal muscle cellular redox state

To evaluate the impact of MitoQ supplementation on the skeletal muscle redox state, we measured antioxidant protein content, 4HNE protein adduct content, and PRDX2 and 3 oxidation state in skeletal muscle collected before and after 12 weeks of supplementation. A main effect of time was observed for superoxide dismutase (SOD) 2 protein content (p = 0.02, Fig. 4A and B) with post hoc analysis revealing that SOD2 protein content was higher following MitoQ supplementation (p = 0.01). Thioredoxin (TRX) 1 protein content was lower following supplementation (main effect of time p < 0.001) and this effect was similar between groups (Fig. 4A and B). Skeletal muscle catalase (CAT), PRDX2, PRDX3, SOD1, and TRX2 protein content were not affected by MitoQ supplementation (Fig. 4A and B). Similarly, MitoQ supplementation did not impact 4HNE protein adduct content (Fig. 4C) or the oxidation state of cytosolic PRDX2 (Fig. 4D) or mitochondrial PRDX3 (Fig. 4E) in skeletal muscle.

Exercise-induced changes in the skeletal muscle cellular redox state

To determine whether MitoQ supplementation impacts acute exercise-induced changes in the cellular redox state in aged muscle, we measured the oxidation state of cytosolic PRDX2 and mitochondrial PRDX3 in skeletal muscle samples collected before and after a single bout of exercise. The oxidation state of PRDX2 was not impacted by exercise (Fig. 5A). In contrast, PRDX3 oxidation was increased following exercise (main effect of time p < 0.001, Fig. 5B) but this effect was not impacted by MitoQ supplementation. Skeletal muscle 4HNE protein adduct content was also increased following exercise (main effect of time p = 0.01, Fig. 5C) and this effect was similar between groups.

Exercise-induced phosphorylation of redox-sensitive protein kinases in skeletal muscle

Redox-sensitive protein kinases play a central role in exercise-induced redox signalling and adaptation in skeletal muscle. However, dysregulation of redox signalling may impair protein kinase activation and adaptive responses in aged muscle. To determine whether MitoQ supplementation impacts acute exercise-induced activation of redox-sensitive protein kinases in aged muscle, we assessed AMPK, p38 MAPK, and ERK1/2 phosphorylation in skeletal muscle samples collected before and after an acute exercise bout. Exercise increased phosphorylation of AMPK (main effect of time p = 0.04), p38 MAPK (main effect of time p = 0.004), and ERK1/2 (main effect of time p = 0.01) in skeletal muscle and this effect was not impacted by MitoQ supplementation (Fig. 6A–D).

Exercise-induced skeletal muscle transcriptional responses

To determine whether MitoQ supplementation impacts acute exercise-induced transcriptional responses in aged muscle, we measured the expression of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defences in skeletal muscle samples collected before and after an acute exercise bout. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1-α) is a transcriptional coactivator involved in exercise-induced mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defences encoded by the PPARGC1A gene. Skeletal muscle PPARGC1A mRNA expression increased following exercise (main effect of time p < 0.001, Fig. 7A) and this effect was not impacted by MitoQ supplementation. A main effect of group was observed for COXIV mRNA expression (p = , Fig. 7B), indicating COXIV mRNA expression was lower in the MitoQ group. Neither exercise or MitoQ supplementation impacted the expression of mitochondrial genes TFAM, PPARA, PGC1B, and CS or antioxidant genes GPX1, CAT, and SOD1 in skeletal muscle (Fig. 7C–F).

Discussion

Age-related increases in mtROS emission have been linked to the attenuation of exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle [14]. In this study, we investigated the effects of supplementation with the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoQ on the basal myocellular redox state and redox responses to exercise in skeletal muscle of older individuals. In contrast to our original research hypothesis, the basal myocellular redox state, reflected by the oxidation state of PRDXs localised to specific subcellular compartments, was not impacted by MitoQ supplementation. Furthermore, MitoQ did not impact exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle, as determined from PRDX oxidation, phosphorylation of redox-sensitive protein kinases, or the upregulation of mitochondrial and antioxidant genes.

This is the first study to assess the effects of MitoQ supplementation on mitochondrial bioenergetics and H2O2 emission in skeletal muscle of older individuals. Similar to previous studies in middle-aged men [25], we report decreased succinate-supported mitochondrial H2O2 emission in skeletal muscle following MitoQ supplementation. MitoQ may attenuate superoxide production via reverse electron transport and subsequent H2O2 formation by interacting with protonmotive forces (uptake decreases proton motive force) and the coenzyme Q pool redox state [22]. Mitochondrial H2O2 emission in the presence of succinate, and the subsequent reverse flow of electrons from complex II to complex I, represents the capacity of mitochondria to produce ROS. However, succinate provides a small percentage of the electrons entering the electron transport chain (∼17 %) and the delivery of electrons to complex I promotes forward electron flow, which attenuates succinate-mediated ROS formation [26]. Furthermore, resting skeletal muscle has ∼25–100 μM free ADP [27], and ADP transport is a highly regulated process involving voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC, outer mitochondrial membrane) and adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT, inner mitochondrial membrane). The movement of ADP into the matrix, and the subsequent binding of ADP to ATP synthase, decreases proton motive force and mtROS production [28]. Therefore, assessments of mtROS emission in the absence of ADP may not adequately reflect the in vivo environment. While maximal mitochondrial H2O2 emission rates are largely unaltered with ageing, the ability of ADP to decrease ROS emission is impaired in older individuals [13]. Given that ANT has three known cysteine sites located on the matrix-facing surface that can influence ADP transport [29], mtROS may contribute to age-related declines in mitochondrial respiration and ADP sensitivity through redox-induced inhibition of ANT. Despite this, MitoQ supplementation did not impact absolute mitochondrial respiration or the sensitivity for ADP to stimulate respiration and attenuate H2O2 emission.

The inability of MitoQ to decrease mitochondrial H2O2 emission under conditions reflecting in vivo resting muscle may explain why exercise-induced redox responses were not altered by MitoQ supplementation. It has been proposed that mitochondrial oxidative stress in aged muscle induces an adaptive upregulation of cytoprotective antioxidant proteins through retrograde signalling, thereby shifting the redox state of cysteines on key ROS-sensitive signalling proteins toward a more reduced state, making them less sensitive to oxidation/activation by exercise-induced ROS [14]. Therefore, targeted reduction of mtROS may restore redox homeostasis and redox-regulated exercise responses in skeletal muscle from older individuals. Given that ROS play an important role in exercise-induced redox signalling that brings about exercise responses and adaptations in skeletal muscle, it may seem counterintuitive that MitoQ supplementation could promote adaptive exercise responses. However, NADPH oxidase enzymes appear to be the major source of exercise-induced ROS [30,31], which may explain why MitoQ supplementation does not attenuate exercise responses and adaptations in skeletal muscle [23]. In the present study, both antioxidant protein content and the cellular redox state, assessed through the oxidation state of PRDXs localised to specific subcellular compartments, were unaltered following MitoQ supplementation. As a result, it is perhaps not surprising that we observed little effect of MitoQ supplementation on exercise-induced skeletal muscle redox responses, including PRDX3 oxidation, phosphorylation of redox-sensitive protein kinases (AMPK, p38 MAPK, and ERK1/2), or the upregulation of mitochondrial and antioxidant gene expression. While MitoQ is currently the only mitochondria-targeted antioxidant supplement commercially available, other mitochondria-targeted peptides with the capacity to reverse age-related changes in skeletal muscle mitochondrial bioenergetics and H2O2 emission are currently in clinical trials. Treatment with the mitochondria-targeted peptide Elamipretide (SS-31), which binds directly to ANT, improves mitochondrial ADP sensitivity and decreases mtROS emission in skeletal muscle of aged mice [32]. Future studies investigating the potential for Elamipretide treatment to restore redox homeostasis and exercise-induced redox responses in aged muscle are warranted. Interestingly, skeletal muscle TRX1 expression decreased in both groups in this study. While speculative, this may have resulted from a slight improvement in mitochondrial function and subsequent remodelling of the cellular redox state following the V˙ O2peak test, which was completed seven days before the exercise trial. A single bout of endurance exercise is sufficient to induce structural changes in the mitochondrial network that promote augmented function of the organelle network [33]. Furthermore, some redox responses to exercise are preserved in skeletal muscle of masters athletes [5], highlighting the potential for exercise to maintain redox homeostasis and exercise responses in aged muscle.

Age-related changes in mitochondrial bioenergetics represent one mechanism through which mtROS generation increases in aged muscle [13,21]. However, the role of motor neurons and neuromuscular junctions in regulating skeletal muscle ROS levels is becoming increasingly acknowledged. Skeletal muscle from older individuals shows substantial loss of neuromuscular junction integrity [34], and partial denervation has been shown to increase mitochondrial H2O2 generation in denervated fibers and neighbouring innervated fibers in the same muscle [12]. These data suggest that the loss of innervation observed in some muscle fibers during aging may contribute to increased mtROS generation previously reported in aged muscle. Therefore, interventions that decrease mitochondrial H2O2 emission through the maintenance of neuromuscular junction integrity may also improve redox homeostasis and promote exercise-induced redox responses in aged muscle. In this regard, there is some evidence that the preservation of muscle mass and function in masters athletes is associated with attenuated indices of denervation and greater re-innervation capacity [35], presenting a potential mechanism through which lifelong EXT maintains some exercise-induced redox responses in older individuals [5].

Changes in the redox state of cysteines on redox-sensitive proteins play an important role in skeletal muscle redox signalling. It is thought that physiological concentrations of H2O2 during muscle contractions are too low to directly oxidize most redox targets [36]. Therefore, highly oxidizable effectors of redox signalling could allow transmission of the oxidizing equivalents from H2O2 to less oxidizable target signalling molecules in a “redox relay”. Redox relays have been identified with the transcription factors apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) with PRDX1 [37] and PRDX2 [38], respectively. Contraction of isolated mouse muscle fibers results in the rapid and transient oxidation of PRDX1, 2 and 3 [17], and PRDX2 plays a key role in regulating mitochondrial adaptations to exercise in C. elegans [18]. Taken collectively, these data suggest that PRDX oxidation may be an early step in exercise-induced redox signalling. Similar to previous studies in humans [16], we observed exercise-induced oxidation of PRDX3 in skeletal muscle, but not PRDX2. Due to its relatively high concentration and reactivity with H2O2, PRDX3 is the major H2O2 detoxifying enzyme (∼90 % of H2O2) in mitochondria [39]. Given that exercise-induced increases in skeletal muscle H2O2 appear to be primarily derived from non-mitochondrial sources [30,31], and muscle redox signalling is highly compartmentalised [40], it is perhaps surprising that exercise results in the oxidation of the mitochondria-localised PRDX3. Exercise is understood to decrease net mitochondrial superoxide production by increasing ATP demand, stimulating OXPHOS, and dissipating proton motive force [28]. However, the continued availability of NADH means the flavin mononucleotide (FMN) group of complex I continues to produce superoxide at an appreciable rate in metabolic conditions mimicking exercise in isolated mitochondria [41]. Therefore, a potential source of H2O2 necessary for PRDX3 oxidation continues to operate. Furthermore, it is possible that the active recovery employed within the high-intensity interval exercise bout in this study creates a switch in ATP demand, potentially increasing mtROS production [40]. Exercise has also been shown to result in increased oxidative damage to mitochondrial DNA in skeletal muscle [42], highlighting our limited understanding of mitochondrial redox dynamics in contracting human muscle. Whether oxidation of PRDX3 plays a role in mitochondrial signalling during exercise requires further exploration. If so, PRDX3 could prove a novel target to optimize the beneficial effects of exercise, although this would require the identification of key binding partners to characterize and elucidate the pathways potentially lying downstream of its oxidation during skeletal muscle contraction.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results of this study. Due to the participant burden related to the collection of muscle biopsies, exercise responses were only measured in skeletal muscle after supplementation. Therefore, there may have been within-group changes in exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle that were not observed using the current study design. Additionally, the absence of a young comparison group limits our ability to determine whether the participants in this study exhibited age-related increases in skeletal muscle mitochondrial H2O2 emission or oxidative stress. Although elevated mitochondrial H2O2 emission has been reported in healthy, untrained older adults [13], the levels observed in our cohort were lower than previously reported. As a result, this cohort may not have exhibited sufficiently elevated oxidative stress to benefit from mitochondria-targeted antioxidant supplementation. While this study involved the longest MitoQ supplementation period to date (12 weeks) and we observed decreased mitochondrial H2O2 emission in skeletal muscle following supplementation, we did not quantify MitoQ levels in skeletal muscle biopsy samples and therefore cannot confirm its accumulation in muscle following supplementation. Skeletal muscle mitochondrial bioenergetics and H2O2 emission were assessed in vitro under conditions that do not perfectly replicate the in vivo environment. Furthermore, basal skeletal muscle PRDX3 oxidation was significantly higher in this study compared to previously published data [16]. This difference may result from the use of NEM as an alkylating agent instead of S-methyl methanethiosulfonate, highlighting the importance of using appropriate methods to prevent artificial oxidation of PRDXs during sample collection and homogenization. Finally, only selected redox-sensitive kinases and mitochondrial and antioxidant genes were measured. While the full extent of exercise-induced redox-sensitive signalling pathways in skeletal muscle are still being elucidated, it is possible that other important signalling pathways were impacted by MitoQ supplementation and were not captured in our analyses.

In conclusion, this study is the first to determine the effect of MitoQ supplementation on mitochondrial bioenergetics and H2O2 emission and exercise-induced redox responses in skeletal muscle of older individuals. We provide evidence that MitoQ supplementation failed to impact the basal myocellular redox state or redox responses to exercise in skeletal muscle of older individuals. Future research should focus on investigating the potential for alternative interventions that decrease skeletal muscle mitochondrial H2O2 emission (e.g., Elamipretide treatment) to restore exercise-induced redox responses in aged muscle as they may promote exercise adaptations and facilitate the maintenance of muscle mass and function during ageing.
