The impact of humidex on other infectious diarrhea: a study of seasonal heterogeneity and lagged effects using a distributed lag non-linear model
Authors: Ruoyu Gui, Wei Zhou, Jinsong Deng, Leiyu Shi, Guangli Hu, Gang Sun, Yingrong Lao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGlobal climate changehas led to frequent extreme hot and humid events. The Humidex, as a comprehensive temperature and humidity indicator, is closely related to infectious diseases. Other infectious diarrhea is an important public health issue, but the expode-response relationship between it and Humidex, especially the seasonal effect modification and lag effect patterns, still lacks systematic research. This study aims to evaluate the impact of Humidex on the incidence of other infectious diarrhea and explore its seasonal heterogeneity and time lag effect.MethodsUsing daily OID case and meteorological data from Guangdong Province (2014–2023), a Distributed Lag Non-linear Model was applied to assess the cumulative effects of Humidex, adjusting for season, day of the week, and holidays. Analyses included establishing the overall exposure-response relationship, stratifying by warm (May–October) and cold (November–April) seasons to examine effect modification, and conducting sensitivity analyses to verify robustness.ResultsAt the extreme Humidex exposure level (P95, 42.27), the cumulative relative risk (CRR) for OID was 1.183 (95% CI: 1.057–1.323), indicating a significant 18.3% increase in risk, with an attributable fraction (AF) of 15.4%. Conversely, a protective effect was observed at the low Humidex level (P5, 11.3) (RR = 0.832, 95% CI: 0.748–0.926). Lag analysis at high Humidex levels (e.g., P75, P90) showed that the highest risk occurred within 0–2 days after exposure, indicating a short-term lag effect. Seasonal stratification revealed significant effect modification: during the cold season, extreme Humidex showed a protective effect (RR = 0.523, 95% CI: 0.319–0.856), while the effect was non-significant in the warm season (RR = 0.957, 95% CI: 0.880–1.040), with a seasonal effect ratio of 1.83. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings to variations in lag periods and degrees of freedom for splines.ConclusionHumidex is significantly associated with OID incidence, showing a non-linear exposure-response relationship and clear seasonal effect modification. While extreme Humidex generally increases OID risk, anomalously high levels during the cold season exhibit a protective effect. These findings support the development of season-specific early warnings and targeted public health interventions based on the Humidex index.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27645-1.

Introduction

Infectious diarrhea is one of the most common infectious diseases in the world and an important indicator of regional hygiene, food safety and public health [1]. In 2010, diarrheal diseases ranked second among global infectious disease burdens. By 2015, approximately 2.3 billion people worldwide had experienced diarrhea [2]. other infectious diarrhea (OID) refers to infectious diarrhea other than cholera, dysentery, typhoid or paratyphoid [3]. Due to the wide range of pathogens and the lack of effective vaccine protection, the incidence rate is relatively high in many areas [4]. In addition, there are regional differences in the seasonal pattern and spatial distribution of OID [5]. Existing research has begun to elucidate the impacts of specific environmental stressors and socio-medical factors on OID. For instance, extreme weather events like typhoons and floods have been shown to significantly increase OID risk [6, 7], while temperature extremes are also key drivers [8]. Beyond environmental factors, the availability of primary healthcare resources [9] and specific population characteristics, such as those of preschool children [10], are crucial modifiers of disease burden. Therefore, understanding the role of environmental and meteorological exposure in these changes, within the broader context of interacting factors, is crucial for formulating targeted public health intervention measures.

Research on the relationship between environmental climate and health has been increasing in recent years. However, existing studies have predominantly focused on the impacts of ambient temperature or extreme heat on influenza [11, 12], hand, foot and mouth diseases [13, 14], respiratory diseases [15–19], cardiovascular diseases [20–22] and heat-related mortality [23–27]. In contrast, studies investigating the independent health effects of relative humidity are significantly fewer [28–30]. Furthermore, multicollinearity often exists among environmental factors [31]. Relying solely on temperature or humidity indicators makes it difficult to disentangle their individual effects and, more importantly, limits a comprehensive assessment of the true impact of combined temperature-humidity exposure on human health.

Therefore, this study introduces the Humidex (humidity index), a composite metric, to more comprehensively reflect the combined effect of temperature and humidity on apparent temperature (how hot it feels), thereby enabling a more accurate assessment of the impact of hot and humid conditions on Other Infectious Diarrhea. Modern research confirms that hot and humid environments can alter gut microbiota and increase intestinal mucosal permeability, creating conditions conducive to pathogen invasion [32]. Based on this, this study conducts an analysis of the association between Humidex and OID in Guangdong Province, aiming to systematically evaluate the impact of the humidex index on OID incidence from a modern epidemiological perspective. To accurately characterize the potential non-linear relationship and lag effects between them, this study employs a Distributed Lag Non-Linear Model(DLNM) for analysis, aiming to provide a more precise epidemiological basis for early warning and effective prevention and control of related diseases.

Methods

Study area​

This study was conducted in Guangdong Province, located in southern China (20°13′N–25°31′N, 109°39′E–117°19′E), which is characterized by a typical subtropical monsoon climate (Fig. 1). As one of the most populous and economically developed provinces in China, Guangdong had a resident population of approximately 127 million in 2023. Its climate is marked by high temperatures and humidity, with long, hot summers and mild, moist winters, providing an ideal geographical setting for examining the association between Humidex and OID. The mean values of meteorological variables during our observation period (mean temperature 22.68°C) mean relative humidity 76.42% mean Humidex index 29.69) are consistent with the perennial characteristics of the aforementioned ‘hot and humid’ subtropical monsoon climate in Guangdong Province [33], indicating that our data are climatically representative.

Data sources​

The daily case count data for Other Infectious Diarrhea (OID) used in this study were obtained from the Guangdong Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention, covering the period from January 2014 to December 2023. Cases of “Other Infectious Diarrhea” (OID) were defined according to the Chinese national standard Diagnostic Criteria for Infectious Diarrhea (WS 271–2007). This classification excludes specific notifiable diseases that are monitored separately, including cholera, bacillary and amebic dysentery, typhoid fever, and paratyphoid fever. The use of this OID category ensures consistency with China’s infectious disease surveillance practice and improves the homogeneity of the study cohort in terms of disease management, reporting mechanisms, and the spectrum of predominant pathogens. Meteorological data were sourced from the Guangdong Meteorological Bureau, including daily average temperature (°C), average humidity (%), sunshine hours (h), and daily rainfall (mm). This study utilized daily temperature and humidity observations from the Guangzhou Meteorological Bureau as a representative time series for provincial-level analysis. The data underwent strict quality control managed by professional meteorological monitoring personnel to ensure completeness and accuracy. Population data were processed using the annual constant method, based on year-end resident population figures published in the Statistical Bulletin of Guangdong Province on National Economic and Social Developmentreleased by the Guangdong Provincial Bureau of Statistics. We used the logarithmically transformed population number as the offset term to control for the influence of population size on case counts.

Statistical analysis​

In this study, we used the temperature- and humidity-based composite index Humidex as the exposure metric to better represent human-perceived temperature ( the temperature humans actually feel, rather than the objective air temperature). Humidex was proposed by Canadian meteorologists Richardson and Masterton and has been widely used in Canadian weather services [21]. Tdenotes the daily mean temperature (°C) for a specific city, and Rdenotes the daily relative humidity (%) for that city. This index assumes a linear relationship, in which relatively high humidity amplifies the physiological impact of temperature on the human body, particularly under warm conditions. The index highlights how the vapour pressure associated with relative humidity interacts with air temperature to increase perceived heat, making it a useful tool for understanding temperature–humidity interactions in moderate to high temperature environments [34]. Before constructing DLNM, we evaluated the necessity of adopting this method. The relationship between meteorological factors and the onset of OID usually has both nonlinear and hysteresis effects simultaneously, while traditional linear models (such as GLM) or single hysteresis models are difficult to fully depict such complex exposing-response relationships. The preliminary analysis results of this study also support this judgment: (1) There is a nonlinear exposing-response relationship between the number of daily Humidex and OID cases (Supplementary Figure S1); (2) Incorporating more lag days in simple GLM can improve model fitting, suggesting that Humidex has a lag effect on OID (Supplementary Table S1). Therefore, in order to simultaneously characterize the nonlinear features of the exposing-response relationship and the time distribution structure of its lag, this study adopts the distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) for analysis. This model, by constructing cross-basis functions [35], can quantify the three-dimensional “exposure-lagged response” relationship between exposure variables and health outcomes, and has been widely applied to evaluate the impact of lagged exposure on health [36, 37].

The response variable Yt follows a quasi-Poisson distribution. When constructing the distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM), the influence of the core explanatory variable Humidex is captured through the cross-basis function, which combines the basis functions of the expoth-response and lag response dimensions. In the expode-response dimension, a natural cubic spline function was used, and four internal nodes were set at the isometric percentiles of the annual Humidex data to flexibly capture its nonlinear association with disease risk. In the lag response dimension, a natural cubic spline function is also adopted, with three degrees of freedom set to obtain a smooth lag response curve. The longest lag period is set at 21 days to cover the common incubation periods of major intestinal pathogens (such as norovirus, rotavirus, and common bacteria) and the delayed effects of related environmental exposure [38]. The control variables in the model include: binary seasonal variables used to control baseline risk and as stratification factors for effect modification, and multi-class day variables and holiday variables. In addition, the long-term trends and complex seasonal patterns that were not fully captured by the above categorical variables were controlled by introducing natural cubic splines with date variables (7 degrees of freedom per year, totaling 70 degrees of freedom during the study period), and the case count was converted to the incidence scale using the population offset term.

The analysis is carried out using a multi-stage framework. Firstly, establish the overall exposure-response relationship and visualize the temporal distribution characteristics of the Humidex effect through the three-dimensional exposure-hystere-response surface. Subsequently, stratified analysis was conducted by season (warm season: May-October; cold season: November - April), and the effect modification was formally tested within the DLNM framework by establishing a model that included the interaction terms of seasonal variables and Humidex cross-basis functions. The statistical significance of the interaction was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test. Finally, sensitivity analysis was conducted by adjusting the maximum lag period, spline degrees of freedom, and handling extreme values, etc., to verify the robustness of the model results. All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Project for Statistical Computing, version 4.1.0), specifically the “dlnm” package (version 2.4.10). A two-sided p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Exposure-response relationship

This study employed a Distributed Lag Non-linear Model (DLNM) to assess the cumulative effect of Humidex on the risk of infectious diarrhea. In this study, "extreme humidex" was defined as the 95th percentile (P95, value = 42.27) of the Humidex distribution.Using the median Humidex value (31.34) as the reference (Fig. 3), at the extreme Humidex exposure level (P95, 42.27), the cumulative relative risk (CRR) for infectious diarrhea was 1.183 (95% CI: 1.057–1.323), indicating a statistically significant 18.3% increase in the risk of onset (P < 0.05).

Overall exposure-response relationship between humidex and infectious diarrhea. The green dotted line represents P50, and the red dotted line represents P95

Correspondingly, the attributable fraction (AF) was 15.4%, suggesting that approximately 15.4% of the cases during the study period were attributable to high Humidex exposure. Conversely, at the low Humidex exposure level (P5, 11.3), a significant protective effect was observed, with a relative risk of 0.832 (95% CI: 0.748–0.926), representing a 16.8% reduction in the risk of onset (P < 0.05). The attributable fraction was -20.2%, reflecting that approximately 20.2% of cases could be prevented under low-exposure conditions.

By constructing a three-dimensional exposure-lag-response surface (Fig. 4), we visualized the temporal distribution pattern of the Humidex effect. The surface shows that the relative risk fluctuated within the range of 0.90–1.00, with an overall relatively flat shape, consistent with the small effect size observed in this study. In the low Humidex (<20) region, the risk remained stable at 0.99–1.00. At moderate Humidex (20–30) combined with moderate lags (5–15 days), a slight depression appeared (RR ≈ 0.95), suggesting a possible protective effect. The overall shape of the three-dimensional response surface was relatively flat. However, in the region combining high Humidex (>30) and short lags (<5 days), an elevation of risk above the baseline level (RR >1.0) can be observed. This aligns with the finding in Figure 3 of a cumulative relative risk (CRR) significantly greater than 1 at extreme Humidex (P95). This finding is consistent with the overall mild effect observed in this study, providing visual evidence for understanding the health impacts of humid-heat exposure.

Stratified analysis

By constructing an extended model that adds the interaction term between Humidex cross-basis functions and seasonal variables on the basis of formula (2), we formally examined the effect modification effect of seasons.The interaction between season and Humidex on OID risk is statistically significant (likelihood ratio test, p < 0.001). The seasonal stratified analysis (Fig. 6) revealed an effect modification between the warm season (May–October, red) and the cold season (November–April, blue). In the exposure-response relationship (Panel A), the warm season (red curve) showed relative risks (RR) predominantly below 1.0 across the Humidex range of 20-35. In contrast, the cold season showed a distinct inverted J-shaped curve, demonstrating a protective effect at extreme Humidex levels (RR ≈ 0.5). The risk difference analysis (Panel B) indicated that the effect difference between the two seasons was most pronounced in the extreme Humidex range (>40), with a difference reaching 0.4–0.5 RR units. The quantile-specific effect comparison (Panel C) further confirmed the seasonal heterogeneity: at lower quantiles (P10–P25), the risk was higher in the cold season than in the warm season, whereas at higher quantiles (P75–P90), the risk was significantly lower in the cold season. The comparison at extreme Humidex (42.3, P95) (Panel D) showed a significant protective effect in the cold season (RR = 0.523, 95% CI: 0.319–0.856), while the effect in the warm season was not significant (RR = 0.957, 95% CI: 0.880–1.040). The seasonal effect ratio (warm/cold) reached 1.83, confirming a strong effect modification.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the model, we calculated the cumulative relative risk at extreme Humidex levels and compared the results under different parameter Settings. Firstly, regarding the maximum lag period, it was set at 21 days in the main analysis. This selection is based on the common incubation period, course of the disease, and possible delay from exposure to medical records of the main pathogens causing infectious diarrhea, and is consistent with the research methods in this field. In the sensitivity analysis, we adjusted the maximum lag period from 21 days to 14 days for testing (Fig. 7). The results show that the main conclusion remains stable: the directions and statistical significance of the overall effect, warm season effect and cold season protection effect have not changed (overall CRR=1.146 vs. 1.183; warm season CRR=0.984 vs. 0.957; cold season CRR=0.698 vs. 0.523). Secondly, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on the degrees of freedom of natural cubic splines (df=3, 4, 5), and the results were equally robust. The CRR estimates for extreme Humidex effects fluctuated within the range of 1.127 to 1.201, and all remained statistically significant. Furthermore, to assess the impact of extreme values, we defined the values outside the 1st and 99th percentiles of Humidex as extreme values and performed tailing processing. After re-fitting the model, no substantial changes occurred in the key results. The above analyses jointly support the reliability of the main analysis methodology and the robustness of the conclusions.

Discussion

This study uncovers a pivotal yet previously overlooked nuance in the environment-health relationship: in Guangdong’s subtropical climate, extreme humid-heat exerts opposite effects on infectious diarrhea risk depending on the season. While it increases risk overall, the same exposure significantly protectsduring the cold season but shows no effect during the warm season. This profound “same exposure, opposite effect” seasonal modification moves beyond merely identifying a hazard. It compellingly argues that the population’s adaptive state—a composite of physiological readiness, behavioral response, and seasonal context—is the critical determinant that transforms an environmental exposure into a health outcome. Our findings challenge the use of static environmental thresholds in public health and underscore the necessity for dynamic, context-specific risk assessment.

Interpretation of findings and plausible mechanisms

The observed 18.3% increase in OID risk at extreme Humidex (P95) aligns with established biological pathways: high temperature and humidity promote enteropathogen survival and replication [39], increase the risk of food and water contamination [40], and may compromise intestinal mucosal immunity under heat stress. However, the modest effect size and the lack of a strong, monotonic lag pattern in the overall average effect (Figure 5, P25, P50) suggest that the independent signal of Humidex is attenuated at the population level. This attenuation likely occurs because the raw climatic signal is filtered and modified by powerful human factors.

The seas-specific effects provide the clearest evidence for this filtering. The protective association (RR=0.523) during the cold season is particularly instructive. We propose that this results from a confluence of mechanisms where the population’s adaptive status alters the exposure context and its consequences. First, behavioral thermoregulationplays a key role: unseasonable warmth in winter may reduce indoor crowding (lowering contact transmission), while pervasive air conditioning use in summer may decouple individuals from ambient heat, diluting measurable effects. Second, pathogen ecology and population susceptibilityshift seasonally. The stronger association between high humidex in the warm season and the risk of infectious diarrhea observed in this study may be different from the composition of viral pathogens dominated by the cold season, reflecting the seasonal differences in the climate sensitivity of pathogens. Third, the physiological adaptations are different; People adapt to the heat of the warm season but not to that of the cold season. After being exposed to the heat of the warm season, the body's tolerance to high temperatures and physiological regulation ability may be different from the situation when suddenly encountering abnormal high temperatures in the cold season [41].

Therefore, Humidex should not be viewed as a direct toxicological stressor but as a catalyst whose health effect is determined by the receptor system’s state. The “net” risk we measure is the integrated output of environmental stress, physiological capacity, and behavioral buffering. This framework explains why a simple exposure-response function is insufficient and why stratification by season—a proxy for the population’s adaptive mode—reveals effects of opposite direction.

Public health and research implications

Our results carry immediate implications for climate-health adaptation. First, early-warning systems must be seasonally stratified. The "relative extremity" and "actual observed health risk" represented by the same absolute Humidex value in different seasons are different. Public health alerts and interventions should be triggered by deviations from seasonal norms, not by annual absolute thresholds. Second, the concentration of risk within 0-2 days post-exposure for high percentiles points to a critical window for ultra-short-term, targeted interventions, such as real-time public advisories on food safety, water quality, and hygiene for vulnerable groups.

From a methodological perspective, this study demonstrates the utility of combining composite climate indices like Humidex with analytical models capable of capturing complex temporal dynamics. The DLNM framework, coupled with rigorous stratification, was essential in moving past a null average effect to uncover significant but countervailing seasonal risks that would remain hidden in traditional models. This approach provides a blueprint for studying other climate-sensitive health outcomes in regions with strong seasonal cycles.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as an ecological study, it cannot control for confounding factors at the individual level, such as personal hygiene habits and diet. Second, OID is a syndrome encompassing various pathogens, which may respond differently to meteorological factors. This study did not conduct pathogen-specific stratified analysis. Third, our analysis focused on meteorological factors. Future studies could consider a broader set of environmental exposures to better understand the multifactorial nature of OID risk. Fourth, the research results mainly rely on the official report data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Although this data system has undergone strict quality control, there may be subtle and difficult-to-quantify differences between it and the actual incidence rate or disease burden in the community. This potential reporting insufficiency or classification bias is an inherent limitation of retrospective monitoring data. Finally, The meteorological data used in this study were sourced from a single station (Guangzhou) and thus may not fully capture the spatial heterogeneity of weather conditions within Guangdong Province. Future studies could employ gridded data or data from more stations covering a wider area for more in-depth analysis.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the association between the Humidex index and the risk of Other Infectious Diarrhea (OID) in Guangdong Province using a Distributed Lag Non-linear Model (DLNM). The findings indicate that the influence of Humidex on the onset of OID has a nonlinear relationship, and its effect is mainly characterized by a short time lag. Under extreme humidex exposure, the risk is most concentrated within 1-2 days after exposure. This study further revealed a strong seasonal effect modification: during the cold season, high Humidex exposure was associated with a reduced risk of OID, showing a protective effect, whereas the same level of exposure showed no significant association during the warm season. This seasonal pattern of "same exposure, different effects" highlights that the population's adaptive state—shaped by physiological, behavioral, and contextual factors—is crucial in determining health outcomes. Therefore, early warning systems for OID should adopt seasonally differentiated thresholds and focus on short-term exposure windows, providing a scientific basis for precise and timely public health interventions.
