GSH-derived carbon dot-mediated interfacial electron transfer modulates pH-Dependent redox catalysis of Ce-MOF nanozymes for wound healing
Authors: Yaping Chen, Kaichen Zeng, Yunxin Ye, Xinyi Sun, Ling Yan, Ziya Lao, Yifan Wu, Tao Luo, Li Yang, Lvhua Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infected wounds require therapeutic materials that can control bacterial infection without aggravating oxidative tissue damage. Here, glutathione-derived carbon dots (GSH-CDs) were introduced during the formation of a cerium metal-organic framework (Ce MOF) through an in situ integration strategy to construct a carbon-dot-integrated Ce MOF nanozyme (Ce MOF-CD). Electronic-structure analyses supported interfacial electron redistribution from GSH-CDs toward Ce MOF, accompanied by changes in the local electronic environment and Ce3+/Ce4+ distribution of the Ce active centers. Ce MOF-CD exhibited a distinct pH-dependent redox catalytic preference, with oxidase-like catalysis favored under acidic conditions and superoxide dismutase-like activity becoming more pronounced near neutrality. Accordingly, Ce MOF-CD markedly reduced the viability of MRSA and E. coli and disrupted established biofilms under acidic conditions. Under near-neutral cellular conditions, it reduced intracellular oxidative stress, attenuated inflammatory activation, and promoted repair-associated macrophage polarization. In an MRSA-infected wound model, Ce MOF-CD reduced bacterial burden and local inflammation, promoted tissue reconstruction, and accelerated wound closure with favorable biosafety. Overall, GSH-CDs-mediated interfacial electronic regulation modulates the pH-dependent redox catalytic behavior of Ce MOF, enabling antibacterial activity to be coordinated with oxidative-stress regulation during infected wound healing.

Introduction

Bacterial infection and biofilm formation cause a persistent bacterial burden and exacerbate local oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby delaying wound repair [[1], [2], [3]]. Antimicrobial resistance and biofilm-associated tolerance further reduce the efficacy of conventional antibiotics. Moreover, antibiotics generally lack the ability to directly regulate the wound redox microenvironment [[4], [5], [6]]. Therefore, alternative therapeutic strategies that combine effective bacterial control with redox regulation are needed to restore the wound microenvironment and facilitate tissue repair.

Nanozymes have attracted considerable attention because they combine enzyme-mimicking catalytic activity with the high stability, structural tunability, and facile functionalization of nanomaterials [7,8]. By catalytically generating or scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), nanozymes provide a potential strategy for integrating bacterial control with redox microenvironment regulation in infected wounds. Among various nanozyme platforms, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) possess abundant and accessible metal active sites, open porous structures, and tunable compositions and coordination environments, making them promising platforms for the construction of high-performance nanozymes [9]. In particular, the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple within the Ce-oxo nodes of cerium-based UiO-66 (Ce MOF) supports oxidase (OXD)-like and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities, which may contribute to bacterial elimination and oxidative stress alleviation, respectively [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Because these activities are sensitive to the local electronic environment and Ce3+/Ce4+ distribution, regulating the electronic structure of Ce centers provides a potential means of tuning the catalytic behavior of Ce MOF [14].

Electronic structure engineering can regulate electron transfer, intermediate adsorption, and reaction kinetics at catalytic centers [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Heterometal doping and defect engineering can modulate catalytic activity by altering the local coordination environment and electronic states of active sites, but their effectiveness is typically dependent on the type, location, and spatial distribution of the introduced dopants or defects [20,21]. Moreover, for MOFs with highly ordered coordination structures, excessive structural modification may disrupt the original node coordination and pore architecture. In contrast, interfacial engineering offers a complementary strategy for electronic regulation. Close contact between electronically functional components and the MOF framework can induce interfacial charge redistribution and establish directional electron transport pathways, thereby modulating the electronic environment of active sites near the interface without the deliberate introduction of bulk defects into the parent framework [22,23]. For Ce MOF, interfacial electronic coupling may modulate the local electronic environment and Ce3+/Ce4+ distribution of Ce-oxo nodes, thereby influencing Ce-centered redox catalytic behavior [20,24]. Accordingly, introducing functional components with both efficient electron transport capability and interfacial compatibility provides a feasible route for constructing an electronically coupled interface and regulating the catalytic behavior of Ce MOF nanozymes.

Carbon dots (CDs) are promising interfacial components because of their electron-transfer capability, biocompatibility, and abundant surface functional groups [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. In particular, glutathione-derived carbon dots (GSH-CDs) contain amino, carboxyl, and sulfur-containing groups that can interact with metal ions and organic ligands, facilitating their integration with MOF structures [27,29]. Their intrinsic antioxidant activity may also benefit applications involving oxidative stress regulation [30]. Previous studies have incorporated carbon dots into Ce-based MOFs for sensing and adsorption applications, demonstrating the feasibility of combining these two components [31,32]. Studies on semiconductor/Ce-UiO-66 systems have further shown that interfacial charge transfer can modulate the electronic behavior of Ce-based UiO frameworks [33]. Nevertheless, whether GSH-CDs can function as interfacial electron donors to regulate the Ce3+/Ce4+ states within structurally defined Ce-oxo nodes remains poorly understood. It is also unknown how such electronic regulation affects the balance between the oxidizing and superoxide-scavenging activities of Ce MOF under different pH conditions.

In this study, GSH-CDs were introduced during Ce MOF formation through an in situ integration strategy to construct an electronically coupled Ce MOF-CD nanozyme. Comparisons with the corresponding physical mixture and post-loaded composite were used to evaluate the influence of the assembly strategy on catalytic performance. Spectroscopic and computational analyses linked the enhanced catalytic activity of Ce MOF-CD to CD-to-Ce electron transfer, charge redistribution around the Ce centers, and an altered Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio. Ce MOF-CD displayed a pH-dependent catalytic preference, favoring OXD-like antibacterial catalysis under acidic conditions and showing more pronounced SOD-like superoxide-scavenging activity near neutral pH. Ce MOF-CD consequently combined bacterial elimination and biofilm disruption with the alleviation of oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, promoting the healing of MRSA-infected wounds (Scheme 1). Experimental section

Materials

Ammonium cerium nitrate, terephthalic acid (BDC), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), citric acid, glutathione (GSH), acetate buffer solution, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), Actin-Tracker Red-555, 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay kits were purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Tiron, mannitol, and l-histidine were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Dihydroethidium (DHE) was purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The SYTO 9/propidium iodide (PI) live/dead bacterial viability kit was purchased from Maokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), penicillin–streptomycin solution, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), trypsin, crystal violet staining solution, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco (Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Rabbit polyclonal primary antibodies against inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and CD206, together with Alexa Fluor 594- and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies, were purchased from HuaAn Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

Synthesis of Ce MOF

Ce MOF was synthesized according to a previously reported method with slight modifications [34]. Briefly, 1.17 g of ammonium cerium nitrate ((NH4) 2Ce (NO3) 6) and 122 μL of acetic acid were dissolved in 4 mL of deionized water and heated at 60 °C for 30 min to obtain a cerium precursor solution, which was then cooled in an ice-water bath. Separately, 0.355 g of BDC was dissolved in 18.76 mL of DMF to prepare the organic ligand solution. Under continuous stirring, the ligand solution was added dropwise to the cerium precursor solution, and the resulting mixture was further heated at 80 °C for 30 min. After the reaction, the suspension was cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min to collect the precipitate. The product was washed three times each with DMF and absolute ethanol to remove residual impurities and unreacted precursors. The purified Ce MOF was finally collected by centrifugation and dried under vacuum for subsequent use.

Synthesis of GSH-CDs

GSH-CDs were synthesized via a one-step hydrothermal method [30]. Briefly, citric acid (3.95 mmol) and glutathione (0.79 mmol) were dissolved in 20 mL of ultrapure water under magnetic stirring to form a homogeneous precursor solution. The solution was then transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 160 °C for 12 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the resulting suspension was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove insoluble aggregates. The filtrate was subsequently purified by dialysis against ultrapure water using a cellulose ester dialysis membrane (molecular weight cut-off: 500–1000 Da) for 24 h to remove unreacted precursors and small molecular byproducts. Finally, the purified GSH-CDs solution was collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Synthesis of Ce MOF-CD

Ce MOF-CD was synthesized via an in situ integration strategy, in which CDs were introduced during Ce MOF assembly to promote intimate interfacial coupling with the MOF framework. The cerium precursor solution was prepared according to the procedure described for Ce MOF synthesis. Briefly, 0.355 g of terephthalic acid was dissolved in 18.76 mL of DMF to prepare the ligand solution. The as-prepared CD solution was then added at the desired amount, and the mixture was stirred at 400 rpm for 10 min to obtain a homogeneous CD–ligand mixture. The resulting mixture was added dropwise to the cerium precursor solution under continuous stirring, followed by reaction at 80 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min. The precipitate was washed and purified following the same procedure used for Ce MOF synthesis and then vacuum-dried to obtain Ce MOF-CD. For optimization, samples prepared with different CD-to-ligand feed ratios were designated 1:15CM, 1:20CM, and 1:30CM, corresponding to CD-to-ligand ratios of 1:15, 1:20, and 1:30, respectively.

Characterization

The morphology and elemental distribution of the prepared materials were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan). The surface morphology and microstructural features were further examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, SU8010, Hitachi, Japan). The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the samples were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Nicolet iN10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed to analyze the functional groups and bonding characteristics of the materials. The crystal structure and phase composition were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan). Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra were recorded with a UV-3600i spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The elemental composition and chemical states of the samples were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a K-Alpha XPS system (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Oxidase-like (OXD) activity assay

The OXD-like activity of the materials was evaluated using a 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)-based chromogenic assay. Briefly, each material was dispersed at the same concentration and added to PBS (pH 5.5) containing TMB. The final concentrations of TMB and the materials were adjusted to 1 mM and 0.1 mg mL−1, respectively. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, the OXD-like activity was quantified by measuring the absorbance of oxidized TMB at 652 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. For evaluation of pH-dependent OXD-like activity, the assay was performed in PBS at different pH values following the same procedure.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity assay

The SOD-like activity of the samples was evaluated using a commercial SOD Activity Assay Kit (WST-1) according to the manufacturer's instructions. WST-1 reacts with superoxide radicals (O2•-) to generate a water-soluble formazan product with a characteristic absorption maximum at 450 nm. Materials with SOD-like activity scavenge O2•-, thereby inhibiting the formation of the formazan product. After incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the SOD-like activity was quantified based on the inhibition of O2•--mediated WST-1 reduction. For evaluation of pH-dependent SOD-like activity, the assay was performed in buffer solutions at pH 4.0, 5.5, 6.5, 7.4 and 8 following the same procedure.

DPPH radical scavenging assay

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of the materials was evaluated using UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy. Briefly, an ethanolic solution containing DPPH radicals (DPPH•) was mixed with different material suspensions, and the final concentration of each material was adjusted to 0.2 mg mL−1. After incubation in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, the DPPH radical scavenging activity was quantified by measuring the absorbance of the reaction mixture at 517 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

Density functional theory calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [35,36]. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and the projector augmented wave (PAW) method were employed. The strong correlation effects of Ce 4f electrons were considered using the DFT + U method, with an effective U value applied according to previous reports. The electronic self-consistent convergence criterion was set to 1 × 10−5 eV, and structural optimization was carried out until the residual force on each atom was below 0.03 eV Å−1 [37]. The structural models of Ce MOF, CD, and the Ce MOF-CD heterostructure were constructed based on experimental characterization results and fully optimized prior to electronic structure calculations. The charge density difference was calculated to investigate interfacial electron redistribution according to the following equation:Δρ = ρ (Ce MOF-CD)-ρ (Ce MOF)-ρ (CD)where ρ (Ce MOF-CD), ρ (Ce MOF), and ρ (CD) represent the charge densities of the Ce MOF-CD heterostructure, Ce MOF, and CD, respectively.

Mott–Schottky analysis

Mott–Schottky analysis was performed to evaluate the semiconductor characteristics and flat-band potentials of CD, Ce MOF, and Ce MOF-CD. Measurements were conducted in 0.5 M Na2SO4 aqueous electrolyte at a frequency of 10 Hz using a three-electrode configuration, with Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode. The Mott–Schottky plots were obtained by recording the capacitance as a function of the applied potential. The flat-band potentials (E_fb) were determined from the x-intercepts of the linear regions of the corresponding Mott–Schottky plots. The potentials measured versus Ag/AgCl were converted to the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE) scale for comparison of the relative electronic energetics of the three materials.

Bacterial viability assay by plate counting

The antibacterial activity of the prepared materials was evaluated using the standard plate counting method. Three experimental groups were established, including a PBS control group, a Ce MOF group, and a Ce MOF-CD group. Escherichia coli (E. coli) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) suspensions at a concentration of 5 × 106 CFU mL−1 were separately incubated with Ce MOF or Ce MOF-CD at final concentrations of 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 μg/mL. After co-incubation at 37 °C for 16 h, 20 μL of each bacterial suspension was spread onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for an additional 16 h. Colony images were recorded using a digital camera, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software. The antibacterial rate was calculated as follows:Antibacterialrate(%)=Ncontrol−NtreatmentNcontrol×100%Where Ncontrol and Ntreatment represent the numbers of bacterial colonies in the control and treatment groups, respectively.

Bacterial live/dead staining assay

Bacterial viability after treatment was assessed using a SYTO 9/PI Live/Dead Bacterial Viability Kit. Briefly, E. coli and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) were cultured in LB broth at 37 °C with shaking until the logarithmic growth phase. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with sterile PBS, and incubated with different materials for 16 h. After treatment, the bacterial suspensions were stained with a SYTO 9/PI working solution and incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Fluorescence images were then obtained using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). Live bacteria emitted green fluorescence (SYTO 9), whereas dead bacteria exhibited red fluorescence (PI).

Antibiofilm activity assay

The biofilm eradication activity of the materials was evaluated using a crystal violet staining assay. Briefly, 0.1 mL of E. coli or MRSA suspension (1 × 107 CFU mL−1) was mixed with 1 mL of LB broth and transferred into a 48-well plate. The plate was incubated statically at 37 °C for 48 h to allow mature biofilm formation. Subsequently, the established biofilms were treated with PBS, Ce MOF, or Ce MOF-CD at 37 °C for 24 h. After treatment, the wells were gently rinsed with sterile PBS to remove planktonic bacteria and residual materials, followed by fixation in methanol for 15 min. The biofilms were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min and washed thoroughly with PBS to remove excess dye. After air drying, the biofilm morphology was observed and photographed using a stereomicroscope. To quantify the residual biofilm biomass, 1 mL of absolute ethanol was added to each well to dissolve the bound crystal violet. Subsequently, 100 μL of the extracted solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance at 590 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

To further visualize the residual biofilms, biofilms treated with PBS, Ce MOF, or Ce MOF-CD were stained with the SYTO 9 working solution in the dark for 20 min. After staining, the samples were washed twice with sterile PBS to remove unbound dye. The three-dimensional morphology of the residual biofilms was then observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Bacterial morphology observation by SEM

After treatment, bacterial cells were collected, washed three times with sterile saline, and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), followed by drying and sputter-coating with gold. The morphological changes of the bacteria were subsequently observed using a scanning electron microscope (Sigma 360, Zeiss, Germany).

Electron paramagnetic resonance detection of reactive oxygen species

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was employed to identify reactive oxygen species generated by Ce MOF-CD under acidic conditions using spin-trapping methods adapted from previously reported protocols. Ce MOF-CD (80 μg/mL) was dispersed in acetate buffer (pH 5.5). For the detection of O2•--associated radicals, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO, 100 mM) was used as the spin-trapping agent. A parallel group containing Tiron (10 mM) was included to evaluate the sensitivity of the detected signal to O2•- scavenging. For 1O2 detection, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP, 100 mM) was used as the spin-trapping agent, with l-histidine (10 mM) included in a parallel group as a 1O2 scavenger. Hydroxyl radical (•OH)-associated signals were examined using DMPO (100 mM) as the spin-trapping agent under the same acidic conditions. The freshly prepared reaction mixtures were immediately subjected to EPR measurement. EPR spectra were recorded using an EMXplus spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) operating in the X-band. The measurement parameters were as follows: microwave frequency, approximately 9.837 GHz; center magnetic field, 3500 G; sweep width, 200 G; sweep time, 10.04 s; microwave power, 2.0 mW; modulation frequency, 100 kHz; modulation amplitude, 1.0 G; receiver gain, 30 dB; number of scans, 10; and number of acquisition points, 1400.

ROS-scavenger-assisted OXD-like activity assay

The contributions of different ROS-associated processes to the OXD-like activity of Ce MOF-CD were evaluated using selective ROS scavengers. Ce MOF-CD (80 μg/mL) was incubated with TMB (1 mM) in acetate buffer (pH 5.5) in the absence or presence of different scavengers. Tiron (10 mM), mannitol (100 mM), and l-histidine (20 mM) were used to perturb O2•--, •OH-, and 1O2-associated processes, respectively. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and TMB oxidation was evaluated by measuring the absorbance at 652 nm.

To exclude the possibility that Tiron directly reduced oxidized TMB (oxTMB), an additional control was performed in which Tiron (10 mM) was added after completion of the Ce MOF-CD-mediated TMB oxidation reaction, followed by measurement of the absorbance at 652 nm.

Fluorescence-probe detection of ROS-associated signals in bacteria

Dihydroethidium (DHE) and Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG) were used to monitor O2•--associated oxidative signals and 1O2-associated fluorescence, respectively, in E. coli and MRSA. Bacterial suspensions were incubated with Ce MOF-CD (80 μg/mL) at 37 °C for 1h under acidic conditions (pH 5.5). For DHE analysis, a parallel group containing Tiron (10 mM) was included to perturb O2•--associated processes. For SOSG analysis, l-histidine (20 mM) was included as a 1O2-scavenging control. After treatment, bacteria were collected and washed with PBS. The bacterial suspensions were subsequently incubated with DHE or SOSG for 30 min in the dark. After removal of excess probe, fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical imaging settings among groups. DHE-derived red fluorescence and SOSG-derived green fluorescence were used to evaluate O2•--associated oxidative signals and 1O2-associated signals, respectively.

ROS scavenger-assisted colony-counting assay

E. coli and MRSA suspensions were adjusted to 5 × 106 CFU mL−1 and divided into eight groups: control, Tiron, mannitol, l-histidine, Ce MOF-CD, Ce MOF-CD + Tiron, Ce MOF-CD + mannitol, and Ce MOF-CD + l-histidine. For the scavenger-containing groups, Tiron, mannitol, or l-histidine was added to the bacterial suspensions at a final concentration of 10 mM and pre-incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Ce MOF-CD was then added to the corresponding groups at a final concentration of 80 μg/mL. All treatments were performed in acetate buffer at pH 5.5. The bacterial suspensions were further incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. After treatment, the suspensions were serially diluted with sterile PBS, and 20 μL of an appropriate dilution was plated onto LB agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h, after which bacterial colonies were photographed and counted. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell viability assay

L929 fibroblasts and RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at densities of 5 × 103 and 1 × 104 cells per well, respectively, and cultured overnight in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were subsequently incubated with Ce MOF-CD at concentrations of 0, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 μg/mL for 24 or 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

In addition, cell viability was further assessed using a Calcein-AM/PI Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit. After the indicated treatments, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with the staining solution at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were subsequently captured using a fluorescence microscope. Live and dead cells were identified by green fluorescence (Calcein-AM) and red fluorescence (PI), respectively.

Intracellular ROS scavenging assay

The intracellular ROS-scavenging activity of the materials was evaluated using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. Briefly, RAW264.7 macrophages and L929 fibroblasts were seeded in culture plates and cultured until reaching an appropriate density. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing 100 μM H2O2, and the cells were incubated for 2 h to induce oxidative stress. After stimulation, the H2O2-containing medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS.

Subsequently, fresh complete medium containing different materials was added, and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. DCFH-DA stock solution was diluted with serum-free medium to obtain a working solution with a final concentration of 10 μM. The cells were incubated with the probe at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark, followed by staining with Hoechst 33342 (1:100 dilution) for an additional 10 min. After staining, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove excess probe. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan). The intracellular ROS level was evaluated based on the fluorescence intensity of DCF.

DHE detection of intracellular O2•--associated oxidative signals

DHE staining was used to evaluate intracellular O2•--associated oxidative signals in RAW264.7 macrophages and L929 fibroblasts. Cells were first exposed to H2O2 (100 μM) for 2 h to establish an oxidative-stress model. After removal of H2O2 and washing, the cells were treated with the indicated materials at 80 μg/mL for 24 h. A Tiron-treated group (10 mM) was included as a superoxide-scavenging control.

Following treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with DHE for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Excess probe was removed by washing with PBS, and DHE-derived red fluorescence was observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

RAW264.7 macrophages were pretreated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h to induce an inflammatory response, followed by incubation with Ce MOF or Ce MOF-CD (80 μg/mL) for an additional 24 h. Total RNA was extracted from the cells and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) according to the manufacturer's protocols. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was subsequently performed to determine the mRNA expression levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-α (Tnf-α), interleukin-6 (Il-6), interleukin-1β (Il-1β) and the anti-inflammatory cytokines arginase-1(Arg-1), interleukin-4(Il-4), and transforming growth factor beta (Tgf-β). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

RAW264.7 cells were divided into four groups: control, LPS, LPS + Ce MOF, and LPS + Ce MOF-CD. Except for the control group, cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h. After LPS stimulation, the cells were washed with PBS to remove LPS and replaced with fresh culture medium. Ce MOF or Ce MOF-CD was then added to the corresponding groups at a final concentration of 80 μg/mL, while the LPS group was cultured in fresh medium without material treatment. After an additional 24 h of incubation, the cells were collected and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF (Beyotime, China). The cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatants were collected. Total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China).

Equal amounts of total protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 1 h and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TNF-α (1:1000, Proteintech, China), IL-6 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), IL-10 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), CD206 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and GAPDH (1:20000, Proteintech, China). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using BeyoECL Star chemiluminescence reagent (Beyotime, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and the expression levels of target proteins were normalized to GAPDH.

Immunofluorescence staining

RAW264.7 macrophages were stimulated with LPS to establish an in vitro inflammatory model and subsequently cultured in fresh medium containing different materials for 24 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and washed thoroughly with PBS. After blocking with 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against iNOS and CD206. The following day, the cells were brought to room temperature and washed three times with PBS to remove unbound primary antibodies. Subsequently, the corresponding fluorescently labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min, followed by washing with PBS to remove excess dye. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan).

Cellular uptake assay

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight to allow cell attachment. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing FITC-labeled Ce MOF-CD and incubated for 0.5, 1, and 2 h, respectively. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS to remove uninternalized materials and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the actin cytoskeleton was stained with phalloidin for 60 min in the dark, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI for 10 min. After washing with PBS to remove excess dye, fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan) under identical imaging settings for all groups. The intracellular FITC fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. For each group, representative fields were analyzed, and the mean fluorescence intensity was calculated to evaluate the time-dependent cellular uptake of Ce MOF-CD.

Hemolysis assay

The hemocompatibility of Ce MOF-CD was evaluated using a hemolysis assay. Fresh whole blood was collected from mice and washed once with PBS. The obtained red blood cells (RBCs) were diluted with PBS at a volume ratio of 1:1.25. Subsequently, the diluted RBC suspension was incubated with Ce MOF-CD at different concentrations (20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 μg/mL). Deionized water and PBS were used as the positive and negative controls, respectively. All samples were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After incubation, the suspensions were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 545 nm using a microplate reader. The hemolysis ratio was calculated according to the following equation:[(At - An) / (Ap - An)] × 100%where (At), (Ap), and (An) represent the absorbance values of the treatment group, positive control group, and negative control group, respectively.

Wound healing scratch assay

L929 fibroblasts were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells mL−1 (2 mL per well) and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 until approximately 90% confluence was reached. A uniform linear scratch was then created in the center of each well using a sterile pipette tip. The cells were gently washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells and debris generated during scratching. Subsequently, the cells were divided into three groups: a control group cultured in serum-free DMEM, a Ce MOF treatment group, and a Ce MOF-CD treatment group. Images of the scratch area were captured at 0 and 24 h using an optical microscope to evaluate cell migration and wound closure.

In vivo wound healing evaluation

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from Zhuhai Bes Test Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangdong Huawei Testing Co., Ltd. (HWT-BG-117b; No. 202512003). The mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): Control, Vancomycin, Ce MOF, and Ce MOF-CD groups. The Control and Vancomycin groups served as the negative and positive controls, respectively. All mice were housed individually throughout the study. Under local anesthesia, a full-thickness excisional wound (∼12 mm in diameter) was created on the dorsal skin of each mouse. Subsequently, 20 μL of MRSA suspension (1 × 108 CFU mL−1) was inoculated onto the wound bed to establish an infected wound model. After 24 h of infection, the wounds received the corresponding treatments. Specifically, 10 μL of the respective agents were topically instilled onto the wound surface once daily: the Vancomycin group received vancomycin solution, the Ce MOF and Ce MOF-CD groups received the corresponding nanomaterial suspensions, and the Control group received an equal volume of sterile PBS. Body weight and wound appearance were recorded at predetermined time points throughout the experimental period. Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ software, and the wound closure rate was calculated accordingly. In addition, the pH value of the wound bed was measured directly in situ on the wound surface using a portable pH meter (Leici, PH-30P, China) on a daily basis to dynamically monitor changes in the wound microenvironment.

On day 10 post-treatment, wound tissues were harvested for bacterial burden analysis. The bacterial load at the wound site was quantified using the standard plate-counting method. To evaluate the local inflammatory response, wound tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer, and the supernatants were collected after centrifugation. The levels of IL-10 and TNF-α were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' instructions, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard calibration curves.

Histological and immunohistochemical evaluation

After 10 days, the mice were euthanized, and wound tissues along with major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested. Serum samples from the mice were collected for biochemical analysis to evaluate liver and kidney function using various indices. The collected specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded, and subsequently sectioned for histological analysis. The major organs were subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, while the wound tissues underwent both H&E and Masson trichrome staining. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to detect the expression levels of IL-6 and CD206 in the wound tissues, and quantitative analysis was then performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analyses

All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons between more than two groups, while two-way ANOVA was employed for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8), and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was indicated as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and ns = not significant.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of Ce MOF-CD

Glutathione-derived carbon dots (CD) were synthesized by a hydrothermal method [30]. Ce MOF-CD was subsequently prepared through an in situ integration strategy. Before addition of the cerium precursor, CD were introduced into the BDC ligand solution, allowing them to participate in the subsequent nucleation and growth of Ce MOF. Upon addition of the cerium precursor, Ce ions coordinated with BDC ligands to form the Ce MOF framework, yielding the Ce MOF-CD composite.

TEM analysis showed that the as-prepared CD were well dispersed, with an average particle size of approximately 4 nm. HRTEM images revealed lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.22 nm (Fig. 1A), corresponding to the (100) plane of graphitic carbon and indicating the presence of local graphitic domains in the CD [32]. In contrast, pristine Ce MOF exhibited a regular polyhedral morphology with a particle size of approximately 200 nm and good dispersibility, which was further confirmed by SEM (Fig. S1). In Ce MOF-CD, lattice fringes similar to those observed for CD were detected (Fig. 1C and S2), whereas no corresponding lattice features were observed in pristine Ce MOF under the same HRTEM conditions (Fig. 1B and S1). Localized TEM–EDS elemental mapping detected S signals across the analyzed Ce MOF-CD particles (Fig. 1D). Because no sulfur-containing precursor was used in the synthesis of pristine Ce MOF, the detected S signal was consistent with the presence of CD in the composite. Together with the CD-like lattice fringes observed by HRTEM, these results provide complementary structural and elemental evidence supporting the incorporation of CD into the Ce MOF-CD composite.

DLS analysis showed that the average hydrodynamic diameter decreased from approximately 214.8 nm for Ce MOF to 142.0 nm for Ce MOF-CD (Fig. 1E and S3), consistent with the particle-size change observed by TEM. This decrease may reflect changes in nucleation and crystal-growth processes following CD incorporation, as the particle size of UiO-type MOFs is highly dependent on nucleation and growth kinetics [38,39]. The hydrodynamic diameter of CD was 6.38 nm (Fig. S4), slightly larger than the approximately 4 nm core size observed by TEM, which can be attributed to the hydration layer associated with hydrophilic surface groups.

Zeta-potential measurements showed values of approximately +24.8 mV for Ce MOF and −5.7 mV for CDs. After CD incorporation, the zeta potential of Ce MOF-CD decreased to approximately +7.5 mV (Fig. 1F). This shift indicates a change in the surface charge environment after CD incorporation and is consistent with the contribution of negatively charged surface groups from CDs to the composite.

The FTIR spectra are shown in Fig. 1G. Ce MOF exhibited characteristic Ce–O vibration bands at 584.3 and 514.9 cm−1, together with a broad O–H stretching band at 3407.6 cm−1.

After CD incorporation, the O–H stretching band of Ce MOF-CD shifted to lower wavenumbers and became broader with reduced intensity, consistent with interfacial interactions between CD surface groups and the Ce MOF framework. The characteristic Ce–O bands were retained, indicating preservation of the primary Ce MOF framework. The CO band was also markedly attenuated in the composite, suggesting that carboxyl groups on the CD surface may participate in interactions with Ce centers.

The XRD patterns are shown in Fig. 1H. Ce MOF exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ values of approximately 7.1°, 8.2°, and 24.8°, consistent with the simulated pattern and confirming formation of the crystalline Ce MOF phase. CDs displayed a broad diffraction band in the 18°–25° range, characteristic of low-crystallinity carbon materials.

Ce MOF-CD retained the main diffraction peaks of Ce MOF at nearly unchanged positions, indicating preservation of the primary crystalline structure after CD incorporation. The reduced peak intensity and slight peak broadening suggest decreased long-range order and/or crystallite size, consistent with the particle-size changes observed by TEM and DLS. In addition, the enhanced broad background in the 18°–25° region is consistent with the low-crystallinity carbon structure of CD (Fig. S5).

XPS was further used to examine the surface elemental composition and chemical states of Ce MOF, CD, and Ce MOF-CD. The survey spectra showed that Ce MOF mainly contained Ce, C, O, and N, whereas CD contained C, O, N, and a small amount of S (Fig. 1I and S9). No distinct S peak was observed in the survey spectrum of Ce MOF-CD. This result is not necessarily inconsistent with the S distribution detected by TEM-EDS because the two techniques differ in detection depth and sampling region. The relatively low loading of GSH-CDs, together with partial coverage of CD during in situ MOF growth, may further attenuate the S signal detected by surface-sensitive XPS. In contrast, TEM-EDS probes selected local regions of individual particles and can therefore detect low-abundance sulfur within these regions [40,41].

The high-resolution C 1s spectrum was fitted with three components at 284.8 eV, approximately 286.4 eV, and approximately 288.7 eV, assigned to C–C/C–H, C–O/C–N, and CO, respectively (Fig. 1J and S6). Compared with Ce MOF, Ce MOF-CD showed an increased proportion of the C–O/C–N component, consistent with the contribution of oxygen- and nitrogen-containing groups from GSH-CDs.

The O 1s spectrum was fitted with three components at approximately 529.7, 531.7, and 533.2 eV, assigned to Ce–O, hydroxyl-related oxygen, and C–O/adsorbed water, respectively (Fig. 1K and S7). After CD incorporation, the proportion of the hydroxyl-related component increased, further supporting interactions between CD surface groups and Ce centers.

The N 1s spectrum showed increased proportions of pyrrolic N and graphitic N in Ce MOF-CD, accompanied by a decrease in the nitrate-related component (Fig. S8), indicating that GSH-CDs incorporation altered the surface nitrogen environment of the composite.

High-resolution Ce 3d spectra confirmed the coexistence of Ce3+ and Ce4+ in both Ce MOF and Ce MOF-CD (Fig. 1L). After CD incorporation, the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio increased from 0.68 to 1.13, accompanied by an increase in the fitted Ce3+ components and a corresponding decrease in the Ce4+ components. This shift indicates that CD incorporation altered the Ce valence distribution and local electronic environment, providing a structural basis for the subsequent investigation of the redox catalytic behavior of Ce MOF-CD.

pH-dependent redox catalytic behavior of Ce MOF-CD

To examine the influence of the assembly strategy on catalytic performance, three Ce MOF-CD formulations were compared. Ce MOF-CD1 was prepared as a physical mixture of preformed Ce MOF and CD, Ce MOF-CD2 was obtained by post-synthetic loading of CD onto preformed Ce MOF, and Ce MOF-CD3 was synthesized by introducing CD during MOF formation. All three formulations were prepared using the same nominal CD-to-MOF ratio. Ce MOF-CD3 exhibited the highest OXD-like and SOD-like activities among the three formulations (Fig. S10), indicating that the assembly strategy influenced the catalytic performance of the CD/Ce MOF system. The in situ integration strategy was therefore used for subsequent optimization.

The nominal CD-to-ligand feed ratio was then optimized within the in situ system. The formulation prepared at a CD-to-ligand ratio of 1:15 showed the highest OXD-like activity, whereas the 1:20 formulation exhibited the highest O2•--scavenging activity (Fig. 2B and C). OXD-like activity was evaluated using TMB as the chromogenic substrate (Fig. 2A). At pH 5.5, the substrate-only blank, containing TMB and buffer without nanomaterial, showed negligible absorbance at 652 nm, excluding appreciable spontaneous TMB oxidation under the assay conditions. CD alone also produced negligible TMB oxidation. Both Ce MOF and Ce MOF-CD catalyzed oxTMB formation, with Ce MOF-CD producing a higher absorbance than Ce MOF (Fig. 2D). The oxTMB absorbance increased progressively as the Ce MOF-CD concentration increased from 5 to 40 μg/mL (Fig. 2E). Across the tested pH range, OXD-like activity reached its maximum at pH 4.0 and decreased as the pH approached neutrality, with only weak TMB oxidation observed at pH 7.0 and 8.0 (Fig. 2F). These results demonstrate that the OXD-like activity of Ce MOF-CD is favored under acidic conditions.

SOD-like activity was evaluated using the WST-1 assay (Fig. 2G). The blank group, consisting of the complete WST-1 reaction system without nanomaterial, showed an inhibition rate close to zero. CD alone produced a measurable inhibition rate, demonstrating its intrinsic superoxide-scavenging activity. Ce MOF exhibited pronounced SOD-like activity, whereas Ce MOF-CD increased the inhibition rate to approximately 90% (Fig. 2H). Under the matched assay conditions, Ce MOF-CD exhibited greater SOD-like activity than either CD or Ce MOF alone.

The WST-1 absorbance remained relatively high at pH 4.0 and 5.5 but decreased progressively at pH 6.5, 7.4, and 8.0 (Fig. 2I). Because lower WST-1 absorbance corresponds to stronger inhibition of O2•--mediated formazan formation, these results indicate that the SOD-like activity of Ce MOF-CD became more pronounced as the environment approached neutrality. Together with the OXD-like results, the data establish a pH-dependent catalytic preference in which acidic conditions favor OXD-like catalysis, whereas near-neutral conditions favor SOD-like superoxide scavenging.

DPPH• was used as a complementary model to evaluate general radical-scavenging activity (Fig. 2J). Relative to the material-free blank, CD, Ce MOF, and Ce MOF-CD all decreased the characteristic DPPH• absorbance at 517 nm (Fig. 2K). CD produced the greatest decrease, consistent with its intrinsic radical-scavenging activity, whereas Ce MOF-CD showed greater scavenging than pristine Ce MOF. The DPPH• absorbance decreased progressively as the Ce MOF-CD concentration increased from 5 to 40 μg/mL, demonstrating concentration-dependent radical scavenging (Fig. 2L).

Interfacial electron redistribution was examined by charge-density-difference analysis. Electron depletion occurred predominantly on the CD side, whereas electron accumulation was observed around the Ce-oxo clusters of Ce MOF (Fig. 3C). The plane-averaged charge-density-difference profile showed corresponding charge-density variations across the interface (Fig. 3D), consistent with directional electron redistribution from CD toward Ce MOF. This direction was also consistent with the increase in the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio from 0.68 in Ce MOF to 1.13 in Ce MOF-CD, jointly supporting electron enrichment and valence redistribution at the Ce centers following CD integration.

TDOS analysis was used to evaluate the effect of interfacial association on the electronic states of Ce MOF. Compared with pristine Ce MOF, Ce MOF-CD exhibited an increased density of occupied states near the Fermi level and changes in the unoccupied-state distribution between 2 and 4 eV (Fig. 3E). These changes demonstrate that CD integration perturbed the overall electronic-state distribution of Ce MOF.

UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy was subsequently used to characterize the optical properties of the materials. Ce MOF-CD exhibited enhanced absorption and a slight red shift of the absorption edge relative to Ce MOF (Fig. 3F). Tauc analysis yielded estimated optical band gaps of 2.76, 2.83, and 2.80 eV for Ce MOF-CD, Ce MOF, and CD, respectively (Fig. 3G). The modest decrease in the estimated optical band gap of Ce MOF-CD was consistent with modification of its electronic structure following interfacial integration.

Mott–Schottky measurements were performed to estimate the flat-band potentials of the materials (Fig. 3H). The positive slopes of the linear regions were consistent with n-type semiconductor characteristics. The flat-band potentials of Ce MOF-CD, Ce MOF, and CD were −1.43, −1.32, and −1.49 V versus Ag/AgCl, respectively, corresponding to −1.23, −1.12, and −1.29 V versus NHE. For these n-type materials, the flat-band potentials were used as approximations of their conduction-band-edge potentials. Notably, the flat-band potential of CD was more negative than that of Ce MOF, whereas Ce MOF-CD exhibited an intermediate value. This energetic ordering was consistent with electron redistribution from CD toward Ce MOF upon interfacial contact and complemented the charge-density-difference and XPS results.

The superoxide-scavenging capacities of the materials were further evaluated using EPR spectroscopy with DMPO as the spin-trapping agent. The material-free superoxide-generating system produced a characteristic DMPO–OOH signal (Fig. 3I). Addition of CD or Ce MOF attenuated this signal, whereas Ce MOF-CD produced the greatest attenuation. These results demonstrate the intrinsic superoxide-scavenging activity of CD and the greater superoxide-scavenging capacity of the composite under the assay conditions, consistent with the SOD-like activity measurements.

Based on the estimated optical band gaps and approximate conduction-band-edge potentials, the valence-band potentials of Ce MOF-CD, Ce MOF, and CD were calculated to be 1.53, 1.71, and 1.51 V versus NHE, respectively. The resulting energetic alignment is illustrated in Fig. 3J. The approximate conduction-band-edge potential of CD was more negative than that of Ce MOF, consistent with the energetic tendency for electron redistribution from CD toward Ce MOF indicated by the DFT results. Moreover, the approximate conduction-band-edge potentials of all three materials were more negative than the reported O2/O2•- redox potential of approximately −0.33 V versus NHE, indicating that electron transfer to O2 and the formation of O2•- were thermodynamically feasible. Together with the EPR detection of O2•--associated signals, these energetic relationships support the involvement of O2 activation in Ce MOF-CD-mediated redox catalysis.

Collectively, the DFT, XPS, TDOS, UV–vis DRS, and Mott–Schottky results support interface-induced electron redistribution from CD toward the Ce-oxo nodes. The resulting electron enrichment and altered Ce3+/Ce4+ distribution modified the electronic environment of the Ce centers and their interactions with oxygen-derived species. The energetic alignment was consistent with O2 reduction during acidic OXD-like catalysis, whereas the increased Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio and intrinsic radical-scavenging activity of CD contributed to the enhanced superoxide removal and SOD-like activity of Ce MOF-CD. Thus, the catalytic enhancement of Ce MOF-CD reflects both interface-induced regulation of Ce-centered redox catalysis and the intrinsic radical-scavenging contribution of CD.

In vitro antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of Ce MOF-CD

The enhanced OXD-like activity of Ce MOF-CD under acidic conditions prompted us to evaluate its antibacterial performance. MRSA and E. coli were selected as representative Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains, respectively. Antibacterial activity was evaluated by colony counting at different pH values and material concentrations. At pH 7.4, abundant colonies were observed following both Ce MOF and Ce MOF-CD treatment, with no significant differences from the corresponding control group (Fig. 4A). By contrast, at pH 5.5, the number of surviving colonies decreased progressively with increasing concentrations of both materials. CFU analysis confirmed the concentration-dependent antibacterial activity of Ce MOF-CD. At 80 μg/mL, Ce MOF-CD achieved bacterial reduction rates exceeding 95% against both MRSA and E. coli, significantly outperforming Ce MOF at the same concentration (Fig. S11). SYTO 9/PI staining was used to examine changes in bacterial membrane integrity. The control group exhibited predominantly green fluorescence and only weak red fluorescence, consistent with a bacterial population dominated by membrane-intact cells (Fig. 4B). Ce MOF treatment produced a moderate reduction in green fluorescence and an increase in red fluorescence. These changes were more pronounced following Ce MOF-CD treatment in both MRSA and E. coli, indicating greater membrane damage. Fluorescence-intensity analysis confirmed decreased green fluorescence and increased red fluorescence following Ce MOF-CD treatment (Fig. S12), consistent with the CFU results.

SEM was subsequently used to examine treatment-induced morphological changes. MRSA and E. coli cells in the control group retained intact morphologies and smooth, continuous surfaces (Fig. 4C). Following Ce MOF treatment, some cells showed mild shrinkage and surface depressions. Ce MOF-CD caused more pronounced morphological damage, including severe deformation and collapse of E. coli and disruption of the spherical morphology of MRSA. These observations further demonstrate greater bacterial structural damage following Ce MOF-CD treatment than following Ce MOF treatment.

The above results demonstrate that Ce MOF-CD possesses excellent bactericidal activity against planktonic E. coli and MRSA. However, in actual infected wounds, bacteria often secrete extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) to form biofilms, which substantially enhance their tolerance to external stresses and antibacterial agents. Therefore, it is of great importance to further evaluate the ability of Ce MOF-CD to inhibit biofilm formation and eradicate established biofilms.

The activity of Ce MOF-CD against established biofilms was evaluated using crystal violet staining and CLSM. Biofilms were allowed to develop for 48 h before treatment with the materials for an additional 24 h. Ce MOF produced only a limited reduction in crystal violet staining, whereas Ce MOF-CD markedly decreased the amount of retained stain (Fig. 4D). Absorbance measurements of ethanol-extracted crystal violet confirmed that the remaining biofilm biomass was significantly lower in the Ce MOF-CD group than in the control and Ce MOF groups (Fig. 4E). SYTO 9-stained CLSM images showed extensive and continuous bacterial coverage in the control group, whereas Ce MOF-CD treatment resulted in markedly reduced fluorescence coverage and fragmented biofilm structures (Fig. 4F and G). Together, these results demonstrate that Ce MOF-CD exhibits enhanced antibacterial activity against planktonic bacteria and effectively reduces and disrupts established biofilms.

ROS identification and antibacterial mechanism of Ce MOF-CD

To identify the ROS involved in the antibacterial activity of Ce MOF-CD under acidic conditions, EPR spin-trapping measurements were performed at pH 5.5. In the presence of DMPO, Ce MOF-CD produced a pronounced superoxide-associated EPR signal, which was attenuated by Tiron, supporting the formation of O2•--associated species (Fig. 5A). TEMP-based EPR analysis produced a characteristic three-line TEMPO signal, whose intensity decreased in the presence of l-histidine, supporting the formation of 1O2 (Fig. 5B). A four-line DMPO–OH-type signal was also observed under the •OH-trapping conditions (Fig. 5C). To examine the contributions of these ROS to OXD-like catalysis, Tiron, mannitol, and l-histidine were introduced into the TMB oxidation assay. Tiron markedly suppressed Ce MOF-CD-mediated TMB oxidation. Addition of Tiron after completion of the reaction did not appreciably reduce oxTMB absorbance, excluding direct reduction of preformed oxTMB as the primary explanation for the observed inhibition. In contrast, neither mannitol nor l-histidine suppressed TMB oxidation (Fig. 5D). When considered together with the EPR results, these findings support the involvement of O2•--associated processes in Ce MOF-CD-mediated TMB oxidation, whereas mannitol and l-histidine did not measurably affect TMB oxidation under the tested conditions.

DHE and SOSG fluorescence imaging was subsequently used to monitor ROS-associated signals following bacterial treatment. Ce MOF-CD markedly increased DHE fluorescence in both E. coli and MRSA, whereas Tiron significantly attenuated this signal. Ce MOF-CD also enhanced SOSG fluorescence in both bacterial systems, and this increase was markedly reduced by l-histidine (Fig. 5F). Together with the EPR findings, these observations support the detection of both O2•--associated oxidative signals and 1O2 in association with Ce MOF-CD-mediated bacterial treatment.

The functional contributions of these ROS to bacterial inactivation were further evaluated using the corresponding scavengers. Representative plate-counting images showed little change in colony numbers following the addition of Tiron or mannitol compared with Ce MOF-CD treatment alone, whereas l-histidine markedly increased the number of surviving colonies (Fig. 5G). Quantitative CFU analysis confirmed that Tiron and mannitol did not significantly alter the antibacterial efficacy of Ce MOF-CD, whereas l-histidine significantly increased bacterial survival (Fig. S13). Although Tiron reduced both TMB oxidation and DHE fluorescence, it did not significantly restore bacterial survival, suggesting that O2•--associated processes contributed to catalytic oxidation but were not the predominant determinant of bacterial killing. Although a DMPO–OH-type signal was detected, mannitol did not measurably suppress TMB oxidation or significantly restore bacterial survival, suggesting that •OH-associated processes were detectable but did not make a major functional contribution under the tested conditions. In contrast, the combined SOSG and antibacterial scavenger results support a major role for 1O2 in Ce MOF-CD-mediated bacterial inactivation.

Collectively, the EPR, fluorescence-probe, and scavenger experiments support distinct functional contributions of ROS to the acidic antibacterial activity of Ce MOF-CD. O2•--associated processes were involved in TMB oxidation, whereas 1O2 made a major contribution to bacterial inactivation, with only a limited functional contribution from •OH. Combined with the electronic-structure analyses, these results are consistent with a model in which interface-induced electron redistribution may modulate Ce-centered oxygen activation, thereby enabling ROS-mediated antibacterial catalysis under acidic conditions (Fig. 5E).

Biocompatibility of Ce MOF-CD

The cytocompatibility of Ce MOF-CD was evaluated in RAW264.7 macrophages and L929 fibroblasts using the CCK-8 assay. At concentrations up to 80 μg/mL, both cell types maintained more than 80% viability at the evaluated time points (Fig. S14). At 160 μg/mL, however, L929 cell viability decreased, indicating concentration-dependent cytotoxicity at higher concentrations. Consistent with the CCK-8 results, Calcein-AM/PI staining showed predominantly viable cells and few PI-positive cells at concentrations up to 80 μg/mL (Fig. S15).

Hemocompatibility was assessed using a hemolysis assay. The hemolysis rate remained below 5% throughout the tested concentration range, and no visible erythrocyte lysis was observed at 80 μg/mL (Fig. 8C). Based on the cell-viability and hemolysis results, 80 μg/mL was selected for subsequent cellular experiments.

Cellular uptake of Ce MOF-CD by RAW264.7 macrophages was subsequently evaluated using FITC-labeled Ce MOF-CD. Cell-associated green fluorescence progressively increased as the incubation time was extended from 30 min to 1 and 2 h (Fig. S16A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant time-dependent increase in mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. S16B), consistent with the progressive cellular association and uptake of Ce MOF-CD.

As shown in Fig. 6A, exposure to 100 μM H2O2 markedly increased intracellular DCF fluorescence in both RAW264.7 and L929 cells. Compared with the H2O2-treated group, CD and Ce MOF treatment reduced the fluorescence intensity to different extents, whereas Ce MOF-CD produced the greatest reduction, with the overall signal approaching that of the PBS group. In vivo evaluation of bacterial-infected wound healing

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of Ce MOF-CD in vivo, an MRSA-infected full-thickness skin wound model was established in mice. The animals were randomly assigned to four groups: control, vancomycin, Ce MOF, and Ce MOF-CD. The vancomycin group received vancomycin at 2 mg/mL, whereas the Ce MOF and Ce MOF-CD groups were treated with the corresponding material dispersions at 80 μg/mL. Wound closure was monitored on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 after treatment (Fig. 8A). On day 5, the wound-closure rate in the Ce MOF-CD group reached approximately 65% and was significantly higher than those in the other groups (Fig. 8B). Representative agar plates obtained on day 10 showed markedly fewer colonies in the Ce MOF-CD group than in the other groups (Fig. 8G). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that the bacterial burden was significantly lower in the Ce MOF-CD group than in the control, vancomycin, and Ce MOF groups (Fig. 8F). Thus, the in vivo pH trajectory was consistent with the pH-dependent catalytic preference observed in vitro, suggesting that Ce MOF-CD may contribute to early bacterial control and subsequent redox regulation during wound repair.

Body weight remained stable in all groups throughout the 10-day treatment period (Fig. 8E). Histological examination of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney revealed no evident inflammatory cell infiltration, tissue necrosis, or other pathological abnormalities (Fig. S18). No abnormal changes were detected in the measured blood biochemical parameters (Fig. 8H). Collectively, these findings indicate no evident systemic toxicity under the tested conditions.

Collectively, Ce MOF-CD treatment reduced bacterial burden and local inflammation while improving epidermal reconstruction, skin appendage regeneration, and collagen remodeling. Together with the temporal recovery of wound pH in vivo and the pH-dependent catalytic preference observed in vitro, these findings suggest that antibacterial activity, redox regulation, and attenuation of the local inflammatory response jointly contributed to accelerated MRSA-infected wound healing.

Discussion

This study shows that the incorporation of GSH-derived carbon dots alters the electronic environment of Ce active centers within Ce MOF, thereby modulating pH-dependent catalytic behavior on Ce MOF-CD and enabling coordination between antibacterial activity and host tissue protection under different wound conditions. The significance of this system lies in regulating the relative contribution of oxidative and antioxidative redox functions according to the local chemical environment rather than simply increasing a specific enzyme-like activity. This property allows the catalytic behavior of the material to adapt to the dynamic redox requirements of infected wounds.

From a physicochemical perspective, CD functions as an interfacial electronic regulator rather than an inert carrier or independent functional additive. The reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox transition provides the fundamental basis for Ce-centered redox catalysis, whereas limited electron transport within pristine Ce MOF may constrain electronic communication at Ce active centers. The interface formed between CD and Ce MOF facilitates intercomponent electron exchange and modifies the local electronic state and valence distribution of Ce sites. The contribution of CD therefore extends beyond its surface functionalities and intrinsic radical-scavenging capacity to the regulation of adjacent Ce-centered redox processes. These interfacial electronic interactions provide a mechanistic explanation for the distinct catalytic preference of Ce MOF-CD compared with pristine Ce MOF.

Direct pH measurements confirmed that infected wound sites exhibited a weakly acidic microenvironment, providing a pathologically relevant condition for the pH-dependent catalytic behavior of Ce MOF-CD. Environmental pH may influence substrate availability, proton participation, and interfacial reaction processes, thereby affecting the relative predominance of different enzyme-like activities. Under weakly acidic conditions, Ce MOF-CD predominantly exhibits oxidase-like activity, whereas SOD-like activity becomes more prominent as the environment approaches neutrality. Therefore, the catalytic response of Ce MOF-CD is better described as a pH-dependent shift in catalytic preference rather than a simple enhancement or attenuation of overall catalytic activity.

This catalytic behavior is particularly relevant to the redox biology of infected wounds. Bacterial elimination requires sufficient oxidative pressure at the site of infection, whereas persistent or excessive ROS accumulation can damage host cells, prolong inflammatory responses, and impair tissue regeneration. Effective treatment requires maintaining a balance between antibacterial oxidation and protection against excessive oxidative injury. By favoring pro-oxidative catalysis in the weakly acidic infected environment and superoxide removal under near-neutral conditions, Ce MOF-CD enables the same Ce-based redox system to address two distinct therapeutic requirements. Such environment-dependent regulation of catalytic preference helps reconcile antibacterial efficacy with preservation of host tissue redox homeostasis, thereby supporting inflammation resolution and wound repair.

Many Ce-based nanozyme designs enhance individual enzyme-like activities by increasing active-site density, adjusting the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio, or incorporating additional catalytic components. Although these strategies can improve overall catalytic performance, the resulting functions are often optimized toward maximizing catalytic output rather than regulating catalytic behavior according to environmental changes. For Ce-based nanozymes possessing both pro-oxidative and antioxidative activities, the ability to dynamically regulate the relative contribution of different redox pathways is important for achieving appropriate biological responses. In contrast, the CD/Ce MOF interface enables the relative predominance of oxidase-like and SOD-like activities to vary with environmental pH. This study demonstrates that interfacial electronic regulation provides an alternative approach for controlling microenvironment-dependent redox functions beyond conventional strategies focused on increasing catalytic activity alone.

The conclusions of this study are primarily applicable to the present Ce MOF-CD system and the infected wound model examined here. Within Ce-based nanozyme systems, rational regulation of the CD/Ce MOF interface provides an effective route for modulating electron exchange and pH-dependent catalytic behavior without relying solely on increasing active-site density or incorporating additional catalytic modules. By controlling the electronic interaction between functional carbon components and Ce active centers, this interface-oriented approach offers a means to tune redox catalytic preference according to pathological microenvironments. Further exploration of the relationship between CD surface chemistry, interfacial coordination, and Ce electronic structure may provide additional insights into the rational design of adaptive Ce-based redox catalysts.

Conclusion

In this work, GSH-derived CD was incorporated during the formation of Ce MOF through an in situ integration strategy. The resulting interfacial electronic coupling promoted charge redistribution, altered the local electronic environment and Ce3+/Ce4+ distribution of the Ce active centers, and enhanced the redox catalytic behavior of Ce MOF. Ce MOF-CD exhibited a distinct pH-dependent catalytic preference, with OXD-like catalysis favored under acidic conditions and SOD-like activity becoming more pronounced near neutrality. This behavior enabled the same Ce-based catalytic system to support antibacterial oxidation under acidic conditions and oxidative-stress regulation under near-neutral conditions.

Ce MOF-CD effectively inhibited bacterial growth and disrupted established biofilms under acidic conditions, while reducing intracellular oxidative stress and inflammatory activation under near-neutral conditions. In an MRSA-infected wound model, Ce MOF-CD reduced bacterial burden, alleviated local inflammation, and promoted tissue reconstruction and wound closure with favorable biosafety. Overall, this study demonstrates that interfacial electronic regulation can modulate the pH-dependent redox catalytic behavior of Ce-based nanozymes, providing a strategy for coordinating infection control with host tissue protection during infected wound repair.
