Small-Amplitude Periodic Traveling Waves in Dimer Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou Lattices Without Symmetry
Timothy E. Faver, Hermen Jan Hupkes, J. Douglas Wright

Abstract
. We prove the existence of small-amplitude periodic traveling waves in dimer Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou (FPUT) lattices without assumptions of physical symmetry. Such lattices are infinite, one-dimensional chains of coupled particles in which the particle masses and/or the potentials of the coupling springs can alternate. Previously, periodic traveling waves were constructed in a variety of limiting regimes for the symmetric mass and spring dimers, in which only one kind of material data alternates. The new results discussed here remove the symmetry assumptions by exploiting the gradient structure and translation invariance of the traveling wave problem. Together, these features eliminate certain solvability conditions that symmetry would otherwise manage and facilitate a bifurcation argument involving a two-dimensional kernel.

Introduction

1.1.
The traveling wave problem

A dimer Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou (FPUT) lattice is a chain of infinitely many particles coupled to their nearest neighbors by springs, with motion restricted to the horizontal direction, and with at least one of the following material heterogeneities: either the particle masses alternate, or the spring potentials alternate, or both alternate.
A dimer with alternating particles and identical springs is called a mass dimer; one with alternating springs and identical masses is a spring dimer.

These lattices, and their many variants and generalizations, are prototypical models of wave dynamics in granular media

Specifically, we construct nontrivial periodic traveling waves for general dimers with wave speed close to a certain critical threshold called the lattice’s “speed of sound”—that is, periodic traveling waves in the long wave limit.
We state our precise results below in Theorem

and discuss the connection of these traveling waves to the long wave problem in dimers, and related problems, in Section

Here is our problem.
Index the particles by integers
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
\displaystyle u_{j}
be the displacement of the
\displaystyle j
th particle from its equilibrium position, let
\displaystyle m_{j}
be the mass of the
\displaystyle j
th particle, and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{j}
be the potential of the spring connecting the
\displaystyle j
th and
\displaystyle(j+1)
st particles.
To ensure a dimer structure, we assume

m_{j+2}=m_{j}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{V}_{j+2}=\mathcal{V}_{j}

for all
\displaystyle j
.
More precisely, after nondimensionalization
[
14
, Sec. 1.3, App. F.5]
, we take

(1.1)

m_{j}=\begin{cases}1,\ j\text{ is odd}\\
m,\ j\text{ is even}\end{cases}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\prime}(r)=\begin{cases}r+r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{3})\\
\kappa{r}+\beta{r}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{3}).\end{cases}

The minimum regularity required for our proofs is that
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{7}(\mathbb{R})
for precise technical reasons detailed in Appendix
A.5
, but for broader applications to FPUT traveling wave problems, we may as well assume
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
Our methods require that the heterogeneity appear at the linear level, so we will always assume

(1.2)

\frac{1}{m}>1\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\kappa>1.

We emphasize that
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle\kappa
, and indeed all of the material data, are fixed throughout our analysis and that virtually all operators, quantities, and thresholds depend on at least these quantities; we do not indicate such dependence in our notation.
Additionally, beyond the regularity requirements on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{j}
, the nonlinear terms, even the quadratic ones, play no important role.

Newton’s second law requires that the displacements
\displaystyle u_{j}
satisfy

(1.3)

m_{j}\ddot{u}_{j}=\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\prime}(u_{j+1}-u_{j})-\mathcal{V}_{j-1}^{\prime}(u_{j}-u_{j-1}).

Under the traveling wave ansatz

(1.4)

u_{j}(t)=\begin{cases}p_{1}(j-ct),\ j\text{ is odd}\\
p_{2}(j-ct),\ j\text{ is even},\end{cases}\qquad\mathbf{p}:=\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix},

these equations of motion become the advance-delay problem

(1.5)

\begin{cases}c^{2}p_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{1}p_{2}-p_{1})-\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(p_{1}-S^{-1}p_{2})\\
c^{2}mp_{2}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(S^{1}p_{1}-p_{2})-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(p_{2}-S^{-1}p_{1}).\end{cases}

Here, for
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle S^{\theta}
is the shift operator

(S^{\theta}p)(X):=p(X+\theta).

1.5
).
We use the notation for periodic Sobolev spaces developed in Appendix

.

Remark 1.2
.

The majority of traveling wave results (periodic or not) for lattices are stated in relative displacement coordinates:
\displaystyle r_{j}=u_{j+1}-u_{j}
.
See Figure
2
.
We find it more convenient to work in the original equilibrium displacement coordinates
\displaystyle u_{j}
, from which relative displacement results can easily be obtained (though the converse is not necessarily true).

We finally state the actual periodic traveling wave problem that we solve to prove Theorem
1.1
; the following notation was not strictly necessary above, but all of our subsequent work depends on it.
Since we are interested in periodic traveling waves, we adjust the original traveling wave ansatz (
1.4
) by decoupling the profile and frequency via the additional ansatz

\mathbf{p}(X)=\boldsymbol{\phi}(\omega{X}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\phi}:=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The new profiles
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
are now
\displaystyle 2\pi
-periodic and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
The traveling wave equations (
1.5
) then become

(1.6)

\begin{cases}c^{2}\omega^{2}\phi_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})-\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{2})\\
mc^{2}\omega^{2}\phi_{2}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1}).\end{cases}

We compress (
1.6
) in the form

(1.7)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0,

where

(1.8)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega):=c^{2}\omega^{2}M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{2})-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\\
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})-\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\end{pmatrix}

and

(1.9)

M:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&m\end{bmatrix}.

The primary challenge that we confront is that the linearization
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})
has a three-dimensional kernel and cokernel.
Translation invariance allows us to reduce both dimensions to two, but, in the absence of symmetry, we cannot get below that.
We now discuss the broader relevance of this periodic traveling wave problem and, in the process, why this dimension counting is so important.

1.2.
Motivation, context, and connections other FPUT traveling wave problems

Our primary motivation in constructing these particular periodics is the long wave problem for dimers.
This limit looks for traveling waves whose profiles are close to a suitably scaled
\displaystyle\sech^{2}
-type solution of a KdV equation that acts as the “continuum limit” for the lattice.
(More precisely, one posits
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(X)=\epsilon^{2}\mathbf{h}(\epsilon{X})
and
\displaystyle c^{2}=c_{\star}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}
, with
\displaystyle c_{\star}
given by (
2.8
).
We discuss this further in Section
6.2
.)
The motivation for this ansatz is that in a “polyatomic” FPUT lattice, for which the material data repeats with some arbitrary period, long wave-scaled solutions to certain KdV equations (whose coefficients depend on the lattice’s material data) are very good approximations to solutions to the equations of motion over very long time scales
[
36
,
26
,
8
]
.

Faver and Wright constructed long wave solutions for the mass dimer
[
20
]
and Faver treated the spring dimer
[
15
]
.
Faver and Hupkes produced a different development of mass and spring dimer nanopterons via spatial dynamics in
[
19
]
and obtained results for equilibrium displacement coordinates as we do; Deng and Sun
[
13
]
performed a related spatial dynamics analysis to yield similar results.
These dimer traveling waves were not solitary waves, as Friesecke and Pego found for the monatomic lattice
[
23
]
, but rather nanopterons
[
7
]
: the superposition of a leading-order localized (here,
\displaystyle\sech^{2}
-type) term, a higher-order localized remainder, and a high frequency periodic “ripple” of amplitude small beyond all algebraic orders of the long wave parameter.
Both constructions relied on lattice symmetries in two critical steps to adapt functional analytic techniques from Beale’s work on capillary gravity water waves
[
5
]
and its later deployment by Amick and Toland
[
3
]
for a model fourth-order KdV equation.

First, as mentioned above, the periodics in
[
20
,
15
]
were constructed with a modified “bifurcation from a simple eigenvalue” argument in the style of Crandall and Rabinowitz and Zeidler
[
11
,
39
]
.
We adapt further this bifurcation analysis in our arguments, and our preferred reference is
[
29
, Thm. 1.5.1]
.
Symmetry permitted the restriction of the traveling wave problem
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
from (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) to function spaces on which the linearization
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega)
at
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=0
and
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{c}
, with
\displaystyle\omega_{c}
as the “critical frequency” from Theorem
2.1
, had a one-dimensional kernel and cokernel.
This was the key to the modified bifurcation from a simple eigenvalue argument.

Up to a useful linear change of coordinates that diagonalized the traveling wave problem and the long wave scaling, the long wave periodics in
[
20
,
15
]
have the same structure as ours from Theorem
1.1
.
However, the main technical accomplishment of our results here is that we manage a
two
-dimensional kernel and cokernel in the absence of symmetry via other inherent properties of the lattice—namely, the special “orthogonality condition” that
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle_{L_{\per}^{2}}=0
, proved in Corollary
3.2
and Lemma
4.1
.
While there certainly exist other results on bifurcations with two-dimensional kernels, their hypotheses are inappropriate for our problem.
For example,
[
31
]
and
[
32
]
assume certain “nondegeneracy” conditions on what for us would be the second derivative
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})
, over which we expect to have no control (beyond its existence), while
[
1
]
and
[
4
]
assume some (non)resonance conditions among their critical frequencies.

The second use of symmetry in the full nanopteron constructions of
[
20
,
15
]
was somewhat subtler and involved the actual need for periodics in the first place.
The obstacle was that attempting to solve the traveling wave problem (
1.5
) by a merely localized perturbation from the
\displaystyle\sech^{2}
-type continuum limit resulted in an overdetermined system with two unknowns (the two components of the localized perturbation) but four equations.
These are the expected two components from (
1.5
) and a surprising “solvability condition”: the vanishing of the Fourier transform of a certain related operator at
\displaystyle\pm\omega_{c}
.
Symmetry ensured that the vanishing at
\displaystyle\omega_{c}
implied the vanishing at
\displaystyle-\omega_{c}
, reducing the overdetermined problem to only three equations, which were managed by adding a third variable via the periodic amplitude—which is exactly why we seek periodics in Theorem
1.1
that are parametrized in amplitude.
While the full nanopteron problem in the general dimer without symmetry remains challenging and beyond the scope of our work here to address, the periodic solutions constructed here will be a fundamental component of the nanopteron ansatz for the general dimer’s long wave problem.

Periodic traveling waves for lattices have been constructed in several other “material limit” regimes in addition to the long wave limit.
These include the small mass limit for mass dimers by Hoffman and Wright
[
27
, Thm. 5.1]
, the equal mass limit for mass dimers by Faver and Hupkes
[
18
, Prop. 3.3]
, and the small mass limit for the mass-in-mass variant of monatomic FPUT by Faver
[
16
, Thm. 2]
.
Each of these limits views the heterogeneous lattice as a small material perturbation of a monatomic FPUT lattice, and the nanopteron is a small nonlocal perturbation of a monatomic solitary wave
[
23
,
25
]
.
While each limit has a nontrivially different solvability condition that makes the traveling wave problem overdetermined, all of the periodic constructions are fundamentally alike and can be deduced from our Theorem
6.2
.

These are not the only methods for producing periodic traveling waves for FPUT, and we give a brief, selected overview of others here.
Friesecke and Mikikits-Leitner
[
22
]
adapted the perturbative approach for monatomic solitary waves from
[
23
]
to prove the existence of long wave periodics in the monatomic lattice that were small perturbations of a KdV cnoidal profile.
Pankov constructed periodics in the monatomic lattice using variational methods
[
34
]
, as did Qin for mass dimers
[
35
]
with spring force given by the FPUT
\displaystyle\beta
-model, i.e., roughly of the form
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(r)=r+\mathcal{O}(r^{3})
.
Betti and Pelinovsky used an implicit function theorem argument to produce periodics in mass dimers with Hertzian spring potentials
[
6
]
, and we note with interest that their proofs also relied on symmetry to reduce the dimension of a key linearization’s kernel.
Finally, we mention that Lombardi’s spatial dynamics method for nanopterons under very general hypotheses includes the full development of periodics from that point of view
[
33
]
, with the more stringent requirement that the spring potentials be real analytic.

2.
Linear Analysis

We assume familiarity here with the notation and conventions of Appendix
A.2
on periodic Sobolev spaces and Fourier coefficients.
Briefly,
\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)
is the
\displaystyle k
th Fourier coefficient of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the
\displaystyle L_{\per}^{2}
-inner product (we no longer keep the subscript here).

Our bifurcation analysis naturally hinges on a careful understanding of the linearization

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]:=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega)

of the problem
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
at
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=0
, where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
was defined in (
1.8
).
Using that definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
and recalling from the hypotheses (
1.1
) that the spring potentials satisfy

\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(r)=r+\mathcal{O}(r^{2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(r)=\kappa{r}+\mathcal{O}(r^{2}),

we have

\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]\boldsymbol{\phi}=c^{2}\omega^{2}M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{D}[\omega]\boldsymbol{\phi},

where

(2.2)

\mathcal{D}[\omega]:=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\kappa)&-(S^{\omega}+\kappa{S}^{-\omega})\\
-(\kappa{S}^{\omega}+S^{-\omega})&(1+\kappa).\end{bmatrix}.

We follow the strategy of the existing bifurcation arguments
[
20
, App. C]
,
[
15
, Sec. 3]
,
[
27
, Sec. 5]
,
[
17
, Sec. 3]
,
[
16
, Sec. 3]
and begin by considering the kernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]
.
We have
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]\boldsymbol{\phi}=0
if and only if

(2.3)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}(\omega{k})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)=0

for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}
, where

(2.4)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}(K):=-c^{2}K^{2}M+\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(K)

and

(2.5)

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(K):=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\kappa)&-(e^{iK}+\kappa{e}^{-iK})\\
-(\kappa{e}^{iK}+e^{-iK})&(1+\kappa)\end{bmatrix}.

Then (
2.3
) is equivalent to

c^{2}(\omega{k})^{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)=M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\omega{k})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k),

and so, if
\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)\neq 0
, then
\displaystyle c^{2}(\omega{k})^{2}
must be an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\omega{k})
.
Any eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda
of
\displaystyle M^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(K)
must satisfy the characteristic equation

\lambda^{2}-(1+w)(1+\kappa)\lambda+2\kappa{w}(2\cos^{2}(K)-1)=0,

and so the eigenvalues are

(2.6)

\widetilde{\lambda}_{\pm}(K):=\frac{(1+\kappa)(1+w)}{2}\pm\frac{\widetilde{\varrho}(K)}{2},

where

(2.7)

\widetilde{\varrho}(K):=\sqrt{(1+w)^{2}(1-\kappa)^{2}+4\kappa((1-w)^{2}+4w\cos^{2}(K))}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad w:=\frac{1}{m}.

The following is proved in
[
14
, Prop. 2.2.1]
about these eigenvalues.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that at least one of the inequalities
\displaystyle\kappa>1
or
\displaystyle w>1
holds and define the “speed of sound” to be

(2.8)

c_{\star}:=\sqrt{\frac{4\kappa{w}}{(1+\kappa)(1+w)}}.

(i)

If
\displaystyle|c|>c_{\star}
, then
\displaystyle c^{2}K^{2}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{-}(K)
if and only if
\displaystyle K=0
.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle|c|>c_{\star}
, then there exists
\displaystyle\omega_{c}>0
such that
\displaystyle c^{2}K^{2}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{+}(K)
if and only if
\displaystyle K=\pm\omega_{c}
.

(iii)

This “critical frequency”
\displaystyle\omega_{c}
satisfies the estimates

(2.9)

\frac{\sqrt{\widetilde{\lambda}_{+}(\pi/2)}}{c}\leq\omega_{c}\leq\frac{\sqrt{(1+\kappa)(1+w)}}{c}.

and

(2.10)

\inf_{|c|>c_{\star}}2c^{2}\omega_{c}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{+}^{\prime}(\omega_{c})>0.

We will ultimately run several bifurcation arguments from the critical frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{c}
.
To do that, we need a good understanding of the kernel and cokernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]
.
We carefully compute the following in Appendix
B.1
.

Corollary 2.2
.

The kernels of both

\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\colon H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*}\colon L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

are spanned by the orthonormal vectors
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
defined by

(2.11)

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(2.12)

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}(x):=\frac{e^{-ix}}{\Nu_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\omega_{c}}+\kappa{e}^{i\omega_{c}}\\
1+\kappa-c^{2}\omega_{c}^{2}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{e^{ix}}{\Nu_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\omega_{c}}+\kappa{e}^{-i\omega_{c}}\\
1+\kappa-c^{2}\omega_{c}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

and

(2.13)

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}(x):=\frac{e^{-ix}}{\Nu_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}i(e^{-i\omega_{c}}+\kappa{e}^{i\omega_{c}})\\
i(1+\kappa-c^{2}\omega_{c}^{2})\end{pmatrix}+\frac{e^{ix}}{\Nu_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}-i(e^{i\omega_{c}}+\kappa{e}^{-i\omega_{c}})\\
-i(1+\kappa-c^{2}\omega_{c}^{2})\end{pmatrix},

where

(2.14)

\Nu_{c}:=\sqrt{2}\left(\big[(1-\kappa)^{2}+4\kappa\cos(\omega_{c})\big]^{2}+\left[\frac{(1+\kappa)(1-w)+\widetilde{\varrho}(\omega_{c})}{2}\right]^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The eigenfunctions
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
satisfy the derivative identities

(2.15)

\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}

and the shift identity

(2.16)

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=S^{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}.

A function
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies

(2.17)

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0\qquad\text{ if and only if }\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)=0.

Thus the kernel and the cokernel of the linearization of the traveling wave problem (
1.7
) are ostensibly three-dimensional.
Translation invariance (Corollary
3.3
) will allow us to rule out
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
, and so we are down to two dimensions.

We also need to understand the interaction of the mixed partial derivative
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]:=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})
with the eigenfunction
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}
.
It follows from Appendix
A.4
, specifically the identity (
A.6
), that
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]
is the Fourier multiplier given by

(2.18)

\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)=k\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}^{\prime}(\omega_{c}k)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)

with
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}^{\prime}
as the componentwise derivative of the matrix
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}
from (
2.4
).
We prove the following estimate in Appendix
B.2
.
This is the direct analogue of the classical Crandall–Rabinowitz–Zeidler transversality condition
[
29
, Eqn. (I.5.3)]
for our approach.

Corollary 2.3
.

\displaystyle\inf_{|c|>c_{\star}}|\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle|>0
.

Last, we will need the following coercive estimate on
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]
, proved in Appendix
B.3
.

Corollary 2.4
.

There is
\displaystyle C>0
such that the following holds for all
\displaystyle c
with
\displaystyle|c|>c_{\star}
and all
\displaystyle r\geq 0
.
If
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\eta}
for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in H_{\per}^{r+2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0,

then

\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{H_{\per}^{r+2}}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{H_{\per}^{r}}.

3.
The Gradient Formulation

3.1.
The gradient structure of the traveling wave problem

We rewrite the traveling wave operator
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
from (
1.8
) as the
\displaystyle L_{\per}^{2}
-gradient of a certain “kinetic + potential energy” functional on
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
This formulation yields transparent proofs of certain properties of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
from shift invariance, and from these properties follow our first existence proof in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
.

First, we need some new notation; all of the consequences below of this notation are straightforward calculations, which we omit.
For
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, put

(3.1)

\Delta_{+}(\omega):=\begin{bmatrix}[r]-1&S^{\omega}\\
1&-S^{-\omega}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Delta_{-}(\omega)=\begin{bmatrix}[r]1&-1\\
-S^{-\omega}&S^{\omega}\end{bmatrix}.

We then have the adjoint relationship

(3.2)

\langle\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\Delta_{-}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle

for any
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Next, let

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{p}):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{V}_{1}(p_{1})\\
\mathcal{V}_{2}(p_{2})\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\mathbf{p}):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(p_{1})\\
\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(p_{2})\end{pmatrix},

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2}
are the spring potentials from (
1.1
), and
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
For
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, define componentwise multiplication as

\mathbf{v}.\mathbf{w}:=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}v_{1}\\
v_{2}v_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

We then have the derivative formula

(3.3)

D_{\mathbf{p}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{p})\grave{\mathbf{p}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\mathbf{p}).\grave{\mathbf{p}}

for any
\displaystyle\grave{\mathbf{p}}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Define
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}(x):=(1,1)
; then since
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
have real entries, the useful identity

(3.4)

\langle\mathbf{v}.\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle

is true.

Last, we have

\Delta_{-}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})-\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{2})\\
\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})\end{pmatrix}.

Comparing this to the second term in
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
from (
1.8
), we conclude

(3.5)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=c^{2}\omega^{2}M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime}-\Delta_{-}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}).

This version of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
allows us to recognize it as a gradient; similar calculations for the monatomic lattice appear in
[
34
, Prop. 3.2]
and for mass dimers with the FPUT
\displaystyle\beta
-model in
[
35
, Lem. 3.1]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Define

(3.6)

\mathcal{T}\colon H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\colon(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\mapsto\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\langle M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle

and, with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}(x):=(1,1)
,

(3.7)

\mathcal{P}\colon H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\colon(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\mapsto\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle

Put

(3.8)

\mathcal{G}_{c}:=c^{2}\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{P}.

Then

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\nabla\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)

in the sense that

(3.9)

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The proof is just a careful calculation using the definition of the derivative and the inner product
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the various identities stated above.
More precisely, we compute the following.

First, for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\omega
,
\displaystyle h\in\mathbb{R}
, we use the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
in (
3.6
) to compute

\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+h\boldsymbol{\eta},\omega)-\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=h\langle\omega^{2}M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle+h^{2}\frac{\omega^{2}\langle M\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle}{2}.

This uses two applications of the integration by parts identity (
A.3
) to compute
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime\prime}\rangle
, the symmetry of
\displaystyle M
, and the assumption that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}
are
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued.
It follows that

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}=\langle\omega^{2}M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle.

Next, we use the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
in (
3.7
) to compute

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi})\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle\text{ by the chain rule}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}).\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle\text{ by \eqref{eqn: Vb derivative}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi},\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle\text{ by \eqref{eqn: dot mult oneb}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\langle\Delta_{-}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle\text{ by \eqref{eqn: Delta pm adjoint}}.

All together, we have

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}+D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}=\langle c^{2}\omega^{2}M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle-\langle\Delta_{-}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle.

By our rewritten formula for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
in (
3.5
), this proves (
3.9
).
∎

We collect two families of properties of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
.
The proofs for
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}
are easy consequences of its definition in Theorem
3.1
, while those for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
are also straightforward and could in fact be done (somewhat more laboriously) using just the definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
in (
1.8
).
However, we present proofs for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
here as a consequence of the gradient formulation to emphasize the utility and efficiency of this formulation.

Corollary 3.2

(Shift invariance)
.

The following hold for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.

(i)

The functional
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}
is shift-invariant:

(3.10)

\mathcal{G}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)

for all
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

The operator
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
is also shift-invariant:

(3.11)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)

for all
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

The operator
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
has the “derivative orthogonality property”

(3.12)

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle=0.

Proof.

(i)

We use the identities

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}=0

to obtain
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega)=\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega)=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
, respectively.
Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}=c^{2}\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{P}
, the identity (
3.13
) follows.

(ii)

The chain rule and the identity (
3.10
) imply

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\eta}

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
At the level of gradients, this reads

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle.

On the right, we use the adjoint relation (
A.2
) for shifts to rewrite

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle.

It follows that

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and so
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
.
Applying
\displaystyle S^{\theta}
to both sides yields (
3.11
).

(iii)

Now we differentiate the identity
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
from (
3.10
) with respect to
\displaystyle\theta
and evaluate the result at
\displaystyle\theta=0
.
This yields

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(S^{0}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}[S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}]\bigg|_{\theta=0}\right)=0.

Differentiating the shift operator yields the identity

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}[S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}]\bigg|_{\theta=0}=\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},

which is valid in the
\displaystyle L_{\per}^{2}
-norm since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}
.

Thus

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}=0,

and in the language of the gradient formulation, this says

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle=0.\qed

Corollary 3.3

(Translation invariance)
.

The following hold for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.

(i)

The functional
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}
is translation-invariant in the sense that

(3.13)

\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)

for all
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
is defined in (
2.11
).

(ii)

The operator
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
is also translation-invariant:

(3.14)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)

for all
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

The range of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
is orthogonal to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
:

(3.15)

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=0.

Proof.

(i)

This follows from the integral structure of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}=c^{2}\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{P}
from Theorem
3.1
, the
\displaystyle 2\pi
-periodicity of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
, and the identity

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x+\theta)\ dx=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\ dx,

which is valid for all
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and all integrable,
\displaystyle 2\pi
-periodic
\displaystyle f\colon[-\pi,\pi]\to\mathbb{C}
.

(ii)

We differentiate the identity
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
from (
3.13
) with respect to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
to find

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and thus

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle.

Since this holds for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}
, we obtain (
3.14
).

(iii)

Now we differentiate the identity
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
with respect to
\displaystyle\alpha
and evaluate the result at
\displaystyle\alpha=0
.
This yields

0=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}+(0\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}),\omega)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}[\boldsymbol{\phi}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}]\bigg|_{\alpha=0}\right)=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle.\qed

Remark 3.4
.

The derivative orthogonality property (
3.12
) of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
is the key to resolving the overdetermined periodic problem.
While this property follows quickly from the shift invariance of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}
, as proved above, it is not quite as easy to prove directly from the definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
as are all the other consequences of shift and translation invariance.
We discuss that direction of proof further in Lemma
4.1
.
A similar derivative orthogonality property, deployed in somewhat different language, enabled Wright and Scheel
[
38
, Sec. 4, p. 548]
to complete a Lyapunov–Schmidt analysis in which the linearization also had a two-dimensional kernel that, in the absence of symmetry, could not be reduced in dimension.

3.2.
Function spaces and projection operators

The translation invariance identities (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) mean that we can effectively ignore the contributions of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
to the problem
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
.
So, we put

(3.16)

\mathcal{Y}:=\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ \middle|\ \langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=0\right\},

(3.17)

\mathcal{X}:=H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathcal{Y},

and

(3.18)

\mathcal{Z}_{c}:=\spn(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}).

It follows that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\in\mathcal{Y}
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, and also

\mathcal{Z}_{c}=\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}])\cap\mathcal{X}=\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*})\cap\mathcal{Y}.

Define

(3.19)

\Pi_{c}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}_{c}\colon\boldsymbol{\phi}\mapsto\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}.

Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
are orthogonal, per Corollary
2.2
, the operator
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}
is the orthogonal projection of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
(and
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
) onto
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{c}
.
In particular,

(3.20)

\langle\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

It turns out to be quite useful for us that the projection
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}
and the first derivative
\displaystyle\partial_{x}
commute.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Then

(3.21)

\Pi_{c}\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\partial_{x}\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\phi}.

Proof.

We use the integration by parts identity
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle
from (
A.3
) and the derivative identities (
2.15
) to compute

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\phi}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}-\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}-\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}+\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\partial_{x}\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\phi}.\qed

Last, we state precisely the regularity of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
and some of its derivatives on periodic Sobolev spaces.
The technical challenge here is that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
is infinitely differentiable from
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to
\displaystyle L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with respect to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
, but any order derivative with respect to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
is only once continuously differentiable with respect to
\displaystyle\omega
.
This is ultimately a consequence of the limited differentiability of shift operators between periodic Sobolev spaces, as we discuss in Appendix
A.4
.
We prove the next lemma in Appendix
B.4
.

Lemma 3.6
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times\mathbb{R},L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
and
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times\mathbb{R},L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
.

3.3.
The Lyapunov–Schmidt decomposition: infinite-dimensional analysis

The approach here is classical and follows, for example, the proof of the Crandall–Rabinowitz–Zeidler theorem in
[
29
, Thm. 1.5.1]
.
The difference appears in the following section, when we manage the two-dimensional kernel.

We use the projection operator
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}
from (
3.19
) to make a Lyapunov–Schmidt decomposition for our problem
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
.
First, with the spaces
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
defined in (
3.17
) and (
3.16
), let

(3.22)

\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}:=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\Pi_{c})(\mathcal{X})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}:=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})(\mathcal{Y}),

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}
are the identity operators on
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
, respectively.
Consequently,

(3.23)

\mathcal{Z}_{c}\cap\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}=\mathcal{Z}_{c}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}=\{0\}.

Next, write
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi}
, where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{Z}_{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
.
Then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
if and only if

(3.24)

\begin{cases}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=0\\
\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=0.\end{cases}

We solve the first equation in (
3.24
) quickly with a direct application of the implicit function theorem
[
29
, Thm. I.1.1]
.

Define

(3.25)

\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}\colon\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}\times\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}\colon(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)\mapsto(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega).

Certainly
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}(0,0,\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
, and we have

D_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}(0,0,\omega_{c})=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c}){\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}}}.

This operator has trivial kernel in
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
and trivial cokernel in
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}
by (
3.23
), and so it is invertible.
(More precisely, the closure of its range is the orthogonal complement of its cokernel, which is all of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}
.
But the range is closed by the coercive estimate in Corollary
2.4
.)
Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
by Lemma
3.6
, with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
, the implicit function theorem yields
\displaystyle\delta_{c}
,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{c}>0
and a map
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\big(\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}}((0,\omega_{c});\delta_{c}),\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}}(0;\epsilon_{c})\big)

such that

(3.26)

\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)=0.

Moreover, if
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)=0
for some
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}}(0;\epsilon_{c})
and
\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}}((0,\omega_{c});\delta_{c})
, then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)
.
(Recall that
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0};r)=\!\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\ \middle|\ \|x-x_{0}\|_{\mathcal{X}}<r\right\}
for
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle r>0
.)
We pause to collect some useful properties of this map
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{Z}_{c}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\nu}\|_{H_{\per}^{2}}+|\omega-\omega_{c}|<\delta_{c}
.
Then the following identities hold.

(i)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}\rangle=0
for
\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}}((0,\omega_{c});\delta_{c})
.

(ii)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega)=0
for
\displaystyle(0,\omega)\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}}((0,\omega_{c});\delta_{c})
.

(iii)

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})=0
.

(iv)

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}}((0,\omega_{c});\delta_{c}),\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{Z}_{c},\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}))
.

Proof.

(i)

The derivative orthogonality property of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
from (
3.12
) implies

(3.27)

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\partial_{x}[\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)]\rangle=0,

and by (
3.26
), we have

(3.28)

(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega)=0.

Then we compute

(3.29)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\partial_{x}[\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)]\rangle\text{ using \eqref{eqn: Psib ortho 2} in \eqref{eqn: Psib ortho 1}}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\Pi_{c}\partial_{x}[\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)]\rangle\text{ by \eqref{eqn: Pi adjoint}}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\partial_{x}\Pi_{c}[\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)]\rangle\text{ since }\partial_{x}\text{ and }\Pi_{c}\text{ commute}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}\rangle\text{ since }\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}\text{ and }\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)=0.

(ii)

By definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}
in (
3.25
), we have

\mathcal{F}_{c}(0,0,\omega)=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega)=0

for all
\displaystyle\omega
.
By the uniqueness property of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}
, we have
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}(0,\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
.

(iii)

We differentiate (
3.28
) with respect to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{Z}_{c}
to find the operator-valued identity

(3.30)

(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega)\big(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}}+D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)\big)=0.

Here
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}}
is the identity operator on
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{c}=\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}])
.
Taking
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}=0
and
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{c}
collapses (
3.30
) to

(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\big(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}}+D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})\big)=0,

recalling
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})
from (
2.1
).
Since
\displaystyle{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}}}=0
, this further reduces to

\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})-\Pi_{c}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})=0.

Because
\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}])=\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*})
, it follows from the definition of
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}
in (
3.19
) that
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]=0
.
Thus

\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})=0,

and so the range of
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})
is contained in
\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}])=\mathcal{Z}_{c}
.
But the range of
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})
is also contained in
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{c}=\{0\}
by (
3.23
).
Thus the range of
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})
is trivial.

(iv)

This follows from the implicit function theorem, which guarantees that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}
is as regular as
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
.
Since
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
by Lemma
3.6
,
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}
inherits this regularity on
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}}((0,\omega_{c});\delta_{c})
.
∎

3.4.
The Lyapunov–Schmidt decomposition: finite-dimensional analysis

Now we solve the second equation in the Lyapunov–Schmidt decomposition (
3.33a
) with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)
.
This amounts to solving the pair of equations

(3.31)

\begin{cases}\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=0\\
\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega),\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0,\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}
is a linear combination of the two linearly independent eigenfunctions
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
.
The apparent quandary is that we want solutions parametrized in amplitude, so formally this suggests
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega)=\mathcal{O}(a)
.
This leads to our taking
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
below, which may appear to remove a degree of freedom from the ansatz.
In turn, this could appear to be problematic, given that we have two equations to solve above in (
3.31
).
Ostensibly we could have stayed with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}
as a combination of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
.
None of this, however, is a problem, and we discuss at length in Section
3.5
why.

With the choice of

(3.32)

\boldsymbol{\nu}=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}

for
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}
sufficiently small, we need

(3.33a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega),\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=0

(3.33b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega),\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0.

We claim that (
3.33b
) always holds.
Indeed, for
\displaystyle a=0
, it is trivially true, since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega)=0
, while for
\displaystyle a\neq 0
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega),\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=a^{-1}\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega),\omega),a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-a^{-1}\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega),\omega),\partial_{x}[a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}]\rangle\text{ by \eqref{eqn: nub derivatives}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0\text{ by part \ref{part: Psib1} of Lemma \ref{lem: Psib}}.

We emphasize that our success here traces back to the derivative orthogonality property (
3.12
).

We conclude by solving (
3.33a
) with another application of the implicit function theorem, and this, again, is effectively the remainder of the proof of the Crandall–Rabinowitz–Zeidler theorem
[
29
, Thm. 1.5.1]
.
Define

\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}\colon\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}((\omega_{c},0);\delta_{c}/2)\to\mathbb{R}\colon(\omega,a)\mapsto\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega),\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle.

The threshold
\displaystyle\delta_{c}
arose from the infinite-dimensional implicit function theorem argument in Section
3.3
.

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,0)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,a)=a\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,a),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,a):=\int_{0}^{1}D_{a}\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,a\alpha)\ d\alpha.

It therefore suffices to solve
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,a)=0
by selecting
\displaystyle\omega
as a function of
\displaystyle a
, and we do this by checking
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0)=0
and
\displaystyle D_{\omega}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0)\neq 0
.

Toward this end, we first differentiate

D_{a}\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,a)=\langle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega))\big(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\big),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle.

We put
\displaystyle a=0
and use
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega)=0
to find for any
\displaystyle\omega
that

(3.34)

\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,0)=\int_{0}^{1}D_{a}\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,0)\ d\alpha=D_{a}\mathcal{F}_{c}(\omega,0)=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]\big(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\big),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle.

In the special case of
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{c}
, we can use either
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega)=0
from part
(iii)
of Lemma
3.7
or the condition
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=0
to reduce (
3.34
) to

\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0)=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=0.

Next, with
\displaystyle\omega
arbitrary, we differentiate (
3.34
) with respect to
\displaystyle\omega
and use the product rule to find

D_{\omega}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,0)=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle+\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega]D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle+\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle.

Here we are using the shorter notation from (
2.18
) of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega]=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega)
.
Taking
\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{c}
, we use
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})=0
to find that the second term is
\displaystyle 0
.
At
\displaystyle\omega_{c}
, the third term is
\displaystyle 0
since
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=0
.
And so

D_{\omega}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0)=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\neq 0,

by Corollary
2.3
.

We are now in position to invoke the implicit function theorem once more, and we find
\displaystyle a_{c}
,
\displaystyle b_{c}>0
and a map
\displaystyle\Omega_{c}\colon(-a_{c},a_{c})\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{H}_{c}(\Omega_{c}(a),a)=0

for
\displaystyle|a|<a_{c}
, while if
\displaystyle|\omega-\omega_{c}|<b_{c}
and
\displaystyle|a|<a_{c}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,a)=0
, then
\displaystyle\omega=\Omega_{c}(a)
.
In particular,
\displaystyle\Omega_{c}(0)=\omega_{c}
.

In short, taking

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a}:=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1},\Omega_{c}(a))\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\omega_{c}^{a}:=\Omega_{c}(a)

solves our original problem
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a},\omega_{c}^{a})=0
.
We can expose uniformly the “amplitude” parameter of
\displaystyle a
in
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a}
by setting

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(a):=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1},\Omega_{c}(a)),

and computing

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(0)=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c})=0,\quad\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(a)=a\int_{0}^{1}D_{a}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(a\alpha)\ d\alpha,\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}=a\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}+\int_{0}^{1}D_{a}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(a\alpha)\ d\alpha\right).

Likewise, we can write

\omega_{c}^{a}=\omega_{c}+a\xi_{c}^{a},\qquad\xi_{c}^{a}:=\int_{0}^{1}D_{a}\Omega_{c}(a\alpha)\ d\alpha.

This concludes our first proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3.5.
Remarks on the choice (
3.32
)

We discuss our decision at the start of Section
3.4
to specialize the finite-dimensional component
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}
to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
.
We consider two aspects of this choice to allay any concerns about its peculiarity or restrictiveness.

First, up to a shift, any solution
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
has this form
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi}
, with
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}
orthogonal to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}
solves
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
, then there exist
\displaystyle a
,
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
such that

(3.35)

\boldsymbol{\phi}=S^{\theta}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi}).

In particular,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=0
, as well.

Proof.

We prove the last sentence first.
If a solution
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
has the form (
3.35
), then the shift invariance of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
from (
3.11
) implies

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(S^{\theta}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi}),\omega)=S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0.

It remains to prove the decomposition (
3.35
).
We can always write

(3.36)

\boldsymbol{\phi}=a_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+a_{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}

for some
\displaystyle a_{1}
,
\displaystyle a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
.
Write

a_{1}-ia_{2}=ae^{i\theta}

in polar coordinates, where
\displaystyle a
,
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
(If
\displaystyle a_{1}=a_{2}=0
, just take
\displaystyle a=0
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
.)
The identities
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}(x)=2\re[e^{ix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)]
from (
2.12
) and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=S^{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
from (
2.16
) then imply

a_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}(x)+a_{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}(x)=a_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}(x)+a_{2}S^{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}(x)=2\re[(a_{1}-ia_{2})e^{ix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)]=a(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c})(x).

Returning to (
3.36
), we have

\boldsymbol{\phi}=S^{\theta}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\psi}:=S^{-\theta}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}.

We conclude by checking that if
\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
, then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=S^{-\theta}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
.
That is, we assume

(3.37)

\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0,

and we want to show

(3.38)

\langle S^{-\theta}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle S^{-\theta}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0.

By the orthogonality condition (
2.17
), our assumption (
3.37
) is equivalent to

(3.39)

\widehat{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)=0,

and our desired conclusion (
3.38
) is equivalent to

(3.40)

(e^{-i\theta}\widehat{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}(1))\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)=0.

Certainly (
3.39
) implies (
3.40
).
∎

Consequently, trying an ansatz of the form
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+b\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi}
in the hope that
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
would be enough to manage the two equations in (
3.31
) will not be effective; informally, the problem simply does not “see” the two unknowns
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
simultaneously.
And such an ansatz would not expose the single uniform amplitude parameter that we desire, anyway.

There is nothing special about
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
here, and we could just as easily show that any solution to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
is a shifted version of a solution of the form
\displaystyle a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi}
.
In fact, we could have run the bifurcation argument above using
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
throughout in place of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
.
This hinges on expressing the transversality inequality of Corollary
2.3
in terms of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
, which is possible because of the calculation

(3.41)

\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=-\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\partial_{x}^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle.

The second equality relies on the commutativity of the Fourier multipliers
\displaystyle\partial_{x}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]
on
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
However, since we can write any solution in the form

S^{\theta}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi})=S^{\theta}(aS^{\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi})=S^{\theta+\pi/2}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}+S^{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\psi})

with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}
, and thus (by the end of the proof of Lemma
3.8
)
\displaystyle S^{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\psi}
, orthogonal to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
, there is not much point to this line of inquiry.

Next, we consider further the special form of solutions to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
as given in Theorem
1.1
: they are
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=a(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi})
with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}
again orthogonal to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
.
This may appear to be less general than the result of Lemma
3.8
, which says that, up to a shift, any solution has the form
\displaystyle a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
, with
\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
satisfying the perennial orthogonality conditions.
Of course, if
\displaystyle a\neq 0
, then this solution factors as
\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+a^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}})
, and that has the form given by Theorem
1.1
.
It turns out that all
small
nontrivial solutions to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
have this special factored form (again, up to a shift from Lemma
3.8
).

We prove a negative version of this result, which says that if the shifted solution from Lemma
3.8
has the form
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
alone, i.e., if
\displaystyle a=0
, and if this solution is sufficiently small, then it is trivial.

Lemma 3.9
.

There exists
\displaystyle\delta_{c}^{\infty}>0
such that if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{H_{\per}^{2}}+|\omega-\omega_{c}|<\delta_{c}^{\infty}
, and if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=0
, then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=0
.

Proof.

Define

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{c}^{\infty}\colon\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}\colon(\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)\mapsto(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega).

The notation and structure of this map are intentionally similar to those of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}
in (
3.25
), and the spaces
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}
are defined in (
3.22
).
Then
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{c}^{\infty}(0,\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
and

D_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{c}^{\infty}(0,\omega_{c})=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c}){\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}}}

As with the analogous linearization in Section
3.3
, this operator has trivial kernel and cokernel and therefore is invertible.
The implicit function theorem gives
\displaystyle\delta_{c}^{\infty}
,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{c}^{\infty}>0
and a map

\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}^{\infty}\colon(\omega_{c}-\delta_{c}^{\infty},\omega_{c}+\delta_{c}^{\infty})\to\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}

such that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{c}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}^{\infty}(\omega),\omega)=0
for
\displaystyle|\omega-\omega_{c}|<\delta_{c}^{\infty}
.
Moreover, if
\displaystyle|\omega-\omega_{c}|<\delta_{c}^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{H_{\per}^{2}}<\epsilon_{c}^{\infty}
, and if
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{c}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=0
, then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}^{\infty}(\omega)
.
But
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{c}^{\infty}(0,\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
, and so we must have
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}^{\infty}(\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
.
Conversely, if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=0
then
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{c}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)=0
, too, and so if
\displaystyle|\omega-\omega_{c}|<\delta_{c}^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{H_{\per}^{2}}<\epsilon_{c}^{\infty}
, then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}^{\infty}(\omega)=0
.
∎

Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
together effectively tell us that the only worthwhile form of solutions to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=a(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\psi})
.
When we study this problem quantitatively in Section
6.1
, we will start directly with an ansatz of this form.

4.
The Lyapunov Center Formulation

We solve the problem

(4.1)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)+\gamma\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}=0

for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\omega
,
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
The extra unknown
\displaystyle\gamma
closes the overdetermined system that results from the two solvability conditions induced by the two-dimensional cokernel; we show momentarily that any solution to (
4.1
) necessarily has
\displaystyle\gamma=0
, and so solving (
4.1
) really returns solutions to our original problem
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
.
This strategy is based on the work of Wright and Scheel in
[
38
, Sec. 8]
; in their words, “[t]he idea is to augment the Hamiltonian equation with a dissipation term, for instance
\displaystyle\gamma\nabla{H}
, so that for
\displaystyle\gamma\neq 0
, the system is gradient-like and does not possess any small non-equilibrium solutions.”
In turn, the proof there was motivated by a proof of the Lyapunov center theorem
[
2
, Thm. 3.2]
.

Some (though not all) of the implicit function theorem arguments are quite similar to those in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
, so we move rather more briskly here.
We emphasize that while the existence proof developed in this section is not strictly necessary for logical completeness of our argument, we see it as a potentially useful alternative to the first proof in that it is completely independent of the gradient structure.

First we show that any nonconstant solution to (
4.1
) has
\displaystyle\gamma=0
; the following calculation is similar to
[
2
, Lem. 3.1]
, which was done in preparation for their proof of the Lyapunov center theorem.
If (
4.1
) holds, then

(4.2)

0=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)+\gamma\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle+\gamma\|\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\|^{2}=\gamma\|\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\|^{2}.

Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
is nonconstant, we must have
\displaystyle\gamma=0
.

In (
4.2
) we used the derivative orthogonality property

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle=0,

as established in Corollary
3.2
using the gradient formulation.
However, with some more work, this can be checked directly from the definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and define

(4.3)

\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega):=c^{2}\omega^{2}\frac{(\phi_{1}^{\prime})^{2}}{2}+c^{2}\omega^{2}\frac{(\phi_{2}^{\prime})^{2}}{2w}+\mathcal{V}_{1}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})+\mathcal{V}_{2}(\phi_{1}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{2})+\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),

where

\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)(x):=\int_{x}^{x-\omega}\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}+\int_{x}^{x-\omega}\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\phi_{2}^{\prime}.

Then

(4.4)

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\partial_{x}\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega).

In particular, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
is
\displaystyle 2\pi
-periodic,

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\rangle=0.

Proof.

The proof of (
4.4
) is a direct calculation using the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}
above and the definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
in (
1.8
), but, for clarity, we provide some details as to how
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}
naturally arises.
First, computing the dot product yields

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}=c^{2}\omega^{2}\phi_{1}^{\prime\prime}\phi_{1}^{\prime}+\frac{c^{2}}{w}\omega^{2}\phi_{2}^{\prime\prime}\phi_{2}^{\prime}+\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})\phi_{2}^{\prime}-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}\\
+\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{2})\phi_{1}^{\prime}-\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\phi_{2}^{\prime}.

The first two terms are perfect derivatives, but the others involving
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}
need some modification.
We work with just the
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}
terms to show the origin of the first of the two integrals in
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
.
Adding zero, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})\phi_{2}^{\prime}-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})\phi_{2}^{\prime}-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})S^{-\omega}\phi_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})S^{-\omega}\phi_{1}^{\prime}-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})(\phi_{2}^{\prime}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle+(S^{-\omega}-1)[\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}].

Here we have factored

\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})S^{-\omega}\phi_{1}^{\prime}=S^{-\omega}[\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}].

to get the second term in the second equality above.
In the first term of that second equality, we immediately recognize the perfect derivative

\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})(\phi_{2}^{\prime}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1}^{\prime})=\partial_{x}[\mathcal{V}_{1}(\phi_{2}-S^{-\omega}\phi_{1})].

Finally, we use the identity

\partial_{x}\left[\int_{x}^{x-\omega}f\right]=f(x-\omega)-f(x)=[(S^{-\omega}-1)f](x)

to rewrite

(S^{-\omega}-1)[\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}]=\partial_{x}\left[\int_{x}^{x-\omega}\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}(S^{\omega}\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi_{1}^{\prime}\right].

Repeating these calculations on the
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\prime}
terms shows
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}=\partial_{x}\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
, and that is (
4.4
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The structure of the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}
in (
4.3
) bears some resemblance to the first integral in
[
19
, Prop. 3.10]
for the spatial dynamics formulation of the traveling wave problem.
Indeed, the existence of that conserved quantity from the spatial dynamics viewpoint inspired us to search for a related conserved quantity in this traveling wave framework, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}
naturally emerged.
Moreover,
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}
is constant on solutions to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
in the sense that if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\omega
satisfy this equation, it can be checked that
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
.
This leads to another (related) proof that the existence of a nonconstant solution to (
4.1
) forces
\displaystyle\gamma=0
: if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\omega
meet (
4.1
), it follows that

\partial_{x}\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=-\gamma\left((\phi_{1}^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{(\phi_{2}^{\prime})^{2}}{w}\right).

If
\displaystyle\gamma>0
, then
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
is nonpositive and not identically zero; since
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
is periodic, this is impossible.
A similar contradiction results if
\displaystyle\gamma<0
.

Now we study the problem (
4.1
) with a Lyapunov–Schmidt decomposition as in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
.
Using the projection operator
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}
and the function spaces
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{c}
from Section
3.2
, we split (
4.1
) into the pair of equations

\begin{cases}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)+\gamma(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\Pi_{c})(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime})=0\\
\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)+\gamma\Pi_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime})=0,\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{Z}_{c}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
.
We can simplify the terms involving
\displaystyle\gamma
:

\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}=\partial_{x}\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}=\partial_{x}\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\psi}=0,

since
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}
and
\displaystyle\partial_{x}
commute by Lemma
3.5
and since
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}
while
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\psi}=0
.
Then the decomposition reads

(4.5a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)+\gamma\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}=0

(4.5b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)+\gamma\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}=0,

and this is the problem that we will solve here.

First we address the infinite-dimensional equation (
4.5a
).
Using the same notation as in Section
3.3
, define

\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}\colon\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}\times\mathcal{Z}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}\colon(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega,\gamma)\mapsto(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\Pi_{c})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\omega)+\gamma\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}.

Since
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}=0
as computed above, and since

\langle\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle=0

by integration by parts and the identity
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}^{\prime}=0
from (
2.11
), we do indeed have
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}
for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}
.
That is,
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}
does indeed map into
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{\infty}
.
Next,
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}(0,0,\omega,\gamma)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
and
\displaystyle\gamma
, and
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}(0,\omega_{c},0,0)=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\Pi_{c}){\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}^{\infty}}}
is invertible.
Consequently, by the implicit function theorem, all suitably small solutions to
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega,\gamma)=0
have the form
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\omega,\gamma)
with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega,\gamma)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
and
\displaystyle\gamma
.
Before proceeding, we note that the same proof as for part
(iii)
of Lemma
3.7
(which did not rely on the gradient structure at all) yields

(4.6)

D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(0,\omega_{c},0)=0.

Now we specialize to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and solve the finite-dimensional equation (
4.5b
) by studying

\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,\gamma,a):=\Pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega,\gamma),\omega)-\gamma{a}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=0.

Here we have used
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
.
Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,\gamma,0)=0
, as in Section
3.4
we have the factorization

\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,\gamma,a)=a\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,\gamma,a),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,\gamma,a):=\int_{0}^{1}D_{a}\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,\gamma,a\alpha)\ d\alpha.

We solve
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,\gamma,a)=0
.

First, we compute

D_{a}\mathcal{F}_{c}^{0}(\omega,\gamma,a)=\Pi_{c}D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega,\gamma),\omega)\big(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\omega,\gamma)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\big)-\gamma\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}.

This, together with (
4.6
) and
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]=0
, implies
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0)=0
.
Next, differentiate with respect to
\displaystyle(\omega,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and write this derivative as a linear combination of partial derivatives:

(4.7)

D_{(\omega,\gamma)}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0)(\omega,\gamma)=\omega{D}_{\omega}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0)+\gamma{D}_{\gamma}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0).

We compute each of these partial derivatives separately.

For
\displaystyle D_{\omega}\mathcal{H}_{c}
, we calculate

\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,0,0)=\Pi_{c}D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(0,\omega)\big(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega,0)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\big),

and use the product rule and the identities
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]=0
and
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c},0)=0
to obtain

(4.8)

D_{\omega}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0)=\Pi_{c}\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}.

Above we used the following lemma to simplify the projection calculation.

Lemma 4.3
.

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0
.

We give two proofs of this lemma in Appendix
B.5
, one using the gradient formulation, and one using directly the definitions of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
.

Next, we work on
\displaystyle D_{\gamma}\mathcal{H}_{c}
.
Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega,\gamma)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
and
\displaystyle\gamma
, we have

\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},\gamma,0)=\Pi_{c}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\big(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+D_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(0,\omega_{c},\gamma)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\big)-\gamma\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2},

thanks to
\displaystyle\Pi_{c}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]=0
once again.
Thus

(4.9)

D_{\gamma}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0)=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}.

We combine (
4.7
), (
4.8
), and (
4.9
) to find

D_{(\omega,\gamma)}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0)(\omega,\gamma)=\omega\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}-\gamma\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}.

Since
\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle\neq 0
by Corollary
2.3
, and since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
form a basis for
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{c}
, we conclude that
\displaystyle D_{(\omega,\gamma)}\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega_{c},0,0)
is an invertible linear operator from
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{c}
.
By the implicit function theorem, for suitably small
\displaystyle\omega
,
\displaystyle\gamma
, and
\displaystyle a
, we can solve
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}(\omega,\gamma,a)=0
with
\displaystyle\omega=\Omega_{c}(a)
and
\displaystyle\gamma=\Gamma_{c}(a)
for some maps
\displaystyle\Omega_{c}
and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}
with
\displaystyle\Omega_{c}(0)=\omega_{c}
and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}(0)=0
.

It follows that taking

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a}:=a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\Omega_{c}(a),\Gamma_{c}(a))\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\omega_{c}^{a}:=\Omega_{c}(a)

solves
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a},\omega_{c}^{a})+\Gamma_{c}(a)\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}=0
.
Since
\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a}}(\pm 1)\neq 0
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a}
is nonconstant, and so by the calculation in (
4.2
) we really have
\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}(a)=0
for all
\displaystyle a
.
Additionally, if we put
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(a)=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{c}(a\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\Omega_{c}(a),0)
, then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(0)=0
, and so

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{c}^{a}=a\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\int_{0}^{1}D_{a}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{c}(a\alpha)\ d\alpha\right),

which is the representation that we want.
This concludes our second proof of Theorem
1.1
.

5.
Periodic Solutions with Symmetry

We first work out an abstract notion of symmetry and quickly show how bifurcation unfolds in its presence.
Then we prove that mass and spring dimers actually possess such symmetries.
The point of this analysis is that when the lattice has a symmetry, the periodic traveling wave solutions can be chosen to respect that symmetry.

5.1.
Symmetry operators and their properties

Definition 5.1
.

A bounded linear operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\colon L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a
symmetry
if the following hold.

(i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and any
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}
is defined in (
3.8
).

(ii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\phi}
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(iv)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}=-\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

We point out that while shift operators
\displaystyle S^{d}
do satisfy the invariance property
(i)
above, and while
\displaystyle S^{\pm\pi}
also satisfies
(ii)
, shifts in general do not meet
(iii)
and
(iv)
.
The symmetries that we construct will not rely on shift operators.

Here are some useful properties of symmetries for our problem.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
be a symmetry.

(i)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega]
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega]\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega]
for all
\displaystyle\omega
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=\mp\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
.

Proof.

(i)

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, we differentiate with respect to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
and use the chain rule (much as we did in the proof of part
(ii)
of Corollary
3.2
) to find

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\boldsymbol{\eta}=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{G}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\eta}

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Using the gradient formulation, this reads

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle,

where the second equality is the adjoint property of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
.
Since this is true for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
.

(ii)

This follows from part
(i)
and the chain rule.

(iii)

We use the relations
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
from Corollary
2.2
.
If
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
, then

\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=-\mathcal{S}\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\pm\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\mp\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}.

Conversely, if
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=\mp\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
, then

\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\mathcal{S}\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=-\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=-(\mp\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c})=\pm\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}.\qed

Now we adapt the nonconstant eigenfunctions
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
from Corollary
2.2
so that they respect symmetry.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
be a symmetry and define

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}:=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\ \mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\\
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c},\ \mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\\
(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c})/\|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\|_{L_{\per}^{2}},\ \mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\end{cases}

and

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}:=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c},\ \mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\\
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\ \mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\\
(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}-\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c})/\|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}-\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\|_{L_{\per}^{2}},\ \mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

(i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}
.

(ii)

The vectors
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}
form an orthonormal basis for
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{c}
as defined in (
3.18
).

(iii)

\displaystyle\inf_{|c|>c_{\star}}\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}\rangle>0
.

Proof.

We first remark that part
(iii)
of Lemma
5.2
ensures that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\pm}^{c}
is defined in the third case of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
: if
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
, then also
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
, and so both
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}-\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
are nonzero.

(i)

This is a direct calculation.

(ii)

This is obvious in the cases
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
.
In the third case, we use part
(ii)
of Lemma
5.2
to compute

\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=0

and likewise
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=0
.
This shows
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\pm}^{c}\in\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}])
.
Next,

\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=-\langle\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c},\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=0

and likewise
\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=0
.
This shows
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\pm}^{c}\in\mathcal{Z}_{c}
.

For orthogonality, we compute

(5.1)

\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}-\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle+\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle.

Now we use properties of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
to rewrite

\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle.

From this and (
5.1
), we obtain
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}+\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}-\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0
.
Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{c}
is already two-dimensional, it follows from orthogonality and linear independence that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}
are a basis.

(iii)

The first case that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
is Corollary
2.3
.
The second case that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
is equivalent to
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
by part
(iii)
of Lemma
5.2
, and then we can use the calculation in (
3.41
).
For the third case that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
, we start by taking
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{Z}_{c}=\spn(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c})
and then computing via the orthonormality of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
, (
3.41
), and Lemma
4.3
that

\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}\rangle=\|\boldsymbol{\nu}\|_{L_{\per}^{2}}^{2}\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle.

With
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}
, we have
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}\in\spn(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c})
and
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}\|_{L_{\per}^{2}}=1
, so

\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle,

from which the positive infimum follows.
∎

5.2.
Bifurcation in the presence of symmetry

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
be a symmetry and define

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}:=\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ \middle|\ \langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=0\text{ and }\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}:=H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}.

Part
(i)
of Lemma
5.2
shows that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}
for each
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
The effect of restricting
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}
to map from
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}\times\mathbb{R}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}
is that the restriction
\displaystyle{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}}
now has a one-dimensional kernel and cokernel.
This, along with the transversality condition from part
(iii)
of Lemma
5.3
, puts us in a position to use the classical Crandall–Rabinowitz–Zeidler theorem directly, without the work in Sections
3.4
or
4
to manage the extra finite-dimensional equation.

Remark 5.4
.

While the Crandall–Rabinowitz–Zeidler theorem is often used to solve a problem of the form
\displaystyle F(x,\lambda)=0
with
\displaystyle F
twice continuously differentiable, this regularity is not strictly necessary; the proof in
[
29
, Thm. 1.5.1]
really hinges on having
\displaystyle F
and
\displaystyle F_{x}
once continuously differentiable.
This allows us to avoid the annoying insufficient regularity in the frequency parameter
\displaystyle\omega
in our problem; recall Lemma
3.6
.

More precisely, we know that the three vectors
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}
, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}
form an orthonormal basis for
\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}])
and
\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*})
; now suppose that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}\cap\spn(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c})
.
Then by orthonormality

\boldsymbol{\phi}=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}+\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}+\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}.

By definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}
, we already have
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\rangle=0
, and now we compute

(5.2)

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle.

Thus
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=0
, and so
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\spn(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c})
.
This proves our claim above that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}
spans both the kernel and cokernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]
.

Alternatively, we could follow the bifurcation argument in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
and replace
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}
.
The only change would be the new version of the finite-dimensional problem (
3.31
)

(5.3a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{+}^{c}\rangle=0

(5.3b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=0.

By a calculation similar to (
5.2
), we always have (
5.3b
).
Specifically, for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}
, we have

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle,

thus
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{-}^{c}\rangle=0
regardless of the form of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
(This is actually a stronger result than our managing of the second finite-dimensional equation (
3.33a
) in Section
3.4
, as here there are no restrictions on the form of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
.)
Last, we can solve (
5.3a
) using the transversality condition from part
(iii)
of Lemma
5.3
, exactly as we did (
3.33a
) in Section
3.4
.
The major difference in the results here is that the solutions
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
now respect the symmetry.

5.3.
Existence of symmetries for the mass and spring dimers

We will build the symmetries primarily on a “reflection” operator and a “flip” operator.

Lemma 5.5
.

The operator

(5.4)

(R\boldsymbol{\phi})(x):=\boldsymbol{\phi}(-x)

has the following properties.

(i)

\displaystyle R\partial_{x}=-\partial_{x}R
.

(ii)

\displaystyle RS^{\theta}=S^{-\theta}R
for all
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\langle R\boldsymbol{\eta},R\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

(i)

This follows from the chain rule.

(ii)

We compute

(RS^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi})(x)=(S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi})(-x)=\boldsymbol{\phi}(-x+\theta)=\boldsymbol{\phi}(-(x-\theta))=(R\boldsymbol{\phi})(x-\theta)=(S^{-\theta}R\boldsymbol{\phi})(x).

(iii)

This follows from substitution.
∎

We will also use the “flip” operator

(5.5)

J:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},

which commutes with
\displaystyle R
.
These reflection and flip operators also appeared in the manifestation of symmetries for spatial dynamics coordinates
[
18
, Sec. 3.2]
.

5.3.1.
Symmetry in the mass dimer

The mass dimer symmetry is

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}:=-R\boldsymbol{\phi}.

The subscript here is meant to emphasize the role of the mass ratio
\displaystyle m=w^{-1}
in the mass dimer analysis.

We show that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}
satisfies property
(i)
of Definition
5.1
; all of the other properties of symmetries are quick and direct calculations.
That is, we show

\mathcal{G}_{c}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)

with
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}=c^{2}\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{P}
as defined in (
3.8
).
The operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
is defined in (
3.6
) and
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
in (
3.7
), and it is important here that in (
3.7
) we are assuming
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}=\mathcal{V}_{2}=:\mathcal{V}
.
In particular, we take
\displaystyle\kappa=1
.

First we compute

\frac{2}{\omega^{2}}\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle-M(R\boldsymbol{\phi})^{\prime\prime},-R\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\langle RM\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},R\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\langle M\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\frac{2}{\omega^{2}}\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega).

Here we have used
\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}R=R\partial_{x}^{2}
, which follows from part
(i)
of Lemma
5.5
, and also part
(iii)
of that lemma to get the penultimate equality.

Next,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle,

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{p})=(\mathcal{V}(p_{1}),\mathcal{V}(p_{2}))
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}=(1,1)
, and
\displaystyle\Delta_{+}(\omega)
is defined in (
3.1
).
Since
\displaystyle S^{\pm\omega}R=RS^{\mp\omega}
by part
(ii)
of Lemma
5.5
, we have

(5.6)

\Delta_{+}(\omega)\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}=-\Delta_{+}(\omega)R\boldsymbol{\phi}=-R\Delta_{+}(-\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}=RJ\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}.

Here we used the property that

(5.7)

-\Delta_{+}(-\omega)=J\Delta_{+}(\omega)

with
\displaystyle J
from (
5.5
).

Thus

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(RJ\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\langle R\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(J\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle.

Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}
is constant,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}=R\boldsymbol{1}
, and so part
(iii)
of Lemma
5.5
implies

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle R\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(J\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),R\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(J\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle.

Last, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{p})=(\mathcal{V}(p_{1}),\mathcal{V}(p_{2}))
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})
, we have

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(J\mathbf{p}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big(\mathcal{V}(p_{2})+\mathcal{V}(p_{1})\big)=\langle\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{p}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle.

We conclude

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega).

Last, we use the definitions of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
from (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) to compute, assuming
\displaystyle\kappa=1
,

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}(x)=\frac{2\cos(x)}{\Nu_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}2\cos(\omega_{c})\\
2-c^{2}\omega_{c}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}(x)=\frac{2\sin(x)}{\Nu_{c}}\begin{pmatrix}2\cos(\omega_{c})\\
2-c^{2}\omega_{c}&2\end{pmatrix}.

This shows
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}
directly.
Consequently, when we run the symmetric bifurcation argument for the mass dimer, we can just use the first case for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\pm}^{c}
in Lemma
5.3
.

5.3.2.
Symmetry in the spring dimer

The spring dimer symmetry is

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}:=-RJ=-JR

with
\displaystyle R
defined in (
5.4
) and
\displaystyle J
defined in (
5.5
).
The subscript is meant to emphasize the role of the linear spring coefficient ratio
\displaystyle\kappa
in the spring dimer analysis.

Again, we just check that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)
in the case
\displaystyle w=m^{-1}=1
, as the other symmetry properties from Definition
5.1
are evident.
With
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}=c^{2}\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{P}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
defined in (
3.6
) and
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
in (
3.7
), we have

\frac{2}{\omega^{2}}\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle-(RJ\boldsymbol{\phi})^{\prime\prime},-RJ\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\langle RJ\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},RJ\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\langle J\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},J\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\langle J^{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle\\
=\frac{2}{\omega^{2}}\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega).

Here we have again used properties of
\displaystyle R
from Lemma
5.5
and also
\displaystyle J^{*}=J^{-1}=J
.

Next,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle,

with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{p})=(\mathcal{V}_{1}(p_{1}),\mathcal{V}_{2}(p_{2}))
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}=(1,1)
, and
\displaystyle\Delta_{+}(\omega)
defined in (
3.1
).
We have

\Delta_{+}(\omega)\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi}=-\Delta_{+}(\omega)RJ=RJ\Delta_{+}(\omega)J

by (
5.6
) and (
5.7
), and so

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(RJ\Delta_{+}(\omega)J\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(J\Delta_{+}(\omega)J\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle.

We compute

J\Delta_{+}(\omega)J=\Lambda(\omega)\Delta_{+}(\omega),\qquad\Lambda(\omega):=\begin{bmatrix}S^{\omega}&0\\
0&S^{-\omega}\end{bmatrix},

and, for
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Lambda(\omega)\mathbf{p}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathcal{V}_{1}(S^{\omega}p_{1})+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathcal{V}_{2}(S^{-\omega}p_{2})=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big(\mathcal{V}_{1}(p_{1})+\mathcal{V}_{2}(p_{2})\big)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{p}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle.

We conclude

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Lambda(\omega)\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}(\Delta_{+}(\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{1}\rangle=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega).

Unlike in the mass dimer, it is not always the case that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
for the spring dimer.
Indeed, the situation is rather more complicated here, as we outline below.
It is for this reason that we developed Lemma
5.3
, which is unnecessarily elaborate for the mass dimer.

Lemma 5.6
.

Assume
\displaystyle w=1
.

(i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
if and only if
\displaystyle\omega_{c}=j\pi
for some even
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(ii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
if and only if
\displaystyle\omega_{c}=j\pi
for some odd
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We prove this lemma in Appendix
B.6
.
A consequence is that outside the isolated situations
\displaystyle\omega_{c}=j\pi
for some
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we must use the third, more complicated case of Lemma
5.3
to obtain symmetric eigenfunctions for the spring dimer.

We conclude this discussion of symmetry by noting that not all solutions to the traveling wave problem are symmetric.
Indeed, since the traveling wave problem is shift invariant (part
(ii)
of Corollary
3.2
), any solution
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=0
generates other solutions
\displaystyle S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}
for
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Still working in the spring dimer, suppose that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
is symmetric with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}
, so
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\phi}
.
We compute

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}=-JRS^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}=-JS^{-\theta}R\boldsymbol{\phi}=S^{-\theta}(-JR)\boldsymbol{\phi}=S^{-\theta}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi}=S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}.

Typically
\displaystyle S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}\neq\boldsymbol{\phi}
unless
\displaystyle\theta
is an even integer multiple of
\displaystyle\pi
.
Thus the shifted solution need not be symmetric.

6.
Quantitative Results

Our previous proofs have fixed the wave speed
\displaystyle c
to be greater than the speed of sound and yielded families of periodic solutions parametrized in “amplitude,” where the range of the amplitude has been allowed to depend on
\displaystyle c
.
Here we develop tools to track dependence on
\displaystyle c
and its variation from the speed of sound.
Such quantitative results have been essential to all of the existing nanopteron proofs that incorporate periodic solutions, and we expect the same to be necessary in any future constructions.

6.1.
An abstract quantitative bifurcation theorem

We first prove a very abstract bifurcation result from which our quantitative result for lattice periodics follows easily.
This result subsumes all of the existing quantitative periodic constructions for lattices and does not strictly depend on the long wave structure of the problem considered more broadly here.
That is, we claim that any of the prior quantitative periodic proofs follows from Theorem
6.2
below.

We rely on the following fixed-point theorem, which was proved as
[
20
, Lem. C.1]

Lemma 6.1
.

For
\displaystyle 0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
, let
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\rho}
be a Banach space and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\rho}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{X}^{\rho}
be a family of maps.
Suppose that for some
\displaystyle C_{0}
,
\displaystyle a_{0}
,
\displaystyle b_{0}>0
, if
\displaystyle x
,

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{X}^{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
be a family of Hilbert spaces such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{r+s}
is continuously embedded in
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{r}
for each
\displaystyle s\geq 0

Denote the norm on
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\rho}
by
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{\rho}
and the inner product on
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{0}
by
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Suppose that for
\displaystyle 0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
and some
\displaystyle\rho>0
, there is a map

with the following properties.

(i)

[Branch of trivial solutions]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}

(ii)

[Regularity]
The partial derivatives
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}
and
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}
exist and are continuous from
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\rho}\times\mathbb{R}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{0}

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\cdot)
exists and is continuous on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

[(Co)kernel dimensionality]
There is
\displaystyle\omega_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\ker(D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon}))=\spn(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{\epsilon})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\ker(D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})^{*})=\spn(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon})

for some vectors
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{\epsilon}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{\epsilon}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{X}^{0}
with
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}\|_{0}=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}\|_{0}=1
(the cases
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{\epsilon}=0
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon}=0
are allowed).

(iv)

[Uniform transversality]

\displaystyle\inf_{0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}}|\langle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}\rangle|>0

(v)

[Uniform coercivity]
For each
\displaystyle r\geq 0

\displaystyle C_{r}>0

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{r+\rho}\leq C_{r}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{r}
.

(vi)

[Bootstrapping]
If
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rho}
such that
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}^{r}
for some
\displaystyle r\geq 0

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}^{r+\rho}
.

(vii)

[Uniform mapping and Lipschitz estimates]
There is
\displaystyle b_{0}>0
such that the following estimates hold for each
\displaystyle r\geq 0
(not necessarily uniformly in
\displaystyle r

\sup_{0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}}\|D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\|_{\mathcal{X}^{r+\rho}\to\mathcal{X}^{r}}<\infty,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}\\
|\omega-\omega_{\epsilon}|<b_{0},\ |\grave{\omega}-\omega_{\epsilon}|<b_{0}\\
\omega\neq\grave{\omega}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)-D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\grave{\omega})\|_{\mathcal{X}^{r+\rho}\to\mathcal{X}^{r}}}{|\omega-\grave{\omega}|}<\infty,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{r+\rho}+|\omega-\omega_{\epsilon}|<b_{0}\end{subarray}}\|D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\|_{\mathcal{X}^{r+\rho}\times\mathcal{X}^{r+\rho}\to\mathcal{X}^{r}}<\infty,

and

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{r+\rho}+|\omega-\omega_{\epsilon}|<b_{0},\ \|\grave{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\|_{r+\rho}+|\grave{\omega}-\omega_{\epsilon}|<b_{0}\\
(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)\neq(\grave{\boldsymbol{\phi}},\grave{\omega})\end{subarray}}\frac{\|D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)-D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\grave{\boldsymbol{\phi}},\grave{\omega})\|_{\mathcal{X}^{r+\rho}\times\mathcal{X}^{r+\rho}\to\mathcal{X}^{r}}}{\|\boldsymbol{\phi}-\grave{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\|_{r+\rho}+|\omega-\grave{\omega}|}<\infty.

(viii)

If
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon}\neq 0
, then there are a Banach space
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\epsilon}
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\rho}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{0}
and a nonzero linear operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\epsilon}\colon\mathcal{W}_{\epsilon}\to\mathcal{X}^{0}
with the following properties.

\displaystyle\bullet

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega),\mathcal{T}_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle=0
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rho}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle\bullet

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}
.

\displaystyle\bullet

There is
\displaystyle\tau_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}=\pm\tau_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon}=\mp\tau_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}
.

Then there is
\displaystyle a_{\star}>0
such that for
\displaystyle|a|<a_{\star}
and
\displaystyle 0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
, there exist
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\epsilon}^{a}\in\cap_{r=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}^{r}
and
\displaystyle\omega_{\epsilon}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\epsilon}^{a},\omega_{\epsilon}^{a})=0

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\epsilon}^{a}=a(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\epsilon}^{a}),\qquad\langle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\epsilon}^{a},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}\rangle=0,\qquad\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\epsilon}^{0}=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\omega_{\epsilon}^{a}=\omega_{\epsilon}+\xi_{\epsilon}^{a},\qquad\xi_{\epsilon}^{0}=0.

The following mapping and Lipschitz estimates also hold for each
\displaystyle r
(not necessarily uniformly in
\displaystyle r
):

(6.5)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}\\
|a|<a_{\star}\end{subarray}}\|\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\epsilon}^{a}\|_{r}+|\xi_{\epsilon}^{a}|<\infty\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}\\
|a|<a_{\star},\ |\grave{a}|<a_{\star}\\
a\neq\grave{a}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\epsilon}^{a}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\epsilon}^{\grave{a}}\|_{r}}{|a-\grave{a}|}+\frac{|\xi_{\epsilon}^{a}-\xi_{\epsilon}^{\grave{a}}|}{|a-\grave{a}|}<\infty.

Proof.

We break the proof into several steps.

1.

The Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction.

Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)=0
for all
\displaystyle\omega
by Hypothesis
(i)
and
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}
exists and is continuous on
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\rho}\times\mathbb{R}
by Hypothesis
(ii)
, the fundamental theorem of calculus implies

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(0,\omega)\boldsymbol{\phi}+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}tD_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(st\boldsymbol{\phi},\omega)[\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi}]\ ds\ dt

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rho}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
Next, since
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\cdot)
exists and is continuous on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
by Hypothesis
(ii)
again, another application of the fundamental theorem of calculus yields

D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega+\xi)=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)+\xi{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)+\int_{0}^{1}\xi\big(D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega+t\xi)-D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)\big)\ dt

for all
\displaystyle\omega
,
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
Together with
\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}=0
from Hypothesis
(iii)
, these two expansions give

(6.6)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(a(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi}),\omega_{\epsilon}+\xi)=aD_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\psi}+a\xi{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}-a\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\xi,a)

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rho}
with
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{\epsilon}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{\epsilon}\rangle=0
and
\displaystyle\xi
,
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}
, where

(6.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\xi,a)

\displaystyle:=-\xi{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\psi}

\displaystyle-a\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}tD_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(st{a}(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi}),\omega_{\epsilon}+\xi)[\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi}]\ ds\ dt

\displaystyle-\xi\int_{0}^{1}\big(D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon}+t\xi)-D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\big)(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi})\ dt.

We will use the expansion (
6.6
) to obtain a pair of fixed-point equations for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}
and
\displaystyle\xi
.
Put

(6.8)

\Pi_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\phi}:=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}\rangle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{\epsilon}+\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon}\rangle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}^{\epsilon}.

Then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(a(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi}),\omega_{\epsilon}+\xi)=0

(6.9a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{0}}-\Pi_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(a(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi}),\omega_{\epsilon}+\xi)=0

(6.9b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(a(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\psi}),\omega_{\epsilon}+\xi)=0.

2.

The preliminary equation for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}
.

It follows from the expansion (
6.6

6.9a

(6.10)

(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{0}}-\Pi_{\epsilon})D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\psi}=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{0}}-\Pi_{\epsilon})\big(\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\xi,a)-\xi{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\psi}\big).

Put

(6.11)

\mathcal{X}_{\epsilon}^{\infty}:=\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rho}\ \middle|\ \langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{\epsilon}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{\epsilon}\rangle=0\right\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{Y}_{\epsilon}^{\infty}:=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{0}}-\Pi_{\epsilon})(\mathcal{X}^{0}).

Since, by Hypothesis
(iii)

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\epsilon}^{\infty}
and trivial cokernel in
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\epsilon}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\epsilon}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}

The preliminary equation for
\displaystyle\xi

We now turn our attention to the second, finite-dimensional equation (

Put

\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\xi,a):=D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{0}}-\Pi_{\epsilon})\big[\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\xi,a)-\big(\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\xi,a)\big)D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\omega}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\epsilon}(0,\omega_{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\epsilon}\big]

All of our estimates for

\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{r}

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle r\geq 0

Appendix A
Fourier Analysis

A.1.
Vectors and matrices

The following is wholly standard, but we include it in the hopes of completeness and clarity.
For
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we set

\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{k}\overline{w_{k}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|\mathbf{v}|_{2}:=\big(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}\big)^{1/2}.

Also, we define
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
to be the vector whose entries are the conjugates of those in
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and likewise if
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
(where
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is the space of all
\displaystyle m\times n
matrices with entries in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
), then
\displaystyle\overline{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is the matrix whose entries are the conjugates of those in
\displaystyle A
.
We denote by
\displaystyle A^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
the conjugate transpose of
\displaystyle A
.

For a matrix
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
, we put

|A|_{2}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\\
|\mathbf{v}|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}|A\mathbf{v}|_{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|A|_{\infty}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq m\\
1\leq j\leq n\end{subarray}}|A_{ij}|

with
\displaystyle A_{ij}
as the entries of
\displaystyle A
.
Then we have the inequalities

(A.1)

|A\mathbf{v}|_{2}\leq|A|_{2}|\mathbf{v}|_{2},\ \mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|A|_{2}\leq\sqrt{mn}|A|_{\infty}.

Let
\displaystyle I_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be the identity matrix.
If
\displaystyle|A|_{2}<1
, then
\displaystyle I_{n}-A
is invertible by the Neumann series, and

|(I_{2}-A)^{-1}|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-|A|_{2}}.

A.2.
Periodic Sobolev spaces

This material is developed in
[
30
, Sec. 8.1]
,
[
28
]
, and
[
14
, App. C.2]

Let
\displaystyle L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be the completion of

\mathcal{C}_{\per}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):=\!\left\{\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([-\pi,\pi],\mathbb{C}^{n})\ \middle|\ \phi(-\pi)=\phi(\pi)\right\}

under the norm

\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}:=\big(\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle_{L_{\per}^{2}}\big)^{1/2},\qquad\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}(x)\ dx.

For
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the
\displaystyle k
th Fourier coefficient of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-ikx}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\ dx.

For
\displaystyle r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, let

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(1+k^{2})^{r}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(k)\big),\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}:=\big(\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle_{H_{\per}^{r}}\big)^{1/2}

Finally, we put

H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):=\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\ \middle|\ \|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}<\infty\right\}.

Since we will primarily use the
\displaystyle L_{\per}^{2}
-inner product, we abbreviate it as

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle:=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}(x)\ dx.

We will employ two elementary identities involving this inner product.

First, with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, we substitute to obtain

(A.2)

\langle S^{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},S^{-\theta}\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle.

Second, with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{1}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, we integrate by parts to find

(A.3)

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle.

A.3.
Fourier multipliers

Let
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
be measurable.
A bounded linear operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\colon H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to H_{\per}^{s}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
is a Fourier multiplier with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
if the identity

\widehat{\mathcal{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)

holds for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In this case, the operator norm of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is

(A.4)

\|\mathcal{M}\|_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to H_{\per}^{s}(\mathbb{C}^{m})}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+k^{2})^{(s-r)/2}|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)|_{2}.

Conversely, if
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is such that the supremum in (
A.4
) is finite, then defining

(\mathcal{M}\boldsymbol{\phi})(x):=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ikx}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)

gives a Fourier multiplier
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\in\mathbf{B}(H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),H_{\per}^{s}(\mathbb{C}^{m}))
with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.
This and (
A.4
) are proved in
[
14
, Lem. D.2.1]
.

The adjoint of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is the bounded linear operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{*}\colon H_{\per}^{s}(\mathbb{C}^{m})\to H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying

\langle\mathcal{M}\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{H_{\per}^{s}(\mathbb{C}^{m})}=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}

for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}\in H_{\per}^{s}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
.
We can calculate
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{*}
explicitly via the formula

(A.5)

\widehat{\mathcal{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{\eta}}(k):=(1+k^{2})^{s-r}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)^{*}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(k),

where
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)^{*}
is the conjugate transpose of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)
.

A.4.
Differentiating the shift operator

We prove that the map

\mathbb{R}\to\mathbf{B}(H_{\per}^{r+2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))\colon\omega\mapsto S^{\omega}

is differentiable and that its derivative is Lipschitz continuous on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.
This is proved more generally in
[
14
, Thm. D.3.1]
for a “scaled” Fourier multiplier, but we include the calculation here for completeness and because all Fourier multipliers that we consider ultimately boil down to shifts.
The derivative at
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
is the operator
\displaystyle(S^{\omega})^{\prime}
given by

(A.6)

\widehat{(S^{\omega})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k):=k(ie^{i\omega{k}})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k).

For
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H_{\per}^{r+2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\displaystyle h\neq 0
, we compute

\left\|\left(\frac{S^{\omega+h}-S^{\omega}-h(S^{\omega})^{\prime}}{h}\right)\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\|_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}^{2}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(1+k^{2})^{-2}\left|\frac{e^{ihk}-1-ihk}{h}\right|^{2}(1+k^{2})^{r+2}|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)|_{2}^{2}.

Two applications of the fundamental theorem of calculus yield

(A.7)

e^{ihk}-1-ihk=(ihk)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}te^{ihkts}\ ds\ dt,

from which we bound

(A.8)

(1+k^{2})^{-2}\left|\frac{e^{ihk}-1-ihk}{h}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{(1+k^{2})^{-2}h^{4}k^{4}}{h^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}t\ dt=h^{2}\left(\frac{k^{4}}{2(1+k^{2})^{2}}\right).

It follows that

\left\|\left(\frac{S^{\omega+h}-S^{\omega}-h(S^{\omega})^{\prime}}{h}\right)\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\|_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}^{2}\leq Ch^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{H_{\per}^{r+2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}^{2},

from which we have differentiability.
The mismatch in regularity between the domain and codomain (
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{r+2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
vs.
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
) arises because of the factor of
\displaystyle k^{2}
in (
A.7
); squaring that
\displaystyle k^{2}
in (
A.8
) requires us to introduce the factor of
\displaystyle(1+k^{2})^{-2}
to compensate.
This agrees with the regularity requirements in
[
14
, Thm. D.3.1]
.

Now we check Lipschitz continuity for the derivative and calculate

\|\big((S^{\omega})^{\prime}-(S^{\grave{\omega}})^{\prime}\big)\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}^{2}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(1+k^{2})^{-2}\big|k(ie^{i\omega{k}})-k(ie^{i\grave{\omega}k})\big|^{2}(1+k^{2})^{r+2}|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(k)|_{2}^{2}.

Since

(A.9)

e^{i\omega{k}}-e^{i\grave{\omega}k}=ik(\omega-\grave{\omega})\int_{0}^{1}e^{i\grave{\omega}k+ik(\omega-\grave{\omega})t}\ dt,

we bound

(1+k^{2})^{-2}\big|k(ie^{i\omega{k}})-k(ie^{i\grave{\omega}k})\big|^{2}\leq|\omega-\grave{\omega}|^{2}\left(\frac{k^{2}}{(1+k^{2})^{2}}\right),

and this yields

\|\big((S^{\omega})^{\prime}-(S^{\grave{\omega}})^{\prime}\big)\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}^{2}\leq C|\omega-\grave{\omega}|^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{H_{\per}^{r+2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}^{2}.

This is the Lipschitz continuity for the derivative.
Here we did not strictly need the domain to be
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{r+2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and could have viewed
\displaystyle S^{\omega}
as an operator from
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{r+1}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
to
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, as we only have one power of
\displaystyle k
emerging from (
A.9
).
This too agrees with the regularity requirements in
[
14
, Thm. D.3.1]
.

A.5.
Composition operators in periodic Sobolev spaces

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{C}^{7}(\mathbb{R})
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(0)=0
.
We briefly sketch the argument that the composition operator

\mathcal{N}\colon H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\colon\phi\mapsto\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\circ\phi

is well-defined and twice-differentiable, and its second derivative is (locally) Lipschitz continuous.
First, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(0)=0
, we have

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(r)=r\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}(tr)\ dt\qquad\text{ and therefore }\qquad(\mathcal{N}(\phi))(x)=\phi(x)\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}(t\phi(x))\ dt.

Next, differentiating under the integral, we can express
\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}[\mathcal{N}(\phi)]
as a sum of products of derivatives of
\displaystyle\phi
up to second order and, by the periodic Sobolev embedding
[
28
, Thm. 7.9]
, continuous and periodic functions (involving integrals of the form
\displaystyle\textstyle{\int}_{0}^{1}\mathcal{V}^{(k)}(t\phi)\ dt
for
\displaystyle k=2
,
\displaystyle 3
,
\displaystyle 4
).
It follows from
[
30
, Cor. 8.8]
that
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\phi)\in H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Last, differentiability of
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
is straightforward to establish using the fundamental theorem of calculus; the proof is similar to the composition operator work in
[
24
, Lem. A.2]
.
We obtain
\displaystyle D_{\phi}\mathcal{N}(\phi)\eta=(\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}\circ\phi)\eta
and
\displaystyle D_{\phi\phi}^{2}\mathcal{N}(\phi)[\eta,\grave{\eta}]=(\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ\phi)\eta\grave{\eta}
, and (local) Lipschitz continuity follows from the fundamental theorem again.
For that, using
\displaystyle(\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ\phi)\eta\grave{\eta}=\phi\eta\grave{\eta}\textstyle{\int}_{0}^{1}\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(t\phi)\ dt
and estimating in the
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-norm, we need up to
seven
continuous derivatives on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
.

If we assume
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then the composition operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
is also infinitely differentiable on
\displaystyle H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and so (more importantly, for the purposes of Theorem

) by the Sobolev embedding all of its derivatives are locally bounded and locally Lipschitz.
This can be proved using the composition operator techniques in
[
15
, App. B]
, and we omit the details.

\kappa-1,\ w=1\end{cases}

B.1.4.
A basis for the kernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]

We are assuming
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\phi}=0
and so far know that

\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)=e^{-ix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(-1)+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(0)+e^{ix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1).

Since we always assume that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}
is real-valued, we have

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(-1)=\overline{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)},

and then

\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(0)+2\re[e^{ix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)].

Write
\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)=a_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}
with
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}
defined in (
B.1
) and suppose
\displaystyle a_{1}=a_{r}+ia_{i}
for
\displaystyle a_{r}
,
\displaystyle a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

\re\big[e^{ix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)\big]=\re\big[(a_{r}+ia_{i})\big(\re[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}]+i\im[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}]\big)\big]=a_{r}\re[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}]-a_{i}\im[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}].

Thus

\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(0)+2\re[e^{ix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)]=a_{0}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}+a_{r}\re[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}]-a_{i}\im[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}].

It follows that the vectors

(B.4)

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\qquad\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}(x):=\frac{1}{\Nu_{c}}\re[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}],\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}(x):=\frac{1}{\Nu_{c}}\im[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}],\qquad\Nu_{c}:=|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}|,

span the kernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]
.
We check orthonormality as follows and obtain linear independence, so they are a basis for the kernel.
First, that
\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=0
follows directly from the formulas above and the identity

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{\pm{ix}}\ dx=0.

Next, for any
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we compute

(B.5)

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=2\re\big[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\big],

(S^{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c})(x)=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\re[-ie^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}]=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\im[e^{ix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}]=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}(x),

and

(B.6)

\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},S^{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=2\re\big[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)\cdot(-i\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1))\big]=2\im[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\big].

Combining (
B.5
) and (
B.6
), incidentally, proves the orthogonality equivalence condition (
2.17
).
From (
B.6
), we have

\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}\rangle=2\im[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)]=2\im[|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}|_{2}^{2}]=0.

This concludes the orthonormality proof.
Last, the derivative identities (
2.15
) follow directly from the formulas (
B.4
).

B.1.5.
The kernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*}

As discussed in Appendix
A.3
, the adjoint operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*}\colon\mathcal{L}_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to H_{\per}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies

\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]^{*}\boldsymbol{\eta}}(k)=(1+k^{2})^{-2}(-c^{2}(\omega_{c}k)^{2}M+\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\omega_{c}k)^{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(k).

Here

Happily,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\eta}=0

Consequently, the kernel of

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}
, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}^{c}

\displaystyle 2c^{2}\omega_{c}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{+}^{\prime}(\omega_{c})

\displaystyle c
away from
\displaystyle 0

By (

\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle=2\re\big[\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}{1}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\big]=2\re\big[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}(\omega_{c})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\big].

-i(\kappa{e}^{i\omega_{c}}-e^{-i\omega_{c}})&-2c^{2}w^{-1}\omega_{c}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\omega_{c}}+\kappa{e}^{-i\omega_{c}}\\
1+\kappa-c^{2}\omega_{c}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\omega_{c}}+\kappa{e}^{-i\omega_{c}}\\
1+\kappa-c^{2}\omega_{c}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Some preparation and attention to detail will simplify what would otherwise be a burdensome calculation into a slightly less burdensome calculation.
Suppressing dependence on

In particular,

Then (
B.10

\displaystyle\Nu_{c}^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}^{\prime}(\omega_{c})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}&-iz_{2}\\
i(\overline{z_{2}})&w^{-1}z_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=z_{1}\big(|v_{1}|^{2}+w^{-1}v_{2}^{2})+2\re\big[i(\overline{z_{2}})v_{1}v_{2}\big].

This immediately shows that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}^{\prime}(\omega_{c})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)

\displaystyle w

\frac{w\Nu_{c}^{2}\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle}{2}=w\re\big[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{c}^{\prime}(\omega_{c})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)\big]=-2c^{2}\omega_{c}\big(w|v_{1}|^{2}+v_{2}^{2})+2w\re\big[i(\overline{z_{2}})v_{1}v_{2}\big].

B.8

Now we work on the second term in (
B.13

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{+}^{\prime}(\omega_{c})

We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{z_{2}}v_{1}

\frac{\Nu_{c}^{2}w\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}^{\prime}[\omega_{c}]\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}\rangle}{2}=-2c^{2}\omega_{c}\big(w|v_{1}|^{2}+v_{2}^{2})-\widetilde{\varrho}(\omega_{c})v_{2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{+}^{\prime}(\omega_{c}).

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}=\pm\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)=\pm\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{c}}(1)

We compute

From the definition of
