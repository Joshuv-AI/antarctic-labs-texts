Association between stress hyperglycemia ratio and all-cause mortality in patients with coronary heart disease
Authors: Hailin Liang, Chujuan Zeng, Junhong Huang, Yongluan Lin, Songming Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in hospitalized patients with acute coronary syndromes (ACS). However, its role in long-term prognosis remains controversial. This study aims to investigate the correlation between SHR and post-discharge all-cause mortality in coronary heart disease (CHD) patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted on a comprehensive dataset encompassing 5,273 coronary heart disease (CHD) patients from The First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College, spanning from January 2020 to April 2024. Cox regression analysis, smooth curve fitting techniques, and subgroup analyses were employed to evaluate associations.ResultsDuring a maximum follow-up of 1,649 days, 8.1% of patients experienced all-cause mortality. After multivariable adjustments, a U-shaped association emerged between SHR and mortality risk (HR = 1.35, 95%CI= (1.03 ~ 1.77), P = 0.031). Both low and high SHR levels were linked to increased mortality.ConclusionsSHR demonstrates a U-shaped association with post-discharge all-cause mortality in CHD patients, highlighting the need for glycemic management within an optimal range.

Introduction

The Global Burden of Disease Study and the World Health Organization have reported that cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death worldwide, both before and after the COVID-19 pandemic [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease study, there has been a significant downward trend in the age-standardized mortality rates for coronary atherosclerotic heart disease per 100,000 people from 1990 to 2019 and then to 2021, with rates of 158.9, 110.9, and 108.7, respectively. Despite this decline, CHD continues to top the list of global age-standardized causes of death. Furthermore, from 2010 to 2021, the global mortality rate attributable to cardiovascular diseases increased from 7.43 million to 8.99 million, thereby underscoring the persistent and escalating global burden imposed by these conditions [2]. A multitude of risk factors, including hypertension, elevated cholesterol, smoking, diabetes, and obesity, are known to be significant contributors to the progression of coronary heart disease and the occurrence of adverse cardiovascular events [1, 3].

Both acute glycemic excursions—hyper- or hypoglycemia—and chronically elevated glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) independently predict adverse outcomes in coronary heart disease [4]. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), by indexing admission glucose to ambient HbA1c, filters out the underlying chronic glycemic milieu and thereby more accurately captures the magnitude of acute stress-related metabolic dysregulation. Moreover, the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) is emerging as a pivotal biomarker for the identification of acute hyperglycemic states [5]. Research has demonstrated a significant correlation between these factors and the severity of cardiovascular events, particularly in the context of acute myocardial infarction and acute heart failure [6–12]. In previous studies involving patients with acute coronary syndrome or acute myocardial infarction (AMI), a J-shaped association was observed between stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) levels and in-hospital mortality rates [7–9]. However, the relationship between stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and long-term post-discharge mortality in coronary heart disease is complex, with evidence suggesting a J-shaped, U-shaped, or linear positive association. This fluctuation in the observed relationship indicates that the predictive significance of SHR for patients’ long-term results continues to be a subject of continuous discussion and calls for additional research [6, 8, 13, 14].

In this study, we aimed to investigate the association between SHR and post-discharge all-cause mortality in patients diagnosed with coronary heart disease (CHD). Although previous studies have investigated the association of SHR with all-cause mortality in CHD patients, they mainly focused on in-hospital outcomes, whereas our study uniquely explored the long-term prognostic impact of SHR on CHD out of hospital using a large single-center cohort, addressing inconsistencies in the existing literature. The study was conducted using data from the First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College, aiming to elucidate the prognostic significance of SHR in this specific patient population.

Method

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (approval no. B-2015-012).

A total of 15,543 patients diagnosed with CHD who were hospitalized at The First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College between January 2020 and April 2024 were consecutively screened. Patients meeting the following criteria were included: (1) age over 20 years old, (2) Patients diagnosed with coronary heart disease may also have hypertension, diabetes, stroke and/or heart failure. Patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) missing follow-up information, (2) insufficient or missing important laboratory results (glucose on admission or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c)), (3) missing information on smoke, drink, total cholesterol(TC), triglycerides(TG), and creatinine(CR), and aspartic transaminase (AST). Finally, 5273 participants were included. (Fig. 1)

Flowchart of the study cohort

Coronary heart disease (CHD)

In the cohort, CHD is diagnosed according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes, including coronary artery disease (I20-I25.9), myocardial infarction (I21-I23), and angina pectoris (I20.0-I20.9).

Outcome ascertainment

For the cohort, the outcome of interest was all-cause mortality. If a patient passed away in the hospital, the time of death was recorded as the official date of death in the hospital records. For patients who did not pass away in the hospital, we obtained detailed information regarding their survival status and exact date of death through routine telephone follow-ups. The time of an outcome event was defined as the time of death, loss to follow-up, or the time of the last telephone follow-up, whichever occurred first.

Covariate data measurement and definition

Patient demographics (age, sex, smoking status, and drinking status), medical history (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, and heart failure), and laboratory test result (HbA1c, glucose (GLU), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and creatinine (CR), and aspartic transaminase (AST)). Smoking status was categorized into the following 3 groups: never smoker, former smoker and current smoker. Alcohol drinking status was also categorized into 3 groups: never drinker, former drinker and current drinker. Blood glucose value refers to the first random measurement of blood glucose in the serum within 24 hours after admission. Blood specimens for the analysis of HbA1c, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), creatinine (CR) and aspartic transaminase (AST) were collected from the cubital vein after at least eight hours of fasting. Hypertension was recorded if the patient had previously been diagnosed with hypertension, used (currently or previously) antihypertensive drugs, or the SBP ≥ 140mmHg and/or the DBP ≥ 90mmHg. Diabetes was recorded if the patient had previously been diagnosed with diabetes, used (currently or previously) oral hypoglycemic drugs or insulin, or had HbA1c > 6.5%. Stroke was defined as a previous history of hemorrhagic stroke, ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack. Heart failure was defined as heart failure with previously diagnosed reduced ejection fraction, heart failure with improved ejection fraction, heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction, and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.

Statistical analysis

We divided patients into 4 groups based on quartile of SHR level in the cohort. For all data, the normal distribution of continuous data is represented by the mean (SD), the non-normal distribution is represented by the median (IQR), and the classified data is represented by counts and percentages. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed with one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact tests.

We conducted univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses to determine the relationship between Stress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR) levels and the risk of all-cause mortality among participants in our study. Variables that were found to have significant differences at baseline or were considered clinically important and prevalent in our cohort were selected for inclusion in the multivariable models.

To further explore the relationship between SHR and all-cause mortality, we applied restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, adjusting for the same set of covariates used in the multivariable regression. The findings are reported using hazard ratios (HR) along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To find the SHR level that corresponds to the minimum risk of all-cause mortality, we performed an inflection point analysis with the Cox proportional hazards model. We also conducted subgroup analyses using this model, with a focus on distinguishing between participants with and without diabetes and hypertension. The subgroup analyses aimed to evaluate the potential modifications and interactions within these subgroups, with the likelihood ratio test serving as the method for assessing these effects. Furthermore, we also conducted a KM survival analysis and plotted KM survival curves to compare the long-term survival rates of different subgroups of SHR. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.2.2, http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and the Free Statistics analysis platform (Version 2.0, Beijing, China).

We conducted a multivariate adjustment and developed three models, each adjusted for different variables, to demonstrate that SHR is an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality in CHD patients. Model 1 was not adjusted for any variables, model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, smoke and drink, model 3 was adjusted for the variables including model 2 plus hypertension, diabetes, stroke and heart failure, and model 4 was adjusted for the variables including model 3 plus CR, TC, TG and AST.

After adjusting for covariates in model 2 (HR = 1.58; 95% CI = ((1.18 ~ 2.10), P = 0.002), model 3 (HR = 1.50; 95% CI = ((1.13 ~ 2.00), P = 0.005) and model 4 (HR = 1.35; 95% CI = ((1.03 ~ 1.77), P = 0.031), SHR was identified as an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality in patients with CHD (Table 2).

After adjusting for all covariates, patients with SHR in the quartile 3 had a lowest risk of all-cause mortality. Patients with SHR in quartile 1 (HR = 1.66, 95%CI= ((1.22 ~ 2.26), P = 0.001), quartile 2 (HR = 1.27, 95%CI= ((0.92 ~ 1.75), P = 0.139), and quartile 4 (HR = 1.88, 95%CI= ((1.4 ~ 2.52), P < 0.001) had a significantly increased risk of all-cause death compared with quartile 3 (HR = 1). In other words, the risk of all-cause mortality in patients with SHR in quartile 1,2, and 4 was 1.66, 1.27, and 1.88 times higher than that in quartile 3, respectively (Table 2).

Following extensive multi-factorial adjustments, an analysis employing restricted cubic splines revealed a U-shaped association between stress hyperglycemia ratio levels and the risk of all-cause mortality in the cohort during the follow-up period. Inflection point analysis

Due to the U-shaped association between SHR and all-cause mortality in patients with CHD, we also conducted an inflection point analysis. The inflatable point was 0.652, indicating that when SHR < 0.652, with the increase of SHR, the risk of all-cause death decreased (HR = 0.122, 95%CI= ((0.023 ~ 0.643), P = 0.0131). However, when SHR > 0.652, the risk of all-cause death increased 2.3 times for every 1 unit increase in SHR (HR = 3.3, 95%CI= ((2.132 ~ 5.11), P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

To explore whether the association between SHR and all-cause mortality in patients with coronary heart disease is stable in both diabetic and hypertensive states (with or without), we performed subgroup analyses and interactive test in multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis.

The results demonstrated that the risk of all-cause mortality was significantly higher in SHR for quartile1, 2, and 4 compared to quartile 3, irrespective of hypertension status. Likewise, SHR in quartile1, 2, and 4 also markedly increased the risk of all-cause mortality relative to quartile 3, regardless of diabetes status.

There was no interaction between SHR and all-cause mortality in patients with and without hypertension or diabetes, indicating the robustness of the results. (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig 3)

Discussion

To date, only a limited number of studies have investigated the association between the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and all-cause mortality, with most focusing on intensive care patients and their in-hospital mortality rates or short-term outcomes (28-day all-cause mortality) [7–9]. Additionally, SHR has been evaluated as a prognostic predictor across various disease states, including chronic heart failure, heart failure complicated by sepsis, ischemia and nonobstructive coronary arteries, and severe community-acquired pneumonia in older adults [15–18]. However, findings on the association between SHR and long-term post-hospital mortality in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) have been inconsistent [6, 8, 13, 14]. In contrast, our study centers on the association between SHR and all-cause mortality in CHD patients, while also exploring its impact on CHD prognosis. After adjusting for relevant covariates, including age, sex, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, the cohort demonstrated a U-shaped association between SHR and all-cause mortality.

Mechanistically, the relationship between SHR and the risk of all-cause death may be closely related to factors such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [5, 8, 19].

The increased risk of all-cause mortality associated with high SHR may be attributed to elevated blood glucose levels, which could subsequently lead to increased systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [5, 20]. Inflammatory response and oxidative stress can impair vascular endothelial function, thereby exacerbating coronary atherosclerosis and ultimately resulting in myocardial ischemia as well as cardiac dysfunction [21]. In addition, a high SHR may compromise endothelial function, thereby increasing the risk of myocardial ischemia and heart failure through abnormal vasoconstrictor function and reduced myocardial perfusion [19, 22].

High SHR usually indicates that the patient is in an acute stress state, such as acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, or severe infection. Under such stress conditions, the body releases a large number of stress hormones (such as adrenaline and cortisol) by activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathoadrenal system, which leads to an increase in blood glucose [23]. This stress-induced hyperglycemia not only serves as a marker for the severity of the disease but is also significantly correlated with an increased risk of mortality.

In addition, high SHR may also be associated with insulin resistance. Insulin resistance leads to an increase in blood glucose, especially under stress. Insulin resistance not only compromises blood glucose regulation but also amplifies inflammation and oxidative stress, consequently imposing additional damage on the cardiovascular system [24, 25]. Elevated SHR levels may further promote thrombosis, activate the coagulation cascade, and heighten the risk of coronary thrombotic events, thereby exacerbating the incidence of cardiovascular events [23].

The mechanism by which low SHR increases the risk of all-cause mortality may be closely associated with patients experiencing a hypoglycemic state. Hypoglycemia can activate the sympathoadrenal system and promote the release of catecholamines such as epinephrine and norepinephrine [26]. These hormones lead to an increased heart rate, enhanced myocardial contractility, and peripheral vasoconstriction, thereby imposing a significantly greater cardiac workload. For patients with CHD, this stress response may lead to insufficient blood supply to the coronary artery and further aggravate myocardial ischemia [27]. In addition, frequent hypoglycemic events may have a cumulative effect on the cardiovascular system, increasing the incidence of cardiovascular events and the risk of death. This cumulative effect is particularly striking in patients with CHD, who have poorer coronary reserve and are more susceptible to the negative effects of hypoglycemia [28].

In addition, low SHR may also indicate the presence of malnutrition. Malnutrition causes the body to have insufficient energy reserves and is unable to effectively mobilize blood glucose to meet physiological needs during stress. The impairment of energy metabolism may further compromise the function of the cardiovascular system, thereby elevating the mortality risk among patients [8]. Low SHR may also be associated with adrenal insufficiency. Adrenocortical hormone plays an important role in stress response, and its insufficient secretion may lead to the weakening of stress hyperglycemia response, weaken the body’s ability to regulate stress, and then affect the stability of the cardiovascular system [29]. Finally, a low SHR may also indicate the presence of underlying metabolic disorders in patients, such as abnormal insulin secretion or impaired glucose metabolism. These metabolic disorders could potentially worsen the condition of patients with cardiovascular disease, thereby leading to poorer clinical outcomes [27, 30].

High or low SHRS are closely related to the level of glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Both high HbA1c and low HbA1c increased the risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality [31]. \

Our study has several key strengths. These include a sufficiently large sample size limited to a single region, as well as a population-based CHD design, which enables the simultaneous evaluation of SHR-associated risks for all-cause mortality and subgroup outcomes. This unique methodological approach enhances the study’s novelty and clinical relevance, offering findings with significant potential for real-world application. By rigorously accounting for potential confounders and biases, our study offers robust evidence for a U-shaped association between stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and all-cause mortality risk among coronary heart disease (CHD) patients.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the initial findings provide valuable insights, our study was single-center and focused on people in the Chaoshan area, and future validation in multi-ethnic, multi-center cohorts is needed, which is essential to enhance confidence in these conclusions. Second, during the patient’s hospitalization and during the follow-up after discharge, blood glucose measurements should have been conducted. However, due to the limitations in data collection and the inconsistency in the timing of the subsequent blood glucose measurements, we were unable to conduct a comparative analysis before and after, nor could we explore the impact of the dynamic changes in SHR on the all-cause mortality rate. This limitation underscores the need for standardized and serial glycemic monitoring in future prospective studies. Third, the possibility of unmeasured selection bias and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Although we adjusted for a series of clinically relevant covariates, several potential confounders were not captured in the database or were insufficiently documented for inclusion. These include medication adherence, detailed anthropometric data (such as height and weight), nutritional status, and psychosocial variables. In particular, the absence of body mass index (BMI) data may have limited the comprehensive assessment of metabolic risk profiles. Additionally, the retrospective nature of the study inherently involves incomplete patient information and potential misclassification, despite our efforts to minimize these through strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. The exclusion of a substantial number of patients due to missing data may also have introduced selection bias. Despite these limitations, the reasonably homogeneous study population, shared ethnicity, and adjustment for significant confounding factors suggest that the results are unlikely to change substantially due to the absence of certain factors.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests a U-shaped association between SHR levels and all-cause mortality in patients with CHD, implying that both low and high SHR levels may be linked to an elevated risk. These observations could inform clinical assessment of CHD prognosis and highlight that the relationship between SHR and mortality merits further validation in multiethnic, multicenter cohorts.
