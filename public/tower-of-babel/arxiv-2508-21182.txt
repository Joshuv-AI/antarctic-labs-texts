The Impact of Spectroscopic Redshift Errors on Cosmological Measurements
Etienne Burtin

Abstract
Spectroscopic redshift errors, including redshift uncertainty and catastrophic failures, can bias cosmological measurements from galaxy redshift surveys at sub-percent level. We investigate their impact on full-shape clustering analysis using contaminated mock catalogs. We find that redshift uncertainty introduces a damping effect on the power spectrum. This damping is scale-dependent and absorbed by counterterms in the clustering model, keeping parameter biases below 5 % 5\% for the DESI survey. Catastrophic failures reduce the power spectrum amplitude by an approximately constant factor scaling with contamination rate f c f_{c} . While this effect is negligible for the DESI ELG populations ( f c = 1 % f_{c}=1\% ), the slitless-like errors, combining redshift uncertainty with f c = 5 % f_{c}=5\% catastrophics, introduce significant biases in cosmological constraints. For this case, shifts from 6 % 6\% to 16 % 16\% ( ∼ 2.2 ​ σ \sim 2.2\sigma level) arise in estimating the fractional growth rate d ​ f ≡ f / f fid df\equiv f/f^{\rm{fid}} and the log primordial amplitude ln ⁡ ( 10 10 ​ A s ) \ln(10^{10}A_{s}) . Applying a correction factor ( 1 − f c ) 2 (1-f_{c})^{2} on the galaxy power spectrum mitigates the bias but weakens the parameter constraints due to new degeneracies. Alternatively, fixing f c f_{c} to its expected value during fitting successfully restores the unbiased posterior without loss in constraint. Our results indicate that for space-based slitless surveys such as Euclid , an accurate estimation of f c f_{c} and its incorporation into the clustering model are essential to get unbiased cosmological constraints. Extending to evolving dark energy and massive neutrino cosmologies, we find that redshift errors do not bias the dark energy properties parametrized by w 0 w_{0} and w a w_{a} , but lead to slightly weaker constraints on ∑ m ν \sum m_{\nu} .

Spectroscopic Survey and Redshift Errors

Spectroscopic surveys measure the redshifts of objects with high precision by analyzing their spectral features, such as emission and absorption lines. Two massive ongoing surveys, DESI and
Euclid
, are using spectroscopy to measure galaxy clustering with unprecedented accuracy. In its first three years, the ground-based DESI survey has observed over 14 million galaxies
[
5
]
and plans to extend its initial plan to 50M galaxies across
18{,}000\,\rm{deg}^{2}
. Its dark matter tracer includes Bright Galaxies (BGS) at
0.1<z<0.4

[
30
]
, Luminous Red Galaxies (LRG) at
0.4<z<1.1

[
31
]
, Emission Line Galaxies (ELG, mainly [O
ii
] emitters) at
0.8<z<1.6

[
32
]
, and Quasi-Stellar Objects (QSO) at
0.8<z<3.5

[
33
]
. The
Euclid
mission conducts a slitless spectroscopic survey from space, aiming to observe tens of millions of galaxies over
15{,}000\,\rm{deg}^{2}
, primarily targeting [
\rm{H\,\alpha}
] emitters in the redshift range
0.9<z<1.8

[
13
]
. Its Quick Data Release 1 is already publicly available
[
34
]
. The large cosmic volumes probed by DESI and
Euclid
enable sub-percent precision in cosmological measurements and require a rigorous understanding of systematic errors, particularly those related to redshift measurements.

In this work, the spectroscopic systematic redshift errors are referred to as
redshift errors
. The redshift errors exist in both ground-based and space-based galaxy redshift surveys. They include redshift uncertainty, the small statistical fluctuations in redshift measurements, and redshift catastrophic failures, the large errors from the true values. Both types of error persists in secure redshift measurements, i.e., those that have been calibrated and successfully passed the redshift determination pipeline. Consequently, these hidden errors can distort the observed clustering pattern and potentially bias our cosmological analyses. Since both redshift uncertainty and catastrophic failures can be quantified from repeated redshift observations, it is practical to analyze them together. This combined treatment is also relevant for space missions such as
Euclid
, which apply slitless spectroscopy to measure redshifts and are therefore more susceptible to both types of contamination. In the following, we will describe how redshift uncertainty and redshift catastrophic failures are characterized and modeled, and what are the redshift errors expected in slitless spectroscopic surveys.

2.1
Redshift uncertainty

Redshift uncertainty is an inevitable systematic error in spectroscopic redshift measurements. It arises because the spectral lines used to determine redshifts have specific widths rather than being ideal delta functions. These widths can result from a combination of physical effects (e.g., thermal motions, turbulence, gravitational broadening) and the instrumental resolution. Spectroscopic surveys report redshift uncertainty for different types of targets. For example, in eBOSS galaxy samples, the mean redshift uncertainty for LRG is
65.6\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}

[
35
]
. For QSO, the typical uncertainty is around 300
\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}

[
36
]
. Repeat observations of ELG indicate that 50% of redshift estimates have a precision better than
20\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}

[
37
]
. In the recent DESI Data Release 1 (DR1), the redshift precision reaches
\sim 10\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}
for BGS and ELG,
\sim 50\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}
for LRG, and
20
-
125\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}
for QSO in
0.8<z<1.8

[
27
]
.

The effect of redshift uncertainty is equivalent to randomly smearing the peculiar velocity of an observed object along the line-of-sight. This smearing velocity is empirically quantified using redshift difference,
\Delta z
, from repeated observations, given by
\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}=c\,\Delta z/(1+\bar{z})
. The
c
is the speed of light and
\bar{z}
is the preferred redshift measurement of the object, typically taken as the mean or the highest-confidence one from repeats. The distribution of observed
\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}
can be statistically modeled. For galaxies, this distribution is typically Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\,\sigma_{\Delta v})
, with
\sigma_{\Delta v}
characterizing the redshift uncertainty dispersion
[
35
,
25
,
26
]
:

\mathcal{N}(\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}\,|\,\mu,\,\sigma_{\Delta v})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\Delta v}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\Delta v}}\right)

(2.1)

For QSO, the distribution of
\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}
usually exhibits extended wide tails. In such case, a multiple-Gaussian model
[
36
,
33
]
or a Lorentzian distribution
[
26
]
, provides a better fit to repeat measurements. In our work, we use the Lorentzian profile,
\mathcal{L}(\,p,\,w_{\Delta v})
, to describe the QSO redshift uncertainty, which can be expressed as:

\mathcal{L}(\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}\,|\,p,\,w_{\Delta v})=\frac{A}{1+((\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}-p)/w_{\Delta v})^{2}}

(2.2)

Here,
w_{\Delta v}
captures the width of the distribution, thus characterizing the redshift uncertainty in the Lorentzian profile. In our analyses, we adopt both Gaussian and Lorentzian profiles to model QSO redshift uncertainty. In this way, we can access how the presence of long-tailed uncertainty distributions may impact clustering and cosmological measurements.

2.2
Redshift catastrophic failures

Redshift catastrophic failures (or redshift catastrophics) arise in a small fraction of redshift measurements, typically identified through significant redshift differences in repeated observations. These errors can result from misidentification of sky residuals or confusion between spectral lines of different galaxies
[
14
,
21
,
17
]
. Faint or ambiguous spectral features can also lead to catastrophically incorrect redshift measurements. The catastrophics contamination rate,
f_{c}
, and the threshold for identifying it vary in different surveys. For example, the WiggleZ dark energy survey reports a 5%
f_{c}
by defining redshift blunders with
|\Delta z|>400\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}
(larger than
4\sigma_{\Delta v}
)
[
14
]
. In eBOSS, the reported
f_{c}
is less than 1% for LRGs and ELGs (
|\Delta z|>1000\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}
)
[
37
,
35
]
and 1.6% for quasars (
|\Delta z|>3000\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}
)
[
36
]
. In DESI DR1, the
f_{c}
is estimated to be less than 1% for all tracers at
z<2.1

[
27
]
.

In the study of DESI DR1 repeated ELG observations, redshift catastrophics are classified into two main categories: sky confusion at
z=1.32
and random catastrophics
[
21
]
. Sky confusion, responsible for 24% of these errors, arises from misidentifying residuals of sky emissions at around 8625 Å and can be approximated as a Gaussian distribution. The remaining 76% random catastrophics encompass various error patterns and are modeled using a log-normal profile as:

\mathcal{LN}(\Delta\hat{v}_{\rm{catas}}\,|\,\mu_{\text{ran}},\sigma_{\text{ran}}^{2},v_{0})=\frac{A}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\text{ran}}\Delta\hat{v}_{\rm{catas}}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\left(\Delta\hat{v}_{\rm{catas}}-\mu_{\text{ran}}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\text{ran}}^{2}}\right]

(2.3)

This function models the absolute log-nomral distribution of redshift catastrophics (
\Delta\hat{v}_{\rm{catas}}=\log_{10}(|\Delta v_{\rm{catas}}|)
) with parameters
\mu_{\text{ran}}
,
\sigma_{\text{ran}}^{2}
and
v_{0}
to characterize. In our study, we focus on the random pattern while varying the contamination rate
f_{c}
to generalize catastrophics patterns for different surveys.

2.3
Combined Errors for Slitless Redshift Surveys

Space missions such as
Euclid

[
13
]
, Roman
[
38
]
and CSST (scheduled for launch in 2027)
[
39
]
employ slitless spectroscopy to map the 3D distribution of galaxies over wide areas of sky. Early datasets from
Euclid
(Quick Data Release 1;
[
34
]
) are now publicly available, providing initial evaluations of the performance of the redshift measurement. Unlike traditional slit-based spectroscopy that captures individual targets using fibers, slitless spectroscopy disperses the light from all sources in the field simultaneously, enabling efficient and continuous spectroscopic observations without the need for target pre-selection
[
29
,
17
]
. This approach allows for high sky coverage and dense sampling of galaxies in the redshift range relevant to LSS studies.

However, the slitless design magnifies the redshift errors.
Euclid
spectroscopic redshifts are derived from spectra provided by the Near Infrared Spectro-Photometer (NISP), which operates with a resolution of
R\simeq 500

[
29
]
. In comparison, the ground-based DESI survey uses instruments with resolution
R\sim 2000
to
5500

[
10
]
. The lower spectral resolution in slitless survey leads to broader spectral lines and consequently increased redshift uncertainty. Additionally, the lack of spatial isolation between sources causes spectral contamination, typically through cross-contamination and self-contamination
[
17
]
. Cross-contamination arises from overlapping spectrograms of neighboring sources (redshift interlopers;
[
23
]
), while self-contamination results from the degeneracy between spatial and spectral information along the dispersion direction. These combined effects lead to a higher rate of redshift catastrophics in the sample. To address these challenges, we adopt a hypothetical redshift error that combines both redshift uncertainty and catastrophics to simulate the expected observational systematics in slitless surveys. The model assumes larger uncertainty than those typical of ELGs (i.e., LRG-like) and a higher rate of catastrophic errors (5% with
|\Delta v|>1000\,\rm{km}\cdot s^{-1}
). This hypothetical redshift error will help us to assess their influences on cosmological analyses obtained from space slitless surveys.

4.1
Power Spectrum Estimator

We estimate the power spectrum of mock catalogs using the weighted galaxy density field with a convenient normalization
[
42
]
:

F(\textbf{r})=\frac{w(\textbf{r})\left[n_{g}(\textbf{r})-\alpha n_{s}(\textbf{r})\right]}{A^{1/2}}

(4.1)

where
w(\textbf{r})
is the weight function.
n_{g}
and
n_{s}
are the observed number densities of galaxy and random catalogs, respectively. The factor
\alpha=N_{g}/N_{s}
normalizes the contribution from the random, and the normalization factor
A
is defined with the expected mean space density of galaxies
\bar{n}(\textbf{r})
as
A=\int d\textbf{r}\left[\bar{n}(\textbf{r})w(\textbf{r})\right]^{2}
. The estimator of power spectrum multipoles
P_{\ell}
is given by
[
42
,
43
]
:

P_{\ell}(\vec{k})=(2\ell+1)\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\left[\int d\textbf{r}_{1}\int d\textbf{r}_{2}F(\textbf{r}_{1})F(\textbf{r}_{2})\times e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\textbf{r}_{1}-\textbf{r}_{2})}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\textbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\textbf{r}}_{h})-\rm{SN}_{\ell}\right]

(4.2)

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
is the Legendre polynomial and
\textbf{r}_{h}=(\textbf{r}_{1}+\textbf{r}_{2})/2
. The shot noise term
\rm{SN_{\ell}}
is expressed as
{\rm{SN}}_{\ell}(\textbf{k})=(1+\alpha)\int d\textbf{r}\,\bar{n}(\textbf{r})w^{2}(\textbf{r})\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\textbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\textbf{r}})
, which only contribute to monopole
(\ell=0)
and is negligible relative to
P_{\ell}
for
\ell>0
.
In practice, we use the
pypower
1
1

1

https://github.com/cosmodesi/pypower

[
44
]
to compute the power spectrum multipoles from our mock catalogs.

Galaxy Clustering Models

4.2.1
Effective-field theory of LSS

Effective field theory (
EFT
) has become a powerful tool to model the LSS of the Universe. By studying small-scale nonlinear physics, the
EFT
of LSS extend the standard perturbation theory to scales beyond the breakdown of the fluid and single-stream approximations
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
. With loop corrections and improved modeling of biased tracers with redshift-space distortions,
EFT
enables reliable predictions of the galaxy power spectrum multipoles up to
k\approx 0.25\,h\,\rm{Mpc}^{-1}
in redshift space
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
. In general, the galaxy redshift-space power spectrum in
EFT
framework is expressed as
[
8
]
:

P^{\rm{\texttt{EFT}}}_{g,\,s}(k,\mu)=P_{g,\,s}^{\rm{PT}}(k,\mu)+(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{2}\mu^{2}+\alpha_{4}\mu^{4}+...)k^{2}P_{g,\,s}^{\rm{L}}(k)+\left(\rm{SN}_{0}+\rm{SN}_{2}k^{2}\mu^{2}+...\right),

(4.3)

Here,
P_{g,\,s}^{\rm{PT}}
is the galaxy redshift-space power spectrum based on perturbation theory (Standard, Euclidian or Lagrangian…). It is derived from the linear matter power spectrum, incorporating loop corrections and galaxy bias parameters, such as
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
and
b_{s}
. The second terms are the general
EFT
counterterms parameterized by
\alpha_{i}
, which models the non-perturbative physics and the nonlinear mapping between real and redshift space. The last term corresponds to the stochastic contributions characterized by the shot noise parameters
\rm{SN}_{i}
. The
EFT
of LSS approach has been successfully applied in recent analyses of BOSS, eBOSS and DESI, leading to improved constraints on cosmological parameters when using the full-shape of power spectrum
[
7
,
9
]
. Several
EFT
codes are publicly available
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
, and have demonstrated consistent performance in the full-shape analysis
[
55
]
. In our work, we use the
FOLPS
\nu
2
2

2

https://github.com/henoriega/FOLPS-nu
code to calculate the
EFT
power spectrum
[
54
]
.

4.2.2
Full-Shape Fitting Approaches

We adopt two fitting strategies to perform full-shape cosmological inference: ShapeFit compression
[
56
]
and Full-Modeling. These approaches have been extensively tested and validated in analyses of the BOSS and DESI surveys
[
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
.

Compression analysis focuses on fitting a set of compressed physical parameters that describe the shape and the anisotropies of the galaxy power spectrum. These include the
f\sigma_{8}
and the scaling parameters
\alpha_{\parallel}
and
\alpha_{\perp}
, which are often reparameterized into the isotropic dilation parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}}\equiv\alpha_{\parallel}^{1/3}\alpha_{\perp}^{2/3}
and the Alcock-Paczynski (AP) parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{ap}}\equiv\alpha_{\parallel}\alpha_{\perp}^{-1}
. ShapeFit further introduces two shape-related parameters,
m
and
n
, to capture information from the early-time transfer function. In this work, we vary only one shape parameter
m
, while keeping
n
fixed, and we report ShapeFit results relative to a reference cosmology, specifically using
dm\equiv m-1
and
df\equiv f/f^{\rm{fid}}
.

Another fitting approach, known as Full-Modeling analysis, avoids the compression steps and directly fits cosmological models to the power spectrum signals. In this way, the parameter dependence of the linear power spectrum along and geometrical information are not kept separate. This allows for a direct comparison between theoretical predictions and observational data, leading direct constraints on cosmological parameters. In our baseline fitting procedure, we sample five cosmological parameters:
h
,
\omega_{\rm cdm}
,
\omega_{b}
,
\ln(10^{10}A_{s})
,
n_{s}
. These parameters are directly used in theoretical models, which facilitates the calculation and a direct comparison of cosmological biases introduced by redshift errors.

In the context of redshift error studies, ShapeFit provides a direct view of how redshift errors distort the shape of observed power spectrum, as reflected by their impact on compressed physical parameters. This makes the interpretation of redshift errors effect straightforward. The Full-Modeling approach, on the other hand, gives cosmological constraints, allowing us to quantify the biases introduced by redshift errors in final cosmological parameter constraints.

4.3
Clustering effect of redshift errors

4.3.1
Redshift uncertainty damping

As pointed out in the eBOSS QSO analysis
[
62
,
24
,
20
]
, a straightforward approach to model the impact of redshift uncertainty is to introduce an additional Gaussian damping term:

P_{g,\,s}(k,\mu)=P_{\rm{novir}}(k,\mu)\,F_{\rm{FOG}}(k,\mu)\exp\left[-(k\mu\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\right]

(4.4)

Here,
P_{\rm{novir}}
is the non-linear kaiser power spectrum and
F_{\rm{FOG}}
is finger-of-god (FOG) factor. In the last Gaussian damping term, the parameter
\sigma^{\prime}
quantifies the scale of redshift uncertainty and is related to the equivalent velocity smearing with
\sigma^{\prime}=\Delta v_{\rm{smear}}*{(1+z)}/{H(z)}

[
62
]
. Using a Taylor expansion of the Gaussian exponential term:

\exp\left[-(k\mu\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\right]\,\rightarrow 1+a_{2}(k\mu)^{2}+a_{4}(k\mu)^{4}+\ldots

(4.5)

We note that the second and third terms scale as
P(k)(k\mu)^{2}
and
P(k)(k\mu)^{4}
, respectively. These terms show similar structures to those of
EFT
counterterms in
equation

4.3
. In the
EFT
of LSS, the general form of such counterterms can be derived from a moment expansion of the redshift-space power spectrum
[
51
]
, as well as from comparisons with phenomenological models
[
6
]
. These studies suggest that this part of
EFT
counterterms, parameterized by
\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{4}
particularly, are capable of absorbing nonlinear effects introduced by redshift uncertainty. We will demonstrate this explicitly in
section

5
with detailed explanation in
appendix

B
.

4.3.2
Redshift catastrophics correction

Redshift catastrophics have been addressed in theoretical models for redshift surveys like WiggleZ
[
14
]
and recent
Euclid

[
23
]
. Assuming that the redshift catastrophics contamination rate is
f_{c}
, an approximated correction is to multiply the
EFT
galaxy power spectrum by a factor of
(1-f_{c})^{2}
:

P^{\texttt{EFT}+f_{c}}_{g,\,s}(k,\mu)\approx(1-f_{c})^{2}P^{\texttt{EFT}}_{g,\,s}(k,\mu)

(4.6)

The validity of this approximation depends on both
f_{c}
and the typical displacement caused by catastrophics. Based on our mock catalogs study with contamination
f_{c}=1\%
and
f_{c}=5\%
, we find that the relative difference between the correction factor,
R_{\rm{corr}}=(1-f_{c})^{2}
, and the ratio from the mocks,
R_{\rm{mock}}
, satisfies:

\Bigg\lvert\frac{R_{\rm{corr}}(k)-R_{\rm{mock}}(k)}{R_{\rm{mock}}(k)}\Bigg\lvert<10\%,\quad\rm{For}\ k\gtrsim 0.02\,h\,\rm{Mpc}^{-1}

(4.7)

This suggests that the approximation is sufficiently accurate for full-shape power spectrum analyzes, especially considering that additional nuisance parameters in the
EFT
can absorb residual mismatches. However, this correction is not applied in the standard
EFT
full-shape analysis. This is because, as we will demonstrate in
section

5.2.2
, introducing this free parameter
f_{c}
in the fitting process will lead to the loss of constraining power on certain cosmological parameters.

4.4
Cosmological Inference and Covariance Matrices

For cosmological inference, Bayesian statistics is employed to infer constraints on physical or cosmological parameters. Assuming the evidence from the data is normalized to one, the posterior probability is expressed as
P(\Theta|\mathcal{D})\propto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}|\Theta)P(\Theta)
, with
\Theta
being the set of input parameters, including compressed parameters
\left\{\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}},\;\alpha_{\mathrm{ap}},\;df,\;dm\right\}
by ShapeFit or cosmological parameters
\left\{h,\;\omega_{cdm},\;\omega_{b},\;\rm{ln}(10^{10}A_{s}),\;n_{s}\right\}
by Full-Modeling. It also contains nuisance parameters considered in the
EFT
model:
\left\{b_{1},\;b_{2},\;b_{s},\;\alpha_{0},\;\alpha_{2},\;\alpha_{4},\;\rm{SN}_{0},\;\rm{SN}_{2}\right\}
. Assuming that we have Gaussian-distributed data, the likelihood function can be expressed as
\mathcal{L}(\Theta)\propto e^{-\chi^{2}(\Theta)/2}
. The
\chi^{2}
statistic quantifies the goodness of fitting and is computed based on the difference between the observed data and the model predictions, which reads as:

\chi^{2}(\Theta)=(\mathcal{D}_{\rm{data}}-\mathcal{D}_{\rm{model}}(\Theta))^{T}\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}\,(\mathcal{D}_{\rm{data}}-\mathcal{D}_{\rm{model}}(\Theta))

(4.8)

In our study, the vector
\mathcal{D}
includes a combination of the power spectrum monopole
P_{0}(k)
and quadrupole
P_{2}(k)
. The subscript ’data’ and ’model’ refers to the measurements from observations (here mocks) and theoretical model predictions. The covariance matrix
\mathcal{C}
is extracted from
Quijote
500 mock catalogs. It incorporates the variances observed in the data and is normalized to the number of the datasets,
N_{d}
, with the formulation:

\mathcal{C}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{N_{d}-1}\ \,\langle(\mathcal{D}_{i}-\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{i})(\mathcal{D}_{j}-\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{j})\rangle

(4.9)

Where
\mathcal{D}_{i}
signifies the power spectrum from a single realization and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{i}
is ensemble mean. In
Quijote
suite, the volume of each realization is
V_{1}=1\,h^{-3}\rm{Gpc}^{3}
. To model the reduced cosmic variance expected from galaxy surveys, we use the rescaled covariance by volume as:
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}=({V_{1}}/{V_{n}})\,\mathcal{C}
, where
V_{n}
is the target survey volume. In our analyses, we rescale the covariance to an effective volume of
V_{25}=25\,h^{-3}\rm{Gpc}^{3}
to match the expected survey volumes of DESI and
Euclid
.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we investigate the impact of spectroscopic redshift errors on the cosmological measurements.
section

2
introduces two main types of redshift errors, redshift uncertainty and redshift catastrophic failures, which can affect redshift measurements in both ground-based (fiber-fed) and space-based (sltiless) spectroscopic surveys such as eBOSS, DESI and
Euclid

[
35
,
21
,
17
]
. While redshift uncertainty refers to small errors present in all redshift measurements, redshift catastrophic failures correspond to large deviations from the true values in a small fraction of the measurements. Both types of error distort the observed clustering pattern and can lead to potential biases in the inferred cosmological parameters.

The effect of redshift errors can be quantified and modeled using repeat redshift observations. Based on this, in
section

3
we use the
Quijote
simulation suite to constructed our contaminated mock catalogs. For redshift uncertainty, we consider three cases: LRG-like Gaussian, QSO-like Gaussian and QSO-like Lorentzian smearings, with the velocity dispersions informed by DESI EDR studies
[
26
]
. For redshift catastrophics, we use a log-normal Gaussian distribution
[
21
]
with contamiantion rate
f_{c}=1\%
to model ELG-like catastrophics and
f_{c}=5\%
for hypothetical slitless-like errors. The slitless-like errors also combines LRG-like Gaussian smearing to simulate the observing condition expected in slitless spectroscopic survey such as
Euclid
. The following
section

4
describes the methodologies to perform the full-shape power spectrum analysis. We use clustering models based on the effective field theory (
EFT
) of LSS and implement both ShapeFit and Full-Modeling approaches to extract cosmological information, and we fit the power spectrum monopole and quadrupole for
k\in[0.02,0.20]\,h\,\rm{Mpc}^{-1}
. The analysis is targeted on DESI- and
Euclid
-like survey with effective volume
V_{25}=25\,h^{-3}\rm{Gpc}^{3}
. Our key findings are summarized below:

Redshift uncertainty introduces diffusive effect in the clustering, leading to a scale-dependent suppression to the observed power spectrum. This smearing effect can be modeled by a Gaussian damping term
[
20
]
. We find that the
EFT
cournterterms, parameterized by
\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{4}
, also capture this smearing effect, resulting in compressed and cosmological parameter estimates difference
<5\%
compared to the uncontaminated case. In contrast, redshift catastrophics cause an overall reduction on the power spectrum amplitude that approximately scales as
(1-f_{c})^{2}
. While this effect is negligible (difference
<6\%
) for ELG-like catastrophics, it significantly biases the estimation of amplitude-related parameters for slitless-like errors, namely
df

\ln(10^{10}A_{s})
in the Full-Modeling. In that case, we observe differences of
6\%{-}16\%

\sim 2.2\sigma

(1-f_{c})^{2}
applied to the power spectrum in
equation

4.6
. When freeing

f_{c}

\ln(10^{10}A_{s})

0.85\sigma

f_{c}
and applying the appropriate correction model to ensure unbiased cosmological results in slitless spectroscopic surveys.

Beyond the vanilla
\Lambda
CDM model, we explore the impact of redshift errors on measurements of evolving dark energy and massive neutrinos. For the
w_{0}w_{a}\rm{CDM}
model, we find that redshift errors do not bias the estimates of
w_{0}
and
w_{a}
, although they can enhance projection effects. In the
\nu\Lambda\rm{CDM}
case, redshift uncertainty weaken the constraint on
\sum m_{\nu}
by approximately 30%, while redshift catastrophics do not produce a noticeable difference. These suggest that redshift errors need to be also carefully checked to constrain extended cosmological models through full-shape analysis in redshift surveys.

Future work should focus on refining the model of redshift errors, exploring higher-order statistics to complement the power spectrum analyses, using light-cone mocks and more observational data to validate and extend these findings. In particular, we plan to apply our framework to DESI DR2 analysis, which provides a large sample of repeat observations well-suited for characterizing redshift errors. Using this dataset, we aim to jointly model redshift uncertainty and redshift catastrophics for each tracer population, across a wide range of redshift, enabling a more realistic and tracer-specific treatment of redshift systematics for cosmological inference.
