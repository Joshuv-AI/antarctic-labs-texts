Preemptive governance of online public opinion risks in public hospitals
Authors: Yuhong Chen, Ting Yu
Published: 2026

The rapid proliferation of mobile internet, short-video platforms, and generative artificial intelligence (AI) has made information dissemination increasingly immediate, fragmented, emotionally charged, and community-segmented. By December 2025, China had 1.125 billion internet users (internet penetration rate hit 80.1%), with short-video and generative AI users reaching 1.074 billion and 602 million, respectively (1). Public hospitals face unique vulnerabilities in this new media environment. They are characterized by high technical barriers, asymmetric physician-patient information, and intense public visibility and scrutiny (2). These features render even minor oversights prone to escalation. A small incident can rapidly evolve into a major online public opinion through online contagion, ultimately undermining hospital operations and eroding physician-patient trust. Recent years have seen frequent incidents involving service conflicts, privacy breaches, medical disputes, and medical anti-corruption (3,4). The conventional “blocking, deleting, and suppressing” or post-hoc firefighting approach no longer suits the current communication landscape. The 2026 National Health Work Conference mandated “improving mechanisms for preventing and managing online public opinion” (5), yet a gap persists between policy formulation and institutional implementation. Preemptive governance—shifting public opinion management upstream for source-based risk prevention—has become a research priority in hospital administration. This paper systematically reviews the domestic literature on this topic, evaluating theoretical applications, practical challenges, and governance pathways, while identifying research gaps and proposing future directions.

Theoretically, Heath’s 4R model—reduction, readiness, response, and recovery—is the most widely applied framework of public opinion research in healthcare, covering the crisis lifecycle (6). Domestic studies have introduced it into public hospital settings through tertiary hospital case studies, providing a foundational analytical framework (2,3,7,8). However, its direct application to online public opinion in the medical field reveals three fundamental misfits. First, the model was developed in the traditional media era and inadequately addresses emerging online communication scenarios. Second, risk identification in the reduction phase focuses on organizational and institutional dimensions, neglecting frontline micro-interactions like physician-patient communication and emotional dynamics. Third, online public opinion in the medical field tends to escalate into questioning of systemic frameworks. Trust restoration exhibits a “long-tail effect”: implicit trust deficits accumulate persistently over time. It may reactivate during subsequent incidents. This renders any short-term repair effort inherently superficial.

To address these limitations of the application of Heath’s 4R model in online public opinion in the medical field, the authors propose a contextual revision and operational extension of the 4R model, advocating a preemptive governance framework. First, the framework shifts risk control from upstream to routine physician-patient interactions, as over 70% of public opinion in the medical field crises originate from grassroots touchpoints like communication quality and service attitudes (3). Second, it applies differentiated technological empowerment to readiness and response. Routine monitoring should incorporate affective computing and AI-driven multimodal analytics. Crisis response, in turn, should leverage digital tools to reduce decision latency. Third, it extends recovery to industry-wide trust restoration and organizational learning. This framework requires establishing an industry-level communication review mechanism that uses positive narratives and opinion leader guidance to counter long-tail trust deficits. These measures are essential to break the dual cycle of “repeated errors” and “persistent trust erosion”. This paper does not deny the classical value of the 4R model in crisis management; rather, it identifies the model’s applicability boundaries in the specific context of online public opinion in the medical field and, accordingly, advocates a localized, scenario-specific adaptation that retains its lifecycle analytical logic.

Available evidence and case analyses consistently point to a critical conclusion that public hospitals face structural dilemmas in preemptive online public opinion governance. These dilemmas span the reduction, readiness, response, and recovery dimensions. They stem from misaligned organizational structures, institutional processes, and technological capacities. In the reduction dimension, frontline staff scored 83.63±5.58 (out of 100) on risk sensitivity, with physicians (81.02±9.52) scoring lower than nurses (P<0.05) (9), with a gap between self-reported competence and actual practice. This gap is rooted in traditional medical education’s overemphasis on biomedical knowledge at the expense of psychosocial communication skills, compounded by heavy clinical workloads that afford little capacity for addressing patients’ emotional needs (9). Meanwhile, patient satisfaction surveys, complaint systems, and online monitoring operate in silos, remain at the level of mere record-keeping rather than feeding back into service reengineering, causing recurrent issues. In the readiness dimension, existing monitoring technologies lag behind the evolving communication ecology. Most hospitals rely on “manual retrieval plus keyword matching” (10), with poor coverage of segmented, emotion-driven platforms like Douyin, Xiaohongshu, and WeChat Channels—high-risk zones for opinion escalation (11). The proliferation of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) introduces fabricated medical videos and tampered surgical records that evade traditional verification mechanisms, thereby complicating early warning efforts.

In the response dimension, bureaucratic hierarchies conflict with the time-sensitive demands of new-media crisis management. Algorithm-driven content recommendation has compressed the “golden four-hour” response window, yet initial official responses from large public hospitals are typically delayed. Inter-departmental information silos and inadequate data sharing produce unclear verification and rigid communication, exacerbating public antagonism. In the recovery dimension, restoration remains fragmented and trust reconstruction is largely absent. Consider the following example: a municipal hospital handled over 100 negative incidents over 3 years. Only a fraction generated institutional rectification reports (11). Many hospitals treat “waning attention” with closure. They prioritize content removal and comment control over structured review (11). Proactive reputation management is lacking once public attention subsides, allowing negative stereotypes to persist and spread across institutions. Fragmented rectification at the individual hospital level cannot resolve industry-wide trust deficits, rendering institutions prone to recurrent falls into the “Tacitus Trap”.

To address this issue, the authors propose a paradigm shift from reactive response to proactive immunization, operationalized across four interconnected dimensions—perception, computation, intervention, and restoration. In perception, risk identification should be embedded throughout the clinical workflow. Healthcare professionals should receive conflict management training with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)-based role-playing (9) to identify patient anxiety, cost concerns, and dignity sensitivity, as mandatory modules for residency training, promotion programs, and new-staff orientation. Medical administration should map high-frequency risk touchpoints across the entire patient journey, including registration, waiting, examination, billing, hospitalization, and surgery, and then establish internal early-warning indicators for preemptive intervention. In computation, a progressive intelligent early-warning system should be built, addressing monitoring gaps on emerging platforms with a “machine pre-screening plus manual verification” model and build a multimodal online public opinion monitoring system. Generic models should be locally adapted to healthcare discourse to reduce false positives, with risk-level standards tailored to hospital management, translating online sentiment into quantitative references. Given the immature state of authentication technologies in the medical domain, hospitals should avoid blind technological interception and instead adopt a rapid manual verification mechanism led by medical departments with third-party support.

In intervention, a tiered rapid response mechanism should be established. This paper proposes the blue-yellow-red framework with situational criteria for classification: blue (general)-isolated dissemination on a single platform, a mild emotional tone, and no media follow-up; yellow (moderate)-cross-platform diffusion or involvement of local media, with a negative emotional tone; red (major)-extensive nationwide dissemination or intervention by central media/top-tier trending lists, with highly polarized emotions. Under this framework, blue (general) is managed by public relations via offline communication; yellow (moderate) by multi-departmental deliberation led by the responsible executive, reporting verified facts promptly while avoiding premature causal attribution; red (major) by a joint command center led by top leaders, combining official statements with science communication and third-party engagement to counteract polarization. A rapid information notification channel should be established based on the internal office system, pushing sensitive information to relevant departments with clear responsibility and verification timelines. In restoration, crisis management should evolve into institutionalized organizational learning. Institutions should abandon the “waning attention = closure” paradigm. Instead, structured post-event reviews should produce diagnostic reports. These reports should specify rectification responsibilities and deadlines. Outcomes should be tied to departmental performance evaluations (12), translating lessons into institutional improvements. Hospitals should also establish routine reputation restoration mechanisms—by establishing channels with online public opinion leaders, showcasing technological and humanistic accomplishments, and appropriately disclosing rectification outcomes to bridge information gaps. Health authorities and hospital associations should spearhead regional collaborative alliances, sharing de-identified case databases and response toolkits, standardizing the interpretation of common industry issues, and conducting positive industry-level narratives, thereby advancing from single-hospital reputation restoration to industry-wide trust ecology maintenance.

Overall, existing studies have established a foundational conceptual framework for preemptive governance, yet most remain at the level of conceptual propositions rather than empirical validation. Empirical evidence is predominantly derived from single healthcare institutions, with few multi-regional, multi-tiered, large-sample studies. The literature is largely confined to Chinese databases, with insufficient international integration and comparative analysis. Actionable countermeasures against generative AI-related public opinion risks remain in an exploratory stage. Future research should: first, conduct multi-case comparative studies to empirically validate existing frameworks; second, develop operational protocols for the identification, early warning, and management of medical disinformation in the AIGC environment; third, investigate the operational mechanisms of regional and industry-level collaborative governance alliances; fourth, quantitatively evaluate the effects of various preemptive interventions on reducing crisis incidence and restoring public trust, thereby providing a stronger evidence base for policy formulation.
