The Nuclear and Mitochondrial Genomes of the Lined Rockskipper, Istiblennius lineatus (Teleostei: Blenniidae)
Authors: Víctor M Valencia-Cárdenas, Nikita Kulikov, Raúl Contreras-Martín, Francisco J Ruiz-Ruano, Alexander Ben Hamadou, Carola Greve, Sanaz Estekani, Diego F B Vaz, Terry J Ord, Iker Irisarri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe generated nuclear and mitochondrial chromosome-scale genome assemblies for Istiblennius linneatus (Valenciennes, 1836), an amphibious fish of the family Blenniidae. The assemblies were produced from Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) High Fidelity (HiFi) long-read data and Arima high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) technology. The nuclear genome assembly is 643.23 Mb in size, with a scaffold N50 of 24 Mb, a contig N50 of 6.39 Mb, and a GC content of 43.34%. We reconstructed the 24 pseudochromosomes expected from karyotype studies, which account for 86.95% of the genome assembly. After manual assembly curation, the obtained genome assembly is highly complete (97.3% BUSCO completeness) and free of haplotypic duplication and contaminations. The genome was annotated using direct RNA-seq and protein homology evidence, obtaining 27,357 gene models (95.4% BUSCO completeness). Genome size and macro-synteny is overall conserved with other available combtooth blenny genomes. The mitochondrial genome is 16.5 kb in length and has the gene content and order typical of the family and of most vertebrates. These new genomic resources can provide new insights into the ecology and evolutionary history of Istiblennius.

The lined rockskipper, Istiblennenius linneatus, is an amphibious fish of the family Blenniidae distributed throughout the Indo-Pacific intertidal coastal shores. Taxonomic delimitation of this species is tenuous; it was previously grouped in a species complex with two other cryptic congeners. To clarify their interrelationships and species boundaries, we here generate the nuclear and mitochondrial chromosome-scale genome assemblies for this species.

Introduction

The lined rockskipper Istiblennius lineatus (Valenciennes, 1836) is a tropical species of the family Blenniidae with a wide geographic distribution throughout the Indo-Pacific region, including the Laccadive Islands, the Maldives, and Mangareva, and the western central Pacific region in Guam (Kami et al. 1968), reaching Toyohama (Japan) in the north and the Makran Sea (Iran) (Esmaeili and Mehraban 2017). It typically occupies intertidal areas of rocky coastlines and reef flats (Lieske and Myers 1994), including rock pools, harbors, and mangrove habitats (Kuiter and Tonozuka 2001). I. lineatus is considered to be an amphibious species that can temporarily leave water (Stanaland 1991; Ord and Cooke 2016) to graze on algae growing on the substrate. It has the characteristic comb-like dentition of the family, accompanied by enlarged canines that assist in scraping algal films from rocky substrates.

There remains substantial taxonomic uncertainty over the species boundaries and interrelationships within the genus Istiblennius. In fact, Springer and Williams (1994) determined that the Lineatus species complex is formed by three allopatric species (I. lineatus, I. pox, and I. steindarchneri) grouped by dark or dusky striping across their bodies in both sexes. However, distinction among these species is tenuous, with I. lineatus lacking a spotted occipital crest in mature males. Meristic characters listed by Springer and Williams (1994) have large overlapping, with the only observable difference being that I. pox usually has lower modes of dorsal fin and vertebral counts. In addition to the lack of clear distinction among species, phylogenetic analyses of cytochrome oxidase I barcodes did not recover the reciprocal monophyly of I. lineatus and I. pox (Mehraban et al. 2021). New genomic data could help clarify this apparent taxonomic conundrum that cannot be solved based on the few distinguishing morphological characters (Springer and Williams 1994). Likewise, a new reference genome would provide a valuable resource for exploring adaptation, ecology, and evolutionary history of Istiblennius and the broader family Blenniidae. It also contributes toward a better representation of genomic data for the family in global databases, which has thus far remained limited.

We used long-read sequencing (PacBio HiFi) and Arima Hi-C scaffolding technology with gene expression data (RNA-seq) to generate an annotated chromosome-scale nuclear and mitochondrial genomes of I. lineatus. Overall, the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes conform to the expected size and organization known from other combtooth blenny fish.

Results and Discussion

Nuclear Genome

The total length of the assembled nuclear genome is 643 Mb, with a contig N50 of 6.39Mb and a scaffold N50 of 24 Mb (Table 1, Fig. 1a). Using Hi-C data, most contigs were scaffolded into 24 pseudochromosomes (Fig. 1f and g), as expected based on the Istiblennius lineatus karyotype (Arai and Shiotsuki 1974). These pseudochromosomes range in size from 11.74 to 35.55 Mb (Fig. 1a) and collectively represent 86.95% of the total assembly. Chromosome one contained a telomere at one of its extremes, whereas all other telomeric regions were found in shorter scaffolds that could not be assigned to pseudochromosomes. In spite of this, the Hi-C contact map after manual curation indicates that the assembly is close to chromosome-level resolution, with all 24 pseudochromosomes being correctly oriented.

Based on k-mers, GenomeScope estimated a genome size of 548.27 Mb and a moderate heterozygosity level of 0.693%. Still, the Merqury assembly spectrum plot indicates that haplotypes were effectively collapsed (Fig. 1b and c). Merqury also estimated a QV of 63.35, corresponding to a 99.9% per-base accuracy. The spectra-copy number plot (Fig. 1c) shows a single main peak, suggesting that most k-mers from the sequencing reads are present only once in the assembly, which indicates low redundancy and overall good assembly quality. BUSCO analysis showed high completeness (97.3%) based on the actinopterygii_odb10 dataset, with 3,484 single-copy, 57 duplicated, and 28 fragmented BUSCOs (Fig. 1a). The genome was also inspected for potential contaminant sequences using BlobTools and BLASTn, removing a total of three contigs (Fig. 1d).

Genome size, chromosome number, assembly contiguity, and BUSCO completeness of the I. lineatus genome are comparable to those of other available combtooth blenny genomes (Table 1). We also evaluated the Hi-C scaffolding step by performing a synteny analysis with three other available combtooth blenny genomes. This analysis revealed the pseudochromosome 1 in O. macclurei is formed by the fusion of two pseudochromosomes (13 + 24 in I. lineatus and S. pavo, and nc043760.1 + nw021941230.1 in S. fasciatus) (Fig. 1e). This is further supported by karyotype data, which identified 23 chromosomes in the closely related species Ophioblennius trinitatis (Galvão et al. 2011). For the rest, macro-synteny is largely conserved across all 24 pseudochromosomes, together with several small-scale inversions and translocations among the four species, which is expected given the phylogenetic divergence among them (Hundt et al. 2014; Muñoz-Sánchez et al. 2026).

Using RepeatMasker, 19.59% of the genome was identified as repetitive, including SINE (0.13%), LTR (1.45%), and LINE (1.98%) retroelements as well as DNA transposons (3.25%) (Table 1). The proportion of repetitive elements is intermediate between Ophioblennius macclurei (14.98%; 529.5 Mb genome size; Kulikov et al. 2026) and Salaria pavo (26.87%; 735.9 Mb genome size; Cardoso et al. 2024). The relative frequency of type I and II transposons varies substantially across genomes (Table 1), suggesting varied transposition bursts in the family Blenniidae.

Direct RNA-seq data and protein sequences from actinopterygian fish (see Materials and Methods) were used to perform the structural annotation of the assembly, which resulted in 27,357 protein models (Table 1). A BUSCO completeness analysis (‘actinopterygii_odb10') identified 3,473 (95.4%) complete gene models, of which 2,884 were single-copy and 589 duplicated, in addition to 21 fragmented BUSCOs. Regarding the number of annotated gene models, the genome of I. lineatus is intermediate between those of O. macclurei (∼44% more annotated proteins) and S. fasciatus (∼31% fewer proteins). BUSCO results suggest that the genome of S. fasciatus might contain some redundancy at the sequence level (6.4% duplicated BUSCOs vs. 1.6% in I. lineatus) as well as in the annotated gene models (30.0% duplicated BUSCOs vs. 16.2% in I. lineatus), which can probably be explained by the use of different structural annotation pipelines.

Mitochondrial Genome

Assembly and annotation of the mitochondrial genome were performed with MitoHiFi (Uliano-Silva et al. 2023), resulting in a circular molecule of 16.5 kb with a GC content of 45.79%. The genome exhibits gene composition and organization typical of vertebrates, including 13 protein-coding genes, 22 transfer RNA genes, 2 Ribosomal RNA genes, and a non-coding D-loop region, consistent with other combtooth blenny genomes (Kulikov et al. 2026).

Conclusion

We assembled and annotated high-quality reference nuclear and mitochondrial genomes for the lined rockskipper using HiFi long-reads, Arima Hi-C scaffolding technology, and RNA-seq data. These genomes represent key resources for better understanding the ecology, behavior, and evolution of this group of cryptobenthic fish. Though comparative data from other species of the family Blenniidae is still scarce, a comparison with three closely-related combtooth blenny nuclear genomes supports the high quality of our new assembly and provides the first hints about the dynamics of genome evolution in the fish family Blenniidae.

Materials and Methods

DNA and RNA Extraction and Sequencing

A single individual of I. lineatus (Guam-0069; voucher MNCN-ADN 205431) was collected in Guam in 2023 (coordinates: 13.4083096, 144.7834715) under the DAWR collection and export permit SCR-23-006). The specimens were euthanized using clove oil solution in seawater, dissected, and tissues were appropriately stored for DNA and RNA extraction, in 96% ethanol and RNAlater (Thermo-Fisher Cat. #AM7020), respectively. This procedure was approved by the University of Guam Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee UOG-IACUC permit #UGO23-01 and the University of New South Wales Animal Care and Ethics Committee permit #4523025/1.

DNA was extracted from muscle tissue using the New England Biolabs High Molecular Weight DNA extraction kit (Cat. #T3050L) following the manufacturer's instructions. Genomic DNA was sequenced on a Pacific Biosciences’ Revio 25 M SMRT cell (HiFi protocol) by the Uppsala Genome Centre, NGI-SciLifeLab (Uppsala, Sweden). Chromatin conformation data was generated from frozen, ethanol-preserved muscle tissue using the Arima High Coverage HiC kit v2 (Arima Genomics Cat. # A410110) and Arima Library Preparation Modules (Cat. #A311035, #A311036, #A311041, #A311042), following the manufacturer's instructions. The resulting library was sequenced at Novogene using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus technology (2 × 150 bp reads). For RNA-seq, we extracted RNA from brain, eye, heart + liver, and skin using the New England Biolabs Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Cat. #T2010S) and sequenced by Novogene using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform 2 × 150 bp reads.

Nuclear Genome Assembly

FastQC v.0.11.9 (Andrews 2011) was used for assessing the quality of raw sequencing reads. We inferred the expected genome size and heterozygosity rate using GenomeScope v.2.1.0 (Ranallo-Benavidez et al. 2020) with k = 31 and a maximum coverage threshold max_kmercov = 10,000,000. Based on the final assembly size, HiFi reads provided 66.5× and Hi-C reads provided 36.2× sequencing depth. We assembled the genome following the pipeline outlined by the Vertebrate Genomes Project (Lariviere et al. 2026) using the Galaxy platform (Hiltemann et al. 2023). Briefly, this pipeline performs the initial assembly using Hifiasm v.0.25.0 based on HiFi high-fidelity long reads and Hi-C contact information to separate haplotypes and improve the structural consistency of contigs. General assembly contiguity statistics are calculated using gfastats v.1.3.11 (Formenti et al. 2022). Contigs are then scaffolded using chromatin conformation information (Hi-C) data, which are first mapped against the assembled genome using BWA-MEM2 v.2.2.1 (Vasimuddin et al. 2019), and YaHS v.1.2 (Zhou et al. 2023) is used to perform the scaffolding step. A first Hi-C contact map was generated using PretextMap v.0.1.9 (https://github.com/sanger-tol/PretextMap) and Pretext Snapshot v.0.0.5 (https://github.com/sanger-tol/PretextSnapshot).

We detected potential contaminants using BlobToolKit v.4.5.1 (Challis et al. 2020), which categorizes contigs based on sequencing depth, GC content, and taxonomic classification. For this, we used DIAMOND v.2.1.22 (Buchfink et al. 2015) for sequence similarity searches against the NCBI nr database (at the protein level), as implemented in Galaxy, following the ERGA post-assembly QC tutorial (Recktenwald et al. 2026). Forty scaffolds (50, 155, 193, 196, 206, 224, 254, 258, 317, 348, 352, 367, 398, 424, 448, 510, 530, 540, 565, 575, 598, 599, 604, 623, 633, 637, 640, 641, 644, 646, 649, 651, 666, 669, 670, 671, 672, 692, 696, 699) were flagged as potential contaminants. These were further investigated using BLASTn v.2.16.0 (Camacho et al. 2009) against the NCBI nt database, enabling more sensitive searches at the nucleotide level. Scaffolds 398 and 666 were confirmed as contaminants and removed. Scaffold 554, corresponding to the mitochondrial genome, was identified using BLASTn and excluded from the nuclear assembly. A final assembly quality check was performed using Merqury v.1.3 (Rhie et al. 2020) with k = 31.

We performed manual curation of the assembly following the Sanger-ToL pipeline (Pointon 2025; https://github.com/sanger-tol/curationpretext). Briefly, the initial Hi-C contact map was visualized with PretextView, and Hi-C contact interactions were used to order by size and orient scaffolds and correct likely misjoins during the YAHS scaffolding step, and off-diagonal signals were used to assign unplaced scaffolds. For example, the longest scaffold produced by YAHS was split into two based on the lack of continuous interaction of Hi-C data across the join. The final curated assembly included 24 longest scaffolds that were assigned to pseudochromosomes, showing strong and continuous intra-chromosomal interaction signal.

Candidate telomeric sequences were identified using seqtk-telo from the seqtk toolkit v1.5-r133 (Li 2026) with default settings and the vertebrate telomeric repeat motif (TTAGGG) as seed. A total of 24 telomeric repeat signals were identified in the assembly, one was located at one of the extremes of pseudochromosome 1 and the remaining 23 were found in unplaced scaffolds (31, 55, 65, 70, 86, 90, 98, 140, 148, 150, 164, 173, 190, 195, 210, 215, 237, 292, 298, 391, 393, 557, 563). The final genome assembly completeness was assessed with BUSCO v.4.0.6 (Manni et al. 2021) with default parameters and the “actinopterygii_odb10” database. The completeness of the genome assembly and annotation of S. pavo was also re-assessed using BUSCO. Genome assembly metrics were visualized in a snail plot generated with BlobToolKit v.4.4.5.

Nuclear Genome Annotation

Structural annotation followed a Snakemake pipeline originally used for fish genome annotation (Oriowo et al. 2025; https://github.com/YanisChrys/phoxinus_annotation). Briefly, repetitive elements were identified de novo with RepeatModeler v.2.0.1 and repetitive DNA was masked with RepeatMasker version v.4.1.0 (Smit et al. 2013) using the repeat library previously identified via RepeatModeler and using RMblastn v.2.10.0 (Flynn et al. 2020) as a search engine. The masked genome assembly was used for structural annotation of proteins using direct RNA-seq evidence and homology to known fish proteins using BRAKER v.3.0.7 (Gabriel et al. 2024). RNA-seq alignment maps were generated by mapping the newly generated reads onto the masked genome using STAR v.2.7.10 (Dobin et al. 2013). The protein database consisted of annotated proteins from 13 fish species: Acanthochromis polyacanthus (NCBI Acc. No. GCF_021347895.1), Cyprinodon variegatus (GCF_000732505.1), Danio rerio (GCF_000002035.6), Gouania willdenowi (GCF_900634775.1), Lepisosteus oculatus (GCF_000242695.1), Mugil cephalus (GCF_022458985.1), Oreochromis niloticus (GCF_001858045.2), Oryzias latipes (GCF_000313675.1), Salarias fasciatus (GCF_902148845.1), Stegastes partitus (GCF_000690725.1), and Xiphophorus maculatus (GCF_002775205.1) downloaded from NCBI, Salaria pavo downloaded from Figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.26854312), and Ophioblennius macclurei downloaded from ENA (Kulikov et al. 2026; PRJEB95139).

Chromosome-Scale Collinearity

We performed a synteny analysis with GENESPACE (Lovell et al. 2022) to assess the quality of the genome scaffolding. This analysis included the new I. lineatus genome and its annotated proteins, as well as annotated genomes from: O. macclurei (Kulikov et al. 2026; GCA_966212585.1), S. pavo (Cardoso et al. 2024; GCA_043647535.1), and S. fasciatus (GCF_902148845.1).

Mitochondrial Genome

Using raw PacBio HiFi reads, the mitochondrial genome was assembled and annotated with MitoHiFi v. 3.2.3 (Uliano-Silva et al. 2023). The pipeline was run using the Salariopsis fluviatilis (NCBI Acc. No. PP928772.1) as a seed reference, and the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code for the annotation. The assembled mitochondrial genome was used as a query in BLASTn searches against the nuclear genome assembly to identify and remove the corresponding scaffold from the nuclear assembly.
