A dichotomy of finite element spaces and its application to an energy-conservative scheme for the regularized long wave equation
Dimitrios Antonopoulos, Dimitrios Mitsotakis

Abstract
. Certain energy-conservative Galerkin discretizations for nonlinear dispersive wave equations have revealed an unusual convergence behavior: optimal convergence is attained when continuous Lagrange finite element spaces of odd polynomial degree are employed, whereas the use of even-degree polynomials leads to reduced accuracy. The present work demonstrates that this behavior is intrinsic to the structure of the finite element spaces themselves. In particular, it is shown to be closely connected to the standard L 2 L^{2} -projection of derivatives, which possesses a super-approximation property exclusively for odd polynomial degrees. We also examine the implications of this feature for an energy-conservative Galerkin approximation of the regularized long-wave equation where the energy is a cubic functional. Although the resulting scheme conserves both mass and energy, we further show that the impulse is approximated with high accuracy, and we establish a priori error bounds for the associated semi-discrete formulation.

1.
Introduction

This work primarily establishes a property of one-dimensional continuous Lagrange finite element spaces that exhibits a fundamental distinction between odd- and even-degree piecewise polynomials. Let
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
denote the standard Lagrange finite element space of continuous, periodic, piecewise
k
-degree polynomial functions over a grid with uniform mesh length
h
. For a sufficiently smooth function
u
, the stability of the
L^{2}
-projection together with its standard error estimate in the
L^{2}
-norm implies that

(1)

\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|\leq\|(Pu-u)_{x}\|\leq Ch^{k}\ ,

for some constant
C>0
independent of
h
, where
P:L^{2}\to\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
is the usual
L^{2}
-projection onto the finite element space.
However, this bound is not optimal. In this work, we prove that if
u
is a periodic function in
C^{k+2}
for
k=2\kappa+1
with
\kappa=0,1,2,\dots
, then
\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|=O(h^{k+1})
. In addition, when
k=1
and
u
is periodic in
C^{5}
, the sharper estimate
\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|=O(h^{4})
holds. By contrast, for even degrees
k=2\kappa
with
\kappa=1,2,\dots
, the estimate (
1
) remains optimal, and no further improvement can be achieved.
The analysis presented in this work is carried out under periodic boundary conditions, although the main results remain valid under other boundary conditions including Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The super-approximation property described above is not restricted to the one-dimensional setting. The same theoretical arguments extend naturally to two-dimensional tensor-product Lagrange finite element spaces defined over rectangular grids, where an analogous distinction between odd and even polynomial degrees is expected. However, numerical experiments indicate that this behavior may not persist on general triangulations. In particular,
[
4
]
reports that for a conservative finite element method applied to two-dimensional Boussinesq systems similar to the one presented here, the convergence rate remains optimal and independent of the polynomial degree of the finite element space.

This property explains the observations of
[
26
]
, where a clear distinction between odd- and even-degree Lagrange finite element spaces arose in the numerical treatment of nonlinear dispersive systems. That work introduced a conservative method preserving a cubic energy functional, and optimal convergence was observed only for odd-degree spaces. Since the same mechanism operates in cubic energy-conserving schemes, we demonstrate its relevance by presenting an energy-conserving finite element method for the regularized long wave (RLW) equation, also known as the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation, which serves as a fundamental model in this class of problems.

The RLW equation was originally derived for the study of long surface water waves of small amplitude over a flat bottom, as an approximation of the Euler equations in water wave theory and as a regularization of the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation
[
6
,
31
,
25
,
12
]
. In dimensionless but unscaled variables, the RLW equation can be written in the form

(2)

u_{t}+u_{x}+uu_{x}-u_{xxt}=0\ .

where
u=u(x,t)
denotes the free surface elevation of the water from its rest position, while
x
and
t
are the spatial and temporal independent variables.

Ideally, a water wave equation should not only possess well-posedness properties, asymptotic justification, and the existence of classical solitary waves, but also be applicable to practical problems and satisfy fundamental conservation laws such as mass and energy. For flat-bottom topography in a non-dissipative continuous medium, it is common to also consider impulse (momentum) conservation. Impulse is typically represented by a quadratic functional, which remains invariant when the physical system is translation-invariant in the
x
-direction
[
7
]
. The RLW equation is known to admit only three independent and non-trivial conservation laws, the mass, impulse and energy conservation laws
[
30
]
.

Multi-symplecticity of some nonlinear and dispersive wave equations is another powerful structure directly related to these conserved quantities
[
17
,
18
]
. The RLW equation is also equipped with a multi-symplectic structure
[
15
,
34
,
14
,
13
]
. Due to the uniqueness of the three fundamental conservation laws of the RLW equation, its multi-symplectic conservation law happens to be equivalent to impulse conservation. However, since the impulse functional is no longer preserved when the bottom topography is uneven
[
22
]
, energy and mass conservation remain the only reliable measures that can serve as high-accuracy indicators in long-term numerical simulations
[
21
,
4
,
1
,
23
]
.

In this work, we introduce a modified finite element method that preserves both a cubic energy functional and mass. When odd-degree polynomials are employed, the numerical solutions converge with the optimal order
O(h^{k+1})
, whereas for even-degree polynomials the convergence is suboptimal, of order
O(h^{k})
, reflecting the inherent dichotomy of the finite element spaces. Moreover, the approximation of the derivative
u_{x}
exhibits the same order of convergence as
u
, revealing a notable super-approximation property. Although the proposed modified Galerkin method does not preserve the impulse functional, it approximates it with an error of
O(h^{k+1})
. Other similar Galerkin finite element methods for the RLW equation are presented in
[
20
]
.

The structure of the manuscript is the following: In Section
2
we prove the approximation property of the
L^{2}
-projection that bisects the Lagrange finite element spaces. The demonstration of how this property affects the convergence of an energy-conservative Galerkin method to the RLW equation is demonstrated in Section
3
. The conclusions are presented in Section
4
.

2.
A dichotomy of finite element spaces

2.1.
Approximation spaces

For the purposes of the analysis, and for the sake of simplicity, we consider the interval
I=[0,1]
. For any
N>0
, let
h=1/N
and define the partition

\Delta=\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
of
[0,1]

where
0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}=1
and
x_{i}=ih
for each
i=0,1,\dots,N
.
This partition is periodically extended to a partition of
\mathbb{R}
by setting
x_{jN+s}=x_{s}
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
s=0,1,\dots,N-1
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
with
k\geq 1
. We define the Lagrange finite element space

\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}=\{\phi\in C_{p}~:~\phi|_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,2,\dots,N\}\ ,

where
C_{p}=\{f\in C(\mathbb{R})~:~f(x)=f(x+1)\quad\text{for all}~x\in\mathbb{R}\}
, and
\mathbb{P}_{k}
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
k
. In what follows,
C_{p}^{k}
is the set of functions
u
in
C_{p}
such that the
j
-derivative
u^{(j)}
is also in
C_{p}
for all
j=1,\dots,k
.

In this work, we employ the Sobolev space
H^{s}_{p}
, consisting of
1
-periodic functions in
H^{s}
, equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{s}
evaluated over one period. We also use the space
L^{2}(0,1)=H^{0}(0,1)
with the standard
L^{2}
-norm induced by the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
. In addition,
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
will denote the usual
L^{\infty}
-norm.

Our proof relies on a direct-sum decomposition
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}=\mathcal{S}_{h}^{1}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
, following closely the construction in
[
16
]
. We define

\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}=\{\phi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}~:~\phi(x_{i})=0\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,2,\dots,N\}\ ,

and set
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{1}=(\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2})^{\perp}
, the
L^{2}
-orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
.
Note that when
k=1
we have
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}=\{0\}
. Let
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
denote the corresponding
L^{2}
-projections onto
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
, respectively. The associated
L^{2}
-projection on
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
is then given by
P=\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}
.

The projection
\pi_{2}
is defined locally on each interval
I_{i}=(x_{i-1},x_{i})
,
i=1,2,\dots,N
, by requiring that for any
v\in L^{2}
,

(\pi_{2}v,q)_{I_{i}}=(v,q)_{I_{i}}\quad\text{for all}\quad q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}^{i,0}=\{q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}~:~q(x_{i-1})=q(x_{i})=0\}\ ,

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{I_{i}}
denotes the usual inner product on
L^{2}(I_{i})
.

Any
p\in\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
restricted in
I_{i}
can be written in the form
p(x)=(x-x_{i})(x-x_{i-1})s(x)
, where
s\in\mathbb{P}_{k-2}(I_{i})
. The space
\mathbb{P}_{k-2}(I_{i})
has dimension
k-1
, and because the interval
I
consists of
N
sub-intervals, we conclude that the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
has dimension
(k-1)N
.
A basis of
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
may be constructed using the functions
\phi_{j}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\phi_{j}(x)=x(1-x)b_{k-2,\,j-1}(x),\quad j=1,2,\dots,k-1\ ,

where

b_{k-2,\,j-1}(x)=\binom{k-2}{j-1}x^{j-1}(1-x)^{k-1-j},\quad j=1,2,\dots,k-1\ ,

are the Bernstein polynomials of degree
k-2
. These polynomials are linearly independent and span the space
\mathbb{P}_{k-2}(0,1)
.

The dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{1}
is
\dim\mathcal{S}_{h}^{1}=\dim\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}-\dim\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}=N
, and each function in this space is determined by its values at the nodes
x_{i}
. To construct a basis for this space, we make use of the function

\psi(x)=\frac{1}{k!}\frac{1}{x(1-x)}\frac{d^{k-1}}{dx^{k-1}}\left[x^{k+1}(1-x)^{k}\right],\quad x\in[0,1]\ ,

which satisfies
\psi(0)=0
,
\psi(1)=1
, and

(3)

\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)p(x)~dx=0\quad\text{for all}\quad p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}^{0}=\{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}~:~p(0)=p(1)=0\}\ .

The construction of
\psi
relies on the following characterization theorem for orthogonal polynomials; see
[
32
, Th. 12.5, p. 141]
:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
w:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. A function
v\in C[a,b]
satisfies the orthogonality conditions

\int_{a}^{b}w(x)v(x)p(x)~dx=0,\quad p\in\mathbb{P}_{m}\ ,

if and only if there exists a function
f\in C^{m+1}(a,b)
such that

w(x)v(x)=f^{(m+1)}(x),\quad a\leq x\leq b\ ,

with

f^{(j)}(a)=f^{(j)}(b)=0,\quad j=1,2,\dots,m\ .

In addition, we will need the following properties of
\psi
:

Lemma 2.1
.

If
k=2\kappa+1
,
\kappa=0,1,\dots
, then

(i)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{2}(x)~dx=\frac{1}{k(k+2)},

(ii)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)\psi(1-x)~dx=\frac{1}{k(k+1)(k+2)},

(iii)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{j}\psi(x)~dx=\frac{1}{k(k+2)},\hskip 8.50012ptj=1,2,\dots,k,

(iv)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)~dx=\frac{1}{k(k+1)},

(v)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)^{j}\psi(x)~dx=\frac{1}{k(k+1)(k+2)},\hskip 8.50012ptj=1,2,\dots,k,

(vi)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(0)=\frac{k+1}{2},\hskip 8.50012pt\psi^{\prime}(1)=\frac{k^{2}+2k-1}{2},

(vii)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(x)\psi(1-x)~dx=\frac{1}{k+1}.

Proof.

The identities (
i
), (
ii
) are established in
[
16
]
, as well as (
iii
) for
j=1
. Equation (
iii
) can be extended for
j=2,3,\dots,k
by induction, using the relation
x^{j}=x^{j-1}(x-1)+x^{j-1}
together with (
3
).

Since the function
\psi(x)+\psi(1-x)-1
belongs to
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{0}
, it follows from (
3
) that

\int_{0}^{1}[\psi(x)^{2}+\psi(x)\psi(1-x)-\psi(x)]~dx=\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)(\psi(x)+\psi(1-x)-1)~dx=0\ ,

and, combined with (
3
) and (
ii
), this yields (
iv
).
Using the identity
(1-x)^{j}=(1-x)^{j-1}-(1-x)^{j-1}x
, together with (
3
), equation (
v
) can be established by induction.

From the definition of
\psi
, and using the Leibniz rule for the
(k-1)
-st derivative of a product, we obtain

\psi(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{{k-1}\choose{j}}\frac{(k+1)!(-1)^{j}}{(k+1-j)!(j+1)!}x^{k-j}(1-x)^{j}\ ,

which, for
k=1
gives
\psi(x)=x
, and for odd

, yields

\psi(x)=x^{k}-\frac{(k-1)(k+1)}{2}x^{k-1}(1-x)+s(x)+\frac{k+1}{2}x(1-x)^{k-1}\ ,

where
s\in\mathbb{P}_{k}^{0}
satisfies
s^{\prime}(0)=s^{\prime}(1)=0
. Thus,

\psi^{\prime}(x)=kx^{k-1}+\frac{(k-1)(k+1)}{2}x^{k-1}+\widetilde{s}(x)+\frac{k+1}{2}(1-x)^{k-1}\ ,

with
\widetilde{s}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}^{0}
, and therefore (
vi
) follows.

The function
s:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,
s(x)=\psi^{\prime}(x)-x\psi^{\prime}(1)-(1-x)\psi^{\prime}(0)
belongs to
\mathbb{P}_{k-1}^{0}
, and thus

\int_{0}^{1}s(x)\psi(1-x)~dx=0\ ,

which yields

\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(x)\psi(1-x)~dx=\psi^{\prime}(1)\int_{0}^{1}x\psi(1-x)~dx+\psi^{\prime}(0)\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)\psi(1-x)~dx\ .

Finally, Equation (
vii
) follows from the right-hand side of the above identity together with (
iii
), (
v
) and (
vi
).

Remark 2.1
.

For any even integer

, the right-hand side of (

) is
-1/(k(k+1)(k+2))
, and the right-hand side of (

We consider the functions

where, for fixed

Alternatively, such a function can be written in the form

where for

It is straightforward to verify that

, where

and

.
Finally, for

The functions

2.2.
Estimation of the projections
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}

Let
k=2\kappa+1
,
\kappa=0,1,\dots
an odd integer and

be a 1-periodic function. From the previous analysis, we write
(\pi_{1}v)(x)=d_{1}\ell_{1}(x)+d_{2}\ell_{2}(x)+\cdots+d_{N}\ell_{N}(x)

\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{N})^{T}

From (

\mathbf{A}=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}{\rm Circulant}(2k+2,1,0,\dots,0,1)\ ,

where

Thus, there exist constants

and

, depending on

\sigma_{1}h|\boldsymbol{\beta}|^{2}\leq\langle\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}\rangle\leq\sigma_{2}h|\boldsymbol{\beta}|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\ ,

where

Indeed, from

,

Furthermore, we can express

\mathbf{A}=\frac{2h}{k(k+2)}\left(\mathbf{I}_{N}+\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\right)\ ,

.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

,
\kappa=0,1,\dots

be a 1-periodic function, and let

and

be positive constants independent of

\text{if}\quad\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|(v,\ell_{i})|\leq C_{1}h^{\alpha}\quad\text{for some}\quad\alpha>0,\quad\text{then}\quad\|\pi_{1}v\|\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}h^{\alpha-1}\ ,

and

(8)

\text{if}\quad\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
1\leq m\leq k-1\end{subarray}}|(v,q_{i,m})|\leq C_{2}h^{\beta}\quad\text{for some}\quad\beta>0,\quad\text{then}\quad\|\pi_{2}v\|\leq\widetilde{C}_{2}h^{\beta-1}\ ,

for some positive constants
\widetilde{C}_{1}
and
\widetilde{C}_{2}

Proof.

We write
\pi_{1}v=d_{0}\ell_{0}+\cdots+d_{N}\ell_{N}
. Then,

(9)

\|\pi_{1}v\|^{2}=(v,\pi_{1}v)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v,\ell_{i})d_{i}\ .

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with the hypothesis in (

\|\pi_{1}v\|^{2}\leq C_{1}h^{\alpha}N^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=C_{1}h^{\alpha-1}\sqrt{h}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\ .

\widetilde{C}_{1}=C_{1}/\sigma_{1}

\|\pi_{1}v\|^{2}\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}h^{\alpha-1}\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{d},\mathbf{d}\rangle}=\widetilde{C}_{1}h^{\alpha-1}\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{d}\rangle}=\widetilde{C}_{1}h^{\alpha-1}\|\pi_{1}v\|\ ,

Similarly, if
\pi_{2}v=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}c_{i,m}q_{i,m}

(v,\pi_{2}v)=\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v,q_{i,m})c_{i,m}\ .

Because

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v,q_{i,m})c_{i,m}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}|(v,q_{i,m})|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|c_{i,m}|^{2}\leq C_{2}^{2}h^{2\beta-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|c_{i,m}|^{2}\ ,

we obtain

\|\pi_{2}v\|^{2}\leq C_{2}h^{\beta-1/2}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|c_{i,m}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=C_{2}h^{\beta-1}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\sqrt{h}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|c_{i,m}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\ .

The estimate in (
8
) then follows from this inequality with the help of (
6
).
∎

We now proceed with an error estimate for
\pi_{1}v
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
k=2\kappa+1
,
\kappa=0,1,\dots
and let
v\in C_{p}^{k+1}
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

(10)

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|(\pi_{1}v-v)(x_{i})|\leq Ch^{k+1}\|v^{(k+1)}\|_{\infty}\ .

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
has entries
\gamma_{i}=(\pi_{1}v)(x_{i})-v(x_{i})
for
i=1,2,\dots,N
. Then the entries of the product
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
are

(11)

(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\gamma})_{i}=(v,\ell_{i})-\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}[(2k+2)v_{i}+v_{i+1}+v_{i-1}],\quad 1\leq i\leq N\ ,

where
v_{i}=v(x_{i})
, for
i=1,2,\dots,N
, and
v_{N}=v_{0}
and
v_{N+1}=v_{1}
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(v,\ell_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}}v(x)\ell_{i}(x)~dx+\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}v(x)\ell_{i}(x)~dx

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}}v(x)\psi\left(\frac{x-x_{i-1}}{h}\right)~dx+\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}v(x)\psi\left(\frac{x_{i+1}-x}{h}\right)~dx

\displaystyle=h\int_{0}^{1}\left[v(x_{i}-h(1-x))+v(x_{i}+h(1-x))\right]\psi(x)~dx\ .

If we define
w_{i}(x)=v(x_{i}-h(1-x))+v(x_{i}+h(1-x))
and we denote
v_{i}^{(j)}
the
j
-th derivative of
v
at
x_{i}
, then using Taylor expansions of
v
around
x_{i}
we obtain

w_{i}(x)=2v_{i}+\frac{2h^{2}}{2!}(1-x)^{2}v_{i}^{\prime\prime}+\cdots+\frac{2h^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}(1-x)^{k-1}v_{i}^{(k-1)}+h^{k+1}r_{i,k}(x;h)\ ,

where the residual
r_{i,k}(x;h)
satisfies
\max_{0\leq x\leq 1}|r_{i,k}(x;h)|\leq C_{k}\|v^{(k+1)}\|_{\infty}
, for some positive constant
C_{k}
depending on
k
but independent of
h
. The dependence of
r_{i,k}
on
h
is due to the presence of
h
inside the argument of
v^{(k+1)}
.

Therefore,

(12)

\displaystyle(v,\ell_{i})

\displaystyle=h\int_{0}^{1}\left[2v_{i}+\frac{2h^{2}}{2!}v_{i}^{\prime\prime}(1-x)^{2}+\cdots+\frac{2h^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}v_{i}^{(k-1)}(1-x)^{k-1}\right]\psi(x)~dx

\displaystyle+h^{k+2}\int_{0}^{1}r_{i,k}(x;h)\psi(x)~dx\ .

Using the properties (
v
) and (
iv
) of Lemma
2.1
for
\psi
, we then have

(13)

(v,\ell_{i})=h\left[\frac{2}{k(k+1)}v_{i}+\frac{1}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\left(\frac{2h^{2}}{2!}v_{i}^{\prime\prime}+\cdots+\frac{2h^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}v_{i}^{(k-1)}\right)\right]+h^{k+2}\widetilde{r}_{i,k}\ ,

where
\widetilde{r}_{i,k}
denotes the last integral in (
12
).

Similarly, using Taylor expansions of
v_{i+1}=v(x_{i}+h)
and
v_{i-1}=v(x_{i}-h)
about
x_{i}
, we obtain

(14)

(2k+2)v_{i}+v_{i+1}+v_{i-1}=2(k+2)v_{i}+\frac{2h^{2}}{2!}v_{i}^{\prime\prime}+\cdots+\frac{2h^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}v_{i}^{(k-1)}+h^{k+1}\rho_{i,k}(h)\ ,

where
|\rho_{i,k}(h)|\leq\widetilde{C}_{k}\|v^{(k+1)}\|_{\infty}
with
\widetilde{C}_{k}>0
independent of
h
and
v
.

Multiplying (
14
) with
h/(k(k+1)(k+2))
and subtracting from (
13
), shows that for (
11
) there exists a constant
C
, independent of
h
, such that

|(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\gamma})_{i}|\leq Ch^{k+2}\|v^{(k+1)}\|_{\infty},\quad i=1,2,\dots,N\ .

Finally, the equivalence of (
5
) implies
|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|_{\infty}\leq Ch^{k+1}\|v^{(k+1)}\|_{\infty}
for
i=1,2,\dots,N
, which completes the proof of the estimate (
10
).
∎

In addition to the previous error estimate, for odd values of
k
, the projection
\pi_{1}
satisfies a super-approximation property.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
k=2\kappa+1
,
\kappa=0,1,\dots
and let
v\in C_{p}^{k+2}
. Then, there exists a positive constant
C
, independent of
h
, such that

(15)

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left|(\pi_{1}v-v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}\right|\leq Ch^{k+3}\|v^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty},\qquad I_{i}=(x_{i-1},x_{i})\ .

Proof.

Writing
\pi_{1}v=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}\ell_{i}
and defining
\beta_{i}=(\pi_{1}v-v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}
, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}}\left[d_{i-1}\ell_{i-1}(x)\ell_{i}(x)+d_{i}\ell_{i}^{2}(x)\right]~dx-(v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}

\displaystyle=h\ d_{i-1}\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)\psi(1-x)~dx+h\ d_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{2}(x)~dx-(v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}\ .

Using the properties (
i
) and (
ii
) of Lemma
2.1
for
\psi
, this simplifies to

\beta_{i}=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\left[d_{i-1}+(k+1)d_{i}\right]-(v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}\ .

Thus, the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
with entries
\beta_{i}
can be written as

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}+(k+1)\mathbf{d}\right]-\mathbf{c}\ ,

where
\mathbf{c}
is the vector with entries
(v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}
is the vector with
\widetilde{d}_{i}=d_{i-1}
.
Because
\mathbf{A}
is circulant and
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
with
b_{i}=(v,\ell_{i})
, we then have

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}+(k+1)\mathbf{b}\right]-\mathbf{c}\ ,

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}
has entries
\widetilde{b}_{i}=b_{i-1}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta})_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\left[(v,\ell_{i-1})+(k+1)(v,\ell_{i})-(2k+2)(v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}-(v,\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}-(v,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\left[(v,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}-(k+1)(v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}+(k+1)(v,\ell_{i})_{I_{i+1}}-(v,\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}[(v,(k+1)\ell_{i}-\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}-(v,(k+1)\ell_{i}-\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}]\ .

If we denote the terms in the brackets by
\gamma_{i}=(v,(k+1)\ell_{i}-\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}-(v,(k+1)\ell_{i}-\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}
, then

\gamma_{i}=h\left(\int_{0}^{1}v(x_{i}+hx)[(k+1)\psi(1-x)-\psi(x)]~dx-\int_{0}^{1}v(x_{i}-hx)[(k+1)\psi(1-x)-\psi(x)]~dx\right)\ .

Using Taylor expansions of
v(x_{i}\pm hx)
about
x_{i}
, we obtain

(16)

v(x_{i}+hx)-v(x_{i}-hx)=2hxv_{i}^{\prime}+\frac{2h^{3}}{3!}v_{i}^{(3)}x^{3}+\cdots+\frac{2h^{k}}{k!}v_{i}^{(k)}x^{k}+h^{k+1}R_{i,k}(x;h)\ ,

where
|R_{i,k}(x;h)|\leq Ch\|v^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}
for some positive constant
C
independent of
h
and
v
depending on
k
.

From the properties of
\psi
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}x^{j}\left[(k+1)\psi(1-x)-\psi(x)\right]~dx=0,\quad j=1,2,\dots,k\ ,

which implies

|\gamma_{i}|\leq C_{k}h^{k+3}\|v^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}\ .

Hence,

|(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta})_{i}|=\frac{h}{k(k+1)(k+2)}|\gamma_{i}|\leq\widetilde{C}_{k}h^{k+4}\|v^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}\ ,

where
C_{k}
,
\widetilde{C}_{k}
are positive constants that depend on
k
but are independent of
h
and
v
. By the equivalence (
5
), it follows that there exists a positive constant
C
, independent of
h
, such that
|\boldsymbol{\beta}|_{\infty}\leq Ch^{k+3}\|v^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}
, which establishes the estimate (
15
).
∎

2.3.
The dichotomy property

Now we are ready to prove the main results of this section.

Theorem 2.2
.

(i)

Let
k=2\kappa+1
,
\kappa=0,1,2,\dots
, and
u\in C_{p}^{k+2}
. Then there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
independent of
h
and
u
such that

(17)

\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|\leq C_{k}h^{k+1}\|u^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}\ .

(ii)

If
k=1
and
u\in C_{p}^{5}
, then there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
, independent of
h
and
u
, such that

(18)

\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|\leq C_{1}h^{4}\|u^{(5)}\|_{\infty}\ .

Proof.

First we prove (i). It’s worth noting that when
\kappa=0
, the proof becomes considerably simpler because
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
is reduced to just
\{0\}
, and the computations related to elements of
S_{h}^{2}
are no longer required. However, to avoid repetition, we present a single proof that includes
k=1
. Let
u\in C_{p}^{k+2}
and define the error
e=Pu-u
. Then, using the decomposition of
P
, we have

Pu=\pi_{1}u+\pi_{2}u=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}\ell_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}c_{i,m}q_{i,m}\ ,

and

P(e_{x})=\pi_{1}(e_{x})+\pi_{2}(e_{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\ell_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\gamma_{m,i}q_{m,i}\ .

From the definition of the
L^{2}
-projection, we have

\|P(e_{x})\|^{2}=(e_{x},P(e_{x}))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(e_{x},\ell_{i})\delta_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}(e_{x},q_{i,m})\gamma_{i,m}\ .

To establish (
17
), it suffices to show the existence of a positive constant
C
, independent of
h
and
u
, such that

|(e_{x},\ell_{i})|+|(e_{x},q_{i,m})|\leq Ch^{k+2}\|u^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq i\leq N,~1\leq m\leq k-1\ .

This is due to (
7
) and (
8
) of Lemma
2.2
.

To estimate
(e_{x},\ell_{i})
, we first decompose it using the projections
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
:

(19)

(e_{x},\ell_{i})=((\pi_{1}u)_{x},\ell_{i})+((\pi_{2}u)_{x}-u_{x},\ell_{i})\ .

For the first term, using property (
vii
) of Lemma
2.1
for
\psi
, we obtain

(20)

((\pi_{1}u)_{x},\ell_{i})=d_{i-1}(\ell_{i-1}^{\prime},\ell_{i})+d_{i+1}(\ell_{i+1}^{\prime},\ell_{i})=\frac{1}{k+1}(d_{i+1}-d_{i-1})\ .

For the second term, integration by parts gives

((\pi_{2}u)_{x}-u_{x},\ell_{i})=-(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})\ .

We now consider the function
p_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
defined by

p_{i}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x)-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{-})\ell_{i}(x)-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})\ell_{i-1}(x),&x\in I_{i}\ ,\\
\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x)-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{+})\ell_{i}(x)-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i+1}^{-})\ell_{i+1}(x),&x\in I_{i+1}\ ,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

where
I_{i}=(x_{i-1},x_{i})
.
Since
p_{i}
is continuous and vanishes at
x_{i}
,
p_{i}(x_{i})=0
, by definition of
\pi_{2}
, and the orthogonality of
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})_{I_{i}}+(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})_{I_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=(\pi_{2}u-u,p_{i})_{I_{i}}+\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{-})(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}+\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}

\displaystyle+(\pi_{2}u-u,p_{i})_{I_{i+1}}+\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{+})(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i+1}}+\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i+1}^{-})(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{-})(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})(u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{+})(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i+1}}-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i+1}^{-})(u,\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}\ .

This can be further simplified to

(21)

\displaystyle(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{h}\psi^{\prime}(1)(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}-\frac{1}{h}\psi^{\prime}(0)(u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}+\frac{1}{h}\psi^{\prime}(1)(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i+1}}+\frac{1}{h}\psi^{\prime}(0)(u,\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\psi^{\prime}(1)\left[(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i+1}}-(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}\right]+\frac{1}{h}\psi^{\prime}(0)\left[(u,\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}-(u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}\right]\ .

On the other hand,

(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i+1}}=\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}u(x)\psi\left(\frac{x_{i+1}-x}{h}\right)~dx=h\int_{0}^{1}u(x_{i}+hx)\psi(1-x)~dx\ ,

(u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}=\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}}u(x)\psi\left(\frac{x-x_{i-1}}{h}\right)~dx=h\int_{0}^{1}u(x_{i}-hx)\psi(1-x)~dx\ ,

and

(u,\ell_{i+1})_{I_{i+1}}=h\int_{0}^{1}u(x_{i}+hx)\psi(x)~dx,\quad(u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}=h\int_{0}^{1}u(x_{i}-hx)\psi(x)~dx\ .

Substituting these inner products into (
21
), leads to

(22)

(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}\left[u(x_{i}+hx)-u(x_{i}-hx)\right]\left[\psi^{\prime}(1)\psi(1-x)+\psi^{\prime}(0)\psi(x)\right]~dx\ .

Note that from the properties (
iii
), (
v
) and (
vi
) of Lemma
2.1
for
\psi
we have that

\int_{0}^{1}x^{j}\left[\psi^{\prime}(1)\psi(1-x)+\psi^{\prime}(0)\psi(x)\right]~dx=\frac{1}{k+1},\quad j=1,2,\dots,k\ .

Using Taylor expansions of
u(x_{i}+hx)
and
u(x_{i}-hx)
about
x_{i}
, we obtain

(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{k+1}\left(2hu_{i}^{\prime}+\frac{2h^{3}}{3!}u_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}+\cdots+\frac{2h^{k}}{k!}u_{i}^{(k)}\right)+h^{k+1}R_{i,k}(h)\ ,

where
|R_{i,k}(h)|\leq C_{k}h\|u^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}
, with
C_{k}>0
independent of
h
and
u
.

Similarly, using Taylor expansions for
u(x_{i}+h)
and
u(x_{i}-h)
, we have

(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{k+1}\left(u(x_{i}+h)-u(x_{i}-h)\right)+h^{k+1}\widetilde{R}_{i,k}(h)\ ,

where
|\widetilde{R}_{i,k}(h)|\leq\widetilde{C}_{k}h\|u^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}
for some constant
\widetilde{C}_{k}>0
independent of
h
and
u
.
Combining this with (
19
) and (
20
) gives

(23)

(e_{x},\ell_{i})=\frac{1}{k+1}\left((d_{i+1}-u_{i+1})-(d_{i-1}-u_{i-1})\right)-h^{k+1}\widetilde{R}_{i,k}(h)\ .

Applying Lemma
2.3
with
v=v(x)=u(x+h)-u(x-h)
and using (
23
), we then obtain

|(e_{x},\ell_{i})|\leq Ch^{k+2}\|u^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}\ .

Note that due to periodicity
(\pi_{1}v)(x)=(\pi_{1}u)(x+h)-(\pi_{1}u)(x-h)
.

For
(e_{x},q_{i,m})
, we have

(24)

(e_{x},q_{i,m})=-(e,q_{i,m}^{\prime})=-(\pi_{1}u+\pi_{2}u-u,q_{i,m}^{\prime})=-(\pi_{1}u,q_{i,m}^{\prime})-(\pi_{2}u-u,q_{i,m}^{\prime})\ .

It suffices to consider
m=1
and
m=k-1
, since each of the two inner products on the right-hand side of (
24
) vanishes for
m=2,3,\dots,k-2
and
k>3
(see Remark
2.2
). Note that if
k=3
, then
m=1
or
m=k-1=2
, and such cases are analyzed below.

Case
m=1
: Let

p_{i}(x)=q_{i,1}^{\prime}(x)-q_{i,1}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})\ell_{i-1}(x),\quad x\in I_{i}\ ,

so that
p_{i}(x_{i-1})=p_{i}(x_{i})=0
. Then

\displaystyle-(\pi_{1}u,q_{i,1}^{\prime})-(\pi_{2}u-u,q_{i,1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-q_{i,1}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})\left[(\pi_{1}u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}+(\pi_{2}u-u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=-q_{i,1}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})(\pi_{1}u-u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i}}\ ,

which implies

(e_{x},q_{i,1})=q_{i,1}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})(\pi_{1}u-u,\ell_{i-1})_{I_{i-1}}\ .

Using (
15
) of Lemma
2.4
and the fact that
q_{i,1}^{\prime}(x_{i-1}^{+})=1/h
, we obtain

|(e_{x},q_{i,1})|\leq C_{1}h^{k+2}\|u^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}\ ,

for some constant
C_{1}>0
, independent of
h
and
u
.

Case
m=k-1
: Similarly, we have

(e_{x},q_{i,k-1})=-q_{i,k-1}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{-})(\pi_{1}u-u,\ell_{i})_{I_{i}}\ .

Thus, from (
15
) of Lemma
2.4
and the fact that
q_{i,k-1}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{-})=-1/h
, we obtain

|(e_{x},q_{i,k-1})|\leq C_{2}h^{k+2}\|u^{(k+2)}\|_{\infty}\ ,

for some constant
C_{2}>0
, independent of
h
and
u
, and the proof of (i) is complete.

For the case (ii), recall that for
k=1
, we have
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}=\{0\}
. Let
e=Pu-u
. Then
Pu=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}\ell_{i}
, and
P(e_{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\ell_{i}
. Moreover,

\|P(e_{x})\|^{2}=(e_{x},P(e_{x}))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}\delta_{i}\ ,

where
\zeta_{i}=(e_{x},\ell_{i})
. In particular, similarly to (
20
),

\zeta_{i}=((Pu)_{x}-u_{x},\ell_{i})=\frac{1}{2}(d_{i+1}-d_{i-1})-(u_{x},\ell_{i})\ .

Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
denote the vector with entries
\zeta_{i}
. Then,

(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\zeta})_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(u,\ell_{i+1}-\ell_{i-1})-\frac{h}{6}(u_{x},4\ell_{i}+\ell_{i+1}+\ell_{i-1})=(\tfrac{1}{2}v-\tfrac{h}{6}w,\ell_{i})\ ,

where
v(x)=u(x+h)-u(x-h)
and
w(x)=4u_{x}(x)+u_{x}(x+h)+u_{x}(x-h)
.
Using Taylor expansions of
u
and
u_{x}
around
x
, we can write

v(x)=2hu_{x}(x)+\frac{h^{3}}{3}u^{(3)}(x)+h^{5}r(x;h),\qquad w(x)=6u_{x}(x)+h^{2}u^{(3)}(x)+h^{4}\widetilde{r}(x;h)\ ,

where
|r(x;h)|+|\widetilde{r}(x;h)|\leq c\|u^{(5)}\|_{\infty}
, for some constant
c>0
independent of
h
and
u
.
Consequently,

|(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\zeta})_{i}|\leq\tilde{c}h^{6}\|u^{(5)}\|_{\infty}\ ,

for some constant
\tilde{c}>0
, independent of
h
and
u
. The last estimate together with (
5
) and (
4
) then yields the desired estimate (
18
).
∎

Repeating the preceding construction for even values of
k
, one obtains that the estimate (
1
) cannot be improved.

Remark 2.3
.

The previous results hold exactly the same if one assumes that
v\in W_{p}^{r,\infty}
instead of
v\in C_{p}^{r}
.

2.4.
Experimental validation

We experimentally validate the Theorem
2.2
using the function
u(x)=\sin(2\pi x)
on the interval
[0,1]
. The interval was discretized into
N(m)
subintervals of length
h=1/N(m)
for
m=1,2,\dots,5
, and the error
E_{m}^{k}=\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|
was computed for
k=1,2,\dots,7
using the FEniCS library of Python
[
2
]
. The experimental convergence rate
r_{m}
approximating the exponent
r
of the inequality

2.5.
Other approximation properties of
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}

We close this section by reviewing some approximation properties and an inverse inequality that will be used in the error estimates of the subsequent sections.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer, and let
\cup_{i=1}^{N}\{q_{i,1},q_{i,2},\dots,q_{i,k-1}\}
be a basis of
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{2}
. We define the operator
I_{h}:C_{p}\to\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
by

I_{h}v=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[v(x_{i})\ell_{i}+\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}c_{i,m}q_{i,m}\right]\ ,

where the coefficients
c_{i,1},c_{i,2},\dots,c_{i,k-1}
are chosen such that, on each interval
\bar{I}_{i}=[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
, the function
I_{h}v
is the interpolating polynomial of
v
at the points
x_{i-1}+j/h
,
j=1,2,\dots,k-1
. For the interpolation error we have the following estimate, and we provide an elegant proof for the sake of completeness:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u\in H_{p}^{k+1}
. Then

(25)

\|u-I_{h}u\|+h\|(u-I_{h}u)_{x}\|\leq h^{k+1}\|u^{(k+1)}\|\ .

Proof.

Let
r_{h}(x)=u(x)-(I_{h}u)(x)
. Then
r_{h}
has at least
k+1
distinct roots in each interval
\bar{I}_{i}
. By applying Rolle’s Theorem inductively, we obtain that
r_{h}^{(\nu)}
, for
\nu=1,2,\dots,k
has
k+1-\nu
distinct roots in
I_{i}
. In particular, for
\nu=k
, if
\xi_{i}
is a root of
r_{h}^{(k)}
in
I_{i}
, then for all
x\in\bar{I}_{i}
,

r_{h}^{(k)}(x)=\int_{\xi_{i}}^{x}r_{h}^{(k+1)}(y)~dy=\int_{\xi_{i}}^{x}u^{(k+1)}(y)~dy\ .

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

[r_{h}^{(k)}(x)]^{2}\leq h\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}}[u^{(k+1)}(y)]^{2}~dy\ .

Integrating over
I_{i}
gives
\|r_{h}^{(k)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I_{i})}\leq h^{2}\|u^{(k+1)}\|^{2}
.
Summing over
i=1,2,\dots,N
, yields
\|r_{h}^{(k)}\|\leq h\|u^{(k+1)}\|
.
Applying the same argument recursively, we obtain
\|r_{h}^{(n-1)}\|\leq h\|r_{h}^{(n)}\|
, for
n=k,k-1,\dots,1
, and the desired estimate follows.
∎

It is worth noting that, for any
\chi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
, there exists a constant
C
, depending on
k
and independent of
h
, such that

(26)

\|\chi^{\prime}\|\leq Ch^{-1}\|\chi\|\ .

This is the standard inverse inequality for one-dimensional Lagrange finite element spaces, and its proof follows the same lines as in the classical references cf. eg.
[
19
]
.
Finally, using the previous estimates for the interpolant and the inverse inequality, we obtain the classical error estimate
\|u-Pu\|+h\|(u-Pu)_{x}\|\leq Ch^{k+1}\|u^{(k+1)}\|
for any
u\in H_{p}^{k+1}
. For more information, we refer to
[
19
]
.

3.
Application to a conservative finite element method

We consider the initial-periodic boundary value problem for the RLW equation
[
11
]
:

(27)

\displaystyle u_{t}+u_{x}+uu_{x}-u_{xxt}=0\quad\text{for $x\in(a,b)$ and $t>0$}\ ,

\displaystyle\partial^{i}_{x}u(a,t)=\partial^{i}_{x}u(b,t)\quad\text{ for $t\geq 0$ and $i=0,1,\dots$}\ ,

\displaystyle u(x,0)=U_{0}(x)\quad\text{for $x\in[a,b]$}\ .

This problem effectively mimics the Cauchy problem on
\mathbb{R}
and is therefore particularly suitable for studying long-time propagation phenomena, including the interaction of solitary waves and the resolution properties of arbitrary initial conditions.

Any sufficiently smooth solution
u=u(x,t)
of (
27
) satisfies the following conservation laws:

(28)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{M}(t;u)=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{M}(t;u)=\int_{a}^{b}u~dx\qquad\text{(Mass)}\ ,

(29)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t;u)=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{I}(t;u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}u^{2}+u_{x}^{2}~dx\qquad\text{(Impulse)}\ .

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t;u)=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{E}(t;u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}u^{2}+\frac{1}{3}u^{3}~dx\qquad\text{(Energy)}\ .

Note that these are the only independent conservation laws of (
27
), cf.
[
30
]
.
It is apparent that the RLW equation is a Hamiltonian partial differential equation, with the Hamiltonian functional given by
\mathcal{E}
. In this framework, it can be expressed in the canonical form

(31)

u_{t}=-\mathcal{D}\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta u}\ ,

where
\mathcal{D}=(I-\partial_{xx})^{-1}\partial_{x}
is a skew-adjoint operator, and
\delta\mathcal{E}/\delta u
denotes the Gateaux derivative of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{E}
.

3.1.
A conservative finite element semi-discretization

While the following analysis also holds on any interval
(a,b)
, we focus again on the simpler case
\bar{I}=[a,b]=[0,1]
, and use a uniform grid as in the preceding sections.

A modified Galerkin/finite element semi-discretization of (
27
) is defined as follows: Determine
\tilde{u}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
for
k=1,2,\dots
, such that

(32)

(\tilde{u}_{t},\chi)+(P[\tilde{u}_{xt}],\chi_{x})=(P[\tilde{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}],\chi_{x})\quad\text{for all $\chi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}$}\ ,

with initial condition
\tilde{u}(x,0)=P[U_{0}(x)]
and
T>0
the maximal time of existence of the analytical solution
u
of (
27
). This semi-discretization is energy-conservative, and we have the following:

Proposition 3.1
.

Any solution
\tilde{u}(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
of the semi-discretization (
32
) of the initial-periodic boundary value problem (
27
) conserves the mass and energy functionals in the sense that

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{M}(t;\tilde{u})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t;\tilde{u})=0\ .

Proof.

The mass conservation follows directly from (
32
) by choosing
\chi=1
.
To prove energy conservation, rewrite (
32
) as

(33)

(\tilde{u}_{t},\chi)=(P[\tilde{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}-\tilde{u}_{xt}],\chi_{x})\quad\text{for all $\chi\in S_{h}$}\ .

Denote
R=\tilde{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}-\tilde{u}_{xt}
and set
\chi=P[R]
in (
33
). Due to the periodic boundary conditions, we then have

(34)

(\tilde{u}_{t},R)=(\tilde{u}_{t},P[R])=(P[R],P[R]_{x})=0\ .

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{t},R)

\displaystyle=(\tilde{u}_{t},\tilde{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}-\tilde{u}_{xt})=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\tilde{u}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}\tilde{u}^{3})_{t}-(\tilde{u}_{t}^{2})_{x}~dx=\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{u}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}\tilde{u}^{3}~dx=\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t;\tilde{u})\ .

Combining this with (
34
) yields the desired energy conservation.
∎

The semi-discretization (
32
) can also be expressed in the form:

(35)

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{t},\chi)+(\tilde{w}_{t},\chi_{x})

\displaystyle=(\tilde{z},\chi_{x})

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all $\chi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}$}\ ,

(36)

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{xt},\psi)-(\tilde{w}_{t},\psi)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all $\psi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}$}\ ,

(37)

\displaystyle(\tilde{z},\phi)

\displaystyle=(\tilde{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{u}^{2},\phi)

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all $\phi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}$}\ .

In analogy, we can write the RLW equation as

(38)

\displaystyle u_{t}-w_{xt}=-z_{x},

(39)

\displaystyle w=u_{x},

(40)

\displaystyle z=u+\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2}\ .

Note that if
u(x,0)=U_{0}(x)
, then naturally
w(x,0)=W_{0}(x)=U_{0}^{\prime}(x)
.
We therefore consider as initial conditions of the semi-discrete system (
35
)–(
37
) the projections

(41)

\tilde{u}(x,0)=PU_{0}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{w}(x,0)=PW_{0}(x)=P[U_{0}^{\prime}](x)\ ,

for all
x\in[0,1]
.

Remark 3.1
.

From (
36
), we have
\tilde{w}_{t}={P[\tilde{u}_{x}]}_{t}
. Integrating in time over any interval
[0,t]
, gives
\tilde{w}=P[\tilde{u}_{x}]+\tilde{w}(x,0)-P[U_{0}^{\prime}]=P[\tilde{u}_{x}]+PW_{0}-P[U_{0}^{\prime}]
. Using the initial condition (
41
), we immediately obtain

(42)

\tilde{w}=P[\tilde{u}_{x}]\ .

3.2.
Proof of convergence

In this section, we prove that the semi-discrete solution
\tilde{u}
exists and converges to the classical solution with optimal order when
k\geq 1
is an odd integer for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
u\in C([0,T];C^{k+2}_{p})
be the unique solution of the initial-periodic boundary value problem (
27
), and let
k=2\kappa+1
for
\kappa=0,1,\dots
. Then, for sufficiently small
h>0
, there exists a unique solution
\tilde{u}(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
of the semi-discrete problem (
32
) (and equivalently of (
35
)–(
37
)) for all
t\in[0,T]
, and a constant
C>0
independent of
h
such that

\|u-\tilde{u}\|\leq Ch^{k+1},\quad\|u_{x}-P[\tilde{u}_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\|u_{x}-\tilde{u}_{x}\|\leq Ch^{k}\ .

Proof.

We define

\zeta=u-Pu,\quad\rho=Pu-\tilde{u},\quad\sigma=w-Pw,\quad\theta=Pw-\tilde{w},\quad\tau=z-Pz,\quad\xi=Pz-\tilde{z}\ .

Note that
\tilde{w}=P[\tilde{u}_{x}]
and
\tilde{z}=P[\tilde{u}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}]
.
Throughout the analysis, we will use a generic positive constant
C
independent of
h
.
We also assume that the exact solution is bounded, i.e., there is a constant
M
such that
\|u\|+\|u_{x}\|\leq M
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Then, for sufficiently small
h
, the semi-discrete solution satisfies
\|\tilde{u}(\cdot,0)\|+\|\tilde{w}(\cdot,0)\|\leq 2M
.

Upon choosing appropriate basis functions
\varphi_{i}(x)
,
i=1,2,\dots,kN
for the finite element space
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
, the semi-discrete solution can be represented as a linear combination of the basis functions:

\tilde{u}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{kN}u_{i}(t)\varphi_{i}(x)\ \text{ and }\ \tilde{w}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{kN}w_{i}(t)\varphi_{i}(x)\ .

Denote
\mathbf{y}
the vector
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})^{T}
where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{0},u_{1},\dots)^{T}
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},w_{1},\dots)^{T}
.
Then, the finite element system (
35
)–(
37
) can be written as a system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations

(43)

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})\ ,

where

\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

with
\mathbf{A}_{ij}=(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})
,
\mathbf{B}_{ij}=(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}^{\prime})
, and

\mathbf{F}=(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{0})
with
\mathbf{z}_{j}=(\tilde{z},\varphi_{j}^{\prime})
. Here,
\mathbf{A}
is symmetric and positive definite, while
\mathbf{B}
is skew-symmetric. Moreover, for any vector
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})^{T}
we have

\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{1}>\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{B})^{T}=0\ ,

which shows that
\mathbf{M}
is positive definite and therefore invertible.

For the system
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})
, the mapping
\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{F}
is locally Lipschitz as well as
\mathbf{F}
. Thus, there is a
t^{\ast}\leq T
such that the system (
35
)–(
37
) has a unique solution
(\tilde{u},\tilde{w})
satisfying

\|\tilde{u}\|+\|\tilde{w}\|\leq\tilde{M}
for all
t\in[0,t^{\ast}]
, for some constant
\tilde{M}>0
independent of
h
.

We define
\omega_{w}=P[w_{xt}]-w_{xt}
,
\omega_{z}=P[z_{x}]-z_{x}
and
\omega_{u}=P[u^{2}]-u^{2}
. Then, for
\chi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle(\rho_{t},\chi)

\displaystyle=(P[u_{t}],\chi)-(\tilde{u}_{t},\chi)=(P[w_{x}]_{t}-P[z_{x}],\chi)+(\tilde{w}_{t}-\tilde{z},\chi_{x})

\displaystyle=(P[w_{xt}]-w_{xt},\chi)+(w_{xt},\chi)+(\tilde{w}_{t},\chi_{x})-(P[z_{x}]-z_{x},\chi)-(z_{x},\chi)-(\tilde{z},\chi_{x})

\displaystyle=(\omega_{w},\chi)-(w_{t}-P[w_{t}],\chi_{x})-(P[w_{t}]-\tilde{w}_{t},\chi_{x})-(\omega_{z},\chi)+(z-Pz,\chi_{x})+(Pz-\tilde{z},\chi_{x})

\displaystyle=(P[\omega_{w}],\chi)+(P[{(\sigma_{t})}_{x}],\chi)+(P[{(\theta_{t})}_{x}],\chi)-(P[\omega_{z}],\chi)-(P[\tau_{x}],\chi)-(P[\xi_{x}],\chi)\ .

Equivalently, in operator form, this gives

(44)

\rho_{t}=P[\omega_{w}+(\sigma_{t})_{x}+(\theta_{t})_{x}-\omega_{z}-\tau_{x}-\xi_{x}]\ .

From (
36
) and (
39
), we have that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}

\displaystyle(\theta,\psi)

\displaystyle=(Pw-\tilde{w},\psi)=(w-\tilde{w},\psi)=(u_{x}-P[\tilde{u}_{x}],\psi)=-(u-\tilde{u},\psi_{x})

\displaystyle=-(u-Pu,\psi_{x})-(Pu-\tilde{u},\psi_{x})=-(\zeta,\psi_{x})-(\rho,\psi_{x})=(P[\zeta_{x}],\psi)+(P[\rho_{x}],\psi)\ .

Hence, we obtain the identity

(45)

\theta=P[\zeta_{x}+\rho_{x}]\ .

Using the last formula, we have

\|P[\rho_{x}]\|=\|\theta-P[\zeta_{x}]\|\leq\|\theta\|+\|P[\zeta_{x}]\|\ .

According to Theorem
2.2
, we have
\|P[\zeta_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k+1}
. (If
k=1
and
u\in C_{p}^{5}
, the exponent in the last estimate is
4
. However, this won’t improve the error estimate because, for instance, (
47
) below).
Using this estimate, we obtain

(46)

\|P[\rho_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k+1}+\|\theta\|\ .

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
. Then,

\displaystyle(\xi,\phi)

\displaystyle=(Pz-\tilde{z},\phi)=(Pu-\tilde{u},\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}(P[u^{2}]-\tilde{u}^{2},\phi)=(\rho,\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}(P[u^{2}]-u^{2},\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}(u^{2}-\tilde{u}^{2},\phi)

\displaystyle=(\rho,\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}(P[u^{2}]-u^{2},\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}((u+\tilde{u})(u-\tilde{u}),\phi)=(\rho,\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}(\omega_{u},\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}((u+\tilde{u})(\zeta+\rho),\phi)\ .

Taking
\phi=\xi
in the previous relation yields

\|\xi\|^{2}\leq\|\rho\|\|\xi\|+Ch^{k+1}\|\xi\|+\|u+\tilde{u}\|_{\infty}(Ch^{k+1}+\|\rho\|)\|\xi\|\ ,

which can be simplified to

(47)

\|\xi\|\leq C(h^{k+1}+\|\rho\|)\ .

Now, we can proceed with the main estimate. From (
44
) and (
45
) we have

\displaystyle(\rho_{t},\rho)+(\theta_{t},\theta)

\displaystyle=(P[\omega_{w}+(\sigma_{t})_{x}+(\theta_{t})_{x}-\omega_{z}-\tau_{x}-\xi_{x}],\rho)+(\theta_{t},P[\zeta_{x}+\rho_{x}])

\displaystyle=(\omega_{w}-\omega_{z},\rho)+(P[(\sigma_{t}-\tau)_{x}],\rho)-(\theta_{t},P[\rho_{x}])-(\xi_{x},\rho)+(\theta_{t},P[\zeta_{x}])+(\theta_{t},P[\rho_{x}])

\displaystyle=(\omega_{w}-\omega_{z},\rho)+(P[(\sigma_{t}-\tau)_{x}],\rho)-(\xi_{x},\rho)+(\theta_{t},P[\zeta_{x}])

\displaystyle=(\omega_{w}-\omega_{z},\rho)+(P[(\sigma_{t}-\tau)_{x}],\rho)+(\xi,P[\rho_{x}])+\tfrac{d}{dt}(\theta,P[\zeta_{x}])-(\theta,P[(\zeta_{t})_{x}])\ .

The last term in the previous inequality can be bounded using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

(\theta,P[(\zeta_{t})_{x}])\leq\frac{1}{8}\|\theta\|^{2}+2\|P[(\zeta_{t})_{x}]\|^{2}\leq C(\|P[(\zeta_{t})_{x}]\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2})\ .

By Theorem
2.2
,
\|P[(\zeta_{t})_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k+1}
. Therefore,

(48)

(\theta,P[(\zeta_{t})_{x}])\leq C(h^{2(k+1)}+\|\theta\|^{2})\ .

Similarly, using the inequalities (
47
) and (
46
), we obtain

(49)

(\xi,P[\rho_{x}])\leq C(h^{2(k+1)}+\|\xi\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2})\leq C(h^{2(k+1)}+\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2})\ .

Thus,

(50)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}(\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2})\leq C(h^{2(k+1)}+\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2})+\tfrac{d}{dt}(\theta,P[\zeta_{x}])\ .

Integrating this relation yields

(51)

\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}\leq Ch^{2(k+1)}+\frac{1}{8}\|\theta\|^{2}+2\|P[\zeta_{x}]\|^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}(\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta^{2}\|)\ ,

where, from now on,
C
depends on
t^{\ast}
.
Taking into account that
\|P[\zeta_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k+1}
, we can simplify (
51
) to

(52)

\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}\leq Ch^{2(k+1)}+C\int_{0}^{t}(\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta^{2}\|)\ .

Applying Gronwall’s inequality, we obtain the optimal error estimate

(53)

\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}\leq Ch^{2(k+1)}\ ,

for all
t\in[0,t^{\ast}]
, which immediately gives

(54)

\|u-\tilde{u}\|^{2}+\|u_{x}-P[\tilde{u}_{x}]\|^{2}\leq Ch^{2(k+1)}\ .

Finally, using (
53
) and the inverse inequality (
26
), we have

\|u_{x}-\tilde{u}_{x}\|\leq\|(u-Pu)_{x}\|+\|\rho_{x}\|\leq C(h^{k}+h^{-1}\|\rho\|)\leq Ch^{k}\ .

Choosing
h>0
sufficiently small such that
\|\tilde{u}\|\leq\|u\|+Ch^{k+1}<2M
for
0\leq t\leq t^{\ast}
,
we ensure that the numerical solution
\tilde{u}
will remain bounded in
L^{2}
up to
t=t^{\ast}
. Similarly, since
\|u_{x}-P[\tilde{u}_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k+1}
, we have

\|\tilde{w}\|\leq\|u_{x}\|+Ch^{k+1}<2M
for
0\leq t\leq t^{\ast}
.
By standard extension arguments from the theory of ordinary differential equations, we conclude that the solution (and the error estimates) can be extended to the full interval
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

If
k=2\kappa
for
\kappa=1,2,\dots
, we have that
\|P[\varepsilon_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k}
, where
\varepsilon=\zeta
or
\zeta_{t}
. Therefore, modifying the proof of Theorem
3.1
accordingly, we have that

\|u-\tilde{u}\|\leq Ch^{k}
,
\|u_{x}-P[\tilde{u}_{x}]\|\leq Ch^{k}
and
\|u_{x}-\tilde{u}_{x}\|\leq Ch^{k-1}
.

Remark 3.3
.

It was shown in
[
3
]
that if
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{5}_{p}
, then in the space of periodic cubic splines,
\|P[(v-I_{h}v)_{x}]\|=O(h^{k+1})
, where
I_{h}
is the cubic spline interpolant. Consequently, the proof of Theorem
3.1
can be modified to remain valid if we consider periodic cubic splines with small changes.

Building on the preceding analysis, we show that the conservation of the impulse functional
\mathcal{I}
for any smooth solution
\tilde{u}
of (
35
)–(
37
) is approximated with high accuracy.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
\tilde{u}(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{k}
with
k=1,2,\dots
be a solution of the initial value problem (
35
)–(
37
), (
41
), and let
h>0
sufficiently small. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

(55)

\max_{t\in[0,T]}|\mathcal{I}(t;\tilde{u})-\mathcal{I}(0;\tilde{u})|\leq Ch^{r}\ ,

where
r=2k
if
k=2\kappa+1
with
\kappa=0,1,\dots
, and
r=2k-2
if
k=2\kappa
with
\kappa=1,2,\dots
.

Proof.

Taking
\chi=\tilde{u}
and using (
42
) in (
35
), gives

(56)

(\tilde{u}_{t},\tilde{u})+({P[\tilde{u}_{x}]}_{t},\tilde{u}_{x})=(\tilde{z},\tilde{u}_{x})=(P[\tilde{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}],\tilde{u}_{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}(P[\tilde{u}^{2}],\tilde{u}_{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{u}^{2},P[\tilde{u}_{x}])\ .

Thus,

(\tilde{u}_{t},\tilde{u})+(\tilde{u}_{xt},\tilde{u}_{x})+(P[\tilde{u}_{xt}]-\tilde{u}_{xt},\tilde{u}_{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{u}^{2},P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x})\ .

Using the definition of the
L^{2}
-projection, we write the last relationship into the form

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t;\tilde{u})-(P[\tilde{u}_{xt}]-\tilde{u}_{xt},P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{u}^{2}-P[\tilde{u}^{2}],P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x})\ ,

which is equivalent to

(57)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t;\tilde{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{u}^{2}-P[\tilde{u}^{2}],P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x})\ .

Using Theorem
3.1
, we have that
\|P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x}\|\leq\|P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-u_{x}\|+\|u_{x}-\tilde{u}_{x}\|\leq Ch^{k}
if
k=2\kappa+1
for
\kappa=0,1,\dots
while
\|P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x}\|\leq Ch^{k-1}
for
k=2\kappa
,
\kappa=1,2,\dots
. Therefore, after integration of (
57
), and taking absolute values, we obtain

|\mathcal{I}(t,\tilde{u})-\mathcal{I}(0,\tilde{u})|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}T(\max_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{u}^{2}-P[\tilde{u}^{2}]\|^{2}+\max_{t\in[0,T]}\|P[\tilde{u}_{x}]-\tilde{u}_{x}\|^{2})\ ,

which completes the proof.
∎

In general, the standard Galerkin method has been used alongside the symplectic two-stage implicit Gauss–Legendre Runge–Kutta method, which is also known to preserve quadratic conservation laws due to its symplecticity. Conclusions

In this work, we proved that the
L^{2}
projection of sufficiently smooth and periodic function
u
onto Lagrange finite element spaces of periodic piecewise polynomial functions satisfies

\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|=O(h^{4})
if
k=1
,
\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|=O(h^{k+1})
if
k=2\kappa+1
,
\kappa\geq 1
, and
\|P[(Pu-u)_{x}]\|=O(h^{k})
if
k=2\kappa
,
\kappa\geq 1
.
This approximation property of finite element spaces directly influences the convergence of energy-conservative finite element methods applied to certain nonlinear and dispersive wave equations. We illustrate this effect by deriving
a priori
error estimates of a finite element method for the regularized long-wave equation that conserves a cubic energy functional and the linear mass functional. The theoretical findings are further confirmed by numerical experiments.
