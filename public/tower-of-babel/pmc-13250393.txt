Household Water and Sanitation Practices and Their Association With Diarrheal and Acute Respiratory Infections Among Children Under Five in Ghana: A Cross‐Sectional Predictive Model and Spatial Analysis
Authors: Yula Salifu, Paul Gyan, Joseph Lasong, Williams Walana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDiarrhea and acute respiratory infections (ARI) remain leading causes of under‐five morbidity in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Although Ghana achieved improved drinking water and sanitation coverage, significant regional and socioeconomic inequities persist. This study examined the associations between household water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) practices and diarrhea, ARI, and their comorbidity among under‐five children in Ghana.MethodsA cross‐sectional analysis of 6344 children aged 0–59 months from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey was conducted. Survey‐weighted descriptive statistics and multilevel logistic regression were applied. Predictive performance was assessed using fivefold cluster‐preserving cross‐validation, Brier scores, calibration slopes, calibration plots, and cross‐validated area under the curve (AUC). Spatial patterns were described using choropleth maps, Global Moran's I, Getis‐Ord Gi*, and Local Moran's I (LISA). Counterfactual WASH reductions were estimated with bootstrap confidence intervals (200 replicates). A complementary random‐effects meta‐analysis quantified between‐region heterogeneity (I2).ResultsTwo‐week prevalence of diarrhea, ARI, and comorbidity was 14.4% (95% CI: 12.7%–16.3%), 5.6% (95% CI: 4.7%–6.6%), and 1.8% (95% CI: 1.3%–2.5%), respectively, with a pronounced north–south gradient. No WASH variable reached significance in adjusted multilevel models. Diarrhea was associated with child age 6–35 months, maternal secondary and higher education, and household wealth. ARI was associated with co‐occurring diarrhea and stunting. Stunting was the sole significant predictor of comorbidity. Community‐level factors explained 21%–35% of variance. Cross‐validated AUC was 0.787 (diarrhea), 0.896 (ARI), and 0.577 (comorbidity); calibration slopes were 3.68–11.68 (all poor). Global Moran's I showed no significant spatial autocorrelation for all three outcomes. Improving cooking fuel universally was the only scenario associated with a statistically significant absolute reduction in diarrhea prevalence: 1.4% points (relative reduction 13.9%; 95% CI: 6.2%–21.6%).ConclusionsHousehold WASH infrastructure showed no significant adjusted association with childhood diarrhea, ARI, or comorbidity after controlling for socioeconomic and nutritional confounders. A pronounced north–south disease gradient calls for geographically targeted, multisectoral interventions. Predictive models demonstrated adequate discrimination but poor calibration, limiting their use to population‐level risk stratification.Keywords: acute respiratory infections, children under five, demographic and health survey, diarrhea, Ghana, multilevel modeling, predictive modeling, spatial analysis, WASH

Introduction

Childhood diarrhea and acute respiratory infections (ARI) are the leading preventable causes of under‐five morbidity and mortality in low‐middle‐income countries (LMICs), accounting for more than one third of all under‐five deaths globally [1, 2]. These conditions disproportionately affect children in settings characterized by inadequate household water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), suboptimal nutritional status, and socioeconomic deprivation [3]. In Ghana, substantial progress has been achieved: improved drinking water coverage rose from approximately 63% in 2000 to 86.6% by 2022, and improved sanitation coverage increased from 14% to 60.4% over the same period [4, 5]. Despite this progress, these national averages conceal persistent regional and rural–urban inequities. The Northern, Savannah, and North‐East regions, characterized by poverty rates exceeding 50% in some districts, lower educational attainment, and vulnerability to climate variability, consistently report the highest under‐five morbidity and mortality rates [4, 6, 7]. Recent analyses confirm that concentration indices for under‐five illness outcomes in Ghana are substantially higher among the poorest wealth quintiles and in northern regions; however, evidence on the effectiveness of WASH expansion in narrowing these gaps remains limited [4, 6, 7].

The determinants of childhood diarrhea and ARI operate across individual, household, and community levels. At the individual level, child age, nutritional status (stunting, wasting, underweight), and breastfeeding practices shape susceptibility to infections [8, 9]. Maternal education and health literacy translate into improved hygiene practices, feeding behaviors, and appropriate care‐seeking [10, 11], generating protective effects that persist even after adjustment for household wealth. At the household level, cooking fuel type, water source quality, and sanitation facilities modulate pathogen exposure [12, 13]. At the community level, shared sanitation environments, healthcare access, local hygiene norms, and environmental pathogen load shape disease transmission [11, 12]. Critically, malnutrition and infection interact in a self‐reinforcing cycle: chronic undernutrition impairs mucosal immunity and cell‐mediated immune responses, heightening susceptibility to both respiratory and enteric pathogens, while recurrent infection further exacerbates nutritional deficits [9, 13, 14].

Prior research in Ghana and comparable West African settings has predominantly relied on single‐level logistic regression, which inadequately accounts for community‐level clustering [6, 7]. The co‐occurrence of diarrhea and ARI, though epidemiologically important, remains under‐studied, partly because most analyses treat these conditions independently [14, 15, 16]. Moreover, spatial heterogeneity is seldom formally assessed, limiting geographic targeting of interventions. Finally, few studies have quantified potential health gains from universal WASH improvement using counterfactual frameworks with explicit uncertainty.

This study addresses these gaps by examining the associations between household WASH practices and diarrhea, ARI, and their comorbidity among under‐five children in Ghana, using an integrated framework combining multilevel regression, spatial autocorrelation diagnostics, cross‐validated predictive modeling with calibration assessment, and bootstrap counterfactual estimation. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) estimate the national and regional 2‐week prevalence of diarrhea, ARI, and comorbidity; (2) identify significant individual, household, and community‐level predictors; (3) formally assess spatial autocorrelation and map disease distribution; (4) evaluate out‐of‐sample predictive performance and calibration; and (5) quantify potential disease burden reductions under scenarios of universal WASH access. These objectives directly support Sustainable Development Goals 3.2 (eliminating preventable under‐five deaths) and 6 (universal WASH access).

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This study employed a cross‐sectional analytical design using nationally representative data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (2022‐GDHS). Data were obtained from the DHS Program (www.dhsprogram.com) after formal authorization. The 2022‐GDHS was conducted by the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) in collaboration with the Ghana Health Service (GHS) and ICF International between 17 October 2022 and 14 January 2023. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross‐sectional studies were adhered to throughout.

Study Setting

Ghana is a lower‐middle‐income country in West Africa with an estimated population of 33 million (2021 census) [15]. The country comprises 16 administrative regions spanning four ecological zones: coastal, forest, transition, and savannah. A pronounced north–south socioeconomic gradient defines the country's health landscape. The Northern, Savannah, Upper East, Upper West, and North‐East regions have under‐five mortality rates and malnutrition prevalences substantially above the national average; poverty rates exceed 50% in some northern districts [4]. Urbanization and economic development are concentrated in the southern coastal belt, particularly Greater Accra, Ashanti, and Western regions. This geographic and socioeconomic heterogeneity makes Ghana an informative setting for spatial analyses of childhood illness.

Population and Sampling

The 2022‐GDHS used a two‐stage stratified cluster sampling design based on the 2021 Ghana Population and Housing Census [15]. In the first stage, 618 enumeration areas (clusters) were selected with probability proportional to size across 16 regions and urban–rural strata. In the second stage, 30 households per cluster were randomly selected, yielding 18,540 targeted households; 15,014 were successfully interviewed (response rate: 98%). The analytic sample was restricted to children aged 0–59 months with complete data on all outcome and predictor variables. After listwise deletion of cases with missing data (< 5% overall) and application of survey weights, the final sample comprised 6,344 under‐five children nested within 618 primary sampling units (PSUs) across 16 regions.

Variables

Outcome Variables

Three binary outcomes were defined: (1) Diarrhoeal infection: caregiver‐reported occurrence of three or more loose or watery stools in the 2 weeks preceding the survey (yes/no). (2) ARI: caregiver‐reported cough accompanied by short, rapid, or difficult breathing not attributable to a known chronic condition in the preceding 2 weeks (yes/no). (3) ARI–diarrhea comorbidity: co‐occurrence of both conditions in the same 2‐week period (yes/no). The 2‐week recall period is standard for DHS child morbidity assessment.

WASH Exposure Variables

Household WASH conditions were classified per 2021 WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) standards [5]. Water source: improved (piped water, borehole, protected well/spring, rainwater, bottled/sachet water) versus unimproved (surface water, unprotected well/spring, tanker truck, other). Sanitation: improved (flush/pour‐flush toilet, ventilated improved pit latrine, pit latrine with slab, composting toilet) versus unimproved (open defecation, hanging latrine, unprotected pit). Cooking fuel: clean (electricity, liquefied petroleum gas, natural gas, biogas) versus unclean (charcoal, wood, dung, crop residue, kerosene, other).

Covariates

Models adjusted for: child‐level variables (age in months: < 6, 6–11, 12–35, 36–47, 48–59; sex; nutritional status; breastfeeding); maternal‐level variables (age in 5‐year groups: 15–19 to 45–49; education: none, primary, secondary, higher); and household‐level variables (wealth quintile; place of residence). Nutritional status was derived from WHO Child Growth Standards: stunting (height‐for‐age z‐score [HAZ] < –2 SD), underweight (weight‐for‐age z‐score [WAZ] < –2 SD), and wasting (weight‐for‐height z‐score [WHZ] < –2 SD); children with biologically implausible values were excluded. The DHS wealth index is a composite of household asset ownership derived by principal component analysis of asset variables (flooring type, water and sanitation access, ownership of consumer goods); quintiles (poorest to richest) are constructed from this index and supplied directly by the DHS Program, without further recoding.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive and Bivariate Analysis

Survey‐weighted frequencies and proportions (with numerators and denominators) were computed for all variables. Bivariate associations between each outcome and all explanatory variables were assessed using the Rao‐Scott adjusted chi‐squared test (design‐based F statistic), which accounts for survey stratification, clustering, and weighting. All tests were two‐sided; p < 0.05 was the pre‐specified threshold for statistical significance.

Multivariable Multilevel Analysis

To account for the hierarchical structure of the data (children nested within PSUs), survey‐weighted logistic regression models (svyglm, quasibinomial family) with cluster‐robust standard errors were fitted in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). Three models were estimated: Model 1 (diarrhea); Model 2 (ARI, with diarrhea co‐occurrence included as a predictor to capture the concurrent vulnerability pathway, this represents contemporaneous association, not temporal precedence, since both outcomes were measured over the same 2‐week recall window); and Model 3 (comorbidity). All models adjusted for the full covariate set. Associations are expressed as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals. Goodness‐of‐fit was assessed using McFadden's pseudo‐R 2, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Multicollinearity was evaluated using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF); all values were below 5. In parallel, multilevel mixed‐effects logistic regression (melogit command, STATA/MP 17.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) was used with individual‐level survey weights (pweight option) and cluster‐robust standard errors (vce(cluster v001)) to estimate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Median Odds Ratio (MOR), quantifying between‐community variation. Survey stratification (strata v023) and PSU (id v021) were specified in the svyset command; variance estimation used Taylor linearized standard errors.

Predictive Performance and Calibration

To obtain unbiased out‐of‐sample predictive performance, fivefold cross‐validation was conducted with all observations from the same PSU cluster always assigned to the same fold (cluster‐preserving), preventing data leakage. For each fold, the model was trained on the remaining four folds and predictions generated for the held‐out fold. Using the pooled cross‐validated predictions, four performance metrics were computed: (1) Brier score (mean squared error between predicted probability and observed binary outcome; ranges from 0 [perfect] to 1; interpreted relative to a null model Brier score based on the marginal outcome prevalence); (2) Calibration slope (coefficient from a survey‐weighted logistic regression of the observed outcome on the logit of the predicted probability; a slope of 1.0 indicates perfect calibration; values substantially above 1.0 indicate overconfident, overly extreme predictions, a pattern commonly observed when models overfit training folds); (3) Calibration plots (observed outcome proportions across deciles of predicted probability vs. mean predicted probability); and (4) Cross‐validated AUC using the pROC package. AUC was interpreted as: 0.5–0.7 = poor, 0.7–0.8 = acceptable, 0.8–0.9 = excellent, > 0.9 = outstanding.

Spatial Analysis

Spatial analyses were conducted in R. Regional‐level weighted prevalence estimates were computed for each of Ghana's 16 administrative regions. A shapefile of Ghana's regions (GADM version 4.0) was used; invalid geometries were repaired with st_make_valid (sf package). Queen‐contiguity spatial weights were constructed using poly2nb and nb2listw (spdep package) with zero.policy = TRUE to accommodate regions with limited neighbors. Global Moran's I (randomization = FALSE, using the theoretical distribution under normality) was computed to assess whether disease prevalence showed spatial autocorrelation across regions. We explicitly acknowledge that with n = 16 spatial units, the Global Moran's I test has limited statistical power for detecting moderate autocorrelation; non‐significant p‐values should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as definitive evidence against spatial structure. Local Getis‐Ord Gi* and Local Moran's I (LISA) statistics were computed to identify local hot/cold spots and spatial cluster types; these local statistics provide spatially disaggregated evidence of regional concentration that is informative independent of the global test. Choropleth maps, hot‐spot maps, LISA maps, and Moran scatter plots were produced using ggplot2 and viridis. A summary of Global Moran's I results is presented in Table 3.

Counterfactual Analysis

Using the fitted survey‐weighted logistic models, the predicted prevalence of each outcome was estimated under four counterfactual scenarios: (i) universal improved water (water_improved = 1 for all observations); (ii) universal improved sanitation (toilet_improved = 1); (iii) universal clean cooking fuel (fuel_clean = 1); (iv) all three simultaneously improved. For each scenario, the relevant WASH variable was set to 1 while all other variables remained at their observed values. The mean predicted probability under each scenario was computed as the weighted average across all observations. Absolute reductions (percentage points) and relative reductions (%) were calculated by comparing counterfactual and observed prevalence. Uncertainty was quantified using cluster‐resampling bootstrap (200 replicates), resampling PSU clusters with replacement, yielding 95% percentile confidence intervals. These estimates are model based and assume that observed associations reflect the direction of causal effects and should be treated as illustrative predictions rather than definitive evidence of intervention impact.

Regional Meta‐Analysis and Robustness Check

A complementary random‐effects meta‐analysis of the 16 regional prevalence estimates was conducted using the DerSimonian–Laird method (meta package, Stata). This meta‐analysis serves a distinct purpose from the direct survey‐weighted national estimate (computed via svyprop): it quantifies between‐region heterogeneity through the I 2 statistic and Cochran's Q test, which the svyprop approach cannot provide, and it enables leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis (each region sequentially omitted) to identify influential regions. Both estimates, direct and meta‐analytic, are reported alongside each other as a robustness check. Agreement between the two confirms the robustness of the national prevalence figure. High I2 (> 75%) is interpreted as evidence of substantial true regional heterogeneity warranting geographically targeted interventions.

Software and Significance

All analyses were performed using STATA/MP 17.0 (StataCorp LLC) and R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). All statistical tests were two‐sided; p < 0.05 was the pre‐specified threshold for significance. All analyses using survey data incorporated sampling weights, stratification (v023), and PSU clustering (v021) using the svyset framework with Taylor linearized standard error estimation.

Ethical Considerations

The protocols for the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey were approved by the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee and the ICF Institutional Review Board. Informed consent was obtained from all adult participants; for participants aged 15–17 years, consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians. This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available, de‐identified data obtained from the DHS Program (https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm) and did not require additional ethical approval. All data were anonymised at source.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Table 1 presents the survey‐weighted distribution of background characteristics of the 6344 children included in the analysis. Most households had access to improved drinking water (5,401/6,238 weighted; 86.6%) and improved sanitation (3763/6,238; 60.4%); use of clean cooking fuel was considerably lower (1372/6,226; 22.0%). The age distribution spanned five categories: < 6 months (443; 13.8%), 6–11 months (443; 13.8%), 12–35 months (1492; 46.4%), 36–47 months (491; 15.3%), and 48–59 months (344; 10.7%). Sex was approximately equal (3,108 males; 51.3%). Among children with valid anthropometric measurements (n = 3160), stunting was identified in 498 (15.8%), underweight in 398/3,166 (12.6%), and wasting in 212/3,156 (6.7%). Most mothers were aged 25–34 years (46.1%) and had attained at least secondary education (53.6%). The sample was evenly distributed across wealth quintiles (each approximately 20%) and between urban (3,185; 50.2%) and rural (3,159; 49.8%) residences. Slightly more than half of children (3,424; 54.0%) were not currently breastfed. Regional representation was highest in Ashanti (1,178; 18.6%), Greater Accra (830; 13.1%), and Northern (664; 10.5%) regions. In the 2 weeks preceding the survey: 5,198 (81.9%) reported no illness; 800 (12.6%) reported diarrhea only; 232 (3.6%) ARI only; and 115 (1.8%) both conditions.

National and Regional Burden and Prevalence Robustness Check

The direct survey‐weighted national 2‐week prevalence was 14.4% (95% CI: 12.7%–16.3%) for diarrhea, 5.6% (95% CI: 4.7%–6.6%) for ARI, and 1.8% (95% CI: 1.3%–2.5%) for comorbidity. These primary estimates are presented alongside the complementary meta‐analytic pooled estimates in Table 2. The close agreement between the two approaches confirms the robustness of the national figures. High between‐region heterogeneity (I 2 = 88.0% for diarrhea, I 2 = 80.3% for ARI, I 2 = 77.3% for comorbidity) indicates that the national average substantially masks regional variation. Forest plots visualizing the regional estimates (Supporting Figure S1) demonstrate that northern regions are consistently above the national average, confirming the need for geographically disaggregated analysis.

Spatial Distribution and Autocorrelation

Choropleth maps (Figure 1) reveal a distinct north–south gradient in disease prevalence. Diarrhea prevalence exceeds 20% across the Northern, Savannah, and North‐East regions and falls below 10% in the Western, Greater Accra, and Upper West regions. ARI shows moderate regional variation with elevated rates in the Northern, North‐East, and Volta regions. Comorbidity prevalence is uniformly low but marginally higher in northern areas.

Spatial distribution of 2‐week prevalence (expressed as proportions, scale 0–0.25) of ARI (left), ARI–diarrhea comorbidity (center), and diarrhea (right) across Ghana's 16 administrative regions, 2022 GDHS. Darker shades indicate higher prevalence. Data source: 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey.

Global spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed no statistically significant clustering for any of the outcomes examined. Diarrhea showed a weak positive spatial pattern (Moran's I = 0.155, p = 0.092), suggesting a tendency toward clustering; however, this did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. ARI exhibited a negative but non‐significant spatial association (Moran's I = −0.162, p = 0.703), indicating a pattern consistent with spatial dispersion, though not statistically distinguishable from randomness. Comorbidity demonstrated minimal spatial dependence (Moran's I = 0.067, p = 0.208), also indicating a distribution not significantly different from random. These may likely reflect low case counts and the limited statistical power of Moran's I with n = 16 units.

In contrast, local spatial analyses identified geographically specific patterns. Getis‐Ord Gi* statistics highlighted localized hot spots of elevated prevalence for diarrhea and comorbidity, primarily concentrated in northern regions (the Northern, Savannah, and North‐East regions) (Supporting Infomation S1: Figure S2). Similarly, Local Moran's I (LISA) cluster maps revealed High–High clusters for diarrhea and comorbidity, indicating areas where regions with relatively higher prevalence (northern‐central belt) are surrounded by neighboring regions with similarly elevated values (Supporting Infomation S1: Figure S3). No consistent clustering pattern was observed for ARI across local indicators. Moran scatter plots further illustrated these findings, with most regions distributed around the mean and only limited evidence of strong spatial dependence (Supporting Infomation S1: Figure S4).

Bivariate Associations

Bivariate analyses (Supporting Infomation S1: Tables S1–S3) showed that unimproved sanitation was significantly associated with ARI–diarrhea comorbidity (p = 0.005) and diarrhea (p = 0.008). Unclean cooking fuel was significantly associated with comorbidity (p = 0.001), diarrhea (p < 0.001), and ARI (p = 0.0003). Unimproved water was associated with diarrhea (p = 0.0004) but not ARI or comorbidity. Stunting was significantly associated with comorbidity (p = 0.007) and ARI (p = 0.017). Maternal education was significantly associated with all three outcomes (all p < 0.001). Household wealth, rural residence, and child underweight were also significantly associated with diarrhea. All WASH associations were attenuated and rendered non‐significant in the fully adjusted multivariable models.

Model 1: Diarrhea

Child age was the most important predictor. Compared with infants aged < 6 months, the odds of diarrhea were significantly elevated among children aged 6–11 months (AOR: 2.45; 95% CI: 1.35–4.43; p < 0.01) and 12–35 months (AOR: 2.35; 95% CI: 1.39–3.99; p < 0.001), reflecting the high‐risk period of complementary food introduction. Maternal secondary education (AOR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.39–0.85; p < 0.01) and higher education (AOR: 0.27; 95% CI: 0.12–0.62; p < 0.01) were strongly protective. Children in the poorer wealth quintile had lower odds of diarrhea compared with the poorest quintile (AOR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.43–0.91; p < 0.01), a non‐linear pattern likely reflecting residual confounding within the lowest wealth stratum. Underweight showed a marginally non‐significant trend (AOR: 1.58; 95% CI: 0.96–2.60; p = 0.094).

Model 2: ARI

Co‐occurrence of diarrhea in the same 2‐week recall window was the strongest predictor of ARI (AOR: 3.21; 95% CI: 2.11–4.87; p < 0.05), indicating that children with concurrent diarrhea had more than three times the odds of ARI compared with those without. This association represents contemporaneous co‐occurrence rather than a temporal sequence; it should not be interpreted as evidence that diarrhea precedes or causes ARI, since both were measured over the same recall window. The most plausible explanation is shared pathways of immunological vulnerability, including impaired mucosal immunity, common environmental exposures, and nutritional debilitation, that simultaneously predispose children to both infections [14]. Stunting was also significantly associated with higher ARI odds (AOR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.08–2.61; p < 0.01). No WASH indicator reached significance.

Model 3: ARI–Diarrhea Comorbidity

Stunting was the only statistically significant predictor of comorbidity (AOR: 2.85; 95% CI: 1.29–6.29; p < 0.01). Children with stunting had nearly three times the odds of concurrent diarrhea and ARI compared with non‐stunted children. No WASH, socioeconomic, or other covariate reached significance.

Random Effects and Community‐Level Variation

The Median Odds Ratio (MOR) ranged from 2.77 (diarrhea) to 3.61 (comorbidity), indicating that a child drawn from a randomly selected higher‐risk community would have 2.8–3.6 times higher odds of illness than a child from a randomly selected lower‐risk community, independent of all measured individual and household covariates. The ICC ranged from 0.209 (diarrhea) to 0.345 (comorbidity), indicating that 21%–35% of total variance in illness risk was attributable to unmeasured community‐level factors. McFadden's R 2 ranged from 0.542 to 0.571, suggesting the models explain approximately 54%–57% of the variation in illness.

Table 5 presents the cross‐validated predictive performance metrics. The cross‐validated AUC was highest for ARI (0.896; excellent discrimination), intermediate for diarrhea (0.787; acceptable), and poor for comorbidity (0.577; near‐chance) (Supplementary Figure S5). Brier scores were 0.131 for diarrhea (compared with a null Brier score of approximately 0.124 based on the marginal prevalence of 14.4%), 0.068 for ARI (null ≈ 0.053), and 0.024 for comorbidity (null ≈ 0.018). The small improvements over the null Brier scores for all three models indicate modest incremental accuracy, with the diarrhea model providing the least improvement proportionally.

Calibration slopes were 3.68 (diarrhea), 5.44 (ARI), and 11.68 (comorbidity), all substantially above the ideal value of 1.0, indicating that the cross‐validated models produce predicted probabilities that are too extreme relative to the observed rates (overconfident predictions). Specifically, low‐risk individuals are assigned lower predicted probabilities than they actually experience, and high‐risk individuals are assigned higher probabilities than observed. This pattern commonly arises when models trained on complete data are applied to held‐out folds of a small dataset: some degree of overfitting in training folds leads to extreme coefficient estimates that produce overly confident predictions in the test fold. It is also a recognized challenge when PSU clusters vary considerably in size. The calibration plots (Figure 2) illustrate this: for all three outcomes, the observed proportions in the lower deciles of predicted probability lie above the ideal calibration line, while the upper deciles are closer to the line. These findings indicate that the models have adequate utility for ranking populations by risk (AUC) but cannot reliably assign precise individual‐level probabilities without post‐hoc recalibration (e.g., shrinkage correction or Platt scaling). Their appropriate use is therefore as risk‐stratification tools, identifying high‐burden geographic areas or subgroups, not as clinical decision‐making instruments.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analytical assessment of childhood diarrhea, ARI, and their comorbidity among under‐five children in Ghana, integrating multilevel regression, formal spatial diagnostics, cross‐validated predictive modeling with calibration assessment, and counterfactual estimation. Four principal findings emerge. First, in this cross‐sectional analysis, household WASH infrastructure showed no significant adjusted association with any outcome after accounting for community‐level clustering and socioeconomic confounders; child stunting, maternal education, and age were the dominant predictors. Second, a pronounced north–south disease gradient was confirmed by statistically significant spatial autocorrelation. Also, predictive models achieved adequate discriminative performance for ARI and diarrhea but were systematically miscalibrated, with implications for their appropriate use. Additional, of all WASH counterfactual scenarios, only universal clean cooking fuel access produced a statistically significant modeled reduction in diarrhea burden.

National Burden and Heterogeneity

The 2‐week prevalences of diarrhea (14.4%) and ARI (5.6%) are broadly consistent with sub‐Saharan African benchmarks [2] and with the 12.6% and 5.9% reported in a 45‐country analysis [16]. The comorbidity prevalence (1.8%) is approximately six times lower than the 12.6% in that multi‐country study, potentially reflecting Ghana's progress in WASH coverage and IMCI implementation, though differences in survey methodology and recall windows may contribute. The close agreement between direct survey‐weighted national estimates and meta‐analytic pooled estimates confirms the robustness of the primary figures. However, the high between‐region heterogeneity (I2 > 77% for all outcomes) underscores that national averages conceal profound sub‐national inequalities that are policy‐relevant.

Spatial Concentration and Geographic Targeting

The findings indicate that childhood illnesses in Ghana are not strongly spatially structured at the regional level, as evidenced by the absence of statistically significant global spatial autocorrelation. This suggests that, when considered across all regions simultaneously, the distribution of diarrhea, ARI, and comorbidity does not deviate substantially from spatial randomness.

However, local spatial analyses provide important additional insight by identifying geographically concentrated pockets of higher disease burden, particularly in the northern belt. The presence of localized hot spots and High–High clusters for diarrhea and comorbidity indicates that spatial heterogeneity exists at a sub‐national level, even in the absence of strong global patterns. This distinction between global and local spatial statistics is well‐recognized; global measures summarize overall spatial dependence, while local indicators are more sensitive to localized clustering [5, 6, 11, 17].

These findings have practical implications for public health planning. The identification of localized high‐prevalence areas supports the need for geographically targeted interventions, rather than uniform national strategies. At the same time, the lack of strong global spatial autocorrelation suggests that interventions should not be limited exclusively to specific regions, as disease burden is not uniformly concentrated across the country.

It is important to interpret these results considering methodological constraints. The analysis was conducted at the regional level (n = 16), which limits statistical power, particularly for global spatial tests such as Moran's I. As a result, weak or moderate spatial patterns may not reach statistical significance. Thus, findings should be interpreted directionally. Additionally, the aggregation of data at the regional level may obscure finer‐scale spatial variation that could be detected using district‐ or cluster‐level data. Overall, the combined evidence from global and local spatial analyses suggests that while broad national clustering is limited, meaningful localized spatial concentrations of childhood illnesses exist and may warrant targeted attention in policy and programmatic responses.

The northern belt (Northern, Savannah, and North‐East regions) emerges as a persistent disease concentration area, reflecting entrenched poverty, lower educational attainment, and vulnerability to climate‐related water and sanitation disruptions [12, 17]. These findings demand geographically differentiated resource allocation rather than uniform national programs. It is also a plausible hypothesis (not demonstrated in our data) that climate variability moderates the relationship between WASH and disease risk in this region [17].

Association Between WASH and Childhood Illnesses

The absence of significant adjusted associations between household WASH and any outcome, despite significant bivariate associations, is a substantive and policy‐relevant finding. It does not imply that WASH ‘does not matter’ for child health; rather, several mechanisms may plausibly explain the attenuation, though these are hypotheses rather than findings of the current study. First, socioeconomic confounding: wealthier, more educated households are more likely to have improved WASH and simultaneously to practice better hygiene, seek care earlier, and provide more nutritious diets; after adjusting for these variables, the independent contribution of WASH hardware may be minimal [2, 13]. Second, coverage saturation: with improved water access already at 86.6%, the remaining unimproved households represent a small, highly selected, multiply disadvantaged group, creating limited residual variation for models to detect. Third, measurement error in WASH exposure: the survey captures infrastructure type rather than actual usage, quality at the point of consumption, or reliability; seasonal contamination of nominally improved water sources has been documented in Ghana and may further attenuate measured associations [12]. Additionally, unmeasured community‐level contextual factors (ICC: 21–35%; MOR: 2.77–3.61) including local pathogen load, environmental pollution, community hygiene norms, and healthcare access, may exert dominant influences on illness risk that operate independently of household infrastructure [11]. These findings are consistent with WASH trial evidence from LMICs suggesting that infrastructure improvements alone have limited impact in the absence of complementary behavior change and hygiene promotion [2, 12]. Programmatically, this underscores that expanding WASH hardware without concurrent investment in community‐led sanitation, hygiene behavior change communication, and health systems strengthening is unlikely to maximize health impact.

Maternal Education, Child Age, and Nutritional Status

Maternal education was the strongest modifiable predictor of diarrhea, with secondary education conferring 42% lower odds and higher education 73% lower odds compared with no education, after full adjustment for wealth and other covariates. This effect, persisting after wealth adjustment, reflects cognitive pathways through which education shapes hygiene behavior, food safety practices, and care‐seeking [10, 11]. Sustained investment in girls' education and adult female literacy programs is a long‐term, cost‐effective strategy for reducing childhood enteric infection burden. The peak risk period for diarrhea (6–35 months) corresponds to the complementary feeding transition, when exposure to enteric pathogens through contaminated weaning foods is substantially elevated [8, 18]. Targeted interventions supporting safe preparation, storage, and hygiene of complementary foods during this window are warranted.

Stunting was the dominant predictor of ARI and the sole significant predictor of comorbidity. This confirms the vicious cycle of chronic malnutrition and infection: stunted children have impaired mucosal barriers and cell‐mediated immunity, increasing susceptibility to both respiratory and enteric pathogens, while recurrent infections further exacerbate nutritional deficits [9, 13]. The fact that stunting, a marker of chronic rather than acute undernutrition, predicts comorbidity more strongly than any WASH or socioeconomic variable underscores the primacy of nutrition‐sensitive programming. Systematic integration of stunting screening, micronutrient supplementation, and targeted feeding support into routine under‐five care visits is an evidence‐based priority.

Bidirectional Diarrhea–ARI Association

The strong association between co‐occurring diarrhea and ARI is consistent with findings from Ethiopia [14] and multi‐country analyses [16], suggesting shared pathways of immunological vulnerability. Because both conditions were measured over the same 2‐week recall window, this association reflects concurrent co‐occurrence rather than a temporally ordered causal sequence; it should not be interpreted as evidence that diarrhea precedes or causes ARI or vice versa. The most plausible interpretation is that children with compromised mucosal immunity, poor nutritional reserves, or high environmental pathogen exposure are simultaneously predisposed to both infections [19]. This reinforces the value of the IMCI framework: a child presenting with ARI should be assessed and managed for diarrhea concurrently, and vice versa.

Predictive Models

The cross‐validated AUC values indicate adequate discrimination for ARI (0.896; excellent) and diarrhea (0.787; acceptable), but near‐chance discrimination for comorbidity (0.577). However, the calibration slopes (3.68 for diarrhea, 5.44 for ARI, and 11.68 for comorbidity), reveal a consistent pattern of overconfidence: the cross‐validated models assign predicted probabilities that are too extreme relative to observed outcome rates. Values substantially above 1.0 indicate that the logit of the predicted probability is too large in absolute magnitude, typically arising from some overfitting in training folds that produces extreme coefficient estimates. This pattern is well‐documented in epidemiological prediction models developed from survey data with clustered structures [9]. Practically, the poor calibration means that while these models can reasonably rank individuals or areas by relative risk (adequate AUC), they cannot be used to assign meaningful absolute individual‐level probabilities without post‐hoc recalibration. Their appropriate use is as risk‐stratification tools, for identifying high‐burden geographic areas or demographic subgroups to target for surveillance and intervention, rather than as clinical prediction instruments. The poor discrimination for comorbidity further reflects both the rarity of the outcome (1.8%) and the likely role of unmeasured determinants (pathogen co‐exposure, detailed dietary history, healthcare access) not captured in the DHS.

Counterfactual Analysis

The only statistically significant counterfactual finding was the modeled reduction in diarrhea prevalence under universal clean cooking fuel access (1.4 pp; 95% CI: 0.6–2.2; relative: 13.9%). Although household air pollution from unclean fuel is primarily associated with respiratory injury, several indirect mechanisms may plausibly link it to diarrhoeal susceptibility: combustion products may damage intestinal mucosal immunity through systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [3, 13]; unclean fuel use may correlate with cooking practices that contaminate food through airborne particulates; and residual confounding by unmeasured hygiene practices in households using unclean fuel may contribute to the modeled effect, a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism. The similar magnitude of reduction in the all‐three‐WASH scenario (1.3 pp) compared with the fuel‐only scenario confirms that fuel drives the combined estimate, and that the models detect no additional benefit from universalizing water or sanitation at observed coverage levels. The non‐significant and sometimes paradoxical point estimates for water improvement (suggesting a marginal increase in some outcomes) likely reflect positive confounding in the observed data, which is disrupted in the counterfactual. These estimates are model based, should not substitute for randomized evidence, and require confirmation through well‐designed interventional studies.

Strengths and Limitations

Key strengths include the use of a large, nationally representative, survey‐weighted dataset; rigorous fivefold cluster‐preserving cross‐validation with four complementary performance metrics; formal spatial autocorrelation diagnostics with local cluster identification; transparent counterfactual quantification with bootstrap uncertainty; simultaneous modeling of three outcomes including their comorbidity; and a robustness check comparing direct and meta‐analytic national estimates. The multidimensional analytical framework provides a comprehensive evidence base.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference: all associations may reflect residual confounding or unmeasured mediators, and temporal ordering cannot be established. Self‐reported recall of diarrhea and ARI over 2 weeks is susceptible to recall bias, under‐reporting (normalization of illness in high‐burden settings), and social desirability bias. WASH variables represent infrastructure type rather than actual usage, reliability, or quality at point of consumption, which may attenuate true associations. Nutritional z‐scores were available for only approximately half the sample (those with valid anthropometric measurements), introducing a potential selection concern. The spatial analysis at n = 16 regions has limited statistical power for local statistics; findings should be interpreted as directional rather than definitive. The counterfactual estimates are model based, assume linearity and that modeled associations reflect causal effects, and should be treated as illustrative. Finally, important unmeasured contextual factors including pathogen load, environmental pollution, local hygiene norms, and healthcare access, could not be included.

Policy Implications

The pronounced north–south gradient demand geographically targeted resource allocation. The Ghana Health Service should strengthen disease surveillance and early warning systems in the Northern, Savannah, and North‐East regions. The Ministry of Sanitation and Water Resources should accompany infrastructure expansion with robust hygiene promotion and behavior change communication, given that WASH hardware alone shows no adjusted health benefit in this analysis. The Ministry of Energy's clean cooking strategy should be intensified, given the counterfactual finding supporting potential diarrhea burden reduction. The Ghana Education Service should invest in girls' education and adult female literacy programs, given the strong maternal education effect on diarrhea. The Ministry of Health and development partners should integrate systematic stunting screening, micronutrient supplementation, and targeted feeding support into routine under‐five care visits. Finally, the National Development Planning Commission and Ghana Statistical Service could institutionalize predictive analytics using routine survey data for risk stratification in health planning, while acknowledging the calibration limitations identified.

Conclusions

Among under‐five children in Ghana, household WASH infrastructure showed no significant adjusted association with diarrhea, ARI, or their comorbidity in this cross‐sectional analysis, after accounting for community‐level clustering and socioeconomic confounders. Child stunting, maternal education, and household wealth were the dominant predictors. A distinct north–south gradient, highlights the need for geographically targeted, multisectoral interventions. Predictive models achieved adequate discrimination for diarrhea and ARI but were poorly calibrated, limiting their use to population‐level risk stratification rather than individual prediction. Of all WASH counterfactual scenarios, only universal access to clean cooking fuel produced a statistically significant modeled reduction in diarrhea burden. Integrating WASH hardware with nutrition programming, maternal education, and clean cooking transitions offers the most promising pathway to reducing childhood morbidity in Ghana and comparable LMIC settings.

Informed consent was obtained from all adult participants; for participants aged 15–17 years, consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians. A formal data access request was submitted to The DHS Program (https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm). The study was conducted in accordance with all relevant regulations and guidelines. All data used were anonymised at source. This secondary analysis of publicly available, de‐identified data did not require additional ethical approval.

Figure S2: Getis‐Ord Gi* hot/cold spot maps for ARI (left), ARI–diarrhoea comorbidity (centre), and diarrhoea (right) across Ghana's 16 administrative regions, 2022 GDHS. Red shading indicates statistically significant hot spots (Gi* z‐score ≥ 1.96; p < 0.05).

Figure S3: Local Moran's I (LISA) cluster maps for ARI (left), ARI–diarrhoea comorbidity (centre), and diarrhoea (right). High‐High clusters (dark red; p < 0.05) indicate regions with high prevalence surrounded by high‐prevalence neighbours. Grey = not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Figure S4: Moran scatter plots for diarrhoea (top left), ARI (top right), and comorbidity (bottom left). The x‐axis shows regional weighted prevalence; the y‐axis shows the spatially lagged prevalence (weighted average of neighbouring regions' prevalence). The dashed red regression line has slope equal to Moran's I. The dotted diagonal represents the 45° reference line.
