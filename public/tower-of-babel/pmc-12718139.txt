Efficacy and predictors of pegylated-interferon in hepatitis B surface antigen seroclearance in immune active and inactive chronic hepatitis B patients
Authors: Chao Han, Linlin Jin, Mujia Zhu, Qiuying Qin, Guiping Li, Zhihong Liu, Xiaoyong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesPegylated interferon (PegIFN) is one of the few strategies capable of achieving hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) seroclearance in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients. However, its role in HBeAg-negative chronic HBV infection (also referred to as immune-inactive or “inactive carrier” phase) remains unclear. This study compared PegIFN efficacy between immune-inactive and immune-active patients and explored predictors of treatment response.Research methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 211 consecutive HBeAg-negative patients treated with PegIFN α-2b in our center from 2019 to 2023, including 139 with immune-inactive chronic HBV infection and 72 immune-active CHB patients. Effectiveness was assessed in the full analysis set (FAS) and efficacy in the per-protocol set (PPS). Predictors of HBsAg seroclearance were identified using Cox regression.ResultsAt week 48, overall HBsAg seroclearance was 26.5 % (FAS), with comparable outcomes between immune-inactive and immune-active groups (26.6 % vs. 26.4 %, P > 0.05). Inactive patients receiving PegIFN + nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) showed no added benefit. In the PPS, clearance reached 69.1 %, but 40.3 % discontinued prematurely—mostly due to subjective intolerance—highlighting a major gap between real-world effectiveness and theoretical efficacy. Multivariate analysis identified two independent predictors: baseline HBsAg <100 IU/mL (HR 2.999, 95 % CI 1.625–5.536, P < 0.001) and on-treatment HBsAg decline ≥1 log10 IU/mL within any 12-week interval (HR 11.205, 95 % CI 4.379–28.674, P < 0.001).ConclusionsPegIFN α-2b achieved clinically meaningful HBsAg seroclearance in both immune-inactive and immune-active patients. Early discontinuation markedly reduced real-world effectiveness. Baseline low HBsAg and dynamic on-treatment decline are pragmatic predictors for optimizing patient selection and guiding interferon-based strategies.Keywords: Chronic hepatitis B, Pegylated interferon α-2b, HBsAg seroclearance, inactive carrier, predictors

Introduction

According to the statistics of the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 296 million individuals worldwide are currently living with chronic HBV infection.1 As of now, China has an estimated 70–80 million people chronically infected with HBV, making chronic HBV infection a continued critical public health priority in China.2 Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) treatment outcomes can be classified into three categories based on serological and virological status: complete cure, functional cure (also termed clinical cure), and partial cure.3 Although the currently accepted criteria for functional cure, including the guidelines for novel hepatitis B drug development issued by China's National Medical Products Administration,4 require reconfirmation of HBsAg seroclearance 24 weeks after discontinuing all medications to establish a functional cure, HBsAg seroclearance still holds significant clinical value.5,6 A large real-world cohort study in Korea indicated that nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs)-treated patients who achieved HBsAg seroclearance had a significantly lower risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HR 0.13, 95 % CI 0.02–0.95, P = 0.04).7 However, NAs monotherapy yields limited HBsAg clearance. A large prospective cohort study from our center revealed that after up to 8 years of NAs therapy, the cumulative HBsAg seroclearance rate was only 4.7 %.8 Hence, sequential pegylated interferon (PegIFN) add-on after NAs treatment has become a widely explored approach.9 A meta-analysis incorporating 60 studies (33 initial combination therapies, 15 add-on strategies, and 12 switch regimens) revealed that initial NAs-IFN combination therapy significantly suggested HBsAg clearance (RR 15.59, 95 %CI 3.22–75.49) versus NAs monotherapy, with comparable efficacy to IFN monotherapy.10 Other strategies to enhance PegIFN efficacy in NAs experienced patients are currently under investigation, including combinations of PegIFN with NAs, extended treatment duration in HBeAg-negative patients,11,12 as well as adding or switching to PegIFN after HBV DNA suppression with NAs.

According to the 2022 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Chronic Hepatitis B, HBeAg-negative patients can be classified into two distinct phases: HBeAg-negative chronic HBV infection and HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis B.1 One study reported that in inactive chronic hepatitis B virus carriers, also referred to as HBeAg-negative chronic HBV infection, PegIFN treatment achieved notably higher HBsAg clearance (47.9 %) and seroconversion rates (36.6 %) at week 48 compared to controls (1.9 % and 0.6 %, respectively).13Multiple studies have also evaluated the efficacy of IFN therapy in chronic HBV infection. In HBeAg-positive patients, an early meta-analysis revealed that IFN treatment resulted in higher rates of HBeAg seroclearance (33 % vs. 12 %), HBV DNA undetectability (37 % vs. 17 %), and HBsAg seroclearance (7.8 % vs. 1.8 %) compared to placebo, along with increased likelihood of HBeAg seroconversion and ALT normalization.14

In this study, we will compare the differences in HBsAg seroclearance rates following interferon therapy between immune-inactive and immune-active patient groups. Furthermore, within the immune-inactive population, we will assess whether combination therapy with NAs yields superior treatment efficacy. Additionally, factors contributing to treatment discontinuation will be analyzed. The effectiveness and efficacy of interferon treatment will be compared using both the Full Analysis Set (FAS) and the Per Protocol Set (PPS). Finally, predictive factors for treatment response to interferon will be identified through regression analysis.

Patients and methods

Study design and patients

We retrospectively and consecutively identified all CHB patients who initiated Peginterferon alfa-2b treatment at the Hepatology Unit of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University between January 1, 2019 and December 30, 2023. Eligible cases were retrieved from the hospital's outpatient electronic system. During the study period, only 10 physicians had eligible cases. Other physicians were not represented because they either had no patients treated with PegIFN or had concurrent enrollment of their eligible patients in other ongoing studies. Thus, all eligible cases within the participating physicians' caseloads were consecutively included without further selection. Comprehensive treatment records were extracted, including PegIFN α-2b formulation, dosage, initiation date, and baseline characteristics (age, sex). Follow-up timepoints included baseline measurements of HBV-DNA, HBsAg, HBeAg, HBeAb, ALT, AST, ALB, TBIL, WBC, NEU, RBC and PLT prior to PegIFN initiation, with subsequent evaluations of these same parameters (HBV-DNA, HBsAg, HBeAg, HBeAb, ALT, AST, TBIL, ALB, WBC, NEU, RBC and PLT) performed at 12-week intervals (weeks 12, 24, 36 and 48) during treatment.

According to the 2022 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Chronic Hepatitis B, HBeAg-negative patients can be classified into two distinct phases: HBeAg-negative chronic HBV infection and HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis B. In this study, treatment naïve patients meeting the following criteria were defined as immune-inactive: HBsAg positivity for more than 6 months, HBeAg negativity, and anti-HBe positivity, corresponding to the HBeAg-negative chronic HBV infection phase. In contrast, NAs experienced patients were enrolled as immune-active group if they had a documented history of hepatic biochemical activity (ALT elevation above the upper limit of normal) or histological evidence of at least moderate fibrosis prior to NAs therapy and the NAs treatment duration was ≥6 months before PegIFN initiation. This group may include patients with HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis B as well as those with prior HBeAg-positive chronic hepatitis B who had achieved HBeAg seroconversion.

The analysis dataset included all consecutively identified patients meeting the predefined inclusion criteria: age 18–70 years, HBsAg positivity >6 months, HBeAg-negative/HBeAb-positive status, with immune inactive patients having HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL and immune active patients <100 IU/mL, ALT below upper limit of normal (35 U/L for males, 25 U/L for females), and normal hematologic indices. Also, patients without subsequent follow-up after initial injection were excluded. The planned follow-up interval was every 12 weeks, and we applied a ±6-week window around each scheduled visit. If multiple assessments occurred within the window, the measurement closest to the nominal visit date was used. The Full Analysis Set (FAS) included all eligible patients who met the inclusion/exclusion criteria (n = 211). The Per-Protocol Set (PPS) consisted of patients who either completed ≥48 weeks of treatment or reached the treatment endpoint prior to Week 48 (n = 81).

The primary endpoint of this study was the HBsAg seroclearance rate at week 48, while secondary endpoints included the magnitude and rate of serum HBV DNA and HBsAg decline at different timepoints, along with treatment-emergent adverse events and corresponding management strategies. Response was defined as: maintained HBeAg negativity with HBV DNA below the lower limit of detection (<100 IU/mL) and HBsAg seroclearance (HBsAg ≤0.05 IU/mL), irrespective of HBsAb positivity.

The follow-up duration was defined as 48 ± 2 weeks after treatment initiation. For patients achieving the endpoint, the time of the first appearance of HBsAg seroclearance was recorded as the event time. Patients who did not achieve seroclearance and missed the week-48 visit were treated as censored at their last available on-treatment assessment. Early discontinuation was coded as "Yes" for patients who discontinued treatment before 48 weeks without achieving HBsAg seroclearance, and as "No" for those who completed ≥48 weeks or reached seroclearance before week-48. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed in the FAS population. Initial univariate Cox regression analysis was performed for all potential covariates. Variables with clinical importance (immune status, NAs co-treatment, baseline HBsAg) were forced into all models, while additional covariates with univariable P < 0.10 were considered candidates. Collinearity was checked using variance inflation factors. Multivariate Cox models were then built, balancing statistical significance and clinical interpretability, rather than by automatic stepwise elimination. To address missing data inherent in this retrospective study, multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 20) was performed, including all covariates and outcome variables; missingness was <10 % for all variables. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and found not to be violated.

Serology testing

The quantification of HBV DNA was performed using highly sensitive real-time fluorescent PCR (polymerase chain reaction), with a lower detection limit of 10 IU/mL. Results were interpreted as positive for HBV DNA >100 IU/mL and negative for HBV DNA ≤100 IU/mL.

Quantitative HBsAg and HBeAg levels were measured by Elecsys HBsAg II Quant and Elecsys HBeAg II Quant assay (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc). The lower detection limit for HBsAg was 0.05 IU/mL. HBsAg >0.05 IU/mL was defined as positive, while HBsAg ≤0.05 IU/mL was considered negative. For HBeAg, values ≥ 1.0 S/CO were defined as positive, and values < 1.0 S/CO were negative. For HBsAb, we did not include it in the assessment because of the missing rate >30 %.

Colorimetric assays were employed to measure alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), total bilirubin (TBIL, μmol/L), and albumin (ALB, g/L).

Automated five-part differential hematology analyzers were used to assess white blood cells (WBC, × 109/L), neutrophils (NEU, × 109/L), red blood cells (RBC, × 1012/L), and platelets (PLT, × 109/L).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0, with categorical variables expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared between groups using chi-square tests, while ordinal data were analyzed by Mann-Whitney U test. Normally or approximately normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between two groups using t-tests or among multiple groups via one-way ANOVA; skewed distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range, M (p25, p75)) and analyzed by Wilcoxon signed-rank test or Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and Kruskal-Wallis H test for multi-group comparisons. Cumulative response rates were compared between groups using Kaplan-Meier analysis with survival curves and log-rank tests. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with treatment response, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CI) used to assess relative risk reliability. Collinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs); variables with VIF >10 were considered collinear and were not included in the same model. Predictive values from regression analysis were used to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the predictive value of different factors for HBsAg response within 48 weeks. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphical representations of statistical results were generated using GraphPad Prism, SPSS, and R software.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

Between January 1, 2019 and December 30, 2023, a total of 393 patients initiated PegIFN α-2b based therapies at the Hepatology Unit of Nanfang Hospital were enrolled (by 10 physicians that were able to share the data; Fig. 1). Of these, 164 were excluded: present with cirrhosis or advanced fibrosis (n = 88), prior interferon exposure (n = 27), co-infection with HCV (n = 4), severe metabolic disorders (n = 4), or a history of malignancy (n = 41). An additional 18 patients were excluded because of lack of follow-up data after the first injection.

In contrast, baseline viral markers differed significantly. HBV DNA was markedly lower in Group C, with nearly all patients (98.6 %) below 100 IU/mL, compared with 45.7 % in Group A and 39.1 % in Group B (P < 0.001). This pattern was expected because Group C consisted of NAs-experienced patients. HBsAg levels were generally below 3 log10 IU/mL in most patients, although the median was slightly higher in Group C (2.2 log10 IU/mL) compared with Groups A and B (1.6 and 1.7 log10 IU/mL, respectively; P = 0.017). Stratified analysis showed that approximately one quarter of patients had HBsAg <10 IU/mL, while the majority fell between 10 and 1000 IU/mL. Group C contained a greater proportion of patients in the higher HBsAg strata (100–1000 IU/mL), whereas Groups A and B had relatively more patients with lower baseline levels (<100 IU/mL).

Safety and early discontinuation

Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were common but mostly mild to moderate. Overall, 35 patients (16.6 %) experienced hematologic abnormalities, making it the most common category of adverse events, followed by alopecia (9.5 %) and influenza-like symptoms (9.5 %) (Table 2). Other TEAEs, including dermatologic effects (7.1 %), neuropsychiatric symptoms (4.3 %), gastrointestinal disturbances (3.8 %), fatigue (1.9 %), weight loss (1.4 %), myalgia (0.9 %), respiratory symptoms (0.9 %), insomnia (0.9 %), and endocrine/metabolic disorders (0.9 %), occurred at lower frequencies.

The distribution of TEAEs was broadly comparable across groups, with no significant intergroup differences. Hematologic abnormalities were slightly more frequent in Group A (18.1 %) compared with Groups B (13.0 %) and C (15.3 %), while influenza-like symptoms occurred somewhat more often in Group C (12.5 %). Alopecia was most frequently reported in Group A (12.1 %). Nevertheless, the overall safety profile was consistent across groups.

A total of 85 patients (40.3 %) discontinued treatment before week 48. Of these, 35 (16.6 %) discontinued due to objectively documented adverse reactions (e.g., cytopenia, organ toxicity), while 50 (23.7 %) stopped because of subjective intolerance (e.g., alopecia, fatigue, neuropsychiatric complaints, Table S1). This indicates that subjective intolerance, rather than objective toxicity, was the leading cause of treatment discontinuation.

Primary outcomes

In the FAS (n = 211), the overall 48-week HBsAg seroclearance was 26.5 % (56/211): 27.6 % (32/116) in Group A, 21.7 % (5/23) in Group B, and 26.4 % (19/72) in Group C (P = 0.845). In the PPS (n = 81), which included patients completing ≥48 weeks or achieving endpoints earlier, the overall seroclearance rate was markedly higher at 69.1 % (56/81), with group-specific rates of 72.7 % (32/44), 62.5 % (5/8), and 65.5 % (19/29), respectively (P = 0.737; Fig. 2B).

HBsAg decline was similar across groups. Median reductions in serum HBsAg from baseline to Week 48 were –1.20 log10 IU/mL (IQR –2.15 to −0.53) in Group A, −1.05 log10 IU/mL (IQR –1.98 to −0.41) in Group B, and −1.18 log10 IU/mL (IQR –2.09 to −0.47) in Group C, with no significant intergroup differences (P = 0.750; Fig. S1A).

To further examine treatment outcomes by immune status, Groups A and B were pooled as immune-inactive and compared with immune-active patients (Group C). Kaplan–Meier analysis again indicated no significant difference between the two categories (P = 0.985; Fig. 2C). In the FAS, HBsAg seroclearance was 26.6 % in the immune-inactive cohort and 26.4 % in the immune-active cohort, while in the PPS, seroclearance rates were 71.2 % and 65.5 %, respectively, with no significant differences (all P > 0.05; Fig. 2D). Therefore, subsequent analyses were performed mainly in the FAS to ensure completeness of data and minimize selection bias.

Baseline and On-Treatment Predictors of HBsAg seroclearance

A stepwise approach was used to identify predictors, beginning with univariate Cox models, followed by subgroup analyses, and finally multivariate regression.

In the univariate Cox regression analyses, most baseline demographic and biochemical characteristics were not significantly associated with week-48 HBsAg seroclearance (Table 3). Neither age (HR 0.980, 95 % CI 0.948–1.012, P = 0.223) nor gender (HR 1.357, 95 % CI 0.768–2.399, P = 0.293) showed a measurable effect. Similarly, routine liver function markers such as ALT, AST, albumin, total bilirubin, and platelet count yielded hazard ratios close to unity with non-significant confidence intervals. Baseline HBV DNA levels also had no association with response (P = 0.867). In addition, immune-inactive carrier status (HR 1.005, P = 0.985) and concomitant NAs therapy (HR 0.891, P = 0.670) were not predictive. Collectively, these findings suggest that host demographics, conventional biochemical indices, and baseline virological status alone may not explain the variability in interferon response. Nevertheless, given our specific interest in evaluating the differences between inactive and active populations and whether immune-inactive patients benefit from NAs co-administration, these two parameters were deliberately retained in subsequent multivariate models despite their lack of statistical significance in univariate analysis. In addition, baseline HBsAg,15 rate of HBsAg decline,16 treatment duration,17 and ALT trend18 have all been previously reported as key predictors of interferon efficacy, and were therefore also prioritized in our further analyses.

Subgroup stratification by baseline HBsAg level revealed a pronounced gradient in treatment response. Patients with baseline HBsAg <10 IU/mL achieved the highest HBsAg seroclearance, with 42.9 % (21/49) attaining seroclearance by week 48 within 48 weeks. Those with 10–100 IU/mL had a seroclearance rate of 39.2 % (20/51), whereas rates declined sharply in patients with 100–1000 IU/mL (16.3 %, 14/86) and ≥1000 IU/mL (5.6 %, 1/18). None of the seven patients with HBsAg ≥ 3000 IU/mL achieved seroclearance. A clear dose–response relationship was evident (Fig. 3A, χ2 P < 0.001), indicating progressively lower likelihood of seroclearance with increasing baseline HBsAg levels. ROC analysis identified 2.135 log10 IU/mL (≈136 IU/mL) as the optimal cutoff (AUC 0.67, 95 % CI 0.59–0.75; Fig. 3B), supporting the discriminatory ability of baseline HBsAg. Consistently, Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified at 100 IU/mL confirmed baseline HBsAg as a significant determinant of seroclearance (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. S1B). In line with these findings, univariate analysis identified baseline HBsAg <100 IU/mL (HR 3.779, 95 %CI 2.09–6.832; P < 0.001) as predictor (Table 3).

Subgroup exploration of on-treatment HBsAg dynamics revealed distinct kinetic patterns between responders and non-responders. As shown in Fig. S1C non-responders displayed only minimal antigen decline throughout therapy, whereas both early responders (≤ 24 weeks) and late responders (24–48 weeks) exhibited phases of steep HBsAg reduction, with markedly greater slopes of decline during certain treatment intervals. This observation suggested that transient periods of rapid HBsAg reduction might serve as an early signal of treatment success, regardless of the absolute timing of response. Based on this finding, and considering both clinical applicability and ease of use in real-world practice, we evaluated whether achieving a ≥ 1 log10 IU/mL decline in HBsAg within any 12-week interval could serve as a practical predictor of treatment outcome. Patients who met this criterion (n = 98) indicated a significantly higher 48-week seroclearance rate compared with those who did not (52.0 % vs. 5.2 %, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). ROC analysis further confirmed the predictive utility of this parameter, with an AUC of 0.785 (95 % CI 0.718–0.852; Fig. 3D). The sensitivities of the two predictive indicators were 91.1 % and 73.2 %, respectively, while their specificities were 65.9 % and 61.9 %, respectively, indicating stronger discriminatory performance of achieving a ≥ 1 log10 IU/mL decline in HBsAg within any 12-week interval than baseline HBsAg level alone. Consistently, univariate Cox regression identified a ≥ 1 log10 IU/mL HBsAg decline within any 12-week period as a powerful predictor of seroclearance (HR 13.546, 95 % CI 5.398–33.993; P < 0.001). Collectively, these results highlight the prognostic value of dynamic on-treatment HBsAg decline as a clinically feasible predictor of interferon efficacy.

Subgroup analysis according to treatment duration highlighted that patients treated for fewer than 12 weeks had almost no chance of achieving HBsAg seroclearance. As shown in Fig. S1D, none of the nine patients who discontinued within 4 weeks responded, and only 2 of 23 patients (8.7 %) treated for 4–11 weeks achieved seroclearance. By contrast, substantially higher rates were observed among those treated for ≥12 weeks, with seroclearance achieved in 25.0 % (10/40) of patients treated for 12–23 weeks, 32.7 % (34/104) for 24–37 weeks, and 28.6 % (10/35) for ≥ 48 weeks. In this cohort, patients who discontinued treatment within 12 weeks rarely achieved HBsAg seroclearance, while those treated beyond this point showed higher response rates (Log rank P = 0.008). Consistently, univariate Cox regression confirmed that early discontinuation was strongly associated with treatment failure (HR 0.185, 95 % CI 0.045–0.757; P = 0.019). Collectively, these results demonstrate that patients who discontinue prematurely have a negligible probability of seroclearance, underscoring the need to maintain therapy beyond the early phase. Cumulative seroclearance curves and additional group comparisons are provided in Fig. S1E-F.

Analysis of ALT dynamics during treatment showed that the majority of patients experienced ALT elevations irrespective of treatment response. As illustrated in Fig. S1G, ALT levels typically rose during the early treatment phase, peaking around week 12 at approximately three times baseline, before gradually declining yet remaining above pretreatment levels at week 48. Importantly, responders and non-responders exhibited similar temporal patterns, with no substantial differences in the magnitude or timing of ALT flares. When patients were stratified according to peak ALT levels relative to the upper limit of normal (ULN), those with higher peaks appeared to have numerically greater rates of HBsAg seroclearance (≤1 × ULN: 15.0 %, 1–2 × ULN: 28.4 %, 2–5 × ULN: 32.8 %, >5 × ULN: 38.5 %). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance (χ2 P = 0.091; Fig. S1H). Consistently, univariate Cox regression showed no significant association between ALT flare (defined as ALT elevation >5 × ULN) and HBsAg seroclearance (HR 2.019, 95 % CI 0.806–5.060; P = 0.134; Fig. S1I). Taken together, these findings suggest that ALT elevation is a common on-treatment phenomenon but does not independently distinguish responders from non-responders.

The final multivariate model identified two independent predictors of HBsAg seroclearance (Table 3). Patients with baseline HBsAg <100 IU/mL had approximately a three-fold higher likelihood of achieving clearance compared with those with higher levels (HR 2.999, 95 % CI 1.625–5.536; P < 0.001). Achieving a ≥1 log10 IU/mL decline during any 12-week interval was the strongest predictor, associated with more than an eleven-fold increased probability of clearance (HR 11.205, 95 % CI 4.379–28.674; P < 0.001). In contrast, treatment duration, immune-inactive status, concomitant NAs therapy, and ALT trend did not retain independent significance after adjustment. No substantial collinearity was detected among variables included in the final multivariate model.

Discussion

Interferon therapy remains one of the reliable approaches to achieve HBsAg seroclearance13 in selected patients. In this retrospective study, we provide novel real-world evidence on the efficacy and safety of pegylated interferon–based therapy in HBeAg-negative immune-inactive patients, a population in which the role of interferon has been under debate. We further identified baseline HBsAg level and on-treatment HBsAg decline as major determinants of treatment response.

The efficacy of interferon therapy in immune-inactive patients has remained uncertain.19 An observational study reported that NAs monotherapy yields a low HBsAg seroclearance rate of only about 3 %, while combining PegIFN after NAs antiviral therapy can improve HBsAg seroclearance.9 In this study, among the 139 immune-inactive patients, the 48-week HBsAg seroclearance rate was 26.6 % in the FAS, which is broadly consistent with previous reports. Immune-active patients with prior NAs exposure was included as a contemporaneous reference group in this study. The observed seroclearance rates were similar between immune-inactive and immune-active patients in this cohort. Besides, the lack of significant differences among group A and B indicates that no clear benefit of NAs addition was observed, though interpretation is limited by the small size of the combination subgroup and imbalanced baseline characteristics.

When interpreting these findings, it is important to recognize that the observed differences in baseline viral markers reflected biological distinctions between immune-inactive and immune-active phases, rather than sampling bias. At our center, most patients who initiated PegIFN therapy had baseline HBsAg levels below 1000 IU/mL, regardless of immune phase. However, as we enroll continuously to construct a natural cohort, those classified as inactive carriers (Groups A and B) tended to have even lower HBsAg levels. Limited by the sample size and the patients’ diversity, propensity scores matching or weighting could not be applied. Nevertheless, multivariate Cox regression confirmed that neither inactive carrier status (HR 0.44, 95 % CI 0.16–1.20; P = 0.109) nor concomitant NAs therapy (HR 0.59, 95 % CI 0.23–1.51; P = 0.270) was independently associated with HBsAg seroclearance, after adjusted by the baseline HBsAg levels.

Safety outcomes were generally comparable across groups, with adverse events mostly mild to moderate. Nevertheless, our study revealed a marked discrepancy between the theoretical efficacy (PPS) and real-world effectiveness (FAS) of interferon therapy. While the PPS showed a seroclearance rate close to 70 %, the FAS analysis indicated only 26.5 %, largely because about 40 % of patients discontinued prematurely before week 48 and also reflects the enrichment of patients with very low baseline HBsAg levels, who inherently have a higher likelihood of achieving HBsAg loss. Importantly, discontinuations were not only attributable to objectively documented adverse events but were largely driven by subjective intolerance (accounting for nearly 60 %). Although treatment duration was not retained as an independent predictor in multivariate analysis, patients with shorter course exhibited minimal effectiveness. None of the nine patients treated for fewer than 4 weeks achieved HBsAg seroclearance, and only 2 of 32 patients (6.2 %) treated for less than 12 weeks responded, which was significantly lower than among those treated beyond this threshold (P = 0.008). In line with this, we incorporated treatment duration into our predictive framework by using a ≥1 log10 IU/mL HBsAg decline within any 12-week interval as a practical marker, which inherently reflects that patients treated for fewer than 12 weeks are unlikely to benefit. Moreover, whether patients with subjective intolerance but without objective contraindications should continue interferon therapy could be guided by their on-treatment response. A decline in HBsAg ≥1 log10 IU/mL within any preceding 12-week treatment interval may serve as a practical indicator to support individualized decisions on treatment continuation.

The substantial gap between the FAS and PPS outcomes underscores the real-world challenges of PegIFN therapy, primarily related to tolerability and adherence. Identifying early predictors of treatment response may therefore support individualized therapy and reduce unnecessary exposure. Previous studies have identified several clinical factors for prediction of interferon response, including baseline HBV DNA, HBsAg level, rate of HBsAg decline, and ALT flare.20 In our final multivariate analysis, two factors remained independently associated with HBsAg seroclearance: baseline HBsAg <100 IU/mL (HR 2.999) and an on-treatment HBsAg decline ≥1 log10 IU/mL within any 12-week interval (HR 11.205). Baseline low-level HBsAg has been consistently identified across previous studies as a key pretreatment predictor, reflecting a lower antigen burden or a more active immune system. In our study, this parameter was not collinear with on-treatment kinetics, suggesting complementary roles between HBsAg baseline levels and on-treatment kinetics. Besides, a HBsAg decline ≥1 log10 IU/mL within any 12-week interval conferred more than an eleven-fold increased chance of seroclearance and yielding an AUROC of 0.785 (Fig. 3D). The high sensitivity of this parameter implies that patients showing only modest declines are very unlikely to achieve seroclearance. This distinction may be particularly valuable for patients experiencing subjective intolerance, whereas its absence might justify early discontinuation.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively limited, which restricted our ability to apply propensity score matching or weighting methods to fully balance baseline characteristics. Nevertheless, given the substantial number of endpoint events, the multivariate regression analysis still provided adequate statistical power to identify independent predictors. In addition, due to constraints such as detection costs and methodological limitations, clinical testing for biomarkers including HBV genotype was not performed. Based on findings from other researches,21 more comprehensive testing and analysis should be conducted to identify additional predictive biomarkers for interferon treatment response. Second, an external validation cohort was lacking. Therefore, we did not attempt to establish a prediction model, and the generalizability of our findings requires further confirmation. Third, follow-up was confined to the treatment period, and we did not extend observation to 24 weeks off treatment after HBsAg seroclearance. As a result, our endpoint does not fully meet the current definition of functional cure.22 Nevertheless, previous studies have indicated that relapse after interferon-induced HBsAg seroclearance is rare, supporting the clinical relevance of our findings. The last but not the least, due to the real-world setting of this study, treatment initiation for inactive carriers was occasionally influenced by patient preference rather than uniform indications. Therefore, subtle inter-physician differences in treatment initiation criteria may exist, which should be considered when interpreting our findings.

In conclusion, PegIFN remains an appropriate treatment option for selected immune-inactive and immune-active patients, achieving clinically meaningful rates of HBsAg seroclearance in real-world settings. The divergence between efficacy and effectiveness underscores the challenge of early discontinuation, particularly driven by subjective intolerance. Readily applicable predictors based on HBsAg kinetics provide a pragmatic tool to guide treatment decisions, helping to identify patients most likely to benefit from Peg-IFN while avoiding unnecessary exposure in those unlikely to respond.
