Bacterial 3D genome architecture: organization, regulation, and synthetic biology applications
Authors: Mao Chen, Ziyi Zhu, Yuhuan Huang, Bo Wu, Mingxiong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial chromosomes are compacted into hierarchical three-dimensional (3D) architectures that underpin essential processes such as replication, transcription, and segregation. However, detailed principles governing genome architecture and its regulation remain incompletely understood. Here we review current knowledge on structural organization of bacterial 3D genomes and their regulatory factors, integrating insights from chromosome conformation capture and imaging studies. We highlight how genome architecture dynamically responds to environmental cues. We also discuss potential applications of bacterial 3D genomics, particularly in synthetic biology. These findings provide a framework for understanding bacterial genomic homeostasis and adaptation, offering opportunities for engineering microbial systems and novel therapies.

Introduction

In all living organisms, chromosomal DNA is compressed by more than 10,000-fold, to accommodate it in the limited volume of cells [1]. The organization of the chromosome is not random and demonstrates a certain pattern, although these rules are not fully understood yet. The 3D structure of chromosomes is associated with various processes such as transcription, DNA replication, and chromosome segregation [2]. These structural configurations are now considered a useful framework for elucidating structure–function relationships. In recent years, chromosome conformation capture (3C) based techniques, imaging approaches, and protein-DNA interaction methods have become the main tools for studying chromosome architecture [3–5]. For eukaryotes, high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) technology reveals that chromosomes are organized into a series of topologically associating domains (TADs) [6]. The factors that generate, maintain, and influence these structural domains are difficult to understand, although many chromosomal structural proteins are involved in maintaining chromosome conformation [7–9]. In 2011, the 3C-derived method 5 C (Chromosome Conformation Capture Carbon Copy) was first applied to capture the chromosome architecture of the Caulobacter genome [10]. Since then, Hi-C with enhanced throughput and resolution has been applied to reveal the chromosomal organization of many other bacterial species [11–14]. These studies have provided insights into the diversity of chromosome architectures and common features among bacteria, as well as the roles of diverse factors in chromosomal organization at different levels of structural complexity. In bacteria, the chromosome is typically a single double-stranded DNA circle, whereas some bacterial species possess chromosomal variants, such as linear chromosomes [15]. The distinct architecture and regulation of bacterial genomes result in unique mechanisms of chromosomal organization, which may rival the complexity observed in eukaryotes [16]. While chromosomes in different bacteria often display similar organizational patterns, these similarities provide an opportunity to investigate evolutionary trends in bacterial genomics from a 3D structural perspective. Recent studies have begun to elucidate the connections between 3D genome organization, gene expression, and environmental adaptation. Nevertheless, the fundamental biological significance of the bacterial 3D genome remains unclear, and synthesizing the various proposed models into a unified theoretical framework remains a significant challenge. In this review, we summarize current advances in bacterial 3D genomics and explore their potential applications in bacterial biology.

Principles of bacterial chromosome architecture

The first chromosome architecture captured by Hi-C was in Caulobacter crescentus, with a resolution of 10 kb [11]. The 3D structures of many well-known bacterial chromosomes, including those of Escherichia coli [13], Bacillus subtilis [12, 17–19], Mycoplasma pneumoniae [20], Pseudomonas aeruginosa [21], Corynebacterium glutamicum [22], Brucella melitensis [23], Streptomyces [24–26], Zymomonas mobilis [14], among others, have since been successfully resolved. Unconventional bacteria with specialized functions have also been studied, broadening our understanding of microbial genome conformation, as reviewed by Lioy and others [15, 27–29]. Interestingly, the spatial conformation of chromosomes in the most commonly studied bacteria, whether they have single or multiple circular chromosomes, has been modeled as ellipsoids [10, 12, 13, 20, 26].

The bacterial circular chromosome is divided into two replichores (arms) by a line traversing the origin and terminus of replication. Each replichore represents one half of the chromosome and defines a direction of replication from origin to terminus. At the genomic scale, cross-species Hi-C analyses of bacteria with circular chromosomes have revealed two dominant chromosomal architectures: (i) Transversal disposition, represented by E. coli, where the chromosome arms are located in the opposite halves of the nucleoid (Fig. 1). In this arrangement, the replication origin and terminus are closer to the cell center, and there is no interaction between the chromosomal arms [13, 30]. (ii) Longitudinal disposition, as exemplified by C. crescentus and B. subtilis, where the two chromosome arms are arrayed along the long axis of the cell (Fig. 1). In general, high-frequency interactions, commonly referred to as intra-arm interactions, are frequently observed between adjacent genomic loci within chromosome arms. These interactions manifest as a main diagonal in Hi-C contact maps [11, 13, 14]. In longitudinal configuration, although the two replichores are linearly separated and often occupy distinct longitudinal positions within the cell, Hi-C contact maps reveal that they are nonetheless spatially proximate in 3D space, allowing for frequent inter-arm interactions [11, 16]. These interactions appear as a secondary diagonal in the Hi-C contact map. The presence of both main and secondary diagonals in bacterial Hi-C maps usually reflects a longitudinal spatial arrangement of the chromosome [12, 21, 29]. It should be noted, however, that the absence of inter-arm interactions does not conclusively indicate a transversal configuration, as longitudinal arrangements without significant inter-arm interactions have also been observed [20]. In these conformations, the absence of inter-arm contacts is reflected by the presence of only a primary diagonal in the Hi-C contact map [13, 20, 31, 32]. Inter-chromosomal contacts have also been observed on the linear chromosome of Streptomyces [24–26]. Therefore, for bacteria exhibiting either transversal or longitudinal genome organization, methods such as Fluorescent Repressor-Operator System (FROS) should be employed to accurately determine the positions of the origin and terminus relative to the cell poles [33, 34]. Importantly, the Hi-C results in bacteria demonstrated that two loci, which may be distant in terms of their linear genomic positions, can still interact. These interactions are often reflected as scattered high-frequency points on the contact map, a scattering pattern frequently observed in bacterial Hi-C studies. The term “contact” refers to the predefined window sizes (bins) used to segment the bacterial genome in the construction of the Hi-C contact map [14]. The resolution of the contact map is determined by the size of these windows. It is important to note that current insights into bacterial chromosome organization are primarily based on population-averaged Hi-C data, which can mask cell-to-cell heterogeneity and only provide a snapshot of chromosome conformation at a specific growth phase. As a result, these data may not fully capture the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of bacterial chromosome organization. Nevertheless, Hi-C has been invaluable for uncovering the hierarchical architecture of bacterial chromosomes at multiple spatial scales.

Macrodomains are a global characteristic of some bacterial chromosomes. In E. coli, the chromosome is segmented into four macrodomains: the origin of replication (Ori), the terminus of replication (Ter) region, and the left and right regions [13, 35, 36]. In B. subtilis, the nucleoid adopts a helicoidal shape, an observation consistent with the bottle-brush model, wherein a confined bottle-brush polymer naturally forms spiral patterns [37]. Within this architecture, the Ori and Ter macrodomains remain distinct. Furthermore, the chromosomal arms are in close juxtaposition, leading to strong interactions between the two arms that form a third macrodomain [12]. While these bacteria exhibit conserved chromosomal folding principles, macrodomains are not universal architectural features. For example, Z. mobilis exhibits a chromosome conformation similar to that of E. coli but lacks distinct macrodomains, resulting in a more uniform chromosomal structure [14]. While the existence of macrodomain-like structures in Streptococcus pneumoniae has been hypothesized, their definitive confirmation awaits further experimental validation [38]. Thus, these results indicate that macrodomains are a characteristic feature of only a subset of bacterial species.

Eukaryotic chromosomes are organized as hierarchical structures, and TADs are critical structural units in the 3D organization of eukaryotic genomes [39, 40]. Like hierarchical structures, bacterial chromosomes are also organized into domains, referred to as chromosomal interaction domains (CIDs, Fig. 2a) [11]. The size of CIDs ranges from a few kilobases (kb) to several thousand kb (Table 1). This variation is primarily influenced by biological factors, including genome size, the presence of large and highly expressed genes (HEGs), and the physiological state of the bacteria [11–13]. Additionally, technical parameters such as experimental method and bioinformatics algorithms can influence the observed size and resolution of domains [41, 42]. Mapping the CID boundaries is an important step in identifying these domains. In bacteria, directional preference analysis remains the most widely used method for this purpose, while insulation scores and frontier index provide complementary insights into specific species [13, 14, 43]. Bacterial genome sizes vary substantially, ranging from the highly reduced genomes of M. pneumoniae to the large, complex genomes of Streptomyces species (Table 1). The disparity in genome size between these organisms is nearly tenfold, which largely accounts for the substantial differences in domain sizes observed at comparable contact map resolutions. Notably, even bacterial genomes of similar size can exhibit variations in both the number and size of CIDs, highlighting the dominant role of the other aforementioned biological factors. Furthermore, similar to eukaryotes, several studies have confirmed the existence of nested organizational hierarchies within bacterial chromosomes, referred to as nested domains [11, 14, 16, 17, 33]. These structures are hypothesized to be associated with the macrodomains identified in E. coli [20].

Currently, the resolution of contact maps generated by Hi-C technology typically ranges from 2 to 10 kb (Table 1). However, functional genomic elements such as genes and operons operate at scales below this resolution limit, making it difficult to establish clear connections between basal transcriptional activity and higher-order chromosome architecture. Recent advances in sub-kb Hi-C have enabled the identification of discrete transcription-induced domains (TIDs, 1–20 kb) formed by active transcriptional units (Fig. 2a) [41]. These TIDs are now recognized as fundamental building blocks of bacterial chromosome folding. They impose localized mechanical and topological constraints and, through their aggregation, are likely responsible for the emergence of the larger-scale CIDs observed in macro-scale analyses [41]. The enhanced Micro-C chromosome conformation capture technique has resolved the ultra-high-resolution 3D structure of the E. coli nucleoid at 10 bp resolution. This study reveals that all actively transcribed genes form distinct operon-sized CIDs (OPCIDs) in a transcription-dependent manner [42]. Despite the Hi-C potential to unravel the bacterial genome architecture, it is important to acknowledge that current Hi-C methodologies still fall short of providing a complete, high-resolution picture of its intricate structure. Therefore, the continuous development of higher-resolution methods will significantly advance the study of how genome 3D architecture drives cellular functions. Altogether, current studies suggest that domains play a fundamental role in genome organization throughout the evolution of bacteria.

As mentioned previously, the formation of bacterial chromosomal domains is closely linked to transcription [41, 42]. The recent phase separation model provides a framework for understanding this complicated functional organization (Fig. 2b and c). This model proposes that the nucleoid forms a DNA phase separate from the riboprotein phase (the riboid) [44]. The nucleoid-riboid interphase is a transcription zone enriched with NAPs, RNA polymerase, and nascent transcripts. For instance, when E. coli enters the log phase, the rapid transition of RNAP from a dispersed to a clustered localization pattern provides direct evidence for phase separation [45]. This model aligns with the formation of TIDs and OPCIDs within the nucleoid phase, which are driven by transcriptional activity. We hypothesize these transcription-driven, self-organizing units subsequently aggregate to form larger CIDs and macrodomains, as observed in lower-resolution Hi-C contact maps, analogous to the side polymers in bottle-brush models [37]. Therefore, the phase separation model reframes the nucleoid as an active, liquid-like condensate that coordinates central dogma activities [44].

Chromosomes of higher eukaryotes possess distinct compartments characterized by differing transcriptional activities [46]. The well-characterized A/B compartments in eukaryotes distinguish between transcriptionally active and inactive regions. Similarly, in archaea such as Sulfolobus species, the genome contains three replication origins and is organized into two compartments. Their Hi-C contact maps exhibit a “plaid pattern”, demonstrating a compartmentalization of transcriptional states analogous to the chromosome architecture observed in eukaryotes [47, 48]. Compartments have been identified in only a few bacterial species. In Streptomyces, the 3D architecture of the linear chromosome displays dynamic compartmentalization, which is closely linked to gene expression and life cycle [24–26]. Studies have shown that the chromosome can be partitioned into structurally distinct compartments: a transcriptionally active central region (central compartment), bounded by conserved and highly expressed genes and divided into multiple domains, and two terminal regions that are enriched in biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) for specialized metabolites and are typically transcriptionally silent [25, 26]. Notably, this compartmentalization is not static. As the bacterium transitions from the growth phase to metabolic differentiation (e.g., antibiotic synthesis) or sporulation, the overall chromosome architecture undergoes significant restructuring, shifting from a relatively “open” conformation to a “closed” one. During this process, highly expressed BGCs form new boundaries and become integrated into the genomic architecture [25]. The dynamic nature of this compartmentalization may be associated with a phase separation mechanism.

In eukaryotes and archaea, genomic compartments are distributed throughout the genome, whereas in Streptomyces, compartmentalization is more localized. Despite these structural differences, transcriptional activity consistently serves as a key driver of higher-order chromosomal architecture across these domains of life, suggesting a conserved organizational principle. However, in bacteria, compartmentalization is relatively rare and represents just one aspect of higher-order chromosome architecture. Overall, from global genome folding to local structural arrangements, the 3D organization of bacterial genomes is more complex than previously appreciated.

Regulators of bacterial genome architecture

The folding of the bacterial genome involves the interplay of diverse factors, including DNA supercoiling, transcriptional regulation, and some specialized chromosomal proteins [13, 16, 30]. The commonly known chromosomal factors in bacteria are the structural maintenance of chromosome (SMC) complex and NAPs. In bacteria, the SMC complex, also known as the condensin complex, can be classified into three major types: Smc-ScpAB, MukBEF, and MksBEF. SMC complexes are widely distributed across most bacterial phyla, with MukBEF predominantly found in Gammaproteobacteria, whereas MksBEF shares structural similarities with MukBEF and is distributed sporadically across the phylogenetic tree [13, 15]. The primary function of SMC proteins is chromosome organization. In certain bacterial species, such as B. subtilis and C. crescentus, SMC proteins also promote inter-arm contacts, which contribute to the formation of the secondary diagonal observed in contact maps [11, 12, 18]. In contrast, the deletion of the condensin complex weakens inter-arm interactions, whereas the intra-arm contacts and domain boundaries are largely unaffected [11].

The SMC complex has been reported to play a role in chromosomal loop formation [49, 50]. The SMC dimer anchors the kleisin complex, which captures and loops two DNA segments to form a chromosomal loop. Alternatively, two SMC monomers cooperate to drive loop formation [16]. In C. crescentus, the SMC complex is recruited to parS sites near the origin and initiates translocation toward the terminus, and mediates arm alignment [51]. However, this translocation is slowed upon encountering HEGs, interfering with arm alignment [51]. The parS sites are clustered DNA sequences typically located near the origin of replication; they serve as the binding sites for ParB, a key protein in the parABS system [52–54]. This system is found in many but not all bacteria and plays a major role in chromosome segregation in some species, while others utilize alternative mechanisms [54–56]. In species that rely on the parABS system for chromosome segregation, the binding of ParB to parS sites promotes the loading of SMC complexes near the origin of replication, and ParB mutants in these species exhibit defects in chromosome segregation [22, 52, 57]. In B. subtilis, the replenishment of ParB promotes SMC binding, driving the compaction of chromosomal loops and the progressive organization of chromosomal arms from the origin to the terminus [12, 18]. On the other hand, repositioning of parS in C. crescentus induces chromosomal rotation, altering the subcellular localization of numerous genes without affecting their transcription [10]. ParB binds to DNA at sites distal to parS and recruits additional ParB molecules from the bulk pool [53]. Both SMC and ParB are required for the formation and maintenance of the origin-associated macrodomain [11, 17]. The chromosome structure of Streptomyces during the exponential phase correlates with genetic compartmentalization, and ParB and SMC promote proximity between chromosomal arms [24, 25]. Deacetylation of ParB facilitates chromosome segregation in S. coelicolor by enhancing its binding to the centromere-like parS sequence [57].

In bacteria such as E. coli, which lack inter-arm contacts, the SMC homolog MukBEF facilitates long-range chromosomal interactions (> 280 kb). However, these interactions exclude the replication terminus region because the folding activity of MukBEF is restricted by the MatP protein [13, 58, 59]. Similarly, in Borrelia burgdorferi, the MksB protein promotes long-range intra-arm DNA interactions [60, 61]. Collectively, these studies indicate that condensin-mediated chromosome organization varies among bacterial species, with global chromosome architecture arising from either the independent or hierarchical activities of condensin complexes.

In addition to condensin complexes, NAPs constitute a major class of chromosomal factors that shape bacterial genome structure by modulating local chromosome architecture [13, 62]. NAPs are ubiquitously present across bacteria, with major members including H-NS, IHF, HU, and Fis. The abundance of these proteins varies significantly between species, driving species-specific 3D chromosome organization [16]. NAPs are generally considered to bind nonspecifically, with a preference for AT-rich regions, thereby driving chromosome folding into distinct spatial scales and conformations [16]. NAPs modulate DNA topology through bending, bridging, or wrapping, which in turn regulates transcription and shapes bacterial gene expression [62]. The roles of canonical NAPs in bacterial chromosome architecture have been well characterized. In E. coli, HU (histone-like protein from strain U93) promotes short-range interactions (50–280 kb), Fis favors communication, and H-NS (histone-like nucleoid-structuring protein) has only local effects on DNA conformation [13]. HU binds distorted DNA regions as homodimers or heterodimers, compacting nucleic acids through DNA bending or wrapping [13, 63]. HU plays a fundamental and highly species-specific role in shaping chromosome architecture. In E. coli, HU facilitates long-range chromosomal interactions, while in C. crescentus, it promotes short-range DNA compaction [11, 13]. Its functional diversity is further illustrated by the role of the HU homolog HupS in organizing terminal chromosomal domains in Streptomyces and the requirement of HBsu for DNA replication initiation in B. subtilis [24, 64]. Moreover, in Deinococcus radiodurans, HU facilitates stress-induced nucleoid reorganization by modulating its diffusion dynamics, which enables rapid condensation followed by controlled decompaction to restore nucleoid structure before cell division [65]. HU is likely to function as a multifunctional regulator in bacteria, facilitating diverse chromosomal organization. Its evolutionary adaptability enables it to perform both structural and regulatory functions, which allows it to participate in shaping the bacterial genome.

H-NS, a histone analog, binds to AT-rich regions and serves as a sensor for environmental changes in specific bacteria [66–69]. H-NS functions as a transcriptional repressor that silences horizontally acquired genes, whose expression could impair bacterial fitness. At the chromosome conformation level, localized H-NS binding restricts interactions between its target loci and adjacent regions, whereas H-NS depletion has minimal impact on global chromosome architecture [13]. Furthermore, diverse H-NS-like proteins, known as Lsr2, MvaT, MvaU, Rok, and MucR, are also present in various bacterial species [70–74]. Among these, Lsr2 is conserved in mycobacteria and actinomycetes, where it functions as a DNA-bridging protein that compacts the bacterial chromosome [74]. In B. subtilis, Rok, a functional homolog of H-NS, organizes nucleoprotein complexes that mediate long-range chromosomal interactions, driving loop formation via mechanisms analogous to those of the eukaryotic insulator protein CTCF [75]. Rok’s DNA-bridging activity exhibits intrinsic resistance to physico-chemical perturbations, highlighting its mechanistic divergence from canonical H-NS proteins, although both share roles in genome organization and xenogeneic silencing [71]. MucR serves as a primary functional analog of H-NS in α-proteobacteria, where it acts as a nucleoid structuring protein that silences xenogeneic AT-rich DNA to regulate important processes such as symbiosis and pathogenesis [72, 76]. Similar to other H-NS-like proteins, MvaT and MvaU preferentially bind to intrinsically curved AT-rich regions of the genome. However, in contrast to the AT-hook motif used by H-NS and Lsr2, MvaT employs a unique “AT-pincer” mechanism to silence foreign DNA [73].

Overall, NAPs are defined by two conserved properties: their capacity to bind DNA and organize chromosomal structures [16, 62]. Additionally, certain NAPs exhibit transcriptional regulation, either fully or partially [62, 77]. Interestingly, transcription factors and NAPs share functional overlaps in both DNA binding and genome organization [78]. In reviewing the classic Jacob-Monod model, it becomes evident that gene expression is controlled by promoters, with their activity being regulated by transcription factors. Transcription factor binding not only fulfills regulatory functions but also shapes chromosome architecture [78]. The boundary between transcription factors and NAPs is increasingly blurred, as many proteins exhibit hybrid roles spanning both functional categories [78]. Amemiya and colleagues developed a computational screening pipeline to identify candidate NAPs by integrating Gene Ontology (GO) annotations and transcript abundance across diverse bacterial species [77]. This approach identified multiple transcription factors capable of modulating genome architecture, including the well-characterized Rok and ferric uptake regulator (Fur) [77]. Our previous study revealed that the global regulators Fur and Zur, two members of the Fur family in Z. mobilis, directly modulate the 3D organization of the genome by mediating long-range chromosomal interactions spanning over 200 kb [14]. Notably, transcription factors are thought to have evolved from NAPs [79]. Thus, classifying these proteins solely by functional criteria is inadequate. Their functions may constitute a continuous spectrum, with dynamic and diverse roles in gene regulation and genome organization.

In summary, bacterial chromosome organization is governed by a variety of specific regulatory factors that act across a hierarchy of spatial scales and exhibit a spectrum of functions, and increasing evidence reveals the complexity of this multilayered regulatory system. Therefore, deciphering universal principles of 3D genome architecture requires cross-species comparative analyses, which disentangle lineage-specific regulatory adaptations from evolutionarily conserved architectural constraints.

Adaptive dynamics of the bacterial 3D genome to environmental stress

The 3D genome displays dynamic plasticity in its spatial organization, influencing key processes such as chromosome replication and segregation [57, 64, 80, 81]. This structural flexibility is closely associated with changes in cellular metabolic states and responses to external stressors. However, it remains unclear whether structural remodeling is primarily driven by topological changes or is a secondary consequence of transcriptional reprogramming.

In bacteria, chromosomal interactions and transcriptional regulation are tightly coupled: (i) chromosomal interactions exhibit positive or negative correlations with transcriptional activity in specific species, such as the positive correlations in Z. mobilis, E. coli, B. subtilis and S. coelicolor [13, 14, 26]; (ii) CID boundaries are frequently enriched with HEGs or long genes, which are proposed to contribute to domain formation. Specifically, extended and transcriptionally active regions promote local chromosome segregation, generating spatially insulated domains that reduce DNA interactions between adjacent regions [11, 82]; (iii) Structural domain reorganization and interaction remodeling occur during chromosome replication and across bacterial growth phases [12]. In response to environmental stimuli or the cell cycle, bacteria undergo rapid transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 3). Given the predominantly negative correlation between transcription and local compactness observed in E. coli under heat stress, the reduced short-range interactions may reflect increased transcriptional activity at specific loci such as heat shock genes [84]. d Short-term cold shock. Across growth phases, bacterial chromosome interaction patterns undergo significant reorganization. For example, short-range interactions are notably strengthened during the transition from the mid-logarithmic phase to the late-logarithmic phase, whereas both short-range interactions and CID boundary insulation attenuate in the stationary phase (Fig. 3b) [13, 23, 26]. Additionally, bacteria adapt to environmental stresses (e.g., nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress, osmotic stress, and thermal stress) by rapidly modulating chromosomal dynamics (Fig. 3c-f) [86]. Under cold stress, boundary insulation diminishes, whereas under heat stress, the frequency of short-range interactions decreases, and long-range interactions within the Ter macrodomain increase (Fig. 3c and d) [13, 16, 84, 85]. In Z. mobilis, short-range interactions are significantly enhanced under acetic acid and furfural stress (Fig. 3f) [14]. These findings underscore that dynamic chromosomal interaction remodeling is closely associated with bacterial adaptation to diverse physiological states and environmental perturbations. Transcriptional activity appears to be a major driver of stress-induced chromosomal remodeling [11, 41, 42]. Here, we can categorize stress responses associated with structural changes into two broad types. In some cases, such as growth cycle transitions, cold and heat stress, chromosomal remodeling is driven mainly by transcriptional changes, with certain chromosomal factors participating in this regulatory process. In others, such as osmotic, acetic acid, and furfural, specific NAPs directly regulate both transcription and chromosome conformation. Across both categories, structural dynamics ultimately reflect the need for transcriptional reprogramming. Transcriptional reprogramming and chromosomal remodeling are thus integral to maintaining genomic homeostasis.

Genomic homeostasis represents an important bacterial strategy for environmental adaptation. Here, using H-NS as a model system, this regulatory protein reveals a link between environmental adaptation and genomic organization in bacteria. As a thermo-osmo sensor, H-NS dynamically modulates its oligomeric state and DNA-binding activity in response to temperature, osmolarity, acidity [66, 87], and second messengers such as c-di-GMP [88]. These physico-chemical cues regulate its capacity to condense AT-rich DNA regions—often by silencing horizontally acquired genes while derepressing stress-responsive or virulence loci under host-mimicking conditions [89, 90]. For example, elevated temperatures destabilize H-NS multimers, alleviating transcriptional silencing [66], whereas c-di-GMP binding directly inhibits H-NS-DNA interactions in Salmonella, enabling adaptive gene expression [88]. Concurrently, H-NS shapes the 3D genome architecture by bridging distal DNA segments into loops, repressing xenogeneic elements [90], and coordinating motility or biofilm-related genes, as observed in Vibrio parahaemolyticus [91, 92]. This dual role—environmental signal transduction and chromosomal organization—suggests that H-NS plays a significant role in bacterial survival, balancing genome stability with phenotypic plasticity in diverse habitats. As evidenced by comprehensive investigations into H-NS and H-NS-like proteins, chromosomal remodeling may represent a characteristic feature of transcriptional regulation in bacterial systems [93]. Interestingly, the H-NS-like protein Rok, whose ability to bridge DNA is not affected by changes in physico-chemical conditions, responds indirectly to environmental stimuli (e.g., salt concentration) through its interaction partner sRok [71]. While H-NS presents a compelling case for a protein that directly senses the environmental changes and shapes architecture, it is atypical. For most other NAPs and architectural regulators, the signaling pathways that connect external cues to their DNA-binding activity remain elusive. Alternatively, transcription factors may function as environmental sensors that simultaneously mediate transcriptional regulation and coordinate chromosomal architecture. Under dual-stress conditions induced by acetic acid and furfural, Fur family proteins maintain chromosomal structural stability while modulating global gene expression patterns [14]. Considering the ambiguous boundary between NAPs and transcription factors, more environmental regulators will likely emerge in future studies. However, we can still conclude that NAPs act as mediators that facilitate adaptation through structural and gene expression regulation, despite many underlying principles awaiting clarification.

Potential applications of bacterial 3D genomics

The spatial organization of bacterial chromosomes is closely linked to gene expression and genome stability, both of which influence bacterial adaptation. As previously described, bacterial chromosome folding is primarily driven by transcription in coordination with additional chromosomal factors [13, 16, 30]. Consequently, the 3D genome architecture provides a blueprint for designing novel synthetic biology and antimicrobial strategies (Fig. 4). In other words, the adaptive responses of bacteria to environmental cues or synthetic biological metabolic alterations are often mediated through dynamic transcriptional changes, which in turn induce specific structural reconfigurations of the genome. These structural features could potentially serve as useful biological markers for desired physiological outcomes, such as antibiotic susceptibility, though this remains to be further validated. Therefore, using the 3D genomic data to screen for regulatory factors associated with the 3D structure and indirectly linked to transcription represents a feasible strategy. In antibacterial drug discovery, a promising therapeutic strategy involves treating the genomic architecture as a phenotype and interfering with the regulatory factors that shape it (Fig. 4b). To operationalize this concept, it is essential to correlate specific 3D structural features with bacterial pathogenicity or survival, and to elucidate the roles of key structural maintenance factors (such as NAPs, topoisomerases, and SMC proteins). Recent research has identified specialized phage-encoded proteins that disrupt the architecture of the host chromosome, underscoring the potential of phage-derived inhibitory strategies to guide the rational design of novel antibacterial agents at the 3D genome level [94, 95]. These regulatory factors represent potential targets for novel antibacterial agents, as disrupting proteins important for maintaining spatial genomic stability significantly impairs bacterial fitness [94]. Alternatively, leveraging the understanding that virulence or drug resistance genes undergo coordinated regulation through 3D structure, researchers aim to design small molecules that disrupt key regulators of chromosome architecture [38]. The goal is to alter the 3D genome organization associated with gene expression, thereby potentially inhibiting virulence or restoring antibiotic sensitivity. Furthermore, targeting multifunctional regulators such as H-NS and Fur family proteins that coordinate both virulence gene expression and chromosomal architecture represents an attractive strategy [72, 96, 97]. Inhibiting multifunctional proteins may simultaneously disrupt virulence programs and their associated 3D genomic architecture, resulting in stronger antimicrobial activity. In synthetic biology, chassis cells play a critical role in enabling green biomanufacturing. A robust chassis cell must possess both high product synthesis efficiency and strong tolerance to inhibitors present in fermentation environments [98]. Insights from bacterial 3D genome can inform rational gene circuit design and stable integration of large DNA fragments into the genome to avoid positional effects (Fig. 4d). For instance, Streptomyces species are widely studied because of their ability to produce bioactive natural products. Research on Streptomyces has revealed that dynamic 3D chromosome reorganization during metabolic shifts correlates with the activity of BGCs, implying that spatial positioning could be leveraged to optimize natural product yields [25, 26]. Specifically, Deng and collaborators demonstrated that integrating single reporter genes or complex BGCs into chromosomal regions with frequent spatial interactions significantly increased their expression [26]. This approach is inspired by correlations observed in “spatial position effects”, suggesting that deciphering the 3D genome architecture could identify favorable integration sites to maximize expression of synthetic genetic circuits [99]. It should be considered that other local factors (e.g., DNA supercoiling, NAPs binding) near the integration site, as well as the gene dosage, may also contribute to this effect [99]. In addition, 3D genome insights assist artificial chromosome construction, providing design principles for more stable, larger-capacity bacterial artificial chromosomes that mimic native 3D organization to retain functionality [100, 101]. Building on these findings, we further put forward the conceptual framework of “spatial synthetic biology”—a framework that utilizes information on spatial organization and gene expression profiling to refine the efficiency and stability of synthetic genetic circuits in microbial chassis. This strategy aims to rationally design robust strains capable of maintaining functionality and genomic homeostasis even under environmental or genetic stress (Fig. 4c and d). While spatial synthetic biology is promising, its application faces significant challenges. For instance, the integration of large DNA fragments could disrupt the local chromosome architecture that this approach aims to exploit. Moreover, the subsequent impact of such structural changes on transcriptional regulation remains difficult to predict.

Systematic manipulation of the bacterial chromosome can be approached through structural elements and chromosomal factors. The relationships between structure and genomic homeostasis, as well as between structure and gene expression, may provide valuable insights and design principles for future genome engineering efforts. Large-scale sequencing data can be leveraged to dissect 3D chromosomal dynamics, whereas machine learning-based prediction of chromosome kinetics offers complementary strategies. With artificial intelligence (AI) tools, engineered genome design could bridge DNA sequence-to-3D architecture mapping, endowing unnatural genomes with desired functions to advance biomanufacturing, biocontainment, and pathway optimization [100, 102]. CRISPR-based editing technologies further enhance predictable genome engineering by enabling precise structural interventions [103]. In summary, the systematic engineering of bacterial chromosomes depends on a comprehensive understanding of their structural principles. However, current limitations persist owing to the scarcity of bacterial chromosome structure databases and insufficient characterization of non-model organisms with exceptional performance traits [15]. These knowledge gaps constrain our ability to decipher the evolutionary mechanisms and adaptive dynamics inherent to bacterial genomes.

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

Our understanding of bacterial 3D genomes remains limited, constrained by technical challenges such as insufficient resolution, low throughput, and difficulties in live-cell imaging. Methods for studying prokaryotic 3D genomes lag behind those available for eukaryotes, particularly in resolving protein-DNA interactions and capturing dynamic architectures at the single-cell level. Nevertheless, the structural plasticity of the bacterial 3D genome is a key feature linked to survival, evolution, and pathogenicity. Regulatory factors that govern this plasticity offer opportunities to optimize synthetic gene circuit expression, improve chassis robustness, and identify new therapeutic targets. A major future challenge is to move from correlation to causation, establishing direct links between regulatory factors and phenotypic outcomes to enable practical applications. As 3D genomics and synthetic biology converge, achieving spatial and temporal control over genome architecture could transform biological engineering. To realize this potential, computational modeling must evolve from descriptive to predictive frameworks, incorporating evolutionary constraints to elucidate the principles governing bacterial chromosome organization. Advancing dynamic 3D genome research through high-resolution technologies, computational modeling, and multi-omics integration will be essential for enabling transformative genomic applications.
