A case of suspected 2, 4-D ethyl ester poisoning mimicking organophosphate toxicity: a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge
Authors: Jyothi Koteswara Rao, Sireesha Chilakapati
Published: 2025

Dear Editor:

2, 4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2, 4-D) is a selective herbicide commonly used to control broad-leaved weeds, particularly in wheat cultivation, with extensive usage in northern India. Poisoning with 2, 4-D is rare, often associated with suicide attempts, and its true incidence is likely underreported due to clinical similarities with anticholinesterase poisoning. Acute toxic levels range between 447 and 826 mg/L, and its effects involve multiple organ systems, including the heart, nervous system, liver, kidneys, muscles, lungs, and endocrine system [1]. Without a specific antidote, management is primarily supportive, emphasizing early recognition. Blood levels of 2, 4-D can be accurately measured using gas-liquid chromatography with electron-capture detection, although this facility was not available in our setting. Here, we present a case of 2, 4-D ethyl ester poisoning leading to multiorgan failure, managed in intensive care, underscoring the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in such cases.

A 26-year-old male presented with a history of consuming 200–250 mL of 2,4-D ethyl ester. He initially visited a local hospital in an unresponsive state, where he was treated presumptively for organophosphate (OP) poisoning due to the presence of frothy secretions and altered sensorium. He received atropine (20 mg) and pralidoxime (1 g). Upon referral to our tertiary care center approximately eight hours after ingestion, the patient remained unresponsive. We obtained further history from the patient’s relatives, and on our request, they brought the container of the substance ingested, which confirmed the presence of 2,4-D ethyl ester. The absence of classical cholinergic signs such as salivation, lacrimation, diarrhoea, fasciculations, and crepitations, along with container verification, shifted the diagnosis from OP poisoning to suspected 2,4-D poisoning.

On arrival, he had threatened airway, respiratory rate of 30/min, blood pressure 100/48 mm Hg, and pulse rate of 130/min. Chest auscultation revealed normal vesicular breath sounds. Neurological examination showed a Glassgow Coma Scale of E1V2M2, mid-dilated pupils sluggishly reactive to light, and bilateral extensor plantar reflexes. He was intubated with an 8.5-mm endotracheal tube and placed on controlled ventilation. Initial management included fluid boluses and norepinephrine infusion. Investigations revealed respiratory alkalosis with metabolic acidosis on blood gas analysis, normal electrolytes, and lactate of 0.9 mmol/L. Point-of-care ultrasound showed an ejection fraction of 60% with no cardiac abnormalities or evidence of pulmonary embolism. Lung ultrasound revealed normal A-lines with no pleural effusions or consolidation. Non-contrast computed tomography brain was unremarkable. Routine labs showed hemoglobin 16 g/dl, white blood cell (WBC) 23,000, platelets 148,000, international normalized ratio (INR) 2.02, normal liver function, and creatinine 1.78 mg/dl. Creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 1,898 U/L, with a C-reactive protein of 196.3 mg/dl and lactate dehydrogenase 631 IU/L. As per literature guidance, forced alkaline diuresis was initiated using intravenous fluids, sodium bicarbonate, and furosemide to maintain high urine output and minimize nephrotoxicity. The patient’s sensorium improved by the next day, and tachycardia resolved. He met extubation criteria on day 2 and was extubated. However, within 30 minutes, he desaturated, and arterial blood gas showed respiratory alkalosis with mild hypoxemia (PaO2 58 mm Hg). He was reintubated due to suspected myopathy and respiratory muscle weakness. Repeat labs showed hemoglobin 17.1 g/dl, WBC 25,000, platelets 79,000, INR 1.6, creatinine 2.06 mg/dl, with persistently elevated CK levels and renal parameters despite good urine output. On day 4, he was successfully extubated and remained vitally stable. He was discharged in stable condition on day 6 without requiring haemodialysis.

2, 4-D is available as ester-based emulsifiable concentrates and has been widely used as a herbicide for many years. However, there are only a few reported cases of occupational exposure or suicidal ingestion [2]. Underreporting may be due to misdiagnosis, as the clinical presentation overlaps significantly with the more common anticholinesterase poisoning, particularly in peripheral centers lacking reliable history, distinct clinical signs, or diagnostic tests to identify the offending agent [3].

2, 4-D toxicity is known to affect multiple systems, it is cardiotoxic, myotoxic, neurotoxic, hepatotoxic, nephrotoxic, and causes hematologic disturbances. Its toxic mechanisms include dose-dependent cell membrane damage, uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, and disruption of acetyl-CoA metabolism [4]. Initial symptoms include nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and sometimes gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Hypotension is common due to intravascular volume loss, vasodilation, and direct myocardial depression. Respiratory failure may result from central nervous system depression or respiratory muscle weakness. Muscle injury can lead to rhabdomyolysis and secondary renal dysfunction due to myoglobinuria. Neurological manifestations include impaired coordination, unconsciousness, coma, hypertonia, hyperreflexia, hallucinations, and paralysis.

Our patient showed muscle twitching and fibrillations on the second day, with no fever or transaminitis. In our case, extubation failure was attributed to central nervous system depression and myopathy, but there were no excitatory neurological features. Muscle weakness was localized to proximal limb muscles and respiratory muscles, causing respiratory distress with no pharyngeal involvement like nasal regurgitation and hoarseness.

Since there is no specific antidote for 2, 4-D poisoning, treatment remains largely supportive, focusing on hydration, respiratory support, prevention of aspiration, and physiotherapy. Forced alkaline diuresis achieved through urine alkalinisation with sodium bicarbonate and high urine flow using intravenous fluids and diuretics may help enhance herbicide elimination and prevent nephrotoxicity caused by myoglobinuria [4]. Although haemodialysis has been reported as beneficial in patients with renal failure due to 2,4-D poisoning [5], our patient recovered without requiring dialysis. Physiotherapy played a key role in his recovery from neuromuscular weakness and was instrumental in achieving a favorable outcome. Confirmatory toxicological analysis (e.g., gas chromatography–electron capture) was not available, and this remains a limitation. The diagnosis was based on clinical features, exclusion of other common toxidromes, and confirmation via container verification.

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of suspected 2, 4-D ethyl ester poisoning, especially in resource-limited settings where its clinical presentation may closely resemble that of OP poisoning. Notably, this case underscores several novel aspects like the role of forced alkaline diuresis in renal protection, reintubation due to suspected respiratory muscle myopathy, and eventual recovery without haemodialysis. We hope this report adds to the existing literature by reinforcing the importance of early history verification, appropriate diagnostic reconsideration, and the therapeutic utility of urine alkalinisation in severe herbicide toxicity. It further emphasizes the value of individualized, resource-sensitive supportive care and increased clinical awareness of herbicide poisoning in agricultural regions.
