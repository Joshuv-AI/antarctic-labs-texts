When MIS and Maximal Matching are Easy in the Congested Clique
Keren Censor-Hillel, Tomer Even, Maxime Flin, Magnús M. Halldórsson

Abstract
Two of the most fundamental distributed symmetry-breaking problems are that of finding a maximal independent set (MIS) and a maximal matching (MM) in a graph. It is a major open question whether these problems can be solved in constant rounds of the all-to-all communication model of Congested Clique , with O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ Δ ) O(\log\log\Delta) being the best upper bound known (where Δ \Delta is the maximum degree). We explore in this paper the boundary of the feasible, asking for which graphs we can solve the problems in constant rounds. We find that for several graph parameters, ranging from sparse to highly dense graphs, the problems do have a constant-round solution. In particular, we give algorithms that run in constant rounds when: • the average degree is at most d ⁡ ( G ) ≤ 2 O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) d(G)\leq 2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})} , • the neighborhood independence number is at most β ⁡ ( G ) ≤ 2 O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \beta(G)\leq 2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})} , or • the independence number is at most α ⁡ ( G ) ≤ | V ⁡ ( G ) | / d ​ ( G ) μ \alpha(G)\leq|V(G)|/d(G)^{\mu} , for any constant μ > 0 \mu>0 . Further, we establish that these are tight bounds for the known methods, for all three parameters, suggesting that new ideas are needed for further progress.



1
Introduction

Symmetry breaking is fundamental to distributed computing. Two of the most fundamental symmetry-breaking problems are that of computing a maximal independent set (MIS) and a maximal matching (MM) in the distributed network. We consider these problems in the
Congested Clique
model, which has a machine for each graph node and in each synchronous round, each machine can send a
O(\log n)
-bit message to every other node.

There is now a fairly good understanding of the complexity of these problems in the central model of locality,
LOCAL
. There is an algorithm that runs in
O(\log\Delta+(\log\log n)^{O(1)})
rounds
[
Gha16
,
GGR21
,
FGG+23
]
that works even in the bandwidth-constrained CONGEST model and a lower bound of
\Omega\left\lparen\min\left\{\frac{\log\Delta}{\log\log\Delta},\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}\right\}\right\rparen

[
KMW16
]

for the
LOCAL
model.
Much less is known in the
Congested Clique
model.

Ghaffari
[
Gha17
]
gave a
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log\Delta})
-round
Congested Clique
algorithm for MIS
which beats the
LOCAL
lower bounds
when
\Delta
is small enough. It was followed by
[
Kon18
,
GGK+18
]

which improved the complexity to
O(\log\log\Delta)
by using a sparsification
technique pioneered by
[
ACG+15
]
for correlation clustering in streaming.
For MM, the state of the art is the
O(\log\log\Delta)
-round algorithm
of Behnezhad, Hajiaghai and Harris
[
BHH23
]

which implements a sparsification mechanism comparable to the one for MIS.
Meanwhile, problems such as minimum spanning tree
[
JN18
]
,
(\Delta+1)
-vertex-coloring
[
CFG+19
]
or 2-ruling set
[
CKPU23
]
have
O(1)
-round algorithms in
Congested Clique
. The 2-ruling set problem in particular is a slight relaxation of the MIS problem which has a similar complexity in the
LOCAL
model
[
BBO22
,
BBKO22
]
.

Proving lower bounds in
Congested Clique
is known to be hard
[
DKO14
]
.
Interestingly, as the algorithm of
[
GGK+18
]

can be implemented in
O(\log\log\Delta)
passes of streaming,
a very recent streaming lower bound by Assadi, Konrad, Naidu and Sundaresan
[
AKNS24
]

shows that such algorithms cannot break the
O(\log\log n)
barrier in general graphs.
Contrary to MIS, there exist no such results for MM because it is trivial to compute in the streaming model.
Unconditional lower bounds for MIS and MM are only known in the much weaker

broadcast congested clique
, where nodes must send the same message to everyone in a round.
In this model, Assadi, Kol and Zhang
[
AKZ22
]
recently showed that any MIS or MM
algorithm with
(\log n)^{O(1)}
bandwidth needs
\Omega(\log\log n)
rounds.
Meanwhile, the best upper bound in this model is
O(\log n)
rounds
[
Lub86
]
.

Turning back to
Congested Clique
, we change perspective and ask:

When
can MIS and MM be solved in
O(1)
rounds of
Congested Clique
?

In other words, for what kind of graphs can we solve the problems fast, and for which ones are current techniques insufficient?

We consider both sparse and dense families of graphs, pinpointing properties that allow us to quickly converge toward a solution. On one hand, we focus on graphs of low-to-medium
average degree
, which subsumes other sparse graphs such as low-degree or low-arboricity graphs. On the other hand, we consider graphs with sublinear
independence number
, which are graphs that can be highly dense. Another related notion is
neighborhood independence number
, which is the largest number of independent nodes in the neighborhood of a given node.

Our Results.

We summarize our algorithmic results succinctly as follows.
Let
n
be the number of nodes in the graph
G
,
d(G)
its average degree,
\alpha(G)
the size of the largest independent set, and
\beta(G)=\max_{v\in V(G)}\alpha(G[N(v)])
the neighborhood independence number.
We say that a problem is
easy
if it can be solved in
O(1)
rounds of
Congested Clique
(on the respective graph class.

MIS and MM are easy on graphs of
average degree
at most
d(G)\leq 2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}
.

The same holds in terms of neighborhood independence:

MIS and MM are easy on
graphs of
neighborhood independence
at most
\beta(G)\leq 2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}
.

Additionally, we get bounds in terms of independence number.

MIS and MM are easy on
graphs with
independence number
at most
\alpha(G)\leq n/d(G)^{\mu}
, for any constant
\mu>0
.

For larger ranges of these parameters, we get tradeoffs, with complexities as a function of the parameters:
O(\log(\log d(G) / \sqrt{\log n})+1))
,
O(\log(\log(\betag)/\sqrt{\log n})+1)
, and
O(\log 1/\mu+1)
, respectively.
Note that classes of graph where those parameters are small have been studied and are known to appear in practice; see
Section

1.2
for more details.

Our results are tight for all three graph parameters. More generally, we give a family of hard graphs
for which current techniques require
\Omega(\log\log n)
rounds. The parameters
\Delta,d(G),\beta(G),\alpha(G)
in our hard graphs are such, that our bounds for them are tight given the known methods.
Our construction provides concrete evidence of instances for which new approaches are needed.

1.1
Technical Overview

Upper bounds.

Our starting points for computing MIS and MM are the algorithms of

[
GGK+18
,
BHH23
]
which take
O(\log\log(\Delta))
rounds for MIS and MM, respectively. They both use degree reduction for sparsification.

For MIS, the algorithm samples a subset of vertices that induce a graph with
O(n)
edges. One designated vertex collects the edges of this graph and locally computes an MIS
S
on it. To extend the set
S
into an MIS, the algorithm then proceeds by computing an MIS on
G[V\setminus\Gamma(S)]
, where
\Gamma(S)
contains all vertices in
S
or with at least one neighbor in it (i.e., vertices that are
covered
by
S
).
Computing an MIS on this residual graph
G[V\setminus\Gamma(S)]
is an easier task as it has a maximal degree of
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\Delta})
, w.h.p. This algorithm is repeated

O(\log\log(\Delta))
times to get a residual graph of a maximum degree
\poly\log(n)
, at which point it switches to simulating a
LOCAL
algorithm by letting each vertex learn its
\log\log(\Delta)
neighborhood.

Average degree:
As a warm-up to our main technical contribution, we observe that one can push the degree-reduction method beyond the parameter of the maximum degree, which can be replaced by the
average degree
.
The key idea is to sample according to the average degree, rather than the maximum degree, and leverage a concentration bound that allows for dependencies.
With efficient routing, this leads us to a constant-round algorithm when the average degree is at most
2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}
, which we use next.

Independence number:
One of our main technical contributions is to bound the running time of the repeated degree reduction algorithm in terms of the
independence number
of the graph.
To give a flavor of this, consider a graph
G
with
n
vertices, average degree
d(G)
, and an independence number as small as possible, i.e.,
\alpha(G)=n/(d(G)+1)
.
After one round of degree reduction, we get an independent set
S
and a residual graph
H=G[V\setminus\Gamma(S)]
, where the maximal degree in
H
is
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d(G)})
.
Note that since
H
is an induced subgraph of
G
, its independence number cannot be larger than that of
G
. We use Turán’s bound to obtain the following bound:

\frac{n(H)}{2d(H)}\leq\alpha(H)\leq\alpha(G)\leq n/(d(G)+1)
.
Further, we can bound the number of edges of
H
by
multiplying both sides by
(d(H))^{2}
to get that

|E(H)|=n(H)\cdot d(H)/2\leq n\cdot\frac{(d(H))^{2}}{d(G)+1}\leq\tilde{O}(n)
,
where the last inequality follows as
d(H)=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d(G)})
.
This, together with efficient routing, leads us to a constant-round algorithm, as the residual graph has average degree is at most
2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}
.

This toy example was only for
\alpha(G)=n/(d+1)
. We extend it by showing a
O(\log(1/\mu)+1)
round algorithm for MIS, where
\mu>0
is such that the independence number of
G
is
\alpha(G)=n/(d+1)^{\mu}
.

This framework also applies for MM. Consider the algorithm of
[
BHH23
]
. It partitions the vertices of the graph into random sets, such that each set induces a graph with at most
O(n)
edges. Then, a maximal matching is computed on each part. Let
M^{\prime}
denote the union of those computed matchings. The algorithm then proceeds with a clean-up phase, in which vertices of high degree are matched. Let
M
denote the union of
M^{\prime}
and the edges computed in the clean-up phase. The algorithm has the property that
G[V\setminus V(M)]
has a maximal degree of
\tilde{O}(\Delta^{0.92})
.

As for the MIS case, we turn this into an algorithm which reduces the maximal degree in terms of the average degree and show that if the input graph has a small independence number then we can compute an MM faster.

Neighborhood independence:

Our second main technical contribution involves graphs with small neighborhood independence. A graph
G
has neighborhood independence
\beta(G)
if the size of any independent set in the neighborhood of any vertex is at most
\beta(G)
.
Note that
\beta(G)
and
\alpha(G)
are not comparable as
\beta(G)\leq\Delta(G)
, and
n/(\Delta(G)+1)\leq\alpha(G)\leq n\beta(G)/\delta(G)
.

Suppose a graph has a minimum degree of at least
(d(G))^{1/2}
, and that
\beta(G)
is, say, at most
(d(G))^{1/4}
. Then we get that
\alpha(G)\leq n/(d(G))^{1/4}
, allowing us to use our framework for low independence number. This motivates us to find subgraphs for which this holds. However, a direct degree reduction is insufficient.
The challenge here is that the minimum degree might be much smaller than the average degree.

Our approach for this case is to partition the vertices into
\log n
degree classes where the
i
-th degree class,
denoted by
V_{i}
, contains vertices of roughly the same degrees, and then use
the fact that each
G[V_{i}]
has relatively small independence number. Our final step here is to replace sampling of vertices with a uniform probability of
1/\sqrt{d(G)}
, by a
non-uniform
probability of
1/\sqrt{\deg_{G}(v)}
(such sampling was applied for 2-ruling sets in
[
CKPU23
]
).
We show that this algorithm has the property that within the residual graph, each node
has at most
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{x})
neighbors that had degrees in
G
in the range
[x,2x]
.
This allows us to show that after one iteration, we get a residual graph
H
, where
H[V_{i}]
contains only
\tilde{O}(n\beta(G))
edges w.h.p, and therefore so does
H
. We can therefore find an MIS on
H
in time
O(\log(\log(\betag)/\sqrt{\log n})+1)
w.h.p.

For matching, our algorithm in terms of
\beta(G)
is simpler. We partition the vertices into degree classes as before. In parallel, each class computes a matching on the edges inside the class, using the degree reduction algorithm. We claim that finding a maximal matching in each degree class takes only a constant number of rounds, after which, the residual graph
H
contains only

\tilde{O}(n\beta(G))
edges w.h.p. The crucial point here is as follows. If
W
is
a set of unmatched vertices inside the same degree class, i.e., of degree between
a
and
2a
, then
W
is an independent set, from which we obtain that

\absolutevalue{W}\leq n\beta(G)/a
.
Therefore, these vertices contribute at most
n\beta(G)
edges to
H
.

We provide a hard distribution over graphs, on which the state-of-the-art methods require
\Omega(\log\log n)
iterations to complete. Notably, the maximum degree in these graphs, the average degree, and the global- and neighborhood- independence numbers are all values for which our bounds with respect to them become tight given the known methods.

MIS:

We provide a tight analysis for the running time of all algorithms that compute an MIS using the following framework:

(i)

samples a set of vertices
S
, such that the induced subgraph
G[S]
has
O(n)
edges w.h.p,

(ii)

computes a greedy MIS over
G[S]
,

(iii)

removes all covered vertices, and repeats.

The high-level intuition behind our construction is as follows. We split the
n
vertices into roughly
\log\log n
levels, and denote by
k
the number of vertices in each level. Within level
i
, we partition the vertices into
k^{1-1/4^{i}}
equal-size clusters, each forming a clique.
Note that if the algorithm sampled at least one vertex
v
from some cluster
C
, then a vertex from
C
joins the MIS, and all other vertices in
C
are covered.
We say that a cluster is sampled if at least one of its vertices is sampled.
Yet, if for a given cluster, no vertex gets sampled in a certain iteration, then none joins the independent set, then none of its vertices get covered.
As the level number increases, so does its number of clusters, while their size decreases, making them less likely to be sampled.

The running time of an algorithm that samples vertices with some uniform probability might be
\Omega\left(\log\log n\right)
, but an algorithm that samples each vertex with probability
1/\sqrt{d_{v}}
, is likely to hit all clusters very quickly (plus it can be easily handled in a single iteration of a Luby approach).

To overcome this, we add random edges to the graph, obtaining a distribution over graphs. A random edge is added between two vertices in levels
i,j
for
i<j
with probability that is roughly the inverse of the number of clusters in level
i
(up to
\poly\log n
factors).
The obtained graph becomes nearly regular, and then it is unlikely that the smaller clusters (of the higher levels) are sampled, even if the sampling probability is based on the vertices degree.
The random edges complicate the analysis as a vertex is covered when its cluster is sampled, or when a neighbor of the vertex in a different cluster is sampled.
A crucial step in this analysis requires proving that w.h.p. at least
1-o(1)
fraction of the vertices in the
r
-th level are not covered before iteration
r
is over.
Since the graph has roughly
\log\log n
levels, we conclude that at least
\Omega\left(\log\log n\right)
iterations are needed to cover all vertices.
The full details of this proof appear in
Section

5
.

MM:
We also provide a tight analysis for the running time of all algorithms that compute an MM using the following framework:

(i)

randomly partitions the vertices into sets
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
,
such that for every
i
the induced subgraph
G[S_{i}]
has
O(n)
edges w.h.p,

(ii)

computes a greedy MM over each
G[S_{i}]
,

(iii)

invokes a clean-up phase that matches high-degree vertices,

(iv)

removes all covered vertices, and repeats.

Our hard graph distribution for MM is only a slight modification of the one for MIS: The number of vertices in each level is increased by
4
as the level increases. This ensures that any maximal matching must “reveal” the last level of the graph.
We also ensure that graphs taken from this distribution have a perfect matching, by setting the number of vertices in each cluster to be even.

A crucial step in the analysis of the running time of such algorithm requires proving that w.h.p. at least
\frac{2}{3}\cdot(1-o(1))
fraction of the vertices in the
r
-th level are not matched before iteration
r
is over.
Using this property and the fact that
3/4
fraction of the vertices are in the last level, and
3/4
fraction of the remaining vertices are in the second-to-last level, we conclude that after
\ell/2
iterations, where
\ell
is the number of levels, at least
\frac{8(1-o(1))}{9}
fraction of the vertices are not matched, and therefore the computed matching is not maximal, as any maximal matching must in a graph with a perfect matching must match at least half of the vertices.
Therefore, the running time of the algorithm must be at least
\Omega\left(\log\log n\right)
w.h.p.

Sparse Graphs.

Small Independence Number.

While there is no concrete characterization of graphs with small independence number,

are known to have independence number
\approx 2n/d\log d
(see, e.g.,
[
BE76

In particular, for random graphs, the Turán bound is nearly-tight and

Bounded Neighborhood Independence.

Graphs of bounded neighborhood independence capture a wide range of dense graphs.
This includes line graphs, bounded-growth graphs (e.g., interval graphs
[
HKS03

[
HK15

Graphs with

[

Motivated by applications to wireless networks,

[

Preliminaries

Graph Theoretic Notation.

For a graph
H
, let
d(H)
be its average degree,
\Delta(H)
its maximum degree, and
n(H)=|V(H)|
its number of vertices. For a vertex
v
, let
N_{H}(v)
its set of neighbors, and
\deg_{H}(v)=|N_{H}(v)|
its degree.
The hop-distance between
u
and
v
is
\dist_{H}(u,v)
.
For a subset
S\subseteq V
, let
N_{G}(S)=\bigcup_{v\in S}N_{G}(v)
,
\Gamma_{G}(S)=S\cup N_{G}(S)
, and for sets
A,B\subseteq V(H)
, let
E_{H}(A,B)=\{e=(a,b)\in E(H):a\in A,b\in B\}
.

Throughout this paper, the input graph is
G=(V(G),E(G))
, with
n=|V(G)|
and
m=|E(G)|
.
We denote the independence number of the graph by
\alpha(G)
.
Let
\beta(G)\triangleq\max_{v\in V}\alpha(G[N(v)])
denote the maximum independence number of an induced neighborhood subgraph.

We say that a vertex set
A
is
covered
by an independent set
S
if
A\subseteq\Gamma(S)
.
The lexicographically first MIS (LFMIS) is defined with respect to an
ordering
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
of the vertices as the outcome of the following greedy algorithm:
beginning with an empty independent set
S
,
loop through vertices in the given order and add a vertex to
S
if it is uncovered when reached by the algorithm.
The lexicographically first MM (LFMM) is defined similarly, with respect to adding edges to the matching.

2.1
Lenzen’s Routing Lemma
[
Len13
]
.

The following is equivalent to the
Congested Clique
model:
In every round each node can send (receive)
\{b_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
bits to (from) the
i
-th node, for any sequence
\{b_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=O(n\log n)
. In other words, any routing scheme in which no vertex sends or receives more than
O(n)
messages can be performed in
O(1)
rounds.

4.1

The goal of this section is to prove

We also use the following standard result on the size of independent sets in

Proof of

Split the vertices into degree classes as follows: for each

\displaystyle d_{i}=2^{i}\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad V_{i}\triangleq\left\{v\in V\mid d_{i}\leq\deg_{G}(v)<d_{i+1}\right\}\;.

Fix

We have

\displaystyle\frac{\absolutevalue{X_i}}{2s\sqrt{d_{i}}}\leq\alpha(G[X_{i}])\leq\alpha(G[V_{i}])\leq\frac{n\beta(G)}{d_{i}}\;.

The first inequality follows by Turán’s bound, by the assumed degree bound,

Multiply both sides by

4.4

4.5

Fix some

Let

Proof.

Consider a vertex

The algorithm can be described as running the following martingale

5.1
MIS

In this section, we analyze the following algorithm that we refer to as
ReduceMIS
. The state-of-the-art algorithm of
[
GGK+18
]
as well as the algorithms we presented here can be seen as special cases of
ReduceMIS
.
The algorithm is as follows. Let
G_{0}=G
be an
n
-vertex
m
-edge graph.
In the
i
-th iteration, the algorithm samples a subset
F_{i}
of vertices from the current remaining graph
G_{i}
, where each vertex
v
is sampled independently with probability
p_{i,v}\leq 1/\sqrt{\delta(G_{i})}
, where
\delta(G_{i})
is the minimum degree of
G_{i}
.
Then, the algorithm computes the lexicographically first MIS
S_{i}
on the induced graph
G[F_{i}]
, and repeats with the graph
G_{i+1}=G[V\setminus\Gamma(S_{\leq i})]
, where
S_{\leq i}=\bigcup_{j\in\{0,1,\dots,i\}}S_{j}
.
When
G_{i}
is empty, the set
S_{\leq i}
is a maximal independent set and the algorithm ends.

In particular, two special cases of this algorithm are obtained when we set
p_{i,v}=1/\sqrt{d(G_{i})}
(as in
[
GGK+18
]
) or
p_{i,v}=1/\sqrt{\deg_{G_{i}}(v)}
(as in
Proposition

3.4
).

We claim that, with high probability, this algorithm needs
\Omega(\log\log n)
sampling rounds before it is completed.

Hard graph distribution:

Our hard graph distribution is as follows. We take
n=k\times\ell
vertices and split them into
\ell

levels
, with
k
vertices in each level. In level
i\in\{0,\dots\ell-1\}
, we group vertices into
k^{1-1/4^{i}}

clusters
, which we refer to as the
i
-th level clusters, each containing
k^{1/4^{i}}
vertices. In particular, level 0 contains a single cluster of
k
vertices. We denote the set of vertices in the
i
-th level by
L_{i}
, and use
L_{\geq i}
to denote the set of all vertices of level at least
i
.

Next we add edges to the graph.
First, we form a clique out of each cluster.
This will ensure that any independent set in the graph contains at most one vertex from each cluster.
Next, we add random edges to the graph as follows.
Let
u
be some vertex in the
i
-th level, and let
v
be some vertex in the
j
-th level, where
i<j
. We add the edge
(u,v)
with probability

q_{i}\triangleq(k^{1/4^{i}-1})/\log^{2}n\ .

This completes the description of the hard graph family for MIS, which we denote by
G_{\mathrm{hard}}^{\mathrm{MIS}}{(k,\ell)}
.

Intuition for other graph parameters:

The reason that this lower bound does not contradict the improved algorithms in terms of
\Delta,d,\beta(G)
, is that in this graph family, we have that
\beta(G)
and

are both
\Omega(2^{\log^{0.75}n})

\log(\log(2^{\log^{0.75} n})/\sqrt{\log n})=\log(\log(2^{\log^{0.25} n}))=\Theta(\log\log(n))
.
This holds because
in

, an active vertex
v
of level

has

w.h.p, as this is the number of clusters in the last level, with at least one active vertex which is a neighbor of

, where
k^{1-4/4^{\ell}}q_{r}/2\geq k^{1/4^{r+1}}
.

We now state the main theorem of this section.

5.1

On the hard graph distribution

We denote the level of a vertex

We emphasize that both iterations and levels employ zero-based indexing.
The key ingredient is the following.

5.2

For every

Base Case:

When

G_{r}[F_{r}]
.
We will need to use the fact that
p_{r,v}\leq 4\log n/k^{1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}
, which we deduce
from the induction hypothesis as follows.

5.3
.

If the induction hypothesis holds for
r
, then the graph
G_{r}
has minimum degree smaller than
\Lambda=\frac{k^{1/4^{r}}}{16\log^{2}n}
with probability at most
\chi/2
.

Proof.

We begin with some technical bound on
\Lambda
, which shows that
\Lambda\geq 2^{\log^{3/4}n}
.
First note that

\displaystyle\frac{k^{1/4^{r}}}{2\log^{2}n}\geq 2^{\log k/4^{r}-2\log\log n}\;.

Therefore, to prove that
\Lambda\geq 2^{\log^{3/4}n}
, it suffices to show that
\log k/4^{r}\geq 2\log^{3/4}n
.
We prove that
\log k/4^{r}\geq 2\log^{3/4}n
:

\displaystyle\frac{\log k}{4^{r}}=\frac{\log k}{2^{2r}}\geq\frac{\log k}{2^{2\ell}}=\frac{\log k}{\log^{1/10}n}\geq\frac{\log k}{\log^{1/10}(k^{2})}\geq\log^{8/10}(k)\geq 2\log^{3/4}(k)\;.

We are now ready to prove the claim.
The induction hypothesis states that
G_{r}
has at least
k(1-10\ell\cdot r/\log^{2}n)
vertices in level
j
for
j\geq r
.
Note that
k(1-10\ell\cdot r/\log^{2}n)\geq k/2
.

We split the analysis into two cases: vertices in level at least
r+1
and vertices in level at most
r
.
Consider a vertex
u
in
G_{r}
that is level at
h>r
.
Since every vertex in
L_{r}
is a neighbor of
u
with probability
q_{r}
, independently of all other vertices, we have that
u
has in expectation at least
k\cdot q_{r}/2
neighbors in
G_{r}
, where

\displaystyle\frac{k\cdot q_{r}}{2}=\frac{k^{1/4^{r}}}{2\log^{2}n}=8\Lambda\;.

Therefore, by a Chernoff bound,
u
has degree at least
\Lambda
in
G_{r}
with probability at least
1-2^{-\Lambda/12}
.

Now consider a vertex
u
in
G_{r}
that is in level
h
for some
h\leq r
.
For the same reason,
u
has, in expectation, at least
(\ell-r)\cdot k\cdot q_{r}/2\geq k\cdot q_{r}/2
neighbors in
G_{r}
, and the same analysis applies.
To conclude, we get that the minimum degree of
G_{r}
is at least
\Lambda
with probability at least
1-n/2^{-\Lambda/12}
by a union bound over at most
n
vertices in
G_{r}
.
Since
\Lambda\geq 2^{\log^{3/4}n}
, we have that
1-n/2^{-\Lambda/12}\geq 1-\chi/2
, which completes the proof of the claim.
∎

Let us call
\mathscr{B}
the low-probability (bad) event “
G_{r}
has minimum degree
\delta(G)<\Lambda
”, where by
5.3
,
{\mathrm{Pr}}[{\mathscr{B}}]\leq\chi/2
.
Conditioning on
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
(the complement of
\mathscr{B}
), every vertex
v
is sampled in
S_{r}
with probability at most

p_{r,v}\leq 1/\sqrt{\Lambda}=4\log n/k^{1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}\ .

(3)

However, we emphasize that conditioning on
\mathscr{B}
affects the distribution of random edges in
G_{r}
; in particular, the existence of incident edges in
G_{r}
is not independent anymore.

In what follows, we explain how to prove the induction step by bounding the number of active vertices that are covered by
S_{r}
in each level
j>r
,
that is, the active vertices in
\Gamma(S_{r})\cap L_{j}
.
We partition the random set of active vertices in
\Gamma(S_{r})\cap L_{j}
into two sets, denoted by
L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
and
L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}
.
The set
L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
contains all active vertices
u\in L_{j}
that are
inside
a sampled cluster (a cluster for which at least one vertex is sampled into
F_{r}
). That is,
L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
contains all active vertices
u\in L_{j}
for which
C(u)\cap F_{r}\neq\emptyset
, where
C(u)
is the cluster of
u
.
The set
L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}
contains the rest of the active vertices in
\Gamma(S_{r})\cap L_{j}
. That is, it contains active vertices in
L_{j}
that are covered by
S_{r}
but are
outside
all sampled clusters.

We show that w.h.p.
|{L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}\cup L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}}|\leq k\cdot(10\ell/\log^{2}n)
, and therefore,
\absolutevalue{L_j\setminus\Gamma(S_r)}\geq k(1-10\ell/\log^{2}n)
w.h.p.
This means that
G_{r+1}
has at least
k(1-(r+1)\cdot 10\ell/\log^{2}n)

active vertices in
L_{j}
, w.h.p, and it therefore completes the induction step.

Bounding
|{L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}}|
, the number of vertices whose cluster is sampled.

We prove that for every
j>r
, we have that

|{L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}}|\leq k/\log^{2}n
, with probability at least
1-2^{-\sqrt{k}}-\chi/2
.
Note that the randomness for
L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
is over the choice of
F_{r}
, i.e., of
the random set of vertices that are sampled into
F_{r}
.
So we may condition on
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
, thus use
Equation

3
; randomness over edges of
G_{r}
play no other role in this part of the argument.
Let
X_{j}
denote the number of clusters in level
j
that intersect
F_{r}
.
Consider some cluster
C
in level
j
. Let
P_{j}(C)
denote the probability that it intersects
F_{r}
. We have

\displaystyle P_{j}(C)={\mathrm{Pr}}[{F_{r}\cap C\neq\emptyset~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]=1-(1-p_{r})^{\absolutevalue{C}}\leq p_{r}\cdot\absolutevalue{C}\leq p_{r}k^{1/4^{j}}\;.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[{X_{j}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]=\sum_{C:\mathsf{level}(C)=j}P_{j}(C)=p_{r}\cdot k^{1/4^{j}}k^{1-1/4^{j}}=k\cdot p_{r}
.
By plugging in
Equation

3
, we get that
k\cdot p_{r}\leq 4\log n\cdot\sqrt{k}
, which means that
\mathbb{E}[{X_{j}}]\leq 4\log n\cdot k^{1-1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}
.
Let
\gamma=4\log n\cdot k^{1-1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}
.

Each cluster intersects
F_{r}
independently of all other clusters, as vertices are sampled into
F_{r}
independently of each other.
This means that
X_{j}
is the sum of
k^{1-1/4^{j}}
independent Bernoulli random variables, where each one is equal to
1
with probability at most
p_{r}\cdot k^{1/4^{j}}
.
We can apply Chernoff’s inequality on
X_{j}
to get that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}[{X_{j}\geq 6\gamma~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq 2^{-\gamma}\leq 2^{-\sqrt{k}}\;.

Finally, we have
|{L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}}|\leq X_{j}\cdot k^{1/4^{j}}
because each cluster in level
j
contains at most
k^{1/4^{j}}
vertices, and hence by recalling that
j>r
we get

\displaystyle|{L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}}|\leq 6\gamma k^{1/4^{j}}=24k^{1-1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}k^{1/4^{j}}\log n\leq 24k^{1-1/4^{r+1}}\log n\leq k/\log^{2}n\;,

as needed. By the law of total probability, this holds with probability
{\mathrm{Pr}}[{\mathscr{B}}]+{\mathrm{Pr}}[{X_{j}<6\gamma~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\geq 1-2^{-{\sqrt{k}}}-\chi/2
as claimed.

Bounding
|{L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}}|
, the number of covered vertices whose cluster is not sampled.

To bound
|{L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}}|
, we consider an equivalent process in which we first sample a set
F_{r}
, sample the random edges in
G[F_{r}]
and compute
S_{r}
, and only then sample the random edges between
F_{r}
and
V\setminus F_{r}
. Thus, we can consider
F_{r}
and
S_{r}
as fixed and compute
|{L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}}|
conditioned on their choice. Recall that
S_{r}
must: be a subset of
F_{r}
, and be an independent set in
G_{r}[F_{r}]
.

It is important to note that the set of random edges that we sample between
F_{r}
and
V\setminus F_{r}
are only sampled once, as we only sample edges between active vertices where at least one endpoint is also sampled into
F_{r}
.
This means that in the next iteration, all of these edges touch at least one inactive endpoint, and therefore are not sampled again.

Fix
j>r
. We want to bound the number of active vertices from level
j
that have a neighbor in
S_{r}
, but which are not in
L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
, i.e., their cluster is not sampled.
For this, we partition the vertices of
S_{r}
according to their level. Let
S_{r}^{i}
denote the subset of vertices in
S_{r}
of level
i
for
0\leq i<\ell
.
We define a random variable
X^{i}
equal to the number of active vertices in level
j
that have a neighbor in
S_{r}^{i}
, which are not in
L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
.
Note that
|{L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}}|=\sum_{0\leq i<\ell}X^{i}
. For each
i
,
we have that
X^{i}
is a sum of at most
\absolutevalue{L_j}
indicator random variables, for the events that each active vertex in
L_{j}\setminus L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
has a neighbor in
S_{r}^{i}
. These random variables are independent, because after fixing
F_{r}
and
S_{r}
, we add edges between
S_{r}
and vertices in
L_{j}\setminus L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
independently.

To bound
X^{i}
, we split into two cases.

When
i\leq r
.

Any independent set in
G
that is contained in level
i
has at most
k^{1-1/4^{i}}
vertices – at most one vertex in each cluster in level
i
. In particular,
|{S_{r}^{i}}|\leq k^{1-1/4^{i}}
.
Fix some active vertex
v\in L_{j}\setminus L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
.
Recall that the probability that a random edge is added between vertices in levels
i<j
is
q_{i}=k^{1/4^{i}-1}/\log^{2}n
.
The probability that
v\in N(S_{r}^{i})
is thus equal to

\displaystyle 1-(1-q_{i})^{|{S_{r}^{i}}|}\leq|S_{r}^{i}|\cdot q_{i}\leq 1/\log^{2}n\;.

We therefore get that
X^{i}
has the distribution of a Binomial random variable with at most
|{L_{j}}|
trials and success probability at most
1/\log^{2}n
. Therefore, by a Chernoff bound, we have that
{\mathrm{Pr}}[{X^{i}\geq 6k/\log^{2}n}]\leq 2^{-6k/\log^{2}n}\leq\chi
.
We emphasize that in this case, we do not use
Equation

3
, thus do not need to condition on
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
. And so edges are indeed added in
G_{r}
independently, which allows us to use the Chernoff Bound.

When
i>r
.
Recall that
X^{i}
is the number of active vertices in
L_{j}\setminus L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
, which have a neighbor in
S_{r}\cap L_{i}
.
We define a new random variable
\hat{X}^{i}
equal to the number of active vertices in
L_{j}\setminus L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
, which have a neighbor in
F_{r}\cap L_{i}
.
Note that the latter is a larger set, i.e.,
X^{i}\leq\hat{X}^{i}
. Thus, a bound on
\hat{X}^{i}
directly bounds
X^{i}
.

Let us first bound the number of vertices in
F_{r}\cap L_{i}
. Let
p
be the upper bound on
p_{r,v}
given by
Equation

3
. Conditioning on
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
, a vertex
v
joins
F_{r}
with probability at most
p_{r,v}\leq p
and
\mathbb{E}[{F_{r}\cap L_{i}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq pk
. Since sampling in
F_{r}
is independent for every vertex (even with the condition on
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
), a Chernoff bound shows that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}[{|F_{r}\cap L_{i}|>6kp}]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathrm{Pr}}[{\mathscr{B}}]+{\mathrm{Pr}}[{|F_{r}\cap L_{i}|>6kp~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq\chi/2+2^{-kp}\leq\chi\ .

On the other hand, we can bound the expected maximum degree in
L_{j}
of a vertex in
L_{i}
by
kq_{r+1}
since every edge between the two sets is sampled in the input graph with probability at most
\max\{q_{i},q_{j}\}\leq q_{r+1}
. Note that the bound on expected degrees does not rely on
Equation

3
, hence we do
not
need to condition on
\mathscr{B}
and edges are added to the input graph independently. And so, by a Chernoff Bound, every vertex in
L_{i}
has at most
2kq_{r+1}
neighbors in
L_{j}
with probability at least
1-2^{-kq_{i}}\geq 1-\chi
.
By union bound, with very high probability, both bounds hold and thus the number of vertices in
L_{j}
adjacent to a vertex in
L_{i}\cap F_{r}
is at most

\hat{X}_{i}\leq 2q\cdot p\cdot k^{2}=8k^{1-\frac{1}{2\cdot 4^{r}}+\frac{1}{4^{r+1}}}/\log n\leq k/\log^{2}n\ .

Putting Things Together.

By union bound over pairs
i,j\in\{0,1,\ldots,r\}
, we have that
|{L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}}|+|{L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}}|\leq k\cdot(10\ell/\log^{2}n)
with probability at least
1-O(\ell^{2}\chi)
.
By the induction hypothesis,
G_{r}
has at least
k(1-r\cdot 10\ell/\log^{2}n)
active vertices in level
j
. In iteration
r
, the set of vertices in level
j
that become covered is contained in
\Gamma(S_{r})\cap L_{j}
, where
\Gamma(S_{r})\cap L_{j}\subseteq L^{\mathrm{out}}_{j}\cup L^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}
.
Therefore, the number of active vertices in level
j
in
G_{r+1}
is at least
k(1-(r+1)\cdot 10\ell/\log^{2}n)
with probability at least
1-O(\ell^{2}\chi)
.
A union bound over all
r/2\leq\ell
iterations implies that the induction hypothesis holds in each iteration, with probability at least
1-O(\ell^{3}\chi)\geq 1-2^{2^{-\sqrt{\log n}}}
by definition of
\chi
.
∎

5.2
Maximal Matching

Denote the algorithm for computing a maximal matching specified in
Proposition

3.4
by
ReduceMM
.
In this section, we construct a graph, on which the algorithm
ReduceMM
takes at least
\Omega(\log\log n)
iterations to compute an MIS with high probability.
This analysis also implies that the state-of-the art algorithm for computing maximal matchings presented in
[
BHH23
]
takes
\Omega(\log\log n)
iterations to compute a maximal matching on this graph with high probability, and therefore shows that the analysis in
[
BHH23
]
is tight.

Recall how the
ReduceMM
algorithm works.
Let
G_{0}=G
be an
n
-vertex
m
-edge graph.
In the
r
-th iteration, it randomly partitions the active vertices into

1/p_{r}
parts, denoted
(F_{1}^{r},\ldots,F_{1/p_{r}}^{r})
, where
p_{r}=1/\sqrt{d(G_{r})}
.
The algorithm then computes the lexicographically first maximal matching on each part, denoted by
(M_{1}^{r},\ldots,M_{1/p_{r}}^{r})
.
The final phase of the algorithm is called a clean-up step, in which it matches vertices of degree at least
\Delta^{0.92}
.
We ignore the implementation details of this phase, as it adds at most
n/\Delta^{0.01}
edges to the matching with high probability. Therefore, it does not matter exactly how these high-degree vertices are matched as this algorithm matches too few vertices in each iteration to form a maximal matching faster than in
\Omega\left(\log(\log(n))\right)
iterations.

Let
E_{\mathrm{s}}^{r}\triangleq\bigcup_{i\in[1/p_{r}]}E(G[F_{i}^{r}])
denote the set of edges that are contained in
F_{i}^{r}
for some
i
. We refer to this set as the set of
r
-active edges.
Let
M_{all}^{r}
denote the union of all of the matchings
(M_{1}^{r},\ldots,M_{1/p_{r}}^{r})
, and let
\mathcal{M}^{r}=\bigcup_{j\in\{0,1,\ldots,r\}}M_{all}^{j}
.
We define
G_{r+1}=G[V\setminus V(\mathcal{M}^{r})]
.
We say that a vertex is not active in iteration
r
if it is matched before iteration
r
, and say it is active otherwise.

We construct a hard graph distribution, denoted
G_{\mathrm{hard}}^{\mathrm{MM}}
.
We take a graph with
n=k\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}4^{i}=k(4^{\ell}-1)/3
vertices, instead of
k\ell
, where
\ell=\log\log(k)/20
.
We partition its vertices into
\ell
levels, where the level
i
has
4^{i}\cdot k
vertices.
This change will help us prove that the average degree does not decrease too much from iteration to iteration.
We partition the vertices of level
i
into
4^{i}\cdot k^{1-1/4^{i}}
clusters, where each is a clique of size
k^{1/4^{i}}
.
We also make sure that each cluster has an even number of vertices, which
means the graph has a perfect matching.
This means that every maximal matching matches at least
n/2
vertices, and that if the current graph contains more than
n/2
active vertices, then the computes matching is not maximal.
Finally, random edges are added in the same manner as in the MIS proof: Let
u
be some vertex in the
i
-th level, and let
v
be some vertex in the
j
-th level, where
i<j
. We add the edge
(u,v)
with probability
q_{i}\triangleq(k^{1/4^{i}-1})/\log^{2}n
.

5.4
.

On the hard graph distribution
G_{\mathrm{hard}}^{\mathrm{MM}}(k,\ell)
, where
\ell=\log(\log(k))/20
,
the algorithm
ReduceMM
takes
\Omega\left(\log(\log n)\right)
iterations to compute a maximal matching, with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.

We use the following lemma.

5.5
.

For every
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell/2\}
and every
r\leq j<\ell
, the graph
G_{r}
has at least
(4^{j}\cdot k\cdot\frac{2}{3})\cdot(1-10\ell\cdot r/\log^{2}n)
active vertices in
L_{j}
, with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.

Lemma

5.5
implies that the matching after
\ell/2
iterations,
the number of active vertices is at least

\displaystyle\sum_{j=\ell/2}^{\ell}4^{j}k\cdot(1-o(1))\cdot 2/3

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2k\cdot(1-o(1))}{3}\cdot 4^{\ell/2}\cdot\frac{4^{\ell/2+1}-1}{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2k\cdot(1-o(1))}{9}\cdot(4^{\ell+1}-4^{\ell/2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8n\cdot(1-o(1))}{9}>3n/4\;.

And if the number of active vertices is at least
3n/4
, then the matching is of size smaller
n/2
. Since
G
contains a perfect matching, the algorithm is not done yet.

Proof of
Lemma

5.5
.

We prove the lemma by induction on
r
, where the base case
r=0
is trivial, as
G_{0}=G
, and then every vertex is active.
We need the following bound on the sampling probability
p_{r}
.

5.6
.

Assuming the induction hypothesis holds for
r-1
, we have that

p_{r}\leq\log n/k^{1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}

(4)

with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.

Proof of
5.6
.

Recall that
G_{r+1}=G[V\setminus V(\mathcal{M}^{r})]
, and that
p_{r}=1/\sqrt{d(G_{r})}
.
The induction hypothesis states that for every
j\geq r
, the graph
G_{r}
has at least

(4^{j}\cdot k\cdot\frac{2}{3})\cdot(1-o(1))
active vertices.
We look at the number of edges between level
r
and level
\ell-1
.
We first count the number of pairs of active vertices with one endpoint in level
r
and one in level
\ell-1
.
There are at least
(4^{r}2k/3)\cdot(4^{\ell}2k/3)\geq k^{2}\cdot 4^{r+4}
such active pairs between the levels.
Because we add an edge between each such pair independently with probability
q_{r}
, we get that the expected number of edges in the graph is at least
k^{2}\cdot 4^{r+4}\cdot q_{r}
, and that the graph has at least
k^{2}\cdot 4^{r+2}\cdot q_{i}
edges, with probability at least
1-2^{-k^{2}\cdot q_{i}/64}
, with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.
This means that the average degree of
G_{r}
is at least
k^{2}\cdot 4^{r+4}\cdot q_{r}/n\geq k\cdot q_{r}\cdot 4^{r}
, where the inequality follows because
n\leq k\cdot 4^{\ell+1}
.
We conclude that the average degree is at least
k\cdot q_{r}/64
, with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.
Since the average degree is at least
k\cdot q_{r}/\log n
, we get that
p_{r+1}\leq 1/\sqrt{k\cdot q_{r}/\log n}\leq\log n/k^{1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}
,
with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.
Recall that the clean-up step ensures that

p_{r+2}\geq(p_{r+1})^{1/0.92}
w.h.p, which we indeed satisfy.
∎

Define
\mathscr{B}
the low-probability event that the average degree is too large, so that
Equation

4
holds when
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
occurs.
We have that
{\mathrm{Pr}}[{\mathscr{B}}]\leq 2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.

We now have all the ingredients to prove the
Lemma

5.5
.
We prove the induction step for
r+1
instead of
r
for brevity.
We prove that for every
j\geq r+1
the graph
G_{r+1}
has at least
(4^{j}\cdot k\cdot\frac{2}{3})\cdot(1-10\ell\cdot r/\log^{2}n)
active vertices in
L_{j}
.
To prove the lemma, we bound the number of edges in
\mathcal{M}^{r}
, which is useful as
G_{r+1}=G[V\setminus V(\mathcal{M}^{r})]
.
We use the following notation.
We define the set
E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
as the set of edges with level at most
r
, where we define the level of an edge
e=(u,v)
as the minimum between the level of
u
and the level of
v
.
We split the edges of
\mathcal{M}^{r}
into two sets: the edges in
E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
and other edges.

Bounding
\absolutevalue{\Er\cap\calM^r}
.

We claim that
\absolutevalue{\Er\cap\calM^r}\leq\frac{4^{r+1}-1}{3}\cdot k
. This is because the number of vertices with level at most
r
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}4^{i}k=\frac{4^{r+1}-1}{3}\cdot k\;.

This means that the set of edges
\absolutevalue{\Er\cap\calM^r}
match at most
2\cdot 4^{r+1}\cdot k/3
vertices in total, where at least half of the vertices are of level at most
r
, otherwise the level of the edges is not at most
r
.
Therefore, for every
j\geq r+1
, among the
4^{j}\cdot k
vertices in level

, at most a third of them are matched in

, and the rest are active in
G_{r+1}
.

In what follows, we bound the number of remaining edges in
\mathcal{M}^{r}
, i.e., those in
\mathcal{M}^{r}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
.
We prove that this set of edges contains at most
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}})\ll k/\log n
edges, with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.
This proves that there are at least
(4^{j}\cdot k\cdot\frac{2}{3})\cdot(1-o(1))
active vertices in
L_{j}
in
G_{r+1}
, for every
j\geq r+1
, as the set
\mathcal{M}^{r}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
can deactivate at most
2k/\log n=o(k)
vertices in each level.
This completes the proof, as we show that the induction hypothesis holds for
r+1
.

For the rest of this proof, we focus on bounding the number of edges in
\mathcal{M}^{r}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
.
Recall that we say that an edge is active in iteration
i
, if both of its endpoints fall inside the same part
F_{h}^{i}
for some
h
, and
denote by
E_{\mathrm{s}}^{i}
the set of active edges in iteration
i
.
Let
\bigcup_{i\leq r}E_{\mathrm{s}}^{i}
denote the set of edges that are active in all iterations
i\leq r
. Let
E^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i\leq r}E_{\mathrm{s}}^{i}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
denote the set of edges of level at least
r+1
that are active in some iteration
i\leq r
.
We partition the set of edges
E^{\prime}
into two sets of edges
E^{\mathrm{in}}
and
E^{\mathrm{out}}
.
The set
E^{\mathrm{in}}
contains all edges
e\in E^{\prime}
for which
both endpoints belong to the same cluster.
The set
E^{\mathrm{out}}
contains the rest of the edges in
E^{\prime}
.
We claim that
\mathcal{M}^{r}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}\subseteq E^{\prime}
, which follows as an edge can join the matching
\mathcal{M}^{r}
only if it is active in some iteration
i\leq r
, and therefore
\mathcal{M}^{r}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}\subseteq E^{\prime}=E^{\mathrm{in}}\cup E^{\mathrm{out}}
.
To bound the left-hand side, we bound the size of
E^{\mathrm{in}}
and
E^{\mathrm{out}}
, separately.

We show that
E^{\mathrm{in}}
contains at most
2k/\log n
edges with probability at least
1-2^{-{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}
.

Bounding
|{E^{\mathrm{in}}}|
, the number of in-cluster edges of level at least
r+1
.

For
j\geq r+1
,
let
Z_{j}
be a random variable equal to
|E^{\mathrm{in}}\cap E_{\mathrm{s}}^{r}|
, which is the number of active edges in iteration
r
which belong to a cluster of level
j
.
The probability of an edge becoming active in iteration
r
is significantly larger than in previous iterations.
Therefore, it suffices to bound the size of the set
|E^{\mathrm{in}}\cap E_{\mathrm{s}}^{r}|
, as

\sum_{i=0}^{r}|E^{\mathrm{in}}\cap E_{\mathrm{s}}^{i}|
is dominated by
r\cdot|E^{\mathrm{in}}\cap E_{\mathrm{s}}^{r}|
.

Henceforth, we shall assume
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
holds, so we case use
Equation

4
. Note that the argument here does not rely on the randomness of edges in
G_{r+1}
.

We compute
\mathbb{E}[{Z_{j}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]
as follows.
For every edge
e
inside a cluster of level
j
, let
X_{e}
denote the indicator random variable for the event that
e
is active in iteration
r
.
Then,
Z_{j}
is the sum of
m_{j}
indicator random variables, where

m_{j}\leq 4^{j}\cdot k^{1-1/4^{j}}\cdot(k^{1/4^{j}})^{2}\leq 4^{j}\cdot k^{1+1/4^{j}}
. For each
X_{e}
the probability that
X_{e}=1
is
p_{r}
, where
p_{r}\leq\log n/k^{1/{2\cdot 4^{r}}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{Z_{j}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]=m_{j}\cdot p_{r}\leq 4^{j}\cdot k^{1+1/4^{j}-1/4^{r}}\cdot\log n\leq 4^{j}\log n\cdot k^{1-3/4^{r+1}}\;,

where the last inequality follows because
r<j
.

We can also present
Z_{j}
as the sum of different
independent
random variables, to prove a concentration bound on
Z_{j}
.
For every cluster
C
with
\mathsf{level}(C)=j
, let
X_{C}
denote the number of active edges in
C
.
Note that the random variables
\{X_{C}\}_{C:\;\mathsf{level}(C)=j}
are independent (even subject to the conditioning on
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
). That is because activation of an edge depends only on the part
F_{x}^{r}
that its endpoint belong to and each vertex samples its part independently.
A cluster in level
j
contains at most
k^{1/4^{j}}
vertices, therefore
X_{C}\leq(k^{1/4^{j}})^{2}
(always).
Let
Y_{j}=\sum_{C:\;\mathsf{level}(C)=j}X_{C}
.
We have
Z_{j}=Y_{j}
as they count the same quantity, and therefore
\mathbb{E}[{Z_{j}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]=\mathbb{E}[{Y_{j}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]
.
We apply Hoeffding’s inequality on
Y_{j}
, as it is the sum of
4^{j}\cdot k^{1-1/4^{j}}
independent random variables in the interval
[0,(k^{1/4^{j}})^{2}]
.
Therefore, we get

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}[{Y\geq\mathbb{E}[{Y}]+t~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq\exp(-2t^2/ (4^j\cdot k^{1-1/4^j}\cdot(k^{1/4^j})^4))\leq\exp(-2t^2/ (4^j\cdot k^{1+3/4^j}))\;.

We plug in
t=n/2^{2{\sqrt{\log n}}}
, where
t\geq 2\mathbb{E}[{Y}]
and get that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}[{Y\geq\mathbb{E}[{Y}]+t}]\leq{\mathrm{Pr}}[{\mathscr{B}}]+\exp(-n^{1/4}/2^{4\sl})\;.

Therefore, w.h.p. we get that
Y_{j}\leq 2n/2^{2{\sqrt{\log n}}}
. This means that
|E^{\mathrm{in}}|\leq 2\ell\cdot n/2^{2{\sqrt{\log n}}}
.
This completes the bound on
|E^{\mathrm{in}}|
.

In what follows, we bound the number of edges in
E^{\mathrm{out}}
that joins the matching
\mathcal{M}^{r}
, we denote this quantity by
|E^{\mathrm{out}}\cap\mathcal{M}^{r}|
.

Bounding
|E^{\mathrm{out}}\cap\mathcal{M}^{r}|
, the number of out-cluster edges of level at least
r+1
, that join the matching
\mathcal{M}^{r}
.

Let
U
denote the set of vertices of level at least
r+1
that are active in iteration
r
, i.e.
U=\bigcup_{j\geq r+1}L_{j}\setminus V(\mathcal{M}^{r})
.
For every vertex
v\in U
we define a random variable
Z_{v}
.
Informally, this is an indicator random variable for the event that
v
“can” add an edge
e\in E^{\mathrm{out}}
to
\mathcal{M}^{r}
.
Formally,
Z_{v}
is defined as follows.
Given a partition of the vertices into parts
(F_{1}^{r},\ldots,F_{1/p_{r}}^{r})
,
where a vertex
v
is in part
F_{h}^{r}
for some
h\in[1/p_{r}]
, we define
Z_{v}
as the indicator random variable for the event that
v
has a neighbor
u\in U-C(v)
which is also in
F_{h}^{r}
. Since
u
and
v
are neighbors, and they belong to different clusters, we have that
u
and
v
are not in the same level.

Let
Z=\sum_{v\in U}Z_{v}
, i.e.,
Z
is the sum of all
Z_{v}
over all active vertices of level at least
r+1
.
We claim that
Z\geq|E^{\mathrm{out}}\cap\mathcal{M}^{r}|
, which follows as each vertex
v\in U
can add at most one edge to
\mathcal{M}^{r}\cap E^{\mathrm{out}}
, and if
Z_{v}=0
, then
v
does not add any edge to
\mathcal{M}^{r}
.

We are left with bounding
Z
.
Henceforth, we shall assume
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
holds, so we case use
Equation

4
.
We sample a partition of the vertices into parts
(F_{1}^{r},\ldots,F_{1/p_{r}}^{r})
, and then we sample the edges between the vertices in
U
.
This means that the random variables
\{Z_{v}\}_{v\in U}
are independent.
More precisely, these variables are not independent in general because any one of these variables reveals information about the size of a part in the partition, which then restricts the other variable. Yet, after conditioning on the partition, they become independent, which is sufficient for our needs.

We fix a vertex
v\in U
and show that
\mathbb{E}[{Z_{v}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq 1/k^{1/4^{r}}
.
Assume without loss of generality that
v
is in the part
F_{1}^{r}
.
The expected number of vertices in
F_{1}^{r}
is
\absolutevalue{U}\cdot p_{r}
.
Let
N_{v}
denote the number of neighbors of
v
in
F_{1}^{r}
that are not in the same cluster (or level) as
v
.
Note that every vertex in
F_{1}^{r}
is a neighbor of
v
independently with probability
q_{\mathsf{level}(v)}\leq q_{r+1}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{N_{v}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[{q_{r+1}\cdot\absolutevalue{F_1^r}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq np_{r}\cdot q_{r+1}\;.

We plug in the value of
p_{r}\leq\log n/k^{1/(2\cdot 4^{r})}
from
Equation

4
and
q_{r+1}=k^{1/4^{r+1}-1}/\log^{2}n
. Let
\beta=k^{1/4^{r}}
, then we have that
p_{r}\leq\log n/\sqrt{\beta}
, and
q_{r+1}=\beta^{1/4}/(k\cdot\log^{2}n)
, and therefore,

\displaystyle n\cdot p_{r}\cdot q_{r+1}=\frac{k(4^{\ell}-1)}{3}\cdot p_{r}\cdot q_{r+1}=\frac{4^{\ell}-1}{3}\cdot\frac{k\cdot\log n\cdot\beta^{1/4}}{k\cdot\log^{2}n\cdot\sqrt{\beta}}=\frac{4^{\ell}-1}{3\log n\cdot\beta^{1/4}}\leq 1/\beta^{1/4}\;.

Therefore, we have that
\mathbb{E}[{N_{v}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq 1/\beta^{1/4}
, and since
Z_{v}
is the indicator random variable for the event that
\{N_{v}>0\}
, we get that
\mathbb{E}[{Z_{v}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq 1/\beta^{1/4}
.
By Markov’s inequality, we get that
{\mathrm{Pr}}[{Z_{v}>0~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[{Z_{v}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq 1/\beta^{1/4}
.

Next, we apply a Chernoff bound on
Z
, which is a Binomial random variable with
\absolutevalue{U}
trials and success probability at most
1/\beta^{1/4}
.
Therefore, the expectation of
Z
is at most
n/k^{1/4^{r}}\ll n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}
, and therefore by a Chernoff bound, we get that
Z\leq 6n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}
with probability at least
1-2^{-n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}+{\mathrm{Pr}}[{\mathscr{B}}]
.
This proves that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}[{|E^{\mathrm{out}}\cap\mathcal{M}^{r}|>6n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}]\leq{\mathrm{Pr}}[{\mathscr{B}}]+{\mathrm{Pr}}[{|E^{\mathrm{out}}\cap\mathcal{M}^{r}|>6n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}~|~\overline{\mathscr{B}}}]\leq 2\cdot 2^{-n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}}\;,

which completes this part of the proof.

Putting It All Together.

Fix some level
j\geq r+1
.
We showed that
E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}\cap\mathcal{M}^{r}
covers at most a fraction of
2/3
of the vertices at level
j
.
For the remaining edges in
\mathcal{M}^{r}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}^{r}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
, we defined the sets
E^{\mathrm{in}}
and
E^{\mathrm{out}}
and showed that each set contains at most
O(2n/2^{{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}})
and
6n/{2^{\sqrt{\log n}}}
edges, respectively.
This means
\mathcal{M}^{r}\setminus E_{\mathsf{level}\leq r}
contains at most
o(k)
edges with high probability.
This means that at least
2(1-o(1))/3
fraction of the vertices in level at
j\geq r+1
are active in
G_{r+1}
, which is what we wanted to prove.
This completes the proof of the induction step, and therefore the proof of
Lemma

5.5
.
∎
