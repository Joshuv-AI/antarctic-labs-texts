Symmetric Sextic Freud Weight
Peter A. Clarkson, Kerstin Jordaan, Ana Loureiro

Abstract
In this study we are concerned with the properties of the sequence of coefficients ( β n ) n ≥ 0 (\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0} in the recurrence relation satisfied by the sequence of monic symmetric polynomials, orthogonal with respect to the symmetric sextic Freud weight ω ⁡ ( x , τ , t ) = exp ⁡ ( − x 6 + τ ​ x 4 + t ​ x 2 ) , x ∈ ℝ , \omega(x;\tau,t)=\exp\!\big(\!-x^{6}+\tau x^{4}+tx^{2}\big),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}, with real parameters τ \tau and t t . It is known that the recurrence coefficients β n \beta_{n} for the symmetric sextic Freud weight satisfy a fourth-order nonlinear discrete equation, which is a special case of the second member of the discrete Painlevé I hierarchy, often known as the “string equation”. The recurrence coefficients have been studied in the context of Hermitian one-matrix models and random symmetric matrix ensembles with researchers in the 1990s observing “chaotic, pseudo-oscillatory” behaviour. More recently, this “chaotic phase” was described as a dispersive shockwave in a hydrodynamic chain. Our emphasis is a comprehensive study of the behaviour of the recurrence coefficients as the parameters τ \tau and t t vary. Extensive computational analysis is carried out, using Maple, for critical parameter ranges, and graphical plots are presented to illustrate the behaviour of the recurrence coefficients as well as the complexity of the associated Volterra lattice hierarchy. The corresponding symmetric sextic Freud polynomials are shown to satisfy a second-order differential equation with rational coefficients. The moments of the weight are examined in detail, including their integral representations, differential equations, and recursive structure. Closed-form expressions for moments are obtained in several special cases in terms of generalised hypergeometric functions and modified Bessel functions, and asymptotic expansions for the recurrence coefficients are given. The results highlight the rich algebraic and analytic structures underlying the Freud weight and its connections to integrable systems.



Keywords
: Freud weight; semi-classical orthogonal polynomials; recurrence coefficients; generalised hypergeometric functions; discrete Painlevé equations; Hermitian random matrices

Orthogonal polynomials with symmetric weights

The sequence of monic polynomials
\big\{P_{n}(x)\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of exact degree
n\in\mathbb{N}
is orthogonal with respect to a positive weight
\omega(x)
on
the real line
\mathbb{R}
if

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{m}(x)P_{n}(x)\,\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=h_{n}\delta_{m,n},\qquad h_{n}>0,

where
\delta_{m,n}
denotes the Kronecker delta, see, for example
[
31
,
72
,
109
,
112
]
.
Monic orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}(x)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfy a three-term recurrence relationship of the form

P_{n+1}(x)=xP_{n}(x)-\alpha_{n}P_{n}(x)-\beta_{n}P_{n-1}(x),

where the coefficients
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
are given by the integrals

\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xP_{n}^{2}(x)\,\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{h_{n-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xP_{n-1}(x)P_{n}(x)\,\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

with
P_{-1}(x)=0
and
P_{0}(x)=1
.

The weights of classical orthogonal polynomials satisfy a first-order ordinary differential equation, the
Pearson equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{{\mathrm{d}{x}}}[\sigma(x)\,\omega(x)]=\vartheta(x)\,\omega(x),

(2.1)

where
\sigma(x)
is a monic polynomial of degree at most
2
and
\vartheta(x)
is a polynomial with degree
1
. However for
semi-classical
orthogonal polynomials, the weight function
\omega(x)
satisfies the Pearson equation (
2.1
) with either deg
(\sigma)>2
or deg
(\vartheta)\neq 1
, cf.
[
67
,
90
,
112
]
.
For example, the sextic Freud weight (
1.1
)
satisfies the Pearson equation (
2.1
) with

\sigma(x)=x,\qquad\vartheta(x)={-6x^{6}+4\tau x^{4}+2tx^{2}+1},

so is a semi-classical weight.

For an orthogonality weight that is symmetric, i.e. when
\omega(x)=\omega(-x)
, it follows that
\alpha_{n}\equiv 0
and the monic orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}(x)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfy the simplified three-term recurrence relation

P_{n+1}(x)=xP_{n}(x)-\beta_{n}P_{n-1}(x).

(2.2)

The
k
th moment,
\mu_{k}
, associated with the weight
\omega(x)
is given by the integral

\mu_{k}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{k}\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

while the determinant of moments, known as Hankel determinant, is

\Delta_{n}=\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits\big[\mu_{j+k}\big]_{j,k=0}^{n-1}=\left|\begin{matrix}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}&\ldots&\mu_{n-1}\\
\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\ldots&\mu_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mu_{n-1}&\mu_{n}&\ldots&\mu_{2n-2}\end{matrix}\right|,\qquad n\geq 1,

(2.3)

where
\Delta_{0}=1
and
\Delta_{-1}=0
, which corresponds to the Heine formula (
1.3
). The recurrence coefficient
\beta_{n}
in (
2.2
) can be expressed in terms of the Hankel determinant as

\beta_{n}=\frac{\Delta_{n+1}\Delta_{n-1}}{\Delta_{n}^{2}}.

(2.4)

For symmetric weights
\mu_{2k-1}\equiv 0
, for
k=1,2,\ldots\
, and so
it is possible to write the Hankel determinant
\Delta_{n}
in terms of the product of two Hankel determinants, as given in the following lemma. The decomposition depends on whether
n
is even or odd.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that

\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
\mathcal{B}_{n}
are the Hankel determinants given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left|\begin{matrix}\mu_{0}&\mu_{2}&\ldots&\mu_{2n-2}\\
\mu_{2}&\mu_{4}&\ldots&\mu_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mu_{2n-2}&\mu_{2n}&\ldots&\mu_{4n-4}\end{matrix}\right|,\qquad\mathcal{B}_{n}=\left|\begin{matrix}\mu_{2}&\mu_{4}&\ldots&\mu_{2n}\\
\mu_{4}&\mu_{6}&\ldots&\mu_{2n+2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mu_{2n}&\mu_{2n+2}&\ldots&\mu_{4n-2}\end{matrix}\right|.

(2.5)

Then the determinant
\Delta_{n}
(
2.3
) is given by

\Delta_{2n}=\mathcal{A}_{n}\mathcal{B}_{n},\qquad\Delta_{2n+1}=\mathcal{A}_{n+1}\mathcal{B}_{n}.

(2.6)

Proof.

The result is easily obtained by matrix manipulation interchanging rows and columns.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The expression of the Hankel determinant
\Delta_{n}
for symmetric weights as a product of two determinants is given in
[
27
,
87
]
.

Corollary 2.3
.

For a symmetric weight, the recurrence relation coefficient
\beta_{n}
is given by

\beta_{2n}=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{n+1}\mathcal{B}_{n-1}}{\mathcal{A}_{n}\mathcal{B}_{n}},\qquad\beta_{2n+1}=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{n}\mathcal{B}_{n+1}}{\mathcal{A}_{n+1}\mathcal{B}_{n}},

where
\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
\mathcal{B}_{n}
are the Hankel determinants given by (
2.5
), with
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{B}_{0}=1
.

Proof.

Substituting (
2.6
) into (
2.4
) gives the result.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\omega_{0}(x)
be a symmetric positive weight on the real line and suppose that

\omega(x;\tau,t)=\exp(tx^{2}+\tau x^{4})\,\omega_{0}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

is a weight such that all the moments also exist. Then the recurrence coefficient
\beta_{n}(\tau,t)
satisfies the Volterra, or the Langmuir lattice, equation

\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}=\beta_{n}(\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{n-1}),

(2.7)

and the differential-difference equation

\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}=\beta_{n}\left[(\beta_{n+2}+\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n})\beta_{n+1}-(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n-2})\beta_{n-1}\right].

(2.8)

Proof.

By definition

h_{n}(\tau,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}^{2}(x;\tau,t)\,\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(2.9)

and then differentiating this with respect to
t
gives

\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(x;\tau,t)\frac{\partial{P_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}(x;\tau,t)\,\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}^{2}(x;\tau,t)\,x^{2}\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.10)

Since
P_{n}(x;\tau,t)
is a monic polynomial of degree
n
in
x
, then
\displaystyle\frac{\partial{P_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}(x;\tau,t)
is a polynomial of degree less than
n
and so

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(x;\tau,t)\frac{\partial{P_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}(x;\tau,t)\,\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(2.11)

Using this and the recurrence relation (
2.2
)
in (
2.10
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[P_{n+1}(x;\tau,t)+\beta_{n}(\tau,t)P_{n-1}(x;\tau,t)\right]^{2}\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[P_{n+1}^{2}(x;\tau,t)+\beta_{n}^{2}(\tau,t)P_{n-1}^{2}(x;\tau,t)\right]\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=h_{n+1}+\beta_{n}^{2}h_{n-1}

using the orthogonality of
P_{n+1}(x;\tau,t)
and
P_{n-1}(x;\tau,t)
,
and so it follows from

h_{n}=\beta_{n}h_{n-1},

(2.12)

that

\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}=h_{n+1}+\beta_{n}h_{n}.

(2.13)

Differentiating (
2.12
) with respect to
t
gives

\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}=\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}h_{n-1}+\beta_{n}\frac{\partial{h_{n-1}}}{{\partial{t}}},

and then using (
2.13
) gives

h_{n+1}+\beta_{n}h_{n}=\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}h_{n-1}+\beta_{n}(h_{n}+\beta_{n-1}h_{n-1}).

Since
h_{n+1}=\beta_{n+1}h_{n}=\beta_{n+1}\beta_{n}h_{n-1}
, then we obtain

\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{t}}}=\beta_{n}(\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{n-1}),

as required.

To prove (
2.8
), differentiating (
2.9
) with respect to
\tau
gives

\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(x;\tau,t)\frac{\partial{P_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}(x;\tau,t)\,\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}^{2}(x;\tau,t)\,x^{4}\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Analogous to (
2.11
) we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(x;\tau,t)\frac{\partial{P_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}(x;\tau,t)\,\omega(x;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=0,

and then using the recurrence relation (
2.2
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}\left(P_{n+1}+\beta_{n}P_{n-1}\right)^{\!2}\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(x^{2}P_{n+1}^{2}+2x^{2}\beta_{n}P_{n+1}P_{n-1}+x^{2}\beta_{n}^{2}P_{n-1}^{2}\right)\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\left(P_{n+2}+\beta_{n+1}P_{n}\right)^{\!2}+2\beta_{n}\left(P_{n+2}+\beta_{n+1}P_{n}\right)\left(P_{n}+\beta_{n-1}P_{n-2}\right)+\beta_{n}^{2}\left(P_{n}+\beta_{n-1}P_{n-2}\right)^{\!2}\right]\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[P_{n+2}^{2}+\beta_{n+1}^{2}P_{n}^{2}+2\beta_{n+1}\beta_{n}P_{n}^{2}+\beta_{n}^{2}P_{n}^{2}+\beta_{n}^{2}\beta_{n-1}^{2}P_{n-2}^{2}\right]\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=h_{n+2}+(\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n})^{2}h_{n}+\beta_{n}^{2}\beta_{n-1}^{2}h_{n-2}.

Consequently

\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}=\left[\beta_{n+2}\beta_{n+1}+(\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n})^{2}+\beta_{n}\beta_{n-1}\right]h_{n},

(2.14)

since

h_{n+2}=\beta_{n+2}h_{n+1}=\beta_{n+2}\beta_{n+1}h_{n},\qquad h_{n-2}=\frac{h_{n-1}}{\beta_{n-1}}=\frac{h_{n}}{\beta_{n}\beta_{n-1}}.

Differentiating (
2.12
) with respect to
\tau
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{h_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}h_{n-1}+\beta_{n}\frac{\partial{h_{n-1}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}=\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}h_{n-1}+\beta_{n}\left[\beta_{n+1}\beta_{n}+(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n-1})^{2}+\beta_{n-1}\beta_{n-2}\right]h_{n-1}.

(2.15)

From (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\beta_{n+2}\beta_{n+1}+(\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n})^{2}+\beta_{n}\beta_{n-1}\right]\beta_{n}-\left[\beta_{n+1}\beta_{n}+(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n-1})^{2}+\beta_{n-1}\beta_{n-2}\right]\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{n+2}+\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n})\beta_{n+1}\beta_{n}-(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n-2})\beta_{n}\beta_{n-1},

and therefore

\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{\tau}}}=\beta_{n}\left[(\beta_{n+2}+\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n})\beta_{n+1}-(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n-2})\beta_{n-1}\right],

as required.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

For the symmetric sextic Freud weight (

), the recurrence coefficients
\beta_{n}:=\beta_{n}(\tau,t)
associated with this weight satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\beta(n)}=1,

where
\beta:=\beta(n)
is the positive curve defined by

60\beta^{3}-12\tau\beta^{2}-2t\beta=n.

(3.6)

Proof.

For the weight

\frac{15a_{n}^{6}}{16}-\frac{3\tau a_{n}^{4}}{4}-\frac{ta_{n}^{2}}{2}=n.

Hence, setting

Lemma 3.5
.

The recurrence coefficients
\beta_{n}
satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{n^{1/3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{60}}.

Proof.

We first prove that the limit of
{\beta_{n}}/{n^{1/3}}
as
n\to\infty
exists. For that we start by showing that the non-negative sequence
(\beta_{n}/n^{1/3})_{n\geq 0}
is bounded from above. By taking into account
\beta_{n}\geq 0
, it follows from (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle 6\beta_{n}^{3}-4\tau\beta_{n}^{2}-2t\beta_{n}

\displaystyle\leq n+\left(-12\beta_{n}+4\tau\right)\beta_{n}(\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n+1}).

Suppose the sequence
(\beta_{n}/n^{1/3})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is unbounded, then there exists a positive integer
m
such that for infinitely many
n\geq m
, one has
\beta_{n}>|\tau|\,n^{1/3}
.
Therefore, the latter inequality becomes

6\beta_{n}^{3}-4\tau\beta_{n}^{2}-2t\beta_{n}\leq n,

and, hence,

6\leq\frac{n}{\beta_{n}^{3}}+\frac{2t}{\beta_{n}^{2}}+\frac{4\tau}{\beta_{n}}\leq\frac{n}{\beta_{n}^{3}}+\frac{2|t|}{\beta_{n}^{2}}+\frac{4|\tau|}{\beta_{n}}\leq\frac{1}{|\tau|^{3}}+\frac{2|t|}{|\tau|^{2}n^{2/3}}+\frac{4}{n^{1/3}},

which is only possible for finitely many
n
. Therefore,
(\beta_{n}/n^{1/3})_{n\geq 0}
is a bounded sequence and, for this reason, there exists

A=\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf\frac{\beta_{n}}{n^{1/3}},\quad\text{and}\quad B=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup\frac{\beta_{n}}{n^{1/3}}.

Suppose
(\beta_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a subsequence such that

A=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{n_{k}}}{\sqrt[3]{n_{k}}}.

We evaluate relation (
3.1
) and we take the limits over this subsequence to get

1\leq 6A\left(5B^{2}+2AB+A^{2}\right).

(3.7)

Similarly, we take a subsequence
(\beta_{m_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

B=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{m_{k}}}{\sqrt[3]{m_{k}}},

and, after taking the limits over this subsequence in (
3.1
), we conclude

6B\left(5A^{2}+2AB+B^{2}\right)\leq 1.

(3.8)

The inequalities (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) then give

(B-A)^{3}\leq 0,

which implies
B\leq A
. However, by definition
A\leq B
and hence
A=B
and we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n}/n^{1/3}
exists and equals
A
. Thus, we conclude

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{n}}{\sqrt[3]{n}}=A.

We multiply (
3.1
) by
1/n
and then take the limit as
n\to\infty
to conclude that

60A^{3}=1
and hence the result.

∎

We have shown that

\beta_{n}(\tau,t)=\sqrt[3]{\frac{n}{60}}\left(1+{o}(1)\right),\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

For the purpose of the present research, we do not need further terms. For the record, we hereby note a formal asymptotic expansion for
\beta_{n}
. Since we do not prove the size of the error term, we say that the asympotic expansion is “formal”.

Lemma 3.6

As

satisfying (

.

Proof.

Suppose that as

\beta_{n}=\frac{n^{1/3}}{\gamma}+a_{0}+\frac{a_{1}}{n^{1/3}}+\frac{a_{2}}{n^{2/3}}+\frac{a_{3}}{n}+\frac{a_{4}}{n^{4/3}}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-5/3}),

(3.10a)

with

, then

\displaystyle\beta_{n\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{1/3}}{\gamma}+a_{0}+\frac{a_{1}}{n^{1/3}}+\frac{3\gamma a_{2}\pm 1}{2\gamma n^{2/3}}+\frac{a_{3}}{n}+\frac{3a_{4}\mp a_{1}}{3n^{4/3}}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-5/3}),

\displaystyle\beta_{n\pm 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{1/3}}{\gamma}+a_{0}+\frac{a_{1}}{n^{1/3}}+\frac{3\gamma a_{2}\pm 2}{2\gamma n^{2/3}}+\frac{a_{3}}{n}+\frac{3a_{4}\mp 2a_{1}}{3n^{4/3}}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-5/3}),

The coefficients

in the relation

(4.1)

satisfied by semi-classical orthogonal polynomials are of interest since differentiating this differential-difference equation yields the second order differential equation satisfied by the orthogonal polynomials. Shohat
[

]
gave a procedure using quasi-orthogonality to derive (
4.1
) for weights
\omega(x)
such that
\displaystyle{\omega^{\prime}(x)}/{\omega(x)}
is a rational function. This technique was rediscovered by several authors including Bonan, Freud, Mhaskar and Nevai approximately 40 years later, see
[
100
, pp. 126–132]
and the references therein for more detail. The method of ladder operators was introduced by Chen and Ismail in
[
28
]
. Related work by various authors can be found in, for example,
[
29
,
30
,
57
,
93
]
and a good summary of the ladder operator technique is provided in
[
72
, Theorem 3.2.1]
; see also
[
112
, Chapter 4]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
72
, Theorem 3.2.1]

Let

\omega(x)=\exp\{-v(x)\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
v(x)
is a twice continuously differentiable function on
\mathbb{R}
. Assume that the polynomials
\big\{P_{n}(x)\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfy the orthogonality relation

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(x)P_{m}(x)\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=h_{n}\delta_{mn}.

Then
P_{n}(x)
satisfy the differential-difference equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}{P_{n}}}{{\mathrm{d}{x}}}=-B_{n}(x)P_{n}(x)+A_{n}(x)P_{n-1}(x),

(4.2)

where

\displaystyle A_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{n-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}^{2}(y)\mathcal{K}(x,y)\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

(4.3a)

\displaystyle B_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{n-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(y)P_{n-1}(y)\mathcal{K}(x,y)\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

(4.3b)

where

\mathcal{K}(x,y)=\frac{v^{\prime}(x)-v^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}.

(4.4)

Proof.

Write Theorem 3.2.1 in
[
72
]
for monic orthogonal polynomials on the real line. The result also follows from
[
37
, Theorem 2]
by letting
\gamma=0
.
∎

Next we derive a differential-difference equation satisfied by generalised Freud polynomials using Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For the symmetric sextic Freud weight (
1.1
)
the monic orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}(x;\tau,t)
satisfy the differential-difference equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}{P_{n}}}{{\mathrm{d}{x}}}(x;\tau,t)=-B_{n}(x;\tau,t)P_{n}(x;\tau,t)+A_{n}(x;\tau,t)P_{n-1}(x;\tau,t),

where

\displaystyle A_{n}(x;\tau,t)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}\big\{6x^{4}-4\tau x^{2}-2t+(6x^{2}-4\tau)(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1})\big\}

\displaystyle\qquad+6\beta_{n}\big\{\beta_{n}(\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1})+\beta_{n+1}(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n+2})\big\},

\displaystyle B_{n}(x;\tau,t)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}\big\{6x^{3}-4\tau x+6x(\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1})\big\},

with
\beta_{n}
the recurrence coefficient in the three-term recurrence relation (
2.2
).

Proof.

For the symmetric sextic Freud weight (
1.1
) we have

\omega(x;\tau,t)=\exp\left(-x^{6}+\tau x^{4}+tx^{2}\right),

i.e.
v(x;t)=x^{6}-\tau x^{4}-tx^{2}
, and so
\mathcal{K}(x,y)
defined by (
4.4
) is

\mathcal{K}(x,y)=6(x^{4}+x^{3}y+x^{2}y^{2}+xy^{3}+y^{4})-4\tau(x^{2}+xy+y^{2})-2t.

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle{P_{n}^{2}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=(6x^{4}-4\tau x^{2}-2t)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{P_{n}^{2}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y+(6x^{3}-4\tau x)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{yP_{n}^{2}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(6x^{2}-4\tau)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{y^{2}P_{n}^{2}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y+6x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{y^{3}P_{n}^{2}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+6\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{y^{4}P_{n}^{2}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=(6x^{4}-4\tau x^{2}-2t)h_{n}+(6x^{2}-4\tau)(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1})h_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+6\big\{\beta_{n}(\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1})+\beta_{n+1}(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n+2})\big\}h_{n},

since
\beta_{n}=h_{n}/h_{n-1}
and iteration of the three-term recurrence relation

xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}(x)+\beta_{n}P_{n-1}(x),

yields

\displaystyle x^{4}P_{n}(x)=P_{n+4}(x)

\displaystyle+\big\{\beta_{n}\left(\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1}\right)+\beta_{n+1}\left(\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n+2}\right)\big\}P_{n}(x)

\displaystyle+\beta_{n-1}\beta_{n}\left(\beta_{n-2}+\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1}\right)P_{n-2}(x)+\beta_{n-3}\beta_{n-2}\beta_{n-1}\beta_{n}P_{n-4}(x).

Also

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle{P_{n}(y;\tau,t)P_{n-1}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=(6x^{4}-4\tau x^{2}-2t)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{P_{n}(y;\tau,t)P_{n-1}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad+(6x^{3}-4\tau x)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{yP_{n}(y;\tau,t)P_{n-1}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad+(6x^{2}-4\tau)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{y^{2}P_{n}(y;\tau,t)P_{n-1}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad+6x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{y^{3}P_{n}(y;\tau,t)P_{n-1}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad+6\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{y^{4}P_{n}(y;\tau,t)P_{n}(y;\tau,t)}\,\omega(y;\tau,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=(6x^{3}-4\tau x)\beta_{n}h_{n-1}+6x(\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n}+\beta_{n+1})\beta_{n}h_{n-1},

and the result follows.∎

5
Moments of the symmetric sextic Freud weight

The moments of the symmetric sextic Freud weight (
1.1

\beta_{n}

2.4

\beta_{0}=0,\qquad\beta_{1}=\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{0}},\qquad\beta_{2}=\frac{\mu_{0}\mu_{4}-\mu_{2}^{2}}{\mu_{0}\mu_{2}}.

As such, a description of the moments is crucial. This section discusses properties of the moments in terms of the pair of parameters
(\tau,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\mu_{n}:=\mu_{n}(\tau,t)

5.1

\mu_{0}
as a solution of a third order differential equation in

subject to the initial conditions
\mu_{0}(0,t)

\frac{\partial\mu_{0}}{\partial t}(0,t)
and

\frac{\partial^{2}\mu_{0}}{\partial t^{2}}(0,t)
given in Lemma
5.2
together with Lemma
5.4

\mu_{n}:=\mu_{n}(\tau,t)
via the linear third order differential equation in
t
, given in (
5.3

5.4
). As a consequence, we describe the moments via a linear third order recurrence relation (
5.5
).

Lemma 5.1
.

For the weight (
1.1
),
the first moment is

\mu_{0}(\tau,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-x^{6}+\tau x^{4}+tx^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{-1/2}\exp\left(-s^{3}+\tau s^{2}+ts\right)\mathrm{d}s,

which satisfies the equation

\frac{\partial^{3}{\varphi}}{{\partial{t}}^{3}}-\tfrac{2}{3}\tau\frac{\partial^{2}{\varphi}}{{\partial{t}}^{2}}-\tfrac{1}{3}t\frac{\partial{\varphi}}{{\partial{t}}}-\tfrac{1}{6}\varphi=0.

Proof.

To show this result, interchanging integration and differentiation gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{3}{\mu_{0}}}{{\partial{t}}^{3}}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{2}{3}\tau\frac{\partial^{2}{\mu_{0}}}{{\partial{t}}^{2}}-\tfrac{1}{3}t\frac{\partial{\mu_{0}}}{{\partial{t}}}-\tfrac{1}{6}\mu_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[s^{3}-\tfrac{2}{3}\tau s^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}ts-\tfrac{1}{6}\right]s^{-1/2}\exp\left(-s^{3}+\tau s^{2}+ts\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{{\mathrm{d}{s}}}\left[s^{1/2}\exp\left(-s^{3}+\tau s^{2}+ts\right)\right]\!\right\}\mathrm{d}s=-\tfrac{1}{3}\Big[s^{1/2}\exp\left(-s^{3}+\tau s^{2}+ts\right)\Big]_{0}^{\infty}=0,

as required.∎

\tau=0

with

a parameter, the first moment

{}_{1}F_{2}\left({a_{1};b_{1},b_{2}};z\right)

For the quadratic-sextic Freud weight

Proof.

Theorem 6.12
.

For
\tau,\,\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)}{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{j}}\,\tau^{j}\,L_{j}^{(-1/2)}(\zeta)-\kappa\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{j}}\,\tau^{j+2}\,L_{j}^{(1/2)}(\zeta)\right\},

where

are the Laguerre polynomials of parameter
\alpha
and
\zeta=-\frac{1}{4}\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}
.

Proof.

Consider the Taylor series expansion about
s=0
of the function

\exp\left(-\kappa\tau^{2}s+\tau s^{2}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)\frac{s^{m}}{m!}

where
C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)
are polynomials in
\tau
and
\kappa
given by

\displaystyle C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\lceil m/2\rceil}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{m}m!\kappa^{2j-m}\tau^{3j-m}}{(2j-m)!\,(m-j)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m}m!\kappa^{2\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}m\right\rceil-m}\tau^{3\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}m\right\rceil-m}}{\left(m-\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}m\right\rceil\right)!\left(2\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}m\right\rceil-m\right)!}\,{}_{2}F_{2}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1,\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}m\right\rceil-m}{-\tfrac{1}{2}m+\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}m\right\rceil+\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}m+\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}m\right\rceil+1};-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}j!\,2^{2j}\,\tau^{j}\,L_{j}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right),&\quad\text{if}\quad m=2j,\\
-j!\,2^{2j}\,\kappa\,\tau^{j+2}\,L_{j}^{(1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right),&\quad\text{if}\quad m=2j+1,\end{cases}

where

are the Laguerre polynomials of parameter
\alpha>-1
.

In order to study the radius of convergence of the series

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)}{m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m+n-1/2}\exp(-s^{3})\,\mathrm{d}s=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}m+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\frac{C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)}{m!},

(6.30)

we analyse the ratio

\rho_{m}=\left|\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}m+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)C_{m+1}(\tau;\kappa)}{(m+1)\,\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}m+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)}\right|

as
m\to\infty
. Observe that the two subsequences of
(\rho_{m})_{m\geq 0}
of even and odd order are respectively given by

\displaystyle\rho_{2j}

\displaystyle=\left|\kappa\,\tau^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{(2j+1)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)}\frac{L_{j}^{(1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}{L_{j}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\rho_{2j+1}=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2}{\kappa\,\tau}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{5}{6}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{L_{j+1}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}{L_{j}^{(1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}\right|.

Recall the asymptotic behaviour for the Gamma function, see e.g.
[
101

\kappa\,\tau^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{(2j+1)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)}\sim\frac{\kappa\,\tau^{2}}{\sqrt[3]{12}\,j^{2/3}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{2}{\kappa\,\tau}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{5}{6}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}\sim\frac{2}{\kappa\,\tau}\left(\frac{2j}{3}\right)^{\!1/3}

\frac{L_{j}^{(1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}{L_{j}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}\sim\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}|\tau|^{3}}{4j}\right)^{\!-{1/2}}=\frac{2\sqrt{j}}{|\kappa|\,|\tau|^{3/2}},\qquad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty,

and

\frac{L_{j+1}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}{L_{j}^{(1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}\sim\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}|\tau|^{3}}{4j}\right)^{\!1/2}=\frac{|\kappa|\,|\tau|^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{j}},\qquad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty.

Hence, for fixed
\tau\neq 0
and
\kappa\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{2j}

\displaystyle=\left|\kappa\,\tau^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{(2j+1)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)}\frac{L_{j}^{(1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}{L_{j}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\sim\left|\frac{\kappa\,\tau^{2}}{\sqrt[3]{12}\,j^{2/3}}\,\frac{2\sqrt{j}}{\kappa\,|\tau|^{3/2}}\right|=\left|\frac{\sqrt[3]{\tfrac{2}{3}}\,\sqrt{|\tau|}}{\,j^{1/6}}\right|\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty,

and

\displaystyle\rho_{2j+1}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2}{\kappa\,\tau}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{5}{6}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{L_{j+1}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}{L_{j}^{(1/2)}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\sim\left|\frac{2}{\kappa\,\tau}\left(\frac{2j}{3}\right)^{\!1/3}\frac{\kappa\,|\tau|^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{j}}\right|=\left|\frac{\sqrt[3]{\tfrac{2}{3}}\,\sqrt{|\tau|}}{\,j^{1/6}}\right|\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty.

By the ratio test, the series (
6.30
)
converges absolutely for any
\tau,\,\kappa\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. Therefore, by Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, it follows

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)}{m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m+n-1/2}\exp(-s^{3})\,\mathrm{d}s=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}m+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\frac{C_{m}(\tau;\kappa)}{m!}.

When
\kappa=0
and
\tau\neq 0
, one has

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;0)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{2}{3}m+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\frac{\tau^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{r=0}^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{2}{3}r+\tfrac{1}{6}+2j\right)\frac{\tau^{3j+r}}{(3j+r)!}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{r=0}^{2}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{2}{3}r+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\tfrac{1}{6}n+\tfrac{1}{3}r+\tfrac{1}{12}\right)_{j}\left(\tfrac{1}{6}n+\tfrac{1}{3}r+\tfrac{7}{12}\right)_{j}\,2^{2j}\frac{\tau^{3j+r}}{(3j+r)!},

which, after using the Legendre duplication formula for the Gamma function, can be written as in Lemma
6.10
. Hence, the result holds for any
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.

Finally, (
6.29
) also holds when
\tau=0
, since it gives (
6.4a
).
∎

As a straightforward consequence of the latter result, one has

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)+\mu_{2n}(\tau;-\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2}{3}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)}{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{j}}\,\tau^{j}\,L_{j}^{(-1/2)}(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}})

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;-\kappa)-\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2}{3}\kappa\tau^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}j+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{j}}\tau^{j}\,L_{j}^{(1/2)}(-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}).

Note that the series expansion obtained above, written in terms of Laguerre polynomials, could of course be written using Hermite polynomials.

The expressions given in the latter result have a clear
3
-fold decomposition in
\tau
, and in fact (
6.29
) reads as:

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=F_{n}(\tau;\kappa)+\tau G_{n}(\tau;\kappa)+\tau^{2}H_{n}(\tau;\kappa),

where

\displaystyle F_{n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(2\ell+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\tau^{3\ell}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{3\ell+1}}\Bigg\{\tfrac{3}{2}(2\ell+1)(6\ell+1)L_{3\ell}^{(-1/2)}(\zeta)-\left(2\ell+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\kappa\tau^{3}\,L_{3\ell+1}^{(1/2)}(\zeta)\Bigg\}

\displaystyle G_{n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(2\ell+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{5}{6}\right)\tau^{3\ell}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{3\ell+2}}\Bigg\{\tfrac{3}{2}(2\ell+1)(6\ell+5)L_{3\ell+1}^{(-1/2)}(\zeta)-{\left(2\ell+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{5}{6}\right)}\kappa\tau^{3}L_{3\ell+2}^{(1/2)}(\zeta)\Bigg\}

\displaystyle H_{n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(2\ell+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\tau^{3\ell}}{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{3\ell+2}}\Bigg\{\left(2\ell+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)L_{3\ell+2}^{(-1/2)}(\zeta)-\tfrac{3}{4}(2\ell+1)\kappa\,L_{3\ell}^{(1/2)}(\zeta)\Bigg\}

with
\zeta=-\tfrac{1}{4}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{3}}
.

Besides, the latter result states that

\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=\lceil m/2\rceil}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{m}\kappa^{2j-m}\tau^{3j-m}}{(2j-m)!\,(m-j)!}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}m+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right).

A swap of the order of summation gives

\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=j}^{2j}\frac{(-1)^{m}\kappa^{2j-m}\tau^{3j-m}}{(2j-m)!\,(m-j)!}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{3}m+\tfrac{1}{3}n+\tfrac{1}{6}\right).

The change of variables
(j,m)\mapsto(\ell,3\ell-m)
followed by a change in the order of summation corresponds to

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=\left\lceil m/2\right\rceil}^{m}\frac{(-\kappa)^{m-\ell}}{(m-\ell)!\,(2\ell-m)!}\Gamma\left(\ell-\tfrac{1}{3}{m}+\tfrac{1}{3}{n}+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\tau^{m}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-\kappa)^{\ell}}{\ell!\,(m-2\ell)!}\Gamma\left(-\ell+\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{1}{3}{n}+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\tau^{m}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{2\ell}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}m\right)_{\!\ell}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}m+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{\!\ell}(-\kappa)^{\ell}}{\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}{n}-\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{5}{6}\right)_{\!\ell}\ell!}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{1}{3}{n}+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)}{m!}\,\tau^{m},

where we used in the last identity

\frac{1}{(m-2\ell)!}=\frac{(-m)_{2\ell}}{m!}=\frac{2^{2\ell}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}m\right)_{\!\ell}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}m+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{\!\ell}}{m!}

and

\Gamma\left(-\ell+\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{1}{3}{n}+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)=\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{1}{3}{n}+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}{n}-\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{5}{6}\right)_{\!\ell}}.

Hence, we obtain the series expansion

\displaystyle\mu_{2n}(\tau;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-\tfrac{1}{2}m,-\tfrac{1}{2}m+\tfrac{1}{2}}{-\tfrac{1}{3}{n}-\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{5}{6}};4\kappa\right)\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{2}{3}{m}+\tfrac{1}{3}{n}+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)}{m!}\tau^{m}.

Using the Gauss formula for the hypergeometric function, see
[
100
, §15.4]
, the latter expression evaluated at
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{4}
gives (
6.12
). Similarly, series expansions about
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{3}
can be obtained, from which the expression in Lemma
6.9
appears as a particular case.

9
Volterra lattice hierarchy

The
Volterra lattice hierarchy
is given by

\frac{\partial{\beta_{n}}}{{\partial{t_{2k}}}}=\beta_{n}\Big(V_{n+1}^{(2k)}-V_{n-1}^{(2k)}\Big),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,

(9.1)

where
V_{n}^{(2k)}
is a combination of various
\beta_{n}
evaluated at different points on the lattice, see, for example,
[
4
,
5
]
and the references therein.

The first three flows
V_{n}^{(2k)}
are given by

\displaystyle V_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{n},

(9.2a)

\displaystyle V_{n}^{(4)}

\displaystyle=V_{n}^{(2)}\left(V_{n+1}^{(2)}+V_{n}^{(2)}+V_{n-1}^{(2)}\right)=\beta_{n}\left(\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n}+\beta_{n-1}\right),

(9.2b)

\displaystyle V_{n}^{(6)}

\displaystyle=V_{n}^{(2)}\left(V_{n+1}^{(4)}+V_{n}^{(4)}+V_{n-1}^{(4)}+V_{n+1}^{(2)}V_{n-1}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}\left(\beta_{n+2}\beta_{n+1}+\beta_{n+1}^{2}+2\beta_{n+1}\beta_{n}+\beta_{n}^{2}+2\beta_{n}\beta_{n-1}+\beta_{n-1}^{2}+\beta_{n-1}\beta_{n-2}+\beta_{n+1}\beta_{n-1}\right).

(9.2c)

Higher order flows
V_{n}^{(2k)}
, with
k\geq 4
can be obtained recursively based on the orthogonality of a polynomial sequence with respect to higher order Freud weights, as described in
[
37
, §4(a)]
. For the sake of illustration the next two flows are:

\displaystyle V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(8)}

\displaystyle=V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(2)}\left(V_{n+1}^{(6)}+V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(6)}+V_{n-1}^{(6)}\right)+V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(4)}V_{n+1}^{(2)}V_{n-1}^{(2)}+V_{n+1}^{(2)}V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(2)}V_{n-1}^{(2)}\left(V_{n+2}^{(2)}+V_{n-2}^{(2)}\right),

\displaystyle V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(10)}

\displaystyle=V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(2)}\left(V^{(8)}_{n+1}+V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(8)}+V^{(8)}_{n-1}\right)+V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(6)}V^{(2)}_{n+1}V^{(2)}_{n-1}+V^{(2)}_{n+1}V_{n\phantom{1}}^{(2)}V^{(2)}_{n-1}\left(V^{(4)}_{n+2}+V^{(4)}_{n-2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+V^{(2)}_{n+1}V^{(2)}_{n\phantom{1}}V^{(2)}_{n-1}\left\{\left(V^{(2)}_{n\phantom{1}}+V^{(2)}_{n-1}\right)V^{(2)}_{n+2}+\left(V^{(2)}_{n+1}+V^{(2)}_{n\phantom{1}}\right)V^{(2)}_{n-2}+V^{(2)}_{n+2}V^{(2)}_{n-2}\right\}.

In fact, the Volterra lattice has an infinite hierarchy of commuting symmetries, as explained in
[
24
, §2]
.

10
Discussion

In this paper we have discussed the behaviour of the recurrence coefficient
\beta_{n}
in the three-term recurrence relation for the symmetric sextic Freud weight

\omega(x;\tau,\kappa)=\exp\left\{-\left(x^{6}-\tau x^{4}+\kappa\tau^{2}x^{2}\right)\right\},

(10.1)

supported on the real line, where
\tau
and
\kappa
are real parameters. In three cases, when
\tau>0
and either
\kappa=0
,
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{4}
or
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{3}
, we have obtained explicit expressions for the associated moments in terms generalised hypergeometric functions.

The numerical computations show that there are three particular regions in the
(\tau;\kappa)
-plane of interest:

(i)

if
\tau>0
and
\tfrac{1}{4}<\kappa<\tfrac{2}{5}
then the recurrence coefficient
\beta_{n}
approximately follows the cubic curve

60\beta^{3}-12\tau\beta^{2}+2\kappa\tau^{2}\beta-n=0,

(10.2)

both initially and for
n
large;

(ii)

if
\tau>0
and
-\tfrac{2}{3}<\kappa<\tfrac{1}{4}
then the recurrence coefficients
\beta_{2n}
and
\beta_{2n+1}
initially approximately follow the curves

\displaystyle 6u\!\left(u^{2}+6uv+3v^{2}\right)-4\tau u\!\left(u+2v\right)+2\kappa\tau^{2}u=n,

\displaystyle 6v\!\left(3u^{2}+6uv+v^{2}\right)-4\tau v\!\left(2u+v\right)+2\kappa\tau^{2}v=n,

with
u=\beta_{2n}
and
v=\beta_{2n+1}
, and then follow the cubic (
10.2
) for
n
large;

(iii)

if
\tau>0
and
\kappa\approx\tfrac{1}{4}
then the recurrence coefficients
\beta_{3n}
and
\beta_{3n\pm 1}
approximately follow three curves initially and then follow the cubic curve (
10.2
) for
n
large. When
\tau>0
and
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{4}
then the recurrence coefficients
\beta_{3n\pm 1}
approximately follow the same curve.

In cases (i) and (ii) there is a “transition region”, which Jurkiewicz
[
75
]
and Sénéchal
[
107
]
described as “chaotic”, though as discussed above, was subsequently shown not to be the case cf.
[
17
,
20
,
54
,
55
]
. Our results support this point of view. The structure and size of the “transition region”, such as the value of
n
when transition commences (an issue raised by Sénéchal
[
107
]
), appears to depend on both
\tau
and
\kappa
and it remains an open question to analytically describe this.

In case (iii), in the neighbourhood of
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{4}
, which is between the other cases, there is a “three-fold” structure. The nature of this mutation between the scenarios (i) and (ii) is currently under investigation, and we do not pursue this further here.

Elsewhere the recurrence coefficient
\beta_{n}
either follows a curve, which is monotonically increasing, or
\beta_{2n}
and
\beta_{2n+1}
initially follow two curves which meet and then they follow the same curve, for example as shown in Figures
7.17
and
7.18
.

In a recent paper, Clarkson
et al.

[
33
]
studied the tertiary
dP
I
equation
[
62
,
111
]

v_{n}(v_{n+1}+v_{n-1}+1)=(n+1)\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon>0,

(10.3)

with
v_{-1}=0
, which arises in quantum minimal surfaces
[
2
,
70
]
and found solutions in terms of the modified Bessel functions
I_{\pm 1/6}(z)
and
I_{\pm 5/6}(z)
, i.e. the same modified Bessel functions that arise in the description of the moments of (
6.1
) when
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{4}
.
The tertiary
dP
I
(
10.3
) also arises in connection with orthogonal polynomials in the complex plane with respect to the weight

w(z;t)=\exp\left\{-t|z|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}\mathrm{i}(z^{3}+\overline{z}^{3})\right\},

with
t>0
and
z=x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{C}

[
56
]
, with the
same
unique solution as in
[
33
]
.
Further Teodorescu
et al.

[
110
]
show that
(
10.3
) arises in the theory of random normal matrices
[
114
]
, which motivated the study in
[
56
]
.
It is an interesting open question as to whether there is any relationship between these problems, though again we do not pursue this further here.

As remarked in §
1
, the symmetric sextic Freud weight (
10.1
) is equivalent to the weight

w(x)=\exp\left\{-N\!\left(g_{6}x^{6}+g_{4}x^{4}+g_{2}x^{2}\right)\right\},

(10.4)

with parameters
N
,
g_{2}
,
g_{4}
and
g_{6}>0
, which has been studied numerically by several authors, e.g.
[
5
,
18
,
45
,
75
,
81
,
82
,
83
,
105
,
107
]
. For the weight (
10.4
) the critical quantity is
g_{2}g_{6}/g_{4}^{2}
which plays the role of
\kappa
in (
10.1
). If
\tfrac{1}{4}<g_{2}g_{6}/g_{4}^{2}<\tfrac{2}{5}
, with
g_{4}<0
, then it is equivalent to the case discussed in §
7.1
and if
0<g_{2}g_{6}/g_{4}^{2}<\tfrac{1}{4}
, with
g_{4}<0
, then it is equivalent to the case discussed in §
7.2
. The effect of increasing

is equivalent to increasing
\tau
, as discussed in §
7.9
and illustrated in Figures
7.19
,
7.20
and
7.21
.
