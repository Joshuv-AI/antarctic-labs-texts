Risk and pattern analysis of distant metastases in colon cancer: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yonghua Hu, Qinglin Wei, Yang Yu, Haiyuan Li, Hao Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Understanding the metastatic risks and patterns of colon cancer is crucial for patient evaluation and decision-making. However, high-quality evidence on colon cancer metastases is lacking. This study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the risk of site-specific metastasis and provide a complete profiling of distant metastasis in colon cancer.Methods:This review will include studies reporting the proportion of different-site metastases among colon cancer patients. Clinical trials, follow-up studies, and cross-sectional surveys with English publications are eligible for inclusion. We will search Medline, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from inception to June 2023. Two reviewers will independently screen the title/abstract and full texts of identified records according to the eligibility criteria. Data from eligible studies will be extracted and used for meta-analysis. Meta-analyses will be conducted to estimate the overall proportion of site-specific metastasis. During meta-analyses, two proportions will be analyzed, i.e., the incidence of metastasis and component proportion. We will select the appropriate tools to assess the risk of biases based on the study design of included studies.Results:This study is still in preparation for launch. Once all analyses are complete, the results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.Conclusion:This review will provide a clear and systematic metastatic pattern of colon cancer and evidence-based recommendations for the management of distant metastasis.

Introduction

Colon cancer is one of the most common gastrointestinal cancers worldwide and is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality[1]. Patients with colon cancer may experience various symptoms, many of which are imperceptible in the early stages. Consequently, a significant proportion of patients present with advanced disease at the time of diagnosis. Although significant advances have been made in cancer treatment, there remain challenges in the management of advanced colon cancer.

Distant metastasis is a critical feature of advanced colon cancer. During the invasion of colon cancer, metastatic lesions can target several organs, including the liver, lungs, bones, and brain. Despite advancements in diagnostic and treatment modalities, the metastatic behavior of colon cancer remains a critical challenge. Differences in the site distribution of metastasis have been observed between different primary locations of colon cancer and between different histological types[2-5]. It is crucial to understand the metastatic patterns of colon cancer as this can guide follow-up plans, and treatment decisions, and help predict prognosis. Previous studies have determined an association between metastatic sites and patient survival outcomes, highlighting the prognostic value of metastatic sites[6]. Identifying where colon cancer is likely to metastasize and how many patients are likely to suffer from such metastases can aid in the development of more precise strategies for the surveillance and treatment of patients with colon cancer. However, there is a lack of studies that exhibit site-specific metastatic risks and complete metastatic patterns in colon cancer, and most of the data comes from single-cell cohort studies or retrospective database analyses. This impedes our further understanding the translational roles of metastases in patients with colon cancer.

This systematic review aims to synthesize available data to better understand site-specific metastatic risks and patterns, directly addressing gaps in current research regarding the predictive factors and outcomes associated with different metastatic sites.

Research question

What is the incidence of site-specific metastases and the pattern of distant metastases distribution in patients with colon cancer?

Materials and methods

This systematic review has been designed and will be conducted following the AMSTAR 2 instrument[7]. According to the AMSTAR 2 instrument, our review has received a moderate rating for overall confidence in the expected results. The final review will be reported in accordance with the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline[8]. The protocol is a methodological outline for our proposed systematic review and will be reported in line with the PRISMA for Systematic Review Protocols (PRISMA-P)[9]. The protocol has been registered with the PROSPERO network (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/, registration number: CRD42023447882).

Eligibility criteria

Patients

Patients diagnosed with colon cancer, regardless of cancer stage or grade, will be eligible for inclusion in this review. The evaluation of tumors through imaging or colonoscopy is acceptable. However, the diagnosis must be confirmed through histopathological examination. The histological subtypes should be adenocarcinoma, including generic, signet-ring and mucinous adenocarcinomas. The primary location of cancer is limited to the anatomical colon. Studies reporting on colorectal cancer will be included only if they provide separate data for colon cancer. Patients with multiple primary cancers will be excluded. If information is incomplete or missing, we will contact the author to confirm and make necessary adjustments for inclusion or exclusion of the study.

Intervention and control

There are no restrictions on the interventions in this review. The aim of this review is to evaluate the proportions of metastatic colon cancer in different organs, so patients receiving any type of intervention will be included. Controls will not be required for this review due to its non-interventional nature. Studies with different intervention arms will be excluded if they do not have either a sum value or original data available for each arm.

Studies

We will include reports on the proportions of different target metastases from clinical trials, follow-up studies, or cross-sectional surveys. No restrictions will be placed on patient size, but case reports or small case series will be excluded due to their limited statistical generalizability and higher risk of bias. Studies with unclear classifications will be evaluated independently by at least two reviewers, and any discrepancies will be discussed to reach a consensus. Studies should be peer-reviewed and published in English. If multiple publications report on data from the same population, we will retain the most complete and/or up-to-date publication.

Outcomes

We will include studies reporting on the metastatic patterns of colon cancer, defined as the proportions of patients with metastasis to different sites among those with primary colon cancer. Studies should report the number of patients who develop distant metastases at different sites out of all patients with colon cancer or patients with different metastases out of those with stage IV colon cancer, which can be used to compute the incidence of site-specific metastases and component proportion, respectively. No restrictions will be placed on the approach used to detect distant metastases. As multiple target organs will be considered for metastatic proportions, any organ-specific assessing tool will be eligible as long as it is reliable for detecting metastases. The outcome event should be followed up from the diagnosis of colon cancer, with a minimum follow-up duration of 6 months. The minimum follow-up duration of 6 months is required to ensure that sufficient time has elapsed to observe metastatic progression, thereby enhancing the reliability of the data regarding metastatic sites. Studies will be excluded if their outcomes are only selective reporting for a single site of metastasis rather than a systematic metastatic pattern. In addition, studies with incomplete, inconsistent, or inaccurate data, as determined by our team, will also be excluded.

Information Sources

The databases of Medline, embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane library will be searched from their inception to June 2023. The search language will be limited to English.

Search strategy

The searches will be performed using both Mesh terms and free-text terms. The following keywords will be combined to generate the search strategy and will be adapted accordingly for each database: colon cancer, colon carcinoma, colon adenocarcinoma, colon neoplasm, metastases, metastasis, metastatic, pattern, incidence, proportion, prevalence, rate, and frequency. All searches will be conducted using the online search tool, adhering to the operational guidelines provided by the respective databases. Study selection and data extraction

For the records retrieved from databases, the removal of duplicates will be completed automatically in EndNote software, followed by a manual check by the researchers. In the initial selection, two researchers will read the title and abstract, and the full texts of potentially eligible studies will be obtained online or by contacting the authors. Then, the same reviewers will carefully check the full texts of selected studies according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any studies that meet the eligible criteria will be retained and included for further analyses. Data will be extracted from included studies based on a pre-designed data form. The proportion of site-specific metastasis will be obtained directly from the original articles or recalculated according to the determination of researchers. There are two anticipated outcomes: the incidence of site-specific metastases and the component proportion of metastatic sites. These measures allow for a comprehensive assessment of the risk profile and specific patterns of metastasis in colon cancer. Two independent reviewers will be employed during the data extraction. Disagreements between the two reviewers during the above processes will be solved by face-to-face meetings, and if the controversy persists, a third researcher will be consulted to arbitrate. Data items

We will collect three types of information from each included study, i.e., the bibliometric data of included literature, general study information, and outcomes of interests. Specifically, the following items will be obtained: the first author, publication year, country, study type, sample size, characteristics of patients, primary site of colon cancer, follow-up duration, approach used for metastasis detection, proportion, and location of metastasis.

Assessment of risk of bias

Two reviewers will independently assess the quality of the included studies using the corresponding tools. As different study types will be included, three different quality assessment tools will be applied, including the tool developed by Hoy et al. for prevalence studies[10], the Newcastle–Ottawa scale for nonrandomized studies[11], and the Cochrane’s bias assessment tool for randomized studies[12]. All assessments will be performed according to the guidelines of the specific tools. A consensus will be reached between the two reviewers, and any discrepancies will be resolved by consulting a third reviewer.

Data synthesis

All data analyses will be performed using R software (Version 4.0.2). The overall pooled estimate for the proportion will be calculated and displayed with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The numerators and denominators for each reported proportion will be first extracted or recalculated from the individual studies. Next, data cleaning will be performed using Excel, involving the compilation of total observations and recording metastasis events according to study ID and associated characteristics. With these data, the study-specific proportions will be pooled using a customized R code based on a fixed- or random-effect meta-model[13]. Within this process, a forest plot depicting the pooled data will be generated. The Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation will be used during meta-analysis to stabilize the variance of study-specific proportions. We will perform separate meta-analyses on the incidence and component proportion of metastases due to the differences in their nature of outcomes. Heterogeneity will be assessed qualitatively using Cochrane’s Q test and quantitatively using I2 statistics. This will also be accomplished by customizing the R code. I2 > 50% will be defined as substantial heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses or meta-regression will be performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, such as cancer stage, treatment modalities, or patient characteristics. These analyses will help to identify whether specific factors influence the metastatic patterns observed and will contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the data. If substantial heterogeneity persists even after adjusting models based on the sources of heterogeneity, a narrative statistic will be used. The proportions of metastasis in different sites (such as liver, lung, bone, brain, and peritoneum metastases) will be compared and ranked. The level of significance (α) will be set at 0.05, and a P-value below 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. In our meta-analysis, we will use comprehensive statistical methods to ensure robust synthesis of the data. Specifically, missing data will be addressed through multiple imputation methods to minimize bias, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of our findings. We will employ both fixed and random effects models depending on the detected heterogeneity, ensuring the most appropriate statistical treatment of the data. All meta pooling will be conducted using the R package “meta”[14].

Meta-biases

If ten or more studies are included, we will use Egger’s test to detect publication biases and draw a funnel plot using R codes. The presence of plot asymmetry and a P < 0.05 in the test results will be regarded as indicative of potential publication biases.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The GRADE tool will be used to assess the level of evidence generated from this review[15]. This approach will involve rating the quality of evidence based on several criteria including study limitations, consistency of effect, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias. For each outcome, evidence will be classified into four categories: very low, low, moderate, or high. These ratings will help clinicians and researchers interpret the strength of the findings and the extent to which they can be relied upon for clinical decision-making.

Discussion

Distant metastasis is one of the hallmarks in patients with advanced colon cancer. Our systematic review will evaluate the risks of site-specific metastases and provide a metastatic profile of colon cancer by analyzing the proportions of metastasis in different sites. In clinical practice, some metastatic sites are commonly observed in colon cancer, such as liver metastasis, but distinct patterns of metastasis are also apparent. Currently, evidence from systematic reviews is lacking regarding which sites colon cancer prefers to invade and what the possibility of these metastases is. Our proposed review is promising in addressing these questions and guiding decision-making and follow-up planning. The expected findings of this review could significantly influence current treatment strategies and follow-up planning for colon cancer patients. By identifying prevalent and atypical metastatic pathways, we can guide personalized therapy and surveillance strategies, thus improving patient outcomes. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to further elucidate the impact of metastatic patterns on survival and quality of life.

However, there may be some limitations to our planned study. First, heterogeneity is inevitable due to the inclusion of data derived from different original studies. Heterogeneity can arise from various sources, including clinical, methodological, and statistical differences. Any form of variability within these aspects across studies may complicate the aggregation of results and obscure the true findings. In cases where significant unidentified heterogeneity is apparent, we will refrain from conducting a meta-analysis to prevent potential biases. Instead, we will aim to present a comprehensive evidence summary using descriptive statistics. Second, we plan to include only English publications. Excluding studies published in languages other than English may result in a potential oversight of valuable evidence. Moreover, smaller sample sizes and negative studies are often published in native languages, whereas larger studies with positive results tend to be featured in English-language journals. This discrepancy could introduce a potential publication bias. Recognizing this limitation, it is important to exercise caution when interpreting the findings of this review. Third, there is a possibility of personal biases influencing the selection of studies, which could potentially lead to either overestimation or underestimation of the results. However, we will take measures to mitigate these biases by explicitly stating our inclusion/exclusion criteria and involving a third arbitrator in the selection process.
