Towards Equivalence of Care: Integrating Custodial Settings Into Alcohol and Other Drug Strategies in Australia
Authors: Thileepan Naren, Jocelyn Chan, Sarah Pellicano, Michael Curtis, Shelley Walker, Jocelyn Jones, Jarrah Duckett, Alexander Campbell, Stuart A Kinner
Published: 2026

Introduction

People entering custodial settings (including police custody, youth detention and adult prisons) experience disproportionately high levels of alcohol and other drug (AOD)‐related harms compared with people in the community [1, 2, 3]. Yet Australian AOD policies and strategies have largely overlooked these settings as sites for AOD prevention, treatment and harm reduction. This omission is difficult to reconcile with Australia's commitment to providing people in custody with healthcare equivalent to that available in the community [4, 5]. To explore this gap, we convened a pre‐conference multidisciplinary workshop at the International Society for the Study of Drug Policy Conference in June 2026. Before the workshop, we conducted a structured review of Australian national and jurisdictional AOD strategies and related policy documents. These documents were uploaded into a large language model (Claude Sonnet 4.6 [6]), which was used to apply a series of predefined analytic prompts to assess the extent to which police custody, youth detention and adult prisons were considered in these strategies (see Appendix A). These findings informed and were incorporated into a series of presentations and facilitated workshop discussions, during which participants identified key policy gaps and developed priority recommendations for policy reform. Drawing on the findings of the policy review and discussions from the multidisciplinary workshop, this commentary identifies key policy gaps. It outlines recommendations for better integrating custodial settings into Australian AOD strategies, advancing progress towards equivalence of care in Australian custodial settings.

Substance Use and Injecting Drug Use in Police Custody, Prisons and Youth Detention in Australia

In Australia, almost 50,000 adults and 880 children are incarcerated in prisons and youth detention on any given day [7, 8]. However, the increasing use of remand and short sentences means that the number of people moving between custodial settings and the community each year far exceeds the daily average. In the 2024–25 financial year, 72,421 adults and 4477 young people were released from prisons and youth detention [7, 8, 9]. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are markedly overrepresented in all Australian custodial settings, accounting for 37% of adults and 56% of young people in custody on an average day, despite representing only 3.8% of the total Australian population [8, 10]. Nationally aggregated data on the number of people passing through police custody in Australia each year are not available; however, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people are markedly over‐represented in these settings [11]. This over‐representation reflects the ongoing impacts of colonisation, dispossession, intergenerational trauma, racism and systemic inequities [12].

Substance use is common among people who experience incarceration. Among 773 police detainees who provided a urine sample for the 2021 Drug Use Monitoring in Australia study, 77% (n = 553) tested positive for at least one illicit drug: 50% (n = 356) for methamphetamine, 22% (n = 130) for opioids, and 11% (n = 82) for buprenorphine. Two in five (41%, n = 296) tested positive for multiple illicit drugs [13]. In Australia, an estimated 73% of incarcerated adults used illicit drugs in the year before entering custody [14], and estimates among children and adolescents who experience incarceration are high but highly variable (ranging from approximately 22%–96%) [3].

During periods of incarceration, the frequency of injecting drug use decreases [15], but rates of blood‐borne virus transmission increase, particularly for hepatitis C infection [16]. Relapse to substance use after release from incarceration is common [17, 18], with drug use after release associated with increased risk of hospitalisation [19], premature mortality and return to custody [20].

Incarceration can compound drug‐related and social harms for people who use drugs [1]. However, when properly resourced and governed, custodial settings can be a valuable site for healthcare and AOD interventions. Police custody in particular may represent the earliest opportunity to identify unmet mental and physical health and AOD treatment needs, with decisions made during this period potentially shaping subsequent treatment trajectories in and beyond custody [21, 22]. People in police custody are perhaps uniquely vulnerable to harms as initial screening and triage of healthcare and AOD needs are performed by police officers [22], which can be problematic in a patient population with significant vulnerability. Similarly, the location of some rural and remote police stations can result in people in custody within these locations being disadvantaged in accessing prompt healthcare or an escalation in care if their situation deteriorates [22]. Under the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners (the ‘Nelson Mandela Rules’), to which Australia is a signatory, adults in prisons are entitled to healthcare that is equivalent to that available to the general population [5]. The international standards for juvenile detainees only entitle them to ‘adequate’ instead of equivalent care [23]. Yet people in custody often receive care that fails to meet community‐equivalent standards [24]. Given the high prevalence of AOD use among incarcerated people compared with the general community, and the central role that harm reduction plays within broader Australian drug policy, the lack of harm reduction interventions, such as needle syringe programs [25], and inconsistent access to opioid agonist treatment in prisons [26], access to community‐equivalent harm reduction interventions is not being achieved despite clear consensus on best practice interventions [27]. They are also often subjected to punitive policies that are intended to deter drug use, but may have the opposite effect [28]. All adult correctional systems in Australia are signatories to the ‘Guiding Principles for Corrections in Australia’, [4], within which Standard 3.1.4 stipulates that ‘Prisoners are provided a standard of healthcare equal to services available in the community that meet their individual physical health, mental health and social and emotional care needs, fostering continuity of care between custody and the community’. Standard 3.1.11 states that ‘Identification and access to treatment, education and interventions help minimise harm arising from alcohol and other drug use’ [4]. However, these Guiding Principles are not binding or enforceable, and performance against these standards is not routinely monitored across jurisdictions.

Alcohol and Other Drug Policy Search and Review

Drug strategies provide overarching frameworks to inform policy and practice aiming to reduce or prevent the harms associated with AOD use. Australia's overarching drug strategy, the National Drug Strategy, is structured around the three complementary pillars of demand reduction, supply reduction and harm reduction [29]. We identified Australian national, state and territory AOD strategies and related policy documents that were current at the time of review, through targeted searches of Australian Government and jurisdictional government websites, supplemented by Google searches. Searches combined the name of each Australian jurisdiction with terms including “alcohol and other drug”, “AOD”, “policy”, “framework”, “action plan” and “strategic plan”. Additional searches using terms such as “justice health”, “correctional health”, “prison health” and “police” were undertaken to identify relevant custodial health policy documents. Including the National Drug Strategy, 14 policy documents were identified, including four national strategies or frameworks [29, 30, 31, 32], six state strategies [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38], one territory [39] strategy, and three justice health, custodial health, policing or workforce documents [40, 41, 42] (Table 1). We then asked the large language model prompts focused on the inclusion of incarcerated populations, transition into and out of incarceration, priority populations and consistency with the principle of equivalence of care (Appendix A, Tables A1, A2, A3, A4). Following completion of the policy analysis and workshop, we identified one additional relevant document: the ACT's Alexander Maconochie Centre Drug and Blood Borne Virus Strategy 2025–2030 [43].

People in contact with the criminal justice system and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are separately identified as priority populations in the National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 [29]. However, despite the marked overrepresentation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in custodial settings, the Strategy does not explicitly recognise this population as a distinct priority group requiring specific, targeted, culturally safe AOD responses within custodial settings. This gap is a missed opportunity to build on the strengths of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities by incorporating cultural knowledge, healing approaches and community leadership into the development of those responses.

Each Australian state and territory is responsible for managing its own custodial facilities. Yet, our documentary analysis using Claude Sonnet 4.6 suggested that most state and territory AOD strategies contain little or no explicit consideration of custodial settings, or custodial‐specific AOD interventions. Even among the documents relating specifically to custodial settings, only one policy addressed AOD‐related harms and contained custodial‐specific interventions [40]. However, implementation and enforcement of this Victorian policy is unclear [40]. Furthermore, there are currently no national minimum enforceable healthcare standards in custodial settings, which some have advocated for previously [44]. Although there are consensus statements for the provision of opioid agonist therapy [45] and withdrawal management [46] in custodial settings, these statements are not readily enforceable. Furthermore, the absence of custodial settings from overarching AOD strategies makes it difficult to operationalise or monitor equivalence of care for people experiencing incarceration.

Workshop Discussion and Participant Observations

Informed by our AI‐assisted preliminary policy review, our workshop brought together 29 participants with backgrounds in research, policy, clinical and health service delivery, working in justice and/or corrective services and/or with lived experience of incarceration. Participants reviewed the same 14 national, state and territory AOD strategies, were presented with findings from the AI analysis of strategies, and discussed priorities for strengthening the integration of custodial settings within Australian AOD strategies. Stakeholders described the current state of AOD responses in custodial settings as inconsistent, fragmented, inadequate, and largely unknown. Workshop participants found limited consideration of custodial settings in the documents, and identified no explicit consideration of or commitment to ‘equivalence of care’. Stakeholders observed that the governance and operation of prisons vary considerably between (and within) states and territories, and that responsibilities are shared across both health and justice departments, requiring clear governance and inter‐departmental collaboration, and that there is no legal basis to develop binding prison health policy or legislation at a national level. There was further discussion around options to prevent people from entering prison for non‐violent drug‐related offences. Participants also discussed the possible benefit of national minimum healthcare standards in custodial settings [44] as a mechanism to bypass these jurisdictional and structural issues, and deliver a higher, more consistent standard of AOD care in custodial settings.

Recommendations

Based on our analysis of Australian AOD strategies, and the subsequent multidisciplinary workshop discussions and deliberations, we make the following recommendations:

Commonwealth, state and territory governments should integrate all custodial settings (police custody, youth detention and prisons) and incarcerated people into existing AOD policies and strategies rather than creating standalone policies that govern custodial settings. This is consistent with the World Health Organization's recommendation to include ‘prison health in all policies’ [47]. For this to be feasible, integrated health governance structures between custodial and community healthcare systems will be required, particularly in jurisdictions where the relevant health department does not oversee custodial healthcare.

People with lived experience of incarceration and AOD use must be involved in developing policies and strategies that respond to AOD‐related needs of people in (and released from) custody.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander organisations and communities should lead the design, governance, implementation and evaluation of culturally safe AOD policies and strategies affecting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in custody. Governments should strengthen partnerships with Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations to support continuity of culturally safe AOD care across the transition between custody and community.

Australian governments should jointly develop national minimum healthcare standards for all Australian custodial settings [44] and establish funded enforcement and transparent oversight mechanisms to ensure that AOD policies translate into an equitable level of care.

Currently, the burden of AOD use and related harm among people who experience incarceration is unacceptably high. Without clear, targeted, and evidence‐based guidelines for AOD harm minimisation in custodial settings, and comprehensive integration of these settings into national and jurisdictional AOD strategies, custodial settings will not be able to provide this underserved cohort with community‐equivalent care.

Prompt 1

Could you please answer the following questions separately for adult prisons, police watch houses, and juvenile detention centres?

We define these three custodial settings as follows.

An adult prison (under the administration of a Department of Justice or Corrective Services) is a secure, state‐operated facility used to detain individuals aged 18 and older. It houses both unsentenced individuals (on remand) awaiting trial and convicted offenders serving court‐mandated sentences.

A police watch house is a temporary holding facility attached to a police station where arrested individuals are processed, detained, and held until they can appear in court or be transferred to a long‐term correctional centre.

A juvenile detention centre (also known as a youth justice centre) is a secure facility that holds minors—typically individuals under the age of 18—who have broken the law.

Could you please provide a comprehensive answer, then a summary that could be placed on a single slide?

Comprehensive Answer

Adult Prisons

Adult prisons are, by far, the most extensively considered custodial setting across the document set. This is unsurprising given that two documents are specifically dedicated to this setting: the Victorian Healthcare Services Quality Framework for Victorian Prisons (2025) and the NSW Justice Health Strategic Plan, both of which treat prisons as a primary health service delivery environment requiring dedicated standards.

The depth of consideration includes:

Dedicated quality domains and criteria: specifically for AOD health needs in prison (e.g., the Victorian framework's “Quality Domain 8: Alcohol and Other Drugs‐Health,” covering identification of AOD needs, AOD peer educators, harm reduction sessions, withdrawal management, and Medication Assisted Treatment).

Dedicated quality domains and criteria: specifically for AOD health needs in prison (e.g., the Victorian framework's “Quality Domain 8: Alcohol and Other Drugs‐Health,” covering identification of AOD needs, AOD peer educators, harm reduction sessions, withdrawal management, and Medication Assisted Treatment).

Population‐level justification: documents cite high rates of AOD use among prison entrants (e.g., “67 per cent of prison entrants smoked tobacco daily” and prison entrants in 2018 were more likely than the general population to be non‐drinkers, but those who did drink were more likely to do so at higher‐risk levels), and the disproportionate incarceration of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, who face elevated rates of trauma and psychological distress in this setting.

Why this depth of focus: prisons hold people for extended periods, making them a feasible and high‐value site for structured treatment delivery (screening, withdrawal management, rehabilitation, pharmacotherapy), and the health burden of this population is well‐documented and politically visible (e.g., deaths in custody, Royal Commission findings).

Police Watch Houses

Watch houses receive minimal, fragmentary attention and are never addressed as a setting in their own right with dedicated programs or standards.

Where watch houses (or “police custody”) appear, it is almost always as a data point about the broader population entering the justice system, rather than as a site of intervention:

“30 per cent of people in police custody indicated that illicit drug use contributed to their offending” and “self‐reported alcohol use of people in police custody was also high, with 18 per cent reporting that alcohol contributed to their most recent police detention” (QLD plan).

Police‐level engagement is framed around the point of arrest/contact rather than the holding facility itself—for example, Victoria Police's drug diversion program, which diverts people away from charge at the point of police contact, and partnerships supporting referral to treatment.

Police‐level engagement is framed around the point of arrest/contact rather than the holding facility itself—for example, Victoria Police's drug diversion program, which diverts people away from charge at the point of police contact, and partnerships supporting referral to treatment.

Why so little attention: watch houses are transient (hours to days), administratively sit with police rather than health or justice departments, and none of the documents in this set are produced by a policing agency with a health mandate for this specific setting (the Victoria Police Drug Strategy focuses on enforcement, diversion, and community harm—not the watch house environment itself). Health and AOD strategies generally treat “police contact” as an upstream diversion point rather than a custodial health setting requiring its own service model.

Juvenile Detention Centres (Youth Justice Centres)

Juvenile detention receives moderate, but secondary, attention—substantially less than adult prisons, but markedly more than watch houses. Coverage clusters around several recurring themes:

Disproportionate need and risk: “Young people (aged 10‐17) in youth justice facilities… [use AOD treatment] as likely as young people of the same age in the general population” (implying under‐treatment relative to need), and high prevalence of Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder among detained youth—a 2018 Western Australian study of 99 young people (aged 13–17) in the youth justice system found that 36% of the cohort met fetal alcohol spectrum disorder criteria.

Prompt 3

Question 3: Do these documents characterise people in prison as a ‘population’, or recognise that custodial settings are settings through which members of the community who use substances pass? Is there consideration of continuity of care for people leaving custody?

Answer 3

Based on my review of the documents, here is the analysis for Question 3.

Comprehensive Answer

Adult Prisons: A Population, but Explicitly a Transient One

The documents most fully developed for the prison setting—particularly the Victorian Healthcare Services Quality Framework for Victorian Prisons and the NSW Justice Health Strategic Plan—do treat “the prison population” as a defined population (with its own “Quality Domain 7: Population Health”), but this framing is explicitly paired with recognition that this population is in continuous flow with the broader community.

The Victorian framework is unusually explicit about this: it states that the prison framework recognises that most people in prison are there for relatively short periods, and that population health interventions in the prison system have been proven to generate health benefits in the broader community. It goes further, stating that as most people in prison return to the community, imprisonment presents an opportunity to improve their health and wellbeing and to reduce the potential need for more costly specialist health care—explicitly framing the prison as a point of contact with people who will return to community life, not a closed system.

This “pass‐through” framing is reinforced by:

The explicit statement that the transition to and from custody, and from one custodial unit to another, can be a high‐risk time for people in prison—that is, the boundary between custody and community is treated as a clinically significant transition point, not an endpoint.

The explicit statement that the transition to and from custody, and from one custodial unit to another, can be a high‐risk time for people in prison—that is, the boundary between custody and community is treated as a clinically significant transition point, not an endpoint.

The instruction that transition planning must commence for each person in prison upon commencement of care—meaning release is planned for from the moment of intake, not as an afterthought.

The framing that imprisonment is an episode within a person's broader health journey (“throughout their stay in prison,” “during their prison episode”) rather than a separate population category.

Watch Houses: No “Population” Framing, no Continuity‐of‐Care Consideration

Watch houses are not characterised as a population at all—they appear only as transient data points about people passing through on their way into the justice system (e.g., self‐reported AOD contribution to detention). Prompt 4

Question 4: Is there explicit consideration of whether custodial policies and responses are consistent with a commitment to equivalence of care?

Answer 4

Based on my review of the documents, here is the analysis for Question 4.

Comprehensive Answer

Adult Prisons: Equivalence of Care Is the Most Explicit and Developed Theme

The principle of equivalence of care—the idea that people in custody should receive health care equivalent to what they would receive in the community—is explicitly and directly stated in the Victorian prison framework, in terms that closely mirror the formal international articulation of the principle:

“To ensure people in prison receive the quality of primary health care equivalent to care they would receive in the community, the primary health service provider must deliver primary care services that include, but are not limited to: medical and nursing clinics, primary mental health, medication management, dental services, allied health, reproductive and sexual health… chronic medical condition treatment, advanced care planning, referrals to specialist health care, emergency care.”

This is not an isolated statement—it is operationalised through multiple concrete mechanisms across the Victorian framework:

24‐h access to health and medical services where required, equivalent to community emergency access.

24‐h access to health and medical services where required, equivalent to community emergency access.

Triage by clinical need, not by custodial status, so urgent care is prioritised regardless of security classification.

Triage by clinical need, not by custodial status, so urgent care is prioritised regardless of security classification.

Telehealth and specialist referral pathways explicitly designed so “the quality of care is not compromised” by using remote technologies—an explicit safeguard against a lower standard being substituted for equivalence.

Telehealth and specialist referral pathways explicitly designed so “the quality of care is not compromised” by using remote technologies—an explicit safeguard against a lower standard being substituted for equivalence.

No disadvantage on transfer: active referrals must be reviewed when a person is transferred between facilities, with the receiving prison's triage process considering wait times already incurred “so that the person is not disadvantaged by being moved between facilities”—directly addressing a known equivalence failure point (care disruption due to movement).

No disadvantage on transfer: active referrals must be reviewed when a person is transferred between facilities, with the receiving prison's triage process considering wait times already incurred “so that the person is not disadvantaged by being moved between facilities”—directly addressing a known equivalence failure point (care disruption due to movement).

Rights not compromised by custody: “People in prison understand their rights to be informed of and involved in decisions about their health care which must not be compromised by being in custody,” including the right to refuse treatment, seek second opinions, make complaints, and access a private health practitioner at their own expense.

Rights not compromised by custody: “People in prison understand their rights to be informed of and involved in decisions about their health care which must not be compromised by being in custody,” including the right to refuse treatment, seek second opinions, make complaints, and access a private health practitioner at their own expense.

Access to care in restrictive environments: explicit requirement that people in observation cells, close supervision, and high‐security units still receive appropriate health care—addressing a known risk area where equivalence often breaks down in practice.

The NSW Justice Health Strategic Plan frames equivalence through an explicit human rights lens, stating commitment to upholding the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners (the Nelson Mandela Rules), framing health and wellbeing as “a critical component of those within our care despite the challenges of our settings,” and committing to “improving access to health services in the least restrictive care setting”—including for those requiring urgent or involuntary treatment.

The Queensland AOD plan grounds equivalence in statutory human rights law, citing the Queensland Human Rights Act 2019's guarantees of “humane treatment when deprived of liberty” and “the right to health services,” and stating explicitly that respect for human rights “includes… equitable access to health care of appropriate quality” for people experiencing AOD problems.

The National Framework for Alcohol, Tobacco and Other Drug Treatment articulates an equivalence‐adjacent principle through its “person‐centred” treatment principle, using a worked example of an incarcerated young person with a blood‐borne virus who “has the right to receive tailored treatment, care and support in prison and after their release, and treatment which is age and developmentally appropriate”—explicitly asserting that incarceration does not diminish treatment entitlements.

The Victorian framework notes that “the range of services are delivered within the bounds of security systems and protocols that apply to the custodial environment”—an acknowledgment that security imperatives shape (and potentially limit) how equivalent care is delivered, even while the equivalence goal is maintained.

There is no discussion of equivalence of care in relation to watch houses anywhere in the document set. Cross‐Cutting Observation

Equivalence of care is a mature, explicitly named, and operationally detailed principle only for the adult prison setting, anchored in both clinical quality frameworks (NSQHS standards) and human rights instruments (Nelson Mandela Rules, Queensland Human Rights Act). It is implicitly gestured at for juvenile detention through general treatment‐rights principles, but not operationalised. It is entirely absent for watch houses. Even within the adult prison setting, harm reduction equivalence (needle/syringe programs, supervised injecting, drug checking) is a notable silence—these are core community AOD harm‐reduction measures that are not discussed as part of the prison care model, despite the very high documented prevalence of injecting‐related harms in custodial populations.
