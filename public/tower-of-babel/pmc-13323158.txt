Development and External Validation of an Electronic Health Record‐Derived Model for 30‐Day Periprocedural Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Liqi Shu, Karen L Furie, Ana‐Lucia Garcia Guarniz, Adam de Havenon, Farhan Khan, Thanh N Nguyen, James E Siegler, Eduardo Soares Cirne de Toledo, Siwen Wang, Moxin Wu, Xiangyu Zhao, Henry B Zheng, Shadi Yaghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative ischemic stroke is uncommon overall but frequent in cardiac, major vascular, and neurosurgical procedures. Existing calculators often exclude these settings and rarely incorporate cerebrovascular disease markers available in routine electronic health record data.MethodsWe assembled a retrospective cohort of adults undergoing procedures at 3 hospitals (January 2016–June 2024). The model was derived at Rhode Island Hospital (255 850 procedures) and externally validated at 2 affiliated hospitals (The Miriam Hospital/Newport Hospital; 189 095 procedures). Candidate predictors included age, vascular comorbidities, documented carotid stenosis and intracranial atherosclerosis, procedure setting (ambulatory versus inpatient/emergency), and procedural service (including vascular versus nonvascular neurosurgery and open versus interventional cardiovascular procedures). We fit a multivariable logistic regression model with internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples and assessed calibration by observed versus predicted risk across deciles. External validation applied locked derivation coefficients without refitting.ResultsStrokes occurred in 1235/255 850 derivation procedures (0.48%) and 418/189 095 validation procedures (0.22%). Independent predictors included older age, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 6.66), inpatient/emergency setting (aOR, 4.25 versus ambulatory), vascular neurosurgery (aOR, 6.70 versus general surgery), and open cardiovascular procedures (aOR, 4.14). Discrimination was high (derivation area under the curve, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.87–0.88]; optimism‐corrected area under the curve, 0.87; validation area under the curve, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.84–0.87]) with good calibration. Prespecified risk strata (<1%, 1–5%, >5%) separated observed event rates in both cohorts.ConclusionA pragmatic, electronic health record‐derived model integrating procedural category and cerebrovascular disease accurately predicts 30‐day periprocedural ischemic stroke across diverse procedures and is available as a web‐based calculator to support counseling and targeted prevention.Keywords: ischemic stroke, peri‐procedural stroke, procedure, surgerySubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke, Quality and Outcomes, Primary Prevention

Perioperative stroke, ischemic stroke occurring during or within 30 days of surgery, is a serious but underrecognized surgical complication. 1 Although its overall incidence is low (0.1%–1% in most noncardiac surgeries), the risk rises to 5% to 10% in high‐risk surgeries such as cardiac, major vascular, or neurosurgical procedures. 2 These strokes carry substantial morbidity and mortality, often leading to prolonged hospitalization and functional dependence. 3 With an aging surgical population and increasing prevalence of vascular risk factors, the clinical burden of periprocedural stroke may continue to increase. 4 Despite its impact, perioperative stroke has historically received less attention in surgical risk assessments, rendering it an underappreciated threat to surgical outcomes. 4

Existing perioperative stroke risk prediction models have certain limitations. Most prior tools were developed in narrowly defined procedural populations that frequently excluded cardiac and neurosurgical procedures. 1 , 5 , 6 , 7 Specialty‐specific calculators (eg, Society of Thoracic Surgeons cardiac surgery model) provided modest discrimination for stroke (area under the curve [AUC], ~0.65–0.70), highlighting persistent gaps in risk stratification. 8 In addition, imaging‐based markers of cerebrovascular disease, such as intracranial atherosclerosis, have been shown in recent cardiac and vascular surgery cohorts to confer a 2‐ to 4‐fold increased risk of perioperative ischemic stroke. To our knowledge, it has not been incorporated in risk prediction models. 9

To address these limitations, we developed and validated a comprehensive, pragmatic peri‐procedural stroke risk model derived from electronic medical records. The model incorporates high‐risk procedural subtypes and key vascular risk factors. The model was derived and validated in a large cohort to improve periprocedural stroke risk stratification.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data and study materials will not be made publicly available because of institutional restrictions. Deidentified data and analytic code may be made available to qualified investigators on reasonable request to the corresponding author, subject to institutional review board approval, a data use agreement, and applicable institutional policies.

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients treated with procedures between January 1, 2016, and June 30, 2024, at 3 hospitals within the Brown University health system. Rhode Island Hospital (RIH) was used for model derivation; 2 affiliated community hospitals, The Miriam Hospital (TMH) and Newport Hospital (NPH), served as the validation cohort. The retrospective cohort study was approved by the Lifespan institutional review board with a waiver of informed consent because of the retrospective use of deidentified electronic health record data. Data were deidentified for analysis and reported in aggregate. A separate public study protocol was not prepared for this retrospective cohort study, and the study was not registered. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this study.

Patient Population and Variable Definitions

We queried the enterprise data warehouse for all patients aged ≥18 years who underwent any procedure (including general, endoscopic, cardiovascular, thoracic, orthopedic, neurosurgical, urologic, obstetric, or other procedures) during the study period. The procedures performed under each specialty/service department are listed in Table S1. We excluded patients with age <18 years or missing age, those with unknown procedure type, and procedures performed for deceased organ donation.

From the electronic health record (EHR), we abstracted demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity), procedural characteristics (inpatient versus outpatient status, emergency versus elective, and procedural specialty/service department), and smoking status (never, current, former, unknown) from structured fields. All comorbidities were derived from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10)–coded problem lists (Table S2) based on preexisting EHR records and were coded as present when documented and absent otherwise. Key comorbidities comprised hypertension, type 2 diabetes, atrial fibrillation, prior stroke, carotid stenosis, intracranial atherosclerosis, chronic lung disease, obstructive sleep apnea, chronic kidney disease, active cancer, and coagulopathy. Dedicated preoperative vascular imaging was not required for cohort inclusion or for use of the risk tool, supporting broad clinical implementation. To evaluate the fidelity of ICD‐10–coded carotid stenosis and intracranial atherosclerosis, we randomly selected 50 ICD‐positive and 50 ICD‐negative patients for each condition (total n=200) for adjudication by 2 blinded board‐certified neurologists. For carotid stenosis, computed tomography angiography neck or carotid ultrasound were reviewed, and for intracranial atherosclerosis, computed tomography angiography brain was reviewed. The level of at least moderate stenosis was considered confirmatory.

We also used service‐level procedural groupings to promote cross‐institutional portability and reduce dependence on institution‐specific Current Procedural Terminology granularity. These variables were subsequently used as candidate predictors in the multivariable logistic regression model. Hospital identifiers (RIH, TMH, NPH) were used to define the derivation and validation cohorts.

Outcome Definition

The primary outcome was periprocedural ischemic stroke, defined as any new ischemic stroke occurring within 30 days after the index procedure. Stroke events were extracted by ICD‐10 codes for acute ischemic stroke (I63) recorded in the discharge diagnoses or in any readmission within 30 days post procedure to capture postdischarge events. To validate this outcome variable, we randomly sampled 50 cases and, by manual chart review, confirmed 100% concordance between coded and clinically documented periprocedural ischemic strokes occurring within 30 days of the index procedure.

Statistical Analysis

In the derivation cohort, univariable associations between each candidate predictor and periprocedural stroke were assessed using χ2 tests for categorical variables and Student's t tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Based on prior perioperative stroke literature, clinical relevance, and availability in structured EHR data, the following predictors were specified a priori and forced into the prediction model: age, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack, atrial fibrillation, carotid disease, operative urgency, and procedure type. Additional candidate variables available in the structured EHR were then entered into the multivariable logistic regression model, and backward stepwise elimination was applied only to these noncore variables, whereas the specified a priori predictors were retained regardless of statistical significance. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% CIs were calculated for variables retained in the final model. Model discrimination was quantified by the area under the receiver‐operating characteristic curve. We performed internal validation with 1000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism‐corrected model discrimination. Model calibration was evaluated by comparing observed versus predicted stroke risk across groups of predicted risk. As a sensitivity analysis to assess robustness of variable selection, we fit a penalized logistic regression model with Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator with Bayesian information criterion based penalty selection in the derivation cohort and evaluated discrimination and calibration in the external validation cohort.

For external validation, we applied the final model's intercept and β‐coefficients (derived from RIH) to patients in the combined validation cohort (TMH and NPH) without refitting. Each patient's 30‐day stroke probability was calculated using the derivation model equation. Discrimination was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve with 95% CIs, and calibration was assessed by comparing observed and predicted stroke risk across groups of predicted risk. To contextualize discrimination, we applied the published coefficients from 3 external risk scores (Woo et al., 5 Mashour et al., 6 Platzbecker et al. 7 ) to the same cohort, and generated a single overlay receiver operating characteristic figure displaying all 4 curves. 1 , 5 , 6 To ensure cohort independence and mitigate within‐patient correlation, we repeated sensitivity analysis with external validation after restricting the TMH/NPH cohort to each patient's index (earliest) eligible procedure and excluding any patients who appeared in the derivation cohort.

We additionally stratified patients into 3 risk categories based on predicted stroke probability: <1% (low risk), 1% to 5% (moderate risk), and >5% (high risk). 10 , 11 We compared the observed stroke incidence across these strata in both derivation and validation cohorts to evaluate risk stratification performance. Model discrimination and calibration were examined within selected high‐risk procedural subgroups (eg, vascular versus nonvascular neurosurgery, and open versus interventional cardiovascular procedures) to assess performance consistency. Analyses were conducted on complete data without imputation because missing data were minimal for predictors included in the final model. Comorbidities were defined as binary ICD‐10 derived indicators, which did not generate missing values. All statistical tests were 2 sided, and P<0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were conducted using Stata/SE 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Web Application Development

We developed a simple browser‐based calculator, the Brown Peri‐procedural Stroke Risk Assessment Tool, to implement our periprocedural stroke risk model. Clinicians can enter patient age and relevant clinical factors to obtain an immediate estimated 30‐day stroke probability. The tool was built using the Cursor platform (Version: 1.1.6) and is publicly available on GitHub Pages (https://liqi‐shu.github.io/peri‐procedural‐stroke‐calc/), with source code and the locked model coefficient available in the GitHub repository (https://github.com/liqi‐shu/peri‐procedural‐stroke‐calc).

RESULTS

Patient Cohort

In the derivation cohort (RIH), a total of 255 850 adult procedures were performed during the study period, and 1235 patients (0.48%) experienced a periprocedural ischemic stroke within 30 days of procedure. The external validation cohort, comprising combined cases from TMH and NPH, included 189 095 procedures with 418 strokes (0.22%) in the same period. A detailed flow diagram of cohort inclusion is provided in Figure 1.

Patient flow diagram.

In samples adjudicated by 2 board‐certified neurologists with relevant imaging available (intracranial atherosclerosis n=47; carotid stenosis n=57), ICD‐coded intracranial atherosclerosis showed substantial agreement with gold‐standard chart/imaging review (κ=0.672; agreement 89.4%), and ICD‐coded carotid stenosis showed almost perfect agreement (κ=0.949; agreement 98.3%). These findings support the reliability of these EHR‐derived predictors for pragmatic periprocedural stroke risk modeling.

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics of patients with and without periprocedural stroke are summarized in Table 1. In the derivation cohort, patients who had a periprocedural stroke were older (median age 69 [interquartile range, 60–78] versus 59 [interquartile range, 46–70] years for those without stroke) and less likely to be female (43.4% versus 53.5%). Vascular risk factors and comorbidities were markedly more prevalent among stroke cases than nonstroke cases, including type 2 diabetes (39.9% versus 23.6%), hypertension (81.0% versus 55.9%), prior stroke (35.9% versus 4.1%), carotid stenosis (15.1% versus 2.8%), intracranial atherosclerosis (2.1% versus 0.2%), and atrial fibrillation (29.0% versus 11.5%). The majority (69.6%) of stroke events occurred in patients whose procedure was emergent or occurred during inpatient admission, compared with 28.3% of nonstroke procedures. High‐risk procedural strata were overrepresented among stroke cases, including vascular neurosurgery (3.5% versus 0.2%) and nonvascular neurosurgery (15.2% versus 7.4%), as well as open cardiovascular procedures (15.6% versus 3.3%) and interventional cardiovascular procedures (6.8% versus 3.3%), respectively (Table 1).

Multivariable Predictors of Periprocedural Stroke

We identified several predictors of periprocedural ischemic stroke in the final multivariable logistic regression model, which retained prespecified core predictors and additional noncore EHR variables using backward stepwise elimination (Table 2). Older age was associated with higher risk (aOR per year 1.02 [95% CI, 1.01–1.02]). A history of prior stroke or transient ischemic attack ischemic conferred a near 7‐fold increase in odds (aOR, 6.66 [95% CI, 5.85–7.59]). Patients with carotid artery stenosis had almost double the odds of stroke (aOR, 1.56 [95% CI, 1.30–1.86]), and those with documented intracranial atherosclerotic disease had double the odds (aOR, 1.97 [95% CI, 1.29–3.01]) compared with patients without these conditions. Atrial fibrillation (aOR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.07–1.41]), type 2 diabetes (aOR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.03–1.31]), and hypertension (aOR, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.15–1.59]) were also significant independent risk factors. The urgency and setting of the procedure had a strong influence: procedures performed on an inpatient or emergency basis had about 7‐fold higher odds of stroke than ambulatory outpatient procedures (aOR, 4.25 [95% CI, 3.73–4.84]). Procedural category was a major determinant of risk. Compared with the reference category of general surgery, odds were highest for vascular neurosurgery (aOR, 6.70 [95% CI, 4.46–10.07]) and open cardiovascular procedures (aOR, 4.14 [95% CI, 3.22–5.32]), followed by nonvascular neurosurgery (aOR, 3.40 [95% CI, 2.66–4.35]) and interventional cardiovascular procedures (aOR, 2.07 [95% CI, 1.54–2.80]). Noncardiac medical subspecialty procedure had a more modest increase (aOR, 1.47 [95% CI, 1.17–1.84]), and urologic procedures also carried slightly elevated risk (aOR, 1.57 [95% CI, 1.04–2.36]). Other procedural categories (orthopedic/plastic, head and neck, obstetric/gynecologic, etc) did not differ significantly from general surgery after adjustment.

Model Discrimination and Calibration

The final risk model demonstrated excellent discrimination. In the derivation cohort, the model's AUC was 0.87 (95% CI, 0.87–0.88), indicating high accuracy in distinguishing between patients who would versus would not experience a periprocedural stroke. The apparent AUC was 0.875, and the optimism‐corrected AUC after 1000 bootstrap resamples was 0.872 (mean optimism 0.002), indicating minimal overfitting. When the same model was applied to the external validation cohort, the AUC was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.84–0.87) (Figure 2). This represents only a minimal decrease in performance, suggesting the model's generalizability to another patient population. Calibration assessment showed good agreement between predicted and observed stroke risks. The calibration plots demonstrated close agreement between predicted and observed stroke risk across deciles of predicted probability (see Figure S1 for calibration curve).

Receiver operating characteristic curves comparing periprocedural stroke prediction models in the external validation cohort (TMH/NPH).

Curves shown for the electronic health record‐derived model (current study) and 3 published risk scores (Woo 2021, Mashour 2011, Platzbecker 2021). AUC values are reported in the panel/legend. AUC indicates area under the curve; NPH, Newport Hospital; and TMH, The Miriam Hospital.

In the sensitivity analysis restricting the TMH/NPH cohort to index procedures per patient and excluding derivation‐cohort patients (n=83 945), discrimination remained high with AUC 0.86 (95% CI, 0.83–0.89). In the sensitivity analysis using Bayesian information criterion‐selected Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator logistic regression, discrimination was similar to the primary model (RIH AUC, 0.87 and TMH/NPH AUC, 0.86) with similar calibration across risk deciles.

Notably, the model retained acceptable discriminative ability even in high‐risk procedural subsets. For example, in the combined group of all neurosurgical (vascular/nonvascular) and cardiovascular (open/interventional) procedures (n=3329 in validation), the AUC was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.71–0.84).

Risk Stratification

Using each patient's calculated stroke probability, we stratified the cohort into 3 risk categories: <1% (low risk), 1% to 5% (moderate risk), and >5% (high risk). This stratification meaningfully differentiated actual outcomes. In the derivation cohort, the observed 30‐day stroke rates were 0.22% in the low‐risk group, 2.27% in the moderate‐risk group, and 7.07% in the high‐risk group. Corresponding rates were 0.13%, 1.22%, and 3.45% in the validation cohort, respectively. These nonoverlapping rates indicate the model's utility in clinical risk stratification.

To facilitate clinical uptake, we deployed the final risk model in an open‐access web calculator (https://liqi‐shu.github.io/peri‐procedural‐stroke‐calc/) shown in Figure S2. Clinicians can enter patient age, documented cerebrovascular disease (eg, carotid stenosis, intracranial atherosclerosis), comorbidities, and procedural details to obtain the estimated 30‐day stroke probability.

DISCUSSION

Principal Findings

In this large multicenter study, built on a pragmatic data set derived from routinely collected medical records, we developed and externally validated a periprocedural stroke risk model with excellent discrimination (derivation AUC, 0.87 and external validation 0.86). In comparison to previous literature, this level of performance is comparable to or exceeds existing perioperative stroke risk scores. To our knowledge, the model is the first to encompass all major procedural subtypes (including cardiovascular and neurosurgical cases) within a single framework. We used service‐level procedural categories for portability, and because risk differed significantly within neurosurgical and cardiovascular procedures, we refined these into vascular versus nonvascular and open versus interventional subgroups. Incorporating traditional clinical risk factors alongside vascular imaging markers (carotid stenosis and intracranial atherosclerosis) likely contributed to its high accuracy. 1 These findings demonstrate that periprocedural stroke risk can be quantified with a high degree of precision when both standard risk factors and markers of cerebrovascular disease are considered, even in populations undergoing traditionally high‐risk procedures. However, discrimination was lower in the highest‐risk procedural subgroups, likely reflecting greater heterogeneity and the influence of intraprocedural factors not captured in preprocedural EHR data.

Comparison to Prior Risk Models

Previous risk models for periprocedural stroke showed promising discrimination but were derived in narrower procedural populations. One study reported a validation AUC of ~0.80 in a noncardiac surgical cohort. 6 The American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program calculator, which similarly excludes cardiac and neurosurgical cases, achieved an AUC of ~0.87 in its validation. 5 However, this score's formula and coefficient were not publicly available, making implementation impractical. The recent Stroke after Surgery score, which added carotid stenosis and atrial fibrillation as predictors, reached an AUC of ~0.88, but notably it was not validated for cardiac surgery and did not include intracranial atherosclerosis. 7 In contrast, specialty‐specific tools such as the Society of Thoracic Surgeons cardiac model exhibit only modest stroke prediction accuracy (AUC, ~0.65–0.70). 8

Emerging cohort studies and meta‐analyses now show that intracranial atherosclerosis independently confers a 3‐ to 4‐fold increased risk of periprocedural ischemic stroke, even after adjusting for extracranial carotid disease and traditional risk factors. 9 , 12 In our model, intracranial atherosclerosis carried an adjusted odds ratio of 1.97, filling a critical gap left by earlier scores that lacked this high‐impact imaging marker. By integrating intracranial atherosclerosis alongside carotid stenosis and standard clinical predictors across all major procedural subtypes, our unified model achieves superior discrimination and generalizability that prior tools did not attain.

Clinical Implications

By deploying an interactive, evidence‐based web calculator, clinicians can obtain individualized 30‐day stroke probabilities at the point of care. The calculator, which can be integrated into electronic health record decision‐support platforms, allows real‐time estimation of the periprocedural stroke risk. Access to this tool may enhance periprocedural counseling and shared decision‐making by clearly communicating absolute stroke risks to patients and care teams. High‐risk individuals identified through the calculator could be targeted for intensified preprocedure optimization, such as tighter blood pressure and glycemic control, smoking cessation, optimizing antithrombotic management in accordance with existing guidelines and individualized bleeding risk, vascular imaging review, neurology consultation, tailored intraprocedural and postprocedure monitoring protocols (eg, strict hemodynamic targets, earlier resumption of anticoagulants, more frequent neurologic assessments). To further mitigate stroke risk in such high‐risk patients, current guidelines recommend selective peri‐procedural interventions, including deferring elective procedure for at least 6 to 9 months after a recent ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, 13 performing carotid evaluation with revascularization if severe symptomatic stenosis is present before other major elective procedures, and optimizing periprocedural antithrombotic regimens (eg, continuing aspirin for secondary stroke prevention when surgical bleeding risk permits). 3 In scenarios where predicted periprocedural stroke risk exceeds expected procedural benefit, clinicians and patients may elect to modify the procedural plan or consider alternative therapies. Overall, applying the model in clinical practice may enable more proactive periprocedural management and informed consent discussions aimed at reducing stroke incidence.

Limitations

This study and model have several limitations. First, retrospective EHR data can lead to misclassification because diagnoses may be undercoded or incompletely documented. We captured comorbidities as ICD‐10 derived indicators with present versus absent, which may not fully reflect true disease burden or severity. For carotid stenosis and intracranial atherosclerosis, documentation may come from internal imaging, outside reports, or historical problem lists, and absence of documentation may reflect incomplete data transfer or lack of prior evaluation rather than true absence. Importantly, the model is designed for pragmatic preprocedural risk estimation using routinely available EHR information without requiring additional imaging, which supports real‐world implementability but may yield conservative risk estimates in some patients and heterogeneity in predictor ascertainment across settings. Second, our model focuses on peri‐procedural factors (plus broad procedure type and urgency) and does not capture intraprocedural or postprocedural variables such as hypotension or new‐onset atrial fibrillation, which can also influence stroke risk. In addition, periprocedural antithrombotic exposure and continuation versus temporary interruption could not be reliably ascertained from structured EHR data and therefore were not modeled. Third, the data were drawn from a single regional health care system, which may limit generalizability, and model performance should be confirmed in other geographic and practice settings. Finally, we used service‐level procedural groupings to maximize portability, but heterogeneity within these categories may persist and warrants future refinement. These considerations suggest caution in interpretation. However, the model offers a strong foundation for periprocedural stroke risk stratification using routinely available clinical data.

Future Directions

Prospective studies are needed to determine whether implementing this risk model in practice can improve patient outcomes. Integrating the score into periprocedural care, such as an automatic electronic health record alert for high‐risk patients, should be tested to see if it changes management or reduces stroke rates. The model can also be continuously refined: periodic updates with new data or incorporation of additional predictors (for instance, intraprocedural hemodynamic parameters) may further enhance accuracy, provided such changes maintain clinical usability. Moreover, external validation in diverse populations and hospital systems is important to ensure broad generalizability.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we developed and externally validated a broadly applicable periprocedural stroke risk model that integrates high‐risk procedures and vascular imaging markers with standard clinical predictors, achieving excellent discrimination. This tool improves on prior models and may enable more effective stroke risk stratification and prevention across diverse procedural populations.
