Programmable Domestication: CRISPR, Pan‐Genomics and System Level Engineering for Next‐Generation Crops
Authors: Muhammad Mubashar Zafar, Hina Firdous, Ayesha Siddiqua, Ayesha Naveed, Abdul Razzaq, Sadam Munawar, Aqsa Ijaz, Zunaira Anwar, Sezai Ercisli, Xuefei Jiang, Qiao Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlobal agriculture is increasingly challenged by climate instability, genetic erosion, emerging pathogens and rising food demands, exposing the limitations of conventional breeding and traditional domestication strategies. Recent advances in CRISPR‐based genome editing, pangenomic, synthetic biology, artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted breeding and predictive phenomics are transforming de novo domestication from a slow evolutionary process into a programmable framework for rational crop redesign. This review synthesises recent advances in programmable de novo domestication and highlights how crop wild relatives and underutilised germplasm can be harnessed to develop resilient, climate‐adaptive and sustainable crop systems. The integration of multiplex genome editing, pan‐genomic variation discovery, AI‐driven genomic prediction and predictive breeding enables precise engineering of key domestication traits governing plant architecture, yield potential, stress resilience and nutritional quality. Furthermore, we propose a trajectory‐based framework for programmable domestication comprising Adaptive Rescue, Agronomic Refinement and Novel Chassis Engineering, which illustrates distinct evolutionary pathways, engineering complexity and crop redesign objectives. We also examine the major system level challenges that constrain programmable domestication, including cryptic genetic variation, epistasis, gene regulatory network complexity, genotype phenotype predictability, biodiversity conservation and regulatory considerations. Collectively, programmable domestication represents a transformative shift from conventional crop improvement towards system‐level engineering of next‐generation crops, providing a strategic foundation for enhancing global food security, agricultural sustainability and environmental resilience in the face of accelerating climate change.Keywords: climate‐resilient crops, CRISPR‐Cas genome editing, crop wild relatives, future agriculture, genome engineering, pan‐genomics, predictive breeding, programmable domestication, system‐level crop redesign

Introduction

Global agriculture is confronting the unprecedented challenge of ensuring food security under increasingly unpredictable environmental conditions driven by climate change, emerging pests and pathogens, declining natural resources and a growing global population. Although domestication and modern breeding have transformed wild plant species into highly productive crops, centuries of intensive selection have simultaneously narrowed the genetic diversity available within many cultivated gene pools. This domestication‐driven genetic erosion has depleted reservoirs of adaptive alleles required for resilience to evolving biotic and abiotic stresses, thereby limiting the capacity of conventional breeding to simultaneously enhance productivity, environmental adaptation and resource‐use efficiency. Consequently, broadening the adaptive potential of modern crops has become a central objective of contemporary plant biotechnology and sustainable agriculture (Curtin et al. 2022; Fernie and Yan 2019).

Crop wild relatives, landraces and other underutilised germplasm provide invaluable reservoirs of evolutionary diversity that have largely escaped the genetic bottlenecks imposed by domestication. These genetic resources harbour extensive structural variants, regulatory polymorphisms and adaptive haplotypes associated with stress tolerance, developmental plasticity and environmental adaptation that are frequently absent from elite breeding populations. Recent advances in pan‐genomics and graph‐based genome assemblies have substantially expanded access to this previously hidden diversity by capturing genomic variation beyond the limitations of single‐reference genomes, enabling the identification of candidate genes, structural variants and evolutionary signatures that were previously inaccessible (Gao et al. 2019; Jayakodi et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023; Shang et al. 2022). Recent pangenomic analyses further demonstrate that structural variation can be directly associated with agronomically important traits, strengthening the potential of these resources for trait‐oriented discovery (Zhao, Xue, et al. 2025). However, translating these valuable alleles into elite cultivars remains a major challenge because conventional introgression is often constrained by linkage drag, reproductive barriers and the polygenic architecture of complex agronomic traits, limiting both breeding efficiency and the predictable incorporation of beneficial variation (Curtin et al. 2022; Fernie and Yan 2019).

These limitations have accelerated the emergence of de novo domestication, a programmable crop improvement paradigm that directly converts wild or underutilised species into agronomically valuable crops through the targeted engineering of key domestication traits while preserving their naturally evolved adaptive characteristics. This strategy integrates pan‐genomics, functional genomics, precision genome editing, high‐throughput phenotyping and artificial intelligence to enable rational crop redesign. Rather than relying exclusively on naturally occurring variation and prolonged breeding cycles, programmable domestication seeks to identify adaptive genetic variation, establish robust genotype–phenotype relationships, precisely engineer target loci and accelerate the development of improved crop ideotypes. Landmark studies in wild tomato, groundcherry and wild allotetraploid rice have demonstrated that CRISPR‐mediated genome editing can rapidly reconstruct key domestication traits while retaining the adaptive characteristics of wild germplasm, providing compelling experimental evidence that domestication can be rationally accelerated through targeted genome engineering (Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021; Zsögön et al. 2018). More recent multi‐trait engineering efforts, including the development of pennycress as a new oilseed crop through coordinated editing of key domestication traits, further extend this proof‐of‐concept towards broader crop systems (Gautam et al. 2026). Collectively, these advances have transformed de novo domestication from isolated proof‐of‐concept studies into a promising systems‐oriented strategy for developing climate‐resilient and sustainable crops.

Despite these remarkable advances, the ability to identify genomic variation does not necessarily confer the ability to predict biological function or engineering outcomes. Although pan‐genomic analyses and graph‐based genome assemblies have dramatically expanded the discovery of structural variants, adaptive haplotypes and candidate loci, distinguishing causal variants from linked polymorphisms remains a fundamental challenge, particularly for complex quantitative traits governed by interconnected regulatory networks, pleiotropy, epistatic interactions and genotype × environment effects. Consequently, realising the full potential of programmable domestication requires a systematic transition from genomic discovery to functional validation. Integrating genome‐wide association studies (GWAS), quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping, transcriptomics, epigenomic profiling, chromatin accessibility analyses, mutant characterisation and CRISPR‐based causal validation enables the progressive refinement of candidate loci into biologically validated engineering targets (Cheng et al. 2025; Marand et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023). Recent chromatin‐accessibility studies in tomato further demonstrate how functional regions associated with fruit development and domestication can be prioritised for downstream validation and engineering (Cheng et al. 2025). Such evidence‐driven integration provides the mechanistic foundation for transforming genomic information into predictable and deployable crop improvement strategies.

As programmable domestication advances beyond proof‐of‐concept genome editing, its success will increasingly depend on understanding crops as dynamic biological systems rather than collections of independently engineered genes. Agronomic traits such as yield, stress resilience, plant architecture, nutritional quality and resource‐use efficiency emerge from complex interactions among genetic networks, developmental processes, environmental signals and crop management practices, limiting the predictability of single‐gene interventions. Importantly, these traits differ in their underlying genetic architecture, ranging from relatively tractable major‐effect loci to dosage‐dependent, polygenic, metabolic and regulatory network architectures, and therefore require different engineering strategies, from loss‐of‐function editing to regulatory fine‐tuning and coordinated multi‐locus modification (Aguirre et al. 2023; Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Future crop redesign will therefore require the integration of systems biology, iterative functional validation, predictive modelling, accelerated breeding platforms and rigorous multi‐environment evaluation to ensure that engineered traits remain biologically robust, agronomically effective and suitable for translational deployment. Adopting this system‐level perspective not only enhances biological predictability but also facilitates the translation of molecular innovations into resilient and sustainable agricultural systems.

Building upon these developments, this review presents an integrated perspective on programmable de novo domestication by connecting genomic discovery, functional validation, precision genome engineering, accelerated breeding and translational deployment within a unified conceptual framework. Rather than treating these technological advances as independent innovations, we examine how their coordinated implementation enables the rational redesign of complex crop phenotypes, spanning variant discovery to field‐level application. We further introduce the Trajectory Zones Framework, a system‐based conceptual model that classifies programmable domestication strategies according to their evolutionary starting points and engineering objectives, providing a coherent framework for understanding alternative pathways towards crop redesign. Beyond molecular and agronomic optimisation, we also consider the socio‐economic dimensions of deployment, including seed systems, farmer participation, end‐use quality and market acceptance, because successful crop redesign ultimately depends on compatibility with the agricultural and societal systems in which new crops are adopted. Finally, we critically evaluate emerging technological opportunities, unresolved biological constraints and future research priorities that will shape the next generation of programmable domestication for developing climate‐resilient, sustainable and resilient cropping systems capable of meeting future global food security demands.

Discovery‐to‐Deployment Pipeline for Programmable Domestication

Programmable domestication relies on a coordinated translational continuum advancing from pangenomic discovery of adaptive and regulatory variation to functional validation, precision engineering, accelerated breeding and multi‐environment deployment. Pangenomic analyses expand the accessible target space, multi‐omic approaches prioritise functional variants and genome editing enables their site‐specific modification. Subsequently, high‐throughput phenotyping, speed breeding and predictive modelling accelerate the evaluation of engineered lines across dynamic environments. Pangenomic Frameworks: Uncovering Hidden Structural Variation and Adaptive Diversity

Programmable domestication relies on expanding allele discovery beyond the constraints of single‐reference genomes. While linear assemblies provided initial insights into genome organisation, they can fail to capture extensive structural, regulatory and haplotypic variation across landraces, wild relatives and geographically diverse populations. Comparative multi‐genome analyses across diverse species including maize, soybean, tomato, rice, barley, cucumber and Setaria demonstrate that single‐reference approaches can miss genomic variation underlying adaptation, domestication and complex trait evolution (He et al. 2023; Hufford et al. 2021; Jayakodi et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2020; Shang et al. 2022). Consequently, pangenomic approaches have established a broader basis for systematically uncovering species‐wide structural variants (SVs), presence–absence variations (PAVs), copy‐number variations (CNVs) and complex haplotypes that contribute to genomic and functional diversity through differences in gene content, dosage, sequence composition and genomic configuration (Chen et al. 2023).

Transitioning from linear reference sequences to graph‐based and super‐pangenomic approaches enable multiple genomic configurations to be represented within a unified representation, reducing the limitations of single‐reference analyses. Recent graph‐based pangenome studies across cereal, legume, solanaceous and fibre crops including maize, soybean, rice, potato, cowpea, mung bean and upland cotton demonstrate the capacity of these approaches to resolve structural diversity and associate genomic variation with agronomic traits (Chen et al. 2026; He et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2020; Shang et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2026; Zhang, Sun, et al. 2026). Pangenomic analyses have further linked structural variation with important yield and quality components, including seed size and weight in peanut, metabolic traits in potato and breeding traits in cotton (Zhang, Sun, et al. 2026; Zhao, Xue, et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025). These advances strengthen the capacity of pan‐genomes to move beyond variant cataloguing towards trait‐oriented discovery, although functional interpretation remains dependent on downstream validation.

Beyond coding sequence alterations, pangenomic architectures highlight cis‐regulatory variation as an important driver of phenotypic diversification. Promoter and enhancer‐associated structural variants can alter transcriptional output without disrupting protein function, providing a potential mechanism for quantitative trait modulation. In tomato, widespread structural variation influences gene expression and crop‐improvement‐relevant variation, while pangenomic profiling in tea links structural variation with transcriptional divergence and agronomic trait diversification (Alonge et al. 2020; Tao et al. 2025). Furthermore, eQTL analyses and chromatin accessibility profiling in tomato indicate that cis‐regulatory divergence and population‐specific regulatory variation contribute to fruit development and domestication‐related phenotypes (Cheng et al. 2025; Zhao, Wang, et al. 2026). Modulation of gene activity through regulatory sequence variation therefore represents an important component of the pangenomic discovery space for fine‐tuning quantitative agronomic traits.

Wild relatives serve as essential reservoirs for reintroducing adaptive diversity reduced or lost during historical domestication and breeding. Super‐pangenomic evaluations across wild and cultivated lineages of barley, tomato, Avena and grapevine demonstrate extensive structural and haplotypic diversity in wild germplasm and enable identification of candidate genes associated with stress resilience and disease resistance (Guo et al. 2025; Jayakodi et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025). Mechanistic studies further connect wild‐derived structural variation with adaptive phenotypes: a promoter structural variant in Solanum habrochaites contributes to enhanced cold tolerance, CNVs contribute to ecological adaptation in Solanum chilense and maize pangenomic diversity reveals allelic and regulatory variation associated with drought resistance (Wei et al. 2025; Yang, Wang, et al. 2025). These findings reinforce the value of wild and diverse germplasm as sources of adaptive variation for crop improvement.

Despite their power, pan‐genomic resources remain primarily hypothesis‐generating and target‐prioritisation platforms. Sampling constraints, incomplete representation of species diversity and challenges in detecting and interpreting structural variation can limit discovery and biological interpretation (Hufford et al. 2021; Jayakodi et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023). Moreover, genomic associations do not inherently establish biological causality, as relationships among structural variants, haplotypes, regulatory states and phenotypes may reflect linked or context‐dependent effects (Zhang, Sun, et al. 2026; Zhao, Xue, et al. 2025). Translating candidate SVs and haplotypes into actionable engineering targets therefore requires moving beyond statistical association towards functional evaluation. Accordingly, candidate loci prioritised by pan‐genomes must be integrated with multi‐omic and experimental approaches to establish biological function and refine engineering targets, providing the essential link between genomic discovery and precision target selection in the subsequent phase of the domestication design cycle.

Multi‐Omic Functional Validation Pipeline: From Variant Discovery to Domestication Targets

Pan‐genomic resources have vastly expanded the catalogue of structural, regulatory and haplotypic variation across wild and cultivated germplasm; however, genomic association alone does not establish biological causality. Structural variants, presence–absence variations and candidate loci prioritised by pan‐genomes may reflect genetic linkage or context‐dependent effects rather than direct phenotypic drivers. Consequently, programmable domestication depends on converting candidate genomic signals into validated engineering targets through an integrative functional validation pipeline that unifies genetic mapping, multi‐layer omics and direct experimental validation.

The initial phase of this pipeline leverages genome‐wide association studies (GWAS), quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping and bulked‐segregant analysis (BSA‐seq) to define candidate intervals underlying agronomic traits. Because mapping alone rarely resolves single causal genes, integrating genetic mapping with transcriptomics narrows candidate regions by prioritising loci whose expression aligns with phenotypic variation (Yang et al. 2024). This mapping resolution is substantially enhanced by multi‐omic approaches that integrate transcriptomic, translatomic, metabolic and regulatory datasets, evaluating candidates across complementary molecular layers rather than through statistical association alone (Han et al. 2023; Wu, Guan, et al. 2025). Thus, genetic mapping establishes the candidate genomic space, whereas multi‐omic integration provides the biological resolution required for precise target prioritisation.

Candidate prioritisation is further refined through transcriptomic, epigenomic and chromatin‐accessibility profiling. Beyond coding sequence alterations, profiling active cis‐regulatory elements and chromatin states reveals regulatory mechanisms governing quantitative traits (Li, Fan, et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2024). In tomato, chromatin accessibility profiling (ATAC‐seq) has identified functional cis‐regulatory regions governing fruit development and domestication traits, demonstrating how regulatory elements can be prioritised genome‐wide (Cheng et al. 2025). Furthermore, epigenomic mapping and three‐dimensional genomic profiling resolve higher‐order chromatin interactions and subgenomic regulatory dynamics that contribute to transcriptional regulation beyond primary DNA sequence variation (Xianhui Huang et al. 2024). Single‐cell multi‐omic profiling extends this resolution to individual cell types and cellular states, combining chromatin accessibility and RNA sequencing to resolve cell‐type‐specific regulatory programmes and regulatory hubs that may be obscured in bulk tissue samples (Wang et al. 2025).

Multi‐omic integration additionally enables the reconstruction of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) governing complex domestication phenotypes. Because agronomic traits arise from interconnected developmental and metabolic pathways, network‐level analyses help distinguish candidate regulatory control points from secondary downstream responses. Integrated multi‐omics network maps in maize demonstrate how complementary datasets resolve regulatory hierarchies across biological processes, while domestication‐focused multi‐omic evaluations directly link transcriptional divergence with metabolic selection during crop improvement (Wu, Guan, et al. 2025). Integrated GRN analyses further map regulatory variation to complex architectural pleiotropy and GWAS signals (Bertolini et al. 2025), while large‐scale eQTL meta‐analyses across diverse populations uncover conserved and population‐specific regulatory variation underlying fruit evolution and domestication (Zhao, Nicolas, et al. 2026). Complementing these single‐cell and network approaches, spatial and spatiotemporal transcriptomics preserve tissue spatial context, resolving localised developmental trajectories in cotton embryogenesis and wheat grain development, including regulatory programmes associated with grain yield and quality (Fan et al. 2026; Ge et al. 2025; Li, Wan, et al. 2025).

Despite these analytical advances, multi‐omic datasets remain correlative and are constrained by tissue specificity, environmental dynamics, pleiotropy and genetic background. Increasing multi‐omic resolution narrows candidate hypotheses but cannot replace direct functional verification. Causal validation requires direct experimental testing. Classical genetic and molecular analyses established the molecular identity of foundational domestication genes, such as the wheat Q gene and Shattering1 (Sh1) across cereals (Lin et al. 2012; Simons et al. 2006), whereas targeted regulatory‐region editing now provides a direct route for functionally testing candidate cis‐regulatory elements identified via ATAC‐seq and functional genomics (Cheng et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2023). The identification and subsequent CRISPR‐mediated editing of a functional cis‐accessible region regulating tomato fruit development and size provides a clear template for transitioning from computational association to experimentally validated mechanism (Cheng et al. 2025).

Together, genetic mapping, multi‐omic network reconstruction, spatiotemporal profiling and targeted validation transform candidate loci into actionable domestication targets. This progression is exemplified by multi‐target de novo domestication strategies for orphan crops and wild rice, in which experimentally validated targets were systematically combined via precise genome editing to recreate domesticated phenotypes (Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021). Ultimately, the utility of pangenomic discovery depends on its capacity to prioritise loci that withstand experimental validation and produce the intended phenotypic modifications. While pangenomics defines the broader allele discovery space, convergent multi‐omic and experimental evidence establish the refined engineering targets required for the precision genome‐editing strategies detailed in the following section.

Precision Genome Engineering: The CRISPR Toolkit for Ideotype Design

The transition from genomic discovery to crop redesign requires moving from candidate variant identification to precise targeted engineering. Once actionable targets are established, CRISPR technologies enable programmable interventions within the genetic architectures governing domestication traits. Modern plant genome editing has thus evolved beyond rudimentary gene disruption towards sophisticated regulatory, base and multi‐locus modification, expanding the capacity for rational crop ideotype design.

Early applications of CRISPR relied primarily on loss‐of‐function knockouts to recreate historical domestication phenotypes. Targeted disruption of negative regulators or structural genes in tomato, orphan crops and rice rapidly modified architecture, flowering time, seed shattering and reproductive development, compressing multi‐millennial domestication trajectories into single breeding cycles (Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021; Zsögön et al. 2018). However, complete gene disruption may cause pleiotropic effects or compromise adaptive functions, limiting its utility for traits requiring quantitative phenotypic optimisation. This constraint has shifted the paradigm towards fine‐tuning regulatory architecture and precise allelic modification rather than relying exclusively on binary knockouts.

Regulatory‐region editing provides quantitative precision by modifying promoters and cis‐regulatory elements without altering protein‐coding sequences. CRISPR‐Cas12a promoter editing systems demonstrate that quantitative transcriptional modulation can optimise yield components while circumventing knockout‐associated pleiotropy (Zhou et al. 2023). The development of engineered Cas9 variants with broader protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) compatibility substantially expands targetable cis‐regulatory space that was previously constrained by canonical PAM requirements (Hu et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). Furthermore, high‐throughput, systematic mapping of over 30 000 cis‐regulatory mutations in sorghum demonstrates the feasibility of fine‐tuning gene expression at scale (Groover et al. 2026). Thus, regulatory‐region editing provides a mechanistically robust approach for modulating gene dosage rather than abolishing gene function.

Base and prime editing technologies further extend genome engineering by enabling precise nucleotide substitutions without double‐stranded breaks. Base editing enables targeted nucleotide substitutions that can recreate or modify naturally occurring alleles (Zong et al. 2017), whereas prime editing enables all twelve transition and transversion substitutions, targeted insertions and deletions (Lin et al. 2020). Optimisation of paired pegRNAs and PAM‐flexible systems has improved prime‐editing efficiency and target‐site accessibility across major crops (Lin et al. 2021; Nishizawa‐Yokoi et al. 2025). Optimised prime‐editing systems in soybean further demonstrate the translational extension of these precision tools beyond cereal model systems (Xu, Nie, et al. 2026). Concurrently, near‐PAM‐less toolboxes such as SpRY substantially reduce PAM‐related target‐site restrictions across plant genomes (Ren et al. 2021). Together, these developments establish a robust toolkit for rational allele engineering, although editing efficiency and locus‐specific chromatin dynamics remain active constraints.

Multiplex genome editing further extends ideotype design by permitting simultaneous modification of multiple loci within complex trait architectures. Because complex domestication traits emerge from interconnected pathways, multi‐locus editing can enable coordinated modification of yield, architecture, disease resistance and abiotic stress responses (Luo et al. 2026; Qin et al. 2026). High‐order multiplex demonstrations confirm that up to a dozen loci can be targeted simultaneously in a single step, including the generation of octuple and duodecuple mutants in plant systems (Stuttmann et al. 2021). Furthermore, integrating PAM‐flexible multiplex editors with optimised tRNA‐processing arrays and TREX2–SpCas9 fusions improves multi‐locus editing efficiency and expands target scope (Tan et al. 2025; Xu, Qiu, et al. 2026). These advances establish multiplex editing as a powerful approach for stacked trait engineering and ideotype assembly.

Nevertheless, increasing molecular precision does not guarantee biological predictability. Epistasis, pleiotropy, genetic background effects and genotype × environment interactions can substantially influence the final expressivity of engineered alleles, particularly when modifying polygenic architectures. Consequently, the progression from single‐gene knockouts to regulatory, prime and multiplex editing expands the accessible genetic design space rather than eliminating phenotypic uncertainty. Successful crop redesign ultimately requires the rapid generation, fixation and phenotypic evaluation of engineered alleles within elite breeding material—an operational bottleneck that necessitates the accelerated breeding platforms detailed in the following section.

Acceleration Platforms: Compressing Domestication Timelines and Stabilising Complex Traits

The generation of engineered alleles represents only an intermediate milestone in programmable domestication; operational success requires rapid allele fixation, multi‐environment phenotypic validation and seamless pipeline integration. Consequently, programmable domestication relies on acceleration platforms that compress breeding cycles while maintaining selection accuracy (Gudi et al. 2022; Watson et al. 2018). Speed breeding shortens generation intervals through optimised photoperiod and environmental control, enabling multiple annual generations to expedite the advancement and selection of edited germplasm (Blinkov et al. 2025; Watson et al. 2018). However, rapid generation turnover alone cannot resolve the complex biological constraints of trait expression, necessitating integration with complementary breeding and phenotyping technologies (Blinkov et al. 2025; Gudi et al. 2022).

Rapid allele stabilisation is achieved via haploid induction and doubled haploid (DH) systems, which substantially shorten the timeline to homozygous breeding lines. Integrating genome editing with haploid induction enables direct allele modification of elite germplasm during induction (Kelliher et al. 2019), a system recently extended through haploid‐inducer‐mediated editing to accelerate maize variety development (Li, Fu, et al. 2025). These combined platforms bridge precision editing and commercial cultivar development by facilitating rapid genetic fixation, provided technical feasibility and system compatibility are maintained (Nurtaza et al. 2026).

Following stabilisation, high‐throughput phenotyping becomes essential for evaluating complex agronomic traits at population scale. For instance, unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)‐based hyperspectral imaging enables quantitative temporal estimation of maize grain yield and flowering kinetics under field conditions (Fan et al. 2022). To improve genotype‐to‐phenotype prediction across heterogeneous environments, enviromic data assembly directly enhances genomic prediction of yield plasticity (Costa‐Neto et al. 2021). Furthermore, integrating phenomics and enviromics with physiological temporal indices and multi‐environment machine learning approaches strengthens predictive selection under environmental heterogeneity (Adak et al. 2026; Li, Gao, et al. 2026; Li, Zhao, et al. 2026).

Artificial intelligence (AI) complements these phenotyping and breeding platforms by synthesising multi‐omics, phenotypic and environmental data streams into actionable breeding decisions. Multimodal deep‐learning architectures significantly enhance genomic prediction accuracy in wheat (Montesinos‐López et al. 2023), while AI‐driven disease prediction and intelligent platforms such as AutoGP support automated genomic selection and genotype prioritisation (Vitale et al. 2026; Wu, Luo, et al. 2025). Nevertheless, because predictive AI remains constrained by dataset quality, structure and environmental variance, it serves as a decision‐support accelerator rather than a replacement for field‐based biological evaluation (Wu, Guan, et al. 2025).

Collectively, speed breeding, haploid‐mediated allele fixation, high‐throughput phenomics and AI decision support establish an integrated framework that transforms programmable domestication from laboratory proof‐of‐concept into a scalable breeding engine. Although these platforms substantially compress breeding timelines, they operate within the constraints imposed by genotype × environment interactions and trait plasticity (Adak et al. 2026; Costa‐Neto et al. 2021; Li, Gao, et al. 2026; Li, Zhao, et al. 2026). Their full power is realised when synergised with genomic discovery and precision editing within an iterative system‐level design cycle, establishing the conceptual foundation for the integrative domestication design cycle detailed in the following section.

Integrative Domestication Design Cycle: From Discovery to Deployment

Programmable domestication is no longer defined by isolated technological platforms such as pangenomics, genome editing, phenomics or artificial intelligence but by their coordinated integration within a system‐level design cycle. Although each technology provides distinct capabilities, successful crop redesign depends on the continuous interaction of genomic discovery, functional validation, engineering, acceleration and deployment rather than the performance of any single component (Patron 2020; Patron et al. 2015). Consequently, programmable domestication has evolved from a technology‐driven paradigm into an evidence‐driven design cycle that continuously refines biological knowledge, engineering decisions and breeding outcomes.

Within this cycle, programmable domestication follows a discovery‐to‐deployment continuum. Pangenomic analyses expand access to structural variation, regulatory diversity and adaptive alleles, while functional and multi‐omic analyses progressively connect genomic variation with biological function and regulatory architecture (He et al. 2025; Kundu and Tanti 2026; Mahmood et al. 2022). Functionally prioritised targets are engineered through quantitative regulatory or structural genome editing and advanced through acceleration platforms for rapid genetic fixation, phenotypic evaluation and breeding deployment (Thabet et al. 2026). Progress therefore relies on the systematic refinement of biological evidence across the workflow rather than the independent throughput of individual tools.

This iterative workflow directly operationalises the Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) cycle in crop systems (Patron 2020). The Design phase integrates pangenomic and functional multi‐omics data to prioritise agronomic targets; Build executes targeted genome modifications, multi‐capacity gene stacking and multiplex editing; Test evaluates engineered lines through automated phenotyping and agronomic trials; and Learn feeds experimental data back into predictive models to refine subsequent design cycles (Gao, Zhu, et al. 2026; Patron 2020; Patron et al. 2015). Landmark de novo domestication demonstrations in tomato, groundcherry and rice confirm that combining multiple validated edits can rapidly reconstruct complex domestication phenotypes in wild or orphan germplasm (Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018; Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2021). Rather than a linear pipeline, programmable domestication functions as a closed‐loop decision process in which evidence generated at each stage directly informs subsequent iterations of crop redesign.

The predictive power of this cycle depends on integrating biological information across multiple organisational scales. Linking genomic variation with chromatin dynamics, regulatory networks, phenomic metrics and environmental indices strengthens target prioritisation and genotype‐to‐phenotype prediction (Gao, Ding, et al. 2026; Kundu and Tanti 2026; Mahmood et al. 2022). Furthermore, combining phenomic data with multimodal deep‐learning architecture improves cross‐environment prediction of complex yield components (Kaushal et al. 2024; Montesinos‐López et al. 2026). Automated phenotyping platforms such as EcoBOT extend this capacity by enabling high‐throughput, high‐resolution evaluation under biologically realistic conditions (Andeer et al. 2025). These multi‐layered data streams therefore support increasingly data‐driven and predictive selection.

Ultimately, successful programmable domestication depends on translating engineered genetic variants into stable field performance. Directional multiplex editing of agronomic traits in salt‐tolerant rice highlights the feasibility of translating coordinated genetic modifications into field‐level improvement (Hao et al. 2025). However, as engineering capacity expands, the principal bottleneck shifts from technical execution to biological complexity. Pleiotropy, epistasis, quantitative trait architectures and genotype × environment interactions continue to govern final phenotypic expressivity (Hao et al. 2025; Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Maintaining an iterative design cycle therefore allows field‐derived phenotypic and environmental feedback to refine target selection and subsequent engineering decisions.

In conclusion, programmable domestication establishes an integrated, evidence‐driven cycle uniting genomic discovery, functional validation, precision editing, accelerated breeding, predictive phenotyping and multi‐environment deployment. By coupling these stages through an iterative Design–Build–Test–Learn architecture, this paradigm connects biological discovery with engineering and breeding decisions while retaining feedback from phenotypic evaluation. This integrated cycle provides the basis for examining the proof‐of‐concept studies and emerging principles that have shaped de novo domestication in the following section.

Proof‐of‐Concept Studies: Emerging Principles of De Novo Domestication

Proof‐of‐concept studies demonstrate that de novo domestication is a reproducible, strategy‐driven process applicable across diverse plant taxa rather than a species‐specific phenomenon. Targeted modification of conserved domestication‐associated loci enables the rapid conversion of wild germplasm into agronomically viable forms, establishing recurring biological and engineering principles across independent plant systems (Gasparini et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023). While the convergence of these outcomes supports the broad applicability of genome editing‐mediated domestication, system‐specific biological recalcitrance and technical delivery constraints remain critical parameters. Conserved Domestication Loci as Universal Editing Targets

A recurring strategy across proof‐of‐concept studies is the targeted modification of conserved or orthologous domestication‐associated loci. In diploid model Solanaceae systems, multiplex CRISPR–Cas9 editing of wild tomato ( Solanum pimpinellifolium ) targeted loci controlling plant architecture, flowering time and fruit development (SP, SP5G, CLV3), generating coordinated domestication‐associated phenotypes within a wild genetic background (Li et al. 2018; Zsögön et al. 2018). These independent demonstrations support the capacity of a limited set of conserved loci to induce substantial phenotypic shifts, providing evidence for conserved domestication‐associated genetic pathways within closely related diploid taxa. This strategy extends to orphan and underutilised crop systems, where editing orthologous domestication loci in groundcherry ( Physalis pruinosa ) improved plant compactness, flowering synchrony and fruit characteristics (Lemmon et al. 2018). The convergence between tomato and groundcherry provides evidence that orthologous domestication‐associated targets retain conserved regulatory functions across distinct Solanaceae genera, while highlighting that target selection must remain aligned with species‐specific trait architectures.

In monocot cereal systems, conserved domestication‐associated loci similarly offer tractable entry points for modifying primary agronomic traits. In rice ( Oryza sativa ), stepwise editing of loci controlling seed shattering and panicle architecture demonstrated that domestication syndromes can be systematically reconstructed in wild rice backgrounds (Ishikawa et al. 2022). Furthermore, genomic analysis of wild North American Oryzeae resolved the developmental signatures underlying seed‐shattering loci during early domestication transitions, expanding the comparative basis for target identification in cereal systems (Millas et al. 2026). These studies provide evidence for the recurrence of conserved genetic mechanisms across monocot lineages, while emphasising that phenotypic expressivity remains dependent on developmental and genomic context.

Conversely, polyploid cereal systems illustrate the regulatory complexity of classical domestication loci across multi‐genome backgrounds. In hexaploid bread wheat ( Triticum aestivum ), the major domestication gene Q interacts with TaARF12 to modulate plant architecture through pleiotropic hormonal signalling, demonstrating that classical domestication targets govern complex multi‐trait networks (Liu, Sun, et al. 2025). Similarly, functional analysis of Tenacious glume1 (Tg1) revealed pleiotropic effects on glume tenacity and spike morphology, providing mechanistic insights into polyploid wheat evolution (Dvorak et al. 2026). Importantly, such mechanistic studies provide crucial target‐validation evidence but must be distinguished from direct de novo domestication demonstrations, as editing isolated loci in established polyploid cultivars does not inherently reconstruct a complete domestication syndrome.

Across these four plant‐system categories, current literature supports the view that conserved or orthologous domestication loci serve as high‐priority targets, though their functional equivalence is constrained by ploidy, genetic background and evolutionary distance. Direct de novo domestication demonstrations in tomato, groundcherry and rice provide evidence that targeting these loci can rapidly produce crop‐like phenotypes, whereas mechanistic studies in polyploid cereals illuminate the epistatic and pleiotropic interactions governing their expression (Dvorak et al. 2026; Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018; Li et al. 2018; Liu, Sun, et al. 2025; Zsögön et al. 2018). Consequently, conserved loci establish a rational foundation for target selection, though their predictive utility relies on the broader genetic network. Reconstructing these multi‐trait phenotypes across complex backgrounds requires expanding from single‐locus edits to the coordinated modification of interconnected gene networks, as detailed through multiplex editing in the following section.

Multiplex Editing and Reconstruction of Domestication Syndromes

Proof‐of‐concept studies consistently indicate that domestication is fundamentally a multi‐trait process requiring coordinated modification of interconnected genetic pathways. In diploid domestication syndrome reconstruction, simultaneous editing of multiple domestication‐associated loci in wild tomato ( S. pimpinellifolium ) generated coordinated modifications in plant architecture, flowering time and fruit traits, demonstrating that multiplex genome editing can reconstruct major components of a domestication syndrome within a wild diploid background (Li et al. 2018; Zsögön et al. 2018). These independent demonstrations established that combining a limited set of strategically selected edits accelerates the assembly of complementary domestication phenotypes in parallel rather than through sequential trait modification. This principle extends to polyploid and multi‐genome re‐engineering, where multiplex editing must accommodate heightened genomic complexity. In wild allotetraploid rice ( Oryza alta ), simultaneous editing of multiple domestication‐associated loci enabled coordinated modifications in seed shattering, plant architecture and grain traits, demonstrating that multiplex genome editing can be applied despite polyploid genomic redundancy (Yu et al. 2021). This provides direct evidence that coordinated domestication extends beyond diploid models, though homeologous gene duplication and complex multi‐genome interactions necessitate precise target selection to ensure predictable phenotypic outcomes.

Expanding this approach, recent demonstrations in multi‐trait agronomic and resilience stacking illustrate how multiplex editing extends beyond classical domestication syndromes towards the simultaneous optimisation of modern production traits. In rice ( O. sativa ), multiplex editing has successfully combined directional agronomic improvements with stacked resilience against salinity, cold, insect herbivores and bacterial pathogens (Hao et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2026; Qin et al. 2026). These applications broaden the scope of multiplex editing from recreating historical domestication traits towards multi‐stress adaptive engineering; however, these stacking studies must be distinguished from direct de novo domestication demonstrations, as their primary goal is multi‐trait enhancement in established germplasm rather than de novo syndrome reconstruction from wild progenitor lines. The scalability of such multi‐locus engineering relies directly on enabling technical and reagent platforms that improve target accessibility and multi‐locus editing efficiency. PAM‐flexible multiplex genome editors have expanded the accessible target space for multi‐gene modification in rice, enabling precise single‐step stacking of complex physiological traits like cold tolerance (Tan et al. 2025). Concurrently, optimised tRNA‐processing architectures and TREX2–SpCas9 fusion systems have enhanced multi‐locus editing efficiency in plant systems (Xu, Qiu, et al. 2026). Although these technical platforms address constraints on multiplex editing, higher editing efficiency alone does not overcome the biological complexity of epistatic or pleiotropic interactions among co‐edited loci.

Collectively, evidence across diploid domestication syndrome reconstruction, polyploid re‐engineering, multi‐trait agronomic stacking and enabling reagent platforms demonstrate that multiplex editing can enable more rapid combination of complex multi‐locus traits. Nevertheless, expanding the number of targeted loci introduces potential constraints, including incomplete edits and unforeseen gene regulatory network interactions. Thus, multiplex editing offers an effective mechanism to coordinate multi‐trait transformation, but its ultimate success remains governed by target selection and biological validation. The resulting capacity to drive rapid phenotypic convergence from wild germplasm towards cultivated ideotypes sets the stage for evaluating cross‐system trait stability in the following section.

Rapid Phenotypic Convergence From Wild to Crop Ideotypes

A defining feature emerging across proof‐of‐concept studies is the rapid phenotypic convergence of wild progenitors towards crop‐like ideotypes following targeted genome editing. In primary architectural and reproductive traits, independent studies in wild tomato ( S. pimpinellifolium ) demonstrated coordinated shifts in plant architecture and fruit morphology following multiplex modification of domestication‐associated loci, providing reproducible evidence that targeted editing can rapidly redirect wild phenotypes towards domesticated states (Li et al. 2018; Zsögön et al. 2018). Parallel convergence occurs in groundcherry ( P. pruinosa ), where editing improved compact growth, flowering synchrony and fruit size, while stepwise editing in wild rice ( O. sativa ) systematically reduced seed shattering and altered panicle architecture (Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018). Furthermore, pangenomic and structural variation (SV) mapping across diverse taxa—including broomcorn millet, cucumber, peanut and mung bean—identifies structural variation associated with agronomic and domestication‐related traits across evolutionary lineages (Chen et al. 2026; Chen et al. 2023; Li, Wang, et al. 2022; Zhao, Xue, et al. 2025). Beyond primary morphological features, phenotypic convergence encompasses metabolic and quality trait modulations. Pangenomic analyses in potato, tea and tomato demonstrate that structural, sequence and regulatory variants are associated with variation in secondary metabolic pathways, tuber development and fruit nutritional profiles (Tao et al. 2025; Zhao, Wang, et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2025). Crucially, associations among structural variation, transcriptomic changes and agronomic diversification indicate that phenotypic convergence is not mediated solely by coding‐sequence mutations, but can also involve structural variations affecting gene dosage and cis‐regulatory networks (Tao et al. 2025). These pangenomic insights strengthen the biological foundation for targeting non‐coding and regulatory elements during metabolic re‐engineering, though they reflect natural evolutionary variation rather than direct editing‐mediated trait convergence.

A pivotal consideration during rapid ideotype transition is whether agronomically desirable phenotypes compromise stress resilience and fitness trade‐offs. Wild crop relatives harbour extensive genetic variation supporting adaptation to environmental extremes, including cold, drought, waterlogging and virulent pathogens (Guo et al. 2025; Yang, Wang, et al. 2025; Zhao, Wang, et al. 2026). Structural and copy‐number variations (CNVs) across wild tomato species ( S. habrochaites ) are associated with cold tolerance and ecological adaptation, highlighting the potential value of retaining adaptive variation during target selection (Tao et al. 2026; Wei et al. 2025). Pangenomic evaluations in maize and grapevine similarly uncover structural alleles associated with drought resilience and downy‐mildew resistance, while haplotype‐resolved analyses in hexaploid Actinidia valvata reveal genomic complexity underlying waterlogging tolerance (Guo et al. 2025; Yang, Wang, et al. 2025). Consequently, rapid morphological convergence towards high‐yielding ideotypes must be considered alongside the retention of adaptive resilience to avoid potential fitness penalties.

The predictability and expressivity of convergence become markedly more complex when evaluating pleiotropic and background‐dependent effects in polyploid and complex genomes. De novo domestication of wild allotetraploid rice ( O. alta ) demonstrates that simultaneous multiplex editing can induce coordinated domestication phenotypes across multi‐genome backgrounds, while highlighting the additional complexity associated with homeologous gene copies (Yu et al. 2021). Comparative evidence indicates that natural alleles can exhibit variable effects across divergent genetic backgrounds, as demonstrated by population‐specific CNVs associated with ecological adaptation in wild tomato and conserved versus population‐specific regulatory variation underlying nutritional traits and domestication in tomato (Wei et al. 2025; Zhao, Wang, et al. 2026). Importantly, several edited lines retain key eco‐physiological adaptations from their wild progenitors while expressing domesticated traits, indicating that selected domestication traits can be modified while retaining aspects of the wild genetic background (Lemmon et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021). Nevertheless, long‐term phenotypic stability ultimately depends on dynamic interactions between engineered alleles, genetic background and environmental conditions (Wei et al. 2025; Yang, Wang, et al. 2025). Evaluating how these convergent ideotypes maintain stability across multi‐environment field trials highlights systemic bottlenecks, which are examined in the following section.

Translational Potential and System‐Level Constraints

Collectively, proof‐of‐concept studies demonstrate that de novo domestication has considerable translational potential across diverse crop systems, yet its widespread agricultural implementation remains constrained by distinct biological and technical bottlenecks. The repeated ability to engineer quantitative agronomic traits through genome editing demonstrates that domestication‐associated improvement can extend beyond binary trait modification, although fine‐tuning quantitative phenotypes requires precise control over allelic expressivity and regulatory context (Jin and Ding 2025; Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Consequently, achieving technical editing precision does not automatically guarantee predictable improvement in complex yield and adaptation traits. A primary technical bottleneck involves transformation, regeneration and delivery accessibility, particularly in wild crop relatives where recalcitrance and low regeneration efficiency restrict plant recovery (Gasparini et al. 2024). Overcoming these tissue‐culture barriers, developmental regulators such as GRF–GIF chimeric proteins can enhance regeneration efficiency across plant systems (Debernardi et al. 2020). Recent applications in tomato further demonstrate that GRF–GIF chimeras improve leaf‐based regeneration and transformation efficiency, directly enhancing the technical feasibility of editing recalcitrant germplasm (Lee et al. 2026; Swinnen et al. 2025). While these approaches mitigate plant recovery bottlenecks, they do not resolve higher‐order genetic constraints governing quantitative trait expression.

A second systemic challenge centres on epistatic interactions and quantitative trait fine‐tuning. Targeted editing of cis‐regulatory promoter regions generates continuous quantitative variation rather than binary null alleles, underscoring the necessity of tuning promoter strength to avoid pleiotropic fitness penalties (Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Furthermore, multiplex editing of yield‐related loci in rice demonstrates the capacity to coordinate multiple agronomic components within a single genotype (Jin and Ding 2025). Thus, expanding multi‐locus engineering capacity does not inherently yield deterministic phenotypic outcomes, as genetic interactions and regulatory context can influence overall trait expression.

A third critical frontier involves field stability and phenomic prediction. Evaluating whether edited ideotypes maintain performance under fluctuating field conditions requires advanced predictive modelling. Integrating high‐throughput phenotyping with deep learning enhances genomic prediction accuracy, while temporal phenomic and environmental indices support physiology‐informed yield prediction across diverse environments (Adak et al. 2026; Kaushal et al. 2024). Similarly, multimodal deep‐learning networks improve cross‐environment prediction of complex yield components and plasticity‐aware machine‐learning approaches incorporate environmental variation into genomic prediction across multi‐environment conditions (Li, Zhao, et al. 2026; Montesinos‐López et al. 2026). These predictive tools strengthen the capacity to evaluate environmental plasticity, emphasising that environmental variation remains a governing factor in real‐world trait stability.

Ultimately, transitioning from laboratory proofs‐of‐concept to agricultural deployment relies on a connected pipeline: efficient plant regeneration, precise quantitative trait tuning and multi‐environment performance modelling (Kaushal et al. 2024; Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Breakthroughs in developmental regulators and machine‐learning prediction can reduce translational barriers, yet systemic biological constraints continue to influence phenotypic stability beyond controlled environments. Addressing these multi‐tiered constraints sets the stage for a comprehensive synthesis of de novo domestication strategies in the following section.

Synthesis: Towards a Predictive Approach to Crop Domestication

Taking together, current proof‐of‐concept studies reveal several recurring principles underlying de novo domestication: the strategic targeting of domestication‐associated loci, coordinated modification of multiple traits and rapid generation of agronomically relevant phenotypes from wild genetic backgrounds. In cross‐system de novo domestication, direct demonstrations in groundcherry ( P. pruinosa ), rice ( O. sativa ) and wild allotetraploid rice ( O. alta ) support the applicability of these principles across distinct taxa and genomic architectures, although specific trait combinations remain system‐dependent (Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021). Rather than representing isolated experimental outcomes, these studies collectively indicate that domestication can increasingly be approached as a programmable and design‐oriented biological process guided by conserved genetic and phenotypic principles. Simultaneously, complex‐genome and multi‐trait engineering is expanding the scope of crop redesign from the reconstruction of classical domestication traits towards multi‐locus polyploid modification and contemporary trait enhancement. Multiplex editing in wild allotetraploid rice demonstrates coordinated domestication‐related modification in a polyploid background despite homeologous gene duplication (Yu et al. 2021). Furthermore, recent multiplex engineering in salt‐tolerant rice backgrounds shows that agronomic performance and abiotic stress resilience can be edited concurrently within a single background, extending genome engineering beyond historical domestication traits towards coordinated contemporary crop improvement (Hao et al. 2025).

Achieving predictable outcomes across these complex systems depends fundamentally on quantitative tuning and engineering capacity. Targeted cis‐regulatory promoter editing demonstrates that agronomic phenotypes can be tuned along a continuous functional gradient rather than through binary knockouts, underscoring the necessity of controlling trait expression levels (Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Complementing these editing strategies, high‐capacity gene‐stacking technologies such as DASH expand synthetic biology architectures, providing the technical capacity to assemble and deliver multiple genetic components for complex trait engineering (Zhao, Stepanova, et al. 2025). However, these technological advances also reinforce that de novo domestication cannot yet be reduced to a completely deterministic engineering process. The reproducibility of targeted phenotypic shifts across Solanaceae, orphan crops, cereals and polyploid systems supports the utility of conserved targets, yet differences in genetic background, genomic complexity, quantitative trait architecture and environmental response continue to constrain phenotypic uniformity (Ishikawa et al. 2022; Lemmon et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2021). Expanding the number of target loci or synthetic components enlarges the engineering design space, but it does not eliminate the inherent uncertainty of genotype‐to‐phenotype relationships.

In conclusion, Section 3 demonstrates that de novo domestication is rapidly evolving from isolated proofs‐of‐concept into a flexible approach for converting wild genetic diversity into agronomically relevant crop ideotypes. Transitioning this paradigm into broader agricultural deployment relies on integrating precise target selection, cis‐regulatory trait tuning, multiplex polyploid engineering and multi‐environment phenotypic validation (Hao et al. 2025; Patron et al. 2015; Zhao, Xue, et al. 2025). These synthesised principles establish the biological and technical foundation for moving beyond historical case studies towards the specific trait architectures and agronomic priorities examined in Section 4.

Trait Modules and Genetic Targets in De Novo Domestication

Building on the recurring principles identified across proof‐of‐concept studies, de novo domestication can be organised into distinct trait modules defined by their underlying genetic architectures and regulatory complexity. These modules encompass harvestability, developmental regulation, yield optimisation, nutritional and quality traits and stress resilience, each requiring an engineering strategy matched to its specific biological organisation. Major‐effect loci provide readily actionable targets for discrete domestication traits, whereas complex quantitative, metabolic and adaptive traits increasingly require cis‐regulatory fine‐tuning, pathway‐level engineering and coordinated modification of interacting genetic components. The following sections examine these trait modules and their principal genetic targets, emphasising how differences in genetic architecture influence the choice, precision and predictability of genome‐editing strategies.

Loss‐of‐Function Targets: Seed Shattering and Harvestability

Reduction of seed shattering represents a primary domestication event that directly improves harvest efficiency by disrupting abscission‐layer specification, cell‐wall modification and lignification. In rice, loss‐of‐function mutations in SH4 and qSH1 prevent abscission‐layer differentiation to confer non‐shattering phenotypes (Konishi et al. 2006; Li et al. 2006), while parallel selection at Sh1 across sorghum, maize and rice illustrates convergent genomic targets in cereal domestication (Lin et al. 2012). Recent comparative transcriptomics across independently evolved weedy rice lineages demonstrate that while phenotypic convergence in seed retention is recurring, the underlying regulatory networks exhibit significant lineage‐specific divergence (Li and Caicedo 2025).

Mechanistically, harvestability is governed by an integrated developmental network rather than isolated loci. Digenic interaction between SH4 and qSH3 stabilises non‐shattering traits (Inoue et al. 2015), whereas the KNOX transcription factor OSH15 represses lignin biosynthesis genes within the abscission zone to facilitate grain detachment (Yoon et al. 2017). This regulatory architecture is further expanded by the BELL–KNOX qSH1–OSH71 module, which directly activates the xyloglucanase OsXTH12 to drive abscission‐zone separation (Wu, Yang, et al. 2025) and by SHATTERING ABORTION3 (SHA3), which promotes cell separation within the abscission layer (Lv et al. 2025). Furthermore, the identification of novel loci modulating seed shattering in aus rice ( O. sativa Kasalath) underscores the influence of background‐specific genetic modifiers on trait expression (Shionari et al. 2025).

Targeted genome editing validates the functional utility of these loss‐of‐function targets for de novo domestication. CRISPR‐Cas9 multiplex editing of SH4 and qSH1 successfully re‐engineers non‐shattering phenotypes in weedy rice (Zhang et al. 2022). Conversely, novel candidates such as OsZIPH1, identified via bulk segregant analysis, require extensive functional validation before being prioritised as established engineering targets (Han et al. 2025). This distinction is critical, as expanding locus catalogues must be screened for functional reproducibility and minimal pleiotropic trade‐offs.

Despite shared evolutionary outcomes, the genetic architecture of seed shattering is not universally conserved across taxa. Genomic analysis in wild North American Oryzeae reveals lineage‐specific developmental programmes governing grain retention (Millas et al. 2026), a structural variability mirrored by multi‐locus shattering control in forage species like Italian ryegrass ( Lolium multiflorum ) (Tamura et al. 2025). Consequently, engineering reduced seed dispersal necessitates species and background‐specific target validation rather than direct ortholog transfer. This shift from discrete loss‐of‐function loci towards context dependent genetic networks establishes a logical transition to multi‐genic regulatory pathways governing flowering time and plant architecture.

Regulatory and Developmental Targets: Flowering Time and Plant Architecture

Unlike harvestability, developmental domestication relies on coordinated regulatory networks integrating flowering, meristem identity and plant architecture rather than isolated major‐effect loci. These pathways integrate environmental signals with endogenous developmental programmes, rendering them powerful engineering targets while increasing the potential for pleiotropic and species‐specific responses. Recent evidence reinforces this network‐level organisation by establishing direct linkages between photoperiodic flowering, environmental adaptation and developmental architecture (Xu et al. 2025; Yoshida et al. 2025).

Flowering‐time regulation exemplifies this interconnected architecture. In rice, the photoperiod regulator Hd1 operates within the multimeric OsHAPL1–DTH8–Hd1 transcriptional complex to repress heading date (Yano et al. 2000; Zhu et al. 2017). Photoperiodic sensitivity is continuously fine‐tuned by upstream and parallel pathways: OsFKF1 promotes short‐day flowering by mediating the degradation of the repressor OsPRR37 (Yoon et al. 2026), while OsFTL1 integrates circadian and photoperiodic signals to drive ecological adaptation (Ling Liu et al. 2026). Furthermore, allelic variation at Ehd2 simultaneously modulates heading date and salt tolerance (Xu et al. 2025), while nutrient signalling intersects with photoperiodic regulators to govern nitrogen‐dependent flowering delays (Yoshida et al. 2025). In tomato, regulatory mutations in SELF PRUNING 5G (SP5G) alter photoperiod responsiveness to confer day‐neutrality and accelerated harvest (Soyk et al. 2017). Collectively, these findings support the view that adaptation can arise from the rewiring of conserved transcriptional circuits rather than simple loss‐of‐function events.

Plant architecture is governed by analogous regulatory modules controlling meristem behaviour and shoot growth. In tomato, SELF PRUNING (SP) and the florigen ortholog SINGLE‐FLOWER TRUSS (SFT) coordinate sympodial meristem switching, shoot termination and vegetative‐to‐reproductive transitions (Gao, Ding, et al. 2026; Pnueli et al. 1998; Shalit et al. 2009), with overlapping AP1/FUL‐like MADS‐box genes further defining reproductive meristem specification (Jiang et al. 2025). In rice, OsSHI1 represses IPA1 activity to balance tiller outgrowth, panicle branching and plant height (Duan et al. 2019), whereas the DTH8–SD1 regulatory pair directly couples heading date control with panicle structure and stature (Su et al. 2026).

Targeted genome editing has enabled the precise manipulation of these developmental networks for de novo domestication. Simultaneous multiplex editing of major regulatory loci successfully re‐engineered domesticated panicle morphology and non‐shattering traits in wild rice relatives (Ishikawa et al. 2022). Similarly, fine‐tuning architectural regulators in tomato optimises canopy structure, harvesting efficiency and resource allocation for high‐density production systems (Curtin et al. 2022; Lobato‐Gómez et al. 2026). However, because these central regulators exhibit extensive pleiotropy, binary gene disruption can induce undesirable trade‐offs across interconnected traits. Mitigating these pleiotropic constraints requires a comprehensive pathway‐level understanding to guide quantitative trait engineering, where fine‐tuned expression and phenotypic variation supersede simple loss‐of‐function knockouts.

Dosage‐Dependent and Quantitative Traits: Yield Optimisation

Unlike developmental traits, yield optimisation relies on precise modulation of gene activity rather than complete gene disruption because quantitative agronomic traits are governed by multilocus architectures involving gene dosage, cis‐regulatory variation and epistatic interactions. This principle is illustrated by CRISPR‐mediated promoter engineering, where cis‐regulatory editing generated allelic series with graded transcriptional outputs and corresponding phenotypic variation, establishing regulatory fine‐tuning as an effective strategy for optimising complex traits (Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Similarly, promoter editing of maize CLE genes improved grain‐yield‐related traits by modulating endogenous gene expression while preserving gene function (Liu et al. 2021). Recent evidence further indicates that regulatory variation can influence grain development through distinct molecular mechanisms: redox regulation of G‐protein signalling affects grain size in rice (Liu, Hao, et al. 2025), while natural variation in OsMADS1 transcript splicing alters grain thickness and quality through effects on monosaccharide loading to the endosperm (Liu, Zhao, et al. 2025). Together, these findings reinforce the importance of quantitative regulatory control rather than binary gene disruption for yield‐associated traits.

Successful quantitative engineering depends on understanding the underlying genetic architecture. GS3 was identified as a major regulator of natural grain‐size variation in rice (Mao et al. 2010), while pyramiding GN1a, GS3 and IPA1 produced complementary and additive effects on rice yield, demonstrating that productivity results from coordinated optimisation of multiple loci rather than individual genes (Li, Pan, et al. 2022). However, dose‐dependent epistasis and nonlinear genetic interactions can cause phenotypic outcomes to vary across allelic combinations, emphasising that increasing or combining target effects does not necessarily produce predictable quantitative gains (Aguirre et al. 2023). Thus, the genetic architecture underlying yield requires not only identification of high‐value loci but also consideration of their dosage and interactions within the broader genetic background.

Quantitative improvement also extends to physiological pathways. Manipulation of GWD1 enhanced both yield and grain quality by altering carbohydrate metabolism (Wang et al. 2021), while genome editing of cytokinin oxidase activity further demonstrates the potential of dosage‐based optimisation for rice yield improvement (Solanki et al. 2026). These studies extend quantitative engineering beyond structural determinants of grain size towards physiological processes that influence yield and quality simultaneously. Together, they show that quantitative domestication depends on fine‐tuning regulatory states across interconnected genetic and physiological pathways rather than maximising or eliminating individual gene functions, providing a seamless bridge to metabolic pathway engineering for enhancing crop quality, secondary metabolites and nutritional composition.

Regulatory Networks and Metabolic Pathway Engineering for Nutritional and Quality Traits

Engineering nutritional and quality traits requires moving beyond single‐gene modification to the coordinated manipulation of metabolic pathways, as these phenotypes arise from interconnected biosynthetic and regulatory networks. Accordingly, pathway‐level engineering that integrates structural genes with upstream regulatory modules has become central to crop quality improvement, where the context‐dependent activity of transcriptional regulators influences metabolic outputs (Zhang, Wu, et al. 2026). Genome‐editing studies demonstrate that metabolic pathways can be rationally modified to enhance crop quality. In soybean, CRISPR‐mediated editing of FAD2, GmFATB1 and SEED FATTY ACID REDUCER genes successfully altered fatty‐acid composition and increased total seed oil content, illustrating how targeted modification of distinct nodes within lipid metabolism can redirect seed quality traits (Haun et al. 2014; Liao et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2021). Likewise, disruption of GBSS produced amylose‐free potato lines with modified starch architecture, demonstrating that precise editing of core biosynthetic enzymes can alter industrial and culinary processing quality (Toinga‐Villafuerte et al. 2022). These studies collectively establish that quality improvement relies on targeted manipulation of integrated pathway components rather than simple, isolated phenotypic determinants.

Upstream regulatory factors provide complementary engineering targets by simultaneously coordinating multiple downstream metabolic processes. For example, CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated validation of SNAC9 demonstrated transcriptional regulation of carotenoid metabolism during tomato fruit ripening, while targeted disruption of FtMYB45 promoted flavonoid biosynthesis in Tartary buckwheat (Feng et al. 2023; Wen et al. 2022). Incorporating these regulatory modules expands the scope of metabolic engineering beyond individual structural enzymes, allowing broader pathway control (Zhang, Sun, et al. 2026). However, metabolic engineering remains constrained by extensive crosstalk among lipid, carbohydrate, carotenoid and phenylpropanoid pathways, rendering phenotypic outcomes context dependent and limiting the predictability of single‐gene interventions (Di et al. 2026; Zhang, Sun, et al. 2026). Consequently, optimising nutritional and quality traits requires evaluating these interconnected metabolic and regulatory dynamics in parallel with precise genome editing, creating a logical bridge towards integrated regulatory networks that govern environmental adaptation and stress resilience.

Adaptive Domestication Targets: Engineering Stress Resilience Through Regulatory Networks

Stress resilience is among the most complex domestication modules because adaptive performance arises from interconnected regulatory networks integrating developmental, hormonal and environmental signalling rather than discrete loci. Consequently, engineering stress tolerance focuses on central regulatory hubs that coordinate multiple downstream pathways rather than individual stress‐responsive effector genes. Functional studies demonstrate that transcription factor‐mediated networks underpin this adaptive capacity across abiotic constraints. In rice, manipulating NAC, DREB, bZIP and bHLH regulators enhances drought, salinity and temperature tolerance by coordinating hormone signalling, ion homeostasis, oxidative stress defences and developmental programmes (Alam et al. 2022; Chang et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2018). Specifically, CRISPR‐mediated knockout of OsNAC092 and OsbHLH024 improved drought and salt resistance, respectively (Alam et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022). This regulatory model is reinforced by the functional characterisation of OsNAC25, which enhances drought tolerance and reduces oxidative damage (Yang, Luo, et al. 2025), while the OsbZIP46–SAPK6 signalling module couples phosphorylation‐dependent activation with combined drought and thermal stress tolerance (Chang et al. 2017). A similar paradigm shift towards targeted components is evident in biotic stress engineering. Multiplex CRISPR editing of susceptibility loci generated tomato lines with concurrent resistance to tomato yellow leaf curl virus and powdery mildew, demonstrating how simultaneous modification of susceptibility factors can provide dual pathogen protection (Pramanik et al. 2021). Complementing endogenous gene editing, synthetic immune receptor engineering such as NLR immune receptor–nanobody fusions enables programmable pathogen recognition and customised disease resistance (Kourelis et al. 2023). Together, these approaches extend stress‐resilience engineering from individual defence gene modification towards targeted manipulation of endogenous susceptibility factors and synthetic immune components capable of directing pathogen‐specific immune responses.

Despite these advances, engineering adaptive resilience remains constrained by network‐level complexity. Because central regulatory hubs govern multifaceted physiological and developmental processes, phenotypic outcomes are highly context dependent and prone to pleiotropic trade‐offs across variable environments (Chang et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2018). Therefore, adaptive domestication requires multi‐environment evaluation and system‐level optimisation rather than deterministic engineering of individual stress‐associated genes. This operational transition from single‐trait modification towards network‐level multi‐trait engineering provides the foundation for synthesising integrated crop design strategies in Section 5.

Synthesis: Genetic Logic of Domestication Trait Modules

A unifying principle of modern domestication research is that engineering strategies must be guided by genetic architecture rather than phenotype alone. Trait modules differ fundamentally in regulatory complexity, ranging from major‐effect developmental loci to highly interconnected quantitative, metabolic and adaptive networks (Curtin et al. 2022; Marand et al. 2023). Consequently, successful programmable domestication depends on aligning genome‐editing modalities with the underlying biological organisation of the target trait. While harvestability traits can often be engineered through validated major‐effect loci, fine‐tuning developmental, quantitative, metabolic and adaptive traits requires pathway‐level optimisation and precise regulatory tuning (Curtin et al. 2022; Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Targeted modification of cis‐regulatory elements (CREs) provides a mechanism for modulating spatiotemporal gene expression and influencing developmental and adaptive phenotypes (Marand et al. 2023). Promoter editing can generate continuous promoter allele series that produce quantitative phenotypic variation, shifting crop engineering from binary knockouts towards subtle transcriptional dosage control (Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). However, as regulatory complexity increases, phenotypic outcomes are governed by non‐linear dosage effects and idiosyncratic epistasis among promoter alleles, which can reduce the predictability of single‐gene interventions (Aguirre et al. 2023).

Importantly, domestication trait modules do not function as isolated engineering targets. Developmental regulation influences yield, metabolic pathways interact with growth and stress adaptation is integrated with developmental and physiological processes. These interconnected biological constraints demonstrate that crop redesign relies on shared regulatory architecture rather than discrete trait classifications, highlighting the necessity of accounting for genetic context when stacking multiple traits (Curtin et al. 2022; Marand et al. 2023). Collectively, this synthesis establishes a clear paradigm shift from gene‐centric interventions towards architecture‐informed engineering: major‐effect loci remain effective for tractable domestication traits, whereas complex quantitative and adaptive phenotypes mandate precise cis‐regulatory tuning, pathway‐level optimisation and epistatic evaluation (Aguirre et al. 2023; Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017). Aligning trait‐specific genetic principles with targeted editing capabilities provides the foundation for integrating these individual regulatory modules into system‐level strategies for multi‐trait crop design in Section 5.

A Trajectory Framework for Programmable Domestication

Building on the trait‐specific principles synthesised in Section 4, programmable domestication requires an organising approach that connects diverse crop‐engineering strategies with their biological starting contexts, transformation complexity and translational objectives. In this section, we establish a trajectory‐based approach to organise these routes, define their operational characteristics and examine the biological, translational and regulatory constraints influencing their progression towards deployable crop systems.

Why a New Domestication Framework Is Needed

Recent advances in pangenomics, graph‐genome assemblies, multiplex CRISPR‐Cas genome editing and artificial intelligence‐assisted target prioritisation are transforming de novo domestication from a conceptual possibility into a technically feasible strategy for crop redesign (Fernie and Yan 2019; Wang et al. 2026; Yu et al. 2025). Graph‐based and super‐pangenomic analyses increasingly reveal structural variants, presence/absence variation and complex regulatory diversity that can inform the identification and prioritisation of trait‐associated variation across wild and cultivated germplasm (He et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2022). In parallel, genome‐editing studies demonstrate the feasibility of directly modifying domestication‐associated loci in diverse and previously underutilised germplasm (Gautam et al. 2026; Lemmon et al. 2018; Zsögön et al. 2018). Together, these advances establish substantial technical capacity for programmable domestication, while the methodological basis for organising and comparing cross‐species domestication trajectories remains less developed. The current literature encompasses fundamentally different biological starting materials, engineering strategies and breeding objectives, ranging from improvement of established crops to the domestication of orphan crops and wild germplasm (Fernie and Yan 2019; Gasparini et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023). These systems also differ in genetic complexity, including diploid and polyploid genomes, regulatory variation, structural diversity and pleiotropic constraints (Gautam et al. 2026; Hendelman et al. 2021). Consequently, no single editing strategy can be assumed to operate uniformly across biological contexts. The absence of a structured basis for comparing these differing engineering demands limits the ability to organise cross‐species domestication routes, evaluate their translational potential and identify context‐dependent constraints.

This heterogeneity also challenges simple distinctions between wild and domesticated germplasm. Crop evolution encompasses continuous genomic variation, including structural diversity and complex interactions between genetic backgrounds and ecological contexts (Abbo et al. 2014; Alam and Purugganan 2024; Liu et al. 2020; Pacheco‐Huh et al. 2021). Domestication‐associated phenotypes therefore emerge from genetic architectures that vary among species and backgrounds rather than from universally transferable genetic switches. A useful conceptual approach must consequently account for differences in starting germplasm, trait architecture and intended agricultural function rather than treating domestication as a uniform trajectory (Zhang et al. 2023).

To address this conceptual gap, we propose the Trajectory Framework for programmable domestication as summarised in Figure 1, which organises crop redesign according to distinct trajectories defined by the starting state of germplasm, target trait architecture and intended agricultural function. By integrating evidence from domestication studies, pangenomic analyses and genome‐editing applications, the framework provides a common organisational basis for comparing otherwise heterogeneous crop‐engineering routes. Trajectory Zones Framework for Programmable Domestication

To operationalise programmable crop engineering beyond species‐specific demonstrations, we propose a framework that organises crop redesign into three interconnected strategic domains: Zone A (Adaptive Rescue), Zone B (Agronomic Refinement) and Zone C (Novel Chassis). Rather than treating domestication as a static classification, these zones define distinct engineering contexts across elite cultivars, underutilised germplasm and wild species (Figure 1). Trajectory assignment is determined by initial germplasm state and intended objective, whereas intervention complexity is captured by Engineering Distance (D e ) a qualitative conceptual descriptor integrating genomic background, domestication status, ploidy and target complexity. Crucially, D e is not a quantitative score or a function of edit number alone. For this review, experimental maturity is evaluated independently using evidence/readiness Tiers as an operational classification, distinguishing conceptual feasibility (Tier 1), experimentally validated proof‐of‐concept (Tier 2) and field‐validated performance (Tier 3). Together, trajectory position, (D e ) and evidence/readiness represent complementary dimensions to evaluate heterogeneous domestication routes and benchmark translational potential.

Zone A (Adaptive Rescue: Restoration Vector, lower (De ))

Operates at the shortest engineering distance, targeting resilience‐oriented enhancement of established crops while preserving elite genetic backgrounds. The primary objective is resolving specific vulnerabilities without disrupting elite agronomic performance. Precision promoter and cis‐regulatory editing demonstrate the potential to modify targeted traits while revealing the importance of regulatory context and pleiotropic effects (Hendelman et al. 2021; Oliva et al. 2019). Promoter editing of OsSWEET14 confers broad‐spectrum bacterial blight resistance in rice (Oliva et al. 2019), while cis‐regulatory dissection of homeobox genes enables quantitative trait tuning while revealing conserved pleiotropic effects (Hendelman et al. 2021). Zone A interventions feature focused genetic modifications with lower (De), however, when physiological yield ceilings or background constraints limit further gain, crop engineering transitions to Zone B.

Zone B (Agronomic Refinement: Bridging Vector, intermediate (De ))

Centres on semi‐domesticated landraces, orphan crops and underutilised species possessing stress tolerance or nutritional value but incomplete domestication syndromes. The objective is to overcome harvestability, architectural or reproductive bottlenecks through multi‐trait editing. Multiplex CRISPR editing provides empirical evidence for bridging strategies across underutilised lineages (Gautam et al. 2026; Lemmon et al. 2018; Liu, Sun, et al. 2025). Targeted editing in groundcherry ( P. pruinosa ) rapidly improves growth habit and fruit traits (Lemmon et al. 2018), whereas trait stacking in pennycress ( Thlaspi arvense ) combines pod indehiscence, reduced glucosinolates and synchronised flowering to address multiple domestication objectives (Chopra et al. 2020; Gautam et al. 2026). Species‐specific responses in Physalis pubescens highlight the necessity of trajectory‐tailored strategies (Liu, Sun, et al. 2025). Zone B's intermediate (D e ) reflects multi‐trait coordination and background optimisation. Where desired traits require extensive reconstruction in genetically divergent material, engineering extends to Zone C.

Zone C (Novel Chassis: De Novo Construction Vector, Higher (De ))

Represents bottom‐up construction of crop ideotypes directly from wild or minimally domesticated germplasm or crop wild relatives, harnessing native abiotic resilience and broad genetic diversity. Proof‐of‐concept studies demonstrate that coordinated multiplex editing accelerates domestication across diploid and polyploid lineages (Zsögön et al. 2018). Multiplex editing in wild tomato ( S. pimpinellifolium ) accelerates domestication phenotypes (Li et al. 2018; Zsögön et al. 2018), while de novo domestication of wild allotetraploid rice ( O. alta ) establishes homeolog‐wide editing in complex polyploids (Yu et al. 2021). Systematic characterisation of wild tuber‐bearing Solanum species further informs chassis selection prior to editing (Azariadis et al. 2025). Zone C represents a higher (D e ) due to unadapted genetic backgrounds, high evolutionary distance and epistatic uncertainty.

These trajectory zones form a dynamic operational continuum rather than rigid categories. Trajectory position describes starting germplasm, (D e ) measures biological/engineering effort and evidence/readiness reflects empirical maturity. Interpreting these dimensions independently ensures that higher (D e ) is not conflated with weak evidence, nor lower (D e ) with zero pleiotropic risk. Representative primary literature was selected based on direct relevance to domestication‐oriented engineering, functional or phenotypic validation and characterisation of germplasm relevant to subsequent engineering. This distinction provides a transparent basis to compare domestication routes without equating proof‐of‐concept with deployment readiness. However, executing interventions across increasing engineering distances introduces biological, developmental and governance constraints, establishing the strategic limitations evaluated in the following section.

Trajectory‐Dependent Constraints and the Solution Architecture in Programmable Domestication

Despite advances in genome editing and domestication‐oriented breeding, programmable domestication remains constrained by biological, translational and regulatory complexities that limit the predictability and scalability of engineered phenotypes. As engineering complexity increases, genome editing increasingly interacts with interconnected genetic and developmental networks rather than isolated loci. Epistasis and pleiotropy can alter phenotypic responses and increase their dependence on genetic background and trait architecture (Alam and Purugganan 2024; Dwivedi et al. 2024). Multiplex editing in Physalis pubescens further demonstrates species‐specific phenotypic responses, while hierarchical epistasis can expose cryptic genetic variation and limit predictable additive effects among engineered alleles (Liu, Sun, et al. 2025; Zebell et al. 2025). Thus, increasing engineering complexity requires consideration of genetic context, trait interactions and pleiotropic effects rather than simply increasing the number of edited loci.

Quantitative traits present an additional predictability challenge because regulatory and dosage effects do not necessarily translate proportionally into phenotypic outcomes. Genome editing of regulatory regions can generate quantitative variation, while regulatory modification of rice panicle development demonstrates that yield components can be partially decoupled through targeted regulatory changes (Rodríguez‐Leal et al. 2017; Song et al. 2022). Cryptic genetic variation and genetic interactions further emphasise the dependence of engineered phenotypes on surrounding genetic architecture (Yadav et al. 2016; Zebell et al. 2025). Consequently, target selection and validation must account for genetic background, regulatory architecture and trait interactions rather than assume deterministic genotype‐to‐phenotype relationships. Technical implementation creates an additional bottleneck, particularly for underutilised and wild germplasm. Conventional transformation and regeneration can restrict the genotypes accessible to genome editing, whereas tissue‐culture‐independent de novo meristem induction and GRF–GIF‐mediated regeneration provide strategies for expanding transformation capacity (Debernardi et al. 2020). These advances are particularly relevant to domestication trajectories involving poorly transformable germplasm, where technical limitations can constrain otherwise tractable engineering targets.

Regulatory heterogeneity further affects translation beyond experimental systems. Differences among jurisdictions in the classification, assessment and regulation of genetically modified and genome‐edited crops create variable deployment pathways (Turnbull et al. 2021). Thus, technical feasibility alone does not establish deployment readiness; regulatory compatibility, risk assessment and agricultural context must also be considered (Fernie and Yan 2019; Jingkun Zhang et al. 2023). Addressing these constraints requires integrating target selection with genetic‐context analysis, computational prioritisation, experimental validation, transformation technologies and deployment considerations. Pangenomic resources and quantitative genetic approaches can improve representation of genetic diversity and inform target prioritisation, while improved regeneration technologies expand access to diverse germplasm (Dwivedi et al. 2024; Maher et al. 2020). Iterative and multi‐environment validation remain essential for assessing phenotypic stability across biological and environmental contexts. Accordingly, programmable domestication is better viewed as an iterative process linking genetic design, experimental validation, translational feasibility and regulatory preparedness, providing a basis for progressing from trajectory specific proof‐of‐concept studies towards more reliable and scalable crop redesign.

Translating De Novo Domestication Into Climate‐Resilient Agriculture

The future of de novo domestication is progressing from isolated proof‐of‐concept studies towards integrated platforms for engineering climate‐resilient and environmentally adaptive crop systems (Fernie and Yan 2019; Kumar et al. 2026), as summarised in Figure 2. Advances in pangenomic, long read sequencing and graph‐based genome assemblies are expanding access to structural variants, copy number variations and adaptive alleles that were lost during domestication but remain conserved in crop wild relatives and landraces. Integration of these genomic resources with CRISPR‐Cas technologies is expected to accelerate precision breeding by enabling the targeted reintroduction of favourable alleles while minimising the linkage drag associated with conventional introgression breeding.

Translational requirements for advancing programmable de novo domestication from engineered ideotypes towards deployable climate‐resilient crops. Successful translation requires coordinated consideration of agronomic scaling, including multi‐environment performance, yield stability, genotype × environment responses, mechanisation and resource‐use efficiency; socio‐economic integration, including seed systems, farmer participation, market and end‐use requirements and adoption; and biosafety and governance, including ecological risk, potential gene flow, containment, regulatory approval and responsible deployment. Emerging innovations in pangenomics, predictive breeding, synthetic biology and digital agriculture provide additional opportunities to accelerate future domestication while introducing new requirements for data quality, infrastructure, biosafety and ethical oversight.

Agronomic Scaling: Field Performance, Yield Stability and Mechanisation

Translating de novo domestication from proof‐of‐concept demonstrations to agricultural deployment requires moving beyond targeted single‐gene edits towards engineering agronomically robust ideotypes capable of maintaining stable performance across variable production environments (Fernie and Yan 2019; Hanak et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). Crop wild relatives retain substantial adaptive diversity for abiotic stress tolerance, nutrient‐use efficiency and disease resistance, providing an important genetic reservoir for climate‐resilient crop redesign (Fernie and Yan 2019; Hanak et al. 2022). However, incorporating these adaptive attributes into agronomically useful backgrounds may require coordinated modification of multiple domestication traits across regulatory networks rather than isolated locus interventions, as demonstrated by genome‐editing studies in wild tomato ( S. pimpinellifolium ) and wild allotetraploid rice ( O. alta ) (Yu et al. 2021; Zsögön et al. 2018). Because engineered trait combinations may interact with complex genetic backgrounds and environmental conditions, greenhouse‐validated phenotypes require rigorous multi‐environment evaluation to assess yield stability, environmental responsiveness and lodging resistance—a crucial component of structural performance governed by integrated architectural, anatomical and biochemical traits (Muszynska et al. 2021). Furthermore, commercial deployment demands compatibility with production and harvesting systems; ideotype design must therefore integrate synchronised maturity, appropriate canopy architecture, determinate growth and mechanical harvestability alongside climate resilience. Demonstrating stable agronomic performance and compatibility with practical production systems provides the technical foundation for the broader socio‐economic and seed‐system integration examined in Section 6.2.

Socio‐Economic Integration: Seed Systems, Farmer Participation and Culinary Value

Translating de novo domestication into tangible agricultural impact requires integrating genome‐engineered germplasm within existing breeding infrastructure, seed supply networks and regional farming systems, particularly for orphan crops and underutilised species. Landmark demonstrations in groundcherry ( P. pruinosa ) and wild allotetraploid rice ( O. alta ) show that genome editing can rapidly improve multiple domestication traits while retaining valuable characteristics of the founder germplasm, highlighting the potential to diversify crop portfolios beyond conventional staples (Lemmon et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021). However, genetic modification alone cannot ensure widespread agricultural deployment. Maximising the utility of underexploited germplasm requires coupling genome‐engineering strategies with breeding and selection approaches that support systematic crop improvement (Huang et al. 2025). Scalable deployment further depends on effective seed multiplication and distribution systems capable of delivering high‐quality planting material across diverse production environments. Concurrently, participatory varietal evaluation involving farming communities can help align newly developed cultivars with regional management practices, labour availability, resource input and farmer‐defined priorities (Weltzien and Christinck 2017). Beyond field performance, long‐term adoption also depends on consumer and market expectations; yield and climate resilience should therefore be considered alongside processing efficiency, grain or fruit texture, shelf‐life and other locally relevant end‐use and sensory characteristics when defining target ideotypes. Integrating genome engineering with breeding and selection, effective seed systems, participatory evaluation and market‐oriented quality traits provides a basis for scalable agricultural adoption, establishing the socio‐economic context for the biosafety and governance considerations examined in Section 6.3.

Biosafety and Governance: Regulatory Approval and Ecological Risk

Responsible translation of de novo domesticated crops requires biosafety and governance considerations to operate alongside technical and agronomic development. Transgene‐free genome‐editing platforms, including direct delivery of CRISPR–Cas9 ribonucleoproteins (RNPs), enable targeted modification without stable integration of foreign DNA, as demonstrated by zygote‐mediated editing in rice ( O. sativa ) (Toda et al. 2019). Recent advances in RNP‐mediated delivery and other transgene‐free strategies further expand the technical options for non‐transgenic crop development, while delivery, regeneration and regulatory interpretation remain important translational considerations (Lokya et al. 2025; Ramakrishnan et al. 2025). Although such approaches can reduce concerns associated with stable foreign‐DNA integration, they do not replace molecular characterisation, off‐target assessment, biosafety evaluation or regulatory review before environmental release. Global translation is further complicated by regulatory heterogeneity, as divergent process‐ and product‐based governance approaches create variable requirements for commercialisation and transboundary movement of genome‐edited germplasm (Menz et al. 2020; Turnbull et al. 2021). This heterogeneity may be particularly relevant to de novo domestication, where multiplex modifications are introduced into previously unadopted genetic backgrounds and may generate novel genetic configurations. Furthermore, because de novo domestication can utilise wild or underutilised progenitor germplasm, ecological assessment should consider potential outcrossing, persistence of engineered traits and unintended gene flow into compatible wild populations. Accordingly, transgene‐free editing technologies should be considered alongside transparent, science‐based molecular, ecological and regulatory assessment rather than as substitutes for such evaluation. Together with agronomic performance and socio‐economic integration, these considerations define the translational requirements for moving de novo domestication from experimental proof‐of‐concept towards climate‐resilient, sustainable and responsibly deployable crop systems.

Conclusion

Programmable de novo domestication provides a complementary route to conventional crop improvement by combining the adaptive diversity of wild and underutilised germplasm with pan‐genomic discovery, functional genomics, precision genome editing, accelerated breeding and field‐based validation. Across the trait modules examined in this review, the appropriate engineering strategy depends strongly on genetic architecture: major‐effect domestication traits may be amenable to targeted loss‐of‐function editing, whereas quantitative, developmental, metabolic and adaptive traits require greater emphasis on cis‐regulatory tuning, pathway‐level manipulation and genetic‐context analysis. The Trajectory Zones Framework further provides a basis for comparing domestication routes according to starting germplasm, engineering distance and evidence/readiness, while recognising that biological complexity, agronomic performance, socio‐economic integration and biosafety and regulatory requirements jointly influence translation. Thus, successful programmable domestication requires moving beyond individual edited loci towards an integrated, evidence‐driven approach that connects genetic diversity, trait architecture, engineering precision and real‐world crop performance.
