On products of sets of natural density one
Sandro Bettin, Matteo Bordignon, Alessandro Fazzari

Abstract
. In a previous work, Bettin, Koukoulopoulos, and Sanna prove that if two sets of natural numbers A A and B B have natural density 1 1 , then their product set A ⋅ B := { a b : a ∈ A , b ∈ B } A\cdot B:=\{ab:a\in A,b\in B\} also has natural density 1 1 . They also provide an effective rate and pose the question of determining the optimal rate. We make progress on this question by constructing a set A A of density 1 such that A ⋅ A A\cdot A has a “large” complement.

Introduction

The study of product sets
A\cdot B:=\{ab:a\in A,b\in B\}
of two sets of natural numbers
A
and
B
has long been of interest in mathematics. For finite sets, the classic multiplication table problem, posed by Erdös
[
4
,
5
]
, seeks bounds on the cardinality of the
n\times n
multiplication table. This problem was fully resolved by Ford
[
6
]
, building on earlier work by Tenenbaum
[
17
]
. A multidimensional variation was later studied by Koukoulopoulos
[
12
]
.
For more general finite sets, the cardinality problem has been investigated by Cilleruelo, Ramana, and Ramaré
[
3
]
, as well as by Mastrostefano
[
14
]
and Sanna
[
15
]
.

The analogous problem for infinite sets of natural numbers was considered by Hegyvári, Hennecart, and Pach
[
10
]
. In this context, the role of cardinality is played by the natural density
d(A):=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#(A\cap[1,x])}{x}
of a set
A
, if the limit exists.
Hegyvári, Hennecart, and Pach asked whether, given two sets
A,B
with density
1
, the product set
A\cdot B
also has density
1
.

In
[
2
]
, Bettin, Koukoulopoulos, and Sanna answered this question in the affirmative. In other words, defining

(1.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{R}_{x}(A):=1-\frac{\#(A\cap[1,x])}{x}

for any
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
x\geq 1
, they proved that if
\operatorname{R}_{x}(A),\operatorname{R}_{x}(B)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
, then also
\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot B)\to 0
.
In the same paper, it was also remarked that one could obtain an explicit rate of convergence for
\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)
in terms of the rate of
\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)
. More specifically, their proof (cf.
[
2
, Remark, p.1411]
) gives that if

\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)\ll(\log x)^{-a}\quad\text{ for some }a\in(0,1),

then

\displaystyle\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)\ll(\log x)^{-\frac{a^{2}}{1+a}+o(1)}.

Equivalently, letting

\displaystyle\psi(a)

\displaystyle:=\sup\Big\{b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\ \Big|\ \operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)\ll(\log x)^{-b}\ \forall A\subseteq\mathbb{N}\text{ s.t. }\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)\ll(\log x)^{-a}\Big\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{:}=\inf_{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}\atop\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)\ll(\log x)^{-a}\hskip-7.3612pt}\big\{b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\ \big|\ \operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)=\Omega((\log x)^{-b})\big\}

for
a>0
, the result of
[
2
]
establishes that
\psi(a)\geq a^{2}/(1+a)
for
a\in(0,1)
. In this note, we aim to make progress on determining the function
\psi(a)
by providing an upper bound.
It is easy to see that
\psi(a)\leq a
for all
a\in(0,1)
. Indeed, let us denote by
P
a subset of the primes with relative asymptotic density
a\in(0,1)
, i.e.
\char 35\relax(P\cap[1,x])\sim ax/\log x
. Then, letting

A_{P}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\exists p\in P\text{ s.t. $p|n$}\}\cup\{1\}

we have
A_{P}\cdot A_{P}=A_{P}
and, by the Fundamental Lemma of Sieve Theory
[
11
, Theorem 18.11]
,

\operatorname{R}_{x}(A_{P})=\operatorname{R}_{x}(A_{P}\cdot A_{P})=\frac{1}{x}\#\{n\in[2,x]\mid(p,n)=1\,\forall p\in P\}=(\log x)^{-a+o(1)}.

We improve upon this “trivial” bound for sufficiently small values of
a
. More specifically, we show the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
a\in(0,1/4)
,
B\in[0,\phi^{-1}(4a)]
, let

(1.2)

\displaystyle W(a,B):=\frac{(\phi(B)-\sqrt{\phi(B)^{2}-4a\phi(B)})^{2}}{4\phi(B)}+a\frac{\phi(2B)}{\phi(B)}

\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1]

\displaystyle\phi(x):=\begin{cases}x\log x-x+1,&x\in(0,1),\\

we have

(1.5)

\displaystyle\psi(a)\leq K(a).

Moreover, one has

(1.6)

\displaystyle K(a)<a

\displaystyle\text{ for }a\in(0,0.11717],

(1.7)

\displaystyle K(a)\leq\frac{2a^{2}}{1-\log 2}+o(a^{2})

1.1.
Sketch of the argument

The main idea used in
[
2
]
is as follows: any integer
n
can be factorized as

n=n_{\text{smooth}}\cdot n_{\text{rough}},

where
n_{\text{smooth}}
and
n_{\text{rough}}
are the products of its “small” and “large” prime factors, respectively, with respect to a suitably chosen (small) cutoff. If
n\notin A\cdot A
, then at least one of these factors must be missing from
A
, meaning either
n_{\text{smooth}}\notin A
or
n_{\text{rough}}\notin A
.
If the product set
A\cdot A
does not have density
1
, then
A
must lack its expected proportion of either smooth or rough numbers. Consequently,
A
itself cannot have density
1
.

In the argument above, for any
n\notin A\cdot A
one infers information about
A
from a single factorization of
n
, where
n
is written as a product of two integers. These integer, in addition to not both belonging to
A
, also satisfy the extra condition of being respectively small and smooth, and large and rough. To construct a set
A
such that
\mathbb{N}\setminus(A\cdot A)
is large, we aim to define
A
in a way that naturally forces a typical integer
m
to have all its factorizations in
A\cdot A
constrained by this extra condition.

To achieve this, we define
A
as the set of integers that do not have “too few” large prime divisors, i.e., we consider

A=\big\{m:\Omega^{*}(m)>B\operatorname{M}(m)\big\},

where

\Omega^{*}(m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\nu_{p}}\mid\!\mid m\\
\exp(\delta\log\log m)<p\leq m\end{subarray}}\nu_{p},\qquad\operatorname{M}(m)=(1-\delta)\log\log m.

Notice that
\operatorname{M}(m)
is the expected average value of
\Omega^{*}(m)
. The parameters
\delta,B\in(0,1)
are then chosen so that

\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)=(\log x)^{-a}.

For any factorization
m=n_{1}n_{2}
with
n_{1},n_{2}\in A
and
n_{1}\leq n_{2}
, we have two possibilities:

(a)

If
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are of comparable size, then

\Omega^{*}(m)\gtrsim B(\operatorname{M}(n_{1})+\operatorname{M}(n_{2}))\approx 2B\operatorname{M}(m)

(b)

If
n_{1}
is much smaller than
n_{2}
, then
m
has at least
B\operatorname{M}(n_{1})
prime divisors smaller than
n_{1}
, and thus
m
does not have too few prime divisors of such (small) size.

Both conditions on
m
are stricter (in terms of asymptotic cardinality) than the condition in the definition of
A
. Despite
m
needing to satisfy only one of these conditions (and in fact, condition (b) applies across all possible ranges of
n_{1}
), we obtain that
\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)
is larger than
\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)
.
We then optimize the choice of
\delta
and
B
to maximize
\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)
.

When making this argument rigorous, we need to make an additional refinement. Specifically, we modify
\Omega^{*}(m)
to “discretize” the interval
(\exp(\delta\log\log m),m]
in its definition. See (
3.1
) for the precise definition of the set
A
. This adjustment is needed when handling all the possible range constraints in case (b).

Lemmata

For any set of primes
S

\operatorname{S}(x):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p},\qquad\Omega(n;S):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\nu_{p}}\mid\!\mid n\\
p\in S\end{subarray}}\nu_{p}.

All the preliminary results stated in this section are manifestations of the Poissonian nature of the arithmetic function
\Omega(n;S)
.
The first one is a standard upper bound for the probability that a random integer

has a limited number of prime divisors in an interval.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\phi
be as in (
1.3
). Then, uniformly for
e<U<V\leq\log\log x
and
B\in[0,1)
, we have

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\atop\Omega(n;(U,V])\leq B\log\frac{\log V}{\log U}\end{subarray}}1\ll\bigg(\frac{\log V}{\log U}\bigg)^{-\phi(B)}.

Proof.

Halász
[
9
]
proved sharp bounds for integers
n\leq x
with
\Omega(n;(U,V])=k
. To obtain the claimed result it suffices to sum over
k\leq B\log\frac{\log V}{\log U}
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\underline{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
,
\varepsilon>0
and
x\geq 1
. Let
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}
disjoint sets of primes. Then, for
A_{\varepsilon}
large enough we have

N(\underline{k},x):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\Omega(n;S_{j})\leq k_{j}\;\;\forall j=1,\dots,m\end{subarray}}1\gg x\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\operatorname{S}_{j}(x)^{k_{j}-1}}{(k_{j}-1)!}e^{-\operatorname{S}_{j}(x)}

uniformly in

satisfying
1\leq k_{j}\leq(2-\varepsilon)\operatorname{S}_{j}(x)
and
\operatorname{S}_{j}(x)\geq A_{\varepsilon}
for all
j=1,\dots,m
.

Proof.

Since
\log n\geq\sum_{p|n}\log p
, for all
u\leq 2x
we have

\displaystyle N(\underline{k},2x)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq u\\
\Omega(n,S_{j})\leq k_{j}\;\forall j\end{subarray}}\frac{\log n}{\log 2x}\geq\sum_{p\leq u}\frac{\log p}{\log 2x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\leq u/p\\
\Omega(ph;S_{j})\leq k_{j}\;\forall j\end{subarray}}1\geq\sum_{p\leq u}\frac{\log p}{\log 2x}N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},u/p),

with
\underline{1}=(1,\dots,1)
. Dividing by
2x
and integrating over
u\leq 2x
we then have

(2.1)

\begin{split}N(\underline{k},2x)&\geq\int_{1}^{2x}\sum_{p\leq u}N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},u/p)\frac{\log p}{\log 2x}\,\frac{du}{2x}=\int_{1}^{x}\sum_{p\leq 2x/u}p\frac{\log p}{\log 2x}\,N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},u)\,\frac{du}{2x}\\
&\gg\frac{1}{\log x}\int_{1}^{x}\frac{x}{u^{2}}\,N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},u)\,du\geq\frac{x}{\log x}\int_{1}^{x}\frac{N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},u)}{u^{1+\sigma}}du\end{split}

for any
\sigma\geq 1
.
By integration by parts, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\Omega(n;S_{j})=k_{j}-1\;\forall j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma}}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\Omega(n;S_{j})\leq k_{j}-1\;\forall j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma}}=\frac{N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},x)}{x^{\sigma}}+\sigma\int_{1}^{x}\frac{N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},u)}{u^{1+\sigma}}\,du

and thus, since
N(\underline{k}-\underline{1},x)\leq N(\underline{k},2x)
, for
x
sufficiently large (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) yield

(2.3)

\displaystyle N(\underline{k},2x)

\displaystyle\gg\frac{x}{\log x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\Omega(n;S_{j})=k_{j}-1\;\forall j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma}}.

Now, let
(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
. Assuming
\sigma>1
, by Cauchy’s theorem we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
\Omega(n;S_{j})=k_{j}-1\;\;\forall j\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{m}}\int_{|z_{1}|=r_{1}}\cdots\int_{|z_{m}|=r_{m}}\frac{F(\underline{z},\sigma)}{z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{m}^{k_{m}}}\,dz_{1}\cdots dz_{m}

where for
\underline{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}

\displaystyle F(\underline{z},\sigma):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z_{1}^{\Omega(n;S_{1})}\cdots z_{m}^{\Omega(n;S_{m})}}{n^{\sigma}}=\exp\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{p\in S_{j}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{z_{j}^{\ell}}{\ell p^{\ell\sigma}}+\sum_{p\notin\cup_{j}\!S_{j}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell p^{\ell\sigma}}\bigg)

where the second expression is obtained by expanding
F
in its Euler’s product. We assume
|z_{j}|=r_{j}\leq 2-\varepsilon\;\forall j
and pick
\sigma=\sigma_{v}=1+\frac{1}{\log v}
with
2\leq v\leq x
. We compare
F(\underline{z},\sigma_{v})
with
F(\underline{r},\sigma_{v})
; a simple computation yields

(2.5)

\displaystyle F(\underline{z},\sigma_{v})

\displaystyle=F(\underline{r},\sigma_{v})\exp\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(z_{j}-r_{j})\operatorname{S}_{j}(v)+O_{\varepsilon}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|z_{j}-r_{j}|\Big)\bigg)

and

(2.6)

\displaystyle F(\underline{r},\sigma_{v})=\zeta(\sigma)\exp\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{p\in S_{j}\atop p\leq v}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{r_{j}-1}{p^{\sigma}}+O(1)\bigg)=e^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(r_{j}-1)\operatorname{S}_{j}(v)+O(1)}\log v.

We insert (
2.5

2.4
). For the main term we evaluate the integrals, and we estimate the contribution of the error choosing

and using the inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
\Omega(n;S_{j})=k_{j}-1\;\forall j\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma_{v}}}

\displaystyle=F(\underline{r},\sigma_{v})\prod_{j=1}^{m}\int_{|z_{j}|=r_{j}}\frac{\exp\big((z_{j}-r_{j})\operatorname{S}_{j}(v)\big)}{z_{j}^{k_{j}}}(1+O_{\varepsilon}(|z_{j}-r_{j}|))\,dz_{j}

\displaystyle=(\log v)e^{O(1)}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\operatorname{S}_{j}(v)^{k_{j}-1}e^{-\operatorname{S}_{j}(v)}}{(k_{j}-1)!}(1+O_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{S}_{j}(v)^{-1/2})).

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n>x\\

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

\Omega(n;S_{j})=k_{j}-1\;\;\forall j\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma_{y}}}\gg(\log x)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\operatorname{S}_{j}(y)^{k_{j}-1}e^{-\operatorname{S}_{j}(y)}}{(k_{j}-1)!}

Proof of Theorem

Let
y>1
and

be two real parameters. Notice that
1/y
plays the role of the parameter
\delta
in the introduction.
Let us also introduce the notations

E_{k}:=e^{e^{y^{k}}}\quad\text{ and }\quad D_{k}:=y^{k}-y^{k-1},\quad\text{for }k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Also, let
c=c(y):=\frac{1}{y}(1-\frac{1}{y})
, so that in particular
D_{k-1}=y^{k}c
. For the sake of brevity, we denote

\Omega_{k}(n):=\Omega\big(n;(E_{k-1},E_{k}]\big)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{\nu_{p}}||n\\

Finally, we introduce the following set:

(3.1)

\displaystyle A:=\{n\in(E_{0},\infty)\mid\Omega_{k_{n}}(n)>\max\{1,BD_{k_{n}}\}\}\quad\text{ with }\quad k_{n}:=\max\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid E_{k}<n\}.

3.1.
The density of
A
and

As a first step towards Theorem

, we establish an upper bound for the asymptotic density of the complement of
A
. We recall that
\operatorname{R}_{x}

Proposition 3.1
.

In the above notations, for
x\geq 2
we have

\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)\ll_{y}\frac{1}{(\log x)^{\phi(B)c}}.

Proof.

We write

as

with
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in[0,1)
, so that
\log\log x=y^{M-z}
. We have

(3.2)

\displaystyle x\operatorname{R}_{x}(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq E_{M-2}\\

n\notin A\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E_{M-1}<n\leq E_{M-z}\\
n\notin A\end{subarray}}1.

First we note that

E_{M-2}=\exp(e^{y^{M-2}})=\exp((\log x)^{y^{z-2}})=x^{o(1)},

since

and

.
In particular, the first of the three sums in (

) is negligible.
Moreover, if
E_{M-2}<n\leq E_{M-1}

, so by definition of

(3.3)

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E_{M-2}<n\leq E_{M-1}\\

by Lemma

.
We note that for any

\frac{E_{M-1}}{e^{\phi(B)y^{M-1}c}}\ll_{y}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{\phi(B)c}}.

Indeed, for

for all

Then (

) is

Finally, we deal with the third sum in (

). For

. Hence, by applying Lemma
2.1
we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E_{M-1}<n\leq E_{M-z}\\
n\not\in A\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{x}{e^{\phi(B)D_{M-1}}}=\frac{x}{(\log x)^{\phi(B)c\,y^{z}}}\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{\phi(B)c}}

and the proof is completed.
∎

We now prove an Omega result for the asymptotic density of the complement of
A\cdot A
.

Proposition 3.2
.

In the above notations, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)=\Omega_{y}\bigg(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{\phi(B)y^{-2}+\phi(\min\{1,2B\})c-o(1)}}\bigg).

Proof.

Let
x=E_{M}-1
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
. We will show that (
3.5
) holds with
\gg
for such values of
x
.

Let
n\in(A\cdot A)\cap[1,x]
. Then, at least one of the following must hold:

a)

\displaystyle n\in\Big(\big(E_{M-1},E_{M}\big)\cap A\Big)\cdot\Big(\big(E_{M-1},E_{M}\big)\cap A\Big)
;

b)

n
has a divisor in
(E_{M-r-1},E_{M-r}]\cap A
for some
r\in\{1,\dots,M-1\}
.

By definition of
A
, the condition a) forces
\Omega_{M-1}(n)>2BD_{M-1}
, whereas case b) implies
\Omega_{M-r-1}(n)>BD_{M-r-1}
for some
r\geq 1
. It follows that for
M
large enough

\displaystyle(\mathbb{N}\cap[1,x])\setminus\big(A\cdot A\big)

\displaystyle\supseteq\bigg\{n\leq x\ \bigg|\ {\Omega_{M-1}(n)\leq 2BD_{M-1},\atop\Omega_{M-r-1}(n)\leq\max\{1,BD_{M-r-1}\}\ \forall r=1,\dots,M-2}\bigg\}.

We fix
r_{0}\in[1,M-2]
and let
J=(E_{0},E_{M-r_{0}-1}]
. Clearly,
\Omega(n;J)=\sum_{r=r_{0}}^{M-2}\Omega_{M-r-1}(n)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle(\mathbb{N}\cap[1,x])\setminus\big(A\cdot A\big)

\displaystyle\supseteq\bigg\{n\leq x\ \bigg|\ {\Omega_{M-1}(n)\leq\min\{1,2B\}D_{M-1},\ \ \Omega(n;J)\leq 1,\atop\Omega_{M-r-1}(n)\leq BD_{M-r-1}\ \forall r=1,\dots,r_{0}-1}\bigg\}.

The set above is defined by conditions on the prime divisors of
n
in disjoint intervals. Therefore, we can apply Lemma
2.2
and obtain

\begin{split}\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)\gg_{y,r_{0}}(\log x)^{o(1)}\times\prod_{j=M-r_{0}}^{M-2}\frac{D_{j}^{BD_{j}-1}}{[BD_{j}]!}e^{-D_{j}}\times\frac{D_{M-1}^{\min\{1,2B\}D_{M-1}-1}}{[\min\{1,2B\}D_{M-1}]!}e^{-D_{M-1}}\times e^{-y^{M-r_{0}-1}}.\end{split}

By Stirling’s approximation formula, for fixed
b
, one has

\frac{D^{bD-1}}{(bD)!}e^{-D}\gg\frac{D^{bD}e^{-D}}{D\sqrt{D}(bD)^{bD}}=\frac{1}{D^{3/2}}e^{-D\phi(b)}.

As a consequence, we have

\begin{split}\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)&\gg_{y,r_{0}}(\log x)^{o(1)}\times\prod_{j=M-r_{0}}^{M-2}\frac{1}{D_{j}^{O(1)}}e^{-D_{j}\phi(B)}\times\frac{e^{-D_{M-1}\phi(\min\{1,2B\})-y^{M-r_{0}-1}}}{D_{M-1}^{O(1)}}\\
&\gg_{y}(\log x)^{o(1)}\times\exp\bigg(-\phi(B)\sum_{j=M-r_{0}}^{M-2}D_{j}-D_{M-1}\phi(\min\{1,2B\})-y^{M-r_{0}-1}\bigg)\end{split}

since
\prod_{j=M-r_{0}}^{M-1}D_{j}\ll(\log x)^{o(1)}
. Upon noting that the telescopic sum over
j
above equals
y^{M-2}-y^{M-r_{0}-1}
, the above gives

\begin{split}\operatorname{R}_{x}(A\cdot A)&\gg_{y,r_{0}}(\log x)^{o(1)}\times\exp\Big(-y^{M}\big(\phi(B)y^{-2}+c\,\phi(\min\{1,2B\})+y^{-r_{0}-1}\big)\Big).\end{split}

Since
y^{M}=\log\log x+O(1)
we then obtain (
3.5
) by letting
r_{0}\to\infty
sufficiently slowly.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Equation (
1.5
)

To establish Theorem
1.1
, we now optimize the choice of parameters involved.
Let us consider
a
to be in the image of the function
(0,1)\times(1,\infty)\ni(B,y)\mapsto\phi(B)\frac{1}{y}(1-\frac{1}{y})
, i.e.
a\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
. Then, by Proposition
3.1
and
3.2
we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\psi(a)\leq F(a),

where

\displaystyle F(a)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y>1\\
B\in(0,1)\end{subarray}}\Big\{\phi(B)y^{-2}+\phi(\min\{1,2B\})y^{-1}(1-y^{-1})\ \Big|\ a=\phi(B)y^{-1}(1-y^{-1})\Big\}

\displaystyle=\min_{B,t\in[0,1]}\Big\{\phi(B)t^{2}+\frac{\phi(\min\{1,2B\})}{\phi(B)}a\ \Big|\ a=\phi(B)t(1-t)\Big\}.

The equation
a=\phi(B)t(1-t)
has the unique solutions

(3.7)

t^{\prime}_{a,B}=\frac{\phi(B)+\sqrt{\phi(B)^{2}-4a\phi(B)}}{2\phi(B)}\quad\text{ and }\quad t_{a,B}=\frac{\phi(B)-\sqrt{\phi(B)^{2}-4a\phi(B)}}{2\phi(B)}

in
[0,1]
, and induces the condition
\phi(B)\geq 4a
. By monotonicity, one immediately sees that the solution
t_{a,B}
makes the above minimum smaller (and in fact the solution
t^{\prime}_{a,B}
yields the trivial lower bound
F(a)\geq a
).

Now, we have
t_{a,B}^{2}\phi(B)=G_{a}(\phi(B))
with
G_{a}(x)=\frac{(x-\sqrt{x^{2}-4ax})^{2}}{4x}
. Note that
\phi(B)
is strictly decreasing for
B\in[0,1]
and
G_{a}
is a strictly decreasing function. As a consequence,
t_{a,B}^{2}\phi(B)
is strictly increasing for
B
in its domain,
[0,\phi^{-1}(4a))
. Therefore, since
\frac{\phi(\min\{1,2B\})}{\phi(B)}a=0
for
B\geq 1/2
, the minimum must be attained for
B\leq 1/2
. Hence,

(3.8)

\begin{split}F(a)&=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in[0,\min\{\frac{1}{2},\phi^{-1}(4a)\}]\end{subarray}}\Big\{\phi(B)t_{a,B}^{2}+a\frac{\phi(2B)}{\phi(B)}\Big\}\\
&=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in[0,\phi^{-1}(4a)]\end{subarray}}\Big\{\phi(B)t_{a,B}^{2}+a\frac{\phi(2B)}{\phi(B)}\Big\}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in[0,\phi^{-1}(4a)]\end{subarray}}W(a,B)=K(a)\end{split}

since
\frac{\phi(2B)}{\phi(B)}
is also (strictly) increasing on
B\geq 1/2
, and where
W
and
K
are defined in (
1.2
) and (
1.4
). Putting together (
3.6
) and (
3.8
), one finally has (
1.5
).

3.3.
Proof of Equation (

)

Let
a\in(0,1/4)
. If
\phi^{-1}(4a)>\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.
a<\frac{\phi(1/2)}{4}=\frac{1-\log 2}{8}=0.0383\dots
, then we have

K(a)\leq W(a,\tfrac{1}{2})=\phi(\tfrac{1}{2})t_{a,1/2}^{2}<\phi(\phi^{-1}(4a))t_{a,\phi^{-1}(4a)}^{2}=4a/4=a

since

is strictly increasing in
B
. Thus, we can assume
a\geq 0.038
.

Now let
\beta=5.3071678
and let
B_{a}
be such that
\phi(B_{a})=\beta a
. Since
1\geq\phi(B_{a})=\beta a\geq 4a
, we have
B_{a}\in[0,\phi^{-1}(4a)]
. Hence,

K(a)-a=\min_{B\in[0,\phi^{-1}(4a)]}W(a,B)-a\leq Q(a),

where

\displaystyle Q(a):=W(a,B_{a})-a

\displaystyle=\frac{(\beta-\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\beta})^{2}}{4\beta}a+\frac{\phi(2B_{a})}{\beta}-a.

Moreover, we have
B_{a}^{\prime}=\beta/\phi^{\prime}(B_{a})=\beta/\log B_{a}
, whence

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\beta-\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\beta})^{2}}{4\beta}+2B^{\prime}_{a}\frac{\log(2B_{a})}{\beta}-1=\frac{(\beta-\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\beta})^{2}}{4\beta}+2\frac{\log(2)}{\log(B_{a})}+1.

Letting

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(a)<0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }\eta<B_{a}<1

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(a)>0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }0<B_{a}<\eta.

Equivalently, since
\phi

\phi(B_{a})=\beta a

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(a)<0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }0<a<\tfrac{1}{\beta}\phi(\eta)=0.05236391\dots

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(a)>0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{\beta}\phi(\eta)<a<\tfrac{1}{\beta}=0.1884244\dots

Now, since

Q(0.11717)=-4.02\dots{}\cdot 10^{-6}
and

are both

,

3.4.
Proof of Equation (
1.7
)

We already know that the
B
which provides the minimum in

satisfies

. Then, by Equation (

) we have

t_{a,B}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4a/\phi(B)}}{2}=\frac{a}{\phi(B)}+O(a^{2})

\displaystyle W(a,B)

Taking
