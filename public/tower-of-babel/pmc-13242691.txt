Co‐Designing a Digital Health Application for Assisting Self‐Care Diabetes Patients: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Hema Malini, Misita Anwar, Fitri Mailani, Sovia Susianty, Rozi Sastra Purna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsManaging diabetes involves complex self‐care activities, requiring continuous support. Although digital health applications have shown promise, many fail to meet patients' sustained needs. This study aims to understand the needs and preferences of patients for co‐designing a digital health application focused on diabetes self‐care.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using focus group discussions within two workshops, each lasting between 60 and 90 min, involving 27 participants diagnosed with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). Limiting participants to those with relatively stable disease management may have resulted in underrepresentation of individuals facing acute complications or unstable glycemic control; this was considered when interpreting the findings. Reflexive thematic analysis was applied to identify key themes.ResultsThree main themes emerged: (1) current knowledge and practices in diabetes management, (2) challenges faced in self‐care, and (3) expectations for a digital health application. Participants highlighted the need for user‐friendly, interactive features addressing diet, physical activity, and emotional support.ConclusionThe co‐design process underscored the importance of involving end‐users to ensure the application meets their specific needs, fostering sustainable self‐care.

Introduction

Managing diabetes is complex and requires good disease understanding and regular self‐monitoring in patients' daily lives. This complexity stems from the need to balance diet, physical activity, medication adherence, and emotional well‐being, making it essential to provide sustained and personalized support. The COVID‐19 pandemic, along with social distancing measures and emotional strain, has had repercussions on the self‐care routines of individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus. A noticeable shift in how self‐care is managed during this period is anticipated to affect these patients' overall quality of life [1].

Studies underscore the ongoing necessity for healthcare access among type 2 diabetes mellitus patients [2, 3]. Many have turned to social media platforms like WhatsApp or Facebook for information and support. Additionally, individuals with diabetes have demonstrated adeptness in managing their condition, aided by the support of both family members and healthcare professionals. Ensuring sustainable health education and motivation for patients and their families is crucial for promoting consistent self‐care management, ultimately enhancing the quality of life for those with type 2 diabetes mellitus [4]

While digital health interventions for diabetes self‐management have demonstrated effectiveness in many high‐income Western settings, their applicability in low‐ and middle‐income countries remains uneven. Evidence from the Asia–Pacific region indicates that despite the growing availability of mobile health technologies, their adoption and sustained use are constrained by structural and contextual barriers, including limited internet connectivity, shared smartphone use within families, lower digital literacy among older adults, and dependence on informal social and family support systems. A recent narrative review of digital health technologies for diabetes management in the Asia–Pacific region highlights persistent disparities in digital readiness, healthcare infrastructure, and regulatory capacity across countries, which directly influence the effectiveness of digital interventions in real‐world settings [5]. Studies from Southeast Asia and other resource‐constrained contexts, therefore, suggest that digital health solutions must be culturally grounded, low‐cost, and flexible in delivery to be effective. Unlike many Western interventions that assume individual smartphone ownership and continuous internet access, digital health applications in these settings need to support offline functionality, simplified interfaces, and integration with family and community support structures. These contextual differences underscore the importance of co‐design approaches that actively involve end‐users in shaping interventions that reflect local realities rather than importing Western‐centric digital health models.

The rapid advancement of digital health technologies has offered new opportunities for enhancing self‐care in patients with chronic conditions such as Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). Limiting participants to those with relatively stable disease management may have resulted in underrepresentation of individuals facing acute complications or unstable glycemic control; this was considered when interpreting the findings. Over the past decade, numerous studies have explored digital health solutions aimed at improving self‐management and patient outcomes. A randomized experiment involving 162 participants (72% from racial/ethnic minorities) tested a digital health intervention promoting postpartum type 2 diabetes screening among patients with GDM. Participants received a 10‐min online message with up to four components: values affirmation, personalized risk, motivational interviewing, and action planning [6]. Acceptability averaged 3.9/5, with 91% reading the message, 89% finding the content appropriate, and 87% completing an interactive prompt. Another study linked a 52‐week digital Diabetes prevention Program (DPP) with weight loss among 5516 health insurance members, emphasizing health coach interactions, physical activity, and meal logging, suggesting digital DPPs as promising for diabetes prevention [7]. Despite this progress, many digital applications are designed without adequate input from end‐users, leading to low engagement and limited long‐term effectiveness.

Continuous care support for individuals with diabetes is a well‐established practice in developed nations. In developing countries like Indonesia, however, where the infrastructure for diabetes care is still evolving, policies regarding diabetes care support have been instituted under the Ministry of Health's Prevention and Control of Noncommunicable Diseases (PCND) division [8]. This initiative offers various avenues for access, including developing mobile‐based applications. Specifically, the PCND's policy includes a mobile program utilizing platforms such as Telegram, WhatsApp chatbots, counseling services, and online information accessible via Android devices. Nevertheless, there remains a lack of comprehensive reports detailing how effectively this program delivers ongoing support to both patients and their families [8]

Diabetes management research highlights the need for patient‐centered design that integrates users' real‐world challenges and preferences. For instance, a study from Pienkowska et al. [9] demonstrated that involving patients in the design process significantly improved adherence to diabetes management guidelines and overall satisfaction with digital tools. Therefore, co‐design has emerged as a critical approach in developing effective digital health interventions. Besides, both government and community sectors are increasingly embracing co‐design approaches, which encompasses a variety of activities and processes employed in creating services and products involving individuals who utilize them [8].

Co‐design refers to a participatory process involving key stakeholders, including end‐users, in the development of products or services. This approach integrates exploratory research to define problems and collaboratively design solutions that address users' specific needs [10]. By actively engaging patients and their families, co‐design ensures that digital health applications are tailored to fit within their daily routines and address the unique challenges they face in self‐care. Co‐design adapted to integrate lived experiences and professional insights to identify and produce outcomes or products in the community services sector. It draws from engagement methods such as social democracy and community development, where all key stakeholders, ranging from experts to end users, are encouraged to participate as equal partners, contributing their expertise in service and product design [11]. For the co‐design process to be truly effective, there must be consensus on its definition and operational mechanisms.

Despite the increasing recognition of co‐design's benefits, there remains a knowledge gap in its application within low‐resource settings, particularly in countries like Indonesia. Existing studies primarily focus on high‐income settings, where infrastructure and access to digital tools are more advanced. This study aims to bridge this gap by investigating how co‐design can be applied to create supportive interventions for diabetes self‐care in an Indonesian context. Specifically, we seek to understand patients' and families' needs and preferences to ensure that the resulting digital health application is both relevant and effective.

Methods

This qualitative study employed a participatory co‐design framework [12, 13]. Participatory research actively involves stakeholders in all phases of the research, emphasizing collaboration and mutual learning. By adopting this framework, we ensured that patients contributed meaningfully to the design of the digital application, reflecting their lived experiences. This study established a collaboration between diabetes education researchers and researchers specialized in human‐centered design.

Potential end‐users, specifically patients, were actively involved from the early stages of the study. Two workshops (each with 13–14 participants, a total of 27) were conducted to explore and assess existing knowledge on the challenges faced by individuals managing Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) and to understand their preferences for a self‐care application.

Eligible participants were adults aged 25 years or older with a diagnosis of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). Limiting participants to those with relatively stable disease management may have resulted in underrepresentation of individuals facing acute complications or unstable glycemic control; this was considered when interpreting the findings. Individuals with Type 1 diabetes and those younger than 25 years were excluded because the study aimed to focus on patients with relatively stable diabetes management. The age threshold of 25 was chosen to ensure participants had sufficient life experience managing their condition. Convenience sampling allowed easy access to participants, while purposeful sampling ensured diversity in terms of age, gender, and socioeconomic status.

Recruitment was conducted through healthcare providers who referred potential participants and community postings. Participants completed a screening questionnaire to confirm eligibility and provided informed consent. Recruitment occurred through two rounds, each corresponding to a workshop, throughout April 2024. Convenience and purposeful sampling methods were employed, informing participants to participate. Those interested completed an informed consent form along with a demographic questionnaire. The target sample size for each workshop ranged from 10 to 15 participants. Each participant received a token of appreciation, a common practice to acknowledge their contribution while minimizing potential bias.

The workshops were conducted in person in April 2024 at multiple locations, including hospitals and community health centers in Padang Pariaman and Padang city, Indonesia. These venues were selected to provide an accessible and comfortable environment for participants. The workshops engaged 27 participants divided into three groups of nine, ensuring manageable group sizes for in‐depth discussions. The workshop sessions followed a structured format guided by [14] recommendations for qualitative group discussions. Each session lasted 60–90 min and began with an icebreaker activity to create a comfortable environment. Key discussion topics included current self‐care practices, perceived challenges, and expectations for the digital health application. Field notes complemented audio recordings, capturing non‐verbal cues and contextual insights. The workshops were facilitated by three researchers. The facilitators guided discussions, ensured equitable participation, and addressed any emergent questions.

The workshop guide was structured to cover key themes identified through preliminary research. Topics included: (1) Assessment of current practices (knowledge and skills) in diabetes self‐care management, including diet, physical activity, medication, and blood glucose monitoring; (2) Exploration of challenges in managing blood glucose levels, addressing social and mental health issues, and identifying available resources for health information support. (3) Recognition of the need for information and skill enhancement in diabetes self‐care, including expectations for features within the application, such as the need for a mobile application that can facilitate behavior changes.

Pre‐workshop surveys gathered critical information, including: Duration of diabetes diagnosis (categorized into less than 5 years, 5–10 years, and over 10 years).Medical history beyond diabetes (e.g., presence of comorbidities such as hypertension or cardiovascular diseases).Prior exposure to diabetes education programs or digital health applications. These data provided important context for analyzing variations in self‐care needs and preferences.

Data Analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke [15], was used to identify and interpret patterns within the data. Accordingly, the researchers acknowledged their positionality and its potential impact on theme identification, consistent with Braun and Clarke's guidelines on reflexivity. We assessed data saturation based on the criteria wherein no new codes or themes emerged after analyzing the final transcripts. This ensured a comprehensive representation of participant experiences. Themes were constructed iteratively, reflecting participants' insights into barriers and enablers of effective self‐care. Researcher reflexivity was integral to this process, ensuring that themes were not merely descriptive but provided in‐depth interpretations of participants' lived experiences.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Faculty of Nursing Ethical Research Committee with number 383.layaketik/KEPFKEP/2024. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring that their participation was voluntary and that confidentiality was maintained. Ethical approval ensured that the study complied with established ethical standards for research involving human subjects.

Results

Across all workshops, participants were primarily female (74%), aged 48–79 (mean age = 59). Among the 27 participants, 74% were female. Given that most participants were female, gender‐specific experiences may influence the design and usability of the intervention. Women often play central roles in family health management and may be more receptive to education‐based applications, suggesting the app's design should accommodate their caregiving contexts. While efforts were made to recruit a more gender‐balanced sample, participation remained predominantly female due to the demographic distribution of diabetes patients in the selected settings. Future studies may need to employ targeted recruitment strategies to increase male representation. Participants are aged between 48 and 79 (mean age = 59).

The participants' education levels were as follows: 66% held a bachelor's degree, and 34% had completed high school. Regarding occupation, 59.26% were retired, 29.63% were homemakers, and 11.11% were government employees. Additionally, 96.30% of the participants lived with family or a spouse. Regarding smartphone ownership, a notable proportion of participants (25%) did not own a smartphone (they used their children's smartphone). Participants who used their children's smartphones generally did so independently for calls or messages, but required assistance when accessing applications or educational content. This highlighted the need for simplified user interfaces and offline‐accessible modules, which included in the 36% that lacked both smartphones and internet or social media access. These individuals were included to ensure inclusivity and to capture diverse perspectives, particularly from those who may face technological barriers. While the research focuses on understanding their information needs and preferences, this also posed challenges in understanding accessibility concerns and potential alternative features to accommodate low‐tech users: 96% used WhatsApp, 78% used Google, and 67% used YouTube.

This study identified three main themes: existing knowledge and self‐management practices, challenges in managing diabetes, and the need for and expectations regarding a self‐care diabetes application. The following sections provide detailed insights into these themes, incorporating participant quotes to illustrate key findings.

Theme 1: Knowledge, Current Practices, and Daily Routine in Living With Diabetes

Participants identified three key aspects of diabetes management: diet, physical exercise, and medication. They understood diabetes as a high blood sugar disease characterized by symptoms such as anxiety, fatigue, frequent urination, and excessive thirst. All participants recognized the need to reduce carbohydrate intake and increase vegetable and protein consumption. However, while they demonstrated a strong understanding of hyperglycemia symptoms, awareness of hypoglycemia was noticeably lower, despite many being on diabetic medications.

Regarding physical activity, engagement varied. While some were aware of its benefits, many did not follow recommended exercise levels. Participants acknowledged the importance of physical activity but cited various barriers to regular exercise. One participant expressed that while they knew exercise was beneficial, they often lacked motivation, stating, “I know I should exercise, but I am too lazy and lack motivation.” (P2, WS1) Another participant shared that their exercise routine was limited to cycling once a week, saying, “I only exercise on Saturday, bicycling for 1 h.” (P8, WS1) Time constraints also emerged as a common challenge, with one participant explaining, “I have no time; in the morning, I am busy preparing for work, so I don't exercise.” (P5, WS1)

Many homemakers reported that their daily household chores, such as cooking, cleaning, and washing, were their primary forms of physical activity. One participant noted, “I only do some household activities.” (P11‐13, WS1; P26‐27, WS2) Others mentioned walking as a form of exercise, particularly those who walked to and from the mosque after morning prayer, with an estimated walking time of 5–10 min. (P22‐23, WS2) One participant shared their approach to maintaining an active lifestyle, explaining, “I do Zumba every day for half an hour so I can avoid taking my medication.” (P10, WS1)

Medication use also varied among participants. Some took insulin injections alongside oral medication, while others relied solely on oral medication. Several participants expressed hesitancy in taking their prescribed medication, with one participant saying, “I don't take my pills because I don't think I can take them regularly. I have a catering business and need to taste the food, so I don't need it anyway.” (P1, WS1) Another participant who had been recently diagnosed with diabetes admitted, “The doctors diagnosed me with diabetes 2 months ago and prescribed me pills, but I haven't taken them yet because I want to see if my condition changes to normal.” (P11, WS1)

In addition to prescribed medication, some participants explored traditional remedies they had encountered on social media. One participant expressed skepticism about their effectiveness, stating, “Yeah, it lowers your blood sugar if you reduce your food intake.” (P2, WS1) Many participants also shared concerns about taking pills daily, fearing potential long‐term effects on their kidneys. Others expressed ongoing anxiety, despite following their prescribed treatments, due to inconsistent blood sugar control.

Theme 2: Unexpected Issues and Challenges in Daily Health and Self‐Care

Participants encountered various obstacles in managing their diabetes. Some reported unexpected hospitalizations due to severe complications. One participant recounted a distressing experience: “Instead of visiting my children for a religious celebration, I ended up hospitalized due to edema. The doctor advised hemodialysis, but I refused after discussing it with my children.” (P8, WS1) Others shared their fears about hypoglycemia, with one participant saying, “I frequently experience symptoms of hypoglycemia. Most of the time, I manage it by drinking hot, sweet tea or eating candy, but it scares me…” (P15, WS2)

A significant challenge reported was the lack of structured health education programs. Out of 27 participants, only six had received structured diabetes education in the past year, while the rest relied on brief interactions with doctors. One participant explained, “I never get any detailed information regarding my health. Neither nurses nor doctors provide it.” (P21, WS2) As a result, many participants turned to the internet or social media to seek health information. One participant shared, “My mom saw an advertisement about a medicine. She asked me to buy it, and she tried it. I'm not sure whether it works or not; it depends on her diet intake.” (P2, WS1) Another participant mentioned, “I read a suggestion from Dr. [name]. He said that diabetes could be managed through a traditional diet. I read about it and sometimes try it, but not regularly.” (P9, WS1)

Participants also expressed anxiety about their health status and the potential for complications such as kidney failure. One participant shared their distress, saying, “At times, I feel anxious about my health because I've seen my mother and several relatives pass away due to diabetes. It's a source of worry for me.” (P6, WS1) Another participant voiced concerns about long‐term medication use, explaining, “I'm managing my diabetes by taking multiple pills every day. It makes me wonder about the impact of these medications on my kidneys.” (P1, WS1)

Theme 3: Recognition of the Need and Expectation for a Self‐Care Diabetes Application

Participants highlighted key requirements for a self‐care diabetes application. They agreed on the need for information on diet, lifestyle, exercise, the impact of sugar on other diseases, and diabetes prevention and control. They suggested that such an application should include various formats for content delivery, such as videos, text narration, reminder notifications, one‐on‐one counseling, and group discussions. While some participants suggested Zoom meetings as a means of engagement, 36% of participants lacked internet access, highlighting the need for an offline‐accessible version of the app.

Participants also expressed specific expectations for the application. They emphasized the importance of a dedicated diabetes management application that is free to use, interactive, and user‐friendly. One participant remarked, “If an app for diabetes is being developed, it should use simple language that is easy to understand, avoiding unfamiliar medical terms.” (P5, WS1) Another participant expressed concerns about affordability and data consumption, stating, “I would be open to trying a specific application if it's available, but it should be free to use without charges, and it shouldn't consume too much internet data.” (P25, WS2) Others expressed interest in features that support patient education, with one participant suggesting, “If there's an application, it would be beneficial if it includes features like educational videos, discussion options, and Zoom meetings if needed.” (P21, WS2)

Support Systems and Expectations Beyond Digital Tools

Participants emphasized the need for family, peer, and healthcare support in their diabetes management journey. One participant expressed the importance of family encouragement, stating, “I want my family to support and motivate me. It's okay to remind me to take my medicine, but please don't stress me out by constantly saying no every time I want to eat.” (P15, WS1) Another participant highlighted the need for spiritual and emotional support, explaining, “Spiritual aspects are important. We need guidance on reducing stress, not just religious matters.” (P5, WS1)

The application was seen as a tool that could complement in‐person sessions with healthcare providers and peer support groups. One participant shared, “I would like to talk to friends who have the same disease, but not too much, as it sometimes causes me stress.” (P24, WS2) Others expressed interest in regular educational sessions, with one participant suggesting, “If possible, this hospital could provide regular health education sessions where we can ask questions and discuss our condition.” (P13, WS1)

Discussion

This study investigated the co‐designing of a digital health application for individuals with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) to improve self‐care practices and develop supportive interventions for better diabetes management. Through two co‐design workshops, we aimed to understand users' daily practices, informational needs, and expectations to inform the design considerations for a self‐care diabetes application. The findings underscore the need for a self‐care diabetes application that integrates behavioral support, mental health features, and hybrid accessibility to accommodate users with limited internet access.

The findings of this study align with international evidence demonstrating that co‐designed digital health interventions improve relevance and user engagement. However, our results extend existing literature by illustrating how co‐design operates in a non‐Western, middle‐income context where digital access is uneven. This observation is consistent with evidence from a large systematic scoping review showing that digital health interventions risk exacerbating the digital divide when they assume individual device ownership, stable internet access, and high digital literacy, particularly among older adults and socially disadvantaged groups [16]. In our study, participants frequently shared devices with family members or lacked continuous internet access, leading to strong preferences for hybrid models that combine digital tools with face‐to‐face education, offline educational materials, and family involvement. These preferences align with findings from culturally and contextually adapted digital health interventions, which emphasize that effectiveness and sustained engagement depend on interventions being adaptive, inclusive, and socially embedded within local family and community structures [17]. By foregrounding user experiences in Indonesia, this study provides context‐specific insights into how co‐design can support culturally grounded and equitable digital health solutions, complementing rather than replicating predominantly Western digital health research.

Given the potential risks associated with hypoglycemia, future educational interventions should emphasize its recognition and management. Additionally, discussions on ideal body weight and weight reduction strategies were limited, indicating a need for structured guidance on physical activity and weight management within diabetes self‐care applications.

Participants reported infrequent access to diabetes education programs. Out of the 27 participants, only six had received any form of structured diabetes education in the past year, highlighting the need for more accessible and continuous educational interventions. Participants also expressed anxiety regarding disease management, citing concerns about long‐term complications and medication side effects. These psychological concerns reinforce the importance of integrating mental health support features within self‐care applications.

Findings indicate that participants relied on existing knowledge for self‐care but demonstrated varying levels of understanding of hypoglycemia and physical activity guidelines. The discovery that 36% of participants lacked access to smartphones and the internet highlights the need for designing an application that supports both online and offline functionality. Furthermore, anxiety over disease progression and medication side effects underscores the importance of integrating psychosocial support features within the application. The term “obstacles and issues” in diabetes management primarily referred to participants' psychological concerns, lack of access to structured educational programs, and difficulty in maintaining lifestyle modifications. These aspects, as discussed in Theme 2, emphasize the importance of providing tailored, accessible education and behavioral support within digital interventions.

Additionally, discussions on ideal body weight and weight reduction strategies were limited. These gaps indicate a need for structured guidance on physical activity and weight management in diabetes self‐care applications.

The findings suggest that integrating a co‐designed digital health application into Indonesia's healthcare system could improve diabetes self‐management, particularly in resource‐constrained environments. Health policymakers should explore incorporating digital solutions within national diabetes programs, ensuring accessibility for both tech‐savvy users and those with limited digital literacy. Clinicians can leverage these applications to supplement patient education, improve treatment adherence, and expand healthcare outreach to remote areas.

This study aligns with research by Ogrin et al. [18], which highlights the variability in diabetes education formats. Consistent with studies conducted in high‐income settings, this research emphasizes the necessity of personalized and accessible digital interventions. However, unlike Western studies, this research highlights challenges related to low digital penetration, reinforcing the need for hybrid delivery models incorporating both digital and in‐person interactions to accommodate patients with limited technological access.

Summary and Future Directions

Participants expressed interest in personalized goal setting, progress tracking, and integration with healthcare provider networks, which were not initially emphasized in the findings but are critical for future app development. Additionally, further research should investigate how hybrid models—blending digital and in‐person education—can enhance diabetes self‐care. Expanding digital literacy training as part of the implementation process may also improve adoption and usability. This study highlights the importance of co‐design in developing digital health interventions that align with the specific needs of diabetes patients. Future iterations should prioritize inclusivity, address digital accessibility challenges, and incorporate features that support mental health and self‐management. Policymakers and healthcare providers should consider leveraging digital tools to enhance patient education, engagement, and long‐term chronic disease management, especially in resource‐limited settings.

There is significant variability in the style, structure, and content of diabetes foot care education, with uncertainty about the most effective format. A recent study found that written foot health information improved foot health, while interactive foot health education boosted confidence in performing preventive activities. This suggests that the most effective foot care education should incorporate both components [18].

Many participants in the workshops were elderly, which should be factored into app design. While participants found the content appropriate and valuable, their preferences for information delivery varied. Some preferred the app, others favored a larger screen (such as a tablet or laptop), and some preferred face‐to‐face meetings. Therefore, the information should be provided in multiple formats to ensure maximum accessibility for the target community members. Future digital health interventions should aim to balance user‐centered design with technological inclusivity, ensuring engagement across different demographic groups.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of co‐design in developing digital health interventions that align with the specific needs of diabetes patients. Ensuring inclusivity, accessibility, and personalized guidance will be essential in supporting long‐term diabetes self‐management. Future iterations should prioritize inclusivity, address digital accessibility challenges, and incorporate features that support mental health and self‐management. Policymakers and healthcare providers should consider leveraging digital tools to enhance patient education, engagement, and long‐term chronic disease management, especially in resource‐limited settings.
