Microbial zonation and functional roles in the gut of white grub (Maladera insanabilis) larvae
Authors: Anil, Kurubarahalli Bhoothanna Ramesh, M. N. Rudra Gouda, Sabtharishi Subramanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maladera insanabilis, a widespread and destructive agricultural pest in India, thrives in nitrogen-deficient subsoil environments due to its dependency on gut bacteria. In particular, the hindgut is an anaerobic fermentation chamber, supporting microbial-driven nitrogen transformations essential for larval development. Despite its ecological significance, detailed studies exploring gut bacterial diversity and functional role in M. insanabilis are lacking. This study integrates metagenomics, culture-based techniques, enzymatic assays, and gene expression analyses to characterize the nitrogen-cycling potential of gut microbiota along the different gut compartments. The culture-based analysis isolated 16 aerobic and 8 anaerobic bacterial strains, predominantly from Bacillota and Pseudomonadota. High-throughput 16 S rRNA Illumina sequencing revealed 134 shared amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), with distinct bacterial assemblages, Burkholderia and Pseudomonas in the foregut, Paenibacillus in the midgut, and anaerobic genera such as Bacteroides and Desulfovibrio dominating the hindgut. Functional annotation using the KEGG database indicated that anaerobic gut bacteria are actively involved in nitrification, denitrification, and nitrogen fixation. The Enzyme assays confirmed high nitrate and nitrite reductase activity, with Burkholderia contaminans and Bacillus cepacia showing the highest activities. Michaelis–Menten kinetics and Lineweaver–Burk analysis (R² = 0.9871) showed a higher capacity (Vmax) for nitrate and nitrite reduction; a small Km indicates a high affinity for nitrate and nitrite. Gene expression studies viz., hzo, nifH, amx, nirS, and nirK revealed a significantly high expression level in the hindgut, especially under vermicompost treatment. This study provides the first comprehensive insight into nitrogen-cycling gut bacteria in M. insanabilis, highlighting their role in host nutrition and nitrogen transformation. These findings lay a foundation for future microbiome-targeted pest control strategies aimed at disrupting nutrient acquisition in soil-dwelling grubs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52250-y.

Introduction

The Scarabaeoidea superfamily represents one of the most species-rich lineages within the Coleoptera, encompassing over 30,000 species worldwide. The family Scarabaeidae constitutes approximately 91% of this diversity, with around 27,800 described species¹. In the Indian subcontinent, roughly 2,500 scarab beetle species have been recorded². Among these, the larvae commonly known as white grubs pose a significant threat to agriculture by severely damaging crop roots and causing substantial economic losses³⁻⁵.

Maladera insanabilis (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae), commonly known as the rose beetle, has emerged as a highly destructive pest across India. The subterranean larvae feed voraciously on the roots of various economically important crops such as potato, sweet potato, peanut, sugarcane, and strawberry6. With over 500 species distributed across the Palearctic, Oriental, and Afrotropical regions7, the genus Maladera is widely distributed having ecological significance. The larvae of M. insanabilis disrupt plant establishment by consuming root hairs and damaging tender root tissues, leading to stunted growth and reduced yields8. Insect gut represents specialized and compartmentalized microenvironments that support complex gut microbiota. These microbes contribute significantly to host physiology, including nutrient acquisition, digestion, and detoxification9–11. In polyphagous insects like scarab beetles, gut bacteria provide enzymatic support for the breakdown of recalcitrant plant materials such as lignin, cellulose, and xylan12–15.

The larval gut of Scarabaeidae exhibits clear gut microbial zonation, a spatial pattern in which distinct microbial groups occupy specific gut regions based on their functional roles and the physicochemical microenvironments present. The gut is anatomically divided into the foregut, midgut, and a markedly enlarged hindgut, with the latter serving as an anaerobic fermentation chamber. Each compartment supports unique microbial assemblages adapted to local conditions such as oxygen levels, pH, nutrient availability, and redox gradients16. In scarabaeid larvae such as Anomala dimidiata, zonation becomes particularly evident in the hindgut, where Bacillota and Pseudomonadota are consistently dominant17. These microbes carry out key functions including fermentation and the breakdown of recalcitrant plant-derived polysaccharides, reflecting their specialization for the anoxic, nutrient-rich milieu of the hindgut18. Microbial processes across the zonated gut regions also significantly influence the gut’s chemical landscape. Through their metabolic activities, these communities regulate pH and redox potential, thereby optimizing digestive efficiency and nutrient assimilation for the host insect19–20.

Nitrogen (N) is an essential macronutrient required for amino acid synthesis, nucleic acid formation, and other metabolic processes critical to insect development21. However, most eukaryotes, including plants and animals, lack the ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen. This capacity is confined to select prokaryotes that express nitrogenase enzymes, which convert atmospheric N₂ into bioavailable forms such as ammonia (NH₃)22. Consequently, symbiotic associations with nitrogen-fixing microbes confer substantial advantages to insects inhabiting nitrogen-poor environments, such as subsoil habitats23–25. Nitrogen-fixing capabilities are well documented in bark beetles of the genera Dendroctonus and Ips, where diazotrophic bacteria such as Klebsiella, Stenotrophomonas, and Enterobacter augment the host nitrogen budget26–28. In contrast, data on nitrogen-cycling microbes in root-feeding scarab beetles remain scarce. Microbial communities play a pivotal role in regulating ecosystem nitrogen dynamics through processes including nitrogen fixation, nitrification, denitrification, and anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox)29. These pathways depend on key functional genes, such as hzo and amx genes for anammox, nifH for nitrogen fixation, nirS/nirK for nitrite reduction. The presence and expression of these genes indicate the nitrogen-transforming potential of the host insect’s gut microbiota and its resilience to environmental perturbations30–32.

Despite Maladera being one of the largest and most widely distributed genera in the family Scarabaeidae, the structure and functional significance of its gut microbiota remains poorly understood, particularly the diversity and metabolic roles of nitrogen-cycling bacteria within its gut compartments. Here, we present the first comprehensive host specific investigation of nitrogen-cycling microbial communities in the gut of Maladera insanabilis larvae, employing an integrated multi-methodological approach. High-throughput Illumina sequencing of 16 S rRNA genes characterized bacterial community composition across the foregut, midgut, and hindgut, which represent distinct microbial communities. Functional annotation, combined with culture-dependent isolation of aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, identified metabolically relevant taxa adapted to specific gut compartments. Enzymatic assays quantified nitrate and nitrite reductase activities, while quantitative PCR (qPCR) analyses targeting key nitrogen-cycling genes (hzo, amx, nifH, nirS and nirK) assessed the functional potential of the gut bacterial communities. This study elucidates the role of gut bacteria in nitrogen transformations and provides insights that may inform microbiome-based strategies for sustainable pest management in agroecosystems.

Materials and methods

Insect Collection, rearing and dissection of different gut compartments

Third-instar Maladera insanabilis larvae were collected from sugarcane fields in Ghaziabad, Uttar Pradesh, India, using standardized protocols33. Grubs were immediately transferred to aerated plastic containers and reared on sliced potatoes under controlled conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 70 ± 5% relative humidity, 12:12 h light: dark cycle) at the Division of Entomology, ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute, New Delhi34. To empty the gut contents, larvae were starved for 24 h prior to surface sterilization in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 1 min, followed by three rinses in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). Gut compartments (foregut, midgut, hindgut) were dissected aseptically in a laminar flow hood. Each compartment was sequentially washed in sterile PBS, 70% (v/v) ethanol (30 s), and PBS again to remove residual surface contaminants. Dissected tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until genomic DNA extraction35.

Genomic DNA was extracted from the third instar white grub species leg using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol36. DNA quality and integrity were verified by 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis, with quantification performed using a NanoDrop 3300 Fluoro spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to confirm purity (A260/A280: 1.8–2.0) and concentration (> 20 ng µl− 1). Mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (mtCOI) was amplified using primers LCO1490 (5′-GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG-3′) and HCO2198 (5′-TAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA-3′). PCR reactions (25 µL total volume) contained 12.5 µL 2× Emerald Amp GT PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio), 50 ng template DNA, 0.4 µM each primer, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling was conducted in a Bio-Rad C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA) with the following program: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 10 min; and hold at 4 °C. Amplicons (∼658 bp) were resolved on 1.2% (w/v) agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide under UV trans illumination. Confirmed products were purified and submitted to Green Genome Labs Pvt. Ltd. (India) for bidirectional Sanger sequencing37.

Isolation and enumeration of aerobic gut bacteria

Gut compartments were homogenized in 0.85% (w/v) NaCl (1:10 w/v) following established protocols38. Homogenates were serially diluted (10− 8) in 0.85% NaCl, and 100 µL aliquots from each dilution were spread-plated in triplicate onto five selective media (HiMedia): Nutrient Agar (NA), Tryptone Soy Agar (TSA), Pseudomonas Isolation Agar (PIA), Bacillus cereus Selective Agar (BCSA), and Nitrate Agar (NA). Plates were incubated aerobically at 28 °C for 24–48 h. Viable counts were enumerated as colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of gut tissue, calculated as:

Isolation and enumeration of anaerobic gut bacteria

Anaerobic bacteria from third-instar M. insanabilis larval guts were enumerated and isolated following established protocols. Foregut, midgut, and hindgut (fermentation chamber) were dissected aseptically, homogenized (1:10 w/v) in an anaerobic chamber (BACTRON 300, Shel Lab; 5% H2, 5% CO2, balance N2) pre-sterilized with 70% ethanol, and maintained under strict anaerobiosis. Homogenates were serially diluted (10− 7) in pre-reduced anaerobic diluent, and 100 µL aliquots were spread-plated in triplicate onto pre-reduced Thioglycolate medium (HiMedia; supplemented with 0.5 g L− 1 cysteine-HCl, pH 7.2). Plates were incubated anaerobically at 25 °C for 72 h. Viable anaerobic counts were calculated as CFU g− 1 gut tissue, using sterile diluent controls to verify anaerobiosis. Pure cultures were obtained by repeated streak-plating (3–5 cycles) on matching anaerobic media under the same conditions38.

Bacterial DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequence analysis

Single-colony isolates (aerobic and anaerobic) from M. insanabilis gut compartments were cultured in Nutrient Broth (NB; HiMedia) and Thioglycolate Broth (HiMedia), respectively, at 28 °C with shaking (150 rpm) for aerobes and static incubation for anaerobes. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (13,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and genomic DNA was extracted from pellets using a modified CTAB protocol. DNA quality was verified by 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis, with concentration and purity determined using a NanoDrop 3300 Fluoro spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; A260/A280: 1.8–2.0). Full-length 16 S rRNA genes were amplified using universal primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′)39. PCR reactions (25 µL) comprised 12.5 µL 2× Emerald Amp GT PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio), 50 ng template DNA, 0.4 µM each primer, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling (Bio-Rad C1000 Touch) entailed: 94 °C/5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C/30 s, 52 °C/40 s, 72 °C/90 s; 72 °C/10 min; 4 °C hold. Amplicons (∼1,465 bp) were confirmed by 1.2% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis with ethidium bromide staining. Purified products were sequenced directionally by Green Genome Labs Pvt. Ltd. (Delhi, India).

Phylogenetic analysis of culturable gut bacteria

Phylogenetic reconstruction employed the maximum-likelihood method under the Kimura-2-parameter model40, with tree robustness assessed by 1,000 bootstrap replicates (≥ 70% support threshold). Multiple sequence alignments were generated with default ClustalW settings in MEGA 1141. 16 S rRNA gene sequences from M. insanabilis bacterial isolates were processed, aligned, and analyzed using BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor v7.0.5.3 and MEGA 1142.

Metagenomics of gut bacteria

Third-instar M. insanabilis larvae were selected for metagenomic analysis of compartment-specific gut bacterial communities. To ensure comprehensive representation, gut compartments (foregut, midgut, hindgut/fermentation chamber) were pooled from three larvae each (n = 9 larvae total) representing one biological replicate with a total of nine larvae used in the study. Pooling is commonly used in insect gut microbiome studies where biomass is limited to obtain sufficient DNA for sequencing. Total bacterial community DNA was extracted from pooled gut compartments using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method43. DNA quality and integrity were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, with concentration and purity quantified using a NanoDrop 3300 Fluorospectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA; A260/A280: 1.8–2.0). The V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers V3F (5′-CCTACGGGNBGCASCAG-3′) and V4R (5′-GACTACNVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′)17. Amplicon library preparation and sequencing were performed by Novogene using standard protocols: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 15 cycles of 95 °C/30 s, 55 °C/30 s, 72 °C/30 s; final extension at 72 °C/5 min; followed by index PCR (11 cycles). Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp paired-end reads)44. Raw reads were deposited in NCBI Sequence Read Archive (BioProject PRJNA859284).

Functional annotation

Processed metagenomic reads were submitted to the MG-RAST v4.0 server45 for functional annotation against the KEGG Orthology (KO) database46,47. Nitrogen metabolism pathways were specifically interrogated, with key functional genes such as hzo (hydrazine oxidoreductase), amx (anammox), nifH (nitrogenase reductase) and nirS/nirK (nitrite reductases) were identified via KO assignments. Gene abundances were normalized as reads per kilobase million (RPKM) and mapped onto KEGG pathways to reconstruct nitrogen cycling processes, categorizing gut bacterial functions by their contributions to nitrogen fixation, nitrification, and denitrification.

Enzymatic assays for gut bacterial isolates of M. insanabilis

Nitrate (NO3−) reductase activity of gut bacterial isolates

Nitrate reductase activity was assayed to characterize anaerobic respiration potential among gut bacterial isolates, as nitrate reduction (NO3−→ NO2−→N2) distinguishes key denitrifying taxa48. Nitrate broth (1.5 gL− 1 in distilled water) was autoclaved (121 °C, 15 min), dispensed (10 mL aliquots) into sterile falcon tubes under laminar flow, and inoculated with loopfuls of pure cultures. Tubes were parafilm-sealed and incubated anaerobically at 25–30 °C for 24 h. Qualitative assays followed standard Griess protocol: 1 mL subsamples (including uninoculated controls) were transferred to Durham tubes; 5 drops each sulfanilic acid (Reagent A) and N-(1-naphthyl) ethylene diamine dihydrochloride (Reagent B) were added. Pink/red coloration within 5 min indicated nitrite accumulation (NO3−→ NO2−). Non-reactive cultures received Zn dust: persistent colourlessness confirmed reduction to N2→N2O49. Quantitative enzyme activity was determined spectrophotometrically. About 200 µL subsamples were analyzed at 540 nm including negative control using a microplate reader (BioTek), with nitrite concentrations calculated against NaNO2 standards (0-100 µM).

Nitrite (NO₂−) reductase activity of gut bacterial isolates

Nitrite reductase activity was assayed qualitatively and quantitatively for anaerobic gut isolates. Pure cultures were inoculated into sterile nitrite broth, parafilm-sealed, and incubated anaerobically (25 °C, 24–48 h). Growth-confirmed cultures (1 mL) were transferred to Durham tubes (with uninoculated controls), followed by addition of Griess reagents [sulfanilic acid (Reagent A) + N-(1-naphthyl) ethylene diamine dihydrochloride (Reagent B)]47. Absence of red/pink coloration indicated nitrite depletion (NO2 reduction), with N2 gas accumulation in Durham tubes providing confirmatory evidence of denitrification. Quantitative analysis employed 200 µL subsamples measured spectrophotometrically at 540 nm (microplate reader) against NaNO2 standards (0–100 µM). For the protein quantification, cell pellets from 5 mL cultures were resuspended in 1 mL lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100) and sonicated (3 × 30 s pulses, Bandelin Sonopuls). Soluble protein was quantified using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad Protein Assay Dye Reagent)50 with bovine serum albumin standards (0–1 mg mL⁻¹), read at 595 nm on a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy 2). Total protein per assay was 0.15–0.45 mg mL⁻¹ across isolates. Enzyme specific activity (nmol min− 1 mg− 1 protein) was determined in triplicate biological replicates (n ≥ 3) using the standard Michaelis-Menten formula calibrated against protein content (Bradford assay). nmol/min/mg protein = (Change in OD per minute × total reaction volume (mL) × 1000)/ (Extinction coefficient × path length × protein (mg).

Kinetics of nitrate and nitrite reductase

Kinetic parameters were determined for high-performance nitrate (NO₃⁻) and nitrite (NO₂⁻) reducing isolates using Michaelis-Menten analysis50. Pre-cultures were grown anaerobically (100 mL liquid medium, 250 µL headspace) at 30 °C for 48 h. Kinetic assays employed substrate-amended media (5–900 mg N L⁻¹) incubated at 30 °C. Samples were collected at 1–3 h intervals, filtered (0.45 μm), and analyzed spectrophotometrically. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3 biological replicates). Maximum reduction rates (Vmax, nmol min⁻¹ mL⁻¹) and half-saturation constants (Km, nM) were derived from Lineweaver-Burk double-reciprocal plots fitted to the Michaelis-Menten equation: 1/V = Km/Vmax⋅1/S + 1/Vmax, where V represents the reaction rate, and S represents the initial nitrate and nitrite (substrate) concentration. The Km and Vmax values were derived51.

Characterization of selected nitrogen cycling genes in the gut compartments of M. insanabilis

Maintenance of M. insanabilis culture

Pot experiments were conducted over 31 days to evaluate expression of nitrogen-cycling genes in M. insanabilis larvae, following established protocols52. Experimental pots were sterilized with 70% (v/v) ethanol prior to setup to prevent cross-contamination. Native soil from white grub-infested sugarcane fields in Ghaziabad, India, was used as the base medium (three-quarters pot capacity). Treatments comprised unamended soil (control), farmyard manure (FYM), and vermicompost (VC), with standardized M. insanabilis third-instar densities evenly distributed across replicates (n = 6 per treatment). Pots were maintained under controlled glasshouse conditions optimal for grub development [26 ± 2 °C, 70 ± 5% RH, 12:12 h L: D], with soil moisture adjusted to 20–25% gravimetric water content via daily weighing and irrigation as needed. Drainage holes ensured non-waterlogged conditions throughout.

RNA Isolation from guts of M. insanabilis using RNAzol method

Third-instar M. insanabilis larvae reared in naturally infested soil (Nat_soil), farmyard manure (FYM), and vermicompost treatments. The larvae were starved for 24 h prior to dissection. Whole guts were surface-sterilized in 70% (v/v) ethanol, then dissected aseptically in a laminar flow hood using sterile micro scissors to isolate foregut (FG), midgut (MG), and hindgut (HG) compartments for RNA extraction and gene expression analysis. Dissected compartments were sequentially rinsed [sterile PBS (30 s), 70% ethanol (60 s), PBS (30 s)] to eliminate residual disinfectants, then transferred to sterile 1.5 mL tubes containing 0.85% (w/v) NaCl. Foregut (FG), midgut (MG), and hindgut (HG) tissues were homogenized in 500 µL RNAzol RT at 4°C53. The gut homogenates were centrifuged (12,000 × g, 5 min, 25 °C) to remove the upper lipid layer. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, mixed with 400 µL DEPC-treated water, vortexed (15 s), and incubated at room temperature (10 min). Samples were centrifuged (15 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were precipitated with an equal volume of ice-cold isopropanol (RT, 10 min), followed by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min, 25 °C). RNA pellets were washed twice with 500 µL 75% (v/v) ethanol (8,000 × g, 5 min, RT), air-dried, and resuspended in nuclease-free DEPC-treated water. Tubes were vortexed (2–5 min, 25 °C), briefly centrifuged, and incubated (40 °C, 15 min) for complete dissolution. RNA purity and concentration were quantified using a NanoDrop 3300 Fluorospectrometer (A260/A280: 1.8–2.1; A260/A230: >2.0).

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Expression of nitrogen-cycling genes (hzo, amx, nifH, nirS, nirK) was quantified by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) using RPL13a and TUBb as reference genes (Table S1). Total RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA following the manufacturer’s protocol. Reactions were performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR system using 2× SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each 10 µL reaction contained 5 µL 2× SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.5 µL each primer (500 nM final), 1 µL cDNA (10 ng), and 3 µL nuclease-free water. Primer sequences and annealing temperatures are detailed in Table S1. Standard cycling conditions comprised: 95 °C/3 min; 40 cycles of [95 °C/15 s, 55 °C/30 s, 72 °C/30 s]; melt curve analysis (65–95 °C). For nirS/nirK, conditions were adjusted to: 95 °C/10 min; 40 cycles of [95 °C/15 s, 60 °C/60 s, 72 °C/30 s], fluorescence acquired post-extension54.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

The α and β diversity across different gut compartments were performed using R software. Alpha diversity indices like Chao1, Pielou’s evenness (J′J′)55, observed OTUs/ASVs, and Shannon (H′H′)56 were calculated using phyloseq (v1.46.0) and vegan (v2.6-4) packages in R (v4.3.0), Wilcoxon/Kruskal-Wallis tests (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05p < 0.05). Beta diversity was quantified with unweighted Jaccard, weighted Bray-Curtis, unweighted/weighted UniFrac (FastTree2 phylogenies)57, PCA, PCoA and nMDS using phyloseq and vegan in R58,59. The diversity indices were estimated with the QIIME2 pipeline. Raw FASTQ reads were demultiplexed and quality filtered using the FLASH (v1.2.11, http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/), high-quality clean tags were created with the fastp application; the VSearch software was used to blast clean tags into the database to identify and remove chimera, retrieving the final appropriate data or effective tags. De-noising was done using DADA2 for the bioinformatic analysis, which was carried out using QIIME260. ASVs outperform OTUs regarding precision, accuracy and repeatability when analyzing marker gene data. With the classify-learn approach of QIIME2, a pre-trained Naive Bayes classifier was utilized. All data obtained on the specific activity of nitrate and nitrite reductases were subjected to descriptive statistics and analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SAS software61. Similarly, the qRT-PCR experiments were conducted in triplicate, with at least three independent biological replicates. The qPCR efficiency and sample threshold values were used to calculate the normalized expression ratio of the selected nitrogen-cycling genes relative to RPL13a and TUBb using the 2^−ΔΔCT method. Relative gene expression data were presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical significance was assessed using a two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software62.

The robustness of the phylogenetic clustering supported the reliability of the inferred relationships among the gut bacterial taxa.

Isolation and enumeration of aerobic and anaerobic gut bacteria

The gut bacteria from different compartments of third-instar M. insanabilis grubs were isolated using a culture-dependent approach under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. A total of 16 pure aerobic bacterial isolates and 8 pure anaerobic bacterial isolates were obtained. Enumeration of these isolates was performed on six different media, and the results were expressed as colony-forming units (CFU) per milliliter. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) in CFU counts across the gut compartments. Aerobic bacterial isolates from the foregut and midgut exhibited the highest CFU count on nitrate agar media (3.070 ± 0.116a), whereas the hindgut showed the highest CFU count on Pseudomonas isolation agar (3.467 ± 0.173a). ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD indicated that the CFU count on nitrate agar media was statistically on par with that on Pseudomonas isolation agar. These findings highlight compartment-specific variations in bacterial abundance, suggesting distinct microbial communities adapted to different gut environments. The mean CFU values for all gut bacterial isolates are presented in Table S2, along with statistical groupings. Taxonomic analysis of the aerobic isolates revealed that Bacillota was the most dominant phylum, represented by six bacterial isolates, followed by Pseudomonadota and Bacillota, each with five isolates. For anaerobic bacterial isolates, the CFU count was significantly higher in the hindgut (3.500 ± 0.031a, p < 0.05), indicating a more favourable environment for an anaerobic bacterial proliferation in this gut compartment.

Diversity of culturable aerobic and anaerobic gut bacteria

Isolation of aerobic and anaerobic gut bacteria from different compartments of M. insanabilis revealed distinct taxonomic distributions, with phylum-level nomenclature following the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) release R22063. Among the aerobic isolates, Bacillota was the most dominant phylum, represented by six bacterial isolates, followed by Pseudomonadota and Bacillota, each with five isolates. In contrast, among the anaerobic isolates, Bacillota was the predominant phylum with six isolates, while Pseudomonadota accounted for two isolates. A total of 16 aerobic and 8 anaerobic gut bacterial isolates identified in this study had their 16 S rRNA sequences submitted to GenBank and assigned accession numbers (Tables S3 and S4).

Phylogenetic analysis of culturable aerobic and anaerobic gut bacteria

The sequences of 16 aerobic and 8 anaerobic gut bacteria with high-quality, non-redundant, non-repetitive with uniqueness were selected for further examination. Comparative BLAST analysis of most of the bacterial isolates showed 100% resemblance to their closest relative retrieved from the GenBank database. The evolutionary distances were calculated using the Kimura 2-parameter method and the phylogenetic tree was examined using the Neighbor-Joining method. Seven distinct clades were seen in the phylogenetic tree of the aerobic gut bacterial isolates from M. insanabilis (Fig. 1b). The phylogenetic tree of the gut bacteria of M. insanabilis showed six different clades. Three clades of the phylogenetic tree belong to the group Bacillota with the genus Bacillus (one isolate), Lysinbacillus (one isolate) and Brevibacillus (two isolates) where two clades belong to the group Bacillota represented by the predominant genus Bacillus (six isolates) and Priestia (one isolate). Another major clade is represented by the group Pseudomonadota with the genus Brucella (four isolates) and Serratia (one isolate). The anaerobic gut bacterial isolates form three different clades (Fig. 1c). Bacillus is the most predominant gut habitant of M. insanabilis, accounting for five isolates, followed by Staphylococcus, one isolate and the phylum Pseudomonadota, which contains two isolates of the genus Burkholderia.

Gut microbial diversity based on metagenomics

The metagenomic sequencing analysis of M. insanabilis gut compartments using the Illumina MiSeq™ platform revealed distinct microbial diversity and composition across the foregut, midgut, and hindgut. The midgut exhibited the highest total sequence read length (136,796 bp), followed by the foregut (121,050 bp) and hindgut (120,008 bp). Sequence quality was high, with 89% of bases exceeding Q30 (error rate 0.1%) and 96% surpassing Q20 (error rate 1%). The GC content was highest in the foregut (55.55%), indicating potential differences in microbial community composition (Table S5). Taxonomic followed the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) release R22063 via relative abundance histograms showed Pseudomonadota (= Proteobacteria) as the dominant phylum across all M. insanabilis gut compartments, with a decreasing trend from foregut (90.68%) > hindgut (80.36%) > midgut (68.33%) (P = 0.25, Wilcoxon rank-sum test; Fig. 2a). Bacillota (= Firmicutes) trended highest in hindgut (10.84%) > midgut (5.98%) > foregut (2.23%) (P = 0.25), while Bacteroidota showed a foregut preference (7.22%) > midgut (3.65%) > hindgut (1.01%) (P = 0.25). Other phyla detected included Desulfobacteriota, Euryarcheota, Cyanobacteria, Synergistota, Actinobacteriota, Halobacteria, and Chloroflexi. At the genus level, key taxa such as Burkholderia, Pseudomonas, Bacteroides, Desulfovibrio, Clostridia, Christensenella, Klebsiella, Escherichia, and Candidatus sp. exhibited compositional variation across compartments (overall P = 0.25, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Fig. 2b).

Characterization of nitrogen cycling genes

The study investigated the relative expression levels of five selected nitrogen cycling genes viz., hzo, amx, nifH, nirS, and nirK in the foregut, midgut, and hindgut respectively. The naturally infested soil served as control, whereas, farm yard manure and vermicompost served as treatment respectively (Table S9). The relative expression of the hzo gene exhibited significant differences (p < 0.05) across treatments and gut compartments. In the foregut, naturally infested soil showed the lowest expression 0.846 ± 0.53f, which was significantly lower than both farmyard manure 2.34 ± 0.51e and vermicompost 2.97 ± 0.51d. In the midgut, a similar trend was observed, with expression levels at 1.08 ± 0.52f for naturally infested soil, significantly increasing to 3.17 ± 0.50d for farmyard manure and further to 4.97 ± 0.48c for vermicompost. The hindgut exhibited the highest expression values, with naturally infested soil at 2.22 ± 0.52e, significantly increasing to 7.78 ± 0.46b with farmyard manure and peaking at 10.23 ± 0.46a for vermicompost (Fig. 8a). The statistical analysis confirms that vermicompost significantly enhances hzo gene expression (p < 0.05) across all gut compartments, with the highest upregulation observed in the hindgut. This enhanced expression suggests increased microbial activity, reinforcing the hindgut’s critical role in nitrogen cycling efficiency. The results indicate significant variations (p < 0.05) in the relative expression of the amx gene across different gut compartments in response to various treatments (Fig. 8b). The naturally infested soil resulted in moderate expression levels, with values of 1.13 ± 0.748f in the foregut, 1.69 ± 0.73ef in the midgut, and 1.90 ± 0.72e in the hindgut, which was significantly lower than other treatments. In contrast, farmyard manure treatment led to increased gene expression, particularly in the hindgut 7.15 ± 0.65b, indicating a substantial enhancement in microbial activity. The vermicompost treatment exhibited the highest relative expression across all gut compartments, with a particularly notable increase in the hindgut 8.85 ± 0.74a, demonstrating its significant role in promoting amx gene activity (p < 0.05) (Table S10). Overall, vermicompost treatment resulted in a statistically significant upregulation of amx gene expression across all gut compartments, emphasizing its potential to optimize nitrogen cycling via the anammox process.

Discussion

In the broader context of nitrogen cycling, insects play an important role in transforming nitrogen within terrestrial ecosystems. Many soil-dwelling insects harbour diverse gut microbiota that participate in key nitrogen-cycling processes such as nitrogen fixation, ammonification, nitrification, and denitrification. These microbial activities help convert atmospheric or organic nitrogen into forms that can be utilized by the host insect. Within this framework, the white grub M. insanabilis, a root-feeding scarab larva commonly found in agricultural soils, may represents a relevant model for studying insect-mediated nitrogen transformations. Its compartmentalized gut provides distinct microenvironments that support specialized microbial communities capable of carrying out different nitrogen-cycling functions. Understanding these microbial interactions helps clarify the ecological role of M. insanabilis in nutrient dynamics. Understanding the structural diversity and functional role of gut bacterial communities within insect gut has significant implications for nutrient cycling and pest adaptation in nutrient-limited ecosystems. In this study, we employed an integrative approach combining culture-dependent, metagenomics, enzymatic assays, and gene expression analysis to explore the diversity, structure, and functionality of gut microbiota in M. insanabilis, an important scarabaeid pest in semi-arid agroecosystems. Our findings provide compelling evidence that the gut of M. insanabilis supports a phylogenetically diverse and functionally specialized bacterial community actively involved in nitrogen transformations, with significant implications for insect physiology, host-microbe interactions, and nutrient turnover.

Accurate identification of larval stages, often morphologically cryptic, was achieved through mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (mtCOI) barcoding. The high sequence similarity of M. insanabilis COI genes with globally distributed populations underscores the reliability of molecular taxonomy and indicates potential panmictic behavior across regions, likely driven by passive dispersal or anthropogenic movement64–66. This robust phylogenetic validation ensures taxonomic precision for downstream microbiome analyses and enables cross-regional comparison for pest management strategies.

Our culture-based investigations revealed that the gut of M. insanabilis harbours a diverse assemblage of bacteria, predominantly belonging to the phyla Pseudomonadota and Bacillota. Among the culturable aerobic isolates, 16 sequences exhibited up to 99% similarity to the closest relatives in the NCBI database, confirming their taxonomic affiliation with well-known bacterial groups. Notably, the majority of these aerobic isolates were classified within Bacillota (37.5%) and Pseudomonadota (31.25%). These findings are consistent with previous reports on white grubs such as Anomala dimidiata, where similar dominant taxa were identified35. Further, earlier studies on Holotrichia paralella demonstrated that a substantial proportion of aerobic gut bacteria were classified within the γ-Proteobacteria, with Bacillota also being highly prevalent67. In support of these observations, virtually all cultivable aerobes detected in insect digestive tracts and environmental samples are attributed to Pseudomonadota, highlighting the ecological ubiquity of this phylum. In addition to aerobic isolates, our analysis of anaerobic bacteria yielded eight distinct isolates. One particular anaerobic isolate designated WG-AN-MG5, exhibited a 97% sequence similarity with its closest GenBank match, suggesting that it may possess unique genetic and functional characteristics compared to previously described taxa. This divergence could imply the presence of novel metabolic capabilities or ecological adaptations specific to the gut environment of M. insanabilis. Collectively, these results underscore the diverse microbial ecosystem within the white grub gut. The significant similarity of culturable isolates to known bacterial taxa, coupled with the detection of potentially novel anaerobes, provides an important insight into the gut bacterial assemblages that may support nitrogen cycling and overall gut function in M. insanabilis. Such diversity may likely contribute to the insect’s ability to thrive on nutrient-poor diets by facilitating critical biochemical processes, thereby enhancing host fitness and adaptability.

High-throughput 16 S rRNA gene sequencing provides valuable insights into the gut microbiota of M. insanabilis, offering a culture-independent view beyond traditional methods limited to cultivable taxa. Our data align with patterns in related wood-feeding insects: Pseudomonadota dominated Agrilus mali microbiomes (99.7%)68 alongside Bacillota (= formerly Firmicutes), Acidobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes; shared genera like Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Acinetobacter, Trabulsiella, and Kluyvera appeared across termites69. The emerald ash borer gut similarly featured high Pseudomonadota (= formerly Proteobacteria) (44%) and Bacillota (38%)70. In our pooled samples, Pseudomonadota trended highest in foregut (90.68%) > hindgut (80.36%) > midgut (68.33%), with Bacillota showing a hindgut bias (10.84% vs. 5.98% midgut, 2.23% foregut) non-significant trends (P = 0.25, Wilcoxon) of compartment patterns. In Coptotermes formosanus termites, OTU enrichment followed a foregut > hindgut > midgut gradient71. Due to pooling limitations (three larvae per compartment, n = 9 total), these findings offer exploratory trends of spatial variation potentially linked to gut compartmentalization, diet, and subterranean habitats.

Bacterial alpha and beta diversity analysis

Our study provides a comprehensive examination of the microbial diversity and community structure within the gut of M. insanabilis. The data revealed that the hindgut exhibits the highest microbial diversity and evenness, as evidenced by Pielou’s evenness values (0.434, 0.547, and 0.621) and Shannon diversity indices (3.717, 4.86, and 5.589) across the foregut, midgut, and hindgut, respectively. This pattern is consistent with observations in other scarab beetles such as Pelidnota luridipes, where hindgut communities displayed elevated bacterial diversity compared to other gut compartments72. The pronounced gradient in microbial diversity from the foregut to the hindgut likely reflects the specialized functional adaptations driven by the anatomical and environmental compartmentalization of the insect gut. In scarab larvae, distinct pH gradients, where the midgut typically exhibits high alkalinity and the hindgut maintains a near-neutral pH73 create niche environments that are differentially selected for microbial taxa. These physicochemical conditions are critical in shaping the metabolic processes required for complex digestion, nutrient absorption, and efficient nitrogen cycling. Previous studies have demonstrated that such spatial variation in microbial composition correlates strongly with the host’s digestive needs and overall physiology74.

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on both weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances revealed distinct clustering of microbial communities in the foregut, midgut, and hindgut, underscoring significant differences in species diversity and community structure. The hindgut, in particular, was identified as the most diverse region, aligning with its role as a primary site for microbial fermentation and nitrogen transformation. Similar organ-specific adaptations have been reported in studies on Bactrocera minax, where the microbiota differs significantly between gut tissues and reproductive organs75 and in dung beetles such as Pachysoma endroedyi and Pachysoma striatum, which share a limited number of bacterial OTUs76. These findings suggest that the gut bacterial composition in M. insanabilis is finely tuned to meet the distinct physiological and ecological requirements of each gut compartment.

MG-RAST and KEGG pathway analyses of M. insanabilis gut microbiota predicted genetic potential for nitrogen cycling among anaerobic bacteria, with ~ 80% of annotated groups carrying associated pathways across compartments. Taxa including Burkholderia cepacia, Burkholderia contaminans, Bacillus tequilensis, Bacillus subtilis, and Bacillus licheniformis were identified as harboring genes linked to nitrification, denitrification, and nitrogen fixation representing potential rather than confirmed metabolic activity. These bioinformatically predicted profiles align with patterns in lignocellulose-feeding insects, where gut microbes are hypothesized to supplement host nitrogen on poor diets (e.g., termites, scarab beetles)76,77. Similarly, nitrogen-cycling genes have been noted in passalid beetle (Odontotaenius disjunctus) microbiomes associated with wood degradation78. The predicted nitrogen-cycling pathways in M. insanabilis gut microbiota align with genetic potentials observed across detritivore insects, potentially reflecting conserved strategies for nitrogen acquisition from nutrient-poor diets79. Given the limitations of 16S-based functional predictions and pooled sampling, these findings suggest candidate roles for nitrogen transformation warranting validation through metatranscriptomics/metaproteomics and independent biological replicates (≥ 3 per compartment). These hypothesis-generates findings that highlight microbial nitrogen transformation as a target for future research into detritivore physiology and pest management approaches.

Our study revealed that the majority of gut bacterial isolates from M. insanabilis exhibit robust nitrate and nitrite reductase activity, with anaerobic strains such as Burkholderia contaminans and B. cepacia displaying particularly high activity levels. The capacity for nitrate ammonification where NO₃⁻ is converted into more stable nitrite or further reduced to bioavailable ammonium (NH₄⁺) is critical for conserving nitrogen, especially given the nitrogen-poor organic substrates that scarab larvae typically consume. Such dissimilatory nitrate reduction, resulting in high NH₄⁺ to NO₃⁻ ratios, has been observed in other scarab beetles, underscoring the importance of the gut environment in nitrogen retention80, 81. Furthermore, our findings align with previous reports indicating that both aerobic and anaerobic gut bacteria significantly contribute to nitrogen cycling in related white grub species such as Anomala dimidiata35. Collectively, these results underscore the pivotal role of gut microbiota in maintaining nitrogen homeostasis within M. insanabilis. The high enzymatic activity observed suggests that these microbial communities not only support the host’s nitrogen economy but also enhance its adaptability in nitrogen-limited environments.

The nitrate and nitrite reduction kinetics of Burkholderia contaminans and B. cepacia were characterized using Michaelis-Menten models, with the Lineweaver-Burk plots demonstrating excellent correlations (R² = 0.9871 and R² = 0.947, respectively). These results confirm that both strains possess high capacities and affinities for nitrate and nitrite reduction, which enhances the overall nitrogen-processing efficiency within the gut of M. insanabilis. The Km value determined for B. cepacia during nitrite reduction falls within the range typical for denitrifiers (2–12 mM) and nitrate ammonifiers (0.5–500 mM) as reported by82–86. Moreover, the nitrite-reducing capability of B. cepacia is consistent with that observed in other efficient nitrate-reducing bacteria such as Alcaligenes and Flavobacterium species, which exhibit Km values of approximately 12.9 ± 2.6 µM and 5.6 ± 1.6 µM, respectively87. This efficiency is critical for nitrogen retention, particularly in environments where ammonium (NH₄⁺) production is essential to support growth under nutrient-limited conditions. The ability of these bacteria to reduce nitrite efficiently is fundamental to the adaptive strategy of insect gut microbiomes, as evidenced by studies in wood-feeding termites where the gut microbiota evolves to utilize nitrogen substrates to meet the host’s nutritional requirements88,89. Similarly, research on white grubs such as Anomala dimidiata has shown that anaerobic bacteria contribute significantly to nitrogen homeostasis in the gut35. In dung beetles and other scarab species, active nitrate and nitrite reduction, along with ammonification, maintain nitrogen levels that are beneficial for host metabolism and survival90. Collectively, these findings underscore the evolutionary importance of microbial symbioses in managing nitrogen within insect gut. The robust nitrate and nitrite reduction capacities observed in B. cepacia and related strains enable M. insanabilis to thrive on nitrogen-poor diets by efficiently recycling nitrogenous compounds. This microbial-mediated nitrogen retention is pivotal for maintaining host nutrition and overall fitness, reinforcing the critical ecological role of gut bacteria in nutrient cycling.

In this study, we comprehensively characterized the expression of key nitrogen-cycling genes hzo, amx, nifH, nirS, and nirK across different gut compartments of M. insanabilis. Our results indicate that the hindgut, followed by the midgut, serves as the primary site for nitrogen-cycling activity. Treatments with substrates such as farmyard manure (FYM) and vermicompost significantly upregulated the expression of these genes, suggesting that organic amendments enhance the functional capacity of the gut microbiome. The elevated expression of nifH, a gene encoding dinitrogenase reductase and a well-established marker for nitrogen fixation, mirrors findings in Odontotaenius disjunctus, where the anterior hindgut was identified as the predominant site for nifH expression79. This reinforces the critical role of nitrogen-fixing bacteria in supplying bioavailable nitrogen to the host, particularly under nutrient-limited conditions91, 92. Our analysis also revealed a widespread distribution of the nirS gene within the Gamma-proteobacteria, consistent with its established role in denitrification. The positive correlation between nirS abundance and enhanced denitrification activity as well as increased N₂O emissions supports its functional significance in nitrogen cycling93–95. Notably, the relative abundance of the nirK gene in the grub gut (30%) was higher than in the parent soil (20%), further underscoring the gut’s active contribution to denitrification and its influence on nitrogen dynamics. In addition, the hzo gene, which encodes hydrazine oxidoreductase, was prominently expressed in the hindgut. This enzyme is pivotal in the anammox process, which facilitates nitrogen removal under anaerobic conditions96, 97. Complementary to this, the amx gene often used as a marker for anammox bacteria was also highly expressed, supporting recent isotope-tracking studies that have demonstrated the dominance of anammox pathways in microbial nitrogen cycling98–100. Collectively, these gene expression profiles illustrate a finely tuned spatial organization of nitrogen-cycling processes within the M. insanabilis gut. The enhanced expression of nitrification, denitrification and nitrogen-fixing genes in the hindgut (anoxic fermentation chamber) suggests that this compartment not only supports a more diverse microbial community but also provides an optimal environment for complex nitrogen transformations. This microbial-mediated nitrogen cycling is likely essential for maintaining host nitrogen balance, particularly in the context of nitrogen-limited diets, and may have significant implications for pest management strategies by modulating host nutrient acquisition. These findings underscore the intricate role of gut bacteria in mediating nitrogen transformations and highlight the potential for leveraging microbial symbioses to enhance insect fitness and develop innovative approaches to sustainable pest management.

Conclusion

This study provides the first integrative insight into the spatial organization and functional capacity of gut microbiota in M. insanabilis larvae, revealing a compartment-specific distribution of bacterial communities with pronounced functional specialization in the hindgut. The results demonstrate that anaerobic bacteria residing in the hindgut such as Burkholderia, Paenibacillus, Desulfovibrio, and Bacteroides are key contributors to nitrogen cycling processes, including nitrification, denitrification, and nitrogen fixation, thereby supporting larval development in nitrogen-deficient soils. Culture-dependent and metagenomic approaches, combined with enzymatic assays and gene expression analyses, confirmed the differential distribution of these taxa across gut regions, along with their high enzymatic activity and elevated expression of nitrogen-cycling genes (hzo, amx, nifH, nirS, and nirK), particularly in response to organic substrates like vermicompost. This metabolic flexibility underscores the ecological relevance of hindgut microbes in nutrient acquisition. By elucidating microbial composition, functional capacity, enzymatic activity, and gene expression patterns, our findings highlight the pivotal role of the gut microbiota in M. insanabilis nitrogen homeostasis. Long-term research could build on these basic insights into gut bacteria in Maladera insanabilis including microbial causality, host fitness effects, and ecological specificity in nutrient-poor environment towards applied approaches for microbiome manipulation in pest management. Such applications remain speculative and require extensive validation through targeted experiments. These findings provide an empirical baseline for both fundamental understanding of insect-microbe symbioses and potential biocontrol strategies as a part of sustainable pest management.
