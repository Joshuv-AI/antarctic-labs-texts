Proton-gated multisite concerted proton-electron transfer-like pathway in a dual-atom framework for selective water purification
Authors: Sihan Zhou, Yun Yang, Fengzhi Jiang, Shiping Zhang, Ruidong Wang, Xiaoyong Wu, Sihui Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise oxidant activation is essential for advanced water treatment but is often limited by radical quenching and low oxidant efficiency. Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) is promising; however, directing O-O cleavage towards non-radical pathways requires tightly coupled proton‒electron transfer, which is rarely achieved in heterogeneous catalysts because of spatially mismatched active sites. Here, we report a Co/Ni dual-single-atom metal–organic framework (MOF), in which atomically dispersed Co/Ni sites and adjacent noncoordinated -NH groups form a preorganized local microenvironment for PMS activation. Combined experimental and computational evidence suggests that Ni-assisted electronic coupling facilitates charge redistribution towards Co-bound PMS, while adjacent noncoordinated -NH motifs provide a local proton-relay environment, collectively favoring an MS-CPET-like pathway. This pathway promotes heterolytic O–O cleavage and the formation of a high-valent cobalt–oxo intermediate (Co(IV) = O). Consequently, the catalyst delivers a normalized rate constant of 6.2 × 103 M−1 min−1 with more than 95% PMS consumption under the tested conditions. Remarkably, the system operates stably for more than 1000 h under continuous flow, with a treatment capacity exceeding 1000 L. This work demonstrates a promising strategy for achieving non-radical-dominated and sustainable water purification through electronic regulation and -NH proton coupling within the framework of bimetallic MOFs.

Introduction

Precision oxidation lies at the heart of advanced water purification; however, achieving high selectivity and oxidant utilization remains a formidable challenge. Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) has emerged as a promising oxidant; however, its activation typically proceeds via homolytic cleavage of the peroxide (O−O) bond, generating nonselective radicals (SO4·-/·OH) that are rapidly quenched by background matrix constituents1–4. In contrast, high-valent metal–oxo (M = O) oxidants are generally more tolerant to matrix interference, enabling more selective oxidation in complex waters5,6. While shifting from radical-based pathways to M = O non-radical pathways can fundamentally overcome these limitations, the efficient formation of M = O species is kinetically difficult and urgently needed.

In biological systems (e.g., cytochrome P450), the kinetically demanding O−O bond activation/cleavage towards the formation of the M = O oxidant (Compound I-like species) is orchestrated by proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET), where specific amino acid residues act as “proton channels” to stabilize the leaving group synchronously with electron injection7–10. Inspired by the enzymatic paradigm of coupled proton‒electron transfer, a central challenge in PMS activation lies in endowing heterogeneous catalysts with localized proton management capability to steer O–O Bond cleavage towards the selective generation of M = O species. Metal‒organic frameworks (MOFs) offer an ideal platform for addressing this challenge; their ordered porosity can locally enrich PMS and shorten diffusion pathways, thereby improving the apparent activation efficiency11–13. Moreover, the pore-confined microenvironment (e.g., local electrostatics and hydrogen bonding) may regulate the adsorption configuration and electron/proton transfer of PMS, thereby affecting the kinetics of O–O bond scission2,14. However, even advanced designs—such as dual-single-atom MOFs (DSA-MOFs), which demonstrate exceptional electronic tunability via bimetallic synergy15,16, remain predominantly “electron-centric”. They typically lack intrinsic proton channels; instead, they rely on stochastic solvent protonation, which is kinetically mismatched with rapid interfacial electron transfer. This disconnection leads to uncontrolled O−O scission and competitive radical formation17,18. To bridge this gap, we propose a multisite synergistic concerted proton‒electron transfer (MS-CPET) strategy19. We hypothesize that placing a proton-donating group near a redox-active center can favor coupled reaction coordination, in which electron injection into the O−O antibonding orbital is accompanied by proton delivery to the leaving oxygen atom. Such spatial preorganization may reduce the accumulation of high-energy charged intermediates typical of stepwise electron-transfer (ET) or proton transfer (PT) routes20,21. Theoretically, this synergy can reduce the kinetic and thermodynamic requirements for heterolytic O−O cleavage while suppressing competitive homolytic radical pathways22. Notably, this integrated design contrasts sharply with conventional “functional patchwork” approaches, where external acids/bases are merely paired with oxidants or metal sites to separately mediate proton/electron transfer or with mechanistic descriptions confined to single-center PCET or stepwise ET/PT frameworks19,20,23. In our system, proton donors (noncoordinated -NH) and redox-active centers are preorganized within the MOFs near the angstrom level, thereby favoring an MS-CPET-like PMS activation pathway and preferentially generating Co(IV) = O.

Here, we report the implementation of this concept in nitrogen-rich bimetallic MOFs, where a transferable -NH proton donor is placed near the redox-active Co center and electron-coupled Ni site, which can activate PMS towards the proton-coupled heterolytic O−O cleavage pathway. In this design, Co acts as the main PMS anchor point, Ni acts as the electron pool within the framework that promotes charge redistribution, and adjacent noncoordinated -NH groups provide local proton relay motifs for the distal peroxide oxygen. We used isotope effects, pH-dependent electrochemistry, fs-TA spectroscopy, and comparative density functional theory (DFT) calculations to evaluate whether this preorganized Co/Ni-NH microenvironment is more inclined towards the MS-CPET-like pathway than towards possible stepwise PT/ET or ET/PT alternatives, which involve a synergistic Oβ···H···N transition state. This design endows the catalyst with favorable kinetics (k ≈ 6.2 × 103 min−1·M−1), near-quantitative oxidant utilization ( > 95%), and excellent stability in continuous flow ( > 1000 h). This work establishes a material design approach that involves the “proton gating” capability of solid-state catalysts, achieving highly selective non-radical oxidation for sustainable water treatment.

Results

DFT-guided screening identifies the optimal Co/Ni pairing and activity descriptors

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, Co/Ni-MOF was designed to integrate a Co-centered PMS adsorption/activation site, a neighboring Ni site for framework-mediated electronic modulation, and a noncoordinated -NH motif for local proton assistance. This preorganized Co/Ni-NH microenvironment is expected to promote PMS preorganization and bias O−O bond activation towards a proton-coupled, Co(IV) = O-forming pathway. Guided by this design rationale, DFT calculations were performed to screen different heterometallic pairings and identify the electronic descriptors associated with PMS activation. To identify the optimal heterometallic pairing with strictly comparable structures, an isostructural MOF series was employed as a model platform (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1), and DFT calculations were performed for multiple M1/M2-MOFs (Co/Ni, Co/Mn, Ni/Mn, Co/Zn, Zn/Mn, and Ni/Zn) to evaluate the interfacial electron-transport capability and PMS activation ability. Band structure analysis revealed that Co/Ni-MOF has the smallest band gap (Eg ≈ 0.15 eV; Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2; underlying electronic-structure data are provided in Supplementary Data 2), suggesting enhanced electronic delocalization and potentially more favorable charge transport. Because an adequate electron supply is a prerequisite for PMS activation, PMS adsorption and interfacial charge transfer were subsequently quantified. Co/Ni provides both the most favorable adsorption energy (Eads = −1.20 eV) and the greatest amount of electrons for injection into PMS ( | q | = 0.82 e) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3). By comparison, monometallic Co-MOF has a much larger band gap (0.74 eV; Supplementary Fig. 4a) and weaker interactions with PMS (Eads = -0.62 eV; |q | = 0.54 e; Supplementary Fig. 4 b-d), highlighting the necessity of bimetallic synergy to enhance the electron-injection capability24. Removing the -NH functionality further weakens PMS adsorption and charge transfer in the Co-MOF analog (Supplementary Fig. 4e), indicating that the -NH proton gate not only provides a directional proton delivery site but also reinforces the PMS anchoring and interfacial coupling required for efficient bimetallic electron injection. To rationalize why Co/Ni more readily inject electrons and promote peroxide (O−O) activation, the orbital coupling near Ef was analyzed. Before PMS adsorption, Co/Ni-MOF displays the most pronounced states at Ef and the strongest d-p hybridization (Fig. 1d), which is consistent with an efficient electronic coupling channel that facilitates ET to PMS25. Density-of-states (DOS) analysis further revealed adsorption-induced shifts of the d-band centers that suggest a “push−pull” division of labor: upon PMS binding, the second metal (M2) shifted markedly downwards, stabilizing the adsorbates/intermediates, whereas the first metal (M1) shifted only slightly upwards and remained close to Ef, preserving the reactive states (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, the M2 shift is much larger for Co/Ni than for Co/Mn (1.17 eV vs. 0.479 eV), whereas the M1 shift remains modest (0.127 eV vs. 0.096 eV), which is consistent with a synergistic scenario in which Ni tunes the electronic structure while Co retains catalytic reactivity. These predictions were experimentally validated using the same isostructural bimetallic MOF series. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) confirmed highly consistent crystallographic features across the six materials (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Their activities towards SDZ degradation were subsequently evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 6b) using the pseudo-first-order rate constant (kobs) as the activity descriptor, and Co/Ni-MOF with -NH gating exhibited the best PMS activation ability. Correlating kobs with the calculated parameters shows moderate relationships with the PMS adsorption energy (R2 = 0.74) and band gap (R2 = 0.61), whereas the strongest linear relationship is obtained for the interfacial charge-transfer magnitude |q| (R2 = 0.83) (Fig. 1f), indicating that electron-injection capability is the dominant descriptor governing intrinsic activity in the MOF-PMS systems. Consistently, the results of the PMS consumption measurements further indicate that Co/Ni achieves the highest utilization among all the samples (Supplementary Fig. 6c), which is in line with the prediction of it having the strongest electron injection and hybridization. This combined validation of “high activity + high utilization” motivates the selection of Co/Ni as the central platform for subsequent structural characterization and mechanistic investigations. These calculated parameters are used here as screening descriptors that correlate with activity trends rather than as standalone proof of the complete catalytic mechanism.

Solvothermal construction and atomic-level identification of Co/Ni dual single-atom sites

The single crystal reveals a well-defined Co/Ni coordination environment in which the metal sites are coordinated by N/O atoms and positioned near noncoordinated -NH motifs. This spatial arrangement provides the structural basis for simultaneous PMS anchoring at the metal center and proton delivery from the ligand environment (Supplementary Fig. 7). Heteronuclear Co/Ni-MOF was synthesized via solvothermal assembly (Fig. 2a). The Co/Ni-MOF powder exhibits a distinct color compared with the corresponding monometallic Co-MOF and Ni-MOF powders (Supplementary Fig. 8). More importantly, EPR spectra show that Co/Ni-MOF displays a substantially stronger paramagnetic signal than the monometallic controls (Supplementary Fig. 9a), consistent with Co/Ni-induced changes in the local spin and electronic states. Among the tested samples with Co/Ni ratios of 1:2, 1:1, and 2:1, the 2:1 sample has the highest signal intensity, suggesting the most favorable electronic configuration. The PXRD patterns confirm that the three Co/Ni-MOF variants retain nearly identical framework structures (Supplementary Fig. 9b), thereby excluding gross structural differences induced by compositional tuning as the main origin of the activity variation. Kinetic tests further revealed a clear composition-dependent catalytic trend, with a 1 min removal efficiency following the order of Co/Ni = 2:1 (99.97%) > 1:1 (93.41%) > 1:2 (85.13%), and all three bimetallic samples outperformed Co-MOF (50.41%) and Ni-MOF (37.12%) (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Therefore, the superior activity of the Co/Ni-MOF with a Co/Ni ratio of 2:1 can be attributed to an optimal balance between sufficient Co-centered PMS activation sites and Ni-mediated electronic modulation, which maximizes the cooperative Co−Ni interaction without altering the overall framework structure. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed well-defined rod-like crystals with smooth surfaces and no obvious aggregation that were distinct from the spherical morphologies of Co-MOF and Ni-MOF (Supplementary Figs. 10, 11). EDS mapping confirms uniform distributions of C, N, O, and S as well as Co (10.31%) and Ni (5.92%) within individual rods (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 11), with no detectable segregation or enrichment, which is consistent with the homogeneous incorporation of both metals. Consistent with the PXRD results showing no extra diffraction peaks assignable to metal/metal oxide nanoparticles or clusters, the high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images display abundant discrete bright spots (Fig. 2c) that appear as isolated single-atom features, without continuous lattice fringes or particles larger than 2–3 nm, thereby excluding nanoparticles or clusters. The three-dimensional intensity profiles and two-dimensional contour maps further resolve a near-neighbor double-peak feature with peak-to-peak spacings of 1 nm and 0.304 nm in representative cross-sections, which is consistent with adjacent (or offset) heterometallic single-atom sites being bridged by ligands and coexisting within the same pore. Atomically resolved STEM‒EDS shows nanoscale complementarity between the Co and Ni signals (Fig. 2d), supporting their site occupation within the MOF lattice. X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis was employed to determine the local coordination environments and valence states. The Co and Ni K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) absorption edges lie between those of the low- and high-valence oxide references (Fig. 2e, h), and the edge positions indicate predominantly divalent Co/Ni with minor high-valence contributions. The corresponding k3-weighted EXAFS spectra and k-space fitting results for the Co and Ni K-edges are shown in Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13, respectively. In the Fourier-transformed EXAFS spectra, the Co K-edge shows a dominant Co-N/O peak at 1.65 Å (Fig. 2f, g), whereas the Ni K-edge shows a primary Ni-N/O peak at 1.62 Å (Fig. 2i, j). Importantly, neither spectrum displays characteristic M−M scattering features (Co−Co at 2.21 Å or Ni−Ni at 2.18 Å), supporting atomically dispersed metal sites26. Wavelet-transform EXAFS shows maxima exclusively in the low-k region (Fig. 2k; Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Fig. 15), which is characteristic of coordination by light atoms (N/O). Quantitative fitting (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3) suggests an approximately Ni−N4O2 environment for Ni and a mixed N/O coordination of Co−N3.5O2 for Co, which is consistent with distorted octahedral geometries. The lower N coordination number around Co implies the presence of open metal sites (OMSs) that can serve as key adsorption and activation centers for substrates/oxidants. Collectively, these microscopic and spectroscopic data demonstrate that Co and Ni are stably embedded in the MOF as atomically dispersed, neighboring dual single-atom sites.

PMS activation through a predominantly non-radical high-valent Co–oxo-mediated OAT pathway

Catalytic performance was first assessed using SDZ as a model pollutant. With 0.05 g L-1 Co/Ni-MOF and 0.15 mM PMS (Supplementary Fig. 16), the Co/Ni-MOF + PMS system exhibits an immediate kinetic response and reaches ≈100% SDZ removal within 1 min. In contrast, the ligand (Lg), PMS alone, and Co/Ni-MOF alone are essentially inactive (Fig. 3a), indicating that highly active sites form only through cooperative “MOF + PMS” interplay. The normalized rate constant is k ≈ 6.2 × 103 M-1 min-1, which is among the leading values reported for comparable systems (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 4). In addition, Co/Ni-MOF delivers robust thermal/chemical/operational stability with a wide pH window (up to 450 °C and pH 1−13; Supplementary Fig. 17 a−d) while maintaining high degradation efficiency in complex matrices and across diverse pollutants with good recyclability (Supplementary Fig. 18a–d and Supplementary Table 5).Fig. 3PMS activation and dominant non-radical OAT pathway over Co/Ni-MOF.a SDZ degradation kinetics under different component controls. b Benchmarking of normalized kinetic performance against reported catalysts as a function of PMS and catalyst dosage (The related information was presented Supplementary Table 4). c Reactive-species quenching tests with radical and non-radical scavengers. d PMSO probe test showing PMSO consumption and PMSO₂ generation under different conditions. e Time-resolved in situ Raman spectra tracking PMS conversion and the evolution of Co-related vibrational features during reaction. f Comparison of PMSO-to-PMSO₂ conversion and PMS consumption for Co-MOF, Ni-MOF and Co/Ni-MOF. g Chronoamperometric response of Co/Ni-MOF-modified electrode upon sequential addition of PMS and SDZ. h Schematic of long-range Ni→Co charge transfer enabling inner-sphere electron transfer to PMS. Reactive-species probing indicates a predominantly non-radical pathway. Quenching tests revealed that DMSO nearly completely inhibited SDZ degradation, whereas L-histidine, MeOH, TBA, and p-BQ had only minor effects (Fig. 3c), implying that ·OH/SO4·-/1O2 and the related ROS were not dominant and that the reaction proceeded mainly via a Co(IV) = O-mediated oxygen atom transfer (OAT) pathway. The physical mixture of Co-MOF and Ni-MOF fails to reproduce the DMSO-sensitive, Co(IV) = O-dominated behavior of integrated Co/Ni-MOF (Supplementary Fig. 19). Therefore, the non-radical OAT pathway originates from through-framework Co/Ni electronic coupling within the dual-site structure rather than from the simple coexistence of separate Co and Ni phases. Consistently, the OAT probe methyl phenyl sulfoxide (PMSO) is rapidly and almost quantitatively oxidized to methyl phenyl sulfone (PMSO2) in the Co/Ni-MOF + PMS system27, while negligible conversion occurs in the controls (Fig. 3d). Moreover, the OAT-reactive competitor thioanisole suppressed the reaction to near zero levels (Supplementary Fig. 20a). EPR further reveals a new anisotropic S = 1/2 resonance at g ≈ 2.2–2.3, which is consistent with the reports for Co(IV) species in the literature28,29, and supports the involvement of high-valent cobalt species as major oxidizing intermediates (Supplementary Fig. 20b–e). Time-resolved in situ Raman spectra show that HSO5- (886.8 cm-1) decays as SO42- (988.9 cm-1) emerges, which is accompanied by shifts in Co-N/Ni-N (618.3–627.5 cm-1) and Co-O/Ni-O (477.7–487.1 cm-1) bands30 and the appearance of a new peak at 634.1 cm-1; this finding is consistent with the reversible oxidation of metal centers and Co(IV) = O formation (Fig. 3e)31. The ATR−FTIR results indicate the formation of HSO4--related features (Supplementary Fig. 21). Therefore, “selectivity” refers mainly to the pathway selectivity in PMS activation; that is, the PMS-derived oxidative equivalents preferentially convert into the Co(IV) = O-mediated OAT pathway rather than the free radical pathway of free diffusion.

Co/Ni-MOF achieves near-100% PMSO oxidation and >95% PMS consumption, whereas both metrics remain below 50% for Co-MOF and Ni-MOF (Fig. 3f). These results indicate that the superior non-radical reactivity should not be attributed to a single structural factor. Co provides primary PMS adsorption and Co(IV) = O formation sites; Ni enhances framework-mediated charge redistribution towards Co-bound PMS, and the adjacent -NH motif facilitates local proton delivery. Therefore, the observed high PMS consumption efficiency and Co(IV) = O-dominated OAT reactivity are best understood as the collective outcome of the integrated Co/Ni-NH microenvironment rather than because of proton gating alone or electronic modulation alone. In addition, the physical mixture of Co-MOF and Ni-MOF cannot reproduce the activity or reactive species profile of integrated Co/Ni-MOF. This catalytic advantage requires electronic coupling of Co/Ni within a preorganized dual-site structure. When Co/Ni-MOF is deposited on a glassy carbon electrode (GCE), chronoamperometry reveals a pronounced transient current pulse upon PMS addition (in the absence of a substrate), which is consistent with rapid interfacial charge transfer during PMS adsorption/activation (Fig. 3g). Subsequent additions of SDZ (10 and 20 mg L-1) induce reproducible, concentration-dependent current responses, indicating that the substrate participates in electron balance and facilitates active-site regeneration, thereby accelerating turnover32,33. Notably, the utilization of PMS in pollutant-containing solutions exceeds that in pure water (up to a 14.5% increase), further supporting substrate-coupled interfacial catalytic turnover coupled with PMS activation (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Charge analysis of reaction intermediates (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Table 6), together with density-of-states calculations (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicates that Ni functions as an electronic reservoir rather than a passive spectator. Via long-range metal–metal charge transfer (MMCT), charge analysis indicates a calculated redistribution of approximately 0.190 e towards the Co site (Fig. 3h), increasing the driving force for electron injection into PMS while buffering the Co center against overoxidation during redox cycling34,35. Direct charge transport and electrochemical measurements further support the electronic role of the Co/Ni framework. Pressure-dependent powder conductivity measurements revealed that Co/Ni-MOF has a higher apparent conductivity than the ligand and Co-MOF do (Supplementary Fig. 23a, Supplementary Table 7), whereas CV and EIS analyses revealed a greater redox response and lower charge-transfer resistance for Co/Ni-MOF than for the corresponding controls (Supplementary Fig. 23b, c). Together with the PMS/SDZ-triggered electrochemical response, these results indicate that Co/Ni coupling enhances framework-mediated charge redistribution and interfacial electron transfer during PMS activation (Supplementary Fig. 23d). DFT further distinguishes contributions from framework Co sites and unsaturated Cooms sites: PMS adsorption is stronger (more negative Eads) and is accompanied by greater charge transfer on Co/Cooms than on Ni sites, with Cooms showing the strongest adsorption and most complete charge-transfer characteristics and favoring high-valence species formation (Supplementary Fig. 24). HOMO−LUMO analysis suggested that framework Co sites maintain a smaller energy gap to support overall conductivity and rapid electron transport, whereas Cooms localizes frontier orbitals on Co−O motifs, increasing the local oxidizing power and selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Fig. 26). DOS and pCOHP analyses likewise indicate the strongest coupling between Co 3 d states and PMS (O 2p) states and the highest Co–O bond strength, whereas coupling at Ni sites is weaker (Supplementary Fig. 27 and Supplementary Fig. 28), which is consistent with Ni mainly modulating the redox potential of Co sites through remote electron donation. The dynamic valence evolution during PMS activation was further examined to connect the electrochemical charge-transfer behavior with the proposed Co/Ni redox cooperation. XPS corroborated the valence redistribution during catalysis (Supplementary Fig. 29): the Co(II):Co(III) ratio shifted from 70.03%:29.97% to 75.62%:24.38%, and the Ni(II):Ni(III) ratio shifted from 74.73%:25.27% to 80.02%:19.98%. The increased apparent fraction of lower-valence species likely reflects the transient formation of high-valent states (e.g., Co(III/IV) = O and Ni(III)) during turnover followed by relaxation to a steady-state composition, with Ni exhibiting a stronger tendency to relax, which is consistent with its role as an electron buffer/redox modulator36,37. Time-dependent XAS further revealed that the average Ni valence decreases more markedly than that of Co does during PMS activation, supporting the greater involvement of Ni in reaction-induced electron redistribution (Supplementary Fig. 30). Such ensemble-averaged valence relaxation is consistent with dynamic redox compensation after transient high-valence intermediate formation rather than contradicting the proposed Co(IV) = O-mediated OAT pathway. The N 1 s and O 1 s spectra (Supplementary Fig. 31) further reveal strengthened ligand‒PMS interactions: the M‒N and -NH peaks show slight binding energy shifts (399.42 → 399.59 eV; 400.22 → 400.44 eV), whereas the –NH+ feature exhibits a pronounced redshift and decreased area (401.11 → 400.27 eV). In the O 1 s spectra, the SOx−O component shifts to a higher binding energy and intensifies (531.25 → 531.49 eV), and subtle shifts are also observed for the O−O and -OH components (533.56 → 533.74 eV; 532.25 → 532.38 eV). Together, these changes suggest electron density depletion upon PMS adsorption, strengthened hydrogen bonding/coordination that prepolarizes the peroxide bond, and proton abstraction/migration involving ligand sites during catalysis30,38–40. Beyond the electronic contribution of Co/Ni sites, we further evaluated whether the accessible pore environment contributes to substrate/oxidant colocalization and productive turnover. Pore accessibility regulation and MD/RDF analyses were further performed to clarify the contribution of the accessible MOF pore/channel environment. Pore filling markedly decreased the surface area and pore volume of Co/Ni-MOF (Supplementary Fig. 32), which was accompanied by suppressed SDZ degradation, PMS consumption, and PMS/SDZ-triggered electrochemical responses (Supplementary Figs. 33, 34). MD simulations and RDF analyses further revealed the restricted diffusion and dynamic colocalization of PMS and SDZ near the Co/-NH microenvironment, supporting a confinement-assisted adsorption–activation–oxidation coupling process (Supplementary Figs. 35, 36).

Converging evidence for an MS-CPET-like PMS activation pathway

We use “MS-CPET-like” to describe a proton-coupled activation pathway in which framework -NH proton motion, Co-bound PMS polarization, and interfacial charge redistribution occur in coupled reaction coordinates. This terminology does not imply that all possible stepwise PT/ET or ET/PT contributions are experimentally excluded. Building on the OAT-dominated reactivity and the XPS signatures, an MS-CPET-like mechanism in Co/Ni-MOF was further evaluated by complementary experimental and theoretical evidence. ATR−FTIR revealed that the addition of PMS induces a broad band at 3600–3900 cm-1 that first increases in intensity but then decreases in intensity with time and that is assignable to Co(III)−OH intermediates and -NH···O−HSO4- hydrogen bond vibrations (Fig. 4a). This observation is consistent with the evolution of the local hydroxyl/hydrogen-bonding environment, including Co–OH-containing intermediates and -NH···PMS interactions at Co sites and is effectively captured by -NH functionalities to establish a stabilized hydrogen bond network near the Co center30,41. Consistently, the broad N−H/O−H stretching region at approximately 3500–3300 cm-1 in the FT-IR spectra clearly changes after the reaction and evolves progressively in the in situ DRIFTS spectra upon the addition of PMS (Supplementary Fig. 37a, b)42. These spectral changes indicate that PMS activation perturbs the local hydrogen-bonding environment associated with ligand -NH and/or surface O−H species. The apparent redox potential shifts linearly with increasing pH, with a slope close to the Nernstian value for a coupled 1H+/1e- process. This finding supports proton-coupled redox behavior at the Co/Ni-MOF interface but does not by itself establish a unique concerted transition state (Supplementary Fig. 38). KIE, EIS and EPR collectively support that proton migration participates in and modulates a kinetically important step during PMS activation. A deuterated analog (Co/Ni-MOF-ND) was prepared via isotope exchange (Supplementary Fig. 39a). In the absence of scavengers, replacing H2O with D2O yields a modest solvent KIE of 1.1, whereas deuterating the -NH ligand to -ND gives a much larger site KIE of 1.81 (Fig. 4b). When the Co(IV) = O-mediated OAT channel was competitively intercepted by excess PMSO, SDZ degradation nearly ceased. Together with the substantially larger site KIE than solvent KIE, this result supports the close involvement of proton donation from the -NH ligand in the kinetically controlling PMS activation process. Low frequency EIS analysis showed that Warburg response significantly reduced the interfacial charge transfer resistance in H2O after adding PMS, but this reduction was significantly weakened under D2O or -ND conditions (Supplementary Fig. 39b); this finding suggests that proton migration facilitates interfacial electron injection, which is consistent with PCET-type kinetics. In the EPR spectra, a pronounced DMPO-H signal is observed in H2O but is strongly diminished or disappears in D2O or -ND systems (Fig. 4c), further implying that the formation of DMPO-H relies on rapid proton transfer. Similarly, PMS consumption follows the order H2O(-NH) > D2O(-NH) > D2O(-ND) (Fig. 4d), indicating that -NH-derived protons not only govern the rate but also directly control oxidant utilization43. On this basis, femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy was employed to track the early interfacial dynamics associated with PMS activation. Two-dimensional TA maps show prominent long-wavelength transient features at 650–760 nm when Co/Ni-MOF and PMS coexist; both their formation and decay are highly sensitive to isotopic substitution, with faster buildup and more complete evolution in H2O, an overall slowdown in D2O, and an even more pronounced retardation upon further deuteration of -NH to -ND (Fig. 4e). For quantitative comparison, the kinetics at 750 nm were fitted with a biexponential model (Fig. 4f): τ1 = 3.1 ps and τ2 = 245.9 ps in H2O; τ1 = 5.6 ps and τ2 = 389.3 ps in D2O; and a dramatically prolonged early-time component under -ND conditions (τ1 = 523.8 ps, τ2 = 316.5 ps). The much stronger site isotope effect relative to that of solvent substitution indicates that this early step is highly sensitive to the transferable proton on the ligand, which is consistent with a proton-coupled interfacial process in which ligand proton motion facilitates electron redistribution during PMS activation. A similar trend is observed at 680 nm (Supplementary Fig. 40). Control experiments with the MOF alone (without PMS) and with PMS alone (without the MOF) exclude the intrinsic photoresponses of individual components (Supplementary Fig. 41 and Supplementary Fig. 42), confirming that the isotope-sensitive transient response originates from interfacial coupling in the Co/Ni-MOF–PMS system. Collectively, these results support the preferential involvement of framework -NH sites in forming a local proton relay interaction with the Oβ site of PMS, thereby favoring an MS-CPET-like activation pathway (Fig. 4g).

DFT calculations elucidated the geometric and energetic essence of MS-CPET in terms of the adsorption configuration, proton acceptor site, cooperative transition state, and free-energy preference. PMS adsorption screening revealed that the Co-Oα binding configuration was the most stable, establishing Co as the preferred anchoring site (Supplementary Fig. 43). Proton transfer pathways from the -NH ligand to intramolecular acceptor O atoms in PMS (Oα, Oβ, and sulfate oxygen Oγ) show that proton migration to the distal peroxide oxygen Oβ is most favorable (ΔE = -2.36 eV; Fig. 4h), supporting directional proton gating as the optimal preorganization for subsequent O−O scission; the less favorable Oα/Oγ acceptor scenarios are benchmarked (Supplementary Fig. 44). In Fig. 4i and Supplementary Table 8, the progress along the -NH → Oβ coordinate is captured by the shortening of the H···Oβ distance together with the relative energy profile. At the Co site, the H···Oβ distance decreases from 3.09 Å (IS) to 1.26 Å (TS) and 1.00 Å (FS), with energies of 0.00 → 0.28 → -2.36 eV. At the unsaturated Cooms site, the H···Oβ distance decreases more readily (2.75 → 1.21 → 0.97 Å), and the profile is both lower in relative free energy and more exergonic (0.00 → 0.13 → -2.54 eV), indicating facilitated proton delivery to Oβ and a more favorable overall conversion. Free-energy comparisons between the ET and MS-CPET pathways (Fig. 4j) reveal that Co sites provide the most favorable free-energy evolution in both cases, confirming that Co is the primary PMS activation center (Co > Cooms > Nioms/Ni; Supplementary Fig. 45). However, relative to ET, MS-CPET introduces a key N−H(D)−O transition configuration that globally decreases and “smooths” the reaction coordinate for the critical step PMS → Co(IV) = O44. In contrast to ET, MS-CPET deepens the overall driving force (final ΔG = -3.16 eV; Fig. 4j), and deuteration shifts the MS-CPET profile upwards, which is consistent with the pronounced experimental KIE and indicates that proton migration is a key contributor to rate control. Charge evolution analysis further supports PT, as quantified by the charge shift on the noncoordinated N (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Table 6). Control calculations using a -CH-substituted Co/Ni-MOF model show that the removal of the framework -NH motif weakens the energetic advantage of the proton-coupled PMS activation pathway, supporting the involvement of -NH in local proton delivery and Co(IV) = O formation (Supplementary Figs. 46, 47).

Integrating the experimental and computational evidence, PMS activation on Co/Ni-MOF is most consistently described by an -NH-assisted MS-CPET-like pathway, although possible stepwise PT/ET or ET/PT contributions cannot be fully excluded experimentally. In this model, PMS activation on Co/Ni-MOF can be summarized as a two-step MS-CPET cycle (Fig. 4k). PMS is first anchored between the Co site and a neighboring noncoordinated -NH group to form an -NH···Oβ–HSO4- precomplex. In the first activation step, MS-CPET couples -NH → Oβ proton transfer with Ni–π–Co→PMS electron redistribution within a concerted transition-state configuration, thereby promoting O–O cleavage and the formation of Co(III)–OH. The overall free-energy changes are −2.364 eV for Co and −2.592 eV for Cooms. The corresponding stepwise PT/ET and ET/PT routes reach the same thermodynamic endpoints but proceed through discrete intermediates. For Co, PT/ET involves successive free-energy changes of −0.369 and −1.995 eV, whereas ET/PT involves +0.481 and −2.845 eV; the corresponding values for Cooms are −0.206/ − 2.386 eV and +0.678/ − 3.270 eV, respectively. In particular, the endergonic ET-first states indicate that uncoupled electron transfer is energetically disfavored, whereas the MS-CPET coordinate avoids accumulation of this charge-separated intermediate. If Lg (-NH) is on the other side, its sequential pathway relative free-energy comparison changes, but MS-CPET remains unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 48). In addition, after changing -NH to -CH (adding water as a proton source around it), calculations were conducted for the different pathways mentioned above. After -NH is replaced with -CH and nearby water is introduced as an external proton source, the coupled activation pathway becomes much less favorable, with weakened PMS adsorption, reduced interfacial charge transfer, and a less favorable free-energy profile. This comparison indicates that the framework-NH motif is required to organize an efficient local proton relay geometry for MS-CPET-like PMS activation (Supplementary Fig. 49). IRI analysis indicates that the π-conjugated backbone provides a continuous electron-transport channel and that the -NH-enriched pore mouth stabilizes the Oβ···H···N motif, thereby lowering the local PT-related relative free energy (Supplementary Fig. 50). In the second activation step, a further MS-CPET event couples proton relocation with framework-mediated electron redistribution, converting Co(III)–OH towards Co(IV) = O while regenerating the local -NH proton-relay state. The overall free-energy changes are −0.912 eV for Co and −1.174 eV for Cooms. By comparison, the PT/ET route proceeds through Co(III)–O⁻, with successive free-energy changes of −0.429/ − 0.483 eV for Co and −0.804/ − 0.370 eV for Cooms. The ET/PT route proceeds through [Co(IV)–OH]+, with corresponding changes of +0.297/ − 1.209 eV for Co and −0.112/ − 1.062 eV for Cooms. The coupled MS-CPET pathway therefore provides a continuous route without accumulation of discrete charged intermediates and is particularly advantageous at the Cooms site45–47. PMS is more readily activated than peracetic acid (PAA) or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), enabling greater oxidant utilization (Supplementary Fig. 51). In addition, we conducted theoretical calculations on H2O2 and PAA and reported that their adsorption energy, electron transfer, and Lo-o were not as good as those of PMS were (Supplementary Fig. 52). Therefore, the combined isotopic, fs-TA spectroscopic and DFT results support MS-CPET-like activation as the most plausible pathway in the present Co/Ni-NH model. However, this assignment is based on converging evidence and relative energetic preference rather than direct experimental visualization of a unique concerted transition state. This mechanism features spatial division of labor and temporal synchronization, breaking from conventional single-electron-transfer-dominated paradigms and offering a general route towards enzyme-mimetic hydrogen atom transfer (HAT)/CPET-type high-efficiency oxidation via multimetal–ligand cooperation and proton-gating design48,49.

Continuous-flow demonstration and sustainability assessment for practical water purification

Building on the highly efficient non-radical activation associated with the MS-CPET-like Co/Ni-NH activation pathway, the application potential of the Co/Ni-MOF + PMS system was further validated from three perspectives: engineering operability, real-water adaptability, and environmental sustainability. First, Co/Ni-MOF was grown in situ on SiC ceramics and immobilized into a continuous-flow device (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 53 and Supplementary Table 9), enabling stable purification without external light, heat or electrical activation. After more than 1000 L of SDZ solution prepared with tap water was treated, the endpoint Co and Ni leaching concentrations remained at the μg L-1 level, approximately 5 μg L-1, indicating the long-term structural stability of the immobilized Co/Ni-MOF under continuous-flow operation (Fig. 5b). In contrast, SiC alone showed negligible catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 54a). The immobilized configuration also reduced the amount of SO42- accumulation originating from PMS decomposition (Supplementary Fig. 54b), highlighting the long-term structural integrity, catalytic stability, and engineering safety of the fixed active sites. Total organic carbon (TOC) measurements further revealed that mineralization remained >90% throughout operation (Fig. 5c), indicating substantial mineralization under the tested continuous-flow conditions. Excitation−emission matrix (EEM) fluorescence spectra also evolved from multiple intense peaks before treatment to near-complete disappearance after treatment (Fig. 5d), corroborating the substantial removal of dissolved organics and aromatic/conjugated chromophores. Under multicontaminant conditions, eight representative organics (SDZ, SMX, BPA, CIP, TC, OTC, RhB, and MB) were simultaneously removed with high efficiency (these substrates included N/O/S-containing drugs, conjugated dyes, and phenolic/aromatic compounds), as evidenced by the rapid suppression of their concentrations to very low levels (Fig. 5e). Despite their structural diversity, these pollutants share electron-rich functional motifs (e.g., phenolic, amino, and π-conjugated groups), making them representative substrates for selective oxidation by electrophilic Co(IV) = O, but further substrate-dependent kinetics were revealed in additional high-concentration single pollutant tests (Supplementary Fig. 55), which can be rationalized by differences in the interfacial affinity and intrinsic sensitivity of electron-rich motifs to electrophilic Co(IV) = O oxidation; the corresponding EEM results confirm similar effective abatement (Supplementary Fig. 54c), indicating high turnover, even under competitive adsorption and parallel reaction pathways. Interfacial wetting and mass transfer analyses further explain the performance advantage under high-throughput fixed-bed operation: compared with the hydrophobic surfaces of the ligand and monometallic materials, Co/Ni-MOF exhibits pronounced hydrophilicity and near-complete wetting, which is consistent with its enhanced mass transfer capability (Fig. 5f). In real complex matrices, influent/effluent monitoring of aquaculture wastewater (Supplementary Fig. 56 and Supplementary Table 10) shows substantial decreases in multiple antibiotics and fluorinated persistent pollutants (e.g., PFHxS, PFOS, PFOA, and PFNA) (Fig. 5g). At the µg L-1 level, the total concentration of the 16 monitored contaminants decreased by 95.6%, with 11 compounds were below their respective method detection limits in the effluent. This demonstrates that anti-interference oxidation occurs under high salinity, high organic loading, and mixed-contaminant conditions (the observed decrease in PFAS content is considered removal rather than confirmed degradation), suggesting the feasibility of mitigating the combination of “antibiotics”. From a sustainability standpoint, life-cycle assessment (LCA) comparing Co/Ni-MOF with Co3O4, ZIF-67 and Co-MOF across 18 environmental indicators in terms of both synthesis and degradation phases revealed consistently lower impacts per treated load for Co/Ni-MOF and smaller incremental burdens during operation (Fig. 5h, Supplementary Data 1); this finding is consistent with the high efficiency and long lifetime of Co/Ni-MOF, which reduce the replacement frequency. (Co3O4 was selected as a representative cobalt oxide PMS activator, whereas ZIF-67 represents a commonly used Co-based MOF-derived benchmark, and the standalone Co-MOF highlights the advantages of bimetallic materials. These comparisons are intended to provide material-level references rather than an exhaustive LCA survey of all homogeneous and Fenton-like PMS systems.) In addition, the system exhibits strong broad-spectrum antibacterial performance: Co/Ni-MOF + PMS achieves 99.99% inactivation of both E. coli and S. aureus, markedly outperforming the controls (Fig. 5i), thereby offering an integrated advantage for water treatment that couples contaminant removal with microbial risk control. Discussion

This work overcomes two key bottlenecks in PMS activation—interfacial mass transfer limitations and the lack of directional control over O−O bond scission—by developing a nitrogen-rich Co/Ni dual single-atom MOF that realizes an engineerable MS-CPET-like pathway within a single framework. A superwettable, N/O-rich interface containing noncoordinated -NH motifs is enriched in PMS and provides local proton relay interactions, whereas atomically dispersed Co/Ni sites act as electron injectors to establish a Ni→Co→PMS electron-delivery cascade. Proton transfer along -NH → Oβ and electron redistribution towards Co-bound PMS are proposed to occur along a coupled reaction coordinate, stabilizing an Oβ···H···N motif and biasing O−O cleavage towards Co(IV) = O formation. As a result, the catalyst delivers a normalized rate constant of 6.2×103 M-1 min-1 and achieves >95% effective PMS consumption within 1 min with high selectivity while maintaining long-term stability after immobilization for continuous-flow treatment in complex real water. Mechanistic evidence from in situ Raman/ATR−FTIR, KIE, fs-TA, EPR, and DFT supports the proposed division of labor among proton relay and electron-modulation sites. These results validate the concept of coupling local proton delivery with framework-mediated electronic regulation in bimetallic MOFs for PMS activation. The present design is most directly applicable to PMS, which can simultaneously engage a metal redox site and a nearby proton-relay motif. Extension to other peroxides or broader PCET-type catalytic systems will require comparable spatial matching among the oxidant, proton donor, and electron-transfer site and should be evaluated case by case.

Methods

Chemicals

All the chemical reagents used in this work are listed in the Supplementary Methods.

Synthesis of M1/M2-MOF

A bimetallic MOF was synthesized via a solvothermal method by combining the ligand with two selected metal salts in a MeOH/DMSO mixed solvent, followed by the sequential addition of acetic acid and dilute sulfuric acid. The sealed tube was heated at 140 °C for 48 h to yield yellow blade-shaped crystals. The metal composition was tuned by adjusting the feed ratio using empirical calibration (see Supplementary Methods for full experimental details).

Catalytic performance experiments

All the experiments were conducted at 25 ± 1 °C. Unless otherwise specified, the PMS concentration was maintained at 0.15 mM, with a reaction volume of 50 mL, a catalyst concentration of 0.05 g L-1, and a pollutant concentration of 20 mg L-1. The experiments were carried out in a shaker at 200 rpm, and samples were collected at predetermined time intervals. Each test was performed in triplicate, and all the samples were filtered through 0.45 µm syringe filters to minimize random errors. The detailed synthesis methods for the other catalysts and the characterization techniques used are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

DFT calculation details

Periodic DFT calculations were carried out with the CP2K/Quickstep package using the GPW approach, the PBE functional with D3(BJ) dispersion correction, and GTH pseudopotentials under periodic boundary conditions. Geometry optimizations were performed until the force and electronic convergence criteria were met. The specific calculation details are presented in the Supplementary Methods.
