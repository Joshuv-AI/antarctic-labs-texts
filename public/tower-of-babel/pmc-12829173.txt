Evening preference with evening-type rest-activity rhythm: a risk for poor quality of life
Authors: Sujeong Mun, Jeong-Kyun Kim, Younghwa Baek, Kihyun Park, Siwoo Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronotype reflects an individual’s intrinsic preference for the timing of activity and rest; however, perceived circadian preference may differ from actual behavioral rhythms due to external demands. Both chronotype and rest–activity rhythm have been linked to adverse health outcomes, but their combined association with health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has been less explored. This study investigated how combinations of circadian preference and objectively measured rest–activity timing relate to HRQoL.MethodsWe analyzed data from the Korean Medicine Daejeon Citizen Cohort, including participants who wore a Fitbit for ≥ 7 consecutive days. Chronotype was determined from the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire score quintiles, and rest–activity rhythm type from quintiles of the 5-h lowest activity period (L5 start time). Participants in intermediate quintiles were excluded. Four chronotype–rhythm groups were defined: MM (morning preference/morning rhythm), ME (morning preference/evening rhythm), EM (evening preference/morning rhythm), and EE (evening preference/evening rhythm). HRQoL was assessed using the SF-12 Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS), the EQ-5D index, and the EQ Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Multiple linear and logistic regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and circadian rhythm variables.ResultsIn total, 458 adults were included in the analysis. Compared with the MM group, the EE group had significantly lower scores for PCS, MCS, EQ-5D index, and EQ VAS (all P < 0.05), which persisted after full adjustment. The EM group had lower MCS but no significant differences in other HRQoL measures. In SF-12 subscales, both EM and EE groups reported lower general health and vitality, while only the EE group had lower role-physical and role-emotional scores. In EQ-5D dimensions, the EE group was more likely to report moderate to extreme pain/discomfort.ConclusionsEvening preference combined with an evening-type rhythm was associated with consistently poor HRQoL across physical, mental, and health utility domains, independent of confounders including activity levels, circadian robustness, social jetlag, and variability in sleep onset timing. Alignment between preference and rhythm did not confer benefit in the context of eveningness and may be disadvantageous in morning-oriented societies. Future longitudinal studies should examine underlying mechanisms and potential intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-025-02463-y.

Background

Circadian rhythms, endogenously generated oscillations with a cycle of approximately 24 h, are fundamental to physiological homeostasis and regulate diverse biological processes. They are coordinated by the central clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus and by peripheral clocks in various tissues and organs. These rhythms are also influenced by environmental cues, including light exposure, feeding and sleep schedules, temperature, and physical activity [1]. Individual differences in rhythm phase manifest as chronotypes, reflecting a person’s intrinsic preference for the timing of sleep, activity, and peak cognitive and physical performance within the 24-h cycle.

Chronotype has often been assessed using self-report instruments, such as the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ) and the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire, which capture perceived morningness or eveningness [2]. In general, individuals with a morning chronotype tend to maintain an earlier behavioral rhythm than those with an evening chronotype [3]. However, subjective chronotype does not always correspond to behavior. Objective rest–activity patterns may diverge from preference due to external demands, such as work schedules, social obligations, lifestyle, or environmental conditions. This discrepancy reflects a form of circadian misalignment, namely the mismatch between chronotype and behavioral rhythm.

Circadian misalignment also encompasses broader disruptions in internal timing and desynchrony between internal and external rhythms. Field studies commonly evaluate misalignment using concepts such as shift work or social jetlag (SJL). Both prolonged shift work and SJL have been associated with metabolic risk factors and cardiovascular disease [4, 5]. However, relatively few studies have examined misalignment between subjective chronotype and objectively measured behavioral rhythm in the general population. Moreover, it remains unclear whether alignment between chronotype and rhythm is always beneficial. One study reported that both advanced and delayed misalignment were associated with higher cardiometabolic disease risk than the aligned groups [6]. In contrast, other studies suggested that adopting an earlier lifestyle may benefit both mental and physical health in individuals with morning or evening preferences, regardless of their chronotype [7, 8].

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a multidimensional construct encompassing physical, mental, and social well-being [9]. Previous studies have shown better HRQoL among those with a morning preference [10, 11], whereas objectively later rest–activity rhythms have been linked to a poorer HRQoL [12]. However, few studies have examined how combinations of self-reported circadian preference and objectively measured rest–activity rhythms relate to HRQoL.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate how circadian preference combined with actual rest–activity timing influences multiple dimensions of HRQoL. By classifying individuals into four groups based on circadian preference (morning vs. evening) and rest–activity rhythm (morning-type vs. evening-type), we sought to determine whether specific preference–rhythm combinations are associated with lower physical, mental, or health utility–related domains of HRQoL.

Methods

Study participants

This study used data from the Korean Medicine Daejeon Citizen Cohort (KDCC) study, as previously described [13]. The KDCC study, conducted in Daejeon, South Korea, employed stratified cluster sampling to recruit adults aged 30–55 years without cancer or cardiovascular disease. Beginning in October 2020 and continuing until May 2024, participants who provided consent for wearable monitoring were instructed to wear a wrist-worn commercial activity tracker (Fitbit Inspire 2; Fitbit Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA) for 2 weeks. Data were retrieved through the Fitbit platform’s application programming interface during the monitoring period. We included only days when the device was worn for ≥ 20 h, as verified by minute-level heart rate recordings [14]. Analyses were restricted to participants with at least 7 consecutive valid days.

Chronotype

Chronotype was assessed using the validated Korean version of MEQ [15], originally developed by Horne and Östberg [16]. The MEQ is a 19-item self-report instrument that evaluates diurnal preference by assessing preferred timing for activities, such as sleep onset and offset, physical and mental tasks, and subjective alertness. Items are phrased to capture preference (e.g., preferred wake-up or bedtime) rather than actual habits. Total scores range from 16 to 86, with higher scores indicating stronger morning preference and lower scores indicating evening preference. The MEQ has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.77–0.86) [17] and reproducibility [18].

Rest-activity rhythm

Rest–activity rhythms were assessed using step-count data from Fitbit devices across 7 consecutive days, comprising 5 weekdays and 2 weekend days. The 5-h period with the lowest activity was identified with the nparACT package [19] in R, and the starting time (L5 start time) was recorded for each participant. Times were first logged in “HH: MM: SS” format and then converted into continuous decimal hours (0–24 h) for analysis. Most values clustered between 0 and 5 h, with some extending past 17 h. To reduce skewness and center the distribution around midnight, values after 17:00 were transformed into negative numbers representing hours until midnight. Extreme outliers in L5 start time were defined as values exceeding beyond three times the interquartile range (IQR) from the first and third quartiles. Participants with extreme outlier values in L5 start time were excluded from the analysis.

Additional non-parametric rhythm metrics were calculated: (1) interdaily stability (IS), reflecting rhythm stability across days, calculated as the ratio of variance of the average 24-h activity profile to the total variance of data aggregated by the hour from all days; (2) intradaily variability (IV), indicating rhythm fragmentation, calculated as the mean square of differences between successive hourly aggregated activity values normalized by the total variance of all days; (3) averaged activity of the 5 consecutive hours with minimal activity (L5); (4) averaged activity of the 10 consecutive hours with maximal activity (M10); (5) relative amplitude (RA), calculated by the difference between M10 and L5 divided by their sum; and (6) the circadian function index (CFI), reflecting rhythm robustness, calculated as the mean of the normalized values of IS, inverted IV, and RA [20, 21]. Fitbit step-count data show high concordance with actigraphy for rest–activity rhythm assessment [22], and L5 start time has been used in prior studies [7, 8] as a marker of behavioral timing.

Chronotype–rhythm groups

Participants were classified into four groups based on chronotype and objectively measured rhythm. Chronotype was determined from MEQ score quintiles: the lowest two quintiles indicated evening preference, and the highest two indicated morning preference. The cut-off originally proposed by the developers has been widely used [16]; however, it was developed based on a young population and has been criticized for its limited applicability across diverse demographic groups [23]. Moreover, MEQ scores have been reported to represent a continuous measure rather than a strictly categorical one [24]. Several studies have employed quantile-based classifications instead of fixed cutoffs [25, 26]. In the present study, since no cut-off specific to middle-aged Korean adults has been established and applying the conventional criteria resulted in highly imbalanced groups, with more than half of participants classified as intermediate type, we adopted a quantile-based grouping approach for our analysis. Rest–activity rhythm type was defined by L5 start time quintiles: the first and second quintiles represented morning-type, and the fourth and fifth represented evening-type. Participants in the middle quintile for either measure were classified as intermediate and excluded to ensure clear categorization.

Based on the combination of chronotype and actual rest-activity rhythm, participants were categorized into four groups: MM (morning preference + morning rhythm), ME (morning preference + evening rhythm), EM (evening preference + morning rhythm), and EE (evening preference + evening rhythm). The first letter denotes chronotype, and the second letter indicates the observed rest–activity rhythm.

HRQoL

HRQoL was assessed using two validated instruments: the 12-item short form health survey (SF-12) and the EuroQol 5-dimension (EQ-5D).

The SF-12, derived from the SF-36, is a reliable and valid tool for evaluating physical and mental health in both clinical and population-based studies [27]. It generates eight subscale scores, physical functioning (PF), role limitations due to physical health (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role limitations due to emotional problems (RE), and mental health (MH), which are aggregated into two summary scores: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS). Norm-based scoring was applied, standardizing each summary score to a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, with higher scores indicating better health status [28].

The EQ-5D, developed by the EuroQol Group, is a generic HRQoL measure that evaluates five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each is rated on three levels: no problems, some/moderate problems, or extreme problems. The EQ-5D index was calculated using a Korean-specific time trade-off valuation set [29]. Additionally, the EQ Visual Analog Scale (EQ VAS) assessed self-rated health, with participants rating overall health on a vertical scale from 0 (worst imaginable health state) to 100 (best imaginable health state).

Sleep timing variability

To evaluate variability in sleep onset, offset, and mid-sleep times, the longest daily sleep episode for each day was identified and used in the analysis. Circular means and standard deviations of these sleep timing variables were computed for each participant using the circmean and circstd functions from the SciPy package [30] in Python to account for 24-h periodicity.

SJL is defined as misalignment between biological and socially imposed sleep schedules. In this study, sleep episodes from Friday to Saturday nights were classified as free days, while those from Sunday to Thursday nights were classified as workdays, reflecting the typical South Korean workweek in which most adults work Monday to Friday. Actual SJL (SJLact) was calculated as the difference between the averaged mid-sleep time on free days (MSF) and that on workdays (MSW): SJLact = (MSF – MSW), allowing for negative values in case MSW occurred later than MSF [31]. Absolute SJL (SJLabs) was defined as the absolute value difference between MSF and MSW: SJLabs = |MSF – MSW|. To adjust for sleep debt on workdays, a sleep-corrected midpoint was calculated by adding half of the average sleep duration to the sleep onset time for each condition. Sleep-corrected SJL (SJLsc) was defined as the absolute difference between corrected midpoints, which simplifies mathematically to the absolute difference between sleep onset times on workdays and free days [32].

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were summarized as means ± standard deviations and compared across groups with one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with group differences evaluated using chi-squared tests. For pairwise comparisons, multiple-comparison corrections were applied: Tukey’s method for normally distributed variables and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for non-normal variables.

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess HRQoL outcomes, including the SF-12 PCS, MCS, their subscales, the EQ-5D index, and EQ VAS, using the MM group as the reference. Logistic regression analyses estimated odds ratios (ORs) for reporting moderate/extreme problems in each EQ-5D dimension. Three models were built: Model 1 adjusted for sex and age; Model 2 additionally adjusted for body mass index (BMI), marital status, education, household income, and occupation; and Model 3 further adjusted for alcohol use, smoking, step counts, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep-onset variability, CFI, and whether wearable data were collected during COVID-19 social distancing period. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Processing of 1-minute resolution data from wearable devices and computation of sleep-timing variability variables, including circular statistics, were performed using Python (version 3.9.5). Rest-activity rhythm and all statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2.

Results

General characteristics of the study participants

A total of 797 individuals were initially considered. Participants with missing MEQ data (n = 82) or outliers in L5 start time (n = 6; values outside 3 × IQR from the first and third quartiles) were excluded. The correlation coefficient between MEQ scores and L5 start times was − 0.41 (P < 0.001). Those whose MEQ scores or L5 start times belonged to the intermediate (3rd quintile) group (n = 251) were also excluded. Thus, 458 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Wearable-derived physical activity, sleep, and circadian rhythm parameters by chronotype–rhythm group

Table 2 shows group differences in wearable-derived parameters. Step counts were significantly lower in the EE group than in all other groups (MM vs. EE, P < 0.001; ME vs. EE, P = 0.040; EM vs. EE, P < 0.001). Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and M10 were also significantly lower in the EE group than in the MM group (P < 0.001). For circadian rhythm measures, IS and CFI were significantly lower in the EE and ME groups than in the MM group (IS: MM vs. ME, P = 0.028; MM vs. EE, P = 0.028; CFI: MM vs. ME, P = 0.018; MM vs. EE, P = 0.023). SJLact and SJLsc showed no significant group differences, while SJLabs was significantly lower in the MM group than in the EM and EE groups (MM vs. EM, P = 0.037; MM vs. EE, P = 0.008). No significant group differences were observed in sleep duration, wake after sleep onset, number of awakenings, or sleep efficiency. However, the EE group demonstrated significantly greater variability (higher standard deviation) in mid-sleep and sleep-onset times than the MM group (mid-sleep time SD: P = 0.005; sleep onset time SD: P < 0.001). Circular mean values of mid-sleep, sleep onset, and sleep offset times differed significantly among groups, showing a consistent trend of MM < EM < ME < EE (P < 0.05 for all comparisons).

Discussion

By integrating self-reported chronotypes with objectively measured rest–activity rhythms from wearable devices, we identified four distinct chronotype–rhythm groups, providing novel insights into individual circadian behavior patterns. In this cross-sectional study, participants in the EE group had worse HRQoL values than those in the MM group across all four indices (SF-12 MCS, PCS, EQ-5D index, and EQ VAS), independent of BMI, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, including activity levels, sleep onset variability, and circadian rhythm robustness. The EM group showed significantly worse SF-12 MCS scores than the MM group but did not differ in other HRQoL measures.

Previous studies have reported that individuals with an evening chronotype tend to have worse HRQoL than those with a morning preference. Likewise, objectively measured delayed rest–activity rhythms have been associated with a worse HRQoL [12]. Only a few studies have simultaneously examined self-reported chronotype and objectively derived rest–activity rhythms, and these have focused on outcomes other than HRQoL. These studies have largely analyzed data from the UK Biobank, which assessed chronotype using a single self-report question and various definitions of comparison groups. Two studies conducted by the same research team demonstrated that later behavioral timing was associated with adverse mental and physical disorders, as defined by ICD-10 codes, even among individuals with an evening chronotype [7, 8]. This finding aligns with our results, showing that the EE group had poorer HRQoL across a broader range of HRQoL indices, while the EM group showed impairment in only one type of index. In contrast, another study provided a different perspective [6]. This study reported that both advanced and delayed misalignment were associated with a higher risk of cardiometabolic disease compared with an aligned group. Notably, as the study used the difference between perceived chronotype and mid-sleep timing as the sole criterion for group classification, individuals who were morning-aligned and evening-aligned were aggregated into a single aligned category. Given that these two aligned groups (MM and EE) demonstrated markedly different levels of the outcome of interest–HRQoL–in our dataset, such aggregation was deemed inappropriate for the present study.

In this study, we examined the levels of rest–activity rhythm robustness across chronotype–rhythm groups. Although it is an intuitive assumption that aligned groups (MM and EE) would exhibit greater rest–activity rhythm robustness than misaligned groups (ME and EM), our findings only partially supported this expectation. Rhythm fragmentation, as indicated by IV, did not differ significantly across groups. However, the EE and ME groups exhibited lower rhythm stability, as indicated by reduced IS, which consequently resulted in lower overall rhythm robustness, as reflected by CFI, compared with the MM group. Taken together, these findings indicate that the circadian rhythm disturbances experienced by the EE and ME groups manifest primarily as a loss of regularity in activity timing across days rather than as increased fragmentation of activity patterns within a single day. Importantly, while a robust rhythm was evident in the morning-aligned (MM) group, the lower robustness in the evening-aligned (EE) group suggests that alignment between chronotype and actual behavioral rhythm does not necessarily confer circadian robustness.

In addition to the lower levels of rhythm stability and robustness, the EE group exhibited lower activity levels and greater variability in sleep onset and mid-sleep times than the MM group. Individuals with an evening preference are generally reported to engage in less favorable lifestyle behaviors [33] than those with a morning preference. These unfavorable behavioral patterns may partially mediate the poorer HRQoL observed in the EE group relative to the MM group [11]. Moreover, although the EE group represents alignment between chronotype and behavioral rhythm, individuals with an evening preference may still experience misalignment with societal expectations in a morning-oriented society. This societal misalignment—which may manifest as constrained social participation, increased psychosocial stress, and insufficient exposure to daytime light—may negatively influence HRQoL in the EE group.

In this study, evening preference itself appeared to exert a more detrimental effect on HRQoL than evening behavioral timing, as the EM group generally exhibited less favorable outcome patterns than the ME group. This pattern suggests that intrinsic characteristics of evening chronotype—such as delayed melatonin phase, heightened evening arousal, and a tendency toward health-adverse behaviors—may have a stronger impact on HRQoL than rest–activity timing alone. Consequently, interventions focusing solely on modifying behavioral schedules may have limited efficacy unless they also target the underlying circadian phase and lifestyle factors associated with evening preference. Further research is warranted to clarify the mechanisms underlying these associations and to determine whether addressing these intrinsic and lifestyle aspects can improve HRQoL in evening-oriented individuals.

Our findings also indicated that the ME, EM and EE groups showed lower scores for GH than the MM group, both the EM and EE groups reported lower scores for VT, whereas only the EE group had significantly reduced RP and RE scores. The additional impairments in RP and RE observed only in the EE group further indicate that alignment between chronotype and behavioral rhythm alone does not provide protection and, in the context of eveningness, may even worsen functional limitations.

In this study, the EE group exhibited a significantly higher probability of reporting problems only in the pain/discomfort dimension of the EQ-5D than the MM group. This finding may be partly explained by the known ceiling effect of the EQ-5D in generally healthy populations, where most domains show limited variability, and pain/discomfort remains one of the few dimensions sensitive to subtle differences. This suggests that individuals in the EE group may be more vulnerable to experiencing physical discomfort, potentially due to accumulated physiological or behavioral strain that is not readily captured by other domains of HRQoL.

SJL has been widely used as a proxy for circadian misalignment, quantifying the discrepancy between biological and social clocks. In this study, although SJLact did not differ significantly among groups, SJLabs was significantly higher in the EM and EE groups compared with the MM group. This finding indicates that participants in the EM and EE groups exhibited greater variability in mid-sleep timing between workdays and free days; however, this variability did not necessarily imply a consistent delay or advance in their sleep phase. While SJLabs has been widely used in the literature, it has been argued that this metric reflects not only misalignment between social and biological time but also the accumulation of sleep debt due to insufficient sleep on workdays. To address this limitation, SJLsc has been proposed to account for sleep debt [32]. In our study, the presence of group differences in SJLabs but not in SJLsc suggests that the observed variation in mid-sleep timing among the EM and EE groups, relative to the MM group, was likely driven by sleep debt rather than by true circadian misalignment between workdays and free days. Moreover, the application of SJL has several methodological limitations. First, assessing SJL requires participants to maintain a regular work or school schedule, as the calculation typically relies on the difference in mid-sleep time between workdays and free days, which limits generalizability. This method also assumes that sleep patterns on free days (usually weekends) reflect an individual’s intrinsic circadian preference. However, this assumption may not always hold true, as weekend sleep schedules can be shaped by social activities, family obligations, or compensatory behaviors rather than biological rhythms alone. In this study, the significantly lower HRQoL observed in the EE group persisted even after adjustment for SJL and sleep onset time variability in the regression model, suggesting that the disparity in HRQoL is not explained by either SJL or variability in sleep onset timing (Table S3).

This study has some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between chronotype–rhythm alignment and HRQoL. Second, although participants were recruited from the KDCC cohort, which is population-based in Daejeon, the analytic sample included only individuals who consented to wearing a wearable device for a 2-week period. Thus, the study sample may not fully represent the general population of Daejeon, and selection bias cannot be excluded. Third, there was an average temporal gap of 9.2 months between the assessment of chronotype using the MEQ and the measurement of rest–activity rhythms via wearable devices. However, circadian preference has been reported to be a relatively stable trait [34], and a previous study has shown that chronotype, as measured by the MEQ, shows good temporal stability over extended periods [18]. Fourth, the sample sizes of the ME and EM groups were relatively small, partly due to the exclusion of the intermediate groups to achieve clearer differentiation between chronotype–rhythm categories. While this approach enhanced the distinctness of group comparisons, it inevitably reduced the number of participants in each subgroup, limiting statistical power to detect more subtle differences. Nonetheless, the consistency of observed trends across multiple HRQoL measures suggests that the identified patterns are robust and warrant further investigation in larger and more diverse samples. Fifth, we used the MEQ to assess preferred timing for activities, which captures subjective circadian preference rather than objectively measured internal timing, such as melatonin rhythm. Thus, our findings based on MEQ scores should be interpreted as reflecting perceived rather than physiological circadian characteristics. Additionally, although we used quintiles of MEQ scores to group participants rather than the conventionally used cut-off points—due to their limited applicability across diverse demographic groups and the continuous nature of MEQ scores—this approach may limit comparability with studies using conventional chronotype categories. Sixth, the wrist on which the wearable device was worn (dominant vs. non-dominant) was not standardized, which may have influenced the wearable-derived variables. However, previous studies have reported acceptable reliability between the two wrists [35, 36], suggesting that the potential effect of wrist placement on the present findings is likely limited. Seventh, we did not assess whether participants were engaged in shift work, which could influence both circadian rhythm and HRQoL. Although extreme outliers in L5 start time were excluded, the potential impact of shift work cannot be ruled out and should be acknowledged as a limitation of this study. Eighth, our study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although we adjusted for whether wearable measurements were obtained during or after the COVID-19 social distancing period, we cannot rule out potential effects of COVID-19 infection on the study outcomes, as participants’ infection status was not assessed.

Despite these limitations, our study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to jointly consider self-reported chronotype and objectively measured rest–activity rhythms in evaluating their association with HRQoL, and it offers several notable strengths. First, rather than relying on a single-item measure, we used a validated questionnaire to assess chronotype, providing a reliable indicator of individual circadian preference. Second, instead of focusing solely on sleep timing, we examined rest–activity rhythms using data from a commercially available wearable device, enabling continuous, real-world monitoring of daily behavioral patterns. Finally, we adjusted for a wide range of potential confounders, including BMI, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle variables, such as physical activity levels, sleep variability, and circadian rhythm robustness, thereby identifying associations independent of these influences.

Conclusions

This study, which integrated self-reported chronotype with objectively measured rest–activity rhythms, demonstrated that individuals with an evening preference and evening-type rhythm consistently exhibited poor HRQoL across physical, mental, and health utility measures. These associations persisted after adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and circadian rhythm factors and were not explained by SJL or sleep timing variability. Our findings suggest that the alignment between preference and rhythm does not necessarily confer health benefits and, in the context of eveningness, may even be detrimental.

These results underscore the importance of jointly considering subjective and objective measures of circadian timing when evaluating health outcomes. Future longitudinal research is needed to clarify causal pathways, explore the mechanisms linking evening-type alignment to poor HRQoL, and test whether interventions, such as light therapy, behavioral strategies, or lifestyle modifications, can mitigate these adverse effects.
