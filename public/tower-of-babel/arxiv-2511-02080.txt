A structure theorem for polynomial return-time sets in minimal systems
Daniel Glasscock, Andreas Koutsogiannis, Anh N. Le, Joel Moreira, Florian K. Richter, Donald Robertson

Abstract
We investigate the structure of return-time sets determined by orbits along polynomial tuples in minimal topological dynamical systems. Building on the topological characteristic factor theory of Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss, and Ye, we prove a structure theorem showing that, in a minimal system, return-time sets coincide – up to a non-piecewise syndetic set – with those in its maximal infinite-step pronilfactor. As applications, we establish three new multiple recurrence theorems concerning linear recurrence along dynamically defined syndetic sets and polynomial recurrence along arithmetic progressions in minimal and totally minimal systems. We also show how our main theorem can be used to prove that two previously separate conjectures – one due to Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss, and Ye and the other due to Leibman – are equivalent.

Introduction

Let
T:X\to X
be a homeomorphism of a compact metric space
X
.
Given nonempty, open sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\subseteq X
and a polynomial tuple
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
, the
set of return-times of
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}
along
p
is defined as

\displaystyle R_{p}(U_{1},\dots,U_{d}):=\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ T^{-p_{1}(n)}U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}U_{d}\neq\emptyset\big\}.

(1)

These sets naturally capture the polynomial recurrence behavior of the system
(X,T)
by recording the times
n
for which there exists a point
x\in X
with
T^{p_{i}(n)}x\in U_{i}
for every
i=1,\ldots,d
.
Return-time sets have been important objects of study since the pioneering work of Furstenberg and Weiss
[
8
]
, who introduced topological dynamics as a powerful tool in additive combinatorics and Ramsey theory, linking dynamical recurrence to combinatorial and arithmetic structures in the integers.

In this paper, we expound on the principle that the sets
R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
are, to a large extent, determined by distinguished factors of
(X,T)
with simpler and more rigid dynamics.
Factors that determine properties of return-time sets are known as
characteristic
factors.
A major breakthrough – due to Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss, and Ye
[
11
]
in the linear case and extended by Qiu
[
28
]
and Huang, Shao, and Ye
[
18
]
in the polynomial case – identifies the maximal
\infty
-step pro-nilfactor
(X_{\infty},T)
(see
Section

2.3
) as a characteristic factor.
A later refinement by Ye and Yu
[
30
, Theorem A]
shows that the maximal
k
-step pronilfator is characteristic, where
k
depends only on the polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
.
These results provide long-awaited topological analogues of the existing ergodic-theoretic structure theory of measure-preserving systems established by Host, Kra
[
16
]
and Ziegler
[
31
]
in the linear case and Host, Kra
[
15
]
and Leibman
[
19
]
in the polynomial case.

1.1
The main result

Our main theorem –
A
below – is a refinement of the aforementioned topological characteristic factor results. It is best framed by formulating a statement equivalent to Qiu’s theorem
[
28
, Theorem B]
; the equivalence between
Theorem

1.1
and Qiu’s theorem is shown in
Theorem

3.9
. A tuple of polynomials
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
is called
essentially distinct
if
p_{i}-p_{j}
is non-constant whenever
i\neq j
. We denote by
U^{\circ}
the interior of a subset
U
of a topological space.

Theorem 1.1

(cf.
[
28
, Theorem B]
and
Theorem

3.9
)
.

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal and invertible topological dynamical system. Denote by
(X_{\infty},T)
its maximal
\infty
-step pro-nilfactor, and let
\pi\colon X\to X_{\infty}
be the associated factor map.
For all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, all nonempty, open
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\subseteq X
, and all essentially distinct
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
, if
R_{p}((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ})\neq\emptyset
, then
R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})\neq\emptyset
.

Theorem

1.1
says that recurrence among the sets
U_{i}
along
p
in a system is guaranteed by recurrence among the interiors of their images
(\pi U_{i})^{\circ}
along
p
in the system’s maximal
\infty
-step pronilfactor; in essence, recurrence along
p
can be “lifted” from a system’s
\infty
-step pronilfactor. This is a surprising and highly useful converse to the fact that recurrence in a system implies recurrence in its factors, a simple consequence of the inclusion

\displaystyle R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})\subseteq R_{p}\big((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\big),

(2)

as shown in
Lemma

3.2
.

While
Theorem

1.1
can quickly be upgraded to show under the same hypotheses that the set
R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
is syndetic (see
Lemma

3.6
), it says nothing of how much larger
R_{p}\big((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\big)
might be than
R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
. Our main result addresses this by showing that the two sets differ by a set that is small, in the sense that it is not piecewise syndetic. Syndeticity and piecewise syndeticity are defined in
Section

2.1
.

Theorem A
.

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal and invertible topological dynamical system. Denote by
(X_{\infty},T)
its maximal
\infty
-step pro-nilfactor, and let
\pi\colon X\to X_{\infty}
be the associated factor map.
For all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, all nonempty, open
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\subseteq X
, and all essentially distinct
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
, the difference set

R_{p}\big((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\big)\backslash R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})

is not piecewise syndetic.

We give examples in
Section

5.1
showing that the conclusion of
A
does not hold if
X_{\infty}
is replaced by the system’s maximal equicontinuous factor or if the essential distinctness assumption on
p
is omitted. On the other hand, by the containment in (
2
),
A
is quickly seen to be equivalent to the ostensibly stronger version in which the factor map
\pi:X\to X_{\infty}
is replaced by any factor map
\pi^{\prime}:X\to Y
to a larger factor
Y
, in the sense that there is a factor map
\eta:Y\to X_{\infty}
with
\pi=\eta\circ\pi^{\prime}
.

A
is proved in
Section

3
. The primary ingredients include a set-algebraic mechanism for proving non-piecewise syndeticity (see
Lemma

2.1
); the IP Polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson and McCutcheon
[
5
, Lemma 6.12]
; and the polynomial topological characteristic factor theorem of Qiu
[
28
, Theorem B]
mentioned above.

1.2
Applications

A
serves to reduce questions of multiple polynomial recurrence in minimal systems to ones in inverse limits of minimal nilsystems, a setting where recurrence is much better understood. We present four such applications below. All topological dynamical systems – henceforth, just “systems” – appearing in this work are invertible.

A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is a
set of multiple topological recurrence
if for all minimal systems
(X,T)
, all nonempty, open
U\subseteq X
, and all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
n\in A
such that

U\cap T^{-n}U\cap\cdots\cap T^{-dn}U\neq\emptyset.

We change “multiple” to “single” when
d=1
, and we say that the set
A
is a set of topological recurrence
for a family of systems
when the definition holds for all systems in the family.
Determining necessary and sufficient conditions for a set to be a set of topological recurrence has been an important objective, both for theory and applications.
That
\mathbb{N}
is a set of multiple topological recurrence follows from Furstenberg and Weiss’s topological multiple recurrence theorem
[
9
, Theorem 1.4]
.

The two-point rotation shows that the set of odd numbers fails to be a set of single recurrence.
This system is, however, the essential obstruction: in minimal systems disjoint from the two-point rotation (equivalently, in minimal systems
(X,T)
for which
(X,T^{2})
is minimal), the set of odd numbers is a set of multiple recurrence.
This was proved by Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss, and Ye, who established the following more general result.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
11
, Theorem D]
)
.

Let
(X,T)
be a system and
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
(X,T^{k})
is minimal, then for all open
\emptyset\neq U\subseteq X
, the set

\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ U\cap T^{-n}U\cap\cdots\cap T^{-dn}U\neq\emptyset\big\}

has nonempty intersection with every infinite arithmetic progression of step size
k
.

In the following subsections, we show how
A
allows us to upgrade and extend
Theorem

1.2
to dynamically-defined syndetic sets (
B
), to polynomial multiple recurrence (
C
), and to totally minimal systems (
D
).
We conclude by showing how
A
implies that a generalization of
Theorem

1.2
conjectured by Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss, and Ye is equivalent to a long-standing open conjecture of Leibman (
E
).

1.2.1
Linear recurrence along dynamically syndetic sets

Our first application extends
Theorem

1.2
by replacing arithmetic progressions with sets of visit times in minimal systems satisfying a necessary disjointness condition. Given a minimal system
(Y,S)
, a point
y\in Y
, and a nonempty, open set
V\subseteq Y
, we write

R_{S}(y,V)\coloneqq\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\ \big|\ S^{n}y\in V\big\}

for the set of times that
y
visits
V
.
We call a subset of the integers
dynamically syndetic
if it contains a set of the form
R_{S}(y,V)
. Due to minimality, every dynamically syndetic set is syndetic; the converse is false: the set

\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\leqslant 0\}\cup\bigg(2\mathbb{N}\cap\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
\text{$k$ even}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\big[2^{k},2^{k+1}\big)\bigg)\cup\bigg(\big(2\mathbb{N}+1\big)\cap\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\big[2^{k},2^{k+1}\big)\bigg).

is syndetic, but not dynamically syndetic. For a proof, see
[
13
]
.

Remark 1.3
.

When
y\in V
, the set
R_{S}(y,V)
is a set of polynomial multiple topological recurrence. Indeed, by IP Polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson and McCutcheon
[
5
, Lemma 6.12]
, the set
R_{p}(U,\ldots,U)
has non-empty intersection with every
\mathsf{IP}
set; in particular, it has nonempty intersection with every central set. When
y\in V
, the set
R_{S}(y,V)
is central, and hence has nonempty intersection with
R_{p}(U,\ldots,U)
. For the definitions of
\mathsf{IP}
and central sets, see eg.
[
7
, Ch. 8.3]
.

We show in the following theorem that all sets of the form
R_{S}(y,V)
– not just those for which
y\in V
– are sets of multiple linear topological recurrence for all systems
(X,T)
that are sufficiently disjoint from
(Y,S)
. See
Section

2.2
for the requisite definitions.

Theorem B
.

Let
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
be minimal systems that do not share any nontrivial eigenvalues.
For all nonempty, open sets
U\subseteq X
and
V\subseteq Y
, all
y\in Y
(not necessarily in
V
), and all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the set

\displaystyle\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ U\cap T^{-n}U\cap\cdots\cap T^{-dn}U\neq\emptyset\big\}\cap R_{S}(y,V)

(3)

is dynamically syndetic.

In the case that
(Y,S)
is a rotation of
k
points, the condition that
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
share no common eigenvalues is readily seen to be equivalent to the condition that the system
(X,T^{k})
is minimal.
In this way,
B
generalizes
Theorem

1.2
from infinite progressions to other dynamically syndetic sets
R_{S}(y,V)
, including Beatty sequences
\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
(arising from irrational rotations);
\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\|n^{2}\alpha\|<\varepsilon\}
(arising from nilsystems);
\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\nu_{2}(n)\text{ is even}\}
, where
\nu_{2}(n)
denotes the
2
-adic valuation of an integer
n
(arising from an almost 1–1 extension of an odometer); and so on.

The assumption that the systems
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
have no common eigenvalues is necessary.
Indeed, if
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
share a common eigenvalue, then by
[
27
, Theorem 5]
(see also
[
24
]
), the product system
(X\times Y,T\times S)
is not transitive: there exist nonempty, open sets
U_{1},U_{2}\subseteq X
and
V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq Y
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(U_{1}\times V_{1})\cap(T\times S)^{-n}(U_{2}\times V_{2})=\emptyset
. Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
U\coloneqq U_{1}\cap T^{-n}U_{2}\neq\emptyset
. We see that for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(U\times V_{1})\cap(T\times S)^{-n}(U\times V_{2})=\emptyset
, whereby

\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ U\cap T^{-n}U\neq\emptyset\big\}\cap\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ V_{1}\cap T^{-n}V_{2}\neq\emptyset\big\}=\emptyset.

This contradicts the conclusion in
B
for any point
y\in V_{1}
, since for such points,
R_{S}(y,V_{2})\subseteq\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:V_{1}\cap T^{-n}V_{2}\}
.

The proof of
B
is completed in
Section

4.3
. We first use
A
to reduce it to the case that
(X,T)
is a minimal nilsystem. In that case, we show that the set in (
3
) is dynamically syndetic because it contains the set of times that a point
(x,\ldots,x,y)\in X^{d+1}\times Y
visits the set
U\times\cdots\times U\times V
under the map
I\times T\times T^{2}\times\cdots\times T^{d}\times S
. The existence of such a point follows by
Lemma

4.3
, which guarantees that a generic diagonal point in
X^{d+1}
generates a system that is disjoint from
(Y,S)
.

1.2.2
Polynomial recurrence along arithmetic progressions

Our second application of
A
is a generalization of
Theorem

1.2
to polynomial multiple recurrence with dynamically syndetic intersections. We say that two sets have
dynamically syndetic intersection
if their intersection is a dynamically syndetic set.

Theorem C
.

Let
(X,T)
be a system and
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
. If
(X,T^{k})
is minimal, then for all polynomials
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with
p(0)=0
and all nonempty, open
U\subseteq X
, the set

\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ U\cap T^{-p(n)}U\cap\cdots\cap T^{-dp(n)}U\neq\emptyset\big\}

has dynamically syndetic intersection with every infinite arithmetic progression of step size
k
.

We prove
C
in
Section

4.4
by first establishing the result for profinite nilsystems, then using
A
to lift the result to arbitrary minimal systems.

1.2.3
Recurrence in totally minimal systems

For our third application of
A
, we show that the “
p(0)=0
” assumption in
C
can be omitted in totally minimal systems. This improves on theorems of Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss, and Ye
[
11
, Cor. 6.3]
and Qiu
[
28
, cf. Theorem A]
, who show the
d=1
case for quadratic polynomials and non-constant polynomials, respectively.

Theorem D
.

Let
(X,T)
be totally minimal and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. For all non-constant polynomials
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
and all nonempty, open
U\subseteq X
, the set

\displaystyle\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ U\cap T^{-p(n)}U\cap\cdots\cap T^{-dp(n)}U\neq\emptyset\}

(4)

is dynamically syndetic.

Note that
D
does not follow from
C
by writing
p
as some other polynomial along an arithmetic progression. Indeed, not every
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
can be written as
p(n)=q(kn+j)
for some
q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with
q(0)=0
. To see why, note that if
p(n)=q(kn+j)
holds for all integers
n
, then it holds for all real numbers
n
. In particular, setting
n=-j/k
, we see that
p(-j/k)=q(0)=0
, whereby
p
has a rational root. Therefore, any integer-coefficient polynomial without rational roots cannot be written in this way.

We prove
D
in
Section

4.5
by using
A
to reduce to the case of minimal nilsystems, where we can appeal to the same set of results behind the proofs of Theorems
B
and
C
.

1.2.4
Conjectures of Glasner-Huang-Shao-Weiss-Ye and Leibman

C
takes a step toward a positive resolution of the following generalization of
Theorem

1.2
conjectured by Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss, and Ye. We state it here in an equivalent form.

Conjecture 1.4

(
[
11
, Conjecture 3]
)
.

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal system and
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
. If
(X,T^{k})
is minimal, then for all polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with
p_{i}(0)=0
for
i=1,\ldots,d
, and all nonempty, open
U\subseteq X
, the set

\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ T^{-p_{1}(n)}U\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}U\neq\emptyset\big\}

has nonempty intersection with all infinite arithmetic progressions of step size
k
.

In light of
A
, to prove
1.4
, it suffices to prove the same statement under the assumption that
(X,T)
is a nilsystem.
Polynomial orbits in nilsystems have been extensively studied. Most relevant to the present work is a conjecture of Leibman
[
23
, Conjecture 11.4]
, which gives an explicit description of the orbit closure of the diagonal
\Delta_{X^{d}}=\{(x,\ldots,x):x\in X\}
in a connected nilsystem
(X=G/\Gamma,a)
under a polynomial sequence.
The following conjecture, while formally a special case of
[
23
, Conjecture 11.4]
, embodies what we believe to be the central idea that Leibman’s conjecture seeks to convey.
Notation for nilsystems and nilrotations is found in
Section

2.3
.

Conjecture 1.5

(See
[
23
, Conjecture 11.4]
)
.

Let
(X=G/\Gamma,a)
be a totally minimal nilsystem. Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with
p_{i}(0)=0
, and let
g(n)=a^{p_{1}(n)}\otimes\cdots\otimes a^{p_{d}(n)}
be the corresponding polynomial sequence in
G^{d}
. The set

\overline{\{g(n)\Delta_{X^{d}}:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}

is connected.

Our main result in this section is that these conjectures of Glasner, Huang, Shao, Weiss and Ye and Leibman are, in fact, equivalent. The proof of
E
is completed in
Section

4.6.3
.

Theorem E
.

Conjectures
1.4
and
1.5
are equivalent.

It is interesting to juxtopose the minimality assumption in
1.4
with the total minimality assumption in
1.5
. Many recurrence problems related to
1.4
become much simpler when total minimality is assumed. In fact, it is stated explicitly in
[
11
]
that
1.4
may be easier if we assume
(X,T)
is totally minimal. The equivalence of this conjecture and
1.5
demonstrates that, unexpectedly, assuming total minimality does not make
1.4
easier. We make this fact precise below by formulating
4.20
– a version of
1.4
for totally minimal nilsystems – and showing that it, too, is equivalent to
1.4
.

We conclude by remarking that
1.4
is equivalent to its ergodic-theoretic analogue, in which minimal systems and open sets are replaced by ergodic probability measure preserving systems and sets of positive measure.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic measure preserving system and
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
. If
(X,\mu,T^{k})
is ergodic, then for all polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{d}
over
\mathbb{Z}
with
p_{i}(0)=0
for
i=1,\ldots,d
, and all measurable
A\subseteq X
with
\mu(A)>0
, the set

\big\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ \mu(T^{-p_{1}(n)}A\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}A)>0\big\}

has nonempty intersection with all infinite arithmetic progressions of step size
k
.

Although we will not develop this here, the equivalence between Conjectures
1.4
and
1.6
can be seen by using the structure theory of measure preserving systems to reduce
1.6
from arbitrary ergodic systems to ergodic nilsystems.
This implies that
1.6
is equivalent to

below, which we show in
Section

is also equivalent to

.

2
Preliminaries

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{Z}
the set of all positive integers and integers, respectively.

2.1
Families of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a collection of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
. The
upward closure
of
\mathscr{F}
, denoted
\uparrow\mathscr{F}
, is the set of all subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
that contain at least one member of
\mathscr{F}
. A collection of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
is
upward closed
if it is equal to its upward closure. For
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we define

A-\mathscr{F}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ A-n\in\mathscr{F}\},

that is, the set
A-\mathscr{F}
consists of those elements of
\mathbb{Z}
which translate
A
into
\mathscr{F}
.

Each of the following notions leads to an upward-closed collection of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
important to the intersection of Ramsey Theory and topological dynamics. A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is …

•

syndetic
if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

A\cup(A-1)\cup\cdots\cup(A-N)=\mathbb{Z};

•

thick
if for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
z\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\{z,z+1,\ldots,z+N\}\subseteq A
; and

•

piecewise syndetic
if it is the intersection of a syndetic set and a thick set, or, equivalently, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that the set

A\cup(A-1)\cup\cdots\cup(A-N)\text{ is thick}.

The following elementary lemma plays a crucial role in our proof of
A
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
and let
\mathscr{F}
be an upward-closed family of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
with the property that the intersection of all sets in any finite sub-collection of
\mathscr{F}
is syndetic. If
B\subseteq A\subseteq B-\mathscr{F}
, then
A\backslash B
is not piecewise syndetic.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of a contradiction, that
A\backslash B
is piecewise syndetic, and let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
T:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}(A\backslash B)-i
is thick.
Let
F\subseteq A
be the largest subset of
A
that is contained in an interval of length
\ell
, and let
I\supseteq F
be such an interval.
Since
A\subseteq B-\mathscr{F}
, we have that
B-f\in\mathscr{F}
for all
f\in F
.
By the assumption on
\mathscr{F}
, the intersection
S:=\bigcap_{f\in F}(B-f)
is syndetic.
Since
T
is thick, so is any of its shifts and hence we can find
n\in S\cap\big(\bigcup_{i\in I}(A\backslash B)-i\big)
.
From
n\in S
it follows that
n+F\subseteq B\subseteq A
.
From
n\in\bigcup_{i\in I}(A\backslash B)-i
it follows that
n+I
has an element
x\in A\backslash B
; in particular
x\notin n+F
.
Therefore,
(n+I)\cap A\supseteq\{x\}\cup(n+F)
whence it follows that
|(n+I)\cap A|>|F|
, contradicting the construction of
F
.
∎

The following lemma, when combined with
A
, allows us to lift recurrence from the maximal infinite step pronilfactor of a system. Recall the definition of a dynamically syndetic set from
Section

1.2.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
S,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
. If
S
is (dynamically) syndetic and
S\backslash B
is not piecewise syndetic, then
B
is (dynamically) syndetic.

Proof.

Suppose
S
is syndetic and
S\backslash B
is not piecewise syndetic. To show
B
is syndetic, we will show that
B
has nonempty intersection with every thick set.
Let
T\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be thick.
Since
B\cap T\supseteq(S\cap T)\backslash(S\backslash B)
and
S\cap T
is piecewise syndetic (but
S\backslash B
is not), we deduce that

, as desired.

If

is dynamically syndetic, the conclusion of the lemma follows from
[

, Theorem G]
.
∎

2.2
Topological dynamics

An
(invertible) topological dynamical system
is a compact metric space
X
together with a homeomorphism
T:X\to X
. We frequently refer to invertible topological dynamical systems simply as
systems
in this paper. A set
W\subseteq X
is
T
-invariant
if
T^{n}W\subseteq W
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
W\subseteq X
is nonempty, closed, and
T
-invariant, the system
(W,T)
is called a
subsystem
of
(X,T)
.

Given a subset
V\subseteq X
, the set
\overline{o}_{T}(V)=\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\{T^{n}x:x\in V\}}
is the
orbit closure of
V
under
T
. When
V=\{x\}
is a singleton, then we write
\overline{o}_{T}(x)
instead of
\overline{o}_{T}(\{x\})
and refer to it as the
orbit closure of
x
under
T
. A system
(X,T)
is
transitive
if at least one point has a dense orbit (equivalently, for all nonempty, open
U,V\subseteq X
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
U\cap T^{-n}V\neq\emptyset
);
minimal
if every point has a dense orbit; and
totally minimal
if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the system
(X,T^{n})
is minimal.

A
factor
of a system
(X,T)
is another system
(Y,S)
together with a continuous surjection
\pi\colon X\to Y
, called the
factor map
from
(X,T)
onto
(Y,S)
, such that

\pi\circ T=S\circ\pi.

If
(Y,S)
and
(Z,R)
are two factors of
(X,T)
with corresponding factor maps
\pi\colon X\to Y
and
\eta\colon X\to Z
, then we say that
(Y,S)

contains

(Z,R)
if there exists a factor map
\psi\colon Y\to Z
with
\eta=\psi\circ\pi
.
When
(Y,S)
is a factor of
(X,T)
, we equivalently call
(X,T)
an
extension
of
(Y,S)
. An extension
\pi:X\to Y
is
almost 1–1
if there exists a residual set
\Omega\subseteq X
such that for all
x\in\Omega
,
\pi^{-1}(\pi x)=\{x\}
.

A map
\phi:X\to Y
between two topological spaces
X
and
Y
is
semiopen
if for all nonempty, open
U\subseteq X
, the set
\phi U
has nonempty interior. We use the following fact several times throughout the paper; the proof is contained in the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 2.9]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

A factor map between two minimal systems is semiopen.

A system
(X,T)
is
equicontinuous
if the family of maps
\{T^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is equicontinuous. Every system
(X,T)
has a
maximal equicontinuous factor
(see, eg.,
[
1
, Ch. 9]
); it is an equicontinuous system that is maximal in the sense that it contains every equicontinuous factor of
(X,T)
.

A number
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is an
eigenvalue
of the system
(X,T)
if there exists a continuous function
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
for which
f\circ T=e^{2\pi i\theta}f
.
Note that
0
is an eigenvalue of all systems and hence it is called
trivial
. Any other eigenvalue is called
non-trivial
.
In a transitive system, every eigenfunction
f\in C(X)
has a constant absolute value.
When
\theta
is irrational, the function
f
can be seen (after normalizing) as a factor map
f:X\to S^{1}
to the unit circle under rotation by angle
2\pi\theta
. As this is an equicontinuous factor of
(X,T)
, it is contained in the maximal equicontinuous factor, and thus the set of eigenvalues of a transitive system coincides with those of its maximal equicontinuous factor.
The same is true when
\theta
is rational if one replaces
S^{1}
with one if its closed subgroups.

2.3
Nilsystems and pro-nilsystems

A
k
-step nilsystem
is a system
(G/\Gamma,T)
where
G
is a
k
-step nilpotent Lie group,
\Gamma\leqslant G
is a discrete cocompact subgroup, and
T:x\mapsto ax
is left translation on
X
by some fixed
a\in G
.
There is a unique Borel probability on
G/\Gamma
call the
Haar measure
that is invariant under the action of
G
on
G/\Gamma
. Every orbit closure in a
k
-step nilsystem is uniquely ergodic, and
k
-step nilsystems are always distal. For these and other standard facts about nilsystems, we refer to
[
17
]
.

An inverse limit of
k
-step nilsystems is called a
k
-step pronilsystem
. Every minimal system
(X,T)
admits a unique factor that is a
k
-step pronilsystem and contains all
k
-step nilfactors of
(X,T)
as a factor (see
[
17
, Chapter 17]
). It is called the
maximal
k
‑step pronilfactor
of
(X,T)
, and we denote it by
(X_{k},T)
. Note that
(X_{1},T)
is the maximal equicontinuous factor of
(X,T)
. As
k
tends to infinity, the maximal
k
‑step pronilfactors of
(X,T)
form an increasing tower of factors. The inverse limit

(X_{\infty},T):=\varprojlim_{k\to\infty}(X_{k},T)

is the
maximal
\infty
‑step pronilfactor
of
(X,T)
.

Remark 2.4
.

As an inverse limit, the topology on
X_{\infty}
is determined by the associated projection maps
\pi_{k}:X_{\infty}\to X_{k}
. Thus, if
U\subseteq X_{\infty}
is open, there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
V\subset X_{k}
open with
\pi_{k}^{-1}(V)\subseteq U
.

We will encounter polynomial sequences is nilpotent Lie groups. Generally, a
polynomial sequence
in a group
G
is any sequence of the form

g(n)=a_{1}^{p_{1}(n)}\cdots a_{d}^{p_{d}(n)}

where
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
are fixed elements of
G
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{d}
are fixed polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

3
A structure theorem for return-time sets

Throughout this section – the goal of which is to prove
A
– and the next, it will be useful to recall the definition of the set
R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
from (
1
) in the introduction. Recall also that a tuple of polynomials
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
is essentially distinct if for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant d
, the polynomial
p_{i}-p_{j}
is non-constant. We will need to consider the following subfamily of essentially distinct tuples:

\operatorname{Pol}_{d}:=\Big\{(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}:\ p_{i}(0)=0,\ p_{i}\neq p_{j}\Big\}.

3.1
Basics

The following lemmas will be used several times in this section.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(X,T)
be a system,
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\subseteq X
be nonempty and open,
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
be essentially distinct, and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, define
q_{i}(n)=p_{i}(n+a)-p_{i}(a)
. The tuple
q:=(q_{1},\dots,q_{d})\in\operatorname{Pol}_{d}
and

R_{q}(T^{-p_{1}(a)}U_{1},\ldots,T^{-p_{d}(a)}U_{d})=R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})-a.

Proof.

Clearly
q_{i}(0)=0
, so to show that
q\in\operatorname{Pol}_{d}
, we need only to show that
q_{i}\neq q_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
If
q_{i}=q_{j}
, then
n\mapsto p_{i}(n+a)-p_{j}(n+a)
is constant.
This implies that
p_{i}-p_{j}
is constant, a contradiction if
i\neq j
.

The equivalences

\displaystyle n\in R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})-a

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle n+a\in R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle T^{-p_{1}(n+a)}U_{1}\cap\ldots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n+a)}U_{d}\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle T^{-q_{1}(n)}(T^{-p_{1}(a)}U_{1})\cap\ldots\cap T^{-q_{d}(n)}(T^{-p_{d}(a)}U_{d})\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle n\in R_{q}(T^{-p_{1}(a)}U_{1},\ldots,T^{-p_{d}(a)}U_{d})

imply the second conclusion.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal system and
\pi:(X,T)\to(Y,T)
be a factor map. For all nonempty, open
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\subseteq X
and all
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
,

R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})\subseteq R_{p}\big((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\big).

Proof.

Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\subseteq X
be nonempty and open,
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
, and
n\in R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
. The set
T^{-p_{1}(n)}U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}U_{d}
is open and nonempty.
Since
\pi
is semiopen (
Lemma

2.3
) and commutes with
T^{-1}
, the set

\displaystyle T^{-p_{1}(n)}\pi U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}\pi U_{d}\supseteq\pi\big(T^{-p_{1}(n)}U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}U_{d}\big)

has nonempty interior. It follows that

(T^{-p_{1}(n)}\pi U_{1})^{\circ}\cap\cdots\cap(T^{-p_{d}(n)}\pi U_{d})^{\circ}=(T^{-p_{1}(n)}\pi U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\neq\emptyset.

Since
T
is a homeomorphism,

\big(T^{-p_{i}(n)}\pi U_{i}\big)^{\circ}=T^{-p_{i}(n)}\big(\pi U_{i}\big)^{\circ}.

As a result,

T^{-p_{1}(n)}\big(\pi U_{1}\big)^{\circ}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}\big(\pi U_{d}\big)^{\circ}\neq\emptyset,

whereby
n\in R_{p}\big((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\big)
, as was to be shown.
∎

3.2
Reduction to open extensions

We will show in this subsection that in order to prove
A
, it suffices to prove it under the assumption that the factor map
\pi
is open. First, we need a lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\pi:(X,T)\to(Y,T)
be an almost 1–1 extension of minimal systems. For all nonempty, open
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\subseteq X
and all tuples of polynomials
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
,

R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})=R_{p}\big((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\big).

Proof.

The containment

R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})\subseteq R_{p}\big((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}\big)

follows from
Lemma

3.2
. To see the reverse containment, suppose that
n\in R_{p}((\pi U_{1})^{\circ},\ldots,(\pi U_{d})^{\circ})
so that

\emptyset\neq T^{-p_{1}(n)}(\pi U_{1})^{\circ}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}(\pi U_{d})^{\circ}=(T^{-p_{1}(n)}\pi U_{1})^{\circ}\cap\cdots\cap(T^{-p_{d}(n)}\pi U_{d})^{\circ}.

It follows that the set
T^{-p_{1}(n)}\pi U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}\pi U_{d}
has nonempty interior. Since
\pi
is almost 1–1, there exists
y\in T^{-p_{1}(n)}\pi U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}\pi U_{d}
such that
\pi^{-1}\{y\}
is a singleton, say
\{x\}
. It follows that
x\in T^{-p_{1}(n)}U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{d}(n)}U_{d}
, whereby
n\in R_{p}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
, as was to be shown.
∎

The following lemma describes a property enjoyed by families of sets of visit times among sets whose images have non-empty intersection.

Lemma 3.5

The following lemma shows that in minimal systems, if a return-time set is non-empty, then it is syndetic.

Lemma 3.6

4.1
Criterion for disjointness

In this subsection we establish an analogue for minimal systems of
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
in ergodic theory, describing a criterion for disjointness involving a distal system.

A
joining
of two systems
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
is a subsystem
(W,T\times S)
of
(X\times Y,T\times S)
such that
\pi_{X}(W)=X
and
\pi_{Y}(W)=Y
, where
\pi_{X},\pi_{Y}
are the projections from
X\times Y
to
X
and
Y
, respectively.
We say two systems are
disjoint
if their only joining is the entire product system
(X\times Y,T\times S)
.
Points
x
and
y
in a system
(X,T)
are
proximal
if there exists
z\in X
such that
(z,z)\in\overline{o}_{T\times T}(x,y)
.
A system
(X,T)
is
distal
if for all distinct
x,y\in X
, the points
x
and
y
are not proximal.

For the proof of the following proposition, recall the discussion from
Section

2.2
regarding the maximal equicontinuous factor and eigenfunctions.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(X,T)
be a minimal distal system and let
(Y,S)
be a minimal system. The two systems are disjoint if and only if they do not share any nontrivial eigenvalues.

Proof.

Combining
[
1
, Chapter 11, Theorem 7]
with
[
1
, Chapter 11, Proposition 2 (ii)]
it follows that
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
are disjoint if and only if their maximal equicontinuous factors are disjoint. By
[
1
, Chapter 11, Theorem 9]
, two equicontinuous systems are disjoint if and only if their product is transitive. By
[
27
, Theorem 5]
(see also
[
24
]
), the product of two minimal systems is transitive if and only if they share no nontrivial eigenvalue.
This proves that
(X,T)
and
(Y,S)
are disjoint if and only if their maximal equicontinuous factors share no eigenvalues in common. The conclusion of the proposition now follows from the fact that the set of eigenvalues of a system coincides with the set of eigenvalues of its maximal equicontinuous factor, as was explained in
Section

2.2
.
∎

4.2
Eigenvalues of derived systems

By the

of a system
(X,T)
we mean its eigenvalues as a subgroup of
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.
The spectrum of
(X,T)
is denoted
\sigma(X,T)
.
Since
X
is metrizable, the spectrum
\sigma(X,T)
is countable.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(X,T)
be a nilsystem with
k
-many connected components

(X_{i},T^{k})
are pairwise isomorphic, and
\sigma(X,T)=\sigma(X_{i},T^{k})/k:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}:k\theta\in\sigma(X_{i},T^{k})\}
.

Proof.

For the first claim, observe that for
i,j
,
(X_{i},T^{k})
is isomorphic to
(X_{j},T^{k})
through the isomorphism
T^{j-i}
.

Suppose
TX_{i}=X_{i+1\bmod k}
and note that
(X,T)
is isomorphic to

where
S:X_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}\to X_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
is defined by

S(x,n)=\begin{cases}(x,n+1)&\text{ if }0\leqslant n\leqslant k-2,\\
(T^{k}x,0)&\text{ if }n=k-1.\end{cases}

If
f:X_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
is a continuous eigenfunction with

, then the restriction
f^{\prime}=f|_{X_{0}}
to
X_{0}
is an eigenfunction for
(X_{0},T^{k})
with eigenvalue
k\theta
, showing that
\sigma(X,T)\subseteq\sigma(X_{0},T^{k})/k
.

Conversely, if
\theta\in\sigma(X_{0},T^{k})/k
and

is a continuous eigenfunction of
(X_{0},T^{k})
with eigenvalue
k\theta
, the function
f^{\prime}:X_{0}\times(\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{C}
given by

satisfies

, showing that
\sigma(X_{0},T^{k})/k\subseteq\sigma(X,T)
.
∎

G
a nilpotent Lie group and
\Gamma
is a closed, discrete, cocompact subgroup of
G
. Write
T(x)=ax
for some
a\in G
. Let
\pi:G\to X
be the projection map and let
x
be as in the lemma’s statement.

Without loss of generality, we will assume
x=\pi(1_{G})
where
1_{G}
is the identity element of
G
. Indeed, suppose
x=h\Gamma
. Define
\Gamma_{x}=h\Gamma h^{-1}
. Then
\phi:X\to G/\Gamma_{x}
defined by
\phi(y)=hy
is a homeomorphism between
X
and
G/\Gamma_{x}
and under this map
\pi(1_{G})\mapsto h\pi(1_{G})=x
.

By
[
21
, Proposition 3.14]
, there exist a nilpotent Lie group
\tilde{G}
with a discrete closed cocompact subgroup
\tilde{\Gamma}
, a surjection
\eta:\tilde{G}\to G
with
\eta(\tilde{\Gamma})\subseteq\Gamma
, a unipotent automorphism
\tau
of
\tilde{G}
with
\tau(\tilde{\Gamma})=\tilde{\Gamma}
, and an element
c\in\tilde{G}
such that
a^{p(n)}=\eta(\tau^{n}(c))
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
\tilde{X}=\tilde{G}/\tilde{\Gamma}
. The map
\eta:\tilde{G}\to G
factors to
\eta:\tilde{X}\to X
, so that if
\tilde{\pi}:\tilde{G}\to\tilde{X}
is the projection map, then
\pi\circ\eta=\eta\circ\tilde{\pi}
. Letting
\tilde{x}=\tilde{\pi}(1_{\tilde{G}})
, we have
\eta(\tau^{n}(c\tilde{x}))=a^{p(n)}x
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. As a result,

\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:a^{p(n)}x\in U\}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\tau^{n}(c\tilde{x})\in\eta^{-1}(U)\}.

Note that the left hand side is nonempty according to our lemma’s assumption.

Let
\hat{G}
be the extension of
\tilde{G}
by
\tau
. By
[
21
, Proposition 3.9]
,
\hat{G}
is a nilpotent Lie group. Let
\hat{\tau}
be the element in
\hat{G}
representing
\tau
in the sense that
\tau(h)=\hat{\tau}h\hat{\tau}^{-1}
for any
h\in\tilde{G}
. Let
\hat{\Gamma}=\langle\tilde{\Gamma},\hat{\tau}\rangle\subseteq\hat{G}
. Since
\tau(\tilde{\Gamma})=\tilde{\Gamma}
, we have
\hat{\Gamma}\cap\tilde{G}=\tilde{\Gamma}
, and so
\hat{\Gamma}
is a discrete subgroup of
\hat{G}
and
\tilde{X}=\hat{G}/\hat{\Gamma}
. Therefore, as a transformation on
\tilde{X}
,
\hat{\tau}
is the same as
\tau
, i.e., for any
h\in\tilde{G}
and
y=h\hat{\Gamma}\in\tilde{X}
, we have

\tau(y)=\tau(h)\hat{\Gamma}=\hat{\tau}h\hat{\tau}^{-1}\hat{\Gamma}=\hat{\tau}h\hat{\Gamma}=\hat{\tau}y.

Thus,

\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\tau^{n}(c\tilde{x})\in\eta^{-1}(U)\}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\hat{\tau}^{n}(c\tilde{x})\in\eta^{-1}(U)\}.

Since
(\tilde{X}=\hat{G}/\hat{\Gamma},\hat{\tau})
is a nilsystem and the set in the right hand side is nonempty, this set is a dynamically syndetic set, as was to be shown.
∎

The next lemma is a special case of Theorems

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(X,T)
be a totally minimal nilsystem. For all

, all open

, and all non-constant polynomial

on

is dynamically syndetic.

Proof.

Denote

Given a point

and let

be the orbit closure of

under the map
\tilde{T}

By

4.5
, we can choose

Lemma 4.10

4.6

[

and

In this section we prove that Conjectures

and

are equivalent. We do so by relating both to new conjectures on topological multiple recurrence and nilsystems.
Throughout the section,
(X,T)
denotes a fixed minimal nilsystem.

4.6.1
Technical results

To prove the equivalence, we need some technical results about nilrotations from
[
22
]
.
Let
(X=G/\Gamma,T)
be a nilsystem. A
subnilmanifold

Y
of
X
is a closed subset of
X
for the form
Hx
where
H
is a closed subgroup of
G
and
x\in X
.

If
V
is a connected subnilmanifold of
X
and
g
is a polynomial sequence in
G
, it is shown in
[
22
, Theorem 2.3]
that there exists a subset
Y_{V,g}
of
X
such that for almost every
x\in V
(with respect to the Haar measure on
V
), the orbit closure
\overline{o}_{g}(x)=\overline{\{g(n)x:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
is a translate of
Y_{V,g}
, i.e.
\overline{o}_{g}(x)=aY_{V,g}
for some
a\in G
.
A point
x
with this property is called a
generic point
for
g
on
V
and the set
Y_{V,g}
is called the
generic orbit
of
g
on
V
.

Lemma 4.12

(
[
22
, Theorem 5.9]
)
.

Suppose
X=G/\Gamma
is a nilmanifold and
g
is a polynomial sequence in
G
. Let
V
be a connected subnilmanifold of
X
and let
Y_{V,g}
be the generic orbit of
g
on
V
. Let
Y
be a connected component of
Y_{V,g}
and suppose
Y=Hx,
where
H
is a closed subgroup of
G
and
x\in X
.
Then every connected component of the orbit closure
\overline{o}_{g}(V)=\overline{\{g(n)V:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
is a translate of
HV=\bigcup_{h\in H}hV
.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
X=G/\Gamma
be a nilmanifold and
g
be a polynomial sequence in
G
. Let
V
be a connected subnilmanifold of
X
. If
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
is connected, then

\overline{o}_{g}(V)=\overline{\{g(p(n))V:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}

for every non-constant polynomial
p
on
\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
is connected and let
p
be a non-constant polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
x\in V
be generic for both polynomial sequences
g(n)
and
h(n)=g(p(n))
. (Note that such a point
x
exists since the set of generic points on
V
for each polynomial sequence has full Haar measure.)

By
[
21
, Theorem B]
, there exists a connected closed subgroup
H
of
G
and points
x_{0},\ldots,x_{\ell-1}\in X
, not necessarily distinct, such that the sets
Y_{i}=Hx_{i}
are closed subnilmanifolds of
X
, and

\overline{o}_{g}(x)=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\ell-1}Y_{i}.

Moreover, the sequence
n\mapsto g(n)x
cyclically visits
Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{\ell-1}
and the sequence
n\mapsto g(\ell n+i)x
is well-distributed on
Y_{i}
.

For
i\in\{0,\ldots,\ell-1\}
, define a new polynomial sequence
f_{i}(n)=g(\ell n+p(i))
. Then by
[
21
, Theorem B]
again,

\overline{\{f_{i}(n)x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}=\overline{\{g(\ell n+p(i))x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}=Y_{p(i)\bmod\ell}.

The coefficients of the polynomial
n\mapsto p(\ell n+i)-p(i)
are divisible by
\ell
and so the function
n\mapsto\frac{p(\ell n+i)-p(i)}{\ell}
is a polynomial on
\mathbb{Z}
.
Since
Y_{p(i)\bmod\ell}
is connected, by
Lemma

4.6
,

Y_{p(i)\bmod\ell}=\overline{\left\{f_{i}\left(\frac{p(\ell n+i)-p(i)}{\ell}\right)x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}}=\overline{\{g(p(\ell n+i))x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}.

It follows that

\overline{\{h(n)x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\overline{\{g(p(\ell n+i))x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\ell-1}Y_{p(i)\bmod\ell}.

In other words,
\overline{o}_{h}(x)
consists of some connected components of
\overline{o}_{g}(x)
.
By
Lemma

4.12
, the connected components of
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
and
\overline{o}_{h}(V)
are translates of
HV
; in particular, they have the same dimension. Since
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
is already connected, we deduce that
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
itself is a translate of
HV
.
On the other hand,
\overline{o}_{h}(V)\subseteq\overline{o}_{g}(V)
. Therefore, any connected component of
\overline{o}_{h}(V)
is a subnilmanifold of the connected nilmanifold
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
of the same dimension and so it must be that
\overline{o}_{h}(V)=\overline{o}_{g}(V)
.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
X=G/\Gamma
be a nilmanifold and
g(n)
be a polynomial sequence on
G
. Let
Y\subseteq V\subseteq X
be subsets such that
Y
is dense in
V
. Then

\overline{o}_{g}(Y)=\overline{o}_{g}(V).

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in V
be arbitrary. Since
Y
is dense in
V
, there exists a sequence
(x_{i})\subseteq Y
such that
x_{i}\to x
. It follows that
g(n)x_{i}\to g(n)x
as
i\to\infty
. Thus,

g(n)x\in\overline{o}_{g}(Y).

Since
n,x
are arbitrary, we get
\overline{o}_{g}(V)\subseteq\overline{o}_{g}(Y)
.
∎

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
X=G/\Gamma
be a nilmanifold and
V
be a connected subnilmanifold of
X
. Let
g(n)
be a polynomial sequence on
G
. If
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
is disconnected, then there exist
k,j_{1},j_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\overline{\{g(kn+j_{1})V:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}\cap\overline{\{g(kn+j_{2})V:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}=\emptyset.

Remark 4.16
.

If
V
is a single point, then the lemma follows from
[
21
, Theorem B]
.

Proof.

By
[
22
, Theorem 1.13]
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
, the set of points in
V
which are non-generic for the polynomial sequence
(g(kn+j))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
has zero measure (with respect to the Haar measure on
V
).
Therefore, there exists
x_{0}\in V
which is generic for
(g(kn+j))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N},j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
.
By
[
21
, Theorem B]
there exists
k
such that
\overline{\{g(kn+j)x_{0}:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
is connected for any
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
. Since
x_{0}
is generic for
(g(kn+j))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
for
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
, by
[
22
, Theorem 1.13]
again, for generic
x\in V
and
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
,
\{g(kn+j)x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is connected.

We claim that for any
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V)=\overline{\{g(kn+j)V:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
is connected.
For contradiction, assume that there exist disjoint open sets
U_{1},U_{2}\subseteq X
both of which intersect
\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V)
and

\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V)\subseteq U_{1}\cup U_{2}.

Since
V
is connected,
g(k+j)V
is connected and so it belongs to either
U_{1}
or
U_{2}
.
Without loss of generality, assume
g(k+j)V\subseteq U_{1}
. For generic
x\in V
,
g(k+j)(x)\in U_{1}
and
\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(x)
is connected.
As a result,

\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(x)\subseteq U_{1}.

Now by
Lemma

4.14
,

\overline{\{g(kn+j)V:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}=\bigcup_{x\in V\text{generic}}\overline{\{g(kn+j)x:\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}.

Therefore,

\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V)\subseteq U_{1}

contradicting the assumption that
\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V)\cap U_{2}\neq\emptyset
. Thus
\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V)
is connected for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

If

\overline{o}_{g(kn+j_{1})}(V)\cap\overline{o}_{g(kn+j_{2})}(V)\neq\emptyset

for all

, then all
\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V)
(

) belong to a single connected component of
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
. But this is impossible since
\overline{o}_{g}(V)
is disconnected and

\overline{o}_{g}(V)=\bigcup_{j=0}^{k-1}\overline{o}_{g(kn+j)}(V).

The proof is now complete.
∎

5.1
Essential distinctness and the infinite step pronilfactor

The essential distinctness assumption on

The example in the following proposition shows that

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists a minimal
2
-step nilsystem
(X,T)
and a nonempty, open set

such that if
\pi:X\to Z
is the factor map to the maximal equicontinuous factor, then

R((\pi U)^{\circ},(\pi U)^{\circ},(\pi U)^{\circ})\backslash R(U,U,U)

Proof.

For

denote the distance from
x
to
0

Let

T^{n}(x,y)=\left(x+n\alpha,y+nx+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\alpha\right).

Fix

and define

to be the ball centered at
(0,0)

Suppose

\displaystyle\max\{\lVert x_{0}\rVert,\lVert y_{0}\rVert\}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon/4,

\displaystyle\max\left\{\lVert x_{0}+n\alpha\rVert,\left\lVert y_{0}+nx_{0}+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\alpha\right\rVert\right\}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon/4,\text{ and}

5.4
Positive upper Banach density and piecewise syndeticity

The
upper Banach density
of a set

is

d^{*}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\max_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{|A\cap\{n+1,\ldots,n+N\}|}{N}.

Piecewise syndetic sets have positive upper Banach density; the set of squarefree integers demonstrates that the converse is false. Given upper Banach density’s importance as a tool in the development of ergodic Ramsey theory, it is natural to ask whether the role of piecewise syndeticity as a notion of largeness can be replaced by positive upper Banach density in
A
.

We believe the answer to
5.6

The use of structured factors of topological dynamical systems in the study topological multiple recurrence, initiated in

In this section, we explore this topic in more detail, formulating a concrete question that can be viewed as an ergodic counterpart to

Up to this point in the paper, we have worked with topological dynamical systems

[

We remark that when

is a minimal topological system, its topological

]
, one can show that

\lim_{N-M\to\infty}\frac{1}{N-M}\sum_{n=M}^{N}|\alpha(n)-\beta(n)|=0.

Now we have

R_{Z_{\infty},\varepsilon_{2}}\backslash R_{X,\varepsilon_{1}}\subseteq\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:|\alpha(n)-\beta(n)|>\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}\}

and so has Banach density zero.
∎

Does the conclusion in (
13
) of
Theorem

5.8
hold when
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=0
? If true, this would yield a tighter ergodic-theoretic analogue to

. The argument given above fails when
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=0
, but we could not rule out the possibility that the statement is true.

Question 5.9
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic measure preserving system and
(Z_{\infty},\mu,T)
be its
\infty
-step pronilfactor. Let
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]^{d}
be essentially distinct. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}:X\to[0,\infty)
be bounded measurable functions. Do the sets

R_{X,0}=\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\int_{X}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{d}(n)}f_{d}\ d\mu>0\bigg\}

and

R_{Z_{\infty},0}=\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\int_{X}T^{p_{1}(n)}\mathbb{E}(f_{1}|Z_{\infty})\cdots T^{p_{d}(n)}\mathbb{E}(f_{d}|Z_{\infty})\ d\mu>0\bigg\}

satisfy

d^{*}(R_{Z_{\infty},0}\backslash R_{X,0})=0?

We conclude this subsection by offering a positive answer to
5.9
in the case that
d=2
,
p_{1}(n)=0
, and
p_{2}(n)=n
. First, we need a lemma.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
(Z_{1},m_{Z_{1}},T)
be a Kronecker system. Let
g_{1},g_{2}\colon Z_{1}\to[0,\infty)
be bounded measurable functions and define for every
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
,

D_{\varepsilon}=\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\int g_{1}\cdot T^{n}g_{2}\,\mathrm{d}m_{Z_{1}}>\varepsilon\bigg\}.

Then
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d^{*}(D_{0}\backslash D_{\varepsilon})=0
.

Proof.

We can assume that
Z_{1}
is a compact abelian group and that
m_{Z_{1}}
is the normalized Haar measure on
Z_{1}
.
Let
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(Z_{1},m_{Z_{1}})
consisting of characters, so that

e_{k}\circ T=s_{k}\,e_{k},\qquad s_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

Expand

g_{1}=\sum_{k}a_{k}e_{k},\qquad a_{k}:=\langle g_{1},e_{k}\rangle,\qquad g_{2}=\sum_{k}b_{k}e_{k},\qquad b_{k}:=\langle g_{2},e_{k}\rangle.

By Parseval,

\sum_{k}|a_{k}|^{2}=\|g_{1}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{k}|b_{k}|^{2}=\|g_{2}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.

Hence
(a_{k}),(b_{k})\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
. By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\sum_{k}|a_{k}\overline{b_{k}}|\;\leq\;\Big(\sum_{k}|a_{k}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{k}|b_{k}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\;<\;\infty,

so
(a_{k}\overline{b_{k}})\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
.
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define

\gamma(n)=\langle g_{1},\,T^{n}g_{2}\rangle.

Using the above derived Fourier expansions, we get

\gamma(n)=\sum_{k}a_{k}\,\overline{b_{k}}\,s_{k}^{\,n},

with absolute convergence due to the
\ell^{1}
bound.
For each
k
, let
H_{k}\leq\mathbb{S}^{1}
be the closed subgroup generated by
s_{k}
and set

G=\prod_{k}H_{k}

with the product topology. Define, for every
x=(x_{k})_{k}\in G
,

F(x)=\sum_{k}(a_{k}\overline{b_{k}})\,x_{k}.

Because
\sum_{k}|a_{k}\overline{b_{k}}|<\infty
and
|x_{k}|=1
, the series converges uniformly on
G
, hence
F
is continuous.
Define the rotation

R\colon G\to G,\qquad(Rx)_{k}:=s_{k}x_{k}.

Let
\mathbf{1}=(1,1,\dots)\in G
. Then
R^{n}(\mathbf{1})=(s_{k}^{\,n})_{k}
and

F\big(R^{n}(\mathbf{1})\big)=\sum_{k}(a_{k}\overline{b_{k}})\,s_{k}^{\,n}=\gamma(n).

Thus the correlation sequence
n\mapsto\int g_{1}\cdot g_{2}\circ T^{n}\,d\mu
is realized as a continuous observable evaluated along the orbit of a rotation on a compact abelian group; in particular it is an almost periodic (Bohr) sequence.
Finally, since

D_{\varepsilon}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\gamma(n)>\varepsilon\}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:F\big(R^{n}(\mathbf{1})\big)>\varepsilon\},

The claim
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d^{*}(D_{0}\backslash D_{\varepsilon})=0
follows by the continuity of
F
.
∎

Theorem 5.11
.

When
d=2
,
p_{1}(n)=0
, and
p_{2}(n)=n
,
5.9
has an affirmative answer.

Proof.

Consider the following sets:

\displaystyle R_{X,0}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\int_{X}f_{1}\cdot T^{n}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu>0\bigg\},

\displaystyle R_{X,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\int_{X}f_{1}\cdot T^{n}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu>\varepsilon\bigg\},

\displaystyle R_{Z_{1},0}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\int\mathbb{E}(f_{1}|Z_{1})\cdot T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{2}|Z_{1})\,\mathrm{d}m_{Z_{1}}>0\bigg\},

\displaystyle R_{Z_{1},\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\int\mathbb{E}(f_{1}|Z_{1})\cdot T^{n}\mathbb{E}(f_{2}|Z_{1})\,\mathrm{d}m_{Z_{1}}>\varepsilon\bigg\}.

Note that we have

R_{X,\varepsilon}\subseteq R_{X,0}\subseteq R_{Z_{1},0},

where the first inclusion holds trivially, and the second one follows from basic properties of the conditional expectation operator.
So to finish the proof, it suffices to show that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d^{*}(R_{Z_{1},0}\backslash R_{X,\varepsilon})=0.

(14)

By
Lemma

5.10
, we have
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d^{*}(R_{Z_{1},0}\backslash R_{Z_{1},\varepsilon})=0
, which means that instead of (
14
), it suffices to prove

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d^{*}(R_{Z_{1},\varepsilon}\backslash R_{X,\varepsilon})=0.

(15)

By
Theorem

5.8
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d^{*}(R_{Z_{1},2\varepsilon}\backslash R_{X,\varepsilon})=0
.
Hence, instead of (
15
), it suffices to show

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d^{*}(R_{Z_{1},\varepsilon}\backslash R_{Z_{1},2\varepsilon})=0.

This follows by monotonicity.
∎
