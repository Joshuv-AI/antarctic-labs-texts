Utility of Admission and Intrapartum Cardiotocography for Predicting Umbilical Artery pH Status at Birth: A Retrospective Cohort Study in Iran
Authors: Fatemeh Golshahi, Behrokh Sahebdel, Nafiseh Saedi, Mahboobeh Shirazi, Jafar Golshahi, Azita Zare, Nooshin Faraji, Zahra Hamidi Madani, Sedigheh Hantoushzadeh, Bahram Salmanian, Mohammadamin Parsaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIntrapartum cardiotocography (CTG) is used to detect fetal hypoxia, but static classifications can miss progressive compromise. This study analyzes longitudinal CTG trends to find early predictors of umbilical artery acidemia (pH < 7.20) at birth.MethodsA retrospective cohort study analyzed vaginal deliveries (2019–2022) at a tertiary hospital. Singleton pregnancies were stratified into cases (pH < 7.2, n = 70) and controls (pH ≥ 7.2, n = 182). CTG tracings were assessed for baseline FHR, beat‐to‐beat variability (BTBV), accelerations, and decelerations at four timepoints: admission, latent and active first‐stage labor, and second‐stage labor. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression (adjusted for maternal age, comorbidities, and gestational age) identified predictors, with receiving operating characteristic (ROC) analysis evaluating predictive performance.ResultsWe found that neonatal acidemia at birth was predicted by elevated baseline FHR at admission, contrasted with decreased FHR during active first‐stage and second‐stage labor. Progressive reduction in BTBV showed a strong association at all stages of labor, most prominent at second‐stage labor, absent accelerations during first‐stage labor, and presence of decelerations, especially late decelerations during second‐stage labor. ROC analysis demonstrated excellent predictive performance for second‐stage BTBV (AUC = 0.992) and baseline FHR (AUC = 0.914).ConclusionSpecific evolving CTG patterns effectively predicted fetal acidemia. Parameters from the second stage of labor were most predictive, underscoring the critical importance of continuous monitoring to identify high‐risk cases and guide timely intervention for fetal hypoxia.

Introduction

Intrapartum fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring is an essential aspect of obstetric care, enabling real‐time evaluation of fetal status by identifying signs of hypoxia and allowing prompt clinical intervention [1]. Regulated by the fetal peripheral chemoreflex and autonomic nervous system through sympathetic‐parasympathetic balance, FHR patterns reflect oxygenation status and neurological integrity, and can serve as sensitive markers of hypoxemia and acidemia [2, 3]. The two primary monitoring approaches include intermittent auscultation and continuous electronic fetal monitoring via cardiotocography (CTG) [4]. Introduced in the late 1960s [5], CTG revolutionized fetal monitoring by providing continuous evaluation of fetal heart rate patterns and their relationship to uterine contractions, making it the preferred method in modern practice [2].

Despite its widespread use, the evidence supporting the benefits of continuous CTG over intermittent auscultation in both low‐ and high‐risk pregnancies remains inconclusive [4, 6]. While continuous CTG has been associated with a reduction in neonatal seizures, studies have not demonstrated a significant effect on overall perinatal mortality or cerebral palsy incidence [4]. Furthermore, routine CTG admission for low‐risk pregnancies has been linked to increased rates of cesarean section and instrumental vaginal delivery, without corresponding improvements in perinatal outcomes [4]. Nevertheless, most experts recommend continuous CTG in cases where there is a suspected risk of fetal hypoxia or acidosis [7].

Current guidelines define fixed thresholds for normality in basic CTG parameters (baseline FHR, variability, accelerations, and decelerations) and classify tracings into three‐tier categories (I–III) based on their risk of hypoxia and acidemia [8]. However, it is important to note that fetal hypoxia develops progressively during labor rather than as discrete categorical events [3]. Therefore, evolving FHR patterns may demonstrate pathological trends while still technically falling within “normal” parameter ranges before meeting criteria for categorical abnormality. This highlights the potential value of quantitative trend analysis that extends beyond static categorical assessments, which can help detect the transition from physiologic compensation to fetal compromise at different stages of labor. This may help develop new strategies to avoid fetal hypoxic‐ischemic injury in labor. Through longitudinal analysis of basic CTG parameters from admission to delivery, we will compare trajectory differences between neonates with and without acidemia to evaluate the predictive capacity of each parameter during labor for neonatal acidemia at birth.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary referral center affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran. We systematically reviewed electronic medical records from the hospital's Health Information System to identify all consecutive eligible pregnancies that underwent vaginal delivery at our institution between January 2019 and January 2022.

Ethical Considerations

Before receiving medical care, all participants were thoroughly informed about the treatment procedures, including potential benefits and adverse outcomes, through both verbal explanations and written materials. Written informed consent was obtained following this comprehensive disclosure. According to our institutional universal protocol, patients were additionally counseled about the educational and research objectives of our center, after which written consent was obtained for anonymous data publication. We emphasized that participation was voluntary and that declining consent would not affect the standard of care provided. This study was conducted in full adherence to the ethical principles established by the Declaration of Helsinki [9]. Furthermore, all study findings are reported in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [10].

Study Objectives

This study aimed to evaluate the predictive utility of admission and intrapartum basic CTG parameters (baseline FHR, beat‐to‐beat variability [BTBV], accelerations, variable deceleration, and late deceleration patterns) measured during hospital admission, 1st stage of labor (both latent and active phases), and 2nd stage of labor, for identifying neonates with acidemia (umbilical artery potential of hydrogen [pH] < 7.2) at birth.

While current diagnostic criteria define neonatal acidemia as umbilical artery pH < 7.00 with base deficit ≥ 12 mmol/L [11], we employed a pH < 7.2 threshold for analysis, based on its well‐documented association with fetal compromise and adverse neonatal outcomes in prior studies [12, 13, 14]. Additionally, this higher threshold was necessary to ensure adequate statistical power, as application of the stricter diagnostic criteria would have resulted in insufficient case numbers in our cohort. It has further been noted that, while a pH of 7.00 constitutes the primary cutoff for defining neonatal acidemia at birth, a threshold of 7.2 may also serve as a meaningful criterion for identifying fetuses with abnormal FHR tracings who could benefit from intervention before the development of pathological fetal acidosis and consequent injury [15]. Consequently, employing a higher pH threshold to stratify neonates into low‐ and high‐risk groups may facilitate the detection of more subtle forms of fetal distress at birth through comparison of cardiotocographic findings.

Consequently, we stratified the study population into two groups for comparative analysis: cases (acidemia, pH < 7.2) and controls (no acidemia, pH ≥ 7.2), allowing for a systematic evaluation of CTG parameter differences between these clinically relevant cohorts.

Eligibility Criteria

We included singleton pregnancies in women aged 18–40 years who underwent vaginal delivery with complete CTG assessments at all critical stages: hospital admission, 1st stage of labor (both latent and active phases), and 2nd stage of labor. Only cases with properly recorded umbilical arterial blood gas analysis (ABG) results in the delivery medical records were included in the analysis. Exclusion criteria comprised multiple gestations, maternal age < 18 or > 40 years, gestational age at birth < 34 or > 42 weeks, incomplete CTG data across specified labor stages, missing umbilical ABG results, fetal demise, intrapartum conversion to cesarean delivery, instrumental vaginal delivery, and refusal of data publication consent.

Labor progression was categorized according to the classification by Zhang et al. [16] as follows:

1 st stage: This stage begins with the onset of regular uterine contractions leading to cervical changes and concludes when the cervix is fully dilated to 10 cm, whether occurring spontaneously or through medical induction. It was further divided into two phases based on cervical dilation: Latent phase: The initial part of the first stage, lasting until the cervix reaches 6 cm dilation. Active phase: The subsequent phase, extending from 6 cm to full cervical dilation (10 cm).

Data Collection

For each included case, we systematically extracted the following data from medical records: maternal characteristics (age, gestational age at birth, comorbidities), labor details (indications for admission, induction methods, duration of labor stages), CTG parameters (baseline FHR, BTBV, accelerations, variable and late decelerations) measured at admission, during 1st stage (latent and active phases), and 2nd stage of labor, umbilical ABG results (pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide [pCO2], bicarbonate [HCO3], and base deficit), and neonatal outcomes (birth weight, 1‐ and 5‐min Apgar scores, neonatal intensive care unit [NICU] admission).

CTG Assessments

As our center is an academic referral institution specializing in high‐risk pregnancies, routine continuous fetal monitoring is performed during labor for all patients. From each patient, one 20‐min CTG trace (specifically, the trace demonstrating the most concerning presentation) was selected for each stage of labor. The recorded tracings were printed, analyzed, and reviewed by two board‐certified specialists in Obstetrics and Gynecology, both with training in Maternal‐Fetal Medicine. Any disagreements in interpretation were resolved through consensus and repeated review to minimize interobserver bias. The evaluations were conducted following the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidelines [17], which served as the basis for defining and measuring the following variables: Baseline FHR: The average FHR, rounded to the nearest 5 bpm over a 10‐min period, excluding periodic or episodic changes, segments with significant variability, and baseline shifts exceeding 25 bpm. The normal range was defined as 110–160 bpm. BTBV: Irregular fluctuations in baseline FHR, measured as the peak‐to‐trough amplitude in bpm. Normal variability was considered between 6 and 25 bpm. Acceleration: A sudden rise in FHR (reaching peak within 30 s), defined as an increase of at least 15 bpm above baseline, lasting 15 s or more but less than 2 min. Variable Deceleration: A rapid decline in FHR, with the drop to the lowest point occurring in under 30 s. The decrease had to be at least 15 bpm, lasting 15 s to under 2 min. Late Deceleration: A gradual, typically symmetrical decrease in FHR associated with a uterine contraction, taking 30 s or longer to reach the lowest point. The nadir of the deceleration occurred after the peak of the contraction.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (percentages) and compared between groups (pH < 7.2 vs pH ≥ 7.2) using the chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using Shapiro‐Wilk tests; normally distributed data (p > 0.05) are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non‐normal data (p < 0.05) are presented as median [interquartile range]. Continuous variables were compared between groups using independent samples t‐tests for normally distributed data (incorporating Levene's variance assessment) and Mann‐Whitney U tests for non‐normally distributed variables. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all of the analyses.

To evaluate the predictive utility of individual CTG parameters for identifying umbilical artery pH < 7.2 at birth, we first conducted univariate binomial logistic regression analyses for each CTG variable, reporting odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs. CTG parameters demonstrating significant predictive value (p < 0.05) in univariate analyses were subsequently included in a multivariate binomial logistic regression model to assess their independent predictive capabilities. The multivariate model adjusted for clinically relevant covariates selected a priori based on existing literature and data availability: maternal age, gestational age at delivery, and maternal comorbidities [11, 18].

Furthermore, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to examine and compare the predictive ability of the CTG parameters (continuous variables) that showed superior predictive performance in the logistic regression analyses (p < 0.001). We calculated the area under the ROC curves (AUCs) and standard errors. Optimal cutoff values were determined using Youden's J index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1), with corresponding sensitivity and specificity values reported for each significant predictor [19].

CTG Findings

Between‐Group Differences

A comparative analysis of CTG findings between neonates with umbilical artery pH < 7.2 (cases) and those with pH ≥ 7.2 (controls) revealed significant differences (Table 2). Cases demonstrated a significantly higher baseline FHR at initial assessment (p = 0.005), with comparable values during the latent phase of 1st stage of labor (p = 0.539). Notably, cases exhibited significantly lower baseline FHR during both the active phase of 1st stage of labor and the 2nd stage (p < 0.001 for both), indicating a steeper FHR decline in fetuses developing acidemia as the labor progresses (Figure 1).

Comparison of mean values with 95% confidence intervals for (a) baseline FHR and (b) BTBV across four study timepoints between cases (umbilical artery pH < 7.2) and controls (pH ≥ 7.2).

BTBV was significantly reduced in cases compared to controls across all measured timepoints (p < 0.001 for all comparisons) (Figure 1). These findings demonstrate a persistent depression of BTBV throughout labor in fetuses developing acidemia.

The prevalence of present accelerations was significantly lower in cases compared to controls at all measured timepoints (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). While variable deceleration rates were comparable between groups during initial admission and the latent phase of 1st stage of labor, cases demonstrated significantly higher rates during both the active phase of 1st stage of labor (p < 0.001) and 2nd stage of labor (p < 0.001). Furthermore, late decelerations occurred significantly more frequently in cases across all evaluated timepoints (p < 0.05).

Predictive Utility for Neonatal Acidemia

Our univariate analysis revealed several significant predictors of neonatal acidemia. A higher baseline FHR at admission was associated with increased risk (OR = 1.051 [95% CI: 1.017–1.086]; p = 0.003), while lower baseline FHR during both the active phase of the 1st stage (OR = 0.889 [95% CI: 0.852–0.926]; p < 0.001) and 2nd stage (OR = 0.757 [95% CI: 0.704–0.814]; p < 0.001) showed strong predictive value. Similarly, reduced BTBV at all measured timepoints of initial admission (OR = 0.805 [95% CI: 0.748–0.886]; p < 0.001), latent (OR = 0.727 [95% CI: 0.659–0.803]; p < 0.001) and active (OR = 0.250 [95% CI: 0.156–0.401]; p < 0.001) phases of 1st stage of labor, and 2nd stage (OR = 0.160 [95% CI: 0.084–0.303]; p < 0.001) significantly predicted acidemia.

The absence of accelerations during both latent (OR = 0.137 [95% CI: 0.072–0.263]; p < 0.001) and active (OR = 0.074 [95% CI: 0.035–0.160]; p < 0.001) phases of 1st stage of labor also demonstrated predictive ability. Furthermore, the presence of variable decelerations during the active phase of the 1st stage (OR = 6.166 [95% CI: 2.597–14.637]; p < 0.001) and 2nd stage (OR = 9.167 [95% CI: 4.622–18.181]; p < 0.001), as well as late decelerations during the 2nd stage (OR = 50.000 [95% CI: 11.418–218.957]; p < 0.001), were all strongly associated with acidemia.

In multivariate analysis, all significant univariate predictors maintained independent predictive ability. Baseline FHR at admission remained significant (p = 0.002), while all other predictors showed even stronger associations (p < 0.001). Variables that failed to demonstrate predictive value in univariate analysis were excluded from the final model. The complete results of our regression analyses are presented in Table 3.

We further performed ROC curve analysis to evaluate and compare the predictive performance of baseline FHR during the active phase of the 1st stage and 2nd stage, along with BTBV at all four measurement timepoints, for neonatal acidemia (Figure 2). Detailed results are presented in Table 4. Among the analyzed predictors, BTBV at the active phase of the 1st stage and BTBV and baseline FHR at the 2nd stage of labor showed the strongest predictive ability, with AUC values of 0.914, 0.992, and 0.914, respectively. The optimal BTBV cut‐off at the active phase of the 1st stage was 11.5 bpm, showing a sensitivity of 99.5% and specificity of 64.3%, while at the 2nd stage it was 10.5 bpm, demonstrating a sensitivity of 94.0% and specificity of 100.0%. Discussion

Key Findings

To our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive evaluation of the predictive capacity of individual basic CTG for neonatal acidemia across all labor stages. Our analysis revealed distinct temporal patterns: elevated baseline FHR at admission, followed by decreasing values during active first stage and second stage labor, along with persistently reduced BTBV from admission through delivery, were significantly associated with acidemia at birth. Moreover, we identified that the absence of accelerations during the 1st stage of labor, the presence of variable decelerations in the 1st and 2nd stages, and late decelerations during the 2nd stage of labor predicted neonatal acidemia. These findings demonstrate that specific CTG parameters possess unique predictive value at different labor phases, suggesting their potential utility for identifying high‐risk pregnancies through dynamic assessment rather than static evaluation.

Fetal Compensatory Mechanisms Against Intrapartum Hypoxia

Naturally, labor exposes the fetus to intermittent hypoxemia as uterine contractions reduce placental perfusion [20]. While catastrophic events (e.g., placental abruption) cause severe, sustained hypoxemia, normal labor involves brief, cyclical hypoxemic episodes that healthy fetuses tolerate through anaerobic metabolism and rapid recovery between contractions [3, 21]. The fetus relies on preexisting adaptations—such as fetal hemoglobin's high oxygen affinity, vascular shunting to critical organs, and chemoreflex‐mediated cardiovascular responses (e.g., FHR decelerations, and peripheral vasoconstriction)—to maintain oxygenation [3, 22]. These defenses allow the healthy fetus to tolerate labor's hypoxemic challenges without acidemia or injury, provided adequate reperfusion occurs between contractions [3]. However, placental dysfunction or excessive contraction frequency/duration can deplete this reserve, leading to progressive hypotension, myocardial hypoxia, and eventual decompensation, marked by worsening FHR deceleration patterns (e.g., prolonged, U‐shaped decelerations) [23].

Despite robust fetal adaptations, the transition from compensation to compromise is subtle and difficult to detect via FHR monitoring alone. Early stages show effective chemoreflex‐mediated decelerations with blood pressure recovery, while advanced decompensation involves hypotension, loss of parasympathetic control, and myocardial hypoxia‐driven FHR depression [3]. Clinically, the critical challenge lies in identifying this transition, often obscured by the FHR trace's dynamic nature, before irreversible acidemia occurs.

Our analysis revealed stage‐specific FHR patterns predictive of acidemia, demonstrating distinct physiological responses across labor progression. At admission before labor onset, acidemic fetuses exhibited significantly elevated baseline FHR compared to controls, suggesting this pre‐labor tachycardia may serve as an early warning sign of underlying compromise. This initial hyperdynamic response likely reflects compensatory mechanisms attempting to maintain oxygenation in fetuses with predisposing risk factors such as growth restriction, malposition, or maternal comorbidities such as uterine tachysystole and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [3, 7].

As labor progressed, this differential pattern evolved through distinct phases. During the latent phase of the 1st stage, the FHR disparity between groups normalized, representing a transitional equilibrium state. However, in the active phase, acidemic fetuses developed progressively lower baseline rates culminating in decompensatory bradycardia, mirroring the known trajectory from compensatory tachycardia to acidotic bradycardia as hypoxia advances. This biphasic pattern (higher pre‐labor, lower intra‐labor FHR) provides critical temporal context for risk stratification, where pre‐labor tachycardia may identify at‐risk fetuses while intra‐labor bradycardia signals active decompensation.

Concurrently, acidemic fetuses demonstrated persistent markers of autonomic dysfunction throughout labor, including reduced BTBV, attenuated accelerations, and increased variable/late deceleration frequency. These consistent findings of impaired variability and reflex responses suggest that such fetuses enter labor with diminished cardiovascular reserve, rendering them particularly vulnerable to hypoxic stress during uterine contractions. The combination of these dynamic FHR patterns and chronic variability suppression provides a compelling framework for understanding the transition from compensated stress to biochemical acidosis.

Clinical Implications

Our findings demonstrate that continuous CTG monitoring from admission through delivery provides stage‐specific predictive value for neonatal acidemia. While absent accelerations and variable/late decelerations were consistent risk markers throughout labor, dynamic analysis of baseline FHR and BTBV yielded novel insights. Notably, reduced BTBV showed increasing predictive accuracy as labor progressed, peaking during the 2nd stage with an optimal cut‐off of 10.5 bpm. This temporal pattern reflects known physiological stressors, particularly during active pushing when uterine contractions are most intense and fetal hypoxia risk peaks [7]. The stage‐dependent performance of BTBV suggests current static reference ranges (typically 5–25 bpm) [24] may need refinement to account for labor progression.

While the limited clinical impact of continuous CTG monitoring has been partly attributed to well‐documented intra‐ and interobserver variability inherent in its subjective interpretation [7], our findings suggest an additional contributing factor: current guidelines' failure to account for physiological variations across labor stages. We demonstrate that applying uniform diagnostic thresholds throughout all phases of labor may mask critical pathological patterns, as evidenced by the stage‐dependent predictive values of parameters like baseline FHR and BTBV. These results highlight the need for developing phase‐adapted interpretation criteria that better align with the dynamic physiology of labor, potentially improving CTG's ability to identify at‐risk fetuses while maintaining clinical utility.

While intrapartum CTG monitoring has increased cesarean and instrumental delivery rates due to false positives, altered findings alone should not mandate immediate delivery [7]. Clinicians should first address reversible causes of fetal distress, such as excessive uterine activity (managed by reducing oxytocin or using tocolytics); aortocaval compression (corrected by lateral positioning); maternal hypotension (treated with intravenous volume replacement); or cord compression (improved by amnioinfusion or position changes) [7]. These targeted interventions often resolve hypoxia without operative delivery.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study offers novel insights through robust methodology, rigorous case selection, and comprehensive statistical analysis, demonstrating the potential utility of minimal CTG findings in detecting subtle fetal distress during each stage of labor. These findings may hold meaningful implications for clinical practice.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective, single‐center design and moderate sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, although a systematic approach to CTG analysis was employed, some degree of interobserver bias is inherent, given the subjective nature of CTG interpretation. Although we identified stage‐specific CTG patterns and optimal cut‐offs for acidemia prediction, these findings remain preliminary. Therefore, larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate stage‐specific reference ranges before clinical implementation. Notably, since physiological acidemia occurs during labor, and not all cases progress to hypoxic‐ischemic injury [3], future research should directly assess dynamic CTG parameters' ability to predict clinically significant outcomes (e.g., cerebral palsy, epilepsy) rather than acidemia alone.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that dynamic assessment of CTG parameters during specific labor phases may improve the identification of high‐risk pregnancies compared to static evaluation. While promising, these stage‐specific patterns require validation to establish reliable reference ranges before clinical adoption.
