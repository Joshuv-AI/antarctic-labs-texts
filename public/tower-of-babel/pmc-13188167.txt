Home language and diagnostic co-morbidity predict neuropsychological evaluation recommendation uptake among low income families
Authors: Enitan T Marcelle, Rebecca Blaskopf, Huiyu Yang, Meghan Tomb, Gazi F Azad, Amy E Margolis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Rates of recommendation uptake following a neuropsychological evaluation are low, and are even lower for families living in socioeconomic disadvantage. To identify families most at risk, we explore rates and socioeconomic predictors of uptake in a sample of families receiving low/no-fee evaluations. Additionally, we characterize reported barriers to uptake.Methods:Participants included 2,126 children/families who completed both a neuropsychological evaluation in a hospital-based clinic and an annual Individualized Education Program (IEP) meeting following evaluation. Evaluation in this clinic includes free and optional educational advocacy support. Data regarding rates of and barriers to recommendation uptake were gathered using patient questionnaires and medical records. Predictors of recommendation uptake were investigated using multiple logistic regression analyses.Results:Following evaluation, 40% of families had all recommendations implemented and 54% had recommendations deemed essential by their clinician implemented. Families who spoke mostly Spanish at home were significantly less likely to have all recommendations implemented (OR = 2.22, B = 0.797, p = .005). Children who received more diagnoses after evaluation were significantly more likely to have all essential recommendations implemented (OR = 0.88, B= −0.131, p = .023). The two most commonly reported barriers to recommendation uptake were: (1) parents failing to follow up with clinic staff and (2) clinic-school disagreement regarding services needed.Conclusion:This study is one of the first to examine predictors of recommendation uptake among families living in socioeconomic disadvantage. Families facing language barriers were less likely to access services. Additionally, youth with fewer diagnoses and thus potentially less severe challenges were less likely to have recommendations implemented. Future research should test interventions, such as educational advocacy, to mitigate the impact of these risk factors.Keywords: Recommendations, pediatric, outcomes, implementation, neuropsychology

Introduction

Parents and caregivers seek neuropsychological evaluations in order to better understand and support their child’s learning, attention, and/or behavior challenges. Despite the utility of these evaluations, access to evaluations is limited, particularly by factors encompassed within the construct of socioeconomic status. Specifically, families who speak languages other than English, or rely on insurance (vs. private pay), face longer wait times and therefore receive fewer, and perhaps lower quality, evaluations (Broder-Fingert et al., 2018; Child Health Advocacy Institute, Children’s National Hospital, 2021; Kanauss et al., 2005; Miller et al., 2021). Notably, additional barriers to receiving appropriate intervention persist even after accessing a neuropsychological evaluation. Recommendations made following neuropsychological evaluations are implemented only 40% to 62% of the time (Cheung et al., 2014; Elias et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2022; Quillen et al., 2011). Even more alarming is evidence that these rates are consistently lower among learners living in the context of socioeconomic disadvantage (Elias et al., 2021). Critically, this disparity in turn worsens the existing socioeconomic achievement gap (Duncan et al., 1998; McLaughlin & Sheridan, 2016). Despite findings showing that families from socioeconomically disadvantaged contexts have reduced access to evaluations and treatment, and findings showing the utility of early assessment and intervention, studies have not yet investigated factors that contribute to service initiation. Further, there is even less research on socioeconomically stressed communities specifically, leaving open to question the pathways to health disparities. To this end, the broad goal of this study is to understand barriers that impede recommendation uptake after a neuropsychological evaluation, and to work toward identifying families most at risk for experiencing lower rates of recommendation uptake. We define uptake in the present work as the successful initiation of a recommended evidence-based practice. The present study aimed to investigate rates of recommendation uptake in a philanthropically-supported urban neuropsychology clinic that provides economically disadvantaged families with low- or no-fee neuropsychological evaluations. It is important to note that in order to qualify to receive an evaluation, families must be below local poverty guidelines. We also aimed to examine socioeconomic predictors of uptake within this sample. Finally, we aimed to characterize provider-reported barriers to recommendation uptake.

Research focused on outcomes following neuropsychological evaluation is limited (Fisher et al., 2022). However, a handful of studies have examined the social determinants of health (SDOH) specifically related to recommendation uptake. For example, family income has consistently been identified as a key predictor of low recommendation uptake (Elias et al., 2021). Additionally, severity/comorbidity of disorder and language spoken at home were significant predictors of receiving school-based services (DuPaul et al., 2019). Specifically, students with more severe problems or a greater number of comorbid disorders were more likely to receive services, and families for whom English was the primary language were more likely to have a 504 plan (a path to services without diagnosis). Similarly, among children with autism, children of English speakers received significantly more hours of intervention than children of parents who spoke languages other than English (St Amant et al., 2018).

In addition to child and family characteristics, parent factors also significantly predict uptake, and may represent modifiable barriers to uptake. Parents’ attitude toward recommendations, and specifically concern for being stigmatized due to needing services, represent a key barrier to recommendation uptake for childhood brain tumor survivors (Cheung et al., 2014). Parent perceived stress is also a significant barrier to treatment initiation, adherence, and engagement (Kazdin & Wassell, 2000). Importantly, research from the field of mental health more broadly has shown that individuals with prior treatment history show less negative attitudes toward treatment (Saporito et al., 2011), suggesting that parents who have previously engaged in the evaluation process may hold less stigma related to their child’s learning/attention needs, and in turn, may implement recommendations at higher rates. Taken together, this extant literature points to a number of important candidate predictors of recommendation uptake that are examined in the current study: family income, language spoken at home, severity of presenting conditions, prior participation in the evaluation process, and perceived stress.

The present study aimed to elucidate factors that impact lower income families attempting to initiate services following a neuropsychological evaluation. The study took place within a hospital-based pediatric neuropsychology clinic in a diverse and metropolitan area that provides neuropsychological evaluations and educational advocacy services for children living in the context of socioeconomic disadvantage. Children are referred for evaluation primarily due to learning concerns.

The current study sought to assess rates and predictors of recommendation uptake. We additionally explored barriers to initiating uptake experienced by low income families. Given that the majority of families in the current study live at or below the local poverty level, thereby limiting variability in the sample, we hypothesized that income would not predict recommendation uptake given the limited income variability in our sample. However, given prior findings (Elias et al., 2021; St Amant et al., 2018), we did hypothesize that higher comorbidity/more co-occurring conditions, speaking English at home, and having received a prior evaluation, would predict higher rates of recommendation implementation. Given the dearth of information regarding barriers to implementation impacting predominantly low income neurodiverse children, we did not generate specific hypotheses regarding these barriers. Rather, we present data on the barriers reported by families.

Methods

Sample

Our sample consisted of 2,126 children/families who completed a neuropsychological evaluation in our hospital-based neuropsychology clinic, as well as an annual Individualized Education Program (IEP) meeting following evaluation. The clinic in the present study provides comprehensive neuropsychological assessment and education advocacy services to low-income children and adolescents suspected of having attentional or learning difficulties. Children seen at the clinic are referred by parents, school staff, pediatricians, community mental health providers, and/or various other providers. Information on reasons for referral (of note, some children presented with multiple reasons for referral), along with additional demographic information for the study sample, can be found in Table 1. All demographic information was gathered using the clinic’s standard demographic questionnaire completed by the parent/guardian of the child being evaluated. Parents provided the child’s age, grade, sex (male, female), race (American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White, Other), and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, Not Hispanic or Latino). Parents also reported household income. All study procedures were approved by the Columbia University Irving Medical Center Institutional Review Board.

Procedures

Assessment at the clinic consisted of a screening phone call (information about the referral question is gathered during this call), an intake session, two or three testing sessions, and finally, provision of results and recommendation feedback to the parent/caregiver. Neuropsychologists included schools in the evaluation process by collecting teacher-report rating scales, conducting school observations, completing collateral calls with teachers and other school-based providers, and reviewing all educational records (including prior evaluations completed by the school as well as prior IEPs). Results of the evaluation are shared with the school at the first IEP meeting following evaluation; this IEP meeting aims to review a student’s progress, current needs, and program, and to create a plan for the following year.

Workflow in this clinic included connection to an educational advocate to support the family in accessing recommended school-based services. Educational advocates supported families in realizing recommended services by facilitating meetings with parents, teachers, and school administrators, supporting parents in providing documentation to schools, reviewing recommendations with parents and teachers in order to check for understanding, and advocating for appropriate educational placement. Advocates additionally supported families via ongoing engagement and collaboration with the school, and also attended IEP reconvene meetings to update or amend recommended services. Advocates were trained by education lawyers, and attended a scheduled didactic series led by local community based organizations focused on educational advocacy. Training included a focus on special education law and local special education services, as well as a review of the local public school Standard Operating Procedures Manual. Advocates were also provided the opportunity to attend national conferences focused on educational advocacy. Advocate’s activities were documented and ongoing supervision was provided by the clinic director. Provision of advocacy services varied by year/staffing availability. When staffing allowed, all families were offered advocacy. When staffing did not allow for all families to receive advocacy services, services were offered based on provider-determined need for support. Services were introduced at the time of diagnostic feedback, and families were provided the opportunity to opt-in or out of receiving said services. Families were also informed of their right to opt-out of continuing to receive this service at any time. Once connected, advocates followed families for up to three years. All advocates are bilingual (English/Spanish).

Measures

Recommendation uptake

All data regarding recommendation uptake was gathered from an internally developed questionnaire completed by the advocate working with the family. This questionnaire was completed following the student’s first IEP meeting after parents’ receipt of the evaluation report, and was completed by the educational advocate after discussion with the clinician. The advocate then completed a questionnaire to capture if “all recommendations” were implemented. If not, through conversation advocates and clinicians collaboratively determined a subjective score reflecting if recommendations deemed “essential” were implemented (e.g. a service was implemented but at a lower frequency than recommended, or a service addressing a minor comorbidity or less impairing feature of a child’s learning profile was not implemented). The questionnaire yields the following two metrics used in the current study and described below.

All recommendations implemented.

In addition to discussion with the clinician after the IEP meeting, advocates determined if all recommendations were implemented or not using a combination of educational record review and parent interview. Specifically, in response to the question, “All Clinic recommendations accepted?,” the advocates indicated “Yes,” “No,” or “Nickerson Letter.” Receipt of a Nickerson letter was considered to indicate that all recommendations were implemented. In New York, a Nickerson Letter is a voucher/agreement from the Department of Education to pay for a child’s tuition at a nonpublic special education school and operates similarly to special education vouchers in other parts of the US. These schools provide the highest level of individualized support (Greene & Winters, 2008; US Dept of Ed, 2017), including classrooms with low student-to-adult-ratios, teachers specifically trained to work with specific academic/behavioral profiles and diagnoses, and integration of supports throughout the school-day. Given this higher level of support provided, students with IEPs who received all recommended services were also coded as “Yes,” all recommendations implemented. Students with IEPs whose individualized program did not match provided recommendations were coded as “No.” This item yielded a binary (Yes/No) response.

Essential recommendations implemented.

Through discussion after the IEP meeting, the advocate and clinician determined if a child had essential recommendations implemented. If the response to “All Clinic recommendations accepted?” is “No,” then a response was entered for the binary Yes/No question “Essential recommendations accepted?” Whether or not essential recommendations were accepted was determined holistically per child; this was not determined in advance of the meeting or per individual recommendation. Final determination of whether or not essential recommendations were implemented for a child was made collaboratively by the child’s advocate and clinician via case discussion. For example, if a child was recommended by the neuropsychologist to receive speech therapy three times per week but after the IEP meeting was mandated to receive speech therapy two times a week, then this was coded as “Yes” for essential recommendations implemented. However, if a child was recommended by the neuropsychologist to receive speech therapy three times per week but after the IEP meeting was not mandated to receive any speech therapy, then this was coded as “No” for essential recommendations implemented.

Predictors of recommendation uptake

All predictors listed below were collected using internally developed intake forms completed by parents in either English or Spanish, depending on their preferred language.

Household income.

Prior to evaluation parents completed an intake form on which they entered their annual household income, yielding a continuous variable.

Language spoken at home.

Parents were asked to “Describe the language(s) spoken in the home.” Response options included: “English Only,” “Mostly English, Some Spanish,” “Mostly Spanish, Some English,” or “Other.” The “Other” category included very few participants (n = 10; less than 1% of the total sample) and thus they were dropped from all regression analyses. The resulting variable was coded with three levels: “English Only,” “Mostly English, Some Spanish,” and “Mostly Spanish, Some English.”

Prior evaluation.

Parents were asked to indicate whether their child had ever previously received a school evaluation. This yielded a binary (yes/no) variable.

Levels of experienced stress.

Parents were asked “Has the child experienced any stresses or changes?” If a yes response was given, then parents were asked to further indicate the level of stress caused by these changes. This variable yielded four codes: None, Mild, Moderate, Severe.

Number of total diagnoses.

The sum score of the number of total diagnoses given by the neuropsychologist in the report was extracted from the medical record for this study.

Barriers to recommendation uptake

Once a response of “No” to either “All Clinic recommendations accepted” or “Essential recommendations accepted?” is given the question “If No, why not?” is then asked. Advocates are prompted to select one of the following six provided reasons: (1) Parent declined/did not agree with recommendations; (2) Parent did not follow-up with advocacy team; (3) Patient graduated/IEP no longer applicable; (4) Patient no longer needed services; (5) Patient relocated outside of clinic catchment area; and (6) IEP team did not agree with recommendations.

Data analysis plan

We first examined the distribution of demographic factors in the sample to fully characterize the sample. To address the first research question regarding rates of recommendation implementation, we report the percentage of families that implemented “all recommendations” or “essential recommendations.” Multiple logistic regression tested if family factors (household income, language(s) spoken at home, prior participation in a school evaluation, total number of diagnoses provided at evaluation, severity of experienced stressors) predicted implementation of either “all” or “essential” recommendations, in two separate models. Alpha was set to 0.05 to determine statistical significance of reported odds ratios. All analyses used a complete case analysis. Multicollinearity was evaluated by the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF), with acceptable values < 2. In sensitivity analyses, we examined effects of including current school grade or sex in these models. Finally, we present reasons reported for not implementing recommendations. The number of families reporting each reason and the percent of families reporting each reason are presented in order from most common to least. All analyses were conducted using R (4.3.1; R Core Team, 2021).

Predictors of recommendation uptake

Language spoken in the home predicted likelihood of not having all recommendations implemented. Specifically, speaking mostly Spanish and some English at home was associated with a higher likelihood of not having all recommendations implemented. No other predictors (i.e. household income, prior evaluation, level of experienced stress, or number of total diagnoses) were associated with the likelihood of having all recommendations implemented. Barriers to recommendation implementation

Reported barriers to recommendation uptake are shown in Table 8. Among families who reported not having all recommendations implemented (n = 1,126), the most commonly cited barrier was that the school IEP team did not agree with the clinician’s recommendations (50%). Loss to follow-up, i.e. families who become unreachable or uncontactable by the clinic after evaluation, was the second most commonly cited barrier (29%). The third most commonly cited barrier was parent-driven disagreement with or declining of services (12%). Combined, patients graduating, no longer needing services, or relocating made up a total of 9% of the subset.

Discussion

In this sample, rates of recommendation uptake in a sample of families receiving low- or no-fee neuropsychological evaluations were comparable to rates observed in other published studies of more socioeconomically resourced families (Cheung et al., 2014; Elias et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2022; Quillen et al., 2011). Although prior findings show that families with lower versus higher economic means (Elias et al., 2021) are less likely to initiate recommendations, in this sample, families were provided with educational advocacy support which may have improved outcomes. Additionally, families speaking mostly Spanish at home and children who received fewer diagnoses following evaluation were significantly less likely to have all essential recommendations implemented. Finally, among families who did not have all or all essential recommendations implemented, clinic-school disagreement regarding services needed and lack of parent follow-up with their educational advocate were cited as the most common barriers to recommendation uptake. Sensitivity analyses additionally revealed that students in higher grades were significantly less likely to have all essential recommendations implemented. Taken together, these findings provide critical information for neuropsychologists regarding which patients and families may struggle the most to initiate recommendations following evaluation. Our findings also provide a preliminary roadmap for targets to address in interventions focused on improving rates of recommendation uptake.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate patterns of neuropsychological recommendation uptake after evaluation among economically disadvantaged families. Within this financially stressed sample (that had access to educational advocacy services as part of the low- or no-fee evaluation) rates of recommendation uptake were similar to rates reported in more socioeconomically diverse settings. Specifically, at the time of the student’s first IEP meeting, 40% of families had all recommendations implemented which is aligned with prior reports (Cheung et al., 2014; Elias et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2022; Quillen et al., 2011). Of those families that did not have all recommendations implemented, 54% had essential recommendations implemented. We conjecture that this might be related to the fairly unique provision of educational advocacy services in this setting. Possibly, the provision of educational advocacy within our clinic allowed financially stressed families who might otherwise not have initiated care to successfully navigate the complex process to initiate services. Future research should aim to empirically investigate the mediating role of educational advocates. The advocacy services in the clinic operate similarly to patient navigators used in other medical settings, that is, advocates in our clinic support families through the complex process of accessing needed care. Patient navigation, initially developed to support cancer patients, has been shown to be efficacious in improving healthcare service uptake for numerous underserved groups (Natale-Pereira et al., 2011). Our finding that families who received advocacy support experienced rates of service uptake comparable to those reported in other more resourced groups suggests that advocacy or navigator services may help reduce disparities in service access for families of youth with attention or learning difficulties; however, further research is needed to establish their effectiveness. Future work should aim to investigate the key components of providing successful educational advocacy in a neuropsychological context, in order to appropriately serve families of youth with attention or learning difficulties who are high at-risk of failing to implement recommended services.

Importantly, among families who did not implement all recommendations, 54% implemented all clinician-deemed essential recommendations. This discrepancy between implementation of “all” and “essential” recommendations likely stems from various factors, including resource limitations, administrative constraints, and the prioritization of certain recommendations over others. Indeed, the complexity and presentation of psychological reports can impact their uptake (Keenan et al., 2019; Rahill, 2018). Reports that clearly delineate clinician-determined essential recommendations and provide actionable steps may facilitate better understanding and execution by families and educational teams. For example, Gerstle and colleagues found that reports written with lower text difficulty (e.g. “These results are consistent with her MRI and EEG findings involving the left hemisphere (side) of her brain” vs.” Weaknesses in language are commensurate with prior neuroimaging and neurophysiology findings of dominant (left) hemisphere involvement”) were reported by parents to be easier to use to find key information and easier to follow (Gerstle et al., 2025). Further, research has shown that shorter and more focused writing styles (e.g. “Consider providing school supports under a 504 Plan or Service Learning Plan, given John’s history of multiple concussions and longstanding diagnosis of ADHD”) are also reported to be more understandable and usable by school-based providers (Baum et al., 2018). Future research should explore the effect of enhancing the clarity and prioritization of recommendations in neuropsychological reports on increasing rates of recommendation uptake.

In our second aim, we examined predictors of uptake of recommendations in our clinic sample. Families who primarily speak Spanish at home were significantly less likely to have all recommendations implemented. This finding underscores critical health equity concerns. Existing work has shown that language barriers can impede effective communication between patients and healthcare providers, leading to misunderstandings of diagnostic information and treatment plans (Organi et al., 2024; Pandey et al., 2021). This communication gap often results in decreased adherence to medical recommendations and poorer health outcomes. Cultural differences and lack of culturally competent care likely exacerbates these challenges (Salinas et al., 2015) and further contributes to socioeconomically driven health-care inequities. Future work may aim to examine the effectiveness of culturally and linguistically tailored communication strategies and educational materials, such as incorporating culturally appropriate illness narratives (Trinh et al., 2014) in improving the implementation of neuropsychological recommendations among Spanish-speaking families.

Children who received fewer diagnoses following evaluation were less likely to have all essential recommendations implemented. This finding mirrors the documented “squeaky wheel phenomenon,” in which children with more pronounced or multiple needs receive greater attention and support, and children with fewer or less conspicuous challenges are overlooked (Bradshaw et al., 2008; Papandrea & Winefield, 2011). This phenomenon has been documented in neuropsychology. For example, Elias et al. (2021) found that parents of children diagnosed with ADHD implemented a greater proportion of recommendations compared to those whose children did not receive an ADHD diagnosis, suggesting that children with more externalizing diagnoses are more likely to receive needed intervention. Similarly, Bradshaw et al. (2008) reported that children exhibiting externalizing behaviors, which are more overt and disruptive, received mental health and special education services more promptly than their peers with internalizing issues, who often remain unnoticed. This pattern of service access reveals a systemic bias toward addressing more pervasive problems, in turn leaving children with subtler and fewer needs without adequate support. Importantly, this pattern likely reflects not only child-level characteristics but also broader systemic constraints experienced by providers within the school system. For example, mixed-methods work aiming to understand the disparity in school-based referral for intervention for students with internalizing and externalizing symptoms found that teachers, while aware of the expectation to identify more subtle symptoms, felt unsure of how to execute on this expectation (Papandrea & Winefield, 2011). Future research should investigate strategies to develop partnerships between clinics and schools to ensure equitable implementation of recommendations across varying diagnostic profiles, including identifying specific barriers faced by families of children with fewer diagnoses and developing interventions to promote consistent follow-up and support for these overlooked families.

Finally, we examined reported barriers to care. Families living in economic disadvantage face unique and increased barriers to care. Although rates of recommendations implemented in the present sample fall within the range of rates observed elsewhere, rates in this low-income setting do still fall at the lower end of documented ranges, even when provided with advocacy/navigator services. The most commonly cited barrier to recommendation uptake among families who did not have all recommended services implemented was clinic-school disagreement regarding needed services. Specifically, 50% of families who did not have all recommendations implemented reported that their IEP team did not agree with the provided recommendations. Potentially, this may reflect limited resources at the school level. For example, schools may have to prioritize students with more overt or disruptive presentations because of limited resources, high caseloads, and accountability pressures, which can inadvertently lead to a denial of services to some, and may exclude children with less overt problems, e.g. internalizing or learning-related needs (Heinrich et al., 2023, March et al., 2022, Schäfer et al., 2025). Disproportionality, defined as differences in treatment or outcomes by group membership (e.g. race, socioeconomic status; (Sullivan & Proctor, 2016)), is a phenomenon that leads to both underrepresentation and overrepresentation of minoritized groups in special education (Morgan et al., 2017; Shifrer et al., 2011). In addition to disproportionate identification, our work points to disproportionate uptake of recommendations after evaluation.

It is important to add that research exploring the special education advocacy process via focus groups conducted with parents and advocates found that while most schools responded positively to the presence of an advocate, a significant minority of school teams were either confrontational or defensive in response to the advocate (Burke et al., 2019), suggesting that in a significant minority, additional work may be needed to promote collaboration between families, clinicians, advocates, and schools. Collectively, our findings taken together with existing work point to the need to improve school-provider communication and collaboration. We propose that improving this relationship may optimize rates of recommendation uptake in this community. In turn, reducing friction between neuropsychologists and school-based IEP teams may be one pathway toward reducing significant disproportionality in special education.

The second most commonly cited barrier to recommendation uptake, as reported by educational advocates, was having trouble recontacting patients for follow-up. This finding suggests that those most at risk for being “lost to follow-up” are also at higher risk of not receiving needed interventions. Research examining predictors of missed appointments in psychiatric settings has found that malleable environmental factors (e.g. unclear communication between patient and provider, unreliable transportation, increased time between scheduling date and appointment date) predict increased rates of missed appointments (Mitchell & Selmes, 2007). Neuropsychology clinics should aim to collaborate with patients in order to create environments that support connection with follow-up and advocacy services. Further work examining predictors of loss to follow-up in the patient navigation pipeline is needed, and should focus on designing interventions to reduce this attrition.

As in any study, our work also presents with areas of limitation. Firstly, barriers to intervention uptake were reported solely from the perspective of the advocate working with families. While this provides meaningful insight, this methodology also fails to capture barriers from the perspective of those encountering them. Employing a community-engaged framework, future work should investigate barriers to recommendation uptake from the perspective of parents and students navigating the service initiation process after evaluation. Secondly and similarly, barriers to uptake were bucketed into groups pre-determined by clinicians and advocates. These predetermined groupings may have failed to consider categories that could have been identified by schools, parents, and/or students. In addition to consulting school, parents, and students, in the generation of such groupings, future work should utilize mixed methods and qualitative designs, such as focus groups with participants, in order to capture a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of barriers faced. Whether or not a recommendation was essential for a given child was determined by clinicians and advocates. This subjective categorization may over-value recommendations deemed key by healthcare providers and may underrepresent recommendations considered by parents or academic professionals to be most important. Additionally, this subjective decision-making process may limit reproducibility of findings. Future work may quantify the extent to which a recommendation is essential and take into consideration parent and teacher perspectives. Our sample was notable for having a limited number of families who reported speaking Spanish at home. Importantly, these families experienced barriers to recommendation uptake. Future work should recruit Spanish speaking families, in order to provide a more nuanced understanding of our findings. Finally, all data in this study was collected from one clinic, limiting the generalizability of findings. Further, the inclusion of Nickerson letters and the involvement of bilingual advocates may limit the generalizability of the findings, as these supports are not consistently available across all clinics and school districts. Such resources can influence processes in ways that may differ from settings without comparable procedures. As such, the results should be interpreted within the context of these system-level variations, and future research should examine whether similar patterns emerge in settings with differing advocacy structures, linguistic supports, and special education procedures. Future work should investigate whether the present findings are replicable in additional clinic settings.

Conclusion

Findings of this study align with prior research indicating disparities in access to, and uptake of, recommended services among families experiencing economic disadvantage. Structural barriers such as school disagreement with clinic recommendations and limited parental follow-up with advocacy support were significant contributors to incomplete recommendation implementation. Importantly however, families seen in this low-income-focused clinic that also provides educational advocacy following evaluation achieved recommendation uptake rates comparable to those in more socioeconomically higher settings, suggesting that advocacy services likely mitigate the negative impacts of some systemic barriers. Families predominantly speaking Spanish at home were significantly less likely to see all recommendations implemented, highlighting the critical role of language in shaping outcomes. Further, children who received fewer diagnoses following evaluation were less likely to have needed recommendations implemented. This finding suggests that while parents in high distress/parents of children with higher need may receive needed support, parents of children with fewer yet present areas of concern may face more difficulties accessing needed care for their children. Importantly, these findings taken together demonstrate that merely increasing access to neuropsychological evaluations themselves does not present a sufficient solution for issues of access to intervention. Future work is needed to develop tailored interventions that address language-specific needs, as well as strategies to enhance hospital-school-family collaboration. Such research could inform the development of targeted supports to improve recommendation uptake and, ultimately, long-term outcomes for children in economically disadvantaged contexts. Importantly, structural and situational barriers may also inhibit treatment initiation following a neuropsychological evaluation. Inefficient communication with external systems, such as schools or hospital systems, can also hinder the adoption of recommended strategies, particularly in the face of disagreement regarding needed interventions (Elias et al., 2021). Thus in addition to child, parent, and family characteristics, future work should explore the ways these structural and situational variables impact children’s access to services.
