International Delphi study on developing entrustable professional activities (EPAs) for master’s learners in health professions education
Authors: Omayma Hamed, Daniëlle Verstegen, Ghanim Alsheikh, Mohammed A Alqarni, Rim Taleb, Nirmala Rege, Purnima Barua, Tyler Cymet, Muhammad Shahid Shamim, Mohamed Saad Hegazy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompetencies-based education has gained global recognition, emphasizing the need for educators to align educational outcomes with healthcare system requirements. However, limited literature exists on competency frameworks and Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) specific to health professions educationists, hindering the development of tailored Master’s programs.AimThis study aimed to develop a competency framework outlining the roles and functions for Master’s learners in Health Professions Education (MHPE) and identify the key EPAs that form the foundation of a task-based MHPE curriculum, along with the expected levels of entrustment.MethodsAn international Delphi study was conducted involving three rounds of surveys and qualitative discussions with an expert panel of health professions educationists from diverse contexts (n = 29). The Delphi technique, including open-ended and quantitative rating scales, was employed to reach consensus on the EPAs and their levels of entrustment. The final list of EPAs was validated using the EQual rubric.ResultsA total of 16 EPAs were identified and validated, mapped to core and potential roles and functions of health professions educationists. There is less agreement on the level of entrustment that should be attained at the master level, resulting in EPAs that must be fully entrusted before graduation and others which need further development afterwards.ConclusionThe study presents a comprehensive competency framework and a set of EPAs tailored for MHPE programs, providing a structured approach to curriculum design and learner assessment. The findings underscore the importance of incorporating context-specific considerations and aligning educational objectives with the evolving roles and responsibilities of health professions educationists.Keywords: Competencies-based, Framework, Delphi, MHPE, Entrustable Professional activities

Introduction

The move towards aligning educational outputs with the healthcare system, driven by societal needs and educational insights, led to the adoption of Competencies-Based Medical Education (CBME) globally [1]. CBME emphasizes outcomes over inputs and student-centered learning over teacher-centered approaches [2]. Various professional bodies, such as CanMEDS, ACGME, PROFILES, and SaudiMEDs, have set the competencies that shape this shift [3–6].

Implementing change requires a coalition with shared goals [7]. The rise of student-centered and competencies-based education underscores the necessity of faculty development, with many institutions recognizing the importance of postgraduate training for teaching [8]. The starting point for implementing CBME lies in the education system, with educators playing a crucial role in ensuring quality teaching, learning, and assessment in health professions education (HPE).

Despite its longstanding concept, few studies have proposed competencies-based frameworks for postgraduate HPE curricula [9]. Six core and six specialized competencies in teaching frameworks have been introduced [10]. The competencies relevant to clinician-educationists will be followed by additional competencies required by 2025 [11, 12].

Research by Sherbino and colleagues (2014), found that graduate education and faculty development programs lack evidence in producing proficient clinical educationists, suggesting a need for a hybrid approach combining theoretical knowledge with practical applications. They proposed that a master’s degree in education is more effective than faculty development programs, with 55% agreeing or strongly agreeing [11].

Most master’s degree programs in education follow a traditional time-bound approach, which may not lead to competence in the workplace. Traditional competence-based assessments often break down competencies into specific, detailed abilities of learners, which necessitates fragmented and isolated assessment methods. These methods, while detailed, may lack the authenticity and holistic approach needed to accurately reflect real-world professional practice [13]. In CBME, learners should progress at their own pace until they master specific competencies, tracked through Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) [14].

Real-life professional activities should form the primary curriculum structure, necessitating the development of EPAs as overarching program outcomes [15, 16]. An EPA is a unit of professional practice that can be entrusted to a trainee [17]. EPAs were first introduced into a Master’s program in medical education at the University of Michigan, revealing significant gaps in the literature and frameworks [9]. There is a noticeable knowledge gap regarding the EPAs development process, with limited information on stakeholders involved and pathways for alignment. This prompts the need for clarification on the validity and reliability of those EPAs. Furthermore, the absence of a tailored competency framework for health professions educationists highlights a literature gap, as frameworks for clinical educators exist, but the unique roles of health professions educationists are overlooked.

This study will address these gaps by providing detailed insights into the development and validation process of new EPAs, specifically tailored for health professions educationists, within the context of our study. The rationale for this study stems from the University of Michigan’s EPA-based Master’s program, which highlighted the need for clarity in EPA development and validation. The study aims to develop a competency framework outlining the roles and functions for Master’s in Health Professions Education (MHPE) learners. This framework is grounded in the duties and obligations of health professions educationists. Using the Delphi Technique and expert validation using the EQUAL rubric, the study identifies and defines key EPAs that form the foundation of a task-based MHPE curriculum, suggesting the expected levels of entrustment for Master’s-level learners as they progress towards becoming health professions educationists [18].

Methods

The study was conducted from November 2022 to November 2023 using the Delphi technique and expert validation using the EQUAL rubric, which aligns with the constructivist paradigm emphasizing social knowledge construction and subjective interpretations among experts. Based on the principle of “pooled intelligence,” it enhances individual judgment through a series of rounds where experts’ opinions are shared face-to-face or virtually [19]. This study utilized the electronic route (email) for ease and efficiency [20]. The Delphi technique can be qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-method. Here, a mixed-method approach was used, starting with open-ended qualitative questions and moving to quantitative rating scales in subsequent rounds. This method collects rich, detailed information and synthesizes it into a consensus [21]. In each round, a questionnaire was sent to the expert panel for iterative consultation without interaction, followed by another questionnaire based on the previous results. Summarized comments in subsequent questionnaires informed participants of the range of opinions and the underlying reasons [22]. To validate the final list of EPAs the EQual rubric was used [23].

Participants inclusion criteria and sampling

A purposive “Expert Sampling Approach” was used, allowing for generalizations and in-depth analysis by selecting individuals with shared characteristics. This approach achieves a lower margin of error compared to more general sampling methods by collecting information directly from individuals with specific expertise in the subject matter.” [24]. Delphi study sample sizes vary, typically ranging from 15 to 20 respondents, and usually not exceeding 30 to manage response rates effectively [19, 25, 26]. The sample size was chosen to ensure representative judgment pooling without causing low response rates or delays [21]. Accordingly, 29 health professions education experts were initially recruited and agreed to participate in this Delphi study. The experts were selected based on the following selection criteria: health professions education program directors, having academic leadership roles, HPE credentials, practical experience, and stemming from diverse contexts for data triangulation. Participants were invited by email, and informed consent was obtained from each volunteer. The study progressed through three successive Delphi rounds, followed by a qualitative discussion phase. The retention and participation rates across rounds are detailed in the results section.

For the validation of the EPAs that were developed and gained consensus through the Delphi study, six experts were selected purposively and invited by e-mail. These experts were chosen based on the following criteria: they were health professions educationists who had been awarded at least a Master’s degree in Health Professions Education (HPE) and had more than five years of experience as health professions educationists. These experts then used the EQual rubric to validate the developed EPAs.

Stages of the study and data collection and analysis

Stage-1: preparation

Beforehand, curation of the literature about health professions education helped to identify potential key competencies and roles. Two research teams [10, 11] developed models identifying key competencies for effective health professions educators based on review, conferences, and expert input. They emphasize learner engagement, learner-centeredness, adaptability, and self-reflection. Six core competencies were based on the ACGME framework and four specialized competencies for educators with additional roles, cross-referenced with the CanMEDS framework for comprehensive faculty development. During the AAMC 2017 Learn, Serve, Lead meeting, Simpson et al. [12], outlined six essential job roles for future medical educators, detailing the specific functions for each role anticipated by 2025 in the context of the growing influence of artificial intelligence. These roles were included in our study to expand on the core roles of educators and incorporate new skills and a revised educator identity.

Information from these sources was used to develop a base set of core and potential roles of health professions educationists (HPEs) mapped to corresponding functions. Core roles include mastering health professions education knowledge (F.1), developing and curating curricula (F.1 & F.2), designing assessment programs (F.1 & F.3), and being an educator and scholar (F.1 & F.4). Potential roles mapped to functions include self-developer (F.5 & F.6), professional (F.6, F.7 & F.8), change agent (F.9–F.13), and technology adopter (F.1 & F.14).

In line with the framework of ten Cate [27] the aim of this study is to propose Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) with increasing levels of entrustment.

Stage-2: Delphi-study

Round 1

The first-round survey asked the expert panel to provide at least two EPAs for each core and potential function essential for a Master’s level educationist. Invitations and reminders were sent by email. This round yielded rich information, with each panelist suggesting relevant EPAs. A core group of researchers (OH & MH) inspected and discussed the results, merged similar responses and removed responses that were deemed irrelevant because they were not related to health professions education. The included EPAs were categorized for the second-round questionnaire.

Round 2

Panelists from round one rated the EPAs on a 5-point Likert scale and provided qualitative comments. The scale ranged from (5) very high significance to (1) very low significance [28]. Qualitative comments added a practical perspective to the quantitative data [29]. The content validity index (CVI) was calculated by dividing the number of panelists scoring the EPA 4 or 5 by the total number of panelists [30]. Consensus was defined as ≥ 75% agreement and a median score of 4 or 5. Items meeting these criteria were added to the final list. Items with disagreement (median ≤ 3) were eliminated. Items scoring a median of 4 but with < 75% agreement were included in round three for re-rating [31].

Round 3

The third-round questionnaire, based on the second-round findings, was distributed similarly. Agreement and consensus were recalculated, resulting in a final list of EPAs. Consensus on the level of entrustment was defined using the same scale (1–5) and cutoff value (≥ 75% agreement and a median score of 4 or 5) used for achieving consensus on EPAs. Items with disagreement (median ≤ 3) were eliminated, and items with a median score of 4 but < 75% agreement were revised [31].

Panelists also determined the level of entrustment for each EPA and established suggested timelines to guide learners’ progression through a structured two-year Master’s program. This approach aligns with the Royal College’s framework, categorizing EPAs into foundational, core, and transition-to-practice EPAs, with the understanding that some foundational EPAs should be achieved by the end of the first year while core EPAs may require more than one year to master. While these timelines provide a structured guide, it is acknowledged that the actual time required for individual learners to achieve entrustment may vary. However, it is essential to have a maximum endpoint to avoid a lack of accountability and ensure clear evaluation points. Implementing a maximum time limit provides a definitive endpoint for assessing whether learners have met the required competencies and are ready to proceed. This ensures that learners are not indefinitely in training, which could lead to inefficiencies and uncertainty in their educational journey. By having a two-year duration for the program, we maintain a balance between structured progression and the flexibility to accommodate individual learning paces. This structured yet flexible approach ensures that learners progress through the program efficiently while allowing for variations in the time needed to achieve competencies.

The three rounds were followed by a qualitative discussion to review both the consensus and dissensus results. Qualitative data was thematically analysed. Panelists’ quotes were entered into an Excel spreadsheet and color-coded based on themes and patterns identified in the data. Themes were developed by analyzing the coded data for similarities and recurring patterns [32]. Stability of results across Delphi rounds was tested by calculating the Absolute Difference of Coefficient of Variation (CV). An absolute difference of (CV) < 0.2 indicated no significant variation between rounds, thus no need for additional rounds [28].

Stage-3: validation of final list of EPAs

Following the third round, invited health professions education experts validated the final list of EPAs using the EQual rubric [23]. This rubric, developed for validating EPAs, includes 14 questions across three domains assessing each EPA as a work unit, professional task, and curricular role on a 5-point Likert scale, with open-ended comments. The mean score for each EPA was calculated by summing the scores and dividing by 14. EPAs were included if they had a mean score of ≥ 4.07 and consensus of ≥ 75%. EPAs with a mean score < 4.07 were either revised or excluded based on the same predetermined 75% consensus cut-off: EPAs with ≥ 75% consensus were revised, while those with < 75% were excluded [33].

Results

Demographics of expert panels

The international panel began with 29 experts: 62% from Asia, 24% from Africa, and 14% from the USA, Latin America, Australia, and Europe. Panelists had 5 to over 40 years of experience in health professions education. 73% earned a degree, and the rest obtained certificates in health professions education.

Response rates from three rounds

The first round lasted a month; 29 out of 36 invited experts accepted and all of them filled out the survey, achieving a 100% response rate. The second round took two months with a 75% response rate. The final round lasted five months with 28 out of 29 participants, achieving an 85% response rate. Drop-outs were due to busy schedules.

Out of 385 suggested EPAs in the first round, 42 were excluded due to unclarity and irrelevance; 343 were merged into 51 EPAs for the second round. In the second round, 4 EPAs were excluded, 17 reached consensus, and 30 entered the third round for re-rating. Of these, 28 were excluded, and 2 reached consensus, making a final list of 19 EPAs (Fig. 1). After validation using the EQual rubric, the final list included 16 EPAs.

Themes that resulted from thematic analysis of qualitative data

Themes on EPAs that obtained consensus

The thematic analysis of the 16 Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) that achieved consensus among panelists revealed several key themes. These included: (1) “Alignment with educational principles and frameworks,” reflecting the integration of pedagogical theories such as constructivism and experiential learning into the EPAs. Another theme, (2) “Foundational nature of skills,” emphasized the importance of essential competencies like planning and applying teaching strategies, implementing assessment activities, and developing research proposals. Additionally, the theme (3) “Developmental Suitability” highlighted the varying levels of experience required for different EPAs, with some being suitable for entry-level graduates and others necessitating more advanced expertise. (4) “Dissemination of Knowledge” and (5) “Constructive Feedback” themes focused on the significance of research, publication, and reflective dialogue in health professions education, while (6) “Rational Technology Integration” and (7) “Reflection and Lifelong Learning Orientation” underscored the importance of integrating technology and promoting continuous professional growth.

Themes on EPAs that were rejected and excluded

The thematic analysis also identified themes related to EPAs that were ultimately rejected and excluded from the final list. One prominent theme was (1) “Real-World Implementation Challenges,” where certain EPAs were deemed impractical due to concerns about their feasibility within a Master’s program. Another theme, (2) “Graduates’ Preparedness,” reflected doubts about the ability of graduates to handle complex tasks, such as analyzing system elements influencing the educational environment or navigating paradoxes. The theme (3) “Scope and Complexity” highlighted the skepticism surrounding EPAs requiring advanced strategic planning or organizational leadership, which were considered premature for junior educationalists. Finally, the theme (4) “Abstract Nature and Lack of Specificity” addressed the challenges in assessing EPAs that were too abstract or lacked clear evaluation criteria, leading to their exclusion from the final list. This synthesis of themes aligns with the constructivist paradigm adopted in the study.

Stability of panelists’ judgment from Rounds 2 and 3 using the absolute coefficient of variation (CV)

The absolute difference in the Coefficient of Variation (CV) between Rounds 2 and 3 was zero, with a minimum of -0.1 and a maximum of 0.2, indicating 100% reliability. This denotes stability for each EPA, requiring no further Delphi rounds [34].

Discussion

The development of Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) for Master’s Learners in Health Professions Education streamlines essential skills needed for effective engagement. A Delphi study ensured expert consensus in crafting 16 EPAs, delineating roles in health professions education. EPAs align with core functions like educational theory application, curriculum design, assessment program creation, and scholarly engagement. Additionally, they integrate potential functions such as self-development, professionalism, advocacy, and technology utilization, reflecting evolving needs in healthcare education. This differentiation between essential (core) and potential (optional) EPAs is crucial for Master’s learners, as outlined by ten Cate [35]. By mapping some EPAs to potential future roles related to interpersonal communication, intra-personal development and technology use, the resulting framework contributes to preparing learners for healthcare education’s evolving landscape. Expanding beyond traditional educator responsibilities, by including roles of change agent and self-developer illustrates a changing view on the educator identity. However, this study also shows that there is considerable variation between experts when they are asked when an EPA should be attained and to which level of entrustment.

This discussion critically analyzes the synthesized findings, exploring the alignment between EPAs and practitioners’ roles. By examining EPAs’ relation to educationists’ multifaceted aspects, it illustrates the comprehensive preparation Master’s learners need to succeed as educators and leaders in modern healthcare education contexts.

Qualitative insights on the 16 EPAs

Examining the qualitative insights from panelists on the 16 Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) reveals clear thematic patterns. One theme, “Alignment with educational principles and frameworks,” includes discussing educational theories and applying theoretical knowledge. These EPAs reflect pedagogical frameworks like constructivism and experiential learning. Additionally, they emphasize adapting methodologies to support specific groups, aligning with learner-centred education and the dynamic needs of healthcare education. Anchoring EPAs in these concepts highlights their significance in preparing Master’s learners for effective practice in health professions education. Underlining the “Foundational nature of skills,” several EPAs were deemed fundamental for all educators: planning and applying teaching strategies, implementing assessment activities, and developing research proposals. These are seen as basic requisites, not advanced abilities [9]. Theme 3, “Developmental suitability,” shows some EPAs fit for entry-level graduates (e.g., designing an educational program evaluation), while others need more experience (e.g., evaluating a curriculum). Developing a research proposal is essential at entry-level, whereas conducting research and writing a manuscript require assistance [36]. In the theme of “Dissemination of knowledge,” panelists emphasized learners’ contributions through research, publication, and discourse activities, including moderating educational discussions, writing research proposals, conducting research, and publishing manuscripts [37]. In the theme of “Constructive feedback,” the emphasis on “dialogic” highlights the importance of reflective dialogue in providing and receiving feedback, crucial for improving education, exemplified by the EPA “Communicate learning through questioning and dialogic feedback” [38]. In the theme of “Rational technology integration,” the constructive use of technology is emphasized, enhancing rather than replacing learning. This approach in health professions education is supported, exemplified by the EPA “Utilize emerging technology & data analytics in executing core roles.” The theme of “Reflection and lifelong learning orientation” focuses on continuous growth through self-reflection and improvement plans, preparing graduates for ongoing learning. This is exemplified by the EPA “Develop a self-professional development portfolio,” showcasing reflective practice and learning plans [39].

Reasons for exclusion

Some EPAs were excluded, despite high scores from some panelists. This highlights the complexity of achieving consensus within educational frameworks. One reason that EPAs failed to attain collective agreement was concern about feasibility and practicality. Several EPAs were excluded primarily due to concerns about their practical implementation and the expected competencies level of graduates [37]. Key reasons for exclusion included: Real-World implementation challenges whereby EPAs that required aligning individual interests with organizational needs were viewed skeptically because of the difficulties in real-world application. Experts felt that while these goals are aspirational and indicative of a healthy organizational culture, the practicalities of achieving such alignment within the constraints of a Master’s program were questionable. Also, Graduates’ preparedness constituted another concern. EPAs that focused on analyzing system elements influencing the educational environment and dealing with uncertainty through innovative adaptation plans were also excluded. Despite their importance, experts doubted the ability of graduates to effectively address and manage the complex consequences and practical challenges these EPAs entail. There was a consensus that such tasks might require more advanced training and experience than what is typically expected at the master’s level. Scope and complexity of EPAs that involved devising and implementing innovative strategies within educational constraints faced skepticism. Experts questioned whether graduates could realistically be expected to execute such plans successfully, given the varied and often unpredictable nature of educational environments. Finally, Responsibility and expertise level was another concern. Differing opinions on the EPAs that related to setting vision and strategies for organizations contributed to their exclusion. While some panelists supported the idea of graduates contributing to such strategic decisions, they did not believe graduates should bear sole responsibility for these high-stakes tasks. The expectation for graduates to take on such advanced roles was seen as exceeding the typical competencies level for a Master’s program.

These exclusions underscore divergent perspectives on assessing feasibility and practical applicability within health professions education. The EQual rubric further highlights the need for careful consideration of EPAs’ alignment with educational goals, achievability, and reliable evaluation to ensure their effectiveness in preparing graduates [23].

Other reasons for the rejection of certain EPAs were their abstract nature, lack of specificity, and impracticality for junior educationalists. Specific concerns included: Abstract nature and lack of clear assessment framework where some EPAs were considered too abstract, making it challenging to develop clear and measurable assessment frameworks. For example, EPAs related to navigating paradoxes and fundraising lacked concrete criteria for evaluation, leading to concerns about how these skills could be reliably assessed within the MHPE program. Impracticality for junior educationalists whereby EPAs requiring advanced competencies, such as navigating complex organizational dynamics or leading significant institutional changes, were considered impractical for junior educationalists. Panelists felt that these tasks might be more appropriate for seasoned professionals rather than those at the beginning of their educational careers. Also, some EPAs require Complex competencies premature for MHPE program. Competencies that involved high levels of strategic planning and organizational leadership were seen as premature for the MHPE program. Panelists believed that while these skills are important, they may exceed the realistic expectations and capabilities of graduates from a master’s program designed to build foundational educational competencies.

These concerns highlight the need for tailored competencies that align with the program’s objectives, focusing on practicality and measurability. Ensuring that EPAs are specific, operationally clear, and suited to the developmental level of MHPE graduates is crucial for their successful implementation and assessment [40].

Assessment and evaluation of EPAs

While the primary focus of this study was the development and validation of EPAs, it is worth noting that these EPAs are designed to be integrated into a comprehensive programmatic assessment system [41, 42]. The assessment process involves continuous monitoring through formative assessments embedded within course assignments, which learners implement in their home institutions under mentorship supervision. Learners’ progress is tracked through a combination of formative feedback, individualized learning plans set by the learner that make the learner accountable for achieving them, as well as summative assessments [43, 44]. This assessment framework also follows ten Cate’s A-RICH model to evaluate both technical competencies and professional behaviours [45]. A locally developed software tool monitors performance and generates progression curves that are reviewed regularly, with progress reports issued twice during the program. The detailed implementation and outcomes of this assessment system will be addressed in a forthcoming publication.

Timing and level of entrustment

The EPA set resulting from this study can help MHPE curriculum designers to take conscious decisions on curriculum content and structure. Any MHPE program can take decisions on which EPAs are classified as core and which can be elective categories. This classification reflects a strategic approach to entrustment in health professions education [41, 46]. Core EPAs represent essential competencies that must be fully entrusted at the independence level by MHPE graduation, ensuring all graduates possess fundamental capabilities to function effectively as health professions educators [5]. In contrast, elective EPAs often encompass more complex or specialized activities that may require additional experience, resources, or institutional context for full entrustment. Learners could be graduated with or without non-core EPAs [47]. Table 2 presents an example, but other programs may take different decisions, depending on target group and context. Depending on the curriculum design the sequence may or may not be prescribed. Mapping curricula against these EPAs can help to make explicit how and why different programs make different choices.

This approach enables MHPE programs to maintain consistent graduation standards while offering flexibility to adapt to diverse learner needs and professional aspirations.

Contextual differences and the applicability of EPAs

The set of EPAs for MHPE programs serves as core professional activities guiding the design and evaluation of such programs worldwide, aiming at experiential learning and competence development [48, 49]. Contextual and cultural variations are critical in designing effective educational programs. Learner needs can differ based on prior knowledge, learning styles, and career goals. Educational structures vary widely across institutions, influencing curriculum design and delivery methods. Cultural norms and values shape teaching practices and content priorities, making some approaches more effective in certain settings than others. For example, collaborative learning may be emphasized in some cultures, while individual achievement is prioritized in others. Societal needs also play a significant role, as regions with different public health challenges require tailored educational objectives. Local healthcare infrastructure and resources impact the practical training opportunities available, necessitating adaptations to the curriculum [50].

Education is inherently contextual and culturally grounded, necessitating a critical consciousness to avoid cultural hegemony [51]. Thus, each MHPE program should be locally relevant, tailored to its specific context. This aligns with EPA principles, which emphasize the need for activities to align with learners’ future professional roles and be tailored to local environments [50, 52]. Addressing these variations ensures that MHPE programs are locally relevant, equipping graduates with the skills and knowledge needed to meet the specific demands of their professional environments. By aligning educational programs with their unique contexts, we enhance their relevance, effectiveness, and ultimately, their impact on learners and the communities they serve.

Although there clearly is consensus about a common core for MHPE programs, there are also good reasons for different programs to make different choices regarding the required level of performance and even in the selection of EPAs that should be covered. Other EPAs, for example, the ones which were dissented, could be relevant for a specific program but too advanced or out of scope for other programs. Both consensus and dissent-oriented analyses were done in this study and meaningful conclusions were drawn that provided valuable trends that satisfy diverse cultures and contexts [53]. For example, some MHPE programs might aim more at educating future educational researchers; others might choose to focus more on policymakers or educational innovators. It can be cost-effective to educate a core group of educationists who can then lead local initiatives in their region [54], comparable to the (FAIMER) approach with central and regional institutes [64]. Following this line of thought, different MHPE programs might be complementary just because they choose a different focus. An alternative approach could be to establish a core set of EPAs that should be covered by all MHPE programs, while allowing for a second level of more specialized EPAs tailored to specific cultures and contexts [55]. However, it is important to balance this granularity, as too much specificity may limit the overall usefulness and transferability of the EPA framework [56]. In any cases, the results of this study can help to make explicit how MHPE programs differ and why. So, although the inclusion of EPAs in medical education has emerged due to the contemporary trend towards standardizing teaching and research practices [50, 56–58], yet it is important to note that certain EPAs may be considered essential in some contexts and prospective in others, depending on cultural nuances and specific needs [37].

Limitations and quality assurance of the study

The authors tried to avoid the limitation inherent in the Delphi method by using quality assurance guidelines: Conducting and reporting Delphi Studies (CREDES) [60].

The criteria for selection of the expert panel were transparently displayed, and the non-response over the ongoing iterations was reported. The methodology of the study was described in detail in a flow chart (Fig. 2). To ensure stability of results, they were shared after each round with the panelists [61], as underpinned by the consensus stability calculated by the Absolute Coefficient of Variation (CV) between rounds.

Attainment of consensus was clearly explained, and strategies to deal with non-consensus included an open discussion among panelists who backed their perspectives with evidence from the literature. If panelists misperceive the meaning of a topic, the use of Delphi may only add confidence to their misperception [62]. This was overcome by clearly stating the rationale of the study and stating the definitions of the terms used. The Delphi process besides agreements, it reveals ongoing disagreements, showing the varied and complex nature of expert opinions in the field. The EPAs that did not achieve consensus showed stable disagreement over the second and third rounds. This stable disagreement, as evidenced by the calculated Absolute Difference of the Coefficient of Variation, indicated no significant variation between rounds, thus validating our decision to limit the process to three rounds. The acceptable response rate at each round ranged from 75 to 100% and added credibility to the results of the study [53].

Implications to health professions education & future studies

The proposed EPA framework suggests tailoring MHPE programs globally and locally, with a core tier comprising universally applicable EPAs and a secondary tier addressing specific contexts and needs. Core EPAs may vary in emphasis, with some emphasizing research and others teaching. Options for meeting global and local needs include varying sets of observable practice activities (OPAs) or offering elective modules tailored to specific contexts. Observable Practice Activities (OPAs) are smaller, more specific components of EPAs. They are the individual tasks or actions that can be observed and assessed in daily practice to form entrustment decisions. Essentially, OPAs are the building blocks of EPAs. So, while EPAs are broader and encompass multiple competencies, OPAs are the detailed, observable actions within those EPAs [42]. Contextual variations also influence expected performance levels and entrustment requirements, offering avenues for further investigation [37]. This framework offers multiple benefits, guiding program development and educator recruitment. The educationist can also use it as a self-assessment through a continuous professional development portfolio and applying for promotion [8]. EPAs derived from this approach facilitate observation of individual learning processes, aiding in feedback provision and valid entrustment decisions [37]. Future studies will explore aligning selected EPAs and their milestones with performance expectations according to the Dreyfus model. The Dreyfus Model of Skill Acquisition describes how learners progress through five stages: Novice, Advanced Beginner, Competent, Proficient, and Expert. Each stage reflects increased understanding, context recognition, and decision-making capability, moving from rule-based learning to intuitive, experience-based action. As learners progress through the Dreyfus stages, they gain increasing independence and expertise in performing EPAs, moving from strict adherence to rules to fluid, experience-based practice. Milestones are developed to map this progression, providing specific behavioural descriptors for each stage of EPAs. They ensure that learners are meeting key competencies appropriate to their level of training. This structured progression supports continuous development and assessment of learners’ abilities in performing EPAs [58]. Additionally, research will delve into digitized tracking systems for monitoring learner development and piloting results. Furthermore, studies will unpack each EPA into observable practice activities (OPAs) and investigate potential contextual differences among programs [37].

Conclusion

In crafting a Master of Health Professions Education (MHPE) program, the pivotal focus is ensuring that educational objectives align seamlessly with the practical demands faced by educationists. This alignment ensures that graduates are ‘fit for purpose’ – meaning they possess the specific competencies, skills, and knowledge required to effectively perform their roles as health professions educators in real-world educational settings. Adaptability to dynamic environments stands out among essential competencies required for this fitness. Proactively anticipating future challenges and planning accordingly is imperative for program development, ensuring educationists are suitably equipped for their roles. A collective comprehension of the responsibilities inherent to a health professions educationist is instrumental in pinpointing meaningful educational objectives. This collective perspective not only guides but also enriches the creation of impactful learning activities and assessments, reinforcing their fitness for the profession.
