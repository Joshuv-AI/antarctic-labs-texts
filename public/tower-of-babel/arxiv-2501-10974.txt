Sequential Change Detection for Learning in Piecewise Stationary Bandit Environments
Yu-Han Huang and Venugopal V. Veeravalli

Abstract
A finite-horizon variant of the quickest change detection problem is investigated, which is motivated by a change detection problem that arises in piecewise stationary bandits. The goal is to minimize the latency , which is smallest threshold such that the probability that the detection delay exceeds the threshold is below a desired low level, while controlling the false alarm probability to a desired low level. When the pre- and post-change distributions are unknown, two tests are proposed as candidate solutions. These tests are shown to attain order-optimality in terms of the horizon. Furthermore, the growth in their latencies with respect to the false alarm probability and late detection probability satisfies a property that is desirable in regret analysis for piecewise stationary bandits. Numerical results are provided to validate the theoretical performance results.



I
Introduction

The problem of quickest change detection (QCD) has been widely studied for its various applications in science and engineering. In a QCD problem, an agent sequentially observes a sequence of noisy samples, whose distribution changes at an unknown time due to a disturbance in the environment. The goal is to detect the change as soon as possible while maintaining a constraint on the false alarm probability. See
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
for books and survey articles on the topic.

The QCD problem naturally arises in piecewise stationary (PS) bandit environments, where the distributions of rewards on the arms changes at certain time steps and remains stationary between consecutive changes
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. In our prior work
[
12
]
, we formulated a finite-horizon variant of the QCD problem that is tailored to PS bandits, but under the unrealistic assumption that the pre- and post-change distributions are known. In this QCD problem, the goal is to minimize the
latency
, which is smallest threshold such that the probability that the detection delay exceeds the threshold is below a desired low level, while controlling the false alarm probability to a desired low level. A time-varying threshold Cumulative Sum (TVT-CuSum) test is proposed in
[
12
]
as a candidate solution. The TVT-CuSum test computes the CuSum statistic using knowledge of the underlying distributions, and declares a change whenever the CuSum statistic surpasses a threshold that increases logarithmically with time. However, the TVT-CuSum test is not applicable to PS bandits, since the underlying reward distributions are unknown in PS bandits. The QCD problem with unknown distributions was studied in
[
13
]
, where the metrics used are the expected time to false alarm and the expected detection delay, differing from our prior work
[
12
]
. To our knowledge, the closest variant of the QCD problem to our work appears in
[
maillard2019sequential
]
, where the performance metrics are similar to those in
[
12
]
, and the underlying distributions are also unknown. However, the work in
[
maillard2019sequential
]
does not address order-optimality with respect to the horizon—a critical aspect when employing change detectors in piecewise-stationary bandit settings
[
14
]
.

For the purpose of developing change detectors applicable to PS bandits, we study the QCD problem introduced in
[
12
]
, under the assumption that the distributions before and after the change are not known to the detector. As a first step in this study, we investigate the case where the distribution before change is known, but the distribution after the change is unknown, and we develop change detectors for this case. We then generalize these change detectors to the setting where the distributions before and after change are both unknown.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: The formulation of the QCD problem we study is given in Section
II
. In Section
III
, we propose change detectors for the QCD problem where the agent does not know the distribution after the change. We then generalize these change detectors to the QCD problem where the distributions before and after the change are both unknown in Section
IV
.
Numerical results validating the analysis are given in Section
V
, and the concluding remarks are presented in Section
VI
.

II
Problem Formulation

Let
\left\{X_{n}:n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}\right\}
be a finite set of independent random variables indexed by time step
n
. The agent observes these random variables sequentially over a finite horizon
T
. At a change-point
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
unknown to the agent, the distribution of the stochastic observations changes, i.e.,

\displaystyle X_{n}\sim\begin{cases}f_{0},\;n<\nu\\
f_{1},\;n\geq\nu\end{cases}.

(1)

To be specific, before the change-point
\nu
, the observation
X_{n}
follows the pre-change density
f_{0}
with respect to some dominating measure
\lambda
. After the change-point
\nu
,
X_{n}
follows the post change density
f_{1}
with respect to the same dominating measure. We assume that the pre- and post-change densities are
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian. In Section
III
, we assume that the agent knows the pre-change density
f_{0}
completely but only knows that the post-change density
f_{1}
is
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian. In Section
IV
, we assume that the agent only knows that both densities are
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian. When the pre-change distribution is unknown, the agent needs at least a small number of pre-change observations to estimate
f_{0}
. Hence, following
[
13
]
, we assume the existence of a pre-change window of length
m
, during which no changes occur, i.e.,
\nu>m
. This assumption ensures that the agent has at least
m
pre-change observations. In Section
III
, because the pre-change distribution is assumed to be known, the agent does not need pre-change samples to learn the density
f_{0}
; thus,
m
is set to be
0
. In addition, let
\mu_{i}
be the mean of the pre-change density
f_{i}
for
i\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. We define the change gap
\Delta
to be the absolute difference between the pre- and post-change mean, i.e.,
\Delta\coloneqq\left\lvert\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}\right\rvert
. In this work, given our focus on detecting changes in PS bandits, we assume that the pre- and post-change means are different, i.e.,
\Delta>0
, as change detectors in PS bandits only need to detect changes in the mean reward.

Let
\tau
be the stopping time of a (causal) change detector, an algorithm used by the agent to detect changes in the stochastic observations. In accordance with notation that is commonly used in the QCD literature, we let
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
denote the probability measure and the expectation when the change-point occurs at
\nu
. Similarly, we use
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
to denote the probability measure and the expectation when no change occurs. Employing the metric proposed in
[
12
]
, we define the

latency

d
as

\displaystyle d\coloneqq\inf\{

\displaystyle n:\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}:\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left(\tau\geq\nu+n\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}},

(2)

\displaystyle\forall\,\nu\in\left\{m+1,\dots,T-n\right\}\}.

where
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\in\left(0,1\right)
.
Note that
m=0
in Section
III
. Our goal is to minimize the latency under the constraint that the probability of false alarm over the horizon
T
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau\leq T\right)
, is small. As a result, the QCD problem can be defined as follows: for some (small) values
\delta_{\mathrm{F}},\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\in\left(0,1\right)
,

\begin{split}&\underset{\tau}{\textrm{minimize}}\quad d\\
&\;\textrm{s.t.}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\\
&\quad\quad\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left(\tau\geq\nu+d\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}},\;\forall\,\nu\in\left\{m+1,\dots,T-d\right\}.\end{split}

(3)

Since the information about the horizon is not always available to the agent in bandit problems, we assume that the agent is oblivious to the horizon. The theoretical lower bound in Theorem 3 in
[
12
]
, which is derived under that assumption that the agent knows both the pre- and post-change distributions, demonstrates that the optimal solution to
d
in (
3
) is
\Omega\left(\log T\right)
. In addition, in the regret analyses of PS bandit algorithms in
[
7
,
14
]
,
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
are set to
T^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma>1
. Therefore to ensure that overall contribution of the latency to the regret is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
, we require a good change detector to satisfy the following property:

Property 1
.

The latency
d
of a good change detector should be
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)\right)
.

III
Change Detectors with Unknown Post-Change Distribution

In this section we study the QCD problem in (
3
), under the assumption that the pre-change distribution is known (and
m=0
), while the post-change distribution is unknown except for the fact that it is
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian. In order to develop some insights into the design of good detectors under this assumption, we first revisit (
3
) for the case where the pre- and post- change densities are known and
m=0
, which was studied in
[
12
]
.

With
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
known, we can construct
the Cumulative Sum (CuSum) statistic as follows:

C_{n}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\sum_{i=k}^{n}\log\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{i}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{i}\right)},\;n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}

(4)

which satisfies the recursion:

C_{n}=\max\left\{C_{n-1},0\right\}+\log\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{n}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{n}\right)}

(5)

with
C_{0}=0
. The TVT-CuSum test proposed in
[
12
]
declares a change whenever the CuSum statistic surpasses a time-varying threshold. In particular, the TVT-CuSum test has a stopping time

\displaystyle\tau_{r}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;C_{n}\geq\beta_{0}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)\right\},\;r>1

(6)

where

\beta_{0}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)\coloneqq\log\left(\zeta\left(r\right)\frac{n^{r}}{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)

(7)

with
\zeta\left(r\right)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{r}}
.

The CuSum statistic, however, requires full knowledge of the pre- and post-change densities for computation. Consequently, the TVT-CuSum test cannot be applied when the information about the post-change density is unavailable. One way to tackle the unknown post-change distribution is to replace the CuSum statistic with the Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) statistic (with unknown post-change distribution)
[
15
]
, which can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle G_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{1\leq k\leq n}\log\frac{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=k}^{n}f_{\mu}(X_{i})}{\prod_{i=k}^{n}f_{\mu_{0}}(X_{i})},\;n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}

(8)

where
f_{\mu}
is the Gaussian density with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. We emphasize that in (
8
) we replace the pre-change density
f_{0}
with
f_{\mu_{0}}
, which might be a different from
f_{0}
. The reason for making this substitution is that the GLR statistic with
f_{\mu_{0}}
in the denominator can be expressed in a form with only one supremum, making the computation of the statistic easier. See Lemma
1
in Appendix
A
for details. The statistic in (
8
) can be viewed as the generalization of the CuSum statistic
[
3
]
, where the post-change distribution is Gaussian with arbitrary mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Since the threshold of the TVT-CuSum test
\beta_{0}
is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(n/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right)
, it is reasonable to speculate that the threshold for the test using the GLR statistic is also
\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(n/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right)
. Taking cues from
[
7
,
16
]
, we propose the GLR test for unknown post-change distribution as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{GLR}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;G_{n}\geq\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\}

(9)

where the threshold function is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\coloneqq&3\log\left(1+\log\left(n\right)\right)\\
&+\frac{5}{4}\log\left(\frac{3n^{3/2}}{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)+\frac{11}{2}\end{aligned}

(10)

for
n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}

A popular alternative of the CuSum statistic is the Shiryaev-Roberts (SR) statistic
[
17
,
3
]
, which can be written as

S_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{i=k}^{n}\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{i}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{i}\right)},\;n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}

(11)

which also satisfies the following recursion:

S_{n}=\left(S_{n-1}+1\right)\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{n}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{n}\right)}

(12)

with
S_{0}=0
. In the same manner as we generalized the CuSum statistic, we can construct the Generalized Shiryaev-Roberts (GSR) statistic (with unknown post-change distribution) as follows:

\displaystyle W_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=k}^{n}f_{\mu}(X_{i})}{\prod_{i=k}^{n}f_{\mu_{0}}(X_{i})},\;n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}.

(13)

Then, the corresponding GSR test for unknown post-change distribution can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{GSR}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;\log W_{n}\geq\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\}

(14)

where the threshold function is defined as:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\coloneqq&\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log n.\end{aligned}

(15)

for
n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
.

In the following theorem, we show that both the GLR and GSR tests can effectively control the false alarm probability with a small latency. The proof of the theorem is given in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 1

(GLR and GSR tests with unknown post-change distribution)
.

Consider the GLR test in (
9
) and the GSR test in (
14
). For any
T\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu\in\left\{1,\dots,T-d\right\}
, with
d
defined as

\displaystyle d=\left\lceil\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}+\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{D}}}\right)}\right)^{2}\right\rceil

(16)

the stopping times of the tests satisfy
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left(\tau\geq\nu+d\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
,
where
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}
for the GLR test and
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}
for the GSR test.

Since
\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
and
\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
are
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
, the latency in (
16
) is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
, matching the theoretical lower bound in Theorem 3 in
[
12
]
. In addition, the latency in (
16
) is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)\right)
. Hence, the GLR and GSR tests satisfy Property
1
, indicating that these tests are good candidates for generalization to the QCD problem of interest in PS bandits, where both the pre- and post-change distributions are unknown.

IV
Change Detectors with Unknown Pre- and Post-Change Distribution

In this section we study the QCD problem in (
3
), under the setting of interest for PS bandits, in which both the pre- and post-change distributions are unknown, except for the fact that they are
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian.

Recall that
f_{\mu}
is the density of a Gaussian random variable with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Similar to the GLR statistic in (
8
), we can generalize the CuSum statistic to the GLR statistic (with unknown pre- and post-change distributions) as follows. For
n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{n}\coloneqq

(17)

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq k\leq n}\log\frac{\sup_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}f_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)}{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\mu}\left(X_{i}\right)}.

Then, taking the same cues from
[
7
,
16
]
, we propose the GLR test for unknown pre- and post-change distributions as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{\mathrm{GLR}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;\tilde{G}_{n}\geq\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\}

(18)

where the threshold function is defined as:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\coloneqq&6\log\left(1+\log\left(n\right)\right)\\
&+\frac{5}{2}\log\left(\frac{4n^{3/2}}{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)+11.\end{aligned}

(19)

for
n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
.

Similarly, we can generalize the SR statistic to the GSR statistic (with unknown pre- and post-change distributions) as follows. For
n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{n}\coloneqq

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\sup_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}f_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)}{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\mu}\left(X_{i}\right)}.

We can then construct GSR test for unknown pre- and post-change distributions as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{\mathrm{GSR}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;\log\tilde{W}_{n}\geq\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\}

(21)

where the threshold function is defines as:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\coloneqq&\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log n.\end{aligned}

(22)

for
n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
.

In the following theorem, we illustrate that the GLR and GSR test with unknown pre- and post-change distributions can also detect changes with a low latency, while ensuring that the false alarm probability is small, given that there are enough pre-change observations. The proof of the theorem is given in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 2

(GLR and GSR tests with unknown pre- and post-change distributions)
.

Consider the GLR test in (
18
) and the GSR test in (
21
), with

m\geq\frac{8\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right).

(23)

Then, for
\nu\in\left\{m+1,\dots,T-d\right\}
, with
d
defined as:

d\coloneqq\left\lceil\max\left\{\frac{8\sigma^{2}m\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}{\Delta^{2}m-8\sigma^{2}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)},\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}^{2/3}}{2^{16/15}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}^{4/15}}-m\right\}\right\rceil

(24)

the false alarm probability and the latency satisfy
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left(\tau\geq\nu+d\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
, respectively,
where
\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
in (
19
)
for the GLR test and
\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
in (
22
) for the GSR test.

Recall that
\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
and
\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
are
\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(T/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right)
. In the following corollary, we show that the GLR and the GSR tests satisfy Property
1
with an appropriate choice of
m
if
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
.

Corollary 1
.

If
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
, then the GLR and the GSR tests satisfy Property
1
with
m=\left\lceil\frac{16\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)\right\rceil
, where
\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
in (
19
)
for the GLR test and
\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)=\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
in (
22
) for the GSR test.

Proof.

With the choice of
m
, when
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
, we have

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\max\left\{\frac{8\sigma^{2}m\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}{\Delta^{2}m-8\sigma^{2}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)},\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}^{2/3}}{2^{16/15}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}^{4/15}}-m\right\}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\max\left\{\frac{8\sigma^{2}m\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}{\Delta^{2}\left\lceil\frac{16\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\rceil-8\sigma^{2}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)},1-m\right\}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\max\left\{m,1-m\right\}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\left\lceil\frac{16\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)\right\rceil

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\mathcal{O}\left(\log T+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)\right)

(25)

where step
(a)
follows from the choice of
m
, and (b) is due to the fact that
\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right)
.
∎

As a result, the GLR test in (
18
) and the GSR test in (
21
) both satisfy Property
1
, meaning that they are good change detectors for PS bandits.

V
Experimental Results

In this section, we study the performance of our proposed change detectors through simulations. We compare the latencies of the proposed GLR change detectors with that of the TVT-CuSum test
[
12
]
to illustrate the cost of detecting changes without knowing the (pre- and) post-change distribution(s). To investigate the tightness of the upper bounds in our theorems, we also compare the empirical results for our change detectors with the upper bounds in Theorem
1
and
2
under various choices of
T
,
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
, and
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
.

In our experiments, the pre- and post-change distributions follow
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
and
\mathcal{N}\left(1,1\right)
, respectively. When the pre- and post-change distributions are both unknown, we set the pre-change window
m=T-1000
, since this choice of
m
is greater than
\left\lceil\frac{16\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log\left(1/\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)\right\rceil
in Corollary
1
, ensuring that the latency of the GLR test in (
18
) satisfies Property
1
. According to the definition of the latency in (
2
), for any arbitrary change-point
\nu\in\left\{m+1,\dots,T-d\right\}
, there are approximately
100\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\%
of the simulated trials in which the detection delay exceeds the latency. Therefore, to obtain the empirical value of the latency, we first conducted
200000
trials and recorded the detection delay in each trial for each change-point in a set
\mathcal{N}
, and then took the maximum of the
100\left(1-\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)^{\mathrm{th}}
percentile of the recorded detection delays over all change-points in
\mathcal{N}
. The collection of change-points
\mathcal{N}
is set to
\left\{m+1+nT/10:\;n\in\mathbb{N},\;m+1+nT/10\leq T\right\}
, as conducting
200000
trials over all possible change-points is cumbersome.

To implement the GLR tests in (
9
) and (
18
), we need to compute the GLR statistics in (
8
) and (
17
). However, unlike the CuSum statistic in (
4
), the GLR statistics in (
8
) and (
17
) do not have the recursive property to simplify the computation. As a result, we perform down-sampling by taking supremum over
\mathcal{K}_{n}\coloneqq\left\{n-700,\dots,n\right\}
when computing the GLR statistics in (
8
) and (
17
), i.e., for
n\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
,

\displaystyle G^{\prime}_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}\log\frac{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=k}^{n}f_{\mu}(X_{i})}{\prod_{i=k}^{n}f_{\mu_{0}}(X_{i})},

(26)

\displaystyle\tilde{G}^{\prime}_{n}\coloneqq

VI
Conclusions

We investigated a variant of the QCD problem that is designed for the PS bandits. Since the reward distributions are unknown in bandit problems, we considered scenarios where the information about the post-change distribution is unavailable first, and then generalized the results to the cases where both pre- and post-change distributions are unknown. We proposed the GLR and GSR tests for each setting as candidate solutions. We show that the growth of the latency of the GLR and GSR tests with
T
is order-optimal in both cases. In addition, the latency of the GLR and GSR tests grows logarithmically with
1/\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
and
1/\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
, which is a desirable property in the regret analysis for PS bandits.
Our simulation results validate these theoretical results. However, the simulation results suggest that the upper bound on the latency in Theorem
2
is loose, and it may be worthwhile exploring if this bound can be tightened.
