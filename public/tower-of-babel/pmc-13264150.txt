Prevalence and Risk Factors of Surgical Site Infections in Hospitals Across Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Atheer M. Aljohani, Danah O. Sandaqji, Rana A. Alahmadi, Raha Z. Ishqi, Manal A. Alanazi, Shouq N. Aloufi, Husain I. Sandugji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To determine the prevalence of surgical site infection (SSI) and identify associated risk factors in hospitals in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 363 adult surgical patients from major hospitals in Madinah between 2023 and 2024. We analyzed medical records using descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, and multivariable logistic regression.Results:The overall SSI rate was 5.2% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.3–7.9%). Infection types comprised superficial incisional (57.9%), deep incisional (36.8%), and organ/space infections (5.3%). Significant risk factors included younger age (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.94 per year, p = 0.014), increased body mass index (BMI) (aOR = 1.10 per unit, p = 0.043), orthopedic surgery compared with general surgery (aOR = 47.46, p = 0.033), and dirty wounds compared with clean wounds (aOR = 28.07, p = 0.005). Despite high rates of prophylactic antibiotic use (82.1% preoperative, 17.4% intra-operative, and 90.9% postoperative), no significant association with SSI risk was observed.Conclusion:The SSI rate in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah hospitals is higher than previously reported. Variations across hospitals and surgical specialties highlight the need for targeted infection prevention strategies, particularly for high-risk procedures and in facilities with elevated infection rates.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) represent some of the most critical complications encountered in modern medical practice. They are defined as localized or systemic conditions resulting from an adverse response to infectious agents or their toxins, developing during hospitalization without evidence of infection at the time of admission, and meeting the criteria for specific body sites [1]. Many factors contribute to the occurrence of HAIs, for example advancing participants age, increased case complexity, the use of invasive medical devices, and widespread antimicrobial exposure [2].

Among the various types of HAIs, those linked to invasive medical equipment, infections linked to central venous catheters, urinary tract infections associated with indwelling catheters, and postoperative surgical site infections (SSIs), are considered the most severe [3]. This study focuses on SSIs, these are infections that occur at the site of surgery, involving either the incision itself or the deeper layers, and appear within 30 days following the operation, or within one year in cases involving implant placement [4].

Management of SSIs often necessitates additional surgical intervention [5] and contributes significantly to the economic burden on health systems due to prolonged hospitalisation and the use of resources. A systematic review from 2011 estimated the cost per SSI to range between United States (US) Dollars 5,600 and 12,900 [6]. Similarly, a 2017 systematic review demonstrated that patients with SSIs experienced longer hospitalizations, higher reoperation rates, and increased readmissions, leading to substantially higher treatment costs than infection-free patients [7].

Despite notable advances in surgical techniques and infection prevention strategies, SSI rates remain concerningly high. For instance, a 2013 multicountry study involving 30 nations, including Saudi Arabia, documented 2,580 SSIs across Asian countries and 7,523 in total across all participating regions [8]. Additionally, a recent meta-analysis of post-appendectomy cases estimated an SSI incidence of 8.2% in the Eastern Mediterranean region and 7% globally [9]. A tertiary care study conducted in Al Khobar, Saudi Arabia, reported a 2.6% SSI rate following orthopedic surgical procedures [10].

The prevalence and incidence of SSIs are influenced by several determinants, including healthcare system quality, environmental conditions, and factors related to the patient [11]. Healthcare-related factors encompass using invasive equipment, techniques used in the surgery, and perioperative infection control measures [11]. Environmental determinants include ventilation systems, hospital infrastructure, and the cleanliness of clinical environments [12]. Patient-related factors involve comorbidities, immunosuppressive therapy, nutritional status, and prolonged hospitalization [11].

In Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia, only one known study, published in 2020, has investigated the prevalence of SSIs. Based on data collected in 2017 from orthopedics, general surgery, ophthalmology, urology and ENT departments, the study reported an overall SSI rate of 3.7%, with diabetes mellitus, obesity, and overweight status identified as significant risk factors [13].

The current study seeks to evaluate the extent of improvement in healthcare systems by comparing the present SSI rates with those from the 2020 study, thereby reflecting potential progress in infection prevention practices within Al Madinah Al Munawwarah hospitals over the past 6 years. Furthermore, this research expands upon prior work by including a larger sample size, more hospitals, and a broader range of surgical specialties to provide a comprehensive, updated assessment of SSI prevalence and associated risk factors.

The objective of this study is to determine the current prevalence of surgical site infections (SSIs) and identify their associated risk factors among patients undergoing surgical procedures in three hospitals across Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia. The abjectives are as follows: i) To estimate the overall prevalence of SSIs across major surgical specialties in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah hospitals; and, ii) To identify key patient, procedure-, and system-related risk factors contributing to SSIs.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study utilized medical record data from three major hospitals in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia: King Fahd General Hospital, Ohoud Hospital, and King Salman Medical City, including Madinah General Hospital and the Maternity and Child Hospital. The study sample comprised participants who underwent surgical procedures in the departments of General Surgery, Orthopedic Surgery, Urology, Obstetrics and Gynecology (OBGYN), Ophthalmology, and Otorhinolaryngology (ear, nose, throat [ENT]) between January 2023 and December 2024. We employed stratified random sampling to select participants from the eligible population. Epi Info statistical software was used to calculate the sample size based on the following criteria: an unknown population size, a 2% margin of error, an expected frequency of 8.2%, and a 95% confidence interval (CI). The calculated sample size was 363 patients. The inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥ 18 years, residents of Al Madinah Al Munawwarah province, undergoing surgery during the study period, and with at least 30 days of post-operative follow up. We excluded patients younger than 18 years and those who did not meet the inclusion criteria. We retrospectively collected data from patient medical records. Variables included demographics, diabetic status, wound type, perioperative antibiotics, postoperative antibiotics, and the occurrence of SSIs. Wound classification was performed in accordance with the American College of Surgeons criteria, which categorizes surgical wounds by the degree of contamination. Clean wounds are characterized by the absence of inflammation and do not involve entry into the respiratory, gastrointestinal, or genitourinary systems [14]. Clean-contaminated wounds, however, include otherwise clean wounds with an increased risk of infection due to controlled entry into the gastrointestinal, respiratory, or genitourinary tracts. Contaminated: wounds exposed to external objects or contaminated due to gastrointestinal spillage. Dirty-infected: wounds containing foreign objects, traumatic wounds from unclean sources delayed treatment, infected surgical wounds, or wounds exposed to pus or fecal matter. Additionally, we reviewed culture and antibiotic sensitivity results from infected surgical sites to identify causative agents and evaluate antibiotic resistance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous data as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). For bivariate analysis, the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was applied to categorical variables, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was utilized for continuous variables. Variables exhibiting statistical significance at p ≤ 0.10 in the bivariate analysis were advanced to a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent determinants of SSI. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) along with their 95% confidence intervals were presented. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-tailed p-value <0.05.

Approval for this study was obtained from the appropriate institutional review boards, including formal approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Taibah University (Approval ID: STU-24-03) and the Research Ethics Committee of the General Directorate of Health Affairs, Madinah Region (Approval No. 24-109, dated October 30, 2024). Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis. The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting standards.

Results

The study population included 363 patients, predominantly males (n = 242, 66.7%) compared to females (n = 121, 33.3%). The median age was 39 years (IQR: 29–54). Comorbidities included diabetes, 73 (20.1%) patients, smoking in 22 (6.1%), immunocompromised status in 61 (16.8%) patients, and other comorbidities in 55 (15.2%) patients. The median glycated hemoglobin (HbA1C) level was 5.85% (IQR: 5.10–7.60), and the median body mass index (BMI) was 25.70 kg/m2 (IQR: 23.44–29.32), indicating that the study population was generally overweight (Table 1).

The prevalence of SSIs was found to be 5.2% (95% CI: 3.3–7.9%), with 19 cases identified among 363 patients. Cases were distributed across 3 main hospitals in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah. Surgical cases were relatively evenly distributed among specialties, with Orthopedic surgery having the highest representation (n = 52, 14.3%), followed by neurosurgery (n = 48, 13.2%). Other specialties, including General Surgery, Urology, OBGYN, Ophthalmology, ENT, and Thoracic Surgery, each contributed approximately 11.8–12.4% of cases.

Most procedures were classified as clean (n = 240, 66.9%) or clean-contaminated (n = 100, 27.9%), with fewer contaminated (n = 2, 0.6%) and dirty (n = 17, 4.7%) wounds. The SSIs occurred in 19 cases (5.2%, 95% CI: 3.3–7.9%), with superficial incisional SSIs being the most common (n = 11, 57.9%), followed by deep incisional (n = 7, 36.8%), and organ/space infections (n = 1, 5.3%) (Fig. 1).

Analysis of SSI occurrence across patient characteristics and surgical factors revealed several notable findings. The median age of participants with SSI (38 years, IQR: 25–49) was slightly lower than that of patients without SSI (40 years, IQR: 29–54); however, the observed difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.314). Gender differences in SSI rates were not statistically significant (p = 0.505), with males showing a marginally higher infection rate (5.8%) compared to females (4.1%). Similarly, diabetes status did not significantly influence SSI (p = 0.555) rates, though patients with diabetes exhibited a slightly higher rate of infection (6.8% vs. 4.8%). Among surgical departments, although not statistically significant (p = 0.177), Orthopedic surgery had the highest SSI rate (13.5%), while Ophthalmology reported no infections (0%).

Dirty wounds demonstrated the highest SSI rate (17.6%), followed by clean-contaminated (5%), and clean wounds (4.6%). No infections were observed in contaminated wounds, although this category included a small number of cases. Patients with immunocompromised status (p = 0.338) or comorbidities (p = 0.184) exhibited higher SSI rates (8.2% and 9.1% respectively) compared to patients without these conditions. However, these differences were not statistically significant.

The use of prophylactic antibiotics across perioperative stages showed variable patterns, with none reaching statistical significance: preoperative (p = 0.546), intraoperative (p = 0.115), and postoperative (p = 0.687). Notably, patients who received intraoperative prophylaxis experienced a higher SSI rate (9.5%) compared to those who did not (4.3%).

Although not statistically significant (p = 0.088), hemoglobin A1C levels tended to be higher in patients who developed SSI (median: 6.59%, IQR: 5.72–9.11) compared to those without SSI (median: 5.8%, IQR: 5.00–7.60). Similarly, patients with SSI had a higher median BMI (27.68 kg/m2, IQR: 23.80–34.60) compared to those without SSI (25.67 kg/m2, IQR: 23.44–29.30), although the observed difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.349) (Table 3).

The multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictors of SSI. Age emerged as a significant protective factor, with each additional year associated with a 6% decrease in the odds of developing SSI (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.99, p = 0.014). Obesity was identified as a significant risk factor, with each unit higher in BMI corresponding to a 10% increase in the odds of SSI (aOR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.00–1.20, p = 0.043).

Surgical specialty was also a significant predictor with orthopaedic surgery demonstrating significantly higher odds of SSI compared with general surgery (aOR = 47.46, 95% CI: 1.38–1632.64, p = 0.033). Surgical wound classification was a strong predictor of SSI, with dirty wounds exhibiting substantially higher odds of SSI compared with clean wounds (aOR = 28.07, 95% CI: 2.74–288.17, p = 0.005). Clean-contaminated wounds demonstrated a tendency toward increased risk, although this did not achieve statistical significance (aOR = 4.16, 95% CI: 0.86–19.98, p = 0.075).

Several variables approached statistical significance. Comorbidities showed a trend toward increased SSI risk (aOR = 3.88, 95% CI: 0.97–15.60, p = 0.056). Similarly, surgical specialty subspecialties exhibited higher odds of SSI compared with general surgery (aOR = 8.12, 95% CI: 0.88–74.68, p = 0.064).

Other factors, including gender, diabetes status, immunocompromised status, and antibiotic prophylaxis (preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative), did not achieve statistical significance (Table 4).

Discussion

The incidence of surgical site infection (SSI) observed in our study (5.2%) represents an increase from the 3.67% previously reported in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah hospitals in 2017 [13]. This higher rate exceeds the US National Nosocomial Infections Surveillance (NNIS) system estimate of 1.96–2.21%, indicating a substantially greater infection burden in our healthcare setting [1,3]. By specialty, urology demonstrated an SSI rate of 6.8%, reflecting improvement relative to the 10% reported previously. In contrast, orthopedic surgery exhibited the highest rate (13.5%), an increase from the previously reported 3.3% [13]. suggesting a concerning trend that warrants further evaluation of perioperative practices, sterilization protocols, and patient-related risk factors.

Several alternative explanations may account for the observed increase in SSI rates beyond a true rise in infections. Factors such as improved surveillance and detection methods, differences in patient case mix and surgical complexity, changes in documentation practices, and variations in the distribution of surgical specialties. These factors may have influenced the differences observed in our study relative to those reported in the 2017 study.

Our analysis of risk factors revealed distinct patterns compared with previous regional research. Unlike prior studies, we found no significant association between diabetes mellitus or BMI and SSI occurrence [13,15,16]. This divergence may reflect improvements in perioperative glucose management and patient optimization. However, significant associations were noted for hospital site and surgical specialty, particularly the elevated risk in orthopedic procedures, consistent with international findings [10]. Possible contributing factors include younger patient age, higher prevalence of trauma cases, and under-reported smoking habits [11]. Since smoking status is often inadequately documented in medical records, its true influence on SSI risk may be underestimated.

The strong correlation between wound classification and SSI risk provides vital clinical insight. Dirty wounds demonstrated a 17.6% infection rate compared with 4.6% for clean wounds, highlighting the importance of accurate wound classification in both risk assessment and clinical decision-making [11]. These findings reinforce the need to optimize infection prevention for contaminated and dirty wounds, potentially through tailored antibiotic regimens or advanced wound management strategies.

In contrast to a prior study, our study did not demonstrate a significant association between prophylactic antibiotic use and reduced SSI risk, despite high compliance rates (82.1% preoperative, 17.4% intraoperative, and 90.9% postoperative) [17]. This observation supports recent evidence suggesting that antimicrobial prophylaxis alone may be insufficient for SSI prevention [9,11]. A broader, multimodal approach is therefore recommended, integrating meticulous surgical technique, perioperative temperature regulation, glucose control, and evidence-based antisepsis protocols.

Variations in SSI rates among hospitals suggest that institutional factors play a crucial role in infection prevention. Differences in surgical volume, staff training, facility infrastructure, and infection control protocols may contribute to these variations [3,8]. Hospitals with lower SSI rates may serve as benchmarks for best practices that could be implemented across all facilities in the region.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of our cohort offer valuable context. With a predominance of male patients (66.7%) and a median age of 39 years, the relatively young surgical population differs from patterns seen in some international cohorts. Comorbidities demonstrated a near-significant association (p = 0.051), suggesting that a larger sample could clarify their impact [4,18]. The study's multicenter design and inclusion of multiple surgical specialties enhance external validity, providing a comprehensive overview of SSI epidemiology in the region [6]. Standardized data collection and consistent infection definitions further strengthen the reliability of these findings.

Nevertheless, it is important to recognize several limitations. The retrospective nature of the study limited control over data completeness and precluded the collection of certain relevant variables, potentially introducing selection bias. Post-discharge surveillance was confined to participating hospitals, likely underestimating true SSI incidence. Variable documentation quality and small numbers in some subgroups contributed to wide confidence intervals in logistic regression results, reflecting some degree of estimate instability.

Future investigations should employ prospective designs with extended follow-up periods to capture delayed SSIs and better establish causal relationships between risk factors and outcomes. Emerging domains, such as host microbiome dynamics and genetic susceptibility, merit exploration for their potential to inform individualized prevention strategies. Additionally, economic analyses evaluating the cost-effectiveness of different infection control interventions could guide healthcare resource allocation.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings emphasize the urgent need to review and strengthen infection prevention programs in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah hospitals. In particular, orthopedic surgery warrants targeted interventions, including enhanced preoperative screening, reinforced aseptic protocols, and intensive postoperative monitoring.

Considering the public health perspective, the results stands for coordinated regional action to standardize SSI surveillance systems, implement continuous audit and feedback mechanisms, and ensure regular training of surgical and infection control teams [1,3,19,20]. Policymakers should integrate these data into broader quality improvement and patient safety initiatives aimed at reducing hospital-acquired infections.

In conclusion, this multi-center retrospective study provides an updated assessment of SSI prevalence and associated risk factors in surgical populations across Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia. Among 363 surgical patients, the overall SSI incidence was 5.2%, despite high compliance with prophylactic antibiotic protocols (82.1% preoperative, 17.4% intraoperative, and 90.9% postoperative). The SSIs remain a persistent issue, with significant implications for patient outcomes and healthcare expenditure.

Key risk determinants, particularly hospital site, surgical specialty, and wound classification, underscore the need for continuous monitoring and refinement of infection prevention strategies. Regular SSI surveillance, compliance to standardized perioperative guidelines, and specialty-specific quality improvement initiatives are imperative to reduce infection rates and enhance patient safety.
