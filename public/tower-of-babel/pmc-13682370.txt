Thromboembolic and hemorrhagic risks after mastectomy with vs without direct-to-implant reconstruction
Authors: Jonathan Mokhtar, John Y Ha, Jewel Haddad, Curtis L Cetrulo Jr, David M Smadja, Alexandre G Lellouch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Despite greater operative complexity, immediate implant reconstruction after mastectomy yielded 54% lower DVT and 33% lower hematoma rates.•Procedural complexity alone does not determine clotting and bleeding risk after breast cancer surgery; patient selection matters.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, with an estimated 38% increase in new cases and 68% increase in mortality expected by 2050.1 Surgical resection, whether via lumpectomy or mastectomy, remains a cornerstone of breast cancer management that is indicated for tumor burden, multifocal disease, or prophylactic risk reduction in BRCA mutation carriers.2 In parallel, the extensive options of breast reconstruction, such as autologous tissue transfer or implant-based reconstruction, have become an integral component of comprehensive breast cancer care, offering patients significant psychosocial and aesthetic benefits.3 Among the reconstructive options, direct-to-implant (DTI) reconstruction has emerged as a widely adopted approach, streamlining the reconstructive pathway by eliminating the need for tissue expansion.3

Despite the increasing popularity of immediate DTI reconstruction, concerns persist regarding postoperative complications, specifically hematoma formation and venous thromboembolism (VTE). Patients with breast cancer are known to be in a hypercoagulable state, experiencing a fourfold to sevenfold increased risk of VTE compared with the general population.4 These risks may be amplified by surgical and reconstructive factors such as longer operative duration, reduced mobility, and the use of foreign body materials.5 These procedural elements, although essential to reconstructive success, may inadvertently increase the risk of hematologic complications in the immediate postoperative period.

Emerging data offer a significant perspective on the postoperative risks associated with breast reconstruction after mastectomy.6, 7, 8, 9 Large-scale database studies consistently demonstrated an elevated risk of VTE after immediate reconstruction, particularly with autologous flaps and prosthetic implants, compared with mastectomy alone.5,10 Compounding this risk is the potential for postoperative hematoma, which can necessitate return to the operating room, delay initiation of adjuvant therapies, and compromise reconstructive outcomes.11 Despite its growing use, the procedure-specific risk profile of DTI reconstruction remains undercharacterized in large-scale comparative studies. The existing literature often groups all reconstructive modalities together or does not adequately adjust for oncologic, pharmacologic, and patient-level confounders. As a result, the decision between immediate and delayed reconstruction after mastectomy remains a subject of ongoing debate and clinical uncertainty within plastic and reconstructive surgery.

In this multicenter, propensity score–matched cohort study, we sought to address this knowledge gap. We directly compared the incidence of 90-day postoperative complications, specifically looking at hematoma and VTE rates in patients undergoing mastectomy alone vs those receiving mastectomy followed by immediate DTI breast reconstruction, hypothesizing that DTI, when matched for confounding factors such as body mass index (BMI), preoperative comorbidities, and oncologic and pharmacologic agents, would not be associated with a significantly higher rate of these complications. Through this analysis, we aimed to provide plastic surgeons with procedure-specific risk estimates to inform patient counseling, surgical decision-making, and perioperative planning.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter cohort study used the TriNetX Research Network, a globally federated, deidentified electronic health record (EHR) database that aggregates clinical data from >100 health care organizations worldwide. Data extraction and analysis were performed on the platform, with cohort construction and outcome evaluation completed on 31 May 2025. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines to ensure transparent and reproducible reporting (supplemental Table 1).12

Participants

We identified all adult female patients (≥18 years) with a diagnosis of malignant neoplasm of the breast who underwent mastectomy (simple mastectomy, subcutaneous mastectomy, radical mastectomy including pectoral muscles and axillary lymph nodes, radical mastectomy with internal mammary lymph node dissection, and modified radical mastectomy including axillary lymph node dissection with or without preservation of the pectoralis muscle). Patients who underwent immediate DTI breast reconstruction on the same day were also identified. To isolate the effect of DTI, our exclusion criteria included patients who received any other augmentation methods, including autologous flap reconstruction (latissimus dorsi flap; free flaps, including deep inferior epigastric perforator, transverse upper gracilis, profunda artery perforator, and anterolateral/anteromedial thigh flaps; single-pedicled transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap [TRAM]; supercharged TRAM; and bipedicle TRAM) and staged implant-based reconstruction (tissue-expander exchange and tissue-expander placement), or patients who were deceased at the time of follow-up.

Exposure and outcome definitions

The primary exposure was patients who received a DTI breast reconstruction after an mastectomy. The primary outcomes were defined as 90-day postoperative complications, including (1) postoperative acute deep vein thrombosis (DVT), (2) postoperative pulmonary embolism (PE), and (3) postoperative hematoma formation, all assessed using the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnostic codes. Outcome ascertainment relied on ICD-10 diagnostic coding within the TriNetX EHR network. As with all coding-based observational studies, this approach is susceptible to coding inaccuracies, underreporting, and variability in documentation practices across participating institutions. These outcomes were evaluated from the day of surgery to 90 days postoperatively. Hematoma was defined as a postoperative collection of blood at or near the surgical site within 90 days of surgery, as coded by ICD-10 diagnostic entries. Only hematomas requiring medical or surgical intervention were captured, and diagnoses of nonspecific or minor ecchymosis were excluded.

Covariates and PSM

To mitigate potential selection and confounding bias, we performed a 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) using greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement. Covariates used for matching included demographic characteristics (age, race or ethnicity); comorbid conditions including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, nicotine dependence, von Willebrand disease, primary thrombophilia, activated protein C resistance, prothrombin gene mutation, antiphospholipid syndrome, hemorrhagic disorders due to circulating anticoagulants, and acquired coagulation factor deficiency; and characteristics and management of the malignant neoplasm, including carcinoma in situ of the breast, other malignant neoplasms, radiation history, and chemotherapy.

We also adjusted for relevant preoperative medications, including anticoagulants (heparin, enoxaparin, warfarin, apixaban, and rivaroxaban) and antiplatelet agents (aspirin and clopidogrel). Hormone therapies included tamoxifen, raloxifene, and aromatase inhibitors. Cardiometabolic agents included antilipemic agents, diuretics, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin II receptor blockers, with all database codes presented in supplemental Table 1. Covariate balance between matched groups was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD of <0.1 considered acceptable.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means with standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. For binary outcomes, group comparisons were performed using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), from which the relative risk reduction (RRR) and number needed to treat (NNT) were also derived. Statistical significance was set at 2-sided P value < .05. To evaluate the robustness of our findings to potential unmeasured confounding, we performed a sensitivity analysis by calculating E-value.13 The E-value provides a quantitative assessment of the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the treatment and the outcome to fully account for the observed association in the sample, conditional on the measured covariates.13 E-values were calculated for the relative risk point estimate and the limit of the CI closest to the null for each outcome. Sample size justification was informed by previous large-scale surgical outcome studies that reported statistically significant differences in postoperative complication rates.5,10,14 All statistical methods were selected and executed by the authors in accordance with contemporary guidelines for observational outcomes research and performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The study was approved as exempt from the Western Institutional Review Board under Section §164.514(b)(1) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule because it involved only deidentified data. Therefore, patient consent was not required for this analysis.

Results

Cohort identification and matching

Between January 2010 and May 2025, 45 753 adult female patients who underwent mastectomy were identified. Of these, 6625 patients (14.5%) had undergone an immediate DTI breast reconstruction after mastectomy. These patients were matched 1:1 to mastectomy-only patients using PSM derived from a logistic regression model that included age, race or ethnicity, BMI, preoperative comorbidities, malignancy status, and preoperative medications or procedures. After excluding the unmatched cases, 5769 matched pairs (n = 11 538) were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Baseline characteristics

Before matching, the groups differed significantly in age, comorbidities, and medication use. Patients in the DTI cohort were significantly younger (mean age at index, 52.4 ± 11.9 years) than those in the mastectomy-only cohort (62.6 ± 12.9 years), with notable differences in rates of hypertension (20.3% vs 43.9%), diabetes mellitus (5.3% vs 18.5%), and heart failure (1.1% vs 5.1%) (Table 1).

Outcomes

Within 90 days postoperatively, complications identified through ICD-10 diagnostic coding within the TriNetX network were observed at lower rates in the mastectomy+DTI cohort across multiple domains. Because outcome ascertainment was based on diagnostic coding rather than independent clinical adjudication, the reported event rates should be interpreted as coding-based estimates. Hematoma rates were lower in the mastectomy+DTI group (0.94%) than in the mastectomy-only cohort (1.40%; RR, 0.675; 95% CI, 0.48-0.95; P = .024), reflecting an RRR of 32.5% and an NNT of 217 patients.

DVT occurred at a significantly lower frequency in the mastectomy+DTI group (0.38%) than in the mastectomy-only group (0.83%; RR, 0.458; 95% CI, 0.28-0.76; P = .002), corresponding to an RRR of 54.2% and an NNT of 222 patients. PE occurred at a lower frequency in the mastectomy+DTI cohort (0.29%) than the mastectomy-only group (0.50%; RR, 0.586; 95% CI, 0.32-1.07; P = .076), corresponding to an RRR of 41.4% and an NNT of 476 patients.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the residual imbalance in BMI (SMD, 0.275) after PSM, a sensitivity analysis using E-values was performed to assess the robustness of our findings to potential confounding from this covariate (Table 3). The results indicate that the observed associations for DVT and hematoma are robust. The E-value for the DVT association was 3.79, signifying that residual BMI would need to be associated with both treatment selection and the DVT outcome by an RR of at least 3.79 to nullify the finding. Similarly, the hematoma association was significant, with an E-value of 2.33. In contrast, the association for PE, which was not significant in the primary analysis (P = .076), was not robust to potential confounding, with an E-value of 1.00 for the CI limit.

Discussion

This multicenter propensity score–matched cohort study of 11 538 adult female patients undergoing either mastectomy alone or mastectomy+DTI reconstruction provides robust, clinically meaningful evidence on the short-term safety profile of DTI. In particular, our analysis revealed lower 90-day postoperative rates of hematoma and DVT and a numerically lower rate of PE in the mastectomy+DTI group than the mastectomy-only group. These findings suggest that, when performed in appropriately selected patients, DTI reconstruction is associated with a favorable short-term risk profile compared with mastectomy alone. However, these associations should be interpreted in the context of contemporary patient selection and perioperative practices rather than as evidence of a direct, protective causal effect of reconstruction, given the inherent limitations of database use. Importantly, patients selected for immediate reconstruction often represent a healthier and more carefully selected population than those undergoing mastectomy alone. Although PSM accounted for numerous measured demographic, oncologic, pharmacologic, and comorbidity-related variables, unmeasured factors such as functional status, frailty, tissue quality, surgeon judgment, institutional selection practices, and patient preferences could not be captured within the TriNetX database.

Such results suggest that immediate DTI reconstruction was not associated with higher short-term thromboembolic or hemorrhagic complications in this matched cohort.15,16 Maesaka et al and Nwaogu et al both reported increased rates of postoperative complications, including VTE, hematoma, infection, seroma, and reoperation, suggesting that the prolonged nature of the procedure after mastectomy and additional physiologic burden introduced by reconstruction may contribute to the elevated perioperative risk.14,17 Although those studies reported elevated risks associated with immediate reconstruction, our findings align more closely with recent data suggesting that contemporary surgical techniques and perioperative protocols may significantly mitigate these risks. For instance, Chow et al demonstrated that same-day mastectomy with immediate prosthetic reconstruction did not increase complication rates compared with inpatient procedures, highlighting that enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols and streamlined operative workflows may improve outcomes in well-selected patients.18

Notably, the risk profile of DTI reconstruction seems to be influenced by several patient and procedure-level factors. Among the most prominent is BMI, which is a well-documented contributor to postoperative complications in breast surgery.19, 20, 21 Although PSM successfully balanced most covariates, BMI remained imbalanced (SMD, 0.275), and its influence cannot be discounted. Previous literature demonstrated that elevated BMI confers a dose-dependent increase in complications, with class II to III obesity significantly heightening risks of reconstructive failure.22,23 Incremental increases in BMI have been associated with a 13% rise in adverse outcomes per unit increase, highlighting its central role in perioperative risk stratification.23 Therefore, our results must be interpreted with residual confounding in mind: patients with lower BMIs may have been preferentially selected for immediate reconstruction. This concern is supported by previous literature demonstrating differential thromboembolic risk according to obesity status among patients undergoing breast reconstruction. Consequently, part of the observed association between reconstruction status and postoperative outcomes may reflect residual confounding related to BMI despite PSM. Nevertheless, although higher BMI should prompt careful surgical planning and patient counseling, it should not automatically disqualify candidates from implant-based reconstruction; instead, delayed reconstruction may offer a safer, individualized pathway that balances oncologic safety with reconstructive outcomes.24

The findings regarding PE warrant careful interpretation. Although PE occurred less frequently in the mastectomy+DTI cohort than in the mastectomy-only cohort (0.29% vs 0.50%), the difference was not statistically significant (P = .076). Given the low absolute incidence of PE in both cohorts, relatively small differences in event counts may substantially influence statistical significance. Nevertheless, the observed reduction in PE rates remained directionally consistent with the reductions in DVT and hematoma rates. Given the potentially severe clinical consequences of PE, this trend may be clinically relevant and merits further evaluation in larger studies specifically designed to assess thromboembolic outcomes.

Beyond BMI, other multifactorial contributors merit consideration. Emerging evidence suggests that PE and hematoma risk are determined not only by surgical or biochemical factors but also by broader clinical and patient-specific variables. Treatment-related exposures such as perioperative blood transfusions and corticosteroid use have been linked to increased VTE risk.25 Repeated previous surgeries may compound endothelial injury or venous stasis, predisposing patients to thromboembolic events. Genetic predispositions, including factor V Leiden, the prothrombin G20210A mutation, and deficiencies of protein C, protein S, or antithrombin, significantly increase the risk of hypercoagulability.25 Smoking also independently contributes by damaging the vascular endothelium and impairing circulation, thereby elevating both the risk of DVT and PE.26 In our analysis, several of these variables were accounted for through PSM. In particular, we controlled for smoking status, medication use, oncologic exposures such as chemotherapy and radiation, and relevant hematologic disorders. However, specific surgical history and transfusion data were not available in the TriNetX data set. Therefore, incorporating these unmeasured contributors into future risk stratification models may enhance predictive accuracy and guide more individualized prophylactic strategies.

Surgical adjuncts have also been shown to influence the risk of complications. Of note, the use of acellular dermal matrices (ADMs) has gained attention as a potential modulator of postoperative complication rates.27,28 Although initially thought to contribute to lower postoperative complication rates, emerging evidence suggests that factors such as mastectomy weight, patient age, smoking status, preoperative comorbidities, and adjuvant therapy may play more dominant roles in shaping the postoperative risk profiles.29, 30, 31 Moreover, efforts to mitigate risk through technical modifications, such as perforating ADM, have not demonstrated meaningful differences in complication rates.32 In this context, the favorable outcomes observed in our DTI cohort suggest that ADM utilization, when applied within a modern, risk-adjusted strategy, does not serve as a primary determinant of adverse events.

Furthermore, pharmacologic adjuncts have also gained traction in efforts to reduce postoperative complications. Among these, tranexamic acid (TXA) has shown promise in minimizing hematoma formation without increasing thromboembolic risks in breast surgery.33, 34, 35, 36 A recent meta-analysis of 1139 patients reported a significant reduction in hematoma incidence with systemic TXA administration from 12.9% to 7.9%, corresponding to an odds ratio of 0.43, with no associated increase in thromboembolic events.34 Similarly, Weissler et al reported a hematoma rate of only 0.46% in patients receiving IV TXA compared with 2.9% in controls, with no major adverse events.35 By blocking plasminogen activation and stabilizing fibrin clots, TXA enables localized hemostatic control without compromising systemic coagulation. However, TXA administration was not captured within the TriNetX database and therefore could not be evaluated in the present study. Accordingly, any contribution of TXA to the observed outcomes remains speculative.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study provides large-scale, multicenter evidence comparing thromboembolic and hemorrhagic outcomes after mastectomy with and without immediate DTI reconstruction, several limitations warrant discussion. The first concerns reliance on ICD-10 and Current Procedural Terminology coding within a federated EHR database. Although this approach enables broad population-level analyses, it precludes access to granular operative and perioperative details that may meaningfully influence postoperative risk. Variables such as operative duration, mastectomy flap thickness, implant plane, use and type of ADMs, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion requirements, and surgeon experience could not be directly captured. As such, coding inaccuracies or institutional variability may have resulted in misclassification of outcomes or incomplete ascertainment of clinically relevant complications. Furthermore, because thromboembolic and hemorrhagic events were identified solely through ICD-10 diagnostic coding, event severity, diagnostic confirmation, and clinical context could not be independently verified. Consequently, the true incidence of these outcomes may be under- or overestimated. In addition, although residual imbalance in BMI persisted after matching (SMD, 0.275), our sensitivity analysis using E-values suggested that the associations for hematoma and DVT were relatively robust to unmeasured confounding of this magnitude. Nevertheless, given the established association between obesity and postoperative thromboembolic and reconstructive complications, residual confounding related to BMI remains an important limitation of this study.

Moreover, although the emerging literature supports the safe use of TXA as an adjunct in breast reconstruction, its administration was not captured in the TriNetX data set. Consequently, we were unable to evaluate its use, dosing, route of administration, or timing relative to surgery. Therefore, any discussion regarding TXA as a contributor to reduced hematoma or thromboembolic risk in this cohort must remain speculative. TXA should be considered as a hypothesis-generating factor rather than a demonstrated mechanism underlying the observed associations in our study.

Finally, the retrospective observational design inherently limits causal interpretation. Institutional differences in VTE prophylaxis protocols, early ambulation practices, ERAS pathways, and perioperative monitoring could not be standardized or adjusted for. These unmeasured system-level factors may contribute to heterogeneity in outcomes across participating centers and warrant cautious interpretation of the observed associations. In addition, patients selected for immediate DTI reconstruction may differ from those undergoing mastectomy alone in ways not fully captured by available database variables, introducing the possibility of residual selection bias despite PSM. Nicotine exposure was assessed using available diagnostic coding and may not fully capture smoking history, vaping, smokeless tobacco use, secondhand smoke exposure, or cumulative lifetime tobacco exposure, introducing the possibility of residual confounding.

Future investigations should incorporate detailed operative and perioperative variables, including operative time, estimated blood loss, transfusion requirements, implant characteristics, ADM use, and surgeon experience. Standardized reporting of VTE prophylactic strategies, early mobilization protocols, and adherence to ERAS protocols would enable more granular risk stratification and the identification of modifiable factors influencing postoperative outcomes.

Conclusions

In this large, multi-institutional, propensity score–matched cohort study of 11 538 patients, immediate DTI reconstruction after mastectomy was associated with lower 90-day rates of postoperative hematoma and DVT and a nonsignificant trend toward lower rates of PE. These findings suggest that, in the context of modern perioperative care and appropriate patient selection, DTI reconstruction can be a safe and viable reconstructive option. Nevertheless, given the observational design and inherent limitations of coding-based variables, these associations should be interpreted as hypothesis generating rather than causal. Validation through prospective multicenter studies that incorporate oncologic detail, operative technique, pharmacologic adjuncts, and patient-reported outcomes will be essential to confirm these associations and refine individualized risk stratification, ensuring that reconstructive decision-making remains evidence based and patient centered.
