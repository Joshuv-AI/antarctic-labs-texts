Clinical and CT features of patients with pneumatosis intestinalis
Authors: Weiheng He, Yihui Yin, Yongqi Zhu, Huimin Rao, Lin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the clinical and abdominal computed tomography (CT) characteristics of pneumatosis intestinalis (PI). This retrospective single-center study included 58 patients with PI. Clinical data and CT images were reviewed. CT findings included location, extent, and associated features, and PI was classified into 3 morphological patterns: bandlike, grapelike, and beadlike. Inter-observer agreement was evaluated, and subgroup analysis was performed using analysis of variance and chi-square tests. The mean age of the patients was 68 years. The most common comorbidities were diabetes (44.8%) and hypertension (42.4%). Abdominal pain (62.1%) was the predominant symptom. Inter-observer agreement for CT findings was excellent (kappa 0.88–0.95). The beadlike pattern was most frequent (48.3%), followed by bandlike (34.5%) and grapelike (17.2%). The surgical group (n = 10) was older and had more frequent abdominal pain, peritoneal signs, and ascites (60.0% vs 20.8%, P = .033) than the conservative group (n = 48). Overall prognosis was favorable (96.6% recovery), but both deaths were associated with concurrent portal and mesenteric venous gas. After treatment, a follow-up CT scan obtained 2 to 4 weeks later demonstrated resolution of PI. We propose a novel CT morphological classification method for PI. Clinical signs and specific CT findings (e.g., ascites and portomesenteric venous gas) guide management. While most patients recover with conservative care, concurrent portal and mesenteric venous gas signifies a high mortality risk. CT plays a key role in the follow-up of patients with PI.

1. Introduction

Pneumatosis intestinalis (PI) is a rare condition that may be a primary (idiopathic) or secondary sign of numerous underlying gastrointestinal, pulmonary, and systemic diseases.[1] Primary and secondary PI accounted for 15% and 85% of cases, respectively.

PI is defined as the presence of gas bubbles within the intestinal wall, mostly in the mucosa or submucosa. The true incidence of PI remains unknown, but the growing use of abdominal computed tomography (CT) and colonoscopy has contributed to the increased detection of PI.[2,3] PI, free air within the peritoneal cavity, and portomesenteric venous gas (PMVG) can be accurately detected using advanced multidetector CT.[4]

Primary PI, which is often benign and self-limiting, may require only conservative management or oxygen therapy. In stark contrast, secondary PI is the harbinger of an underlying life-threatening condition, such as bowel ischemia, obstruction, or severe inflammation, demanding immediate and targeted intervention to address the root cause.[5,6]

The clinical characteristics of PI have been described mostly in case reports or images.[7–14] To date, only a few non-case reports have described the clinical features of PI.[15–17] CT has emerged as the primary and most robust imaging modality for the initial diagnosis and evaluation of PI in clinical practice, particularly in emergency settings.[18] Moreover, CT plays a pivotal role in risk stratification and in guiding the management of PI.[6,15,19,20] In addition, recognizing the shape patterns of PI on abdominal CT may be critical for radiologists in clinical practice. However, no studies have investigated the clinical significance of the shape patterns on abdominal CT in patients with PI.

This study aimed to investigate the clinical and imaging characteristics of patients with PI by analyzing shape patterns on CT to improve the overall understanding of PI.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This retrospective, single-center study was performed at a tertiary hospital (People’s Hospital of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, Ningxia Medical University, China). The Institutional Review Board (People’s Hospital of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, Ningxia Medical University) approved this study (approval number: 2022-NZR-157) on March 15, 2022, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

The inclusion criteria were adult patients (≥ 18 years), patients with complete clinical and laboratory data, and adequate CT image quality.

Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age, incomplete clinical or laboratory documentation, significant CT artifacts, and prior gastrointestinal surgery that could alter bowel anatomy and confound the interpretation of PI.

Confirmation of PI diagnosis: The diagnosis of PI was based on established imaging criteria for intramural gas.[18] The key diagnostic feature was the presence of gas cysts within the bowel wall on CT, symptoms (e.g., abdominal pain, distension, and diarrhea), endoscopic reports confirming submucosal cysts, or surgical findings. PI was identified as either primary or secondary in each patient. For patients with multiple episodes of PI, only the data from the first examination were included in the analysis.

2.2. Data collection and analysis

Prior to data collection, a comprehensive and standardized electronic data collection sheet was developed. This sheet predefined all variables of interest, including demographic data, clinical symptoms, laboratory results, and CT imaging features to be assessed (e.g., pattern of pneumatosis, distribution, and location). The use of this sheet ensured that all data extractors and radiologists evaluated the same parameters consistently.

Electronic medical records of patients with PI were extracted and analyzed by the research team. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, clinical signs and symptoms, laboratory data, radiological findings, treatments, and clinical outcomes were obtained. Subsequently, a trained team of physicians reviewed the data. Information assessed for PI included sex, age, body mass index, chronic medical history, symptoms and signs, laboratory data, abdominal CT findings, treatment measures, and outcomes.

Abdominal pain was defined as a subjective report of a sharp, dull, cramping, or aching sensation in the abdomen that the patient explicitly described as “pain.” Abdominal fullness was defined as a sensation of excessive satiety or pressure in the upper abdomen or the entire abdomen. Abdominal discomfort was defined as an unpleasant subjective feeling in the abdomen that the patient did not explicitly describe as “pain.”

Complete blood counts and liver and kidney function tests were performed using Siemens automated analyzers.

2.3. CT scan and image analysis

All abdominal CT examinations were performed using a 512 row CT machine (Revolution CT; GE Healthcare). The imaging protocol included the whole abdomen and pelvis (diaphragm to pubic symphysis, 120 kV, 300–400 mA), 1.25 mm of reconstructed slice thickness, and a standard soft-tissue kernel. After a non-enhanced scan, we intravenously injected iodinated contrast medium (Iopromide, 300 mgI/ml; Zhejiang Haichang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd) at a flow rate of 4 ml/s, followed by an arterial phase (25 seconds, 1.25 mm reconstructed axial slices) and a venous phase (80 seconds, 1.25 mm reconstructed axial slices) in all patients. Bowel preparation before scanning was not routinely performed because of the acute clinical presentation in many patients in this study. Low-dose protocols were used for follow-up CT scans.

CT images were reviewed by 2 radiologists (WH and LL, with 10–15 years of clinical experience) who were blinded to the clinical data, and final decisions were reached by consensus. PI was confirmed when intramural air was observed on the bowel wall on CT.[18]

The CT findings of PI were assessed by reviewing the images. Location (stomach, small bowel, colon), extension (regional [< 50 % of the whole organ wall length involved], extensive [≥ 50 % of the organ wall length involved], or diffuse [involving the whole organ]), shape patterns (beadlike, bandlike, and grapelike), bowel features (wall thickening [> 3 mm, provided that bowel loops were not collapsed], wall edema, luminal dilatation [the small bowel, the colon, and the cecum were considered dilated whenever the lumen measured > 3 cm, >6 cm, and > 8 cm in diameter, respectively[15]], obstruction, and no mural contrast enhancement}, mesenteric vessel findings (atherosclerosis, venous thrombosis), ascites, free gas, PMVG (mesenteric venous gas, hepatic portal venous gas), and mesenteric fat stranding of PI were evaluated. Bowel ischemia was defined as abnormal bowel wall enhancement, specifically PI, with absent mural contrast enhancement. The bowel wall was considered to have “no mural contrast enhancement” if the absolute increase in attenuation between the non-contrast and portal venous phases was < 10 HU.[21] The distinction between PMVG and mesenteric venous gas was primarily based on anatomical location (intrahepatic portal vein vs within the mesentery for mesenteric veins). In cases where a patient’s imaging presented overlapping patterns of PI (e.g., both bandlike and beadlike), the classification was assigned based on the most extensive pattern involved. Specifically, the pattern occupying the greatest continuous segment length of the bowel was considered the predominant and representative category for the patient in the analysis.

Two radiologists independently assessed CT findings in a blinded manner. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using the kappa value.

3. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequency rates and percentages and compared using Fisher exact test. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test continuous variables for normality. Continuous variables were compared using Student t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were also performed. Variables with a P value < .10 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model to identify independent risk factors associated with the need for surgery. Analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 26.0; IBM Corporation) and MedCalc statistical software (version 20; MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

4. Results

4.1. Population characteristics

Between 2017 and 2023, 50,144 patients underwent abdominal CT at our institution. Of these, 62 patients were identified as having PI on CT. Two patients with significant abdominal CT artifacts and 2 with incomplete clinical and laboratory data were excluded. Ultimately, 58 patients with PI were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of the patient selection process. CT = computed tomography.

The average age of patients was 68 ± 13 years; 26 (44.8%) patients were female and 32 (55.2%) were male. The most common comorbidities were diabetes mellitus (26 patients, 44.8%) and hypertension (25 patients, 42.4%). The most common clinical symptom of PI was abdominal pain (36 patients, 62.1%), followed by abdominal fullness (19 patients, 32.8%). The proportion of fever, bloody stools, septic shock, and accidental diagnosis accounted for 3 to 5% of cases. Regarding operation history, 8 (13.8%) patients underwent abdominal surgery and 2 (3.4%) patients underwent cardiovascular surgery. Among the 58 patients with PI, 3 were classified as having primary PI and 55 as having secondary PI.

4.3. CT findings in all patients with PI

The inter-observer agreement for the CT findings was excellent, with kappa values ranging from 0.88 to 0.95.

Typical abdominal CT characteristics of PI included intramural gas in the gastrointestinal tract, ascites, free gas, PMVG, and mesenteric fat stranding (Table 2). PI was located in the gastric wall in 3 patients (5.1%), in the small bowel wall in 18 (31.0 %), and in the colonic wall in 46 (79.3 %) patients. Among them, PI was observed in 1 patient (1.7%) in the gastric and small bowel walls simultaneously and in 6 patients (10.3%) in the small and large bowel walls simultaneously. Nearly half of the patients (26 patients, 44.8%) had extensive intestinal involvement.

4.6. Treatments and outcomes of patients with PI

Conservative treatments, such as anti-inflammatory, fasting water and food, gastrointestinal decompression, nutritional support, and regulation of intestinal microbiota, were adopted. Only a few patients required surgical treatment. The patients underwent follow-up for 3 months following diagnosis.

In this study, 48 (82.7%) patients received conservative treatment and 10 (17.3%) underwent surgery. Two patients (3.4%) with both portal venous gas and mesenteric venous gas died of septic shock, which was associated with more severe PI. The remaining 56 (96.6%) patients recovered and were discharged (Table 3). Follow-up CT imaging after treatment confirmed resolution of the PI (Fig. 5).

5. Discussion

This was a retrospective study of the clinical and imaging characteristics of patients with PI. The main findings of the current study are as follows: we systematically proposed 3 CT morphological patterns of PI (bandlike, grapelike, and beadlike), which may help describe the disease more precisely; although the overall prognosis of PI was favorable, the surgical treatment group presented with more severe clinical manifestations (e.g., abdominal pain, peritoneal irritation signs) and CT findings (ascites); the concurrent presence of portal venous gas and mesenteric venous gas was a sign of poor prognosis, associated with mortality; after treatment, follow-up CT revealed the resolution of PI.

The pathogenesis of PI remains unclear, and 6 pathophysiological mechanisms have been postulated. The postulated mechanisms include inflammation, physical damage to the intestinal mucosa, nutritional imbalance and dysbacteriosis, gastrointestinal dysmotility, and immune dysfunction.[15,19] The most common comorbidities were diabetes mellitus and hypertension. PI is more likely to be detected in elderly patients with comorbidities, possibly due to decreased intestinal peristalsis and/or immune dysfunction in these patients. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is a classic, well-documented risk factor for PI, primarily through the “mechanical theory” or “pulmonary hypothesis.” In severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with emphysema, destruction of alveolar walls can lead to the formation of blebs and bullae. The rupture of these structures allows air to escape into the mediastinum (pneumomediastinum). Gas can then dissect along the perivascular sheaths of the aorta and mesenteric vessels into the retroperitoneum and, ultimately, the intestinal mesentery and bowel wall.[22]

The most common clinical symptom of PI is abdominal pain, followed by abdominal fullness. Some patients with PI are diagnosed incidentally. In addition, increased white blood cell counts and decreased lymphocyte counts are common in patients with PI. The prognosis for patients with PI is relatively good. Intestinal necrosis and severe infection are common causes of death in patients with PI. These results are in line with previous studies.[5,17]

Regarding clinical presentation and distribution, our finding that the colon (79.3%) was the most common site of PI aligns with reports by Wayne et al, who found colonic involvement in 68% of cases[23] and Bao et al, who reported a rate of 76%.[24] The predominance of abdominal pain as the leading symptom in our cohort (62.1%) also fell within the range (55–70%) reported in these studies.[23,24]

We identified 3 morphological patterns (bandlike, grapelike, and beadlike) of PI on CT imaging, and the beadlike pattern was the most common. We propose that these patterns may reflect the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. The bandlike pattern, characterized by linear gas shadows, is typically located within the submucosa. Loose connective tissue of the submucosal layer offers minimal resistance to gas dissection. This pattern is highly consistent with the mechanical theory of PI, where elevated intraluminal pressure (e.g., from obstruction) or loss of mucosal integrity (e.g., from ischemia and inflammation) forces gas through the mucosa into the submucosal plane, where it spreads linearly.[25] Our study identified a beadlike pattern as the most common morphological presentation of PI (48.3%). This may reflect a confluence of the prevalent pathophysiological mechanisms and the enhanced detection capability of modern CT. Conversely, the grapelike pattern, presenting as large, clustered cysts, is often found in the subserosa. This pattern aligns more closely with bacterial theory. In this scenario, gas-forming bacteria may directly invade the intestinal wall, or their metabolic products (e.g., hydrogen) may diffuse into tissue spaces, leading to the formation of discrete cysts. This subserosal location may also be linked to gas trafficking via the mesenteric lymphatic system.[26]

The beadlike pattern, featuring small, discrete bubbles lining the bowel wall, may represent a mixed or early stage process. This could result from low-grade mechanical gas entry or from gas trapped within the lymphatic channels themselves (pulmonary lymphatic theory). The presence of portal venous gas, which was a critical prognostic indicator in our study, is often associated with beadlike or grapelike patterns. It occurs when intramural gas, particularly in the subserosal location, which is drained by the mesenteric venules, dissects into the venous system and travels to the portal circulation.[27]

Typical abdominal CT characteristics of PI include intramural gas in the gastrointestinal tract, ascites, free gas, PMVG, and mesenteric fat stranding. In our study, the most common location of PI was the colonic wall, and nearly half of the patients had extensive intestinal involvement. We found that 10 (17.2%) patients presented with bowel ischemia. Hepatic portal venous gas and mesenteric venous gas were observed in only a few patients. The results are similar to previous studies.[5,18,23]

The overall mortality rate in our cohort (3.4%) was notably lower than the 18 to 22% rates reported in several previous studies.[5,28] We attributed this favorable outcome to several interrelated factors, primarily concerning patient population characteristics and selection bias. First, and most importantly, is the spectrum of the underlying etiology. The prognosis of PI is predominantly determined by the cause of the PI itself rather than the mere presence of intramural gas. Earlier studies often included a higher proportion of patients with PI secondary to life-threatening conditions such as mesenteric ischemia and bowel necrosis.[25,28] In contrast, our cohort likely included a greater number of patients with benign or less acute causes such as medication-induced PI. This is indirectly supported by the high rate of successful conservative management (82.7%). Second, increased detection of incidental PI is likely to play a significant role. With the widespread and growing use of high-resolution CT in clinical practice, radiologists are now more frequently identifying subtle or asymptomatic PI in patients undergoing scanning for other reasons.[24]

In this study, we identified 2 independent factors associated with the need for surgery: lower red blood cell count and the presence of ascites. These findings provide valuable insights into risk stratification and clinical decision-making. A lower red blood cell count, which is indicative of anemia, may reflect several underlying pathological processes. In an acute setting, it can be a marker of hemorrhagic transformation or bleeding from the ischemic bowel.[29] More chronically, it could signal a state of chronic disease or malnutrition, which may compromise the ability of the intestine to recover from an insult, thereby lowering the threshold for surgical intervention.[30] This finding underscores that systemic physiological reserve, as reflected by the red blood cell count, plays a crucial role in the clinical outcomes of PI. The presence of ascites was associated with a nearly 5-fold increase in the odds of requiring surgery (OR: 4.933, P = .045). This finding is consistent with established literature, where ascites in the context of PI is frequently associated with underlying intestinal ischemia, infarction, or perforation.[31,32] The accumulation of fluid can signify compromised bowel wall integrity and transmural inflammation, which often necessitate surgical exploration. Our results corroborate that ascites is not merely an incidental finding, but also a critical clinical indicator of disease severity in PI.

CT plays a key role in the follow-up of patients with PIs. During follow-up, the intramural gas in the gastrointestinal tract disappeared on the CT scan. We suggest a risk adaptation approach. Serial CT imaging may be unnecessary for clinically stable patients with low-risk CT features (e.g., isolated PI patterns without PMVG, significant ascites, or laboratory signs of severe infection). A single follow-up CT scan 1 to 2 weeks after treatment initiation can be considered to confirm resolution, particularly if there is any clinical ambiguity or to provide definitive reassurance. Conversely, for high-risk patients managed conservatively, an initial follow-up CT within 24 to 72 hours is warranted to assess the progression of pneumatosis, development of new ascites, or other signs of clinical deterioration that would prompt surgical intervention. Based on our results, we propose a clinical algorithm that integrates CT findings to guide PI management.

The results of this study have several potential implications for clinical practice. First, our proposed CT morphological classification (bandlike, grapelike, and beadlike) offers radiologists a simple and standardized descriptive system, which may facilitate clearer communication with referring clinicians regarding the extent and character of PI. Second, the identification of ascites as an independent predictor of surgical intervention provides an objective CT marker that can be incorporated into risk stratification algorithms. When ascites is present in a patient with PI, clinicians should have a lower threshold for considering surgical exploration. Third, the uniformly poor prognosis associated with concurrent portal and mesenteric venous gas underscores this finding as a critical “red flag” on CT, necessitating immediate and aggressive management, potentially including early surgical consultation.

This study had some limitations. First, the retrospective, single-center design is inherently susceptible to selection bias, a limitation further amplified by the inclusion of only patients who underwent CT imaging. Second, the sample size was relatively small, particularly in the surgical group with only 10 patients, limiting the power of more robust statistical comparisons and multivariate regression analyses to identify independent predictors. Third, a limitation is the potential for reader bias during the CT image analysis. Fourth, the diagnosis of PI was not pathologically confirmed in all patients, although it was confirmed in all cases based on the characteristic CT findings.

6. Conclusions

The morphological classification we propose offers a new perspective for describing PI. Treatment decisions should be based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical manifestations (e.g., peritoneal irritation signs) and specific CT findings (e.g., ascites and PMVG). Although most patients with PI can be successfully managed conservatively, the concurrent presence of portal and mesenteric venous gas is a critical sign predicting a high risk of mortality, warranting heightened awareness and aggressive management by clinicians. CT plays a key role in the diagnosis and follow-up of patients with PIs.
