Mapping the impact of intrinsic capacity on healthcare use
Authors: I-Tzu Chen, Wan-Hsuan Lu, Fei-Yuan Hsiao, Liang-Kung Chen
Published: 2026

In response to the rapidly aging global population, the World Health Organization (WHO) published the World Report on Aging and Health in 2015, [1] catalyzing global efforts to promote healthy aging such as the development of Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) [2]. Central to this framework of healthy aging, which is defined as a process of maintaining functional ability that enables well-being in older age [3], is intrinsic capacity (IC). IC represents a composite of an individual's physical and mental capacities and can be operationalized across five domains: cognition, locomotion, psychological, sensory and vitality [2].

IC has been receiving significant attention across the aging research community, spanning from basic science to clinical care. Recent review of longitudinal studies has provided evidence supporting the measurement of IC in animal models, particularly in mice, thereby establishing an important foundation for translational biological research [4]. The clinical utility of IC has also expanded, as it serves as an indicator for screening, longitudinal surveillance, and evaluating the efficacy of interventions [5]. Previous studies have shown that impaired IC is associated with a range of adverse outcomes. Poorer overall IC was associated with a greater risk of subsequent loss of both activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs) [6], while cognition and locomotion domains were especially associated with functional limitations [7,8]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies demonstrated that poorer IC was associated with mortality and impairments in ADL and IADL, while maintaining or improving IC was associated with a lower risk of IADL impairment [9].

Compared to outcomes like disability and mortality, the association between IC impairment and increased healthcare utilization, particularly acute care utilization, remains underexplored [10]. Nursing home residents with higher levels of cognitive and vitality domains were reported to have a lower hospitalization risk [11]. In the community setting, studies have found that individuals with higher IC scores were less likely to experience hospitalization in the previous year [12] and had a lower probability of hospitalization [13]. While a latent group characterized by declines across four IC domains—cognition, locomotion, psychology, and vitality—was associated with an increased risk of hospitalization [14], no individual IC domain was independently associated with incident hospitalization [15]. In contrast, declines in cognition and locomotion appeared to predict emergency department visits during the 1-year follow-up based on survey data [15]. In a recent publication of the Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging, Hou, et al. reported that greater severity of IC impairment was associated with a higher risk of emergency room (ER) visits, using data from Taiwan’s national ICOPE program linked to the National Health Insurance database, which covers more than 99% of the country’s population [16], thereby providing a comprehensive, population-level overview of healthcare utilization. In their work, IC impairments were dichotomized based on in-depth functional assessments. While impairments across all IC domains were individually associated with ER utilization, impaired vitality, as reflected by malnutrition, showed the strongest association with increased all-cause ER visits. A dose-response relationship was also observed between the number of impaired IC domains and the rate of ER events. These findings are particularly relevant to aging societies, where growing demand for healthcare services underscores the need for early identification of vulnerable older adults and timely preventive interventions. Moreover, the findings highlight vitality, particularly nutritional status, as a potentially modifiable factor for reducing acute care utilization among older adults. Previous studies have reported that healthier dietary patterns are associated with better IC [17,18], suggesting that nutrition may represent a modifiable factor relevant to maintaining or improving IC. However, intervention studies are needed to determine whether targeted nutritional interventions can improve overall IC and whether this could translate into better health outcomes and reduced potentially avoidable healthcare utilization. Furthermore, because Hou et al.’s study assessed IC only cross-sectionally, future longitudinal research is warranted to examine how changes in IC over time are associated with adverse health outcomes and healthcare utilization.

Taken together, a multidimensional assessment of IC can serve as a useful indicator for identifying older adults at risk of adverse health outcomes and greater healthcare needs. As IC encompasses five key domains, examining both overall IC and its individual subdomains can provide complementary insights into the specific dimensions in which older adults may be vulnerable. This comprehensive and domain-specific information can facilitate the development of targeted interventions and support more efficient and personalized allocation of healthcare resources.
