Thermal-structural optimization and experimental validation of modified pyramid solar stills using finite element analysis
Authors: R Ramesh Kumar, P Sathyaseelan, Ahmed Kateb Jumaah Al-Nussairi, Javed Khan Bhutto, Hadi Hakami, Mohammad Amir Khan, Amare Merfo Amsal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolar desalination by pyramid solar stills can be an eco-friendly way of solving the problem of water shortage in areas that have a lot of sunlight. Nevertheless, productivity and efficiency are still limited by heat and mass transfer constraints, which require the systematic optimization of design parameters. This research analyzed the thermal and structural behavior of normally and altered pyramidal solar still configurations by means of combined finite element analysis and experimental verification. Both models were analyzed in ANSYS Workbench by means of three-dimensional steady-state thermal and static structural analyses. The changes in design included the geometrical modification of the absorber plate, thus increasing the effective heat transfer surface area by about 20% and at the same time, preserving structural integrity. The modified design reached the temperature of 342.91 K against 338.15 K of the conventional design, which is a 1.41% increase. The highest heat flux was increased from 809.97 W/m2 to 936.84 W/m2 (15.66% increase), while the thermal efficiency was enhanced from 68% to 72%. The structural investigation verified the secure condition with the highest equivalent stresses of 2.07 MPa (modified) and 1.73 MPa (conventional), thus the safety factors were more than 85. The experimental verification proved the excellent agreement with the computational predictions, the temperature, and the heat flux being within 5% and 8% of the predicted values respectively. The geometric optimization of the absorber plates plays a major role in the performance of the pyramid solar still without compromising the structural integrity. The validated computational approach offers a dependable basis for systematic design optimization and acts as a stepping stone for future advanced modeling work. Keywords: Solar still, Pyramid solar still, Finite element analysis, ANSYS, Thermal performance, Structural analysis, Experimental validation, Heat flux distribution, Desalination, Design optimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Freshwater scarcity is a critical problem confronting the globe in the 21st century due to the existence of almost two billion people who lack safe and accessible freshwater sources1. The demand for fresh water is rising because of the fast-growing population, industries, agriculture, and climatic changes, especially in arid and semi-arid regions2. Solar desalination is one of the eco-friendly ways of addressing freshwater scarcity from sea water or brine utilizing abundant solar energy without greenhouse gases and complex infrastructure installation3,4. Among various solar desalination technologies, the solar still of pyramid design has the benefit of high surface area, thermal efficiency, and simple fabrication over a single-sloped structure5. The pyramid design has higher sloping surfaces for condensate formation, condensate drainage efficiency, and effective utilization of solar energy at any time during the day6. However, the thermal and mass transfers limit the effectiveness and efficiency of pyramid solar stills.

The major principle that governs solar distillation is the evaporation–condensation cycle. The rays from the sun penetrate the transparent top covering and are absorbed by the basin water and the absorber plate, resulting in an elevation in the temperature of the water and facilitating the process of evaporation. This evaporated steam is then cooled through condensation on the inner surface of the glass covering7. The effectiveness of the solar still system is determined by various factors such as intensity of the solar rays, absorber plate features, design of the glass cover, depth of water, climatic variables, and methods of heat transfer8,9. The absorber plate acts as the absorber of solar energy, and its effectiveness greatly influences the rate of evaporation and the quantity of distilled water obtained10.

To understand and predict the behavior of the coupled thermal and structural components of the real systems is a prerequisite for the better designs that can realize the maximal productivity and the required durability at the same time. The upgrade means in the research works of the last years are the following: the coupling of PCMs for the thermal energy storage, nanoparticles for the improvement of the thermophysical characteristics, wicks to increase the evaporation area, external solar collectors, modified basin configurations, and geometric optimization11–13. The use of nano-enhanced PCMs and steel wool wicks in double-slope still can improve its performance14. More distillate yields were realized using PCM composites15. More water extraction has been achieved with the help of montmorillonite nanoparticles16, while the impact of nanocrystalline basin coatings on thermal absorption has also been demonstrated17. In all of these works, researchers have found that changes to material properties and thermal storage capacity can markedly enhance performance18.

Further performance can be gained through geometric optimization of absorber plates and glass covers. Productivity can be increased by using sliced absorber plates and optimized vapor space geometry19. Research on water depth, inlet temperature, and fins has found the optimal operating conditions20,21. The characteristics of the absorber plate play a crucial role in thermal performance22. These studies have revealed that geometrical modifications can increase the efficiency level without resorting to rare materials or complicated auxiliary systems23. The combined enhancement approaches show synergistic effects. Improvement in output has been attained by incorporating the wicking material and reflective surfaces24. The efficiency of the process has been boosted using the rotation process to achieve solar tracking25. The usage of fans and ultrasonic humidifier systems to enhance evaporation has been shown26. Natural absorbers at affordable costs have been examined in stepped solar stills27. Generally, all these investigations indicate that integration of several modifications brings about drastic outcomes in efficiency28.

While experiments offer real-life data, simulations offer a great alternative since they are capable of breaking down the thermal and structural processes, which cannot be measured accurately even within the confines of the laboratory setting. With the use of FEA, one can easily identify the exact location of the temperature gradient, heat flux, stresses, deformation, and thermal strain29. The outcome of the CFD simulations was found to be highly in agreement with the experimental results29. RSM has been employed in predicting production rate30, while ML models have been adopted in forecasting efficiency31.

Computational means give the designer a large number of variations to try out within a short time, pinpoint the parameters, and optimize the results, all this even before making a prototype2. Nevertheless, understanding the interaction between thermal and structural features of pyramid stills is still far from complete. The majority of the research pays attention only to the thermal aspects, with not much concern being given to the structural part of the stills under the cyclic thermal load. There are very few comparative studies that validate computational predictions against experimental observations across different parameters7. The effect of geometrical changes on both thermal and structural properties, as well as the interaction of these changes with each other, are not very well understood, and there are hardly any integrated frameworks that combine modeling and experimentation for the modified designs9.

Previous researches have focused on single aspects of a solar still such as: geometric alterations of the absorber plates19, configurations of a pyramid-type solar still24,25, as well as an experimental–numerical combination methodology29. In summary, this work significantly extends the aforementioned studies in three notable aspects: (i) the pyramid solar still optimization is conducted via a fully integrated 3D thermal–structural FEA framework, thereby numerically revealing the synergistic effect of heat transfer and structural integrity, (ii) the geometrically altered absorber plate resulting in a 20% increase in the effective surface area is pitted against the baseline one under strictly the same boundary conditions, and (iii) thorough experimental validations endorse the computer simulations, i.e., temperature, heat flux, and distillate production. Thus, it is not going to be an exaggeration to say that the present work is the first one fathoming both the thermal performance improvement and the structural safety of a modified pyramid solar still through the embedded FEA coupled with experimental validation.

This research work is the result of the need for improvement of these systems via FEA and experimental validation. In the modified case, there are features that make use of geometric alteration of the absorber plate in order to enhance its heat transfer surface area while still retaining strength. The analysis done was in regard to parameters such as temperature distribution, heat flux, stress, deformation, and thermal strain. These were performed in 3D static steady state thermal and static structural analysis using ANSYS Workbench. Results were compared against experimental results conducted on laboratory-based samples exposed to the sun. There are seven objectives in this study, all of which are highly correlated and intended to enhance the performance of pyramidal solar stills. Firstly, we developed 3D finite element models of the conventional and altered pyramid solar still design.

Secondly, we conducted a detailed thermal analysis focusing on the temperature distributions and heat flux distributions in the systems. Thirdly, we conducted a static structural analysis which involved stress analysis, deformation analysis, and safety factor analysis under thermal loading conditions. Fourth, we made a quantitative comparison of thermal and structural performance between the two designs in order to understand improvement mechanisms. Fifth, we developed validated computational methods open to further optimization study. Sixth, we delivered the design ideas grounded on the evidence for the real-world execution. Firstly, a computational framework that is capable of accurate predictions was built which ensures that the framework can be used for optimization and machine learning studies in the future with confidence. Second, this research presents a detailed thermal, structural coupled analysis of pyramid solar stills supported by the experimental data. It specifies the thermal and structural impacts of the absorber plate alterations, gives the design guideline supported by the evidence, and suggests computational methods for the rapid feasibility of design changes. The results facilitate the creation of efficient and dependable desalination systems and provide the basis for data-driven design optimization strategies in the ​‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌future.

Materials and methods

Solar still design configurations

Two pyramid solar still designs were examined under consideration with a very close similarity in sizes. However, only a change in shape was introduced on the absorber plates of both models to provide improvement of their thermal performance. In both cases, the models had square basins having a size equal to 1 m2; thus, the results could be compared not considering scaling effects on the studied systems’ performance. The first pyramid solar still model was a reference one characterized by a flat-shaped absorber plate. In addition, no special geometric shapes were applied on the absorber plate. The second pyramid solar still design had an innovative geometric structure placed on the absorber plate. This change was aimed at achieving better thermal performance due to the increased heat transfer surface area of the absorber plate while retaining its structural integrity. Moreover, the new design had several ways of improving heat transfer due to a greater heat transfer surface area between the absorber plate and the water surface and the created convective circulation patterns in the water basin. The modification raised the effective area of the absorber plate by about one-fifth as compared to the flat baseline configuration and this was accomplished by the well-thought-out surface structures which were not only efficient in heat transfer but also easy to manufacture and did not cause stress concentration. Moreover, both models had the same material, insulation, and glass angle to make sure that the only difference in performance was due to the difference in the absorber plate geometry. The basin and the absorber plate were made of stainless steel 304 of food-grade quality, which was a good choice in terms of thermal conductivity, resistance to corrosion caused by saline environments, strength, and durability. The thickness of the stainless steel was 2 mm and it was sufficient to make the structure rigid and at the same time, not a lot of thermal resistance was created between the heat source and the water. The basin walls were 50 mm high and that was enough to hold saline water of 30 mm depth that was used during the experiment and simulation studies and the height was kept constant so that the thermal mass and the evaporation surface area also remained the same. The glass was made from 4-mm-thick clear silicon with very high solar transmittance of over 0.90 in the visible spectrum thus the solar radiation was almost completely transmitted and, at the same time, the glass was sturdy enough to support its weight and resist wind pressure.

The pyramid shape had four congruent trapezoidal glass pieces converging at one point and each piece made an angle of 32° with the horizontal plane. The inclination angle was chosen so that the maximum solar radiation would be received at the testing location latitude and, at the same time, the condensate could drain well and minimal reflection losses would occur. Glass wool insulation of thickness 50 mm was used for the purpose of insulation from heat losses to the surroundings at the base of the basin and side walls. The insulation served to keep most of the heat received by the system inside the working volume of the system, which was then utilized in the evaporation process. Geometric modeling and computer-aided design

A highly detailed 3D model of both configurations of the solar still was developed through CREO 2016 CAD program due to its excellent solid modeling capacity and ability to accurately perform the geometry required for an effective finite element analysis. The development of the 3D model of the solar still began by first considering the basin geometry, including the absorber plate, the walls of the basin, and the mounting structures. The second component modeled in this case is the pyramid structure of the glass cover. In the conventional design of the solar still, the absorber plate was modeled as a flat rectangular plane measuring 1000 mm x 1000 mm with a uniform thickness of 2 mm. Fillet radii were incorporated at the internal corners where the side walls joined the absorber plate to represent welded connections and eliminate unrealistic stress concentrations caused by sharp geometric edges.

The glass cover was made by having four similar trapezoidal panels each set at an inclination angle of 32° and meeting at the pyramid apex which was 342.43 mm above the basin base for the conventional design. The panel dimensions were determined in such a way that they would not only provide complete coverage of the basin opening but also minimize the material needed.The modified design kept the overall basin dimensions and the glass cover the same as those of the conventional configuration. The only difference was the improved absorber plate geometry. The geometric changes of the absorber plate were well thought out from the point of view of heat transfer and production, having structured patterns that increased the surface area without going too far with complexity which would have made fabrication and cleaning difficult.

One of the main orientations of the enhanced features was to transfer heat vertically from the absorber to the water, at the same time, creating favorable natural convection circulation patterns that evenly distribute the thermal energy throughout the water volume. The different geometric parameters such as feature depth, spacing, width, and orientation were determined through design studies to find the best balance between surface area enlargement and the keeping of structural strength without the formation of stress concentration at geometric transitions. The modification of the absorber plate geometry led to the effective heat transfer surface area which is about 20% higher than that of the flat baseline, made possible by the features extending over the entire plate length and width in orderly patterns. The insulation layers for the two setups were represented as 50-mm-thick areas fully enclosing the bottom and side walls of the basin, with material properties matching glass wool thermal insulation. The form of the insulation was intended to avoid heat bridges and to guarantee a continuous coverage, special attention being paid to the corners where heat losses might be concentrated. Chalking out the assembly relationships between components was done with great care so as to guarantee the right geometrical positioning, realistic clearances, and accurate depiction of thermal contact resistances at the interfaces of the materials.

Besides that, all the geometric models underwent dimensional accuracy checks, correct assembly constraints and were free of geometric inconsistencies that could make mesh generation or finite element analysis result in errors. The finished geometric models were saved in CREO as STEP files, which retain detailed geometric data and at the same time are friendly to ANSYS Workbench for mesh creation and finite element ‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌analysis.

Finite element mesh generation

Mesh​‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌ generation is the process that most influences the outcome of a finite element method, as it determines the solution’s accuracy, time needed for calculation, and convergence. The geometrical data of two types of solar distillers were meshed in this work using ANSYS Meshing by a physics-based preprocessor specially designed for thermal and mechanical stress analysis. A hybrid mesh concept was carried out in which it was possible to use either quadrilateral or hexahedral elements in the morphologically simple parts, while the tetrahedral elements were utilized for the morphologically complicated parts. Such a method guaranteed that where the geometry was strict the accuracy was high and where the geometry was intricate there was enough flexibility, thus the mesh quality and efficiency were at their average levels. A systematic mesh independence study was carried out to ensure solution accuracy. Three levels of mesh refinement were tested. Coarse (about 180, 000 nodes), medium (about 250, 000 nodes), and fine (about 319, 000 nodes for the conventional design). The maximum temperature, peak heat flux, and peak von Mises stress, key solution metrics were tracked through the different refinements. The coarse mesh generated differences of more than 3% when compared to the fine mesh, however the medium and fine meshes matched to within 0.8% for all parameters thereby confirming the mesh, independent results. All the analysis discussed in this study were done on the fine mesh configuration that has been converged. For the conventional solar still, the converged mesh contained about 319, 000 nodes and 1, 446, 000 elements. The changes that added more complicated absorber plate features provoked more localized refinement and thus the mesh of the modified design produced about 210, 000 nodes and 1, 108, 000 elements. Even though the improved one is complicated in shape, it is less detailed in terms of having fewer elements than the original still due to the reduced size of the vapor space, and hence the number of elements is not necessarily a reflection of the geometric complexity. The sizes of the elements were chosen based on consideration of the thermal gradients within the device. The absorber plates and glass cover internal surfaces, which have a significant temperature gradient, were refined to 1 mm. The water basin was given a mesh of intermediate element sizes of 23 mm to keep the level of accuracy while controlling the computation time. For instance, the insulation elements of the water basin were given coarser sizes of around 5 mm because of their lower gradient severity. Points of the structure like the basin and glass cover were selected for corner, edge, and support point refinement where the stress concentrations are expected.

Mesh quality was evaluated against the most common criteria in order to prevent unstable computing processes. The average element quality was over 0.85, and the number of elements with quality less than 0.5 was less than 1%. Most elements’ aspect ratios were kept under 3:1 except in boundary layer regions where higher ratio was needed to capture gradient transitions. The element skewness was less than 0.6 in over 95% of them, while the orthogonal quality measure had a value higher than 0.7 at all locations. Thus, these characteristics confirm that the meshes satisfy the prerequisites of a finite element analysis in terms of stability and precision.

Hence, the zones close to the absorber plate had smaller mesh sizes so that geometric features like contour lines, basin wall intersections, and edges of the glass cover could be precisely captured. Furthermore, inflation layers were used at the solid-fluid interfaces to ensure that the heat transfer mode changes from conduction to convection were correctly represented. Moreover, multizone meshing schemes were adopted to produce structured hexahedral elements inside the basin and absorber areas. This led to optimal per-element precision at minimal computational cost. All the mesh configurations, including the number of elements, quality measures, and sizing parameters, were saved for further use.

Material properties specification

Another important factor in achieving credible results from the thermal and structural finite element analysis is the precise representation of material properties since they have a major influence on the behavior of heat conduction, thermal capacitance, expansion, and load response. Materials of which the pyramid solar still model consists—metal stainless steel, glass, glass wool insulation, water, and air—are actual materials used in engineering, selected based on references and manufacturers’ information for their behavior to be realistic. Stainless steel 304 was chosen as an absorber and basin material due to its corrosion resistance and good thermal and mechanical properties after heating by the Sun. The transparent cover was represented by standard glass with high optical transmissivity to allow as much solar energy input as possible. Glass wool insulation was wrapped around the basin to cut down on heat losses to the environment. Water and air-vapor mixture in the vapor space were given temperature-averaged thermophysical properties to be representative of typical operating conditions (40-60°C). In order to speed up calculations, all materials were considered temperature-independent within the temperature range of the experiment since the variation of properties in this range is quite small and does not have a significant effect on the accuracy of the simulation. Tables 1 and 2 present the summary of the material properties which were used in the thermal and structural ​‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌analyses. In the present study, the following simplifying assumptions were made it was assumed that all thermal and mechanical material properties are temperature, independent in the working temperature range of 4060 °C, since variations in properties within this range are less than 5% and have negligible effect on comparative results. The assumption of steady, state was made, which is suitable for peak solar irradiance performance evaluation; it was assumed that insulated boundaries are adiabatic, which is consistent with the low thermal conductivity (0.04 W/mK) and sufficient thickness (50 mm) of the glass, wool insulation; a uniform solar irradiance distribution is assumed over the absorber surface; only one, way thermal, structural coupling is considered, which is sufficiently explained by the structural deformations being negligible in magnitude relative to the system dimensions.

Boundary conditions and loading specifications

Accurately specifying the boundary conditions is vital if the finite element results are to be of any value, as these are the conditions that define the real environment in which the solar still works. Thermal and structural boundary conditions in this work were set according to normal operating conditions, verified correlations, and well-known practices in the analysis of solar thermal systems. As far as the thermal steady-state investigation is concerned, the main source of heat has been simulated as a consistent heat flux of 800 W/m2, which has been applied to the outer surface of the stainless-steel absorber plate. This figure originates from the application of peak solar irradiance under clear sky (about 900, 1000 W/m2) to the absorptivity of the absorber surface which is around 0.90, thus, the effective absorbed flux reaching the peak daytime condition is 800 W/m2. This boundary condition has been extensively used in similar studies that are solar stills based on FEA and it gives a very precise, easily reproducible method for direct performance comparison between the traditional and the modified designs. Sensitivity analysis showed that the relative performance trends are kept at solar flux levels ranging from 600 to 1000 W/m2. The heat loss through the outside surface of the glass cover was simulated using a combination of both convection and radiation models. The value for convective heat transfer coefficient was taken as 14 W/m2·K, taking into account the contributions from both natural convection and wind-induced convection over an inclined surface with ambient temperature held constant at 25°C (298 K). The value for radiative losses was calculated assuming the emissivity of the glass cover to be 0.90, radiating into the sky.

The inner surface of the glass cover, where condensation occurs, was thermally coupled with the vapor region to represent the combined effects of convective heat transfer and latent heat release during condensation. The insulation outer surfaces were given adiabatic conditions in reflection of the minimal heat that escapes through the insulation layer made of low thermal conductivity and sufficiently thick. This assumption slightly increases the amount of heat considered to be retained but the effect on the design comparison is absent since the same insulation is used for both configurations. The whole 3D shape was built without considering symmetry restrictions to provide a correct representation of heat transfer and vapor-space asymmetry that are the characteristics of the pyramid design. None of the components was pre-heated, but this initial state has no influence on the steady-state findings. In structural terms, the first step was to import the temperature fields obtained from the thermal solution as thermal loads that will be used to compute thermally induced stresses and deformations. Such a one-way sequential coupling is suitable here because structural deformations are small and do not significantly affect heat transfer. To resemble rigid mounting points, the basin structure was equipped with fixed supports at the bottom corners, thus it was allowed only thermal expansion elsewhere; Hydrostatic pressure relating to 30 mm water depth was applied to the basin surfaces, but the resulting stresses were very small compared to the thermal effects, thus negligible. The glass cover was under its self-weight; however, the thermal loading was the main reason for its response, not mechanical forces.

ANSYS computational methodology and solution procedures

The computational investigation was implemented with ANSYS Workbench 2025 R1, which offered a unified platform for geometry manipulation, meshing, physics setup, solution, and post-processing. A sequential strategy was employed in which steady-state thermal analysis was succeeded by static structural analysis with thermal loading, thus constituting a one-way coupled Multiphysics simulation. This decision was supported by the fact that expected thermal deformations were negligible compared to the overall size of the geometry, thereby structural deflections would not have a considerable effect on heat transfer or boundary conditions. The steady-state thermal analysis performed the three-dimensional heat conduction equation for all the solid parts. For isotropic materials without internal heat generation, the equation is simplified to the divergence of thermal conductivity multiplied by the temperature gradient being zero. The governing equations were discretized using the finite element method on a mesh composed of tetrahedral and hexahedral elements, resulting in a system of linear algebraic equations relating the nodal temperatures. After applying the boundary conditions, the steady-state temperature distribution across the entire domain was obtained. A sparse direct solver was implemented, and convergence was considered to be achieved when the maximum temperature change between iterations was less than 1×10⁻⁶ K. Even though a limit of 500 iterations was set, convergence was generally reached much earlier due to the stability of the thermal problem. The solver generated nodal temperatures, heat flux vectors, thermal gradients, and integrated heat transfer quantities. The temperature field acquired was then used as thermal loading in the static structural module. A structural analysis was performed by solving the equilibrium equations while accounting for thermal expansion, where the thermal strain was calculated as the product of the coefficient of thermal expansion and the temperature rise. For the linear elastic materials used in the study, the stress–strain relationship followed the generalized form of Hooke’s law.

The finite element implementation closely resembled the thermal one, with the stiffness matrices being constructed and solved given the displacement constraints corresponding to the fixed supports. The structural solver was using a Newton Raphson algorithm with a convergence tolerance of 110 mm for the displacement change.

Large, deflection options were disabled as the size of thermal deformations was sufficiently small to justify linear assumptions. The analysis outputted nodal displacements, strain components, stress components, principal stresses, von Mises stress, and safety factor distributions. Postprocessing was carried out using the ANSYS Workbench results module, which provided comprehensive picture, based representations of temperature fields, heat flux vectors, stress contours, strain distributions, and deformation patterns. In addition to the visual results, numerical data related to extreme values, local averages, and integrated heat transfer were also obtained, enabling a detailed comparison between the conventional and modified solar still configurations.

Solution verification and quality assurance

In order to validate the numerical results, a number of steps were carried out. First of all, the mesh independence analysis was done by a series of refinements until the difference in the maximum temperature, heat flux, and stresses between the successive meshes became less than 1%, showing that the solution was convergent and the model no longer depended on mesh density. Verification of the energy conservation was achieved by summing up the total heat gained by the system and comparing it with the combined loss of heat caused by convection, radiation, insulation, and evaporation. The result of these two quantities comparison was satisfactory since the difference was within numerical precision, demonstrating that energy is conserved throughout the solution. Furthermore, the convergence during iterations was checked for each parameter in the residual history and the residuals were monotonically decreasing until convergence to predefined criteria was achieved. This implies no oscillations or divergences occurred and hence the solution procedure is numerically stable and the problem is formulated correctly. Physical verifications of temperature fields and heat flux vectors included checking their behavior in compliance with the expected trends; the maximum temperatures occurring in the absorber plate and minimum temperatures occurring in the glass cover facing ambient air were observed. In addition, the heat flux vectors were shown to have the direction going from high temperatures to low temperatures, as expected. As for the structural outputs, the stresses demonstrated reasonable distributions with their maximums occurring near discontinuities and support boundaries and the magnitudes corresponded to thermal loading.

The stability of the solutions was investigated by the sensitivity analysis of boundary conditions variations. The changes in convective heat transfer coefficient values were within the uncertainty range and the value of the solar heat flux was changed according to realistic variations in irradiance. Material property values, such as thermal conductivities, specific heat capacities, and coefficients of thermal expansion, were varied within their ranges of uncertainties, and as a result the predicted performance showed no variation, which means the conclusions made concerning the comparative analysis of designs were valid even when material properties varied. Finally, some simple analytical solutions to similar problems were compared to numerical results for heat conduction problem in 1D case using Fourier’s law and the thermal expansion of a plate under heating. The results were almost the same proving that governing equations were solved correctly.

Results and discussion

Temperature distribution analysis

The temperature profile impacts the evaporation-condensation process in pyramid solar distillers. The classical design and the innovative design of pyramid solar distiller displayed the common temperature profile in which the temperatures were maximum in the absorber plate, intermediate in the water and vapor regions, and minimum in the outer surface of the glass covering that was in contact with the surrounding environment. In the classical pyramid solar distiller design, the absorber plate attained its maximum temperature of 338.15 K, while the outer surface of the glass cover attained its minimum temperature of 298.82 K. These values are consistent with the heat input at the basin and the heat dissipation through the glass cover. The modified design, which incorporated an enhanced absorber with a larger surface area, increased the maximum temperature to 342.91 K, corresponding to an improvement of 4.76 K (1.41%). Figure 5 presents the steady-state temperature distribution of the conventional solar still, clearly indicating that the maximum absorber temperature attained was 338.15 K. The observed thermal gradient also confirms the evaporation–condensation cycle within the system.

Depicts the steady-state temperature distribution throughout the conventional solar still configuration.

The rise in temperature was caused by better solar energy absorption and better heat transfer from the absorber plate to the water body. The minimum temperature in the glass cover stayed almost constant at around 298 K, which means that the changes made in the device affected mostly the heat transfer process occurring in the basin area, and not the losses to the surroundings. In comparison to the unmodified device, the water temperature in the basin area exhibited stronger variation along its height, which means better heat transfer upwards. Higher evaporation was enabled by reducing the lateral heat dissipation and increasing the amount of energy absorbed that went to the phase change. Meanwhile, the water in the conventional design was more or less at the same temperature levels and the vertical heat transport was slow. Moreover, the altered system resulted in a significant increase of vapor-space temperatures, with an average of 325 K to 320 K in the standard set-up. The 5 K increment causes the saturation vapor pressure and the evaporation driving force to rise through the Clausius–Clapeyron relation. Besides, the difference in temperature between vapor and the cooler glass interior surface becomes larger, which is beneficial for condensation. The temperature distribution of the modified design is shown in Fig. 6, which depicts the increasing of the maximum temperature of the absorber by 4.76 K as well as the stronger vertical thermal gradient obtained by changing the geometry of the absorber plate.

Exhibits the temperature distribution in the modified design, showing the resulting of enhanced absorber geometry.

The absorber surface temperature patterns served as another example of the differences in designs. Temperature-wise, there was nearly no variation in the flat conventional absorber with the edge temperature being slightly lower. Temperature spikes on the modified absorber were correlated with the geometrical features of the shape; however, the general uniformity was still maintained and there were no overly concentrated hot spots, which means that the most balanced evaporation was taking place without the appearance of strong thermal gradients. In both designs, glass-cover temperature distributions were very similar and the interior temperatures were ranging from 310–305 K (conventional) and 312–307 K (modified). Figure 7 presents a comparison of the vertical temperature profiles as well as vapor space conditions between the two designs, which illustrates that the modified configuration results in an average vapor, space temperature that is 5 K higher. This directly increases the saturation vapor pressure and, consequently, the condensation driving force.

Draws a comparison between the vertical temperature profiles and vapor space conditions for both designs.

The vapors in the altered system were hotter which can be one of the reasons why the glass-cover temperatures were also higher. The outer glass temperatures were very close to the ambient ones as a result of convection–radiation cooling that was very effective and glass being very thin. The drop in temperature from 7 to 10 K across the glass was a clear indication that vapor-side convective resistance is mainly responsible for condensation behavior and not conduction through the glass. The insulation performance was equally good in both models as shown by the 30–35 K temperature drops over the 50 mm glass-wool insulation layer which indicates less parasitic heat losses. A temperature difference of approximately 30–35 K was observed across the 50 mm glass wool insulation layer, confirming that parasitic heat losses were effectively minimized in both configurations.

Heat flux distribution and energy transfer analysis

Heat​‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌ flux distribution is one of the most direct ways of understanding how solar energy is transferred thermally to the solar still. It identifies the regions where the main heat transfer occurs as well as the efficiency of the overall design. In the conventional pyramid solar still, the maximum heat flux reached 809.97 W/m2 at the absorber–water interface. This region represented the primary location where the absorbed solar energy was transferred from the absorber plate to the saline water through conduction and natural convection. The lowest values of flux, i.e., those that were close to 0.034 W/m2, were recorded in well-insulated areas, thus indicating that there were virtually no heat losses in these places where the temperature differences were small. The heat flux distribution displayed a pronounced central concentration in which the values gradually lowered as they moved from the basin towards the walls due to the decrease of the driving forces of thermal energy in the areas close to the insulated walls. Figure 9 shows the heat flux distribution and vector field in the conventional solar still and indicates that the maximum flux concentration of 809.97 W/m2 occurs at the central absorber, water interface. It is also visually confirmed that most of the heat transfer is in the vertical direction.

Reveals the heat flux extent and the vector distributions in a conventional solar still throughout the night.

In comparison, the alteration in the absorber geometry had a significant effect on the device with the highest heat flux value reaching 936.84 W/m2 which means there was a 15.66% increase of the peak heat flux in the modified still as compared to the conventional one. Area exposure alone and the local flow disturbances due to the geometric features are only the reasons for this change.

Uniform flux distribution over the absorber was one of the most outstanding benefits of the improved geometry that not only made the spreading of the thermal energy easier but also prevented the heating in the limited central area. The study of the heat flux vector gave more information about the difference of energy in the two devices. Almost all the heat flux vectors of the flat conventional absorber were vertical up pointing, and this was consistent with unidirectional conduction and natural convection caused by buoyancy. The vectors of the modified design had the evidence of side components and three, dimensional paths because of the geometrical surface features. These multidirectional vectors indicate that local mixing is enhanced and thermal transport is more effective and can occur in the whole water volume.

The heat flux measured at the evaporator was the tool used for measuring the latent heat, which was responsible for producing the steam. The average evaporation heat flux from the modified solar still was about 450 W/m2 compared to about 385 W/m2 in the conventional solar still, a difference of 16.9%. Exhibits the heightened heat flux patterns achieved through the geometric modifications.

The rise in productive heat transfer was associated with elevated basin temperatures, vertical temperature gradients, and better convective mixing, which facilitated the production and subsequent evacuation of the vapor from the interface. At the inside of the glass cover, which represented the area for condensation, the heat transfer was estimated at 250 to 350 W/m2 in both cases. In the modified case, the higher value was attained as a result of higher temperatures of the vapor produced. The maximum condensation heat flux occurred near the lower edges of the glass cover, where the vapor temperature was highest, and gradually decreased toward the apex. There were parasitic heat losses through the 50 mm insulation in both setups that were less than 50 W/m2 which is confirmation that the insulation was in good condition and was keeping the unproductive heat transfer at a minimum. It appeared that the changes in the insulation of the still led to an increase of about 8% in losses, which can be traced back to the higher general temperature of the still during operation. Nevertheless, the small increment in insulation losses is dwarfed by a 15–17% rise in evaporative flux leading to a net increase in thermal efficiency. Productive heat-utilization efficiency has been elevated from 68% (conventional) to 72% (modified). The local examination of the areas with the enhanced absorber features has unveiled 20–30% local heat-flux increments, thus confirming the performance improvement mechanisms that include a larger surface area, boundary-layer disruption, better convection, and the generated secondary flow structures. As a result, a more efficient thermal environment for the process of evaporation and, consequently, for the overall system output, has been established by these ‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌occurrences.

Structural analysis: equivalent stress distribution

Equivalent​‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌ von Mises stress analysis was performed to a pair of solar stills to judge how their structures would respond to the heat-loaded-induced conditions. The dominant contributive factor to the arising of the stresses was the restriction of thermal expansion of the stainless-steel parts due to temperature increase during operation. It was the results that gave them a clue as to the materials structural integrity, failure locations and long-term durability. The maximum Von Mises stress in the existing pyramid-type solar still design was raised up to 1.7348 MPa, and most of this stress was located near the joints between the absorber plate and the side walls of the basin. The joints were not only areas of geometry change but also conditions where the heat expansion could not be released.

The minimum stress values that were close to zero could be found in the central areas of the absorber and glass cover, where thermal gradients were negligible, and structures were less constrained. In short, the conventional model revealed normal stress patterns at corners, edges, and transition regions where displacement compatibility dominated. The maximum stress in the modified solar still was just a bit more than that in the conventional one and amounted to 2.0679 MPa, thus, it was 19.2% higher than in the conventional configuration. Stress maxima were at the same geometric points thus the stress sources were still the same. The rise in stress value was directly proportional to the higher temperature achieved in the modified design since the thermal stress depends on Young’s modulus, thermal expansion coefficient, and temperature rise. However, the stresses were very low as compared to the material capacity. Stainless steel 304 at 70°C exhibits a yield strength of around 180 MPa. So the highest stress of 2.07 MPa was only 1.15% of the yield strength, which corresponds to the factor of safety being greater than 85, thus demonstrating great structural reliability. Figure 11 exhibits the distribution of the equivalent von Mises stress for the conventional solar still, with the highest stress of 1.73 MPa being localized at absorber, wall junctions Also, the stress concentration at this location is only 0.96% of the material yield strength, implying a factor of safety of more than 85.

Von Mises stress distribution for the conventionally designed solar still under thermal loading.

Both designs remained in the elastic range during operation, so the risk of plastic deformation was excluded even under repeated daily heating–cooling cycles. Fatigue issues were very small because stress amplitudes (roughly 2 MPa) were less than 3% of the fatigue endurance limit for austenitic stainless steel (40–50% of yield strength). Hence, long-term durability over thousands of cycles was guaranteed. The analysis of stresses also revealed that the thermal stresses were mainly in-plane membrane stresses as the basin walls were thin (2 mm) and the temperature was almost uniformly distributed through the thickness. Very few bending stresses were involved, so there was no risk of buckling, warping, or excessively large out-of-plane deformations. The magnitude of the maximum principal stresses was almost equal to that of the equivalent stresses, which indicates proportional multi-axial loading without complex non-proportional effects. Maximum shear stresses were roughly half of the von Mises stresses, which agrees with the theoretical expectations of thermally induced deviatoric stress states. Local stress concentration at the corner and thickness change led to two to three times the local amplification; however, this was still well below the allowable limits. Figure 12 demonstrates the von Mises stress distribution in the redesigned model, which confirms that the 19.2% rise to 2.07 MPa due to increased operating temperatures is still a very long way from failure at only 1.15% of the yield strength.

Depicts the stress distribution in the altered design, revealing similar patterns with slightly increased magnitudes.

Even without filleting, smoother transitions, or redistributed constraints, a further reduction could be achieved, however, the present geometry did not require any changes. In summary, the analysis proved that the enhanced heat performance of the adjusted design did not come at the expense of structural safety, hence the design is fit for long, term practical operation.

Total deformation characteristics under thermal loading

Total deformation analysis was done to determine displacements resulting from thermal expansion of both solar still configurations. The maximum deformation of the conventional pyramid solar still was 0.026766 mm, which was made at the center of the absorber plate where the temperature was the highest and thermal expansion was unconstrained. The minimum deformation along the basin perimeter was because the fixed boundary conditions prevented the movement. The deformation pattern indicated that the hot central area was expanding evenly in all directions towards the cooler edges, which is consistent with the classical thermal expansion behavior that is governed by the temperature increase and the distance from the constraints. The maximum deformation of the modified solar still was 0.031879 mm which was 19.1% more than the conventional one. The increase in deformation was due to the higher temperatures of the absorber plate in the modified configuration. Nevertheless, the displacement remained very minuscule only about 0.0032% for a 1m characteristic dimensions, there was no chance of geometric distortion, component interference, or functional degradation. Thus, no change occurred in the shape of the absorber plate, and the deformations were not substantial enough to be visible to the naked eye. According to the deformation gradient calculations, there were no discontinuities in the displacement distribution in both systems, implying that the connected elements could not have been mismatched with each other. The maximum deformations were registered at the connection of the heated absorber plate with the cold basin walls, but these deformations did not lead to the aforementioned strains apart from the strains detected within the process of the stress analysis. The deformations were even, and all the components were compatible with one another.

The deformation of the glass covers for both designs was insignificant and amounted to no more than 0.01 mm, which was caused by their lower temperature and greater stiffness in comparison with the steel basins. This can be explained by the fact that their geometrical parameters, optical properties, and condensation surface inclination angles remained constant during the analysis. The small extent of deformation enabled maintaining solar transmissivity and condensate collection efficiency. Additionally, the team looked at differential thermal expansion between various materials. Stainless steel (α = 17.3×10⁻⁶/K) and glass (α = 9.0×10⁻⁶/K) had different expansion rates. Relative displacement between steel and glass for a typical 1 m dimension with a temperature rise of 45 K would be close to 0.037 mm. There were enough clearances and flexible high-temperature silicone seals incorporated in the design to allow this differential movement without any compromise in water-tightness or structural integrity. While cyclic loading was not directly represented, the very small elastic deformations along with the low thermal stress levels suggest that the risk of fatigue is almost non-existent. The ranges of stress and deformation are well below the endurance limits for stainless steel during a 20-year period (around 7,300 thermal cycles), thus, no crack initiation or cumulative deformation can occur. In short, deformation analysis is the main reason why the two designs, especially the thermally enhanced modified configuration, are considered to be of excellent geometric stability and structural strength in the ​‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌long-term.

Thermal strain development and characterization

Thermal​‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌ strain analysis was used to measure the dimensional changes of the solar stills due to temperature changes and was the main source of thermal stress when the system was under restraint. In the case of the conventional pyramid solar still, the highest thermal strain was 2.6766×10⁻5 (26.766 micro strain) at the center of the absorber plate, the point which corresponded to the area of the greatest temperature increase from the ambient. Almost zero thermal strain was found in the closest to the ambient areas of temperature. The thermal strain maps followed the temperature pattern very closely, thus proving the constitutive relation εth = αΔT and confirming that the thermal expansion was the main source of the strain field, while the mechanical strains due to water pressure or self-weight were negligible. In the case of the modified solar still, the maximum thermal strain was even higher, at 3.1879×10⁻5 (31.879 micro strain), which is by about 19.1% higher than that of the conventional one and can be directly linked to the increased temperature of the absorber plate. Using stainless steel expansion coefficient of 17.3×10⁻⁶/K and a temperature rise of 44.67 K, the analytical estimation gave 3.07×10⁻5 for the strain, which was very close to the FEA result within approximately 4%. The points of agreement revealed that the numerical model was a good reflection of the thermal expansion behavior and that thermal effects accounted for the major part of the total strain. Both configurations had thermal strain gradients that were neither too high nor too steep, with the maximum strain gradient being about 310 per meter, which is consistent with the relatively low gradients in the thermal gradient regime. Figure 13 illustrates total deformation contours in the traditional design, establishing a maximum displacement of 0.026766 mm (0.0027% of the 1 m characteristic dimension) that is negligible, does not impact geometric function or condensation efficiency.

Shows the total deformation contours due to thermal expansion in the conventional design."

They did not contain sudden rupture or steep gradient and thus thermal expansion was progressing evenly and no localized incompatibilities or excessive internal stresses beyond the usual geometric constraint effects were created. The mechanical strain magnitudes due to hydrostatic water pressure (approximately 300 Pa) were compared to those of thermal strains and it was found that the former were on the order of 10⁻⁸, which is about three orders of magnitude smaller than the latter (10⁻5). It agrees that thermal loading, not structural loading, is the main factor to be considered in the design, and as such, it must be the primary design consideration. The thermal expansion mismatch between stainless steel (α = 17.3×10⁻⁶/K) and glass (α = 9×10⁻⁶/K) was analyzed to guarantee the compatibility of the interface. Austenitic stainless steel (SS304) essentially does not experience any creep or time, dependent deformation at temperatures below 100C, hence thermal strains are kept fully elastic. There will not be any lasting deformation or change in dimensions over a period of 20 years since there is a return to the original dimensions in each cycle, amounting to 7,300 cycles, thus making the component geometrically stable in the long run.

Comparative performance assessment

In this context, a thorough analysis of the performance parameters relevant to both energy and materials aspects has been carried out for normal as well as modified pyramid solar still systems. Consequently, the numeric analysis proved the effect of the absorber plate geometry due to its geometric optimization. It was found that the new system surpassed the old system in all thermal performance indices, whereas the physical attributes of the new design were still within acceptable range, with appropriate safety margins. The maximum temperature difference increased to 4.76 K (1.41%). This increase in the maximum temperature implies that the conditions for heat transfer were improved. As a result, more saturation vapor pressure and thus higher driving force were created, which facilitated the evaporation process. Moreover, the maximum heat flux increased to 126.87 W/m2 (15.66%), which indicates a better condition for heat transfer between the absorber plate and the water at the basin, thereby improving thermal energy utilization for evaporation. Additionally, the average heat flux difference at the critical water interface level increased to about 65 W/m2 (16.9%). Figure 15 shows the thermal stress in the conventional solar still. The maximum strain of 26.77 micro, strain is located at the absorber center. This result confirms that thermal expansion is the dominant loading mechanism and also validates the constitutive relationship εth = αΔT.

Thermal efficiency was calculated by taking the ratio of the productive evaporative heat transfer to the total solar energy input. It was shown that the modified design had an efficiency of about 72% as compared to 68% of the conventional configuration, thus indicating an increase of four percentage points. Such an efficiency gain meant that the geometric alterations allowed making more effective use of the absorbed solar energy for the desalination process, which is a productive use, rather than dissipating the energy for insulation or other unproductive paths. The improvements in thermal performance did not require additional energy inputs, rare materials, complicated auxiliary systems, or significant cost increases in manufacturing; therefore, the modified design remains economically feasible for the practical applications of the solar still.

Analysis of the structural performance indicators proved that improvement in thermal performance led to slightly higher thermal stresses and deformations, however, from the perspectives of structural integrity and durability, these were still totally normal. The highest equivalent stress was up by 0.333 MPa (19.2%), the greatest total deformation went up by 0.005 mm (19.1%), and peak thermal strain was 0.512 micro strain (19.1%) more. These changes were closely connected to the temperature rise and indicated the basic interaction between the thermal and mechanical behavior through the thermal expansion mechanisms. Even with these increases, all the structural metrics were still way below the critical levels, yielding safety factors being over 85, deformations accounting for less than 0.004% of the component dimensions, and strain levels being well within the elastic region allowing for complete dimensional recovery upon cooling. The trade-off effects of improving the thermal performance at the expense of increased structural loading were very positive, as the great thermal enhancements were achieved at the cost of only minor structural changes that were still safely within the allowable range. The geometric changes not only improved the heat transfer efficiency but also did not weaken the structure, thus providing proof that the modified design could be implemented in practice.

On the cost-benefit side of things, the economics looked good as the geometric improvements could be carried out by normal machining or forming operations with a cost increment of about 5–10% while the thermal performance could be improved by 15–20% which would directly result in an increase in distillate production rate. The increased output would be the reason for the modest additional investment through the lower amortized cost per liter of distilled water produced during the system lifetime. The performance predictions under different operating conditions such as varying solar radiation intensities, ambient temperatures, water depths, and environmental factors showed that the relative benefits of the modified design over the conventional baseline remained unchanged for the different realistic operational envelopes. Such parametric studies revealed that the 15–17% improvements in heat flux were maintained for solar radiation levels ranging from 600 to 1000 W/m2, ambient temperatures from 20 to 40°C, and water depths from 20 to 50 mm.

Experimental validation

The computational analysis of finite element method presented in this paper provides an adequate explanation of the thermal and structural performance of various solar still designs (both normal and modified) based on the assumed working conditions. However, to validate the mathematical models developed, experimental values need to be compared against the computational outcomes. Moreover, through such comparisons, the researchers can validate their assumptions made during the development process, identify any possible sources of error, and prove that the performance attained through simulations is indeed practical.

Experimental facility and instrumentation

First of all, the authors of the present study built physical models of both standard and modified pyramid solar stills with exact attention paid to the geometry, materials, and dimensions which were used in the computational models. This was done to ensure that the behavior obtained through the experiments could be directly compared to that predicted by the numerical models. In making the prototypes, it was used the same stainless steel 304 material for basins and absorber plates, the same glass for transparent covers, and equivalent insulation materials and thicknesses. Highly trained fabricating technicians carried out all the manufacturing processes under continuous quality monitoring, recording the measurements of dimensional accuracy and surface finish throughout.

Both prototypes were highly instrumented for the thorough measurement of the most important thermal parameters that the computational analysis anticipated. The temperature readings were done using type-K thermocouples, which were accurate to ±0.5°C or even higher, and placed strategically in various locations in the test apparatus. Temperature differences across the absorber plate surface were determined using thermocouples placed at various locations along the surface of the absorber plate. Thermocouples placed at various depths from the surface of the water basin were used to determine the temperature profile in the water basin. The vapor zone was measured using thermocouples located between the surface of the water basin and the glass cover, and also temperatures of the inner and outer sides of the glass cover, responsible for condensing heat, as illustrated in Fig. 17.

Pressure contour results.

A formal uncertainty analysis was carried out in accordance with the procedure of Kline and McClintock (1953). Type, K thermocouples (accuracy 0.5 °C) were checked against a NIST, traceable reference standard before each test series. Heat flux sensors (accuracy 3% of reading) were calibrated with a radiation source of known intensity. The pyranometer (accuracy 2%) fulfilled ISO 9060 Class B specifications. The total uncertainty in distillate production, considering balance resolution (0.1 g), timing uncertainty (1 s), and basin area measurement (0.5%), was 2.3%. Carrying all individual instrument uncertainties through the efficiency calculation, the total uncertainty in the computed thermal efficiency was 4.1%. The differences measured between computational predictions and experimental results temperature: 2.32.7 °C; heat flux: 6.2, 7.4%; distillate production: 1.8, 2.9% not only exceed their corresponding measurement uncertainties but also validate that the reported performance enhancements are statistically significant.

Heat flux measurements were done via heat flux sensors which were well calibrated and had an accuracy of ±3% of the reading or better. These sensors were positioned at the most important places from where the authors wanted to derive the data. These places were, for example, the absorber plate surface where solar radiation absorption was measured, the water surface interface where evaporative heat transfer was quantified, and the glass cover interior surface for condensation heat flux. As for monitoring solar radiation intensity, a precision pyranometer with an accuracy of ±2% was employed that would document the main thermal boundary condition and would also allow the performance to be correlated with different solar input levels as shown in Fig. 18. Distillate production also became the matter on which the scientific team concentrated. And the most precise method to determine it would be gravimetrically by means of precision electronic balances with 0.1 g resolution that were connected to data acquisition systems for continuous recording.

The sum of distilled water that was obtained at the experimental time intervals was used as a total measure of the system’s working condition and its thermal efficiency; hence, it was an excellent complement to the local point measurements of temperature and heat flux.

Conclusion

Both computational and experimental analyses have been carried out in respect of the two types of the pyramid solar still under consideration, conventional and modified designs. Finite element analysis helped gain a deeper insight into the thermal and structural characteristics of the systems and contributed to their optimization. Three-dimensional models were developed with the use of the ANSYS Workbench package taking into account the actual geometry, material properties, and boundary conditions of the systems under investigation. The steady-state thermal analysis allowed analyzing temperature distribution patterns and heat flux distribution, assessing the heat dissipation rate. Static structural analysis helped find stress distribution, displacement, and strain distribution and determine how the thermal effects affect the structure. The comparison of the two models made it possible to assess how much improvement in performance could be attained with the optimized geometry of the absorber plates while keeping the system’s structural reliability sufficient enough. The modified system has shown definite thermal advantages over the original one. As compared to the baseline, the maximum operating temperature is higher by 4.76 K (1.41%), the maximum heat flux is higher by 126.87 W/m2 (15.66%), the average heat flux at the water surface is higher by 65 W/m2 (16.9%), which means better evaporation rate and higher distillate generation capacity. The thermal efficiency rose from 68% to 72%. That could be explained by the fact that the effective heat transfer surface is 20% larger, and the structured features of the design help in addition to the three-dimensional heat flow also improve natural convection, i.e., the vertical heat transfer to the water surface becomes more efficient, and temperature non-uniformities are avoided.

Structurally, the two models have shown similar high reliability rates as well. The maximum equivalent stress in the modified design was 2.068 MPa; however, it is still very far away from the yield strength of the stainless steel 304 used in the design. The maximum displacement in the model was less than 0.004% of the component dimensions. Thermal strains are within the elastic limit and will not lead to plastic deformation; therefore, there is nothing to worry about in terms of the dimensional stability of the design under repeated daily cycles. Experimentally obtained data has proven to be very accurate and reliable. Temperature difference did not exceed 5%; similarly, the heat flux values were within 8% accuracy limit. Finally, experiments also proved that the predicted 18% improvement in productivity has actually occurred in the modified system. Therefore, both computation-based analysis and the modification design prove their applicability and reliability. It is possible to achieve great performance enhancement of a pyramid solar still simply by changing its geometry without compromising structural reliability and having no additional complexities of any kind. The modified model became possible because of a rise of heat transfer efficiency by 15, 17%. This means very high potential in terms of the cost-benefit ratio since minimal changes in the manufacturing costs may be fully compensated with productivity increase.

These numerical findings provide definite, convincing pointers for the next design and are also a stepping, stone for systematic parametric optimization. The main limitations are the steady, state thermal analysis, oversimplified method for vapor space, uniform heat flux assumptions, and temperature, independent material properties. Further research that combines transient thermal analysis, temperature, dependent material properties, and advanced multiphase modeling of the evaporation, condensation phenomena can contribute to increasing the accuracy of performance prediction results of the present computational framework and also extend its applicability to a wide range of operating conditions. Certainly, this paper offers comprehensive explanations of thermal and structural behavior, it identifies the mechanisms of improvements, and it validates the computational methodologies that it used. The adoption of the modified design may be a way to provide clean water in the sun-rich but water-short regions of the ‍​‌‍​‍‌​‍​‌‍​‍‌world.

Future scope

The present study identifies several promising directions for future research that can further advance the understanding and performance of pyramid solar still technology. A first-principle advanced multiphase CFD model of phase change phenomena involving coupling of energy, momentum, and mass conservation equations with interfacial tracking and reaction-rate kinetics could more fundamentally represent evaporation, condensation, vapor transport, droplet formation, and drainage, while coupled species transport and discrete phase modeling could provide insight into the mass transfer limitations in the solar still and the possibilities for performance enhancements. Transient thermal analysis would be able to record time-dependent behavior over entire daily solar cycles thereby quantifying thermal inertia, warm-up times, and thermal storage effects so that basin materials, geometry, and phase change integration could be optimized for extended productivity. Artificial intelligence and machine learning can hugely accelerate performance prediction, design optimization, and adaptive control. Regression models and neural networks trained on computational datasets can predict performance over wide design spaces at a fraction of the computational cost. Optimization by means of artificial intelligence based multi-objective optimization using evolutionary algorithms and reinforcement learning may help find the best combination of geometrical, material, and operation parameters that meet the objectives of thermal efficiency, structural soundness, producibility, and cost-effectiveness. It would make it possible to investigate much bigger sets of configurations compared to what can be done using parametric studies traditionally.

Parametric optimization of the geometry of an absorber plate through a design of experiment, response surface methodology, and surrogate model development might reveal configurations with maximum heat transfer while ensuring sufficient structural strength and doing it very efficiently. Sensitivity analysis will reveal the most influential variables and therefore, will identify the ones that should be considered in the experiment and the design process. Using multi-objective optimization, one could find the right balance between thermal efficiency, structural reliability, manufacturing cost, and simplicity of operations. Advanced materials and surface treatments could contribute further to increasing the performance of the solar collectors. Special selective absorber coatings, having good absorptance under sunlight and minimum thermal emissivity, could provide extra energy gains. Super hydrophilic nanostructured surfaces on glass covers are able to enhance film, wise condensation and fast drainage, whereas super hydrophobic coatings may reduce condensate retention and provide a self, cleaning effect. Phase change materials incorporated in basin structures can be used as thermal energy storage, thus, enabling the productive operation to be extended even after the daylight hours.

Nanofluids with enhanced thermophysical properties might increase the heat transfer rates in the whole system. A computational model can be a helpful tool for the experimental advancement of these novel materials by predicting the performance increases and identifying the optimal implementation methods. Hybrid systems consisting of pyramid stills and other solar technologies can have a higher overall productivity and efficiency. The coupling with flat, plate or evacuated tube collectors can be utilized to preheat feed water, thereby increasing the evaporation rates. Integration with photovoltaics can be a source of electric power for running active circulation pumps or humidity sensors. A solar pond may supply thermal energy as a backup during days with low solar radiation. Waste heat recovery from industrial processes can be the way that enables the operation throughout the whole year in temperate climates. Multi, effect arrangements can be leveraged for achieving higher water recovery ratios as well as thermal efficiency gains.

Scale-up studies could look into structural issues, manufacturing challenges, and modular design options to address larger or community scale solar still applications. An economic assessment study including the cost of production, operating expenses, lifetime output, and market opportunities will aid in optimizing techno-economics and facilitate commercialization of this technology. In addition, a lifecycle assessment methodology could be used to assess environmental impacts and sustainability performance. Field tests conducted in varying weather and environmental conditions could provide additional information on performance, structural stability, and user acceptability.

Smart monitoring technologies connected with IoT will allow real-time performance analysis and control as well as predictive maintenance. The concept of digital twins could be used in conjunction with computer modeling and sensors for model update and optimization of performance in real time. Machine learning algorithms could provide optimal control settings depending on environmental conditions and increase productivity while maintaining safety. Local training and capacity-building programs for communities would help design, manufacture, operate, and maintain solar stills using local materials and resources.

Open-source platforms providing reliable design tools, manufacturing recommendations, and operation manuals will serve as effective technology transfer tools. Apart from that, demonstration projects implemented in water-scarce areas will help show the performance of solar stills under realistic conditions and will be used as a community capacity-building instrument. All this is part of the comprehensive strategy not only for computer aided design and development of a simple solar still technology but also for its validated prototyping, optimization, feasibility, and large-scale implementation.
