Unexpected major venous injury in the setting of percutaneous arterial access for GORE TAG Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis
Authors: Samantha G Doehring, Aurora Moberly, James Pan, Glen Schwartzberg, John Foor, Jordan Sasson, Philip Auyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInferior vena cava (IVC) injury during thoracic endovascular aortic repair is exceedingly rare. We present the case of an 80-year-old woman who developed a fatal IVC perforation at the iliac confluence during large-bore (24F) sheath insertion for thoracic endovascular aortic repair. Despite successful thoracic endograft deployment, the patient developed acute hypotension and hemorrhagic shock. Emergent exploratory laparotomy revealed hemoperitoneum from an IVC tear, likely caused by shearing forces transmitted through heavily calcified iliac arteries. This case highlights the importance of preoperative access planning in patients with small, calcified iliac vessels and maintaining a low threshold for suspecting retroperitoneal venous injury when clinical findings differ from angiography results.Keywords: Vena cava, Inferior, Blood vessel prosthesis implantation, Endovascular aneurysm repair, Aorta, Iatrogenic disease

Inferior vena cava (IVC) injury is a rare but lethal complication with reported mortality rates for traumatic and iatrogenic injury ranging from 34% to 70% despite modern resuscitation and operative techniques.1

Increased complex endovascular procedures have led to additional mechanisms for caval injury. A spectrum of endovascular venous and IVC injuries exists related to the placement of IVC filters, central venous catheters, and hemodialysis catheters. However, direct IVC perforation is extremely rare.2,3 Similarly, during endovascular treatment of chronic IVC occlusion with large-diameter balloon angioplasty and venous stenting, cases of caval rupture are exceedingly rare.4,5

Despite a growing body of literature on traumatic and iatrogenic venous injuries, currently reported cases primarily involve direct venous access or open operative injury to the vena cava and iliac veins. To our knowledge, there are no previously reported cases describing IVC injury occurring during large-bore arterial access for arterial endovascular procedures. Informed consent was obtained from the patient's family for the publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case discussion

An 80-year-old woman with a history of hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea presented with severe chest pain radiating to her back. Computed tomography angiography of the chest revealed an intramural hematoma extending the length of the aorta, distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery and proximal to the celiac trunk, suggestive of a thrombosed descending thoracic dissection. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for medical management with antihypertensive agents. Despite this, the patient continued to experience chest pain, and a subsequent follow-up computed tomography scan revealed an increase in the size of the intramural hematoma. Consequently, the patient was scheduled for placement of a GORE Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis.

Vascular access was obtained percutaneously through the left brachial artery and via a cut down over the right common femoral artery. Angiography revealed a calcified plaque in the right common iliac artery (Fig 1). Intraoperative intravascular ultrasound study was used to assess the extent of the intramural hematoma and to identify proximal and distal landing zones for thoracic endograft placement. During serial dilation and insertion of the 24F Gore DrySeal sheath, resistance to advancement necessitated constant forward pressure while rotating the sheath. There were no changes in the patient's hemodynamics during this portion of the procedure. Therapeutic heparin was administered, and a 34 × 150 mm GORE Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis was deployed, followed by placement of the left subclavian stent.

Computed tomography angiography demonstrating the presence of calcified plaque in the right common iliac artery (arrow).

Devices were removed, and the right common femoral arteriotomy was repaired while manual pressure was applied to the left brachial access site. Protamine was administered for reversal. The patient then developed abrupt, significant hypotension. Intraoperative laboratory results demonstrated an acute drop in her hemoglobin levels from 13.8 to 6.8 g/dL. Given the concern for active hemorrhage, urgent angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed from a new percutaneous access in the left common femoral artery. No active extravasation was visualized; however, a flow-limiting dissection was identified in the right external iliac artery, which was treated with an 8 × 50 mm GORE Viabahn stent (Fig 2). The patient's hypotension was then attributed to a protamine reaction. Completion angiography showed resolution of the dissection in the external iliac artery. The patient responded appropriately to crystalloid and packed red blood cells. The patient's abdomen was soft, and neuromonitoring remained normal throughout the case.

Diagnostic aortic angiography demonstrating no evidence of contrast extravasation with a flow-limiting dissection noted in the right external iliac artery (arrows) that was ultimately treated with a stent graft.

The patient was then extubated and transferred to the ICU for close monitoring. Upon admission to the ICU, the patient developed cardiorespiratory arrest, and a Code Blue was called. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 20 minutes. A central venous catheter was placed in the left common femoral vein, and a rapid infuser was used for resuscitation. The patient's abdomen became distended, and she was taken for an emergent exploratory laparotomy.

Intraoperatively, the patient was found to have significant hemoperitoneum and a small tear in the IVC. The tear extended to the confluence and the right common iliac vein. Obtaining control was difficult because of an inflammatory rind on the right common iliac artery, which caused it to adhere to the right common iliac vein and IVC. The defect was repaired with interrupted, pledgeted 4-0 Prolene sutures. A temporary abdominal wound vac was applied, and the patient was transferred to the ICU for further resuscitation. Unfortunately, the patient was coagulopathic, hypothermic, and acidotic, requiring pressor support by the end of the case despite massive resuscitation efforts. The patient again went into cardiorespiratory arrest, and the family ultimately elected to stop resuscitative measures.

Discussion

Our case illustrates an unusual mechanism of IVC injury occurring during arterial thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) access. The combination of a small, heavily calcified iliac artery and the need to advance a large-profile sheath likely transmitted a shearing force from the right iliac artery to the IVC.6 This force may have disrupted the IVC walls, an injury further exacerbated by the pressurized administration of blood products through the left femoral central venous catheter. In addition, because of aortic tortuosity, the right iliac artery had crossed along the distal IVC. A computed tomography scan did not demonstrate any evidence of an iliac-caval fistula (Fig 3). This unique mechanism of injury serves as a reminder that large-profile devices can injure adjacent venous structures even when arterial angiography is adequate.7, 8, 9, 10, 11

Coronal view of the patient's abdomen/pelvis computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrates tortuosity of her infrarenal aorta and iliac arteries. Note that the right common iliac artery courses over the distal inferior vena cava and along the proximal right common iliac vein (arrow).

The postprocedure hypotension was initially attributed to a protamine reaction; however, the technically difficult iliac access and new hemodynamic instability should have prompted early consideration of retroperitoneal hemorrhage, despite no evidence of active extravasation on arteriography. Iliofemoral artery complications can occur in up to 12% of patients after TEVAR. Abdominal or flank pain with acute blood loss may indicate a retroperitoneal hematoma, which requires prompt intervention.10,12 Angiography may fail to identify venous injuries, and relying solely on pharmacologic explanations can delay the recognition of a mechanical source of bleeding.13

Once identified, the injury was managed with standard damage-control principles for major venous injury, including rapid aortic and iliac control, primary IVC repair, and temporary abdominal closure. Despite successful repair, the patient's advanced age, massive transfusion requirements, and severe coagulopathy contributed to an overall poor prognosis.14, 15, 16 This case offers notable takeaways regarding preoperative access planning in difficult iliac anatomy7,11 and the prior consideration of retroperitoneal venous injury when clinical and imaging findings do not align. External iliac artery diameters less than 7 mm and extensive iliac calcifications are independent predictors of access complications. These factors should prompt consideration of alternative access strategies, including iliac conduits.10,15,17,18 Although the right iliac artery was initially chosen for technical ease, in hindsight, the left iliac artery was slightly larger and less calcified. Advancing the large-bore sheath through the left iliac artery may have prevented this catastrophic outcome.

Preoperative recognition of small, calcified iliac arteries should prompt consideration of surgical iliac conduits, endoconduits, or alternative access routes for TEVAR, rather than attempting to force passage of very large sheaths.18 Surgeons should maintain a low threshold for suspecting caval or retroperitoneal venous injury in patients with difficult large-bore access, unexplained hypotension, and discordant angiographic findings, as prior recognition may be the only modifiable factor in an otherwise high-mortality scenario.
