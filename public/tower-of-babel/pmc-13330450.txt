Circulating biomarkers of carotid plaque vulnerability: current evidence, emerging markers, and barriers to clinical implementation
Authors: Rongchao Ma, Wentong Hu, Xiaoqiong Du, Xuan He, Dujuan Sha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarotid plaque instability is a major cause of ischemic stroke, whereas conventional imaging based mainly on luminal stenosis cannot fully capture the dynamic molecular processes that precede plaque rupture. Circulating biomarkers provide a noninvasive approach to identify vulnerable carotid plaques by reflecting inflammation, lipid dysregulation, endothelial activation, thrombogenicity, oxidative stress, extracellular matrix remodeling, and other biological changes within the plaque microenvironment. This review summarizes current evidence on both traditional and emerging biomarkers of carotid plaque vulnerability, with emphasis on their ability to discriminate unstable from stable plaques and to predict future cerebrovascular events. Among traditional biomarkers, inflammatory markers, modified lipid-related markers, endothelial and neovascularization-related markers, coagulation-fibrinolysis indicators, and oxidative stress/extracellular matrix remodeling markers show the most consistent associations with plaque vulnerability. Emerging evidence further supports the potential relevance of epigenetic markers, macrophage-related immunophenotypic markers, iron metabolism-related markers, TGF-β2, non-traditional lipid parameters, endoplasmic reticulum stress-related proteins, and several exploratory biomarkers. In addition, multi-biomarker and multimodal models integrating circulating markers with imaging features appear to outperform individual biomarkers alone in identifying high-risk plaques. However, despite these advances, most candidate biomarkers remain exploratory and have not yet entered routine clinical practice because of limited specificity and sensitivity, insufficient reproducibility, lack of assay standardization and universally accepted cutoff values, and the predominance of small, single-center, or early-stage studies without adequate external validation. Future progress will depend on large prospective multicenter studies, standardized detection methods, and integrated multimodal risk models that combine biomarkers with imaging and clinical variables. These efforts may ultimately improve early risk stratification and individualized management of patients with vulnerable carotid atherosclerotic plaques.Keywords: Carotid disease, Plaque, Vulnerability, Circulating biomarkers

Introduction

Stroke is a common cause of death and disability worldwide, with ischemic stroke accounting for over 70% of cases [1]. Carotid atherosclerosis is one of the primary risk factors for ischemic stroke [2]. Carotid plaque characteristics are closely associated with thromboembolism and ischemic stroke events (Fig. 1; Table 1). Features such as intraplaque hemorrhage, thin or ruptured fibrous caps, and large lipid cores are associated with a more than threefold increased risk of ischemic stroke [3]. Traditionally, the degree of luminal stenosis has been the primary basis for assessing plaque rupture risk. However, recent studies indicate that non-stenotic plaques also play a significant role in embolic strokes of unknown source [4]. Therefore, plaque size or stenosis alone cannot fully reflect rupture risk. Accurately identifying high-risk vulnerable plaques and implementing early intervention has become an urgent clinical challenge. Traditional imaging techniques excel in assessing morphological features such as stenosis, fibrous cap thickness, and lipid core size, playing a vital role in evaluating carotid plaque stability [5]. However, imaging methods struggle to comprehensively reflect biological processes like apoptosis and matrix degradation, and they are highly equipment-dependent with demanding technical requirements. In contrast, circulating biomarkers can be obtained through routine blood tests and sensitively reflect dynamic processes within plaques, such as inflammatory responses, cellular metabolism, and tissue remodeling. This allows them to capture molecular-level warning signals before plaque rupture occurs [6]. This review summarizes research progress on circulating biomarkers associated with carotid plaque vulnerability, with a specific focus on their ability to distinguish vulnerable/unstable plaques from stable plaques, to provide evidence for the clinical identification of high-risk carotid plaques.

Traditional circulating biomarkers

Traditional circulating biomarkers remain the main focus of current research on carotid plaque vulnerability because they reflect the core pathological processes underlying plaque destabilization, including inflammation, lipid dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, thrombogenicity, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Although most of these markers are not specific to carotid atherosclerosis alone, they provide important biological information on plaque activity and have been the most extensively studied in relation to vulnerable plaque phenotypes, symptomatic carotid disease, and subsequent cerebrovascular events. Inflammation-related markers

Chronic inflammation within the arterial wall is a central mechanism driving atherosclerotic plaque destabilization, with macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses playing a pivotal role [43]. Among circulating inflammatory markers, interleukin-6 (IL-6) remains one of the most robustly validated indicators, independently predicting carotid plaque vulnerability (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.05–1.40) and plaque progression (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.23–1.69) [7]. Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), a key chemokine driving monocyte recruitment into the arterial wall, was likewise associated with vulnerable plaque characteristics (OR range 1.20–1.56; all P < 0.05) and symptomatic plaques (OR 1.36, P = 0.006) [8]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is an established marker reflecting systemic inflammatory status; recent evidence suggests that hs-CRP levels ≥ 2 mg/L are associated with increased plaque vascular density (OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.20–2.09), a larger lipid core (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.02–1.73), and a higher macrophage content (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.02–1.73) [9]. In addition to hs-CRP, monomeric C-reactive protein (mCRP) may better reflect local vascular inflammation relevant to plaque destabilization. In patients with subclinical carotid atherosclerosis, an mCRP level ≥ 5.2 µg/L was associated with a more pronounced increase in plaque number and total plaque height and independently predicted the formation of new carotid plaques (adjusted OR 4.74, 95% CI 1.70–13.24) [10], indicating that mCRP may complement hs-CRP by capturing residual local inflammatory risk more specifically, although its clinical application remains limited by assay availability and methodological heterogeneity. Leukocyte-derived inflammatory indices obtained from routine blood counts have also emerged as practical adjunctive markers. In asymptomatic carotid stenosis, a higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was independently associated with grade 2 intraplaque neovascularization on contrast-enhanced ultrasound (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.03–1.43), and patients in the highest NLR quartile had markedly higher odds of grade 2 intraplaque neovascularization than those in the lowest quartile (OR 4.55, 95% CI 1.69–12.25) [11]. Similarly, a higher monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) was independently associated with high intraplaque neovascularization on AngioPLUS imaging (OR 4.08, 95% CI 1.69–9.88) [12]. In addition, the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count, is a composite marker reflecting the balance between thrombo-inflammatory activation and immune status and was independently associated with vulnerable plaques (OR 2.242, 95% CI 1.378–4.024) and ruptured fibrous caps (OR 3.462, 95% CI 2.031–6.374) [13]. Other complete blood count-derived inflammatory indices, including the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (dNLR), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), have also been investigated in carotid atherosclerosis or carotid plaque-related research. However, compared with NLR, MLR, and SII, the available evidence for these other indices mainly concerns plaque presence, plaque number or echogenic features, renal function-related plaque risk, and post-endarterectomy restenosis or mortality; their direct associations with histologically or imaging-defined carotid plaque vulnerability remain less consistent [44–47]. Notably, LMR has also been reported to reflect plaque instability in intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis (ICAS), although its direct evidence in vulnerable carotid plaques remains limited [48, 49]. These findings suggest that blood count-derived inflammatory indices may help identify biologically active and rupture-prone plaques in a simple and cost-effective manner, although they remain nonspecific markers of systemic inflammation. CD40 and its ligand CD40L belong to the co-stimulatory molecules of the TNF receptor superfamily; levels of sCD40 and sCD40L within plaques were positively correlated with lipid content, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), and the vulnerability index (r = 0.180–0.259; all P < 0.05) [14]. Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) serves as a marker of inflammation and immune activation. Plasma suPAR levels were elevated in symptomatic patients (median: 5.04 vs. 4.61 ng/mL, P = 0.026), and plaque suPAR levels correlated with several pro-inflammatory cytokines, indicating a close association with the vulnerable inflammatory plaque phenotype [15]. Lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2), a pro-inflammatory enzyme secreted primarily by inflammatory and vascular cells, is independently associated with histologically unstable carotid plaques (OR = 1.7, 95% CI 1.1–12.3) and anterior-circulation cerebral infarction-related vulnerable plaques (OR = 1.041, 95% CI 1.017–1.065) [16]. Overall, a panel centered on IL-6, hs-CRP/mCRP-related residual inflammatory risk, MCP-1, leukocyte-derived inflammatory indices, CD40/CD40L, and suPAR may better reflect plaque vulnerability than nonspecific inflammatory markers alone.

Lipid metabolism-related markers

Abnormal lipid metabolism contributes not only to plaque formation but also to carotid plaque destabilization [50]. Among lipid-related biomarkers, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) has some of the most direct evidence for plaque vulnerability: in carotid endarterectomy specimens, oxLDL levels in plaques were nearly 70-fold higher than those in plasma and were significantly higher in macrophage-rich than in macrophage-poor plaques (19.6 ± 2.8 vs. 5.50 ± 0.77 ng/µg apoB, P < 0.0001) [17]. In a more recent carotid endarterectomy cohort, serum oxLDL > 31.14 ng/mL identified unstable plaques with an AUC of 0.86, 91.18% sensitivity, and 78.79% specificity [18]. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) concentration alone may not adequately reflect plaque stability; instead, HDL-related quality markers appear more informative. Low apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I) independently predicted symptomatic/vulnerable carotid plaques (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.96–0.99) [19], and lower HDL-associated paraoxonase-1 (PON1) levels were also associated with symptomatic carotid disease (4.6 ± 1.36 vs. 5.3 ± 1.19 ng/mL; AUC 0.654) [20]. Among apoB-containing lipoprotein measures, the highest tertile of non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C, encompassing atherogenic lipoproteins including LDL-C, VLDL-C, and remnant cholesterol) was associated with the presence of asymptomatic vulnerable carotid plaques (OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.23–1.82) [21]; remnant cholesterol (RC) was independently associated with unstable carotid plaque (OR 1.673, 95% CI 1.113–2.515) [22], and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] ≥ 50 mg/dL predicted 10-year progression of vulnerable carotid plaques (RR 1.93, 95% CI 1.54–2.41) [23]. Collectively, biomarkers reflecting modified LDL burden, residual apoB-containing lipoprotein risk, and HDL quality—such as oxLDL, non-HDL-C, remnant cholesterol, Lp(a), apoA-I, and PON1—may be more informative for identifying vulnerable plaques than isolated HDL-C levels alone.

Endothelial activation and neovascularization-related markers

Endothelial activation and intraplaque neovascularization are key processes in the development of vulnerable carotid plaques, promoting leukocyte recruitment, endothelial dysfunction, immature microvessel formation, intraplaque hemorrhage, and fibrous cap disruption [51]. Among endothelial-related markers, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) appears to be more closely linked to plaque vulnerability than other adhesion molecules, as endothelial VCAM-1 expression was 2.4-fold higher in symptomatic than in asymptomatic carotid plaques, whereas P-selectin, von Willebrand factor (vWF), and lectin-like oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor-1 (LOX-1) did not differ significantly between groups [24]. The association between the vascular endothelial growth factor/receptor (VEGF/VEGFR) axis and plaque instability is also relatively direct: Russell et al. reported that VEGF staining scores were higher in unstable plaques than in stable plaques [4.0 (4.0–4.0) vs. 3.0 (2.0–3.0), P = 0.002], VEGFR1 scores were also higher [4.0 (2.0–4.0) vs. 2.0 (1.0–3.0), P = 0.016], accompanied by increased microvascular density in the cap [12.1 vs. 1.1 vessels/mm², P = 0.017] [25]. Pelisek et al. further found that neovascularization in unstable plaques was 1.8-fold higher (P = 0.013), tissue VEGF was 1.7-fold higher (P = 0.032), and serum VEGF levels were higher in patients (0.32 ± 0.22 vs. 0.22 ± 0.16 ng/mL, P = 0.002) [26]. In addition, platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) may be included as a marker of intraplaque neovascularization. CD31-positive microvessel density has been used to quantify plaque neovascularization in histopathologic studies, and both imaging-pathology and recent histopathologic data further support its relevance. In a carotid endarterectomy cohort, microvessel density determined by CD31 complex staining was significantly correlated with the neovascularization grade assessed by superb microvascular imaging (SMI) (r = 0.788, P < 0.001), while symptomatic plaques showed significantly higher microvessel density than asymptomatic plaques (P = 0.018) [27]. More recently, in advanced unstable carotid plaque segments with intraplaque hemorrhage, CD31 staining demonstrated increased microvessel density and more microvessel hotspots, whereas co-localization of endoglin (CD105) and CD31 indicated an increased proportion of angiogenic plaque microvessels [28]. These findings suggest that CD31 is a useful marker for evaluating intraplaque neovascularization and may provide supportive information for identifying vulnerable carotid plaques. Notably, CD105 may complement CD31 in this setting, as it has been reported to detect plaque microvessels more sensitively and to identify angiogenic microvessels in unstable lesions [29]. In contrast, vWF and endothelial microparticles (EMPs) are more suitable as auxiliary indicators of endothelial injury, thrombotic tendency, or recent cerebral ischemic activity, rather than core markers of vulnerable plaques themselves: Zadi et al. found no independent association between vWF and carotid plaque ulceration [52]; Kandiyil et al. found that EMPs were associated with DWI positivity and clinical stroke, but showed no significant association with plaque hemorrhage on MRI [53]. Therefore, from the perspectives of clinical significance and predictive value, VCAM-1 and the VEGF/VEGFR axis currently have the strongest evidence as markers of endothelial activation and neovascularization in vulnerable carotid plaques, whereas CD31 and CD105 may provide supportive information on intraplaque neovascularization, and vWF and EMPs should be regarded as supporting rather than core evidence.

Vascular calcification-related markers

The role of vascular calcification in atherosclerotic plaque stability is complex, primarily determined by its morphological and distribution characteristics. Current histopathological and imaging evidence suggests that microcalcifications or spotty calcifications are more frequently observed in inflamed and rupture-prone plaques, whereas large, dense calcifications are generally considered to reflect a more stable plaque phenotype. In addition, marginal calcification is often associated with peripheral inflammation and neovessel leakage, further indicating plaque instability and an increased risk of cerebrovascular events [54]. Among calcification-related markers, osteoprotegerin (OPG) and osteopontin (OPN) appear to have a more direct association with carotid plaque vulnerability. Golledge et al. reported that, compared with asymptomatic plaques, symptomatic carotid plaques showed approximately a 2-fold increase in OPG expression and a 4-fold increase in OPN expression in the proximal internal carotid artery region, supporting their close association with plaque instability [30]. In addition, histopathological studies have shown that plaques with high OPN expression are more likely to exhibit microcalcification, surface ulceration, and a large lipid-rich necrotic core, further supporting its potential value as a marker of vulnerable plaque [31]. Future studies integrating circulating calcification-related biomarkers with multimodal plaque imaging may further improve risk stratification for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events.

Coagulation-fibrinolysis-related markers

Thrombosis represents a severe consequence of carotid plaque rupture; therefore, relevant biomarkers possess predictive value for plaque rupture. Among platelet-related markers, platelet factor 4 (PF4) has been associated with symptomatic carotid atherosclerotic disease, with macrophage PF4 staining correlating with the presence of symptoms [32]. In addition, factor VII activating protease (FSAP) appears to be linked to plaque destabilization, as both plaque and plasma FSAP levels were higher in symptomatic than in asymptomatic carotid stenosis, with stronger FSAP immunostaining observed in intraplaque hemorrhage-related structures and lipid-rich areas within the necrotic core [33]. A more direct marker of plaque thrombogenicity may be tissue factor (TF). In high-grade carotid stenosis, plaques with extensive TF staining (> 40% of plaque area) were more frequent in symptomatic than in asymptomatic patients (12/27 vs. 1/9), and increased TF expression was also associated with cerebral microembolism (P = 0.008) [34]. Furthermore, enhanced local fibrin turnover has been observed in unstable lesions: in symptomatic patients, intraplaque D-dimer content was significantly higher in ulcerated-complicated plaques than in fibrous plaques (5.9 ± 1 vs. 1.8 ± 1, P < 0.001), supporting its association with a thrombogenic and rupture-prone plaque phenotype [35]. In carotid endarterectomy specimens, Sayed et al. demonstrated that plaques from early symptomatic patients showed a significantly higher incidence of plaque rupture and intraplaque hemorrhage than those from late symptomatic or asymptomatic patients (58% vs. 18%/18% and 55% vs. 6%/9%, respectively). Further analysis revealed that increased expression of thrombomodulin (TM), tissue-type plasminogen activator (t-PA), and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) was associated with plaque rupture, whereas increased expression of t-PA and urokinase-type plasminogen activator (u-PA) was associated with intraplaque hemorrhage [36]. These findings suggest that activation of the local thrombomodulatory/fibrinolytic pathway is closely linked to the acute symptomatic and histologically unstable phenotype of carotid plaques.

Oxidative stress and extracellular matrix remodeling markers

Oxidative stress contributes to plaque destabilization by promoting lipid peroxidation, endothelial injury, and inflammatory activation. Among oxidative stress-related biomarkers, malondialdehyde (MDA) appears to have a closer association with clinically high-risk carotid plaque phenotypes than general antioxidant markers. In a pilot study of carotid endarterectomy specimens, plasma MDA levels were not significantly different between the overall symptomatic and asymptomatic groups, but were significantly higher in patients with hemispheric stroke or transient ischemic attack than in those who were asymptomatic or had amaurosis fugax (P = 0.002), suggesting a closer relationship with clinically unstable plaque phenotypes [37]. In addition, higher urinary 8-iso-prostaglandin F2α (8-iso-PGF2α) levels were independently associated with lower carotid plaque echogenicity, a recognized surrogate of plaque vulnerability [38]. Extracellular matrix remodeling is directly involved in fibrous cap weakening and plaque rupture. Among matrix remodeling markers, MMP-9 has the most consistent association with carotid plaque instability. In human carotid plaques, the concentration of MMP-9 was significantly higher in the most unstable lesions (median 125.7 ng/mL vs. < 32 ng/mL in other plaques; P = 0.003), and was also higher in plaques with spontaneous cerebral embolization (P = 0.019) and histological features of instability (P < 0.03), whereas no similar differences were observed for MMP-1, MMP-2, or MMP-3 [39]. Circulating MMP-9 has also been associated with symptomatic and unstable carotid plaques, with serum levels being higher in symptomatic than asymptomatic patients (1026.10 ± 412.90 vs. 377.84 ± 164.08 ng/mL; P = 0.001) and in unstable than stable plaques (1006.98 ± 447.09 vs. 496.16 ± 292.78 ng/mL; P = 0.001) [40]. Although tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) may counterbalance extracellular matrix degradation, their clinical utility as independent biomarkers of plaque vulnerability remains less consistent [55]. In carotid endarterectomy specimens, low plaque elastin content was associated with a higher risk of subsequent ipsilateral stroke, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 7.38 (95% CI 1.50–36.31) for plaques with elastin content below the median [41]. In addition, anti-tropoelastin antibody (tropo-AEAb) and anti-α-elastin antibody (α-AEAb) levels revealed that symptomatic carotid stenosis was associated with a markedly lower tropo-AEAb/α-AEAb ratio than asymptomatic stenosis (3.7 vs. 14.2, P < 0.001), indicating altered elastin synthesis/degradation in unstable plaques [42]. These findings suggest that elastin-related markers may have potential value, although the current evidence remains less mature than that for MDA or MMP-9.

Novel circulating biomarkers

In addition to traditional biomarkers, growing attention has been directed toward a range of emerging markers that may capture more specific aspects of plaque biology, including epigenetic regulation, macrophage heterogeneity, iron metabolism, endoplasmic reticulum stress, extracellular vesicle-associated signatures, and other exploratory molecular pathways. These candidates may provide incremental information beyond conventional inflammatory or lipid-related indicators and, in some cases, have shown promising associations with histological plaque vulnerability, high-risk imaging features, or recurrent cerebrovascular events. However, most of these biomarkers remain at an early stage of development, and their clinical applicability still requires further validation. Epigenetic markers

Epigenetic biomarkers may provide additional information on carotid plaque vulnerability, but the current evidence remains limited and heterogeneous across DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs [99], rather than being adequately explained by a simple model of global hypomethylation. In human carotid plaques, Zaina et al. found that only a limited number of CpG sites showed major methylation differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic lesions, whereas more widespread demethylation was observed with increasing time after a cerebrovascular event, together with permissive transcriptional changes at promoters of anti-inflammatory and antioxidative genes. These findings suggest that some methylation changes may reflect post-event remodeling toward plaque stabilization, rather than instability per se [56]. More recent genome-wide methylome profiling further showed that symptomatic plaques harbor widespread methylation changes, with 67% of differentially methylated sites being hypomethylated, and identified 107 genes that were both hypomethylated and transcriptionally upregulated in advanced or ruptured plaques. Among these, PLA2G7 emerged as one of the most prominent gene-specific candidates, with promoter hypomethylation and increased expression in vulnerable plaques [57]. However, not all studies point in the same direction; for example, methylation profiling of the MMP/TIMP gene family in stable versus ulcerated plaque regions demonstrated an overall predominance of hypermethylation in unstable plaque portions [58]. Taken together, these findings indicate that DNA methylation changes associated with plaque vulnerability may be gene-specific and time-dependent rather than uniformly directed. At a more translational level, plaque epigenetic age acceleration was reported to independently predict secondary cardiovascular events after carotid endarterectomy during 3-year follow-up (adjusted HR 1.56, P = 0.02), indicating that methylation-derived markers may also have prognostic value [59].

Evidence for histone modifications in human carotid plaques is currently more limited and is more closely related to lesion severity than to symptom-defined plaque vulnerability. In carotid atherosclerotic lesions, H3K9 and H3K27 dimethylation were reduced compared with healthy arteries, and reduced H3K27 dimethylation in inflammatory cells was observed in advanced versus early lesions. In a companion histone study, H3K9 and H3K27 acetylation in smooth muscle cells increased with lesion severity, while H3K9 acetylation in smooth muscle cells and macrophages was associated with more advanced plaque stages. Therefore, histone marks should currently be interpreted as candidate mechanistic correlates of plaque progression rather than validated clinical biomarkers of carotid plaque instability [60].

Among non-coding RNAs, microRNAs are the best studied epigenetic candidates, although results are not entirely concordant. Cipollone et al. identified a plaque-instability-associated signature in which miR-100, miR-127, miR-145, miR-133a, and miR-133b were more highly expressed in symptomatic than asymptomatic carotid plaques [61]. A later pilot study similarly found that miR-100, miR-125a, miR-127, miR-133a, miR-145, and miR-221 were increased by approximately 500% to 1800% in symptomatic plaques [62]. In apparent contrast, Bazan et al. showed an acute loss of miR-221 and miR-222 specifically in the plaque shoulder during plaque rupture, indicating that the behavior of these miRNAs may depend on the sampled plaque region and the temporal stage of destabilization [63]. Therefore, miR-221/222 should be interpreted as context-dependent markers, rather than uniformly protective molecules.

Among circulating non-coding RNAs, the serum circR-284/miR-221 ratio is one of the more promising translational candidates. In a derivation cohort of 24 asymptomatic and 17 acutely symptomatic patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy, this ratio was significantly elevated in the urgent symptomatic group (P = 0.0002) [64]. The finding was then confirmed in an independent validation cohort of 112 patients, in which elevation of the ratio was observed uniquely in the urgent group (P < 0.001) [64], suggesting potential diagnostic value for acute plaque rupture and stroke. In contrast, lncRNA MIAT is currently better regarded as a mechanistic mediator than as a validated clinical biomarker, as available evidence mainly supports its role in smooth muscle cell phenotypic modulation and advanced lesion destabilization [65].

Overall, epigenetic markers remain promising but exploratory, and further studies are needed to define robust, reproducible, and clinically actionable markers of carotid plaque vulnerability.

Macrophage infiltration and immunophenotypic markers

Macrophage infiltration is one of the most characteristic histopathological features of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques and therefore deserves specific consideration in the evaluation of plaque instability. Histopathological assessment of symptomatic carotid plaques has shown strong correlations between macrophage infiltration and plaque instability (OR 3.39, 95% CI 2.31–4.98, P < 0.001), underscoring the relevance of macrophage-rich inflammation as a tissue-level marker of unstable lesions [66]. CD68 immunostaining is widely used to assess plaque macrophage burden [67]. Within the conventional macrophage-polarization framework markers, such as CD16/32, CD80, CD86, major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) and inducible nitric oxide synthase 2 (iNOS2) are generally associated with M1-like pro-inflammatory macrophages, whereas CD163, CD204, and Arg1 are more often linked to M2-like reparative or remodeling-associated states [67, 68]. In human atherosclerotic plaques, however, these phenotypes coexist and show distinct spatial distributions rather than a strict stable-versus-unstable dichotomy: M1-associated macrophages are enriched in rupture-prone shoulder regions, whereas M2-related signals are also detected in adventitial regions and in areas of intraplaque hemorrhage [68, 69]. Moreover, the traditional M1/M2 classification likely oversimplifies the substantial heterogeneity of macrophages in human atherosclerosis [67]. Importantly, CD163 should not be regarded as an exclusively stable-plaque marker. In human carotid plaques, CD163 + macrophages were enriched in symptomatic lesions and in plaques with a high vulnerability index, and plaque CD163 expression correlated positively with macrophages (r = 0.182), lipids (r = 0.229), hemorrhage (r = 0.260), and MMP-9 (r = 0.224), while correlating negatively with smooth muscle cells (r = − 0.268), elastin (r = − 0.270), and collagen (r = − 0.232) [70]. Among circulating macrophage-related biomarkers, plasma CD163 has also shown prognostic value in carotid artery stenosis, with higher levels independently associated with an increased 2-year risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (HR 1.413, 95% CI 1.022–1.954; P = 0.036) [71]. Taken together, macrophage burden, spatial distribution, and immunophenotypic heterogeneity should be regarded as important markers of plaque vulnerability rather than nonspecific inflammatory readouts.

Multi-biomarker and multimodal models

Although individual inflammatory biomarkers provide insight into specific pathogenic pathways, they are unlikely to fully capture the biological complexity of vulnerable carotid plaques. Recent studies have therefore shifted toward multi-biomarker and multimodal models, integrating circulating inflammatory markers with imaging or clinical variables. For example, in patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy, the combination of serum C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) and lipid-rich necrotic core (LRNC) volume improved the discrimination of plaque stability, increasing the AUC from 0.7544 for CXCL12 alone and 0.8462 for LRNC alone to 0.9001, with 87.31% sensitivity and 92.31% specificity [72]. Similarly, another study showed that a model combining hs-CRP with MRI-defined carotid plaque features, including normalized wall index, intraplaque hemorrhage, and lipid-rich necrotic core, achieved the best performance for predicting recurrent ipsilateral ischemic stroke, with an AUC of 0.855 [73]. Likewise, a 2025 multimodal machine learning study showed that adding blood- and clinical-based variables, including diabetes, neutrophils, monocytes, hs-CRP, HDL-C, and stenosis rate, to ultrasound imaging features improved the test-set AUC for vulnerable plaque prediction from 0.762 to 0.928 [74].

At present, evidence for pure biomarker-only panels remains limited, but a few exploratory studies are noteworthy; for example, a pilot study reported that the combined evaluation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), interferon regulatory factor 5 (IRF5), and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) improved discrimination compared with individual markers alone. In that study, the AUC increased from 0.5879 for HO-1, 0.6307 for IRF5, and 0.6556 for AhR alone to 0.7041 for the combined model. However, the improvement did not reach statistical significance in DeLong’s test, and the authors therefore considered the results preliminary and requiring validation in larger independent cohorts [75]. In addition, Angiopoietin-like protein 4 (ANGPTL4) and sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) may represent exploratory serum markers of carotid plaque stability. In a single-center study including 108 patients with coronary heart disease and 80 control subjects, serum ANGPTL4 and SIRT1 levels were significantly lower in patients with carotid plaques and differed across the no-plaque, stable-plaque, and unstable-plaque groups. Both markers were inversely correlated with plaque instability grade, with correlation coefficients of r = − 0.438 for ANGPTL4 and r = − 0.717 for SIRT1 (P < 0.001). The authors further reported that patients with ANGPTL4 ≤ 30.17 mg/L and SIRT1 ≤ 6.91 µg/L were more likely to have unstable plaques [76]. However, because the available evidence is currently limited to a relatively small single-center ultrasound-based study, ANGPTL4 and SIRT1 are better regarded as preliminary serum candidates rather than validated biomarkers of vulnerable carotid plaque. Another study of circulating exosomal non-coding RNAs showed that the lg(circSCMH1/miR-874) ratio was independently associated with medium-high-risk carotid plaques (OR 0.577, 95% CI 0.455–0.732; P < 0.001) and yielded an AUC of 0.815 with 76.7% sensitivity and 81.4% specificity [77]. Overall, these findings suggest that the clinical value of inflammatory biomarkers may lie less in isolated measurement than in their incorporation into multi-biomarker or multimodal risk models. However, most available studies remain exploratory, with limited sample sizes and insufficient external validation, and therefore further prospective studies are required before such models can be implemented in routine clinical practice.

Iron metabolism

Iron metabolism dysregulation may contribute to carotid plaque vulnerability through macrophage iron retention, oxidative stress, and intraplaque hemorrhage-related injury [100, 101]. In a recent study of carotid endarterectomy patients, serum ferritin (SF) and soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR), together with plaque H-ferritin and transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) expression, were positively associated with plaque vulnerability indices. In particular, plaque TfR1 correlated with lipid-rich necrotic core volume (R = 0.538, P = 0.017) and intraplaque hemorrhage volume (R = 0.707, P = 0.001), supporting a closer relationship between disturbed iron handling and unstable plaque composition [78]. In addition, earlier human studies showed that TfR1 and ferritin overexpression in CD68-positive macrophages was associated with plaque development, severity, and clinical symptoms [79].

Among iron-regulatory markers, hepcidin appears to be a more biologically central candidate. In human carotid plaques, hepcidin expression increased with lesion progression, with high hepcidin expression observed in < 15% of type 1 lesions, approximately 50% of type 2 plaques, and > 70% of type 3 ruptured plaques. Although plaques from symptomatic patients showed numerically higher hepcidin levels than those from asymptomatic patients (1.97 ± 0.3 vs. 1.24 ± 0.4), this difference was not statistically significant. Notably, plaques from male patients exhibited significantly higher hepcidin expression and a higher proportion of advanced/ruptured lesions than plaques from female patients [80, 81]. Taken together, current evidence suggests that plaque hepcidin, TfR1, and ferritin expression—particularly in macrophage-rich and hemorrhagic plaques—are more relevant indicators of carotid plaque vulnerability than circulating iron measures alone.

Transforming growth factor (TGF)-β2

Among TGF-β isoforms, TGF-β2 appears to be more directly associated with carotid plaque stability than with plaque burden alone. In human carotid plaques, TGF-β2 was the most abundant isoform and was significantly higher in asymptomatic than in symptomatic plaques (1435.77 [845.59–2758.60] vs. 1047.71 [710.34–1573.26] pg/g, P = 0.003). TGF-β2 correlated positively with features of plaque stability, including α-smooth muscle actin (r = 0.262), collagen (r = 0.340), and elastin (r = 0.394), and inversely with markers of plaque vulnerability, including lipid content (r = − 0.178), CD68 (r = − 0.133), and intraplaque hemorrhage assessed by glycophorin A (r = − 0.329). In addition, TGF-β2 was the only isoform inversely associated with MMP-9 and plaque inflammation. Importantly, high plaque TGF-β2 levels were also associated with a lower future cardiovascular event risk: patients in the highest quartile had fewer events than those in the lower three quartiles (13% vs. 31%), and this association remained significant after multivariable adjustment (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.2–0.8, P = 0.02). When analyzed as a continuous variable, each 1-SD increase in TGF-β2 was associated with a lower risk of future cardiovascular events (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.52–0.93, P = 0.02) [82]. These findings suggest that plaque TGF-β2 is a promising tissue-level marker of carotid plaque stability and a potential indicator of lower subsequent cardiovascular risk.

Non-traditional lipid parameters

Traditional lipid markers, such as total cholesterol, LDL-C, and HDL-C, primarily reflect overall lipid burden and composition, but do not fully capture plaque vulnerability. By contrast, several non-traditional lipid parameters appear to be more closely associated with unstable carotid plaque phenotypes [102]. In patients with acute ischemic stroke, Zhao et al. showed that residual lipoprotein cholesterol (RLP-C), non-HDL-C/HDL-C, non-HDL-C, Castelli risk index I (CRI-I), and Castelli risk index II (CRI-II) were independent predictors of carotid plaque vulnerability, with odds ratios of 3.361, 1.699, 1.704, 1.573, and 2.022, respectively. In the same study, TG/HDL-C and atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) were associated with the degree of carotid stenosis, but not with plaque vulnerability after multivariable adjustment [83]. In addition, in a large general-population study, the non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio was significantly associated with plaque stability: compared with the lowest quartile, the highest quartile was associated with unstable plaque versus no plaque (OR 2.34, 95% CI 2.06–2.67) and unstable versus stable plaque (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.18–1.61) [84]. These data suggest that non-traditional lipid parameters—particularly RLP-C, non-HDL-C, and the non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio—may be more informative than traditional lipid markers for identifying vulnerable carotid plaques.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress-related proteins

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-related proteins may serve as plaque-level markers of carotid plaque vulnerability. In carotid endarterectomy specimens, glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) and C/EBP-homologous protein (CHOP) were significantly more highly expressed in vulnerable than in stable plaques, and were mainly localized to inflammatory cells and vascular endothelial cells. Importantly, both markers showed discriminatory value for vulnerable plaques, with an AUC of 0.792 for GRP78, 0.850 for CHOP, and 0.870 for their combination. These findings suggest that GRP78 and CHOP may reflect the degree of ER stress associated with plaque instability [85]. More recently, a larger human carotid plaque study further strengthened this association by showing that GRP78 and CHOP expression increased in parallel with clinical instability across asymptomatic, symptomatic, and crescendo TIA/stroke-in-evolution plaques. In co-localization analyses, ER stress markers were significantly associated with clinical instability, including CD68/GRP78 (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.05–1.20), CD68/CHOP (OR 1.092, 95% CI 1.04–1.14), and SMA/CHOP (OR 1.082, 95% CI 1.04–1.13) [86]. Taken together, current evidence supports GRP78 and CHOP as promising tissue-level markers of unstable carotid plaque, although their value as circulating biomarkers and their incremental predictive utility in clinical practice remain to be further established.

Other biomarkers

Recent studies indicate that multiple other biomolecules play crucial roles in regulating atherosclerotic plaque stability. These biomolecules are implicated in various pathophysiological processes: (1) metabolic disorders, such as uric acid and the serum uric acid/creatinine ratio; (2) inflammation and immune regulation, such as pentraxin 3 (PTX3), YKL-40 (chitinase-3-like protein 1, CHI3L1), the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, and Src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 1 (SHP-1); (3) vascular repair, such as endothelial progenitor cells; (4) ECM remodeling, such as ECM metalloproteinase inducer (EMMPRIN), and cleaved osteoglycin; (5) oxidative stress, such as biliverdin reductase B (BLVRB). Together, these elements form a complex regulatory network governing plaque vulnerability.

Among metabolic markers, uric acid appears to be associated with plaque vulnerability at both tissue and circulating levels. Uric acid has been detected within human carotid plaques and has been associated with cerebrovascular symptoms [87], while in elderly patients with carotid atherosclerosis the serum uric acid/serum creatinine ratio (SUA/SCr) was inversely associated with plaque echogenicity, a surrogate of plaque instability (β = −0.567, 95% CI − 0.751 to − 0.384, P < 0.0001), and explained approximately 30.3% of the variability in grayscale median values [88].

Among inflammatory and immune-related markers, PTX3 and YKL-40 may merit brief inclusion as promising adjunctive inflammatory biomarkers. PTX3 may reflect local vascular inflammation more directly than CRP: earlier carotid studies found higher PTX3 levels in both systemic and intracarotid samples of vulnerable plaques and reported an AUC of 0.877 for discriminating vulnerable plaques [89], while a more recent high-resolution vessel wall imaging study showed that serum PTX3 independently predicted carotid vulnerable plaque (OR 2.88, 95% CI 1.16–8.91), fibrous cap discontinuity (OR 2.47, 95% CI 1.32–4.63), and focal inflammation (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.18–4.32) [90]. YKL-40 is another promising inflammatory glycoprotein: in a community-based study of 1,132 participants with carotid plaques, higher YKL-40 quartiles were independently associated with unstable plaques even after adjustment for hs-CRP and plaque thickness (quartile 3 OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.29–3.40; quartile 4 OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.04–2.80) [91]. In addition, NLRP3 inflammasome components in peripheral blood mononuclear cells were increased in patients with vulnerable carotid plaques in a small case-control study (22 vulnerable plaques, 22 stable plaques, and 40 healthy controls), suggesting potential value as a circulating marker of plaque instability [92]. SHP-1 has also been implicated in human carotid plaque destabilization, with plaque protein expression increasing during plaque progression and being associated with greater instability in both symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid stenosis [93]. Because the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, PTX3, YKL-40, NLRP3, and SHP-1 should currently be regarded as promising but still exploratory inflammatory or immune-related markers.

Among vascular repair-related markers, endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) may also be involved in carotid plaque vulnerability. In a carotid endarterectomy study of 34 patients, vulnerable plaques defined as lipid-rich plaques or intraplaque hemorrhage showed significantly higher numbers of CD34-positive microvessels and CD34-, CD133-, and VEGFR2-positive cells than fibrous plaques (P = 0.007, 0.031, 0.013, and 0.002, respectively). In addition, the number of CD34-positive microvessels correlated with the numbers of CD34-positive (R = 0.308, P = 0.009), CD133-positive (R = 0.324, P = 0.006), and VEGFR2-positive cells (R = 0.296, P = 0.013), suggesting that plaque neovascularization may facilitate EPC recruitment and thereby contribute to plaque growth and vulnerability [94].

Among extracellular matrix-related markers, EMMPRIN and cleaved osteoglycin appear to be more relevant to carotid plaque vulnerability. In patients with asymptomatic high-grade carotid stenosis, serum EMMPRIN levels were significantly higher in those with soft, high-risk vulnerable plaques, indicating potential value for pre-operative risk stratification [95]. By contrast, cleaved osteoglycin was associated with a more stable plaque phenotype and a lower future cardiovascular event risk (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.31–0.98; P = 0.043) [96].

Among oxidative stress-related markers, BLVRB and myeloperoxidase (MPO) currently appear more clinically relevant. In the multicenter PARISK cohort of symptomatic carotid atherosclerosis (n = 177, 5-year follow-up), plasma BLVRB levels were associated with MRI-detected intraplaque hemorrhage and independently predicted recurrent ischemic events, including the combined endpoint of recurrent ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack (HR 1.469, 95% CI 1.094–1.974; P = 0.011) [97]. Likewise, in human carotid endarterectomy specimens, intraplaque MPO activity was markedly higher in unstable than in stable plaques (4.2 ± 3.1 vs. 0.2 ± 0.3 nmol/mgp; P < 0.0001) and was also higher in symptomatic patients or those with ipsilateral stroke than in asymptomatic, stroke-free patients (3.7 ± 2.1 vs. 0.1 ± 0.2 nmol/mgp; P = 0.002) [98], supporting MPO as a promising oxidative stress-related marker of unstable carotid plaque.

Conclusion and outlook

Carotid plaque vulnerability is driven by a complex interplay of inflammation, lipid dysregulation, endothelial activation, thrombosis, oxidative stress, extracellular matrix remodeling, and multiple emerging molecular pathways. Compared with conventional luminal imaging, circulating biomarkers provide a noninvasive means of capturing dynamic biological processes that precede plaque rupture and may therefore improve the identification of high-risk carotid lesions. In recent years, a growing number of biomarkers have shown associations with histologically unstable plaques, vulnerable imaging phenotypes, and recurrent cerebrovascular events, and multimodal models combining biomarkers with imaging features have further improved discriminatory performance. However, despite these promising findings, most biomarkers have not yet been translated into routine clinical practice.

Several barriers currently limit clinical implementation. First, many candidate biomarkers lack sufficient specificity for carotid plaque vulnerability, because they also reflect systemic inflammation, metabolic disturbance, oxidative stress, or generalized atherosclerotic burden rather than plaque instability itself. Second, the sensitivity and incremental predictive value of individual biomarkers are often modest, and few markers consistently outperform established clinical and imaging parameters when tested in multivariable models. Third, there remains substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in specimen type (plasma, serum, plaque tissue, exosomes, or peripheral blood cells), assay platforms, sample processing, timing of collection relative to ischemic events, and definitions of “vulnerable” plaque. These issues make it difficult to compare results across studies or to establish universally applicable cutoff values. Fourth, reproducibility remains a major challenge: many studies are single-center, cross-sectional, or exploratory, with relatively small sample sizes, limited external validation, and insufficient assessment of inter-laboratory consistency. Finally, several emerging biomarkers are still supported mainly by mechanistic or early translational studies, and their real-world utility for screening, risk stratification, and treatment monitoring remains uncertain.

Future research should therefore focus on moving from biomarker discovery to clinical qualification. Priority should be given to large, prospective, multicenter studies with standardized sampling protocols, harmonized assay methods, and clearly defined plaque vulnerability endpoints based on pathology, advanced imaging, or recurrent cerebrovascular events. Rather than relying on single analytes, future efforts should emphasize multi-biomarker and multimodal models that integrate circulating markers with imaging, clinical variables, and, where feasible, omics-derived signatures. In parallel, studies should assess analytical robustness, biological variability, cost-effectiveness, and incremental value beyond current risk assessment strategies. Only through such rigorous validation can circulating biomarkers progress from promising research tools to clinically actionable instruments for the early identification and management of vulnerable carotid plaques.
