Integrative analysis of adiponectin-related genes reveals immune subtypes and prognostic significance in melanoma
Authors: Yong Yin, Chun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to explore the prognostic and immune-related associations of adiponectin-related genes in melanoma.MethodsSingle nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and transcriptomic data from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit Open Genome-Wide Association Study database (IEU openGWAS) and The Cancer Genome Atlas Skin Cutaneous Melanoma cohort (TCGA-SKCM) were integrated to identify adiponectin-related genes. Consensus clustering defined molecular subtypes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using functional enrichment, protein–protein interaction (PPI), and immune infiltration (CIBERSORT). In vitro assays assessed transcriptional changes following endogenous albumin (ALB) modulation in melanoma cells.ResultsFive adiponectin-associated SNPs were linked to melanoma prognosis. Four molecular subtypes with distinct survival outcomes were identified, with ALB-high Cluster 3 showing the poorest prognosis. ALB was identified as a PPI hub and positively correlated with memory B cells (R = 0.34, P = 0.012). In vitro, ALB overexpression was associated with increased mRNA levels of IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-17A, and RORγt.ConclusionTumor-associated ALB expression is associated with adverse prognosis and immune-related transcriptional features in melanoma, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and marker of transcriptomic heterogeneity.Keywords: Adiponectin, melanoma, albumin, immune microenvironment, prognosis, bioinformatics analysis

Introduction

Melanoma is a highly aggressive skin cancer originating from the malignant transformation of melanocytes and represents one of the most fatal skin cancers worldwide [1,2]. In recent years, the incidence of melanoma has been steadily increasing, particularly among middle-aged and elderly populations, making it a significant public health concern [3]. The distinct biological features of melanoma, characterized by high metastatic potential and resistance to traditional treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, result in limited therapeutic efficacy for patients with advanced stage [4]. Although immunotherapies, such as Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors, have significantly extended survival in some patients, the overall response rates remain limited [5]. In addition, immune-related adverse events may complicate treatment continuation and clinical management, although treatment discontinuation due to such events does not necessarily reduce clinical benefit in melanoma patients [6]. Therefore, it is important to better understand the molecular and immune-related factors underlying melanoma progression, particularly within the tumor microenvironment.

Adiponectin is an adipocyte-derived hormone primarily known for its metabolic functions, including glucose regulation, insulin sensitivity, and anti-inflammatory effects [7–9]. Recent evidence shows that adiponectin also plays diverse roles in tumor biology, although these effects appear to be tissue-specific. In several cancers, including breast and prostate cancer, adiponectin exhibits tumor-suppressive effects by inhibiting angiogenesis, reducing inflammation, and modulating cellular proliferation [10,11]. However, its role in melanoma remains incompletely understood. Some studies have examined the relationship between adiponectin levels and melanoma risk; however, this association did not reach statistical significance and remains unclear [12]. In parallel, adiponectin has been implicated in immune regulation and IL-17-related inflammatory processes, suggesting potential relevance to tumor microenvironment–associated mechanisms [13]. These observations indicate that, despite inconclusive epidemiological evidence, the biological role of adiponectin-related pathways in melanoma warrants further investigation.

Recent studies have suggested that adiponectin may be associated with immune-related processes in the tumor microenvironment, including effects on immune cell subsets such as regulatory T Cells (Tregs), NK cells, and memory B cells [13–15]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying these immune-related associations in melanoma remain largely unclear. Furthermore, several single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within adiponectin-related genes, such as rs182052 in ADIPOQ, have been linked to cancer susceptibility, but their functional relevance in melanoma has not been well characterized [16].

To address these gaps, this study systematically investigated adiponectin-related genes in melanoma using integrative bioinformatics approaches. We further incorporated gene expression–based in vitro validation and single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis to examine the associations of key genes with immune-related transcriptional features and cellular expression context. By integrating multi-level data, this study provides a transcriptome-based perspective on adiponectin-related gene patterns and their potential relevance to immune heterogeneity in melanoma.

Methods

Data source

Transcriptomic and clinical data of skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database [17]. A total of 103 primary melanoma tumor tissue samples with complete RNA-sequencing and survival information were included in the analysis. RNA-seq data were provided as raw read counts and annotated according to the Ensembl gene ID. ALB expression in this study was defined as tumor-derived mRNA expression obtained from transcriptomic datasets. Adiponectin-related data were acquired from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit Open Genome-Wide Association Study (IEU openGWAS) database [18], focusing on SNPs significantly associated with circulating adiponectin levels. SNPs with genome-wide suggestive significance (P < 5 × 10−6) were selected. Each SNP was mapped to its nearest gene using dbSNP and Ensembl annotations to identify adiponectin-associated target genes for downstream analysis.

Cluster analysis

To investigate molecular subtypes based on adiponectin-related gene expression, we performed unsupervised consensus clustering using the ConsensusClusterPlus package in R. The input data consisted of normalized expression values (transcripts per million, TPM) of adiponectin-associated target genes identified via SNP mapping and functional interaction networks.

Clustering was conducted using the “pam” algorithm with Euclidean distance as the similarity metric. The procedure involved 1,000 repetitions with 80% resampling of samples at each iteration to assess clustering robustness. The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined by evaluating the consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot and the delta area plot, where the inflection point (elbow) indicated the most stable partitioning solution.

Each melanoma sample was assigned to a cluster, and clinical outcomes (e.g., overall survival) were later compared across these subtypes.

Selection of differentially expressed genes

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 R package to identify genes differentially expressed between Cluster 1 and Cluster 3—two subgroups with the most distinct overall survival profiles in the prior clustering analysis. Raw read count data from TCGA melanoma samples were normalized using the median-of-ratios method to correct for sequencing depth and sample composition bias. Genes with an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 were considered differentially expressed. This threshold was selected to ensure both statistical significance and biological sensitivity, allowing detection of moderate yet potentially functional gene expression changes. The resulting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were used for subsequent enrichment and network analyses focused on adiponectin-associated pathways.

Enrichment analysis and protein-protein interaction network

To explore the functional relevance of the identified DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package. GO analysis covered three categories: molecular function, biological process, and cellular component, while KEGG analysis was used to identify significantly enriched signaling cascades involved in immune regulation, inflammation, and tumor progression. Only terms with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

To further assess potential protein-level interactions among DEGs, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed based on the STRING database with a minimum confidence interaction score of 0.7. The resulting interaction network was imported into Cytoscape (v3.9.1) for visualization and topological analysis. The cytoHubba plugin was used to calculate node degrees and identify hub genes based on centrality metrics. The most connected genes within the network were considered functionally important and were selected for downstream correlation analysis with immune cell infiltration and for in vitro experimental validation.

Functional validation of core genes and immune infiltration analysis

To explore the relationship between core gene expression and the tumor immune microenvironment, immune cell composition in each melanoma sample was estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 gene signature matrix and 1,000 permutations. Only samples with CIBERSORT output P-values < 0.05 were retained for further analysis to ensure reliable immune deconvolution. The relative abundance of 22 immune cell types was compared between different clusters using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. In addition, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between the expression levels of hub genes and specific immune cell populations, including regulatory T cells, resting NK cells, memory B cells, and mast cells.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

Publicly available melanoma single-cell RNA-sequencing data (GSE115978; 7,186 cells) were used to examine the cell-type distribution of ALB and immune-related genes (IL6, TNF, TGFB1, IL17A, and RORC). Cell-type annotations from the original dataset were used to define major populations, including malignant cells, immune subsets, and stromal cells. Average gene expression levels and the proportion of expressing cells were visualized using dot plots. Spearman correlation analysis was performed at the single-cell level to assess co-expression patterns among ALB and the selected immune-related genes.

Cell experiments

Cell culture and treatment

Human melanoma cell lines A375 (#CRL-1619) and SK-MEL-28 (#HTB-72) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

To investigate the immunomodulatory role of Albumin (ALB), both cell lines were transfected with either an ALB overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-ALB) or ALB-targeting small interfering RNA (si-ALB) using Lipofectamine 3000 or Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Control groups were transfected with an empty vector (pcDNA3.1, oe-NC) or non-targeting control siRNA (si-NC). All plasmids and siRNAs were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transfection efficiency was assessed 48 hours post-transfection by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) to confirm overexpression or knockdown of ALB. No exogenous human serum albumin or bovine serum albumin was supplemented during the experiments; all treatments focused on genetic modulation of endogenous ALB expression.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from treated A375 and SK-MEL-28 melanoma cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific), and 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The expression levels of ALB and immune-related cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-17A, RORγt) were quantified using gene-specific primers (Table 1). GAPDH was used as the internal control. PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.4.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). For in vitro experiments involving comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to assess statistical significance. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of adiponectin-related genes

Based on the adiponectin GWAS summary dataset comprising 1,338 samples, a total of 501,428 SNP loci were obtained for screening. Using a significance threshold of P < 5e − 6, five adiponectin-associated SNP loci were retained. These variants were further annotated using the dbSNP database [19] and mapped to their corresponding genes for downstream analysis. Results of cluster analysis

To identify molecular subtypes associated with adiponectin-related gene expression, consensus clustering was performed on TCGA melanoma samples using the expression profiles of genes mapped from the five adiponectin-associated SNP loci. The CDF delta area curve showed that clustering stability reached a plateau at k = 4, indicating that four clusters represented the optimal classification (Figure 1(A)). The consensus heatmap further supported clear separation among the four clusters (Figure 1(B)).

Consensus clustering analysis and survival differences among molecular subtypes. (A) CDF delta area plot showing changes in relative area under the CDF curve for k = 2 to 10. The inflection point indicates k = 4 as the optimal cluster number. (B) A heat map of sample clustering at consensus k = 4. (C) Kaplan–Meier survival curves comparing overall survival across the four clusters. (D) Pairwise survival analysis between Cluster 1 and Cluster 3.

Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis revealed significant differences in overall survival across the four clusters (P = 0.017, Figure 1(C)). Notably, patients in Cluster 1 exhibited the most favorable prognosis, whereas those in Cluster 3 had the poorest outcomes. A focused comparison between Cluster 1 and Cluster 3 demonstrated a statistically significant survival difference (P = 0.0042, Figure 1(D)), indicating that adiponectin-related expression patterns were associated with survival heterogeneity in melanoma.

These clustering results provided the basis for subsequent analyses of differential gene expression and immune infiltration to further explore the biological differences between subtypes.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

To investigate transcriptomic differences between adiponectin-related molecular subtypes, we performed differential expression analysis comparing Cluster 1 and Cluster 3, which exhibited the most pronounced differences in overall survival. A total of 702 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 430 significantly upregulated and 272 downregulated genes. The volcano plot (Figure 2(A)) highlighted the distribution of DEGs, with several upregulated genes such as DCD and SALL3, and downregulated genes like FGA and MYOC.

Red dots represent upregulated genes, blue dots represent downregulated genes, and gray dots indicate genes with no significant change. (B) MA plot showing the log2 fold change (M) versus average gene expression (A). Dotted line at M = 0 indicates no change in expression.

The MA plot (Figure 2(B)) further visualized the relationship between average expression level (A) and log-fold change (M), showing a symmetrical distribution of significantly altered genes. These findings indicate that adiponectin-associated expression patterns are linked to distinct transcriptomic profiles across melanoma subtypes and provide a basis for subsequent functional and immune-related analyses.

Functional enrichment and protein-protein interaction network analysis

To further characterize the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the identified DEGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the 702 genes. GO analysis (Figure 3(A)) showed significant enrichment in immune-related biological processes, including immunoglobulin-mediated immune response, B cell-mediated immunity, and leukocyte-mediated immunity. In addition, the DEGs were enriched in molecular functions such as antigen binding and organic acid binding. KEGG analysis (Figure 3(B)) further demonstrated significant enrichment in several immune- and inflammation-related pathways, including the IL-17 signaling pathway, complement and coagulation cascades, and antigen processing and presentation. These findings indicate that the genes distinguishing Cluster 1 from Cluster 3 are closely linked to immune-related processes and inflammatory signaling, suggesting that immune context may represent an important biological feature underlying adiponectin-related subtype heterogeneity in melanoma.

In parallel, a PPI network was constructed based on the DEGs (Figure 3(C,D)). Among the network nodes, ALB showed the highest degree (degree = 40), indicating a high level of connectivity within the interaction network. Other highly connected genes included EGF (degree = 19), LEP (degree = 18), and IL17A (degree = 14). Together, these results identified several highly connected candidate genes that may contribute to the immune- and inflammation-related differences observed between melanoma subtypes.

Functional validation of core genes and immune infiltration analysis

To further characterize the transcriptomic and immune-associated features of ALB as a hub gene, we compared its expression and immune correlations between melanoma subtypes. As shown in the boxplot (Figure 4(A)), ALB expression was significantly higher in Cluster 3 than in Cluster 1 (P < 0.05), suggesting an association with the poor-prognosis subtype. The PPI network (Figure 4(B)) showed that ALB was connected with multiple inflammation- and immune-related proteins, including SERPINA1 and ORM1/ORM2, consistent with its central position in the interaction network.

(B) PPI network of ALB and its interacting proteins related to immunity and metabolism. (C) Comparison of 22 immune cell types between clusters based on CIBERSORT analysis. (D) Correlation between ALB and four differential immune cell subsets. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

CIBERSORT-based immune cell profiling revealed distinct immune infiltration patterns between the two clusters (Figure 4(C)). Specifically, Cluster 1 showed higher proportions of resting NK cells and Tregs, whereas Cluster 3 was enriched in memory B cells and resting mast cells (P < 0.05). These findings indicate clear differences in immune contexture between the two subtypes. Correlation analysis (Figure 4(D)) further showed that ALB was positively correlated with memory B cells (R = 0.34, P = 0.012), whereas correlations with resting mast cells, resting NK cells, and Tregs were weaker and not statistically significant.

Taken together, these findings suggest that ALB expression is associated with selected subtype-related immune features in melanoma, particularly in relation to memory B-cell infiltration, and warrants further investigation.

Single-cell expression context of ALB and immune-related genes in melanoma

To determine the cellular sources of ALB and immune-related genes within the melanoma tumor microenvironment, publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE115978) were analyzed. Dot plot analysis showed that ALB expression was low and sparsely detected across both malignant and nonmalignant cell populations. In contrast, IL6, TNF, and TGFB1 were predominantly expressed by immune and stromal cell subsets, including macrophages and endothelial cells, whereas IL17A and RORC expression was largely restricted to T cell populations (Supplementary Figure S1(A) and Supplementary Table S1). Spearman correlation analysis at the single-cell level revealed minimal co-expression among ALB, IL6, TNF, TGFB1, IL17A, and RORC, including within malignant cells, indicating that these genes are not coordinately expressed within individual cells (Supplementary Figure S1(B) and Supplementary Table S2). These findings suggest that ALB-related signals observed in bulk transcriptomic data likely reflect broader tissue-level or multicellular patterns rather than coordinated expression within individual cell types.

In vitro transcriptional effects associated with ALB modulation in melanoma cells

To validate the bioinformatic predictions at the transcriptional level using a gene-centric in vitro modulation model, we performed ALB overexpression and knockdown experiments in A375 and SK-MEL-28 melanoma cell lines. As shown in Figure 5(A), qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that ALB mRNA expression was significantly increased in the oe-ALB groups and effectively suppressed in the si-ALB groups compared to their respective controls (P < 0.01), indicating successful genetic modulation.

(B) Relative mRNA expression of IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-17A, and RORγt in A375 cells after ALB modulation. (C) Corresponding mRNA expression profiles in SK-MEL-28 cells. In A375 cells, ALB overexpression was associated with significantly increased mRNA levels of multiple immune-related cytokines and transcription factors, including IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-17A, and RORγt (Figure 5(B)). Conversely, ALB silencing resulted in marked downregulation of these genes (P < 0.01). Similar trends were observed in SK-MEL-28 cells (Figure 5(C)), suggesting that ALB modulation was associated with changes in immune-related transcriptional profiles under in vitro conditions.

Collectively, these results provide preliminary experimental support for the bioinformatic analyses and suggest that modulation of endogenous ALB expression is associated with changes in immune-related gene expression at the transcriptional level in melanoma cells. However, these results are limited to mRNA-level observations and do not directly demonstrate cytokine secretion, immune cell interactions, or functional immune responses.

Discussion

This study analyzed melanoma transcriptomic data and adiponectin-related genetic variants to investigate the role of adiponectin-associated genes in the tumor immune microenvironment. Five significant SNP loci associated with adiponectin were found to be closely related to patient clinical prognosis. Differential expression analysis demonstrated 702 significant genes enriched in immune response and inflammatory pathways. Among them, ALB was identified as a key gene with high centrality in the PPI network. Correlation and clustering analyses further indicated that ALB expression was significantly higher in Cluster 3, a molecular subtype associated with poor overall survival, and positively correlated with memory B cell infiltration, suggesting that tumor-associated ALB expression is associated with immune-related transcriptional features in melanoma.

Our findings indicate that adiponectin-related genes may contribute to immune heterogeneity in melanoma. Functional enrichment analysis showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in IL-17 signaling, antigen processing, and complement-related pathways. Previous studies have emphasized the context-dependent roles of Th17-associated signaling in melanoma, with potential effects on both tumor-promoting inflammation and antitumor immunity depending on the surrounding cytokine milieu and cellular context [20,21]. In this setting, our data suggest that adiponectin-related genes may be linked to immune-related transcriptional variation across melanoma subtypes.

Of particular interest, ALB was identified as a core hub gene and was significantly correlated with memory B-cell infiltration. Memory B cells have been reported to contribute to antitumor immunity through antigen presentation and coordination of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses [14,15]. The positive correlation observed in our data suggests a potential association between adiponectin-related gene expression patterns and B cell–related immune features in melanoma. This observation is also broadly consistent with prior literature indicating that albumin-associated biological processes may intersect with immune-related and therapeutic contexts [22].

To further explore the relevance of ALB suggested by the bioinformatic analyses, we modulated endogenous ALB expression in melanoma cell lines and examined immune-related gene expression at the transcriptional level. qRT-PCR analysis showed that ALB overexpression was associated with increased mRNA levels of IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-17A, and RORγt, whereas ALB knockdown resulted in their suppression. These genes are commonly regarded as components or markers of inflammatory and Th17-associated pathways, particularly the IL-17/RORγt axis implicated in immune regulation [13,23]. Accordingly, our findings suggest that ALB modulation is associated with a Th17-related transcriptional signature in melanoma cells. Importantly, these observations remain limited to transcriptional changes and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of cytokine secretion, functional Th17 differentiation, or immune polarization.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis provided further context for these findings. ALB expression was low and sparsely distributed across both malignant and nonmalignant cell populations, whereas IL6, TNF, TGFB1, IL17A, and RORC were predominantly expressed in distinct immune or stromal cell subsets. These findings indicate that the ALB-associated signals identified in bulk transcriptomic analysis are unlikely to reflect coordinated expression within individual cells, but rather tissue-level transcriptomic associations across heterogeneous cell populations. Thus, the transcriptional changes observed after ALB modulation in melanoma cell lines should be interpreted as an in vitro gene-perturbation model, rather than a direct recapitulation of multicellular immune signaling in the tumor microenvironment.

This complexity is important when interpreting the potential biological role of adiponectin-related genes in melanoma. Although adiponectin is often regarded as an anti-inflammatory and tumor-suppressive factor in several metabolic malignancies, including breast and colorectal cancer [24–26], our data suggest that in melanoma, adiponectin-related transcriptional patterns involving ALB may be associated with poor-prognosis and immune-related features that coexist with pro-inflammatory and immune-regulatory contexts. Prior studies have shown that albumin can influence chemotherapeutic behavior through transport, tumor accumulation, and systemic metabolic interactions [27]. In addition, ALB-associated network interactions with proteins such as SERPINA1, LTF, and ORM1 may reflect broader inflammatory or immunometabolic states within the tumor microenvironment [28]. While albumin has been widely studied as a drug carrier that accumulates in tumors [29,30], our data more cautiously suggest an association between ALB expression and immune-related transcriptional patterns in melanoma.

Immune infiltration analysis further showed that Cluster 1, which had better survival, was characterized by higher resting NK cells and Tregs, whereas Cluster 3 showed increased memory B cells and resting mast cells. Despite the enrichment of memory B cells in Cluster 3, this subgroup showed worse survival, indicating that the immune-related associations of ALB may vary depending on the broader cellular and suppressive context. Additional mediators not assessed in the present study may also contribute to this complexity. For example, ox-LDL has been linked to impaired antitumor immunity and resistance to anti–PD-1 therapy [31]. Likewise, the inflammatory and antigen-presenting features observed in Cluster 3 may resemble immune contexts described in other aggressive tumors, where tumor antigens and checkpoint-associated suppressive signals coexist, such as the co-expression patterns involving NY-ESO-1, MAGE-A4, and PD-L1 reported in soft tissue sarcomas [32].

Taken together, these findings suggest that ALB expression may be associated with complex and context-dependent immune-related transcriptional features in melanoma, rather than a single uniform biological effect. This interpretation is more consistent with the broader literature describing the context-dependent nature of adiponectin signaling in cancer [24,26].

The clinical relevance of these observations should also be interpreted carefully. Our findings are based on tumor-associated ALB gene expression derived from transcriptomic analyses, whereas previous clinical studies have evaluated circulating serum albumin as a systemic prognostic biomarker in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors, including melanoma cohorts [33–35]. In this context, pretreatment hypoalbuminemia has been associated with poorer clinical outcomes, reflecting systemic nutritional and inflammatory status rather than tumor-specific transcriptional activity [36,37]. Although both may provide prognostic information, they reflect different biological levels and should not be regarded as interchangeable measures. Accordingly, clinical studies on circulating serum albumin in melanoma provide an important clinical context, but do not directly validate the tumor transcriptomic associations identified in the present study. Nevertheless, the poor-prognosis ALB-high Cluster 3 identified here may represent a relevant subtype for further investigation, particularly in relation to inflammatory pathway activity and future evaluation in the context of immunotherapy.

Despite these insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the transcriptomic and immune analyses were supported by gene expression–based validation in melanoma cell lines, the current in vitro experiments were confined to transcriptional analyses. Protein-level assays for cytokine secretion and RORγt expression, as well as dose–response experiments using exogenous albumin, were not performed and remain important for future study. Second, our conclusions were derived from public datasets and may not fully capture broader clinical or ethnic diversity. Third, although single-cell analysis improved cellular interpretation, further validation in independent cohorts and clinical samples would strengthen the biological and translational relevance of these findings. Finally, although ALB emerged as a prominent hub gene, its upstream regulation and downstream functional consequences in melanoma remain insufficiently defined.

Future studies should focus on clarifying the mechanistic significance of tumor-associated ALB expression in melanoma, including its relationship with inflammatory signaling, multicellular immune interactions, and treatment response. Functional validation using immune co-culture systems, protein-level assays, and clinical specimens will be important to further clarify the role of ALB, while in vivo models may serve as complementary approaches where appropriate. In addition, the biological distinction between tumor ALB expression and circulating serum albumin warrants more direct investigation.

In conclusion, this study systematically characterized the immune-related transcriptomic landscape of adiponectin-associated genes in melanoma and identified ALB as a hub gene associated with inflammatory pathway enrichment, specific immune features, and patient survival. Through integrative analysis and transcription-level experimental validation, our findings suggest that tumor-associated ALB expression may represent a transcriptomic feature linked to prognostic stratification and immune-related heterogeneity in melanoma, warranting further mechanistic investigation and clinical validation.
