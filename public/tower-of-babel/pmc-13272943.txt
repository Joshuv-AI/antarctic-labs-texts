Unusual terminational effect on atomic ripple of Ti3C2Tx MXenes
Authors: Minmin Liu, Liting Yang, Guanyu Chen, Zhengchen Wu, Xiaofen Yang, Guisheng Liang, Renchao Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Terminational chemistry traditionally influences MXenes’ carrier concentration and inter-flake spacing, and thereby electronic properties. Herein, the unusual terminational effect on the periodic atomic displacement, namely ripple, has been demonstrated. The rippling structure strongly depends on the atomic radii of halogen terminations, including -Cl, -Br, and -I. This originates from the atomic response to local strain fluctuation induced by terminations. The atomic ripple in Ti3C2Brx enhances electric coupling among adjacent atoms and promotes their charge transfer, thereby improving the electrical conductivity. Meanwhile, it significantly strengthens dielectric properties and markedly boosts the electromagnetic wave absorption capability. The maximum reflection loss value reaches −44.33 dB, and the effective absorption bandwidth is up to 3.94 GHz with a matching thickness of 1.40 mm. This work elucidated the effect of MXenes’ microstructure on their dielectric properties on the atomic scale and paved an avenue for the development of MXenes-based devices.

Introduction

Two-dimensional transition metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes), an emerging class of two-dimensional (2D) materials, have attracted extensive interest owing to their outstanding mechanical properties1–3, variable surface functional groups4–7, high density of vacancy defective sites8–10, and exceptional electrical conductivity11–14. These attributes endow MXenes with remarkable versatility across a broad spectrum of applications, including wearable electronics15, energy conversion/storage16–19, electromagnetic interference shielding/absorption20,21, wireless communication22, etc. For example, their high electrical conductivity coupled with redox-active surfaces enables fast and reversible charge transfer processes, positioning MXenes as compelling candidates for high-performance batteries, supercapacitors, and related energy storage systems19,23,24. Moreover, their unique flexibility, conductivity, and interfacial properties confer critical advantages in electromagnetic shielding and absorbing materials, mitigating inter-device interference and enhancing signal fidelity. Ti3C2Tx, as one of the earliest studied MXenes, has a 45-micrometer-thick membrane that demonstrates an electromagnetic interference shielding efficiency of 92dB, which is the highest among the synthetic materials of the same thickness produced to date20. In addition, Han et al. conducted a systematic investigation on the electromagnetic interference shielding performance of 16 different MXenes, demonstrating their great potential in this field25. Despite these advances, precise control over MXenes synthesis and the rational engineering of their microstructure and surface chemistry remain formidable challenges. Addressing these hurdles is pivotal for unlocking the potential of MXenes in future integrated device technologies.

Surface terminations, inherently introduced during MXene synthesis, profoundly influence their electronic structure and electrical properties by regulating the density of states near the Fermi level, as revealed by theoretical simulations and experimental studies. Hart et al. established a direct correlation between surface de-functionalization and enhanced electrical conductivity26, while Kamysbayev et al. demonstrated that surface groups define the nature of electronic transport in Nb2CTx MXenes, resulting in superconductivity behavior that depends on the surface terminations27. Beyond their electronic effects, surface terminations can also drive the evolution of layered architectures, thus offering additional degrees of freedom for tuning material properties without altering the in-plane framework. For example, Zhang et al. reported that substitution of -F groups with -Br enlarges the interlayer spacing and significantly improves ion transport capability28. By hydroxyl functionalization, Duan et al. modulated the interlayer spacing and electron transport pathways of Ti3C2Tx, which facilitated more efficient electron migration via surface conduction and interlayer hopping, consequently improving its electromagnetic interference shielding and microwave absorption properties29. Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of surface termination engineering in unlocking a rich chemical design space for MXenes as multifunctional materials. Nevertheless, despite substantial progress, the previous studies have predominantly focused on the effects of electronic states and interlayer spacing on electrical properties of MXenes, while overlooking the strain effect arising from termination-induced local atomic displacements and its impact on the electronic structure.

For conventional 2D materials such as graphene and MoS2, periodic atomic ripple patterns are known to modulate local potentials, offering a promising route for advanced electronic engineering30,31. While controlled ripple texturing has been extensively studied in these systems, the introduction of ripple structures in MXenes was first reported in 2023 by Ding et al.32. Subsequently, we successfully modulated the atomic ripple structures in Nb2CTex-based MXenes through regulating M-site configurational entropy33. To date, all reported ripples have been observed in terminated MXenes. Elucidating the underlying mechanism between surface terminations and ripple structures could provide new insights for the surface chemical design of MXenes and further advance our understanding of the terminational effect on electronic properties.

To address this knowledge gap, we developed a Lewis acidic molten salt etching strategy to selectively introduce various halogen surface functional groups (-Cl, -Br, and -I), enabling precise tailoring of surface chemistry34,35. We systematically investigated the relationship between halogen terminations and microstructural responses, including the interlayer spacing change and the emergence of atomic-scale ripple structures. Notably, Cl terminations form a well-ordered monolayer on Ti₃C₂ without introducing structural defects, maintaining a flat and uniform morphology. In contrast, substitution with the larger Br atoms induces periodic ripple structures at the atomic scale, while further replacement with I leads to a suppression of ripples and a pronounced expansion in interlayer spacing. Mechanistic analysis reveals that -Cl termination, with its smaller atomic radius, generates insufficient lattice strain to trigger ripple formation. In contrast, Br terminations introduce localized strain through surface lattice mismatch, giving rise to coherent ripple patterns with periodic atomic displacements. The even larger I atoms relieve this strain through vertical expansion rather than lateral distortion, thereby hindering ripple formation. The emergence of ripples in Ti3C2Brx enhances interatomic coupling, contributing to improved in-plane electrical conductivity. As a result, Ti3C2Brx exhibits outstanding microwave absorption performance, with a maximum reflection loss (RLmax) of −44.33 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 3.94 GHz. This work uncovers a mechanistic link between surface termination chemistry and atomic ripple structure in MXenes, offering a pathway to bridge microstructural engineering with macroscopic functional performance.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Ti3C2Tx

As shown in Fig. 1a and b, after CdCl2 etching of the Ti3AlC2 MAX phase, the Cl terminations were uniformly distributed on the surface, enabling the conversion from the MAX phase to MXenes. Subsequently, by changing the type of Lewis acidic molten salt, we further synthesized two different MXenes materials, Ti3C2Brx and Ti3C2Ix, thereby achieving the regulation of surface terminations on MXenes. Through X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization, we observed that the (104) diffraction peak of Ti3AlC2 at around ~39° significantly weakened or vanished, indicating that the Al atomic layers have been completely removed. Meanwhile, the shift of the (004) peak toward a lower angle suggests an increase in interlayer spacing, further confirming the formation of Ti3C2Tx MXenes. Due to differences in surface terminal atoms, the crystal structures and diffraction peak intensities of Ti3C2Tx MXenes vary significantly. As shown in Fig. 1c, we observed that the (004) crystal plane positions of Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix exhibit shifts at different angles compared to Ti3AlC2, indicating varying degrees of interlayer spacing changes. According to Bragg’s formula, the interlayer spacings for Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix are 11.2, 12.2, and 12.8 Å, respectively, showing an evident expanded tendency. This can be attributed to the progressively increasing ionic radius of halogen atoms (Fig. 1d), which leads to an increase in interlayer spacing34. The center-to-center interlayer spacing obtained from the HAADF images further demonstrates this expansion trend (Supplementary Fig. 1). To further investigate the species of surface terminations in Ti3C2Tx, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed (Supplementary Figs. 2–4, Supplementary Tables 1–3). As shown in Fig. 1e, the disappearance of the Al signal in Ti3C2Tx MXenes demonstrates the successful transformation from the MAX phase to MXenes. In addition, the characteristic peaks observed at 201 eV and 66 eV correspond to Cl and Br, respectively, while the peaks at 618 eV and 630 eV are attributed to element I. The appearance of these characteristic peaks further confirms the successful incorporation of halogen atoms at the T-sites of the Ti3C2Tx material. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that Ti₃C₂Clₓ, Ti₃C₂Brₓ, and Ti₃C₂Iₓ all exhibit a typical accordion-like microstructure, closely resembling those of MXenes previously obtained via HF or LiF+HCl etching methods (Supplementary Fig. 5). The size of these Ti3C2Tx MXenes ranges from 2 to 20 μm, further confirming the synthesis of halogen-terminated MXenes. The atomic-resolved energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping was conducted to shed light on the elemental organization of Ti3C2Tx. The results show that Cl, Br, I, and Ti all exhibit evident periodic distributions in correspondence with the atomic layer structure (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). The halogen terminal atoms are uniformly distributed on the surface of Ti3C2, while Ti atoms occupy the M sites. Moreover, there is no observed element aggregation. Terminations-modulated ripple structure in Ti3C2Tx and the mechanism analysis

Different surface terminations exert distinct influences on the microstructure of MXenes materials (Fig. 2a). To elucidate the atomic configurations of the three MXenes (Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix) materials, the atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was conducted, which enables precise analysis of atomic positions and arrangements within the material. Figure 2b (i) displays the atomic projections along [100] under electron beam illumination for Ti3C2Clx, it can be seen that the Ti3C2 sublayers still retain the characteristic zigzag structure typical of the Ti3AlC2 MAX phase (Supplementary Fig. 8). In addition, the etching of the A-site element Al creates atomic vacancies between the Ti3C2 layers, providing space for the intercalation of Cl⁻ ions. These Cl⁻ ions bond with Ti atoms, forming a more stable Ti3C2Clx phase. Notably, after changing the type of molten salt, Ti3C2Brx manifests a ripple-like atomic arrangement (Fig. 2c (i)). This should be attributed to the introduction of a large ionic radius Br-, which induces significant local in-plane strain within the Ti3C2 layers. However, when Br⁻ terminal groups are replaced by I⁻ ions, which have an even larger ionic radius, the ripple-like morphology in Ti3C2Ix nearly disappears (Fig. 2d (i)). This change indicates that the large radius of I⁻ ions enhances the repulsive interactions between the Ti3C2 layers, significantly enlarging the interlayer spacing. As a result, the local lattice strain is relieved, leading to the disappearance of the ripple structure. As described above, by utilizing the differences in the atomic radii of the surface termination groups, we achieved controllable modulation of the ripple structure in Ti3C2Clx MXenes, transitioning from absent to present and then back to absent. Besides, the brightness of atoms in HAADF images is proportional to the square of their atomic number. Therefore, the M-site and T-site atoms in Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix MXenes exhibit different levels of brightness contrast in the images. In summary, the construction and modulation of the ripple structure are intimately governed by the nature of the surface terminations. Firstly, regarding the formation of ripple structure, not all terminal groups with large atomic radii can cause the appearance of atomic ripple structures. Secondly, for the modulation of the ripple structure, atomic radii that are either too large or too small have a weaker impact. Therefore, it is necessary to select terminal groups within a specific atomic radius range to precisely control the ripple structure. To further confirm this, we performed inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) processing based on the HAADF images and obtained the corresponding IFFT image (Fig. 2f(ii)). Subsequently, overlaying the IFFT and HAADF images rendered the ripple structure more distinct and visually apparent (Fig. 2f(iii)), further confirming that among the three materials, only Ti3C2Brx exhibits the periodic ripple structure. To further uncover the mechanistic relationship between surface termination types and the regulation of ripple structures in Ti3C2Tx MXenes, geometric phase analysis (GPA) of HAADF images was conducted to examine the microstrain distribution and in-plane lattice parameter variation across different areas36. Figures 2b–d display the in-plane strain (εxx) distribution maps of Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix, respectively. In these maps, the color gradient from green to dark blue indicates increasing levels of compressive strain, while the transition from red to bright yellow shows progressively higher tensile strain, as referenced by the accompanying color scale. For Ti3C2Clx, the Cl atoms with a small radius are insufficient to cause lattice strain and local deformation, the εxx is randomly distributed (Fig. 2b (ii)). In sharp contrast, Ti3C2Brx exhibits a distinct periodic alternation between tensile and compressive strain regions, indicating that the large ionic radius of Br atoms induces a redistribution of in-plane strain. This leads to periodic atomic displacements, ultimately resulting in the formation of the atomic ripple structure (Fig. 2c (ii)). Interestingly, for Ti3C2Ix, which features surface terminations with an even larger atomic radius, the in-plane strain εxx reverts to a random distribution pattern (Fig. 2d (ii)). It can be inferred that, upon implanting large-radius I atoms, MXenes accommodate the strain induced by the I termination through interlayer spacing expansion, thereby further balancing the local strain. To more intuitively and quantitatively analyze the impact of surface terminations on the strain distribution, we performed a statistical analysis of regions 1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 2b–d). The results show that only Ti3C2Brx exhibits a periodic distribution of tensile and compressive strain (Fig. 2g). Our result indicates that the ripple structure in Ti3C2Tx emerges as a response to local strain. When the strain induced by surface terminations is released through mechanisms such as changes in interlayer spacing, the ripple structure vanishes, thereby enabling the construction and modulation of ripple structure in MXenes.

Correlation between ripple structure, atomic electric fields, charge distribution, and electronic behavior

The atomic ripple structure would trigger built-in electric fields, leading to the evolution of local potentials in MXenes and significantly influencing their electrical properties. This localized electric field modulation mechanism is crucial for understanding the electrical behavior of MXenes. To visualize and analyze the local electric field inside the materials, we employed differential phase contrast scanning transmission electron microscopy (DPC-STEM). This technology utilizes a sub-Å electron probe with ultra-high resolution, enabling precise acquisition of the directional orientation and intensity fluctuations of the electric field at individual atomic columns37,38. As shown in Fig. 3d–f, the DPC images (obtained from the HHADF images in Fig. 3a–c) of Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix are presented. In Ti3C2Brx, the introduction of the ripple structure induces an asymmetric distribution of the atomic electric fields, which leads to the merging of electric fields between adjacent atomic columns and ultimately results in a remarkable interatomic coupling effect. In contrast, in Ti3C2Clx and Ti3C2Ix, the electric fields of most atoms exhibit disk-like shapes, with only slight asymmetry observed in a few localized regions. This suggests that the electric-field coupling between these two materials is relatively weak. To further investigate the effect of ripple structures, regulated by surface terminations, on the charge density distribution of MXenes, we performed density functional theory (DFT) to calculate the charge density difference between the surface terminations and the M-site Ti atoms in Ti3C2Tx. The red regions represent electron accumulation, while the blue regions indicate electron depletion. As shown in Fig. 3g–i, the introduction of halogen atoms causes a redistribution of charge density in Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix, manifested as charge transfer from the termination atoms to the Ti atoms. Notably,the formation of the ripple structure facilitates electron transport and transfer across the entire Ti3C2Brx monolayer, resulting in pronounced interatomic coupling (Fig. 3h). In contrast, this phenomenon is not prominently observed in Ti3C2Clx and Ti3C2Ix. To further insight into the electromagnetic (EM) response of surface terminations regulated ripple structure to external electric fields, we measured the EM parameters of Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix using the coaxial method. Considering that all three materials exhibit a non-ferromagnetic characteristic, the discussion in this work primarily focuses on the complex permittivity (εr). Specifically, the real permittivity (ε′) signifies the energy storage ability, while the imaginary permittivity (ε“) reflects the capacity to consume EM waves, respectively40. As shown in Fig. 4d and e, Ti3C2Brx exhibits the highest values of ε′ and ε″. This can be attributed to the previously discussed ripple structures, which cause the atomic coupling within the MXenes layers. Such coupling strengthens charge transfer/transport, thereby enhancing the response of Ti3C2Brx to external electric fields. Moreover, the halogen terminations introduced in Ti3C2Tx are uniformly distributed on the surface of the Ti3C2 layers. These uniform and single-type surface functional group atoms exhibit a highly consistent response to the external electric fields, resulting in the emergence of distinct resonance relaxation peaks (Fig. 4e). These resonance relaxation peaks indicate the presence of polarization relaxation phenomena in Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix. To explain this phenomenon, we propose a microscopic dipole model41. The sample for electromagnetic measurement consists of a paraffin matrix and MXenes micro powder, in which the local structure of paraffin/MXenes/paraffin is regarded as a microcapacitor-like dipole. Under the influence of an external electric field, charge accumulates at the interface between Ti3C2Tx and the paraffin matrix. When the orientation of the external field is altered, the positive and negative charges accumulated at the Ti3C2Tx/paraffin interface slowly redistribute, causing the microscopic dipoles to flip. This process of charge rearrangement and dipole flipping results in a certain polarization relaxation time. Notably, due to differences in electronegativity and atomic radius among the halogen atoms, their responses to external electric fields differ, resulting in variations in both the position and intensity of their resonance relaxation peaks. Polarization relaxation is closely related to the material's dielectric loss. To further elucidate the dielectric loss mechanisms in Ti3C2Tx, plots of ε″ versus ε′ are analyzed (Fig. 4f), denoted as the Cole–Cole semicircle. Effect of atomic ripple structure on the microwave absorption performance of Ti3C2Tx

Additionally, we investigated the microwave absorption (MA) capacity of Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix MXenes with diverse surface terminations42. As displayed in Fig. 5a–c, at a thickness of 1.8 mm, the maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of Ti3C2Clx and Ti3C2Ix are 3.77 GHz and 3.23 GHz, respectively. In comparison, Ti3C2Brx exhibits preeminent EM wave absorption performance with a maximum EAB of 3.94 GHz at a thickness of only 1.4 mm. Meanwhile, the maximum reflection loss (RLmax) of Ti3C2Brx is -44.33 dB (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 12). Compared to most reported MXenes-based and other advanced absorber materials, Ti3C2Brx possesses superior MA performance in terms of RLmax, EAB, and thickness (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Tables 4–6). This can be attributed to the impedance matching (Z), which is a crucial parameter for evaluating EM wave absorbing properties43. For excellent absorbing materials, the Z value typically falls within 0.8–1.2, indicating that most EM waves are absorbed and converted to heat or other forms of energy, thereby enhancing absorption efficiency. Compared with Ti3C2Clx and Ti3C2Ix, Ti3C2Brx exhibits a significantly larger area approaching the ideal Z value within the thickness range of 1.4 to 3.0 mm, indicating better compatibility between the material and electromagnetic waves, thereby demonstrating excellent microwave absorption performance (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 13).Fig. 5Microwave absorption performances of Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXenes.a–c Reflection loss (RL) curve for Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix. d 2D RL diagrams of Ti3C2Brx. e Impedance match (Zin/Z0) of Ti3C2Brx. f Comparison of effective absorption bandwidth (EAB), thickness, and maximum reflection loss (RLmax) for the electromagnetic (EM) absorption properties of various Ti3C2Tx and its composites. Based on the measured EM parameters, radar cross-section (RCS) simulations were conducted to evaluate the EM absorption capabilities under actual far-field conditions44,45. As shown in the 3D RCS plots (Fig. 5g, supplementary Fig. 14), the perfect electric conductor (PEC) plane and the Ti3C2Tx-coated plate were investigated at 11.92 GHz with an angle range from −90° to 90°, where the plane waves were set as the excitation source. Apparently, the Ti3C2Brx exhibits the lowest scattering signal among all samples, implying more effective EM energy absorption. In addition, the angle-dependent RCS values are depicted in Fig. 5h, and it can be observed that the PEC coated with Ti3C2Brx gives out the weakest RCS signal among all detection angles. To more clearly compare the scattering and reflection ability, the RCS reduction values for Ti3C2Tx-coated were calculated by removing the RCS value of the pristine PEC conductor substrate at specific angles (0°, + 20°, + 40°, and + 60°) (Fig. 5i). It is evident that when the EMW is vertically incident to the model surface (the angle is 0°), all samples show the most efficient reduction results. Meanwhile, the RCS reduction values of the Ti3C2Brx coating are higher than the other two coatings at all detection angles. The RCS simulation results demonstrate that the Ti3C2Brx-coated is more favorable to the EM energy dissipation abilities, which is aligned with the above MA absorption performance.

In summary, we discovered a termination effect, namely localized ripple structures, within an already established Ti3C2Tx (Tx = -Cl, -Br, -I) MXenes system. In addition, geometric phase analysis (GPA) was employed to study the modulation mechanism of surface terminations on the ripple structure of Ti3C2Tx MXenes. The introduction of Br atoms induces local strain, leading to periodic atomic displacement. The resulting ripple structure generated an interatomic coupling electric field, which facilitated electron transport and transfer, thereby enhancing the electrical conductivity of Ti3C2Brx. As a result, a satisfactory strong intensity of microwave absorption (-44.33 dB) has been realized for Ti3C2Brx, with an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 3.94 GHz. Traditional termination effects suggest that the electronic properties of MXenes are primarily governed by interlayer spacing and Fermi-level electronic states. Our breakthrough discovery reveals that surface functional groups can modulate the dielectric properties of Ti3C2Tx by inducing the periodical strain and provides an effective pathway to customizing the electronic properties of functionalized MXenes.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Ti (300 mesh, 99.99%, Aladdin Reagent), Al (25 μm, 98%, Aladdin Reagent), C (300 mesh, 99.95%, Alfa Aesar). CdCl2 (anhydrous, 99.99%), CuBr2 (anhydrous, 99.99%), and CuI (anhydrous, 99.99%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. HCl (36-38%), NH3·H2O (25%-28%), and NH4Cl were obtained from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. All the reagents were used directly without further purification.

Preparation of Ti3AlC2 MAX Phase

First, the starting materials powders of Ti, Al, and C were weighed with a molar ratio of 3:1.3:2 (excess Al powder was added to prevent loss). Secondly, the powders were mixed and ground for 30 minutes. Then the mixture was transferred to the tube furnace and heated at 1400 °C for 3 h under the protection of Ar, with the heating rate is 5 °C min−1. After the reaction, the products were washed with HCl to remove the residual metals and dried at 40 °C. Finally, the Ti3AlC2 MAX phase was obtained.

Preparation of halogen-terminated MXenes

The Ti3AlC2 MAX phase and Lewis acid molten salt (CdCl2, CuBr2, and CuI) were mixed thoroughly under an argon atmosphere within a glove box. After that, the powder mixture was taken out of the glove box and removed to a tube furnace. Under the protection of argon gas, the reactants were heated to 650 °C, with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1, and maintained at this temperature for 5 hours. Upon cooling to ambient temperature, the products were washed with HCl, NH3·H2O/NH3NH4Cl to remove the by-products Cd, Cu, and the residual CuI. Finally, the halogen-terminated MXenes (Ti3C2Clx, Ti3C2Brx, and Ti3C2Ix) were obtained.

Preparation of TEM samples

The focused ion beam (FIB) technique was employed to fabricate the slices of various samples for TEM/STEM observation. Firstly, a Pt protective layer was created in the region of interest surface by electron-beam-induced deposition. Subsequently, the sample was cut into a lamella slice under the 30 KV accelerating voltage and 80 pA beam current. Finally, the slice was transferred to a FIB TEM half-grid by a micromanipulator needle for further thinning.

The inverse Fourier transform (IFFT) analysis procedure

A Fourier Transform of the atomic-resolution HAADF image was first performed to obtain Bragg reflections, which include not only the sharp spots associated with the intrinsic Ti3C2Brx lattice but also additional diffuse reflections. A small aperture was then applied to isolate the additional diffuse reflection, followed by an inverse Fourier Transform to reconstruct a two-dimensional lattice image that resolves the enhanced chemical ordering. Overlaying this IFFT image with the original HAADF image reveals bright contrast at specific sites, highlighting the ripple structures within the Ti3C2Brx MXenes.

The geometric phase analysis (GPA) method

The strain distribution was analyzed from the atomic-resolution HAADF image using the geometric phase analysis (GPA) method, a well-established technique for quantitatively mapping displacement and strain fields. GPA leverages spatial frequency components to capture local variations in the crystal lattice. Specifically, a Fourier Transform of the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image is first performed to obtain strong Bragg reflections, which are directly related to the crystalline structure. A small aperture is then applied to isolate a specific Bragg reflection, followed by an inverse Fourier Transform to reconstruct a two-dimensional lattice image that highlights local distortions. By applying this procedure to two non-collinear Fourier components, the displacement field is extracted, from which the strain components are derived.

Material characterization

The crystalline structure was characterized by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8-Advance diffractometer (Germany) equipped with Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation, operated at a scan rate of 2° min⁻¹. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi) was used to determine the surface elements in samples. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-4800,5 kV) in conjunction with EDS was performed to observe the microstructure and chemical composition. The atomic structural analysis (including STEM-EDS, STEM-iDPC, and STEM-HAADF) was conducted on a dual spherical aberration correction field-emission STEM (ThermoFisher Scientific, Spectra 300 TEM, American). MXene slice and four-electrode devices were fabricated using a Helios G4 CX focused-ion-beam (FIB) for atomic structure observation and conductivity measurements.

DFT calculations

The density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed by the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) based on first principles, with the version VASP 6.3.0. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) function was employed to calculate the electronic exchange-correlation energy. For Ti3C2Clx and Ti3C2Ix, the plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 400 eV, and the K-point mesh was set to 9 × 9 × 1. The convergence criteria were set to 1.0 × 10-5 eV for energy tolerance and 0.05 eV·Å-1 for force tolerance. For Ti3C2Brx, we set its curvature based on a sine trigonometric function. The plane-wave cutoff energy was 400 eV, and the gamma point was selected as the K-point, the energy convergence criteria were 1.0 × 10-5 eV.

Conductivity measurements

The Ti3C2Tx MXenes FIB slices were fabricated through a carefully designed and controlled focused ion beam (FIB) procedure. The sample lamellas were transferred to the nanochips and contacted via ion-beam-induced deposition; thus, a four-electrode device was fabricated, in which the two outer electrodes are used for current measurement, and the inner electrodes are designated for voltage sensing. Then the conductivity σ was determined using the following equations: R = U/I, σ = L/(R·a·b), where L and a represent the length and width, stemming from the front view SEM images, b corresponds to the thickness between the voltage-sensing electrodes, as measured from the top view SEM image.

Electromagnetic wave absorption test

In our experiments, the electromagnetic wave absorption performance was measured using the coaxial-line method. Specifically, the as-prepared MXene powders were uniformly mixed with paraffin wax, a dielectric-loss-negligible and microwave-transparent matrix, at a mass ratio of 3:2 (i.e., the MXene mass filling ratio was 60%). The mixture was then pressed into cylindrical ring samples with an outer diameter of 7.00 mm and an inner diameter of 3.04 mm, which matched the coaxial line sample holder. Next, the electromagnetic parameters were measured using a vector network analyzer (Keysight, N5230C) equipped with a coaxial line test fixture, covering the frequency range of 2–18 GHz. Measurements were performed in transmission/reflection mode, where both the reflection parameters (S11) and transmission parameters (S21) were recorded. The complex permittivity (ε′, ε″) and permeability (μ′, μ″) were then extracted from the measured S-parameters via the Nicolson–Ross–Weir (NRW) algorithm. Finally, the reflection loss (RL) values were calculated according to transmission line theory, based on the extracted intrinsic electromagnetic parameters and the actual sample thickness.
