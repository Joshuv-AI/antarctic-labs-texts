The Willmore energy and curvature concentration
Raz Kupferman*, Cy Maor*, David Padilla-Garza

Abstract
We study isometric immersions of a Riemannian surface ( Ω , 𝔤 ) (\Omega,\mathfrak{g}) , where Ω ⊂ ℝ 2 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} , into ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . We consider their bending energy, i.e., the square of the L 2 L^{2} -norm of their second fundamental form, which is equivalent to the Willmore functional. We obtain two new lower bounds for this energy, one in terms of the Gaussian curvature of the surface, and the other in terms of a Burgers vector—a measure of non-flatness connected to torsion. These new estimates provide optimal blowup rates of the energy when the curvature is concentrated (e.g., in a conical geometry). In the more subtle case of dipoles of concentrated curvature, we use the Burgers vector estimates to obtain an optimal blowup rate in terms of the size of the system. Our motivation comes from non-Euclidean elasticity, in which cones and curvature-dipoles play a central role. The lower bounds derived in this work directly yield lower bounds for the elastic energy of thin elastic sheets. The derivation of the curvature-based lower bound involves an isoperimetric inequality for framed loops, which we believe to be of independent interest.



Contents

1
Introduction and main results

This paper is concerned with lower bounds for the
bending energy
of an immersed surface
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

E_{B}(S)=\int_{S}|d\mathcal{N}|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}},

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{N}:S\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is the unit normal (the Gauss map) of
S
,
\mathfrak{g}
is its induced metric and
\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}
is the corresponding volume form.
The integrand is also equal to the square of the norm of the second fundamental form.
Since

|d\mathcal{N}|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}=4H^{2}-2K,

where
H
is the mean curvature and
K
is the Gaussian curvature, this energy is equivalent to the
Willmore functional
,

W(S)=\int_{S}|H|^{2}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}},

when considering immersions of a given closed manifold (in which case the integral of
K
is a topological invariant), or when considering isometric immersions of a given (generally non-closed) Riemannian surface
(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
.

These energy functionals have been studied extensively in relation to the Willmore conjecture
[
MN14
]
, to its gradient flow
[
KS02
]
, and to its critical points
[
Riv08
]
, to name a few directions.
Our initial motivation in studying this functional comes from material science, where the bending energy
E_{B}
plays a prominent role in the elastic theory of thin sheets; see Section
1.5
for more details.

A natural problem is to establish lower bounds on the bending energy, which is extrinsic, i.e., immersion-dependent, in terms of the intrinsic geometric data of the surface.
An elementary and well-known lower bound on the bending energy density of an immersed surface is

|d\mathcal{N}|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}\geq 2|K|,

which is simply the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality for the principal curvatures.
Integrating over the surface, this implies that for every isometric immersion of
(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
,

E_{B}(S)\geq 2\int_{S}|K|\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}=2\,\|K\|_{L^{1}(S)}.

(1.2)

This somewhat trivial lower bound fails to detect two phenomena: concentration of curvature and energetic super-additivity.
By
concentration of curvature
, we mean that it is more energetically costly to immerse isometrically a surface with a given total curvature
\|K\|_{L^{1}}
, when its curvature is concentrated; the example to have in mind is a cone versus a spherical cap having the same total curvature.
By
energetic super-additivity
, we mean that the infimal energy of an isometric immersion of a surface is generally greater than the sum of the infimal energies of isometric immersions of a partition of that surface (in the physics literature, this is sometimes known as superextensive energy).

The aim of this paper is to address the first phenomenon: obtaining a lower bound detecting curvature concentration.
Since this is a local phenomenon, we assume that the surface can be covered by a single chart
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
—that is, we consider immersions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We denote by
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
the
L^{p}
norm with respect to the metric
\mathfrak{g}
induced by the immersion.
With this notation, for
S=f(\Omega)
,

E_{B}(S)=\|d\mathcal{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}.

Our main result shows that, under some technical assumptions (see Theorem
1.7
),

\|d\mathcal{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}\geq\frac{4\pi-\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}}{\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}}\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}.

(1.3)

For simply-connected surfaces, the
L^{1}
and
H^{-1}
subscripts denote the standard Sobolev norms.
For multiply-connected surfaces (whence the subscript MC), they are defined such to encompass Gaussian curvature that may be “hidden in holes”; see below for exact definitions.
As neither of the
L^{1}
and the
H^{-1}
norms control each other, this lower bound is neither stronger nor weaker than (
1.2
).
However, it detects concentrations, and in particular, gives a sharp lower bound for the blowup of the bending energy of isometric immersions of cones (see Section
6
).
This bound extends results of Olbermann
[
Olb17
,
Olb18
]
, whose works motivated our approach; see Section
1.5
for a more detailed comparison.

A main tool in proving the bound (
1.3
) is the estimation of the
L^{1}
norm of
d\mathcal{N}
along closed curves on the surface.
To this end, we derive an isoperimetric inequality pertinent to
framed loops
(Theorem
1.8
), which we believe to be of independent interest.

There is an other instance of curvature concentration that neither of the bounds (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) detect: the case of
curvature dipoles
, where the curvature exhibits two loci of opposite signs.
The importance of this geometry stems from materials science, where curvature dipoles arise from a lattice defect known as an
edge-dislocation
. In this case, the presence of the defect is detected by a geometric measure known as a
Burgers vector
.

In Section
6
, we extend results of
[
Kup17
]
and derive lower bounds for the bending energy based on the Burgers vector content, rather than on curvature.
For cone singularities, this lower bound detects the bending energy blowups in a way that is complementary to (
1.3
)—the result is weaker for small total curvatures, but stronger for large ones (Theorem
1.12
).
Furthermore, and unlike (
1.3
), the lower bound based on the Burgers vector detects the blowup of the bending energy for curvature dipoles (Theorem
1.13
).

1.1
Definitions

In order to state our main results, we first need to introduce several definitions:

Immersions.

This work concerns immersions, which may be of regularity lower than
C^{1}
, or be only defined in open domains:

Definition 1.1

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open domain.
We say that a differentiable map
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an
immersion
if its derivative
df
has full rank, and the eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{g}=f^{*}\mathfrak{e}
, viewed as a map
\Omega\to{\operatorname{Hom}}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, are bounded and bounded away from zero. I.e., the metric and its inverse are bounded.
If
f
is a weakly differentiable map, then we require the conditions to hold almost everywhere.

Throughout this work, we denote by
C^{\infty}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(X;Y)
the space of smooth immersions
X\to Y
, by
C^{k}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(X;Y)
the space of
C^{k}
-immersions, and by
W^{m,p}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(X;Y)
the space of
W^{m,p}
-immersions.
For
p=2
, we write
H^{s}
rather than
W^{s,2}
.
Of particular importance will be the space
H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
In this case, the boundedness of the metric and its inverse imply that the unit normal is in
H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{2})
.

Frames along loops.

Much of the analysis in this work concerns so-called framed loops, which are defined below.
The analysis depends on whether a given frame along a closed curve can be obtained as a Darboux frame along the boundary of an immersed disc.
Here we explain this notion, and how it generalizes to the regularity of
H^{1/2}
frames which arise in our work.

We start with the smooth case.
Let
\gamma\in C^{\infty}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{3})
be an immersed closed curve, and let
\mathcal{N}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})
be a map satisfying

\mathcal{N}\perp\gamma^{\prime}.

We call such a pair
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
a
framed loop
, as it defines a
moving trihedron

(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{n},\mathcal{N})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};{\operatorname{SO}}(3)),

with

\mathfrak{t}=\gamma^{\prime}/|\gamma^{\prime}|\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{n}=\mathcal{N}\times\mathfrak{t}.

(1.4)

Note that not every map in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};{\operatorname{SO}}(3))
is induced by framed loops (for example, constant maps are not).

Definition 1.2
(Extendable framed loop)

We say that a smooth framed loop
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
is
extendable
if there exists an immersed disc
f\in C_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{\infty}(D;\mathbb{R}^{3})
, such that
\gamma=f|_{\partial D}
, and
\mathcal{N}
is the restriction to
\partial D\simeq\mathbb{S}^{1}
of the normal of
f
.

Thus, a framed loop is extendable if its induced moving trihedron can be realized as a Darboux frame at the boundary of an immersed disc.
Extendability has a more topological characterization (see Section
2
for a proof):

Proposition 1.3

A smooth framed loop
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
is extendable
if and only if it is isotopic to the framed loop
(\gamma_{0},\mathcal{N}_{0})
, where

\gamma_{0}(\varphi)=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0)\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{N}_{0}=(0,0,1).

(1.5)

Here, isotopy means that the loops can be connected by a smooth homotopy preserving the orthogonality of
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{N}
.

The isotopy of two framed loops
(\gamma_{i},\mathcal{N}_{i})_{i=1,2}
implies the isotopy of their associated trihedra
(\mathfrak{t}_{i},\mathfrak{n}_{i},\mathcal{N}_{i})_{i=1,2}
.
As the fundamental group of
{\operatorname{SO}}(3)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, the topological space
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},{\operatorname{SO}}(3))
has two connected components.
The trihedron
(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\mathfrak{n}_{0},\mathcal{N}_{0})
associated with
(\gamma_{0},\mathcal{N}_{0})
is a generator (i.e., a non-trivial element) of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
It turns out that the space of framed loops also has two connected components, i.e., that
(\gamma_{i},\mathcal{N}_{i})_{i=1,2}
are isotopic if and only if
(\mathfrak{t}_{i},\mathfrak{n}_{i},\mathcal{N}_{i})_{i=1,2}
belong to the same connected component of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},{\operatorname{SO}}(3))

[
Nee16
, Proposition 3.2.4, Corollary 3.2.5]
.
Thus, we conclude:

Corollary 1.4

A smooth framed loop
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
is extendable if and only if its associated trihedron
(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{n},\mathcal{N})
is non-trivial.

The natural space for our work are frames for which the associated trihedra have
H^{1/2}
-regularity, that is, framed loops
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
where
\gamma\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{3/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathcal{N}\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})
, in which the orthogonality of
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{N}
holds almost-everywhere; for brevity, we will refer to such framed loops as
H^{1/2}
-framed loops
.
In Section
2
we show that the definition of extendability extends naturally to this regularity.
Thus we define:

Definition 1.5
(Extendable
H^{1/2}
-loop)

Let
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
be a
H^{1/2}
-framed loop.
We say that it is
extendable
if its associated moving trihedron
(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{n},\mathcal{N})\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};{\operatorname{SO}}(3))
is
non-trivial
, i.e., is in the same connected component of
H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};{\operatorname{SO}}(3))
as
(\mathfrak{t}_{0},\mathfrak{n}_{0},\mathcal{N}_{0})
.

A framed loop
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
has an associated geodesic curvature,

\kappa_{g}=\left\langle\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}/|\gamma^{\prime}|,\mathfrak{n}\right\rangle

and consequently a total geodesic curvature,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\kappa_{g}\,d\ell=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\langle\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}(\varphi),\mathfrak{n}(\varphi)\right\rangle\,d\varphi=\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}[\mathfrak{n}],

(1.6)

where
d\ell
is the length element, and the last equality is understood as the action of the distribution represented by
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
on
\mathfrak{n}
.
This last equality shows that the total geodesic curvature extends to
(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{n},\mathcal{N})\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};{\operatorname{SO}}(3))
, since
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}\in H^{-1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathfrak{n}\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})
.
Note however that it is only the integral of the geodesic curvature that can be extended, and not its point values.

Finally, the notion of extendability extends to immersions of
2
-dimensional domains in the following sense:

Definition 1.6
(Extendable immersed surface)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open bounded domain, and let
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Denote by
\mathcal{N}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{2})
its unit normal.
We say that
f
is
extendable
if for every
C^{1,1}
simple closed curve
\Gamma\subset\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Omega\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, the trace
(f,\mathcal{N})|_{\Gamma}
is extendable according to Definition
1.5
.

The
C^{1,1}
regularity in the above definition is the regularity needed for the trace operator to map
H^{s}(\Omega)
into
H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma)
for
s=1,2

[
Gri85
, Theorem 1.5.1.2]
.

It follows from Lemma
2.5
below, that it is sufficient to check the extendability of
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})
for
C^{1,1}
generators of the fundamental group of
\Omega
.
In particular, if
\Omega
is simply-connected then every
f
is extendable, and if
\Omega
has a
C^{1,1}
boundary, then it is sufficient to check extendability for each connected component of the boundary.

Curvature norms on finitely-connected domains.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open Lipschitz domain homeomorphic to a disc
D
punctured by
m
(possibly zero) closed, disjoint discs
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}
.
Such domains are called
finitely-connected
.
Denote by
\Gamma_{0}=\partial D
and
\Gamma_{i}=\partial D_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
the outer and inner boundaries.
Let
f\in C^{2}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Omega\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu;\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and denote by
\mathfrak{g}=f^{*}\mathfrak{e}
the induced metric.
Suppose first that
\Gamma_{i}
are
C^{1}
-regular. For
i=1,\dots,m
, set

K_{i}=2\pi-\int_{\Gamma_{i}}\kappa_{g}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}\hskip 20.00003pti=1,\dots,m,

(1.7)

where
\kappa_{g}
is the geodesic curvature of
\Gamma_{i}
, and
\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}
is the induced length form on
\Gamma_{i}
.
In conjunction with the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, the quantity
K_{i}
represent the total Gaussian curvature “enclosed in the
i
-th hole”.
If
\Gamma_{i}
has only Lipschitz regularity, we can define
K_{i}
by taking a smooth simple curve
\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}\subset\Omega
homotopic to
\Gamma_{i}
,
and define

K_{i}=2\pi-\int_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}}\kappa_{g}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}-\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}K\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}},

(1.8)

where
\tilde{\Omega}
is the annulus bounded by
\Gamma_{i}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}
.
Similarly,
K_{i}
is well-defined for
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
, as in this case
\mathfrak{g}\in H^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
, hence one can find
\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}
as above such that
\kappa_{g}\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Gamma}_{i})
, and
K\in L^{1}(\Omega)
by Gauss’ Theorema Egregium (see the proof of Lemma
4.1
for similar arguments).

With this definition, we think of the curvature
K
as a function
K:\Omega\times\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\mathbb{R}
and define the
L^{1}
and
H^{-1}
norms as follows:
we define the total absolute curvature enclosed in
(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
by

\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}=\int_{\Omega}|K|\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}|K_{i}|.

(1.9)

To define the
H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
-norm, we let the space of test functions be the space
H^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
of
H^{1}
-functions vanishing on
\Gamma_{0}
and constant on
\Gamma_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, endowed with the homogenous norm
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
.
We define the norm of
K
dual to
H^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
by

\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}=\sup_{\varphi\in H^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\varphi K\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}K_{i}\varphi|_{\Gamma_{i}}}{\|\varphi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}}.

(1.10)

As with the standard
H^{-1}
norm, this norm equals to the
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
of the solution
u_{K}
of the Poisson equation
-\Delta_{\mathfrak{g}}u=K
with appropriate boundary conditions (this is the “Riesz representer” of
K
), see (
4.1
) below.
In Appendix
B
we study this problem, prove the existence of a solution and some standard estimates.
This duality provides an electrostatics interpretation of
\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
: Viewing
K
has a distribution of electric charge, the function
u_{K}
is the electric potential within that domain assuming that its outer boundary is grounded and that its inner boundaries are conductors loaded with a total charge
K_{i}
.
From this perspective,
\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}
is the corresponding electrostatic energy.

1.2
Main results: curvature estimates

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.7

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a finitely-connected, bounded, Lipschitz domain, and let
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
be an extendable immersion satisfying
one
of the following additional conditions:

1.

f\in C^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

2.

\mathfrak{g}=f^{*}\mathfrak{e}\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
for
p>1
.

Then

\|d\mathcal{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}\geq\frac{4\pi-\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}}{\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}}\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}.

Comments:

(a)

For a simply-connected domain, a corollary is that
K\in H^{-1}
, which is not immediate for
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap C^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
Indeed, if
\|K\|_{L^{1}}<4\pi
this is immediate, and otherwise one can partition the domain into finitely-many parts satisfying this bound.

(b)

For simply-connected domains, the triviality of the bound in Theorem
1.7
for
\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}\geq 4\pi
can be relieved,
as the bending energy can be bounded from below by the bending energy of a submanifold having total absolute curvature less than
4\pi
.
It is however not clear how to exploit in an optimal way a partition of the surface into subdomains.
For multiply-connected domains, the prefactor
4\pi-\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
is, in general, sharp; see comment at the end of Section
6.1
.

(c)

It is possible to extend our analysis to the non-extendable case.
For example, if
\Omega
is an annulus and
f
is non-extendable, then the same bound holds, replacing
K_{1}
with
K_{1}-2\pi
in the definitions of
\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
and
\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
.

(d)

Neither of the two additional conditions implies the other:
If
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}\cap C^{1}
then its metric is in general only in
H^{1}\cap C^{0}
.
On the other hand, a
H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}
map may fail to be
C^{1}
, even if the induced metric is smooth, and a fortiori if it is only
W^{2,p}
(except in special cases such as flat
[
MP05
]
or elliptic
[
HV18
, Theorem 2.1]
metrics).
We do not know whether the theorem can be extended to
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}
with a continuous metric, which seems to be the edge case in which the statement is still well-defined.

(e)

The bending energy, as well as both the
\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
and
\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
norms are dimensionless.
As a result there is not way to infer the functional dependence of the lower bound on those norms based on dimensional arguments.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
combines two parts: an isoperimetric inequality and an “electrostatics” analysis.
We derive an isoperimetric inequality pertinent to framed loops, which yields a lower bound for the derivative of
\mathcal{N}
along the loop.
The “electrostatics” part involves the foliation of
\Omega
with level sets of the “electric potential”
u_{K}
discussed above (the Riesz representer of
K
), and the application of the isoperimetric inequality on these level sets.
The two together yield the desired lower bound for smooth enough immersions.
The extension to
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}\cap C^{1}
is obtained by a direct approximation.
The extension to
f\in H_{\operatorname{Imm}}^{2}
maps with
W^{2,p}
metrics is obtained by approximating the metric by smooth metrics, and using the level sets of a potential obtained by solving the Poisson equation using the approximate metric.

We believe that the first part of the proof, which is the following isoperimetric inequality, is of independent interest:

Theorem 1.8

Let
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
be an
H^{1/2}
-framed loop.
Denote by
d\ell=|\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)|\,d\varphi
the arclength form and by
D_{s}\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}^{\prime}/|\gamma^{\prime}|
the arclength derivative of
\mathcal{N}
.
If
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
is extendable, then

\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|D_{s}\mathcal{N}|\,d\ell\right)^{2}\geq\left(4\pi-\left|2\pi-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\kappa_{g}\,d\ell\right|\right)\left|2\pi-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\kappa_{g}\,d\ell\right|,

(1.11)

where
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\kappa_{g}\,d\ell
the total geodesic curvature of
\gamma
as defined in (
1.6
).
Otherwise, if
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
is non-extendable, then

\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|D_{s}\mathcal{N}|\,d\ell\right)^{2}\geq\left(4\pi-\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\kappa_{g}\,d\ell\right|\right)\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\kappa_{g}\,d\ell\right|.

(1.12)

Comments:

(a)

The left-hand sides of (
1.11
) and (
1.12
) are finite if and only if
\mathcal{N}\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})
.

(b)

By Definition
1.2
, if
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
is extendable and smooth, then there exists an immersion
f\in C^{\infty}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu;\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
\gamma=f|_{{\partial D}}
and
\mathcal{N}
is the restriction of its Gauss map at the boundary.
Let
f
be any such immersion, and denote by
\mathfrak{g}
the induced metric and by
K
the corresponding Gaussian curvature.
It follows from the Gauss-Bonnet theorem
[
Car76
, p. 264]
that (
1.11
) can be rewritten in the form

\left(\int_{{\partial D}}|d\mathcal{N}|_{\mathfrak{g}}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}\right)^{2}\geq\left(4\pi-\left|\int_{D}K\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}\right|\right)\left|\int_{D}K\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}\right|.

(1.13)

In fact, we first prove (
1.13
), and the inequality (
1.11
) is obtained by interpreting the moving trihedron induced by
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
as the Darboux frame of the boundary of an immersed disc.
Interestingly, the extension of the moving trihedron into an immersed disc is essential to the proof, even though the end result, (
1.11
), is oblivious to the extension.

The proof of Theorem
1.8
is obtained by first
identifying the left-hand side in (
1.11
) as the length of the image in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
of the Gauss map.
Weiner’s isoperimetric inequality for non-simple closed curves on the sphere
[
Wei74
]
lower bounds the length of that curve by an integral of an appropriate notion of its winding number;
we then consider an immersion
f:D\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose boundary is the given framed loop (assuming it is extendable), identifying the winding number of the boundary with the degree of the Gauss map, estimating the integral of the degree using Gauss’s theorem, and then using Gauss–Bonnet to obtain the final result.
For non-extendable framed loops, we first make them extendable by concatenating them with the extendable framed loop
(\gamma_{0},\mathcal{N}_{0})
given by (
1.5
) (in the language of
\pi_{1}({\operatorname{SO}}(3))
, we concatenate a trivial loop with a generator).

1.3
Main results: Burgers circuits estimates

Theorem
1.8
is vacuous when the integral of the geodesic curvature is too large; namely larger than
6\pi
in the extendable case or
4\pi
in the non-extendable case.
Even more importantly, it is vacuous when the integral of the geodesic curvature is exactly
2\pi
, which
is the case when the total signed curvature is zero, as in the presence of curvature dipoles, as appearing in many physical systems involving dislocations (see Section
1.4
).
To treat these cases, we complement Theorem
1.8
by another lower bound pertinent to framed loops, one based on the detection of torsion, rather than the detection of curvature.
Guided by the crystallographic theory of dislocations,
this bound is based on a notion of
Burgers vector
(for the relation between Burgers vectors and torsion see
[
KM15
,
KM16
,
EKM20
,
KO20
]
).

A smooth framed loop
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
induces a parallel transport of vectors perpendicular to
\mathcal{N}
, i.e., in the span of
\mathfrak{t}
and
\mathfrak{n}
. A vector field
X\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{3})
of the form

X=a\,\mathfrak{t}+b\,\mathfrak{n}\hskip 20.00003pta,b\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),

is parallel with respect to the induced moving trihedron if

X^{\prime}=\left\langle X^{\prime},\mathcal{N}\right\rangle\mathcal{N}=-\left\langle X,\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\right\rangle\mathcal{N},

(1.14)

where the second equality follows from the fact that
\left\langle X,\mathcal{N}\right\rangle=0
.
This defines a parallel transport operator

\Pi^{\gamma,\mathcal{N}}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}:\operatorname{span}\{\mathfrak{t}(\varphi_{1}),\mathfrak{n}(\varphi_{1})\}\to\operatorname{span}\{\mathfrak{t}(\varphi_{2}),\mathfrak{n}(\varphi_{2})\}

transporting vectors normal to
\mathcal{N}
from
\varphi_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
to
\varphi_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
according to (
1.14
) along the shortest negatively-oriented path.
That is, for
v=a_{0}\,\mathfrak{t}(\varphi_{1})+b_{0}\,\mathfrak{n}(\varphi_{1})
, the vector
\Pi^{\gamma,\mathcal{N}}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}v
is the evaluation at
\varphi_{2}
of the solution to (
1.14
) with initial condition
v
.
It can be shown that the parallel transport depends, in fact, only on the geodesic curvature
\kappa_{g}
, and, if
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
induces a Darboux frame at the boundary of an immersed surface, the parallel transport defined by (
1.14
) coincides with that of the surface.

With that, we assign a notion of a Burgers vector to framed loops, as the integral of the velocity
\gamma^{\prime}
of the loop, which is parallel-transported from its point of evaluation to the base point
\varphi_{0}
(see Figure

The Burgers vector associated with a smooth framed loop

is a vector in
\operatorname{span}\{\mathfrak{t}(\varphi_{0}),\mathfrak{n}(\varphi_{0})\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by

(1.15)

Since

Proposition 1.10

An equivalent definition for the Burgers vector associated with a framed loop

B_{\gamma,\mathcal{N},0}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\left\langle\mathcal{L}(\varphi),\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(\varphi)\right\rangle\mathcal{N}(\varphi)\,d\varphi=\mathcal{N}^{\prime}[\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{N}],

The proof is given in Section

Theorem 1.11

Let

be an

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|D_{s}\mathcal{N}|\,d\ell\geq\frac{|B_{\gamma,\mathcal{N},\varphi_{0}}|}{\operatorname{Length}(\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1}))},

(1.16)

1.5
Related work

Comparison with results of Olbermann on cones.

Inequality (
1.13
) should be compared with the isoperimetric inequality obtained by Olbermann
[
Olb17
, Lemma 1]
:

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the unit disc and let
u\in C^{2}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)
. Then,

\left(\int_{{\partial D}}|D^{2}u|\,ds\right)^{2}\geq 4\pi\left|\int_{D}\det D^{2}u\,dx\right|.

(1.21)

Inequality (
1.21
) can be interpreted as a linearization of (
1.13
).
Specifically, consider an immersed surface
f\in C^{\infty}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(D;\mathbb{R}^{3})
obtained by the graph of a function

f(x,y)=(x,y,\varepsilon^{1/2}\,u(x,y)),

where we fix
u\in C^{\infty}(D)
and view
\varepsilon>0
as a small parameter.
Denote by
\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}
the
\varepsilon
-dependent induced metric and by
K_{\varepsilon}
the corresponding Gaussian curvature.
By (
1.13
), for every
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\left(\int_{{\partial D}}|d\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{2}\geq\left(4\pi-\left|\int_{D}K_{\varepsilon}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}}\right|\right)\left|\int_{D}K_{\varepsilon}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}}\right|.

(1.22)

A direct power expansion yields,

K_{\varepsilon}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon\,\det D^{2}u\,dx+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

and

|d\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}}}=\varepsilon^{1/2}\,|D^{2}u|\,ds+O(\varepsilon^{3/2}),

hence (
1.21
) is obtained by differentiating (
1.22
) with respect to
\varepsilon
at zero.

The tools in the proof of Theorem
1.8
and
[
Olb17
, Lemma 1]
are similar, combining degree theory and an isoperimetric inequality.
In our nonlinear setting, the analysis is carried out on the unit sphere, which is the codomain of the Gauss map. It requires the use of an isoperimetric inequality for non-simple closed curves on the sphere
[
Wei74
]
, identifying the integral of
|d\mathcal{N}|_{\mathfrak{g}}
with the length of
\mathcal{N}({\partial D})\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and combining the appropriate notions of degree with Gauss’s theorema egregium.

Our method of proof of Theorem
1.7
hinges on some of the ideas used by Olbermann
[
Olb17
,
Olb18
]
, with the following major differences:
(i) Olbermann’s work focuses on one specific geometry—a cone of positive curvature—whereas our analysis applies to arbitrary surfaces.
(ii) The foliation of the surface in
[
Olb17
,
Olb18
]
uses the level sets of a logarithmic function;
as our work shows, this gives an optimal bound because this function is the ”Riesz representer” of the
H^{-1}
-norm of the curvature of the truncated cone.
This observation enables the identification of the bound with a negative Sobolev norm of the curvature, and consequently the generalization to general geometries, and multiply-connected domains.
(iii) The bending energy functional used in
[
Olb17
,
Olb18
]
, which is the
L^{2}
-norm squared of the Laplacian of the configuration, is only a linearization of the true (geometric, nonlinear) bending energy.
The bound obtained by Olbermann for this (linearized) energy is similar to (
1.17
) — namely, has a logarithm divergence, and a linear dependence on
\alpha
for small
\alpha
.

On the other hand,
[
Olb17
,
Olb18
]
address a wider scope, in which
one distinguishes between a “reference” metric
\mathfrak{g}
and the “actual” metric
f^{*}\mathfrak{e}
induced by the immersion. The reference metric is the one with respect to which norms and the volume form are evaluated.
The total energy consists of the bending energy supplemented by a penalization—a
stretching energy
—for the discrepancy between the reference and actual metrics.
Extending our nonlinear analysis to allow for metric deformations is left for future work.

Relations to non-Euclidean elasticity.

In a broader context, this work relates to a fundamental problem in Riemannian geometry: estimating the minimal amount of deformation required to immerse one Riemannian manifold into another.
This problem has a direct application to
incompatible elasticity
(also known as
non-Euclidean elasticity
), where the elastic body is a
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with boundary
(M,\mathcal{G})
, and the ambient space is a
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary—Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathfrak{e})
in most applications.
A configuration of the body is an immersion
f:M\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the elastic energy associated with a configuration
f
is a measure of metric discrepancy between the actual metric of the body,
f^{*}\mathfrak{e}
, and its intrinsic reference metric
\mathcal{G}
(note that unlike above, here the codimension is zero).
Heuristically, for orientation preserving maps, this energy is of the type

E_{(M,\mathcal{G})}(f)=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{M}|f^{*}\mathfrak{e}-\mathcal{G}|^{2}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathcal{G}},

where
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int
is the integral divided by the total volume.
The fundamental problem is to estimate the infimal elastic energy in terms of the geometry of the body, notably its curvature.

This problem as stated is widely open.
A first attempt to address it is due to Kupferman and Shamai
[
KS12
]
, resulting in a rather obscure lower bound applicable only in the case of positive scalar curvature.
Lewicka and Mahadevan conjectured in
[
LM22
]
that the infimal energy scales like the
H^{-2}
norm of the Riemann curvature tensor, whereas Kupferman and Maor
[
KM25a
]
showed that this conjecture holds asymptotically in the limit of vanishing curvature.

Another asymptotic regime, of important physical relevance, is that of thin bodies, where
M=M_{t}
is a
t
-tubular neighborhood of a
k
-dimensional submanifold
(S,\mathfrak{g})
(in applications, we typically have
d=3
and
k=1,2
).
In this case, it was shown in
[
MS19
]
that, for
t\ll 1
,

\inf E_{(M_{t},\mathfrak{g})}\gtrsim t^{4}\|R^{\mathcal{G}}\|_{L^{2}(S)}^{2},

where
R^{\mathcal{G}}
is the Riemann curvature tensor of
\mathcal{G}
.
This result is optimal in the case of
k=0,1
(for
k=0
it was generalized to general ambient space in
[
KM25b
]
), but not for higher
k
; indeed, it follows from dimension-reduction results
[
LP11
,
KS14
]
that, in the case that
S
is totally-geodesic in
M
(so-called
non-Euclidean plate theory
), we have that

\inf E_{(M_{t},\mathfrak{g})}=t^{2}\inf E_{B}+o(t^{2}),

where
E_{B}
is as in (
1.1
) (or a higher dimensional analogue), restricted to isometric immersions of
(S,\mathfrak{g})
.
In this context, our work provides a new lower bound to the elastic energy of thin plates.

Relations to compensated compactness results.

As
K
is, by Gauss’ theorema egregium, the Jacobian determinant of the normal
\mathcal{N}
, our result shows that the
\dot{H}^{1}
norm of
\mathcal{N}
controls an
H^{-1}
norm of its Jacobian determinant.
This is reminiscent of the fundamental result of Coifman et al.
[
CLMS93
]
for functions in
W^{1,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, in which the
n
-th power of the
\dot{W}^{1,n}
norm controls the Hardy
\mathcal{H}^{1}
norm of the Jacobian determinant; note that
\mathcal{H}^{1}
controls both
L^{1}
and
H^{-1}
for
n=2
(interestingly, a precursor of this result
[
Mül90
]
also uses an isoperimetric inequality to obtain higher regularity of the determinant).
For sphere-valued maps, Müller and Šverák
[
MS95
]
has even more similar results, relating the
\dot{H}^{1}
norm of a sphere-valued map to the
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-norm (Corollary 3.3.2) and the
H^{-1}
-norm (Proposition 3.5.5, Theorem 3.5.6) of its Jacobian determinant;
interestingly, a constant similar to the
4\pi-\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
prefactor in (
1.3
) appears there as well.
Despite these striking similarities, we were not able to identify a direct relation between these results and ours.
Note, in particular, that these estimates in
[
MS95
]
do not detect the curvature “in the holes” which is essential to our analysis, and that their
\dot{H}^{1}
and
H^{-1}
norms are somewhat different from ours, as they consider maps from the whole plane, with its Euclidean volume form, to the sphere, whereas in our case, both the volume form on the domain and the Gauss map to the sphere depend on the underlying immersion.

Proof of Proposition

:
The immersion

.
In the first direction, let
(\gamma_{1},\mathcal{N}_{1})\sim(\gamma_{0},\mathcal{N}_{0})
. By
[

, to every smooth isotopy

connecting

and

there exists a corresponding variation of smooth immersions

assuming these boundary data (immersions

are said to be flexible).
That is, the framed loop
(\gamma_{1},\mathcal{N}_{1})

The reverse claim hinges on

is extendable and let

be a corresponding extension.
First, using affine transformations, modify
f
smoothly such that
f(0)=0
and
df_{0}=({\operatorname{Id}},0)
.
Then, consider the homotopy of immersions,

H_{t}(r,\varphi)=\frac{f(tr,\varphi)}{t}

As

,
H_{t}
tends to
f_{0}
, hence every

With these two lemmas, we can now justify Definition

Proposition 2.3

The space of
H^{1/2}
-framed loops
(\gamma,\mathcal{N})
has two connected components, corresponding to the two connected components of

.
Namely, any two framed loops

such that their associated frames

In particular, smooth framed loops are dense in the space of

Proof.

The fact that

, Prop. 2.6]
, which also implies that if
(\mathfrak{t}_{i},\mathfrak{n}_{i},\mathcal{N}_{i})_{i=1,2}

The argument of

(which is based on the Smale-Hirsch theorem for the inclusion of framed loops into the space of “formal”, i.e., non-holonomic, framed loops) shows that if

This argument, while stated for smooth framed loops, works also in the regularity of the current proposition, i.e., for

and
\mathcal{N}_{i}\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})

.

Lemma 2.4

\displaystyle\inf_{s\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}(\varphi)-\gamma^{\prime}(s)|

\tilde{\alpha}_{n}=\alpha_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\psi^{i}\,(c^{i}_{n}\times\mathcal{N}_{n}).

satisfies the required conditions.
If the span of

Isoperimetric inequality for framed loops: Proof of Theorem

We start by considering isoperimetric inequalities on the sphere.
Weiner
[

]
proved the following inequality, as a particular case of a broader theorem, based on techniques developed by Banchoff and Pohl
[
BP71
]
:

Theorem 3.1
(Weiner, 1974)

Let
\gamma\in C^{2}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})
be an immersed loop on the sphere and let
\Gamma=\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. The total length
L
of
\Gamma
satisfies the isoperimetric inequality,

L^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}w_{\Gamma}^{2}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\wedge\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}},

where for
p,q\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
w_{\Gamma}(p,q)
is the (signed, or directed) number of intersections of
\Gamma
with a geodesic segment connecting
p
and
q
, and
\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
is the volume element of the sphere.

\Gamma
p
q

For
w_{\Gamma}(p,q)
, which Weiner refers to as a winding number, to be well-defined, the geodesic from
p
to
q
has to intersect
\Gamma
transversely. One can show that for rectifiable curves (and a fortiori for
C^{2}
-immersions),
w_{\Gamma}
is well-defined on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, except possibly on a set of measure zero, and is furthermore integrable (being piecewise constant).
Likewise, since antipodal points have measure zero in
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we may define
w_{\Gamma}
using the shortest geodesic. Alternatively,
w_{\Gamma}(p,q)
can be defined everywhere on
(\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\Gamma)\times(\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\Gamma)
by approximating
\Gamma
uniformly by smooth curves nowhere tangent to the geodesic from
p
to
q
, using the fact that
w_{\Gamma}
is homotopy-invariant.

Theorem
3.1
also holds for maps
\gamma\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})
that are not necessarily immersions. Indeed,
C^{2}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})
is dense in
C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{S}^{2})

[
Hir76
, Thm. 2.12]
, hence the extension follows by approximation.

We proceed to use this isoperimetric inequality in the context of the Gauss map of a disc

immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{\operatorname{Imm}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu;\mathbb{R}^{3})
be an immersion with
\mathcal{N}\in C^{\infty}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu;\mathbb{S}^{2})

The curve
\Gamma=\mathcal{N}({\partial D})
partitions

\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I}

, we denote by

the connected component containing

Examples

6.1
Cones: proof of Theorem
1.12

A cone is a locally-flat surface whose curvature is concentrated at a single point—the apex.
The curvature may be positive or negative; cones having positive curvature can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as surfaces of revolution, unlike cones having negative curvature, which are known in the physics literature as
E-cones
(the E stands for
excess
)
[
MAG08
]
.

Cones of either type can be parametrized using polar coordinates,

(r,\varphi)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{1},

along with a metric of the form

\mathfrak{g}=dr\otimes dr+(c_{\alpha}r)^{2}\,d\varphi\otimes d\varphi,

where

c_{\alpha}=1-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}.

The parameter
\alpha
is the angular deficit/excess of the cone; it is in the range
(0,2\pi)
for positively-curved cones, and negative for E-cones.
A direct calculation via the Christoffel symbols shows that, as expected,
K=0
. On the other hand, let
\Gamma
be any generator of the fundamental group, then

\int_{\Gamma}\kappa_{g}\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}=2\pi-\alpha,

which shows that the curvature is
\delta
-measured at the origin, with intensity
\alpha
.

We truncate the cone into a frustum by letting
r\in(r_{0},R)
.
Thus, we obtain a locally-Euclidean annulus,
\Omega
, endowed with a locally-Euclidean metric
\mathfrak{g}
.
As before, we denote the outer boundary by
\Gamma_{0}
and the inner boundary by
\Gamma_{1}
.
The curvature enclosed in the hole is
K_{1}=\alpha
.

We proceed to solve the elliptic boundary-value problem (
4.1
) for this geometry.
Since the geometry is radially-symmetric, so is the solution
u_{K}
.
The Laplace-Beltrami equation turns out not to depend on
\alpha
, and reads

\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\,\frac{\partial u_{K}}{\partial r}\right)=0,

with boundary conditions

u_{K}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0\hskip 20.00003ptu_{K}|_{\Gamma_{1}}=\text{const}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003pt2\pi c_{\alpha}r_{0}\left.\frac{\partial u_{K}}{\partial r}\right|_{\Gamma_{1}}=-\alpha.

The solution can be written down explicitly,

u_{K}=-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi c_{\alpha}}\log\frac{r}{R}.

The relevant norms of
K
are

\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}=|\alpha|

and

\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}=\|u_{K}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{r_{0}}^{R}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi c_{\alpha}r}\right)^{2}c_{\alpha}r\,drd\varphi=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\pi c_{\alpha}}\log\frac{R}{r_{0}}.

We can now apply Theorem
1.7
to any extendable isometric immersion of
(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})
and complete the proof of the first part of Theorem
1.12
.

We proceed to prove the second part of Theorem
1.12
.
Consider the loop
\gamma:[0,2\pi]\to\Omega
, given in coordinates by

\gamma(t)=(r,t),

for some
r\in(r_{0},R)
.
Let its base point be
(r,0)
.
Using that

\displaystyle e_{1}

\displaystyle=\cos(c_{\alpha}\varphi)\,\partial_{r}+\sin(c_{\alpha}\varphi)\,(c_{\alpha}r)^{-1}\partial_{\varphi}

\displaystyle e_{2}

\displaystyle=-\sin(c_{\alpha}\varphi)\,\partial_{r}+\cos(c_{\alpha}\varphi)\,(c_{\alpha}r)^{-1}\partial_{\varphi}

are parallel orthonormal fields in
\{\varphi\neq 0\}
,
a direct calculation shows that

\Pi^{\gamma,\mathcal{N}}_{t,0}\partial_{\varphi}|_{(r,t)}=(c_{\alpha}r)\sin(c_{\alpha}t)\,\partial_{r}|_{(r,0)}+\cos(c_{\alpha}t)\,\partial_{\varphi}|_{(r,0)}.

The corresponding Burgers vector is given by

\displaystyle B_{\gamma,\mathcal{N},0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Pi^{\gamma,\mathcal{N}}_{t,0}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Pi^{\gamma,\mathcal{N}}_{t,0}\partial_{\varphi}|_{(r,t)}\,dt

\displaystyle=r(1-\cos(2\pi c_{\alpha}))\partial_{r}|_{(r,0)}+\frac{1}{c_{\alpha}}\sin(2\pi c_{\alpha})\partial_{\varphi}|_{(r,0)}.

Therefore,

|B_{\gamma,\mathcal{N},0}|=2r|\sin(\pi c_{\alpha})|,

and thus, by Theorem
1.11
,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|D_{s}\mathcal{N}|\,d\ell\geq\frac{2r|\sin(\pi c_{\alpha})|}{2\pi c_{\alpha}r}=\frac{|\sin(\pi c_{\alpha})|}{\pi c_{\alpha}}.

Thus, for any isometric immersion
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\displaystyle\|d\mathcal{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{r_{0}}^{R}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|D_{s}\mathcal{N}|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}\,d\ell\right)dr\geq\int_{r_{0}}^{R}\frac{1}{2\pi c_{\alpha}r}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|D_{s}\mathcal{N}|_{\mathfrak{g}}\,d\ell\right)^{2}dr

(6.1)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{r_{0}}^{R}\frac{1}{2\pi c_{\alpha}r}\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi c_{\alpha})}{(\pi c_{\alpha})^{2}}dr=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi c_{\alpha})}{2\pi^{3}c_{\alpha}^{3}}\,\log\frac{R}{r_{0}},

which completes the proof of the second part of Theorem
1.12
.

Comment:

Whenever
\alpha=-2\pi k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an isometric immersion with zero bending:

f(r,\varphi)=r(\cos(k+1)\varphi,\sin(k+1)\varphi,0).

This immersion is extendable for even
k
, but not for odd
k
.
This shows that the extendability assumption in Theorem
1.7
is essential, otherwise we would have obtained a contradiction to (
1.17
) for
k=1
.
It also shows, for
k=2
, that the
4\pi-\|K\|_{L^{1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}
prefactor in Theorem
1.7
cannot be relieved.
The same example, restricted to a fixed
r
, shows similarly the tightness of Theorem
1.8
in the extendable and non-extendable cases.

6.2
Cone dipoles (dislocations): proof of Theorem
1.13

Consider now a cone dipole.
As discussed in Section
1.4
, Theorem
1.7

yields a significantly sub-optimal lower bound in this case.
The body we consider here is as in Figure
3
, after cutting out a region of diameter
O(r_{0})
containing the two holes (i.e., the domain enclosed by the curve
\sigma
in Figure
3
).

As proved in
[
KM25b
, Sec. 3.1]
, the magnitude of the dislocation and the dimensions
r_{0}
and
R
essentially determine a unique geometry, modulo the precise shape of the inner and outer boundaries, which are immaterial for the scaling of the lower bound with
r_{0}
and
R
.
Hence, we adopt a parametrization that is the easiest to perform calculation with.
Specifically, we fix
\varepsilon\in(0,r_{0})
, and let

\Omega=B_{R}\setminus B_{r_{0}},

endowed with the metric

\mathfrak{g}_{rr}=1\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{g}_{r\varphi}=0\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{g}_{\varphi\varphi}=\left(r+\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\cos\varphi\right)^{2}

(6.2)

where
(r,\varphi)
are the polar coordinates.

A direct calculation, similar to
[
Kup17
, Appendix A]
shows that the magnitude of the Burgers vector of this body is
\varepsilon
,
for every generator
\gamma
of the fundamental group of the annulus, and in particular, for every constant-
r
curve.
Since from (
6.2
), the perimeter of a constant-
r
curve
\sigma_{r}
is

2\pi r
,
it follows from Theorem
1.11
that

\int_{\sigma_{r}}|D_{s}\mathcal{N}|\,d\ell\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi r}

(6.3)

(cf.,
[
Kup17
, Eq. 8]
).
Foliating the domain with constant
r
-curves and using this lower bound as in Section
6.1
only yields the bound (
1.20
) that does not diverge with
R/r_{0}\to\infty
.

This failure is due to fact that the infimal bending energy of this manifold is significantly higher than the sum of the infimal bending energies of each leaf in the foliation.
This is a manifestation of the super-additivity property mentioned in the introduction, which in practice manifests in the following manner:
if a surface with a dislocation having minimal bending energy is dissected into annuli, each annulus will relax into a lower-energy configuration.
We now overcome this issue and prove Theorem
1.13
.

First we note that we are considering
H^{2}
-isometric immersions of a smooth, flat metric.
Since smooth isometric immersions of flat metrics are dense in the
H^{2}
-isometric immersions
[
Hor11
]
, it is sufficient to consider smooth immersions.

Every smooth, locally-Euclidean submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is developable
[
Mas62
]
:
It can be partitioned into flat points, at which the mean curvature
H
equals zero, and non-flat points, the latter constituting an open set.
Through every non-flat point passes a unique asymptotic line, which is a geodesic (in the surface) whose image in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a straight line (i.e., a geodesic in the ambient space).
Asymptotic lines do not intersect and do not terminate until hitting the boundary
[
Mas62
, Theorem II]
.
Furthermore, since the Gaussian curvature is identically zero,
2|H|=|d\mathcal{N}|
.

Since the inner boundary in our case is geodesically convex, asymptotic lines in
\Omega
either connect the inner boundary
\Gamma_{1}
to the outer boundary
\Gamma_{0}
, or, connect the outer boundary to itself. Asymptotic lines that connect the outer boundary to itself create folds that can be flattened out without affecting the rest of the surface, thus reducing the bending energy. Hence, for the sake of finding lower bounds, we may assume that every asymptotic line emanating from the outer boundary intersects eventually the inner boundary.
Furthermore, consider the marginal case where an asymptotic line emanates from the inner boundary,
\{r=r_{0}\}
, tangentially.
This asymptotic line will intersect the set
\{r=2r_{0}\}
at an angle of
\pi/6
. Thus, by restricting our attention to
r\in(2r_{0},R)
, we may assume that all the asymptotic lines intersect constant-
r
curves at an angle that is at least
\pi/6
.

Let
X
be a unit vector field in
\Omega
, whose flow is from the inner boundary to the outer boundary, and is parallel to asymptotic lines in non-flat points.

Lemma 6.1

The mean curvature flux is conserved:

\operatorname{div}(HX)=0,

from which follows that

d\iota_{X}(H\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}})=0.

(6.4)

Proof.

Let
Y
be a vector field such that
\{X,Y\}
is an orthonormal frame.
By the definition of the asymptotic lines and the definition of the mean curvature as half the trace of the shape operator
S
, we have

S(X)=0\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003ptS(Y)=2H\,Y.

The divergence of
X
is given by

\operatorname{div}X=\left\langle\nabla_{X}X,X\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla_{Y}X,Y\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla_{Y}X,Y\right\rangle,

(6.5)

where the first term vanishes because
X
is a unit vector field.

The Codazzi equations are

(\nabla_{X}S)(Y)=(\nabla_{Y}S)(X),

or equivalently,

\nabla_{X}(S(Y))-S(\nabla_{X}Y)=\nabla_{Y}(S(X))-S(\nabla_{Y}X),

which reduces to

2X(H)\,Y+2H\,\nabla_{X}Y-S(\nabla_{X}Y)+S(\nabla_{Y}X)=0.

Take an inner product with
Y
, using the fact that
S
is symmetric and once again the identity

,

2X(H)+2H\left\langle\nabla_{Y}X,Y\right\rangle=0.

Combining with (

\operatorname{div}(HX)=X(H)+H\,\operatorname{div}(X)=0.

Finally
[
Lee18
, p. 32]
,

0=\operatorname{div}(HX)\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}=d\iota_{HX}\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}=d\iota_{X}(H\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}}).

∎

Using the fact that
H
does not change sign along asymptotic lines, as well as the identity
2|H|=|d\mathcal{N}|
, it follows from
(
6.4
) that

d\iota_{X}(|d\mathcal{N}|\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\mathfrak{g}})=0.

(6.6)

Let
\sigma\subset\Omega
be any loop homotopic to
\Gamma_{1}
.
Integrating (

) over the domain bounded by
\Gamma_{1}
and
\sigma

\int_{\sigma}|d\mathcal{N}|\,\left\langle X,\nu\right\rangle\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}=\int_{\Gamma_{1}}|d\mathcal{N}|\,\left\langle X,\nu\right\rangle\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}},

(6.7)

where

Denote by

the constant-

By the above discussion of the intersection of asymptotic line with constant-

curves, we always have

hence for
every

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma_{r}}|d\mathcal{N}|\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\sigma_{r}}|d\mathcal{N}|\,\left\langle X,\mathfrak{n}\right\rangle\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}\overset{\eqref{eq:dN_sigma_Gamma}}{=}\int_{\sigma_{2r_{0}}}|d\mathcal{N}|\,\left\langle X,\mathfrak{n}\right\rangle\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\int_{\sigma_{2r_{0}}}|d\mathcal{N}|\,\operatorname{dVol}_{\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:disloc_naive_lb_leaf}}{\geq}\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}\pi}\frac{\varepsilon}{r_{0}}.

We proceed now as in (

Appendix A
Multiplication and composition in
H^{s}

In this appendix, we prove some technical results concerning functions with
H^{s}
regularity, used in Section
2
.
We begin by proving that the composition of an
H^{s}
function with a Lipschitz function is well-behaved.

Lemma A.1

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a Lipschitz map, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain (or a compact manifold).
Then, for any
s\in(0,1)
, the map
f\mapsto F\circ f
is a continuous map from
H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{k})
to
H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
.

Proof.

Step 1: Uniform bounds and weak convergence.

Let
\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
denotes the Lipschitz constant of
F
.
From the definition of the Gagliardo
H^{s}
seminorm, it is immediate that
\|F\circ f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\leq\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}
, hence the composition is well-defined.
To prove continuity, let
f_{n}
be a sequence in
H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that
f_{n}\to f
strongly. Then
F\circ f_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and by uniqueness of limits converges to
F\circ f
weakly in
H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

Step 2: Strong convergence.

To prove that this convergence is in fact strong, we will prove that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|F\circ f_{n}\|_{H^{s}}=\|F\circ f\|_{H^{s}}
.
As the weak
H^{s}
convergence
F\circ f_{n}\rightharpoonup F\circ f
implies strong
L^{2}
convergence, it remains to prove convergence of the
\dot{H}^{s}
-seminorm.
To prove this claim we will split the domain into points that are close together and far apart.
Let
\epsilon>0
, then there holds

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|F\circ f_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\iint_{|x-y|>\epsilon}\frac{\left|F(f_{n}(x))-F(f_{n}(y))\right|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy

(A.1)

\displaystyle+\iint_{|x-y|\leq\epsilon}\frac{\left|F(f_{n}(x))-F(f_{n}(y))\right|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\iint_{|x-y|>\epsilon}\frac{\left|F(f_{n}(x))-F(f_{n}(y))\right|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy

\displaystyle+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\iint_{|x-y|\leq\epsilon}\frac{\left|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)\right|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy

\displaystyle=\iint_{|x-y|>\epsilon}\frac{\left|F(f(x))-F(f(y))\right|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy

\displaystyle+\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\iint_{|x-y|\leq\epsilon}\frac{\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\|F\circ f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}+\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\iint_{|x-y|\leq\epsilon}\frac{\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2s}}dxdy,

where in the third passage, the limit of the first addend follows from the
L^{2}
convergence
F\circ f_{n}\to F\circ f
, and in the second addend from the strong
H^{s}
convergence
f_{n}\to f
.
Letting
\epsilon\to 0
we obtain that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|F\circ f_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\leq\|F\circ f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}.

Along with weak lower semicontinuity of the
H^{s}
norm, this concludes the proof.
∎

We move on to the second lemma of this section, which concerns the composition of
H^{s}
functions.

Lemma A.2

Let
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
be bounded Lipschitz domains (or compact Riemannian manifolds) and let
F:\Omega^{\prime}\to\Omega
be a bi-Lipschitz map.
Then, for any
s\in(0,1)
, the map
f\mapsto f\circ F
is a continuous linear map from
H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
H^{s}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, for any constant
L>0
, let
H^{\sigma}_{L}(\Omega^{\prime};\Omega):=\{F\in H^{\sigma}(\Omega^{\prime};\Omega)~:~\text{$F$ is $L$-bi-Lipschitz}\}
. Then the action

H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{\sigma}_{L}(\Omega^{\prime};\Omega)\to H^{s}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{d}),\hskip 10.00002pt(f,F)\mapsto f\circ F

is continuous for any
s\in(0,1)
and
\sigma\geq 1
.

Proof.

Changing variables,

\|{f}\circ F\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}=\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|^{2}K_{F}(x,y)dxdy,

where

K_{F}(x,y)=\frac{\left|\det\nabla F(F^{-1}(x))\right|^{-1}\left|\det\nabla F(F^{-1}(y))\right|^{-1}}{|F^{-1}(x)-F^{-1}(y)|^{d+2s}}.

For an
L
-biLipschitz map, these exists a constant
C(L)
, such that

K_{F}(x,y)\leq\frac{C(L)}{|x-y|^{d+2s}},

(A.2)

Thus
\|{f}\circ F\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\leq C\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}
and the map
f\mapsto f\circ F
is well-defined.
Furthermore, as in Lemma
A.1
, it is continuous with respect to weak convergence, noting that weak convergence
F_{n}\to F
in
H^{\sigma}_{L}(\Omega^{\prime};\Omega)
implies uniform convergence of
F_{n}
and
F_{n}^{-1}
by the uniform bi-Lipschitz bound.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show, as before, that for
f_{n}\to f
in
H^{s}
and
F_{n}\to F
in
H^{\sigma}
we have that
\limsup\|f_{n}\circ F_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq\|f\circ F\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}
.
Since
F_{n}\to F
in
H^{\sigma}
and
\sigma\geq 1
, we can assume that
\nabla F_{n}\to\nabla F
almost everywhere; since we already know that
F_{n}^{-1}\to F^{-1}
uniformly, we have that
K_{F_{n}}\to K_{F}
almost everywhere.
Thus we obtain that
\|f\circ F_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\to\|f\circ F\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}
by the dominated convergence theorem, using (
A.2
), and thus

\|f_{n}\circ F_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq\|f\circ F_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+\|(f_{n}-f)F_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq\|f\circ F_{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+C\|f_{n}-f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\to\|f\circ F\|_{\dot{H}^{s}},

which completes the proof.
∎

The last lemma in this section shows that the multiplication of
H^{s}
functions is well-behaved:

Lemma A.3

Fix
B>0
,
s\in(0,1)
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Then the pointwise multiplication map
(f_{1},f_{2})\mapsto f_{1}f_{2}
is a continuous map
H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)\times H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)\to H^{s}(\Omega)
with respect to the
H^{s}
topology, where
H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)=\{f\in H^{s}(\Omega)~:~\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq B\}
.

Proof.

Step 1: Uniform bounds and weak convergence.

It follows immediately from the definition of the Gagliardo seminorm that
\|f_{1}f_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq C\left(\|f_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+\|f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f_{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\right)
for some uniform constant
C>0
, and similarly for the
L^{2}
part of the norm.
Thus the multiplication is well-defined.

In order to prove continuity, we proceed as in Lemma
A.1
. Let
f_{1}^{n},f_{2}^{n}
be two sequences in
H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)
such that
f^{n}_{1}\to f_{1}
and
f^{n}_{2}\to f_{2}
strongly in
H^{s}
. Then
f_{1}^{n}f_{2}^{n}
is uniformly bounded in the
H^{s}
norm, and by uniqueness of limits converges to
f_{1}f_{2}
weakly in
H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)
.

Step 2: Strong convergence.

As in Lemma
A.1
, it is sufficient to prove that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{1}^{n}f_{2}^{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\|f_{1}f_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}
.
The proof proceed similarly, splitting the integral into
\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}
and
\{|x-y|<\varepsilon\}
for some
\varepsilon>0
, where the convergence away from the diagonal follows from the
L^{2}
convergence
f_{1}^{n}f_{2}^{n}\to f_{1}f_{2}
, and the integrand close to the boundary is estimated similarly and shown to vanish as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

In the following, we will need the following classical elliptic regularity result:

Lemma B.4

\Omega\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}

Let

and assume that the coefficients satisfy:

is uniformly continuous, symmetric and uniformly elliptic, with ellipticity constant
\Lambda
.

(b)

for

(c)

Then.

\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\lesssim C\left(\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}+\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\right).

(B.10)

where

\Omega
,
\Lambda,\max_{i}\|b_{i}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})},\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

If the coefficients

[

. The proof in our setting requires only minor modifications of the proof in

Henceforth, we denote by

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}(u_{0}^{c}-v_{0}^{c})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}

We proceed to estimate

Lemma B.6

\|u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}\lesssim\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}|c_{i}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:combine5},\eqref{eq:combine6}}}{{\lesssim}}\mathbb{K}_{p}.

(B.14)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\|u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:equiv_Wkp}}}{{\lesssim}}\|u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:poincare}}}{{\lesssim}}\|\nabla u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}=\|\nabla(u_{K}^{c}-u_{0}^{c})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla u_{K}^{c}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}+\|\nabla u_{0}^{c}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:equiv_Wkp}}}{{\lesssim}}\|\nabla u_{K}^{c}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}+\|\nabla u_{0}^{c}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:combine4}}}{{\lesssim}}\|K\|_{H^{-1}_{\text{MC}}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}|c_{i}|,

where the last inequality hinges also on the dual norm relation (
4.2
).
∎

Lemma B.7

\|\nabla^{2}u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\lesssim\|u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}+\|K\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{g})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:combine3}}}{{\lesssim}}\mathbb{K}_{p}.

(B.15)

Proof.

Writing the interior equation for
u_{K}^{0}
in coordinates:

\mathfrak{g}^{ij}\partial_{ij}u_{K}^{0}-\mathfrak{g}^{ij}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}u_{K}^{0}=K,

we apply Lemma
B.4
noting that the matrix
\mathfrak{g}^{ij}
is symmetric, uniformly continuous and uniformly elliptic, whereas

\|\mathfrak{g}^{ij}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\lesssim\|\mathfrak{g}^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\mathfrak{g}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}

is controlled by the
W^{2,p}
norms of
\mathfrak{g}
and its inverse.
∎

We finally prove the main result of this appendix.

Proposition B.8

Let
u=u_{K}^{c}
be the solution to (
B.1
), then

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim\mathbb{K}_{p}.

Proof.

Since
u
vanishes on
\Gamma_{0}
, it follows from Sobolev embedding theorem that

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\leq\|\nabla^{2}u_{K}^{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}+\|\nabla^{2}u_{0}^{c}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{e})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:combine4},\eqref{eq:combine2}}}{{\lesssim}}\mathbb{K}_{p}.

∎
