Genetic diversity and breeding efficiency of a salt-tolerant strain of largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) revealed by microsatellite analysis and phenotypic evaluations
Authors: Jing Tian, Yang Liu, Caixia Lei, Lina Cheng, Bo Yang, Jinxing Du, Tao Zhu, Shengjie Li, Hongmei Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the underuse of saline-alkaline water and the shortage of suitable aquaculture species, a salt-tolerant strain of largemouth bass was developed through multigenerational selection. In this study, the genetic structure of two base populations and three successive generations (F3-F5) was analyzed using microsatellite markers. Results showed the observed heterozygosity (Ho) ranged from 0.357 to 0.524, the expected heterozygosity (He) from 0.410 to 0.509, and the polymorphism information content (PIC) from 0.3591 to 0.4593, suggesting a moderate genetic diversity across generations. The Fst value between generations ranged from 0.009 to 0.037, indicating weak genetic differentiation among populations. AMOVA analysis revealed that 95% of the genetic variation occurred within populations. Both the UPGMA dendrogram and the structure analysis grouped the different generations into four clusters. Phenotypic evaluation confirmed the selected strain’s enhanced salinity tolerance, exhibiting significantly lower 96 h mortality when challenged at 12ppt and 14ppt. Furthermore, growth rates of salt-tolerant strains (F4 and F5) improved by 19.52% and 26.50% compared to control after 120d saline culture (8ppt). In conclusion, this study indicates successful breeding of a salt-tolerant largemouth bass strain that maintains genetic diversity while exhibiting enhanced salinity tolerance and growth, providing useful insights for further genetic improvement in saline aquaculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48450-1.

Introduction

The largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides), commonly known as the California bass, is native to North America. It has been widely introduced for aquaculture due to its strong adaptability, low disease incidence, rapid growth, and excellent meat quality1. Since its successful introduction and breeding in China in 1983, the culturing scale has expanded continuously. It’s estimated that the national production reached approximately 938,000 tons in 2024, establishing it as an important freshwater economic fish species2.

Previous studies have shown that largemouth bass can survive in saline water up to 9 ppt (parts per thousand), demonstrating relatively strong saline tolerance and potential for farming in such environments3,4. Moreover, saline water culture has been reported to improve flesh quality and nutritional value5,6. Trial farming has been conducted in saline-alkaline water areas such as Gansu, Tianjin, and Neimeng in China7–9. However, due to the lack of suitable varieties, problems such as slow growth and low farming efficiency persist. To promote the large-scale aquaculture of largemouth bass in inland saline-alkaline waters and develop locally adapted strains, our team has previously made breakthroughs in breeding technology under saline-alkaline conditions and successfully developed a salt-tolerant strain up to the fifth generation (F5). Nevertheless, the changes in genetic structure during the selection process and the genetic stability of salt-tolerant traits require further investigation.

Molecular marker technology, a novel approach that reflects genetic traits in the form of DNA, can accurately reveal genetic information and has been widely applied in the genetic breeding of plants and animals10–13. Among these markers, microsatellites—also known as simple sequence repeats (SSRs) or short tandem repeats (STRs)—consist of repetitive units of 1–6 base pairs14. Their polymorphism arises from variations in the number of repeat units15. Owing to their high polymorphism, co-dominant inheritance, and ease of detection, microsatellites are commonly used in aquatic animals for assessing population genetic diversity, kinship analysis, and marker-assisted breeding12,16–18. Genetic diversity serves as the foundation for species adaptation to environmental changes and sustained breeding progress19. Maintaining sufficient genetic diversity helps prevent germplasm degradation. Therefore, evaluating the genetic diversity of breeding populations is crucial for the development of new varieties.

In this study, seven previously screened polymorphic microsatellite markers were used to analyze the genetic diversity of the basic population and three successive generations (F3–F5) of a salt-tolerant strain of largemouth bass. The aim was to assess the impact of continuous selection on its genetic structure and to compare the salinity tolerance and growth performance of the salt-tolerant strains (F4 and F5 generations) with the control under saline culture conditions. This work would provide theoretical support for the genetic improvement of salt tolerance in largemouth bass.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental protocols of this work were ethically approved by the Animal Research and Ethics Committee of Pearl River Fisheries Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Fisheries Sciences (LAEC-PRFRI-2024-06-07). The authors confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and that the study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Selective breeding population and sample collection

The salt-tolerant stain was derived from a base population consisting of the previously developed largemouth bass variety “Youlu No. 3” (GS-01-001-2018) and an introduced northern subspecies largemouth bass (NB population). Since 2021, continuous mass selection has been applied for five successive generations in saline water ponds at a salinity of 8.5–9 ppt, using long-term survival and rapid growth as the primary selection criteria (Fig. 1). Specifically, for each generation, juveniles were cultured in saline water ponds with a salinity ranging from approximately 8.5 to 9.0 ppt for eight months. Individuals exhibiting faster growth and good health without disease were selected based on high survival rates and superior growth performance (body weight) in the ponds, with a selection intensity of approximately 5%. About 1000 broodstock (with a female-to-male ratio of 1:1) were retained per generation.Fig. 1Selection technology route and experimental design of salt-tolerant strains of largemouth bass.

Selection technology route and experimental design of salt-tolerant strains of largemouth bass.

For this study, 30 individuals were randomly sampled from each population, including two basic populations and three consecutive selective breeding generations, which were designated as Y3, NB, F3, F4, and F5, respectively. After being anesthetized with 150 mg/L MS-222, caudal fin tissues were collected from 30 individuals in each population. Then the collected caudal fin clips were preserved in absolute ethanol and stored at −20 °C for subsequent DNA extraction.

Genomic DNA extraction

Approximately 30–50 mg of tissue was finely minced. Then, 400 μL of tissue lysis buffer and 5 μL of Proteinase K were added. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and incubated overnight at 65 °C for complete digestion. Genomic DNA was subsequently extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions of a magnetic bead-based Tissue and Blood Genomic DNA Extraction Kit. The integrity of the extracted genomic DNA was assessed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. The DNA samples were stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

PCR amplification and microsatellite genotyping

Based on prior research and validation20, seven pairs of microsatellite primers demonstrating high polymorphism and good specificity were selected for the genetic analysis. The specific primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

The microsatellite amplification was performed in a 10.0 μL reaction mixture containing 5.0 μL of 2 × Taq MasterMix, 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primer (10 μmol/L), 1.0 μL of DNA template, and double-distilled water to bring the total volume to 10.0 μL. The PCR protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. After amplification, 2 μL of the PCR product was analyzed by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) to check for clear and complete bands. Selected products with good quality were then sent to Wuhan Tianyi Huayu Gene Technology Co., Ltd. for fluorescent capillary electrophoresis genotyping.

For genotyping, PCR amplicons were separated using a genetic analyzer (Prism 3730, ABI, USA) to identify alleles, with GeneScan™-500 LIZ Size Standard used as the DNA ladder. Genotyping data were analyzed using GeneMarker software (version 2.4), followed by manual review and correction to ensure accurate allele calling.

Evaluation of salinity tolerance in the salt-tolerant strain

To assess the salinity tolerance of salt-tolerant strains, an acute salinity stress experiment was conducted based on previous research to compare the cumulative mortality within 96 h between the salt-tolerant strains (F4) and non-selected lines. A gradient salinity stress experiment was designed with five salinity levels (8, 10, 12, 14, and 16 ppt) in 100 L polyethylene rectangular tanks. Each salinity treatment had three replicates, with 30 fish per replicate. The saline water was prepared using artificial sea-salt. Before the experiment, freshwater was aerated for 24 h.

The initial salinity in all tanks was 0 ppt. Salinity was then increased to the target level for each group at a rate of 2 ppt per hour. Feeding was suspended during the experiment. Water quality was monitored daily to ensure ammonia nitrogen remained below 1.5 mg/L, nitrite below 0.25 mg/L, pH between 6.8 and 8.0, and dissolved oxygen above 3 mg/L. Mortality in each group was recorded every 12 h after reaching the specified salinity.

Growth comparison of the salt-tolerant strain

To evaluate the breeding progress, growth performance was compared among the salt-tolerant strains (F4 and F5 generations) and non-selected control line (control1 and control2) in 2024 and 2025. Control1 and control2 groups used in this study were different populations. The experiment was conducted using cages within a saline water pond (a salinity of 8 ppt; area: 2 acres; depth: 2 m). For each group, 300 healthy fish of uniform size were randomly selected. Two net cages (30 m3 each cage) were deployed in the experimental pond.

Pond salinity was maintained using sea-salt (Jiangxi Yantong Technology Co., Ltd., China) and verified daily with a calibrated salinity refractometer (ATAGO, Japan). Water quality parameters were monitored daily, with temperature maintained at 22–28 °C and pH at 7.8–8.0. Fish were fed a commercial diet (Guangzhou Haida Group) to apparent satiation twice daily (approximately 07:00 and 18:00). Feed intake, leftover feed, and fish survival were recorded. After 120 days of rearing, 40 fish were randomly collected from each group, which were anesthetized with 150 mg/L MS-222 to measure body weight, total length, and body height. Data processing and statistical analysis

Microsatellite genotyping data were imported, read, and processed using the GeneMapper 4.0 software (Applied Biosystems, USA). Genetic diversity parameters, including the number of observed alleles (Na), effective number of alleles (Ne), observed heterozygosity (Ho), and expected heterozygosity (He) for each of the seven microsatellite loci, were calculated using GenAlEx 6.5121. The same software was employed to perform inter-population analyses, namely calculating Nei’s genetic distance (Da), pairwise genetic differentiation coefficient (Fst), principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA). The polymorphism information content (PIC) of each locus and population was calculated using Cervus 3.022. Population genetic structure was inferred using the Bayesian clustering method implemented in the software Structure 2.3.423. The analysis was performed with the number of genetic clusters (K) ranging from 1 to 20. For each run, a burn-in period of 10,000 steps was followed by 100,000 iterations of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm. Each K value was independently run 20 times. The optimal number of genetic clusters (K) was determined by calculating the ΔK value using the online tool Structure Selector24. Finally, an unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) dendrogram was constructed based on Nei’s unbiased genetic distance using MEGA software to visualize the genetic relationships among the five generations25.

Evaluation of salinity tolerance

The cumulative mortality after a 96-h challenge was 0% for both the salt-tolerant strain and the control when the salinity was below 10 ppt. When the salinity was raised to 12 ppt, the cumulative mortality of the selected strain (F4 generation) remained at 0%, whereas that of the control reached 10%. Notably, the cumulative mortality at 14 ppt of the non-selected line surged to 80%, while the selected strain (F4 generation) showed a mortality of only 10%. When the salinity reached 16 ppt, exceeding the known salinity tolerance threshold for largemouth bass, the cumulative mortality reached 100% for both the selected strain (F4 generation) and the control. The mortality progression in the non-selected line was markedly faster than that in the selected strain (F4 generation) (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Cumulative mortality rate of the salt-tolerant strain of largemouth bass under different salinity stresses.

Evaluation of growth performance

As shown in Table 6, the growth performance of the salt-tolerant strains (F4 and F5 generations) was significantly improved compared to the non-selected control line after 120 days of culture at 8 ppt salinity. The final body weight (214.61 ± 45.69 g) and total length (21.12 ± 1.57 cm) of the F4 generation were significantly higher than those of the non-selected line (179.56 ± 48.19 g; 19.38 ± 1.77 cm). Furthermore, the average daily gain (ADG) of the F4 generation (1.06 g/d) surpassed that of the control (0.86 g/d). Similarly, the F5 generation also exhibited significantly higher values than the non-selected line in terms of final body weight, total length, and ADG. Notably, the ADG of F5 generation (1.52 g/d) was higher than F4, indicating significant selective breeding effects. Discussion

China is rich in saline-alkaline water resources, yet its utilization rate remains low26,27. Cultivating improved varieties suitable for saline-alkaline water aquaculture is key to expanding farming space. Largemouth bass, due to its promising potential for saline-alkaline tolerance, has become an important candidate species for saline-alkaline water aquaculture. In this study, a salt-tolerant strain of largemouth bass was developed through continuous multi-generation mass selection. By integrating microsatellite molecular markers and phenotypic measurements, we systematically evaluated changes in genetic diversity, population structure, and salt-tolerant growth performance across two base populations and three generations of selected populations. The results showed that while maintaining moderate genetic diversity, the selected population exhibited improved salt tolerance and growth performance, providing a foundation for subsequent continuous breeding efforts.

Microsatellite markers have become a common tool for population genetic structure analysis and assisted breeding in aquatic animals due to their high polymorphism and codominant inheritance11,28,29. The Polymorphism Information Content (PIC) is an important criterion for evaluating the amount of genetic information provided by microsatellite loci. A locus is considered highly polymorphic when PIC > 0.5, moderately polymorphic when 0.25 < PIC < 0.5, and lowly polymorphic when PIC < 0.2530. In this study, seven microsatellite loci were selected, with an average PIC of 0.422. Except for the Msal21 locus, which showed low polymorphism, all other loci exhibited moderate to high polymorphism, indicating that the selected markers can effectively reveal the genetic variation within the breeding population.

Genetic diversity is the foundation for a population to adapt to its environment and sustain its potential for selective breeding. Expected heterozygosity (He) can more accurately reflect the richness and evenness of alleles within a studied population, making it suitable for revealing the level of genetic variation in inbred animal populations. This study demonstrated that the expected heterozygosity (He) overall showed a declining trend but still fell between the two basic populations as selective breeding generations advanced, decreasing from 0.467 in the F3 generation to 0.446 in the F5 generation. This result indicated that artificial directional selection inevitably leads to a narrowing of the genetic background while aggregating target traits. Similar phenomena were commonly observed in selective breeding programs for fish, shrimp, and shellfish10,16–18. For instance, studies on growth-selected lines of Kumamoto oysters have also reported a decline in genetic diversity across generations31. Notably, the He values across all generations of this breeding population remained above 0.410, and the PIC values were all greater than 0.35, suggesting that the genetic diversity was still at a moderate level and no severe genetic bottleneck has occurred. This may be attributed to the retention of a relatively large number of breeding parents per generation (approximately 1000 individuals), which effectively mitigated the effects of genetic drift. Furthermore, the He values for all populations were higher than the Ho values, indicating a certain degree of heterozygote deficiency within the populations. This was often a manifestation of the gradual accumulation of inbreeding effects under intense selection pressure, suggesting a reduction in the genetic diversity of the selected strain 32. Under closed population selection, the loss of genetic variation in aquaculture species has frequently been reported33–36. Therefore, in subsequent breeding efforts, it is essential to continuously monitor inbreeding levels and consider introducing new genetic materials to enrich the gene pool.

The genetic differentiation coefficient (Fst) is one of the most widely used descriptive statistics for evaluating genetic differentiation between and among populations and can provide important insights into the evolutionary processes that influence genetic variation among populations37. Analysis of population genetic differentiation revealed that the (Fst) among the five groups was all below 0.05, indicating weak genetic differentiation between generations. Molecular variance analysis (AMOVA) further confirmed that 95% of the genetic variation existed within the populations. This weak differentiation might be attributed to two reasons. First, the breeding process was carried out continuously at the same site without geographical. Second, parental individuals of each generation were selected from the superior individuals of the previous generation, resulting in frequent gene flow and a strong continuity in genetic background. The gene flow (Nm) between adjacent generations gradually increased as the breeding process advanced (e.g., Nm between F4 and F5 reached 28.853), indicating that genetic consistency among the populations strengthened with successive breeding generations. A similar phenomenon of weak genetic differentiation has been reported in selective breeding populations of channel catfish, which was considered a manifestation of the genetic structure of directional breeding populations tending toward stability17.

Evaluation of growth and salt-tolerance phenotypes provides direct evidence for the effectiveness of selective breeding. We found that the survival rates of the salt-tolerant strain were significantly higher than the control group at salinities of 12 ppt and 14 ppt, confirming that its salt tolerance has indeed been genetically improved. More importantly, the salt-tolerant breeding strain of largemouth bass showed significantly superior performance in final body weight and daily weight gain compared to the non-selected control group after 120 d saline water culture, demonstrating that the growth advantage of the selected strain was expressed in saline environments. Similar findings have been reported in selection breeding programs for other species such as tilapia and estuarine oysters. For instance, two salinity-tolerant tilapia varieties, namely Jili Tilapia and Guangfu No.1 tilapia, have been successfully developed in China using conventional hybridization techniques38,39. The Guangfu No.1 tilapia, after eight generations of mass selection and field testing, exhibited significantly enhanced growth performance and salt tolerance40. Compared to its parental red Mozambique tilapia and O. hornorum, the Guangfu No. 1 tilapia displayed significant heterosis, with a growth rate 19% faster than that of unselected O. mossambicus41. Similarly, in estuarine oysters, the survival rate of eight-month-old G4-selected strain (35.92%) was 2.32 times that of the wild population (15.43%), and the survival rate of juvenile oysters could reach 90%42,43. These findings, together with reports from other species, suggest that multi-generational selection may offer a viable approach for simultaneously improving growth and stress tolerance in fish intended for challenging environments.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, genetic diversity was assessed using only seven microsatellite markers, which may not capture genome-wide variation. Future studies employing high-density SNP markers could provide more comprehensive insights. Second, while we evaluated acute salinity tolerance, long-term adaptive responses and reproductive performance under saline conditions remain unexplored. Finally, as selection progressed to only F5, continued monitoring of genetic diversity and inbreeding in subsequent generations is necessary to ensure long-term breeding sustainability.

Conclusion

This study analyzed the genetic structure of five breeding populations of the salt-tolerant strain of largemouth bass using seven polymorphic microsatellite markers and evaluated the breeding effectiveness of the selected strain. The results suggested that after five consecutive generations of selective breeding, the salt-tolerant strain of largemouth bass exhibited improved salt tolerance and growth performance while maintaining a moderate level of genetic diversity. These findings provide a useful reference for promoting the industrialization of largemouth bass aquaculture in saline-alkaline waters. In the future, it is essential to continuously monitor genetic diversity parameters and inbreeding coefficients across generations during selective breeding. Additionally, introducing external parental sources or expanding the breeding population size appropriately will help maintain genetic diversity and ensure the long-term sustainability of the breeding program.
