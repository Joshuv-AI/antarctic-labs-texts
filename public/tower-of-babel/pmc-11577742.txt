Outcomes of interprofessional education for pharmacy students: a systematic review
Authors: Jiaqi Ni, Qingming Liu, Xinyao Li, Min Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterprofessional education (IPE) refers to a teaching and learning method in which students from two or more professions learn about, from, and with each other to enable effective collaboration and improve health outcomes. A systematic review was performed to investigate the current practice and the effectiveness of IPE in pharmacy education.MethodsDatabases including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Medline, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP China Science and Technology Journal, and WanFang were searched from inception to September 20, 2024, using search terms such as interprofessional, interdisciplinary, multidisciplinary, IPE, pharmacy, and pharmaceutical education. Studies were screened and included if they were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or cohort studies reporting the outcome differences between pharmacy students participating in IPE activities and those receiving lecture-based learning. Reporting quality was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool 2 and the Newcastle Ottawa Scale. Detailed information including the studies’ general information, research methods, and results were collected. Primary outcomes including pharmacy students’ interprofessional competence, readiness for and attitudes towards IPE, class satisfaction, and professional knowledge were analyzed with meta-analysis or reported descriptively.ResultsSeven RCTs and seven cohort studies were included in the systematic review. Eight studies reported that IPE demonstrated a positive impact on pharmacy students’ interprofessional competence (p < 0.05). Three studies showed that IPE enhanced students’ readiness for participating in interprofessional learning (p < 0.05). Four studies revealed that IPE significantly improved the attitude of pharmacy students towards IPE (p < 0.0001). The meta-analysis of the two cohort studies showed that IPE significantly improved the Attitudes Towards Health Care Teams (ATHCT) scale scores (SMD = 0.41, 95% CI = 0.22–0.60). Students who participated in IPE activities were generally satisfied with the course (average score > 4.0 out of 5.0). Apart from embedded in courses, some novel IPE programs were conducted, such as outpatient clinics operated by interprofessional students.ConclusionsThe study revealed that IPE was widely used in pharmacy education. IPE enhanced pharmacy students’ interprofessional competence, class satisfaction, and their readiness for and attitudes towards interprofessional learning. The effect of IPE on professional knowledge remained controversial across studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06313-1.Keywords: Interprofessional education, IPE, Pharmacy education, Healthcare education, Systematic review

Background

In clinical practice, inadequate communication among healthcare professionals and low team collaboration efficiency are critical reasons for medication events [1]. The cooperation between healthcare professionals is increasingly valued. As an essential component of the healthcare team, pharmacists are responsible for ensuring the rational use of medication treatment and providing physicians, nurses, and other healthcare providers with knowledge related to drug therapy [2]. However, in healthcare practitioner teams, the involvement of pharmacists can sometimes be underestimated, especially in developing countries [3]. Due to the late start of clinical pharmacists’ work in these countries, recognition and understanding of pharmacists’ work need to be improved. Thus, it is essential to cultivate pharmacy students’ ability to work in interprofessional teams, which helps to enhance their confidence and readiness to participate in interprofessional work in future practice.

As defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), interprofessional education (IPE) occurs when students from two or more professions learn about, from, and with each other to enable effective collaboration and improve health outcomes [4, 5]. In 2010, WHO published the groundbreaking Framework for Action on Interprofessional Education and Collaborative Practice [5]. Within the framework, WHO strongly advocates for the integration of IPE into healthcare curricula and emphasizes the importance of strengthening teamwork in healthcare practice. Through IPE participation, healthcare students (who received healthcare-related professional education) are supposed to better understand the roles of different professions in healthcare practitioner teams and learn to communicate and cooperate with people from other professions.

IPE has experienced rapid development since the WHO proposed the IPE initiative [5–7]. A growing number of healthcare education schools in developed countries are making practical investigations to develop IPE projects [8, 9]. The University of Washington conducted IPE projects with healthcare students and confirmed that IPE improved team communication and patients’ safety [10]. The University of California and the University of Sydney have also successively carried out IPE courses and achieved anticipated results about the feasibility and usefulness of IPE [11, 12]. As early as 2012, a survey showed that 55% of healthcare education schools in the United States had already offered IPE courses, and 78% had clear plans to incorporate IPE into the curriculum [13]. Some educational organizations and academic institutions also actively advocated for integrating IPE into compulsory courses for healthcare students [14, 15]. However, attempts at IPE were still scarce for many countries with relatively backward health education experience, especially some developing countries, such as Africa and Latin America [16]. Different countries have different occupational cognition and social backgrounds, and the IPE projects cannot be copied in various regions. It is necessary to investigate the IPE needs of students and then combine their situation with experience from the other areas [17].

For pharmacy education, Darling-Hammond’s research [6] has shown that when pharmacy students participate in IPE projects, their understanding of various professions’ responsibilities and ability to collaborate with other professions are enhanced. In addition, through IPE learning, pharmacy students have a better understanding of their profession’s strengths and strengthen their confidence in collaborating with healthcare practitioner teams [6].

Research on IPE in pharmacy education offered valuable guidance for pharmacy schools to develop IPE programs. Studies have published suggestions and guidelines for IPE implementation [15, 18–22]. Apart from integrating IPE into the currently existing pharmacy courses, various forms of IPE such as clinical case simulations, interprofessional case discussions, and free outpatient clinics operated by interprofessional students were widely investigated [23–28]. Wang et al. developed an IPE model integrating profession-role exchange experience in which students from different professions played one another’s roles [29]. Johnson et al. developed an IPE program in which student teams interviewed a community mentor from a senior living community to explore their mentor’s health journey, assess quality of life, and perform an environmental safety assessment [30]. Interprofessional debate competitions and interprofessional peer teaching events were also developed [31–35]. Moreover, IPE projects could be conducted with collaboration across different schools and regions [36], online sessions [37–40], and incorporation of telemedicine technology [41, 42].

However, previous studies primarily focused on the effectiveness of IPE on students majoring in healthcare-related disciplines [10, 34]. Less studies focused on the effectiveness of IPE in pharmacy education specifically. In addition, fewer studies have been conducted on IPE in pharmacy education compared to other healthcare professions, such as medicine, nursing, and dentistry. Systematic reviews published previously mainly focused on the impact of IPE on students majoring in nursing, dentistry, physical therapy, medicine, or non-specific health disciplines [43–46]. A systematic review of the impact of IPE on pharmacy students has not been conducted yet. Moreover, previous studies investigating IPE in pharmacy education mainly focused on the opinions of pharmacy students, pharmacists, and teachers towards IPE, without reporting the effectiveness of IPE in pharmacy education, which remained to be confirmed with more evidence [43].

The research aimed to incorporate and summarize the current literature on IPE in pharmacy education to reveal the current practice of IPE. Additionally, the study aimed to investigate the effectiveness outcomes of IPE in pharmacy education and offer a meaningful reference for future research and practice.

Methods

The study group conducted the systematic review following the criteria of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [47], and it was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), CRD 42,024,513,264.

Search strategy

To explore the current status of IPE and its effectiveness in pharmacy education, the study group performed a comprehensive search in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Medline, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), VIP China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), and WanFang databases for studies on IPE in pharmacy education from inception to June 20, 2023. An updated search was performed on September 20, 2024, to include the studies published recently. Keywords included inter-professional, interprofessional, inter-disciplinary, interdisciplinary, multi-disciplinary, multidisciplinary, IPE, pharmacy, and pharmaceutical education. The search used a combination of subject headings and free text to represent the concepts of IPE and pharmacy education. The detailed search strategy is demonstrated in Supplementary file 1. We included a study if it fulfilled the following criteria: (1) participants: students who received healthcare-related professional education, and pharmacy students must be involved; (2) intervention: IPE; (3) comparison: lecture-based teaching methods adopted in health disciplines education, with the participation of pharmacy students; (4) outcome measures: we referred to Saragih’s study [44] and categorized the effectiveness outcomes of IPE into five domains, which included students’ competence, readiness, attitudes, professional knowledge, and class satisfaction. Students’ confidence in IPE collaboration, their communication, teamwork, leadership, and critical thinking skills were categorized as students’ competence. Students’ awareness of the profession, professional responsibilities, and patient-centered awareness were categorized as students’ readiness. Students’ attitudes towards IPE, their awareness of the importance and effectiveness of IPE, and their willingness to engage in IPE projects were categorized as students’ attitudes. Students’ exam performance and the mastery of professional knowledge were categorized into the professional knowledge domain. Students’ satisfaction with IPE projects was considered as class satisfaction; (5) research types: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies. Reference lists of the studies included were manually reviewed for additional articles.

Studies were excluded if reported in languages other than English and Chinese. Duplicate articles and those unable to obtain the full text were also excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers (QL and XL) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles after employing the search strategy. Studies included after screening were retrieved for full-text review. Disagreements were resolved by consulting a third reviewer (JN). The original author was contacted for incomplete literature if needed.

A data extraction form was created to extract the following information from each study: (1) general information about the article: title, author, publication year, and publication journal; (2) basic information about the research: research method, country and region, time of the research conducted, courses involved, intervention and comparison methods; (3) general information of research participants: professions, sample size of each group; (4) outcomes; (5) other information required by the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool 2 (Cochrane RoB 2) [48] and the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) [49] for quality evaluation, for example, how the random sequence was generated in the study. Two reviewers (QL and XL) independently extracted data from the studies included.

Risk of bias assessment

We adopted the Cochrane RoB 2 [48] for assessing the quality of RCTs, and the NOS [49] for evaluating the quality of cohort studies. Two reviewers (MC and JN) independently assessed the risk of bias for the studies included and cross-checked the results. Disagreements were resolved by discussing with a third reviewer (QL). For RCTs, the following domains were assessed: (1) random sequence generation; (2) allocation concealment; (3) blinding of participants and personnel; (4) blinding of outcome assessment; (5) incomplete outcome data; (6) selective reporting; (7) other potential sources of bias. The risk of bias graph was generated with Review Manager 5.4.1 to demonstrate high, low, or unclear risks.

Data analysis

Meta-analysis was adopted for quantitative data extracted, and Review Manager 5.4.1 was used to analyze the combined effect size. The mean and standard deviation (SD) of continuous outcome variables for the intervention and control groups were merged and the mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) of the same outcome indicators evaluated with different scales were reported. In addition, the inverse squared difference index (I 2) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was used to measure the heterogeneity of each outcome, with 25% being low heterogeneity, 50% being moderate heterogeneity, and 75% being high heterogeneity [50]. When the heterogeneity among studies was not statistically significant (I2 < 50%), a fixed-effect model was applied for combined analysis; if it was statistically significant (I2 ≧ 50%), the source of heterogeneity was investigated. A random-effects model was used instead if the source of heterogeneity could not be retrieved. All analyses were considered statistically significant with p < 0.05. Microsoft Excel 2019 was used to extract data. A forest plot was made with Revman 5.4.1 to present the statistical results of a meta-analysis. The forest plot was situated within a plane Cartesian coordinate system, centered around a vertical null line (marked as 0 on the horizontal axis). Multiple line segments parallel to the horizontal axis were used to depict the effect size and 95% CI of each study. A prism was employed to illustrate the combined effect size and 95% CI of multiple studies. For outcomes that cannot be analyzed with meta-analysis, results were reported descriptively.

Results

General information of studies included

A total of 5,550 articles were retrieved initially. Following screening titles, abstracts, and full text, 14 studies [51–64], including 7 RCTs [51–57] and 7 cohort studies [58–64], were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1). Studies were conducted in the United States (n = 6) [52, 53, 59, 61, 63, 64], New Zealand (n = 2) [60, 62], Brazil (n = 1) [56], Canada (n = 1) [51], China (n = 1) [55], Germany (n = 1) [57], South Korea (n = 1) [54], and the United Kingdom (n = 1) [58] between 2011 and 2024. The studies included 1,844 students from the first to the last year of college. Among them, 867 received IPE intervention, and 977 received control methods. As for the duration of IPE sessions, 21.4% (3/14) of the projects lasted for two to six hours, and 35.7% (5/14) lasted for over one week. Risk of bias assessment

The quality of RCTs was evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 (revised version 2019) [48]. In RCTs, the most significant risk of bias came from the failure to achieve allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, and blinding of outcome assessment (Fig. 2). The quality of cohort studies was evaluated using the NOS [49], with scores ≥ 7 for all included cohort studies (Table 2). The most significant risk of bias arose from insufficient follow-up time.

The current practice of IPE

IPE was widely used in pharmacy education, however, significant regional differences existed in implementing IPE. Among the 14 studies included, 85.7% (n = 12) of the studies [51–54, 57–64] were conducted in developed countries such as the United States (n = 6) [52, 53, 59, 61, 63, 64] and New Zealand (n = 2) [60, 62].

Various forms were adopted when implementing IPE in pharmacy education, which included in-class lectures [51, 59], virtual activities [52, 61], case-based interactive discussions [53, 63], interprofessional clinic sessions [61], simulated patient interactions based on clinical scenarios [54–57], and interprofessional learning workshops [51, 58, 60–62, 64].

IPE and students’ competence

Eight studies [52–55, 57, 59, 60, 62] evaluated IPE’s impact on pharmacy students’ competence. Different evaluation scales were utilized to assess students’ competence, including Team Skills Scale (TSS) [53, 62], investigator-developed survey measuring improvement in selected Interprofessional Education Collaborative (IPEC) competencies [52], Perception towards Interprofessional Competency (PIC) [54], the Assessment of Interprofessional Team Collaboration in Student Learning Scale (AITCS-II Student) [55], California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CCTDI) [55], Team Objective Structured Clinical Exam (TOSCE) [59], and other investigator-developed surveys [57, 60].

The results revealed that IPE enhanced pharmacy students’ communication skills in interprofessional teams [52, 54, 57], confidence in IPE collaboration [52, 60], teamwork skills [52, 53, 55, 59, 62], leadership skills [54], and critical thinking skills [55]. All outcomes showed statistical differences (p < 0.05). However, three studies [52, 59, 60] did not report the specific score differences between the IPE and control groups. Thus, their results could not be assessed due to the lack of data to support the outcomes.

IPE and students’ readiness

Three studies [52, 56, 60] evaluated the impact of IPE on pharmacy students’ readiness for participating in interprofessional learning with the Interprofessional Education Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS) [52, 56] and investigator-developed scale [60]. The results showed that IPE enhanced students’ awareness of the profession, professional responsibilities [52, 56, 60], and patient-centered awareness [60]. A statistically significant difference was identified in the results (p < 0.05). Two studies [52, 60] did not report the exact score differences between the IPE and control groups.

IPE and students’ attitudes

Four studies [54, 57, 62, 63] evaluated the impact of IPE on the attitude of pharmacy students towards IPE with Perceptions towards Interprofessional Education (PIPE) and Attitudes Towards Health Care Teams (ATHCT) scale and other investigator-developed surveys. Two cohort studies [62, 63] evaluated the IPE attitudes using the ATHCT scale. A random effect model was employed to analyze the results of the two studies, with an intergroup SMD of 0.41 (95% CI = 0.22–0.60, I2 = 0%). The results showed that IPE significantly improved the attitude of pharmacy students towards IPE (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). In addition, IPE enhanced students’ awareness of the importance and effectiveness of IPE [54, 57, 62, 63] and improved their willingness to engage in IPE projects [62].

IPE and professional knowledge

Three cohort studies [60, 61, 64] and one RCT [57] evaluated the impact of IPE on the mastery of professional knowledge among pharmacy students. Exams or questionnaires on professional knowledge were conducted to assess outcomes. One study [64] showed that students in the control group achieved better professional knowledge than those in the IPE group (p < 0.05). However, three other studies [57, 60, 61] revealed the opposite result that students in the IPE group achieved better mastery of knowledge (p < 0.05).

IPE and class satisfaction

Two studies [54, 59] evaluated students’ satisfaction with IPE. The results showed that most participants were satisfied with the IPE projects they participated in (average score > 4.0 out of 5.0).

Open-ended questions

Five studies [53, 55, 61, 62, 64] collected the views of IPE participants through questionnaires or interviews with open-ended questions. In general, IPE participants reported increased confidence in collaboration with other health professionals, improved understanding and appreciation of other health professions, and improved awareness of the importance of communication and teamwork [53, 55, 61, 62, 64].

Some suggestions were made to improve IPE activities. Students in Zhou’s study [55] recommended extending the duration of IPE sessions and enriching the simulated case scenarios. Participants in Scheinberg’s study [64] suggested the modifications of session logistics including commute, start time, group allocation, larger rooms, and getting more types of health professions involved.

Discussion

Our study identified various forms of IPE in pharmacy education. Regional differences were identified in implementing IPE. In addition, the study investigated the effectiveness of IPE in pharmacy education regarding five domains, which included students’ competence, readiness, attitudes, professional knowledge, and class satisfaction. In terms of students’ competence, IPE significantly improved pharmacy students’ communication skills in interprofessional teams, confidence in IPE collaboration, teamwork, leadership, and critical thinking skills. Regarding students’ readiness, IPE significantly enhanced students’ awareness of the profession, professional responsibilities, and patient-centered awareness. For students’ attitudes, IPE improved their awareness of the importance and effectiveness of IPE, and their willingness to engage in IPE projects. The ATHCT scale score was significantly higher in the IPE group. However, the studies on the impact of IPE on students’ professional knowledge revealed inconsistent results. Further investigation was required. Moreover, most students were satisfied with the IPE projects they participated in with an average score of > 4.0 out of 5.0. Students in the IPE group believed that the main shortcoming of IPE projects was the short duration of each session; a longer time of IPE participation would be more beneficial.

Saragih et al. conducted a systematic review of the outcomes of IPE for healthcare students [44]. They found that IPE had a significantly positive impact on healthcare students’ knowledge, but its effect on readiness for and attitude toward interprofessional learning and interprofessional competence was not significant. On the contrary, our study revealed that IPE showed a significantly positive impact on pharmacy students’ interprofessional competence, readiness for, and attitude toward interprofessional learning. Its effect on professional knowledge remained controversial. Some differences were identified between the two systematic reviews. Firstly, the study population included in our study were healthcare students, and pharmacy students must be involved; whereas Saragih’s review included healthcare students, without specific limitation to pharmacy students’ involvement. We reviewed the eight studies included in Saragih’s review and found that only two of them involved pharmacy students. Secondly, only RCTs were included in Saragih’s review, while our review included both RCTs and cohort studies. Although differences were identified between the two studies, both of them supported the application of IPE in healthcare education. Therefore, the systematic review included more comprehensive and high-quality research, which can provide reliable evidence to support the effectiveness of IPE for pharmacy students.

To evaluate the effectiveness of IPE in pharmacy education, various scales were adopted, resulting in heterogeneity and low quality between studies. Nineteen types of evaluation tools were utilized across the fourteen studies, encompassing nine investigator-developed questionnaires and ten standardized scales. Additionally, five studies employed open-ended questions for qualitative evaluations. The investigator-developed questionnaires lacked rigorous quality assessment, potentially indicating quality issues. Furthermore, most of the standardized scales employed were either less frequently used in IPE research or lacked representativeness, hence we do not recommend their continued use in future studies. The evaluation tools utilized across studies lacked consistency and reliable quality, which indicated a need to enhance the standardization and consistency of IPE research in pharmacy education. Based on published literature [74, 78], we summarized some widely used scales to evaluate the effectiveness of IPE (Supplementary file 2): (1) IPE competence: TSS [79]; (2) IPE readiness: RIPLS [80], TeamSTEPPS Teamwork Attitude Questionnaire (T-TAQ) [72], TeamSTEPPS Teamwork Perceptions Questionnaire (T-TPQ) [81]; (3) IPE attitudes: ATHCT scale survey [82, 83], The General Role Perception Questionnaire (GRPQ) [77]; (4) Change in Organizational Practice: the Index for Interdisciplinary Collaboration (IIC) [76], the Interprofessional Socialization and Valuing Scale (ISVS) [84], the Healthcare Team Vitality Instrument (HTVI) [85]. Future research with uniformed scales will offer more convincing results.

Some limitations were identified in the study: (1) only Darlow’s study [62] examined whether the effectiveness of IPE in pharmacy could be sustained for a long time, covering not only the final year of preregistration training but also the first three years of professional practice. The long-term efficacy of IPE was unclear in other studies; (2) due to the limitations in intervention methods, blinding cannot be achieved, making it difficult to avoid the subjective factors of researchers and participants affecting the research results; (3) the setting of IPE courses (intervention methods) varied among different studies, as most IPE project was integrated into the existing pharmacy courses, resulting in deviations in course qualities; (4) the majority of the effectiveness outcomes were reported descriptively because different studies generally adopted different scales or investigator-developed questionnaires to evaluate the outcomes, resulting in significant heterogeneity and difficulty in quantitative analysis between studies; (5) the study did not indicate whether IPE had a positive impact on the professional knowledge of pharmacy students, and further research was required; (6) only studies reported in English or Chinese were included. The current practice of IPE in non-Chinese and non-English speaking countries might been investigated inadequately.

IPE is an essential part of pharmacy education to promote the development of the pharmacy profession and improve healthcare in future practice. In the United States, IPE is a part of pharmacy education as a stated curricular goal in 78% of colleges and schools of pharmacy. The majority of colleges and schools (90%) indicated that the Introductory Pharmacy Practice Experience (IPPE) incorporating IPE was a required experience for pharmacy students [13]. In the future, how to optimize the ways to offer IPE to students, enhance their professional awareness and sense of responsibility, and improve their readiness and confidence in IPE will be the main focus of pharmacy education. In addition, developing comprehensive and standardized IPE guidelines would be valuable to promote pharmacy education.

Conclusions

The study revealed that IPE was widely used in pharmacy education in various forms. In addition, IPE enhanced pharmacy students’ interprofessional competence and their readiness for and attitudes toward IPE. Pharmacy students who participated in IPE projects were generally satisfied with and benefited from it. Controversy existed over the impact of IPE on professional knowledge, and further research was needed. There are various IPE projects, but significant regional differences exist in implementing IPE. Further international cooperation is crucial for advancing pharmacy education across different regions.
