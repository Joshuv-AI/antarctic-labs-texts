Circulating tumor DNA-based assessment of disease progression after liver resection or transplantation for metastatic colorectal cancer or hepatocellular carcinoma—study protocol for a randomized, controlled trial
Authors: I Górzyńska, O Kornasiewicz, A Koniczka, A Moskowicz, K Skubisz, J Pepłowski, A Lesiak, T Stokłosa, A Wójcicka, M Grąt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM) carry high recurrence rates after surgery or transplantation. Conventional imaging and biomarkers have limited sensitivity for detecting minimal residual disease (MRD). Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a promising biomarker that may enable earlier recurrence detection compared with standard methods. The aim of this randomized controlled trial is to evaluate the clinical utility of ctDNA monitoring in the early detection of disease recurrence and its impact on monitoring progression in patients undergoing liver resection or transplantation for HCC and CRLM.MethodsThis randomized, controlled trial will enroll 300 patients undergoing liver resection or transplantation for CRLM or HCC. Participants will be randomized 2:1 to ctDNA monitoring or standard follow-up. ctDNA will be analyzed at three predefined time points (baseline, 1 month, and 6 months postoperatively) and compared with tumor tissue mutation profiles generated by next-generation sequencing (NGS). All patients will be followed for 24 months with imaging, tumor markers, and quality-of-life assessments. The primary endpoint is ctDNA-defined recurrence. Secondary endpoints include recurrence-free survival, overall survival, time to recurrence detection, patient-reported outcomes, and cost-effectiveness.DiscussionThis study will assess whether ctDNA-guided monitoring enables earlier recurrence detection and improves clinical decision-making after liver resection or transplantation. Incorporating ctDNA into surveillance may support earlier therapeutic intervention, optimize follow-up strategies, and enhance long-term outcomes.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07001085. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary malignant tumor of the liver, accounting for approximately 75–90% of all primary liver cancer cases [1]. Liver cancer, predominantly HCC, is among the fastest-growing cancers worldwide. This trend is evident in both incidence and mortality rates. Liver cancer ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, following lung and colorectal cancers [2]. The 5-year survival rate for liver cancer remains low, at approximately 18%, underscoring its poor prognosis [3]. According to the latest global data, there were approximately 866,136 new cases and 758,725 deaths from liver cancer in 2022. These account for approximately 5.4% of all malignant tumors worldwide representing a mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR) of 0.86 [4].

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy globally, representing approximately 10% of all cancer cases [5]. In 2022, there were approximately 1,926,425 new CRC cases and around 904,019 related deaths worldwide (https://www.wcrf.org/preventing-cancer/cancer-statistics/worldwide-cancer-data). In Poland, CRC ranks third in cancer incidence, with an estimated 15,000–16,000 new diagnoses annually [6]. Most cases are detected at advanced stages (III or IV) [7], which considerably limits treatment options. Approximately 50% of patients with CRC develop liver metastases during the course of their disease [8], highlighting the critical need for early detection and effective surveillance strategies to improve prognosis.

Surgical resection of liver metastases remains the only potentially curative treatment option. However, even after successful resection and systemic chemotherapy, disease progression remains a major risk [9]. Conventional imaging techniques (computed tomography [CT], magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], positron emission tomography [PET]) are routinely used for surveillance but have limited sensitivity in detecting minimal residual disease (MRD), particularly after liver surgery or targeted therapy [10]. Similarly, serum biomarkers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) lack the specificity and sensitivity needed for reliable recurrence prediction [11]. Consequently, many patients are diagnosed with measurable disease only after radiologically visible recurrence has occurred, often too late for curative intervention. Moreover, failure of first-line treatment requires second-line therapies, which are associated with lower response rates and poorer survival outcomes [12]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for non-invasive, highly sensitive tools for longitudinal tumor monitoring, earlier recurrence detection, and more accurate assessment of treatment efficacy, especially in stage IV disease.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) represents fragments of DNA shed by tumor cells into the bloodstream, carrying tumor-specific genetic alterations [13]. ctDNA has emerged as a promising biomarker for detecting MRD and early recurrence in oncology [14]. After liver resection or transplantation, the presence of detectable ctDNA in plasma often precedes the appearance of radiologically visible metastatic lesions by several months [15]. Notably, ctDNA detected 6 months postoperatively can indicate residual tumor burden or early disease progression and is strongly associated with reduced long-term survival [16].

Despite emerging prospective and pilot studies showing promising results for ctDNA monitoring in postoperative cancer settings including hepatic malignancies [17–20], the absence of robust, randomized evidence specifically in liver resection or transplantation cohorts leaves unanswered questions regarding its clinical utility. This gap underlines the necessity for a rigorously designed randomized study in this context.

Choice of comparators {6b}

The comparator is standard oncological treatment consisting of liver resection or liver transplantation (for CRLM or HCC) followed by 18-month postoperative surveillance without ctDNA testing. Surgical removal or transplantation remains the only potentially curative strategy and is the recommended, validated standard of care in this patient population. Postoperative follow-up includes radiological imaging (CT or MRI) and tumor marker assessment every 3 months, in line with current clinical guidelines.

The comparator was chosen because this approach represents the best available and widely accepted standard of oncological management with proven effectiveness in prolonging survival.

Timing and administration of the comparator

Patients will be randomized into two groups: the study group (with ctDNA testing at three time points) and the comparator group (no ctDNA testing). The comparator period begins at patient enrollment and includes surgery/transplantation and routine oncological follow-up for 18 months. Participation in the study will not alter the standard treatment or follow-up procedures in the comparator group.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the clinical utility of ctDNA monitoring at three predefined time points—preoperatively, 4 weeks postoperatively, and 6 months postoperatively—for early detection of disease progression and its impact on recurrence-free survival over 18 months following liver resection or transplantation. Secondary objectives include assessing ctDNA as a predictive biomarker for early disease recurrence and progression, correlating dynamic changes in ctDNA levels with radiological findings and tumor marker fluctuations, determining whether ctDNA monitoring influences postoperative imaging schedules and therapeutic interventions, and evaluating the potential impact of ctDNA-guided surveillance on overall survival, quality of life, and healthcare utilization.

Trial design {8}

This research will be conducted as a prospective, randomized, single-center clinical trial involving a total of 300 patients, with 200 assigned to the experimental cohort and 100 to the control cohort. The experimental cohort will comprise 100 patients with colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) and 100 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), while the control cohort will include 50 patients with CRLM and 50 patients with HCC, all receiving standard postoperative care without ctDNA monitoring. Patients in both groups will undergo routine follow-up according to current clinical guidelines, including imaging studies and tumor marker assessments. Randomization will occur on a 2:1 basis to ensure sufficient statistical power while taking patient willingness and logistical feasibility into account (Fig. 1).

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The trial will be conducted in collaboration with the Medical University of Warsaw, including the Department of Biology and Cancer Genetics, the University Clinical Center Genetics Laboratory, and the Clinic of General Surgery, Transplantation, and Hepatology.

Eligibility criteria {10}

For CRC, the study will include patients aged ≥18 with liver-only metastatic disease and no history of other malignancies. Patients with both synchronous and metachronous liver metastases (according to updated definitions) will be eligible. All patients must have undergone complete resection of the primary tumor. Chemotherapy will not be an exclusion criterion; patients may be included regardless of prior or ongoing systemic treatment.

The aim of surgical intervention will be to achieve R0 resection of all liver metastases. Patients with radiologically confirmed recurrence after liver resection may remain in the study, based on the decision of the oncology team. Hepatic or extrahepatic recurrence will not automatically exclude participation.

For HCC, eligible participants will include patients with resectable HCC undergoing liver resection or those with unresectable HCC who are qualified for liver transplantation. The study will include patients with or without liver cirrhosis.

Transplant eligibility will be assessed using the French Model [21] for HCC, which considers tumor size, number, and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels. For resection, eligible patients must be between 18 and 75 years old. All patients approved for liver transplantation during formal evaluation meetings may be included. Interventions {11}

All patients enrolled in the study will undergo standard postoperative care following liver resection or transplantation, consistent with established clinical guidelines. For patients with colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), this includes routine radiological imaging such as CT or MRI scans approximately every 3 to 4 months during the first 2 years after surgery, alongside regular assessment of serum tumor markers, including CEA and CA19-9. Patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) will undergo similar imaging and AFP measurements. Standard systemic therapies will be administered as clinically indicated, and all patients will continue regular consultations with their surgical and oncology teams.

Control group

Patients randomized to the control group will receive only standard postoperative follow-up, without ctDNA testing. In this group, clinical decisions, further imaging, or adjustments to systemic therapy will be made solely based on conventional clinical and laboratory findings, including imaging studies and tumor marker assessments. The absence of ctDNA monitoring ensures that these patients follow current standard-of-care protocols, serving as a comparator to evaluate the clinical impact of ctDNA-guided interventions. The management in this group reflects real-world practice and is not altered for the purposes of the study, ensuring that participation does not compromise patient safety or outcomes.

ctDNA-monitored group

Patients assigned to the experimental arm will receive the same standard postoperative care as the control group, with the addition of serial ctDNA monitoring. Prior to surgery, blood samples will be collected to establish baseline ctDNA levels, and tumor tissue obtained during the procedure will undergo comprehensive genomic profiling using next-generation sequencing (NGS) to identify somatic mutations specific to each patient’s tumor. Based on these tumor-specific mutations, individualized droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assays will be designed, allowing highly sensitive and quantitative detection of ctDNA in plasma.

Following surgery, patients in the ctDNA-monitored group will provide blood samples at two predefined time points: 4 weeks and 6 months postoperatively. Plasma ctDNA will be analyzed using the patient-specific ddPCR assays to detect tumor-derived genetic variants, serving as an early indicator of residual disease or tumor recurrence. In cases where ctDNA levels are elevated or rising, clinicians may initiate additional imaging or consider modifications to systemic therapy. This strategy allows for earlier identification of disease progression, potentially preceding radiologic detection by several months, and provides a personalized, molecularly informed approach to postoperative management.

By combining standard postoperative care with personalized ctDNA monitoring, the intervention aims to enhance early detection of recurrence, improve clinical decision-making, and optimize the timing of additional interventions. This approach is expected to facilitate more precise follow-up, potentially improve long-term outcomes, and inform future standards of care for patients undergoing liver resection or transplantation for CRLM or HCC.

Detailed description of the main medical procedures

The trial involves patients undergoing either liver resection or liver transplantation for primary hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) or colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM). These surgical interventions represent the current standard of care and the only potentially curative options for these patient populations.

Liver resection

Liver resection is performed according to established surgical protocols at the Department of General, Transplant, and Liver Surgery, Medical University of Warsaw. Preoperative evaluation includes imaging studies such as CT, MRI, and ultrasound to assess tumor size, location, vascular involvement, and residual liver volume. Intraoperatively, patients undergo careful parenchymal transection, with preservation of vascular and biliary structures. Postoperative care involves monitoring in the intensive care unit for 1–2 days, routine laboratory and imaging studies, and early detection of complications, particularly biliary leaks or bleeding. The procedure carries a mortality risk of 1–2% and postoperative complication rate of approximately 15%, consistent with international standards for high-volume centers.

Liver transplantation

Liver transplantation is indicated primarily for patients with HCC within accepted transplant criteria, particularly those with underlying cirrhosis. Donor livers are obtained from deceased donors following national allocation protocols. The procedure involves total hepatectomy of the diseased liver followed by implantation of the donor organ. Transplantation surgery can last from 4 to 12 h, depending on patient complexity and intraoperative challenges. Postoperative care is conducted in the intensive care unit with 24-h monitoring, early detection and management of vascular or biliary complications, and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. First-year post-transplant mortality may reach up to 10%, while overall complication rates can be as high as 30%.

Blood sampling for ctDNA analysis

In addition to standard care, patients will have peripheral blood drawn at pre-specified time points: preoperatively, 4 weeks postoperatively, and 6 months postoperatively. Blood samples are processed to isolate circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) from plasma. ctDNA analysis is performed using highly sensitive digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) targeting patient-specific somatic mutations identified through prior next-generation sequencing (NGS) of tumor tissue. This procedure allows early detection of minimal residual disease (MRD) and molecular recurrence, potentially preceding radiological findings by several months.

Follow-up and postoperative monitoring

All patients, regardless of group assignment, will receive standard postoperative oncological follow-up, including imaging (CT, MRI, or ultrasound) and tumor marker assessment (AFP, CEA, Ca19-9) every 3–4 months for the first 2 years post-surgery. Patients in the ctDNA group will have additional monitoring informed by ctDNA results, which may prompt earlier imaging and therapeutic interventions if molecular recurrence is detected. This approach aims to optimize early detection of disease progression and improve long-term outcomes.

Outcomes {12}

The study is designed to evaluate the clinical utility of ctDNA monitoring in patients undergoing liver resection or transplantation for colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Outcomes will focus on assessing the ability of ctDNA to detect residual disease or recurrence earlier than conventional methods, as well as its impact on long-term survival and postoperative management.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study is the clinical value of ctDNA measurement at three selected time points—preoperatively, 4 weeks postoperatively, and 6 months postoperatively—for the early detection of cancer progression and its association with recurrence-free survival over 18 months following surgery. Specifically, we will assess whether detectable ctDNA or rising ctDNA levels precede radiological or biochemical evidence of disease recurrence, including the emergence of new intrahepatic or extrahepatic lesions. By comparing recurrence-free survival between the ctDNA-monitored and control groups, the study aims to determine whether integrating ctDNA testing into routine follow-up can provide clinically actionable insights that improve the early detection of disease progression and guide timely interventions.

Sample size {14}

The study aims to enroll a total of 300 patients, randomized in a 2:1 ratio (200 in the ctDNA-monitored group and 100 in the control group). This sample size was determined based on the clinical throughput of the Department of General, Transplant, and Liver Surgery at the Medical University of Warsaw—the largest liver surgery and transplantation center in Poland—and a formal power analysis to ensure the study’s ability to detect clinically meaningful differences in oncological outcomes.

Recruitment {15}

Recruitment will occur at the Department of General, Transplant, and Liver Surgery, Medical University of Warsaw, and affiliated centers.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Participants will be randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to either the experimental group (ctDNA monitoring arm) or the control group (standard follow-up arm). Randomization will be stratified by age (≤75 years vs. >75 years), sex, and type of surgical procedure (liver resection vs. liver transplantation) to ensure balanced distribution. Patients who withdraw after randomization will not be replaced. Permuted block randomization will be applied with varying block sizes to ensure concealment, and the sequence will be generated automatically by the electronic case report form (eCRF) system.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The allocation sequence will be embedded in the eCRF system, ensuring concealment until the point of assignment. The study is conducted in a blinded manner; participants will not be informed of their group allocation, and investigators involved in clinical care will remain unaware of the assignment to minimize potential bias.

Implementation {16c}

Investigators will be responsible for enrolling eligible patients, while allocation will be automatically assigned by the system once inclusion and exclusion criteria are confirmed in the eCRF.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Patients will be blinded to their group allocation (ctDNA monitoring vs. standard care). Randomization is performed centrally in a concealed manner; at the time of allocation, each patient draws an opaque envelope (or equivalent blinded assignment procedure), so they do not know in which arm they are assigned. The processing of blood samples (for ctDNA) will be performed by laboratory personnel who are aware of allocation; clinicians, data managers, and study coordinators will also be unblinded to allocation to allow appropriate decision-making and monitoring.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Although the study is designed as patient-blinded, unblinding may be performed in case of serious adverse events or trial emergencies. The unblinding request must be approved by the principal investigator and overseen by the Data Monitoring Committee. The reasons and timing of unblinding will be documented in the trial records and reported to the ethics committee.

Data collection and management

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18}

At each follow-up visit, patients will receive information about the importance of adherence to the monitoring schedule for early detection of recurrence. Participant retention will be promoted by coordinating study visits with routine clinical follow-up appointments. Reminder systems (telephone calls, emails) will be employed to reduce loss to follow-up. Patients who relocate or discontinue care at the study center will be contacted to ensure outcome data are collected whenever possible. In case of withdrawal or deviation from study procedures, available clinical data and follow-up results from standard oncological care will be collected, if consent is maintained.

Data management {19}

All essential study documents and datasets will be stored securely in compliance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and national regulatory requirements. The eCRF system will include built-in validation checks to ensure data integrity. Access will be restricted to authorized personnel only.

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The primary analysis will evaluate whether postoperative ctDNA positivity is associated with earlier detection of disease recurrence compared with standard radiological follow-up. Time-to-event outcomes, including disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS), will be estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between-group comparisons will be performed using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be derived from Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for stratification variables (age, sex, and type of surgery).

For secondary outcomes, ctDNA dynamics will be analyzed as a time-dependent covariate to assess their predictive value for radiologically confirmed recurrence. Diagnostic accuracy parameters (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value) will be calculated for ctDNA detection compared to standard imaging. Associations between ctDNA status and clinical or pathological factors (tumor size, nodal status, histological features) will be explored using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, and t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, depending on data distribution.

Longitudinal changes in ctDNA concentration will be modeled using mixed-effects regression to account for repeated measures within patients. Subgroup analyses will be conducted for liver resection and liver transplantation cohorts separately, as well as for HCC and colorectal cancer metastases.

All statistical tests will be two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses will be performed using validated statistical software (e.g., R or SAS). Missing data will be handled by censoring for time-to-event endpoints and, if appropriate, by applying multiple imputation techniques for secondary exploratory analyses.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of coordinating center and trial steering committee {21a}

The trial will be coordinated by the Department of General, Transplant, and Liver Surgery at the Medical University of Warsaw, a high-volume center with extensive experience in liver resection and transplantation, including over 500 HCC transplants and more than 2500 liver transplants overall. The steering committee will consist of principal investigators, transplant surgeons, oncologists, and molecular biologists overseeing trial conduct, recruitment, surgical procedures, and the integration of ctDNA analyses. The committee will ensure adherence to protocol, regulatory compliance, and patient safety throughout the study.

Data monitoring committee {21a}

An independent Data Monitoring Committee (IDMC), comprising external oncologists, transplant surgeons, and biostatisticians, will supervise trial progress. The IDMC will review ongoing safety data, monitor recruitment, evaluate interim results, and make recommendations regarding continuation, modification, or early termination of the study based on predefined stopping rules or safety concerns.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

All adverse events related to trial procedures—including venipuncture, blood collection for ctDNA analysis, liver resection, or transplantation—will be reported and documented in electronic case report forms (eCRFs). Serious adverse events will be immediately communicated to both the ethics committee and the IDMC. Participants experiencing adverse events will receive appropriate medical care, and any complications will be carefully monitored and followed until resolution.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

The study will undergo regular internal monitoring by the coordinating center to ensure adherence to protocol and data quality. Additionally, independent audits may be conducted by the Medical Research Agency or other funding bodies as required. Audit reports will be reviewed by the steering committee, and corrective actions will be implemented as necessary.

Discussion

The concept of “liquid biopsy,” based on detecting ctDNA released by cancer cells into the bloodstream, has gained prominence as a minimally invasive method for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and disease monitoring. This approach shows particular promise in CRLM and HCC, where early detection of MRD or recurrence can influence outcomes.

Retrospective analyses in heterogeneous cohorts of patients with metastases to the liver, lungs, peritoneum, or multiple organs have shown that serial ctDNA measurements strongly correlate with disease progression and overall survival [22, 23]. For example, the multicenter PROTIGE-14 trial investigated ctDNAs in patients with potentially resectable CRLM receiving perioperative chemotherapy. Persistent ctDNA detection before surgery was associated with shorter postoperative survival and lower rates of complete (R0/R1) resections [22]. These findings highlight ctDNA’s potential for relapse detection, surgical risk stratification, and adjuvant therapy guidance.

Similar trends have been reported in HCC. Persistent postoperative ctDNA elevation is associated with lower long-term survival and higher metastasis risk [24]. Serial monitoring detected relapse in approximately 59% of patients before it became radiologically apparent. Preoperative ctDNA positivity was linked to early tumor recurrence, independent of tumor size or count, highlighting ctDNA’s value as a sensitive marker of tumor burden and genetic heterogeneity [25]. Notably, ctDNA was detectable in 96.5% of plasma samples, affirming its feasibility for routine clinical use.

ctDNA analysis depends on highly sensitive molecular methods. NGS offers comprehensive genomic profiling, enabling the simultaneous assessment of multiple genetic alterations, including mutations, amplifications, and rearrangements. This broad molecular characterization facilitates the creation of patient-specific genomic maps critical for personalized monitoring.

Digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) complements NGS by quantifying rare alleles with high sensitivity. Unlike conventional quantitative PCR, ddPCR partitions samples into thousands of droplets, allowing detection of very low ctDNA levels without reference genes or standard curves [26]. This feature is particularly important for detecting MRD and early relapse in postsurgical and post-therapeutic settings.

Clinical integration of ctDNA analysis offers several advantages over traditional imaging modalities. Its ability to detect molecular signs of tumor progression months before radiological changes enables earlier intervention, which may improve long-term survival. In addition, ctDNA testing can be performed using peripheral blood samples obtained in outpatient settings, allowing frequent and cost-effective monitoring without hospital visits or invasive procedures.

Moreover, ctDNA-guided follow-up may optimize healthcare resource allocation by identifying patients at a high risk of relapse who require intensified surveillance or treatment, while sparing low-risk patients from unnecessary interventions.
