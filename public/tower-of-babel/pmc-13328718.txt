Structure-based virtual screening identifies VX-809 as a candidate dual-pathway modulator in fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy
Authors: Shunsuke Noda, Kota Imai, Taisei Okino, Taisuke Numao, Theofilos Tourtas, Ursula Schlötzer-Schrehardt, Friedrich Kruse, Noriko Koizumi, Naoki Okumura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is a progressive degenerative disease of the corneal endothelium and remains a leading indication for corneal transplantation worldwide. FECD is characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, disruption of proteostasis with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and progressive endothelial cell loss; however, no pharmacological therapy is currently available. Given that FECD involves multiple interacting pathogenic pathways, we asked whether a structure-based polypharmacology approach could identify a single small molecule capable of modulating distinct disease-relevant targets. We performed virtual screening of 1,178 FDA-approved compounds against transforming growth factor-β receptor type II (TGF-βR2) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK), which contribute to ECM dysregulation and stress-induced apoptosis. VX-809 was the only compound predicted to bind both targets, with docking scores of − 8.5 kcal/mol for TGF-βR2 and − 10.7 kcal/mol for p38 MAPK; molecular dynamics simulations further supported stable protein–ligand interactions. In patient-derived FECD corneal endothelial cells, VX-809 attenuated TGF-β2–induced apoptosis, suppressed activation of Smad2/3 and p38 MAPK signaling, and reduced ECM overproduction and global protein synthesis. VX-809 also decreased aggresome formation and dampened activation of the PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6 arms of the unfolded protein response, consistent with improved protein homeostasis under stress conditions. Together, these findings show that structure-based screening can reveal previously unrecognized multi-target activities in existing drugs and identify candidate modulators of converging pathogenic pathways in FECD. Introduction

Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is a progressive degenerative disorder characterized by corneal endothelial cell loss, formation of guttae on Descemet’s membrane, and eventual corneal edema that leads to visual impairment. Corneal transplantation remains the only definitive treatment for advanced disease; however, increasing donor shortages and the risk of postoperative complications underscore the need for alternative mechanism-based therapeutic strategies.

Accumulating evidence indicates that FECD is a multifactorial disease in which several intertwining pathogenic processes—including oxidative stress, protein misfolding, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and dysregulated signaling cascades—collectively drive endothelial cell apoptosis1–4. Oxidative stress initiates and further amplifies ER stress and apoptosis, creating a feed-forward loop that accelerates endothelial degeneration2,3,5. Among these pathways, dysregulation of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) signaling has been implicated in endothelial injury6–9. Activation of the TGF-β/Smad pathway induces excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and contributes to ER stress6,7, whereas p38 MAPK promotes stress-induced apoptosis independently of Smad phosphorylation9. Previous experimental work has suggested that inhibiting either pathway can ameliorate endothelial abnormalities in FECD models6,7,9, indicating that both pathways may represent complementary therapeutic targets within protein misfolding-linked and ER-stress–linked disease networks.

Despite these mechanistic insights, no pharmacologic agent has been identified that can simultaneously modulate these multiple pathogenic pathways in FECD. This knowledge gap reflects a broader challenge shared by many complex multifactorial diseases in which no single signaling cascade fully accounts for disease progression. Consequently, multi-target or “polypharmacology” approaches have gained increasing attention as potential therapeutic strategies to meet this challenge10–14. One such strategy is computational structure-based screening, because it enables systematic evaluation of how a single compound interacts with multiple disease-relevant proteins—an undertaking that is experimentally laborious or impractical.

Although in vitro experiments remain essential, they are inherently limited in scale and throughput. Therefore, in silico approaches can serve as valuable complementary tools that can broaden target exploration and accelerate the identification of compounds with multi-pathway therapeutic potential10–14. This potential is particularly relevant for complex disorders such as FECD, where endothelial injury reflects a multifactorial interplay of pathogenic signaling pathways, including the TGF-β/Smad and p38 MAPK axes. In the present study, we have explored whether any existing small molecule might simultaneously engage these pathways by employing a structure-based virtual screening approach for the comprehensive evaluation of 1,178 Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved compounds across both targets to distinguish candidates with predicted dual-pathway activity. This computational–experimental framework enabled an assessment of the usefulness of a structure-based screening approach to identify compounds with the potential to modulate multiple pathogenic pathways in FECD.

Results

In silico identification of compounds with high affinity for both TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK

Because FECD involves multiple signaling cascades contributing to corneal endothelial cell dysfunction1–4, our aim was to identify small molecules capable of targeting both the TGF-β and p38 MAPK pathways6–9. To this end, in silico docking simulations were performed using UCSF ChimeraX (v1.9) against TGF-βR2 (PDB ID: 1M9Z) and p38 MAPK (PDB ID: 1A9U). From a library of 1,178 FDA-approved compounds, we identified the top-ranking candidates exhibiting the strongest binding affinities for each target. For TGF-βR2, the leading compounds were tucatinib (–8.7 kcal/mol), ezetimibe (–8.5 kcal/mol), ingenol mebutate (–8.5 kcal/mol), VX-809 (–8.5 kcal/mol), and etoposide phosphate (–8.4 kcal/mol) (Table 1). For p38 MAPK, the top five compounds were VX-809 (–10.7 kcal/mol), olaparib (–10.6 kcal/mol), nilotinib (–10.6 kcal/mol), natamycin (–10.5 kcal/mol), and lonafarnib (–10.3 kcal/mol) (Table 2). Notably, VX-809 appeared among the top-ranked compounds for both targets and was the only compound common to both lists. Based on these findings, VX-809 was selected for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations and in vitro validation experiments to evaluate its structural stability and biological relevance.

Molecular docking and dynamics simulations reveal stable interactions between VX-809 and both TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK

We explored how VX-809 interacts with its predicted molecular targets by visualizing and analyzing the docking poses of VX-809 with TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK. In the three-dimensional models, the large cyan (emerald-green) structures denote the protein backbones of TGF-βR2 (Fig. 1A) and p38 MAPK (Fig. 1B). The small green molecules correspond to VX-809, positioned within the ligand-binding pocket of each protein, while the red regions highlight the residues or side chains of the proteins that are directly interacting with VX-809. VX-809 occupied a defined cavity in each target and formed several noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, which helped stabilize the overall complex. These interactions reflect favorable steric complementarity between the ligand and receptor, supporting the computational prediction that VX-809 could bind to both TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK signaling proteins in a stable and energetically favorable manner.

For TGF-βR2, the RMSD trajectory of the VX-809–bound complex remained within 1–3 Å, comparable to that of the apo protein, indicating that VX-809 binding did not alter the overall protein structure (Fig. 1C). The RMSF profile also demonstrated minimal residue-level fluctuations (< 2.5 Å), except at the terminal regions, suggesting that VX-809 binding did not increase local flexibility (Fig. 1D). Similarly, for p38 MAPK, the VX-809–bound complex exhibited RMSD values comparable to those of the unbound form throughout the 10 ns simulation (Fig. 1E). The RMSF profile showed limited atomic fluctuations (< 2.5 Å) across most residues, confirming that VX-809 binding stabilized the protein conformation without introducing structural strain (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings indicated that VX-809 maintains stable and non-disruptive interactions with both TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK.

Validation of VX-809 as a dual inhibitor of Smad and p38 MAPK signaling in an FECD cell model

We validated the in silico predictions by determining whether VX-809 could mitigate TGF-β2–induced cytotoxicity in iFECDs. Before evaluating its protective effects under stress conditions, we first examined whether VX-809 alone affected cell viability under basal conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1). The iFECDs used in this experiment were derived from an FECD patient carrying an expanded TCF4 CTG repeat (≥ 50 repeats), which represents the most common genetic background associated with FECD. Exposure to TGF-β2 (20 ng/mL) for 24 h caused marked morphological deterioration and cell death, whereas pretreatment with VX-809 (1 µM or 10 µM) visibly preserved the normal cobblestone-like cell morphology, indicating a protective effect against TGF-β2–induced damage (Fig. 2A). Consistent with these morphological findings, TGF-β2 stimulation markedly increased the cleavage of caspase-3 and PARP, which are hallmarks of apoptosis. The VX-809 treatment caused a concentration-dependent reduction in the expression of both cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP (Fig. 2B). Densitometric quantification confirmed a significant inhibition of apoptotic signaling by VX-809 (Fig. 2C, D). The reproducibility of these findings was confirmed by conducting similar experiments using iFECDs derived from two additional FECD patients, which yielded comparable results (Supplementary Fig. 2).

We also investigated whether the cytoprotective effect of VX-809 involved a suppression of canonical TGF-β/Smad signaling. Western blot analysis revealed that VX-809 (10 µM) markedly decreased the phosphorylation of Smad2 and Smad3 induced by TGF-β2, whereas the total protein levels of Smad2 and Smad3 remained unchanged (Fig. 2E). Quantitative analysis confirmed significant reductions in p-Smad2 and p-Smad3 expression (Fig. 2F, G), suggesting that VX-809 inhibits the activation of the TGF-β downstream pathway.

Because activation of p38 MAPK is known to contribute to endothelial injury under endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress conditions, we also examined the influence of VX-809 on this noncanonical stress pathway. Treatment with tunicamycin, an ER-stress inducer, enhanced phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and its downstream effector ATF2, confirming pathway activation; however, cotreatment with VX-809 (10 µM) significantly suppressed both of these phosphorylation events (Fig. 2H–J). Taken together, these results demonstrated that VX-809 protects corneal endothelial cells by simultaneously inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad and p38 MAPK/ATF2 signaling cascades, thereby attenuating apoptosis.

Validation of VX-809 as a modulator of protein homeostasis and ER-stress signaling in an FECD cell model

Excessive production of ECM proteins is a well-recognized phenotypic feature of FECD and is thought to contribute to guttae formation as well as ER stress–mediated endothelial cell death. We investigated whether VX-809 affects this pathological protein overproduction by analyzing ECM synthesis and accumulation in iFECDs. Incorporation assays using puromycin revealed that TGF-β2 (20 ng/mL) stimulation markedly enhanced global protein synthesis, indicating increased translational activity associated with ECM overproduction in FECD model cells. VX-809 treatment (1 µM or 10 µM) caused a concentration-dependent suppression of this TGF-β2-induced increase (Fig. 3A, B).

We also evaluated the expression of fibronectin, a representative ECM protein that accumulates abnormally in the Descemet’s membrane and guttae of FECD corneas. Western blot analysis demonstrated that TGF-β2 strongly upregulated fibronectin expression, while VX-809 (10 µM) effectively inhibited this increase (Fig. 3C, D). These findings indicate that VX-809 suppresses the excessive ECM synthesis and protein production that characterize the FECD phenotype.

Consistent with these observations, immunofluorescence staining using aggresome markers showed that TGF-β2 stimulation induced a marked accumulation of misfolded and aggregated proteins, whereas VX-809 treatment substantially reduced aggresome formation (Fig. 3E). In addition, to assess whether VX-809 preserves corneal endothelial cell morphology under stress conditions, we examined endothelial junction markers by immunofluorescence staining. TGF-β2 stimulation disrupted the typical junctional localization of N-cadherin and ZO-1, indicating impaired cell–cell junction integrity in FECD model cells. In contrast, VX-809 treatment partially restored the peripheral localization of these junctional proteins along cell borders (Fig. 3F), suggesting that VX-809 mitigates stress-induced morphological disruption of corneal endothelial cells. We then examined whether VX-809 modulates activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) pathways triggered by ER stress. TGF-β2 stimulation activated the PERK–ATF4–CHOP axis, as indicated by phosphorylation of PERK and induction of ATF4 and CHOP expression, and these effects were attenuated by VX-809 (10 µM) (Fig. 3G; quantitative analysis is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3A–D). The IRE1α branch was similarly activated by TGF-β2, leading to phosphorylation of IRE1α and its downstream targets ASK1 and JNK, but these phosphorylations were suppressed by VX-809 (Fig. 3H; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 3E–I). Moreover, VX-809 (10 µM) reduced the cleavage of ATF6α, indicating inhibition of the ATF6 pathway (Fig. 3I; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 3J). Collectively, these data demonstrated that VX-809 alleviates TGF-β2–induced overproduction of ECM proteins and limits subsequent ER-stress activation by suppressing all three canonical UPR signaling branches—PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6—in FECD model cells.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that using a structure-based virtual screening framework can be an efficient means of identifying multi-target therapeutic candidates for FECD. By integrating molecular docking with supportive molecular dynamics simulations, we were able to conduct a systematic evaluation of 1,178 FDA-approved compounds, which identified VX-809 as a compound capable of interacting with both TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK. Subsequent in vitro validation using immortalized FECD patient–derived corneal endothelial cells confirmed that VX-809 suppresses TGF-β2–induced apoptosis and ameliorates ER-stress–related protein dysregulation, thereby establishing a conceptual foundation for using structure-based approaches like molecular docking in ophthalmic drug discovery. Proposed multi-pathway effects of VX-809 in FECD Schematic summary of the proposed mechanism of action of VX-809 in Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD). VX-809 suppresses TGF-β/Smad and p38 MAPK signaling, leading to reduced extracellular matrix (ECM) overproduction, attenuation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and unfolded protein response (UPR) activation (PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6), and decreased apoptosis. These effects are consistent with improved protein homeostasis and preservation of corneal endothelial cell integrity.

Molecular docking is a structure-based virtual screening technique that predicts how small molecules interact with target proteins by sampling and scoring numerous ligand poses within three-dimensional binding pockets15–18. Unlike quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling or machine-learning–based predictors, which depend heavily on preexisting bioactivity datasets19,20, docking requires only structural information and can therefore be applied flexibly—from de novo hit identification to multi-target profiling17,18, Ligand-based virtual screening offers complementary strengths; however, it assumes structural similarity among active compounds—a condition not always met in multifactorial diseases. Conversely, although molecular dynamics simulations provide atomistic insight into binding stability, they are computationally intensive for large-scale screening21,22. Compared with these approaches, docking offers a balanced combination of scalability and mechanistic detail—it is data-independent, computationally efficient, and uniquely capable of evaluating a single compound across multiple unrelated targets, thereby supporting polypharmacology-oriented discovery17,23,24.

In silico approaches are increasingly recognized as valuable tools in ocular drug discovery, as they complement traditional experimental methods and expand opportunities for early-stage target exploration25. Consistent with this trend, our previous transcriptome-based screening study demonstrated that non–structure-based computational approaches can identify compounds capable of mitigating FECD-associated molecular abnormalities26, thereby highlighting the complementary nature of diverse in silico strategies and providing broader context for incorporating docking into research on multifactorial degenerative diseases like FECD.

The multifactorial nature of FECD encompasses oxidative stress, protein misfolding, ER stress, ferroptosis, and other stress responses, whose downstream pathways converge to impair endothelial function1–4,27. Oxidative stress can act both as a cause and a consequence of these events, amplifying UPR activation and cellular vulnerability28–30. For these reasons, many pathogenic mechanisms have been proposed to explain this complexity. We previously identified TGF-β/Smad signaling and p38 MAPK activation as contributors to endothelial injury in a model that complements, rather than competes with, other published hypotheses6–9. The resemblance of FECD to other multifactorial diseases that also show no single pathway fully driving disease progression makes a polypharmacologic strategy conceptually appealing.

Advances in computational drug discovery now overcome the prohibitively labor-intensive experiments that would be required for the parallel evaluation of multiple protein targets using a single compound library. Here, our application of a multi-target screening strategy to FECD and our selection of the TGF-β/Smad and p38 MAPK pathways as an illustrative test case resulted in the identification of VX-809 as a compound with predicted affinity for both pathways. This identification was made despite the lack of any prior evidence of dual activity, thereby demonstrating how multi-target docking can uncover unexpected yet biologically plausible candidates.

VX-809 is best known as a cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) corrector, where it was developed to treat the common cystic fibrosis F508del mutation that causes protein misfolding, ER retention, and premature degradation of the chloride channel31–34. By acting as a pharmacologic chaperone, VX-809 improves CFTR folding and trafficking to the plasma membrane. Clinically, it is used with an ivacaftor drug such as Orkambi®35–37. From the standpoint of FECD biology, a notable feature is that a compound identified through docking as a potential modulator of TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK also exhibited strong effects on protein homeostasis—another core aspect of FECD pathogenesis1,6,7. In our iFECD model, VX-809 reduced nascent protein synthesis, suppressed fibronectin overproduction, decreased aggresome formation, and attenuated the activation of all three major UPR branches (PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6), indicating that its folding-correction capacity extends beyond CFTR to the stressed protein environment of corneal endothelial cells. Interestingly, the magnitude of VX-809–mediated suppression appeared to differ among the individual UPR branches, suggesting that the compound may differentially modulate specific stress-signaling pathways rather than uniformly suppressing the entire UPR network. The emergence of a misfolding-targeted drug from a screening strategy focused on TGF-β/Smad and p38 MAPK supports the biological plausibility of our approach and suggests that VX-809—or other future folding correctors—may hold therapeutic relevance for FECD and for other disorders in which protein misfolding and ER stress play central roles.

This study has several limitations. First, it was designed primarily as a proof-of-concept assessment of whether a docking-based, polypharmacology-oriented strategy can be applied to FECD, rather than as a full evaluation of VX-809 as a therapeutic agent. Consequently, we did not determine whether VX-809 can be formulated or delivered in an ophthalmically appropriate manner, such as through topical administration, nor did we assess its ocular pharmacokinetics or tissue penetration, all of which will be essential for translational development. A major limitation of this study is the lack of in vivo validation. Although our in vitro findings support the biological relevance of dual targeting, they do not fully recapitulate the complex physiological and pathological environment of the cornea in vivo, including factors such as tissue architecture, microenvironmental stress, and systemic influences. In addition, although our in vitro findings support the biological relevance of dual targeting, its in vivo validation is still required in FECD model systems, such as transgenic or stress induced models38–40. Among these, we recently reported a knock-in mouse model carrying CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the Tcf4 gene, which reproduces key pathological features of FECD, including progressive guttae formation and endothelial cell loss39. Evaluation of VX-809 using such disease-relevant models will be important for establishing its therapeutic potential in vivo. Moreover, because FECD involves a broader network of stress-related mechanisms, including oxidative stress, ferroptosis, RNA toxicity, and RAN translation2–4, the optimal combination of pathways for polypharmacologic intervention will require clarification. Determining whether modulation of these additional mechanisms might provide superior therapeutic benefits will be an important focus for future research.

In conclusion, this study provides a proof of concept that structure-based docking is a practical approach for identifying multi-target therapeutic candidates for treating FECD. Although formulation, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and in vivo validation will be required before therapeutic development can be pursued, our findings highlight the potential of using computational multi-target screening to uncover unexpected activities in existing drugs and to accelerate early-stage discovery efforts for the treatment of FECD and other multifactorial ocular diseases.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by both the Ethics Committee of Friedrich-Alexander University Erlangen–Nürnberg (FAU; reference number: 140_20 B) and the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research at Doshisha University (reference number: 20009). Donor Descemet membranes containing corneal endothelial cells (CECs) were obtained from patients diagnosed with Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) who underwent Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) at the University of Erlangen–Nürnberg. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to tissue collection.

In silico screening of candidate compounds

The three-dimensional structures of human transforming growth factor-beta receptor type II (TGF-βR2; PDB ID: 1M9Z) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK; PDB ID: 1A9U) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). A total of 1,178 FDA-approved small-molecule compounds were obtained from the PubChem database to construct a virtual drug library for screening. All protein structures were preprocessed to remove crystallographic water molecules, heteroatoms, and co-crystallized ligands using PyMOL (version 3.1.3; Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, USA). The structures were then optimized by adding hydrogen atoms and Gasteiger partial charges using UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.9; University of California, San Francisco, CA, USA) to ensure proper protonation and electrostatic representation. Ligand structures were similarly prepared, and their rotatable bonds were defined to enable flexible docking.

Molecular docking simulations were performed using ChimeraX. Docking grid boxes were centered on the active sites of TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK and defined to cover the entire ligand-binding pocket to ensure comprehensive sampling of potential interaction sites. Default exhaustiveness and energy range parameters were used (exhaustiveness = 8, energy_range = 3 kcal/mol). For each docking run, the binding affinity was calculated as the predicted free energy of binding (ΔG, kcal/mol), where lower ΔG values represent stronger predicted binding interactions. The resulting docking poses were ranked according to their binding affinities, and the top 10 compounds with the most negative scores were selected for each protein.

Molecular dynamics simulations

We evaluated the structural stability of VX-809 binding to each target by performing molecular dynamics simulations using GROMACS (version 2023.1; Homebrew package manager) with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. Among the compounds identified in the docking analysis, VX-809 was selected for further simulation because it was the only molecule that exhibited a high predicted affinity for both TGF-βR2 and p38 MAPK.

Topology files were generated for each protein–ligand complex, and ligand parameters were prepared using GAFF (AmberTools; University of California, San Francisco, CA, USA) with Antechamber (AmberTools; University of California) and AM1-BCC charge assignment (AmberTools; University of California). Each system was placed in a cubic periodic box and solvated with TIP3P water molecules, maintaining at least a 10 Å buffer between the solute and the box edges. Counterions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) were added to neutralize the system and achieve a physiological ionic strength of 0.15 M. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm until the system reached convergence (< 1000 kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻¹), followed by equilibration in two stages: an NVT ensemble at 300 K for 500 ps, and an NPT ensemble at 1 bar (100 kPa) for 1 ns using the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat.

Production molecular dynamics simulations were then conducted for 10 ns under periodic boundary conditions with a 2 fs time step. Particle Mesh Ewald (PME)41 was used for long-range electrostatics, and all hydrogen-containing bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm42. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of the Cα atoms were calculated to evaluate conformational stability relative to the apo forms of each protein.

Cell culture and establishment of the FECD cell model

Immortalized Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (iFECD) cells, previously established and characterized in our laboratory from Descemet membranes containing corneal endothelial cells obtained from FECD patients, were used in all experiments, as previously described6,7,43. The iFECDs were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, and the culture medium was replaced every 2 days. When the cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were subcultured using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA (Nacalai Tesque).

The iFECDs were stimulated with 20 ng/mL transforming growth factor-beta 2 (TGF-β2; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) for 24 h; this treatment induces cellular stress and apoptotic signaling characteristic of FECD pathology.6,7,43 To evaluate the pharmacological effects of VX-809 (ChemScene LLC, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), cells were pretreated with VX-809 at final concentrations of 1 µM or 10 µM for 24 h before TGF-β2 stimulation. Tunicamycin (10 µg/mL; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) was used as a positive control to induce p38 MAPK activation through endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, independently of TGF-β signaling activation. All compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Nacalai Tesque) as stock solutions, while ensuring that the final DMSO concentration in the culture medium did not exceed 0.1% (v/v). Control cultures received an equivalent volume of DMSO vehicle.

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed twice with cold 1× phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Nissui Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) and lysed on ice using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The lysates were briefly sonicated on ice and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for protein analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of total protein were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 3% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST).

The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti–caspase-3 (1:1000), anti–PARP (1:1000), anti–Smad2 (1:1000), anti–phospho-Smad2 (1:1000), anti–Smad3 (1:1000), anti–phospho-Smad3 (1:1000), anti–p38 MAPK (1:1000), anti–phospho-p38 MAPK (1:1000), anti–ATF2 (1:1000), anti–phospho-ATF2 (1:1000), anti–PERK (1:1000), anti–phospho-PERK (1:1000), anti–ATF4 (1:1000), anti–IRE1α (1:1000), anti–ASK1 (1:1000), anti–phospho-ASK1 (1:1000) (all from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); mouse anti–CHOP (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology); anti–phospho-IRE1α (1:500; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA); mouse anti–JNK (1:500) and anti–phospho-JNK (1:250) (both from BD Transduction Laboratories, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA); mouse anti–ATF6 (1:1000; Bio Academia, Osaka, Japan); mouse anti–fibronectin (1:15000; BD Transduction Laboratories); mouse anti–puromycin (1:1000; Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan); and mouse anti–GAPDH (1:3000; Medical & Biological Laboratories Co., Ltd., Nagoya, Japan), which was used as a loading control.

After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Protein bands were visualized using Chemi-Lumi One Ultra (Nacalai Tesque) and imaged with an ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini system (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). Densitometric analysis was performed using Fiji (ImageJ-based software; version 1.0; NIH; https://imagej.net/software/fiji/downloads), and the results were expressed as the mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments.

Aggresome detection and immunofluorescence staining

Cells were cultured on glass coverslips (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) placed in 24-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 10⁴ cells per well and treated according to the experimental conditions described above. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. For aggresome detection, protein aggregates were visualized using the ProteoStat Aggresome Detection Kit (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated with the ProteoStat aggresome detection reagent (1:1000 dilution) for 60 min at room temperature in the dark. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against N-cadherin (1:300; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and ZO-1 (1:300; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark.

After staining, nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:2000; Vector Laboratories). Coverslips were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd.), and fluorescence images were obtained using a DM2500 fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) under identical imaging conditions for all experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently at least three times. Quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Multiple groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test using the control group as the reference. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0), and the multcomp package (version 1.4–25) was employed to perform Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
