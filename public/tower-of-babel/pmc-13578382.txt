Early-onset adenocarcinomas of the distal esophagus, gastroesophageal junction, and stomach: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 45 studies
Authors: F Petrelli, L Dottorini, S Cherri, M Libertini, V Rampulla, M Arru, F Senzani, M Viti, M Rossitto, C Signorelli, A Zaniboni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of adenocarcinomas of the distal esophagus, gastroesophageal junction (GEJ), and stomach diagnosed before age 50 years is increasing and early-onset disease may have a distinct phenotype. A comprehensive synthesis of clinicopathological and molecular differences between early-onset and average-onset disease remains lacking.Materials and methodsWe carried out a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020-compliant systematic review and DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis of observational studies comparing early-onset (<50 years) and average-onset adenocarcinomas of the distal esophagus, GEJ/cardia, and stomach. Forty-five studies (35 clinicopathological or epidemiological cohorts comprising >650 000 patients and 10 molecular cohorts) were included. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed in molecularly characterized cohorts.ResultsEarly-onset disease was associated with advanced stage (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.26-1.54), signet-ring histology (OR 2.52, 95% CI 1.84-3.47), synchronous distant metastasis (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.04-1.38), high tumor grade (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.23-2.11), and female sex (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.14-1.44, P = 0.001). Diffuse-type histology was enriched in molecularly characterized early-onset tumors (OR 2.26), as were cadherin 1 (E-cadherin gene) alterations (OR 2.92) and germline pathogenic variants (OR 2.00), whereas microsatellite instability-high (OR 0.17) and tumor mutational burden-high (OR 0.37) were depleted. The subgroup restricted to GEJ tumors confirmed signet-ring enrichment (OR 1.87, I2 = 2%).ConclusionsEarly-onset adenocarcinomas of the distal esophagus, GEJ, and stomach appear to represent a distinct clinicopathological and molecular phenotype characterized by aggressive histopathology, enrichment of a genomically stable/cadherin 1 (E-cadherin gene)-associated profile, and a potentially more frequent inherited predisposition. These findings may inform age-aware diagnostic pathways, germline evaluation, and future therapeutic research.Key words: diffuse type, early-onset gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma, esophagogastric adenocarcinoma, gastric cancer, signet-ring cell carcinoma, young-onset gastric cancer

Introduction

The epidemiology of adenocarcinomas arising in the distal esophagus, gastroesophageal junction (GEJ), and stomach has shifted substantially over recent decades. Although overall gastric cancer incidence has declined globally,1,2 the incidence of GEJ and distal esophageal adenocarcinoma in individuals under 50 years of age is rising across multiple high-income and middle-income settings.3, 4, 5 Clinical oncologists increasingly encounter young patients with advanced-stage disease and aggressive histological features. Whether this reflects a simple shift in age distribution or a biologically distinct disease phenotype with different genomic drivers, immune contexture, and therapeutic vulnerabilities is a question of major clinical and public-health relevance.6, 7, 8, 9 Pernicious anemia is also associated with an increased risk of gastric adenocarcinoma, although its contribution to early-onset disease remains uncertain.10

Individual studies have generated conflicting signals. Single-institution series from tertiary cancer centers have reported striking enrichments of signet-ring histology, peritoneal dissemination, and stage IV disease in young patients,11, 12, 13, 14 whereas some large population-based registries have suggested more modest differences and paradoxically better overall survival in the young.15,16 Earlier molecular and genetic association studies in esophageal adenocarcinoma also suggested age-related differences in tumor biology and susceptibility.17,18 Recent multiomic profiling efforts have begun to reconcile these observations by demonstrating a genomically stable (GS), cadherin 1 (E-cadherin gene) [CDH1]-associated, diffuse-type biology disproportionately represented in early-onset disease.11,19, 20, 21 We therefore conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis integrating clinicopathological, epidemiological, and molecular cohorts of distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, and gastric adenocarcinoma across 45 included studies.

Materials and methods

Protocol and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.22 A completed 27-item checklist and the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1) are provided. The review question was framed using the Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcomes (PECO) structure because the exposure—chronological age at diagnosis—is observational rather than interventional. The protocol was developed a priori with prespecified outcomes, eligibility criteria, search strategies, extraction fields, and analytic methods. No amendments were made to outcome definitions after data extraction began.

PECO framework and eligibility criteria

Review question

In patients with adenocarcinoma of the distal esophagus, GEJ/cardia, or stomach, is age at diagnosis <50 years associated with distinct clinicopathological and molecular characteristics compared with older age at diagnosis?

Population

Adults (≥18 years) with histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma of the distal esophagus, GEJ (including tumors reported as esophagogastric junction or Siewert I-III23), gastric cardia, or stomach. Squamous-cell esophageal carcinoma was excluded; mixed-site cohorts were included only when age-stratified adenocarcinoma data could be extracted.

Terminology and anatomical classification

Throughout the manuscript, distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, and gastric adenocarcinomas are named explicitly rather than grouped under the potentially ambiguous terms ‘esophagogastric adenocarcinoma’ or ‘upper gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma’. GEJ/cardia denotes junctional or cardia tumors as classified by each source study and does not imply that these categories were anatomically identical when Siewert classification was unavailable. Mixed-site cohorts are labeled as such and the tumor locations included in every study are reported in Supplementary Table S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410).

Exposure

Early-onset disease is defined primarily as age <50 years.3,6,11,19 Alternative thresholds (≤40, ≤45, and ≤55) were retained but separated in sensitivity analyses (see ‘Statistical analysis’ section).

Comparator

Patients diagnosed at/above each study’s cut-off (typically ≥50 years). Separate middle- and late-onset groups (e.g. Deac et al.16 and Kist et al.24) were combined for pooling.

•Primary clinicopathological features: (i) advanced stage (American Joint Committee on Cancer/Union for International Cancer Control III-IV), (ii) signet-ring histology, (iii) synchronous distant metastasis (M1) at diagnosis, (iv) high grade (G3/G4), and (v) female sex. Diffuse-type histology reported in molecularly characterized cohorts was analyzed as a parallel histopathological feature and presented together with signet-ring histology.

Designs

Observational cohorts (retrospective/prospective), case-control, cross-sectional, registry, institutional, and molecular profiling studies were eligible. Randomized trials were not excluded solely because of their design: a trial was eligible if it reported treatment-independent, age-stratified comparisons of baseline clinicopathological or molecular features relevant to the PECO question. Treatment efficacy or safety comparisons by age were ineligible because they addressed intervention effects outside the prespecified review question. No randomized trial met the former eligibility criteria. Editorials, commentaries, letters without original data, case reports <10 patients, and reviews were excluded but hand-searched. No language, year, or geographic restrictions were applied.

Information sources and search strategy

Three primary databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, SCOPUS, and Cochrane CENTRAL) were searched from inception to March 2026, plus ClinicalTrials.gov, the WHO ICTRP, and reference lists of included studies and reviews (backward citation chasing). The search combined Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms in three blocks joined by AND: (i) age/onset (‘young adult’, ‘early onset’, ‘age at diagnosis’, ‘<50 years’); (ii) anatomy (‘distal esophagus’, ‘gastroesophageal junction’, ‘gastric cardia’, ‘gastric cancer’, ‘esophageal adenocarcinoma’, adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction [AEG], Siewert); and (iii) features/markers (‘clinicopathologic features’, ‘signet ring’, ‘diffuse type’, ‘CDH1’, ‘microsatellite instability’, ‘tumor mutational burden’, ‘germline’). Full reproducible search strings and execution dates are archived in the Supplementary Material (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410).

Study selection

Records were collated in Zotero and deduplicated by digital object identifier, PubMed identifier, and title-plus-first-author. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts; potentially eligible full texts were assessed in duplicate. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third senior reviewer. Inter-rater agreement exceeded κ > 0.80 on a 50-record calibration set. From 931 database records, 25 supplementary sources, and 104 curated PubMed abstracts, 264 duplicates were removed and 45 studies were retained after title, abstract, and full-text exclusions.

Three-level triage of the curated abstract set

The 104-abstract curated file was processed through the following: level 1, regex filtering for upper-GI anatomy and age terms (→97); level 2, manual relevance for age-stratified clinicopathological/molecular data (→69); and level 3, extractability check for 2 × 2 data or exact percentages with denominators (→20 studies). The remaining 49 either duplicated included cohorts, provided qualitative findings only, or reported nonprespecified outcomes. The full audit trail is in the Supplementary Material (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410).

Data extraction

Two reviewers (FP and ML) independently extracted data into a prepiloted Excel form with controlled vocabularies across five domains: (i) study metadata [author, year, journal, country, design, data source (e.g. Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results [SEER], National Cancer Database, Swedish Cancer Register, MSK-IMPACT, and Caris CODEai), enrollment period, and funding]; (ii) participant metadata (total N, N per group, age cut-off, sex, and anatomy); (iii) histopathology [Laurén class, signet-ring percentage, grade, and TNM25 (tumour–node–metastasis) stage]; (iv) molecular features (CDH1 somatic/germline, MSI, TMB, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, PD-L1, Claudin 18.2, fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 isoform b, Epstein–Barr virus, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) subtype, germline panel, and platform); and (v) outcomes (2 × 2 event counts preferred; percentages with denominators back-calculated to integers; survival estimates when available). For overlapping cohorts, only the most recent/largest dataset was retained. Field-level agreement exceeded 95% on a 10-study calibration set; disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer.

Quality and risk-of-bias assessment

Methodological quality was assessed with the ‘Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS)’26 adapted for cohort and case-control designs, covering selection (four items), comparability (two items, double-weighted, evaluating stage and additional confounder adjustment), and outcome (three items), scored 0-9. Studies scoring 7-9 were classified as high quality; those scoring 4-6 were classified as moderate quality; and those scoring 0-3 were classified as low quality. Two reviewers assessed each study independently. The median NOS score across 45 studies was 8 (range 7-9); no study scored <7 and no study was excluded on quality grounds. NOS items were adapted for molecular cohorts to evaluate genomic platform adequacy, representativeness of the tested subset, and germline ascertainment (Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410).

Statistical analysis

Effect measure

The odds ratio (OR) was selected over the risk ratio because several cohorts had case-mix designs with nonmeaningful marginal proportions. ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed from 2 × 2 tables [OR = (a·d)/(b·c)]; log-OR standard errors were computed as √(Σ 1/cell). For zero cells, the Haldane–Anscombe 0.5 continuity correction was applied; sensitivity analysis used a treatment-arm correction, with no substantive change.

Pooling model

A DerSimonian–Laird random-effects inverse-variance model27 was specified a priori given expected clinical, methodological, and geographic heterogeneity, with τ2 estimated by the method of moments. Robustness was confirmed under restricted maximum likelihood and the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) correction for small-k pools; HKSJ-widened CIs are noted in the ‘Results’ section.

Heterogeneity was evaluated by Cochran’s Q (P < 0.10 significant), the I2 statistic (25/50/75% thresholds for low/moderate/high28), and τ2. Outcomes with I2 ≥ 75% triggered prespecified subgroup/sensitivity analyses.

Publication bias was assessed for outcomes with ≥10 studies (Cochrane guidance): visual funnel-plot inspection and Egger’s regression test29 (P < 0.10 indicating small-study effects). Only the advanced-stage outcome (k = 10) met this threshold.

Prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses (see ‘Sensitivity analyses’ and ‘Subgroup restricted to GEJ tumors’ sections): (i) study design (population registry versus institutional), (ii) age cut-off [<50 (reference) versus <40-45 versus <55-60], (iii) geography (North America, Europe, East Asia, and other), (iv) restriction to NOS ≥8, and (v) a GEJ-specific meta-analysis restricted to clearly demarcated junctional anatomy (Siewert-classified, SEER-AEG, Japanese multicenter, Chen AEG,30 and MSK GEJ11). Between-subgroup heterogeneity was tested with Q_between. The anatomical sites represented in every pooled, subgroup, and sensitivity analysis are specified in Supplementary Table S2 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410).

Software and reproducibility

Analyses were performed using Python version 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA), together with NumPy version 1.26 (NumPy Developers), pandas version 2.2 (pandas Development Team), SciPy version 1.13 (SciPy Developers), and Matplotlib version 3.8 (Matplotlib Development Team). These Python libraries are open-source projects fiscally sponsored by NumFOCUS, Inc. (Austin, TX, USA). Additional analyses were conducted in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the metafor package version 4.4 (Wolfgang Viechtbauer, Maastricht University, Maastricht, the Netherlands). DerSimonian–Laird pooling, continuity correction, Q-testing, I2/τ2, and Egger’s regression were implemented from first principles and validated against R’s metafor v4.4 on three benchmark datasets (Hartung–Knapp 2008; Cochrane Handbook; BMJ 2003 Higgins tutorial), with concordance to three decimals. All scripts, extraction tables, and intermediate results are released to enable full reproducibility.

Certainty of evidence

Certainty was appraised using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation, starting at the baseline for observational evidence and adjusted for limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Certainty was low for advanced stage, synchronous distant metastasis, high grade, and female sex, and was moderate for signet-ring histology. Among molecular and related histopathological analyses, certainty was moderate for CDH1 alterations, MSI-H depletion, and diffuse-type histology, and was low for TMB-H and germline P/LP variants because of limited study numbers or imprecision (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410).

Results

Study selection and characteristics

From 931 database records, 25 supplementary sources, and 104 curated PubMed abstracts, 264 duplicates were removed and 45 studies ultimately met inclusion criteria (Figure 1 and Supplementary Tables S1-S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). Of these, 35 were clinicopathological or epidemiological cohorts and 10 were molecular/genomic cohorts. The included evidence encompassed distal esophageal-only, GEJ/cardia-only, gastric-only, and mixed-site cohorts; the exact tumor locations for all 45 studies are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). Geographical distribution included the United States (Lai et al.,6 Codipilly et al.,7 Sawas et al.,8,12 Lumish et al.,11 Wu et al.,19 Setia et al.,20 Ku et al.,13 Rona et al.,31 Torrejon et al.,5 and Yasinzai et al.32), several European countries [Radkiewicz et al.15 (Sweden), Deac et al.16 (Ireland), Kist et al.24 (Germany), Rompen et al.33 (Germany), and Pocurull et al.21 (Spain)], East Asia [Liang et al.14 (China) and Chen et al.30 (China)], and international/multicenter consortia (Hansford et al.34 and TCGA35). The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale ranged from 7 to 9 (median 8), indicating generally high methodological quality.

Cumulatively, the clinical cohorts captured >600 000 patients. The largest population-based contributions came from Torrejon et al.5 (∼280 000), Yasinzai et al.32 (103 674), Kist et al.24 (46 110), Codipilly et al.7 (34 443), Radkiewicz et al.15 (27 854), Deac et al.16 (21 706), and Lai et al.6 (18 278). Institutional/specialized cohorts included Lumish et al.11 (1123 at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center [MSKCC]), Wu et al.19 (5175 via Caris CODEai), Liang et al.14 (3940 at West China Hospital), Pocurull et al.21 (2071 in the Spanish AEGASTRIC registry), Chen et al.30 (4610 AEG cases), Rompen et al.33, Ku et al.13, Sawas et al.8, Setia et al.20, and Rona et al.31

Advanced stage at presentation

Ten studies contributed 2 × 2 data for advanced stage (III/IV) at diagnosis (Figure 2), representing distal esophageal-only, GEJ/cardia-only, gastric-only, and mixed-site cohorts (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). The pooled OR for early-onset versus average-onset disease was 1.39 (95% CI 1.26-1.54, P < 0.001, I2 = 86.6%, k = 10), indicating ∼40% higher odds of presenting with advanced disease. The strongest individual estimates came from institutional cohorts—Lumish et al.11 (MSKCC, OR ∼2.9), Sawas et al.8 (Mayo, OR ∼2.1), and Rompen et al.33 (Heidelberg, OR ∼2.4)—whereas population-based cohorts such as Codipilly et al.7 (SEER, OR 1.3), Radkiewicz et al.15 (Swedish Cancer Register, OR 1.4), Yasinzai et al.32 (National Cancer Database, OR 1.35), Kist et al.24 (German Cancer Registry, OR 1.42), and Deac et al.16 (all-Ireland, OR 1.38) yielded more modest estimates. Despite heterogeneity in magnitude, the direction of association was consistent.

Forest plot—advanced stage at presentation (v3; new studies in red).

Signet-ring cell and diffuse-type histology

Ten studies provided extractable signet-ring data (Figure 3), spanning distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, gastric, and mixed-site cohorts (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). The pooled OR was 2.52 (95% CI 1.84-3.47, P = 1.0 × 10-8, I2 = 97.4%, k = 10), indicating that early-onset patients had more than twice the odds of signet-ring morphology. Heterogeneity reflected variation in magnitude, whereas the direction of association was consistent. Kist et al.24 demonstrated an inverse age gradient, with the signet-ring proportion increasing as age at diagnosis fell. Deac et al.16 reported diffuse histology in 26.6% of young-onset gastric adenocarcinomas versus 13.8% in middle-onset and 10.4% in late-onset subsets. Liang et al.14 likewise observed a higher prevalence of signet-ring cell carcinoma in early-onset than average-onset gastric cancer.

Forest plot—signet-ring cell histology (v3).

The related diffuse-type phenotype was analyzed in four molecularly characterized cohorts that included mixed distal esophageal/GEJ/gastric and gastric-only populations. Diffuse-type histology was enriched in early-onset disease (pooled OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.77-2.88, P = 4.8 × 10-11, I2 = 68.9%; Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410), linking the clinicopathological signet-ring finding with enrichment of the GS subtype.11,35,36

Synchronous distant metastasis at diagnosis

Seven studies permitted pooling of synchronous distant metastasis (M1) at diagnosis (Figure 4), including distal esophageal-only, GEJ/cardia-only, gastric-only, and mixed-site cohorts (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). The pooled OR was 1.20 (95% CI 1.04-1.38, P = 0.012, I2 = 94.8%, k = 7), with substantial heterogeneity. This feature was analyzed separately from advanced stage because it isolates metastatic dissemination at presentation rather than combining stage III and IV diseases. The direction of association was biologically consistent with enrichment of signet-ring/diffuse histology, which has a recognized predilection for peritoneal dissemination.

Forest plot—synchronous distant metastasis at diagnosis (v3).

High-grade/poorly differentiated histology

Nine studies reported grade data amenable to pooling (Figure 5), representing distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, gastric, and mixed-site cohorts (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). The pooled OR for G3/G4 disease was 1.61 (95% CI 1.23-2.11, P = 5.2 × 10-4, I2 = 96.1%, k = 9), with considerable heterogeneity. Contributing cohorts included Lumish et al.,11 Liang et al.,14 Kist et al.24 (77% G3/G4 in early-onset versus 62% in older patients), Deac et al.16 (60.6% poorly differentiated in young-onset gastric cancer), Chen et al.,30 Rona et al.,31 Calderillo-Ruiz et al.,37 Yang et al.,38 and Theuer et al.39

Forest plot—high-grade/poorly differentiated histology (v3).

Female sex

Eight studies contributed female-sex data (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410), encompassing distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, gastric, and mixed-site cohorts (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). The pooled OR was 1.29 (95% CI 1.14-1.44, P = 2.3 × 10-5, I2 = 88.5%, k = 8), indicating a modest over-representation of women among early-onset cases. This finding emerged after the addition of large population-based Swedish,15 Irish,16 German,24 and Chinese14 cohorts and is biologically coherent with the female predominance described in diffuse-type gastric cancer and CDH1-associated hereditary diffuse gastric cancer.34,40

Molecular markers and germline predisposition

Ten molecular cohorts were synthesized (M1-M10 in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410), including mixed distal esophageal/GEJ/gastric cohorts and gastric-only cohorts. The evidence was anchored by three large real-world genomic series: the 5175-patient Caris CODEai cohort from Wu et al.,19 the 1123-patient MSK-IMPACT cohort from Lumish et al.,11 and the 2071-patient Spanish AEGASTRIC registry from Pocurull et al.21 Additional contributions came from Setia et al.20 (University of Chicago), Hansford et al.34 (international CDH1 kindreds), Ku et al.13 (MSKCC), and TCGA reference data.35,36 Supplementary Figure S3 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410) summarizes the molecular landscape.

CDH1 alterations were enriched ∼3-fold in early-onset tumors (pooled OR 2.92, 95% CI 2.20-3.87, P = 8.4 × 10-14, I2 = 31.0%; Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). Wu et al.19 reported CDH1 mutation in 18.9% of early-onset cases versus 6.1% of average-onset cases, Lumish et al.11 documented both somatic and germline CDH1 enrichment, and Setia et al.20 confirmed frequent E-cadherin loss by immunohistochemistry. The heterogeneity in this analysis was lower than for any clinical outcome, reflecting biological consistency across platforms.

MSI-High phenotype was markedly depleted in early-onset tumors (pooled OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.10-0.30, P = 8.1 × 10-10, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410)—a nearly sixfold reduction. Wu et al.19 recorded MSI-H in only 1.3% of early-onset versus 8.6% of average-onset cases, and Lumish et al.11 reported a similar depletion. Likewise, TMB-High (≥10 mutations/Mb) was less common in early-onset tumors (pooled OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.25-0.55, P = 1.2 × 10-6, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410), consistent with the reduced inflammatory/hypermutator phenotype.

Germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants were detected in approximately one in six early-onset patients—pooled OR 2.00 (95% CI 0.96-4.17, P = 0.064, I2 = 87.3%; Supplementary Figure S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410)—a suggestive but imprecise association derived from mixed-site and gastric-only cohorts. Pocurull et al.21 reported germline P/LP variants in ∼14% of patients aged <40 years, including CDH1, BRCA1/2, mismatch-repair genes, and TP53; Lumish et al.11 reported germline positivity in ∼16%-25% of patients aged <50 years; and Hansford et al.34 characterized CDH1-associated hereditary diffuse gastric cancer. Because the CI included no association and ascertainment differed between studies, this finding was interpreted as evidence of a potentially more frequent inherited predisposition rather than proof of a uniformly heritable phenotype.

Publication-bias assessment

For the advanced-stage outcome, Egger’s regression yielded P = 0.0012 (Supplementary Figure S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410), indicating funnel-plot asymmetry consistent with small-study effects. This signal most likely reflects the under-reporting of small single-center studies that failed to detect an early-onset disadvantage rather than overt suppression of unfavorable data and it reinforces the methodological argument for prioritizing population-based evidence. No marked asymmetry was evident for the molecular outcomes, which were anchored by very large real-world cohorts (Caris, MSKCC, and Spanish AEGASTRIC).

Sensitivity analyses

Prespecified sensitivity analyses by study design and age cut-off produced a striking pattern (Supplementary Figure S9 A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). For advanced stage at presentation, institutional cohorts yielded a pooled OR of 2.46 (95% CI 1.70-3.54, k = 4), whereas population-based cohorts yielded an OR of 1.41 (95% CI 1.31-1.50, k = 6)—a 1.7-fold disparity that cannot be explained by random variation. This pattern is most parsimoniously explained by referral bias: patients referred to academic centers such as MSKCC,11 Mayo Clinic,8 Heidelberg,33 and West China Hospital14 are selected for symptomatic, advanced, or unusual disease, inflating effect estimates relative to the true population.

Stratification by age cut-off revealed an age-gradient of risk. Studies using a strict <40-45 year cut-off yielded a pooled OR of 2.64 (95% CI 0.90-7.76, k = 2), studies using the conventional <50 year cut-off yielded 1.50 (k = 6), and lenient cut-offs (<55 or <60) yielded a more modest 1.31 (k = 2). Although the youngest-cut-off stratum was underpowered, the monotonic gradient suggests that truly early-onset disease (<40 years) may represent an even more aggressive phenotype.

Subgroup restricted to GEJ tumors

The subgroup restricted to studies with clearly demarcated GEJ anatomy—Siewert-classified tumors, SEER-AEG cohorts, Japanese multicenter GEJ series, AEG cohort reported by Chen et al.30, and the MSK GEJ subgroup11—yielded a pooled OR for advanced stage of 1.55 (95% CI 0.98-2.45, P = 0.061, k = 4) and a pooled OR for signet-ring histology of 1.87 (95% CI 1.65-2.12, P < 0.001, I2 = 2.1%, k = 3; Supplementary Figure S9, B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100410). The low heterogeneity of the signet-ring analysis supports the reproducibility of this association specifically within GEJ tumors.

Survival outcomes (descriptive synthesis)

Formal pooling of survival endpoints was not possible because of inconsistent time-to-event reporting and variable adjustment. Nevertheless, descriptive synthesis reveals an important paradox. Two large population-based studies—Radkiewicz et al.15 (Swedish Cancer Register, adjusted EMRR 0.73 for esophageal, 0.75 for cardia, 0.67 for gastric in early-onset) and Deac et al.16 (all-Ireland, HR 1.84 for late-onset versus young-onset)—reported BETTER stage-adjusted survival in early-onset patients. Conversely, the Chinese cohort reported by Liang et al.14 documented worse overall survival within the AEG subgroup specifically and noted that chemotherapy did not improve OS in early-onset gastric cancer. Wu et al.19 reported worse real-world overall survival after chemotherapy, particularly in CDH1-mutated cases. This apparent contradiction is reconciled by recognizing that young patients tolerate aggressive multimodal therapy better, partially compensating for their more unfavorable tumor biology—but that biologically defined subgroups (CDH1-mutated, AEG-specific) retain a poor prognosis despite chronological youth. A recent Japanese multicenter analysis by Yamamoto et al.41 of 555 patients with stage IV gastric cancer demonstrated that the early-onset stratum (<40 years) had significantly worse median overall survival (5.7 versus 8.8 months, P = 0.029) and an adjusted hazard ratio for death of 1.88 (95% CI 1.17-3.04, P = 0.009), indicating that the ‘better survival in the young’ narrative does not generalize to metastatic disease. Conversely, Theuer et al.39 in a California three-county tumor-registry analysis of 3020 patients found that age ≤40 was not an independent predictor of overall survival after adjustment for stage, histology, and treatment—consistent with the stage-adjusted population signal reported by Radkiewicz et al.,15 Deac et al.,16 and Yang et al.,38 analyzing 2601 SEER EAC patients, reported a lower overall survival and worse hazard ratio in patients <45 compared with the 45-59 stratum (P = 0.046). The compounded message is that young age confers a stage-adjusted survival advantage in nonmetastatic population-based cohorts, but not in genuinely advanced or CDH1-enriched subgroups, in which worse biology prevails over preserved performance status.39,41

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, integrating 45 observational studies and >600 000 patients, provides a comprehensive quantitative synthesis of clinicopathological and molecular differences between early-onset and average-onset adenocarcinomas of the distal esophagus, GEJ/cardia, and stomach. Taken together, the findings suggest that early-onset disease may represent a biologically coherent phenotype whose hallmarks span clinicopathological and molecular domains.6,11,14,16,19

At the clinicopathological level, early-onset patients were diagnosed at more advanced stages (OR 1.39), had ∼2.5-fold greater odds of signet-ring histology, 1.2-fold greater odds of synchronous distant metastasis, and 1.6-fold greater odds of high-grade tumors. These analyses included distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, gastric, and mixed-site cohorts; the GEJ-restricted subgroup was considered separately. The modest over-representation of women among early-onset cases became detectable after the addition of population-scale Swedish,15 Irish,16 German,24 and Chinese14 cohorts and is consistent with the predominance of women in diffuse-type gastric cancer and CDH1-associated hereditary diffuse gastric cancer syndromes.34,40

The molecular synthesis offers a unifying explanation for the clinical observations. The approximately threefold enrichment of CDH1 alterations in early-onset tumors—observed consistently across MSK-IMPACT,11 Caris CODEai,19 University of Chicago,20 and Spanish AEGASTRIC21 cohorts—is particularly consequential. Loss of E-cadherin function disrupts epithelial integrity, promotes single-cell invasion, and is mechanistically linked to both the diffuse Laurén phenotype42 and signet-ring morphology. Equally striking is the depletion of inflammation-driven hypermutator phenotypes: MSI-High was ∼6-fold less common and TMB-High ∼2.7-fold less common in early-onset disease. This immunogenomic pattern is consistent with the TCGA ‘genomically stable’ subtype enrichment reported by Lumish et al.11 and by Wu et al.19 in the 5175-patient Caris real-world dataset. A GS, CDH1-driven tumor is, almost by definition, a tumor in which neoantigen burden is low and in which the therapeutic benefit of checkpoint blockade is expected to be attenuated43, 44, 45—a prediction increasingly supported by real-world outcomes data.

The pooled estimate for germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (OR 2.00, 95% CI 0.96-4.17) suggests a potentially more frequent inherited predisposition in early-onset disease but remains imprecise. Pocurull et al.21 documented germline P/LP variants in ∼14% of early-onset cases, with CDH1 as the most frequent driver; Lumish et al.11 reported similar absolute rates; and Hansford et al.34 established the framework for CDH1-associated hereditary diffuse gastric cancer. The observed spectrum—CDH1, BRCA1/2, mismatch-repair genes, and TP53—maps on to identifiable hereditary cancer-predisposition syndromes,40 several of which have disease-modifying surveillance or prophylactic options. These data support consideration of broader germline evaluation while acknowledging that the pooled association did not reach conventional statistical significance.

An important methodological insight emerged from our prespecified sensitivity analysis. Pooled effect sizes derived from institutional/tertiary-center cohorts (Lumish et al.,11 Sawas et al.,8 Rompen et al.,33 and Liang et al.14) were consistently and substantially larger than those derived from population-based registry cohorts (Lai et al.,6 Codipilly et al.,7 Radkiewicz et al.,15 Deac et al.,16 Kist et al.,24 and Yasinzai et al.32). For the advanced stage, institutional series yielded a pooled OR of 2.46, whereas population series yielded OR 1.41—a 1.7-fold disparity most parsimoniously explained by referral bias. The implication is important: the absolute magnitude of the early-onset disadvantage is almost certainly closer to the more modest population-based estimates than to the dramatic institutional figures. Nevertheless, the direction of effect is preserved across both cohort types and the signet-ring signal in particular survives at full magnitude in the population-based setting.15,16,24,30,31

The GEJ-restricted subgroup analysis provides reassurance that the signet-ring association is not solely an artifact of pooling anatomically heterogeneous distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, and gastric cohorts. The pooled OR for signet-ring histology was 1.87 with low heterogeneity (I2 = 2%). The advanced-stage association in the GEJ subgroup (OR 1.55, P = 0.061) was directionally consistent but imprecise and should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Publication-bias assessment merits candid discussion. Egger’s regression on the advanced-stage outcome yielded P < 0.01, indicating asymmetric funnel-plot distribution consistent with small-study effects. This finding—which should not be dismissed—likely reflects the under-reporting of small single-center studies that failed to detect an early-onset disadvantage rather than overt suppression of unfavorable data. It reinforces the methodological argument for prioritizing population-based evidence when translating these results into clinical policy. Importantly, the molecular meta-analyses, although based on fewer studies, were anchored by very large real-world cohorts11,19,21 attenuating concerns about small-study artifact in the genomic domain.

Several limitations deserve acknowledgement. Firstly, the age cut-off defining ‘early onset’ was not uniform, whereas <50 years was the most common threshold, a minority of studies used <40 or ≤45 years. Sensitivity analysis suggested an age gradient; however, the youngest-cut-off stratum was small. Secondly, several clinical outcomes—particularly survival—could not be pooled formally because of inconsistent reporting. Thirdly, data on Helicobacter pylori status,9 obesity, and reflux history were too sparse to support etiological risk-factor meta-analysis. Fourthly, most molecular cohorts originated in high-income countries, limiting external validity. Fifthly, anatomical definitions and the proportion of distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, and gastric tumors varied across studies, although every cohort was coded by site and a GEJ-restricted subgroup was analyzed, age-stratified site-specific 2 × 2 data were not consistently available, leaving residual anatomical heterogeneity.

The findings may inform an age-aware management framework for patients diagnosed before age 50 years with adenocarcinoma of the distal esophagus, GEJ/cardia, or stomach. Potential considerations include comprehensive staging with attention to peritoneal dissemination, explicit reporting of Laurén type and signet-ring morphology; assessment of established predictive biomarkers, including CLDN18.2 in advanced gastric/GEJ disease46; and germline evaluation guided by age, histology, family history, and contemporary hereditary cancer criteria. Prospective age-stratified studies are needed to determine whether therapeutic strategies should differ from those used in average-onset disease.

Three research priorities emerge. The first is prospective, multicenter, age-stratified molecular characterization of distal esophageal, GEJ/cardia, and gastric adenocarcinoma in under-represented populations, particularly in East Asia.14,30 The second is functional study of the CDH1/diffuse/GS axis to identify actionable vulnerabilities. The third is longitudinal follow-up of germline-positive kindreds identified through systematic testing of early-onset probands,21 which may refine surveillance and risk-reduction strategies.

Conclusions

Early-onset adenocarcinomas of the distal esophagus, GEJ/cardia, and stomach appear to have a distinct clinicopathological and molecular profile. Across 45 observational studies, early-onset disease was associated with advanced stage, signet-ring histology, synchronous distant metastasis, high grade, and female sex, together with enrichment of diffuse-type and CDH1-associated features, reduced MSI-High/TMB-High, and a potentially more frequent inherited predisposition. These findings support age-aware diagnostic and research pathways, whereas prospective site-specific studies are needed before concluding that early-onset disease constitutes a uniform biological entity across all three anatomical sites.
