Experiences and coping strategies related to constipation among individuals with cancer: a qualitative study
Authors: Derya Bıçak Ayık, Ayşe Serpici, Derya Akça Doğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeConstipation is a common and distressing symptom among individuals with cancer, yet its subjective impact on patients’ daily lives remains insufficiently understood. This study aimed to explore the experiences of constipation and the coping strategies used by individuals receiving cancer treatment.MethodsA qualitative descriptive design was used. Between February and June 2024, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 20 cancer patients in the oncology unit of a hospital. Data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using inductive content analysis.ResultsFour themes emerged from the analysis: physical effects, psychological effects, social and lifestyle effects, and coping strategies. Participants described constipation as a multidimensional experience characterized by abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, fatigue, reduced mobility, emotional distress, persistent symptom-related concerns, social withdrawal, and disruption of daily activities. Participants frequently reported difficulties maintaining household responsibilities, social relationships, and everyday routines. To manage symptoms, they employed a range of dietary, behavioral, and psychological coping strategies, including increased fluid intake, dietary modifications, physical activity, positive thinking, and support from family members. Across all themes, constipation was experienced as a loss of bodily and daily-life control that extended beyond bowel dysfunction and affected physical, emotional, and social well-being.ConclusionConstipation was perceived not merely as a gastrointestinal symptom but as a multidimensional burden affecting autonomy, daily functioning, and quality of life. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive constipation assessment and person-centered supportive care that addresses physical, psychological, and social dimensions of symptom burden. Early identification and holistic management of constipation may help improve quality of life among individuals living with cancer.Keywords: Cancer, Constipation, Coping strategies, Qualitative research, Patient experience

Introduction

Constipation is commonly defined as infrequent bowel movements or difficulty in defecation, often accompanied by symptoms such as excessive straining, incomplete evacuation, hard or lumpy stools, or the need for manual maneuvers, and in some cases, occurs without obvious symptoms [1, 2]. It is a prevalent symptom among patients with advanced-stage cancer and significantly impairs quality of life [1, 3]. The prevalence of constipation in this population is estimated to range between 30% and 80% [2, 4]. The incidence of constipation increases as cancer progresses, particularly due to the use of opioid analgesics and anticholinergic medications [1, 5].

The primary causes of constipation in cancer patients include medications (especially opioids), chemotherapy and other cancer treatments, inadequate fluid intake, anorexia, and physical inactivity. Chemotherapy and several supportive medications used during cancer treatment may impair gastrointestinal motility and contribute to the development or worsening of constipation [1, 5, 6]. When left untreated, constipation may lead to distressing symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and psychological stress, and even life-threatening complications such as bowel obstruction and perforation [2, 4, 6, 7].

Despite its serious consequences, constipation is often underrecognized and ineffectively managed by nurses in clinical practice [8]. This situation may exacerbate patients’ physical discomfort and psychological distress [1, 9].

In cases of opioid-induced constipation, lifestyle modifications such as the use of laxatives or stool softeners, increased fluid intake, fiber-rich diets, and enhanced physical activity are typically recommended [3, 10]. It is also essential to closely monitor patients to detect laxative-related side effects and other symptoms at an early stage [5–7].

As healthcare professionals who spend extensive time with patients and play a key role in identifying those at risk, nurses need to be knowledgeable about the assessment, causes, and management strategies of constipation in cancer patients [8]. They should also contribute to generating evidence-based data, facilitate knowledge sharing among healthcare providers, and promote interdisciplinary collaboration [5].

A limited number of studies have focused on the assessment of constipation in cancer patients, its causes, symptom burden, and management strategies [2, 5–7]. Although constipation is widely recognized as a common symptom in cancer care, limited research has explored how patients themselves perceive and cope with this experience. Understanding patient-driven coping strategies may help inform more person-centered nursing interventions. The aim of this study was to explore how individuals with cancer experience constipation and the coping strategies they use to manage this symptom.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design using inductive content analysis.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted between February and June 2024 in the oncology inpatient ward and outpatient chemotherapy unit of a university-affiliated hospital in Türkiye. During the study period, all adult patients attending these clinical settings were consecutively approached by the first researcher and assessed for eligibility. Participant recruitment followed a purposive sampling strategy, aiming to include individuals who had experienced constipation during cancer treatment and were able to provide rich descriptions of their experiences. Eligibility was assessed through face-to-face interviews conducted by the first researcher using the Rome III diagnostic criteria for constipation (Table 1). Patients who met the Rome III criteria and fulfilled the remaining eligibility criteria were invited to participate in the study. The Rome III criteria were administered prospectively during the recruitment process and were not assessed retrospectively from medical records.

Inclusion criteria were being 18 years of age or older, being able to maintain oral intake, having no cognitive or physical impairment that would interfere with communication, and meeting the Rome III diagnostic criteria for constipation. Patients diagnosed with irritable bowel syndrome, gastrointestinal perforation, gastrointestinal bleeding were excluded. Patients with abdominal malignancies were not excluded by the study protocol; some participants had abdominal malignancies, but none had documented peritoneal malignancy. During the study period, 25 patients met the eligibility criteria and were invited to participate. Twenty patients agreed to participate, while five declined because of fatigue, pain, or unwillingness to take part in the interview. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved.

No sampling quotas or predefined selection criteria based on cancer type, age, or gender were applied. The diagnostic distribution of the participants reflected the patients who attended the oncology inpatient ward and outpatient chemotherapy unit during the recruitment period, met the eligibility criteria for constipation, and agreed to participate. Therefore, the relatively high proportion of women and patients with breast and gynecological cancers was not the result of deliberate diagnostic selection but reflected the clinical profile of the eligible and consenting participants recruited during the study period.

Data collection

Data were collected using a participant information form, the Constipation Assessment Scale (CAS), and semi-structured individual interviews.

Semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were conducted by the first author in a quiet and private room within the oncology unit to ensure participant comfort and confidentiality, and each interview lasted approximately 40–60 min. The interviews aimed to gain an in-depth understanding of patients’ experiences of constipation, its impact on daily life, and coping strategies. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes, categories, or insights emerged from the interviews. Saturation was determined through ongoing comparison of new interview data with existing codes, and no novel themes emerged in the final interviews. Additional interviews were conducted to confirm the stability and redundancy of the data, and the study was completed with a total of 20 participants. Although interview data constituted the primary data source, information obtained from the participant information form and CAS was used solely to describe participants’ characteristics and provide contextual background for the qualitative findings. These data were not included in the qualitative analysis.

Data collection tools

Patient information form

This form was developed by the researchers to collect socio-demographic and clinical information, including age, gender, marital status, educational level, cancer type, and time since cancer diagnosis, duration of chemotherapy, and perceived health status. Perceived health status was assessed using a single self-rated question: “How would you describe your overall health?” Participants rated their overall health on a scale from 1 to 10, with higher scores indicating better perceived health. For participants who were illiterate, all form items were read aloud by the researcher, and responses were recorded verbatim to ensure accurate data collection.

Constipation Assessment Scale (CAS)

The scale was originally developed by McMillan and Williams (1989). The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Demir-Doğan and Aktuğ (2017). The scale consists of eight items and is scored between 0 and 16, with higher scores indicating more severe constipation. In the original study by McMillan and Williams, Cronbach’s alpha was reported as 0.70, while Demir-Doğan and Aktuğ found it to be 0.78 [11]. In this study, the CAS was used only to describe constipation severity as background information, not for analytical purposes.

Interview form

The researchers developed a semi-structured interview form to explore patients’ experiences with constipation, the treatments they received, and their coping strategies. The form consisted of five open-ended questions:

Data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using the inductive content analysis approach described by Elo and Kyngäs [12]. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ permission, transcribed verbatim and read repeatedly to achieve immersion in the data. Coding was performed manually without the use of qualitative analysis software. During analysis, meaning units were first identified and labeled with initial codes. Through constant comparison, related codes were grouped into subthemes based on conceptual similarities and differences. These subthemes were then further refined and synthesized into overarching themes that captured the underlying patterns in participants’ experiences.

Two researchers (DBA and AS) independently coded the transcripts line by line. The coding process and theme development were discussed iteratively within the research team, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus to enhance analytic rigor. Field notes and unstructured observations recorded during data collection were used to support and contextualize the interview data. Data saturation was considered achieved when no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews. To ensure confidentiality, each participant was anonymized using the letter “P” followed by a participant number (e.g., P1, P2).

Reflexivity and trustworthiness

The research team has professional experience in oncology nursing and prior training in qualitative research methods. To enhance the credibility and dependability of the findings, two researchers independently coded all interview transcripts line by line before comparing and discussing their coding. Discrepancies were resolved through reflective dialogue until consensus was reached, and theme development was conducted collaboratively.

An audit trail was maintained throughout the research process, documenting coding decisions, theme refinement, and analytic reflections to ensure transparency and confirmability. Field notes and observational memos recorded during data collection were used to contextualize participants’ narratives and support interpretation of the findings.

Reflexivity was addressed through ongoing discussions among the researchers to critically examine how their clinical backgrounds, assumptions, and experiences in oncology care might influence data interpretation. These reflexive considerations were documented and revisited during analysis to minimize potential bias.

Credibility was further strengthened by the use of rich, verbatim participant quotations to illustrate themes and allow readers to assess the connection between the data and interpretations. Data saturation and redundancy of themes were also considered indicators of analytic rigor.

Reporting guideline

This study was reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guideline.

Results

The mean time since cancer diagnosis was 22.75 ± 25.11 months, and the mean duration of chemotherapy was 13.75 ± 10.92 months. The mean perceived health status score was 5.80 ± 1.90. Theme 1: physical effects

Participants described constipation as a distressing physical experience that extended beyond bowel dysfunction and affected their overall physical well-being. Two subthemes emerged: bodily symptoms and mobility limitations.

Subtheme 1: bodily symptoms

Constipation was commonly associated with a range of unpleasant physical symptoms, including abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, headache, and fatigue. Participants frequently portrayed these symptoms as persistent and overwhelming, often using vivid expressions to describe their discomfort. Rather than viewing constipation as an isolated bowel problem, many participants experienced it as a condition that affected their entire body. Participants described feeling “blocked,” “swollen,” “tense,” and “exhausted,” indicating that the burden of constipation extended beyond gastrointestinal discomfort. Several participants emphasized that abdominal fullness and pain interfered with their ability to rest comfortably and contributed to a constant sense of physical unease.

“Constipation ruins me. It hits my stomach—I feel bloated and nauseous.” (P2).

“Constipation ruins me. It hits my stomach—I feel bloated and nauseous.” (P2).

“My stomach hurts. It’s like there’s a knot inside me.” (P5).

These accounts suggest that constipation was experienced as a persistent source of bodily distress that heightened symptom burden during cancer treatment. The physical discomfort associated with constipation appeared to amplify participants’ awareness of illness and contribute to an overall decline in perceived well-being.

Subtheme 2: mobility limitations

Beyond physical symptoms, participants reported that constipation reduced their ability to move comfortably and engage in everyday activities. Severe abdominal discomfort, weakness, and fatigue limited mobility and made routine tasks increasingly difficult.

“I can’t walk—I feel paralyzed.” (P18).

“I can’t walk—I feel paralyzed.” (P18).

“I constantly feel tired. I don’t want to do anything. I don’t even want to get out of bed.” (P13).

For several participants, constipation was associated with reduced energy levels and loss of physical functioning. Participants described spending more time resting, avoiding movement, and experiencing difficulty performing activities that were previously manageable. These experiences suggest that constipation may contribute to functional decline by restricting mobility and diminishing patients’ sense of physical capability and independence.

Theme 2: psychological effects

Participants described constipation as a psychologically distressing experience that affected both their emotional well-being and their thoughts about daily life. Two subthemes emerged: emotional disturbances and symptom-related thoughts and concerns.

Subtheme 1: emotional disturbances

Participants frequently reported experiencing negative emotions associated with constipation, including irritability, stress, sadness, and frustration. Persistent discomfort, repeated unsuccessful attempts to defecate, and the inability to obtain relief appeared to contribute to emotional distress.

“I become very irritable when I am constipated. Everything bothers me.” (P7).

“I become very irritable when I am constipated. Everything bothers me.” (P7).

“I feel stressed because I cannot relieve myself.” (P11).

For many participants, constipation represented more than a physical symptom. The ongoing discomfort and uncertainty surrounding bowel function appeared to affect their mood and emotional well-being, making it more difficult to cope with the broader challenges of cancer and its treatment.

Subtheme 2: symptom-related thoughts and concerns

In addition to emotional distress, participants reported that constipation frequently occupied their thoughts. Many described persistent concerns about bowel movements and uncertainty regarding when or whether relief would occur. These concerns often led to recurring negative thoughts and constant preoccupation with symptoms.

“I think about it all the time. I keep wondering whether I will be able to go today.” (P4).

“It occupies my mind constantly.” (P18).

Participants’ narratives suggested that constipation became an ongoing psychological concern rather than a temporary physical complaint. The persistent focus on bowel function appeared to reinforce feelings of helplessness and reduced control over daily life. Some participants described constantly monitoring their symptoms and adjusting their daily activities around the possibility of bowel-related discomfort. This ongoing preoccupation contributed to the psychological burden of constipation and influenced how participants experienced everyday life during cancer treatment.

Theme 3: social and lifestyle impacts

Participants described constipation as a symptom that extended beyond physical discomfort and interfered with their ability to maintain normal daily routines and social relationships. Two interrelated subthemes emerged: disruption of daily activities and social withdrawal.

Subtheme 1: disruption of daily activities

Many participants reported that constipation affected their ability to carry out routine activities, including household responsibilities, personal tasks, and leaving the home. Participants described planning their day around bowel-related discomfort and the uncertainty of bowel movements. For some, persistent abdominal pain, bloating, and the constant urge to defecate reduced motivation and energy, making even simple daily activities difficult. Several participants indicated that constipation limited their independence and altered their usual roles within the family. Inability to perform household tasks created feelings of frustration and inadequacy, particularly among those who were accustomed to being actively involved in domestic responsibilities.

Subtheme 2: social withdrawal

Constipation also influenced participants’ social lives. Many described avoiding social situations because of discomfort, fear of worsening symptoms, embarrassment, or concerns about access to a toilet when outside the home. Integrative interpretation of the findings

Across all themes, participants described constipation as a multidimensional experience extending far beyond bowel dysfunction. Physical discomfort, emotional distress, social withdrawal, and self-developed coping strategies were closely interconnected. Participants frequently portrayed constipation as a symptom that reduced their sense of control over their bodies and daily lives. The findings suggest that the burden of constipation is shaped not only by symptom severity but also by its influence on psychological well-being, social participation, and perceived autonomy. These interconnected experiences highlight the importance of holistic symptom assessment and person-centered supportive care in oncology and palliative care settings.

Discussion

Constipation is a common yet often underestimated symptom in cancer care [4, 6, 13, 14]. Although it is frequently conceptualized as a gastrointestinal problem, the findings of this study suggest that patients experience constipation as a multidimensional burden affecting physical functioning, emotional well-being, social participation, and everyday life. A key interpretive finding of this study is that participants experienced constipation as a loss of bodily and daily-life control. Rather than describing constipation solely as difficulty with bowel movements, participants portrayed it as a condition that disrupted their routines, restricted their activities, and diminished their sense of autonomy. This interpretation is consistent with recent qualitative syntheses demonstrating that bowel dysfunction is often characterized by unpredictability, uncertainty, and difficulties maintaining control over daily life [7, 9, 15, 16].

Participants frequently described abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, fatigue, and reduced mobility. Similar symptoms have been documented among individuals with cancer experiencing constipation and are recognized as major contributors to symptom burden [1, 4, 6]. Moschen et al. (2022) emphasized that constipation remains an underestimated and frequently overlooked symptom despite its substantial impact on quality of life [4]. Likewise, evidence-based guidelines recommend comprehensive assessment of bowel habits, medication use, dietary intake, fluid consumption, physical activity, comorbidities, and environmental factors when evaluating constipation in cancer populations [1, 8]. However, participants in the present study described constipation not simply as a gastrointestinal symptom but as a condition that limited mobility, reduced energy, and interfered with everyday functioning. Similar observations were reported in a qualitative synthesis of bowel dysfunction experiences, where patients described sleep disruption, fatigue, pain, fear of symptom exacerbation, and difficulty maintaining normal activities because of bowel-related symptoms [16].

The physical burden of constipation was closely intertwined with psychological distress. Participants frequently described frustration, irritability, sadness, helplessness, and persistent preoccupation with bowel function. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that constipation negatively affects psychological well-being and quality of life [1, 7, 9]. In a qualitative synthesis of bowel dysfunction experiences, participants frequently reported fear, embarrassment, anxiety, loss of confidence, and passive avoidance behaviors resulting from unpredictable and uncontrollable symptoms [16]. Similarly, Lin et al. (2024) reported that individuals living with constipation often experience emotional suffering and communication difficulties because bowel-related symptoms remain socially sensitive and stigmatized [7]. The present findings suggest that emotional distress may arise not only from symptom severity but also from uncertainty surrounding symptom occurrence and the perception that bodily functions are no longer under one’s control. Within the context of cancer, where uncertainty and vulnerability are already prominent, constipation may therefore represent an additional threat to autonomy and psychological well-being.

An important contribution of this study is the insight it provides into the social consequences of constipation. Participants reported difficulties leaving home, maintaining social relationships, and fulfilling family and household responsibilities. These findings align with previous evidence demonstrating that bowel dysfunction often restricts participation in social activities because of concerns about embarrassment, lack of toilet access, and fear of unexpected bowel events [16, 17]. Similarly, Kim et al. (2025) identified social isolation and disconnection as one of the dominant themes among individuals living with bowel dysfunction following cancer treatment [15]. The unpredictability of symptoms may compel individuals to remain close to toilets, avoid social situations, and withdraw from previously meaningful activities. In the present study, constipation appeared to affect not only social participation but also participants’ sense of independence and ability to maintain valued social roles. These findings support growing recognition that bowel symptoms should be evaluated according to their broader impact on quality of life and daily functioning rather than symptom severity alone [18].

Participants employed a variety of coping strategies, including dietary modifications, increased fluid intake, physical activity, distraction techniques, positive thinking, and support from family members. Similar self-management approaches have been described in previous studies, where patients frequently relied on dietary adjustments, fluid management, physical activity, complementary and alternative therapies, and peer recommendations to manage bowel symptoms [10, 16, 19]. The diversity of coping strategies observed in the present study suggests that symptom management was often individualized and largely based on trial-and-error experiences. Although exercise has been reported as a potentially effective intervention for constipation management, evidence remains mixed [20]. While increased physical activity has been associated with improved symptom control and quality of life among cancer survivors [21], a meta-analysis by Nakano et al. (2018) found that exercise interventions had limited direct effects on constipation despite improving other symptoms such as fatigue and pain [13]. Similarly, lifestyle interventions may be difficult to implement in patients with advanced cancer, particularly when constipation is opioid-related [1].

Complementary and alternative therapies also emerged as relevant coping strategies. Previous research conducted in Türkiye found that phytotherapy was among the most commonly used approaches for chemotherapy-induced constipation [19]. In addition, acupuncture and acupressure-based interventions have demonstrated promising effects in alleviating constipation symptoms among patients with cancer [2, 14]. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of many non-pharmacological interventions remains uncertain, highlighting the need for further research and individualized clinical guidance.

Another noteworthy finding was the importance of emotional and social resources in coping with constipation. Family support and positive thinking were frequently described as valuable strategies for managing distress. Similarly, previous qualitative studies have highlighted the importance of peer support, shared experiences, emotional adaptation, and acceptance in helping individuals adjust to chronic bowel dysfunction [15, 16]. These findings suggest that effective constipation management should extend beyond symptom relief and include psychosocial support aimed at enhancing coping capacity and resilience.

The findings also have important implications for nursing and supportive care practice. Nurse-led interventions have been shown to improve symptom burden and quality of life among individuals with cancer [22]. Recent evidence further suggests that nurses play a central role in providing individualized bowel management plans, monitoring psychosocial adaptation, supporting self-management, and facilitating timely referrals when needed [13, 15]. Educational interventions, reflective symptom diaries, and ongoing supportive counseling may strengthen patients’ confidence in managing both constipation and other treatment-related symptoms [13]. Recognizing constipation as a multidimensional experience rather than a minor treatment-related side effect may therefore facilitate more holistic and person-centered approaches to cancer and palliative care.

Although constipation imposed a considerable physical and psychological burden on participants, only one-quarter reported using laxatives. This finding may reflect multiple factors, including limited symptom recognition, normalization of constipation as an expected consequence of cancer treatment, concerns about medication use or adverse effects, insufficient patient education, or delayed communication with healthcare professionals. Previous studies have suggested that constipation in individuals with cancer is frequently under-recognized and undertreated despite its substantial impact on quality of life [6, 23, 24]. These findings highlight the importance of proactive symptom assessment and timely supportive interventions by healthcare professionals, particularly nurses.

The relatively low educational level observed in the present sample may also have influenced participants’ ability to recognize constipation as a symptom requiring treatment, seek professional support, and adopt evidence-based self-management strategies. Although health literacy was not directly measured in this study, previous research has shown that lower educational attainment and limited health literacy may reduce symptom awareness, delay help-seeking, and negatively influence symptom management [25–27]. Future studies should investigate the role of health literacy in constipation management among individuals with cancer.

Clinical implications

The findings suggest that constipation assessment should extend beyond the evaluation of bowel function and include physical, psychological, and social dimensions of symptom burden. Routine screening for constipation, early symptom management, individualized patient education, and nurse-led supportive interventions may help reduce the negative impact of constipation on quality of life. Healthcare professionals should recognize constipation as a potentially distressing symptom that can compromise autonomy, social engagement, and emotional well-being. Holistic approaches that address both symptom relief and psychosocial support may therefore be particularly valuable in oncology and palliative care settings.

Limitations

This study has several potential limitations. First, the small sample size, which is typical of qualitative research, may limit the transferability of the findings to the broader population of individuals with cancer experiencing constipation. However, this limitation was mitigated by the richness of the data and the purposive sampling approach applied until data saturation was achieved.

Another limitation is that participants were selected from the researcher’s own clinical setting, and individuals from diverse educational, ethnic, or racial backgrounds were not included, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the uneven distribution of cancer types in the sample may limit the transferability of the findings to broader oncology populations. Because the study focused on participants’ lived experiences of constipation rather than its clinical etiology, constipation was not categorized according to specific underlying causes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting experiences across different constipation contexts within cancer care. Lastly, the study relied on participants’ ability to recall and reflect on their past experiences, which may have introduced recall bias.

Conclusion

This study revealed that constipation in individuals diagnosed with cancer is a multidimensional issue that extends beyond physical discomfort to encompass emotional distress and limitations in social life. The experiences of the participants indicated that constipation significantly diminishes quality of life and that patients develop various individual coping strategies to manage this condition.

In this context, constipation should be assessed in physiological terms and with consideration for its psychological and social dimensions. Healthcare professionals should consider patients’ individual experiences and develop personalized, supportive interventions accordingly. Nutritional regulation, physical activity, and psychosocial support components should be integrated into care processes. Patient-centered and holistic approaches should be prioritized to ensure comprehensive and effective constipation management in cancer care.
