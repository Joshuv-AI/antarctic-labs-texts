Anomalous transparency of photons in non-Markovian coupled waveguides Preprint: APS/123-QED
J. R. Silva, P. A. Brandão

Abstract
The transmission of photons through a pair of coupled single-mode waveguides is studied in detail. It is assumed that one of the waveguides is coupled to a non-Markovian reservoir, described by a Lorentzian spectrum distribution. We identified four distinct regimes for the transmission coefficient and found that, in some conditions, it is more efficient to launch the photons in the lossy waveguide to achieve high transmission. We also report on a loss-induced transparency effect that is induced by the distribution of the frequencies present in the reservoir.



I
Introduction

Integrated photonics has experienced significant advancements in recent years, driven by the emergence of quantum technologies
Flamini et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Bao et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
. By using arrays of coupled waveguides, researchers have demonstrated various key quantum photonic operations and effects, including the controlled-NOT logic gate
Politi et al. 2008
, multiphoton quantum interference
Marshall et al. 2009
, on-chip multiphoton entanglement
Matthews et al. 2009
, quantum walks
Peruzzo et al. 2010
, polarization-encoded qubits
Crespi et al. 2011
, boson sampling
Crespi et al. 2013
, Mach-Zehnder interferometry
Wang et al. 2014
, single-photon
W
-state generation
Gräfe et al. 2014
, experimental studies on quantum complexity
Carolan et al. 2014
, Bloch oscillations
Lebugle et al. 2015
and perfect quantum state transfer
Chapman et al. 2016
to cite a few.

The experimental systems mentioned, and indeed every physical system in the real world, are subject to dissipation. The coupling of a quantum system to its environment can lead to decoherence, a topic of extensive and highly active research
Breuer et al. 2016
;
De Vega and Alonso 2017
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
.

Relevant to the present analysis is the physical system of two coupled waveguides, where one is subjected to a dissipative environment. This system has been studied experimentally by first using classical optical fields
Guo et al. 2009
. Counterintuitively, instead of reducing transmission as one might expect, increasing the loss level in one of the waveguides led to an increase in the transmission coefficient. The authors in
Guo et al. 2009
attributed this phenomenon to the Parity-Time (PT) invariant properties of the structure having exceptional points
Miri and Alù 2019
;
Heiss 2012
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Bender and Boettcher 1998
;
Longhi 2018
;
Bender 2019
. A review of PT-symmetric quantum mechanics, which explains many of the observed effects in waveguide systems, has been published recently
Bender and Hook 2024
.

The importance of PT symmetry in the description of quantum mechanics was suggested by C. M. Bender and S. Boettcher many years ago in the context of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics
Bender and Boettcher 1998
. They provided numerical evidence on the reality of eigenvalues of non-hermitian Hamiltonians having an exact PT symmetry
Bender et al. 1999
and later found the correct inner product necessary to maintain hermiticity, which involves a new symmetry, named
C
by the authors
Bender et al. 2002
. Mostafazadeh was the first to point out that PT-symmetric Hamiltonians are a subclass of a more general class of pseudo-hermitian Hamiltonians
Mostafazadeh 2002
;
Mostafazadeh 2010
;
Mostafazadeh 2003
.

The first experimental results revealing PT-symmetric effects made use of classical optical fields, which started by Guo and collaborators in 2009
Guo et al. 2009
(see also
Rüter et al. 2010
). A genuine PT-symmetric phenomenon related to a quantum measurement was observed and published only six years ago
Klauck et al. 2019
. The authors considered the coincidence properties of photons arriving at the detectors after propagating in the coupled waveguide system under Markovian conditions
Gräfe et al. 2013
and measured the non-Hermitian HOM dip effect. A few theoretical proposals have been published exploring the quantum dynamics in this waveguide system, including anti-parity-time symmetry
Qin et al. 2021
and unequal losses of the waveguides
Zhou 2022a
;
Zhou 2022b
.

We focus here on the transmission properties of such coupled waveguides in the presence of non-Markovian environments. Thus, the average number of photons leaving the system is the main quantity of interest. The anomalous (or, loss-induced) transparency effect, verified in
Guo et al. 2009
, has been theoretically explored in the quantum realm very recently
Beder and Brandão 2024
. It was found that the effect still persists at the single-photon level under Markovian conditions. Also, the transmission depends on the quantum correlation properties of the incident photons. Since it is now a common practice to engineer environments having non-Markovian properties
Li et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2011
;
Liu et al. 2018
, one may ask how the propagation of photons is affected by its interaction with a reservoir having memory effects.

In section II(A), we describe the theoretical model by defining the Hamiltonian of the system and the Heisenberg equations of motion. Section II(B) is devoted to a general discussion of the properties of the Lorentzian environment. Section III presents the results and discussions for two initial conditions of the system that generates zero and nonzero interference terms in the transmission coefficient. In section IV we present our conclusions.

II
Theoretical Model

This section is divided into two subsections. Subsection A deals with the description of the Hamiltonian and the Heisenberg equations of motion. Section B is devoted to a discussion of the non-Markovian environment.

II.1
The Hamiltonian and Heisenberg equations

Consider a pair of single-mode coupled waveguides,
W_{A}
and
W_{B}
, where
W_{B}
is connected to a reservoir (see Fig. 1). The total system is described by the Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+H_{I}
, where
(\hbar=1)

H_{0}=\omega(a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b)+\sum_{n}\beta_{n}r_{n}^{\dagger}r_{n},

(1)

and

H_{I}=\kappa(a^{\dagger}b+ab^{\dagger})+\sum_{n}\alpha(\beta_{n})(b^{\dagger}r_{n}+br_{n}^{\dagger}),

(2)

where
a
(
b
) is the annihilation operator for waveguide
W_{A}
(
W_{B}
),
\omega
is the propagation constant, assumed equal for both waveguides,
r_{n}
is the annihilation operator for the (bosonic) reservoir mode having propagation constant
\beta_{n}
,
\kappa
is the coupling between waveguides
W_{A}
and
W_{B}
and
\alpha(\beta_{n})
is a real-valued coupling constant between waveguide
W_{B}
and mode
\beta_{n}
of the reservoir.

Figure 1
:
A pair of coupled single-mode waveguides,
W_{A}
and
W_{B}
having coupling constant
\kappa
is considered. The waveguide
W_{B}
is connected to a non-Markovian reservoir. The waveguides have a constant cross-section and lie along the
z

In the Heisenberg picture, the operators
a(z)
,
b(z)
and
r_{n}(z)
evolve according to the set of coupled differential equations,

\frac{da}{dz}=-i\omega a-i\kappa b,

(3)

\frac{db}{dz}=-i\omega b-i\kappa a-i\sum_{n}\alpha(\beta_{n})r_{n},

(4)

\frac{dr_{n}}{dz}=-i\beta_{n}r_{n}-i\alpha(\beta_{n})b.

(5)

After substituting the integral equation of Eq. (
5
),

\begin{split}r_{n}(z)=r_{n}(0)&e^{-i\beta_{n}z}\\
&-i\alpha(\beta_{n})\int_{0}^{z}e^{-i\beta_{n}(z-z^{\prime})}b(z^{\prime})\,dz^{\prime},\end{split}

(6)

into Eq. (
4
) and following the usual approach of taking the continuous limit of all sumations over the reservoir modes,
\sum_{n}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}\,d\beta\rho(\beta)
,
\rho(\beta)
being the reservoir mode density, Eq. (
4
) turns into

\begin{split}\frac{db}{dz}=&-i\omega b-i\kappa a\\
&-\int_{0}^{\infty}\,d\beta\,\rho(\beta)\alpha^{2}(\beta)\int_{0}^{z}e^{-i\beta(z-z^{\prime})}b(z^{\prime})\,dz^{\prime}\\
&-i\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\beta)\alpha(\beta)e^{-i\beta z}r(\beta,0)\,d\beta.\end{split}

(7)

The positive quantity
S(\beta)=\rho(\beta)\alpha^{2}(\beta)
is the spectral density of the reservoir and Eq. (
7
) can be written in a more interesting form in terms of the autocorrelation function
R(\tau)
, defined by

R(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}S(\beta)e^{-i\beta\tau}\,d\beta,

(8)

which is a manifestation of the Wiener-Khichin theorem
Goodman 2015
. The usual Markovian approximation assumes that
R(\tau)\propto\delta(\tau)
so that the dynamics of
db(z)/dz
depends only on
z
and not
z^{\prime}<z
. We keep the analysis general and do not assume the Markovian approximation in what follows. Finally, one obtains

\begin{split}\frac{db}{dz}=&-i\omega b-i\kappa a-R\ast b\\
&-i\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\omega)\alpha(\beta)e^{-i\beta z}r(\beta,0)\,d\beta,\end{split}

(9)

where
R\ast b
is the convolution between
R
and
b
.

Our objective is to find
a(z)
and
b(z)
from the pair of coupled equations (
3
) and (
9
). Notice that, once
b(z)
has been found, Eq. (
6
) can be used to obtain
r(\beta,z)
, if necessary. We proceed by using the Laplace transform. Equations (
3
) and (
9
), in Laplace domain, are given by

sA(s)=a(0)-i\omega A(s)-i\kappa B(s),

(10)

\begin{split}sB(s)&=b(0)-i\omega B(s)-i\kappa A(s)-M(s)B(s)\\
&-i\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(\beta)\alpha(\beta)}{s+i\beta}r(\beta,0)\,d\beta,\end{split}

(11)

where
A(s)
and
B(s)
are the Laplace transforms of the operators
a(z)
and
b(z)
,
\text{Re}(s)>0
and
M(s)
is the Laplace transform of
R(\tau)
:

M(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s\tau}R(\tau)\,d\tau.

(12)

The solutions to the coupled algebraic operator equations involving
A(s)
and
B(s)
, can be written as

\begin{split}A(s)&=F_{1}(s)a(0)+G(s)b(0)\\
&+\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{1}(\beta,s)r(\beta,0)\,d\beta\end{split}

(13)

\begin{split}B(s)&=F_{2}(s)b(0)+G(s)a(0)\\
&+\int_{0}^{\infty}H_{2}(\beta,s)r(\beta,0)\,d\beta\end{split}

(14)

To simplify notation, the parameters
F_{j}(s)
,
G(s)
and
H_{j}(\beta,s)
were introduced. They are defined by

F_{j}(s)=\frac{s+i\omega+\delta_{1j}M(s)}{(s+i\omega)\Big[s+i\omega+M(s)\Big]+\kappa^{2}}

(15)

G(s)=\frac{-ik}{(s+i\omega)\Big[s+i\omega+M(s)\Big]+\kappa^{2}}

(16)

H_{1}(\beta,s)=-\frac{iG(s)\rho(\beta)\alpha(\beta)}{s+i\beta}

(17)

and

H_{2}(\beta,s)=-\frac{iF_{2}(s)\rho(\beta)\alpha(\beta)}{s+i\beta}.

(18)

The inverse Laplace transform can now be applied directly to Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) to obtain

\begin{split}a(z)=f_{1}(z)a(0)&+g(z)b(0)\\
&+\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{1}(\beta,z)r(\beta,0)\,d\beta,\end{split}

(19)

\begin{split}b(z)=f_{2}(z)b(0)&+g(z)a(0)\\
&+\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{2}(\beta,z)r(\beta,0)\,d\beta,\end{split}

(20)

where
f_{j}
,
g
and
h_{j}
are given by the respective inverse Laplace transforms of
F_{j}
,
G
and
H_{j}

The functions
f_{j}(z)
,
g(z)
and
h_{j}(z)
complete characterize the dynamics of the system in Markovian and non-Markovian situations. The integrals in Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) are the random noise source terms, necessary to guarantee the correct commutation relations between the operators
a
and
b
at every
z
.

II.2
Lorentzian environment

We assume that waveguide
W_{B}
is coupled to an environment described by a Lorentzian distribution,

S(\beta)=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\pi}\frac{\gamma/2}{(\beta-\beta_{c})^{2}+(\gamma/2)^{2}},

(21)

where
\beta_{c}
is the frequency where the Lorentzian peak is located,
\gamma
is the FWHM of the distribution and
\alpha
is the coupling parameter between waveguide
W_{B}
and the reservoir.

Assuming that
\gamma\ll\beta_{c}
, the integral in Eq. (
8
) can be extended to
(-\infty,\infty)
and the autocorrelation function
R(z)
is given by
R(z)=\alpha^{2}e^{-i\beta_{c}z}e^{-\gamma z/2}
. In the following, we consider
\beta_{c}=\omega
. Finally, the Laplace transform of
R(z)
[Eq. (
12
)] is given by

M(s)=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{s+i\omega+\gamma/2}.

(22)

The function
M(s)
given by Eq. (
22
) must then be substituted back into Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) and the inverse Laplace transform taken to obtain
f_{j}(z)
and
g(z)
, which completely determine the field dynamics.

It is possible to drawn some general conclusions about the behavior of
f_{j}(z)
and

) and (

). Since the denominator is a cubic polynomial with real coefficients
(s\rightarrow s-i\omega)
,

(23)

there are four situations to consider depending on the nature of the discriminant of
Q(s)
: (a) Three distinct real roots
c_{1}\neq c_{2}\neq c_{3}\neq c_{1}
. In this case, the Laplace inversion behaves as
\xi_{1}e^{c_{1}z}+\xi_{2}e^{c_{2}z}+\xi_{3}e^{c_{3}z}.
(b) Three identical real roots
c_{1}=c_{2}=c_{3}
. In this case, the function behaves as
(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}z+\xi_{3}z^{2})e^{c_{1}z}
. (c) Two equal roots
c_{2}=c_{3}
with
c_{1}\neq c_{2},c_{3}
. This time the solution is given by
\xi_{1}e^{c_{1}z}+(\xi_{2}+\xi_{3}z)e^{c_{2}z}

c_{1}
and the other two forming a complex-conjugated pair
c_{2}=c_{3}^{*}

\xi_{1}e^{c_{1}z}+e^{\text{Re}(c_{2})z}[\xi_{2}\cos(z\text{Im}c_{2})+\xi_{3}\sin(z\text{Im}c_{2})]

In closing this section, we would like to highlight that other equivalent approaches could be used to simulate the present model
Garraway 1997
;

;

is coupled to a Markovian bath (through an effective coupling parameter

W_{a}

W_{b}
and
W_{c}
satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{db}{dz}

\displaystyle=-i\omega b-i\kappa a-i\alpha c,

\displaystyle\frac{dc}{dz}

\displaystyle=-i\omega c-i\alpha b-\gamma c.

The integral equation for the annihilation operator

c(z)=c(0)e^{-(\gamma+i\omega)z}-i\alpha\int_{0}^{z}b(z^{\prime})e^{-(\gamma+i\omega)(z-z^{\prime})}dz^{\prime},

III
Results and discussion

Let us assume that the initial state of the reservoir is the vacuum state
\ket{0_{R}}
, so that the initial state of the total system (waveguides + reservoir) is given by

\ket{\Psi_{0}}=\ket{\phi_{ab}}\otimes\ket{0_{R}},

(27)

where
\ket{\phi_{ab}}
indicates the initial state of the photons in both waveguides,
W_{A}
and
W_{B}
, and
\ket{0_{R}}=\ket{0_{r_{1}}}\cdots\ket{0_{r_{n}}}
represents the empty state of the oscillators in the reservoir. As mentioned in the introduction, the main quantity of interest here is the average number of photons leaving the structure. Therefore, we define the transmission
T
as

T(z)=\frac{n_{a}(z)+n_{b}(z)}{n_{a}(0)+n_{b}(0)},

(28)

where
n_{a}(z)=\bra{\Psi_{0}}a^{\dagger}(z)a(z)\ket{\Psi_{0}}
and
n_{b}(z)=\bra{\Psi_{0}}b^{\dagger}(z)b(z)\ket{\Psi_{0}}
are the mean photon numbers in waveguides
W_{A}
and
W_{B}
at distance
z
, and
a(z)
[
b(z)
] is given by Eq. (
19
) [Eq. (
20
)].

After substituting Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) into (
28
), the transmission
T
can be written in the general form

\begin{split}T&=\frac{n_{a}(0)}{n_{a}(0)+n_{b}(0)}(|f_{1}|^{2}+|g|^{2})\\
&+\frac{n_{b}(0)}{n_{a}(0)+n_{b}(0)}(|f_{2}|^{2}+|g|^{2})\\
&+\frac{2i(f_{1}-f_{2})g}{n_{a}(0)+n_{b}(0)}\text{Im}\left[\langle a^{\dagger}(0)b(0)\rangle\right],\end{split}

(29)

where
\langle a^{\dagger}(0)b(0)\rangle=\bra{\phi_{ab}}a^{\dagger}(0)b(0)\ket{\phi_{ab}}
. Notice the presence of an interference term (the last term) which depends on the initial photon state correlation
\langle a^{\dagger}(0)b(0)\rangle
. The transmission is independent of the noisy operators, even in the non-Markovian regime, if the reservoir is initially empty
Gräfe et al. 2013
.

Our analysis begins by exploring Eq. (
29
) in cases where the interference term (the last term in the equation) vanishes. This is the case, for example, for
p
photons initially incident at the lossless waveguide
W_{A}
and
q
photons at the lossy waveguide
W_{B}
,
\ket{\phi_{ab}}=\ket{p_{a}}\ket{q_{b}}
. The transmission
T
depends only on the relative initial populations,
\Gamma_{j}=n_{j}(0)/[n_{a}(0)+n_{b}(0)]
(
j=a,b
):

T=(|f_{1}|^{2}+|g|^{2})\Gamma_{a}+(|f_{2}|^{2}+|g|^{2})(1-\Gamma_{a}),

(30)

where
\Gamma_{a}+\Gamma_{b}=1
. Figure
3
shows the transmission
T
as a function of
\kappa z
for three values of
\Gamma_{a}
, starting from the configuration where all photons are initially located inside the lossless waveguide
W_{A}
(
\Gamma_{a}=1)
until they are initially located at the lossy waveguide
W_{B}
(
\Gamma_{a}=0
). The nonoscillatory solutions are shown in parts (a), (b) and (c) while the oscillatory solution (yellow region of Fig.
2
) is shown in part (d).

The plots in Fig.
3
illustrate the asymmetric
behavior of the system. Symmetric initial conditions (
\Gamma_{a}=0
and
\Gamma_{a}=1)
do not lead to a symmetric dynamics. In fact, Fig.
3
(d) clearly shows that, to achieve a high transmission coefficient
T
in a waveguide system with total length
\kappa z
between approximately
\pi/2
and
\pi
, it is more effective to inject photons into the lossy
W_{B}
waveguide.

To gain deep insight into the role of the coupling coefficient
\alpha
and the non-Markovianity of the reservoir, represented by the parameter
\gamma
, on the transmission properties of the structure, Fig.
4
(a) and (b) plots
T
in the
(\gamma/\kappa,\alpha^{2}/\kappa^{2})
-plane for a propagation length of
\kappa z=2.3
. Part (a) is for
\Gamma_{a}=1
and part (b) assumes that
\Gamma_{a}=0
. We thus see that the transmission
T
strongly depends upon
\alpha
,
\gamma
and
\Gamma_{a}
. In particular, note that there are oscillations of the transmission as
\gamma\rightarrow 0
in the
\Gamma_{a}=0
case. This occurs because the reservoir has strong memory effects and interacts with the incident photons at waveguide
W_{b}
, impacting on their future dynamics.

There is a very intuitive explanation for the behavior shown in Figs.
4
(a) and (b) based on the quantum Zeno dynamics
Facchi and Pascazio 2008
. The interaction strength
\alpha
between waveguide
W_{b}
and the reservoir can be regarded as a constant observation amplitude between the system being observed, in this case the
W_{b}
waveguide, and the observer (reservoir), which makes continuous measurements on
W_{b}
. In the case where all incident photons are in the lossless waveguide,
\Gamma_{a}=1
, the increase in
\alpha
induces a freezing on the initial state and most photons stay in
W_{a}
during the propagation since the state of
W_{b}
must remain empty
Beder and Brandão 2024
. This explains the increasing values of
T
in Fig.
4
(a) for large
\alpha
(ideally,
T\rightarrow 1
in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
).

IV
Conclusions

We conclude by stating that there is a rich dynamics involved in the propagation of photons through non-Markovian coupled waveguides. By considering a Lorentzian model for the reservoir, we demonstrated that, in some circumstances, more light can be transmitted through the structure if the initial photons are located at the lossy waveguide. An increase in transmission was also found that depends only on the distribution of the frequencies in the dissipative environment, for fixed interaction strength between the lossy waveguide and the reservoir. A condition on the state of the incident photons to generate a nonzero contribution of the interference term in the overall dynamics was also discussed.
