PGC-1α: key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and cellular differentiation in metabolic and regenerative tissues
Authors: Lijuan Cao, Yanan Li, Artem Smirnov, Ramouna Voshtani, Tingting Wang, Changshun Shao, Eleonora Candi, Gerry Melino, Yufang Shi, Jiankai Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1α (PGC-1α) is a crucial coactivator that regulates mitochondrial biogenesis and function across diverse tissues, including the brain, heart, skeletal muscle, bone marrow, and liver. The diversity of PGC-1α isoforms in distinct tissues allows this co-transcription factor to exert wide-ranging biological effects, including regulating mitochondrial functions, oxidative stress, and endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis. Here, we focus on the key roles of PGC-1α in cell differentiation. Initially identified in brown adipose tissue in response to cold exposure, PGC-1α regulates cell differentiation by modulating gene expression networks involved in mitochondrial biogenesis. PGC-1α influences cell fate in several cell types, including adipocytes, skeletal muscle cells, and bone marrow-derived cells. A deeper understanding of PGC-1α provides valuable insights into developmental biology, tissue formation, and potential therapeutic targets for regenerative medicine and disease treatment. This review explores recent progress in understanding the roles of PGC-1α in cell differentiation, offering an integrated perspective on its significance in tissue and organism development.

Background

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) coactivator 1α (PGC-1α), an important coactivator of several nuclear receptors, plays a central role in regulating mitochondrial functions across various tissues [1]. It has been recognized as a crucial regulator of numerous physiological adaptions through a variety of signaling pathways [2]. Initially, PGC-1α was discovered as a PPARγ-binding protein in brown adipose tissue in response to cold exposure [3]. Emerging evidence has established PGC-1α as a central regulator of multiple metabolic processes, including oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid and lipid metabolism, as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis. This transcriptional coactivator exhibits predominant expression in tissues with high metabolic demands, particularly the liver, kidneys, skeletal muscle, central nervous system, and adipose depots [4–9].

Given its central role in metabolic and transcriptional regulation, PGC-1α has emerged as a critical modulator of cell differentiation, a process tightly coupled to mitochondrial dynamics and energy homeostasis. Cell differentiation, a fundamental biological process, enables stem cells to develop into specialized cells with specific functions. PGC-1α orchestrates the transcriptional activation of genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, a process that is often central to cellular differentiation [10, 11]. PGC-1α interacts with various transcription factors and regulates gene expression networks that determine cell fates in several tissue-derived cell types, including adipocytes, skeletal muscle cells, immunocytes and bone marrow-derived cells. For example, it controls epidermal stem cell differentiation by maintaining nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) homeostasis after wounding or inflammation and enhances differentiation in response to stress [12]. It also contributes to adipocyte differentiation and the conversion of white adipocytes into thermogenic brown or beige adipocytes [13]. Elevated PGC-1α has been linked to cancer cell migration and invasion [14].

Understanding how PGC-1α regulates cell differentiation provides key insights into developmental biology, tissue formation, and new potential therapeutic targets for regenerative medicine and disease treatment. Targeting PGC-1α-mediated differentiation pathways holds promise for treating metabolic disorders (e.g., obesity) and degenerative diseases (e.g., muscle atrophy). Structure features and expression patterns of PGC-1α

Members of the PGC-1 family of transcriptional coactivators coordinate their activity in response to increased nutrient and energy demands, thereby promoting physiological adaptions in various tissues. Among them, PGC-1α is expressed in tissues with high metabolic activity, including brown adipose tissue (BAT), skeletal muscle, heart, kidney, liver, and brain. PGC-1α is known to be induced in response to energy-demanding conditions such as cold exposure in BAT [15] and exercise in skeletal muscle [16]. PGC-1α expression is controlled by several signaling pathways, including of calcium signaling [17–19], calcineurin A [18], cyclic AMP [20, 21], AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [21] and insulin [22]. The transcription of PGC-1α gene is controlled by multiple promoter regions, which give rise to different coactivator variants, each with distinct transcript and protein structures. Studies have shown that the proximal promoter of PGC-1α gene exhibits higher basal activity, while the alternative promoter is more responsive to external stimuli. For instance, physical exercise and cold exposure can induce a shift in promoter activity towards an upstream transcription start site (TSS) in skeletal muscle and BAT [23, 24]. As mentioned above, PGC-1α can be markedly upregulated by adrenergic receptor (AR) activation in skeletal muscles to cold exposure [15, 25]. Initially, PGC-1α mRNA was thought to consist of a single transcript (PGC-1α-a), transcribed from exon 1 (exon 1a). However, transcript analysis in expressed sequence tag libraries revealed two novel isoforms of PGC-1α mRNA, named PGC-1α-b and PGC-1α-c, which are expressed in mice tissues. These two isoforms are transcribed from a different exon 1 (exon 1b) and produce slightly smaller proteins compared to the canonical PGC-1α [23, 24]. In addition to the alternative promoter of the PGC-1α gene, other TSSs have been identified in the human PGC-1α gene. One site is located within intron 2 of the PGC-1α gene and is used exclusively in the fasted human liver [26], producing a transcript called PGC-1α-L. This transcript PGC-1α-L contains a novel exon 1L, which is spliced to exon 3 and follows the canonical PGC-1α transcript sequence. Various brain-specific PGC-1α isoforms have also been reported in both mice and humans. Another TSS, located 587 kb upstream of PGC-1α exon 2, generates several brain-specific isoforms [27]. These PGC-1α-B transcripts contain novel exons B1-5 located upstream of the canonical exon 2, with many transcripts consisting of exons B1, B4 and B5, followed by exons 2–13 [27]. Zhang et al. using a BAT cDNA library, cloned a PGC-1α variant expressed under the control of the proximal promoter that undergoes alternative splicing between exons 6 and 7, introducing a premature stop codon [28]. This conserved intronic sequence in the PGC-1α gene, present across mammals, contains two distinct splicing acceptor sites. When the upstream acceptor site is used, it generates a 270 amino acid (aa) protein named NT-PGC-1α. This protein corresponds to the activation domain (aa 11–80) and part of the repression domain (aa 180–403 in PGC-1α1; 180–267 in NT-PGC-1α) [28]. PGC-1α exhibits tissue-specific expression through multiple promoters and splicing variants, enabling it to perform distinct functions in tissues with high energy demands. Although several isoforms have been identified, their precise tissue-specific roles and regulatory interactions remain to be fully elucidated. In the future, single-cell transcriptomics and spatial omics technologies may help uncover the dynamic changes of different isoforms during development and disease progression.

Regulation of PGC-1α gene expression

PGC-1α expression levels are tightly regulated in response to a wide range of stimuli, such as insulin, cytokines, exercise, temperature and glucagon (Fig. 2B). Nerve stimulation-mediated calcium signaling activates Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase IV (CaMKIV) and calcineurin A (CnA), both of which play pivotal roles in regulating PGC-1α transcription [18]. CnA has been shown to interact with and activate the myogenic transcription factors myocyte enhancer factor 2C (MEF2C) and MEF2D, which subsequently drive PGC-1α transcription [18]. CaMKIV also targets the cAMP response element (CRE)-binding protein (CREB), a transcription factor that binds to PGC-1α gene [18, 29]. In vivo imaging has shown that nerve stimulation induces PGC-1α mRNA expression via CREB and MEF2-dependent pathway [30]. Interestingly, PGC-1α itself can increase the expression of MEF2C and MEF2D [8, 31], forming an autoregulatory feed-forward loop where PGC-1α increases its own expression. This feedback mechanism underscores the complex and tightly regulated nature of PGC-1α transcription in response to physiological demands.

Another mechanism regulating PGC-1α expression in muscle post-exercise involves exercise activates the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38-MAPK) pathway, which regulates PGC-1α expression. p38-MAPK pathway can activate transcription factors, such as MEF2 and activating transcription factor 2 (ATF2) [32]. During exercise, or when overexpressed, p38-MAPK increases PGC-1α expression in muscle, with this induction being dependent on ATF2 activity [33]. Additionally, PGC-1α activity has also been shown to be necessary for AMPK-mediated mitochondrial activation [34]. AMPK can be specifically activated pharmacologically using the drug 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide-1-β-d-ribofuranoside (AICAR), which leads to an increase in PGC-1α transcription when applied to muscle cells [35]. These signaling pathways highlight how muscle cells fine-tune PGC-1α expression in response to metabolic demands, with p38-MAPK and AMPK pathways playing crucial roles in adapting energy metabolism during exercise. AMPK not only stimulates PGC-1α transcription, but also activates it by phosphorylating threonine-177 and serine-538, enhancing PGC-1α co-transcriptional activity and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis [34]. However, the exact mechanisms by which AMPK enhances PGC-1α transcription remain unclear.

PGC-1α levels in muscle is also regulated by insulin. Ling et al. found that insulin can increases PGC-1α transcription [36]. However, since insulin primarily promotes energy storage and diminishes catabolic pathways, it might also inhibit PGC-1α activity. Supporting this view, the transcription factor forkhead box class-O (FOXO1) has been shown to bind and stimulate the PGC-1α promoter in muscle. In this context, insulin inhibits PGC-1α transcription by activating Akt, which phosphorylates and inactivates FOXO1 [22]. In response to cold temperatures, the sympathetic nervous system signals through the β3-adrenergic receptor (β3-AR) [37], leading to enhanced cAMP signaling and the activation of protein kinase A (PKA). A well-characterized target of PKA targets is CREB. The cAMP-stimulating compound forskolin enhances PGC-1α expression, while a dominant negative mutant of CREB blocks this activation [18]. In brown adipose tissue, activation of β3-AR and the cAMP/PKA pathway also stimulates p38-MAPK, which subsequently inducing PGC-1α transcription via ATF2, similar to how PGC-1α is activated by muscle contraction [38]. The expression of PGC-1α is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, including classical metabolic response pathways such as CaMK/CREB, p38-MAPK, and AMPK. Notably, the spatiotemporal coordination among these signaling pathways likely determines the tissue specificity and functional outcomes of PGC-1α activation. Future studies should focus on elucidating the integrative mechanisms of these pathways and their interactions with epigenetic regulation.

Post-translational modification of PGC-1α

Besides transcriptional regulation, PGC-1α expression and activity are also modulated by post-translational modifications (Fig. 2C). Acetylation of PGC-1α occurs across various residues throughout the protein, making it difficult to associate this modification with a specific structural or functional pattern [39]. GCN5 has been identified to be the specific acetyltransferase for PGC-1α [40]. SIRT1, one of the mammalian homologs of the yeast silent information regulator 2 (Sir2), plays a key role in transcriptional silencing and has been linked to lifespan extension in yeast [41], worms [42], and flies [43]. SIRT1 activity typically increases during fasting, exercise, or redox stress, promoting cellular energy production to help the cell adapt to these conditions. In skeletal muscle, SIRT1-mediated deacetylation activates PGC-1α, leading to increased transcription of its target genes [44, 45]. In the liver, PGC-1α activity in regulating gluconeogenic is modulated by SIRT1. Knockdown of SIRT1 in mouse liver results in down-regulation of PGC-1α-mediated gluconeogenic genes, while overexpression of SIRT1 leads to their upregulation. This highlights SIRT1’s role in controlling PGC-1α activity in different tissues [46].

Akt, activated by insulin signaling, phosphorylates PGC-1α at serine-570, leading to inhibition of its activity [47]. p38-MAPK phosphorylates PGC-1α at threonine-262, serine-265, and threonine-298 in response to cytokine stimulation in muscle cells [48]. This phosphorylation increases PGC-1α stability [48] and disrupts its interaction with the corepressor p160MBP in myoblasts, thereby enhancing its transactivation potential [49]. PGC-1α is also phosphorylated by glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), which inhibits PGC-1α by enhancing its proteasomal degradation in the nucleus during oxidative stress [50]. The splicing isoform of PGC-1α (NT-PGC-1α), which lacks the C-terminus and nucleus localization signal of full-length PGC-1α, is phosphorylated by PKA at serine-194, serine-241, and threonine-256 [51]. These regulatory pathways ensure precise control of PGC-1α’s functions, allowing cells to adjust energy metabolism in response to changing physiological conditions.

Skp1/Cullin/F-box-cell division control 4 complex (SCFCdc4), as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, regulates the ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis of PGC-1α so that promoting its degration [52]. PGC-1α undergoes methylation by protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1) at arginine residues 665, 667, and 669, which strongly boosts PGC-1α–mediated transcription [53]. Additionally, PGC-1α is targeted by O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) transferase (OGT), which transfers the O-GlcNAc group to serine-333 of PGC-1α, repressing another layer of regulation affecting its activity and stability [54]. Acetylation, phosphorylation, methylation, and glycosylation constitute the core layers of PGC-1α’s fine-tuned regulation. These diverse modifications highlight the complex and multifaceted control of PGC-1α in response to various physiological signals. The interplay among these modifications determines its transcriptional activity under metabolic stress. However, current studies mainly focus on individual types of modification, lacking a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic balance among multiple modifications. Future research should integrate mass spectrometry-based proteomics and structural biology to elucidate the modification network and its functional consequences.

PGC-1α-mediated cell metabolism

As a member of transcriptional coactivators, PGC-1α integrates physiological signals related to metabolism, differentiation and cell growth into the transcriptional machinery that controls mitochondrial function. PGC-1α exerts its regulatory effects by coactivating several transcription factors, including PPARs, estrogen-related receptor-α (ERRα), FOXO1, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α), and nuclear respiratory factors (NRFs) (Fig. 2D). Through these interactions, PGC-1α modulates the expression of genes involved in critical metabolic pathways, such as gluconeogenesis, fatty acid synthesis, and oxidation or glycolysis. This broad regulatory role enables PGC-1α to fine-tune mitochondrial functional capacity and overall cellular energy homeostasis.

PGC-1α exhibits no significant homology with other known nuclear receptor coactivators, but it contains a key LXXLL motif, which is recognized as a crucial element mediating nuclear receptor-coactivator interactions [55]. PGC-1α specifically interacts with PPARγ and other members of the nuclear hormone receptors superfamily, such as TRβ, retinoic acid (RA) receptor and estrogen receptor (ERα) to control lipid metabolism in adipose [56], muscle [57], and cancer [58]. Increased PGC-1α activation and subsequently augmented NRF2 expression and nuclear translocation, providing protection against cardiac remodeling [59]. Additionally, PGC-1α, in complex with ERRα, controls cancer cells invasion by regulating MYC levels [14]. Both NRF2 and ERRα collaborate with PGC-1α to coordinate the mitochondrial metabolism and function [60, 61]. In the liver, PGC-1α coactivates the trans-activators FOXO1 and HNF4a, promoting hepatic glucose production, a process that can contribute to the development of type 2 diabetes [62]. PGC-1α interacts with transcription factors such as PPARs, NRFs, and FOXO1 to integrate signals from oxidative metabolism, gluconeogenesis, and lipid oxidation, serving as a central hub for mitochondrial homeostasis and energy balance. Its coupling with transcriptional networks like ERRα and NRF2 reveals the unity between energy metabolism and antioxidant defense. Future studies should further elucidate the roles of these networks in tissue remodeling and tumor metabolic reprogramming.

PGC-1α and muscle myogenesis

Skeletal muscle accounts for about 40% of adult human body weight. Muscle myofibers constitutes with blood vessels, muscle stem cells (MuSCs), fibro-adiponectin progenitors (FAPs) and various supporting cell types. PGC-1α expression in skeletal muscles is important for muscle tissue formation, development, and quality maintenance. Deana Haralampieva et al. genetically modified human muscle precursor cells (MPCs) to overexpress the PGC-1α and thereby these hMPCs with high PGC-1α expression exhibited an increased capacity for myofiber formation in vitro [63]. Animal models also underscore the importance of PGC-1α, as PGC-1α knockout (PGC-1α−/−) animals display abnormal skeletal muscle function [64] and cardiac energetics [65]. PGC-1α−/− mice develop accelerated heart failure under stressed [66] and exhibit significantly reduced exercise performance compared to wild-type controls [67], likely due to PGC-1α’s role in myonuclear accretion [68]. Additionally, PGC-1α regulates muscle fiber type determination. PGC-1α predominantly expressed in type I muscle fibers [8]. The occurrence of PGC-1α triggers the muscle fiber type conversion [8], indicating its pivotal role in muscle fiber specification.

Muscle satellite cells are essential for muscle injury regeneration, as they proliferate, differentiate, and fuse to form new myofibers. Myoblast differentiation is a crucial process in the development and repair of skeletal muscles, leading to the formation of multinucleated, contractile muscle fibers [69]. During muscle regeneration, the onset of differentiation is marked by a significant upregulation of PGC-1α [70]. The other study also demonstrated that constitutive PGC-1α expression induces myoblast differentiation [71]. During myogenic differentiation, PGC-1β is also highly expressed. More than that, knockdown of endogenous PGC-1β by siRNA leads to a decrease in the expression of several mitochondria-related genes. Overexpression of PGC-1β stimulates target mitochondrial associated genes, such as cytochrome c, ATP synthase β, and ALAS-1 through its interaction with the transcriptional factors NRF1 and ERRα [72], highlighting the crucial role of PGC-1β in muscle metabolism and differentiation.

Myoblasts differentiation regulated by PGC-1α is controlled by various genes (Fig. 3). Lisa S Chow et al. investigated the epigenetic changes that occur during the transition from activated satellite cells to myoblasts using chromatin accessibility profiling (ATAT-seq) on human and murine muscle samples. This indicates that PGC-1α is a potential target of PAX7, a key regulator of muscle stem cell function [73]. Yuki Takakura et al. suggested that Vestigial-like family member 3 (VGLL3) promotes slow-twitch muscle differentiation by upregulating PGC-1α expression [74]. This highlights PGC-1α’s role in determining muscle fiber types during differentiation. Additionally, Xiaobo Wang et al. found that the obesity-associated gene (FTO) expression level increases during myoblasts differentiation, with FTO-deficient mice exhibiting impaired skeletal muscle development. Mechanically, the connection between FTO and muscle differentiation is associated with the mitochondrial mass and mitochondrial DNA content through mTOR-PGC-1α pathway [75]. Uncoupling protein 3 (UCP3) has also been shown to promote myogenic differentiation, specifically of type Ⅱ b myotubes in C2C12 cells, with PGC-1α levels increasing in response to UCP3 overexpression [76]. Moreover, knock-down or pharmacological inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β) increases the expression of key elements in the PGC-1 signaling network. This promotes myogenic differentiation by boosting mitochondrial respiration, mitochondrial DNA copy number, oxidative phosphorylation protein levels, and the activity of enzymes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation [77]. Various metabolic substrates and factors can significantly influence myoblast differentiation through the regulation of PGC-1α. For instance, nicotinamide riboside (NR), a precursor to NAD+, improves muscle mitochondrial biogenesis and promotes satellite cells differentiation by upregulating PGC-1α expression [78]. This highlights the crucial role of NAD+ metabolism in muscle regeneration. Additionally, a study by Hojun Lee et al. reported that a combination of oleate and L-carnitine serves as a potential therapeutic approach to facilitate biological activation of myogenic progenitors with upregulation of PGC-1α [79]. H Staiger’s research further supports that fatty acids can differentially regulate the expression of PGC-1α isoforms, thereby elevating oxidative metabolism in muscle tissue [80]. Moreover, icariin and ZLN005 (the activator of PGC-1α) promote cardiomyocyte differentiation by elevating PGC-1α expression [81, 82]. Insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2) has also been found to induce myoblast differentiation through constitutive PGC-1α expression [71], further emphasizing its role in myogenesis. These findings suggest that both internal and external factors, ranging from metabolic substrates like NAD+ precursors and fatty acids to regulatory proteins like IGF2, can influence PGC-1α-mediated myoblast differentiation, highlighting the diverse mechanisms through which PGC-1α integrates metabolic and environmental cues to regulate muscle development. PGC-1α is a key regulator of muscle regeneration and fiber-type switching. It supports myoblast differentiation and myofiber formation by modulating mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism. However, the roles of different PGC-1α isoforms in muscle development and aging remain controversial. Comparative studies using animal models and clinical samples will be crucial in clarifying its therapeutic potential in muscle degenerative diseases.

PGC-1α and adipogenesis

Adipose tissue plays a central role in lipid storage, energy homeostasis, and whole-body insulin sensitivity. Adipose hypertrophy results from both the enlargement of existing adipocytes and the formation of new adipocytes through precursor-cell differentiation. PGC-1α is a key regulator during adipogenesis, with its expression increasing throughout the differentiation process [83, 84]. However, its precise role in adipocyte differentiation versus thermogenesis remains contentious. Marc Uldry et al. reported that PGC-1α deficiency did not affect brown fat differentiation but severely impaired the induction of thermogenic genes, suggesting that PGC-1α is essential for brown fat thermogenesis but dispensable for its differentiation [85]. In contrast, other studies indicate that PGC-1α is required not only for thermogenesis but also for the proliferation and differentiation of brown adipocytes [86]. Moreover, allelic and tissue-specific differences in the regulation of PGC-1α have been observed between brown adipocytes in white fat and classical interscapular brown fat, implying distinct regulatory mechanisms that determine the thermogenic capacity of these depots [87]. Together, these findings highlight the complex, context-dependent functions of PGC-1α in adipose tissue and underscore the need to carefully distinguish its roles in differentiation and in regulating thermogenic programs.

A variety of extracellular cues modulate adipogenesis, primarily by altering PGC-1α expression or activity (Fig. 4). For example, an increase in extracellular calcium applied after the induction phase selectively suppresses PGC-1α transcription [88], whereas melatonin upregulates PGC-1α in differentiated adipocytes in both rodents and humans [89]. Pharmacological agents also impact PGC-1α and mitochondrial function: the antiretroviral drug stavudine increases PGC-1α expression and affects mitochondrial respiration [90], while resveratrol elevates PGC-1α levels in mature adipocytes [91]. Components of the extracellular matrix also contribute: Lama4 knockout mice show enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and increased PGC-1α expression in subcutaneous white adipose tissue, indicating LAMA4’s involvement in restraining PGC-1α-driven mitochondrial programs [90]. Inhibition of activin receptor IIb has been reported to activate myoglobin and PGC-1α coregulators in brown adipocytes, and recombinant activin can promote brown adipogenesis via PGC-1α–dependent pathways [92]. Various transcription factors, extracellular factors, and dietary inputs regulate the differentiation of adipocytes via PGC-1α. A combination of transcription factors, extracellular cues, and dietary factors regulate PGC-1α expression, which plays a central role in the differentiation of precursor adipocytes. PGC-1α supports the formation of metabolically active fat cells that help regulate energy balance. This pathway is crucial for maintaining metabolic health and preventing obesity-related complications.

Dietary components and bioactive compounds further influence adipocyte fate and metabolic function by engaging PGC-1α–related pathways. For instance, dietary fat alters PGC-1α–regulated pathways that control white adipose tissue energy metabolism. The peptide PDBSN has been shown to inhibit adipogenic differentiation in vitro and to improve metabolic homeostasis in vivo, concomitant with marked upregulation of PGC-1α at both the mRNA and protein levels [93]. Similarly, the anthocyanin cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G), abundant in many fruits and vegetables, induces early increases in thermogenic markers, including UCP1 and PGC-1α, during preadipocyte differentiation [94]. These findings suggest that nutritional and pharmacological interventions can shift adipocyte programs toward enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and thermogenesis by upregulating PGC-1α.

In summary, PGC-1α exhibits a dual role in adipogenesis—it can promote the differentiation of thermogenic brown adipocytes while being inhibited by external factors that affect white fat formation. Current studies suggest that its function depends on microenvironmental cues and cellular energy status. Future research should focus on elucidating the differential regulatory mechanisms of PGC-1α in adipose progenitor cells derived from distinct tissues, providing guidance for interventions in obesity and metabolic diseases.

Bone regulation by PGC-1α

Osteoblasts differentiated from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) that reside in the bone marrow are important for guaranteeing proper skeletal development and homeostasis. The differentiation of MSCs toward the osteogenic lineage is tightly controlled by different molecular factors, including the bone morphogenic proteins (BMPs) [95, 96], and wingless-related integration site (WNT) pathway [97, 98]. PGC-1α is also necessary for osteoblast differentiation due to the critical role in mitochondrial biogenesis. PGC-1α knockdown in vitro attenuates osteogenic differentiation in bone marrow derived-MSCs. Mechanistically, the expression of PGC-1α was increased in osteoporosis under hyperlipidemia conditions to improve mitochondrial function [99]. BMP-2 promotes osteogenic differentiation of MSCs by enhancing mitochondrial activity through the upregulation of PGC-1α [100]. Additionally, PGC-1α-SOD2 mediated osteogenic differentiation can be controlled via Sirtuin pathway [101]. Energy metabolism also regulates the PGC-1α-mediated osteogenic differentiation. Restriction in glucose supply promotes the osteocyte transcriptional program triggered by the activation of the AMPK/PGC-1 pathway. PGC-1α overexpression are sufficient to enhance osteocyte associated-gene expression in murine primary osteoblasts and osteocytes. Conversely, osteoblasts and osteocytes deficient in PGC-1α and β are difficult to control the effects of glucose restriction [102].

Besides bone formation by osteoblasts, bone absorption from osteoclasts is also crucial for skeletal development and bone tissue homeostasis. Osteoclasts are giant multinucleated cells that differentiate from monocyte/macrophage lineage cells. These cells play key roles in the processes of endochondral ossification, bone modeling, and bone remodeling throughout life. Osteoclast formation depends on macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) from the bone microenvironment [103]. Osteoclasts resorb bone by secreting protons and collagenolytic enzymes, which dissolve both the inorganic and organic components of the matrix in an energy-intensive process [104]. Based on this, the osteoclasts obviously contain abundant mitochondria and is assumed to undergo metabolic adaptation during differentiation to be able to meet the increased bioenergetics demand. It has demonstrated that the transcription of PGC-1β, but not PGC-1α, is induced during osteoclast differentiation by CREB via ROS [105]. But it also has reported that propranolol could lead to the lower trabecular bone volume fraction owing to increased osteoclast-mediated erosion by upregulating PGC-1α and UCP1 [103]. But Masaya Baba et al. found that folliculin downregulates oxidative phosphorylation and purine metabolism by suppressing the nuclear localization of the transcription factor TFE3, thereby inhibiting the expression of its target gene PGC-1 and ultimately decelerating osteoclastogenesis [106]. These results suggest the role of PGC-1α in the process of osteoclastgenesis remains controversial and requires further investigation on it.

Besides osteoblasts and osteoclasts, PGC-1α is also crucial for the differentiation of hematopoietic cells. During dendritic cell differentiation, PGC-1α is necessary to trigger the subsequent activation of downstream transcription factors, NRF1 and TFAM, which leads to mitochondrial biogenesis [107]. PGC-1α is also essential for hematopoietic stem cell homeostasis by maintaining mitochondrial biogenesis and function [108, 109]. In osteoblasts, PGC-1α enhances mitochondrial activity and participates in the bone remodeling process, but its role in osteoclast differentiation remains controversial. The coupling between energy metabolism and bone remodeling provides new insights into metabolic bone diseases. Future studies should investigate the interactions between PGC-1α and WNT/BMP signaling within the bone marrow microenvironment.

Effect of PGC-1α on epithelial tissue

Skin is the first barrier of body and plays central roles in systemic physiology. It is composed of three primary layers: (1) The epidermis that enriches of keratinocyte, which provides waterproofing and serves as a barrier to infection; (2) the dermis, which serves as a location for the appendages of skin; (3) the hypoderm (subcutaneous adipose layer). PGC-1 family are also key regulators of skin function. Silencing of PGC-1 s in keratinocytes reduced the thickness of reconstructed living human epidermal equivalent [110]. PGC-1α is necessary for the epithelial stem cells differentiation. Sustained silencing of PGC-1α in differentiating human retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells affected mitochondria and autophagy function, redox state, and impaired energy sensor activity. Adult conditional knockout of PGC-1 coactivators in mice resulted in rapid RPE dysfunction, indicating that PGC-1 is necessary for maintaining the functional and phenotypic status of RPE. PGC-1 supported the cells’ oxidative metabolism and autophagy-mediated repression of epithelial mesenchymal transformation (EMT) [111]. Indeed, specific deletion of PGC-1α in epidermal tissue exhibited greater inflammation and UVB-induced cell differentiation, reduced proliferation, and slower wound healing. However, NAD+ precursor treatment restored epidermal growth [12]. Mice with epidermal PGC-1α deletion showed lower level of cell differentiation via its regulation of cellular NAD+ [12]. These results further indicate that PGC-1α manipulates epidermal stem cell fate and skin repair through maintaining NAD+ homeostasis. PGC-1α also provides an important metabolism regulator involved in keratinocytes differentiation, influencing both proliferation and terminal differentiation of these cells [110]. PGC-1α coactivators thus help shape epidermal physiology by modulating these crucial cellular processes.

Besides that, PGC-1 s also play a key role in skin aging by promoting mitochondrial biogenesis, replication, and oxidative phosphorylation, thereby enhancing free radical scavenging [4]. David Walker discovered that increasing PGC-1 gene activity in the intestine can slow aging at both the cellular and organismal levels, delaying the initiation of cellular changes and improving mitochondrial function. Regarding the role of the PGC-1 gene in processes such as mitochondrial biogenesis and increased oxidative phosphorylation, it appears that higher induction and expression of PGC-1 can regulate mitochondrial synthesis and activity in cells like epithelial cells, preserving the structure and function of skin cells and delaying their aging [4]. In epithelial tissues, PGC-1α maintains NAD⁺ homeostasis, mitochondrial function, and stem cell differentiation, playing an important role in skin repair and anti-aging. It suppresses epithelial–mesenchymal transition by regulating oxidative metabolism and autophagy pathways. Future studies may explore skin regeneration and anti-aging therapies based on NAD⁺ precursors or PGC-1α activators.

Effect of PGC-1α in the neural system

PGC-1α is abundant in the brain due to its high metabolic demands. Despite its key role in mitochondrial adaption, the regulation of PGC-1α is necessary for neuronal function. Failure in maintaining brain energetic has been clearly linked to neurodegenerative disorders [112]. PGC-1α is also associated with the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). In the brains of AD patients and animal models of amyloidosis, PGC-1α expression was reduced compared with healthy individuals [113]. PGC-1α deficiency in specific brain areas including GABAergic neurons causes short-term habituation, hyperactivity, and exaggerated startle reactivity [114].

PGC-1α plays key role in neuronal cells differentiation [115–117]. The expression level of PGC-1α is enhanced during neural differentiation process. During neuronal differentiation from stem cells, neurons express a specialized PGC-1α isoform [118]. The differentiation process also can be influenced by diversity of factors. Vitamin A derivative can induce neural differentiation through upregulation of PGC-1α [119]. TIGAR promotes metabolic reprogramming and regulates neuronal stem cells differentiation through enhancing the expression level of PGC-1α [120]. Some cascade pathway also involved in this differentiation process. AMPK activation dependent on PGC-1α/NRF-1 is activated during neuroendocrine cell differentiation [121]. PGC-1α overexpression has been shown to protect neural cells in culture from oxidative stress-mediated death and to enhance the formation and maintenance of dendritic spines in hippocampal neurons. In contrast, the opposite effect was observed in PGC-1α knockout neurons. PGC-1α is also expressed in glial cells, such as astrocytes, where it regulates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. In AD, the most likely mechanism by which PGC-1α decreases the generation of Aβ appears to be through reducing the expression of BACE1, the rate-limiting enzyme for Aβ production [122]. These findings emphasize the multifaceted role of PGC-1α in brain metabolism and neurogenesis, making it a central player in neuronal health. The loss of PGC-1α is closely associated with various neurodegenerative diseases. A deeper understanding of its role in the metabolic reprogramming of neural stem cells could provide new targets for promoting neuroregeneration and combating brain aging.

Therapies based on PGC-1α

Considering the regulatory effect of PGC-1α on mitochondrial biogenesis, there are numerous therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating PGC-1α expression levels. PGC-1α offers two major protective benefits: (1) it activates the body’s natural antioxidant enzymes, protecting cells from harmful free radicals. PGC-1α upregulates mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes such as SOD2 and catalase, thereby reducing ROS-mediated damage in cells [123–125]; (2) it reduces inflammation by lowering inflammatory proteins and inhibiting key inflammation pathways. PGC-1α attenuates inflammation through suppression of NF-κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, linking mitochondrial function to inflammatory control [126–128]. Medical treatments targeting PGC-1α, particularly PGC-1α itself, have shown significant potential in various disease models in preclinical studies. Resveratrol: Both preclinical experiments and clinical trials of resveratrol achieved tremendous benefits in a variety of human diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases [129], neurodegeneration [130], and cancers [131], in which PGC-1α is a potential target. For instance, resveratrol reestablishes the balance of mitochondria fission–fusion and regulates autophagic flux and mitochondrial biosynthesis through the SIRT1/SIRT3-Mfn2-Parkin-PGC-1α pathway in myocardial IRI [132]. It also exerts neuroprotective effects against neuronal cell injury via attenuating autophagy, suppressing the release of inflammatory cytokines and mitochondrial biogenesis [133]. In conclusion, resveratrol, as a classical agonist of SIRT1, combats oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction by activating the SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway, eventually providing protection against various diseases.

Curcumin: Curcumin is a crucial polyphenol present in Curcuma longa L. rhizome, exemplifying a promising traditional medicinal agent. Recent studies have revealed anti-apoptotic and anti-oxidative properties of curcumin that arise from its modulation of PGC-1α. In the liver, curcumin activates AMPK and increases PGC-1α expression, then inhibiting collagen I and hepatic stellate cell activation, thus effectively preventing liver fibrogenesis [134]. In chronic unpredictable mild stress-induced depression-like behavior, curcumin supplementation promotes neurocyte proliferation and inhibits neuronal apoptosis, while PGC-1α inhibitor SR18292 reverses the beneficial effects of curcumin on depressed rats [135]. These shed light on curcumin as a therapeutic adjuvant in the clinical setting. Berberine is an eminent component of traditional Chinese medicine for more than 2000 years [136]. It possesses the ability to suppress many diabetic complications. Berberine treatment inhibits lipid disorder by counteracting lipid accumulation, ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, and deficient fatty acid oxidation through PGC-1α pathway [137]. It also facilitates PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis and redox imbalance, thus inhibiting diabetic neuropathy [138]. It also affects the lipid redeposition of skeletal muscle and liver [139, 140]. Berberine treatment can ameliorates aging-related reductions in cognitive ability and muscular function, which benefits from the activation of the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway [141].

Berberine: Berberine, a representative isoquinoline alkaloid and a major constituent of traditional Chinese medicine used for over 2,000 years [136], exhibits potent therapeutic effects against various diabetic complications [138, 142]. In db/db mice, berberine alleviates lipid disorder–induced podocyte injury and delays the progression of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) by reducing lipid accumulation, ROS production, and mitochondrial dysfunction, while restoring fatty acid oxidation [137]. These effects are largely dependent on PGC-1α–mediated mitochondrial bioenergetics. In neuronal cells, berberine enhances PGC-1α–driven mitochondrial biogenesis and redox balance, thereby protecting against diabetic neuropathy [138]. Additionally, berberine mitigates lipid accumulation in skeletal muscle and liver by activating the AMPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and fatty acid oxidation to prevent lipid overload [137, 140]. Beyond its metabolic benefits, berberine also counteracts age-related cognitive decline and muscle dysfunction through activation of the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α cascade [141]. Furthermore, in APP/PS1 mice, berberine suppresses tau hyperphosphorylation and neuroinflammation via modulation of the GSK3β/PGC-1α signaling pathway [143].

Metformin: Metformin, the first-line and broad-spectrum therapeutic agent for type 2 diabetes mellitus, is well known for its ability to activate AMPK. Numerous studies have demonstrated that metformin enhances the expression of PGC-1α through AMPK phosphorylation in various experimental models [144]. Under high-glucose conditions, metformin promotes INS-1 cell proliferation, enhances glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, and suppresses apoptosis by activating the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling pathway. This activation further upregulates irisin expression and induces autophagy, contributing to improved β-cell survival and function [145]. Moreover, metformin exerts protective effects on skeletal tissue by preventing glucose- and lipotoxicity-induced osteoblast apoptosis and mitigating type 2 diabetes mellitus-associated bone deterioration. Importantly, silencing or depletion of PGC-1α abolishes these protective outcomes, underscoring its pivotal role as a downstream effector of metformin-mediated metabolic and cytoprotective actions [146].

Eugenol: Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly heterogeneous and aggressive myeloid neoplasm, with mitochondrial function playing a central role in its progression. Eugenol has been shown to downregulate PGC-1α expression and reduce mitochondrial membrane potential in AML cells, inhibiting their growth and promoting apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway in AML patients [147]. These findings suggest that modulating mitochondrial metabolism, particularly through targeting PGC-1α signaling, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming AML cell survival and resistance mechanisms.

AICAR (5-Aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide, AMPK agonist): AICAR is a well-characterized pharmacological activator of AMPK. The first study on the safety and tolerance of AICAR was conducted in 1991, primarily to determine its pharmacokinetics as a potential adenosine-regulating agent—long before its recognition as an AMPK agonist [148]. Since 1995, AICAR has served as a classical and reliable tool compound for elucidating AMPK-dependent metabolic pathways in a variety of tissues and cell types, both in vivo and in vitro[149]. Functionally, AICAR mimics AMP and binds to the γ-subunit of AMPK, leading to conformational stabilization and subsequent phosphorylation of the catalytic α-subunit. This activation triggers a wide range of downstream effects, including enhanced glucose uptake, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial biogenesis. Notably, AICAR treatment has been shown to modulate muscle fiber composition by upregulating PGC-1α, promoting a shift toward oxidative, fatigue-resistant fibers [21, 150]. In addition, AICAR can counteract mitochondrial dysfunction induced by isoniazid through the activation of the SIRT1–PGC-1α signaling pathway, suggesting its potential therapeutic relevance in drug-induced mitochondrial impairment [151]. Beyond skeletal muscle, AICAR-mediated AMPK activation has been implicated in improving insulin sensitivity [152], reducing hepatic lipid accumulation [153], and exerting cytoprotective effects under metabolic stress [154], further underscoring its importance as a molecular probe and potential metabolic modulator.

Bezafibrate: Bezafibrate, a pan-agonist of PPARs, has been widely used for decades in the clinical management of primary biliary cholangitis [155, 156], dyslipidemia [157, 158], and rhabdomyolysis-associated metabolic disorders [159]. Beyond its well-established lipid-lowering and hepatoprotective functions, accumulating evidence has revealed that bezafibrate also exerts pleiotropic metabolic and cytoprotective effects that extend into the fields of oncology and mitochondrial medicine. Recent studies have demonstrated that bezafibrate can inhibit cancer progression by reprogramming tumor metabolism, attenuating inflammation, and inducing mitochondrial oxidative metabolism [160]. Moreover, bezafibrate has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate for mitochondrial diseases such as Leigh syndrome, a severe neurodegenerative disorder caused by mitochondrial respiratory chain deficiency. In models of Leigh syndrome, bezafibrate treatment restores neuronal morphogenesis and synaptic integrity by promoting metabolic reprogramming through PGC-1α induction [161]. As a transcriptional coactivator, PGC-1α orchestrates mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and antioxidant defense, thereby enhancing neuronal energy homeostasis and survival. Activation of the PPAR–PGC-1α axis by bezafibrate not only improves mitochondrial respiration and ATP production but also mitigates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, ultimately contributing to the restoration of neuronal function. Collectively, these findings highlight bezafibrate as a metabolic modulator capable of restoring mitochondrial function and energy metabolism, offering therapeutic potential not only in metabolic and hepatic disorders but also in cancer and mitochondrial encephalopathies.

Epicatechin: Epicatechin and its derivatives, particularly epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), are bioactive polyphenols abundantly found in green tea and are well recognized for their diverse pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory effects. The anti-obesity effects of epigallocatechin have been extensively characterized, demonstrating its capacity to modulate lipid metabolism, enhance energy expenditure, and attenuate adipogenesis in both cellular and animal models [160, 162]. Recent studies have identified that the chitooligosaccharide–epigallocatechin conjugate (CE) exhibits synergistic anti-obesity activity that surpasses the effects of either compound alone. CE effectively alleviates lipid accumulation, reduces adipocyte hypertrophy, and promotes the browning of white adipose tissue in obese rats. Mechanistically, CE activates the PGC-1α–UCP1 signaling axis, a critical pathway involved in mitochondrial biogenesis and thermogenesis. Through upregulation of PGC-1α, CE enhances mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and induces the expression of UCP1, thereby converting energy-storing white adipocytes into energy-dissipating beige adipocytes. In addition, CE treatment results in decreased body weight gain, improved serum lipid profiles, and enhanced insulin sensitivity, suggesting a comprehensive improvement in systemic metabolic health [163]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of CE as a natural metabolic activator capable of restoring mitochondrial function and promoting energy expenditure through the PGC-1α–UCP1 axis, offering promising implications for obesity management and related metabolic disorders.

L-Carnitine: L-Carnitine is a quaternary ammonium compound that plays a crucial role in mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism, primarily by facilitating the transport of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix for β-oxidation. It acts as a vital regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, mitochondrial integrity, and redox balance. Due to its central role in energy metabolism, L-carnitine supplementation has been investigated in a wide range of metabolic, cardiovascular, hepatic, and neurodegenerative disorders [164, 165]. In metabolic diseases such as obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, L-carnitine enhances mitochondrial oxidative capacity and promotes fatty acid oxidation, thereby reducing lipid accumulation and improving insulin sensitivity. It has been reported to decrease plasma triglycerides, improve glucose utilization, and alleviate hepatic steatosis by activating the PGC-1α signaling pathway, which stimulates mitochondrial biogenesis and energy expenditure [166]. Collectively, these findings highlight L-carnitine as a mitochondria-targeted metabolic modulator with broad therapeutic implications. By restoring mitochondrial function and energy homeostasis, L-carnitine offers potential benefits across diverse pathological conditions characterized by metabolic dysfunction and oxidative stress.

Quercetin: A natural flavonol abundantly found in fruits, vegetables, and medicinal plants, quercetin exerts a wide range of biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and metabolic regulatory effects [167, 168]. Increasing evidence suggests that many of the beneficial effects of quercetin are mediated through the activation of PGC-1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism. In skeletal muscle and cardiac tissues, quercetin has been reported to enhance mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production, thereby improving exercise endurance and protecting against myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury [169]. These effects are closely linked to the upregulation of PGC-1α transcriptional activity, which supports mitochondrial regeneration and energy metabolism in high-demand tissues [169]. Collectively, quercetin acts as a potent mitochondrial biogenesis enhancer and metabolic modulator through the activation of the PGC-1α axis. By restoring mitochondrial function, quercetin contributes to the prevention and treatment of metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting its potential as a natural therapeutic agent targeting mitochondrial health.

Pharmacological interventions targeting PGC-1α show broad therapeutic potential. Natural compounds such as resveratrol and curcumin, as well as drugs like metformin, can improve metabolic and antioxidant functions through the AMPK/SIRT1–PGC-1α axis. Future studies should focus on elucidating the tissue-specific effects of these agents and validating their clinical feasibility.

Conclusion and perspective

PGC-1α acts as a transcriptional coactivator, meaning it does not directly bind DNA but instead interacts with other transcription factors to enhance their activity [195, 196]. It is involved in regulating genes linked to energy metabolism. One of PGC-1α’s primary roles is to stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis, the process by which new mitochondria are formed in the cell. This process is crucial for maintaining cellular energy levels, particularly in tissues with high energy demands, such as skeletal muscle, the heart, and the brain. Based on this, PGC-1α plays a significant role in the differentiation of various cells, including muscle satellite cells, adipose progenitor cells, bone marrow-associated cells, and neuronal stem cells. The expression of PGC-1α is regulated by various physiological stimuli, including exercise, cold exposure, and dietary factors. Exercise has been shown to significantly increase PGC-1α levels in muscle tissue. PGC-1α activity is modulated by post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation, which can either enhance or inhibit its function. Dysregulation of PGC-1α has been implicated in several metabolic disorders, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, and insulin resistance. Reduced expression or activity of PGC-1α can lead to impaired mitochondrial function and decreased oxidative capacity. Given its role in mitochondrial function, PGC-1α is also linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s [197] and Parkinson’s [198]. Enhancing PGC-1α activity might offer therapeutic benefits by improving mitochondrial function and reducing oxidative stress. PGC-1α is also critical for maintaining cardiac function, particularly under stress conditions [199]. It supports the heart’s energy needs by promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and enhancing fatty acid oxidation. Additionally, PGC-1α is associated with the systemic metabolic milieu through epigenetic consequences [200]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting PGC-1α are highly desirable.

Compounds that activate PGC-1α are being explored as potential exercise mimetics, offering the benefits of exercise to individuals unable to engage in physical activity. Targeting PGC-1α pathways provides promising avenues for developing treatments for metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases. For instance, drugs that enhance PGC-1α activity could potentially improve mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. Ongoing research is focused on better understanding the precise molecular mechanisms by which PGC-1α regulates energy metabolism and how its activity is controlled within the cell. Clinical trials are investigating the effects of PGC-1α modulation in various diseases, which is crucial for determining the safety and efficacy of potential therapies targeting PGC-1α. Moreover, studying genetic variations in the PGC-1α gene and their association with disease susceptibility could provide insights into individual differences in metabolic health and disease risk.

PGC-1α serves as a central hub for both metabolic regulation and cellular differentiation, exerting critical functions across multiple tissues, including muscle, adipose, bone, epithelial, and neural systems. Acting as a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism, PGC-1α integrates diverse signaling pathways to maintain energy homeostasis and promote tissue regeneration. Future research should focus on clarifying the functional distinctions among its isoforms, elucidating the crosstalk between epigenetic regulation and metabolic signaling, and developing specific PGC-1α agonists for the treatment of metabolic and degenerative diseases. Integrating multi-tissue omics data and systems biology approaches will be essential for constructing a comprehensive regulatory network of PGC-1α, thereby advancing our understanding of its role in tissue homeostasis, regeneration, and disease intervention.
