Hybrid qubit-oscillator module with motional states of two trapped interacting atoms
Jaeyong Hwang, Tianrui Xu, Sean R. Muleady, Steven Pampel, Gur Lubin, Dawson Hewatt, Cindy A. Regal, Ana Maria Rey

Abstract
We propose the use of motional states of two interacting atoms trapped in a potential stroboscopically engineered by an optical tweezer as a means to implement a qubit-oscillator system, in analogy to those implemented in circuit quantum electrodynamics and trapped ions. In our setting, the center of mass degree of freedom of the atoms plays the role of a photon or phonon mode, while the interacting, relative mode acts as a qubit. No internal state is involved in our system, which makes this motional qubit robust to spin-dependent noise. We show that a universal set of bosonic operations, including displacement, rotation, squeezing, and the corresponding set of gates controlled by the qubit, can be implemented through precise temporal modulation of the optical tweezers. We numerically check that these gates can be generated with high fidelity, and discuss possible schemes for initial state preparation and final state readout. While we restrict the discussion to a single qubit-oscillator module, scalability can be achieved by coupling arrays of atoms via dipolar or Rydberg-dressed interactions.



Trapped neutral atom systems, coupled via atom–atom interactions, have served as a foundational platform in atomic physics for several decades. Since the pioneering work on the manipulation of quantum degenerate gases
Bloch et al. 2008
;
Bloch et al. 2012
;
Schäfer et al. 2020
;
Gross and Bakr 2021
, interest has steadily shifted toward achieving precise control over individual atoms, particularly using optical tweezers
Ashkin 1997
;
Kaufman and Ni 2021
. In these systems, strong and highly controllable Rydberg interactions between atoms in an array have become the leading way to couple qubits encoded in internal atomic levels
Saffman et al. 2010
;
Browaeys and Lahaye 2020
;
Bluvstein et al. 2024
. Over the years, residual atomic motion in tweezers has remained as one of the sources of decoherence that limits the fidelity of neutral atom qubit gates, due to limitations in cooling and trapping
Saffman et al. 2011
;
Tsai et al. 2025
. However, experiments are reaching a point at which control over motional states is enabling new opportunities. For example, recent experimental efforts in optical tweezers have demonstrated full tomography of the motional degree of freedom for a single atom
Brown et al. 2023
, manipulation and squeezing of an atom’s motional states
Lienhard et al.
, generation of Laughlin states with two atoms
Lunt et al. 2024
, and the use of motional states for erasure detection of neutral atom qubits
Shaw et al. 2025
.

Furthermore, theoretical efforts
Grochowski et al. 2025
;
Huie et al.
have shown the richness of the bosonic mode as a quantum resource — a potential already harnessed in other experimental platforms via coupling it to the qubit
Lloyd and Braunstein 1999
;
Andersen et al. 2015
;
Grimsmo et al. 2020
;
Liu et al.
;
Crane et al.
. A prominent example is circuit quantum electrodynamics (cQED) systems where superconducting qubits (spins) are made to strongly and controllably interact with microwave photons
Blais et al. 2004
;
Clarke and Wilhelm 2008
;
Xiang et al. 2013
;
Ofek et al. 2016
;
Blais et al. 2021
. Another evident example is trapped ion arrays, where intriguing physics is possible via spin-motion coupling thanks to the strong Coulomb interaction that naturally couples phonon modes
Porras and Cirac 2004
;
Blatt and Roos 2012
;
Schneider et al. 2012
;
Hou et al. 2024
.

Despite these prospects, achieving full control over both internal and motional degrees of freedom for a single atom has remained challenging, partly due to competing spin-motion decoherence mechanisms such as differential light shifts or sideband heating. Still, to continue advancing atomic tweezer platforms, it is essential to explore the potential of quantum information processing using atomic motional levels, leveraging the microscopic control of atomic motion as a resource, rather than a hindrance, for quantum advantage.

In this Letter, we propose a way to overcome this challenge by taking advantage of contact interactions, a natural resource present in neutral atoms. Specifically, we discuss how to engineer a versatile qubit-oscillator module comprised of a pair of identical neutral atoms trapped in a stroboscopically engineered potential generated via a single optical tweezer. This setting can be robust to environmental effects because motional states do not interact directly with electromagnetic fields. We use the center-of-mass (COM) mode of the two atoms to encode an oscillator, and the relative motional state to encode a qubit. Restricting the relative motion to two isolated levels is possible due to the nonlinearity induced by the inter-atomic contact interaction. The main advantage of not involving internal degrees of freedom is the immunity to detrimental processes such as magnetic field fluctuations
Hartke et al. 2022
. In our proposed system, the COM oscillator couples to the motional qubit by utilizing the intrinsic nonlinearity of the trapping potential, which can be controlled by modulating the intensity of the optical tweezer beam. We show this can be used as a resource to precisely engineer the basic couplings needed for a universal gate set that includes displacement, rotation, and squeezing operators, as well as their controlled counterparts conditioned on the qubit state.

We consider two bosonic atoms, with s-wave scattering length

Qubit-oscillator system.—

We define the dimensionless position operator in the COM coordinate in terms of bosonic mode annihilation operators as
\hat{R}_{x}\equiv(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
, and similarly
\hat{r}_{x}\equiv(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
in the relative coordinate.
In terms of these, the time-independent part of the Hamiltonian,
\sum_{i=1,2}\Big(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}+V(x_{i},t=0^{-})\Big)+g\int dr_{y}dr_{z}|\psi_{0,y}(r_{y})|^{2}|\psi_{0,z}(r_{z})|^{2}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{r})\frac{\partial}{\partial|\vec{r}|}|\vec{r}|
,
becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{0}=&\penalty\ \hbar\omega_{x}\big(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}\big)+\sqrt{\pi}u\delta\big(\hat{r}_{x}\big),\\
\end{split}

(5)

where
u\equiv g/(\pi^{3/2}x_{0}y_{0}z_{0})
is the interaction strength between two atoms in the non-interacting ground state.
For a quantum harmonic oscillator with a contact
\delta
-potential, the eigenenergies and corresponding eigenstates of the relative-motional Hamiltonian
\sum_{n}\tilde{E}_{2n}\ket{\widetilde{2n}}\bra{\widetilde{2n}}\equiv\hbar\omega_{x}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+\sqrt{\pi}u\delta(\hat{r}_{x})
can be obtained analytically
Busch et al. 1998
;
Idziaszek and Calarco 2005
. In the regime
u\ll\hbar\omega_{x,y,z}
where the 1D approximation is valid, the values of
\tilde{E}_{2n}
are just slightly perturbed from the non-interacting energies
E_{2n}=\left(2n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega_{x}
. We keep
(\tilde{E}_{2n}-E_{2n})
up to linear correction terms in
u
, namely,
+u
,
+\frac{1}{2}u
, and
+\frac{3}{8}u
for
n=0,1,2
, respectively (See Fig.
1
). The energy shifts caused by the interaction introduce the necessary anharmonicity to energetically isolate the two lowest relative motional levels
\{\ket{\uparrow}\equiv\ket{\tilde{0}},\ket{\downarrow}\equiv\ket{\tilde{2}}\}
from the rest.

The qubit encoded in the relative coordinate, together with the harmonic modes in the COM coordinate, can thus be treated as a qubit-oscillator system described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{0}\simeq&\penalty\ \hbar\omega_{x}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\frac{\hbar\tilde{\omega}}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\end{split}

(6)

where
\hbar\tilde{\omega}\equiv\tilde{E}_{2}-\tilde{E}_{0}=2\hbar\omega_{x}-u/2+u\mathcal{O}(u/\hbar\omega_{x})
, and
\hat{\sigma}_{z}\equiv\ket{\uparrow}\bra{\uparrow}-\ket{\downarrow}\bra{\downarrow}
. We note that the anharmonicity of the eigenstates in the relative coordinate, given by
\tilde{E}_{4}-2\tilde{E}_{2}+\tilde{E}_{0}=3u/8+u\mathcal{O}(u/\hbar\omega_{x})
, increases as
u
grows in the small
u
limit, but reaches a maximum value of
0.084\hbar\omega_{x}
at optimal
u/\hbar\omega_{x}=0.61
across all trap depths at which the 1D approximation remains valid
sup
.

Quantum gate generation.—
Different types of modulation of
{\bf{V}}(t)
lead to different dynamics, captured by the Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}^{\prime}(\hat{R}_{x},\hat{r}_{x},t)
, with

\hat{H}^{\prime}(\hat{R}_{x},\hat{r}_{x},t)=\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{k_{max}}\hat{H}^{\prime(k)}(\hat{R}_{x},\hat{r}_{x},t).
The two lowest order terms are
\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}(\hat{R}_{x},\hat{r}_{x},t)=\epsilon_{x}\tilde{V}_{1}(t)\sqrt{2}\hat{R}_{x}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}(\hat{R}_{x},\hat{r}_{x},t)=\epsilon_{x}^{2}\tilde{V}_{2}(t)\left[\hat{R}^{2}_{x}+\hat{r}^{2}_{x}\right]
.

In the rotating frame of
\hat{H}_{0}
, we have
\hat{R}_{x}\rightarrow\hat{R}_{x}(t)=\hat{a}\exp(-i\omega_xt)+\text{h.c.}
, and
\hat{r}_{x}^{2}\rightarrow\hat{r}_{x}^{2}(t)\simeq\sqrt{1/2}[\hat{\sigma}_{x}\cos(\tilde{\omega}t)+\hat{\sigma}_{y}\sin(\tilde{\omega}t)]-\hat{\sigma}_{z}+3/2
. To generate the desired quantum gates, one can keep desired terms in
\hat{H}^{\prime(k)}
while averaging out other unwanted terms by modulating
\tilde{V}_{k}(t)
with appropriate sets of frequencies that are integer multiples of
\omega_{x}
,
\tilde{\omega}
, and/or their linear combinations. For instance, with
{\bf{V}}(t)=\{\lambda V_{0}\sin(\omega_xt+\theta),2V_{0},0,0,0\}
and
k_{max}=5
, only terms
\propto-i\lambda\epsilon_{x}V_{0}(\hat{a}e^{i\theta}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{-i\theta})
survive. In this case, the corresponding time-evolution operator for duration
T
is a displacement operator
\hat{D}(\alpha)\equiv\exp(\alpha\hat{a}^\dagger-\alpha^*\hat{a})
with
\alpha\propto\lambda\epsilon_{x}V_{0}Te^{-i\theta}/\hbar
. Similarly, the rotation (R), spin-rotation (SR), and squeezing (S) operators defined in Fig.
3

Hybrid quantum gates that entangle the qubit and the oscillator can be generated by modulating higher-order terms of

State preparation and readout.—
To prepare an initial state
\ket{\uparrow}\ket{0}
, one should be able to adiabatically merge two cold atoms in different optical tweezers into a single trap with negligible heating
Pampel et al. 2025
, followed by further Raman sideband cooling while avoiding photoassociation
Urvoy et al. 2019
. For the measurement, we consider taking advantage of the internal degree of freedom of the atoms by coupling it to the motion via Raman transitions. As explained in Ref.
Flühmann and Home 2020
, full quantum tomography of the motional state of a particle can be achieved by the variable application of
(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})\hat{S}_{x}
, where
\hat{S}_{x,y,z}
are spin operators acting on the
internal
spin, followed by measurements of
\langle\hat{S}_{z}\rangle
. In the case of two neutral atoms, relevant information of the quantum state can be obtained by evolution under
(\hat{a}_{1}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1})\hat{S}_{x,1}+(\hat{a}_{2}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2})\hat{S}_{x,2}
, followed by population measurements such as
\langle\hat{S}_{z,1}\hat{S}_{z,2}\rangle

sup
.

Conclusion.—
We have introduced protocols, using native contact interactions between two atoms in a time-averaged optical tweezer potential, to realize a controllable hybrid qubit-oscillator module using
only
the atomic motion, without involving any internal atomic states for gate operations. While here we focus on only a single pair of atoms, the scalability of our system for quantum simulation or computing can be achieved by coupling motional states in different tweezers via direct dipolar or Rydberg-dressed interactions. Such a qubit-oscillator array could become a resource for quantum computing with bosonic modes
Grimsmo et al. 2020
;
Gottesman et al. 2001
;
Campagne-Ibarcq et al. 2020
;
Flühmann et al. 2019
; for hybrid continuous-discrete variable quantum processors
Andersen et al. 2015
;
Liu et al.
;
Crane et al.
;
Singh et al.
; for quantum metrology
Zurek 2001
;
Tino 2021
;
Grochowski and Filip 2025
; and for quantum simulation of spin-boson models, such as those relevant for quantum chemistry
Kang et al. 2024
;
Navickas et al. 2025
; or others difficult to implement with only qubits or bosons.

Quantum Tomography of the motional state of two atoms

It is known that full quantum tomography of a bosonic mode is available in a spin-boson system by utilizing a spin-dependent force. We first briefly introduce the protocol discussed in Ref.
Flühmann and Home 2020
, where the Wigner function of the motional state of a single atom/ion is obtained.

Let’s assume an atom with two relevant internal states
\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}
is initially prepared in the state
\ket{g}\ket{\psi}
, where
\ket{\psi}
is the motional state we want to characterize. Obtaining the Wigner function
W(\gamma)
for a complex number
\gamma
is equivalent to obtaining the characteristic function,
\chi(\beta)\equiv\bra{\psi}\hat{D}(\beta)\ket{\psi}
of the displacement operator
\hat{D}(\beta)\equiv\exp(\beta\hat{a}^\dagger-\beta^*\hat{a})
where
\hat{a}
is the bosonic mode annihilation operator, then obtain the Wigner function via
W(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\chi(\beta)e^{\gamma\beta^{*}-\gamma^{*}\beta}d^{2}\beta
. To measure
\chi(\beta)
, one can use the spin-phonon coupling (spin-dependent force) of the form
\hat{S}_{x}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})
, where
\hat{S}_{x}=(\ket{g}\bra{e}+\ket{e}\bra{g})/2
is the Pauli-x operator. The protocol is as follows: first, a
\hat{S}_{x}
rotation by angle
\theta
is applied to the internal state, so that

\displaystyle\begin{split}e^{-i{\hat{S}}_{x}\theta}\ket{g}\ket{\psi}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(e^{-i\theta/2}\ket{+}+e^{i\theta/2}\ket{-}\right)\otimes\ket{\psi},\end{split}

(S19)

where
\ket{\pm}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{g}\pm\ket{e}\right)
. Next, the spin-dependent force term is applied for a displacement
\beta
, resulting in

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{D}(\beta\hat{S}_{x})e^{-i{\hat{S}}_{x}\theta}\ket{g}\ket{\psi}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(e^{-i\theta/2}\ket{+}\otimes\hat{D}(\beta/2)\ket{\psi}+e^{i\theta/2}\ket{-}\otimes\hat{D}(-\beta/2)\ket{\psi}\right).\\
\end{split}

(S20)

Lastly,
\hat{S}_{z}=(\ket{g}\bra{g}-\ket{e}\bra{e})/2
is measured, which provides information of the characteristic function:

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\langle\hat{S}_{z}\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\bra{\psi}\Big(e^{i\theta}\hat{D}(-\beta)+e^{-i\theta}\hat{D}(\beta)\Big)\ket{\psi}\\
&=\cos(\theta){\rm Re}[\chi(\beta)]+\sin(\theta){\rm Im}[\chi(\beta)],\end{split}

(S21)

and the real and imaginary parts of
\chi(\beta)
can be obtained by varying
\theta
.

For two atoms with identical internal states
\ket{g}_{1}
and
\ket{g}_{2}
, their motional state
\ket{\psi}
can be measured by applying an analogous procedure. We define annihilation operators for the COM and relative coordinates as
\hat{a}_{R}\equiv(\hat{a}_{1}+\hat{a}_{2})/\sqrt{2}
and
\hat{a}_{r}\equiv(\hat{a}_{1}-\hat{a}_{2})/\sqrt{2}
, respectively, so that the displacement operator in each coordinate becomes
\hat{D}_{R}(\sqrt{2}\beta)=\exp\left(\sqrt{2}(\beta\hat{a}_{R}^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}\hat{a}_{R})\right)=D_{1}(\beta)D_{2}(\beta)
and
D_{r}(\sqrt{2}\beta)=D_{1}(\beta)D_{2}(-\beta)
.
Assuming the spin-dependent force is applied identically to each atom (i.e.
\beta\hat{S}_{x,1}(\hat{a}_{1}+\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger})+\beta\hat{S}_{x,2}(\hat{a}_{2}+\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger})
), and assuming the contact interaction is small and can be neglected during this measurement protocol, the state after the rotation and spin-dependent force becomes:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\prod_{j=1,2}\left(\hat{D}_{j}(2\beta\hat{S}_{x,j})e^{-i{\hat{S}}_{x,j}\theta}\right)\ket{g}_{1}\ket{g}_{2}\ket{\psi}&=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j=1,2}\left(e^{-i\theta/2}\ket{+}_{j}\otimes\hat{D}_{j}(\beta)+e^{i\theta/2}\ket{-}_{j}\otimes\hat{D}_{j}(-\beta)\right)\ket{\psi}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-i\theta}\ket{+}_{1}\ket{+}_{2}\hat{D}_{R}(\beta\sqrt{2})+e^{i\theta}\ket{-}_{1}\ket{-}_{2}\hat{D}_{R}(-\beta\sqrt{2})\right)\ket{\psi}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{+}_{1}\ket{-}_{2}\hat{D}_{r}(\beta\sqrt{2})+\ket{-}_{1}\ket{+}_{2}\hat{D}_{r}(-\sqrt{2}\beta)\right)\ket{\psi}.\end{split}

(S22)

A joint measurement of both spin operators yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}4\langle\hat{S}_{z,1}\hat{S}_{z,2}\rangle_{\theta}&=\frac{1}{2}\cos(2\theta){\rm Re}[\chi_{R}(\beta^{\prime})]+\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta){\rm Im}[\chi_{R}(\beta^{\prime})]+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Re}[\chi_{r}(\beta^{\prime})]\end{split}

(S23)

where
\beta^{\prime}=2\sqrt{2}\beta
,
\chi_{R}(\beta^{\prime})=\bra{\psi}\hat{D}_{R}(\beta^{\prime})\ket{\psi}
, and
\chi_{r}(\beta^{\prime})=\bra{\psi}\hat{D}_{r}(\beta^{\prime})\ket{\psi}
. Note that due to the indistinguishability of the two atoms, the relative coordinate can only occupy even-numbered states. Since
\hat{D}_{r}(\beta^{\prime})-\hat{D}_{r}(-\beta^{\prime})
only includes odd numbered powers of
\hat{a}
or
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
terms,
{\rm Im}(\chi_{r}(\beta^{\prime}))=\bra{\psi}\frac{\hat{D}_{r}(\beta^{\prime})-\hat{D}_{r}(-\beta^{\prime})}{2i}\ket{\psi}=0
, and thus
{\rm Re}[\chi_{r}(\beta^{\prime})]=\chi_{r}(\beta^{\prime})
. Therefore, we can obtain both
\chi_{R}(\beta^{\prime})
and
\chi_{r}(\beta^{\prime})
from the relations

\displaystyle\begin{split}4\langle\hat{S}_{z,1}\hat{S}_{z,2}\rangle_{\theta}-4\langle\hat{S}_{z,1}\hat{S}_{z,2}\rangle_{\theta+\pi/2}&=\cos(2\theta){\rm Re}[\chi_{R}(\beta^{\prime})]+\sin(2\theta){\rm Im}[\chi_{R}(\beta^{\prime})],\\
4\langle\hat{S}_{z,1}\hat{S}_{z,2}\rangle_{\theta}+4\langle\hat{S}_{z,1}\hat{S}_{z,2}\rangle_{\theta+\pi/2}&=\chi_{r}(\beta^{\prime}).\end{split}

(S24)

While these measurements enable tomography of either the COM or the relative motional states, they do not provide full quantum state tomography of the joint state. However, one can in principle learn more about the state by applying the above protocol in parallel with various control gates coupling the relative and COM motion, such as CD, CR, and CS gates.
