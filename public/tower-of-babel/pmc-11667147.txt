Torticollis as an early manifestation of basilar invagination in a paediatric patient
Authors: Nehal Shata, Jumanah Ali, Nouf ALNosani, Ramsay MacNay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis case report describes the clinical journey of a male patient in early childhood with developmental delay, failure to thrive, worsening right-sided head tilt torticollis and regression of motor skills with spasticity of the lower limbs. The case was complex due to the early onset and gradually worsening symptoms, including a decline in established motor milestones. Genetic testing to investigate the delayed neurodevelopment revealed a FRA10AC1 variant that did not fully explain the patient’s phenotype. Investigations were delayed due to the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic and other factors related to the patient. Eventually, MRI revealed basilar invagination with cord compression and cord oedema at the C1–C2 junction. Treatment included foramen magnum decompression and management of symptoms using a multidisciplinary approach. Treatment resulted in a remarkable recovery. The patient regained some motor abilities and reached the 50th weight percentile.Keywords: Neuroimaging, Spinal cord, Failure to thrive, Neurosurgery, Pediatrics

Background

Torticollis, characterised by an abnormal rotational head tilt, can be either congenital or acquired.1 Congenital cases often arise from muscular or skeletal anomalies, while acquired torticollis in older children is typically caused by trauma, infections or tumours.1 In such cases, early imaging is crucial to prevent long-term complications, particularly if neurological involvement is suspected.1

Basilar invagination (BI) is a rare but serious developmental anomaly of the craniovertebral junction (CVJ), characterised by the prolapse of the odontoid process into the foramen magnum, which can lead to compression of the brainstem and spinal cord.2 3 The prevalence of BI is approximately 1% in the general population and is often associated with other congenital conditions, such as Chiari malformation, syringomyelia and Klippel–Feil syndrome.2 3 Clinically, BI can present a spectrum of symptoms, ranging from persistent headaches and limited neck mobility to more acute neurological deterioration.2 3 Because of its varied presentation, BI can easily be misdiagnosed as a benign musculoskeletal disorder, making careful diagnosis crucial to avoid missing this life-threatening condition.2,4

Patients with BI are classified based on the presence (Group A) or absence (Group B) of craniovertebral instability.5 Group A BI involves craniovertebral instability and typically presents with acute or subacute neurological symptoms, such as torticollis and other neurological deficits.5 Group B BI lacks craniovertebral instability, and it is related to bone malformations or dysplasia (eg, platybasia or condylar hypoplasia).5 Group B BI involves an upward displacement of the odontoid process but usually lacks atlantoaxial dislocation; its clinical progression is more gradual.5

The COVID-19 pandemic strained healthcare systems, and the focus on triaging COVID-19 cases contributed to delays in investigating other conditions.6 In our case, these challenges, combined with patient-related factors, resulted in significant delays in accessing diagnostic procedures, such as MRI, which were crucial for timely diagnosis and management. This delay contributed to the late diagnosis of BI and C1–C2 spinal cord compression.

The patient presented with progressive torticollis, developmental regression and spasticity, aligning with Group A BI.5 There was no history of trauma or Chiari malformation. A previous study identified torticollis in 41% of patients with Group A BI.5

Surgical intervention for this case involved foramen magnum decompression and stabilisation from the occipital bone to C5, which is typical for cases with craniovertebral instability.5 Furthermore, genetic testing was conducted as part of the workup for developmental delay.7 Whole-exome sequencing (WES) revealed a pathogenic FRA10AC1 variant of uncertain significance (VUS), which did not fully explain the patient’s torticollis and spastic paraparesis.8 Delayed imaging was critical in monitoring the progression of neurological symptoms, emphasising the need for timely diagnostics and intervention.

Overall, this case highlights the importance of early imaging and a coordinated multidisciplinary management strategy for atypical and complicated torticollis cases associated with BI to ensure better outcomes in paediatric patients.

Case presentation

Patient presentation

A male patient in early childhood was referred by a community paediatrician to the Neuromuscular and Neurometabolic Clinic for evaluation due to failure to thrive (FTT) and hypotonia. He was born at term (39+5 weeks) to consanguineous parents (first cousins), weighing 2.740 kg, with a head circumference of 34 cm. There were no complications at birth, and Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were both 9. The mother, in her early 30s and healthy, had an unremarkable antenatal course with no detected risk factors.

Developmental history

The patient exhibited delayed motor milestones, with concerns emerging at 6 months of age when decreased motor skills were noted. He developed head control by 8 months, was able to sit at 9 months and began crawling at 11 months. He started walking at 20 months. By 16 months, the family doctor made an urgent referral to a community paediatrician due to hypotonia, hypermobility, severe gross motor delay and concerns regarding fine motor skills. The patient had failed to thrive, with height and weight below the first percentile, but his head circumference remained in the 25th percentile. The family first noticed a right-sided head tilt when he began supporting his head at 8 months. On analysing old photographs, it became evident that torticollis was not present at birth but became slightly apparent because of the right-sided head preference within the first few months of life.

Family history

The patient had no reported family history of dysmorphic features or developmental delays.

Neurological examination

At 2 years and 1 month of age, the patient was referred to a neuromuscular clinic, where an examination revealed axial and appendicular hypotonia, 2+knee reflexes, absent ankle reflexes, a bilateral extensor plantar response and a spastic gait. The patient needed to tripod sit and displayed tightness in the right sternocleidomastoid muscle, contributing to the torticollis. His gag reflex was intact, and there were no concerns regarding chewing, swallowing, coughing, gagging or choking. The examination also revealed several dysmorphic features, including mild ptosis, a high-arched palate, dolichocephaly, large low-set ears, two dimples under his lower lip, a very narrow beak-shaped nose, long lashes, a high forehead and clinodactyly of the fifth toe.

Torticollis and motor regression

Over time, the torticollis worsened. Concurrently, the patient experienced motor developmental regression, eventually losing the ability to walk, crawl or sit without support.

Imaging and diagnosis

At 3 years and 1 month of age, the patient underwent a whole-spine MRI, which revealed severe BI causing spinal cord compression at the C1–C4 level. No other abnormalities were observed throughout the remainder of the spinal cord. The imaging results confirmed the severity of the condition, and the patient underwent decompression and fusion surgery of the craniocervical junction to prevent further spinal cord injury.

Investigations

Nerve conduction studies were normal. A microarray, creatine kinase and metabolic testing (lactate and amino acids) were normal. Trio WES was conducted due to the developmental delay, FTT, dysmorphic features and consanguinity of the parents. The corticospinal tract findings suggested brain or spinal cord pathology; thus, urgent MRI of the brain and cervical spine was requested. However, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly delayed the process, and the child did not undergo an MRI until later due to several issues, including the patient arriving in a non-fasted state and a viral illness that led to the cancellation of two MRI attempts. WES revealed a homozygous missense variant of VUS in the FRA10AC1 gene (c.34A>T; p.Ser12Cys). This rare variant has a gnomAD frequency of 0.00071%, no recorded homozygotes and is predicted to be pathogenic based on multiple in silico models. The known phenotype of children with pathogenic variants in the FRA10AC1 gene was consistent with several symptoms, including developmental delay, postnatal growth retardation, hypotonia and craniofacial dysmorphic features, but not with abnormalities of the corpus callosum (eventually excluded by MRI) or other phenotypic abnormalities. Consequently, the VUS in FRA10AC1 was a possible contributor to the phenotype but did not explain the torticollis and spastic paraparesis. The MRI revealed BI and severe compression of the spinal cord at the C1–C2 level, resulting in cord oedema. Aside from the upper cervical spine abnormalities, the remainder of the spinal cord and spinal canal was essentially normal (see figure 1 and figure 2 for details). The patient was urgently referred to neurosurgery.

Differential diagnosis

The patient’s presentation of developmental delay, progressive right-sided head tilt (torticollis) and worsening spasticity in the lower limbs initially raised concerns for a neurogenetic disorder or a structural abnormality of the central nervous system. The regression of motor milestones and spasticity suggested a possible neurodegenerative disease or spinal cord pathology, while the head tilt indicated a potential musculoskeletal or neurological cause.

Genetic testing revealed a FRA10AC1 variant, which suggested a possible neurogenetic aetiology. However, this finding did not fully explain the phenotype, including the motor regression and spasticity. The persistence and progression of symptoms, despite the identified genetic variant, led to further investigation into structural abnormalities.

Delays in imaging, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and other patient-related factors, complicated the diagnostic process. Nevertheless, the worsening motor function and the presence of torticollis increased suspicion of BI or other craniocervical junction pathologies.

Subsequent MRI revealed BI with cord compression and cord oedema at the C1–C2 junction, which clarified the aetiology of the patient’s symptoms. The imaging findings confirmed that the neurological deterioration was primarily due to structural compression rather than solely a genetic cause. The BI and associated cord compression, leading to oedema, explained the progressive motor dysfunction and torticollis, highlighting the importance of thorough imaging in cases with complex neurodevelopmental symptoms.

Treatment

To treat the BI, the patient underwent foramen magnum decompression and stabilisation from the occipital bone to C5. Postoperatively, the patient encountered multiple complications, including recurrence of BI, FTT, constipation, dysautonomia, visceral hyperalgesia and neurogenic bladder, necessitating specific interventions. The recurrence of BI was diagnosed via follow-up imaging; the recurrence manifested as worsening symptoms, severe nausea, vomiting, FTT and reduced motor skills. The recurrence was managed with additional surgical intervention, followed by 3 months of halo-vest traction to stabilise the spine. The patient then transitioned to an Aspen collar for continued support. The FTT was initially managed with total parenteral nutrition to address the critical nutritional deficiencies. A gastrostomy tube (G tube) was inserted and later advanced to a gastrojejunostomy tube to manage the severe gastro-oesophageal reflux, enhance nutritional intake and support growth. Oral intake was optimised with medications such as pantoprazole, famotidine and cisapride. Constipation was managed with polyethylene glycol and sennosides, supplemented with bisacodyl suppositories as needed. The symptoms of dysautonomia included tachycardia, excessive sweating and lethargy, which were treated with medications, including clonidine and cyproheptadine. Visceral hyperalgesia was diagnosed based on symptoms of abdominal pain, bloating, nausea and sensitivity to touch. The pain and discomfort of visceral hyperalgesia was treated with gabapentin. The neurogenic bladder was managed with clean intermittent catheterisation, which was integrated into the patient’s daily routine to prevent complications related to bladder dysfunction.

The complex postoperative challenges were successfully managed with a holistic and multidisciplinary approach. Collaboration across various specialties, including neurosurgery, paediatrics, occupational and physical therapy, dietetics, rehabilitation and urology, was required. Additional support from child life specialists, a speech-language pathologist and targeted pharmacotherapy was essential for addressing the comprehensive needs of the patient. These interventions collectively contributed to the patient’s notable recovery, enabling him to regain motor abilities and achieve significant health improvements. The patient is currently at the 50th percentile for growth, can stand with support and is receiving ongoing rehabilitation at a specialised centre.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient’s progress was impressive. The patient can stand with support and mobilise both arms and has reached the 50th weight percentile on the growth chart. The patient could be weaned off clonidine, indicating significant recuperation.

Discussion

In this article, we report the case of a male patient who presented with progressive right-sided torticollis and significant motor skill decline in early childhood. The patient was initially referred to the clinic due to FTT and developmental delays. Neurological examination revealed corticospinal findings indicative of potential brain or spinal cord pathology or a neurogenetic disorder.

Patients with BI often experience spinal cord compression caused by the odontoid process. In these patients, torticollis occurs as a protective response to mechanical instability at the CVJ,5 where the body twists the neck to alleviate the compression.2 5 This natural adaptation minimises stretch and pressure on the spinal cord, allowing torticollis to develop as a means to prevent further neurological damage.5 Early recognition of this compensatory response is crucial, as it can prompt earlier diagnosis and intervention to prevent more severe spinal cord injury. Clinicians should be aware that torticollis can signal underlying CVJ instability, particularly in the presence of other neurological findings.

BI can present with a spectrum of symptoms, ranging from neck pain to severe neurological compromise, including dysautonomia.49,13 In our patient, dysautonomia manifested as orthostatic hypotension, tachycardia and delayed gastric emptying. Dysautonomia is generally managed with medications, including clonidine, cyproheptadine and gabapentin, which may show varying efficacy in different clinical contexts.49,13 The clinical presentation of dysautonomia in this case reinforces the complexity of BI and the importance of a tailored management approach.

In our patient, genetic testing revealed a homozygous missense VUS in the FRA10AC1 gene, which is typically associated with developmental delays, dysmorphic features and various neurological manifestations, but not specifically with torticollis or spastic paraparesis.8 14 Individuals with this VUS commonly present with FTT, hypotonia, motor delays and distinctive craniofacial dysmorphisms, similar to the symptoms observed in our patient.8 14 However, the absence of other clinical features, typically associated with pathogenic FRA10AC1 mutations (such as epilepsy, microcephaly or corpus callosum anomalies) combined with the fact that this specific variant has not been previously reported, could indicate the variability in genetic expression. This variability suggests that the variant may be non-pathogenic or only partially responsible for the patient’s phenotype.8 14 When no definitive mutation is identified on WES, and alternative genetic testing results do not fully explain the phenotype, other potential contributors or alternative diagnoses should be considered.8 14

This report adds to the body of literature exploring the potential clinical significance of FRA10AC1 mutations, although its role in this case remains unclear. Given the variability in presentation, further research is required to clarify the full spectrum of conditions associated with this gene. Additionally, clinicians should consider early genetic testing in similar cases to identify possible underlying aetiologies, even if the clinical significance of detected variants is not immediately apparent.

The remarkable progress of this patient after surgical intervention with a multidisciplinary rehabilitation strategy reaffirms the importance of individualised treatment plans. The collaboration between neurosurgery, physical therapy and neurogenetics played a pivotal role in the patient’s recovery. This case also highlights the value of early intervention in patients with CVJ anomalies, as well as the need for a comprehensive, coordinated approach.

This case contributes to the growing evidence that torticollis, particularly when associated with CVJ anomalies, can have diverse aetiologies and presentations, thus necessitating a thorough and nuanced diagnostic approach. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for CVJ instability in patients presenting with torticollis alongside other neurological symptoms. Furthermore, genetic testing should be considered to capture potential underlying causes, even if the genetic findings are inconclusive.

Patient’s perspective

From our experience, it is crucial to avoid delay and go to the emergency room immediately for adverse concerns. We are thankful to the multidisciplinary team and the rehabilitation centre. Their efforts have made a significant impact on the patient’s recovery.

Learning points

Torticollis in children requires a comprehensive diagnostic approach beyond musculoskeletal assessment, including evaluation of CVJ anomalies, especially when standard interventions for congenital muscular torticollis do not yield expected improvements.

The regression of motor function and evidence of corticospinal tract involvement presented diagnostic inconsistencies that did not align with initial assessments. These findings underscored the urgency of obtaining an MRI to accurately identify the underlying causes. However, the process was significantly hindered by the patient’s recurrent illnesses and related delays, demonstrating the complexities of the timely diagnosis of serious neurological conditions.
