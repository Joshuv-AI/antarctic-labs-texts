Determining reduced functional capacity in older adults using research-grade wearable accelerometers: a secondary analysis of the study of muscle, mobility, and aging
Authors: Anthony Hung, Nancy W Glynn, Reagan E Garcia, Megan Hetherington-Rauth, Peggy M Cawthon, Daniel E Forman, Eileen Johnson, Daniel S Rubin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReduced functional capacity (FC) is associated with adverse surgical outcomes in older adults. Current FC assessments rely on questionnaires; however, it remains unclear whether accelerometer-measured daily activity provides a more accurate evaluation. Our primary aim was to identify accelerometer-based variables associated with reduced FC.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of the Study of Muscle, Mobility and Aging (SOMMA) cohort. Participants were community-dwelling adults (non-surgical) aged ≥70 yr and recruited between the years 2019 to 2021 at the University of Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and Wake Forest University School of Medicine (Winston-Salem, NC, USA). Participants were included if they completed cardiopulmonary exercise testing and had valid wear time (≥3 days) for two accelerometers used in the SOMMA study (ActiGraph GT9X and activPAL4). We applied classification and regression tree and random forest models to accelerometry-derived metrics. For comparison, we constructed a logistic regression model using modified Duke Activity Status Index 4-Question (M-DASI-4Q) scores extrapolated from the Community Healthy Activities Model Program for Seniors questionnaire.ResultsThe final cohort included 640 participants (57.2% [366/640] women; mean age 76.3 [5.0] yr), of whom 18% (114/640) had reduced FC (peak oxygen uptake [VO2peak] <16 ml kg−1 min−1). Participants with adequate FC had higher daily step counts (5843.9 [2950.4] vs 2988.3 [1757.2] steps per day; P<0.001) and more time in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (118.0 [62.2] vs 59.9 [42.4] min day−1; P<0.001) compared with those with reduced FC. The accelerometer-based random forest model (AUC 0.79) did not significantly outperform the M-DASI-4Q model (AUC 0.72; P=0.16).ConclusionAmong community-dwelling older adults, daily step count and time in moderate-to-vigorous activity were most associated with FC, but the accelerometer-based model showed only fair discrimination to identify participants with reduced FC. Validation in surgical populations is needed.Keywords: cardiopulmonary exercise testing, Duke Activity Status Index, physical activity, preoperative risk assessment, questionnaires, wearable devices

Functional capacity (FC) is the ability to perform aerobic work or respond to physiological stress, including surgery.1 Reduced FC, defined as peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) <16 ml kg−1 min−1, strongly correlates with adverse outcomes in older surgical patients.2 These outcomes include complications and persistent disability, with ∼30% of older adults with reduced FC experiencing disability persisting up to 12 months after surgery.3,4

Guidelines recommend FC assessment for all older adults before noncardiac surgery.5,6 While cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is the gold standard,7,8 resource limitations restrict its use. Thus, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Joint Committee on Clinical Practice Guidelines recommend structured questionnaires such as the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) to estimate FC before major noncardiac surgery.5,9 However, DASI’s validation studies included few participants >75 yr old, and its surveyed activities may not reflect typical activities of many older surgical candidates. Furthermore, these questionnaires significantly overestimate FC. In a study of 632 patients undergoing preoperative assessment for cancer surgery, self-reported physical performance questions overestimated VO2peak by 13 ml kg−1 min−1 compared with CPET,10 a finding corroborated by other studies,11,12 highlighting the need for more accurate methods.

Daily physical activity correlates with maximal physical function, particularly in older, lower-functioning individuals.13 Accelerometers objectively measure actual performed daily physical activity and avoid the bias of self-reported questionnaires, which include exercise preferences, cultural/racial differences, cognitive status, and reading comprehension. Previous studies demonstrating the potential of accelerometers to identify reduced FC have been limited by small sample sizes, reliance on commercial-grade devices with unknown validity, or both.14, 15, 16, 17, 18

Our study’s primary aim was to identify accelerometer-based variables associated with reduced FC in older adults. The secondary aim was to compare the performance of accelerometer-based variables with a FC structured questionnaire to identify participants with a reduced FC. We hypothesised that the objective accelerometer-based measures would outperform the modified DASI (M-DASI) questionnaire to identify participants with reduced FC.

Methods

Participant sample

We performed a secondary data analysis of the Study of Muscle, Mobility and Aging (SOMMA).19 Briefly, SOMMA is a prospective cohort study of community-dwelling adults designed to identify the biological determinants of mobility and fitness.20 Participants were aged ≥70 yr recruited between April 2019 and December 2021 at the University of Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and Wake Forest University School of Medicine (Winston-Salem, NC, USA).19 SOMMA is not a surgical cohort but rather a cohort of community-dwelling older adults. Eligibility required body mass index 18–40 kg m−2, ability to undergo magnetic resonance imaging and muscle biopsy, self-reported ability to walk a quarter of a mile and climb a flight of stairs, and ability to complete a 400 m walk test at a usual pace within 15 min during the initial baseline exam. Those who seemed unable to complete the 400 m walk at the in-person screening visit had to complete a 4 m walk at ≥0.6 m s−1 to qualify. Exclusions included anticoagulation therapy, certain implants, active cancer, advanced heart failure, dialysis-dependent renal failure, dementia, or Parkinson’s disease. Further baseline assessments occurred over 3–4 non-consecutive days within 6 weeks of the baseline exam. All participants provided informed consent, and SOMMA was approved by the WIRB-Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board. The Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) checklist was used to ensure complete and transparent reporting (see Supplementary Information).

Outcome measure

The primary outcome was reduced FC, defined as CPET-measured peak oxygen consumption (VO2peak) <16 ml kg−1 min−1, based on prior perioperative literature.2,6,21 Participants completed a three-phase exercise test with monitoring of breath-by-breath ventilatory measurements, heart rate, blood pressure, and rating of perceived exertion.22 Phase I consisted of 5 min of treadmill walking at the participant’s preferred speed. Phase II involved progressively raising treadmill incline (up to 10%) and then speed until volitional fatigue, followed by a 20 min rest. Phase III involved 5 min of walking at 1.5 mph with continued measurements. VO2peak was defined as the highest 30-s average of VO2 during phase II; data from phases I and III were not used in this analysis.

Accelerometry

Participants wore two accelerometers concurrently for 7 days: the ActiGraph GT9X (ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL, USA) on the non-dominant wrist, and the activPAL4 (PAL Technologies Ltd, Glasgow, UK) affixed to the right thigh. The ActiGraph measures activity intensity, duration, and volume, while the activPAL4 detects posture transitions, walking cadence, and sedentary behaviour. Using both enabled a more comprehensive physical activity assessment and allowed our study to evaluate which metrics best discriminate reduced FC. Devices were placed at the first baseline visit. Data for participants with ≥3 valid days (defined as ≥17 h day−1 for ActiGraph, ≥10 h day−1 for activPAL4) of recorded activity for both accelerometers were analysed. Day 1 of wear was excluded from analyses because of performance tests during the baseline visit.

Raw ActiGraph acceleration data collected at 80 Hz were processed (in units of gravity; g) using Euclidean Norm Minus One (ENMO) in the GGIR R package to derive mean time spent in and number of bouts of inactivity (<18 mg) while awake, light intensity physical activity (18 to <60 mg), and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA, ≥60 mg), using ENMO cut points established from a cohort demographically similar to SOMMA (Supplementary Information).23 To understand movement trends, we analysed activity data by continuous activity or inactivity bouts. Steps were determined using a modified Verisense algorithm in the GGIR R package (version 3.1.1).24

ActivPAL4 data collected at 20 Hz were analysed with the VANE standard activPAL algorithm in PALanalysis (version v8.11.1.49, PAL Technologies Ltd) to determine daily step count and activity bouts of varying duration and cadence bands (e.g. <75, ≥75 to <125, or ≥125 steps min−1) across all wear time, including sleep. A cadence band refers to a range of steps min−1 used to categorise stepping activity intensity or patterns.

Extrapolated modified Duke Activity Status Score Index 4-Question

The Modified Duke Activity Status Index 4-Question score (M-DASI-4Q),2 a simplification of DASI, was extrapolated from responses to the Community Healthy Activities Model Program for Seniors (CHAMPS) questionnaire.25 M-DASI-4Q is a screening tool to identify patients who may benefit from CPET. Scores range from 0 to 4, assigning one point for each positive response to four criteria: ability to climb stairs or walk uphill, ability to perform heavy housework, ability to do yard work, and ability to participate in strenuous sports (assessed through CHAMPS items including swimming, singles tennis, team sports, and strength training).

Covariates

Age, sex, medical history, and health status were self-reported. Study site and baseline visit season were recorded. A modified Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) score assigned one point for each of: history of congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease or renal failure, stroke, coronary artery disease, and insulin use within 30 days of any study visit.26 A mobility assessment tool-short form (MAT-sf) score was calculated based on self-reported ability to complete a number of mobility movements demonstrated by video.27 A SOMMA multimorbidity index score assigned one point for each of 11 chronic conditions reported during the baseline visit, including cancer, chronic kidney disease or renal failure, atrial fibrillation, lung disease (including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchitis, asthma, or emphysema), coronary heart disease (including blocked arteries or myocardial infarction), depressive symptoms, heart failure, dementia, diabetes, stroke, and aortic stenosis.28

Statistical analyses

The analytical sample included participants with CPET data and ≥3 valid days from both accelerometers using data released as of November 2024 (Fig. 1). The sample was split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets using a consistent random seed, and the same partitioning was maintained across all models (classification and regression tree [CART], random forest, and logistic regression) to ensure comparability. The analysts were aware of both the accelerometer results and CPET reference standard results when performing the analyses.

Flow diagram of participant inclusion. From 879 initial Study of Muscle, Mobility and Aging (SOMMA) participants, 820 participants had cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) data. Of these, 180 participants did not have valid accelerometry data (≥3 days) from one or both devices, and 640 participants had valid data from both devices. Data from these 640 participants were included in the analyses.

Baseline characteristics were reported as mean (standard deviation), median (inter-quartile range [IQR]), or as proportions (n, %) and compared using t-tests or χ2 tests. Four variables (RCRI, MAT-sf, SOMMA multimorbidity index score, and M-DASI-4Q) were compared using Kruskal–Wallis tests because of non-normality. Statistical significance was considered at P-value <0.05. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.2).29

Eighty variables, including patient characteristics (age, sex), site, season, SOMMA multimorbidity index score, and 75 device-specific activity metrics, were used to fit the CART and random forest models (Supplementary Table S1).

CART analysis was performed to identify predictors of reduced FC. CART constructs a binary decision tree by selecting optimal splits in variables that maximise homogeneity in the response variable. The root node is chosen as the predictor that most reduces response variable impurity, with subsequent splits continuing until a minimum size threshold is met or further divisions add no discriminative value. CART has been previously applied in FC assessment.30 The rpart algorithm in the rpart R package (version 4.1.23)31 was used to construct two trees. A full tree was constructed with a minimal complexity parameter of 10−6. The complexity parameter balances explanatory power against model complexity, optimising discriminative accuracy while minimising overfitting. A pruned tree was constructed using the same parameters, but also prevented nodes from splitting if they contained <5% of the training data. The primary outcome in each CART was reduced FC. Variable importance was assessed using the rpart algorithm, which measures each variable’s contribution to reducing node impurity across the tree.31

A random forest model containing 10 000 trees was fit to the 440 complete cases in the training set using the randomForest R package (version 4.7.1)32 to discriminate reduced FC. This ensemble method enhances discriminative robustness and reduces overfitting by constructing bootstrapped classification trees. Variable importance was assessed via mean change in model accuracy when permuted in out-of-bag samples and mean contribution to the Gini index, which quantifies node impurity in decision trees.33

A logistic regression model was fit with M-DASI-4Q score as the sole predictor of reduced FC using the glm function in R with a binomial family and logit link function.34

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated with the pROC R package (version 1.18.5) for each model using the test set.35 Model-specific optimal classification thresholds were identified using Youden’s J statistic,36 which maximises the difference between the true-positive and false-positive rate to balance sensitivity and specificity. These thresholds were used to calculate each model’s accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity on the test set. Discriminative performance of the random forest and logistic regression models was compared using DeLong's test for paired ROC curves.37

Cross-classification analysis of model predictions

A cross-classification analysis was conducted to evaluate whether the M-DASI-4Q logistic regression and the random forest models classified the same individuals as having reduced FC in the test set. Participants were categorised into four groups: classified as having reduced FC by both models, by random forest only, by M-DASI-4Q only, or by neither. These groups were compared by prevalence of reduced FC, age, sex, study site, comorbidities, M-DASI-4Q scores, and accelerometry-derived metrics. Wilcoxon rank sum tests compared M-DASI-4Q score, mean daily step count from ActiGraph, and time spent in MVPA between the random forest-only M-DASI-4Q-only groups. Cohen’s kappa statistic was calculated to assess agreement between the binary classifications from the logistic regression and random forest models.38

Post hoc power analysis

To assess whether our study was adequately powered to detect the observed difference in discriminative performance between models, we performed a simulation-based power analysis. Using our observed sample size (n=640, with 114 poor FC cases), we simulated paired test scores for two classifiers with AUCs equivalent to those of our two models. Within-subject correlation between predicted probabilities was incorporated into the simulation based on the empirical Pearson correlation from our test set. We generated 1000 replicate datasets under these conditions, applied DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves to each, and estimated statistical power as the proportion of simulations with P<0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Among 879 SOMMA participants, 820 had CPET data, and 640 further had valid accelerometry data from both devices (Fig. 1). Among these 640 participants, 57.2% were women, with mean age 76.3 (5.0) yr, and 18% had reduced FC (VO2peak <16 ml kg−1 min−1) (Table 1). Participants with reduced FC were more often women (79.8% vs 52.3%, P<0.001), were slightly older (77.8 [5.6] vs 75.9 [4.8] yr, P<0.001), and had lower MAT-sf scores (median 61 [IQR 57–67] vs 70 [IQR 66–73], P<0.001). The reduced FC group had an ∼49% lower mean daily step count from ActiGraph (2988 vs 5844 steps/day, P<0.001), and less time spent in MVPA (60 vs 118 min day−1, P<0.001). Conversely, they spent a larger percentage of time in inactivity (61% vs 55% of non-sleep wear time, P<0.001) and had longer bouts of inactivity (385 vs 316 min day−1 in ≥30-min bouts, P<0.001). Statistics for all variables considered in CART and random forest models are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Classification trees

The full accelerometer-based classification tree achieved 0.74 accuracy, 0.67 sensitivity, and 0.76 specificity (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. S1). The pruned tree achieved 0.80 accuracy, 0.53 sensitivity, and 0.86 specificity (Table 2, Fig. 2a). The primary split in the pruned tree used mean daily step count from ActiGraph (threshold: 3224 steps). A secondary split used mean daily step count in ≥1 min bouts of cadence band <75 steps min−1 (threshold: 1490 steps day−1). Variable importance analysis identified mean daily step count from ActiGraph as the strongest predictor, followed by various MVPA measures (Fig. 2b).

Random forest model

The random forest model achieved 0.68 accuracy, 0.81 sensitivity, 0.65 specificity, and 0.79 AUC (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.70–0.88) (Table 2, Fig. 3). Variable importance analysis confirmed CART findings, with mean daily step count from ActiGraph and MVPA measures showing highest importance across mean impact on model accuracy when randomly permuted and contribution to Gini index (Fig. 4b).

Modified Duke Activity Status Score Index 4-Question model

The M-DASI-4Q logistic regression model achieved 0.72 AUC (95% CI 0.64–0.80), 0.55 accuracy, 0.86 sensitivity, and 0.48 specificity (Table 2, Fig. 3). DeLong’s Test showed no significant difference in AUC between the M-DASI-4Q and random forest models (P=0.162). The empirical Pearson correlation between model-predicted probabilities was ρ=0.449. The simulation-based power to detect the observed AUC difference (0.79 vs 0.72) was 75.1%.

Cross-classification of participant classifications

Participants classified as having reduced FC by both models had the highest proportion of CPET-measured reduced FC (45%, 27/60), followed by those classified by neither model (6.9%, 4/58), those classified by M-DASI-4Q only (6.2%, 3/48), and those classified by random forest only (4.8%, 1/21) (Supplemental Table 2).

Participants in the random forest-only group self-reported higher participation in activities than those in the M-DASI-4Q-only group (M-DASI-4Q scores: median 2 [IQR 2–3] vs median 1 [IQR 0–1], P<0.001), but they demonstrated lower mean step counts from ActiGraph (3636.7 [1817.1] vs 6676.7 [2560.7], P<0.001), and lower time spent in MVPA (71.8 [41.4] vs 135.5 [52.6] p<0.001).

Cohen’s kappa coefficient assessing agreement between the logistic regression and random forest models was 0.28 (P<0.001).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of the SOMMA cohort of community-dwelling older adults, accelerometer-based metrics, particularly daily step count and time spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity(MVPA), were among the strongest correlates of reduced FC. However, the accelerometer-derived models only demonstrated fair performance to identify participants with a poor FC. Similarly, the m-DASI-4Q also performed fairly, and the accelerometer-based models did not clearly outperform this questionnaire-based measure. Mean daily step count and MVPA time were the two accelerometer-derived variables most strongly associated with FC. Mean daily step count reflects the overall volume of activity, whereas MVPA time reflects its intensity—an association with FC that is both expected and intuitive. Surprisingly, cross-classification between the accelerometer derived models and M-DASI-4Q models was limited. Participants identified by both models had the highest prevalence of CPET-measured reduced FC (45%), whereas classification by only one model was less consistent (4.8–6.2%). This pattern may indicate that concordance between questionnaire- and accelerometer-based measures could enhance discriminative ability. Participants identified only by the M-DASI-4Q reported lower activity levels but demonstrated higher accelerometer-measured activity than those identified only by the random forest, suggesting that questionnaires and accelerometers may capture different aspects of physical activity and behaviour. Overall, these findings should be interpreted cautiously but raise the possibility that accelerometer- and questionnaire-based measures, while similar in aggregate performance, may provide complementary information for understanding FC and warrant further study.

Our analysis identified a step activity threshold of 3224 steps day−1 (measured by ActiGraph) associated with reduced FC. This threshold is lower than those reported in studies evaluating surgical outcomes. For example, in a retrospective analysis of the All of Us Research Program using participant-owned Fitbits, a threshold of 7500 steps day−1 was associated with increased odds of postoperative complications (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 2.06, 95% CI 1.05–4.06).39 Applying such a high threshold to our older cohort would classify most participants as high risk, limiting its value for stratification. In another study of pancreatectomy patients provided with Fitbits, a lower threshold of 4275 steps day−1 was linked to severe complications (OR 7.5, 95% CI 2.1–26.8).40 Cui and colleagues18 examined chest-worn accelerometers in 50 surgical patients, relating daily accelerations to CPET measures such as peak VO2, and found fair correlations. However, their reliance on accelerations rather than standardised measures such as MVPA time or step counts makes direct comparison with our results challenging. Collectively, these studies differ in endpoints, populations, and devices, each with unique step-detection algorithms. Such variability highlights the need for outcome-, population-, and device-specific thresholds when applying accelerometer-derived measures to clinical decision-making.

Our study has several notable strengths. We used gold standard CPET to define FC and paired it with two research-grade accelerometers, allowing for robust objective measurement of both physiological and behavioural correlates of physical function. The prevalence of reduced FC in our cohort (18%) closely mirrored that observed in the largest prospective CPET study in surgical patients (16% in the Measurement of Exercise Tolerance before Surgery study, N=1401),12 supporting the representativeness of our findings. In addition, the M-DASI-4Q model in our cohort demonstrated an AUC comparable to that reported in the original validation study,2 underscoring its discriminative capability. Our analytic approach also incorporated strategies to reduce overfitting, including cross-validation, pruning, and limiting tree depth. We acknowledge several limitations. SOMMA participants were not drawn from a surgical cohort, and the requirement to complete a 400 m walk test may have excluded frailer adults with significant mobility limitations, potentially limiting generalisability to higher-risk surgical populations. Furthermore, our M-DASI-4Q scores were extrapolated from CHAMPS data rather than directly collected. From the overall SOMMA cohort, we excluded 180 participants (∼20%) because of missing data, primarily from the activPAL device. The activPAL is worn on the anterior thigh, and several technical issues rather than low participant activity levels reduced the number of participants with valid wear days. Importantly, we did not find evidence that exclusions for insufficient wear time disproportionately removed individuals with reduced FC. However, it remains possible that these exclusions introduced some bias into our findings. Finally, although we leveraged a relatively large sample (n=640), further external validation in perioperative cohorts is warranted. Our post hoc simulations indicate that the study had moderate power (∼75.1%) to detect the observed difference given the empirical within-subject correlation (ρ=0.449) and sample size (n =640; 114 cases). This suggests that while the study was reasonably well powered, there remains a non-negligible risk of a type II error. Larger datasets or pooled analyses would reduce this uncertainty and more definitively characterise comparative performance.

In a population of community-dwelling (non-surgical) older adults accelerometer data did not outperform the standard M-DASI-4Q questionnaire for identifying reduced FC. Future research is needed to examine whether combining accelerometer metrics with questionnaires could improve discrimination and to explore whether activity thresholds may help identify patients at risk for surgical complications or post-operative disability.
