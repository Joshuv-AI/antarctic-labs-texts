Single-cell spatial transcriptomics identifies SERPINE1 as a vascular endothelial mediator of cisplatin resistance in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Hongxia Su, Yuan Zhang, Jia Wang, Lei Liu, Yulin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Endothelial cells are identified as key mediators of cisplatin resistance in HNSCC.•Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics reveal SERPINE1 vascular localization.•SERPINE1 marks resistant endothelial states in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors.•High SERPINE1 expression predicts poor survival and immune suppression.•SERPINE1 may guide resistance-directed precision treatment strategies.

Introduction

Head and neck tumors are among the most common malignant tumors globally, with over 90% being head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). These tumors primarily occur in anatomical sites such as the oral cavity, pharynx (including the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx), and larynx [1]. According to the latest statistics from GLOBOCAN, head and neck tumors rank among the highest in both new cases and mortality rates of malignant tumors worldwide. They have become a significant public health issue that poses a serious threat to human health, placing a heavy burden on patients' quality of life, the social workforce, and healthcare resources [2]. Despite the continuous optimization of multimodal comprehensive treatment strategies, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy in recent years, the overall survival rate of HNSCC patients has still seen limited improvement. One of the core limiting factors is the widespread presence of treatment resistance [1,2]. Cisplatin, as a classic platinum-based chemotherapeutic agent, remains the first-line treatment foundation for locally advanced and recurrent metastatic HNSCC. Its antitumor effects primarily operate through the formation of DNA cross-links, induction of DNA damage, and apoptosis. However, a substantial body of clinical and basic research indicates that cisplatin resistance is extremely common during treatment, significantly undermining the initial therapeutic effects, and is closely associated with tumor recurrence, metastasis, and poor prognosis [3]. Previous studies on cisplatin resistance have primarily focused on the genetic and epigenetic alterations within tumor cells, such as enhanced DNA repair, increased drug efflux, and inactivation of apoptotic pathways. However, these mechanisms are insufficient to fully explain the heterogeneity of resistance observed clinically [4]. An increasing number of studies have begun to recognize that the tumor microenvironment, as an important regulatory aspect of tumor growth and treatment response, also plays a significant role in the development of chemotherapy resistance. In recent years, persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) has been confirmed as an independent risk factor for HNSCC, particularly playing a significant role in oropharyngeal cancer [5,6]. Compared to HPV-negative tumors, HPV-positive HNSCC exhibits significant differences in molecular characteristics, immune infiltration patterns, and treatment responses. HPV-positive patients are generally more sensitive to chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy, and they tend to have relatively better clinical outcomes. However, despite the widespread application of HPV status in clinical risk stratification, there is still a lack of systematic and multidimensional research evidence on how it influences the occurrence and evolution of chemotherapy resistance by regulating the tumor microenvironment, particularly the components related to blood vessels and stroma [7]. Among the regulatory factors related to drug resistance in the tumor microenvironment, SERPINE1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor-1) has garnered increasing attention in recent years. Previous studies have indicated that SERPINE1, as a crucial regulator of the fibrinolytic system, participates in extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, tumor invasion, and treatment resistance. Its aberrant expression has been associated with poor prognosis in multiple solid tumors [8]. Emerging evidence further suggests that SERPINE1-associated vascular and stromal remodeling may contribute to abnormal tumor perfusion, hypoxia maintenance, extracellular matrix deposition, and activation of pro-survival signaling, thereby creating a microenvironment permissive to therapeutic resistance [9]. However, there is currently a lack of systematic single-cell and spatial evidence regarding the specific cellular sources and spatial localization characteristics of SERPINE1 in HNSCC, as well as whether it mediates cisplatin resistance through the tumor microenvironment, particularly via vascular endothelial cells. This critical scientific question has not yet been adequately elucidated. Therefore, this study integrates single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses to characterize the cellular origin and spatial distribution of SERPINE1 in HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC and to investigate its potential association with endothelial remodeling, cisplatin resistance-related phenotypes, and clinical outcomes. This provides new research perspectives and theoretical foundations for the biological understanding of the disease, molecular stratification, and the optimization of resistance-related therapeutic strategies. Whether tumor endothelial-derived SERPINE1 spatially orchestrates cisplatin resistance remains largely unknown.

Methods

Single cell data processing

Based on the literature, single-cell data were obtained, and decontX was used to remove environmental contamination [10]. The PercentageFeatureSet function was employed to calculate the percentage of mitochondrial genes, filtering for cells with >200 expressed genes, <20% mitochondrial gene expression, and <1% erythrocyte gene proportion. Subsequently, the merged ScRNA-seq data underwent normalization, followed by batch correction using the harmony algorithm, to cluster cells and identify cell clusters. Batch effects derived from different datasets and sequencing platforms were corrected using the Harmony algorithm, and the effectiveness of correction was assessed by comparing clustering distributions before and after integration using dimensionality reduction methods. Next, we utilized the t-SNE method for visualization. Additionally, the inferCNV package was applied for tumor cell identification, and the Monocle2 package was used for pseudotime analysis of key subpopulations [11]. We also performed observed-to-expected ratio (Ro/e) analysis to evaluate the relative enrichment of cell states across endothelial subpopulations. Ro/e values >1 indicate enrichment and values <1 indicate depletion; more extreme values reflect stronger deviations from the expected distribution. Spatial transcriptomics employed HPV-negative HN48 and HN47, along with HPV-positive HN42 and HN43, as the datasets for subsequent spatial analysis.

In addition, transcriptomic data for HNSCC and pan-cancer with survival information were obtained from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC) database (https://xenabrowser.net/), along with the GSE65858 dataset downloaded from the GEO database for subsequent transcriptomic level validation [12]. All data required for this study can be retrieved from public databases. We used the limma package to calculate the differentially expressed genes between the Tumor and Normal groups, with a selection criterion of p.value < 0.05 [13].

Acquisition and scoring of cisplatin resistance gene set

The cisplatin-resistant gene set was obtained from the HGSOC-Platinum database (http://ptrc-ddr.cptac-data-view.org/). By intersecting this gene set with differentially expressed genes, candidate cisplatin resistance-associated genes were identified. This gene set was subsequently used to quantify cisplatin resistance-related activity in single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data. Based on single-cell data, we employed five scoring methods for single-cell data scoring, namely AUCell, UCell [14], singscore [15], ssGSEA [16], and AddModuleScore [17]. Subsequently, we normalized the data and merged it, ultimately obtaining a Score. Based on the median of the Score, we divided the samples into high and low expression groups for further analysis.

Integrated SCENIC, functional enrichment, and cell–cell interaction analysis

SCENIC17 is utilized for identifying different states of cells in single-cell data and constructing regulatory networks [18]. Based on Python 3.10, the pySCENIC analysis of the regulatory networks of transcription factors (TF) in malignant tumor subgroups was conducted. Enrichment analysis was performed using the RcisTarget database to detect the regulatory networks between transcription start sites (TSS) and genes. Visualization was carried out using the pheatmap package. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis is a commonly used method for conducting large-scale functional enrichment studies, encompassing biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a widely used database that stores information related to genomes, biological pathways, diseases, and drugs [19]. The clusterProfiler R package was utilized for GO annotation analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes, with a critical FDR threshold of p < 0.05 considered statistically significant [20]. We utilized the R package CellChat (V1.6.0) to perform cell communication analysis using single-cell data alongside our cell classifications. The built-in CellChatDB.human database was employed as a reference to analyze the interactions between cells, and we examined the relationships among 32 pathways between these cells [21]. In this study, we utilized the Nichenet21 package to gather protein-protein interaction information and interaction data of regulatory factors from multiple databases. We specifically identified downstream signaling targets with endothelial cells as ligands to validate our findings with CellChat [22].

Spatial transcriptomics and downstream prognostic, immune infiltration, and drug screening analyses

Spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed in R using Seurat (v4.0). Data were normalized with SCTransform, followed by PCA/UMAP-based clustering and visualization with SpatialFeaturePlot. Tumor boundary and core regions were identified using the Cottrazm package in combination with tumor CNV features. 10x Visium data were integrated by deconvolution based on the Sparkle database, and spatial microregions were defined according to cell type composition. Average gene expression across microregions was Z-score normalized and visualized using pheatmap. Gene set activity in each microregion was quantified by AUCell using AUC scores and spatially visualized, and Spearman correlation analysis was applied to evaluate relationships between cell composition and gene set activity, with visualization performed using linkET. We calculated the optimal cutoff value using the survival package based on the expression profile of SERPINE1, and subsequently grouped the data into high and low categories. Following this, we plotted the Kaplan-Meier curves based on these groupings. We then utilized the rms package for calibration analysis and visualization. We utilized the ESTIMATE algorithm based on expression data to evaluate the stromal and immune cell scores in malignant tumor tissues. The immune cell distribution differences between high and low prognosis groups were calculated using four methods from the IOBR package: CIBERSORT, EPIC, MCPCounter, and TIMER. Additionally, the submap algorithm was employed to predict the response level of the prognostic model to immunotherapy [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. We used Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to analyze the activation status of carcinogenic pathways in high-risk patient groups. Expression data for human cancer cell lines (CCLs) were obtained from the Broad Institute Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [30]. The CTRP v.2.0 (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp) and PRISM Repurposing datasets (19Q4; https://depmap.org/portal/prism/) were used to obtain drug sensitivity data for CCLs. Subsequently, we calculated the area under the AUC curve for both high and low groups to further assess drug sensitivity.

In vitro validation of SERPINE1 expression

To validate the expression of SERPINE1 at the cellular level, human keratinocyte cell line (HaCaT) and HNSCC cell line (FaDu) were used. Total RNA was extracted using RNAisoPlus and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial kit (Novoprotein, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix, and relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCT method with GAPDH as an internal control. For protein analysis, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against SERPINE1 and β-tubulin, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using ECL and quantified using ImageJ. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates.

Results

Single-cell landscape of HNSCC reveals tumor-specific cellular reprogramming

We annotated the data and identified a total of 15 cell types, including CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, Cycling T cells, NK cells, B cells, MonoMacs, Endothelial cells, Fibroblasts, Pericytes, DCs, Neutrophils, Mast cells, Plasma cells, and epithelial cells (Fig. 1A). Significant expression differences among various subpopulations were observed in tumor and adjacent normal tissues. For instance, B cells were significantly depleted in tumor tissues, while epithelial cells, mononuclear-macrophages, and Cycling T cells were significantly increased in tumor samples (Fig. 1B). The top 3 markers of each subpopulation and the enrichment of marker genes also validated our cell annotation and subpopulation localization (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, the characteristic markers of single-cell subpopulations were highly expressed in their corresponding subpopulations, confirming the accuracy of our annotations (Fig. 1D). The functional enrichment of single-cell subpopulations was consistent with the annotations, further validating our accuracy (Supplementary Figure 1A). Endothelial cell–associated cisplatin resistance signature in hnscc

By intersecting cisplatin resistance-related genes with differentially expressed genes in HNSCC (logFC > 1 and P < 0.05), we identified candidate cisplatin resistance-associated genes. Univariate Cox regression analysis with bootstrap resampling further identified 23 prognosis-related cisplatin resistance-associated genes (Fig. 2A). These genes are primarily enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion and ECM (Supplementary Figure 1C). At the single-cell level, these genes were predominantly enriched in epithelial and stromal compartments (Fig. 2B). GSVA analysis of TCGA-HNSCC showed that patients with high cisplatin-resistance scores had poorer survival than those with low scores (Fig. 2C). Single-cell scoring further showed relatively high cisplatin resistance-related activity in endothelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 2D). Ro/e analysis demonstrated preferential enrichment of high-score cells in specific stromal populations (Fig. 2E). Stratification by HPV status showed higher resistance scores in HPV-negative endothelial cells, whereas no comparable difference was observed in fibroblasts (Fig. 2F). Finally, resistance scores were higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, enrichment analysis showed that the score of endothelial cells was higher in HPV-negative tumor endothelial cells, suggesting that HPV-negative tumor endothelial cells may be preferentially associated with a cisplatin resistance-related phenotype, while fibroblasts showed no significant differences. High cisplatin-resistance endothelial cells exhibit distinct transcriptional programs and immune–stromal interactions

We further reannotated endothelial cells into transcriptionally distinct endothelial subtypes according to canonical endothelial marker genes and subsequently classified cells into high- and low-resistance-score groups based on the median cisplatin resistance score. Ro/e analysis showed preferential enrichment of high-resistance-score endothelial cells in CapEC and Tip-cell subpopulations, suggesting an association between the cisplatin resistance-related phenotype and angiogenic or microvascular endothelial states (Fig. 3A–B). Moreover, High-resistance-score endothelial cells exhibited higher CytoTRACE scores, consistent with a less differentiated transcriptional state (Fig. 3C). SCENIC analysis revealed that High DR endo significantly expressed pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic transcription factors such as TEAD4, CLOCK, SMAD5, and ILF2. Studies have shown that these transcription factors may be involved in tumor resistance (Fig. 3D). Pseudotime analysis positioned High DR endothelial cells preferentially toward the early region of the inferred trajectory (Fig. 3E), suggesting that the cisplatin resistance-associated endothelial state may emerge relatively early during endothelial state transitions. Further heatmap analysis demonstrated distinct gene-expression patterns between the two inferred states (Fig. 3F). Prognostic significance and immunosuppressive role of SERPINE1 in hnscc

Furthermore, we conducted differential expression analysis based on TCGA-HNSCC, revealing that SERPINE1 is significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues (Fig. 6A). In both TCGA and GSE65858 datasets, patients with high SERPINE1 expression exhibited poorer prognoses, and the AUC curves for 1–3 years also indicated that SERPINE1 has certain prognostic predictive value (Figs. 6B–E), which is corroborated by the Calibration curve (Fig. 6F). Additionally, immune infiltration analysis demonstrated that SERPINE1 is negatively correlated with CD4 Tcm, NK, and mast cells, while showing a positive correlation with the APM signaling pathway (Fig. 6G). Based on the analysis of the expression differences of SERPINE1, the immune cell enrichment in the high expression SERPINE1 group was significantly lower than that in the low expression group (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, the ESTIMATE score indicated that the high expression SERPINE1 group had higher ESTIMATE and Stromal scores, while the tumor purity was relatively lower (Figs. 7B–E), further demonstrating its significant regulatory role in tumor drug resistance. Additionally, pan-cancer immune infiltration analysis showed that, although SERPINE1 was positively correlated with several immune-cell populations in many tumor types, it displayed a negative association with T-cell infiltration in HNSCC (Fig. 7F). This HNSCC-specific correlation pattern may reflect a distinct immune context associated with SERPINE1, although the underlying regulatory mechanism cannot be determined from the present observational analyses. Submap analysis indicated that patients with high expression of SERPINE1 had poorer responses to immunotherapy, with a higher CP/PR ratio (Fig. 7G). CTRP-based drug sensitivity prediction showed lower predicted AUC values for afatinib, canertinib, dasatinib, and gefitinib in the SERPINE1-high group, indicating higher predicted sensitivity to these agents (Fig. 8A). Similarly, PRISM-based analysis showed lower predicted AUC values for cobimetinib and trametinib in the SERPINE1-high group, suggesting higher predicted sensitivity to these MAPK-pathway inhibitors. These drugs primarily target the EGFR/SRC and MAPK signaling pathways, suggesting that tumors with high SERPINE1 expression may be more reliant on these proliferation and invasion-related pathways. Spatial transcriptome localization and correlation analysis

Based on spatial transcriptomics data, we systematically analyzed the spatial distribution characteristics of SERPINE1 in tumors with different HPV statuses and its cellular origins, in conjunction with the vascular-related marker genes VWF and PECAM1 for spatial localization. The results showed that in HPV− tumor samples (HN48, HN47), SERPINE1 exhibited a distinct regional high expression, with its spatial distribution highly overlapping with the vascular structures marked by VWF and PECAM1, indicating that SERPINE1 is primarily enriched in vascular-associated regions (Figs. 9A–B). In contrast, in HPV+ tumor samples (HN42, HN43), the overall expression level of SERPINE1 was lower, and its spatial distribution was more dispersed, with a weakened co-localization trend with the vascular marker genes (Figs. 9C–D). Quantitative analysis revealed that in HPV− samples, the expression of SERPINE1 in malignant and mixed regions was significantly higher than in normal regions, whereas in HPV+ samples, the differences were no longer significant, suggesting that the spatial expression pattern of SERPINE1 may be influenced by the HPV status (Figs. 9A–D, middle bar chart). Cell source analysis indicates that in HPV− samples, SERPINE1 shows a significant spatial correlation with endothelial cells and fibroblasts, while its correlation with epithelial cells is relatively weak. In HPV+ samples, the overall correlation of SERPINE1 with various cell types is diminished, and no clear vascular-related enrichment characteristics are observed (Figs. 9A–D, right side correlation analysis). Experimental validation of SERPINE1 expression in HNSCC cells

To further validate the bioinformatics findings, we examined SERPINE1 expression in vitro using HaCaT (normal epithelial cells) and FaDu (HNSCC cells). Consistent with our computational analysis, both mRNA and protein levels of SERPINE1 were significantly upregulated in FaDu cells compared with HaCaT cells. qPCR analysis demonstrated a marked increase in SERPINE1 transcriptional levels in tumor cells (P < 0.01), while Western blot analysis confirmed elevated protein expression (P < 0.01). These results provide independent experimental support for the aberrant upregulation of SERPINE1 in HNSCC cells. However, these expression-level findings do not establish a direct functional role for SERPINE1 in cisplatin resistance.

Discussion

This study systematically characterizes the cellular composition and spatial distribution features associated with cisplatin resistance in HNSCC by integrating single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and multi-layered bioinformatics analyses. Additionally, it explores the interactions between these cellular characteristics and the tumor microenvironment [31,32]. Through the scoring of cisplatin resistance gene sets, pseudo-temporal analysis, and the construction of cellular communication networks, we found that the signals associated with cisplatin resistance do not primarily originate from tumor epithelial cells, but are significantly enriched in tumor endothelial cells. This characteristic is particularly pronounced in HPV-negative tumors. This result reveals the potential core role of the vascular-related microenvironment in the formation of cisplatin resistance in HNSCC from both single-cell and spatial perspectives, providing a new insight into the cellular basis of resistance. Cisplatin resistance is a critical clinical challenge that limits the efficacy of treatment for HNSCC. The mechanisms underlying this resistance exhibit significant multi-layered and heterogeneous characteristics [33]. Previous studies primarily explain the phenomenon from the intrinsic mechanisms of tumor cells, including enhanced DNA damage repair capacity, upregulation of drug efflux transporters, inhibition of apoptosis pathways, and metabolic reprogramming. However, these mechanisms fail to adequately account for the significant differences in responses to cisplatin among different patients and tumor regions in clinical settings. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have indicated that the tumor microenvironment plays a crucial regulatory role in the development of chemotherapy resistance by modulating vascular structure, oxygen supply status, extracellular matrix barriers, and immune cell functions. This suggests that resistance is not merely an autonomous event of tumor cells, but rather the result of multicellular cooperation [34]. The results of this study indicate that SERPINE1 does not function as a universally upregulated intrinsic resistance molecule in tumor cells, but rather specifically marks a subset of tumor endothelial cell states associated with cisplatin resistance. Although SERPINE1 has been reported to be related to aggressiveness and poor prognosis in various solid tumors, its function is often broadly attributed to the tumor cells themselves. This study further refines this understanding through single-cell resolution analysis, demonstrating that the primary expression source of SERPINE1 is tumor-associated endothelial cells, which exhibit a spatial distribution closely surrounding vascular structures, particularly pronounced in HPV-negative HNSCC. This finding suggests that the resistance effects associated with SERPINE1 may more accurately reflect changes in the vascular microenvironment rather than direct activation of acquired resistance programs in tumor cells. Further integrating the results of pathway activity and cell communication analysis, the high expression of SERPINE1 in endothelial cells may represent a functionally reprogrammed vascular substate characterized by enhanced extracellular matrix remodeling, abnormal vascular structure, and the synergistic activation of multiple pro-survival signaling pathways [32,35]. In this context, cisplatin resistance may not stem directly from the tumor cells' escape from drug-induced damage, but rather from the restricted drug delivery and the formation of a localized protective microenvironment caused by abnormal vasculature and stroma. The negative association between SERPINE1 and T-cell infiltration observed specifically in HNSCC may therefore reflect an endothelial–stromal state characterized by hypoxia, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune exclusion. However, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating, and the tumor-type-specific mechanism requires further experimental investigation. The drug resistance regulatory model centered on endothelial cells provides a reasonable explanation for the more stubborn cisplatin resistance phenomenon in HPV-negative tumors. It also suggests that the differences in treatment responses under different HPV statuses may partially stem from the functional differences in the vascular microenvironment [36]. Certainly, this study has several limitations. First, the present findings are primarily based on public datasets and computational analyses. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses are inherently observational and therefore cannot establish a causal relationship between SERPINE1 expression and cisplatin resistance. Although the multi-omics results are concordant across several analytical levels, endothelial-specific perturbation experiments, including SERPINE1 knockdown or overexpression combined with cisplatin-response assays and endothelial–tumor co-culture models, will be required to establish causality [37].

Future studies integrating functional genomics approaches, such as CRISPR-based perturbation screening and perturbation-resolved single-cell profiling, may further identify upstream regulators and downstream effectors of SERPINE1 and strengthen causal inference within the cisplatin resistance-associated endothelial program [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms underlying the upstream regulatory network of SERPINE1 under different HPV statuses and its relationship with alterations in vascular function remain to be thoroughly elucidated. Future studies could integrate functional experiments and clinical samples to further assess the clinical translational value of SERPINE1 as a predictive biomarker for drug resistance and a potential therapeutic target.

Conclusion

This study identifies endothelial-specific enrichment of SERPINE1 as a key feature associated with a cisplatin resistance-related phenotype in HNSCC, particularly in HPV-negative tumors. These findings highlight a vascular microenvironment–driven resistance pattern and provide a rationale for future validation of endothelial-targeted therapeutic strategies.
