Beyond petrochemicals: challenges and opportunities in industrial-scale biomanufacturing
Authors: Ji Yeon Kim, Hye Eun Yu, Min Ho Kim, Sang Yup Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biomanufacturing offers a sustainable alternative to petrochemical manufacturing by harnessing engineered microorganisms to convert renewable feedstocks into diverse value-added products. Systems metabolic engineering has driven its advancement, enabling rational design of microbial strains with enhanced performance and robustness. Despite these achievements, large-scale commercialisation remains limited, highlighting the persistent gap between laboratory success and industrial deployment. We examine the challenges and opportunities in advancing biomanufacturing towards industrial implementation through two cases, succinic acid and polyhydroxyalkanoates, that exemplify products nearing, yet not fully reaching, commercial maturity. Finally, we discuss how integrating artificial intelligence with systems metabolic engineering can accelerate sustainable biomanufacturing.

Introduction

Over the past century, the chemical industry has propelled global progress by transforming fossil resources into fuels, chemicals, and materials that underpin modern society1. Yet the continued dependence on non-renewable resources has intensified global sustainability challenges by escalating environmental burdens, accelerating climate change, and depleting finite fossil resources1. These challenges underscore the need for a paradigm shift towards sustainable and resource-efficient manufacturing2.

Biomanufacturing, which harnesses whole biological systems or their components to convert renewable feedstocks into diverse value-added products, has emerged as a sustainable alternative to conventional petrochemical production2. With advances in systems metabolic engineering, microbial cell factories can be rationally designed to produce various chemicals and materials from non-edible biomass or even from CO23,4. Systems metabolic engineering provides a holistic framework that incorporates process-level constraints into the design of cellular metabolism through system-oriented strategies by integrating the principles of metabolic engineering, systems biology, synthetic biology, and evolutionary engineering5. At the upstream stage, microbial chassis are systematically designed and optimised through a comprehensive understanding of cellular physiology and identification of metabolic bottlenecks, allowing precise control of metabolic flux distribution, redox balance, and cofactor regeneration via targeted gene modification or synthetic pathway construction3,5,6. At the midstream stage, fermentation processes using microbial strains are scaled across bioreactors of varying sizes to ensure production performance and strain robustness under industrially relevant conditions3,5,6. At the downstream stage, products are efficiently recovered and purified from the fermentation broth by tailoring the physicochemical properties of intermediates to facilitate separation, or by integrating in situ product removal with fermentation3,5. Driven by rapid advances in systems metabolic engineering, biomanufacturing has moved beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations towards industrial implementation, thereby establishing itself as a cornerstone of the emerging circular and carbon-neutral chemical economy2,6,7. Several representative examples, including the bio-based production of 1,4-butanediol (BDO), poly(lactic acid) (PLA), amino acids, and gas fermentation-derived products, demonstrate successful industrial-scale deployment of biomanufacturing (Box 1)8–13.

Despite these successes, such achievements remain confined to a limited range of products8. This pervasive failure, where many projects ultimately fail to cross the “valley of death” in biomanufacturing (Fig. 1), underscores that sustained industrial-scale implementation continues to face formidable barriers2,6. Even with substantial advances in systems metabolic engineering, the development of microbial cell factories capable of maintaining stable production performance under industrially relevant conditions remains unpredictable3,5,6. Economic competitiveness further constrains commercialisation. Most commodity chemicals produced via mature petrochemical routes are sold at low market prices, typically around $1-5 kg−1, making it difficult for bio-based counterparts to compete2,8. Moreover, the capital costs associated with constructing new biomanufacturing facilities are considerably higher than those of conventional refineries, many of which are fully depreciated and continue to benefit from economies of scale14. Even when technical feasibility is demonstrated, investment in new bio-based production plants is often hindered by uncertain market growth and strong dependence on volatile oil prices, both of which complicate long-term financial forecasting and impede large-scale deployment8. These supply-side constraints are further compounded by demand-side dynamics. Although consumers often express a willingness to pay a green premium for bio-based products over petrochemical counterparts, this premium remains limited in magnitude and depends on third-party certification15. Beyond economic competitiveness and consumer demand considerations, supply chain resilience has emerged as a critical driver for biomanufacturing. Petrochemical supply chains remain heavily concentrated in a few key regions, including Germany, China, and the Middle East, exposing producers to geopolitical disruptions and price volatility16. Recent disruptions around the Strait of Hormuz, a critical artery for global oil transport, have further underscored the vulnerability of petroleum-dependent supply chains and their far-reaching economic consequences. In contrast, biomanufacturing can be deployed closer to feedstock sources, enabling shorter and more circular supply chains that reduce dependence on oil, monoculture agriculture, or large land footprints17,18. In response, several national strategies, including those of the United States19, the European Union20, and China21, have explicitly framed biomanufacturing as a means of strengthening industrial sovereignty and supply chain security alongside sustainability goals16.

Collectively, these economic, demand-side, and supply chain considerations explain why translating laboratory-scale success into industrial reality remains challenging for most bio-based processes. To further illustrate both the challenges and opportunities in advancing biomanufacturing, we highlight two representative cases (Fig. 2), microbial production of succinic acid (SA) and polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs), both of which have approached, but not yet achieved, successful full-scale commercialisation. The two cases present distinct challenges across the biomanufacturing workflow, including feedstock utilisation, strain performance, fermentation, downstream processing, and market adoption, enabling a multifaceted examination of barriers that are broadly representative of bio-based products. The industrial feasibility of biomanufacturing has been demonstrated through several landmark cases. One representative example is the bio-based production of 1,4-butanediol (BDO), an important precursor for plastics, elastic fibres, and polyurethanes8. Because BDO is a non-native metabolite, its production required the de novo construction of a biosynthetic pathway converting the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediate succinyl-CoA into BDO through multiple heterologous enzymatic steps9. Industrial deployment was achieved through the integration of metabolic modeling, high-throughput pathway and strain construction, as well as fermentation and downstream process development, demonstrating the commercial scalability of non-natural commodity chemicals9. This integrated platform was subsequently extended to the biosynthesis of 1,3-butanediol, a key ingredient in the fragrance and personal-care sectors, and caprolactam, the monomer of nylon-68.

Another notable case is the bio-based production of poly(lactic acid) (PLA), which represents one of the first fully integrated biomanufacturing value chains for polymers10. In this case, lactic acid is produced at industrial scale via microbial fermentation of sugar-based feedstocks, followed by purification, catalytic conversion to lactide, and solvent-free ring-opening polymerisation to yield high-molecular-weight PLA10. Continuous improvements in fermentation performance and downstream processing, including the adoption of low-pH lactic acid fermentation to substantially reduce lime consumption and gypsum byproduct formation, have enabled large-scale and resource-efficient production10. This integrated fermentation-conversion-polymerisation process has supported the development of a broad portfolio of application-specific PLA grades, spanning packaging, fibres, textiles, and durable consumer products10.

Amino acids constitute the most mature and economically robust class of biomanufactured products, with several compounds, including l-glutamate, l-lysine, and l-threonine, produced at the million-tonne scale11,12. Their industrial success is rooted in decades of metabolic engineering to relieve feedback inhibition, enhance precursor supply, and optimise redox and energy metabolism, together with the development of large-scale fermentation processes11,12. This platform has extended beyond bulk amino acids to encompass specialised non-proteinogenic amino acids, reinforcing amino acid fermentation as a cornerstone of industrial biotechnology11.

In contrast to the predominantly sugar-based platforms described above, gas fermentation represents a distinct industrial biomanufacturing paradigm in which microorganisms convert industrial waste gases containing CO, CO2 and H2 into fuels and chemicals, thereby directly valorising carbon streams inaccessible to conventional petrochemical or sugar-based processes13. Successful industrialisation of gas fermentation has required the co-development of specialised microbial metabolism and advanced gas–liquid mass transfer reactor technologies, highlighting how biomanufacturing can move beyond feedstock substitution towards fundamentally new value chains based on waste carbon utilisation13.

Case study I. SA

Challenges in commercialising SA

Despite advances in microbial production of SA (Box 2), a four-carbon dicarboxylic acid that serves as a versatile platform chemical, commercialisation of bio-based SA has been constrained by interconnected bottlenecks across feedstock, strain performance, fermentation, downstream recovery, and overall process economics, which collectively create a cost gap with petrochemical-based SA (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Despite extensive efforts to optimise microbial strains through systems metabolic engineering, improvements in titre, rate, and yield (TRY) are often not sustained upon scale-up (Fig. 2c)2. The transition from laboratory-scale to industrial-scale bioreactors alters hydrodynamic and mass transfer characteristics, generating nutrient and oxygen gradients (in aerobic fermentations) that disrupt metabolic homogeneity and impair cell growth6. Larger characteristic length scales for substrate and gas transfer further exacerbate imbalances between consumption and transport, leading to spatiotemporal heterogeneity and reduced production efficiency22. Oxygen limitation additionally induces transcriptional reprogramming, activating stress-response pathways that increase ATP demand and divert carbon flux away from SA biosynthesis22. Prolonged exposure to such stresses also elevates mutation rates, generating phenotypic heterogeneity that compromises strain stability and long-term production performance23.

Beyond these metabolic constraints, fermentation process configuration itself further shapes industrial feasibility. Selection of the operating mode must be aligned with the metabolic characteristics of the production strain and the overall process design (Fig. 2c). Fed-batch fermentation remains the predominant strategy for SA production owing to its operational robustness and relative simplicity; however, continuous fermentation has attracted interest as a means to enhance volumetric productivity and facilitate integration with downstream recovery24. In particular, continuous systems employing cell-recycle bioreactors integrated with membrane separation have been demonstrated for A. succinogenes25 and M. succiniciproducens26. Nevertheless, their implementation requires advanced process control and complex equipment and control architectures, complicating large-scale deployment24. For aerobic or facultative hosts, dual-phase fermentation strategies have also been explored, in which biomass is first accumulated under aerobic conditions, followed by a metabolic shift to anaerobic conditions to drive SA production24. Although such two-stage processes can enhance production performance, precise regulation of the phase transition is critical, as flux redistribution depends on coordinated enzyme activities across central carbon metabolism, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle27.

Physicochemical constraints further intensify these metabolic and operational challenges. Maintaining optimal pH requires continuous addition of neutralising agents such as ammonia, magnesium hydroxide, or sodium hydroxide28. Accumulated SA itself also imposes stress, further suppressing metabolic activity29. Moreover, the use of low-cost or crude feedstocks to improve process economics introduces inhibitory byproducts, including acetic acid and phenolic compounds, which further reduce TRY and strain robustness30. The severity of these inhibitory effects varies with biomass type, creating feedstock-dependent trade-offs between economic competitiveness and process complexity31.

These feedstock-dependent trade-offs are quantitatively reflected in techno-economic benchmarking analyses31. Corn stover-derived SA demonstrated the most favourable baseline economics among the evaluated feedstocks (corn stover, glycerol, and seaweed), achieving the lowest minimum product-selling price (MPSP; $1.60 kg−1) and the highest net present value (NPV; $57.2 million)31. However, its high lignin content necessitated energy-intensive acid thermal hydrolysis pretreatment, increasing capital expenditure ($71 million) and operational complexity31. In contrast, glycerol required no costly pretreatment and entailed lower capital investment ($34–44 million), resulting in enhanced process robustness and reduced economic risk (5%)31. Here, economic risk refers to the probability of incurring a loss at a market selling price of $2 kg−1, estimated through Monte Carlo simulation31. Seaweed, despite its potential as a non-terrestrial biomass resource, exhibited low conversion efficiency and high upstream costs, leading to a higher MPSP ($1.90 kg−1) and substantial economic risk (85%)31. Collectively, these results highlight how feedstock choice fundamentally governs the trade-offs among cost efficiency, process robustness, and investment stability in large-scale SA biomanufacturing.

In parallel with upstream and fermentation constraints, downstream processing represents a major barrier, as it directly determines cost competitiveness and scalability. The complex composition of fermentation broths, together with high purity requirements for market entry, increases both energy consumption and waste generation32,33. Although a variety of separation technologies have been developed to enhance recovery efficiency (Fig. 2d), each entails distinct trade-offs among purity, cost, and operational stability33. Extraction-based processes can achieve higher yields with relatively lower energy input but require precise pH control, pretreatment of fermentation broth, and costly reagents34,35. Membrane-based systems offer high selectivity and modular scalability with comparatively low energy demand but are susceptible to membrane fouling and limited operational lifetimes. Ion-exchange chromatography can improve SA purity by removing ionic impurities with relatively low direct energy consumption, but it exhibits limited selectivity and recovery efficiency and generates secondary wastewater streams during resin regeneration, increasing disposal burdens and process hazard potential36. Consequently, despite notable technological advances, integrated assessments that jointly evaluate downstream efficiency, environmental burden, and economic feasibility remain limited, constraining the industrial translation of bio-based SA33,37.

Beyond these technical limitations, economic viability continues to represent the dominant barrier to SA biomanufacturing, owing to limited market demand and weak cost competitiveness relative to petroleum-derived chemicals38. The collapse of BioAmber’s manufacturing plant exemplified these challenges, as the venture was terminated when market expansion and price stability fell short of expectations38. Although the potential market size had been projected to exceed $10 billion, the actual global SA market in 2017 was only $279 million38. When most SA biomanufacturing plants were conceived in 2013, crude oil prices averaged $108.56 per barrel; however, by 2016, when large-scale operations commenced, prices had fallen to $43.64 per barrel36, substantially eroding competitiveness relative to petrochemical routes39. This commercial failure occurred despite substantial public and private support; BioAmber’s Sarnia plant had received financing from Canadian federal and provincial agencies as well as interest-free and commercial loans, but these financial supports could not offset high production costs and underutilised capacity38. It should be noted that this failure also reflected supply-chain vulnerabilities, including exposure to corn-sugar feedstock price fluctuations and heavy dependence on a single customer, with 71% and 58% of SA sales concentrated in one buyer in 2016 and 2017, respectively38. As a result, Myriant’s bio-based SA facility also ceased operation in 2016 and was later acquired by Stepan Company for conversion to bio-based surfactant production in 202140, while Succinity’s plant was reportedly inactive by 2019 due to insufficient market adoption41. The pronounced price sensitivity of the SA market further complicates accurate techno-economic forecasting38. Variability in co-product valuation, product pricing, and feedstock costs amplifies financial uncertainty, often resulting in negative NPVs in which returns fail to offset capital and operational expenditures38. Taken together, these techno-economic constraints indicate that, without simultaneous improvements in process efficiency and market expansion, large-scale commercialisation of bio-based SA remains economically unviable.

SA, a four-carbon dicarboxylic acid, is a versatile platform chemical that serves as a key precursor for a wide range of value-added products, including BDO, γ-butyrolactone, tetrahydrofuran, and bio-based polymers such as nylon and polybutylene succinate4,137. Its fermentative production requires carboxylation of phosphoenolpyruvate or pyruvate to generate a four-carbon intermediate that is subsequently converted to SA, thereby directly fixing one molecule of CO2 per molecule of SA produced138. Owing to its broad applicability and carbon-negative potential, SA has been widely recognised as a promising chemical driving the transition towards sustainable biomanufacturing.

Various microorganisms, including Actinobacillus succinogenes, Corynebacterium glutamicum, Escherichia coli, Mannheimia succiniciproducens, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Yarrowia lipolytica have been metabolically engineered for bio-based SA production (Fig. 2b)137,138. Natural high SA producers such as A. succinogenes and M. succiniciproducens inherently direct most of their carbon flux towards SA via the reductive TCA cycle139, whereas other bacteria including C. glutamicum and E. coli offer advantages in genetic tractability and established bioprocesses139. Yeast platforms, in turn, exhibit higher tolerance to acidic conditions, enabling low-pH fermentations that reduce neutraliser use and simplify downstream processing139. Depending on the host metabolism and cultivation mode (aerobic or anaerobic), systems metabolic engineering strategies, such as redox balancing, enhancement of CO2 utilisation, and reinforcement of strain robustness, have been applied to improve production performance under industrially relevant conditions45,140,141. For instance, a hyper-SA-producing strain of M. succiniciproducens was constructed by reconstructing the Mg2+ ion transport system through introduction of an efficient Mg2+ ion transporter from Salmonella enterica, producing 152.23 g L−1 of SA with a maximum productivity of 39.6 g L−1 h−1 through high-inoculum fed-batch fermentation137.

Despite substantial progress in strain development, downstream processing remains a principal cost-determining step in SA biomanufacturing. SA purification typically involves three major steps: removal of cells, separation of SA from culture broth containing other organic acids, and purification often involving crystallisation32 (Fig. 2d). Achieving the high SA recovery (>90%) and purity (>99.5%) required for commercial deployment remains challenging due to the presence of biomass and structurally similar organic acids in fermentation broth33,37. To address these challenges, a range of downstream processing strategies, including adsorption, electrodialysis, direct crystallisation, and reactive extraction, have been explored to improve SA recovery and purity32,33,37,138 (Fig. 2d).

As advances in strain optimisation and downstream processing strengthened the industrial feasibility of SA biomanufacturing, several companies initiated industrial-scale operations to bridge the gap between laboratory research and market implementation (Supplementary Table 1). Reverdia, a joint venture between DSM and Roquette, implemented a low-pH yeast fermentation process in 2012; following dissolution of the joint venture in 2019, the plant has been operated solely by Roquette under licence from DSM54,57. The following year, Myriant launched North America’s first commercial-scale bio-based SA plant142, while Succinity, a collaboration between BASF and Corbion, adopted Basfia succiniciproducens as the production host143, a strain closely related to M. succiniciproducens144. Building on this momentum, BioAmber, in partnership with Mitsui Chemicals, commissioned a 30 kt yr−1 facility in 2014, representing the largest reported capacity for bio-based SA production38,143. Although these industrial initiatives demonstrated the technological maturity of SA biomanufacturing, market penetration was ultimately limited by competition with petroleum-derived products, underscoring the need for continued advances in strain engineering and process optimisation to achieve true industrial feasibility.

Opportunities for commercialising SA

Overcoming the techno-economic and process barriers to commercialisation of bio-based SA requires a comprehensive strategy that integrates raw material selection, strain development, fermentation processes, and downstream processing to achieve industrial feasibility (Supplementary Table 2). Techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) have become indispensable tools for quantitatively assessing both profitability and environmental performance prior to industrial deployment (Fig. 2f)42. TEA provides a predictive framework that integrates capital and operating expenditures with feedstock variability and market volatility, enabling a rational assessment of production economics43. For example, sugarcane-based SA production coupled with autonomous distillation achieved an estimated cost of $2.32 kg−1 and a breakeven price of $2.92 kg−1, corresponding to an internal rate of return of 12%44. Similarly, bio-based SA production using engineered Issatchenkia orientalis with sugarcane juice as the carbon source achieved an MPSP of $1.37 kg−1 at pilot scale, lower than the reported market range of $2.53–2.89 kg−1 (adjusted to 2016). Sensitivity analysis identified SA yield, feedstock cost, operating time, and plant capacity as key determinants of economic feasibility45, demonstrating how TEA can guide rational decision-making for cost reduction and scale-up optimisation.

While individual case studies demonstrate the feasibility of cost reduction under specific configurations, a more systematic interpretation of TEA outcomes reveals the structural determinants of economic competitiveness. In particular, the cost competitiveness of bio-based SA is governed by the relative distribution of direct fixed capital investment (CAPEX) and annual operating expenditure (OPEX) across pretreatment, fermentation, and downstream purification rather than by optimisation of any single unit operation. Petroleum-based SA is typically benchmarked at approximately $2.0 kg−1, whereas reported MPSP for industrial bio-based configurations range from $2.3–3.2 kg−146,47. In lignocellulose hydrolysate-based routes, reduced feedstock costs lower the raw material contribution to OPEX but increase CAPEX due to pretreatment and separation infrastructure47. In purification schemes dominated by crystallisation and evaporation, downstream processing alone can account for 60–70% of total operating expenses36. By contrast, process-intensified platforms integrating immobilised biocatalysts with in situ electrolytic extraction can simultaneously mitigate separation burdens and capital requirements, enabling MPSP of approximately $1.4 kg−147. These findings indicate that economically competitive SA production emerges from system-level coordination of cost structures across unit operations, rather than from incremental optimisation of fermentation metrics alone.

Complementary to TEA, LCA quantifies the environmental benefits of process design by accounting for fossil energy use, greenhouse gas emissions, and water consumption48. For example, replacing purified glycerol with crude glycerol reduced the global warming potential (kg CO2-eq kg−1 SA) by 47% while maintaining comparable yields, illustrating how feedstock selection can simultaneously improve carbon efficiency and economic viability49. Similarly, LCA-based modeling of corn stover-derived biorefineries revealed that directing all C5 sugars towards SA biosynthesis yields a lower environmental cost ($1.48 kg−1) than fossil-based routes ($2.24 kg−1)42. Integrating insights from TEA and LCA thus enables holistic optimisation of process economics and environmental performance, providing quantitative evidence to guide investment and policy decisions in biomanufacturing.

Building on these quantitative evaluations, the outcomes of TEA and LCA provide performance benchmarks that define design criteria for industrially relevant strains. Guided by these benchmarks, computational tools can expand the metabolic design space for microbial SA production. For example, SSDesign, an algorithmic framework integrating elementary mode analysis with flux modeling, identified optimal gene knockout combinations that enhance carbon flux towards SA, increasing yield to nearly twice that of the wild type50. Dynamic systems modeling of A. succinogenes further revealed that intracellular ATP/ADP balance governs optimal SA production, elucidating energetic constraints that limit carbon flux through the reductive TCA cycle29. In parallel, AI-guided enzyme engineering can accelerate catalytic improvement in key reactions. Integration of protein language models (e.g., ESM-2), DLKcat-based catalytic activity prediction, AlphaFold2 structural modeling, and molecular docking simulation enabled the rational redesign of fumarate hydratase with enhanced substrate specificity and turnover for SA biosynthesis51. Collectively, these advances highlight how coupling AI with systems metabolic engineering can help overcome cellular constraints and achieve production metrics compatible with industrial-scale production.

Beyond upstream optimisation, diversification of downstream valorisation routes could further strengthen the commercial potential of bio-based SA. Industrial demonstrations by BioAmber, Myriant, Roquette (formerly Reverdia), and Succinity established catalytic upgrading pathways from SA to BDO, demonstrating the feasibility of integrating bio-based SA into existing petrochemical value chains52,53. Techno-economic projections estimated an MPSP of approximately $1.82 kg−1 for bio-based BDO, below the petrochemical benchmark of $2.50 kg−153, underscoring its potential cost competitiveness. Continued advances in solvent recovery, catalyst stability, and co-product valorisation are expected to further improve process robustness and profitability, positioning SA as a pivotal biorefinery intermediate bridging renewable feedstocks with established chemical production systems54–56.

Complementary to these process- and product-level strategies, strategic positioning in specialty markets provides an alternative route to commercial viability by circumventing direct price competition with petrochemical counterparts. This strategy is exemplified by Roquette (which acquired Reverdia, formerly a joint venture between DSM and Roquette), which has sustained bio-based SA production by supplying high-purity, certified SA to pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food applications rather than competing in the bulk commodity market57. By aligning product quality with the stringent standards of these high-value sectors, Roquette has effectively mitigated the cost disadvantage inherent to bio-based production of bulk chemicals. Such specialty market positioning effectively mitigates the cost disadvantage inherent to bio-based production and provides a durable route to commercialisation.

Case study II: PHAs

Challenges in commercialising PHAs

Despite advances in microbial PHA production (Box 3), a structurally diverse family of biodegradable polyesters spanning short-chain-length (SCL), medium-chain-length (MCL), and SCL-MCL classes, the transition to commodity-scale markets remains constrained by coupled economic, process, and material-performance limitations58 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Cost competitiveness remains the primary bottleneck59. Current TEA studies estimate PHA production costs at approximately $4–6 kg−1, substantially exceeding the $1–2 kg−1 benchmark typical of commodity plastics60. These costs can be broadly decomposed into OPEX, dominated by feedstock (40–50% of total production costs), fermentation (30–40%), and downstream processing (10–20%), alongside CAPEX (10–15%), primarily associated with fermentation and separation infrastructure60,61.

Within this framework, feedstock selection fundamentally governs process economics62. Most industrial operations still rely on refined sugars and plant oils that compete with food supply and remain sensitive to agricultural market fluctuations, exposing production economics to raw material price volatility and feedstock supply disruptions60. Although alternative feedstocks, including lignocellulosic hydrolysates, agricultural residues, food-waste hydrolysates, and C1 substrates (e.g., CO2, CO, formate, and methanol), have been investigated60,63, stable industrial implementation remains limited. TEA further demonstrates the strong influence of feedstock on overall process viability. For instance, a modular PHA production system processing approximately 450 MT day−1 of mixed waste feedstocks (food waste, fats, oils, and grease, and glycerol waste) reported an MPSP of approximately $3.64 kg−1 with a fixed capital investment of about $110 million, even under assumptions of low-cost or negatively priced substrates64. Sensitivity analysis identified feedstock cost, solvent use, and PHA yield as the dominant economic drivers64, indicating that reductions in substrate cost alone are insufficient to achieve commodity-level competitiveness without concurrent improvements in midstream and downstream processing.

These feedstock-related limitations arise from distinct yet convergent constraints: waste-derived substrates exhibit compositional heterogeneity and require costly pretreatment65, whereas C1-based assimilation remains kinetically inefficient and energy-intensive due to redox and energetic imbalances66. Consequently, upstream constraints propagate into intrinsic fermentation limitations, where ceilings in TRY elevate unit production costs and restrict economies of scale comparable to petrochemical polymer manufacturing59,67.

Studies on pilot-scale PHA production further indicate that achieving industrially relevant production performance remains challenging68. Although laboratory-scale bioprocesses can reach high cell densities and PHA titres, scale-up frequently leads to reductions in oxygen transfer efficiency and production performance, resulting in lower volumetric productivity and longer reactor residence times (Fig. 2c)68. Reported pilot-scale fermentations span volumes from 30 L to 5000 L, yet many studies exhibit reduced biomass concentrations and intracellular polymer accumulation compared with laboratory benchmarks, highlighting scale-dependent mass-transfer constraints as a key commercialisation barrier68. Because fermentation performance can directly determine reactor sizing and capital intensity, insufficient TRY performance propagates through the overall economic structure of PHA production, limiting the achievable economies of scale68.

Downstream processing further amplifies structural cost pressures. As PHAs are accumulated intracellularly, their recovery requires multi-step processing including biomass separation, cell disruption, polymer purification, and drying69 (Fig. 2e). Intracellular PHA content (wt% of cell dry mass) critically determines the overall recovery and purification costs; lower polymer content increases solids handling, solvent and utility demand, and wastewater generation, thereby elevating recovery costs62. The distribution of this cost burden varies with the extraction strategy70 (Fig. 2e). Solvent extraction can achieve high purity but concentrates expenditure in solvent make-up and recovery energy, whereas solvent-free approaches, such as alkaline digestion or enzymatic biomass digestion, reduce solvent usage at the expense of increased wastewater burdens and operational complexity71. At the strain level, morphology engineering provides a complementary route to alleviate downstream burdens. For example, co-expression of the E. coli ftsZ gene with the Alcaligenes eutrophus (currently Cupriavidus necator) PHA biosynthesis genes was previously shown to suppress cell filamentation during PHA production, enhancing PHB accumulation by more than two-fold in a defined medium72. Following such approaches, there were several attempts to engineer cell morphologies for enhanced PHA production. In Halomonas bluephagenesis, engineering of cell shape- and division-related genes such as mreB and minCD leads to enlarged cell size and rapid gravity settling, simplifying biomass separation73. Deletion of exopolysaccharide biosynthesis further induces self-flocculation and self-lysis, reducing lysozyme and solvent demand in downstream recovery74. Despite these strain-level advances, LCA indicates that solvent consumption and energy inputs, particularly electricity and steam associated with extraction and drying, remain dominant contributors to environmental impacts, even when low-cost waste feedstocks are used64. Consequently, downstream processing can offset sustainability gains achieved through inexpensive substrates or waste diversion, reinforcing its role as a critical bottleneck in economically and environmentally viable PHA production.

Even if process economics are improved, the intrinsic material characteristics of PHAs impose additional barriers to their substitution for commodity plastics75. The archetypal homopolymer, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (P(3HB)), exhibits high crystallinity (approximately 60–80%) and a narrow thermal processing window between melting (170 °C) and decomposition (200 °C)69. This limited thermal stability induces chain scission and molecular weight loss during melt extrusion, resulting in deteriorated material properties, increased brittleness upon ageing, and reproducibility challenges at the industrial scale. Copolymerisation strategies, including poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (P(3HB-co-3HV)), poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-4-hydroxybutyrate) (P(3HB-co-4HB)), and poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyhexanoate) (P(3HB-co-3HHx)), broaden mechanical tunability and enhance flexibility69 but are often accompanied by slow crystallisation kinetics and structural heterogeneity76. Consequently, PHAs are generally not suitable as direct “drop-in” replacements, necessitating precise copolymer tailoring and formulation to achieve standardised material grades with reproducible mechanical performance comparable to polyethylene and polypropylene69.

These economic and material constraints ultimately converge in the marketplace, where the global PHA industry remains largely confined to policy-driven or premium sectors capable of tolerating higher costs60. Regulatory restrictions on single-use petroleum-based plastics, together with financial incentives and public research funding, have created market conditions under which higher-cost bio-based alternatives can compete77. For example, in China, national restrictions on single-use plastics, combined with active promotion of biodegradable alternatives, have driven substantial expansion of PHA production capacity. PhaBuilder has secured state-affiliated investment alongside private capital to enable large-scale plant construction78. However, even with such momentum, leading PHA producers continue to face diverse commercial challenges60. Danimer Scientific, once regarded as a frontrunner in P(3HB-co-MCL monomers) commercialisation, filed for bankruptcy amid low plant utilisation, liquidity shortages, and dependence on costly plant-oil feedstocks, illustrating the fragility of cost-driven bioplastic ventures79. Kaneka Corporation, a pioneer of P(3HB-co-3HHx), achieved marine-biodegradable certification and global brand recognition; however, its reliance on plant oils and inconsistent 3HHx incorporation continues to introduce cost and quality variability80. CJ Biomaterials, which acquired Metabolix, developed amorphous and P(3HB-co-4HB) variants with improved softness and thermal stability, yet precise monomer-ratio control and extensive compounding increase manufacturing complexity and cost81. Even Bluepha, employing Halomonas extremophiles for open, non-sterile fermentation, continues to face challenges related to polymer consistency and mechanical robustness that hinder further scale-up58. Collectively, these cases demonstrate that technological feasibility alone cannot ensure commercial success unless advances in process efficiency, material performance, and market adoption progress in parallel.

Plastics have become indispensable to modern society. However, their petroleum-based production and post-use accumulation have imposed a persistent environmental burden, leading to the accumulation of plastic waste in the environment and contributing approximately 4.5% of global greenhouse gas emissions82. In response, bioplastics have emerged as sustainable alternatives with the potential to reduce both plastic pollution and carbon footprints59. Nevertheless, currently commercialised bioplastics, such as polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT) and PLA, exhibit intrinsic limitations. PBAT remains a petroleum-derived copolymer whose biodegradability largely depends on industrial composting, whereas PLA, although plant-based, typically requires specific industrial conditions for complete degradation108.

PHAs, a class of microbial and biodegradable polyesters, have emerged as one of the most promising alternatives75. Because PHAs contain hydrolysable ester linkages and possess molecular structures naturally recognised by a wide range of PHA depolymerases, they can be completely biodegraded under diverse natural conditions, including soil and marine environments59. PHAs are naturally synthesised by numerous microorganisms75, among which Cupriavidus necator and Pseudomonas putida are the most extensively used platform strains, typically accumulating PHAs under nutrient-limited conditions in the presence of excess carbon sources (Fig. 2b)145. PHAs comprise a structurally diverse family, including short-chain-length (SCL) PHAs such as poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (P(3HB)), medium-chain-length (MCL) PHAs such as poly(3-hydroxyoctanoate) (P(3HO)), and copolymers such as poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (P(3HB-co-3HV)) and poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-4-hydroxybutyrate) (P(3HB-co-4HB))75. This structural diversity enables tunable material properties, ranging from rigid thermoplastics to flexible elastomers, which can be precisely controlled through monomer composition and fraction, thereby supporting applications across packaging, agriculture, and biomedical sectors146.

Commercialisation efforts have primarily focused on five representative PHA types, each reflecting distinct material properties58 (Supplementary Table 1). These include P(3HB), which is highly crystalline but prone to brittleness upon ageing59; P(3HB-co-3HV), in which incorporation of 3HV units reduces the crystallinity of P(3HB) and improves flexibility (e.g., originally developed by Imperial Chemical Industries (ICI) and later by several companies, including Metabolix, Biomer, and TianAn Biopolymer)75; P(3HB-co-4HB), in which the introduction of 4HB significantly enhances ductility (e.g., Tianjin GreenBio Materials Co. and CJ Biomaterials)75; poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyhexanoate) (P(3HB-co-3HHx)), commercialised by Kaneka Corporation, and poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-MCL-hydroxyalkanoates), originally developed by Procter & Gamble as Nodax™ and later by Danimer Scientific, which copolymerise MCL monomers with 3-hydroxybutyrate to impart flexible properties75,80; and MCL-PHAs composed of MCL monomers, resulting in highly amorphous, rubber-like materials147.

The trajectory of PHA commercialisation reflects a persistent gap between biological production capabilities and materials-market requirements. Early large-scale initiatives established the feasibility of high intracellular PHA accumulation through microbial fermentation; however, market penetration remained constrained by challenges in achieving both cost competitiveness and adequate material performance58. This gap was evident in early commercialisation efforts by ICI in the 1980s and later revisited through the Metabolix-Archer Daniels Midland (Telles) venture, where technical feasibility did not translate into durable economic viability relative to petrochemical counterparts75. Consequently, industrial development has progressed from maximising polymer titres towards integrated strategies that combine strain engineering, composition control, and the optimisation of fermentation and downstream processes, as exemplified by CJ Biomaterials’ platform, based on Metabolix technologies, achieving high-level PHA accumulation with precisely controlled 3HB/4HB ratios to tailor material properties148.

More recent developments have extended beyond strain-level optimisation to reconfigure manufacturing strategies aimed at alleviating cost and scalability constraints. Halophilic production systems enable non-sterile, open fermentation, thereby reducing sterilisation requirements and the associated capital and operating expenditures (e.g., Bluepha and PhaBuilder)58. In parallel, plant oil-based fermentation processes have expanded feedstock flexibility and enabled the production of copolymers such as P(3HB-co-3HHx) and other P(3HB-co-MCL-hydroxyalkanoates), which exhibit improved thermal processability and certified marine biodegradability (e.g., Kaneka and Danimer Scientific)59,149,150. Furthermore, Newlight Technologies has introduced a commercial gas-fermentation route utilising methane or CO2, illustrating the integration of alternative carbon feedstocks into biopolymer synthesis59. Collectively, these technological advances underscore the necessity of systematic techno-economic evaluation and integrated process design to translate PHA biomanufacturing from niche applications to commodity-scale production.

Opportunities for commercialising PHAs

The persistent gap between academic innovation and industrial implementation in PHA commercialisation largely reflects a misalignment of priorities58,82,83. Academic research often focuses on expanding monomer diversity and biosynthetic novelty, whereas industry prioritises cost efficiency, process robustness, and product standardisation75. Bridging this divide requires a system-level approach that cost-effectively optimises all stages, from raw material processing and strain development to fermentation and downstream processing, alongside material-property improvement and coordinated market-policy alignment84 (Supplementary Table 2).

Reducing production costs requires simultaneous improvements in strain performance and process efficiency85. Achieving higher TRY depends on the coordinated design of cellular metabolism and bioprocess operation. AI now accelerates this process by identifying flux bottlenecks, predicting optimal growth–production trade-offs, and guiding data-driven optimisation of culture conditions7,86,87. Coupled with automation in strain screening and monomer composition analysis, AI-enabled platforms shorten development cycles and enhance reproducibility across production scales88. Downstream processing, traditionally regarded as a cost-intensive step, is also being redefined through integrated process design89. Conventional recovery methods based on centrifugation, solvent extraction, and drying impose substantial energy and environmental burdens89, whereas emerging strategies seek to minimise non-PHA cell mass, exploit self-flocculating or autolytic strains, and develop direct polymer excretion routes that eliminate cell disruption61. Introducing these concepts early in strain and process design can improve polymer purification processes, maintain molecular-weight stability, and significantly reduce solvent and energy requirements89.

Beyond process intensification, improving material properties offers a decisive route to ensure that PHAs are functionally compatible with existing plastics and readily adopted by markets (Fig. 2f). Enzyme engineering, particularly tailoring PHA synthases (PhaC), has expanded both monomer incorporation and control over polymer architecture75. While native PHAs are typically composed of 3-, 4-, 5- or 6-hydroxyacids, expanding monomer portfolios to include 2-hydroxyacids has previously been limited by the inherently low activity of PhaCs towards these substrates. To overcome this limitation, early engineering efforts employed site-directed and random mutagenesis of PhaCs from strains such as Pseudomonas sp. 61-3 and MBEL 6-19, successfully extending the substrate scope to 2-hydroxyacids and other non-natural monomers. Representative examples include lactate-containing polyesters, such as poly(3HB-co-LA) and even the homopolymer PLA90–92. While PLA provides mechanical stiffness and market compatibility, LA incorporation into PHA backbones improves flexibility and processability by disrupting crystallinity.

Building on these breakthroughs, recent studies have further expanded the polymer range to poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)93,94, aromatic polyesters95,96, and even poly(ester amide)s (PEAs)97. For example, the biosynthesis of PEAs was recently achieved in engineered E. coli by constructing a new-to-nature amino acid polymerisation pathway. This pathway combines β-alanine CoA transferase-mediated amino acid activation with homology modelling- and docking simulation-guided rational engineering of PHA synthase, enabling efficient incorporation of amino acyl-CoA and direct production of various PEAs from glucose through fed-batch fermentation97. These new polymers offer tunable degradation rates, superior thermal resistance, and improved mechanical strength, thereby broadening the application scope of PHA-based materials. The increasing availability of high-resolution structural data, AI-based protein modeling, and advanced synthetic biology tools has transformed PhaC engineering from an empirical, mutation-driven practice into a predictive, structure-guided discipline98–100. Such mechanistic insight enables rational tuning of substrate-binding pockets to enhance catalytic efficiency and thermostability, thereby facilitating the production of polyesters with controllable flexibility and thermo-mechanical performance suitable for high-value industrial and biomedical applications101.

Strategic market positioning is equally critical for translating these technological advances into sustainable growth. Near-term competitiveness is most realistic in high-value sectors where the intrinsic biodegradability, biocompatibility, and bioabsorbability of PHAs offset their current cost disadvantage. For example, Tepha’s P(4HB)-based absorbable suture (TephaFLEX®) received U.S. Food and Drug Administration clearance in 2007 as the first medical device derived from the PHA family of biopolymers102,103. Copolymers such as P(3HB-co-4HB), P(3HB-co-3HV), PLGA, and SCL-MCL PHAs have already gained traction in medical applications, including drug delivery, absorbable sutures, and tissue-engineering scaffolds69,104,105. Demonstrating superior biocompatibility, purity, and predictable degradation relative to chemically synthesised analogues will further strengthen regulatory and industrial acceptance69,104. Regulatory milestones, such as U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval of CJ Biomaterials’ PHA for food-contact applications (FCN No. 1119), represent important steps towards broader adoption in consumer packaging106. As AI-driven process intensification and integrated downstream processing continue to reduce production costs, PHAs are expected to expand into larger-volume sectors, including flexible packaging and consumer goods107. Concurrently, well-structured policy instruments, ranging from restrictions on single-use fossil-based plastics to carbon-credit incentives and bio-based feedstock subsidies, will be essential to internalise environmental costs and establish a fair competitive landscape for bioplastics84. The effectiveness of such instruments, however, depends on their deployment in support of technologies that are already approaching cost parity with petrochemical counterparts, and on alignment between policy objectives and broader societal priorities such as rural development or industrial competitiveness84,108.

Outlook

Sustainable biomanufacturing has matured to a point where reconciling inherent biological complexity with the demands of industrial predictability has become a central challenge. Although industrial-scale validations have increased, significant gaps remain between laboratory performance and industrial feasibility38. As illustrated in the commercialisation trajectories of bio-based SA and PHAs, these gaps arise not from limitations in individual unit operations alone but from insufficient coordination across the entire production pipeline, including raw material procurement, strain design, fermentation performance, and downstream processing (Fig. 2a). To conceptualise potential pathways towards commercially viable bioprocesses, idealised process scenarios that integrate feasible pathways across unit operations can serve as practical design references (Supplementary Fig. 1). Beyond process-level integration, strategic positioning within specialty markets, as exemplified by Roquette’s high-purity SA for pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food applications and Tepha’s P(4HB)-based medical suture, provides a complementary route to commercial viability by aligning product attributes with markets that reward tangible performance benefits rather than competing on price alone. For such high-value products to serve as platforms for expansion into related derivatives, the feedstock, main product, and co-product supply chains should be co-designed from the outset to prevent cascading disruptions across the value chain.

For SA production, industrially competitive configurations can be achieved by integrating low-cost renewable feedstocks with fermentation strategies that reduce neutralising agent consumption, including low-pH fermentation, followed by process-integrated downstream recovery. For example, coupling fermentation with membrane-based electrochemical separation can simultaneously recover and purify SA while reducing salt accumulation, chemical consumption, and downstream processing burden compared with conventional neutral-pH fermentation followed by multi-step purification109,110. For PHA production, TEA and LCA indicate that industrial feasibility is governed by the combined effects of feedstock cost, intracellular polymer content, fermentation performance, solvent consumption during recovery, and overall energy demand, highlighting the importance of integrating inexpensive or waste-derived substrates with strains capable of high polymer accumulation and robust production performance together with simplified downstream processing64. In addition to cost and performance considerations, the carbon emission reduction potential of bio-based SA and PHAs, quantified through LCA, provides a further criterion for improving industrial feasibility. For example, recent studies have demonstrated direct conversion of CO2 into both products by coupling electrocatalytic CO2 reduction with microbial fermentation, enabling SA production in engineered E. coli from CO2-derived ethanol111 and MCL-PHAs production through a chemical-biological interface that utilises C2 intermediates for efficient electro-microbial conversion112. Most recently, integrated electrochemical and biological systems have enabled direct conversion of CO2 into PHB by C. necator113 and into l-lactic acid—a building block for PLA and PHA copolymers—by engineered E. coli114, using formate as both a major carbon source and a source of reducing power generated via electrochemical CO2 reduction. Integrating such CO2-based routes with LCA-based verification and carbon credit generation can serve as an additional economic driver for bio-based SA and PHAs, particularly in carbon-pricing markets. Taken together, these considerations indicate that commercial feasibility requires coordinated optimisation across the entire production pipeline rather than incremental improvements in any single process step, thereby providing a practical framework for identifying engineering priorities and guiding AI-assisted optimisation.

Integrating AI with systems metabolic engineering can facilitate the transition towards predictive and self-optimising development by linking strain design, process operation, and techno-economic evaluation within a unified framework87,115. Within this framework, TEA and LCA need to function not only as retrospective evaluation tools but also as quantitative design criteria that define engineering priorities across the entire biomanufacturing value chain116. Achieving market competitiveness relative to petrochemical counterparts requires that bioprocesses be designed and optimised based on TEA-derived economic indicators such as MPSP and NPV, while simultaneously considering LCA metrics to minimise environmental impacts. AI-assisted metabolic engineering platforms can expand the accessible design space by analysing reaction networks, sequence data, and flux distributions to identify effective pathway rewiring or enzyme engineering strategies117. In addition, adaptive bioprocess control using online sensors combined with machine learning-based process models can mitigate fermentation heterogeneity during scale-up118,119. By continuously monitoring metabolite concentrations, dissolved oxygen concentration, pH, and physiological state, such systems can dynamically adjust nutrient feeding, aeration, and neutralisation in response to spatial and temporal gradients, thereby reducing performance losses during scale-up. Embedding these predictive outputs within iterative Design–Build–Test–Learn frameworks enables continuous refinement of model accuracy through experimental feedback, thereby accelerating the development of robust, high-performing strains120.

In parallel, AI-assisted modeling and control can rationalise process scale-up beyond empirical rules. By emulating reactor dynamics, including mixing, gas transfer, and pH control, digital-twin platforms can provide in silico guidance before implementation121,122. When coupled with TEA and LCA, these predictive systems can help adjust operating parameters in response to feedstock variability or market fluctuation, maintaining a balance among TRY, cost, and environmental impact123. Extending this approach to downstream processing, data-driven modeling can identify optimal separation strategies for specific products, predict fouling behaviour, and guide solvent and energy minimisation, while early integration of recovery considerations into strain design such as engineering strains with enhanced product secretion or reduced biomass formation can significantly decrease purification complexity and resource demand64,124.

Beyond strain and process optimisation, AI offers a promising route to address the persistent fragmentation of knowledge distributed across literature, patents, and industrial data125. Retrieval-augmented generation and agent-based systems can consolidate these heterogeneous datasets into decision-oriented frameworks that integrate TEA, LCA, and regulatory information, enabling transparent evaluation of design alternatives under evolving policy and market conditions126,127. The practical reliability of such systems depends on the completeness, traceability, and update frequency of the underlying datasets, which together influence the accuracy and timeliness of AI-assisted decision-making128. As digital validation becomes increasingly embedded in process qualification, regulatory frameworks may evolve to recognise computational predictions as complementary evidence alongside experimental results, thereby reinforcing transparency and accountability in industrial deployment129.

Realising such AI-assisted frameworks, however, requires addressing barriers beyond algorithmic capability. Industrial process, supply chain, and market data remain largely proprietary, limiting the ability of AI systems to learn from both successful and unsuccessful commercial operations. Federated learning approaches that preserve commercial confidentiality have recently enabled large-scale cross-company collaboration in drug discovery130,131, and extending such consortium-based strategies to biomanufacturing operations will be essential to consolidate operational knowledge distributed across individual operators. Beyond addressing these data limitations, functioning as a unifying framework across the biomanufacturing value chain will further require AI advances specific to this domain. Extending foundation models from biological sequence and single-cell omics data132 to multi-scale biomanufacturing data, including strain physiology, fermentation characteristics, and downstream recovery, can enable generalisable prediction beyond narrow, task-specific models. Uncertainty quantification frameworks established for bioprocess calibration and modelling133 can likewise be developed into uncertainty-aware architectures that propagate predictive confidence across strain design, process design and simulation, and techno-economic evaluation to support risk-informed investment and regulatory decisions. Multi-agent large language model systems have demonstrated autonomous design and execution of scientific experiments134,135. Collaborative multi-agent frameworks, in which specialised agents coordinate across TEA, LCA, supply chain, strain design, and bioprocess operation domains, can thus explore cross-domain solution spaces that are difficult to navigate using conventional, discipline-specific approaches.

At the strategic level, supply chain resilience is emerging as an additional design criterion for biomanufacturing, complementing the cost and environmental performance captured by TEA and LCA. However, these frameworks do not directly quantify resilience attributes such as geographical distribution of feedstock supply, redundancy in production capacity, exposure to geopolitical disruptions, or recovery time after shocks18. Developing resilience-oriented metrics that integrate with TEA and LCA will therefore be essential for evaluating biomanufacturing in line with the strategic priorities of governments and industry16,18. AI-assisted scenario modelling can further support this evaluation by simulating supply chain disruptions, mapping alternative feedstock and logistics options, and identifying configurations that preserve production continuity under disruption136.

Taken together, the future of sustainable biomanufacturing will depend on the convergence of biological innovation, AI, and economic feasibility rather than isolated technological advances. AI can serve as the analytical infrastructure that interconnects these domains, enabling continuous learning from molecular design to industrial operation. Beyond individual products such as SA and PHAs, this paradigm enables the development of adaptive, data-driven bioprocesses capable of maintaining both economic competitiveness and environmental sustainability, offering a practical pathway towards carbon-neutral and resource-efficient biomanufacturing.
