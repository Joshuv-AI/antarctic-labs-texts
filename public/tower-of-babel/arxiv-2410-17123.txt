Pythagoras Numbers for Ternary Forms
Grigoriy Blekherman and Alex Dunbar and Rainer Sinn

Abstract
. We study the Pythagoras numbers py ( 3 , 2 ​ d ) \mathop{\rm py}(3,2d) of real ternary forms, defined for each degree 2 ​ d 2d as the minimal number r r such that every degree 2 ​ d 2d ternary form which is a sum of squares can be written as the sum of at most r r squares of degree d d forms. Scheiderer showed that d + 1 ≤ py ( 3 , 2 ​ d ) ≤ d + 2 d+1\leq\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)\leq d+2 . We show that py ( 3 , 2 ​ d ) = d + 1 \mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)=d+1 for 2 ​ d = 8 , 10 , 12 2d=8,10,12 . The main technical tool is Diesel’s characterization of height 3 Gorenstein algebras.



1.
Introduction

A central object in real algebraic geometry is the cone of sums of squares of homogeneous polynomials (forms). Forms usually have multiple representations as sums of squares, and the
length
of a form
f
is the minimum number
r
such that
f
is a sum of
r
squares, i.e.,
f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}^{2}
for some degree
d
forms
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{r}
. We are concerned with finding the
Pythagoras number

\mathop{\rm py}(n,2d)
, defined as the maximum length of any form in
n
variables of degree
2d
that is a sum of squares.
In terms of Gram matrices connecting sums of squares to semidefinite programming
[
BPT13
,
Chu+17
]
, the Pythagoras number provides information on the existence of low-rank Gram matrices for a form
f
. If a form
f
in
n
variables of degree
2d
is a sum of squares, then there is a positive semidefinite matrix
A
of rank
\mathop{\rm rk}\nolimits(A)\leq\mathop{\rm py}(n,2d)
with the property
f(x)=[x]_{d}^{\top}A[x]_{d}
, where
[x]_{d}
is a vector of all degree
d
monomials in the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
.

Pythagoras numbers are so far only known exactly in very few cases; bounds on Pythagoras numbers are well-studied in real algebraic geometry starting with Hilbert’s seminal paper
[
Hil88
]
. Since we can diagonalize quadratic forms in any number of variables, we have
\mathop{\rm py}(n,2)=n
for any
n\geq 1
. Hilbert showed in
[
Hil88
]
that
\mathop{\rm py}(3,4)=3
. Modern approaches to this theorem in
[
Ble+19
,
Ble+22
,
Chu+17
,
Sch17
]
have led to new results. However, the exact Pythagoras number has only been determined in few additional cases since Hilbert’s work. Scheiderer showed that
\mathop{\rm py}(3,6)=4
and
\mathop{\rm py}(4,4)=5

[
Sch17
]
; both of these cases correspond to varieties of almost minimal degree for which a more general result was shown in
[
Chu+17
]
. In our paper, we establish three additional cases of Pythagoras number for ternary forms (
n=3
).

For ternary forms, it is known that
d+1\leq\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)\leq d+2
and that
\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)=d+1
for
2d=4,6

[
Ble+19
,
Chu+17
,
Sch17
,
Ble+22
]
. Our main result is that the lower bound
d+1
is also sharp for
2d=8,10,12
.

1.1
.

If
2d=8
,
2d=10
, or
2d=12
, then
\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)=d+1
.

More generally, we conjecture that
\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)=d+1
for all
d\geq 2
. However, the proof of our main result for
d=4,5,6
relies on a case analysis. For
2d=10
, we have to discuss
3
cases and for
2d=12
we have
7
. For
2d=14
, the number already increases to
22
. A proof of the conjecture requires therefore a more systematic approach.

1.1.
Outline of the paper

We sketch the key ingredients of our result. Let us fix the notation

\Sigma_{n,2d}=\left\{f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]_{2d}\;\middle|\;f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}^{2}\text{ for some }h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{r}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}\right\}

for the cone of sums of squares so that the Pythagoras number can be formally defined as

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm py}(n,2d)

\displaystyle=\max\{r\;|\;\text{ There exists }f\in\Sigma_{n,2d}\text{ with length }r\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{r\;\middle|\;\text{For each }f\in\Sigma_{n,2d}\text{ there exists }h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{r}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}\text{ with }f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}^{2}\right\}.

The key observation for our proof of Theorem
1.1
is a connection between elements
f\in\Sigma_{n,2d}
which can be written as a sum of strictly fewer than
\mathop{\rm py}(n,2d)
squares and Artinian Gorenstein algebras with socle degree
2d
. Recall that an Artinian Gorenstein algebra with socle degree
2d
is a ring of the form
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]/I
, where the ideal
I
is defined in terms of a linear functional
L
on
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]_{2d}
as follows:

I=I(L)=\left\{f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]\;|\;\deg(f)>2d\text{ or }L(fg)=0\text{ for all }g\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]_{2d-\deg(f)}\right\}.

An ideal of this form is called a
Gorenstein ideal
with socle degree
2d
. To make this connection, we consider the sum-of-
(d+1)
-squares map

\Phi_{d}:\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{d}\to\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{2d}\qquad\Phi_{d}(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}.

If we had
\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)=d+2
, then the image of
\Phi_{d}
would be strictly contained in
\Sigma_{3,2d}
. However, the image
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{2d}
has nonempty interior so that its algebraic boundary has codimension
1
. At a general point
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
on this boundary, the tangent space to
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
is the degree
2d
piece of the ideal generated by the
h_{i}
. That is,
T_{p}(\mathop{\rm im}\Phi_{d})=\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots h_{d}\rangle_{2d}
. We show in Section
2
that if
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
is a generic point on the boundary of
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
which is also an interior point of the cone
\Sigma_{3,2d}
, then
T_{p}(\Phi_{d})
must have codimension one in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{2d}
and the forms
h_{i}
are contained in a Gorenstein ideal
I(L)
, where
L
is a nonzero linear functional vanishing on the tangent hyperplane
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle
.

To derive a contradiction to the existence of such boundary points, we leverage the structure of the associated Gorenstein ideal. Gorenstein ideals in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
are classified by the Buchsbaum-Eisenbud structure theorem, and in
[
Die96
]
, Diesel provides a combinatorial characterization of all such ideals.

In Section
3
, we use a Euclidean distance problem to show that if
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
is a generic point on the boundary of
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
which is also an interior point of
\Sigma_{3,2d}
, then there is
q\in\Sigma_{3,2d}\setminus\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
, such that
p
is a local minimizer over
g\in\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
of
\|q-g\|^{2}
. The optimality conditions for the minimization of
\|q-g\|^{2}
above place strong restrictions on the syzygies of
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
. Indeed, the linear functional
L(f)=\langle q-p,f\rangle
must vanish on
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle
and
L(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2})\leq 0
for any
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{d}
with
\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}h_{i}=0
. Moreover, if
p
is a minimizer of the distance
\|q-g\|^{2}
, then
p
must have length
d+1
. Similar results are proved in
[
Ble+
]
, and here we combine them with structure results on Gorenstein ideals to derive a contradiction.

Diesel’s results
[
Die96
]
provide an explicit list of Gorenstein ideals of socle degree
2d
. In Section
4
, we derive conditions on the dimensions of the graded pieces of the Gorenstein ideals associated to boundary points of
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
which lie in the interior of
\Sigma_{3,2d}
. For
2d=8,10,12
, there are few possibilities for generator and relation degrees of Gorenstein ideals associated to boundary points of
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
: the only possibility for degree
2d=8
is a complete intersection of a cubic and two quartics, there are three cases to consider for
2d=10
, and seven cases for
2d=12
.

In Section
5
, we consider these cases and derive contradictions with the optimality criteria discussed above. For the cases of
2d=10
and
2d=12
, we show that the structure of the Gorenstein ideal associated to any point
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
on the boundary of
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
enforces that
p
has length at most
d
. To make this precise, we define the
Pythagoras number

\mathop{\rm py}(X)
of a real projective variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
with homogeneous coordinate ring
R
to be the minimum integer
s
such that if
f\in R_{2}
is a sum of squares of elements of
R_{1}
, then
f
is a sum of squares of at most
s
elements of
R_{1}
. This is consistent with the notion of Pythagoras number of forms as defined above since
\mathop{\rm py}(n,2d)=\mathop{\rm py}(\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}))
, where
\nu_{d}
is the
d^{th}
Veronese embedding.

In the degree
2d=10
and
2d=12
cases, we show that if
J
is a candidate Gorenstein ideal then there is a basis
\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{r}\}
of
J_{d}
as a real vector space, such the quadratic relations among the
t_{i}
are the same as the quadratic relations in the defining ideal of a toric variety
X_{K}
. Moreover,
\mathop{\rm py}(X_{K})
is computable and provides an upper bound on the length of a sum of squares of elements of
\mathbb{R}[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{r}]_{d}
.

Surprisingly, the
2d=8
case is more technical. Here, the only candidate Gorenstein ideal
J
is defined by a complete intersection of a cubic and two quartics. The upper bound provided by the construction of toric variety as in the degree
2d=10
and
2d=12
cases is
5=d+1
, which does not provide the desired contradiction. However, by leveraging the second-order optimality conditions and the structure of the complete intersection, we once again obtain a contradiction.

1.2.
Notation and Conventions

We fix
S=\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
and denote by
S_{d}\subseteq S
the vector space of degree
d
forms. Similarly, if
I\subseteq S
is a homogeneous ideal, then
I_{d}\subseteq S_{d}
is the
d^{th}
graded piece. If
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\in S
, the ideal they generate is
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\rangle
. The cone of degree
2d
forms which are sums of squares is
\Sigma_{2d}:=\Sigma_{3,2d}\subseteq S_{2d}
.

For a real vector space
V
, the dual vector space is
V^{*}
. If
K\subseteq V
is a convex cone, then we write
K^{*}=\{L\in V^{*}\;|\;L(x)\geq 0\text{ for all }x\in K\}
for its dual convex cone. If
L\in V^{*}
is nonzero, the hyperplane annihilated by
L
is
L^{\perp}=\{v\in V\;|\;L(v)=0\}
. For fixed
d\geq 0
, we identify
S_{d}
with
S_{d}^{*}
through the
apolar inner product
, where
x_{i}
acts by
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
.

If
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
\overline{W}
the closure of
W
and
\mathrm{int}(W)
the interior of
W
. If
W
is closed, its boundary is
\partial W=W\setminus\mathrm{int}(W)
.

2.
Pythagoras Number to Gorenstein Ideals

In this section we consider the set of sums of squares of length at most
d+1
and prove Proposition
2.2
. Fix
S=\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
. We follow a similar strategy to
[
Ble+19
,
Ble+22
,
Hil88
]
. Define the map

(1)

\Phi_{d}:\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}S_{d}\to S_{2d}\qquad\Phi_{d}(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}.

We observe that
\Phi_{d}
induces a map on the projectivizations
\mathbb{P}(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}S_{d})\to\mathbb{P}(S_{2d})
and therefore is closed and proper. The differential of
\Phi_{d}
at the point
h=(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
is the map

(2)

{\rm d}_{h}\Phi_{d}:\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}S_{d}\to S_{2d}\qquad{\rm d}_{h}\Phi_{d}(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{d})=2\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}g_{i}.

We first record the fact that the image of
\Phi_{d}
has nonempty interior. In fact, it is known that that a general form
f\in S_{2d}
has a representation as a sum of at most
2^{2}=4
four
complex
squares
[
FOS12
, Theorem 4]
and that this implies that the set of sums of four squares of real forms of degree
d
has nonempty interior in
\Sigma_{2d}

[
Sch17
, Section 2]
.

Lemma 2.1

(See e.g.
[
FOS12
,
Sch17
]
)
.

For
d\geq 3
,
\mathrm{im}(\Phi_{d})\subseteq S_{2d}
has nonempty interior.

It follows from Lemma
2.1
that if
\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)\geq d+2
, then
\partial\,\overline{\mathrm{int}(\mathop{\rm im}\Phi_{d})}\cap\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{2d})\not=\emptyset
.

We now prove Proposition
2.2
, which states that if
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
is a generic element on the boundary of the sums of squares of length at most
d+1
, then the ideal
I=\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle
is contained in a Gorenstein ideal with socle degree
2d
and
I_{d}
is base point free.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
is a generic element of
\partial\,\overline{\mathrm{int}(\mathop{\rm im}\Phi)}\cap\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{2d})
and
I=\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle
is the ideal generated by the forms
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
, then
I_{d}
is base point free and
I
is contained in a Gorenstein ideal with socle degree
2d
.

Proof.

If
\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)=d+2
, then
\mathrm{im}(\Phi_{d})
is a proper subset of
\Sigma_{2d}
which has interior in
S_{2d}
by Lemma
2.1
. Set
B=\partial\,\overline{\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{im}\Phi_{d})}
to be the boundary of the closure of the interior of the image of the map
\Phi_{d}
. It follows from
[
Sin15
]
that
\operatorname{codim}(B)=1
since
\mathrm{int}(\mathop{\rm im}\Phi_{d})
and
S_{2d}\setminus\mathrm{int}(\mathop{\rm im}\Phi_{d})
are both regular subsets of
S_{2d}
equipped with the Euclidean topology, i.e., they are each contained in the closure of their interiors.

By Sard’s Theorem (see e.g;
[
Sar42
]
,
[
BCR98
, Theorem 9.6.2]
), at a general point
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}\in B
, we have that
T_{p}B
has dimension
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}S_{2d}-1=\binom{2+2d}{2d}-1
and that
\mathop{\rm rk}\nolimits{\rm d}_{h}\Phi=\dim T_{p}B
. By the calculation (
2
), this implies that
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle_{2d}\subseteq S_{2d}
is a hyperplane. Let
L\in S_{2d}^{*}
be a nonzero linear functional annihilating the hyperplane
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle
. Then
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle
is contained in the Gorenstein ideal
I(L)
, which has socle in degree
2d
.

We now show that for general
p\in B\cap\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{2d})
, we can take
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle_{d}
to be a base point free linear series. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there is
z\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}(h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{d})
. If
z
is a real base point of the
h_{i}
then
p
is not in the interior of
\Sigma_{2d}
. If
z
is a complex base point, then so is its complex conjugate
\bar{z}
since the
h_{i}
are defined over
\mathbb{R}
. The set of polynomials which vanish at both
z
and
\bar{z}
is codimension
2
so that a general
p\in B\cap\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{2d})
will not vanish at
z
and
\bar{z}
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

While the results of this section are stated for ternary forms, the analogous statements hold for

. In particular, for the case

, we obtain the upper bound

\mathop{\rm py}(5,4)\leq\max\{\dim(J_{2})\;|\;J\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{5}]\text{ is Gorenstein with socle degree }4\}+1

By

3.
Minimizing the Distance

In this section, we show that a general element
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
of the boundary of
\overline{\mathrm{int}(\mathop{\rm im}\Phi_{d})}
is the minimizer of some distance. More precisely, if
\mathop{\rm py}(3,2d)\geq d+2
, then there is
q\in\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{2d})
such that
p
is the local minimizer over forms
g\in\overline{\mathrm{int}(\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d}))}
of the function
\|q-g\|^{2}
. We then relate this property to the syzygies of the forms
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
. Throughout this section, we denote
M:=\partial\,\overline{\mathrm{int}(\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d}))}\bigcap\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{2d})\subseteq S_{2d}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
p
is a generic element of
M
, there exists an element
q\in\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{3,2d})
and a neighborhood
U\subseteq M
of
p
such that
q
cannot be written as a sum of fewer than
d+2
squares and such that

(3)

p=\arg\min_{g\in U}\|q-g\|^{2}.

Proof.

Let
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
be a generic element of
M
so that
H:=\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle_{2d}
has codimension
1
in
S_{2d}
. Let
L\in S_{2d}^{*}
be the linear functional which vanishes on
H
, and
f\in S_{2d}
be such that
L(g)=\langle f,g\rangle
for all
g\in S_{2d}
. Since
p\in M
, either
p+\epsilon f
or
p-\epsilon f
is not an element of
\mathop{\rm im}(\Phi_{d})
for small
\epsilon>0
. So, replacing
f
with
-f
if necessary, it follows that
q=p+\epsilon f
cannot be written as a sum of fewer than
d+2
squares and
q\in\mathrm{int}(\Sigma_{2d})
for
\epsilon>0
small enough . Moreover, since
L
vanishes on
H
, there is a neighborhood
p\in U\subseteq M
such that
p=\arg\min_{g\in U}\|q-g\|^{2}
.
∎

Our primary application of Proposition
3.1
is to understand the syzygies of the forms
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
when
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
is a generic element of the boundary of
M
. To do so, we examine the first and second-order optimality conditions in the problem (
3
). We first need the following technical lemma regarding second-order optimality conditions.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
Q
and
R
be symmetric
n\times n
matrices with
Q\succeq 0
. Suppose that
Q+\epsilon R\succeq 0
for
\epsilon\geq 0
sufficiently small. Then, the restriction of
R
to
\ker(Q)
is positive semidefinite and if
v^{\top}Rv=v^{\top}Qv=0
, then
Rv=0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
Q+\epsilon R\succeq 0
for
\epsilon\geq 0
sufficiently small. Let
v\in\ker Q
. Then,
0\leq v^{\top}(Q+\epsilon R)v=\epsilon v^{\top}Rv
. So,
R\succeq 0
on
\ker Q
. It then follows that if
v^{\top}Rv=v^{\top}Qv=0
, then
v\in\ker Q
since
Q\succeq 0
and therefore
Rv=0
since
R\succeq 0
on
\ker Q
.
∎

We can now prove the main proposition of this section which relates optimality conditions for the problem (
3
) to syzygies of the forms
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
be a general element of
M
and let
q\in S_{2d}
be as in Proposition
3.1
. Set
L\in S_{2d}^{*}
to be the linear functional
L(g)=\langle q-p,g\rangle
. Then,

(1)

L(g)=0
for all
g\in\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle_{2d}
.

(2)

If
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in S_{d}
are such that
\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}h_{i}=0
, then
L\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2}\right)\leq 0
. If, in addition,
L\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2}\right)=0
, then
a_{i}\in I(L)_{d}
for each
i=0,1,\ldots,d
.

(3)

\pm L\not\in\Sigma_{2d}^{*}

(4)

The length of
p
is
d+1
.

Proof.

We examine perturbations of
p
. For
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, let
p_{\epsilon}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\left(h_{i}+\epsilon a_{i}+\epsilon^{2}b_{i}\right)^{2}
, where
a_{i},b_{i}\in S_{d}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,d
. Then,

\displaystyle\langle q-p_{\epsilon},q-p_{\epsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle q-p,q-p\rangle-4\epsilon\left\langle q-p,\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i}\right\rangle-2\epsilon^{2}\left\langle q-p,\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2}+2h_{i}b_{i}\right\rangle+\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i},\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=L(q-p)-4\epsilon L\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i}\right)-2\epsilon^{2}L\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2}+2h_{i}b_{i}\right)+\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i},\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i}\right\rangle

Since
p
is a local minimum of the distance to
q
, it follows from first order optimality that
L(\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i})=0
for any choice of

, proving the first claim. If we further take
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in S_{d}
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}a_{i}=0
, then

\langle q-p_{\epsilon},q-p_{\epsilon}\rangle=L(q-p)-2\epsilon^{2}L\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2}\right).

It then follows that
L(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2})\leq 0
. By applying Lemma

to the quadratic forms

and
R(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})=-2L(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2})
on

, it follows that if
L(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}^{2})=0
, then
-L(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}g_{i})=0
for any
(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{d})\in S_{d}^{d+1}
. So, each
a_{i}\in I(L)_{d}
.

For part
(3)
, note that since
p\in\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle
, it follows from part
(1)
that
L(p)=-L(p)=0
. Since

, the hyperplane

cannot be a supporting hyperplane of

. Therefore
\pm L\not\in\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
.

For part
(4)
, suppose for the sake of a contradiction that

has length at most
d
so that

for some

. Then, since
\pm L\not\in\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
, there exists

such that
L(a^{2})>0
. It then follows that for each
\epsilon>0

\langle q-p,q-(p+\epsilon a^{2})\rangle=\|q-p\|^{2}-\epsilon L(a^{2})<\|q-p\|^{2},

a contradiction with the construction of

.
∎

Proposition
3.3
suggests methods for eliminating possible Gorenstein ideals from consideration. In particular, given a potential Gorenstein ideal
I=I(L)
, we will show that if
p=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{2}
for some

such that
\langle h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\rangle_{2d}=I_{2d}
, then either

has length

or that if the stationarity conditions (1) and (2) hold then

.

In the remainder of this section, we further investigate the syzygies of the forms
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
. First, we show that the
h_{i}
must be linearly independent.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\in S_{d}
. Suppose that
L\in S_{2d}^{*}\setminus\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
is a linear functional such that
L(\sum_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}^{2})\geq 0
for any
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in S_{d}
which satisfy
\sum_{i=0}^{r}h_{i}a_{i}=0
. Then,
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}
are linearly independent.

Proof.

Suppose for the sake of a contradiction that
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
are not all zero and
\sum_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}h_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}^{2}=1
. Let
f\in S_{d}
be arbitrary. Then,
\sum_{i=0}^{r}(a_{i}f)h_{i}=0
and therefore
L(\sum_{i=0}^{r}(a_{i}f)^{2})=L(f^{2})\geq 0
, which implies that
L\in\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
.
∎

Next, we show that the presence of a nontrivial syzygy of the form
\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq r}\alpha_{i,j}h_{i}h_{j}=0
with each
\alpha_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
certifies that the length of
\sum_{i=0}^{r}h_{i}^{2}
is at most
r
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose that
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\in S_{d}
and that there are
\alpha_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
for
0\leq i\leq j\leq r
, not all zero, such that
\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq r}\alpha_{i,j}h_{i}h_{j}=0
. Then, the length of
\sum_{i=0}^{r}h_{i}^{2}
is at most
r
.

Proof.

Let
A
be the symmetric matrix representative of the quadratic form
\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq r}\alpha_{i,j}h_{i}h_{j}
. Then,

\sum_{i=0}^{r}h_{i}^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{0}&h_{1}&\dots&h_{r}\end{bmatrix}\left(I-tA\right)\begin{bmatrix}h_{0}&h_{1}&\dots&h_{r}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Since
A
is symmetric and not the zero matrix, there is
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(I-tA)
has rank at most
r
.
∎

4.1

If

Theorem
4.1
imposes the following conditions on low degree generators for Gorenstein ideals

. First, we obtain a lower bound on the minimal generator degree of

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix

. If

is a Gorenstein ideal with socle in degree
2d
which contains a base point free linear series

in degree

Proof.

Suppose for the sake of a contradiction that

such that

which contradicts the assumption of the socle degree of

∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let

be a Gorenstein ideal with socle degree
2d
. Then

(1)

If

has at most two generators of degree at most
d

does not contain a basepoint free linear series.

(2)

If

as an independent variable

. Disregarding

for the moment, we get a variety defined by a monomial embedding, and in fact this is the toric variety

with the polytope

given by

where the parametrization of

is given in Cox coordinates
[
CLS11
, Chapter 5.4]
. This is a variety of minimal degree, and of dimension
3
. If we add

, then we get a cone
Y
over

, which is still a variety of minimal degree, of dimension
4
. Specializing,

can only add additional elements to the ideal of

, and therefore
\mathop{\rm Proj}(R)
is contained in a variety of minimal degree of dimension

. Thus
\mathop{\rm py}(\mathop{\rm Proj}(R))\leq 5
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Fix
d
. Let
J\subseteq S
be a Gorenstein ideal with socle degree
2d
and generator and relation degrees
q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\ldots\leq q_{s}
and
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\ldots\geq p_{s}
, respectively. Let
\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{s}\}\subseteq S
be a set of generators of
J
with
\deg(f_{i})=q_{i}
. Let
s^{\prime}
be such that
q_{i}<d
for
1\leq i\leq s^{\prime}
and
q_{j}\geq d
for
j>s^{\prime}
.
Set

\Omega=\{(i,j,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\;|\;1\leq i\leq s^{\prime}\text{ and }(j,k)\in T_{d-q_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}

and denote
|\Omega|=r
. For each
\omega=(i,j,k)\in\Omega
, set

Then, the map
\mathbb{R}[y_{\omega}\;|\;\omega\in\Omega]\to\mathbb{R}[t_{\omega}\;|\;\omega\in\Omega]
which sends

embeds
\mathop{\rm Proj}(\mathbb{R}[t_{\omega}\;|\;\omega\in\Omega])\subseteq X_{K}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
, where

is the toric variety associated to the polytope

K=\mathop{\rm conv}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{s^{\prime}}T_{d-q_{i}}\times e_{i-1}\right)

where we set

and where
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{s^{\prime}-1}
are the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{s^{\prime}-1}
.

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

We show that the quadratic relations on
X_{K}
vanish on the generators
t_{\omega}
of
R=\mathbb{R}[t_{\omega}\;|\;\omega\in\Omega]
. For
(u_{1},u_{2},z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{s^{\prime}})\in\mathbb{C}^{2+s^{\prime}}
set
v_{1,j,k}=u_{1}^{j}u_{2}^{k}
and
v_{i,j,k}=z_{i-1}u_{1}^{j}u_{2}^{k}
. Then,
X_{K}
is the Zariski closure of the map

\mathbb{C}^{2+s^{\prime}}\ni(u_{1},u_{2},z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{s^{\prime}})\longmapsto\left(v_{\omega}\;|\;\omega\in\Omega\right).

The quadratics in the ideal defining

are generated by the binomials
v_{i_{1},j_{1},k_{1}}v_{i_{2},j_{2},k_{2}}-v_{i_{3},j_{3},k_{3}}v_{i_{4},j_{4},k_{4}}
, where
(j_{1}+j_{2},k_{1}+k_{2})=(j_{3}+j_{4},k_{3}+k_{4})
and
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}=\{i_{3},i_{4}\}
. For any such set of indices, we have

t_{i_{1},j_{1},k_{1}}t_{i_{2},j_{2},k_{2}}-t_{i_{3},j_{3},k_{3}}t_{i_{4},j_{4},k_{4}}=\\
x_{1}^{j_{1}+j_{2}}x_{2}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}x_{3}^{2d-(q_{i_{1}}+q_{i_{2}}+j_{1}+j_{2}+k_{1}+k_{2})}f_{i_{1}}f_{i_{2}}-x_{1}^{j_{3}+j_{4}}x_{2}^{k_{3}+k_{4}}x_{3}^{2d-(q_{i_{3}}+q_{i_{4}}+j_{3}+j_{4}+k_{3}+k_{4})}f_{i_{3}}f_{i_{4}}=0

∎
