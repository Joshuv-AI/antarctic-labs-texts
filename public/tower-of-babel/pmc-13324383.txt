Mathematical modeling of immune counter-regulation predicts efficacy of fractionated CD8+ T cell dosing strategies
Authors: Hiroki Kasai, Koji Nagaoka, Artem Lysenko, Kazuhiro Kakimi, Tatsuhiko Tsunoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) with tumor-specific CD8+ T cells (TSTs) induces tumor regression but rarely achieves durable responses in solid tumors. This limitation stems from secondary counter-regulatory mechanisms that are also induced by ACT, notably the recruitment of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells (TIMs). However, the precise dynamics of these responses and the optimal TST dosing strategies to overcome their immunosuppression remain largely unclear. Here, we developed a mathematical model of ACT incorporating TIM-driven counter-regulation and simulated alternative TST dosing strategies based on data from B16F10 melanoma-bearing mice. Our models revealed that, compared with a single administration strategy, fractionated and response-guided dosing strategies reduced tumor burden by up to 83% while using about 40% fewer TSTs. These results were supported by transcriptomic analysis showing that ACT-induced TIMs initially exhibited pro-inflammatory traits but later shifted into suppressive states, suggesting that fractionated TST dosing could repeatedly induce their stimulatory potential. Together, our findings advance understanding of counter-regulatory mechanisms in ACT and highlight the potential of fractionated dosing as a rational strategy to overcome them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50922-3.

Introduction

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) with tumor-specific CD8+ T cells (TSTs) is recognized as a powerful therapeutic strategy against cancer1. While ACT has demonstrated remarkable success in treating hematologic malignancies, its efficacy in solid tumors remains limited and often only transient2. One of the major obstacles to durable tumor control in solid tumors is the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). It inhibits the anti-tumor function of transferred TSTs through counter-regulatory mechanisms, such as the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells and the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules3. Consequently, there is an ongoing effort to characterize these immunosuppressive mechanisms and develop improved strategies to overcome them.

An additional challenge is that ACT itself can trigger host negative feedback mechanisms, which result in immunosuppression and limit its efficacy4. In murine models of ACT for B16F10 melanoma, adoptively transferred TSTs not only suppress tumor growth but also induce the accumulation of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells (TIMs), a population previously described as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)5–7. Although other immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells, may also contribute to ACT resistance, TIMs constitute more than half of the tumor-infiltrating immune cells in this model and therefore represent the predominant immunosuppressive population associated with ACT failure5. To maximize the anti-tumor efficacy of ACT, the dynamics of these immunosuppressive responses must be quantitatively characterized and incorporated into the design of therapeutic strategies8. However, despite accumulating evidence of TIM-mediated immunosuppression, the precise impact of ACT-induced TIMs remains largely unknown.

Mathematical modeling has proven useful for predicting treatment responses and informing dosing strategies across various types of cancer therapy. For example, Beck et al. quantified the tumor-suppressive effects of transferred TSTs in B16F10 melanoma-bearing mice using a model based on ordinary differential equations (ODEs)9. Strobl et al. developed adaptive chemotherapy schedules through iterative integration of experimental data and model simulations, enabling optimization of treatment timing10. Adhikarla et al. analyzed the impact of repeated dosing in CAR-T cell and radiation therapies, showing that the timing of interventions can critically affect tumor control11. Collectively, these studies demonstrate how mathematical modeling contributes to both the mechanistic understanding and rational design of cancer treatments.

In this study, we simulated and optimized TST dosing strategies by incorporating the immunosuppressive effects of TIMs induced during ACT. These processes were modeled as a system of ODEs, calibrated with experimental data from B16F10 melanoma-bearing mice treated with TSTs5,6 and further validated on additional datasets obtained under different conditions. We also performed transcriptomic analyses of B16F10 tumors after TST administration to further characterize ACT-induced TIMs.

Results

Tumor and TIM growth dynamics under untreated conditions fit the Gompertz model

To establish a baseline mathematical model of untreated tumor growth, we analyzed time-series tumor volume data from three independent B16 tumor-bearing mouse cohorts (Dataset 1, n = 55; Datasets 2 and 3, n = 5 and 7, respectively6). We converted tumor volumes to cell numbers for mathematical modeling (Methods) and aligned the time courses by defining day 9 after tumor inoculation as “Day 0,” following the definition used in the original studies5,6. Because Datasets 1 and 2 exhibited similar initial burdens and longer observation periods, we used them for model fitting, whereas Dataset 3, which showed a higher initial burden and a shorter observation window, was reserved for validation (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Despite these differences, all datasets showed a gradual decrease in tumor growth rate over time (Supplementary Fig. S1b). To determine the most appropriate model for tumor growth, we compared the fit of three commonly used mathematical models (exponential, logistic, and Gompertz) to the tumor data (Supplementary Table S1). The Gompertz model provided the best fit for Datasets 1 and 2 (Supplementary Fig. S1c, d), while all three models performed comparably for Dataset 3 due to its limited observation window (Supplementary Fig. S1e, f). Based on these results, we selected the Gompertz model to describe the untreated tumor growth (Fig. 1a).Fig. 1Simulation of tumor and TIM growth dynamics in untreated mice using the Gompertz model. We then extended this model to incorporate the dynamics of the estimated total TIM numbers under untreated conditions (Methods). We simulated the dynamics of TIMs under the assumption that TIM production is proportional to tumor burden12, with the proportionality constant estimated through model fitting. This approach successfully reproduced the total TIM numbers estimated from the experimental data (Fig. 1b; Methods). These results suggest that the temporal changes in the estimated total TIM numbers under untreated conditions were consistent with the model based on Gompertz tumor growth.

Repeated TST dosing can suppress tumor growth

We extended the untreated ODE model to a TST-treated model by incorporating both TST and TIM dynamics (Methods; Supplementary Fig. S2), where TIMs were recruited not only by the tumor burden but also by the administered TSTs. We then considered two alternative formulations of TIM-mediated immunosuppression: Model 1 assumed a linear relationship (Eq. 8 in Methods), whereas Model 2 incorporated a saturable term (Eq. 9 in Methods). To compare these models, we optimized their parameters using the estimated time-course data for the total numbers of B16 cells, TSTs, and TIMs in tumors treated with a single dose of 1 × 107 TSTs (Methods). The initial conditions were set to the baseline estimates derived from this group: 2.5 × 107 B16 cells, 0 TSTs, and 4.2 × 104 TIMs. After parameter optimization, both models reproduced the transient tumor suppression observed after TST administration (Supplementary Fig. S3a, b). Next, we simulated repeated TST dosing at weekly intervals, ranging from one to four administrations of 1 × 107 TSTs each, while using the same initial conditions (Supplementary Fig. S3c, d). The difference between Model 1 and Model 2 became more apparent under repeated dosing than under the single-dose condition. In the four-dose simulation, Model 1 predicted a gradual loss of efficacy due to TIM accumulation, whereas Model 2 predicted transient tumor suppression, with each administration producing a clear but temporary reduction in tumor growth.

To validate these predictions, we conducted an in vivo experiment in which B16 tumor-bearing mice were treated with repeated TST dosing at weekly intervals (Fig. 2a). Each TST administration reduced the tumor growth rate by approximately 0.4, irrespective of tumor size at the time of TST administration (Fig. 2b, black line). We then applied both Model 1 and Model 2 using experiment-matched initial conditions (Methods) and the same TST dosing schedules as in the experiment (Fig. 2a, b; Model 1, red line; Model 2, blue line). These results indicate that Model 2 better represented the nonlinear process than Model 1. Therefore, we used Model 2 for all subsequent simulations of TST dosing strategies. For these simulations, we used the baseline values of B16 cells, TSTs and TIMs estimated from the tumors treated with a single-dose 1 × 107 TSTs as common initial conditions: 2.5 × 107 B16 cells, 0 TSTs, and 5.6 × 105 TIMs, respectively. Simulations show fractionated TST dosing improves tumor control

We simulated different TST dosing strategies to evaluate their impact on tumor control. First, we explored whether sustained tumor control could be achieved with a single TST dose. We simulated the efficacy of various TST doses (0, 1 × 106, 2 × 106, 5 × 106, 1 × 107, and 2 × 107 cells), while fixing the initial tumor size at 2.5 × 107 cells, which corresponds to the mean value observed in the experimental data. Although higher doses resulted in greater initial tumor reduction, none of the tested doses maintained long-term tumor suppression (Fig. 3a). Fractionated dosing regimens are often considered as an alternative strategy in clinical settings, since a single high-dose TST therapy can induce severe side effects such as cytokine release syndrome13,14. To evaluate the impact of dose fractionation, we simulated tumor growth under five dosing schedules in which a total dose of 2 × 107 TSTs was evenly subdivided into 1, 2, 3, 4, or 8 doses administered over an 8-day period (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. S5). The simulation results showed that even with the same total dose, fractionated regimens tended to prolong tumor suppression compared to a single administration (Fig. 3b). While the benefit of dose fractionation was observed at higher total doses, no clear benefit was observed at lower doses (Supplementary Fig. S5), indicating a gradual enhancement of fractionation efficacy with increasing TST doses.

To optimize the dose and timing of the second TST administration, we simulated two-dose schedules where the first TST dose (1 × 107 cells) on Day 0 was fixed, and the second dose (ranging from 0.5 to 4 × 107 cells) was administered between Days 0 and 9. These strategies were compared using progression-free survival (PFS) as an indicator of therapeutic efficacy. Our simulations revealed that the longest PFS was achieved when the second dose was administered around Day 4 (Fig. 3c), which corresponded to the time point when the tumor volume returned to its baseline level after the initial suppression (Fig. 3d).

Response-guided TST dosing strategies based on tumor growth can improve efficacy while reducing total dose

To evaluate whether adjusting the amount of TSTs based on tumor response improves therapeutic efficacy compared with conventional single-dose administration, we simulated three multi-dose strategies (Daily, Adaptive, and On/Off) adapted from a previous chemotherapy study10. Each strategy began with an initial dose of D cells on Day 0, and the daily doses from Day 1 to Day 14 were determined by specified rules: (1) Daily (a fixed dose of D cells administered each day), (2) Adaptive (the dose increased when the tumor grew and decreased when the tumor shrank, with dose levels restricted to [0, D/4, D/2, D]), and (3) On/Off (the same rule as Adaptive but limited to either 0 or D). For comparison, under the Single strategy, the total amount corresponding to 15 daily doses, i.e., 15 × D cells, was administered once on Day 0.

We set D to 2.8 × 106 cells (D0), which was estimated to be the minimum daily dose required to achieve tumor suppression over 15 days of consecutive administration (Supplementary Fig. S6). We then simulated each strategy under conditions in which the maximum daily dose was increased from D0 to αD0 cells (α = 1–2). Compared with the Single strategy, the Daily strategy achieved the strongest tumor suppression, whereas the Adaptive and On/Off strategies provided superior tumor control while requiring fewer total TSTs (Fig. 4a, b). For example, when the maximum dose was doubled (D = 2D0 cells), tumor burden at Day 21 was reduced by 96%, 83%, and 72% under the Daily, Adaptive, and On/Off strategies, respectively (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. S7). In addition, the total TST dose in the Adaptive and On/Off strategies decreased by 37% and 40% relative to the Single strategy (Fig. 4d). TST administration induces a transient inflammatory response

To investigate the broader biological context underlying the effectiveness of the daily TST dosing strategies, we analyzed gene-expression microarray data of bulk tumor-tissues on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 after a single TST administration15. Immune checkpoint genes (Pdcd1 and Lag3) and immunosuppressive genes related to TIMs (Arg1 and Tgfb1) were upregulated between days 3 and 5 (Fig. 5a). In contrast, expression of Ifng, which encodes the cytokine IFNγ, peaked at day 3 but returned to baseline levels by day 7 (Fig. 5b). Notably, T cell–related inflammatory genes such as Cxcl9 (T cell chemotaxis), Cd86 (costimulatory signaling), and Icam1 (cell adhesion) peaked at day 3 and declined thereafter, but remained elevated at day 7 compared with baseline levels (Fig. 5b). ACT-induced TIMs exhibit transient immunostimulatory activity followed by suppressive reprogramming

To identify immune cells associated with immune suppression and activation, we performed scRNA-seq on CD45+ immune cells isolated from B16 tumors at 3, 7, and 14 days after TST administration. Clustering analysis identified seven major immune cell types (Supplementary Fig. S8a, b). CD11b+ TIMs were annotated as Itgam (encoding CD11b)-expressing myeloid clusters (Supplementary Fig. S8c, d), representing the TIM population used in our mathematical model. CD11b+ TIMs expressed multiple immunosuppressive markers, specifically Arg1, Nos2, and Cybb were enriched in this cluster, whereas Vegfa, Cd274, and Tgfb1 were broadly expressed across multiple clusters, including CD11b+ TIMs (Supplementary Fig. S8e). Notably, immunostimulatory genes such as Cxcl9, Cxcl10 and Cd86 were primarily expressed in CD11b+ TIMs cluster (Supplementary Fig. S9). While Cd86 expression in CD11b+ TIMs remained relatively constant from day 3 to day 14, Cxcl9 and Cxcl10 expression clearly declined over this period, suggesting that ACT-induced TIMs may transiently contribute to T cell recruitment and activation.

To characterize the temporal changes in CD11b+ TIM function, we performed GSEA on the CD11b+ TIM component. Pathway enrichment at days 7 and 14 was compared with that at day 3, and the results were visualized as waterfall plots based on the Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (Supplementary Fig. S10a, b). Inflammatory pathways such as IFN-γ and IFN-α response were more enriched at day 3 than at days 7 and 14. In contrast, pathways associated with immunosuppression and tissue remodeling, including epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), TGF-β signaling, and Hypoxia, became more prominent at day 7 than at day 3 (Supplementary Fig. S10a), indicating a transition from an early inflammatory to an immunosuppressive state between day 3 and day 7. This trend was maintained at day 14, where MYC targets (V1 and V2) were preferentially upregulated compared with day 3 (Supplementary Fig. S10b), suggesting a sustained transition toward an immunosuppressive state between day 3 and day 14. These results confirmed that ACT-induced immune activation was transient and subsequently followed by the emergence of immunosuppressive programs.

To further investigate the heterogeneity within CD11b+ TIMs, we performed a clustering analysis within the CD11b+ TIMs. We identified six distinct components based on their top 10 differentially expressed genes (Fig. 6a,b). These subclusters were annotated as tumor-associated macrophages (TAM; Cluster 0: Apoe, Arg1), IFNγ-stimulated monocytes (IFN-Mo; Cluster 1: Cxcl9, Gbp2), Cx3cr1+ monocytes (Cx3cr1+ Mo; Cluster 2: Cx3cr1, Nr4a1), tumor-associated neutrophils (TAN; Cluster 3: S100a8, S100a9, Mmp9), cDC2s (Cluster 4; Itgax, Cd209a), and T cells (Cluster 5; Cd3d, Cd3e). The T cell cluster (Cluster 5) likely reflected minor contamination in the analyzed population. Temporal analysis of cell proportions revealed a progressive reduction in Cluster 1 accompanied by an increase in Cluster 0 from day 3 to day 14 (Fig. 6c). TAMs (Cluster 0) exhibited high expression of Arg1 (Fig. 6d), which has been reported to suppress T cell function8. In contrast, IFNγ-stimulated monocytes (Cluster 1) had IFNγ responsive characteristics such as Cxcl9 and Cxcl10, which is associated with T cell recruitment (Fig. 6d). To clarify the functional implications of this shift, we further performed Gene Ontology enrichment analysis on Clusters 0 and 1 (Fig. 6e). These results indicate that a single ACT administration induces transient immune activation accompanied by the infiltration of CD11b+ TIMs. Initially, these cells exhibit immunostimulatory phenotypes but progressively shift toward suppressive phenotypes. As this immune activation is transient, repeated TST administrations at short intervals can help sustain it.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to simulate and optimize TST dosing strategies by incorporating the immunosuppressive effects of TIMs induced by ACT. We developed ODE models to quantitatively analyze the system behavior that captures the interactions between B16 tumor cells, effector TSTs, exhausted TSTs, and TIMs following ACT by integrating experimental data of B16 murine melanoma models. Our model demonstrated that explicitly incorporating TIM dynamics is essential to accurately reproduce ACT outcomes and provided insights into how TST dosing strategies can be optimized to enhance tumor control. A key finding of this analysis was that fractionated TST dosing strategies sustained tumor suppression more effectively than a single administration, and that response-guided dosing strategies reduced the total TST dose while still achieving substantial reductions in tumor burden. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that ACT-induced CD11b+ TIMs transiently upregulate immunostimulatory genes following each TST dose. These results support the idea that repeated dosing can sustain this transient immune activation, providing a rationale for dynamically scheduled TST dosing to improve ACT efficacy.

For our mathematical models, assuming a limit to TIM-mediated suppression gave the best fit to the data (Fig. 2). Previous spatiotemporal imaging studies of pmel-1 TST therapy in B16 tumor-bearing mice also support this assumption16. In particular, they revealed that transferred TSTs initially interact with lymphoid and dendritic cells upon tumor entry and later establish contact with myeloid cells such as macrophages. This observation suggests that T cell-TIM interactions are spatially and temporally constrained, such that only a limited subset of TIMs is positioned to directly suppress T cells. As a result, the number of effective immunosuppressive interactions does not scale linearly with the number of TIMs and instead reaches a limit of suppressive capacity, which could explain the saturation of immunosuppression observed in our model.

In our experiments, the repeated TST administrations always exerted a high tumor-suppressive effect, regardless of the tumor volume at the time of treatment (Fig. 2). Our simulations also revealed that, even with the same total TST dose, fractionated administrations resulted in a more sustained tumor suppression compared with a single high-dose administration (Fig. 3b). This simulation result aligns with previous reports that repeated TST therapy improved tumor control17,18. One biologically plausible explanation for this finding is that the initial TST administration alters the TME to a therapeutically favorable state. The additional transcriptomic analysis revealed a transient upregulation of immunostimulatory genes in the TME, suggesting temporary enhancement of T cell activity following TST administration (Fig. 5b). Our subsequent single-cell transcriptomic experiment revealed that these immunostimulatory genes were expressed in TIMs, indicating that these TIMs could be contributing to the transient immuno-supportive state of the TME (Fig. 6c,d). These changes may prime the tumor site to respond more effectively to subsequent TST administrations. This is consistent with previous studies indicating that ACT can attract and repolarize myeloid cells into activated effector cells7,19. Collectively, these results suggest that fractionated dosing strategies in ACT can improve its therapeutic efficacy.

These changes in TIM-associated activity and subcluster composition should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect both changes in cell state and fluctuations in the TIM abundance during tumor progression. As indicated by Arginase-1 expression (Fig. 5a), the immunosuppressive activity of TIMs after TST administration increased initially and then declined. This temporal pattern may reflect not only changes in the overall activity state of TIMs but also variations in the number of TIMs that affect the detectable bulk-level signal. Previous reports have shown that TIM numbers decrease from Day 5 to Day 75, suggesting that such fluctuations in cell numbers may contribute to the observed reduction in TIM-associated immunosuppressive activity at the bulk tumor level. Similarly, the reduced relative size of the IFNγ-stimulated monocyte cluster (Cluster 1) observed in the scRNA-seq analysis (Fig. 6c) does not necessarily indicate loss or phenotypic conversion of these immunostimulatory cells, but may instead be explained by relative dilution resulting from the influx or expansion of TAMs (Cluster 0).

Beyond the fixed fractionation schedules, we explored the response-guided TST dosing strategies that adjust administration amounts based on tumor growth dynamics (Fig. 4). Compared with the Single dose regimen, the Adaptive and On/Off strategies achieved at least comparable or improved tumor suppression with a lower total cell dose. In conventional ACT, fixed single dose scheduling is standard, and optimization of dosing schedules based on individual tumor responses has not been extensively investigated. However, recent studies in chemotherapy have demonstrated that tumor dynamics-guided fractionation can improve treatment efficacy10,20,21. Our results suggest that dynamic TST dosing based on real-time tumor response monitoring may similarly improve ACT outcomes in solid tumors. From a translational perspective, repeated recovery of tumor-derived T cells and the timing of ex vivo manipulation pose logistical challenges. To address this, the multi-dosing strategy supported by our model envisions a single cell manufacturing step rather than repeated production cycles. Specifically, a single large-scale expansion followed by cryopreservation of aliquoted doses allows for staged infusions without additional tissue harvesting or long-term culture. This approach is becoming increasingly practical due to advances in manufacturing: streamlined expansion protocols are accelerating TIL production22, while the use of peripheral blood-derived engineered T cells (e.g., CAR-T/TCR-T) further simplifies logistics23. Moreover, response-guided dosing can be implemented using clinically available metrics, such as tumor burden monitored by imaging or circulating tumor DNA, inflammatory markers, or peripheral blood immunosuppression surrogates. We also acknowledge that clinical implementation must account for toxicity monitoring; while not explicitly included in the current mathematical framework, incorporating safety constraints represents an important direction for future model extensions.

Although our model was developed for a murine melanoma ACT setting, the core mechanism–myeloid-driven suppression (here, TIM-mediated immunosuppression)–is widely implicated in limiting ACT efficacy in solid tumors. Therefore, we hypothesize that our main principle–that fractionated and response-guided infusions outperform single-infusion regimens–is potentially translatable to ACT dosing strategies used in other settings, particularly when such suppression is present but not overwhelming. However, in environments with more potent myeloid-driven suppression, our model suggests that the benefits of dosing modulation alone may be limited. Still, our framework serves to quantitatively characterize such suppression mechanisms. This focus on myeloid-driven immunosuppression aligns with increasing evidence supporting use of combinations of strategies involving ACT and immunomodulatory interventions24. While clinical data for combining ACTs with direct myeloid-targeting agents remain limited, strong preclinical evidence supports this approach25, particularly in the context of CAR-T cell therapy8. Pharmacological reduction of TIMs using all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) significantly enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy of GD2-CAR-T cells in sarcoma models, highlighting the functional relevance of myeloid suppression in CAR-T failure and its therapeutic reversibility26. In addition, clinical and translational studies have demonstrated that immune checkpoint blockade can partially restore CAR-T cell activity by reprogramming suppressive myeloid populations27. Consistent with these findings, encouraging preliminary results have been reported in early-phase clinical trials combining mesothelin-targeted CAR-T cells with anti-PD-1 therapy28. We envision extending our framework to incorporate the dynamics of such combinatorial regimens. Through these extensions, our model will serve not only as a tool for dosing optimization but also as a means to determine the optimal timing to integrate immunomodulatory interventions.

We acknowledge several limitations in our work. First, our model assumes a constant cell density to estimate absolute cell numbers, and this assumption ensures applicability to comparable initial tumor burdens. However, linear scaling may not hold for significantly different initial burdens, as unmodeled structural changes–such as necrosis or angiogenesis–alter the actual density. To accommodate these structural variations, it would likely be necessary to modify the mathematical system to reflect the specific tumor physiology. Second, our model is based on the parameters calibrated to the specific immune landscape of murine melanoma. Although our mathematical model incorporates key components–including tumor cells, effector TSTs, exhausted TSTs, TIMs, and IFNγ-mediated tumor suppressive effects–it remains a simplification of the complex TME. Factors such as other immunosuppressive cell types (e.g., regulatory T cells) and polarization of myeloid cells were not considered in this study. Consequently, the current framework is not directly applicable to distinct immune microenvironments where these unmodeled mechanisms are dominant. Expanding the model to capture the greater diversity of tumor landscapes would necessitate incorporating new variables, re-calibrating parameters, or integrating detailed cell profiling data (e.g., scRNA-seq). Third, our models relied on data collected at limited time points, which may restrict the temporal resolution of immune cell dynamics. The full range of immune cell dynamics under multiple dosing regimens is yet to be explored. Although our current model reproduced tumor growth dynamics, future studies should aim to incorporate more detailed temporal immune dynamics.

In conclusion, we performed mathematical modeling and transcriptomic analyses of counter-regulatory mechanisms in ACT and demonstrated that fractionated TST dosing can enhance efficacy of ACT. We believe that the rational design of dynamic, fractionated regimens can further optimize ACT treatment schedules.

Overview of mathematical model development

The aim of our mathematical model was to describe and predict the dynamics of tumor cells, TSTs, and TIMs under different TST dosing schedules. We modeled these dynamics using ODEs. We developed our model in two steps: (i) an untreated tumor model and (ii) a TST-treated model. At each step, we compared multiple plausible model structures and selected the best-fitting one to advance to the next step.

Untreated model

We compared three commonly used models (exponential, logistic, and Gompertz) for untreated tumor growth (Supplementary Table S1)29, where V [cells] is the number of B16 tumor cells. Data summary for modeling

Experimental data for mathematical modeling were obtained from the studies by Hosoi et al.5 and Hirano et al.6, as well as from additional experiments conducted under the same experimental conditions. Briefly, C57BL/6 mice were intradermally inoculated with 1 × 106 B16F10 cells, followed 9 days later by the adoptive transfer of 1 × 107 activated pmel-1 transgenic CD8+ T cells, which recognize the gp100 peptide antigen expressed by B16F10 cells. In this study, these transferred T cells were referred to as tumor-specific T cells (TSTs), and the day of TST transfer was designated as “Day 0,” following the convention used in previous studies5,6,9. Tumor volume measurements were obtained from a total of 34 mice in two experimental groups with or without the adoptive transfer of TSTs (n = 17 per group). Specifically, these data were derived from three experimental datasets: Dataset 1 reported in Hosoi et al.5 (n = 5 per group) and Datasets 2 and 3 reported in Hirano et al.6 (n = 5 and 7 per group, respectively). Tumor volumes were measured longitudinally in the same mice at different time points. Flow cytometry data on transferred TSTs (CD45+CD8+CD90.1+) and TIMs (CD45+CD11b+Gr1+) were obtained from independent experiments conducted under the same experimental conditions as those described in5,6. Since these measurements required sacrificing the mice, each time point represents data from distinct mice (n = 3 per group at each time point, except for Day 5 in the TST-treated group, where n = 5). The numbers of each cell type used for mathematical modeling were estimated from these experimental data, as described in Supplementary Methods. The source data are available in the Supplementary Data: tumor volume measurements are provided in Supplementary Data 1, and flow cytometry data for immune cell populations (including transferred TSTs and TIMs) are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Cell counts of B16F10, TST, and TIM

In this study, our mathematical modeling used the total numbers of B16F10 cells, TSTs and TIMs. All these values were calculated from tumor volume and flow cytometry data. The number of B16F10 cells was estimated from the tumor volume (mm3) under the assumption that 1mm3 of tumor tissue contains 106 cells31. The numbers of TSTs and TIMs in the tumor were calculated using the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Numbers \,of \,TSTs \,and \,TIMs \,[cells]= T \left({\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}}^{3}\right)\times \mathrm{g}\left(\frac{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{g}}{{\mathrm{mm}}^{3}}\right)\times C\left(\frac{cells}{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{g}}\right)$$\end{document}where T is the tumor volume (mm3), g is the scaling parameter converting tumor volume (mm3) to tumor weight (mg), and C is cell concentration obtained from flow cytometry (cells/mg).

To determine this parameter g, we used tumors harvested from TST-treated B16 tumor-bearing mice (n = 15; including 6 tumors that were also used for the scRNA-seq analysis) (Supplementary Data 3) and calculated the ratio of tumor weight W (mg) to tumor volume T (mm3) for each tumor as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g=\frac{W (\mathrm{m}\mathrm{g})}{T ({\mathrm{mm}}^{3})}$$\end{document}

The mean value of g was 1.89 mg/mm3, and this parameter was then applied to compute the total numbers of TSTs and TIMs for each day.

An exception was the Day –1 time point, for which no flow cytometry measurements were obtained; therefore, the corresponding values had to be estimated indirectly. The TST concentration on Day –1 was set to 0, as TSTs had not yet been administered. The TIM concentration on Day –1 was set equal to the Day 1 TIM concentration, since TIM concentrations remained constant over time under untreated conditions and did not differ between untreated and TST-treated groups on Day 1. These observations support the use of the Day 1 value as a reasonable approximation for the Day − 1 TIM concentration in both conditions. These TIM concentration values were then combined with tumor volume measurements to estimate TIM cell numbers used in the model.

Initial conditions of mathematical simulations

The initial conditions on Day -1 used for parameter optimization of the mathematical models were estimated from tumor volume and flow cytometry measurements, as described above. For the untreated condition (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), the initial numbers of B16 cells, TSTs, and TIMs were estimated as 1.4 × 107, 0, and 2.9 × 104 cells, respectively. For the single-dose 1 × 107 TST-treated condition (Supplementary Fig. 3), the initial numbers of B16 cells, TSTs, and TIMs were estimated as 2.5 × 107 (= V0), 0 (= TST0), and 4.2 × 104 (= TIM0) cells, respectively.

For validation simulations (Fig. 2), the initial B16 cell number (= Vv) was set to the mean estimated number of B16 cells in the experimental cohort. Because the initial TIM number was not directly available for this validation cohort, the corresponding initial TIM number (= TIMv) was estimated from the standardized single-dose 1 × 107 TST-treated condition used for parameter optimization. Specifically, assuming that the initial TIM number is proportional to tumor burden, TIMv was calculated as TIMv = TIM0 × Vv/V0. Accordingly, the initial numbers of B16 cells, TSTs, and TIMs were estimated as Vv, 0, and TIMv, respectively.

For simulations under alternative TST dosing strategies, we used the baseline values estimated from the tumors treated with a single-dose 1 × 107 TSTs as common initial conditions. Specifically, the initial numbers of B16 cells, TSTs, and TIMs were set to 2.5 × 107 (= V0), 0 (= TST0), and 4.2 × 104 (= TIM0) cells, respectively.

Parameter estimation

Some of the model parameter values were obtained from the literature, whereas the remaining parameters were estimated by fitting the model to experimental data using global optimization with the Differential Evolution algorithm. The loss function (Eq. 10), which considers the standard deviation of the experimental data, was used to find the best fit of the model parameters:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}Loss= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}{\left(\frac{{y(t}_{i})-\widehat{{y(t}_{i})}}{{\sigma (t}_{i})}\right)}^{2}}\end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y({t}_{i})$$\end{document} is the experimental data at time ti, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma (t}_{i})$$\end{document} is the standard deviation of the experimental data, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{y({t}_{i})}$$\end{document} is the model prediction.

To ensure data integrity, all B16 tumor data for parameter fitting were restricted to Day -1 through Day 11. In the untreated condition, data at Day 13 were excluded because measurements were unavailable for several mice due to humane endpoints, which would otherwise bias the estimated mean. The same fitting window was applied to the TST-treated condition to maintain consistency between conditions.

Parameters were estimated in a stepwise manner. First, the tumor growth rate vr and carrying capacity vk were estimated using the number of B16 cells under the untreated condition. Next, mv were estimated from the number of TIMs under the untreated condition, while keeping the tumor parameters (vr and vk) fixed. Finally, the remaining parameters were estimated using data from the TST-treated condition, including the numbers of B16 cells, TSTs, and TIMs. All estimated parameters are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Treatment simulations

To demonstrate the applicability of our mathematical model, three sets of simulations were performed. In the first simulation, we explored whether sustained tumor control could be achieved by varying the number of TSTs administered as a single dose. In the second simulation, the same total number of TSTs was evenly divided into multiple doses administered over several days. In the third simulation, TST doses were dynamically adjusted over multiple days in response to tumor growth.

Numerical methods and model implementation

Model fitting and simulations were conducted in Python 3.11. ODEs were solved using the odeint function from SciPy 1.11.3.

Mice and cell lines

C57BL/6N female mice (age 6–8 weeks old, weighing 18 g-22 g at the start of the experiments) were obtained from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan). Pmel-1 TCR transgenic mice recognizing the H-2Db-restricted epitope EGSRNQDWL from gp10025-33 were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). All mice were kept in a specific pathogen-free environment. The animal use proposal and experimental protocol have been reviewed and approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tokyo (ID: P10-135, P20-091 and A2023M011). All animal procedures complied with institutional guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines. Efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and the number of animals used, in accordance with the 3Rs principles.

B16F10 melanoma cells is a gp100+ spontaneous murine melanoma cell line, kindly provided by Dr. N. Restifo (National Cancer Institute, MD). These cells were maintained in culture medium consisting of DMEM (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 100 μg/ml streptomycin and 100 U/ml penicillin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation).

For tumor inoculation, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction at 3% and maintenance at 2.0–2.5%) in air at a flow rate of 1.0 L/min using an isoflurane vaporizer (TK-36; Biomachinery, Chiba, Japan). At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by gradual-fill CO2 inhalation at a displacement rate of approximately 30–70% of the chamber per minute.

Bone marrow derived dendritic cells (BMDC) and TST

Bone marrow cells from femurs and tibias were cultured for 8 days in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 mM HEPES (Nacalai tesque), 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), 1% nonessential amino acids (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin and 20 ng/ml recombinant mouse GM-CSF (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). To induce maturation, the cells were stimulated with 1 μg/ml lipopolysaccharide (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) for 16 h and pulsed with hgp100 peptides (KVPRNQDWL) at 1 μg/ml for 2 h.

Splenocytes were isolated from pmel-1 transgenic mice and cocultured with hgp100 peptide-pulsed BMDC for 3 days in the medium containing recombinant human IL-2 (Peprotech) at 50 U/ml. After washing, the cells were administered intravenously.

In vivo experiments for validation of TST dosing regimens

To validate our model’s predictions under several TST dosing conditions, we used tumor volume data from experiments involving one to four doses of 1 × 107 TSTs, administered at 7-day intervals. These experiments were performed under the same experimental conditions as described above5,6, and the data were obtained from the same research group. Tumor volumes were measured from the same mice at multiple time points. These results have not been published previously. Group sizes were: 1 dose, n = 3; 2 doses, n = 3; 3 doses, n = 5; 4 doses, n = 5.

To further validate our model predictions under different TST dose levels, we used tumor volume data from experiments involving B16-fucci tumor-bearing mice. These experiments were performed under the same general conditions as the previous experiments15. Specifically, C57BL/6 mice were intradermally inoculated with 1 × 106 B16-fucci cells, followed 9 days later by the adoptive transfer of activated pmel-1 transgenic T cells at various doses (0, 1 × 106, 2 × 106, 5 × 106, 1 × 107, 2 × 107). These results have not been published previously. Tumor volumes were measured from the same mice at multiple time points. Group sizes were: 0 (no TST), n = 8; 1 × 106, n = 5; 2 × 106, n = 5; 5 × 106, n = 5; 1 × 107, n = 5; 2 × 107, n = 5.

For these in vivo experiments, the experimental unit was a single mouse. Sample size (n) denotes the number of mice per group. In line with prior studies5,6 and the principles of the 3Rs, group sizes were chosen as small as feasible while retaining sufficient precision for model validation. On the day before the first TST administration, mice were grouped to balance baseline tumor volumes among groups (size-matched allocation). Tumor weight was not measured for these tumor growth experiments, as tumor growth was assessed longitudinally using caliper-based measurements of tumor width and length. Administration and measurements were performed at a consistent time of day by the single blinded technician, and the order of caliper measurements was randomized daily.

Analysis of gene-expression microarray data of bulk tumor-tissues

Microarray data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (series GSE57304, samples GSM1379331 to GSM1379344). These data were collected under the same treatment conditions described in the “Data summary for modeling” section. Detailed methods for data acquisition have been described previously. Here, tumor tissue from mice was harvested on different days (1, 3, 5, 7) after TST transfer or on the same day under no treatment (no TST) conditions. Each sample contained 500 ng of pooled RNA from three to four tumors and was subjected to microarray analysis using 45,018 probes to quantify expression levels of target genes (ID: GPL7202). Probes were discarded if the gIsWellAboveBG flag was zero in each sample, otherwise samples were normalized at the 75th percentile.

Single cell RNA-sequencing

For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), mice bearing B16F10 tumors were treated with pmel-1 transgenic TSTs described above (see Data summary for modeling). Tumors were harvested on days 3, 7 and 14 after TST transfer (n = 3 per time point; total n = 9). Tumor measurements associated with the scRNA-seq experiments are provided in Supplementary Data 4 for transparency. This dataset partially overlaps with Supplementary Data 3, with six samples common to both datasets. Tumor tissues were minced and digested in RPMI-1640 containing 0.2% collagenase (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and 2 KU/ml DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 40 min at 37 °C. The digested tissues were passed through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with APC/Cy7-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 antibodies (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and CD45+ cells were sorted using a SH800S cell sorter (Sony, Tokyo, Japan). At each time point, equal numbers of CD45⁺ cells from three mice were pooled to mitigate variability between mice. Accordingly, the experimental unit for scRNA-seq was the pooled library. One pooled library per time point was generated (three libraries in total), in line with the 3Rs. The pooled cells were subjected to scRNA-seq using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3.1 and Chromium Controller (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, quantified using KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche Sequencing Solutions, Pleasanton, CA, USA) and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-G400 platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China). Sequence reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using Cell Ranger software (version 4.0.0, 10× Genomics).

All downstream analyses were performed using Seurat (v5.1.0) in R within a Docker environment based on ShortCake (v3.3.0)32. Cells expressing fewer than 200 or more than 5,000 genes, or exhibiting high mitochondrial gene content (> 10%) were excluded to remove low-quality cells. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were also excluded. All count data were normalized to correct for differences in sequencing depth across cells, and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction and integration across time points. The merged data were scaled and subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. Unsupervised clustering was performed using a graph-based algorithm. After initial clustering, clusters composed of cells with low total RNA counts per cell and without biologically coherent marker expression were considered low-quality and excluded, and variable feature selection, scaling, PCA, and downstream dimensionality reduction were repeated on the filtered dataset. Canonical marker gene expressions and SingleR package (v2.6.0)33 with the ‘ImmGenData’ reference dataset34 were used to annotate major cell clusters (TIMs, NK cells, T cells, mregDCs, B cells, pDCs and cDC1s). For preranked gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the TIM cluster, differential expression analysis between Day 3 and Day 7, and between Day 3 and Day 14 was performed using a Wilcoxon rank sum test, and genes were ranked by log2 fold-change. Preranked GSEA was performed using the Hallmark gene sets from the MSigDB database (mouse version, v2025.1)35–37. For TIM sub-clustering, the TIM cluster was extracted and reanalyzed. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were repeated at higher resolution to assess heterogeneity within the TIM compartment. FindAllMarkers function in Seurat was used to identify marker genes in TIM subclusters, and enrichGO function in clusterProfiler (v4.12.6)38,39 was used to perform Gene Ontology enrichment analysis.
