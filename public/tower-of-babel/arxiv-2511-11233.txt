Abstract
. Table reasoning with the large language models (LLMs) is a fundamental path toward building intelligent systems that can understand and analyze over structured data. While recent progress has shown promising results, they still suffer from two key limitations: (i) the reasoning processes lack the depth and iterative refinement characteristic of human cognition; and (ii) the reasoning processes exhibit instability, which compromises their reliability in downstream applications. In this work, we present STaR (slow-thinking for table reasoning), a new framework achieving cognitive table reasoning, in which LLMs are equipped with slow-thinking capabilities by explicitly modeling step-by-step thinking and uncertainty-aware inference. During training, STaR employs two-stage difficulty-aware reinforcement learning (DRL), progressively learning from simple to complex queries under a composite reward. During inference, STaR performs trajectory-level uncertainty quantification by integrating token-level confidence and answer consistency, enabling selection of more credible reasoning paths. Extensive experiments on benchmarks demonstrate that STaR achieves superior performance and enhanced reasoning stability. Moreover, strong generalization over out-of-domain datasets further demonstrates STaR’s potential as a reliable and cognitively inspired solution for table reasoning with LLMs. 1 1 1 https://github.com/zhjai/STaR



1.
Introduction

Reasoning over structured data is fundamental for building intelligent systems across diverse domains, from web search and business intelligence to scientific discovery and clinical decision support. Among various structured formats, tabular data is particularly widespread and valuable, serving as the primary medium for organizing knowledge in scientific publications, financial reports, and enterprise databases. The prevalence of such data, combined with recent AI breakthroughs

However, effectively solving foundational tasks like table question answering and fact verification remains challenging, requiring the integration of multiple capabilities: precise information retrieval, natural language understanding, multi-step logical reasoning, and accurate numerical computation.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding and generation, suggesting potential for unified reasoning interfaces across both unstructured text and structured tabular formats

(

have unlocked powerful reasoning abilities, enabling models to decompose complex problems into intermediate steps. Furthermore, reinforcement learning approaches like PPO
(
34

and tool-augmented methods
(
15

have enhanced accuracy on quantitative reasoning tasks, demonstrating the potential of these models for structured data understanding.

However, despite these advancements, LLM-based table reasoning remains fundamentally limited in two key aspects. The reasoning processes often lack the depth and iterative refinement characteristic of human cognition, relying instead on shallow pattern matching rather than genuine understanding of table structures and logical relationships. Most existing methods generate final answers directly from prompts without explicitly modeling intermediate reasoning steps, which limits interpretability and verification capabilities. Moreover, these processes exhibit significant instability, with performance varying substantially across different query types, input phrasings, and table formats, compromising their reliability in real-world applications that demand consistent and trustworthy outputs. These limitations highlight critical challenges: how to guide LLMs toward coherent and interpretable reasoning trajectories, how to train models that generalize stably across diverse table schemas, and how to quantify uncertainty at the trajectory level to identify and suppress unreliable outputs.

To address these fundamental challenges, we propose STaR (slow-thinking for table reasoning), a cognitively inspired framework that equips LLMs with deliberate step-by-step reasoning capabilities and trajectory-level uncertainty awareness. Our key insight is that reliable table reasoning requires moving beyond surface-level pattern matching to develop genuine understanding through systematic thinking, while ensuring this reasoning remains stable across diverse scenarios. During training, STaR implements a carefully designed paradigm combining slow-thinking dataset construction with two-stage difficulty-aware reinforcement learning (DRL). We begin by establishing foundational reasoning patterns through self-verified high-quality demonstrations, then progressively challenge models from handling simple queries to mastering complex multi-step reasoning that requires synthesizing information across entire table structures. This approach employs dynamic sample filtering and composite rewards to guide the learning process effectively. During inference, rather than relying on single-pass generation, STaR produces multiple reasoning trajectories and employs sophisticated uncertainty quantification (UQ). By fusing token-level confidence with answer consistency, our framework identifies and selects the most credible reasoning path, effectively transforming latent pass@k
(
2
)
potential into reliable pass@1 performance. Our main contributions are:

•

We introduce a cognitive table reasoning framework that equips models with slow-thinking capabilities through progressive difficulty-aware training, enabling them to develop human-like patterns for complex table understanding.

•

We design trajectory-level uncertainty quantification that fuses token-level confidence with answer consistency, converting generation uncertainty into reliable outputs.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on multiple table reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating that STaR achieves superior performance, enhanced stability, and strong generalization across domains and tasks.

3.
Methodology

This section presents STaR, a cognitive table-reasoning framework with three parts: slow-thinking SFT data, two-stage difficulty-aware RL, and uncertainty quantification for reliable path selection.

3.1.
Framework Overview

STaR is a framework for cognitive table reasoning that integrates three core components: slow-thinking dataset construction, two-stage difficulty-aware reinforcement learning, and trajectory-level uncertainty quantification, as illustrated in Figure
1
. The framework employs structured prompts with self-verification mechanisms to construct high-quality training data. STaR then adopts a two-stage reinforcement learning paradigm: stage 1 foundationally trains from simple examples with minimal steps, while stage 2 progressively masters hard samples through iterative training with dynamic queries filter. During inference, STaR generates multiple reasoning trajectories and quantifies their reliability by integrating token-level entropy with answer consistency, enabling selection of the most credible path through weighted fusion. This trajectory-level uncertainty quantification ensures both accuracy and stability in table reasoning tasks.

3.2.
Slow-Thinking Dataset Construction

To establish foundational cognitive reasoning abilities for table understanding, we construct a high-quality training dataset from WikiTableQuestions, HiTab, and FinQA through an answer-aware generation approach. Specifically, we provide DeepSeek-R1 with the table, question, and ground truth answer, prompting it to generate complete reasoning trajectories.

A key contribution of our data construction is the self-verification mechanism, where the model compares its generated answer against the ground truth and automatically filters out samples with inconsistent outputs. This approach not only ensures alignment between reasoning traces and final answers but also removes potentially ambiguous or mislabeled data from the training set, significantly improving data quality and reliability.

The generated demonstrations follow a structured format: <think> reasoning process </think> followed by <answer> final answer </answer>, where the thinking section contains detailed chain-of-thought reasoning and the answer section provides structured JSON output. This format embodies the slow-thinking paradigm, encouraging models to engage in deep, deliberate, and multi-step reasoning before reaching conclusions. Through this dataset with self-verified quality control, models acquire essential and diverse table reasoning abilities including systematic information extraction, multi-step reasoning, self-reflection, and structured answer generation, laying a solid foundation for the difficulty-aware reinforcement learning phase that follows.

3.3.
Reinforcement Learning

3.3.1.
Enhanced GRPO

Following recent advances in long CoT training, we adopt DAPO’s enhanced GRPO framework that removes the KL divergence penalty and employs asymmetric clipping bounds. The KL removal allows the model to diverge from its initial distribution when discovering complex reasoning patterns essential for table reasoning, while the asymmetric clipping strategy encourages exploration of low-probability tokens that are critical for novel reasoning strategies. These modifications are particularly important for table reasoning, where diverse problem-solving approaches across different table structures require flexible policy adaptation. Our group relative policy optimization (GRPO) objective becomes:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(q,a)\sim\mathcal{D},\left\{o_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old }}}(\cdot\mid q)}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{G}|o_{i}|}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\operatorname{min}\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i,t}\right.\right.}

\displaystyle\left.\left.\operatorname{clip}\left(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon_{\text{low }},1+\varepsilon_{\text{high }}\right)\hat{A}_{i,t}\right)\right],

This stage establishes basic reasoning patterns swiftly without the additional complexity of processing difficult samples. Notably, using a single-stage approach would require extensive time for the model to learn and adaptively filter these easy samples at lower learning rates, which would reduce the efficiency of training.

where

The two-stage design with different learning rate schedules is more efficient than single-stage training, as stage 1’s rapid acquisition of foundational knowledge eliminates the need for stage 2 to process simple samples. This allows it focused refinement on complicated reasoning challenges.

3.3.3.
Reward Function Design

We employ a composite reward function that evaluates both structural compliance and content accuracy through three weighted components. Format compliance (0.2) ensures that the <think> and <answer> tags are correctly aligned and that the answer section is in valid JSON format. This component is essential because incorrect outputs simply cannot be parsed or evaluated at all. Partial correctness (0.3) awards credit when predictions align with any part of multi-answer ground truth lists. This encourages gradual progress toward complete accuracy by providing incremental rewards during training. Complete correctness (0.5) provides the strongest learning signal for exact matches with ground truth. The final normalized reward
R=0.2\times R_{\text{format}}+0.3\times R_{\text{partial}}+0.5\times R_{\text{complete}}
balances between structural requirements and accuracy. It ensures that models learn to generate both parseable and correct outputs while facilitating progressive learning through partial credit mechanisms.

3.4.1.
Token-Level Confidence Metrics

We quantify the reliability of individual reasoning trajectories by computing token-level confidence metrics from the model’s internal probability distributions. For each generated trajectory consisting of tokens
\{t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{n}\}
, we calculate the average log-probability and average entropy:

(3)

\text{logprob}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(t_{i}|t_{<i},x),

(4)

\text{entropy}(y)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v|t_{<i},x)\log p(v|t_{<i},x),

where
x
represents the input query,
\mathcal{V}
denotes the vocabulary, and
p(t_{i}|t_{<i},x)
is the conditional probability of generating token
t_{i}
given the previous tokens.

3.4.2.
Consistency-Confidence Fusion Algorithm

Our trajectory-level uncertainty quantification integrates both token-level confidence and answer-level consistency through a principled fusion approach, addressing the limitations of using either metric in isolation. Relying solely on answer consistency (majority voting) would overlook infrequent but correct reasoning paths. This is particularly apparent in challenging cases where most paths lead to plausible but incorrect solutions. Conversely, focusing exclusively on token-level confidence can result in misguided trajectories when the model displays excessive confidence in its flawed reasoning. Therefore, we utilize a weighted fusion strategy that balances both signals to achieve robust trajectory selection, as shown in Algorithm
1
.

The mathematical formulation of our fusion strategy combines three normalized components:

(5)

S(a)=0.25\cdot\frac{|G_{a}|}{|G_{\text{max}}|}+0.2\cdot\frac{\bar{C}_{a}}{C_{\text{max}}}+0.55\cdot\frac{C_{a}^{\text{max}}}{C_{\text{max}}^{\text{global}}},

where
|G_{a}|
represents the consistency count for answer
a
,
\bar{C}_{a}
is the average confidence within answer group
a
, and
C_{a}^{\text{max}}
denotes the maximum confidence score in group
a
.

3.4.3.
Weight Calibration and Analysis

We utilized grid search optimization
(
1
)
across several benchmarks to identify the optimal weight distribution for our uncertainty-aware selection. The weights were assigned as follows: 0.25 for average confidence, 0.2 for consensus, and 0.55 for maximum confidence.

The strong emphasis on maximum confidence (
w_{\text{max}}=0.55
) is critical for addressing the "minority-correct" phenomenon common in table reasoning, where the correct solution is often a single high-quality outlier. Maximum confidence is excellent at identifying these crucial traces which metrics based on consensus would miss. Furthermore, this trajectory-level metric is more stable than aggregated scores, as it avoids compounding noise from the top-k sampling used for entropy estimation. This allows our framework to reliably convert pass@k potential into pass@1 performance.

4.1.2.

We evaluate STaR using a wide range of state-of-the-art baselines across various categories to ensure a comprehensive comparison across different reasoning paradigms. 4.1.3.
Implementation Details

We employ Qwen3 0.6B and 8B as base models in STaR and train on 4×NVIDIA A800 80GB GPUs. The supervised fine-tuning stage employs a batch size of 256 with a learning rate of 1×
10^{-5}
for 3 epochs. We utilize the Qwen3 0.6B model to split datasets for the two-stage GRPO training. The pass@32 accuracy threshold is 0.6. Stage 1 rapidly establishes foundational capabilities using batch size 512, 5 rollouts per sample, and learning rate 1×
10^{-5}
over approximately 10,000 easy samples for 20 fixed training steps. Stage 2 focuses on challenging samples with batch size 256, 8 rollouts, and learning rate 1×
10^{-6}
with decay rate 0.01. This stage involves training for 168 steps, resulting in a total of 188 steps across both stages, which aligns with the number of steps used in single-stage GRPO baselines for a fair comparison. Both stages use temperature 1.0, generation length 4096 tokens, and asymmetric clipping bounds [0.2, 0.28]. During inference, we generate 8 rollouts per query with temperature 0.6 and maximum length 4096 tokens, enabling comprehensive uncertainty estimation for optimal path selection.

4.7.
Case Study Analysis

Figure
6
illustrates STaR’s slow-thinking reasoning process on a representative table question answering task. When asked "In which competition did Hopley finish first?", the model demonstrates deliberative cognitive reasoning rather than immediate pattern matching. The thinking process begins with explicit task identification, where the model recognizes the need to scan for Position = "1st" across all table entries. STaR reviews the rows from earliest to latest and locates the first-place finish at the 2000 World Junior Championships. Crucially, rather than stopping at the first match, the model performs self-verification by continuing to examine the remaining years (2007–2008), confirming that this represents the only instance of a first-place finish and thereby avoiding premature or incomplete conclusions. This deliberative process showcases key cognitive capabilities gained through our slow-thinking training: systematic reasoning that methodically processes structured data, self-verification that ensures answer completeness, and clear logical progression from observation to conclusion. The case demonstrates how STaR’s training paradigm develops to produce human-like deliberation patterns. This enables accurate and interpretable reasoning over tables and contrasts.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced STaR, a framework that equips LLMs with slow-thinking capabilities for cognitive table reasoning. To address two fundamental limitations in existing approaches, namely insufficient reasoning depth and process instability, STaR integrates two core mechanisms: two-stage difficulty-aware reinforcement learning that progressively guides models from simple to complex queries through dynamic sample filtering and enhanced GRPO optimization, and trajectory-level uncertainty quantification that fuses token-level confidence with answer consistency to select reliable reasoning paths from multiple rollouts. Extensive experiments show that STaR achieves state-of-the-art performance on in-domain benchmarks while exhibiting generalization to out-of-domain datasets, with consistent improvements across model scales validating the framework’s effectiveness. Future efforts could extend STaR across multi-table scenarios, incorporate visual table understanding, and explore the applicability of our difficulty-aware mechanism to alternative structured reasoning domains.
