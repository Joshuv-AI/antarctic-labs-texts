Exploring feature sparsity for out-of-distribution detection
Authors: Qichao Chen, Kuan Li, Zhiyuan Chen, Tomas Maul, Jianping Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection is a crucial problem in practice, especially, for the safe deployment of machine learning models in industrial settings. Previous work has used free energy as a score function and proposed a fine-tuning method that utilized OOD data in the training phase of the classification model, which achieves a higher performance on the OOD detection task compared with traditional methods. One key drawback, however, is that the loss function parameters are highly dependent on involved datasets, which means it cannot be dynamically adapted and implemented in others settings; in other words, the general ability of the energy score is considerably limited. In this work, our point of departure is to enlarge distinguishability between in-distribution features and OOD data. Consequently, we present a simple yet effective sparsity-regularized (SR) tuning framework for this purpose. Our framework has two types of workflows depending on if external OOD data is available, the complexity of the original training loss is sharply reduced by adopting this modification, meanwhile, the adapted ability and detection performance are enhanced. Also, we contribute a mini dataset as a light and efficient alternative of the previous large-scale one. In the experiments, we verify the effectiveness of our framework in a wide range of typical datasets along with common network architectures.

Introduction

Deep nerual networks (DNN) have achieved high accuracy in many application domains over the past years, such as computer vision (CV)1,2. However, it is worth mentioning the well-known assumption3 in the DNN field, whereby the training and testing data must be sampled from the same distribution; otherwise, the performance of DNN models at inference time cannot be ensured. This assumption is a particularly strong and strict condition in the real open world.

The open world is full of unknowns and unanticipated objects and events, therefore we cannot expect that the model will always only meet ideal inputs in an actual production environment thus producing precisely expected outputs. Instead, a model deployed in practice will inevitably encounter a wide range of inputs that have a large covariate shift relative to the training data. Such distribution shifts may lead to serious consequences since the model still attempts to classify the input data into a certain category, even though it may not belong to any known classes in terms of the given training dataset. The above issue gives rise to the importance of OOD detection, which aims to determine whether the input data is in-distribution or OOD data.

In fact, OOD detection is an essential and imperative issue if DNNs are applied to industrial systems or daily applications; especially, in high-risk tasks like autonomous driving4 and medical treatment5. For example, a DNN model in a self-driving car might give dangerously incorrect instructions in a totally strange surrounding when it should not have taken any action and instead should have given control back to the driver, thus, leading the vehicle to make a wrong decision, which possibly will lead to an accident. It is the same with other high-cost or safety-critical scenarios.

The problem of OOD detection was formally defined by Hendrycks and Gimpel6 in 2017, where it was observed that the predicted probability of OOD examples tend to be lower than that of the in-distribution data. The aim of the paper was to evaluate whether an input sample comes from the ID regarding the well-trained DNN model, and they introduced benchmark datasets and metrics for this purpose, and also established the baseline performance by using Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP) as the score to detect OOD examples. Later on, a lot of follow-up works devoted to present various problem-solving strategies and propose a higher dimensional benchmark, whose details will be discussed at the next Section.

OOD detection is a binary classification problem, which means there exists one ideal threshold that is capable of dividing ID and OOD instances completely. In this paper, we observe that few previous works pay attention to the sparsity in the feature space while this property is likely to have an huge impact on OOD detection, because the ideal threshold is calculated based on the input features extracted by the DNN model and this mapping function is sensitive to the sparsity of feature. Thus, there should have a close relationship between the sparsity of features and final scores.

According to the theoretical analysis by Yu et al.7, what deep learning do during its optimization process seems to discover a compressed representation from the provided input training data. In oder to capture sufficient information, the dimention of representation learning from model is quite high. We presume the learned representation from a DNN model exists some redundant information in high-dimensional space, and as such, this information is useless either for classification or OOD detection. Based on this assumption, we devote to explore the impact of sparsity for feature representation of models, and thereby minimizing the deleterious effect of these redundant features for downstream tasks as much as possible.

Concretely, we propose a sparsity-regularized method to fine-tune a well-trained classification model, which makes learning feature representation more sparse. As a result, it improves the performance of OOD detection without negatively affecting its classification performance. Furthermore, vanilla Outlier Exposure8 introduced an auxillary dataset into the training or fine-tuning process. It demonstrated that an external dataset can help the model discover more discriminative representations without significantly harming its classification ability. Taking into account this perspective, we also consider using the auxilliary dataset in our sparsity-regularized framework, and then present Sparsity-Regularized Outlier Exposure (SROE), which indeed bring this advantage to our method and further improves the performance.

Besides, the above available auxilliaray OOD dataset seems suboptimal for the benchmark of CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, because there has an extremely huge gap in terms of their size of dataset — the size of CIFAR-10 or CIFAR-100 is approximately 180M while that of current OOD auxilliary dataset is more than 230G. In this case, we build an another mini dataset as alternative of previous bloated one, and the size of it is only around 40M. Accordingly this new dataset reduces the redundancy of the auxilliary dataset significantly and increases the training efficiency. Related works

Out-of-distribution detection

The closed-world assumption of machine learning models is usually hard to hold in the open world setting, where a model will inevitably meet out-of-distribution examples. Yet neural networks are likely to produce over-confident but unreasonable predictions for those unknown examples, Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection is thus an essential and imperative problem when DNN models are employed in practical systems.

The basic concept of OOD detection is to to identify and reject examples that comes from the unfamiliar distribution, then transferring decision-making power to human beings for further safety handling. There are some comprehensive survey on the research community11–13, and14 analyzed OOD detection in some practical settings from a mathematical aspect, and gave theoretical support for a series of representative works via this theory. Existing OOD detection pipeline can be broadly categorized into generative modeling and discriminative modeling approaches.

Generative modeling approaches

Generative modeling learns the data distribution, with the aim of understanding and simulating how data is generated, enabling it to produce new instances that resemble the training data, it also can estimate the likelihood of the given real input. Generative models can be roughly categorized into four types: Varriational autoencoder-based (VAE)15,16, Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)17,18, Flow-based models19,20, and Diffusion21,22 models.

One line of work perform OOD detection for generative models, using reconstruction error or likelihood as an anomaly score function is a natural choice because they enable the given input to generate new samples and calculate likelihood accordingly. However,23 found that deep generative models, trained on CIFAR-10, unexpectedly assign a higher likelihood values to certain OOD datasets such as MNIST. Subsequent works attempt to overcome this drawback from different points of view, MemAE24 introduced a memory module to present a memory-augmented autoencoder.25 formulated a new autoencoder, which maximumly compress its latent space while ensure its reconstructive power. FRL26 incorporated high-frequency information into training and then guided the model to focus on semantically relevant features. Some works had also proposed different score measurements at test-time27–29.

Discriminative modeling approaches

A separate line of methods addressed OOD detection for discriminative models, those models are usually considered as typical deep learning classification. The majority of OOD detectors rely on classification-based approaches, which derive anomaly scores directly from a deep learning classifier. Nguyen et al.30 first noticed the overconfidence phenomenon of neural networks to OOD data. MSP6 is the most basic baseline of OOD detection, it de facto coined the term “out-of-distribution detection” and showed how to evaluate deep learning based OOD detectors. ODIN31 improved the baseline performance by introducing two manipulations, temperature scaling and input preprocessing, for a pre-trained classification model. Generalized ODIN32 further proposed to decompose confidence and modify the input preprocessing approach. Watermarking33 added watermarking at the data-level by reprogramming the pre-trained model.

OE8 found that leveraging external OOD dataset can bring benefit to OOD detection, subsequent works continue to explore34–38 along this technical route. Notably, the auxilliary dataset used here is the large-scale one, using our tiny can accelerate the reimplementation process of above methods. Apart from MSP score, several score functions were proposed based on pretrained classification models. Mahalanobis39 calculated Mahalanobis distance between test samples and modeling class-conditional Gaussian distribution. GramNorm40 utilized information extracted from the gradient space to compute the OOD score. EBD41 considered discriminative classification models as energy-based model and consequently derived free energy as an excellent score function, which is very close to our work.

In this paper, we explore the impact of sparsity in the feature space of a well-trained classification model, and present a novel sparsity-based framework to significantly enhance the OOD detection performance, which is a simple, easily implementable, and yet effective measure. DICE42 also proposed to rank weights of a pre-trained model via sparsification, while our work is based on fine-tuning and exhibits better performance as indicated by our experimental results.

Sparsity-regularized framework

Technically, the features usually refer to the outputs of the neural network’s final fully-connected layer in a classical deep learning model, then the next layer is the output layer where the output vectors are calculated by features through a linear transformation, called logit, and its dimensionality is equal to the number of classification categories. Lastly, by imposing a softmax normalized operation to logits, we can obtain the predicted softmax vector, which can be used for updating parameters with true labels using cross-entropy loss.

This work pay attention to the feature space — the output space of fully-connected layer. Our intuitive motivation is that the feature vector extracted from a DNN model inevitably contains an amount of redundant information, which is useless both for classification and detection tasks. In other words, the redundancy of feature vectors is likely to be harmful for OOD detection. It might be beneficial to reduce this redundancy to induce sparse vectors, improving the performance of OOD detection while maintaining classification accuracy.

Sparsity regularization

To achieve this goal, we attempt to increase the sparsity of feature vectors and relieve the effect of redundancy. Concretely, we divide the flow of features in the neural network into two paths, one is used for passing to the next output layer for the training classification task as usual, and the other is specifically used for the sparsification of feature vectors.

Sparsity-regularized framework.

Consequently, we expand our sparsity-regularized framework under the case when there has available OOD dataset, forming our Sparsity-Regularized Outlier Exposure (SROE) approach. The basic contextual condition is silmilar to the case of SR. there just has another optimization term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{OE}$$\end{document} and the learning objective is as below:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{SROE} = \mathscr {L}_{CE} + \alpha \cdot \mathscr {L}_{Sparsity} + \beta \cdot \mathscr {L}_{OE}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In the SROE setting, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{CE}$$\end{document} is used for maintaining classification accuracy on ID data and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{OE}$$\end{document} for forcing the predictive probability on OOD samples to tend towards the uniform distribution. As with the SR approach, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{Sparsity}$$\end{document} is used to increase the sparsity of feature vectors extracted from the neural network on ID data. the hyperparameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha >0$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta >0$$\end{document} control the trade-off among terms. The overall pipline is shown in Fig. 1, composed of both solid and dashed lines.

By combining the above three loss terms and adjusting their weights, the overall aim of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{SROE}$$\end{document} is to maintain the performance of classification, while generating sparser vectors on ID data and more uniform vectors on OOD data in the softmax space. In practice, we can adopt different strategies based on whether \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document} is available or on the specific requirements of the task, requiring only very small adjustments, which means our method is flexible and scalable, as well as easy to implement in real tasks.

A new auxiliary OOD dataset.

Currently, in the OOD detection research community, the common auxiliary outlier dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document} for small benchmarks, like CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, is 80 Million Tiny Images9. However, there seems to be a dilemma regarding its efficiency and accessibility due to two reasons:

Firstly, as its name indicates, the dataset contains 80 million images and its volume size is larger than 230GB, compared with when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document} is CIFAR-10 or CIFAR-100, where both contain 50 thousand training images with a volume of about 180MB, which is much smaller compared to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document} in this setting. Secondly, the content for 80 Million Tiny Images was automatically collected from the Internet, which means it inevitably contains some derogatory and offensive images. Thus, it is difficult for people to visually recognize its contents, and manual inspection, even if feasible, fails to guarantee that prejudiced images are absent43.

Considering the problem that there is no available \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document} in benchmarking CIFAR-10 or CIFAR-100 in the context of OOD detection, it is necessary and imperative for the community to create a usable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document} dataset. Thus, this situation motivated us to rebuild an alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document}. In general, a plausible auxiliary dataset is required to meet two constraints: (1) its content must be relatively diverse so that it can be a representation of unknown ODD data in some way; (2) its content must be completely disjoint with respect to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document} and OOD test data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{test},$$\end{document} in case the model knows the pattern of OOD samples in advance.

In practice, we use the Tiny ImagesNet44 as a data pool, which contains 200 categories out of the well-known ImageNet dataset45. It meets diverse constraints in terms of the CIFAR-10 or CIFAR-100 benchmark. At first, the data pool is filled with a wide variety of data. We adopted K-means46 alongside manual inspection to filter the data pool, in order to remove instances that were similar to instances in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{test}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document}. The data pool is shown in Fig. 3 where samples with an “out” label can be kept, whereas samples with an “in” label must be removed.

As a result, we built a usable alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document} to 80 Million Tiny Images, which we refer to as “tiny” due to the fact that it is extremely small, just 40MB. Results

Experimental setup

There are mainly three steps to detect OOD samples in our framework. Firstly, we define a CNN architecture and use the standard cross-entropy loss to train a classifier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_\theta$$\end{document} on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document}; then, we choose a suitable sparsity-regularized loss, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{SR}$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{SROE}$$\end{document}, and fine-tune \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_\theta$$\end{document} to get \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_\theta ^\prime$$\end{document}; finally, we load the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_\theta ^\prime$$\end{document} model and perform inference on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{test}$$\end{document}.

What is worth mentioning is that we cannot use our method to train a classifier directly at the beginning, since over-sparse features might have a negative impact on the learning process on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document} at early stage. Only once the classifier has converged, does the sparsification process avoid being harmful for classification on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document} while also benefiting detection on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{test}$$\end{document}.

In-distribution datasets

For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document}, we use a benchmark commonly used in OOD detection, namely CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, both of which consist of 60,000 32x32 natural color images, with 50,000 for training and 10,000 for testing. As indicated in the name, CIFAR-10 contains 10 categories whereas CIFAR-100 represents 100 categories47. The datasets are disjoint but have similarities. For example, CIFAR-10 has the “automobiles” and “trucks” classes, whereas CIFAR-100 has the “pickup truck” class, in the “vehicles 1” superclass.

Out-of-distribution datasets

For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{test}$$\end{document}, we use a wide range of evaluation benchmarks, which were rigorously selected to ensure that the semantic knowledge in these datasets did not overlap with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document}. Specifically, we use six OOD common test datasets \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{out}^{test}$$\end{document} to evaluate our method, including fine-grained images, scene images, textural images, and others. All the considered datasets are listed here: iSUN48, SVHN49, Texture50, Places36551, LSUN-Crop and LSUN-Resize52. In addition, we use “tiny” as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{out}^{OE}$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{SROE}$$\end{document} that requires extra data during the fine-tuning process.

Evaluation metrics

As for the evaluation metrics for the OOD detection task, we mainly adopt four representative metrics that are commonly recognized in this area: (1) FPR-95 describes the correlation between false positive rate (FPR) of OOD examples and the true positive rate (TPR) of ID, which can be interpreted as the probability that an OOD example is misclassified as ID when the TPR is at 95%; (2) AUROC is the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which tells us how much the OOD detector is capable of distinguishing between ID and OOD samples; (3) AUPR is the area under the Precision-Recall (PR) curve, which can be viewed as a complementary matrix of AUROC; (4) In-dist Error is an important measurement for DNN models, since a good model is required to be able to distinguish OOD samples without losing the ability to classify ID samples.

Performance of sparsity regularization

In this experiment, we employ the above mentioned four neural networks on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{in}$$\end{document} to train a classifier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_\theta$$\end{document}. Ablation studies

To study the effectiveness, scalability and characteristics of the our method, we further conduct several ablation studies. Originally, the energy score function was used as our departure point in the derivation stage, thereby it was employed when calculating the score of input samples in all of the above experiments.

Evaluation on different score function

Accordingly, we believe the advantage of sparse features is not only limited to energy score functions but is also useful for other score functions. Therefor, we deploy some other classical score functions to evaluate the performance of our method. The results verify our empirical expectation, namely that our method improves the detection performance on those score functions consistently, and, of course, that the energy-based score achieves the best results among them. The Tables 5 and 6 shows the detailed results, which illustrates the general scalability of our method.

Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, a novel sparsity-regularized framework was proposed for OOD detection. Well-trained deep learning based classifications frequently exhibit many redundant features in their feature spaces. These redundancies are useless for classification and harmful for OOD detection. A sparse feature space can bring benefits to OOD detection if such useless features can be eliminated, thereby increasing the distinguishability between ID and OOD samples.

Our proposed method is a simple yet effective scheme, and is easy to be employed. Experimental results show that our method establishes a new state-of-the-art performance on the OOD detection task. In the future, we will continue to explore the sparse property in high-dimensional space, and will explore the possibility of applying it to other related machine learning fields, like audios and videos etc.
