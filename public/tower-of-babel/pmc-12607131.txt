Progressive multifocal diffusion-weighted imaging hyperintensities in sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease with positive cerebrospinal fluid real-time quaking-induced conversion: a case report

Background

Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) is a rare yet devastating and invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorder classified under the transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSE) family. Its core pathological hallmark is the uncontrolled accumulation and aggregation of the abnormally folded prion protein (PrPSc) within the central nervous system, leading to widespread neuronal death and characteristic spongiform vacuolation of brain tissue [1]. The disease typically exhibits a rapidly progressive course, presenting with a complex and diverse clinical syndrome. Early manifestations frequently include progressive dementia, swiftly followed by significant neurological dysfunction encompassing myoclonus, cerebellar ataxia, visual disturbances, as well as pyramidal or extrapyramidal signs and dysarthria [2]. Patient deterioration is swift, often culminating in coma and death within a short period. Diagnosing CJD poses significant challenges and relies heavily on the comprehensive assessment of clinical presentation supplemented by investigations including electroencephalography (EEG), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). For familial cases, PRNP gene testing can identify causative mutations. The emergence of the real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) assay, which enables the highly sensitive detection of minute quantities of PrPSc in samples such as CSF [1], has substantially enhanced antemortem diagnostic accuracy. This case report presents the clinical progression of a 74-year-old East Asian male with a definitive diagnosis of sporadic (s)CJD, established through clinical features and ancillary diagnostic investigations.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old East Asian male initially presented in March 2024 with episodes of dizziness, headaches, and memory impairment, particularly affecting recent recall. By July, his condition had markedly deteriorated, characterized by exacerbated dizziness, a progressive decline in speech function, and increasing difficulty in ambulation. Despite prior treatment for cerebral infarction, there was no clinical improvement. He had a history of smoking and diabetes but did not report other chronic conditions or hereditary diseases. Neurological examination unveiled cognitive impairment, evidenced by a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 19 and a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score of 13. The patient exhibited delayed responsiveness and diminished facial expressions, with a positive glabellar tap sign. Bilateral nystagmus and diplopia were present. Motor examination revealed grade 5 muscle strength, with normal muscle tone and tendon reflexes. In addition, fine motor skills on the left side were impaired, and he presented with cerebellar ataxia, balance disturbances, and ataxic gait. The APOE gene was positive for the ε3/ε4 allele.

Cerebral perfusion imaging demonstrated a significant reduction in blood flow perfusion in the bilateral frontal lobes and the right temporal lobe, with a mild decrease noted in the left basal ganglia. Dynamic EEG showed a predominance of slow waves in the left temporoparietal region, accompanied by spike and sharp-slow wave discharges, which were more pronounced in the left temporal area.

Sequential brain MRI scans over a 6-month period illustrated a progressive expansion of scattered punctate and linear high-signal intensity lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) across multiple cortical regions of the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes (Fig. 1).

The patient’s diffusion-weighted imaging follow-up images over 6 months. In the patient’s follow-up images over 6 months, multifocal punctate or linear diffusion-weighted imaging hyperintensities (indicated by arrows) in the cortical and subcortical regions of the left frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes, as well as the bilateral occipital lobes, progressively enlarged as the disease progressed

The CSF appeared slightly turbid, with normal levels of nucleated cells, protein, and chloride. No evidence of bacterial or fungal infection was identified. Tests for autoimmune encephalitis antibodies, a panel of 19 cerebellar ataxia-related markers, 14–3-3 protein isoforms, and genetic sequencing for the PRNP gene associated with hereditary prion diseases all yielded negative results. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of the CSF was also negative. However, the RT-QuIC assay for abnormal prion protein detection returned a positive result (Fig. 2). RT-QuIC assay was performed on the basis of our earlier publication [1]. Briefly, the patient’s CSF was mixed with RT-QuIC reagents and incubated and shaken intermittently for 90 hours. Fluorescence signals were recorded every 30 minutes, and the result was regarded positive if the end reading signals from two wells were both twice the background reading at the beginning of the test or higher among the quadruplicated wells.

The significant fluorescence signal changes of the real-time quaking-induced conversion assay for abnormal prion protein detection. Fluorescence signals were recorded for every 30 minutes for 90 hours. The blue squares represent the combined signal readings (relative fluorescence units) on patient’s sample, while red on positive control and green on negative control samples. The green line represents the negative response, the red line represents the boundary of the positive response, and the blue line represents the patient's outcome

On the basis of the abovementioned findings and in accordance with international diagnostic criteria for CJD, the patient exhibited rapid cognitive decline, signs of cerebellar ataxia, and additional symptoms such as speech disturbances, dizziness, and urinary dysfunction indicative of autonomic nervous system involvement. Furthermore, the patient’s CSF was positive for the RT-QuIC assay, and serial brain MRIs showed multiple scattered DWI hyperintensities in cortical and subcortical regions. EEG revealed the presence of spike-and-slow-wave discharges. Given these clinical and diagnostic findings, a diagnosis of probable sporadic CJD was strongly considered. The attending physician and the chief physician thoroughly communicated the condition to the patient’s family, informing them about the disease progression and prognosis. They demonstrated understanding and remained cooperative with treatment and follow-up. Although no curative options were available, the patient expressed appreciation for the transparent communication and supportive care provided.

Discussion

CJD represents a rare and invariably fatal neurodegenerative condition affecting the central nervous system, characterized by its subacute stroke-like onset, rapid progression, and grim prognosis, with the average patient age at diagnosis hovering around 67 years [2]. According to data compiled by the International Surveillance Network EuroCJD in 2015 and the National CJD Research and Surveillance Unit (NCJDRSU) in 2018, the global incidence of CJD is estimated to range from 1 to 2 cases per million individuals annually, with a noted upward trend over time. International epidemiological studies suggest a median survival duration of approximately 4 months following diagnosis. The most recent figures from China, as of 2024, indicate that the median survival period for patients diagnosed with sCJD stands at 5 months [3], reflecting a slight variance in prognosis when compared to global averages.

The global diagnostic criteria for CJD primarily adhere to the standards established by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1998, which emphasize a thorough evaluation of clinical symptoms and signs, complemented by investigations such as EEG and CSF examinations. Since 1998, there have been no substantial revisions to these criteria. In contrast, the 2021 Chinese guidelines for CJD incorporate updated diagnostic techniques and methodologies, giving due consideration to modern diagnostic tools such as MRI, genetic testing, and laboratory assessments. In this case report, the clinical presentation aligns with the characteristics of sporadic CJD. The diagnosis is further substantiated by positive findings from cranial MRI and RT-QuIC testing. These elements collectively underscore the value and significance of this case report in the context of contemporary diagnostic practices for CJD.

Previous findings have indicated that in patients with CJD, simultaneous involvement of the cortex and striatum is the most common finding [4, 5]. Previous studies have typically highlighted the precentral sparing sign on DWI [6], alongside progressive enlargement of hyperintense gray matter lesions in the basal ganglia [7]. Further multivariate analyses have proposed that restricted diffusion in the caudate nucleus and putamen is an independent prognostic factor associated with lower overall survival rates in sporadic CJD [8]. However, a case report from July 2024 described an atypical finding of persistent, isolated high signals in the precentral gyrus [9]. In contrast, the present case identifies a progressive expansion of multifocal DWI hyperintensities across the cortical and subcortical regions of the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes. This finding adds to the existing body of evidence regarding the radiological manifestations of CJD, offering additional insights into the disease’s radiological manifestations.

Conclusion

This case report details a diagnosis of sporadic CJD, corroborated by clinical symptoms and ancillary diagnostic techniques. The patient’s rapid cognitive decline and cerebellar ataxia raised initial suspicions. Progressive enlargement of multifocal punctate and linear DWI hyperintensities in the cortical and subcortical regions further supports the diagnosis. Finally, the positive RT-QuIC test, which specifically targets prion aggregates, provided a crucial clue toward diagnosing CJD. Ongoing follow-up and monitoring are crucial for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and ensuring optimal management.
