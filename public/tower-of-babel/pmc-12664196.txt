Cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the Persian version of mindfulness during worship scale (MWS) among medical students
Authors: Akram Heidari, Zahra Saboohi, Morteza Heidari, Mahmoud Shokouhi-Tabar, Zahra Taheri-Kharameh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMindfulness during prayer, characterized by focused attention and present-moment awareness in worship, is linked to improved psychological and spiritual well-being. In Islamic practice, mindful engagement in prayer (Salah) is a core spiritual concept. To assess this construct in the Iranian context, a culturally and linguistically validated instrument is required. This study aimed to adapt the Mindfulness during Worship Scale (MWS) into Persian and evaluate its psychometric properties among Iranian university students.MethodsThe MWS was translated into Persian following a standard forward-backward translation procedure. A sample of 170 Iranian university students completed the Persian MWS and the Spiritual Well-Being Scale (SWBS). The scale’s factor structure was examined using a measurement model assessment within a Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) framework. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR), while criterion-related validity was assessed by examining correlations with spiritual well-being.ResultsThe original three-factor structure (Concentration, Presence, Absorption) was confirmed. Two items exhibited factor loadings below 0.5. The Persian MWS demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with composite reliability (CR) values ranging from 0.76 to 0.85 for the subscales. Convergent validity was supported, with Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values above 0.50 for most subscales. A significant positive correlation was found between the total MWS score and spiritual well-being (r = .37, p < .01).ConclusionThe Persian MWS is a valid and reliable tool for assessing mindfulness during worship in a Persian-speaking Muslim population. Its strong psychometric properties support its use in research settings to explore the role of mindful prayer in health psychology and spiritual well-being. Future studies may refine the scale further to enhance its applicability.Keywords: Mindfulness, Prayer, Psychometrics, Students, Iran

Introduction

Spirituality and mindfulness are increasingly recognized in health psychology as important components of mental well-being. A large body of research demonstrates positive associations between religious/spiritual involvement and psychological health outcomes, such as lower levels of depression, suicidality, and substance use [1]. Likewise, mindfulness—commonly defined as purposeful, present-moment awareness without judgment—has been shown to enhance subjective well-being and reduce emotional distress [2]. Although both constructs contribute to mental health, they represent distinct dimensions: spirituality emphasizes broader experiences of connection with the transcendent, whereas religiosity refers to one’s commitment to specific religious beliefs and practices. In the context of prayer, prior studies indicate that religiosity, more than spirituality, often exerts a stronger influence on the quality and depth of worship experiences [3].

Prayer is a central act of devotion in Islam and an important spiritual practice for millions of Muslims worldwide, including in Iran. Recent psychological research has investigated the role of attentional qualities during prayer, often conceptualized as mindfulness during worship. This construct emphasizes being fully attentive, present, and absorbed in the act of worship, avoiding automatic or distracted performance of rituals [4–8]. Studies in Muslim communities have shown that individuals who perform prayer with higher levels of mindfulness report better mental health, enhanced emotion regulation, and greater well-being than those who engage in prayer without such attentional presence [9].

The construct of mindfulness during worship comprises three interrelated sub-domains: attention, awareness, and absorption [10]. Attention refers to the intentional focus on the words, meanings, and movements of prayer, as opposed to its habitual or mechanical performance. Awareness reflects the worshipper’s conscious monitoring of internal experiences—such as thoughts, emotions, and bodily sensations—while maintaining a sense of spiritual presence and connection with God. Absorption denotes a deep, immersive state in which the individual becomes fully engaged in the act of worship, often described as a feeling of spiritual flow or unity with the Divine. These dimensions parallel the core elements of secular mindfulness theory [11], yet they are uniquely situated within a religious framework, emphasizing awareness that is God-centered rather than neutral. Defining these conceptual domains helps clarify the theoretical foundation of the scale and allows for more precise evaluation of item relevance in the current study.

Despite the growing interest in mindful worship, few tools exist to assess this construct. Most existing measures focus either on secular mindfulness or on general religiosity, without capturing the attentional dimensions of prayer. To address this gap, Yousaf et al. (2022) developed the Mindfulness during Worship Scale (MWS)—a 15-item measure that evaluates concentration, presence, and absorption in worship. The MWS demonstrated strong reliability (α = 0.81–0.87) and validity in initial studies (9). However, no Persian-language version of this scale currently exists. Considering that daily prayer is normative in Iran and that health psychologists increasingly emphasize holistic care that integrates spiritual resources, a culturally adapted instrument is needed to assess mindfulness during worship.

The present study therefore aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and validate the Persian version of the MWS among Iranian medical students. Medical students represent an important population because they experience high academic stress and may rely on spiritual coping strategies such as prayer. We hypothesized that the Persian MWS would show a clear factor structure, strong internal consistency, and meaningful correlations with spirituality measures, reflecting its potential utility for research and practice in health psychology.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional validation study involved translation and psychometric evaluation of the Persian MWS. The original English MWS items were translated and culturally adapted following published guidelines [12]. After developing the Persian version, we administered it to a sample of medical students to evaluate its factor structure, reliability, and validity.

Translation and cultural adaptation

The MWS was translated according to standard forward–backward translation procedures [12]. First, two independent bilingual experts translated the English MWS into Persian. The synthesized version was then back-translated into English by two new bilingual translators who were blinded to the original scale. The back-translated version was compared with the original MWS to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence. Finally, the pre-final Persian version was pilot-tested with a small group of students (n ≈ 10) to assess clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness. Minor revisions were made to improve wording and remove ambiguity.

Content validity was examined using a qualitative approach. A panel of ten experts—including four clinical psychologists, two public health faculty members, and four scholars of religious studies—reviewed the Persian items for clarity, wording, relevance, and cultural appropriateness. Their diverse expertise ensured that the items were evaluated from both psychological and religious perspectives. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made to improve comprehension and clarity. These revisions confirmed that the items were understandable, culturally appropriate, and adequately reflected the intended construct of mindfulness during worship.

Participants

Participants were Iranian medical students recruited from Qom University of Medical Sciences using convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria were fluency in Persian, regular practice of Islamic prayer, and enrollment in a medical program. Students with a diagnosed major psychiatric disorder or incomplete survey data were excluded. A total of 170 students completed the study. The sample size was considered adequate based on psychometric recommendations suggesting 5–10 participants per item [13]. Given that the MWS consists of 15 items, a minimum of 150 participants was required; thus, the obtained sample exceeded this threshold, ensuring sufficient statistical power for validation analyses.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected between March to June 2024 at Qom University of Medical Sciences, Iran. After receiving ethical approval from the institutional review board (Approval No. IR.MUQ.REC.1402.159), data collection was conducted in classroom settings. Before participation, the research team introduced the study objectives and procedures, assuring students of anonymity and confidentiality. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to completing the questionnaire.

Measures

The MWS [10] contained 15 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = “never” to 5 = “always”) reflecting frequency of mindful attention during prayer. Items cover aspects such as concentration, presence, and absorption in the prayer experience. Higher scores indicate greater mindfulness in prayer. In the original validation study, the English MWS showed good internal consistency across its subscales (α = 0.81–0.87; Yousaf et al., 2022).

The Spiritual Well-Being Scale (SWBS) [14], developed by Paloutzian and Ellison (1982), was used to assess the criterion validity of the MWS. This 20-item scale measures two dimensions of spiritual well-being: Religious Well-Being (RWB), which reflects the perceived relationship with a higher power, and Existential Well-Being (EWB), which relates to a sense of purpose and life satisfaction. Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “strongly disagree” to 6 “strongly agree”. The total spiritual well-being score is derived by summing the RWB and EWB subscale scores. The Persian version of the SWBS has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in Iranian samples, including high internal consistency (α > 0.85) and established convergent validity with measures of psychological well-being [15].

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qom University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUQ.REC.1402.159). The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained, and participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Data analysis

Psychometric analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27) and SmartPLS 4.0 within a Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) framework [13]. Descriptive statistics were computed for all items to examine distributional properties and to screen for missing values or outliers. To evaluate construct validity, a measurement model assessment was performed, treating the MWS as a second-order construct composed of three first-order latent dimensions: Concentration, Presence, and Absorption. Factor loadings (λ), path coefficients (β), and model fit indices were examined.

Convergent validity was evaluated based on three criteria: (1) factor loadings ≥ 0.50 and statistically significant, (2) Average Variance Extracted (AVE) ≥ 0.50, and (3) Composite Reliability (CR) ≥ 0.70. Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion, whereby the square root of AVE for each construct should exceed its correlations with other constructs [16]. Criterion-related validity was examined via concurrent validity by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients between the MWS total and subscale scores and scores from an established external measure—the Spiritual Well-Being Scale (SWBS). Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable) and Composite Reliability (CR) to provide a more comprehensive estimate of reliability.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The sample comprised 170 university students, including 102 females (60.0%) and 68 males (40.0%), drawn from diverse academic fields. The mean age was 22.4 years (SD = 3.1, range 18–29). The mean self-reported GPA was 16.4 (SD = 1.2) on a 0–20 scale. Descriptive statistics for the Persian MWS indicated moderate levels of mindfulness in prayer. Mean (SD) scores were: Concentration M = 3.73 (SD = 0.58, range 2.0–5.0), Presence M = 3.82 (SD = 0.54, range 2.2–5.0), Absorption M = 3.65 (SD = 0.64, range 1.8–4.9), and Total MWS M = 3.74 (SD = 0.46, range 2.4–4.9).

Measurement model evaluation

A measurement model assessment was conducted using PLS-SEM in SmartPLS 4 to examine the factor structure of the Persian MWS. All items loaded significantly on their respective subscales, with standardized loadings ranging from 0.50 to 0.80 (p <.001), except for two items on the Presence subscale (Items q11 and q12), which exhibited low factor loadings (λ = 0.36 and λ = 0.34, respectively) (Fig. 1).

Criterion-related validity

Criterion-related validity was further supported through significant positive correlations with the Spiritual Well-Being Scale. The total MWS score correlated moderately with overall spiritual well-being (r =.37, p <.01). Each MWS subscale also demonstrated positive associations with RWB and EWB. Specifically, the Concentration subscale was correlated with RWB (r =.25, p <.05) and EWB (r =.18, ns); the Presence subscale was associated with RWB (r =.32, p <.01) and EWB (r =.22, p <.05); and the Absorption subscale showed smaller but significant correlations with RWB (r =.21, p <.05) and a non-significant correlation with EWB (r =.09) (Table 3).

Internal consistency

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability coefficients. The total MWS scale demonstrated excellent reliability (α = 0.810). Subscale reliability estimates were also acceptable: Concentration (α = 0.769), Presence (α = 0.634), and Absorption (α = 0.632). While Cronbach’s alpha values for Presence and Absorption were slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.70, their composite reliability indices (ρc > 0.70) support adequate internal consistency (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study validated the Persian version of the Mindfulness during Worship Scale (MWS) in a sample of Iranian medical students. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the original three-factor structure of the MWS (concentration, presence, absorption), consistent with prior validations in other cultural and religious contexts [10, 17].

Cronbach’s α coefficients ranged from 0.632 to 0.810, indicating acceptable reliability given the conceptual complexity and cultural adaptation involved. Importantly, all composite reliability (CR) values exceeded 0.70, underscoring adequate internal consistency even where α was slightly below the conventional threshold. The somewhat lower α for the Absorption subscale likely reflects the context-specific nature of this dimension in Iranian Muslim worship, where collective and ritualistic forms of prayer may limit expressions of deep personal absorption typically observed in more individualized practices.

The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for the overall scale and the Absorption subscale was slightly below the recommended 0.50 threshold, which is common in exploratory or cross-cultural validation studies where items are retained for theoretical and content validity reasons. As Henseler et al. (2015) note, composite reliability provides a more robust indication of internal consistency in such cases, and our CR values were satisfactory [18]. The decision to retain items with lower loadings on the Absorption factor was made to preserve the scale’s theoretical integrity and capture the full breadth of the mindfulness-in-worship construct.

Regarding discriminant validity, the Fornell–Larcker criterion revealed a potential overlap between the Absorption and Presence subscales. The correlation between these factors exceeded the square root of the AVE for Absorption, suggesting partial convergence. This pattern implies that within Iranian culture, presence and absorption during prayer may not be experienced as fully distinct dimensions but rather as interconnected aspects of spiritual focus and immersion.

Criterion validity was supported through correlations with spiritual well-being, which were low to moderate (r =.25–0.40). These findings were theoretically expected: mindfulness during prayer captures attentional and experiential processes specific to worship, whereas spiritual well-being encompasses broader existential and relational domains. Therefore, moderate correlations indicate that while the two constructs are related, they are not redundant. This distinction underscores the importance of assessing mindfulness in worship as a unique psychological and spiritual process rather than as a general reflection of spirituality [19].

Two items (q11 and q12) showed lower factor loadings in the Iranian sample. These items may not fully resonate with Iranian cultural and religious practices, such as collective prayer (jama’ah) and emphasis on ritual precision, compared to more contemplative or individualistic prayer styles elsewhere. Despite lower loadings, these items were retained to preserve the theoretical breadth of the Absorption dimension and maintain content validity.

Despite the issues with some items, the MWS aligns well with the Islamic practice of mindful engagement during prayer. Qualitative studies, such as Callender et al. (2022), support this, noting that Muslim women view prayer as enhancing awareness and significantly improving mental health (22). In the Iranian context, where religion and faith are integral to daily life, the MWS effectively captures how young adults integrate mindfulness into their devotional experiences. Younger generations in Iran may blend contemporary mindfulness approaches with traditional Islamic spirituality, and our findings indicate that the MWS is both culturally relevant and psychometrically sound for this population.

Our results also show parallels with international validations of comparable instruments. Yousaf et al. (2022) reported a three-factor structure among Christians [10], and later confirmed similar dimensions in a British Muslim sample [17]. Together with our findings among Iranian students, this suggests that mindful worship may share a comparable underlying structure across at least two major faith traditions, though further cross-cultural research remains necessary. Implications for health and well-being

The validated Persian MWS offers utility for health psychology and spiritual care in Iran. By quantifying mindful prayer, clinicians and researchers can examine its role in coping and well-being. For example, the present linkage between MWS scores and spiritual well-being implies potential benefits: higher mindfulness in prayer may reflect greater resilience or life satisfaction. This resonates with findings that regular, mindful prayer is associated with better mental health. Ijaz et al. (2017) reported that Muslims who prayed regularly and mindfully had significantly higher mental health scores than those who did not [9]. Likewise, interventions incorporating Islamic mindfulness have reduced anxiety and depression in student samples: the DAHAGA program for nursing students in Indonesia demonstrated that an app-based Islamic spiritual mindfulness practice significantly lowered depression [20], and an Islamic spiritual mindfulness intervention reduced anxiety among Iranian nursing students [21]. Such evidence suggests that the MWS could serve as an outcome or screening tool in interventions aimed at student well-being or clinical mental health. In student counseling or public health initiatives, assessing mindfulness-in-prayer might help identify those who benefit from spiritual-based coping strategies. Overall, the Persian MWS has potential applications in both research and practice related to psychological health, spiritual care, and educational settings.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the use of a sample comprised exclusively of university students from Qom limits the generalizability of the findings to other demographic groups (e.g., different age ranges, educational backgrounds, or geographical regions within Iran). Additionally, the relatively small sample size may have affected the statistical power of the analyses, potentially limiting the robustness of the findings. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and cannot assess the stability of the measure over time. Third, the reliance on self-report measures introduces the potential for social desirability bias, particularly given the culturally valued nature of mindful prayer, which participants may feel compelled to overreport.

Regarding the psychometric properties, while the scale demonstrated convergent validity with spiritual well-being, its relationships with other relevant constructs (e.g., general mindfulness, psychological stress, neuroticism) remain to be established to fully delineate its nomological network. Furthermore, as a newly adapted instrument, its sensitivity to change (responsiveness) and its criterion validity in predicting clinical mental health outcomes have yet to be demonstrated.

Future research should directly address these limitations. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish the measure’s test-retest reliability and its predictive validity for long-term psychological well-being. Applying the Persian MWS in diverse clinical and community populations (e.g., individuals with depression or anxiety, patients coping with chronic illness) will be crucial for clarifying its utility in applied health contexts. Comparative studies could fruitfully explore how mindful prayer relates to and diverges from secular mindfulness constructs (as measured by scales like the FFMQ) or contemplative practices in other faith traditions.

From a practical standpoint, this instrument holds promise for clinical practice and intervention research. It could be employed as an assessment tool to evaluate the role of mindful prayer in clients’ coping repertoires. Furthermore, intervention studies are needed to investigate whether explicitly integrating mindful prayer into therapeutic frameworks (e.g., Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) improves outcomes for clients for whom spirituality is a salient resource. The MWS could also serve as a key outcome measure in trials evaluating the efficacy of programs designed to enhance spiritual mindfulness.

Conclusion

This study validates the Persian version of the Mindfulness during Worship Scale (MWS) among Iranian university students, demonstrating its psychometric reliability and validity. The scale’s three-factor structure (Absorption, Concentration, and Presence) was supported, with satisfactory internal consistency. While some issues with convergent and discriminant validity were noted, particularly regarding overlap between the Absorption and Presence subscales, these findings are culturally relevant and warrant further investigation. Criterion-related validity was supported by moderate correlations with spiritual well-being, suggesting that mindful prayer is linked to better spiritual health. The scale also demonstrated good reliability, with acceptable Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability values, indicating strong internal consistency. However, limitations, including the small sample size and the focus on university students from Qom, suggest the need for broader and more diverse samples in future studies. Longitudinal research is also needed to assess the scale’s long-term stability and predictive validity.

Overall, the Persian MWS shows promise for both research and clinical use in assessing mindfulness during prayer, offering insights into its role in spiritual and psychological well-being in Iranian Muslim populations.
