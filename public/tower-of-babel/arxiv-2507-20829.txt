Phase structure of below-threshold harmonics in aligned molecules: a few-level model system Preprint: APS/123-QED
Samuel Schöpa 1, Falk-Erik Wiechmann 1,2, Franziska Fennel 1,2, Dieter Bauer 1,2

Abstract
We utilize few-level model systems to analyze the polarization and phase properties of below-threshold harmonics (BTH) in aligned molecules. In a two-level system (TLS), we find that the phase of emitted harmonics undergoes a distinct change. For harmonics with photon energies below the transition between the dominant field-dressed states, the phase alternates by π \pi between successive odd harmonic orders but remains constant above. Exploiting this behavior, we construct a four-level model composed of two uncoupled TLS subsystems aligned along orthogonal directions. We demonstrate that with selected transition frequencies lower-order harmonics follow the polarization of the linearly polarized driving field while higher-order harmonics exhibit a mirrored polarization. The model predicts that aligned systems with orthogonal transition dipoles may show analogous phase and polarization features in the BTH regime.



The mechanisms underlying below-threshold harmonics (BTH) in high-harmonic generation (HHG) from molecules driven by short, intense laser pulses differ fundamentally from those responsible for the generation of higher-order (plateau) harmonics. In the gas phase, the latter typically involve a single active electron undergoing tunnel ionization, followed by quasi-free propagation and recombination with the parent ion
Kulander et al. 1993
;
Corkum 1993
. In contrast, below-threshold, non-perturbative harmonics are produced by bound electrons, and the relevant processes are governed by the time-dependent populations of the electronic states in the molecule and the related time-dependent dipole induced by the electrons. Compared to above-threshold harmonic orders, the properties of the generated lower orders in molecules are much more target sensitive, as the geometric structure of the ion configuration and the many interacting electrons determine the electronic structure and the dipole transition matrix elements.
Its sensitivity to electronic structure might make BTH a valuable tool for high-harmonic spectroscopy
Soifer et al. 2010
;
Dong et al. 2015
;
Xiong et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
while their potential for VUV frequency comb generation
Yost et al. 2009
;
Zhu et al. 2021
;
Xiong et al. 2017
has further expanded their relevance. As a result, interest in this regime has grown significantly in recent years.
The superposition of multiple microscopic contributions, such as laser-driven continuum like motion and bound-bound transitions
Yost et al. 2009
;
Xiong et al. 2017
, makes BTH spectra exceptionally difficult to interpret and analyze.

While most studies apply semi-classical analyses to model BTH
Xiong et al. 2017
, these approaches obscure the role of discrete electronic transitions for polarization properties of the emitted harmonics. To isolate and understand these contributions, we turn to few-level model systems where specific transitions, dipole orientations, and resonances can be precisely controlled. Such simplified models offer a framework to investigate the mechanisms responsible for the complex polarizations of BTH from aligned molecules.
Despite the simplicity of few-level models, they cannot be solved analytically beyond certain edge cases like the rotating-wave approximation or approximate treatments using Floquet-Green formalisms
Martinez 2005
although such approaches have also revealed nontrivial harmonic phase behavior. For example, emitted plateau harmonics exhibit abrupt
\pi
phase jumps at specific Rabi frequencies
Gauthey et al. 1997
.
These investigations were carried out in a parameter regime with extremely high field strengths where most population transfers between the adiabatic states occur at very distinct times. In such cases analogies to the three-step model can be drawn
Figueira De Morisson Faria and Rotter 2002
.
To relate the TLS to BTH in molecular systems, we will investigate the dependence of the harmonic phase in a TLS at more moderate field-strengths and from there construct the most simple system that can create a very anisotropic response to differently oriented linearly polarized laser fields. That is, by combining two decoupled two level systems, we can create a HHG target that will have a ’counter-rotating’ polarization dependence and can emit harmonics that are linearly polarized and orthogonal to the driving polarization.

We start by investigating a TLS with the field-free Hamiltonian

This plot shows several features:

At
\omega_{10}/\omega_{L}=1

\Omega=d\cdot E_{0}

For small deviations from
\omega_{10}/\omega_{L}=1
these satellite peaks move according to the change in the generalized Rabi frequency
\sqrt{\Omega^{2}+(\omega_{L}-\omega_{10})^{2}}

|\omega_{L}-\omega_{10}|\ll|\omega_{L}+\omega_{10}|

Outside strict resonance, non-harmonic spectral features appear due to coupling between Floquet states. The primary resonance manifests with satellite peaks spaced by integer multiples of
2\omega_{L}
, reflecting transitions involving adjacent Floquet quasi-energy states

.

We observe harmonic orders up to the 17th. In Fig.
2

\omega_{10}/\omega_{L}

Gaarde and Schafer 2001

Whenever
\omega_{10}/\omega_{L}

;
Gaarde and Schafer 2001
;
Ganeev et al. 2006
;
Son et al. 2009
;
Ivanov and Kheifets 2008

We observe that harmonic-phase flips correlate with the position of the Floquet multi-photon resonance. Below the resonance the phase alternates between

This observation is similar to the observation of Gauthey
et al.
where a change in the Rabi frequency causes jumps of the harmonic phase precisely at locations of avoided crossings of Floquet states

. However, in this work we investigate the harmonic phase as a function of the frequency of the driving field.

In a system with more than two states this phase behavior can lead to distinct traces in the optical response even when one is not looking at the phase in particular.

The intensity shows maxima for a driving field polarized along

All harmonics are linearly polarized with the angle

The polarization of harmonics 1-7 follow the polarization of the laser field, with a different slope, because in the
x
- and
y
-subsystems the phase of these emitted harmonics share the same value.
This behavior changes for the higher orders where we have the aforementioned phase-shift.
For small deviations from the polarization angle
\alpha
, the harmonics stay roughly linearly polarized in
x
. But as
\alpha
is increased we observe a rotation of the harmonics’ polarization in the opposite direction. The cause of this is that the
y
-contributions to the higher orders are phase-shifted exactly by
\pi
, effectively mirroring the polarization vector about the
x
-axis. This leads to the ’counter-rotation’ and harmonics that are polarized at an
90^{\circ}
angle to the fundamental.

While this behavior looks similar to the two-center interference observed in HHG in aligned diatomic molecules
Lein et al. 2002
, the microscopic origin is distinctly different and not caused by the interference of two contributions but by different electron dynamics in
x
- and
y
-direction due to the transition frequencies
\omega_{21x}
and
\omega_{30y}
. We imagine that this effect might be observed in molecular targets with two distinct axes and excited states that have transition dipoles along both axes.
Crystalline polyacenes, recently shown to generate high-order harmonics with remarkable efficiency
Wiechmann et al. 2025
, meet this criterion and present a highly promising platform thanks to their intrinsic molecular alignment.
Their direction-dependent electronic structure makes them well-suited for probing features such as those presented here. Whether continuum contributions in the generation of BTH in molecules eliminate the observed features remains to be seen.
In future work, our improved understanding of anisotropic phase features might advance existing HHG-based orbital tomography in aligned molecular targets
Itatani et al. 2004
. Focusing on the BTH may enable more detailed reconstructions sensitive to excited-state dynamics.
