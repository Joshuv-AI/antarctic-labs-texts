High intention, low uptake: HPV vaccination knowledge, attitudes, and behavior among women attending a tertiary gynecology clinic in Turkey
Authors: Esra Öncü, Zeynep Ece Utkan Korun, Melis Gökçe Koçer Yazıcı, Mert Yeşiladalı, Rukset Attar, Necip Erkut Attar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHPV vaccination prevents cervical cancer, yet uptake remains variable, particularly where it is not publicly funded. A structured questionnaire assessed sociodemographics, HPV and vaccine knowledge (scored 0–10 and 0–6), attitudes (seven 5-point Likert items), and vaccination history. Background

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is among the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide and is the necessary cause of nearly all cases of cervical cancer [1, 2]. HPV is also implicated in a substantial proportion of vulvar, vaginal, anal, penile, and oropharyngeal cancers, as well as anogenital warts [3]. Globally, cervical cancer remains the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer in women, accounting for an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths each year, with the great majority occurring in low- and middle-income countries [1, 3].

Prophylactic HPV vaccination offers effective primary prevention against the oncogenic HPV types responsible for the most HPV-attributable diseases [4, 5]. Population-level data from regions that have integrated HPV vaccination into routine immunization programs indicate substantial reductions in HPV infection prevalence, high-grade cervical lesions, and, most recently, invasive cervical cancer [4, 6]. The World Health Organization’s global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem includes a target of vaccinating 90% of girls by age 15 by 2030, alongside cervical screening and treatment goals [7].

The achievement of vaccination targets is contingent not only upon the efficacy of the vaccine but also on its uptake at the population level, which is influenced by awareness, attitudes, and access. The extant literature suggests that the expressed willingness to vaccinate often surpasses actual coverage, with the discrepancy between intention and behavior attributable to a combination of informational, attitudinal, and structural factors [8–10]. Cost has frequently been identified as a prevalent barrier in contexts where HPV vaccination is not publicly funded [8, 10]. Additional factors influencing vaccination decisions include healthcare provider recommendations, perceived vaccine safety, knowledge of HPV transmission and its oncogenic potential, and engagement with preventive gynecological care [11, 12].

In Turkey, HPV vaccination is available through individual purchase rather than the national immunization schedule. Existing studies in Turkey have reported varying levels of HPV awareness across different population groups [13, 14]. Internationally, systematic review evidence has shown that healthcare workers exhibit heterogeneous HPV knowledge and vaccine acceptance levels, even in settings where vaccination is publicly funded [15]. However, data describing both stated vaccine acceptance and actual vaccination uptake, along with their associated factors, among adult Turkish women attending gynecology services remain limited.

The present study was conducted to address this gap. Our objectives were to: (i) elucidate the knowledge, attitudes, and sources of information regarding HPV among adult women attending an outpatient gynecology clinic; (ii) ascertain the prevalence of both stated acceptance of the HPV vaccine and actual vaccine uptake within this cohort; (iii) identify the reasons provided for non-vaccination; and (iv) investigate the sociodemographic and health-behavioral factors associated with vaccine acceptance and uptake.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive, cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted at the outpatient gynecology clinic of Yeditepe University Hospital — a private tertiary-care center in Istanbul, Turkey — between January 2026 and April 2026. The aim was to evaluate women’s knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and HPV vaccination, and to identify factors associated with both intended and actual HPV vaccine acceptance.

Participants

Women aged 18 years or older who attended the outpatient clinic during the study period and provided written informed consent were eligible. Exclusion criteria were: (i) refusal to participate; (ii) inability to read or understand Turkish; (iii) incomplete or inconsistent questionnaire responses; and (iv) being under active treatment for cervical cancer or another HPV-related malignancy. Women with a self-reported history of HPV infection without active disease were eligible and included, with HPV history treated as an exposure variable.

A non-probability convenience sampling method was used. This single private tertiary-care setting was deliberately selected to provide efficient access to an adult female population with regular gynecological engagement for an initial assessment of the intention-uptake gap, which strengthens internal validity but limits the generalizability of the findings. Sample size was estimated based on previous cross-sectional studies of similar design [13, 14], assuming an expected proportion of 50% for the primary outcome (HPV vaccine acceptance) — the most conservative assumption — with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, yielding a minimum requirement of 270 participants. Anticipating potential data loss, the recruitment target was set at 300. A total of 306 women completed the questionnaire during the study period; one participant under 18 years of age was excluded because she did not meet the inclusion criteria, leaving 305 valid questionnaires for analysis.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-developed questionnaire prepared by the research team based on a review of the existing literature on HPV knowledge, attitudes, and vaccine acceptance [11, 13, 14]. The instrument was non-validated and intended for descriptive purposes; accordingly, knowledge and attitude items are reported and interpreted at the item level, and no claim is made regarding its psychometric validity as a measurement scale. It was reviewed for face and content validity by two specialists in obstetrics and gynecology and administered as a paper-based, face-to-face questionnaire; no formal pilot testing was performed prior to data collection, which is acknowledged as a limitation. The original Turkish version (as administered), its English translation, and the participant consent form are provided as Supplementary Material.

The questionnaire comprised five sections:

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics: age, education level, marital status, occupation, number of children, smoking status, self-reported history of HPV infection, history of regular gynecological examination, and history of Pap smear testing within the previous three years.

The HPV knowledge score was dichotomized at the sample median (median = 7) into “lower” (< 7) and “higher” (≥ 7) knowledge categories for stratified analyses. As the instrument is study-specific with no established cut-off and the score was left-skewed (range 1–10, mean 7.25, median 7), a data-driven median split was used for stratified tables; the continuous score is additionally reported to confirm that findings are not an artifact of dichotomization. Two distinct vaccine-related outcomes were defined: (i) vaccine acceptance, operationalized as a response of “agree” or “strongly agree” to the statement “I would consider receiving the HPV vaccine,” reflecting intention; and (ii) actual vaccine uptake, operationalized as a self-reported “yes” response to “Have you received the HPV vaccine?“.

Healthcare worker status was defined as employment in any clinical or allied health profession, including physicians, nurses, midwives, dentists, paramedics, medical secretaries, laboratory technicians, biochemists, psychologists, pharmacists, physiotherapists, dietitians, and audiologists.

Questionnaires were administered face-to-face by trained researchers in a private setting, requiring approximately 10–15 min to complete. No personal identifiers were collected.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Yeditepe University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 202510Y0946, dated 07 November 2025). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Data were analyzed anonymously.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 with the SciPy, statsmodels, and pandas packages. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± SD, median (range), or frequency (percentage).

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Group comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U or t-test for two groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test or ANOVA for three or more groups, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared with the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman’s correlation was used for continuous variables, and Cronbach’s alpha for the internal consistency of attitude items.

Multivariable binary logistic regression identified independent factors associated with vaccine acceptance and uptake; education, healthcare worker status, knowledge category, regular gynecological examination, Pap smear history, HPV infection history, and age were entered simultaneously. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Model calibration was summarized using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and Nagelkerke R2. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data were analyzed on a complete-case basis; no imputation or interpolation was performed. Data cleaning was limited to standardizing free-text categorical entries recorded in inconsistent letter case or spelling (for example, education level) and to applying the pre-specified exclusion criteria: one participant aged under 18 years was removed, yielding 305 of 306 records. Denominators are reported per item, and items with missing responses are reflected in the corresponding per-item counts.

Results

Sociodemographic and health characteristics

Among the 306 women who completed the questionnaire, one participant was excluded due to being under 18 years of age, resulting in a final sample size of 305 women. The mean age of the participants was 38.1 ± 10.6 years, with an age range of 18 to 79 years. A majority of the participants held a university degree (n = 209, 68.5%) and were married (n = 183, 60.0%). Healthcare professionals constituted 17.4% of the sample (n = 53), and approximately half of the participants (n = 154, 50.5%) reported having no children. A self-reported history of HPV infection was noted by 17.4% of the women (n = 53), while 24.6% (n = 75) were uncertain about their HPV status. The majority of participants reported attending regular gynecological examinations (n = 227, 74.4%) and undergoing Pap smear testing within the preceding three years (n = 236, 77.4%) (Table 1).

HPV knowledge

Correct response rates at the item level exhibited considerable variability (Table 2). The highest accuracy was recorded for items related to HPV typing (93.4%), genital warts in men (91.8%), sexual transmission (82.3%), and asymptomatic progression (81.3%). Conversely, performance was suboptimal on items necessitating the rejection of false statements: only 36.7% of participants correctly refuted the assertion that “HPV occurs only in women,” 60.0% rejected the statement “HPV always causes symptoms,” and 57.4% rejected the claim that “condom use completely prevents HPV transmission.” The mean HPV knowledge score was 7.25 ± 1.65 (median 7; range 1–10).

HPV vaccine knowledge

Most participants accurately recognized the efficacy of HPV vaccination in reducing the risk of cervical cancer (90.8%), preventing HPV infection (82.6%), and its effectiveness for individuals aged 9–45 (80.0%) (Table 3). However, only 40.7% correctly identified that the HPV vaccine is not currently included in Turkey’s national immunization schedule, and 54.4% were aware that the vaccine is not limited to sexually active women. The mean knowledge score regarding the HPV vaccine was 4.26 ± 1.11 (median 4; range 0–6).

Attitudes toward HPV and HPV vaccination

Attitudinal responses were predominantly positive across most items, as illustrated in Table 4. A substantial majority of participants affirmed that physicians should inform patients about HPV (96.4%), that HPV vaccination should be widely accessible (95.7%), and that the vaccine should be provided at no cost (99.7%). The intention to receive the HPV vaccine was notably high, with 264 women (86.6%) indicating a willingness to consider vaccination. Confidence in the safety of the vaccine was also substantial (86.9%), as was the acknowledgment of HPV as a significant health concern (91.8%). A smaller majority (67.9%) believed they possessed adequate knowledge about HPV.

Correlations among knowledge domains

The correlation between general knowledge of HPV and knowledge specific to the HPV vaccine was found to be moderate, as indicated by a Spearman’s correlation coefficient of ρ = 0.31 (p < 0.001). This suggests that women who possess a greater understanding of HPV in general are also likely to have a more comprehensive knowledge of the HPV vaccine.

Factors associated with HPV knowledge

In the bivariate analyses presented in Table 5, no significant differences in HPV knowledge scores were observed across various demographic factors, including education level (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.18), marital status (p = 0.71), age (Spearman’s ρ = 0.04, p = 0.47), or healthcare-worker status (p = 0.68). However, two notable findings emerged: non-smokers demonstrated higher HPV knowledge scores compared with smokers (7.57 ± 1.42 vs. 7.02 ± 1.77; p = 0.007), and women with a self-reported history of HPV infection exhibited lower knowledge scores than those without such a history (6.81 ± 1.33 vs. 7.56 ± 1.66; p < 0.001), with respondents who were “unsure” about their infection status scoring similarly to those reporting prior infection. Regarding HPV vaccine knowledge, healthcare workers scored marginally higher than other participants (4.57 ± 1.01 vs. 4.19 ± 1.12; p = 0.023), although no significant differences were found based on education level (p = 0.16).

Vaccine acceptance (intention to vaccinate)

Overall, vaccine acceptance was recorded at 86.6% (264/305). Bivariate analyses (Table 6) revealed no significant differences in acceptance based on education level (university 86.1% vs. ≤high school 87.5%; p = 0.88), healthcare worker status (86.8% vs. 86.5%; p = 1.00), HPV knowledge category (higher 87.2% vs. lower 84.6%; p = 0.70), age group (p = 0.76), or marital status (p = 0.29). However, significantly higher acceptance rates were observed among women who regularly attended gynecological examinations (89.4% vs. 78.2%; p = 0.021) and those who had undergone Pap smear testing within the past three years (89.8% vs. 75.4%; p = 0.004). Additionally, women with a self-reported history of HPV infection demonstrated higher acceptance compared to those without such a history (96.2% vs. 83.1%; p = 0.044). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, no variable independently predicted vaccine acceptance, although a history of HPV infection approached statistical significance (aOR 4.29, 95% CI 0.98–18.82, p = 0.054) (Table 8).

In multivariable logistic regression (Table 8), healthcare-worker status was the only independent predictor of HPV vaccine uptake (aOR 2.01, 95% CI 1.07–3.80, p = 0.030); no other sociodemographic or behavioral variable was significantly associated with uptake. In a sensitivity analysis, restricting healthcare-worker status to core clinical roles (physicians, nurses, midwives, and dentists; n = 37) strengthened the association with uptake (aOR 3.30, 95% CI 1.55–7.03, p = 0.002), indicating that occupational clinical exposure rather than health-sector employment in general underlies the association.

Reasons for non-vaccination and information sources

Among the cohort of unvaccinated women (n = 187), the predominant reason cited for not receiving the HPV vaccine was financial cost (132/186, 71.0%). This was followed by concerns regarding potential side effects (30/186, 16.1%), a perceived lack of necessity (19/186, 10.2%), and insufficient knowledge about the vaccine (4/186, 2.2%) (Table 9).

Physicians emerged as the most prevalent source of information related to HPV (75.6%), with the internet (18.8%), social media (3.3%), and television (2.0%) serving as additional sources. Discussion

This cross-sectional study assessed knowledge, attitudes, and vaccination behaviors related to HPV among 305 women attending the outpatient gynecology clinic of a private tertiary-care hospital in Istanbul, Turkey. Three principal findings were identified. The central and most striking result is the marked gap between vaccination intention and actual uptake. Firstly, the intention to receive the HPV vaccine was high (86.6%); however, the actual vaccination uptake was less than half of the intention (38.7%), indicating an intention–uptake gap of nearly 48% points. Secondly, the most frequently cited reason for non-vaccination was cost (71% of unvaccinated women), while concerns about side effects, perceived lack of need, and informational gaps collectively accounted for the remainder. Thirdly, within this educated, healthcare-engaged sample, traditional sociodemographic predictors of HPV vaccination—such as education, general HPV knowledge, age, and marital status—showed no independent association with either vaccine acceptance or uptake; healthcare-worker status was the only variable independently associated with actual vaccination (aOR 2.01, 95% CI 1.07–3.80).

The intention–uptake gap identified in this study aligns with existing international literature, which frequently reports that the expressed willingness to receive the HPV vaccination often surpasses actual vaccination rates, with cost being a commonly cited barrier [6, 9, 16]. Our finding that 71% of unvaccinated women attributed their status to cost—within a sample characterized by high educational attainment and regular engagement with gynecological care—contributes to this body of evidence by highlighting the gap in a population otherwise well-positioned to receive the vaccine. Internationally, vaccination coverage typically increases following the implementation of publicly funded vaccination programs [6, 16].

Because cost was the dominant barrier in a population otherwise well-positioned to vaccinate, our findings suggest that incorporating HPV vaccination into Turkey’s national immunization schedule may help address this barrier. In our sample, 99.7% endorsed that the vaccine should be provided free of charge and 71% of unvaccinated women cited cost; removing this financial barrier through public funding could therefore help narrow the intention–uptake gap, consistent with international experience in which publicly funded programs markedly increase coverage.

Equally noteworthy are the factors that did not predict vaccine uptake. In bivariate analyses, regular gynecological examinations and recent Pap smear testing were correlated with higher reported acceptance rates (89.4% and 89.8%, respectively), indicating that engagement in preventive gynecological care may reinforce positive vaccination attitudes. However, neither factor was associated with actual vaccination uptake, nor did they remain significant after adjustment in the multivariable model.

Similarly, possessing a university education did not independently enhance either acceptance or uptake. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which suggest that while knowledge and education-based factors are linked to positive attitudes, they do not necessarily lead to higher actual vaccination rates [9, 10].

Within our sample, the only independent predictor of actual vaccine uptake was the status of being a healthcare worker. Healthcare workers were nearly twice as likely as other women to have received vaccination (54.7% vs. 35.3%; aOR 2.01, 95% CI 1.07–3.80), and this advantage remained significant after controlling for variables such as education, knowledge, age, and engagement with preventive care. It is noteworthy that the general HPV knowledge score of healthcare workers did not significantly differ from that of other participants (7.28 vs. 7.24), and their expressed intention to vaccinate was nearly identical (86.8% vs. 86.5%). Therefore, the higher uptake among healthcare workers appears to be attributable to factors associated with their occupational context—potentially including exposure to clinical information about HPV-related diseases, peer norms, and practical access—rather than superior knowledge or stronger intention. This pattern aligns with international systematic review evidence indicating heterogeneous levels of HPV-related knowledge and vaccine acceptance among healthcare workers [15].

A sensitivity analysis restricting this definition to core clinical roles strengthened the association, suggesting that direct occupational exposure to patients and to HPV-related care, rather than health-sector employment in general, may drive the higher uptake observed among healthcare workers.

Item-level analysis of HPV knowledge has identified specific content gaps. Although the mean score was relatively high at 7.25 out of 10, performance varied across different items. Notably, 93.4% of participants were aware that HPV comprises multiple types, some of which are oncogenic, and 91.8% recognized that HPV can cause genital warts in men. However, only 36.7% correctly refuted the statement that “HPV occurs only in women,” 57.4% correctly rejected the notion that “condom use completely prevents HPV transmission,” and merely 60.0% understood that HPV infection is often asymptomatic. Two of these specific gaps—the misconception that HPV affects only females and the assumption that HPV always presents symptoms—may be particularly pertinent to vaccination decisions, as they contribute to the perception that HPV is solely a women’s issue and that the absence of symptoms indicates the absence of infection. Targeted educational initiatives addressing these specific knowledge points may prove more effective than general awareness campaigns.

A counterintuitive finding warrants discussion: women who self-reported a history of HPV infection exhibited significantly lower HPV knowledge scores compared with those who reported no prior HPV infection (6.81 vs. 7.56, p < 0.001), with women uncertain about their HPV status displaying similar scores. Several non-exclusive explanations may account for this observation. Women with limited HPV knowledge might be less likely to recognize or recall a previous infection or to seek screening that would identify it; alternatively, some may misclassify other gynecological diagnoses as HPV. Notably, this group also demonstrated the highest stated intention to vaccinate (96.2%), which aligns with the notion that recent personal relevance may drive willingness to vaccinate, even in the absence of comprehensive general knowledge. Caution is advised in interpreting this association due to its cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data. It may also reflect recall or reporting bias or limitations of the non-validated instrument in this subgroup, and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

In the context of Turkey, our research expands upon prior studies that have highlighted the variability in HPV awareness among Turkish women [13, 14]. During the data collection period (January–April 2026), HPV vaccination was only available for purchase by individuals in Turkey. The findings of our study, which include a high acceptance rate of 86.6%, a broad consensus on vaccine safety at 86.9%, and a 71% rate of non-vaccination due to cost among unvaccinated women, suggest that while intentions and attitudes towards vaccination were positive, the actual vaccination rates were notably lower.

An important dimension we did not assess is whether women understood that HPV vaccination does not remove the need for continued cervical screening. Qualitative work has shown that some individuals perceive the vaccine as offering complete protection, a “false sense of security” that may reduce future screening intentions [17]. As our fixed-response instrument did not capture open-ended views, mixed-methods designs and items assessing post-vaccination screening awareness are recommended for future research.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several strengths. The sample size of 305 participants was sufficient for the planned analyses, the questionnaire comprehensively addressed the entire pathway from knowledge through attitudes to behavior, and our analytical approach effectively differentiated between intention and actual uptake—a distinction that is sometimes neglected in cross-sectional research on HPV vaccination.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the study was conducted at a single private university hospital in Istanbul, and the participants—predominantly university-educated, with high rates of regular gynecological follow-up—represent a more advantaged segment of women than the national average. Consequently, findings should be generalized with caution, particularly to populations with lower educational attainment or limited access to preventive gynecological care.

Women attending public clinics, those in rural regions, and those with lower educational attainment may differ substantially in HPV awareness, attitudes, and financial barriers; multi-center studies spanning public and private settings and a broader socioeconomic range are needed before these results can be generalized to Turkish women as a whole.

Second, the use of convenience sampling further constrains the external validity of the study. Third, the questionnaire, specifically developed for this research, underwent only a content review by two specialists rather than formal pilot testing or psychometric validation. Consequently, the attitude items demonstrated low internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.36), indicating that they captured distinct attitudinal dimensions and were thus analyzed individually. The instrument should therefore be regarded as a non-validated, descriptive questionnaire, and its knowledge and attitude items interpreted accordingly.

Fourth, all data, including HPV history and vaccination status, were self-reported and not corroborated with medical records, introducing the potential for recall and social-desirability bias. Fifth, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes any causal inference. The questionnaire also relied on fixed-response items and did not capture open-ended responses.

Finally, the healthcare workers in our sample were defined broadly and did not constitute a homogeneous group; therefore, the multivariable findings for this category should be interpreted with consideration of this heterogeneity.

Conclusion

In a cohort of women attending a private tertiary-care gynecology clinic in Istanbul, the stated acceptance of the HPV vaccine was high; however, the actual vaccination uptake was significantly lower, with cost being the most frequently cited barrier to vaccination. In our sample, education, general knowledge of HPV, and engagement with preventive gynecological care were not independently correlated with actual vaccination, whereas healthcare-worker status was. These findings elucidate the intention–uptake gap and its associated factors in this context and may inform future research on HPV vaccination behavior among Turkish women, including studies involving more diverse populations and employing formally validated assessment instruments. Because intention and uptake were measured directly, these behavioral findings—the intention–uptake gap and cost as the dominant barrier—are independent of the psychometric properties of the descriptive knowledge instrument.
