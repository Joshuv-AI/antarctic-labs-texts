Post-conflict restoration of health facilities in Amhara region, Ethiopia
Authors: Negese Sewagegn, Gizew Dessie Asres, Belay Bezabih, Gizachew Yismaw, Damtie Lankir, Abraham Amsalu Berneh, Mulatu Melese, Lealem Gedefaw, Melashu Balew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to assess the levels, types and barriers of post-conflict restoration of health infrastructure in war-affected health facilities in the Amhara region, Ethiopia.ResultsOut of 180 health facilities, only 5(2.8%) were fully restored in health system infrastructures and service delivery. Eighty (54.4%) generators, 24(20.2%) water supply, 41(23.6%) furniture, 45(46.9%) ambulance and 27(16.2%) buildings infrastructure were not restored. Health information system devices were also damaged during the war. Among 145 health centers with looted or damaged desktop computers, 41(28.3%) were not restored. Mental health care, cardiac disease screening and diabetes mellitus screening services were not initiated in 29.4%, 20.0% and 5.6% of health facilities respectively. Tuberculosis (TB) laboratory damage, shortage of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) test kit, lack of trained personnel, and HIV test algorithm unavailability were among the barriers for restoration of health services in the setting. In conclusion, this study found that very few health facilities were fully restored. Therefore, major efforts are required to return healthcare facilities infrastructure to their pre-war state. Ministry of Health and Amhara Regional Health Bureau, in collaboration with other stakeholders, should prioritize restoring accessible and secure health facilities affected by the northern war in the Amhara region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07882-8.Keywords: Amhara region, Health facilities, Infrastructure, Restoration status, War affected areas

Introduction

Armed conflict remains a persistent global health threat, often resulting in widespread morbidity, mortality, and the collapse of health infrastructure [1]. Reaching the most vulnerable populations in conflict-affected nations must be a top priority to achieve Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and fulfill the commitment to leave no one behind [2–4].

Health actors face numerous challenges in conflict affected areas [5, 6]. According to a study conducted in Syria showed that the occurrence of conflict affected routine health services, and limited access to the healthcare [7]. The health systems and the capacity of health actors to address demands are put under pressure when the health requirements of displaced people diverge from those of host populations [8, 9].

Moreover, armed conflict leads to closure of health facilities, shortages in supplies, increased risk of infectious disease outbreaks, and increases in malnutrition and gender-based violence [4]. It affects all aspects of the existing health system, including medical and health-related infrastructure, policy and planning, human resources and finance [10].

Similarly, conflict negatively affects health infrastructure, which can be damaged, destroyed, or looted intentionally or unintentionally by warring parties. Those health facilities that are not completely destroyed may be forced to reduce or shut down their services leading to collapse of the health service delivery [11–16].

Therefore, restoring the health system, developing workable solutions, and conducting proper assessments are essential [17]. There are different mechanisms to rebuild the health system based on the urgency, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness of the activities being carried out, the health system recovery has two phases: early recovery and transitional phase, and long-term recovery are some of these stages [18].

In Ethiopia, the northern conflict severely affected the health system. The estimated cost of damage to health facilities alone in the war-affected areas exceeded 15 billion Ethiopian Birr [19]. However, the restoration status of health infrastructure is unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the restoration status of health facilities and identify underlying challenges in the Amhara region.

Methods and materials

Study area

This study was conducted in Amhara region. The Amhara region is found in the northern part of Ethiopia. The capital city of the region is Bahir Dar, and it has 14 zones and eight administrative towns. Of which, eight zones/towns were affected by the Northern war namely North Wollo, Waghimera, South Wollo, Oromo Special zone, North Shewa, South Gondar, North Gondar, Dssie city. The projected population is estimated to be 34, 500,768 [20]. According to the 2022 Amhara Regional Health Bureau report, the region has 97 hospitals, 896 health centers, and 3636 health posts with 40,512 health care workers and 2028 supportive staff (Unpublished Amhara National Regional State-plan commission 2023 report). The study was conducted in selected health facilities of seven war affected zones (North Gondar, North Wollo including Woldia town, South Wollo, South Gondar, North Shewa, Waghimra, Oromo Special) and one town administration (Dessie town).

Study design and period

A cross-sectional study was conducted in seven war-affected zones and one town administration in the Amhara region. The study was conducted from 18th of July, 2023 to 15th of August 2023.

Study participants

The study participants were health facilities including hospitals and health centers in war-affected areas of seven zones and one town administration. The data related to the restoration status of the health facilities was obtained from facility heads, unit focal and health service providers.

Study design and periodize and sampling procedures

In the Amhara region, 673 health facilities (81 hospitals and 592 health centers) were affected by the northern war [21]. Of which, only 180 health facilities (155 Health centers and 25 hospitals) were accessible during the study period. So, all 180 participants were included in the sample size for this study. List of health facilities in war affected zones were identified before the beginning of data collection. Accessible and secured areas were selected using convenient sampling technique due to insecurity.

Operational definition

Fully restored The health service is considered fully restored when a health facility resumes services just as in a pre-war state.

Partially restored The health service is considered partially restored when a health facility provides its services in a compromised manner as compared to its pre-war state.

Not restored The health service is considered not restored when a health facility did not start providing service at all.

Data collection tools and procedures

The data collection tool was adapted from the District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) and Ethiopian demographic health survey (EDHS). The tool incorporated restoration related variables. Data collectors were assigned to assess communicable and non-communicable disease outpatient department (OPD) units. Electronic based data collection, open data kit (ODK) collect, was used. One-month data were collected to verify the service restoration status with same season before the war.

A total of 20 data collectors, five supervisors and two data managers were selected and trained for two days to aware about the objective of the study and data collection tool. Data quality was guaranteed with proper recruitment, adequate training and close supervision of data collectors. The supervisors were providing daily feedback for data collectors during the whole data collection period. They also cross-checked all collected questionnaires independently for completeness, accuracy, and consistency daily.

Data processing and analysis

Data was exported from ODK server to excel and it was cleaned. The data were imported into SPSS version 21, variables were coded, and analysis was performed using the SPSS software. A descriptive and summary statistics were calculated. Characteristics of respondents and restoration status of health service delivery among selected health facilities of war affected zones.

Health service restoration

Malaria diagnosis and treatment service was fully restored. But mental health care service, cardiac disease screening, Diabetes Mellitus screening were not restored in 53 (29.0%), 36 (20.0%), and 10 (5.6%), respectively (Additional file 1).

Barriers for restoration

Among assessed facilities, health centers were not giving tuberculosis screening service due to TB clinic room damage and others were due to TB laboratory damage. HIV screening service from five health centers and from a hospital was not giving due to shortage of test kit, lack of trained personnel, and HIV algorithm unavailability. Similarly, diabetes mellitus screening was interrupted due to lack of testing equipment and supply from seven health centers and a hospital.

Unavailability of hypertension screening service was occurred in three health centers due to service interruption and shortage of human power. Similarly, health centers had no cardiac disease screening due to unavailability of service (n = 18), no trained personnel (n = 12) and shortage of materials (n = 6). Mental health service was not provided due to lack of trained personnel in 17 health centers and unavailability of service in 9 health centers (Additional file 2).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess restoration status and related challenges at war affected health facilities. Only 2.78% of health centers were fully restored to their pre-war state. Most facilities, health centers (90.3%) and hospitals (96%), were only partially restored to their pre-war state. The rest of health centers (6.5%) and hospitals (4%) were not getting any intervention to restore them back to their prewar status.

Although this study was conducted in accessible and secured areas, the low restoration (2.78%) status of health facilities suggests that the situation may be worse in inaccessible and insecure areas. This finding is comparable with a study conducted in Sudan with severe disruption of all health system in war-affected areas [22] Nevertheless, other studies reported relatively good restoration status [23, 24]. The difference might be due to commitments and strategies followed and methodological difference that we only included accessible health facilities.

In this study, power supply was not resumed after the war. For instance, out of 102 health facilities which lost their solar power, only 21 (20.6%) health centers were restored. In addition, only 52 (35.4%) generators and 79 (57.7%) main electric line were restored in the affected health facilities. All this could affect the health system delivery including biological products such as vaccines, medicines, diagnostic test kits of imaging and laboratory need power source to keep its temperature [25–28]. Similarly, a study in Yemen indicated severe power outages, which made it impossible to maintain laboratory services, the cold chain for vaccines, and the availability of fans and air conditioners for critically ill patients [29]. On the other hand, a study done by Mueller et al. in 2017 showed almost full recovery (95%) of power source [30], which is a model for others war affected settings. In this study, health information systems were also poorly restored. The health facilities only restored 12.0% desktop computer dedicated for DHIS2, 10.9% laptop for reporting of health service delivery.

Valid and timely information on various domains of public health underpins the effectiveness of humanitarian public health interventions in crises. However, obstacles including insecurity, insufficient resources and skills for data collection and analysis and lack of validated methods combine to hamper the quantity and quality of public health information available to humanitarian responders [31, 32].

Health facilities themselves were damaged during the war. Out of 166 health facilities encountered building damage, only 24(14.5%) of health facilities were rebuilt their buildings. Previous studies also documented major destruction of buildings and low restoration [16].

In this study, 29% mental healthcare and 20% cardiac diseases services were not restored. This could result increased mortality, higher rates of hospital readmissions, and worsened quality of life patients residing in the conflict affected areas [33, 34]. Similarly, limited health services of mental health was reported in Gaza [35]. Furthermore, the HIV care system was severely harmed by armed conflicts. Another study conducted in Libya examined higher HIV infection in armed conflict settings [36]. A study conducted in Somali Regional State, Ethiopia, patients from conflict areas experienced longer wait times for TB diagnosis and engaged in higher rates of self-treatment [37].

Limitation

War affected health facilities that may influence the findings of this study were not included during data collection due to security problem. In addition, this assessment focuses only restoration of damaged or looted commodities and buildings but not addressed how health service qualities provided by restored health facilities in comparison with the non-war affected counter parts. In addition, this study used convenient sampling techniques, which might introduce selection bias and underestimate the true restoration status. Service restoration was inferred from one-month retrospective data, which may not reflect status of restoration.

Conclusion

This study found that only few health facilities were fully restored. Specifically, buildings, power source, health information system and health service delivery were not recovered to the pre-war state. There was shortage of manpower, inadequate training for available staff, and a scarcity of supplies and shortage of equipment for the restoration of health services. Therefore, the Federal Ministry of Health, Amhara Regional Health Bureau, in collaboration with stakeholders, should take action to restore accessible and secured healthcare facilities infrastructure to their pre-war state.
