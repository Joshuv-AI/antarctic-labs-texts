SIRT5-dependent desuccinylation licenses UBR5-mediated degradation of TAMM41 to regulate mitochondrial metabolism in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Haoyang Yuan, Wenxuan Hu, Xin Lv, Yonghao Cao, Kang Hu, Yongsen Li, Zhike Chen, Jian Yang, Yin Wang, Jun Zhao, Chun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming is essential for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) progression, yet the regulatory mechanisms governing mitochondrial phospholipid synthesis remain poorly understood. TAMM41 is a mitochondrial inner-membrane enzyme required for cardiolipin biosynthesis, but its role in LUAD has not been defined. Here, we show that TAMM41 is significantly upregulated in LUAD tissues and cell lines, and its high expression correlates with poor patient prognosis. Functional studies demonstrate that TAMM41 promotes LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas genetic ablation of TAMM41 suppresses malignant phenotypes. Mechanistically, TAMM41 maintains mitochondrial complex I activity, ATP production, and redox homeostasis, thereby limiting oxidative stress and apoptosis. We identify the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR5 as a specific regulator of TAMM41 stability through ubiquitination at lysine 206. Moreover, the mitochondrial desuccinylase SIRT5 directly interacts with TAMM41 and removes succinylation at lysine 45, enhancing UBR5 binding and promoting TAMM41 degradation. In vivo, TAMM41 knockout markedly inhibits LUAD xenograft growth, accompanied by impaired mitochondrial respiration and increased apoptosis. Collectively, these findings identify TAMM41 as a mitochondrial oncogenic driver in LUAD and reveal a SIRT5–TAMM41–UBR5 axis that links lysine succinylation to mitochondrial metabolic control, highlighting TAMM41 as a potential therapeutic target. Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with persistently high incidence and mortality rates [1]. Among all malignant tumors, lung cancer not only has the highest incidence rate but also the lowest overall survival rate. Global cancer statistics for 2020 show that lung cancer accounts for approximately 11.4% of all new cancer cases and causes 18.0% of cancer-related deaths. The occurrence of lung cancer is closely related to a variety of risk factors, including smoking, air pollution, occupational exposure, ionizing radiation, gene mutations, and a history of chronic lung infections, making it a major public health problem urgently needing global attention [2].

Lung cancer originates from the bronchial mucosal epithelial cells and is mainly divided into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) based on histopathological characteristics. NSCLC is the most common pathological type of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the most prevalent subtype [3]. Early-stage LUAD primarily relies on surgical resection, but once it progresses to an advanced stage, the prognosis remains unfavorable. Currently, the standard treatment strategy for advanced LUAD includes a combination of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy; however, most patients inevitably develop drug resistance during treatment [4]. This clinical dilemma highlights the urgent need to identify new prognostic biomarkers and systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms of LUAD to promote the development of more precise and effective targeted therapies.

Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles with significant metabolic activity and are widely regarded as key hubs integrating biosynthesis, bioenergetics, and signal transduction functions. Recent studies from human samples and mouse models have consistently demonstrated that mitochondrial metabolism is highly active during tumor growth and plays an indispensable role in tumorigenesis and maintenance. Mitochondrial metabolism not only provides essential metabolic intermediates for macromolecule synthesis but also supports the anabolic needs of tumor cells by generating various helper metabolites, thereby maintaining their malignant phenotype [5–8].

Cardiolipin (CL) is a unique and crucial phospholipid component of mitochondria, playing a central role in maintaining the structural integrity of the inner mitochondrial membrane, regulating oxidative phosphorylation, and inducing apoptosis [9]. Initial synthesis of CL occurs on the matrix side of the inner mitochondrial membrane. Its precursor, phosphatidic acid (PA), can be synthesized by various enzymes located in the endoplasmic reticulum or outer mitochondrial membrane, and may also be partially derived from the catalytic action of glycerol kinase in the inner membrane. PA from the endoplasmic reticulum is transported across the mitochondrial intermembrane space via the TP53-regulated apoptosis inhibitor protein-evolutionary and lymphocyte-associated domain complex (TRIAP1–PRELID1), and binds to cytidine triphosphate (CTP). Under the catalysis of the mitochondrial-associated protein TAMM41, it is converted into CDP-diacylglycerol (CDP-DAG) [10, 11]. As the initiating enzyme of the CL synthesis pathway, TAMM41 plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial phospholipid homeostasis; however, its expression characteristics and biological functions in lung adenocarcinoma have not been systematically elucidated. This study aims to investigate the role of TAMM41 in LUAD and its potential molecular mechanisms, providing new theoretical basis for a deeper understanding of the role of mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming in lung adenocarcinoma.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissue and its corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissue were collected from patients who underwent surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. All patients had not received chemotherapy or radiotherapy prior to surgery. After surgical resection, tissue specimens were immediately rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. All patients signed written informed consent forms before sample collection. Specimen collection and use complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University.

Cell culture

Immortalized normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B and lung adenocarcinoma cell lines H1650, A549, PC-9, and H1299 were obtained from the Department of Thoracic Surgery, First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. 293T cells were purchased from Novoprotein (Shanghai, China, catalog number: XCK07).

All cells underwent routine quality control, including mycoplasma and microbial contamination detection, short tandem repeat (STR) identification, and morphological evaluation. Cells were cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator.

Reagents and antibodies

Cell culture reagents used in this study, including fetal bovine serum, matrix gel, culture medium, rotenone, antimycin A, and antibiotics, were all purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Anti-TAMM41 antibody was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Other antibodies were generously provided by Dr. Wang and Dr. Xu [12, 13].

Polybrene and other chemical reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The usage of fluorescent dyes has been reported in previous studies [14, 15]. Pre-stained protein markers were purchased from APExBIO (Houston, USA, catalog number: F4005).

Apoptosis flow cytometry kits, Caspase-3 activity assay kits, and mitochondrial complex I activity assay kits were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China) or GenScript Corporation. Trypsin-EDTA digestion buffer (FL20011) was purchased from Bioland Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China).

JC-1 staining

Cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/well in 24-well plates and cultured for 48 h. JC-1 dye was then added and incubated for 2 h. Mitochondrial membrane depolarization was assessed by detecting the fluorescence intensity of the JC-1 green monomer. A representative image is a combined image of green fluorescence (490 nm) and red fluorescence (625 nm).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were cultured to an appropriate density in 10 cm culture dishes and then lysed on ice with pre-chilled IP lysis buffer. The lysis buffer was incubated with the corresponding antibody and Protein A/G agarose beads at 4 °C for 3 h. The cells were then washed three times with pre-chilled IP buffer, and the resulting immunoprecipitate was used for Western blotting or mass spectrometry analysis.

Western blotting

Cell or tissue samples were lysed on ice, collected with a scraper, and centrifuged. The supernatant was used for protein quantification. Subsequent SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting were performed according to previously reported methods [16]. Xenograft: Five-week-old female nude mice were obtained from GemPharmatech. Two million cells were suspended in serum-free medium and mixed with Matrigel to a final volume of 200 μL. The cell suspension was subcutaneously injected into the left axilla of each mouse. Mice were divided into two groups according to the injected cell type: Cas9-C group (control) and TAMM41-KO group (knockout). Body weight and tumor volume were measured every 10 days. All animal operations were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Soochow University. Transwell migration and invasion assays: Genetically modified cells were suspended at 1.0 × 104 cells/well in serum-free medium and added to the upper cavity of a Transwell chamber. 24 h later, cells migrating in the sac were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained, and photographed. The staining solution was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (catalog number: G4740).

Caspase activity assay

The activities of Caspase-3 and Caspase-9 in cell lysates were detected using a Caspase-3/-9 colorimetric assay kit (BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cellular fluorescence staining

Cells were seeded at 4 × 104 cells/well in 12-well plates and cultured for the specified time. Afterward, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the appropriate fluorescent dye, washed, and observed under a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Lentiviral transduction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

All lentiviral vectors were purchased from Anzhenbio (China). Cells to be transduced were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well in 6-well plates and subjected to overnight serum starvation with medium containing 1% FBS (JYK-FBS-301, INNER MONGOLIA JINYUAN KANG BIOTECHNOLOGY CO.,LTD.) to synchronize the cell cycle.

The following day, the required viral load was calculated using the formula

Virus Volume = (Number of Cells × MOI) / Virus Titer (MOI determined by preliminary experiments). The virus was serially diluted and added to the cells using maintenance medium containing 1% FBS. After 12–24 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium containing 10% FBS. After 48 h, when cell confluence was approximately 80%, selection was performed for 3–5 days using complete medium containing 2 μg/mL puromycin.

RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed with β-actin as an internal control. Experimental and data analysis methods followed previous reports [13, 17–19]. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis

Basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured before treatment. ATP-related respiration was assessed by sequentially adding the ATP synthase inhibitor oligomycin, followed by the addition of the uncoupling agent FCCP to determine maximum respiration capacity. Finally, mitochondrial complex I and III inhibitors rotenone and antimycin A were added as negative controls to completely inhibit mitochondrial respiration and verify detection specificity.

EdU staining

Genetically modified cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well in 12-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Cell proliferation was detected using the EdU Apollo 567 kit (Invitrogen), and DAPI was used for nuclear staining.

Flow cytometry

In short, cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended, and stained with 10 μL Annexin V-APC and/or 10 μL propidium iodide (PI) (Sigma). Apoptosis was then analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

TUNEL staining

Transgenic cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well in 12-well plates and cultured for 48 h. Apoptosis was detected using the Invitrogen TUNEL assay kit and observed under a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

All data were normally distributed and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Each experiment was repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using a two-tailed test, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Upregulated TAMM41 expression in lung adenocarcinoma and its Association with poor prognosis

We first performed a systematic analysis of the expression characteristics of TAMM41 in lung adenocarcinoma based on the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Analysis of the TCGA-LUAD dataset showed that the expression level of TAMM41 transcripts was significantly increased in lung adenocarcinoma tissue (“Tumor”) compared with normal lung epithelial tissue (“Normal”) (Fig. 1A). Further survival analysis showed that high TAMM41 expression was significantly associated with poor clinical prognosis in LUAD patients (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we validated the expression profile of TAMM41 in clinical lung adenocarcinoma tissue samples. This study included 12 patients with primary lung adenocarcinoma (n = 12), and paired tumor tissues (“T”) and adjacent normal lung epithelial tissues (“N”) were analyzed. Compared with normal tissues, TAMM41 mRNA expression was significantly increased in LUAD tumor tissues (Fig. 1D). Consistently, Western blot results showed that TAMM41 protein levels were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues in four representative patient samples (T1–T4) (Fig. 1E). Quantitative analysis of all 12 tissue pairs further confirmed that TAMM41 protein expression in LUAD tumor tissues was significantly higher than that in the corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 1F).

Furthermore, we examined the expression of TAMM41 in different lung adenocarcinoma cell lines. Compared with the normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B, TAMM41 mRNA was significantly increased in various immortalized LUAD cell lines (H1650, PC-9, H1299, and A549) (Fig. 1G). Protein level analysis also showed that TAMM41 was consistently upregulated in the aforementioned LUAD cell lines, while maintaining a low expression level in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 1H, I).

To clarify the subcellular localization of TAMM41, we performed immunofluorescence co-localization analysis in BEAS-2B, A549, and H1299 cells. The results showed that TAMM41 (green fluorescence) highly co-localized with the mitochondrial-specific dye MitoTracker (red fluorescence), suggesting that it is mainly located in mitochondria. Notably, the fluorescence signal intensity of TAMM41 in the LUAD cell lines A549 and H1299 was significantly higher than that in normal lung epithelial cells BEAS-2B (Fig. 1J). Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining was performed on tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues from six pairs of non-small cell lung cancer patients, and two representative samples are shown, further confirming the significantly enhanced expression of TAMM41 in tumor tissues (Fig. 1K).

In summary, these results consistently indicate that TAMM41 is significantly highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma tissues and cells, closely associated with poor patient prognosis, and is mainly localized in mitochondria.

TAMM41 silencing inhibits malignant biological behavior of LUAD cells

Previous studies have shown that abnormally enhanced mitochondrial metabolism is one of the important factors driving the occurrence and progression of LUAD [20–22]. Given the high expression and oncogenic effect of TAMM41 in lung adenocarcinoma, we further investigated the effect of TAMM41 silencing on the malignant biological behavior of LUAD cells in an in vitro model.In LUAD cells, we used a CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing strategy to knock out TAMM41. Specifically, we constructed two lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9 knockout vectors encoding TAMM41-specific sgRNA sequences and transduced them into LUAD cells stably expressing Cas9. After screening with puromycin and verifying the knockout efficiency, we successfully obtained two monoclonal TAMM41 knockout cell lines (TAMM41–KO1 and TAMM41–KO2). Compared to CRISPR/Cas9 control cells transduced with nonsense sgRNA (“Cas9-C”), TAMM41 protein expression was significantly reduced in TAMM41 knockout cells (Fig. 3A, B). Functional experiments showed that TAMM41 silencing significantly inhibited the growth capacity of LUAD cells, manifested as a significant reduction in colony formation assays (Fig. 3C) and a significant decrease in the proportion of EdU-positive nuclei (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, Transwell assays further demonstrated that TAMM41 knockout significantly impaired the in vitro migration and invasion abilities of LUAD cells (Fig. 3E and F). To validate the above bioinformatics analysis results, we further examined the effect of TAMM41 knockout on mitochondrial function in LUAD cells. Compared to CRISPR/Cas9 control cells (“Cas9-C”), TAMM41 knockout cells (TAMM41–KO1 and TAMM41–KO2) showed significantly reduced mitochondrial complex I activity (Fig. 4B), accompanied by a significant decrease in cellular ATP levels (Fig. 4C). Simultaneously, TAMM41 knockout significantly increased the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio (Fig. 4D) and decreased the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 4E), indicating that its silencing led to a significant imbalance in redox homeostasis in LUAD cells.

Furthermore, by detecting the cellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR), we found that TAMM41 knockout significantly inhibited basal and maximal respiratory capacity in LUAD cells (Fig. 4F, G), suggesting a significant suppression of mitochondrial aerobic metabolic activity. Consistent with this, in TAMM41 knockout A549 and H1299 cells, the level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) was significantly increased, manifested as enhanced CellROX red fluorescence signal (Fig. 4H) and increased DCFH-DA green fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, mitochondrial membrane potential probe detection showed that TAMM41 knockout significantly promoted the conversion of red fluorescent aggregates to green fluorescent monomers, suggesting significant mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 4J). Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed that, compared with control cells, the mitochondrial cristae structure in TAMM41 knockout LUAD cells was severely damaged (Fig. 4K), supporting the occurrence of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage at the ultrastructural level.

TAMM41 deficiency induces apoptosis activation in LUAD cells

Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the key triggers for apoptosis activation [23], we further evaluated the effect of TAMM41 silencing on LUAD cell apoptosis. The results showed that TAMM41 knockout significantly enhanced the activities of Caspase-3 (Fig. 5A) and Caspase-9 (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, TUNEL staining results showed a significantly increased proportion of TUNEL-positive nuclei in TAMM41 knockout cells (Fig. 5C), while cell loss counting further confirmed that TAMM41 silencing effectively induced apoptosis activation in LUAD cells (Fig. 5D). Importantly, the inhibition of TAMM41 expression did not significantly induce apoptosis in BEAS-2B cells (Figure S1A, B). Among the identified candidate proteins, N-recognin 5 (UBR5), as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, showed the highest specific peptide coverage, suggesting that it may be an important interacting protein of TAMM41. E3 ubiquitin ligases are a core component of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), responsible for recognizing specific substrate proteins and catalyzing the covalent linkage of ubiquitin molecules to them. Ubiquitination, as a key form of post-translational modification, not only mediates proteasome-dependent protein degradation but also regulates its stability, subcellular localization, and protein-protein interaction networks [24–28]. UBR5, a HECT domain-type E3 ubiquitin ligase, is an important member of the N-terminal degradation pathway and has received widespread attention in the field of tumor biology in recent years. Previous studies have shown that UBR5 can maintain the activity of the β-catenin signaling pathway in ovarian cancer cells by regulating p53 protein levels, thereby promoting cell adhesion, colonization, and organoid formation [29]; in colorectal cancer, upregulation of UBR5 can mediate chemotherapy resistance by inhibiting ferroptosis [30]. Furthermore, UBR5 can inhibit SUMOylation of TRIM28 by mediating K63-linked ubiquitination, thereby relieving the transcriptional repression of the RLR signaling pathway by TRIM28 and enhancing antiviral immune responses [31]. Previous studies have also suggested that UBR5 is involved in the progression of various malignant tumors, including triple-negative breast cancer and pancreatic cancer [32, 33], but its role in lung adenocarcinoma still lacks systematic investigation.

Based on mass spectrometry screening results, we further performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments in A549 cells, confirming a stable interaction between endogenous TAMM41 and UBR5 (Fig. 6B). Given UBR5‘s biological function as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, we hypothesize that it may regulate TAMM41 protein homeostasis by mediating TAMM41 ubiquitination. To verify this hypothesis, we co-transfected A549 cells with FLAG-labeled TAMM41 (FLAG–TAMM41), MYC-labeled UBR5 (MYC–UBR5), and HA-labeled ubiquitin (HA–Ub). The results showed that exogenous overexpression of UBR5 significantly enhanced the ubiquitination level of TAMM41 (Fig. 6C). Conversely, in A549 cells pre-transfected with FLAG–TAMM41 and HA–Ub, knockdown of UBR5 (si1 and si2) significantly reduced the ubiquitination modification level of TAMM41 (Fig. 6D), further confirming that UBR5 has a specific ubiquitination regulatory role for TAMM41. Cycloheximide (CHX) tracking experiments showed that UBR5 overexpression not only reduced the homeostatic protein level of TAMM41 but also significantly accelerated its degradation process (Fig. 6E).

To clarify the specific molecular site of UBR5-mediated TAMM41 ubiquitination, we conducted further library searches using mass spectrometry data to screen for potential ubiquitinated lysine residues in TAMM41. Subsequently, a series of lysine mutants were constructed and ubiquitination was detected. The results showed that under UBR5 overexpression conditions, the K337R mutant still underwent significant ubiquitination, while the K206R mutant almost completely lost the UBR5-induced enhanced ubiquitination effect (Fig. 6F), indicating that K206 is a key and specific site for UBR5-mediated TAMM41 ubiquitination modification. Notably, the K206 site of TAMM41 is highly conserved across different species, further supporting its important biological significance in the functional regulation of TAMM41 protein (Fig. 6G).

Furthermore, to assess whether UBR5 can functionally antagonize the oncogenic effect of TAMM41 in LUAD cells, we simultaneously overexpressed UBR5 in A549 cells overexpressing TAMM41. Western blot results showed that further overexpression of UBR5 significantly reduced TAMM41 protein levels (Fig. 6H). Functional analysis indicated that UBR5 overexpression, to some extent, reversed the enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. 6I), increased mitochondrial complex I activity (Fig. 6J), and increased ATP content (Fig. 6K) induced by TAMM41 overexpression, while simultaneously increasing the NAD+/NADH ratio (Fig. 6L). These results suggest that UBR5 inhibits TAMM41-driven mitochondrial function enhancement and energy metabolism reprogramming in LUAD cells by mediating TAMM41 ubiquitination and degradation.

Further mitochondrial isolation experiments showed that SIRT5 and TAMM41 were almost completely co-localized in mitochondrial components (Fig. 7B). SIRT5 is an important member of the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent Sirtuins family and has been widely reported as a mitochondrial desuccinylase [34, 35]. Based on this, we further investigated whether SIRT5 affects the protein function and stability of TAMM41 by regulating its succinylation modification. The results showed that under SIRT5 overexpression, the succinylation level of TAMM41 was significantly reduced (Fig. 7D); conversely, interfering with SIRT5 expression significantly enhanced the degree of TAMM41 succinylation modification (Fig. 7E). Notably, exogenous overexpression of SIRT5 was accompanied by a significant decrease in TAMM41 protein levels, while knockdown of SIRT5 significantly upregulated TAMM41 protein expression (Fig. 7D, E), suggesting that SIRT5 may regulate the protein stability of TAMM41 by affecting post-translational modifications.

To further verify the above hypothesis, we used cycloheximide (CHX) to block protein catabolism and examined the degradation kinetics of TAMM41. The results showed that SIRT5 overexpression significantly accelerated the protein degradation process of TAMM41 (Fig. 7F). Meanwhile, ubiquitination assays showed that SIRT5 overexpression significantly enhanced TAMM41 ubiquitination levels (Fig. 7G), while SIRT5 knockdown significantly reduced TAMM41 ubiquitination (Fig. 7H).

Given that previous studies have confirmed that the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR5 can mediate TAMM41 ubiquitination degradation, we further analyzed whether SIRT5 affects the interaction between UBR5 and TAMM41 using Co-IP experiments. The results showed that SIRT5 overexpression significantly enhanced the binding ability of UBR5 to TAMM41 in A549 cells (Fig. 7I), suggesting that SIRT5 may promote TAMM41 recognition by regulating its post-translational modification state.

Based on mass spectrometry data analysis, we further identified lysine 45 (K45) as the major succinylation site of TAMM41 (Fig. 7J). Subsequently, a K45 mutant (K45R) was constructed to simulate the desuccinylated state. Compared to wild-type TAMM41 (TAMM41-WT), the succinylation level of TAMM41–K45R was significantly reduced. More importantly, the K45R mutation significantly enhanced the interaction between UBR5 and TAMM41 (Fig. 7K).

In summary, these results indicate that succinylation modification of TAMM41 at the K45 site significantly inhibits the binding of the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR5 to it, thereby maintaining the protein stability of TAMM41; while SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation modification relieves this inhibition and promotes UBR5-dependent ubiquitination degradation.

Furthermore, we found that the expression levels of the SIRT5 protein in BEAS-2B cells were significantly higher than those in lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, whereas there was no significant difference in the protein expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR5 (Figure S2A). This finding suggests that the reduced level of TAMM41 desuccinylation in lung adenocarcinoma may be one of the key factors contributing to its high protein expression.

Discussion

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is one of the most common subtypes of lung cancer worldwide, characterized by high heterogeneity and extremely poor clinical prognosis, remaining a major challenge in the treatment of malignant tumors [36, 37]. Although immunotherapy and molecular targeted therapy have significantly improved survival outcomes in some patients, a considerable proportion of LUAD patients still lack clear and effective therapeutic targets [38, 39]. Therefore, systematically identifying new key molecules and elucidating their mechanisms of action in the development and progression of LUAD is of great significance for expanding treatment strategies.

In recent years, numerous studies have gradually recognized the irreplaceable role of mitochondrial metabolic activity in tumor cell growth and survival [5]. In addition to providing ATP to tumor cells, mitochondria also maintain the malignant phenotype of cancer cells by supplying anabolic intermediates, regulating redox homeostasis, and participating in cell fate determination. Mitochondrial dysfunction is closely related to uncontrolled tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis escape. In-depth analysis of its molecular regulatory mechanisms can help discover new anti-tumor intervention targets and promote the clinical translation of related strategies [40, 41].

The energy production capacity of mitochondria is highly dependent on the composition of its inner membrane phospholipids, especially cardiolipin (CL) [42]. CL is mainly located in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) and plays a crucial role in the assembly, stability, and functional maintenance of respiratory chain complexes. CL biosynthesis begins with the transport of phosphatidic acid (PA) from the endoplasmic reticulum to the mitochondria, followed by transmembrane transport via the PRELID–TRIAP1 complex, and activation catalyzed by CDP-DAG synthase. Subsequently, phosphatidylglycerol phosphate synthase (PGS1) converts CDP-diacylglycerol to phosphatidylglycerol phosphate (PGP), which, under the action of phosphatase PTPMT1, generates phosphatidylglycerol (PG), which is ultimately catalyzed by cardiolipin synthase CLS1 to generate CL [43, 44]. As a key lipid molecule for maintaining mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), CL is crucial for the ideal activity of complexes I–IV [45, 46].

TAMM41 is a CDP-diacylglycerol synthase located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, responsible for catalyzing the conversion of PA to CDP-DAG, and is one of the rate-limiting initiation steps of the cardiolipin synthesis pathway [47, 48]. Previous studies have shown that loss of TAMM41 function leads to impaired CDP-DAG production and decreased cardiolipin levels, thereby inducing the OXPHOS deficiency phenotype. Furthermore, the function of TAMM41 in neurological diseases has gradually attracted attention. It maintains protein stability through interaction with the Sigma-1 receptor and participates in the regulation of neuroinflammation, epileptic seizures, and emotional behavior. In patients with depression and animal models, decreased TAMM41 expression is closely associated with depressive-like behaviors [49–52]. However, the expression pattern, biological function, and clinical significance of TAMM41 in tumors, especially non-small cell lung cancer, have not yet been systematically elucidated.

In this study, we systematically revealed for the first time the pro-cancer role of TAMM41 in LUAD. We found that compared with normal lung epithelial tissue, the mRNA and protein levels of TAMM41 in LUAD tumor tissue were significantly increased, and its high expression was closely associated with poor patient prognosis. Functional experiments showed that knockout of TAMM41 significantly inhibited the proliferation and migration of LUAD cells, while overexpression of TAMM41 promoted the malignant phenotype of tumor cells. Mechanistically, TAMM41 silencing significantly reduces mitochondrial complex I activity, ATP content, and the GSH/GSSG ratio, leading to mitochondrial redox homeostasis imbalance. Simultaneously, TAMM41 deficiency also causes decreased oxygen consumption, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, ultimately inducing mitochondrial dysfunction-related apoptosis activation.

Notably, we further elucidated the molecular mechanism by which TAMM41 is regulated by fine post-translational modifications. We identified UBR5 as a specific E3 ubiquitin ligase for TAMM41, mediating ubiquitination and degradation of TAMM41 at the Lys206 site. This site is highly conserved across multiple species, suggesting its crucial role in TAMM41 homeostasis regulation. Furthermore, we discovered that Lys45 is a specific succinylation modification site for TAMM41, and the mitochondrial desuccinylase SIRT5 can remove this modification, thereby enhancing the interaction between UBR5 and TAMM41 and promoting its protein degradation. This finding establishes a potential functional link between metabolism-dependent post-translational modifications, protein homeostasis and mitochondrial function. But the spatial context and subcellular dynamics of UBR5-mediated regulation of TAMM41 remain to be fully elucidated.In vivo experiments further confirmed that TAMM41 knockout significantly inhibited mitochondrial energy metabolism and tumor growth in LUAD xenografts.Our findings support, but do not directly demonstrate, a role for TAMM41 in cardiolipin metabolism. Future studies incorporating lipidomic analyses will be important to further validate this mechanism.

In summary, this study revealed the pro-cancer role of TAMM41, a key enzyme in mitochondrial cardiolipin synthesis, in the development and progression of LUAD, and elucidated its molecular mechanism of synergistic regulation by UBR5-mediated ubiquitination and SIRT5-dependent desuccinylation (Fig. 9).Although our findings suggest that TAMM41 may play a role in the progression of LUAD, larger-scale clinical cohort studies and in vivo studies are still required to validate its prognostic and therapeutic relevance. Furthermore, whilst there is currently a lack of specific small-molecule inhibitors targeting TAMM41, this study provides an important theoretical basis for targeted therapeutic strategies for LUAD that focus on mitochondrial lipid metabolism.
