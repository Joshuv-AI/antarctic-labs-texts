Evaluation of the effects of diabetes on tooth radiodensity, bond strength of root canal sealers to dentin, and interfacial elemental distribution
Authors: Aysenur Oncu, Ecem Ozgur, Serhat Efeoglu, Berkan Celikten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess dentin radiodensity, the bond strength of various root canal sealers, and the elemental distribution at the dentin–sealer interface in both healthy and diabetic teeth.MethodsNinety-six extracted teeth from diabetic and healthy individuals were included. Radiodensity of root dentin was measured using cone beam computed tomography and quantified via Hounsfield Unit (HU) values. Samples were divided into six groups: diabetic or healthy teeth treated with one of three sealers (Total Fill BC Sealer Hi Flow, BioRoot RCS, or AH Plus). Root canal treatment was applied to all teeth. A 1-mm-thick mid-root section was obtained from each sample. Push-out bond strength testing was conducted on half of the samples to measure adhesion (in MPa), while the other half were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) to examine the elemental composition at the dentin–sealer interface.ResultsAlthough HU values were found to be lower in diabetic teeth, the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Push-out tests revealed significantly lower bond strength in diabetic teeth compared to healthy ones (p < 0.05). However, the choice of root canal sealer did not significantly influence bond strength (p > 0.05). SEM-EDS results indicated that the elemental composition of the interfacial layer was influenced more by the sealer type than by diabetic status.ConclusionsDiabetes did not significantly affect dentin radiodensity, but it was associated with lower bond strength between dentin and sealers.The chemical composition of the root canal sealer plays a key role in interfacial interactions, influencing the elemental characteristics of the bonding interface.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Root canal sealer, Bond strength, Interfacial layer

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic condition characterized by dysfunctional insulin production or activity, resulting in persistent hyperglycemia [1]. DM type 2 is also known to affect many systems, including the cardiovascular system, eyes, lungs, kidneys, and oral tissues [2]. It has been reported that the dentin tissue of patients with diabetes shows structural differences compared to that of healthy patients. These variations are evident in changes to trace element levels, crystallite quality, and quantity [3]. Such alterations may critically influence the clinical outcomes and prognosis of endodontic therapies in individuals with diabetes. Clinical studies have reported higher rates of endodontic complications in patients with systemic diseases such as diabetes and delayed healing of periradicular lesions, emphasizing the importance of understanding dentin–material interactions in this population [4]. Importantly, previous investigations have suggested that hyperglycemia-induced collagen glycation and impaired mineral metabolism in dentin can compromise its mechanical integrity, increase brittleness, and alter the bonding potential of restorative and endodontic materials [5, 6].

To achieve an effective root canal filling, the sealer must penetrate the dentin and bond tightly [7]. A durable adhesion is fundamental because root canal sealers are primarily responsible for filling the irregularities, accessory canals, and dentinal tubules, thereby enhancing the seal of the obturation. Inadequate sealing ability can lead to microleakage, reinfection of the canal system, and ultimately compromise the long-term success of root canal treatment [8]. AH Plus (Dentsply DeTrey GmbH, Konstanz, Germany) is a commonly used epoxy resin-based root canal sealer, known for its excellent physicochemical properties, including high radiopacity, low solubility, favorable flow characteristics, and strong adhesion to dentin [9]. BioRoot RCS (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France) and Total Fill BC Sealer Hi Flow (FKG Dentaire SA, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) are calcium silicate-based endodontic sealers representing a new generation of materials with advanced bioactive and biocompatible properties [10]. When bioactive materials come into contact with biological fluids, apatite crystals form on the surface of the material, at the material/dentin interface, and within the dentinal tubules [11]. This provides good adhesion and sealing. Migration of elements from the material to the dentin leads to changes in the dentin, increasing its chemical resistance and physical strength against acids, while also promoting remineralisation [12]. The interfacial layer and tag-like structures formed between the material and dentin can reduce microleakage, improving the marginal adaptation of bioactive cements and increasing push-out resistance [13].

Previous studies have shown that changes in dentin mineral content and structural alterations are observed in the teeth of diabetic individuals, such as changes in tubule diameter and density [5, 6, 14]. Although limited knowledge exists in the literature evaluating bone density of diabetic patients [15, 16], the radiological aspect of dentin density has received little attention in the literature. The present study aimed to assess the bond strength of various root canal sealers and the elemental composition at the interface of healthy and diabetic teeth, while also evaluating the radiodensity differences between diabetic and healthy teeth.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and sample size calculation

The study included single-rooted mandibular premolars of patients with diabetes mellitus but no other systemic diseases, in which extraction of the premolars was indicated during dental examinations and for reasons unrelated to the present study. The reason for the collection of extracted teeth was explained to the patient and legal representative, and the patient signed an informed consent form for research purposes. Patients with diabetes were between 32 and 51 years old, and the control group of healthy individuals was matched to the same age range. All procedures were carried out after receiving approval from the ………. University Faculty of Dentistry Ethics Committee (Decision Number: 10/4). A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1). Based on an alpha error probability (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1–β) of 0.80, the minimum required total sample size was calculated to be 96. The effect size was determined to be d = 0.52 for density evaluation and f = 0.561 for both bond strength and interfacial layer analysis. Figure 1 shows a flow chart of the experimental study.

CBCT imaging and density evaluation

96 teeth, both diabetic and healthy, were scanned in the cone beam computed tomography unit (Newtom 7G, Verona, Italy). The imaging parameters of the device were set as 120 kV, 19 mA, and 3.8 s for dose time. In our study, the root dentin density of mandibular premolar teeth in healthy and diabetic individuals was evaluated using NNT software (Verona, Italy) on cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images. The root dentin density of each tooth was determined numerically using the software’s Hounsfield Unit (HU) calculation feature. Three separate measurements were taken from the apical, middle, and coronal thirds of each tooth’s root. The mean HU value of the root dentin tissue of each tooth was then calculated by taking the arithmetic mean of all three values for each sample. All measurements were performed by an experienced observer in oral and maxillofacial radiology. Figure 2 presents a section from CBCT images of healthy and diabetic tooth samples.

Sample preparation

Freshly extracted teeth were stored in distilled water containing 0.1% thymol crystals at room temperature until the commencement of experimental procedures, following the removal of debris and soft tissues using periodontal curettes. To standardize the specimens, crowns were separated from the roots using a high-speed milling cutter under continuous water cooling. The root lengths were adjusted to a uniform length of 13 ± 1 mm. Root canal preparation was performed up to size F2 utilizing the ProTaper Gold rotary file system (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland). Irrigation with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (Merck, Sigma-Aldrich) was applied between each instrument use. The final irrigation protocol consisted of sequential application of 2 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Merck, Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite, and 2 mL of distilled water (Merck, Sigma-Aldrich). All samples were subsequently dried with paper points before being allocated into experimental groups.

Total Fill BC Sealer Hi Flow, being pre-mixed and injectable, was delivered into the root canal using its proprietary plastic cannula. In contrast, BioRoot RCS and AH Plus pastes were prepared according to the manufacturers’ instructions and applied using a gutta-percha cone. All specimens were obturated employing the cold lateral condensation technique. Following obturation, the samples were embedded in acrylic resin molds. Subsequently, a 1-mm-thick cross-sectional slice was obtained from the middle third of each root, perpendicular to the long axis, using a low-speed, water-cooled microtome saw (Isomet 5000; Buehler, Lake Bluff, IL). The specimens were then immersed in distilled water and incubated at 37 °C for 15 days prior to further analysis.

Push-out test

A push-out test was performed using a universal testing machine (Lloyd Instruments Ltd., Bognor Regis, UK) to evaluate the bond strength of the root canal filling to the dentin. For the mechanical evaluation, each specimen was carefully placed in the test setup and fitted with a 1 kN load cell to enable the precise measurement of displacement forces. A uniaxial compressive load was then applied in the corono-apical direction at a constant crosshead velocity of 1 mm/min. The force was increased via a pusher tip until the filling material detached from the dentin surface. The maximum load at the moment of separation was recorded in Newton (N), after which the bond strength was converted to megapascals (MPa) using the following formula: Bond Strength (MPa) = Load at Failure (N)/Adhesion Area (mm²).

SEM-EDS analysis

To determine the basic elemental composition of the interfacial layer between the dentin and the material, eight samples from each group were selected and analysed using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS). Elemental mapping was performed, with each element represented by a distinct colour. The sample preparation protocol for the SEM analysis was adapted from a previously published study [17]. Following incubation, the specimens were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (70% to 100%) and immersed in 100% ethanol with ultrasonic agitation for five minutes. Then, 37% phosphoric acid was applied to the dentin and material surfaces for five seconds, followed by deproteinisation with 1% sodium hypochlorite for five minutes. The samples were air-dried overnight at room temperature and then mounted on aluminium stubs using carbon tape. The samples were then coated with a gold/palladium alloy for one minute under a current of 52 mA using an SBS-800 Ion Sputtering Coater. Finally, the specimens were examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) (GAIA3, Tescan), equipped with an Oxford XMax 150 EDS detector, at an accelerating voltage of 12 kV and a working distance of 7.3 mm.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi software (version 2.6.44; Sydney, Australia). The assumption of normal data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-way ANOVA was used to analyze bond strength, and intergroup comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test. An independent samples t-test was performed to compare the density values between healthy and diabetic groups.

Results

Dentin density measurements were conducted using the Hounsfield Unit (HU) scale, with the average values illustrated in Fig. 3. The healthy group demonstrated a higher mean HU value (M = 1610, SD = 93.3) compared to the diabetic group (M = 1557, SD = 136); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.192). These results indicate that, within the parameters of this study, diabetes mellitus had no statistically significant impact on dentin density as measured by HU values.

The interfacial layer between the root canal sealer and dentin was assessed using SEM-EDS, and elemental mapping was applied to the images obtained (Fig. 4). The elemental composition for each group is presented in Table 3. The systemic condition of the teeth, whether diabetic or healthy, did not significantly affect the elemental distribution within the interfacial layer. Nonetheless, differences in elemental composition were noted depending on the specific type of sealer used. Post-hoc analysis using the Tukey test identified significant variations in the chemical makeup of the interfacial layer formed by different sealers. In particular, statistically significant differences were detected in the levels of carbon (C), oxygen (O), calcium (Ca), and phosphorus (P). The BioRoot RCS group exhibited significantly lower carbon levels than the AH Plus group (p = 0.024). Conversely, oxygen levels were significantly higher in the BioRoot RCS group compared to the AH Plus group (p = 0.018). Additionally, calcium content was greater in the BioRoot RCS group (p = 0.027), while phosphorus levels were markedly elevated in this group compared to AH Plus (p = 0.004). No statistically significant differences were found for elements such as zinc (Zn), silicon (Si), chlorine (Cl), and zirconium (Zr) (p > 0.05). Discussion

Systemic health conditions can significantly impact the outcomes of endodontic treatments, especially root canal therapy [18]. Among these, diabetes mellitus poses particular challenges due to its systemic influence on the dentin–pulp complex [19]. This study aimed to investigate the effects of diabetes mellitus on dentin radiodensity, the push-out bond strength of different root canal sealers, and the elemental composition at the dentin–sealer interface, as analyzed using SEM-EDS.

Diabetes causes a chemical change called glycation in the collagen proteins in dentin tissue [5]. This causes dentin to become harder but more fragile. Within the dentin tissue, there are small canals called tubules. The diameter and density of these tubules are greater in the parts of the tooth closest to the internal pulp region. The peritubular dentin is a structure that surrounds these tubules and contains more minerals. The other region that makes up the majority of the tooth is the intertubular dentin [20]. In diabetic patients, this peritubular dentin may be more affected because diabetes impairs calcium deposition and reduces mineralization. This can lead to enlarged tubule diameters and increased tubule density [6]. Previous studies have highlighted that diabetes can cause structural changes in dentin, including variations in tubule density and diameter [12, 21, 22].

CBCT provides the opportunity for detailed three-dimensional imaging of dental and bone tissues, as well as for the radiological evaluation of various parameters in the field of dentistry [23]. For instance, the HU scale, developed in 1972, is used to quantitatively measure mineral density in CBCT images [24]. Density measurements of mineralized tissues, including bone and dentin, can assign numerical values based on radiodensity thanks to the HU scale [25]. Therefore, in our study, the density of healthy and diabetic root dentin tissues was radiologically evaluated by conducting HU measurements on CBCT sections. There was no significant difference detected between diabetic and healthy teeth using HU-based radiodensity measurements. This may be attributed to the potential limitations of CBCT in detecting subtle histochemical changes in dentin. One limitation of the present study is the lack of complementary analytical techniques; therefore, future investigations employing methods such as FTIR or Raman spectroscopy may provide more precise insights into dentin mineralization and organic matrix characteristics, enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of diabetic dentin.

Only two previous studies have investigated the bonding of composite to coronal dentin [16] and the bond strength of MTA to root dentin in diabetic teeth [6]. The present study was the first to evaluate the bond strength of permanent obturation with AH Plus, BioRoot RCS, and Total Fill BC sealer-Hi Flow to diabetic tooth dentin. The push-out test findings clearly demonstrated that diabetes negatively affects the bonding of root canal sealers to dentin. This result confirmed previous findings that diabetes adversely influences the bonding of materials to dentin. On the other hand, the 3 root canal sealers used showed similar bond strength values in both diabetic and healthy teeth. Although the mechanism of adhesion of these materials to the dentin layer is not fully understood [26], AH Plus interacts with the collagen network of root dentin to form resin tag bonds [27]. In the case of BioRoot RCS and Total Fill BC Sealer Hi Flow, chemical bonding is based on hydration reactions [28].

The reduced fracture resistance observed in diabetic specimens may be attributed to decreased absorption of calcium and phosphorus in diabetic individuals, which in turn negatively impacts the integrity of dental hard tissues [29]. On the other hand, calcium silicate-based materials are an excellent source of calcium ions and also have a significant effect on the durability of teeth [30]. Therefore, in our study, the interfacial layer was examined because the bioactivity of calcium silicate-containing materials may have an effect on the mineralization and trace element levels in the root canal dentin of diabetic patients. EDX systems allow for detailed elemental analysis of dental materials, tooth tissues, and their interactions [31]. According to our results, samples filled with BioRoot RCS showed a higher percentage of Ca and P ions in the interfacial space compared to samples filled with AH Plus. Also, Total Fill Sealer Hi Flow showed higher Ca and P levels than AH Plus, but the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, a previous study indicated that calcium silicate sealers contained more Ca at the interface than AH Plus [32].

Previous studies have indicated that calcium silicate-based materials can exhibit distinct surface precipitate formations depending on the type of immersion medium used, such as distilled water, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), or Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) [33, 34]. In this study, however, the specimens were immersed solely in distilled water, enabling the assessment of the materials’ intrinsic biological activity without the influence of additional ions or buffers.

The use of ex vivo human teeth is one limitation of this study, as it does not fully replicate the intraoral conditions or the effects of diabetes on dentin over time. Furthermore, while SEM-EDS offers valuable insights into elemental distribution, it primarily reflects surface interactions and does not reflect those occurring in dentin tubules. With an extensive sample size, different tooth groups can be measured for radioopacity levels. The present study examined the initial condition of diabetic and healthy teeth after root canal treatment in vitro. Further studies could investigate whether providing Ca and P with bioactive root canal sealers contributes to the long-term durability of teeth in patients with diabetes and improves the bonding of root canal fillings.

Conclusions

No significant difference was observed between diabetic teeth and healthy teeth in terms of radiodensity when evaluated according to the HU scale. The bond strength of diabetic teeth was found to be significantly lower than healthy teeth, regardless of the root canal sealer used. However, the chemical structure of the filling material may play an important role in its interaction with dentin, leading to variations in the elemental levels of the interface layer.
