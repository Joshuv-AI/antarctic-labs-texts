On balance properties of hypercubic billiard words
N. Bédaride, V. Berthé, A. Julien

Abstract
This paper studies balance properties for billiard words. Billiard words generalize Sturmian words by coding trajectories in hypercubic billiards. In the setting of aperiodic order, they also provide the simplest examples of quasicrystals, as tilings of the line obtained via cut and project sets with a cubical canonical window. By construction, the number of occurrences of each letter in a factor (i.e., a string of consecutive letters) of a hypercubic billiard word only depends on the length of the factor, up to an additive constant. In other words, the difference of the number of occurrences of each letter in factors of the same length is bounded. In contrast with the behaviour of letters, we prove the existence of words that are not balanced in billiard words: the difference of the number of occurrences of such unbalanced factors in longer factors of the same length is unbounded. The proof relies both on topological methods inspired by tiling cohomology and on arithmetic results on bounded remainder sets for toral translations.

Introduction

Given an infinite word
u
over a finite alphabet, the function which counts the number of factors of a given length of
u
(i.e., the strings of consecutive letters that occur in
u
) is a classical measure of disorder, which
extends naturally to tilings, by counting patterns of a given size, and to higher-dimensional words, by counting e.g. the number of rectangular factors of a given size.
This has led to striking results, in particular in the direction of Nivat’s conjecture, which aims to extend the following simple characterization of periodicity: if an infinite word over a finite alphabet has at most
n
factors of length
n
, then it
is ultimately periodic. This result, which is known as Morse and Hedlund’s theorem
[
MH40
]
, admits a simple and combinatorial proof.
Nivat’s conjecture then states that if a two-dimensional word admits at most
mn
rectangular configurations of size
(m,n)
, then it admits at least one direction of periodicity. There have been exciting recent developments concerning this conjecture and despite its apparent simplicity, one of the most promising approach involves non-trivial algebraic methods.
The algebraic approach developed by J. Kari and his coauthors in
[
Sza18
,
KM19
,
KS20
,
KM20
,
KM23
]
is indeed based on the representation of configurations as formal power series; periodicity for a configuration is then expressed as having a non-trivial annihilator which, through Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz,
yields powerful decomposition results. Hence,
low complexity configurations can be decomposed into a sum of finitely many periodic configurations, but with possibly infinite alphabet
(which also yields an asymptotic version of Nivat’s conjecture)
[
KS20
]
.

We focus here on the notion of balance which can also be considered as a measure of disorder. This is a priori a purely combinatorial notion, and yet,
we will see here also that the involved methods go far beyond combinatorics.
An infinite
u
over a finite alphabet is said to be
balanced
if letters occur evenly in the factors of
u
of the same length. More precisely,
there exists a constant
C
such that for every letter
a
and for every pair
w,w^{\prime}
of factors of
u
of the same length,
the number of occurrences of
a
in
w
differs from its number of occurrences in
w
by at most
C
. In other words, the number of occurrences of each letter only depends on the length of the word, up to an additive constant. This notion extends to factors by replacing occurrences of letters by occurrences of factors.

For more on the subject, see the survey
[
Vui03
]
.

Balance was first studied by Morse and Hedlund in the form of
1
-balance (i.e., with
C=1
) for letters in the setting of words over a two-letter alphabet in the seminal papers
[
MH38
,
MH40
]
; they prove that the family of
1
-balanced aperiodic infinite words over a two-letter alphabet coincides with the family of Sturmian words. Moreover, Sturmian words have been proved to be balanced on their factors in
[
FV02
]
.
Words over a larger alphabet that are
1
-balanced have been characterized in
[
Hub00
]
(see also
[
Gra73
]
) and shown to be closely related to Sturmian words.

In the multidimensional framework, balance has been considered both for multidimensional words

[
BT02
]
and for tilings
[
Sad16
]
.
The study of balance belongs to the general domain of aperiodic order which refers to the mathematical formalization of quasicrystals (see e.g.
[
BG13
]
). For tilings and Delone sets, balance is closely related to the notion of bounded distance equivalence to lattice
[
Lac92
,
HKK17a
]
.
More generally, the notion of balance occurs in number theory in connection with Fraenkel’s conjecture
[
Fra73
,
Tij00
]
, in operations research, for optimal routing and scheduling (see e.g.
[
AGH00
,
BC04
,
BJ08
,
Tij80
]
). Balance is also closely related to symbolic discrepancy, as
investigated in
[
Ada03
,
Ada04
]
.

There is a classical way of constructing balanced sequences, and in particular Sturmian sequences, in terms of cutting sequences.
Sturmian sequences are indeed codings of trajectories of billiards on a square table with an irrational direction and, by unfolding trajectories, they code whether a horizontal or a vertical side of a lattice square is hit. Billiard words generalize Sturmian words
to larger alphabets by considering hypercubic billiards, as studied in
[
AMST94b
,
AMST94a
]
.
Billiard words can equivalently be defined in terms of codings of toral translations.
Indeed, billiard words on
d+1
letters code translations of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Balance properties for letters for billiard words have been considered in
[
AV22
,
AV23
]
; see also
[
BCC+24
]
which studies
generalizations of billiard words with low discrepancy.

The aim of this paper is to study balance properties for factors of hypercubic billiard words by using two types of results.
We use on the one hand topological properties concerning the cohomology of tilings for cut and project sets (see
Section

5
), and, on the other hand, results developed in
[
GL15
,
GL16
]
, whose main concerns are bounded remainder sets for toral translations, with methods relying on Fourier analysis.

Definition 3
.

The symbolic dynamical system associated with the biinfinite word

.

be a subshift with

2.2
Balance and discrepancy

There are some factors for which the convergence in (
1
) can be significantly improved. This is the situation discussed in this section in terms
of balance and bounded discrepancy.
A biinfinite word
x
is said to be
balanced
on the factor
v
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every pair
(u,u^{\prime})
of factors of
x
of the same length we have

||u|_{v}-|u^{\prime}|_{v}|\leq C.

A biinfinite word
x\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is
balanced on letters
if it is balanced on each letter in
\mathcal{A}
. It is
balanced on factors of length
n
if it is balanced on all its factors of length
n
, and, lastly, it is
balanced on factors
if it is balanced on all its factors.
Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift. Similarly, it is said to be balanced on letters if there exists
x\in X
which is balanced on letters,
it is balanced on factors of length
n
if there exists
x\in X
which is balanced on factors of length
n
, and it is balanced on factors if it is balanced on all its factors.
By minimality, since all the elements of a minimal subshift have the same language, one can replace above “there exists
x\in X
” by “for all
x\in X
”.
By
[
Ada03
, Lemma 23]
, if a minimal subshift
(X,S)
is balanced for length
n
, then it is balanced for all lengths
k
which are smaller than or equal to
n
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, and let
w\in{\mathcal{L}}(x)
. One has

S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w})=\Card\bigl\{i\in[0,n-1]\,:\,x_{i}\cdots x_{i+|w|-1}=w\bigr\}=|x_{[0,n-1]}|_{w}.

The quantity
\sup_{n}|S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w})-n\mu[w]|
is called the
discrepancy
of
x
with respect to
w
. Observe that
S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w})-n\mu[w]=S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w}-{\mathbf{1}})
where
{\mathbf{1}}
is the constant function.

The following result is a folklore result (see e.g.
[
BT02
]
). We recall its proof for the sake of self-containedness.

Proposition 4
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift and let
x\in X
. Let
w\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
.
The word
x
is balanced on
w
if and only if there exist
C\geq 0
and
\mu_{w}\geq 0
such that

|S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w})-n\mu_{w}|\leq C
for all
n
.

Proof.

Let
C>0
be such that
x
is
C
-balanced on
w
.
For every non-negative integer
p
, let
N_{p}
be such that
for every factor
v
of length
p
of
x
,
|v|_{w}

belongs to the set
\{N,N+1,\cdots,N+C\}
.

We first observe that the sequence
(N_{p}/p)_{p\in{\mathbb{N}^{*}}}
is a Cauchy sequence.
Indeed, set
v=x_{0}\cdots x_{pq-1}
, with
p,q
positive integers.
One has

pN_{q}\leq|v|_{w}\leq pN_{q}+pC,\ \quad qN_{p}\leq|v|_{w}\leq qN_{p}+qC.

We deduce that
-qC\leq qN_{p}-pN_{q}\leq pC
and thus
-C\leq N_{p}-pN_{q}/q\leq pC/q
.

Let
\mu_{w}
stand for
\lim_{q}N_{q}/q
. By letting
q
tend to infinity, one then deduces that

-C\leq N_{p}-p\mu_{w}\leq 0
. Observe that
\mu_{w}
is the frequency of
w
in
x
. Indeed, by minimality, this is sufficient to consider the non-negative one-sided part of
x
,
i.e.,
(x_{n})_{n\geq N}
.

The other direction comes from triangular inequality.
∎

We now give a geometric interpretation of balance on letters.

Lemma 5
.

Let
(X,S)
, with

. Let

.
The biinfinite word

We assume that

is balanced on letters. Consider the line directed by the vector

, where the

are given by

2.3
Balance and spectral properties

We now relate balance with the existence of eigenvalues.
Let
(X,S)
be a subshift.
A non-zero complex-valued continuous function
f
in
{\mathcal{C}}(X,\mathbb{C})

is an
eigenfunction
for
(X,S)
if there exists

\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
for all
x
in
X
, one has
f(Sx)=\lambda f(x).

The eigenvalues corresponding to those eigenfunctions
are called the

of
(X,S)
. If
\theta
is such that

is an eigenvalue,

\theta
is said to be an
additive topological eigenvalue
. We denote by

the additive group of additive continuous eigenvalues of
(X,S)
.

We assume that
(X,S,\mu)
is minimal and uniquely ergodic (with
\mu
being the unique shift-invariant probability measure of
(X,S)
).
Let

be the additive subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}
generated by the measures of cylinders. One has by
[

, Proposition 2.3]
:

I(X,S)=\left\{\int fd\mu\,:\,f\in\mathcal{C}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})\right\}=\langle\mu[w]\,:\,w\in\mathcal{L}(X)\rangle,

with the notation
\langle\mu[w]\,:\,w\in\mathcal{L}(X)\rangle
standing for the additive group generated by the
\mu[w]
.
Moreover, one has
E(X,S)\subset I(X,S)
by
[
CDP16
, Proposition 11]
and
[

, Corollary 3.7]
.

Balance on letters provides a sufficient condition
for the additive group generated by measures of one-letter cylinders to be included in
E
under the assumption of unique ergodicity.
We recall indeed the following classical theorem from topological dynamics. Let

{\mathcal{C}}(X,\mathbb{R})
stand for the set of continuous functions defined on
X
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 6

(Gottshalk-Hedlund’s Theorem
[
GH55
]
)
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift and
f\in{\mathcal{C}}(X,\mathbb{R})
.
Then, there exists
x_{0}\in X
such that
(S^{n}f(x_{0}))_{n}
is a bounded sequence if and only if
f
is a coboundary, i.e., there exists a function
g\in{\mathcal{C}}(X,\mathbb{R})
such that
g\circ S-g=f.

Remark 7

Let
(X,S,\mu)
be a minimal and uniquely ergodic subshift and
f\in{\mathcal{C}}(X,\mathbb{R})
. If
f
is coboundary, then

\int fd\mu=0
, as a consequence of the ergodic theorem.

We thus deduce a classical folklore result that allows the reformulation of balance in spectral terms.

Proposition 8

Let
(X,S,\mu)
be a minimal and uniquely ergodic subshift. Let

be a factor of
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
.
If
(X,S)
is balanced on
w
, then
\mu[w]
is an additive topological eigenvalue of
(X,S)
.

Proof.

We assume that
(X,S)
is balanced on
w
.
Let
f={\bf 1}_{w}-\mu[w]{\bf 1}
, where

Since

is balanced on

Note that

Bounded remainder sets

So far, we have mostly considered symbolic dynamical systems made of sets of infinite words on which the shift map acts. We now consider their geometric counterpart in this setting, namely toral translations. We will see indeed in Section

Let

We first recall a classical spectral result (see e.g.

Lemma 9

3.1
Definitions

In this section, we discuss billiards in the hypercube in

Billiard trajectories in the hypercube

Billiard words are defined as codings of trajectories in hypercubic billiards when considering elastic reflexion on the boundary: the trajectories are obtained by moving with constant speed along straight lines until a face of the hypercube is hit.
More formally, consider the unit hypercube
C=[0,1]^{d+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. A billiard ball, i.e., a point mass, moves inside
C
with unit speed along a straight line until it reaches the boundary
\partial C
of
C
, then instantaneously changes direction according to the mirror law, and continues along the new line. Thus, we have a map
T
defined on a subset of
\partial C\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
as follows:
T(m,\boldsymbol{\theta})=(m^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})
, with the line
(mm^{\prime})
having direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
being the image of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
by the reflection along the face containing
m^{\prime}
. Note that the map
T
is not defined if the line
m+\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{\theta}
is included in a face of
C
, or if this line intersects
C
on a face of dimension strictly less that
d
. This is why we consider here a subset of
\partial C\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, but it is a subset of full measure.

Billiard subshifts
Let us then label the faces of the hypercube
C
by
d+1
symbols from the finite alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}=\{1,\cdots,d+1\}
such that any two opposite faces of the hypercube are coded by the same symbol. With the orbit of a point
(m,\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we associate the word with values in the alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
which is given by the sequence of faces of the billiard trajectory. We work with biinfinite words by considering orbits in the past and in the future, since the map
T
is invertible. We denote by
\phi
the map which sends
(m,\boldsymbol{\theta})
to the word which codes the billiard orbit.
Then, we have two subshifts. The first subshift
(X_{(m,\boldsymbol{\theta})},S)
is defined as the closure of the shift orbit of

. The second one, denoted as
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
,
is defined as the closure of all the orbits of points in the initial direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}

We will say that the direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is

under the action of

-action on the set of generic points of the window
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Moreover
m
is generic if and only if
\sigma(m)
is generic. Via this identification, the symbolic shift map
x\mapsto Sx
is related to the map
\sigma
acting on the set of generic points of
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as follows:
if
x(m)
is the word coding
\Lambda_{m}
, then
Sx(m)
codes
\Lambda_{\sigma(m)}
.

The question is now to find out how much bigger is
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
than the set of words
x(m)
obtained by considering generic points
m
of
W
. It turns out
that it is not that bigger:
each non-generic point
m
of
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
corresponds to a finite and bounded amount of words in
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, as stated below.

Lemma 15
.

[
FHK02b
]

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(1,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}
assumed to be irrational.
The dynamical systems
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
and
(W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\sigma)
are measurably conjugate, i.e.,
there exists a map
\eta:X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, that is
one-to-one precisely on the preimage of the set of generic points of
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
:

\begin{CD}X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}@>{S}>{}>X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\\
@V{}V{\eta}V@V{}V{\eta}V\\
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}@>{\sigma}>{}>W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\end{CD}

Moreover, the cardinality of
\eta^{-1}(\{m\})
is bounded and only depends on the dimension
d+1
of the space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Note that continuous functions defined on
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
can be pulled back to continuous functions on
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
; however there are more continuous functions on
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
than the pullbacks from
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(for example, the subshift
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is totally disconnected, it has therefore many locally constant functions and the map
\eta
is not a topological conjugacy, it is not a homeomorphism).
This almost-identification of
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
allows us to have a geometric interpretation of statements on the subshift
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(see
Section

5
).

4
Cohomology and balance

The main concept for proving
Theorem

1
is cohomology, especially some cohomology classes defined by Clark and Sadun
[
CS03
,
CS06
]

for tiling spaces and identified in the cut and project setting by Kellendonk and Sadun
[
KS17
, Section 6]
. We focus namely on the class of asymptotically negligible cocycles. Note that corresponding notions exist in the symbolic dynamics setting, which are strongly related to dimension groups for subshifts
(a dimension group is an ordered abelian group which is related to the orbit structure of the subshift).
For more on the cohomology of tilings, see for instance
[
Kel03
,
KP06
,
Sad07
,
Sad08
,
AR16
]
and for more on the symbolic view point, see e.g.
[
GPS95
,
DP22
]
.

4.1
First cohomological definitions

Cohomology classes can be represented by functions on
X
or on
X\times\mathbb{Z}
, which satisfy specific properties.
In all that follows,
\mathbb{A}
stands either for
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 16
.

Let
(X,S)
be a subshift.
A cochain with values in
\mathbb{A}
is a function
\phi:X\times\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
.
It is a
cocycle
if it satisfies the following condition, for all
x\in X
and all
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\phi(x,n+m)=\phi(x,n)+\phi(S^{n}x,m).

A cochain is a
coboundary
if there exists a function
g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
such that, for all
x\in X
and all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, one has

\phi(x,n)=g(S^{n}x)-g(x).

(3)

If we need the precision, we say that
\phi
is the coboundary of
g
.

Example 17
.

Let
(X,S)
be a subshift defined over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. Let
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
We recall that the notation
{\mathbf{1}}_{w}
stands for the characteristic function of the cylinder
[w]
on
X
.
We define the factor-counting cochain
\psi_{w}
as

\psi_{w}(x,n)=\Card\bigl\{i\in[0,n-1]\,:\,x_{i}\cdots x_{i+|w|-1}=w\bigr\}\mbox{ for }n\geq 0

\psi_{w}(x,n)=\Card\bigl\{i\in[n,-1]\,:\,x_{i}\cdots x_{i+|w|-1}=w\bigr\}\mbox{ for }n<0

for all
x
in
X
and for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
One checks that
\psi_{w}(x,n)=S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w})(x)
, for all
x
in
X
and for all
n
,
which implies that the cochain
\psi_{w}
is in fact a cocycle.

Remark 18
.

Note that all coboundaries are automatically cocycles.
Observe also that the one-dimensional tilings of the line
E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
induced by the billiard words in
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(as evoked in
Section

3.3
) provide a decomposition of
the line into
0
-cells (vertices) and
1
-cells (edges/tiles). A
k
-cochain is an assignment of a real number to each
k
-cell.
Technically, we should call cochains “
1
-cochains”, while the function
g
shoud be called “
0
-cochain”, in order to be able to say
that “a
1
-coboundary is the coboundary of a
0
-cochain”. Since we only work in degree
0
and
1
(we work with words and one-dimensional tilings), we have chosen not to keep
the reference to the degree in the terminology used in the present paper.

In the symbolic dynamics literature, it is common to see coboundaries defined in terms of functions on
X
with values in
\mathbb{A}
. In fact, there is a one-to-one correspondance between functions and cocycles (as considered in
Definition

16
).
Indeed, given a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
, we associate with it the cochain
\phi
defined as follows
for all
x\in X
:
\phi(x,1)=f(x)
and for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}

\begin{array}[]{ll}\phi(x,n)&=f(x)+f\circ S(x)+\cdots+f\circ S^{n-1}(x),\mbox{ for }n\geq 0,\\
\phi(x,n)&=f\circ S^{-1}x+\cdots+f\circ S^{n}(x),\mbox{ for }n<0.\end{array}

(4)

In other words, for
n\geq 0
,
\phi(x,n)
is equal to the value at
x
of the Birkhoff sum
S_{n}f
of order
n
associated with
f
.
One then checks that the cochain
\phi
satisfies the cocyle relation. This thus allows us to extend the notion of a coboundary to functions.

Definition 19
.

Let
(X,S)
be a subshift.
Let
{\mathcal{F}}(X,\mathbb{A})
stand for the set of functions defined on
X
with values in
\mathbb{A}
.
A function
f\in{\mathcal{F}}(X,\mathbb{A})
is said to be a coboundary if there exists
g
in
{\mathcal{F}}(X,\mathbb{A})

such that

f=g\circ S-g.

(5)

The set of coboundaries of
{\mathcal{F}}(X,A)
is denoted as
\partial{\mathcal{F}}(X,A)
.

Two functions
f,g

in
{\mathcal{F}}(X,A)
are said to be
cohomologous
if
f-g
is a coboundary.
This provides an equivalence relation on
{\mathcal{F}}(X,A)
. This equivalence relation can also
be defined on the set of cochains that are cocycles:
two cocyles are
cohomologous
if their difference is a coboundary.

Note that when restricting to continuous functions and to
\mathbb{A}={\mathbb{Z}}
, we recover the group part
{\mathcal{C}}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})/\delta{\mathcal{C}}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
of the dynamical dimension group; see for instance

[
GPS95
]
.

4.2
Strong and asymptotically negligible cocycles

We now define two notions of cohomologies depending on topological properties of the associated functions, both in the setting of cocycles and functions. Examples will be provided below and in
Section

5.1
. A crucial point is that we consider
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 20
.

Let
(X,S)
be a subshift.

•

A cocycle is said to be a strong cocycle if, for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the following map is locally constant:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}X&\to&\mathbb{R}\\
x&\mapsto&\phi(x,n).\end{array}

We define
H^{1}_{\tt{strong}}(X,\mathbb{R})
as the group of classes of locally constant cocycles modulo coboundaries of locally constant functions (such as defined in
Eq.

3
),
and analogously
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\tt{strong}}(X,\mathbb{R})
as the group of classes of locally constant functions,
modulo coboundaries of locally constant functions (such as defined in
Eq.

5
).

•

A strong cocycle is said to be
asymptotically negligible
if it is a coboundary of a continuous function.
Analogously, a locally constant function is said to be asymptotically negligible if it is a coboundary of a continuous function.

We will use the notation
H^{1}_{\tt{an}}(X,\mathbb{R})
for the group generated by the classes of asymptotically negligible cocyles, modulo coboundaries of locally constant functions,
and
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\tt{an}}(X,\mathbb{R})
for the group generated by asymptotically negligible functions, modulo coboundaries of locally constant functions.

Note that with the previous notation,
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\tt{an}}(X,\mathbb{R})
is equal to the classes of elements of
\delta{\mathcal{C}}(X,{\mathbb{R}})
, where
\delta{\mathcal{C}}(X,{\mathbb{R}})

stands for the set of continuous functions defined on
X
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Considering locally constant functions in the notion of strong cohomology is related to strong pattern equivariance in the tiling literature
[
Sad16
]
, whereas considering continuous functions corresponds to weakly pattern equivalent functions.

Remark 21
.

We recall that a continuous function with values in
{\mathbb{Z}}
is locally constant. Moreover, if a continuous function with values in
{\mathbb{Z}}
is a coboundary, then it is the coboundary of some continuous function
[
DP22
, Proposition 3.1.3]
.

Consider now the case of functions taking real values. An asymptotically negligible function
f=g\circ S-g
, with
g
continuous with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
, has no reason to be be locally constant (this would be the case if
functions would take integral values). It thus makes sense to consider classes in
\mathcal{H}_{\tt{strong}}(X,{\mathbb{R}}
) of asymptotically negligible elements (they are not necessarily reduced to the
zero class when considered modulo coboundaries of locally constant functions).

Lemma 22
.

Let
(X,S)
be a subshift on
{\mathcal{A}}
.
For every
w\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
of length
k
,
the factor-counting map
\psi_{w}
defined by

\psi_{w}(x,n)=\Card\bigl\{i\in[0,n-1]\,:\,x_{i}\cdots x_{i+k-1}=w\bigr\}

(6)

is a strong cocycle.

Proof.

We have seen that the cochain
\psi_{w}(x,n)=S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w})(x)
, for all
x
in
X
and for all
n
, is a cocycle (by
Example

17
).
Now for all
n
, the map
x\mapsto\psi_{w}(x,n)
is a continuous function that takes integer values. It is thus locally constant and we have a strong cocycle.
∎

Let us now revisit the results of
Section

2.3
.
By direct application of Gottshalk-Hedlund’s Theorem (see
Theorem

6
) we deduce the following characterization of asymptotically negligible cocyles.

Corollary 23
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift.
A strong cocycle
\phi:X\times\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is asymptotically negligible if and only if for all (equivalently for one)
x
in
X
, the map
n\mapsto\phi(x,n)
is bounded.

Let us investigate what balance means in terms of cohomology.

Let
{\mathbf{l}}:X\times\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
stand for the length-counting cocycle defined
{\mathbf{l}}(x,n)=n
for all
x\in X
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that it is not an asymptotically negligible cocycle.

Proposition 24
.

Let
(X,S,\mu)
be a minimal and uniquely ergodic subshift. Let
w
be a finite factor of its language
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
.
The subshift
(X,S)
is balanced on
w
if and only if the cocycle
\psi_{w}-\mu[w]\mathbf{l}
is asymptotically negligible, which is in turn equivalent to
S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\mu[w]{\mathbf{1}})
being bounded uniformly in
n
.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
22
that
\psi_{w}
is a strong cocyle. This is also the case of
\psi_{w}-\mu[w]\mathbf{l}
.
By Corollary
23
the symbolic discrepancy
S_{n}({\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\mu[w]{\mathbf{1}})
is bounded uniformly in
n
if and only if the cocycle is asymptotically negligible.
We then use
Proposition

4
.
∎

5
On the cohomology group of the billiard

We first recall a key fact about the cohomology group of the hypercubic billiard which is central for the proof of
Theorem

1
.

Theorem 25
.

[
FHK02b
, Theorem 6.10]

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}_{+}
be an irrational direction. Let
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
be the billiard shift associated with the direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Let
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
stand for the hyperplane with normal vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
If
d\geq 2
, then the cohomology group
H^{1}_{\tt{strong}}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
of the subshift
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
(as defined in
Definition

20
) is not finitely generated.

The aim of this section is to discuss in more details asymptotically negligible elements. This is the second ingredient required for the proof of
Theorem

1
.

5.1
Examples of asymptotically negligible elements for billiards

We first provide in this section a geometric way for constructing asymptotically negligible elements for an irrational dimensional hypercubic billiard in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
.

We use the following notation: if
a\in\{1,2,\ldots,d+1\}
,
e_{a}
stands for the corresponding basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, and if
w\in\{1,2,\ldots,d+1\}^{*}
,
e_{w}=\sum_{i}e_{w_{i}}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}_{+}
be an irrational direction.

Let
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
be the billiard shift associated with the direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Given a vector
v\in E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
, the cocycle
\phi_{v}
is defined on
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\times\mathbb{Z}
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
as follows: for all
x\in X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, one has

\phi_{v}(x,n)=\langle v,e_{x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n-1}}\rangle,\mbox{ for all }\ n\geq 0,

\phi_{v}(x,n)=\langle v,e_{x_{-n}\cdots x_{-1}}\rangle,\mbox{ for all }\ n<0.

This cocycle is defined with respect to the geometric realization of prefixes of elements of
X
obtained by “abelianizing” words
w
in
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
by vectors
(|w_{a}|)_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
.
The corresponding function is

f_{v}:X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\ x\mapsto\langle v,e_{x_{0}}\rangle.

Lemma 26
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}_{+}
be an irrational direction. Let
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
be the billiard shift associated with the direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Let
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
stand for the hyperplane with normal vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
For any
v\in L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
, the map
\phi_{v}
is an asymptotically negligible strong cocycle.

Proof.

We have to prove that the map
\phi_{v}
is a locally constant cocyle that is the coboundary of a continuous function.
First, we prove that the map
\phi_{v}
is a cocycle. Let
x\in X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. One has

\phi_{v}(x,n+m)=\langle v,e_{x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n+m-1}}\rangle=\langle v,e_{x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n-1}}\rangle+\langle v,e_{x_{n}x_{n+1}\cdots x_{n+m-1}}\rangle,

i.e.,

\phi_{v}(x,n+m)=\phi_{v}(x,n)+\phi_{v}(S^{n}x,m).

One similarly checks the cocyle relation for
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In addition, for any fixed
n
,
\phi_{v}(x,n)
only depends on
x_{[-n,-1]}
(
n<0
) or on
x_{[0,n-1]}
(
n\geq 0
): it is therefore locally constant, it is thus a strong cocycle.

Let us check that the map
\phi_{v}
is the coboundary of the function
b
defined on
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as:

b(x)=\langle v,\eta(x)\rangle,\mbox{ for all }x\in X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

where
\eta
is the almost everywhere one-to-one factor map
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
defined in
Section

3.3
(see
Lemma

15
).
Let
x\in X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. We first assume
n\geq 0
.
One has

b(S^{n}x)-b(x)=\langle v,\eta(S^{n}x)\rangle-\langle v,\eta(w)\rangle=\langle v,\sigma^{n}(m)\rangle-\langle v,m\rangle,

with the map
\sigma
being defined in
Section

3.3
.
Since
\sigma(m)=m+\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(e_{x_{0}})
, we deduce that
\sigma^{n}(m)=m+\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(e_{x_{0}\dots x_{n-1}}),

which yields

b(S^{n}x)-b(x)=\langle v,\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(e_{x_{0}}\dots e_{x_{n-1}})\rangle.

Since
v
belongs to
E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
, we obtain

b(S^{n}x)-b(x)=\langle v,e_{x_{0}\dots x_{n-1}}\rangle=\phi_{v}(x,n).

The case
n<0
is handled in the same way.

The continuity of
b
is a consequence of the continuity of
\eta
.

∎

5.2
On the rational rank of asymptotic negligibles

It turns out that the asymptotically negligible cochains
\phi_{v}
from
Section

5.1
are generators of
H^{1}_{\tt an}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 27
.

[
KS17
, Theorem 1.3]

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}_{+}
be an irrational direction.
Let
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
be the hypercubic billiard subshift with direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Let
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
stand for the hyperplane with normal vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The real vector space of asymptotically negligible cochains
H^{1}_{\tt an}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
has dimension
d
and is generated by the classes of the functions
\phi_{v}
, for
v\in L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
(as defined in
Section

5.1
).

We recall that equivalence classes are taken up to coboundaries of locally constant functions.
We complete this statement by providing explicitly a set of generators
of
H^{1}_{\tt an}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
below. This will be used in the discussion of
Section

6.3
.

Proposition 28
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}_{+}
be an irrational vector and
let
(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},S)
be the associated hypercubic billiard subshift.

The real vector subspace of
H^{1}_{\tt strong}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
generated by the
\psi_{a}
(
a\in\mathcal{A}
) coincides with the
real vector subspace generated by the
\phi_{v}
(
v\in L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
) and
\mathbf{1}
. It has dimension
d+1
.

Moreover, the
d
-dimensional real vector space of asymptotically negligible cochains
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\tt an}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\mathbb{R}})
is generated by the classes of the functions
{\mathbf{1}}_{a}-\mu[a]{\mathbf{1}}
, for
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Note first that the characteristic functions
{\bf 1}_{w}
, for
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, are locally constant.
Their classes are elements of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\text{strong}}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
.
Let
v\in E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
. The cocycle
\phi_{v}
defined in
Section

5.1

is an asymptotic negligible cocyle by Lemma
26
.

\bullet

We now prove that the (real) vector subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\tt{strong}}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
spanned by the classes
of characteristic functions
{\bf 1}_{a}
, for the letters
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, is equal to the subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\tt{strong}}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
spanned by the classes
of
\psi_{v}
(
v\in L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
) and
{\mathbf{1}}
.

For any given letter
a
in
\mathcal{A}
,
we define
v_{a}
in
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
as the vector uniquely defined by the following system of
d
equations

\langle\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(e_{b}),v_{a}\rangle=1,\quad b\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a\}.

This system of
d
equations has indeed a unique solution since
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
has dimension
d
together with the
irrationality of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Indeed the additive group generated by the vector
f_{i}
, i.e.,
\langle\mathbb{Z}f_{i}\,:\,i=1,\ldots,d+1\rangle
is dense in
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
. It implies that any subfamily of
d
vectors among the set of vectors
\{f_{i}\,:\,i=1,\ldots,d+1\}
has to be independent,
and the system above has a unique solution.
We then set
\alpha_{a}=\langle\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(e_{a}),v_{a}\rangle.
Since
v_{a}\in L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
, note that
one has

\langle e_{b},v_{a}\rangle=1\mbox{ for }b\neq a,\mbox{ and }\langle e_{a},v_{a}\rangle=\alpha_{a}.

Let us first prove that
\alpha_{a}<0
for all
a
. One has, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{v_{a}}(x,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\langle v_{a},e_{x_{i}}\rangle=\alpha_{a}|x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}|_{a}+1\bigl(n-|x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}|_{a}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{a}-1)\psi_{a}(x,n)+{\mathbf{l}}(x,n).

(7)

Now for all
n
,
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(e_{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}})\in W_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(see the notation of
Section

3.1
), and in particular, it is bounded (with respect to
n
).
Therefore,
\phi_{v_{a}}(x,n)
is bounded uniformly in
n
.
It implies (by unique ergodicity) that

(\alpha_{a}-1)\mu[a]=-1
, which implies that
\alpha_{a}-1=-\frac{1}{\mu[a]}<-1
, and thus
\alpha_{a}<0.

We now can deduce that any set of
d
vectors among the
v_{a},a\in\mathcal{A}
, is a set of
d
linearly independent vectors.
Indeed, assume, up to permutation and by contradiction, that there is a linear combination of the
d-1
first vectors such that

\sum_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\lambda_{i}v_{i}=v_{d}.

By construction, one has
\langle v_{i},e_{d+1}\rangle=1
for
1\leq i\leq d
. Therefore, by considering the scalar product with
e_{d+1}
, one obtains
\sum_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\lambda_{i}=1
.
Besides,
\langle v_{i},e_{d}\rangle=1
for
i\leq d-1
and
\alpha_{d}=\langle v_{d},e_{d}\rangle<0
, as proved in the previous paragraph. Therefore,
by considering the scalar product with
e_{d}
, one obtains

\sum_{1\leq i\leq d-1}\lambda_{i}=\alpha_{d}<1
, which provides the desired contradiction.

We thus deduce that the
d
-dimensional space
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
is generated by
v_{1},\cdots,v_{d}
.
Since the map
v\mapsto\phi_{v}
is a linear map, this implies that any
\phi_{v}
, with
v\in L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
, is a linear combination of the
\phi_{v_{i}}
’s for
i=1,\cdots,d
.
Hence, we have proved that the subspace of
H^{1}_{\tt{strong}}(X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbb{R})
spanned by the classes
of
\phi_{v}
(
v\in L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\perp}
) is generated by
\phi_{v_{1}},\cdots,\phi_{v_{d-1}}
.
Moreover, we deduce from (
7
) that, for all
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
,

It remains now to prove that the rank

. For all nonnegative integer

Remark 29
.

We deduce from Proposition
28
that if
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is balanced on
w
, then
\psi_{w}
is cohomologous to a linear combination of the cocyles
\psi_{a}
and
\mathbf{1}
.
We also can deduce it directly.
Indeed, let
w
be such that
{\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\mu[w]
is a coboundary of a continuous function.
Then,
\mu[w]
is an additive eigenvalue, that is,

\mu[w]
belongs to the additive group generated by the
\mu[a]
, by Proposition
8
.
Let
\lambda_{a}
, for
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, be integers such that

\mu[w]=\sum\lambda_{a}\mu[a]
.
One has

{\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\sum\lambda_{a}{\mathbf{1}}_{a}={\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\mu[w]-\sum\lambda_{a}({\mathbf{1}}_{a}-\mu[a]).

Hence

{\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\sum\lambda_{a}{\mathbf{1}}_{a}
is a coboundary of a continuous function, as a linear combination of coboundaries.
Since it takes integer values, it is a coboundary of a locally constant function.
Indeed an integer valued continuous function that is a real coboundary is a coboundary of a locally constant function (
[
Orm00
, Proposition 4.1]
).
One thus has
{\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\sum\lambda_{a}{\mathbf{1}}_{a}\equiv 0
.
This implies that the class of
{\mathbf{1}}_{w}-\mu[w]
coincides with the class of
\sum\lambda_{a}({\mathbf{1}}_{a}-\mu[a])
. It thus belongs to the

{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space generated by the
functions

{\mathbf{1}}_{a}-\mu[a]
, for
a
in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
