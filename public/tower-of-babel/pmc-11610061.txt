ABO blood type is not a risk factor of outcome in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage patients
Authors: Ruoran Wang, Jing Zhang, Jianguo Xu, Min He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between ABO blood types and outcome of aneurysm subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) has not reached a conclusion. This study was performed to testify the difference of outcome between ABO blood types.Methods663 aSAH patients hospitalized in the West China hospital were enrolled. The association between ABO blood types and outcomes including mortality and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) was verified by the univariate and multivariate logistic regression. Glasgow Outcome Score (GOS) at discharge, length of ICU stay, length of hospital stay were also compared between different ABO blood types.Results663 aSAH patients were divided into four groups according to their ABO blood types: AB type (46, 6.9%), A type (211, 31.8%), B type (169, 25.4%),O type (237, 35.7%). The GCS, WFNS, Hunt Hess, modified Fisher, aneurysm location and incidence of intraventricular hemorrhage did not show different distribution among ABO blood types. Among four coagulation indexes, only the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) showed different distribution among ABO blood types (p < 0.001). The AB group had the lowest level of APTT while O group had the highest level of APTT. Outcomes including delayed cerebral ischemia, mortality, GOS, length of ICU stay, and length of hospital stay did not show any significant differences among ABO types. Univariate logistic regression showed the ABO blood type was not associated with the development of DCI (p = 0.654) and the mortality (p = 0.430). The multivariate logistic regression showed ABO blood types were still not associated with DCI and mortality after adjusting confounding effects of significant risk factors.ConclusionsThe ABO blood type is not associated with the prognosis of aSAH patients. ABO blood types may not be helpful on risk stratification of aSAH.Keywords: Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Intracranial aneurysm, ABO type, Risk factor, Delayed cerebral ischemia

Introduction

Having been discovered for over a century, the ABO blood type is determined by the oligosaccharide types in agglutinogen expressed on the surface of red blood cells. The identification of ABO blood type is helpful to avoid hemolysis during blood transfusion. In addition, a growing number of researches found the ABO blood type was associated with risks of some diseases, including cancer, myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, and stroke [1–5]. And the ABO blood type also has influence on severities and prognoses of various patients, including sepsis, trauma, intracerebral hemorrhage, and viral pneumonia [6–10].

There are several studies with small sample size exploring the relationship between the ABO blood type and prognosis of aSAH patients [11–14]. While conclusions of these studies are not consistent with each other. Two studies concluded that the O type was associated with higher risk of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) [12, 13] while another one found the A type showed higher risk of DCI than the non-A type [14]. Furthermore, one study from the Germany confirmed that ABO blood types did not influence outcomes of aSAH, including cerebral vasospasm, intracerebral hemorrhage, and delayed infarction [11]. The divergence may be attributable to the sample size, race, region, and other potential confounding factors. We design and perform this study to testify the difference of outcomes among aSAH patients with different ABO blood types using relatively larger sample size in the southwestern region of China.

Methods

Patients

aSAH patients hospitalized in the neurosurgery department of West China hospital for treating ruptured aneurysm between January 2017 and June 2019 were enrolled for this study. The finding of aSAH was identified by typical clinical symptoms and further radiological examinations, including computed tomography angiography (CTA) and digital subtraction angiography (DSA). A part of aSAH patients were excluded if they met following criteria: (1) no records of ABO blood types (n = 21); (2) no records of coagulation indexes (n = 60); (3) transfer from other hospitals or admission to our hospital 48 h after the onset of typical symptoms (n = 6); and (4) history of coagulation system diseases and use of anticoagulants or antiplatelets (n = 2). 663 aSAH patients were included for the analysis after exclusion. This study obtained approvement from the ethics committee of West China hospital (2021–1684) and performed adhering to the Helsinki declaration. Informed consent forms of joining observational studies were signed by patients or their legal relatives once they admitted to our hospital.

Data collection

Demographical variables including age, gender, history of smoking, alcoholism, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension were collected. Vital signs on admission including systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and clinical severity scores including Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), WFNS, Hunt Hess, modified Fisher (mFisher) were recorded. Location of ruptured aneurysm and existence of intraventricular hemorrhage were identified. Laboratory examinations including glucose, hemoglobin, platelet, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrinogen were obtained by analyzing the first blood sample once patients admitted to our hospital (within 6 h after admission). The ABO blood type was also tested. The main outcome of this study was the in-hospital mortality. Other outcomes including delayed cerebral ischemia, Glasgow Outcome Score (GOS) at discharge, length of ICU stay, and length of hospital stay were also compared between different ABO blood groups. The diagnosis of delayed cerebral ischemia manifesting as clinical deterioration was confirmed based on the existence of a new focal functional defect (hemiplegia, aphasia, apraxia, hemianopsia or neglect) or a decrease of GCS at least 2 points, excluding other causes leading the secondary neurological deterioration (fever, infectious complications, hydrocephalus, seizures, respiratory failure, or electrolyte disorders).

Statistical analysis

The normality of variables was verified by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range). Counted variables were shown as numbers (percentage). The difference of variables between ABO blood types was testified by the Independent Student’s t test (normally distributed variables), Mann–Whitney U test (non-normally distributed variables), χ2 test and Fisher test (counted variables). The association between ABO blood types and outcomes including mortality and delayed cerebral ischemia was verified by the univariate and multivariate logistic regression. Significant factors in the univariate logistic regression were included in the subsequent multivariate logistic regression to adjust confounding effects and explore the independent effect of ABO blood types on outcomes. The two-sided p value below 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA).

Results

Baseline information and difference of aSAH patients among ABO blood types

633 aSAH patients were divided into 4 groups according to their ABO blood types: AB type (46, 6.9%), A type (211, 31.8%), B type (169, 25.4%),O type (237, 35.7%) (Table 1). Age, gender, smoking, alcoholism, diabetes mellitus, hypertension did not show different distribution among ABO blood groups. Furthermore, GCS, WFNS, Hunt Hess, modified Fisher, aneurysm location and incidence of intraventricular hemorrhage also did not show different distribution among ABO blood groups (Fig. 1). Among four coagulation indexes, only the APTT showed different distribution among ABO blood groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The AB group had the lowest level of APTT while O group had the highest level of APTT. Outcomes including delayed cerebral ischemia, mortality, GOS, length of ICU stay, and length of hospital stay did not show any significant differences among ABO groups.

Association between ABO blood types and outcomes of aSAH patients

Univariate logistic regression showed age (p = 0.003), diabetes mellitus (p = 0.025), GCS (p = 0.001), WFNS (p < 0.001), Hunt Hess (p < 0.001), intraventricular hemorrhage (p = 0.001), glucose (p = 0.021), INR (p = 0.023) were associated with the development of DCI. The ABO blood type was not associated with the development of DCI (p = 0.654) (Table 2). In addition, univariate logistic regression showed age (p < 0.001), GCS (p < 0.001), WFNS (p < 0.001), Hunt Hess (p < 0.001), mFisher (p < 0.001), intraventricular hemorrhage (p = 0.004), glucose (p < 0.001), INR (p < 0.001), fibrinogen (p = 0.018), delayed cerebral ischemia (p < 0.001) were associated with the mortality. The ABO blood type was not associated with the mortality (p = 0.430) (Table 3). The multivariate logistic regression showed ABO blood types were still not associated with DCI risk and mortality risk after adjusting confounding effects of significant risk factors (Table 4).

Discussion

The correlation between ABO blood types and outcomes of aSAH has been explored in four previous studies. One study from the Germany enrolling 470 aSAH patients published in 2017 initially reported that no significant differences of WFNS, Fisher grade, cerebral vasospasm, intracerebral hemorrhage, and delayed infarction were observed among ABO blood types [11]. Subsequently, one American study with 204 aSAH patients and another Pakistan study with 175 aSAH patients both indicated the O blood type showing increased risks of DCI after aSAH [12, 13]. In contrast, one UK study with 412 aSAH patients discovered the A blood type had a significantly higher risk of developing DCI [14]. Our study found the ABO blood type was not correlated with the risk of DCI and mortality of aSAH, which was consistent with the finding from the Germany. It's worth noting that all four previous studies have serious statistical flaws. Although the Germany study and the UK study both enrolled more than four hundred aSAH patients, they just compared the incidence of DCI among ABO blood types but not analyzed the independent association between blood types and the DCI through adjusting effects of confounders such as age, WFNS, aneurysm location. The American study with only 217 aSAH patients and 27 DCI events also did not adjust enough confounders. The Pakistan study with only 175 aSAH patients and 26 DCI events adjusted four factors, including BMI, mFisher, WFNS, and external ventricular drain placement. The limited positive outcomes and too many confounding factors limited the reliability of the study. In addition, the divergence of these studies may also be caused by different races and severities.

A plenty of studies have found that the ABO blood type was associated with prognoses of various diseases, including sepsis, trauma, intracerebral hemorrhage, viral pneumonia and a serious of thrombotic and bleeding events [6–10, 15–17]. The influence of ABO blood types on thrombotic and bleeding events is considered mediating by the level of von Willebrand factor (vWF) and factor VIII [18, 19]. The blood type O has lower levels of vWF and factor VIII than others three types [20, 21]. Secreted by vascular endothelial cells and platelets, the vWF plays an important role in the blood coagulation through binding to the glycoprotein GPIb receptor on the surface of platelets and promoting platelets adhesion to the vascular endothelium. In addition, as the carrier of factor VIII, the vWF protects it from proteolytic degradation and assists it to localize at the site of damaged endothelium. Therefore, the blood type O is usually correlated with higher risks of hemorrhage and bleeding events while blood types A, B and AB generally show higher risks of thromboembolic events [16, 17, 22–24]. Although one study enrolling intracerebral hemorrhage patients found blood type B blood was related with more hematoma expansion than other blood types, another study showed blood types do not influence the incidence of thromboembolic complications after intracerebral hemorrhage [25, 26]. Some studies also showed negative results that blood types were not associated with the mortality of critically ill patients [27–29]. Similarly, our negative result indicates the different influence of ABO blood types on levels of vWF and factor VIII is not critical to the progression of aSAH and the different effect on coagulation may be weakened and offset by other procoagulant factors. Certainly, the finding of our study should be verified by other medical centers with a larger sample size.

Though the statistical analysis has been improved and the sample size is larger than previous studies, our study still had some limitations. Firstly, this study enrolled aSAH patients from the West China hospital, which was a regional medical center in the southwestern region of China. The majority of included aSAH patients were Han Chinese. Therefore, the generalization of our conclusion is worthwhile to be verified in others regions and other nationality. Second, we did not collect the level of coagulation factors and vWF so that we could not analyze whether there were differences of them among ABO blood groups of aSAH patients due to the non-routine examination of coagulation factors in our hospital. Thirdly, we only analyzed the phenotypes of ABO antigen but not measured the genotypes of ABO types. The difference of outcomes among heterozygotes and homozygotes of ABO genotypes after aSAH has not been testified. Finally, only DCI and in-hospital mortality were analyzed but not other outcomes, including functional status and cognitive status.

Conclusion

ABO blood type is not associated with the risk of delayed cerebral ischemia and mortality among aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage patients. The finding of our study deserves future verification.
