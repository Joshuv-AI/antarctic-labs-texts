Benjamin Przybocki, Guilherme V. Toledo, Yoni Zohar

Abstract
. We study properties that allow first-order theories to be disjointly combined, including stable infiniteness, shininess, strong politeness, and gentleness. Specifically, we describe a Galois connection between sets of decidable theories, which picks out the largest set of decidable theories that can be combined with a given set of decidable theories. Using this, we exactly characterize the sets of decidable theories that can be combined with those satisfying well-known theory combination properties. This strengthens previous results and answers in the negative several long-standing open questions about the possibility of improving existing theory combination methods to apply to larger sets of theories. Additionally, the Galois connection gives rise to a complete lattice of theory combination properties, which allows one to generate new theory combination methods by taking meets and joins of elements of this lattice. We provide examples of this process, introducing new combination theorems. We situate both new and old combination methods within this lattice.

1.
Introduction

Given decidable first-order theories
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
with disjoint signatures, when is their combination
\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{2}
decidable? This question has spawned an entire subfield of logic called
theory combination
(see
(
7
)
for a survey). Theory combination goes back to Nelson and Oppen’s early work on decision procedures
(
28
)
and has since become a central topic in Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT)
(
3
)
, a field with applications ranging from hardware and software verification to automated theorem proving. Since SMT solvers often need to reason about multiple theories at once, they make essential use of theory combination methods.

The applicability of SMT solvers in software verification stems from their support for multiple theories, and in particular, their combinations.
Important examples include
the theories of
bit-vectors
(
25
)
, floating points
(
9
)
, and
strings
(
12
)
, which are used to model primitive
types in programming languages;
as well as the theories of
uninterpreted functions
(
25
)
,
data types
(
24
;
4
)
, and
arrays
(
15
)
, which
are often used to model container types.
While reasoning about certain programs only requires
a single theory (e.g., purely numerical functions or generic functions on lists),
software verification often relies
on combinations of the aforementioned theories,
as well as other theories (e.g., for reasoning about lists of machine integers).

Despite the importance of theory combination for SMT solving and the serious attention it has received from researchers, our theoretical understanding of theory combination remains unsatisfactory. Several conditions on
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
that are
sufficient
for their combination to be decidable have been identified. On the other hand, some examples are known of decidable theories whose combination is undecidable, which have been used to show that certain conditions do not suffice for theory combination
(
8
;
16
)
. However, prior to the present work, and to the best of our knowledge, no necessary and sufficient condition for a theory to be combinable with some set of theories has been proven. In particular, it has remained open whether existing theory combination methods can be improved to apply to larger sets of theories. In this paper, we provide a general framework for studying theory combination properties, which allows us to prove necessary and sufficient conditions for a theory to be combinable with every theory satisfying certain properties. Our results apply to all of the well-known disjoint theory combination methods: Nelson–Oppen
(
28
)
, shiny
(
41
)
, polite
(
33
;
22
)
, and gentle
(
18
)
.
1
1

1

One combination method we do not discuss is Shostak’s method
(
37
)
, which can be viewed as a refinement of the Nelson–Oppen method that is
less generally applicable
(
5
;
19
)
.
Our techniques also facilitate the discovery of new combination methods, which we demonstrate by introducing several new methods.

To motivate and contextualize our results, we start with a non-exhaustive history of theory combination
and situate in it some of the implications of our results.

1.1.
History of Theory Combination

Theory combination as a field began in 1979 when Nelson and Oppen
(
28
)
proposed their combination method, which was proved correct by Oppen
(
29
)
subject to a technical condition he called
stable infiniteness
. Specifically, the Nelson–Oppen theorem states that two disjoint and decidable theories can be combined so long as both are stably infinite.

The Nelson–Oppen method was the state-of-the-art for two decades and continues to be widely used, but the requirement of stable infiniteness has proven to be a hindrance in some applications, as many practical theories are not stably infinite (e.g., the theory of fixed-width bit-vectors). To address this limitation, in 2003, Tinelli and Zarba
(
41
)
proved a new combination theorem using a property they called
shininess
. Shininess is a stronger property than stable infiniteness, but shiny theories can be combined with any other decidable theory, including those that are not stably infinite.

The ability to combine a theory with any other decidable theory is very convenient, so researchers naturally began looking for other sets of theories with this property. In 2005, Ranise, Ringeissen, and Zarba
(
33
)
introduced
polite theory combination
. They claimed that so-called
polite
theories can also be combined with any other decidable theory. But, in 2010, Jovanović and Barrett
(
22
)
discovered a mistake in the proof of their combination theorem. They were however able to recover the result by imposing a stronger property than politeness, what later came to be called
strong politeness

(
11
)
. Strongly polite theory combination is very practical and is used in the SMT solver cvc5
(
1
)
.

There were still several loose ends to tie up regarding politeness. First, it was unknown whether strong politeness was actually strictly stronger than politeness; that is, whether there are any theories that are polite but not strongly polite. A negative answer to this question would have been desirable,
because establishing politeness of a theory is easier than establishing strong politeness.
There were some promising initial results by Casal and Rasga
(
10
)
in 2013 and later by Sheng et al.
(
35
)
in 2020 proving conditions under which politeness implies strong politeness. But then, in 2021, Sheng et al.
(
36
)
found an example of a polite theory that is not strongly polite. Still, one could have hoped that the polite combination criterion might still be correct, albeit with a different proof. Alas, we recently dashed this hope by finding a pair of decidable theories, one of which is polite, whose combination is undecidable
(
16
)
. This refuted the polite combination criterion, but it left open other possibilities that could provide other possible applications of politeness. Even if polite theories cannot be combined with
every
decidable theory (as originally claimed), is there some set of theories with which they can be combined (besides the stably infinite theories, given that politeness implies stable infiniteness)? Or is there some property intermediate in strength between politeness and strong politeness that suffices for combinability with every decidable theory? We answer both questions negatively.

The other loose end was investigating the relations between strong politeness and other theory combination properties. In 2018, Casal and Rasga
(
11
)
showed, perhaps surprisingly, that shininess and strong politeness coincide for decidable theories. In 2023, Toledo, Zohar, and Barrett
(
42
)
proved that theories satisfying a property seemingly weaker than strong politeness can be combined with any other decidable theory; however, they conjectured that their property is in fact equivalent to strong politeness, which was later confirmed in 2024 by Przybocki et al.
(
31
)
. These equivalences are nontrivial, and it has been somewhat mysterious why every attempt to improve the shiny combination theorem has resulted in a property equivalent to shininess. Casal and Rasga
(
11
)
expressed a desire to find “a class of theories strictly containing the shiny/strongly polite theories for which there exists an indiscriminate Nelson–Oppen method, in the sense that they can be combined with an arbitrary theory with a decidable quantifier-free satisfiability problem.” We prove that there is no such class.

Another combination method intended to address the limitations of the Nelson–Oppen method is
gentle theory combination
, introduced by Fontaine
(
18
)
in 2009. Gentleness is weaker than shininess and incomparable with stable infiniteness. Fontaine proved that gentle theories can be combined with any theory satisfying a mild technical condition. Recently,
(
16
)
proved that gentle theories can be combined with any theory having
computable finite spectra
, which is weaker than the condition Fontaine gave. Most theories of practical interest have computable finite spectra, but it is a theoretically interesting question whether this condition can be weakened even further. We prove that it cannot.

All of the above combination theorems that came after Nelson–Oppen require one theory to satisfy a property strictly stronger than or incomparable with stable infiniteness. Over four decades after Nelson and Oppen’s seminal paper, we still have no combination method that is strictly more general than theirs, and not for a lack of interest or effort. In 1996, Tinelli and Harandi
(
39
)
conjectured “that there might exist weaker requirements on the component theories which are sufficient for the [Nelson–Oppen] procedure’s correctness”. We finally disprove this conjecture, assuming we interpret it as stating that the requirements for
both
component theories can be strictly weaker than stable infiniteness.

1.2.
Our Contributions

First, in
Section

3
, we introduce a Galois connection between sets of decidable theories,
in which every set of decidable theories corresponds to the largest set of decidable theories with which they can be combined. This Galois connection forms the core of our abstract framework for studying theory combination. It induces a complete lattice of sets of decidable theories, each of which is involved in some combination theorem. It also induces a closure operator on sets of decidable theories, which can be seen as a tool to improve a combination theorem to a version that applies to as many theories as possible.

Second, in
Section

4
, we show that well-known disjoint theory combination methods cannot be further improved. Specifically, we show that shiny theories are the largest set of decidable theories that can be combined with all decidable theories (
Theorem

4.4
), stably infinite theories are the largest set of decidable theories that can be combined with all stably infinite theories (
Theorem

4.7
), and gentle theories are the largest set of decidable theories that can be combined with all theories with computable finite spectra (
Theorem

4.10
) and vice versa (
Theorem

4.9
). This shows that the following combination theorems cannot be improved to apply to larger sets of theories: the shiny combination theorem
(
41
)
, the strongly polite combination theorem
(
22
)
, the Nelson–Oppen combination theorem
(
29
)
, and the gentle combination theorem
(
18
)
after incorporating an improvement from
(
16
)
.
Our proof of
Theorem

4.7
also shows that the original polite combination theorem
(
33
)
is unsalvageable: the largest set of theories with which polite theories can be combined is the set of stably infinite theories. This concludes a line of research about the possibility of repairing this claimed theorem
(
10
;
35
;
36
;
16
)
.
As a byproduct of our proofs, we also obtain results about “maximally difficult” theories from the standpoint of theory combination (e.g.,
Theorem

4.5
), which may be of independent interest.

One of the virtues of our abstract framework is that it facilitates the discovery of new combination theorems, which we demonstrate in
Section

5
,
where we also prove their sharpness.
Specifically,
Section

5.1
sharpens a combination theorem from
(
16
)
using our technique.
In
Section

5.2
, we show that by taking meets and joins of elements of the lattice induced by the Galois connection, we can use existing combination theorems to discover a new combination theorem (
Theorem

5.7
), which is sharp. This method turns out to be an improvement of a combination theorem from
(
8
)
. In
Section

5.3
, we show yet another way to use our abstract framework to discover new combination theorems: start with a natural set of decidable theories and take its closure with respect to the Galois connection. We use this to discover a countably infinite family of combination theorems, which are sharp by construction and substantially improve a family of combination theorems from
(
26
)
. In
Section

5.4
, we go further by exhibiting an uncountably infinite family of combination theorems, each of which is sharp. These combination theorems can be seen as natural variants of gentleness, and their cardinality demonstrates how rich the space of combination theorems is.

Finally, in
Section

6
, we situate all of the above combination theorems within the lattice induced by the Galois connection, allowing one to visualize the relationships between these theorems. We also make a few remarks about the properties of this lattice.

All of our results deal with one-sorted theories.
We leave their generalization to many-sorted theories for future work.

To keep the paper concise and easy to read, numerous technical proofs are deferred to the appendix.

1.3.
Related Work

To the best of our knowledge, only two papers have studied
negative
results in theory combination, that is, results demonstrating that theories cannot be combined in certain cases. Since negative results are necessary for characterizing when theories can be combined, these papers have a close affinity to our work.

In 2006, Bonacina et al.
(
8
)
gave an example of a pair of decidable theories, only one of which is stably infinite, whose combination is undecidable. This is notable because it shows that the Nelson–Oppen theorem cannot be weakened to only assume that one of the component theories is stably infinite.

Recently, in 2025, we extended the previous work to prove that other combination methods cannot be extended in certain ways
(
16
)
. That is, for each well-known combination method (namely, Nelson–Oppen, shiny, strongly polite, and gentle), we constructed a pair of decidable theories meeting all but one of the requirements for the method such that their combination is undecidable. This shows that none of the requirements for these theory combination methods can be omitted, but it does not tell us whether the requirements can be weakened. Indeed, we showed in the same paper that one of the requirements for gentle theory combination can be weakened.

The present paper can be seen as a continuation of this line of work given that we make essential use of (variants of) the theories constructed in those papers. The main innovation of the present paper is to realize that these theories can be used as what we call
test theories
to deduce properties of theories with which they can be combined. The idea is that, given an arbitrary decidable theory
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, if we assume it can be combined with a test theory
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, then that forces
\mathcal{T}_{1}
to satisfy some desired property. Probing theories with enough carefully constructed test theories allows us to prove upper bounds on the set of decidable theories that can be combined with a given set of theories. If we have a matching lower bound coming from a theory combination method, this gives a sharp characterization.

2.2.
Spectra, Löwenheim–Skolem, and Compactness

The
spectrum
of a formula
\varphi
in a theory
\mathcal{T}
, denoted
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{\omega}

such that there is a
\mathcal{T}
-interpretation

. The restriction to

Theorem 2.1 (
(
27

Let
\Delta
be a set of formulas over a countable signature, and let
\kappa\geq\aleph_{0}
. Then,
\Delta

\kappa

\aleph_{0}
.

Another standard fact we will use is the compactness theorem:

Theorem 2.2 (
(
27
, Theorem 2.1.4)
).

Let
\Delta
be a set of formulas over a countable signature. Then
\Delta
is satisfiable if and only if every finite subset of
\Delta

The compactness theorem applied to the set

has the following consequence, which we will use repeatedly:

Corollary 2.3.

If

2.4.
Fontaine’s Lemma

The possibility of disjointly combining two theories is captured by a result that we call
Fontaine’s lemma
, since a version of it was proven by Fontaine in
(
18
, Corollary 1)
.
7
7

7

A similar result was proven earlier by Tinelli and Harandi
(
39
, Proposition 3.1)
, and the essential idea goes back to Robinson
(
34
)
.

Lemma 2.4 (Fontaine’s lemma).

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be theories over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, respectively. Then, the disjoint combination
\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{2}
is decidable if and only if the following problem is decidable: given conjunctions of literals
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
over
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively, determine whether
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
.

In
(
18
, Corollary 1)
, Fontaine only stated and proved the “if” direction of this lemma; we state and prove the “only if” direction for completeness.

In one sense, Fontaine’s lemma characterizes when theory combination is possible. But the lemma is seldom applied directly to a pair of theories to prove that their combination is decidable. This is because devising an algorithm to check whether
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
is a difficult problem in general and requires a separate, ad hoc analysis for each pair of theories we wish to combine. Thus, the field of theory combination is chiefly concerned with which
sets
of theories can be combined (equivalently, which properties of theories suffice for combinability), where Fontaine’s lemma can be used as a tool to that end.

It is interesting to note that Fontaine’s lemma does not require
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
to be decidable. And indeed, there are pairs of undecidable theories whose combination is decidable (e.g., if their combination is inconsistent). Nevertheless, we restrict our attention to decidable theories in this paper, as is typical in theory combination.

2.5.
Lattice Theory

A partially ordered set, or poset,
(A,\leq)
is said to be a
lattice
when the supremum (denoted
a\vee b
,
also called the
join
of
a
and
b
) and infimum (denoted
a\wedge b
and called the
meet
) of any two of its elements
a
and
b
exists; a lattice is
complete
if the supremum and infimum of every subset
S\subseteq A
exists.
An
isomorphism
between posets
(A,\leq_{A})
and
(B,\leq_{B})
is a bijective function
f:A\rightarrow B
such that
a\leq_{A}b
if and only if
f(a)\leq_{B}f(b)
.
If
(A,\leq)
is a poset, we define its
dual

(A,\leq^{\prime})
, over the same set
A
, by making
a\leq^{\prime}b
if and only if
b\leq a
;
if
(A,\leq)
is also a lattice then the supremum in its dual is its infimum and vice versa.

Given posets
(A,\leq_{A})
and
(B,\leq_{B})
, a function
f:A\rightarrow B
is said to be
antitone
if
a\leq b
implies
f(b)\leq f(a)
for all
a,b\in A
.
If
f
is a function from
(A,\leq)
into itself, it is said to be an
involution
if
f\circ f
is the identity on
A
.
Given antitone functions
f:A\rightarrow B
and
g:B\rightarrow A
, we say that
(f,g)
is an
antitone Galois connection
if

b\leq_{B}f(a)\Longleftrightarrow a\leq_{A}g(b)

for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
. In the sequel, we will use basic properties of Galois connections, which can be found, for example, in
(
30
)
and
(
6
, Chapter V)
.

A
closure operator
on a poset
(A,\leq)
is a function
Cl:A\rightarrow A
such that for all
a\in A
:

(i)

a\leq Cl(a)
;

(ii)

a\leq b
implies
Cl(a)\leq Cl(b)
;
and
(iii)

Cl(Cl(a))=Cl(a)
.
Given a closure operator, an element
a
is said to be
closed
when
Cl(a)=a
.

A
chain
in a poset
(A,\leq)
is a subset
X\subseteq A
such that, for all
a,b\in X
, either
a\leq b
or
b\leq a
;
in an
antichain

Y\subseteq A
, for any two
a,b\in Y
, neither
a\leq b
nor
b\leq a
unless
a=b
.
The
height
of a poset is the supremum of the cardinalities of its chains;
the
width
is the supremum of the cardinalities of its antichains.

3.
The Galois Connection

In this section we introduce a key ingredient for establishing the results of this paper.
Namely, we define a Galois connection between
sets of theories.
This Galois connection not only allows us to concisely and clearly describe sharpness of combination theorems
in terms of closures in
Section

4
,
but will also give rise to
the discovery of
new combination theorems
in
Section

5
.

Antitone Galois connections formalize an intuitive concept. They are relationships between objects whose sizes vary inversely. The usual example from logic is that of formulas and models:
if you increase a set of formulas, the collection of models that satisfy them shrinks;
and if you increase a collection of models, the set of formulas satisfied by all of them shrinks.
We observe that theory combination also has an underlying Galois connection, as requiring more properties of the first component theory allows one more freedom when choosing the second.
For example, stably infinite theories are combinable with other stably infinite theories (by Nelson–Oppen);
but if we want one theory to be less than stably infinite, say only decidable, then we need more from the other, namely being shiny. The Galois connection we describe formalizes this trade-off.

Formally, let
\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}})
be the power set of
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}
, which forms a complete lattice ordered by the
\subseteq
relation. We define a function
G:\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}})\to\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}})
as follows. Given
X\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}
, let
G(X)
be the set of decidable theories that are combinable with every theory in
X
; that is,

G(X)=\{\mathcal{T}\in\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}:\forall\,\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\in X.\>\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}\}.

Proposition 3.1.

G
is antitone and for all
X,Y\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}
, we have

X\subseteq G(Y)\Longleftrightarrow Y\subseteq G(X).

Hence,
(G,G)
is an antitone Galois connection.

Proof.

First, suppose
X\subseteq Y
, and let
\mathcal{T}\in G(Y)
. Then, for each
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\in Y
,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is decidable. Since
X\subseteq Y
,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is decidable for each
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\in X
, and thus
\mathcal{T}\in G(X)
. This proves that
G(Y)\subseteq G(X)
.

Suppose now that
X\subseteq G(Y)
and
\mathcal{T}\in Y
.
For each
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\in X
, we have that
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is decidable, as
X\subseteq G(Y)
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}\in G(X)
, and so
Y\subseteq G(X)
. This proves that
X\subseteq G(Y)\Longrightarrow Y\subseteq G(X)
, and the converse follows by symmetry.
∎

From the fact that
(G,G)
is an antitone Galois connection, many nice properties immediately follow. The following is an instantiation of a general fact about Galois connections
(
30
, p. 496)
.

Proposition 3.2.

For all
X,Y\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}
, we have

•

X\subseteq G(G(X))
;

•

X\subseteq Y\Longrightarrow G(G(X))\subseteq G(G(Y))
; and

•

G(G(G(X)))=G(X)
.

In particular,
Cl\coloneqq G\circ G
is a closure operator.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of closed elements of
\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}})
(equivalently, the image of
G
). The Knaster–Tarski theorem
(
23
;
38
)
implies the following.

Proposition 3.3.

\mathcal{C}
is a complete lattice with meets and joins given by

\bigwedge_{i\in I}X_{i}=\bigcap_{i\in I}X_{i}\qquad\bigvee_{i\in I}X_{i}=Cl\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}\right).

Note that
G
is an antitone involution on
\mathcal{C}
, which makes
\mathcal{C}
isomorphic to its dual. In particular, for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
G(X\vee Y)=G(X)\wedge G(Y)
and
G(X\wedge Y)=G(X)\vee G(Y)
.

Concretely, if
X\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}
, then there is a combination theorem for the pair of sets
(X,G(X))
:
every theory in
X
is combinable with every theory from

G(X)
. If
X
is closed, then this combination theorem is
sharp
: we cannot enlarge
X
to a larger set of theories that are all combinable with those in
G(X)
.
This fact is used in
Section

4
.
Otherwise, it can be sharpened to a new combination theorem for
(Cl(X),G(X))
, which applies to more pairs of theories. Another way to discover new combination theorems goes through the meet and join operators.
We provide examples for both techniques in
Section

5
.

Remark 3.1.

We defined our Galois connection over sets of decidable theories. One could define a similar Galois connection over all theories, since there are pairs of undecidable theories whose combination is decidable. We choose to focus on decidable theories, both because this is more relevant in practice and because this assumption is theoretically convenient.

4.
The Sharpness of Known Combination Theorems

In this section, we show that well-known theory combination methods cannot be improved, by investigating the requirements that they impose on theories.
For each requirement that arises from a combination theorem, we precisely identify the set of theories that can be combined with theories that admit these requirements.
This provides both positive and negative boundaries for all well-known combination methods.

Our technique can be sketched as follows:
for each set
X
of theories, we identify a restricted set of theories
such that any other theory can be combined with all
theories from
X
if and only if it can be combined with this set. We call these theories
test theories
.
We then prove properties of this restricted set of theories
in order to get the desired sharpness results.

Specifically: in
Section

4.1
we introduce
test theories.
Then, in
Section

4.2
, we prove that the shiny combination theorem is sharp; that is, shiny theories are the only decidable theories that can be combined with all decidable theories. Next, in
Section

4.3
, we prove that the Nelson–Oppen combination theorem is sharp; that is, stably infinite theories are the only decidable theories that can be combined with all decidable stably infinite theories. Finally, in
Section

4.4
, we prove that the gentle combination theorem as improved by
(
16
)
is sharp; that is, gentle theories are the only decidable theories that can be combined with all decidable theories with computable finite spectra (and vice versa).

4.2.

Tinelli and Zarba

G(\mathfrak{T})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{shiny}}

G(\mathfrak{T})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{shiny}}
in virtue of the shiny combination theorem. In particular,
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{shiny}}
is closed with respect to our Galois connection.

If

Proof.

Suppose

First, we show that

\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)=\{\aleph_{0}\}
. Since
\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is decidable,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is decidable. But
\varphi\land P_{n}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
-satisfiable if and only if
F(n)=\aleph_{0}
, which is undecidable by our assumption on
F
. This contradicts the decidability of
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
.

Second, we show that
\mathcal{T}
has a computable minimal model function. Given a formula
\varphi
, we know that it has some finite
\mathcal{T}
-interpretation by the previous paragraph. Since
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable. Hence, we can compute the size of the minimal
\mathcal{T}
-interpretation of
\varphi
by searching for the smallest
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
\varphi\land P_{n}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
-satisfiable.

Finally, it remains to show that
\mathcal{T}
is smooth. If not, then for some formula
\varphi
, we have
m\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
and
n\notin\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}
with
m<n
. In fact, by compactness, we can take
m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Since
\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}
is decidable,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}
is decidable. But
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}
-satisfiable if and only if
k\notin U
, which is undecidable since
U
is undecidable. This contradicts our assumption that
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every decidable theory.
∎

The proof shows that a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
is shiny if and only if it can be combined with the test theories
\{\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}},\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}\}\cup\{\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}:m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\;m<n\}
; that is,
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{shiny}}=G(\{\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}},\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}\}\cup\{\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}:m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\;m<n\})
. We can improve this characterization to only use a
single
test theory.

Theorem 4.5.

There is a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that, for every decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
, if
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
then
\mathcal{T}
is shiny (and therefore combinable with every decidable theory).

The theory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is maximally difficult from the standpoint of theory combination, in the sense that combinability with
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
implies combinability with every other decidable theory. This is analogous to the phenomenon of complete problems in computability theory and complexity theory (i.e., completeness in the sense of
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness). Thus, we say that
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{}}
-complete
with the following meaning.

Definition 4.6.

A theory
\mathcal{T}
is said to be
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}}
-complete
when
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}}=Cl(\{\mathcal{T}\})
.

When a
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}}
-complete theory
\mathcal{T}
exists, we can think of
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}}
as being “generated” by the singleton
\{\mathcal{T}\}
. Perhaps surprisingly, most, but not all, of the combination properties we study turn out to have a corresponding
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}}
-complete theory.

Note that the results of this section also apply to strongly polite theories, because Casal and Rasga
(
11
)
showed that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{shiny}}=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SP}}
, where
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SP}}
is the set of decidable strongly polite theories. Incidentally, given the combination theorem for strongly polite theories
(
22
)
,
Theorem

4.4
gives a new proof that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SP}}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{shiny}}
.

4.3.
Combination of Stably Infinite Theories

Oppen
(
29
)
proved that any two decidable stably infinite theories are combinable; that is,
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}})
. We prove that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}
, which implies that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}
is closed.
If a theory is not stably infinite, then it cannot be combined with every decidable stably infinite theory.

Theorem 4.7.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every decidable stably infinite theory, then
\mathcal{T}
is stably infinite, i.e.
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every decidable stably infinite theory.
If
\mathcal{T}
is not stably infinite, then there is a
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable formula
\varphi
such that
\aleph_{0}\notin\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
.
By compactness, there is a maximal
n\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
.
Then, the formula
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{>n}^{P}
-satisfiable if and only if
k\notin U
, which is undecidable: for the converse, notice that if
k\notin U
, then the axioms of
\mathcal{T}_{>n}^{P}
are trivially satisfied by a model in which
P_{k}
is true and
P_{k^{\prime}}
is false for
k^{\prime}\neq k
.
But
\mathcal{T}_{>n}^{P}
is decidable and stably infinite, so
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{>n}^{P}
is decidable by our assumption on
\mathcal{T}
, a contradiction.
∎

The proof shows that a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
is stably infinite if and only if it can be combined with the test theories
\{\mathcal{T}_{>n}^{P}:n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\}
; that is
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}=G(\{\mathcal{T}_{>n}^{P}:n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\})
. We can again improve this characterization to only use a single test theory, exhibiting a
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}
-complete theory.

Theorem 4.8.

There is a decidable stably infinite theory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that, for every decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
, if
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
then
\mathcal{T}
is stably infinite.

Since
\mathcal{T}_{>n}^{P}
is polite, the proof of
Theorem

4.7
shows that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{polite}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}
, where
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{polite}}
is the set of decidable polite theories. Hence,
Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{polite}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SI}}
. Intuitively, this means that, for the purpose of theory combination, politeness is no more valuable than stable infiniteness.

4.4.
Combination of Gentle Theories With Theories That Have Computable Finite Spectra

Fontaine
(
18
)
proved that gentle theories are combinable with theories satisfying a disjunction of three properties.
This was recently improved in
(
16
)
, where we proved that gentle theories are combinable with every decidable theory having computable finite spectra; again in terms of our Galois connection, that means
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}})
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}})
.
We prove that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
and that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}
, which together imply that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}
are closed.
If a theory is not gentle, then it cannot be combined with every decidable theory with computable finite spectra (and vice versa).

First, we prove that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
with the following theorem.

Theorem 4.9.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every gentle theory, then
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra, i.e.
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every gentle theory. Let
\varphi
be a quantifier-free formula and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Then, we have
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
if and only if
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
-satisfiable. Since
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is gentle,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable, so we have an algorithm for checking whether
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
, as desired.
∎

The proof shows that a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra if and only if it can be combined with the test theory
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}
-complete.

Next, we prove that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}
.

Theorem 4.10.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every decidable theory with computable finite spectra, then
\mathcal{T}
is gentle, i.e.
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every decidable theory with computable finite spectra. Let
\varphi
be a quantifier-free formula.

First, we show that
\mathcal{T}
has computable spectra. Since
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable and has computable finite spectra,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable. Hence, given
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we can compute whether
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
by checking if
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
-satisfiable. Thus,
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra. Further, since
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is decidable and has computable finite spectra,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is decidable. Thus, we can compute whether
\aleph_{0}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
by checking if
\varphi
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
-satisfiable.

Suppose that
\aleph_{0}\notin\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
. Then, by compactness,
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{*}
. It remains to show how to compute an explicit representation of this finite set. Since
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra, it suffices to compute an upper bound on
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
. We can do this by searching for the smallest
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
\varphi\land\neq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is
\mathcal{T}
-unsatisfiable (where the variables
x_{i}
are fresh).

The other case is when
\aleph_{0}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
. We first need to show that
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
is cofinite. Suppose not. Then,
S\coloneqq\mathbb{N}^{*}\setminus\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
is infinite. Since
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra,
S
is a decidable set. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{S}
is decidable and has computable finite spectra, so
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{S}
is decidable. But
\varphi\land P_{n}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{S}
-satisfiable if and only if
F(n)=\aleph_{0}
, which is undecidable, a contradiction.

We have shown that
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
is cofinite. It remains to actually compute the spectrum, by which we mean compute an explicit representation of its complement. Since
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra, it suffices to show that, given
\varphi
, we can compute the cardinality of
S\coloneqq\mathbb{N}^{*}\setminus\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
. Since
Th_{\mathcal{T}}
is decidable and has computable finite spectra,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup Th_{\mathcal{T}}
is decidable. Now,
\varphi\land P_{\varphi,n}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup Th_{\mathcal{T}}
-satisfiable if and only if
n>|S|
. Thus, by testing formulas of this form for satisfiability, we can compute the cardinality of
S
.
∎

In the above proof, some of the test theories with which we combine
\mathcal{T}
(namely,
\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{S}
and
Th_{\mathcal{T}}
) depend on our choice of
\mathcal{T}
. This is no accident. Indeed, there is no
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
-complete theory:

Theorem 4.11.

There is no decidable theory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with computable finite spectra such that for every decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
, if
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, then
\mathcal{T}
is gentle.

5.
New Combination Theorems

While
Section

4
studied the sharpness of existing combination theorems from the literature, in this section
we propose new combination theorems, allowing for new
ways to perform theory combination.
The new theorems are found by utilizing our Galois connection
G
, its closure operator
Cl
, and its induced lattice of theory combination properties
\mathcal{C}
, as defined in
Section

3
.
In
Section

5.1
, we provide a combination theorem that is based on smooth theories with computable spectra and show
that such theories can be combined with all infinitely
decidable theories.
Then, in
Section

5.2
, we prove a new symmetric combination theorem, based
on computable spectra, namely that every two decidable theories
with computable spectra can be combined.
For each of these combination methods, we also prove sharpness, in the spirit of
Section

4
.
In
Section

5.3
we take this technique a step further, and provide, at once, countably many distinct
and sharp combination theorems, based on a
generalization of shiny theories.
Finally, in
Section

5.4
, we present a larger set of sharp combination
theorems, which is uncountable.
These combination theorems are based on a variant of gentleness.
All these new results are, in one way or another, motivated by the Galois connection
G
:

Sections

5.1
and
5.3
rely on the closure operator
Cl
being applied to some set of theories which, by itself, is not closed;

Section

5.2
on the algebraic structure of the lattice
\mathcal{C}
induced by
G
, specifically taking meets and joints of known sets of theories;
and
Section

5.4
is motivated by a desire to demonstrate the structural richness of
\mathcal{C}
by exhibiting uncountably many combination theorems.

5.1.
Combination of SM+CS Theories With Infinitely Decidable Theories

In
(
16
)
, we proved that decidable smooth theories with a computable minimal model function (call this set of theories
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+MM}}
) are combinable with every decidable theory that is infinitely decidable.
It turns out that this combination theorem is not sharp, something our techniques helped us discover. Indeed,
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+MM}}
is not closed with respect to the Galois connection (although
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
turns out to be), which means that there is an improved combination theorem between
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
and
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}})=Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+MM}})
. We show that
Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+MM}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}
. To that end, we first prove a new combination theorem improving the one from
(
16
)
and then apply our test theories to show that the combination theorem is sharp.

Theorem 5.1.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories. Suppose that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is smooth and has computable spectra and that
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is infinitely decidable. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{2}
is decidable, meaning
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}})
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}})
.

In light of Fontaine’s lemma, the following implies
Theorem

5.1
:

Lemma 5.2.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories over signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively. Suppose that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is smooth and has computable spectra and that
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is infinitely decidable. Then, it is decidable whether
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
, where
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are conjunctions of literals over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively.

Proof.

Let
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
be conjunctions of literals over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively. We describe our decision procedure as follows. If
\varphi_{1}
is
\mathcal{T}_{1}
-unsatisfiable, then
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
, so suppose
\varphi_{1}
is
\mathcal{T}_{1}
-satisfiable.

If
\aleph_{0}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
(which we can check since
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is infinitely decidable), then
\aleph_{0}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
, since
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is smooth. Otherwise, by compactness, there is a maximal
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
. We can compute this
k
by checking the
\mathcal{T}_{2}
-satisfiability of
\varphi_{2}\land\neq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
(where the variables
x_{i}
are fresh) for increasing values of
n
. Then, since
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is smooth, we have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
if and only if
k\notin\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})
, which we can check since
\mathcal{T}_{1}
has computable spectra.
∎

Since every decidable smooth theory with a computable minimal model function has computable spectra (each nonempty spectrum is of the form
\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:m\geq k\}
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}
can be computed),
Theorem

5.1
is indeed a strengthening of the result from
(
16
)
mentioned at the start of this subsection. To show that it is a
strict
improvement, the following example shows that there is a smooth theory with computable spectra whose minimal model function is not computable.

Example 5.3.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\geq}
be the theory axiomatized by
\{P_{n}\rightarrow\psi_{\geq F(n)}:n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\;F(n)\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\}\cup\{P_{n}\rightarrow\psi_{\geq m}:m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\;F(n)=\aleph_{0}\}
, where
F
is the function defined in
Section

4.1
.
Then,
\mathcal{T}_{\geq}
is smooth and has computable spectra, but its minimal model function is not computable.

Now that we’ve proved that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}})
, we prove the converse.

Theorem 5.4.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every smooth theory with computable spectra, then
\mathcal{T}
is infinitely decidable, i.e.
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every smooth theory with computable spectra. Since
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is a smooth theory with computable spectra (
Theorem

4.3
),
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is decidable. But
\varphi
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
-satisfiable if and only if
\aleph_{0}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
, so
\mathcal{T}
is infinitely decidable.
∎

The proof shows that a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
is infinitely decidable if and only if it can be combined with the test theory
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}
-complete.

Theorem 5.5.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every decidable theory that is infinitely decidable, then
\mathcal{T}
is smooth and has computable spectra, i.e.
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every decidable theory that is infinitely decidable.

First, we show that
\mathcal{T}
is smooth. If not, then for some formula
\varphi
, we have
m\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
and
n\notin\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}
with
m<n
. In fact, by compactness, we can take
m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Then,
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}
-satisfiable if and only if
k\notin U
, which is undecidable since
U
is undecidable. This contradicts our assumption that
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every decidable theory that is infinitely decidable, given that
\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}
is infinitely decidable (
Theorem

4.3
).

Next, we show that
\mathcal{T}
has computable spectra. Since
\mathcal{T}
is decidable and smooth, it is infinitely decidable, so it remains to show that
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra. Let
\varphi
be a quantifier-free formula and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Then, we have
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
if and only if
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
-satisfiable. Since
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable and infinitely decidable (
Theorem

4.3
),
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable, so we have an algorithm for checking whether
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
, as desired.
∎

The proof shows that a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
is smooth and has computable spectra if and only if it can be combined with the test theories
\{\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}\}\cup\{\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}:m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\;m<n\}
; that is,
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}=G(\{\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}\}\cup\{\mathcal{T}^{n}_{m}:m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\;m<n\})
. We can improve this characterization to only use a single test theory, exhibiting a
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
-complete theory.

Theorem 5.6.

There is a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
that is infinitely decidable and such that for every decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
, if
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, then
\mathcal{T}
is smooth and has computable spectra.

We have shown that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
are closed. Since
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is smooth and has a computable minimal model function by
Theorem

4.3
, the proof of
Theorem

5.4
shows that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+MM}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
. Hence,
Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+MM}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}
(but also
Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
), giving a precise sense in which
Theorem

5.1
is the sharp version of the weaker combination theorem from
(
16
)
.

5.2.
Combination of Theories That Have Computable Spectra

We prove a new combination theorem stating that any two decidable theories with computable spectra are combinable; that is
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})
. Then, we prove that, in fact,
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
. Thus, in addition to the Nelson–Oppen theorem, we have another symmetric combination theorem (i.e., a combination theorem imposing the same requirement on both component theories).

We start by giving some motivation to show how one could have discovered this combination theorem using our abstract framework. The idea is that by taking meets and joins of closed sets of theories, we can discover new combination theorems. By definition, we have
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}\wedge\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}}
, so we know that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
is closed. Thus, purely by abstract considerations, we know that there is a new combination theorem involving
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
. We have

G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})=G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}\wedge\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}})=G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}})\vee G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{ID}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}\vee\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}.

Determining the join of two sets of theories is not totally straightforward, but a modicum of guesswork can lead us to the right answer. We are looking for a closed set of theories containing both
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}
. Since
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
satisfies those criteria, it is only natural to conjecture that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}\vee\mathfrak{T}_{\text{SM+CS}}=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
. And indeed, this conjecture is correct, as we now proceed to show.

Theorem 5.7.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories with computable spectra. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{2}
is decidable, meaning
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})
.

In light of Fontaine’s lemma, the following implies
Theorem

5.7
:

Lemma 5.8.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories over signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively. Suppose that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
have computable spectra. Then, it is decidable whether
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
, where
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are conjunctions of literals over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively.

Proof.

Let
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
be conjunctions of literals over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively. We describe our decision procedure as follows. First, check if
\aleph_{0}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
(which we can do since both theories are infinitely decidable). If so, then
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})\neq\emptyset
. Otherwise, without loss of generality,
\aleph_{0}\notin\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})
. If
\varphi_{1}
is
\mathcal{T}_{1}
-unsatisfiable, then
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
; otherwise, by compactness, there is a maximal
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})
. We can compute this
k
by checking the
\mathcal{T}_{1}
-satisfiability of
\varphi_{1}\land\neq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
(where the variables
x_{i}
are fresh) for increasing values of
n
. Then, since
\mathcal{T}_{1}
has computable spectra, we can compute an explicit representation of the finite set
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})
. And since
\mathcal{T}_{2}
has computable spectra, we can check whether
m\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
for any of the finitely many elements
m\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})
.
∎

Now that we’ve proved that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})
, we prove that, in fact,
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
.

Theorem 5.9.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every decidable theory with computable spectra, then
\mathcal{T}
has computable spectra, i.e.
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
, and so
Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every decidable theory with computable spectra. Since
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable and has computable spectra by
Theorem

4.3
,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable. Hence, given
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we can compute whether
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
by checking if
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
-satisfiable. Thus,
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra. Further, since
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is decidable and has computable spectra by
Theorem

4.3
,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is decidable. Thus, we can compute whether
\aleph_{0}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
by checking if
\varphi
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
-satisfiable.
∎

The proof shows that a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
has computable spectra if and only if it can be combined with the test theories
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
. We can improve this characterization to only use a single test theory, exhibiting a
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CS}}
-complete theory.

Theorem 5.10.

There is a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with computable spectra such that for every decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
, if
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, then
\mathcal{T}
has computable spectra.

In
(
8
)
, the authors proved a similar combination theorem, which states that decidable theories
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
can be combined provided that, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
,

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{i}
is over a finite signature
\Sigma_{i}
;

(2)

\mathcal{T}_{i}
is infinitely decidable; and

(3)

it is decidable whether a finite
\Sigma_{i}
-interpretation is a model of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
.

These requirements imply that
\mathcal{T}_{i}
has computable spectra. Indeed, we have infinite decidability by assumption, and we have computable finite spectra because, given a quantifier-free
\varphi
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we can check if
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(\varphi)
by enumerating the finitely many
\Sigma_{i}
-interpretations of size
k
and checking whether some interpretation is a model of both
\mathcal{T}_{i}
and
\varphi
. Thus, the computable spectra combination theorem is strictly more general than the one from
(
8
)
.

5.3.
Combination of
n
-Shiny Theories With
n
-Decidable Theories

In
(
26
, Theorem 7)
, Manna and Zarba proved that, for every fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, any two decidable theories all of whose models have size
n
are combinable. This combination theorem is not sharp; for example, it is subsumed by the gentle combination theorem and the computable spectra combination theorem. Nevertheless, using our abstract framework, we can use this combination theorem as a means to discover a new, sharp combination theorem.

Let
\mathfrak{T}_{n}
be the set of decidable theories all of whose models have size
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. The idea is that we can discover a new combination theorem by determining
Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{n})
. By Fontaine’s lemma,
G(\mathfrak{T}_{n})
is the set of decidable theories
\mathcal{T}
such that there is an algorithm that takes a quantifier-free formula
\varphi
and returns whether
n\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
; call such theories
n
-decidable
and denote the set of decidable theories that are
n
-decidable by
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}
(this property is a natural variant of infinite decidability). Thus, we know that there is a new combination theorem involving
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}
. It remains to determine
Cl(\mathfrak{T}_{n})=G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}})
(notice that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}
is closed, since
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}=G(\mathfrak{T}_{n})
).
At this point, we consult our table of test theories, looking for ones that are
n
-decidable, and we use these test theories to deduce properties of
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}})
; it may be helpful to take inspiration from the proof of
Theorem

4.4
. It turns out that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}})
is the set of decidable theories
\mathcal{T}
such that there is an algorithm that takes a
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable quantifier-free formula
\varphi
and returns a pair
(t,k)
, where
t\in\{0,1,2\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, such that:

•

if
t=0
, then
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)=\{n\}
;

•

if
t=1
, then
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)=\{n\}\cup\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:m\geq k\}
; and

•

if
t=2
, then
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)=\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:m\geq k\}
.

Call such theories
n
-shiny
and denote the set of decidable
n
-shiny theories by
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-shiny}}
.

Theorem 5.11.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories. Suppose that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is
n
-decidable and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is
n
-shiny. Then
\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{2}
is decidable, meaning
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-shiny}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}})
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-shiny}})
.

In light of Fontaine’s lemma, the following implies
Theorem

5.11
:

Lemma 5.12.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories over signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively. Suppose that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is
n
-decidable and that
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is
n
-shiny for some
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Then, it is decidable whether
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
, where
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are conjunctions of literals over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively.

Proof.

Let
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
be conjunctions of literals over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively. We describe our decision procedure as follows. If
\varphi_{2}
is
\mathcal{T}_{2}
-unsatisfiable, then
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
, so assume
\varphi_{2}
is
\mathcal{T}_{2}
-satisfiable.

We have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\{n\}
,
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\{n\}\cup\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:m\geq k\}
, or
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:m\geq k\}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
(and we can compute

and which case holds). If the first or second case holds, first check if
n\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})
(which we can do since
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is
n
-decidable); if so, we have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})\neq\emptyset
. Otherwise, if the second or third case holds, check if
\varphi_{1}\land\neq(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is
\mathcal{T}_{1}
-satisfiable (where the variables
x_{i}
are fresh); if so, we have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})\neq\emptyset
. If neither check passes, we have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
.
∎

Example 5.13.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and denote the theory of fixed-width bit-vectors of length
n
by
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{B}
.
This is an important fragment of the SMT-LIB theory of bit-vectors
(
2
)
.
The theory
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{B}

is
2^{n}
-shiny, as all models of all formulas that are satisfiable in it have size
2^{n}
.
By
Theorem

5.11
,
its combination with any
2^{n}
-decidable theory is decidable.
This improves the decidability results for combinations of bit-vectors with other theories, obtainable from the existing shiny and gentle combination theorems.

For our sharpness results, we need to state two more properties of our test theories.

Lemma 5.14.

(1)

If
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
k<k^{\prime}
, and
k\neq n
, then
\mathcal{T}^{k^{\prime}}_{k}
is
n
-decidable.

(2)

\mathcal{T}_{=n}
is
n
-shiny.

Theorem 5.15.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every decidable theory that is
n
-decidable, then
\mathcal{T}
is
n
-shiny, meaning
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-shiny}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every
n
-decidable theory.

First, we show that
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra. Since
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable and has computable finite spectra (
Theorem

4.3
), it is
n
-decidable,
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is decidable. Hence, given
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we can compute whether
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
by checking if
\varphi\land P_{k}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
-satisfiable. Thus,
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra.

Now, it suffices to show that for every
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable quantifier-free formula
\varphi
, we either have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)=\{n\}
,
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)=\{n\}\cup\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:m\geq k\}
, or
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)=\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:m\geq k\}
for some

; the fact that
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra implies that we can actually compute
k
and which case holds. To show that each spectrum has the desired form, it in turn suffices to show that for every quantifier-free formula
\varphi
: (i) if
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\setminus\{n\}\neq\emptyset
, then
(\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\setminus\{n\})\cap\mathbb{N}^{*}\neq\emptyset

k^{\prime}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)

First, we show that if
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\setminus\{n\}\neq\emptyset

(\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\setminus\{n\})\cap\mathbb{N}^{*}\neq\emptyset
. Suppose not. Then,
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi\land\neq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1}))=\{\aleph_{0}\}
(where the variables
x_{i}
are fresh). Hence,
\varphi\land\neq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})\land P_{m}

F

\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
has computable finite spectra (
Theorem

4.3

n
-decidable.

Second, we show that if
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\setminus\{n\}

k^{\prime}\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
for all
k^{\prime}\geq k
. If not, then we have
k\in\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\setminus\{n\}

. Then,
\varphi\land P_{m}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}^{k^{\prime}}_{k}

).
∎

The proof shows that a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}

. We can improve this characterization to only use a single test theory, exhibiting a
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}
-complete theory.

Theorem 5.16.

There is a decidable theory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
that is
n
-decidable and such that for every decidable theory
\mathcal{T}
, if

can be combined with

Finally, we prove that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-shiny}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}
(half of this was proven in

).

Theorem 5.17.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every decidable

-shiny theory, then
\mathcal{T}
is
n
-decidable.
This means
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-shiny}})\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$n$-decidable}}
, and thus

\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}_{=n}
is a decidable

5.4.
Combination of
\mathfrak{F}
-QG Theories With Co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG Theories

Section

5.3
exhibited
\aleph_{0}
many combination theorems; now we go further and exhibit
2^{\aleph_{0}}
many combination theorems. This isn’t just a theoretical curiosity: the corresponding sets of theories are natural generalizations of gentle theories, and for that reason, we call them
quasi-gentle
.
To motivate them, recall gentle theories can be combined with themselves, although
this was later improved to allow gentle theories to be combined with theories having computable finite spectra.
Taking into account the fact that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
, one may wonder whether there is a property
X
such that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
and
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{X}}
.
Looking for such a solution leads to the methods to be described below, some of them being the fixed points we were looking for.

We start with some terminology on filters, see
(
17
)
for a general reference.
A
filter

\mathfrak{F}
on
\mathbb{N}
is a subset of the power set
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N})
such that:

(i)

\emptyset\notin\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathbb{N}\in\mathfrak{F}
;

(ii)
if
A,B\in\mathfrak{F}
, then
A\cap B\in\mathfrak{F}
;
and
(iii)
if
A\in\mathfrak{F}
and
A\subseteq B
, then
B\in\mathfrak{F}
.
We say that
\mathfrak{F}
is
free
if the intersection of all of its elements is empty;
this is equivalent to
\mathfrak{F}
containing all cofinite sets.
All sets in a free filter are infinite.
An ultrafilter
\mathfrak{F}
is a maximal filter; equivalently,
\mathfrak{F}
is an ultrafilter if for every
A\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N})
, either
A\in\mathfrak{F}
or
\mathbb{N}\setminus A\in\mathfrak{F}
.
Free ultrafilters exist assuming the axiom of choice. More generally, we can define filters and ultrafilters on any lattice by substituting
\wedge
for
\cap
and
\leq
for
\subseteq
.

Definition 5.18.

Let
\mathfrak{F}
be a free filter.
A decidable theory is said to be
\mathfrak{F}
-quasi-gentle, or simply
\mathfrak{F}
-QG, when it has computable finite spectra, and the spectrum of every quantifier-free formula is either a finite set of finite cardinalities, or
A\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
for an
A\in\mathfrak{F}
. The set of decidable
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theories is denoted
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}
.

A decidable theory is said to be co-
\mathfrak{F}
-quasi-gentle, or simply co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG, when it has computable finite spectra, and the spectrum of every quantifier-free formula is either a finite set of finite cardinalities, or
A\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
for an
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{N}\setminus A\notin\mathfrak{F}
. The set of decidable co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theories is denoted
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{co-$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}
.

Notice that the spectrum of quantifier-free formulas in a
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theory can be
A\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
for any cofinite set
A
, since
\mathfrak{F}
contains every cofinite set.
Notice too that if
\mathfrak{F}
is an ultrafilter then a theory is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG if and only if it is co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG:
indeed,
A\in\mathfrak{F}
if and only if
\mathbb{N}\setminus A\notin\mathfrak{F}
.
This will give us a collection of fixed points for
G
between
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{CFS}}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{gentle}}
.

As a concrete example, let
\mathbb{F}
be the free filter of cofinite sets, usually called the
Fréchet filter

(
21
)
. Then,
\mathbb{F}
-QG theories are those with computable finite spectra such that the spectrum of every quantifier-free formula is either a finite set of finite cardinalities or a cofinite set of cardinalities; co-
\mathbb{F}
-QG theories are those with computable finite spectra such that the spectrum of every quantifier-free formula is either a finite set of finite cardinalities or an infinite set of cardinalities. Observe that being
\mathbb{F}
-QG is a natural weakening of being gentle: we require the spectra to be finite or cofinite, but we do not require a computable method to determine which case holds or to output an explicit representation of the spectrum (or its complement). This justifies the name
quasi-gentleness
. Also notice that being co-
\mathbb{F}
-QG is a slight strengthening of having computable finite spectra.

Theorem 5.19.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories. Suppose that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{2}
is decidable, i.e.
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{co-$\mathfrak{F}$-QG{}}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{F}$-QG{}}})
and
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}\subseteq G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{co-$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}})
.

In light of Fontaine’s lemma, the following implies
Theorem

5.19
:

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be decidable theories over signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and

\mathcal{T}_{1}

\mathfrak{F}

\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
, where
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are conjunctions of literals over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}

Take conjunctions of literals
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}

\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
.
For each

, starting with

\varphi_{1}

:
if so, we return
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
is not empty.
If not, we test whether
\varphi_{1}\wedge\neq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\varphi_{2}\wedge\neq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
are, respectively,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
- and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
-satisfiable:
if not, we return
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
is empty;
if so, we proceed to

.
This procedure always terminates, for if either
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})
or
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})
is finite there will come a point where
\varphi_{1}\wedge\neq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
or
\varphi_{2}\wedge\neq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
are no longer satisfiable in their respective theories;
and if both
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})=A\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
for an

, and
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})=B\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}

\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\varphi_{1})\cap\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(\varphi_{2})\cap\mathbb{N}
is not empty and the algorithm will eventually find the first shared element.
Indeed, had we

, this would imply

, and given

we would find
\mathbb{N}\setminus B\in\mathfrak{F}
, a contradiction.
∎

For our sharpness results, we need to state more properties of our test theories.

Lemma 5.21.

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG and co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG for all free filters
\mathfrak{F}
.

(2)

If

, then

\mathfrak{F}
-QG.

\mathfrak{F}
-QG and co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG for all free filters
\mathfrak{F}
.

We now prove one direction of

, the other having been proven in

.

Theorem 5.22.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theory, then
\mathcal{T}
is co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG, meaning

.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
can be combined with every
\mathfrak{F}
-QG decidable theory, and take a quantifier-free formula
\varphi
.

We first show that
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra.
Indeed,
\varphi\wedge P_{n}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
-satisfiable if and only if

, and
\mathcal{T}_{=}^{P}
is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG by

.

We then show by contradiction that, if
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
is finite, then it cannot contain
\aleph_{0}
.
Were that not the case, let
n
be the maximum of
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\cap\mathbb{N}
, and consider the
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theory

(

):

\varphi\wedge\neq{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})}\wedge P_{m}
, for fresh variables

, is then

-satisfiable if and only if

, which is undecidable by our assumption on

. This contradicts the fact that

is decidable.

Now we prove, again by contradiction, that if
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
is infinite, then
S\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\setminus\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\notin\mathfrak{F}
. Suppose

.
Then,

must be decidable, since it is the complement of the decidable set

(given that
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra).
Then
\varphi\wedge P_{m}
is
\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{S}
-satisfiable if, and only if,
F(m)=\aleph_{0}
;
notice that, of course,
\mathcal{T}_{\leq}^{S}
is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG (
Lemma

5.21
).
∎

Next, we prove that
G(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{co-$\mathfrak{F}$-QG{}}})=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{F}$-QG{}}}
, half of which was proven in

5.19
.

Theorem 5.23.

If
\mathcal{T}
is a decidable theory that can be combined with every co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theory, then
\mathcal{T}
is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG, i.e.

.

The proof is more or less the same as the one for the previous theorem;
let

be a quantifier-free formula.

\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\cap\mathbb{N}
is decidable, and so
\mathcal{T}
has computable finite spectra, as

is

-satisfiable if and only if

is both decidable and co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG (

).

By contradiction, if
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)
is finite and contains

\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\cap\mathbb{N}

5.4.1.
The Structure of
\mathfrak{F}
-QG and Co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG Theories

We now prove a few propositions that tell us how the sets of
\mathfrak{F}
-QG and co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theories relate to the lattice
\mathcal{C}
induced by our Galois connection.

Let
REC
be the lattice, indeed a Boolean algebra, of decidable subsets of
\mathbb{N}
. Every filter
\mathfrak{F}
on
\mathbb{N}
induces a filter on
REC
by restricting to
\mathfrak{F}\cap REC
. We first show that
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{(co-)$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}
is determined by
\mathfrak{F}\cap REC
.

Proposition 5.24.

If
\mathfrak{F}\cap REC=\mathfrak{G}\cap REC
, then
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{(co-)$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}=\mathfrak{T}_{\text{(co-)$\mathfrak{G}$-QG}}
.

Proof.

We prove the proposition for quasi-gentleness, with the proof for co-quasi-gentleness being similar. For any
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theory
\mathcal{T}
and quantifier-free formula
\varphi
, we have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\cap\mathbb{N}\in\mathfrak{F}
if and only if
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\cap\mathbb{N}\in\mathfrak{G}
, since
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\varphi)\cap\mathbb{N}
is decidable. Thus,
\mathcal{T}
is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG if and only if it is
\mathfrak{G}
-QG.
∎

Thus, we are justified in restricting our attention to free filters on
REC
. We abuse notation by referring to
\mathfrak{F}
-QG and co-
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theories even when
\mathfrak{F}
is a free filter on
REC
.

Proposition 5.25.

Let
\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathfrak{G}
be free filters on
REC
. We have
\mathfrak{F}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}
if and only if
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{G}$-QG}}
if and only if
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{co-$\mathfrak{G}$-QG}}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{co-$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}
.

Proof.

We prove the proposition for quasi-gentleness, with the proof for co-quasi-gentleness being similar.

Suppose
\mathfrak{F}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}
and let
\mathcal{T}
be a
\mathfrak{F}
-QG theory:
then the spectra of its quantifier-free formulas are either finite sets of finite cardinalities, or
A\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
for an
A\in\mathfrak{F}
.
Since
\mathfrak{F}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}
, the spectra of the quantifier-free formulas of
\mathcal{T}
are either finite sets of finite cardinalities, or
A\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
for an
A\in\mathfrak{G}
, meaning it is a
\mathfrak{G}
-QG theory.

Conversely, suppose
\mathfrak{F}\not\subseteq\mathfrak{G}
, and let
S\in\mathfrak{F}\setminus\mathfrak{G}
. Then, let
\mathcal{T}
be the
\Sigma_{1}
-theory axiomatized by
\{\lnot\psi_{=n}:n\notin S\}
. We have
\textit{Spec}_{\mathcal{T}}(\top)=S\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
, and
\mathcal{T}
is
\mathfrak{F}
-QG but not
\mathfrak{G}
-QG. Thus,
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}\not\subseteq\mathfrak{T}_{\text{$\mathfrak{G}$-QG}}
.
∎

Given a family of sets
\{A_{i}:i\in I\}
, we say that it has the
strong finite intersection property
if

\bigcap_{i\in I^{\prime}}A_{i}

is infinite for any finite
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
. If
\{A_{i}:i\in I\}
has the strong finite intersection property, we say that the filter
generated
by
\{A_{i}:i\in I\}
is the smallest filter containing every
A_{i}
; this filter is free. If each
A_{i}
is decidable, we can similarly generate a free filter on
REC
.

Proposition 5.26.

There is set
\mathcal{F}
of
2^{\aleph_{0}}
free filters on
REC
such that the sets of theories
\{\mathfrak{T}_{\text{(co-)$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}:\mathfrak{F}\in\mathcal{F}\}
form a chain in the lattice
\mathcal{C}
induced by the Galois connection. Additionally, there is set
\mathcal{F}
of
2^{\aleph_{0}}
free filters on
REC
such that the sets of theories
\{\mathfrak{T}_{\text{(co-)$\mathfrak{F}$-QG}}:\mathfrak{F}\in\mathcal{F}\}
form an antichain in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

5.25
, it suffices to construct a set of
2^{\aleph_{0}}
free filters on
REC
that form a (anti)chain when ordered by inclusion.

For each
i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, let
A_{i}
be the natural numbers whose
i
th bit is a 0 (starting at the least significant bit); for example,
A_{1}
is the even numbers, and
A_{2}=\{0,1,4,5,\dots\}
. Then,
\{A_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\}
has the strong finite intersection property, and each
A_{i}
is decidable. Given a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
, let
\mathfrak{F}_{S}
be the free filter on
REC
generated by
\{A_{i}:i\in S\}
. Note that if
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
, then
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathfrak{F}_{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{S^{\prime}}
. Thus, given a (anti)chain of size
2^{\aleph_{0}}
in
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
, we have a (anti)chain of
2^{\aleph_{0}}
free filters on
REC
.
∎

We have shown that
\mathcal{C}
is not only infinite, but quite large as well: it has height and width at least
2^{\aleph_{0}}
.

Recall from

7.
Conclusion and Further Directions

Ever since Nelson and Oppen’s seminal paper
(
28
)
, the central theoretical question in theory combination has been when sets of theories can be disjointly combined. We provided a systematic answer to this question by developing a general technique for identifying the largest set of theories that can be disjointly combined with a given set. We demonstrated this technique by characterizing the theories that can be disjointly combined with sets of theories to which existing theory combination methods apply, in each case either showing that the method is sharp or improving the method to make it sharp. Moreover, we showed how to use our framework to easily generate new combination methods.

The potential impact of our work is twofold. First, existing as well as future combination methods
can be better understood by viewing them through the lens of our Galois connection. Specifically, given a new combination method, we can determine whether it is sharp and compare it to previous combination methods based on its position in the lattice.
Second, this work paves the way to discover new combination methods that could be implemented in SMT solvers for combined theories. Thus,
when a new “container theory” (such as that of uninterpreted functions, arrays, etc.)
is introduced, its various properties can be evaluated in order to produce
a combination method that allows it to be combined with the largest possible
set of decidable theories.

Our work suggests several avenues for further research. One is to generalize our results to many-sorted logic, which is particularly important for applications to SMT. We are also interested in whether analogous results can be proved for non-disjoint theory combination. There is some work on non-disjoint theory combination (e.g.,
(
40
;
20
;
13
;
14
)
), although it has been less thoroughly explored than the disjoint case.

Another direction is to develop more efficient combination methods inspired by the methods we have considered, several of which are computationally expensive. Once a problem is known to be decidable, it is often possible to develop tractable algorithms. Indeed, one motivation for (strongly) polite theory combination was to be a more efficient alternative to shiny theory combination. On the theoretical side, one could study which pairs of theories are
efficiently
combinable, meaning roughly that they can be combined without an increase in computational complexity. Oppen
(
29
)
proved some results along these lines, demonstrating the role that convexity plays in the efficiency of the Nelson–Oppen method.

The “Only if” Direction of Fontaine’s Lemma

We recall the statement of Fontaine’s lemma:
See
2.4

We prove the “only if” direction of this lemma, since Fontaine

Lemma B.1 (

).

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and

Now, we can prove the “only if” direction of Fontaine’s lemma.

Lemma B.2.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be theories over the signatures
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively. If the disjoint combination

\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
over

and

Let
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
be conjunctions of literals. We may assume that the variables in
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are disjoint by renaming variables if necessary. For

, let

\mathcal{T}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textbf{eq}}

(3)

(4)
