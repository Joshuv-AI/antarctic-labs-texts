Melatonin Protects BEAS‐2B Cells Against Hypoxia/Reoxygenation‐Induced Injury By Reducing MLKL Levels, Suppressing Oxidative Stress and Preserving Mitochondrial Function
Authors: Zehra Yilmaz, Askin Tas, Ozlem Oz, Ismail Koyuncu, Ebru Temiz, Ozgur Yuksekdag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNecroptosis has been demonstrated to play a role in the process of lung ischemia‐reperfusion injury (LIRI) in a variety of clinical conditions, including cardiopulmonary bypass and pulmonary embolism. Melatonin has been reported to exert a protective role by reducing oxidative stress and acute inflammatory reactions in LIRI. However, the effect of melatonin on necroptosis in LIRI remains unclear. The aim of this study was to investigate whether the protective effects of melatonin against H/R‐induced injury are associated with modulation of necroptosis and related changes in mitochondrial function and oxidative stress. The cells were exposed to H/R (16/4 h) or normoxia, in the absence or presence of 2.5 µM melatonin. Cell viability was determined by the MTT method, while morphological changes in BEAS‐2B cells resulting from H/R exposure were evaluated by fluorescence microscopy using acridine orange/ethidium bromide (AO/EtBr) staining. Mechanistic analyses, including flow cytometry‐based cell cycle assessment, apoptosis detection (Annexin V‐FITC), mitochondrial membrane potential evaluation (JC‐1), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements, were performed on BEAS‐2B cells. The level of the mixed lineage kinase domain‐like pseudokinase (MLKL), a key component of the necroptosis complex, was also quantified using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay. In addition, the ATP, pH, and lactate levels were determined. The findings indicated that melatonin treatment reduced the H/R‐induced increase in the percentage of necrotic cells and increased the percentage of viable cells (p < 0.05). In addition, melatonin treatment reduced the increase in MLKL, ROS, and lactate levels, preserved mitochondrial membrane integrity, and increased intracellular ATP levels in BEAS‐2B cells exposed to H/R (p < 0.05). In conclusion, this study suggests that melatonin protects BEAS‐2B cells against H/R‐induced injury, and that this protective effect may be associated with reduced oxidative stress, preservation of mitochondrial function, and suppression of necroptosis‐related signaling.Keywords: hypoxia, lung, melatonin, mitochondria, necroptosis, reperfusion injury

Introduction

Lung ischemia‐reperfusion injury (LIRI) occurs when oxygen supply to the lungs is impaired by reduced blood flow or ventilation, followed by reperfusion/ventilation [1]. Clinical conditions such as cardiopulmonary bypass, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, single‐lung ventilation, and pulmonary embolism can result in LIRI, which can lead to respiratory failure [1, 2, 3]. Severe LIRI frequently occurs following lung transplantation and is a significant cause of early mortality and primary graft dysfunction [4].

LIRI is a complex pathological situation in which multiple molecular mechanisms are involved. Ischemia can trigger oxidative enzyme activation and mitochondrial dysfunction in alveolar epithelial cells, pulmonary endothelial cells, macrophages, and neutrophils. This situation leads to increased ROS production and decreased clearance, resulting in higher ROS levels [5, 6]. During the ischemic and anoxic phases, the arrest of aerobic metabolism triggers the rapid degradation of cellular ATP and ADP stores into AMP. The cessation of electron transport in the absence of oxygen terminates oxidative phosphorylation, while the concomitant accumulation of acidic metabolites lowers both intracellular and extracellular pH, thereby inducing tissue acidosis [6, 7]. Ultimately, this rapid depletion of cellular ATP compromises mitochondrial membrane potential, leading to calcium leakage into the cytosol and, consequently, activating cell death pathways [7].

As demonstrated in experimental models, LIRI involves cell death through both apoptosis and necrosis [8, 9, 10]. Recent studies have also provided evidence that necroptosis may also be involved in the pathogenesis of LIRI [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Understanding the role of necroptosis—a distinct form of programmed cell death separate from apoptosis—and its pathways in LIRI is crucial for identifying new therapeutic targets, as there is currently no specific or fully effective treatment for ischemia‐reperfusion (I/R).

Necroptosis is a kinase‐dependent programmed death pathway that exhibits morphological similarities to necrosis. The principal effectors of the necrosome complex in the necroptosis pathway are protein kinases, receptor‐interacting protein kinase‐1 (RIPK1), RIPK3, and mixed lineage kinase domain‐like (MLKL) pseudokinase. It has been demonstrated that RIPK1 activity is responsible for RIPK3 phosphorylation, which, in turn, drives MLKL phosphorylation and oligomerization. Subsequently, MLKL translocates to the plasma and intracellular membranes, such as mitochondria, thereby permeabilizing the membranes and serving as one of the execution mechanisms of necroptosis [17, 18, 19, 20]. RIPK1 links RIPK3 to death receptor signaling and can regulate both apoptotic and necroptotic signaling, but is not always required for RIPK3 activation. Furthermore, it has been reported that RIPK3 may also regulate apoptosis, whereas MLKL is not involved in this process [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Therefore, MLKL is considered a necroptosis‐specific marker because it is the final step in activating the necrosome complex and does not affect apoptosis. Therefore, MLKL is considered a necroptosis‐specific marker because it is the final step in activating the necrosome complex and does not affect apoptosis. The MLKL inhibitor necrosulfonamide has shown protective effects against I/R injury of the lung, brain, and heart [15, 26, 27]. These studies support the hypothesis that necroptosis may play a significant role in I/R injury, and that inhibition of necrosome components may contribute to IR treatment.

Melatonin, a circadian‐regulated indoleamine, mitigates I/R injury through its well‐established antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and mitochondrial‐protective properties [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. It has been shown to attenuate LIRI by modulating cell death pathways, including apoptosis and mitophagy [33, 34, 35]. However, the relationship between melatonin and necroptosis in LIRI remains unclear. The aim of this study was to investigate whether necroptosis contributes to the protective effect of melatonin on LIRI and to elucidate the changes in mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS, ATP, and lactate production, and pH levels in human lung epithelial cells associated with the suppression of necroptosis.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Cell culture materials were provided by Gibco BRL (Scotland) and Sigma‐Aldrich Chemical Company (Germany). RPMI 1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), streptomycin, and penicillin were provided Gibco BRL, while melatonin, 3‐(4,5‐Dimethyl‐2‐thiazolyl)−2,5‐diphenyl‐2H‐tetrazolium Bromide‐1 (MTT), acridine orange (AO), ethidium bromide (EtBr), trypsin‐EDTA solution, phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and culture plates were supplied by Sigma‐Aldrich.

Cell Culture

BEAS‐2B cells, a human bronchial epithelial cell line (official name: BEAS‐2B; tissue/site of origin: lung/bronchus epithelium; ATCC CRL‐3588), were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and stored in liquid nitrogen until use. Cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 and 95% air. Cell morphology was routinely monitored during culture to assess cell health, growth characteristics, and confluency; however, morphological assessment was not considered a definitive authentication method. To minimize passage‐related variability, cells were used at passage numbers below 30 for all experiments. Cultures were screened for mycoplasma contamination by DAPI staining before each experiment, and all tests were negative.

Hypoxia/Reoxygenation Conditions and Melatonin Treatment

Cells were subjected to a hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) protocol to establish an in vitro injury model. Given the hypoxia and reoxygenation durations employed in previous studies, the present study tested increasing hypoxia durations (4, 6, 12, 16, 18, 24 h) prior to a fixed 4 h reoxygenation period [36, 37]. The duration of hypoxia was chosen as the shortest period that would allow for the best assessment of treatment efficacy and induce significant cell death.

Cells were incubated in a hypoxia chamber (Thermo Steri‐Cycle™ i160, USA) maintained at 37°C with a gas mixture of 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2 for 16 h. At the end of the hypoxic period, the culture medium was maintained without replacement, and cells were transferred to standard normoxic culture conditions for reoxygenation. Reoxygenation was performed for 4 h at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 and 95% air. Melatonin was administered at varying concentrations (1, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100 µM) during H/R or reoxygenation to determine its effective dose and efficacy when administered during reperfusion or throughout ischemia/reperfusion. The effective dose (2.5 µM) and treatment schedule (during H/R 16/4 h) identified in these experiments were used in subsequent analyses.

Melatonin was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution, which was subsequently diluted in culture medium to obtain a final concentration of 2.5 μM. The final DMSO concentration in the culture medium did not exceed 0.1% in any experimental group. To control for potential vehicle‐related effects, the same final DMSO concentration was added to the corresponding control and H/R groups. Melatonin treatment was administered throughout the H/R protocol, and all groups were maintained under identical vehicle conditions.

MTT Assay

The MTT assay was used to evaluate the effects of hypoxia duration and melatonin treatment on cell viability. Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates and exposed to hypoxic conditions for different durations (0 h [normoxia], 4 h, 6 h, 12 h, 16 h, 18 h, and 24 h) to determine the optimal hypoxic exposure time. Melatonin was applied at different concentrations (0, 1, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 µM) during hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R; 16/4 h) or during the reoxygenation phase. After hypoxic exposure and/or melatonin treatment, cells were incubated with MTT solution for viability assessment. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, with 630 nm used as the reference wavelength.

ATP Assay

The ATP assay (Promega ATP Assay Kit, Cat. No: G7572) was used to assess intracellular ATP levels as an indicator of cellular energetic status. Measurements were performed using a microplate reader (Spectramax M5, Biotech, VT, USA).

AA/EtBr Staining Assay

BEAS‐2B cells were seeded into 12‐well plates at 5×105 cells/mL and treated under normoxia, H/R (16/4 h), and H/R + M (2.5 µM) conditions. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated with AO/EtBr solution (100 µg/mL) for 2–5 min at room temperature. Morphological changes were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus CKX 51, DP73).

Annexin V‐PI Assay

Apoptosis was assessed using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA). Cells were seeded into six‐well plates and treated under normoxia, H/R (16/4 h), and H/R + M (2.5 µM) conditions. Following incubation, cells were detached by trypsin, centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 1X binding buffer. The cells were transferred into new tubes at a concentration of 100 µL per tube, and 400 µL 1X binding buffer was added. The samples were then analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACs Via, New Jersey, USA).

Measurement of Total MLKL Levels

Cells were seeded into six‐well plates and treated under normoxia, H/R (16/4 h), and H/R + M (2.5 µM) conditions. Following incubation, cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. The cells were homogenized in PBS and centrifuged, and the supernatants were transferred to clean tubes. Protein levels in the supernatant were quantified using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Technology, USA). MLKL protein levels were measured with an ELISA kit (Human MLKL, Cat. log No: MBS2884611) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and values were determined using a microplate reader (Thermo Multiskan GO, USA).

JC‐1 Staining

Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using JC‐1 dye (5,5′,6,6′‐tetrachloro‐1,1′,3,3′‐tetraethylimidacarbocyanine iodide‐BD™ Mito Screen, BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA). Cells were incubated in six‐well plates at a density of 1×106 cells and treated under normoxia, H/R (16/4 h), and H/R + M (2.5 µM) conditions. Following incubation, cells were stained with JC‐1 solution (JC‐1 dye concentration: 1–5 µM) in the dark at 37°C for 15 min, washed twice with 1X washing solution, and analyzed via flow cytometry (BD FACs Via, New Jersey, USA).

Detection of Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

ROS levels were measured using the Millipore‐Merck oxidative stress assay kit (Cat. log No: MHC100111). Cells were incubated in six‐well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells and treated under normoxia, H/R (16/4 h), and H/R + M (2.5 µM) conditions. Cells were detached with trypsin, washed twice with PBS, and all culture media were removed. Samples were stained with ROS dye, incubated at 37°C for 30 min, washed twice with 1X washing solution, and then analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACs Via, New Jersey, USA).

Detection of pH and Lactate Levels

pH and lactate (cLac) levels of the medium were measured using the ABL90 Flex Plus blood gas analyzer (Radiometer, Denmark) [38].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical presentations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. For variables that did not meet the assumption of normality, comparisons among three independent groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons with correction for multiple testing. For comparisons between two independent groups with non‐normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. A P‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Determination of the Hypoxia Duration in BEAS‐2B Cells

An MTT assay was conducted to determine the optimal duration of hypoxia for BEAS‐2B cells. Cells were exposed to increasing hypoxia durations (0 [normoxia], 4, 6, 12, 16, 18, and 24 h), followed by a constant reoxygenation period of 4 h. Cell viability was significantly reduced after 16 h (p = 0.04), 18 h (p= 0.02), and 24 h (p = 0.006) of hypoxia compared with normoxia (Figure 1). Furthermore, no significant difference in cell viability was observed among the 16 h, 18 h, and 24 h hypoxia periods. Based on these findings, the H/R cycle was set to 16 h of hypoxia followed by 4 h of reoxygenation.

Evaluation of the effect of increasing hypoxia and constant reperfusion (4 h) times applied to BEAS‐2B cells on cell viability compared to normoxic conditions by MTT. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3) (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 vs. normoxia group).

Melatonin Applied to BEAS‐2B Cells during the H/R Period Increases Cell Viability

BEAS‐2B cells were treated with increasing concentrations of melatonin (1, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100 µM) during the H/R period. H/R exposure caused a significant decrease in cell viability (p = 0.015) compared with normoxic conditions. When the protective effect of melatonin against H/R injury was assessed using the MTT assay, treatment with 2.5 µM (p = 0.02) and 5 µM (p = 0.009) significantly increased cell viability, restoring it to levels close to those observed under normoxic conditions. In contrast, higher concentrations of 10 µM (p = 0.03), 25 µM (p = 0.04), 50 µM (p = 0.03), and 100 µM (p < 0.001) significantly decreased cell viability compared with normoxic conditions (Figure 2A and B). Melatonin exhibited non‐monotonic dose responses, not a dose‐dependent effect. These findings indicate that low concentrations of melatonin (2.5–5 µM) applied during H/R exerted a protective effect against H/R‐induced cell damage in BEAS‐2B cells. When cell viability was assessed by ATP assay, the H/R group showed significantly lower intracellular ATP levels than the normoxia group (p = 0.01). Treatment with 2.5 µM melatonin increased ATP levels compared with the H/R group, with the strongest effect observed at 2.5 µM (p =0.02) (Figure 3).

Melatonin Applied to BEAS‐2B Cells during Reoxygenation Does Not Affect Cell Viability

After 16 h of hypoxia, BEAS‐2B cells were treated with various melatonin concentrations (1, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 µM) for 4 h under normoxic conditions. Melatonin treatment during reoxygenation did not significantly alter cell viability compared to the H/R group (p > 0.05). Therefore, to evaluate the protective effect of melatonin treatment on H/R injury, BEAS‐2B cells were treated with melatonin at concentrations of 2.5 µM during the H/R period (Figure 4).

Evaluation of the effect of different concentrations of melatonin incubated during reoxygenation on cell viability in H/R (16/4 h) exposed BEAS‐2B cells by MTT. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3) (*p < 0.05 vs. normoxia group). H/R, Hypoxia/Reoxygenation; M, Melatonin.

Melatonin Treatment Preserves the Morphology of BEAS‐2B Cells in H/R Conditions and Reduces Necrotic Cell Death

Cell morphology was assessed in BEAS‐2B cells using acridine orange/ethidium bromide (AO/EtBr) double staining (Figure 5A). Normoxic cells showed predominantly green fluorescence, indicating viable cells, whereas H/R exposure induced morphological changes characterized by yellow‐orange and red fluorescence, consistent with early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cell death. Annexin V‐PI staining was performed to determine the proportion of cells undergoing apoptotic and necrotic death (Figure 5B). In normoxic BEAS‐2B cells, viable cells, apoptotic (early and late) cells, and necrotic cells were 97.4% ± 0.6, 0.7% ± 0.2, and 1.9% ± 0.4, respectively. As shown in Figure 5C, H/R exposure caused a significant decrease in cell viability (51.8% ± 2.8, p = 0.002) and a significant increase in necrotic cell death (46.9% ± 2.7, p = 0.002) in BEAS‐2B cells while it exerted no influence on apoptotic cell death (1.3% ± 0.1 p > 0.05). Melatonin (2.5 µM) significantly increased cell viability (70.5% ± 0.6, p = 0.04) and reduced necrotic cell death (23.7% ± 0.9, p = 0.04). Melatonin increased apoptotic death at 2.5 µM (5.9% ± 0.5; p = 0.006) (Figure 5B and C). These findings indicate that necrosis is a major contributor to cell death following H/R (16/4 h) exposure in BEAS‐2B cells, and that melatonin significantly reduces necrotic cell death and induces a slight shift towards the apoptotic pathway in the cell death profile.

Melatonin Treatment Reduces MLKL Levels in BEAS‐2B Cells Under H/R Conditions

MLKL protein, a marker of necroptosis, was quantified using ELISA in BEAS‐2B cells. H/R significantly increased MLKL levels compared to normoxia (p = 0.02). Melatonin administration (2.5 µM) markedly reversed the H/R‐induced increase in MLKL protein (p = 0.04), restoring levels to those observed under normoxic conditions (p > 0.05) (Figure 6). The H/R‐induced increase in total MLKL protein levels, together with the predominance of necrotic cell death, is consistent with the possible involvement of necroptosis‐related signaling. Melatonin treatment reduced total MLKL levels, suggesting a potential modulatory effect on this pathway. However, as phosphorylated MLKL and upstream regulators were not measured, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require further mechanistic validation.

Melatonin Treatment Preserves Mitochondrial Membrane Integrity in BEAS‐2B Cells Under H/R Conditions

JC‐1 staining was used to assess the effect of melatonin on mitochondrial membrane integrity in BEAS‐2B cells under H/R (16/4 h) conditions (Figure 7). H/R exposure significantly reduced cell viability (p = 0.007), as indicated by impaired membrane integrity. In cells treated with 2.5 µM melatonin during H/R, melatonin significantly enhanced cell viability (p = 0.03) (Figure 7A,B). These data indicate that melatonin effectively preserves mitochondrial membrane integrity in BEAS‐2B cells under H/R stress.

Melatonin Treatment Significantly Reduces Reactive Oxygen Levels in BEAS‐2B Cells Under H/R Conditions

The effect of melatonin on intracellular ROS levels in BEAS‐2B cells under H/R (16/4 h) conditions was assessed using ROS staining. Whereas H/R significantly increased ROS levels (p= 0.007), melatonin administration at 2.5 µM (p = 0.006) markedly attenuated this elevation (Figure 8A‐B). These data indicate that the effective reduction of ROS production contributes to the protective effect of melatonin on BEAS‐2B cells under H/R stress.

Discussion

This study suggests that melatonin may reduce cell death in BEAS‐2B cells by inhibiting H/R‐induced necroptosis. H/R exposure resulted in more necrotic cell death than apoptotic cell death in human lung epithelial cells. It was shown that necroptosis may also play a role in this process. Melatonin treatment significantly reduced MLKL levels, preserved mitochondrial membrane integrity, decreased ROS and lactate accumulation, and restored ATP levels in BEAS‐2B cells exposed to H/R.

Recent studies have reported that necroptosis may be a cellular death pathway contributing to LIRI [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Inhibiting necroptosis with agents such as the RIPK1 inhibitor Nec‐1 and the MLKL inhibitor NSA has been shown to confer protection. Similarly, in the present study, increased MLKL levels under H/R conditions were consistent with activation of necroptosis‐related signaling. Additionally, in a rat LIRI model, both Nec‐1 and the pan‐caspase inhibitor z‐VAD‐fmk demonstrated protective effects, with the benefit being significantly greater when Nec‐1 was combined with z‐VAD‐fmk [16]. Similarly, another study showed that Nec‐1 mitigated LIRI in both in vivo and in vitro experiments by inhibiting necroptosis and apoptosis in epithelial cells [11]. The MLKL inhibitor NSA also improved physiological functions and reduced histological injury in a rat LIRI model by inhibiting necroptosis and decreasing inflammatory cytokine levels [15]. Previous studies, together with the present findings, suggest that necroptosis plays a role in LIRI.

Recent studies have shown that melatonin protects against LIRI by inhibiting oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and mitophagy [10, 34, 35]. A comprehensive literature review revealed no prior studies investigating melatonin's effect on necroptosis in LIRI. However, numerous studies have demonstrated that melatonin exerts a protective effect by inhibiting necroptosis in cerebral, cardiac, and spinal cord I/R injuries [39, 40, 41, 42]. In these studies, the expression of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL increased with I/R injury, and melatonin administration significantly reduced both their expression and the extent of I/R injury. Our study showed that the increased MLKL levels in BEAS‐2B cells during H/R exposure were significantly reduced by melatonin treatment during the H/R period. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that melatonin may provide protection against LIRI by inhibiting necroptosis.

In necroptosis, following phosphorylation by RIPK3, MLKL oligomerizes and binds to phosphatidylinositol lipids, enabling its translocation from the cytosol to plasma and organelle membranes such as mitochondria and lysosomes. This disrupts membrane integrity and leads to necrotic cell death [20]. Using JC‐1 staining, our study demonstrated that H/R (16/4 h) disrupted mitochondrial membrane integrity in BEAS‐2B cells and elevated MLKL levels, whereas melatonin restored MLKL and preserved mitochondrial integrity. These findings align with earlier work showing that hypobaric hypoxia [43] and H/R (18/4 h) [13] impair mitochondrial integrity in BEAS‐2B cells.

Mitochondria play a central role in the pathogenesis of I/R injury. When I/R opens the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), the inner mitochondrial membrane's permeability barrier is disrupted, leading to swelling, loss of membrane potential, ATP depletion, and a transient rise in ROS [44, 45, 46]. In rat alveolar epithelial cells [47] and human A549 adenocarcinoma cells [48], H/R has been shown to induce mPTP opening, decrease mitochondrial membrane potential, and increase ROS production. In accordance with previous reports, our study showed that H/R (16/4 h) in BEAS‐2B cells—a healthy human alveolar epithelial cell line—decreased ATP levels, increased ROS, and disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential. However, melatonin treatment during H/R reversed these alterations. Moreover, inhibiting the necrosome kinases similarly ameliorates I/R‐induced elevations in ROS and declines in ATP. For instance, Nec‐1 mitigated H/R‐induced cardiomyocyte damage by suppressing increases in necroptosis markers and ROS levels and by preserving mitochondrial membrane potential [45]. RIPK3 deficiency has been shown to prevent the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and the increase in ROS levels in a mouse model of middle cerebral artery occlusion, ultimately reducing both necroptosis and apoptosis [22]. In line with these findings, our results indicate that melatonin's protective effect against H/R in BEAS‐2B cells stems from its ability to reduce MLKL, preserve mitochondrial function, and reverse increased ROS and lactate levels and decreased ATP.

In H/R‐exposed human lung epithelial cells, melatonin exhibited a nonmonotonic dose–response pattern. Non‐monotonic dose‐response curves have been demonstrated in various biological systems for natural hormones and endocrine disruptors, exhibiting diverse biological activities, including cell proliferation and endocrine disruption. Non‐monotonic dose‐response mechanisms suggest that dose and effect do not act together in a predictable manner. This can complicate the risk and efficacy assessment of drugs/toxic substances with non‐monotonic dose‐response [49, 50, 51]. The present study showed that melatonin was not protective when administered only during reoxygenation, whereas significant protective effects were observed when it was present throughout the entire H/R period. Similarly, in a rat single‐lung transplant model, the most pronounced protective effect of Nec‐1 was achieved when administered during both cold ischemic storage and the post‐implantation reperfusion period [12]. Viewed from this perspective, our results suggest that melatonin may exert a protective effect in H/R‐exposed human lung epithelial cells within a narrow concentration range of 2.5–5 µM, whereas it may not be protective at higher concentrations.

It is essential to acknowledge that this study has some limitations. Firstly, in this study, phosphorylated MLKL (p‐MLKL) and upstream regulators (RIPK1/RIPK3) were not investigated. Because MLKL is a necrosome component not involved in the apoptotic process and is the last protein of the necrosome complex to be activated, we measured total MLKL as a marker of necroptosis. Secondly, no markers were examined in this study to validate the H/R model, but experiments were performed using an incubator, which is structured for hypoxic culture conditions and provides precise and stable control of oxygen levels. Therefore, the experimental hypoxic environment was created under validated technical conditions. Thirdly, a limitation of the present study is the relatively small number of biological replicates included in the experimental design, which may reduce the statistical robustness of the findings and limit their generalizability. Therefore, the protective effect observed with 2.5 μM melatonin under H/R conditions should be interpreted with caution. Future studies involving larger numbers of independent biological replicates are needed to confirm the reproducibility, reliability, and robustness of the present findings.

In conclusion, this study suggests that melatonin may protect human lung epithelial cells against H/R‐induced injury, at least in part, by suppressing necroptosis. This effect may be associated with reduced oxidative stress, preserved mitochondrial membrane integrity, and restored ATP levels. These findings suggest that melatonin may represent a promising therapeutic candidate for LIRI; however, further in vivo and translational studies are needed to confirm its therapeutic potential.
