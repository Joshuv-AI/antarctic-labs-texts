From the Solar System to cosmological distances: a complete formalism for gravitational wave astrometry
Daniele Bertacca

Abstract
The presence of a gravitational wave background (GWB) can be established not only via exquisitely precise pulsar timing array (PTA) measurements, but also via astrometric observations. Indeed, the very same background responsible for the delay in the arrival time of pulse causes an apparent displacement of galactic objects as stars and asteroids. In this work we provide a framework that allows to derive the displacement of sources overcoming the usually adopted “infinite distance” approximation. We also present how this formalism can be used to study the displacements of objects at distances comparable to the GW wavelength, as asteroids, and of objects with a non-trivial three-dimensional distribution, as stars in the Milky Way. Thus, it can be used to probe frequencies beyond PTA experiments, reaching the mHz GWs, also detectable by LISA. We forecast the capability of observing the astrometric deflection induced by a GWB evaluating the harmonic signal-to-noise ratio including correlations between different probes. We find an SNR greater than one for the relevant cases considered and as a consequence a promising Fisher forecast, suggesting a constraining power up to the percent level for a flat background.

Probing the gravitational wave background

2.1
Characterization of the background

For the purpose of this work, we rely on a description of the GWB that is common to both astrophysical and cosmological backgrounds.
We consider the metric tensor fluctuation
h_{ij}
that describes the GWB made up of the linear superposition of individual GW modes as

h_{ij}(t,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\lambda}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}df\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{\lambda}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})e^{-2\pi if(t-\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\mathbf{x})},

(2.1)

where
(t,\mathbf{x})
indicate the cosmic time and comoving position,
(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})
label the GW frequency and direction of propagation,
\lambda
indicates the polarization degree of freedom,
h_{\lambda}
is the mode amplitude for each polarization, and
e^{\lambda}_{ij}
is the polarization tensor.
Fourier transform conventions are explicitly reported in appendix
A
.
The variance of metric perturbations in the case of a stationary, unpolarized, and isotropic GWB reads as (see, e.g., ref.
[
3
]
)

\left\langle h_{\lambda}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta^{K}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{D}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}-\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\frac{S_{h}(f)}{8\pi},

(2.2)

where the asterisk indicates the complex conjugate, and
\delta^{K}
and
\delta^{D}
are the Kronecker and Dirac delta functions, respectively.
The one-sided GWB power spectrum
S_{h}
is often rewritten as

S_{h}(f)=\frac{h_{c}^{2}(f)}{f}=\frac{3H^{2}_{0}}{2\pi^{2}f^{3}}\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}(f),

(2.3)

where
h_{c}(f)
is the characteristic strain,
H_{0}
is the present-day Hubble expansion rate, and
\Omega_{\mathrm{GWB}}(f)=\rho^{-1}_{0c}d\rho_{\mathrm{GWB}}/d\log f
is the relative energy density per log frequency, with
\rho_{\mathrm{GWB}}
and
\rho_{0c}
being the GWB energy density and today critical density, respectively.

2.2
Astrometric measurements

The presence of a GWB perturbs the propagation path of photons, thus it induces an apparent deflection in the observed position of their emitting sources from their original “true” position
[
38
]
.
Therefore, by probing any astrophysical object (asteroids, stars, galaxies, quasars, etc.) position over the course of a long period of time, we can infer their apparent motion induced by GWs.
Suppose a generic source at position
\mathbf{r}=r\hat{\mathbf{n}}
, where
r
is the distance and
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
is the line-of-sight, emits a photon with unperturbed frequency
\bar{\omega}
that travels to the observer position
(t,\mathbf{0})
.
The corresponding photon unperturbed world-line reads as

x^{\mu}(\Lambda)=(t+\bar{\omega}\Lambda,-\bar{\omega}\Lambda\hat{\mathbf{n}}),

(2.4)

where the affine parameter
\Lambda
takes values in the range
[\Lambda_{r},\Lambda_{0}]=[-r/\bar{\omega},0]
.
However, because of the presence of even a single GW, the photon is observed in the direction
\hat{\mathbf{n}}+\delta\mathbf{n}
, where the components of the angular displacement are given by
[
38
]

\delta n^{i}=\left(\delta^{K}_{ik}-n^{i}n^{k}\right)n^{j}\left[\frac{1}{2}h_{jk}(\Lambda_{0})-\left(\delta^{K}_{kl}-\frac{p^{k}n^{l}}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}\right)\left(h_{jl}(\Lambda_{0})-\frac{1}{\Lambda_{r}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{r}}\!\!\!\!\!d\Lambda\ h_{jl}(\Lambda)\right)\right].

(2.5)

This displacement is orthogonal to the line-of-sight, i.e.,
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\delta\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{0}
, as one can immediately check.
Via a direct substitution of equation (
2.1
), we have that the apparent angular displacement in the position of a single object induced by a GWB reads as

\displaystyle\delta n^{i}(t,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\int dfd\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{\lambda}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})e^{-2\pi ift}\left[\frac{p^{i}+n^{i}}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})-\frac{1}{2}n^{j}e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\right.

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left.+i\mathcal{D}(f,\mathbf{r})\left(\frac{p^{i}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}-n^{j}e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})+\frac{(2+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}n^{i}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\right)\right],

where we introduce the “distance” form factor

\mathcal{D}(f,\mathbf{r})=\frac{e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}-1}{2\pi fr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}.

(2.7)

For sources in the “long-arm limit”, where
\tilde{x}\equiv 2\pi fr\gg 1
, the form factor vanishes and we recover the known “infinite distance” result given by the terms in the first line of equation (
2.6
).
However, the magnitude of the distance form factor for sources not in that regime is substantially different, and induces a non-negligible deviation in the apparent angular displacement of astrophysical objects.

In this work, we rely on a harmonic decomposition to estimate the importance of a synergic effort between astrometric and pulsar timing observations.
Since the harmonic power spectra, the
C_{\ell}
s, are obtained by integrating the frequencies and the solid angle, in the following, we find it convenient to work with the frequency domain angular displacement

\displaystyle\delta n^{i}(f,r,\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{\lambda}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})\left[\frac{p^{i}+n^{i}}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})-\frac{1}{2}n^{j}e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\right.

(2.8)

\displaystyle\left.+i\mathcal{D}(f,\mathbf{r})\left(\frac{p^{i}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}-n^{j}e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})+\frac{(2+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}n^{i}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\right)\right],

which, given the vectorial nature of the deflection, we further decompose as

\delta\mathbf{n}(f,r,\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{\ell m}\left[E^{f,r}_{\ell m}\mathbf{Y}^{E}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})+B^{f,r}_{\ell m}\mathbf{Y}^{B}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\right]\,.

(2.9)

Here
\mathbf{Y}^{E}_{\ell m},\mathbf{Y}^{B}_{\ell m}
are vector spherical harmonics and
E^{f,r}_{\ell m},B^{f,r}_{\ell m}
are frequency- and distance-dependent harmonic coefficients.
The explicit definitions of vector spherical harmonics are reported in appendix
A
, while in appendix
B
we report the definition of the E- and B-mode harmonic coefficients.
The expectation value of the E and B coefficients reads

\displaystyle\left\langle E^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(E^{f^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}C^{EE}_{\ell}(f,r,r^{\prime})\,,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\left\langle B^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(B^{f^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}C^{BB}_{\ell}(f,r,r^{\prime})\,,

where the angular power spectra result

\displaystyle C^{EE}_{\ell}(f,r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}S_{h}(f)\,,

(2.11)

\displaystyle C^{BB}_{\ell}(f,r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}S_{h}(f)\,.

The derivation of this result is reported in appendix
B
.
Their frequency-integrated counterpart are

\displaystyle C^{EE}_{\ell}(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dfdf^{\prime}\left\langle E^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(E^{f^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}\right\rangle

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\ \mathrm{Re}\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}\right]S_{h}(f)\,,

\displaystyle C^{BB}_{\ell}(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dfdf^{\prime}\left\langle B^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(B^{f^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\ \mathrm{Re}\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}\right]S_{h}(f)\,.

Contrary to previous analyses, these angular power spectra include the dependence on the distances of the sources.
One of the main results of this paper, explicitly derived in appendix

\displaystyle F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=i^{\ell}e^{i\tilde{x}}\left[j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2\tilde{x}}+\tilde{x}\right)-j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left(i\tilde{x}-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell^{2}-1)}{4\tilde{x}^{2}}+\ell\right)\right],

(2.13)

\displaystyle F^{B}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=(-i)i^{\ell}e^{i\tilde{x}}\left[j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left(i\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}+\frac{\ell(\ell^{2}-1)}{2\tilde{x}}+\tilde{x}\right)+j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})\left(i\tilde{x}-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\right)\right],

that precisely arise from the finite distance contribution coming from the term proportional to
\mathcal{D}(f,\mathbf{r})
.

2.3
Pulsar timing residual measurements

Millisecond pulsars are characterized by a very stable rotational period
T_{\mathrm{psr}}
.
However, the presence of a GWB modifies the geodesics of the emitted photons, and it is responsible for an additional delay
\Delta T_{\mathrm{GWB}}
in the time-of-arrival (TOA) of pulses.
In other words, the inferred period is given by

T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathrm{psr}}=\mathrm{TOA}_{j}-\mathrm{TOA}_{j-1}=T_{\mathrm{psr}}+\Delta T_{\mathrm{GWB}},

(2.14)

where the index
j
labels the pulses, on top of the other astrophysical effects that are commonly implemented in a timing model, see, e.g., ref.
[
52
]
.
Therefore, by repeatedly measuring the TOA of pulses, we can infer the presence and properties of an intervening GWB.

For each pulsar at distance
\mathbf{r}=r\hat{\mathbf{n}}
from Earth, we define as the primary observable of the GWB the “redshift” variable

z(t,\mathbf{r})=\frac{\Delta T_{\mathrm{GWB}}}{T_{\mathrm{psr}}}=\sum_{\lambda}\int dfd\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{\lambda}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})\frac{n^{i}n^{j}e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}})}e^{-2\pi ift}\left[1-e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}\right].

(2.15)

In reality, all galactic pulsars that are typically included in a PTA analysis are at distances of order
r\sim\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{kpc})
, i.e., in the so-called “long-arm limit” for a nHz GWB, i.e.,
2\pi fr\approx 10^{3}\gg 1
.
In this scenario, the content of square brackets in equation (
2.15
) can be safely neglected because its rapidly oscillatory contribution averages out.
Therefore, we have that the redshift observable in the frequency domain is well-approximated by

z(f,\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{\lambda}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{\lambda}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})F_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{p}})\,,

(2.16)

where the antenna pattern function reads as

F_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{n^{i}n^{j}e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}})}\,.

(2.17)

It immediately descends from the GWB statistical properties that the redshift two-point function is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle z(f,\hat{\mathbf{n}})z^{*}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})S_{h}(f)\mathrm{HD}\,,

(2.18)

where
\mathrm{HD}(\cos\theta)
represents the Hellings-Downs curve
[
16
]
, explicitly defined as

\mathrm{HD}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})=\int\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{p}}}{4\pi}\sum_{\lambda}F_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{p}})F_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1-\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}{12}+\frac{1-\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}{2}\log\frac{1-\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}{2}.

(2.19)

By decomposing the redshift in spherical harmonics as

z(f,\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{\ell m}z^{f}_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}),

(2.20)

where
Y_{\ell m}
are the spherical harmonics and
z^{f}_{\ell m}
are the frequency-dependent harmonic coefficients, we obtain that their two-point function reads as

\left\langle z^{f}_{\ell m}z^{f^{\prime}}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}C^{zz}_{\ell}(f),

(2.21)

where the angular power spectrum is

C^{zz}_{\ell}(f)=4\pi\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}S_{h}(f),

(2.22)

or, in a more realistic scenario, where we integrate the two-point function over frequencies, we have

C_{\ell}^{zz}=\int dfdf^{\prime}\left\langle z^{f}_{\ell m}z^{f^{\prime}}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}C^{zz}_{\ell}=\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}\times 4\pi\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int dfS_{h}(f).

(2.23)

Also in this case, the interested reader can find explicit definitions and properties of spherical harmonics in appendix
A
, and the full derivation of the angular power spectrum in appendix
B
.

3
Observational targets

3.1
Compressed statistics

In the previous section, we have characterized both the time delays and the apparent displacement of individual astrophysical objects.
However, in order to process any vast amount of data, as in the case at hand, we are forced to work with compressed statistics, especially in the context of astrometric probes, where the number of individual sources ranges from millions to billions.

In this sense, for objects not in the infinite distance limit, it is convenient to introduce a “volume-averaged” displacement to describe the statistics of the population instead of focusing on individual objects.
Given a finite volume element
V
of a spherical shell at distance
\bar{r}
and with half-width
\Delta r
containing
N_{\mathrm{obj}}
objects, we define the average displacement as

\delta\mathbf{n}(f,\bar{r},\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\frac{\sum_{j\in V}\delta\mathbf{n}(f,r_{j},\hat{\mathbf{n}})}{N_{\mathrm{obj}}}=\frac{1}{N(\bar{r},\Delta r)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\frac{dN_{\mathrm{obj}}}{dr}W_{r}(r,\bar{r},\Delta r)\delta\mathbf{n}(f,r,\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,,

(3.1)

where the RHS represents the description in the continuum limit,
dN_{\mathrm{obj}}/dr
is the radial number density distribution, and
W_{r}
is the window function defining the distance and thickness of the spherical shell.
The function
N(\bar{r},\Delta r)
is a normalization constant defined as

N(\bar{r},\Delta r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\frac{dN_{\mathrm{obj}}}{dr}W_{r}(r,\bar{r},\Delta r)\,.

(3.2)

On the practical side, the quantity that is going to appear in the angular power spectra is a distance-averaged form factor

F^{X}_{\ell}(f,\bar{r})=\frac{1}{N(\bar{r},\Delta r)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\frac{dN_{\mathrm{obj}}}{dr}W_{r}(r,\bar{r},\Delta r)F^{X}_{\ell}(2\pi fr).

(3.3)

In the following, we implement a number density radial distribution of the form

\frac{dN_{\mathrm{obj}}}{dr}=N^{\mathrm{tot}}_{\mathrm{obj}}\Phi_{\mathrm{obj}}(r),

(3.4)

where
N^{\mathrm{tot}}_{\mathrm{obj}}
is the total number of objects of our sample between
[r_{\mathrm{min}},r_{\mathrm{max}}]
, i.e., between the minimum and maximum distance of the objects from the observer, and
\Phi_{\mathrm{obj}}
is a probability distribution that describes the shape of the radial distribution and is normalized to unity, i.e.,
\displaystyle\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}dr\Phi_{\mathrm{obj}}(r)=1
.
Additionally, when the uncertainty on the distance is typically much smaller than the distance itself, a reasonable choice of window function is a top-hat function of the form

W_{r}(r,\bar{r},\Delta r)=\Theta_{H}\left(\bar{r}+\Delta r-r\right)\Theta_{H}\left(r-\bar{r}+\Delta r\right).

(3.5)

Finally, we note that no individual experiment is sensitive to the entire range of GW frequencies, especially in light of the fact that none of the measurements occurs on a continuous basis in time.
To account for this effect, we introduce a window function in frequency-space
W_{f}
, so that for each observable
X(f,\mathbf{r})
what we actually compare with data is

X(\bar{f},\mathbf{r})=\int dfW_{f}(f,\bar{f},\Delta f)X(f,\mathbf{r}),

(3.6)

where
\bar{f}
and
\Delta f
are the representative average frequency and width of the chosen frequency bin and, in this case, the window function does not need to be normalized to unity, since we want to consider the integrated effect of all frequencies.
The most simple and conservative choice is represented by

W_{f}(f,\bar{f},\Delta f)=\Theta_{H}\left(f-\bar{f}+\Delta f\right)\Theta_{H}\left(\Delta\bar{f}+\Delta f-f\right)=\Theta_{H}\left(f-f_{\mathrm{min}}\right)\Theta_{H}\left(f_{\mathrm{max}}-f\right),

(3.7)

where the minimum
f_{\mathrm{min}}=T^{-1}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and maximum
f_{\mathrm{max}}=\Delta t^{-1}
frequencies correspond to the inverse of the total observation time and cadence of observations, respectively.
Other window choices are possible; in particular, it might be convenient to introduce multiple frequency bin to probe the shape of the GWB power spectrum and to remove noisy frequency channel from the analysis.

In the end, the final form of the angular displacement power spectra is

C^{X_{i}X_{j}}_{\ell}=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int df\mathrm{Re}\left[F^{X}_{\ell}(f,\bar{r}_{i})\left[F^{X}_{\ell}(f,\bar{r}_{j})\right]^{*}\right]W_{f}^{i}W_{f}^{j}S_{h}(f)\,.

(3.8)

When
i=j
, one straightforwardly obtains
W_{f}^{i}W_{f}^{i}=|W_{f}|^{2}
.
However, for different sources (and eventually different detectors), as in the case of cross-correlations, only those frequencies that lie in the overlapping region of the two detectors should be considered, otherwise the integral will vanish.
For the redshift angular power spectra we have

C^{zz}_{\ell}=4\pi\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int df|W_{f}|^{2}S_{h}(f)\,.

(3.9)

4
Statistical analysis

4.1
Gravitational wave background models

We test our formalism by examining different GWB signals. We model the GW signal by means of the usually adopted power law formula, i.e.

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)=\Omega_{s}\left(\frac{f}{f_{s}}\right)^{\gamma}

(4.1)

where
f_{s}
is the pivot frequency,
\Omega_{s}
is the amplitude at the pivot scale, and
\gamma
is the spectral index.
The model considered are

•

PTA GW backgrounds.

Recent PTA experiments found evidence for a GWB for the first time and the exact source of the signal is still under debate. Similarly as in PTA analysis, we consider two representative backgrounds in the PTA frequency band.
For the first we adopt the median values obtained for a general model of the timing-residual power spectral density with variable power-law exponent,
obtaining
\Omega_{s}=1.12\times 10^{-7}
,
f_{s}\simeq 3.17\times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{Hz}
and
\gamma\simeq 1.8

[
17
]
.
For the second we adopt the median values obtained assuming the background is attributed to super-massive black hole binaries (SMBHBs) in their inspiralling phase; following the latest PTA analyses we take

\Omega_{s}=1.5\times 10^{-8}
,
f_{s}\simeq 3.17\times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{Hz}
and
\gamma=2/3

[
17
]
.

•

Cosmological background.
As an example, we consider the case of a cosmic string signal, given its broadness in frequency range. We choose, in order to get an illustrative example, the following reference values
\gamma=0
and
\Omega_{s}=10^{-8}
.

4.2
Statistical tools

Given the potential of these probes, also used in combination with each other, here we are interested in exploring two aspects.
First, we aim at quantifying the detectability of the signal for different combinations of theoretical models.
Then, once we identify the most promising experiments, we want to provide a sensitivity forecast for the most important GWB models.

In terms of detectability, we define a cumulative Signal-to-Noise ratio (SNR) of the form

\mathrm{SNR}^{2}=f_{\mathrm{sky}}\sum_{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\ell}+\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\ell}+\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\right)^{-1}\right],

(4.2)

where
f_{\mathrm{sky}}
is the fraction of the sky covered by the experiment, and
\mathcal{C}_{\ell},\,\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
are covariance matrices for the signal and noise.
Regarding the signal covariance matrix, in its most general form it is constructed as a symmetric matrix with entries given by the angular power spectra and reads as

\mathcal{C}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}&&C^{zz}_{\ell}&C^{zE_{(1,1)}}_{\ell}&\cdots&C^{zE_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}&C^{zB_{(1,1)}}_{\ell}&\cdots&C^{zB_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}\\
&&&C^{E_{(1,1)}E_{(1,1)}}_{\ell}&\cdots&C^{E_{(1,1)}E_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}&C^{E_{(1,1)}B_{(1,1)}}_{\ell}&\cdots&C^{E_{(1,1)}B_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}\\
&&&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&&&C^{E_{(n,p)}E_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}&C^{E_{(n,p)}B_{(1,1)}}_{\ell}&\cdots&C^{E_{(n,p)}B_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}\\
&&&&&&C^{B_{(1,1)}B_{(1,1)}}_{\ell}&\cdots&C^{B_{(1,1)}B_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}\\
&&&&&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&&&&&&C^{B_{(n,p)}B_{(n,p)}}_{\ell}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(4.3)

Although cumbersome, this form of the covariance matrix allows the possibility of including redshift correlations, and
n
and
p
distance and frequency bins, respectively, for the angular displacement.
However, in many instances, the analysis is extremely simplified because of the properties of the background.
For example, when different polarization degrees of freedom are independent, we have
C^{zB_{(k,l)}}_{\ell}=C^{E_{(i,j)}B_{(k,l)}}_{\ell}\equiv 0
for all possible combinations of indices.
Additionally, if each GWB source emits in a single frequency bin during the entire course of observations, all frequency bins are fundamentally uncorrelated.
Finally, in the case of searches for broad-band GW signals, where the frequency ranges typical of redshift and angular displacement measurements do not overlap, also all
z-E
cross-correlations vanish (
C^{zE_{(k,l)}}_{\ell}\equiv 0
, unless the same sources emit in different frequencies, as previously commented).
In this circumstance, redshift and astrometric measurements completely decouple, and the total SNR is simply the sum of the individual SNRs.

In the case of the noise covariance matrix, in this work we consider a purely diagonal noise matrix of the form
[
49
]

4

Suppose to have measured a displacement

Each of the two components of the noise is sampled from a Gaussian distribution

(4.4)

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{tot}}}{N_{\mathrm{obj,j}}},

is the number of objects in the

(4.6)

In particular, for the astrometric noise, we assume a future-generation Gaia-like satellite for stellar observations and a next-generation Roman-like mission for asteroid measurements.
This choice is motivated by the extremely small magnitude of expected deflections and the need for a precise characterization of the velocities of the objects to robustly assess a detection.

(4.7)

where the matrix

has for elements the derivatives of the angular power spectra with respect to the desired parameter.

As shown in the figure, each probe taken individually is, in principle, capable of detecting the signal with a cumulative SNR that depends on the angular resolution of the instrument considered.
In particular, our analysis indicates that a Roman-like detector with enhanced angular precision could achieve detections up to

The significance of the detection can be further improved in several ways.
First, increasing the angular resolution enables a more accurate characterization of the signal and a more precise measurement of the deflection.
Second, adopting a smaller observational cadence broadens the accessible frequency range, thereby extending the portion of the signal that can be probed and consequently enhancing the overall detectability.
In addition, if also high multipoles are detectable, we would be able to further reduce the error on our GWB amplitude measurement.
However, these experiments are constrained by a relatively large cadence, which reduces the accessible frequency range compared to Roman.
Nevertheless, extending the mission total observation time would probe the low frequency regime, which necessarily requires dropping the long-distance approximation and adopting our result.

In the case of pulsars, given the current findings and the timeliness of the results, we estimate the prospects of detection for the SKAO accounting for different numbers of pulsars and observation times.

In a more futuristic scenario, for an observation time of 20 years and 500 monitored pulsars, we find that SKA would sensibly improve the detection significance of the GWB by being able to observe up to

Finally, we perform a complete analysis including all the probes and their cross-correlations.
We report the corresponding lines (dot-dashed) in the figure, considering the two different benchmark cases for pulsars observations.
In all cases, we find that including all possible cross

The most significant effect appears at the lowest multipoles: the SNR is substantially increased compared to the single-probe cases.
For the first benchmark scenario (67 pulsars over 5 years), and under the noise assumptions of a future GAIA-like detector, we predict sensitivity up to

Nevertheless, we find a substantial increase up to

5.3
Fisher forecast results

Finally, we perform a Fisher forecast injecting the three GW signals discussed in section
4.1
, in order to assess the potential of a synergistic effort between astrometric and pulsar timing experiments to detect a GWB.
In the case of a powerlaw-like spectrum as the one detected by current PTA data or as the one produced by supermassive black hole binaries, we include in our forecast the amplitude and the spectral index.
Instead, in the case of a flat spectrum we include only the amplitude in the analysis.

Our findings for the first two benchmark cases are reported in figure

Since we have already emphasized the importance of combining the different probes in the SNR analysis, we perform the forecast including directly all the auto- and cross-correlations for each probe as explained in section
4.2
.
We repeated the analysis for two different benchmark cases, first with 67 pulsars and for 5 years of observation time and then considering 500 pulsars for 20 years of observation time.
For the generic PTA-like signal, we find that in both cases it is possible to detect the amplitude with an error of less than the 50% of the central value.
When the number of pulsars and observation time are increased, the error decreases to less than 40%, due to the increased sensitivity of pulsar timing.
In detail, we find
\sigma_{A}=0.49\times 10^{-7}
in the former case and
\sigma_{A}=0.44\times 10^{-7}
in the latter.
Regarding the spectral index, instead, our results suggest that future astrometric and redshift measurements would constrain it with an error of the percent, respectively
\sigma_{\gamma}/\gamma=0.05/1.8\simeq 3\%
and
\sigma_{\gamma}/\gamma=0.04/1.8\simeq 2.2\%
.
The enhanced constraining power on the spectral index, as opposed to the amplitude, can be understood as follows: when the amplitude is fixed at a reference frequency, the spectral index determines the relative weighting of power across the entire relevant frequency band.
Consequently, even a small change in the spectral index produces appreciable modifications in the spectrum, correspondingly leading to a stronger impact on the analysis, as will also be explained in the following.

In the case of a GWB generated by super-massive black hole binaries, we find slightly worse results.
This is due to the lower amplitude of the GW considered (with a decrease of about one order of magnitude, given the latest PTA constraints).
We find
\sigma_{A}=2.7(2.8)\times 10^{-8}
and
\sigma_{\gamma}=0.25(0.238)
in the two cases considered.
Finally, in the case of a flat (cosmological) background, we forecast a relative error of the 22% and 17% respectively for the same cases considered before, even though the amplitude is now lower than in the first case and comparable to the second.
Specifically we find
\sigma_{A}=2.19\times 10^{-9}
and
\sigma_{A}=1.74\times 10^{-9}
for the two cases considered.
This improvement can be understood by noting that, for a flat spectrum, all frequencies contribute equally to the signal and in addition, having one less parameter reduces the degeneracies.

Conclusions

In this work we studied in detail the potential of astrometric surveys and their synergy with pulsar timing arrays.
First, we presented a new approach which allows to evaluate the astrometric deflection for any frequency, without limitations on the distance of the source considered, i.e., for any value of
\tilde{x}=2\pi fr
.
This result represents the main theoretical novelty of this work.
We also introduced the form factors for the E- and B-mode in equation (
2.13
), which asymptotically reach
(-1)^{\ell}
in the infinite distance limit
\tilde{x}\to\infty
and show a non-trivial dependence for finite value of
\tilde{x}
otherwise.
Our results show that for the E-mode when
\ell=2
the form factor reaches a higher value with respect to the long-distance approximation, highlighting the need of a full approach.
Including the dependence on distance enables the modeling of the three-dimensional distribution of the targeted sources, allowing for a more comprehensive estimate of the deflection induced by a GWB.
We also demonstrated that a fully consistent treatment can lead to either an overestimation or underestimation of the harmonic power spectrum.
Additionally, it allows to evaluate all possible correlations between different observational probes, including redshift measurements obtained from pulsar timing arrays.
Furthermore we show how the distance–frequency plane is sampled by current and future surveys using both astrometric and redshift data and we provide a simple mapping, given a survey, to understand the frequency range spanned and the sources to be considered corresponding to different values of
\tilde{x}
.

On the astrometric side, in this work we considered the distribution of asteroids in the Main Belt and stars in the Milky Way, assuming a GAIA-like and Roman-like detector to monitor their position.
For the redshift measurements, instead, we consider 67 pulsars monitored for 5 years by SKA for 5 years and 500 pulsars monitored for 20 years.
We performed an SNR analysis first only considering the single probes and then fully including their correlation, finding in all cases a SNR greater that one showing that future observation the astrometric deflection would be fundamental for the characterization of a GWB.
We specify that our results are dependent on the angular sensitivity considered for the different satellites considered and on the number of sources monitored.
Furthermore, we estimate the capability of this method to constrain a GWB with a Fisher analysis.
Considering the latest results from PTA, we consider as benchmark cases the GWB responsible for the Hellings-Downs pattern observed, the upper bounds on the background given by SMBHB in their inspiralling phase and a flat GWB.
Depending on the spectral index and on the amplitude, we find a percentage error going from less than the
50\%
of the central value up to the
2.2\%
in the optimal scenario.

Although in this work we focus exclusively on asteroids populating the Main Belt, other asteroid families are also present in the Solar System and could potentially be monitored to detect the presence of a background.
However, their current known number of is at least one/two orders of magnitude smaller than that of Main Belt asteroids, also for the Kuiper Belt asteroids, where we found only
N_{\mathrm{ast}}\approx 4100
with known orbital motion
5
5

5

The total Small-Body Database contains approximately
1.47\times 10^{6}
objects with known orbital motion, i.e., asteroids not in the Main Belt represent only
10\%
of the database.
.
Some of these limitations are purely observational, and future missions would very likely increase these numbers, since it might be the case that there are around
10^{5}
objects in that region.

In addition, we note that our analysis assumes an ideal reconstruction of the angular displacement, neglecting the influence of the sources’ peculiar velocities or orbital motion.
This constitutes a primary limitation of the method, whose detailed modeling is beyond the scope of the present work and we leave to a future more detailed analysis.

A.1
Fourier transform

In this work, we adopt the following convention for the Fourier transform

f(\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}f(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}},\qquad f(\mathbf{k})=\int d^{3}xf(\mathbf{x})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}.

(A.1)

In the case of a real scalar field, for which
f^{*}(\mathbf{k})=f(-\mathbf{k})
, the two-point function in Fourier space reads as

\left\langle f(\mathbf{k}_{1})f^{*}(\mathbf{k}_{2})\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{D}(\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{k}_{2})P(k),

(A.2)

where
P(k)
is the power spectrum.
Therefore, in the case of real tensor field, as for the case of a GW perturbation moving at the speed of light, we have

\displaystyle h_{ij}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}h_{ij}(k,\mathbf{k})e^{-ik(t-\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{x})}=\sum_{\lambda}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}h_{\lambda}(k,\mathbf{k})e_{ij}^{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})e^{-ik(t-\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{x})}

(A.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\int dfd\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{\lambda}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})e_{ij}^{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})e^{-2\pi if(t-\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\mathbf{x})},

after the change of variable
k_{j}=2\pi f_{j}
such that
d^{3}k=(2\pi)^{3}f^{2}dfd\hat{\mathbf{p}}
, and a redefinition of
h_{\lambda}

A.2
Scalar and vector spherical harmonics

In this work, the decomposition in spherical harmonics of a scalar function follows the convention

X(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{\ell m}X_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}),\qquad X_{\ell m}=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}X(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}),

(A.4)

where
X_{\ell m}
are the harmonic coefficients, the spherical harmonics are explicitly defined as

Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},

(A.5)

and
P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)
are the associated Legendre polynomials.
This definition ensures that spherical harmonics are an orthonormal basis, i.e.,

\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}.

(A.6)

On the other hand, a 2D vector tangential to a sphere of radius
r
can be decomposed into its “electric” and “magnetic” components, also known as E- and B-modes, as

\mathbf{X}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{\ell m}\left[E_{\ell m}\mathbf{Y}^{E}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})+B_{\ell m}\mathbf{Y}^{B}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\right],

(A.7)

where the vector spherical harmonics are defined as
[
54
]

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{E}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\nabla_{\Omega}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}r\nabla Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}),

(A.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{B}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\nabla_{\Omega})Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times r\nabla)Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}),

and
\nabla_{\Omega}
is the angular part of the
\nabla
operator.
The inverse transformation for electric and magnetic components is given by

E_{\ell m}=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\ \mathbf{X}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\cdot\mathbf{Y}^{E*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}),\qquad B_{\ell m}=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\ \mathbf{X}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\cdot\mathbf{Y}^{B*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}).

(A.9)

Also in this case, vector spherical harmonics are an orthonormal basis, for which

\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{Y}^{I}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\cdot\mathbf{Y}^{J}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\delta^{K}_{IJ}\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}.

(A.10)

Since by construction the vector
\mathbf{X}
is tangential to the sphere, i.e.,
\mathbf{X}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla r=0
, the computation of the electric and magnetic components reduces to

\displaystyle E_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\ \mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y}^{E*}_{\ell m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\left[\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{X}\ rY^{*}_{\ell m})-(\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla r)Y^{*}_{\ell m}-(r\nabla\cdot\mathbf{X})Y^{*}_{\ell m}\right]

(A.11)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})(r\nabla\cdot\mathbf{X}),

\displaystyle B_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\ \mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y}^{B*}_{\ell m}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\left[\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{X}\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}\ rY^{*}_{\ell m})-rY^{*}_{\ell m}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{X}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+rY^{*}_{\ell m}\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}+Y^{*}_{\ell m}\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla r\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\ (\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times r\nabla)\cdot\mathbf{X},

by using the periodicity of the spherical harmonics functions to cancel the surface contributions, and the identities
\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0

\nabla r\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0
and
\mathbf{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})=(\mathbf{A}\times\nabla)\cdot\mathbf{B}

A.3
Legendre polynomials

In this work, we make extensive use of the Legendre (
P_{\ell}

The two classes of polynomials are connected by the relation

P^{m}_{\ell}(\mu)=(-1)^{m}(1-\mu^{2})^{m/2}\frac{d^{m}P_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu^{m}},

(A.12)

and follow

P_{\ell}^{-m}(\mu)=(-1)^{m}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}P_{\ell}^{m}(\mu)\,,\quad P_{\ell}^{m}(1)=\delta^{K}_{m,0}\,,\quad P_{\ell}^{m}(-\mu)=(-1)^{\ell}P_{\ell}^{m}(\mu)\,.

(A.13)

However, in the following, we are mostly interested in the

P^{2}_{\ell}(\mu)=(1-\mu^{2})\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu^{2}},\qquad P^{-2}_{\ell}(\mu)=\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}P^{2}_{\ell}(\mu).

(A.14)

Legendre polynomials are the solution of the differential equation

(1-\mu^{2})\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu^{2}}-2\mu\frac{dP_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu}+\ell(\ell+1)P_{\ell}(\mu)=0\,,

(A.15)

and also form an orthogonal basis such that

\int d\mu P_{\ell}(\mu)P_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)=\frac{2}{2\ell+1}\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}.

(A.16)

Additionally, they also satisfy the following relations

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{2}-1}{\ell}\frac{dP_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu}

\displaystyle=\mu P_{\ell}(\mu)-P_{\ell-1}(\mu),

(A.17)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{\ell-1}(\mu)}{d\mu}

\displaystyle=(\ell+1)P_{\ell}(\mu)+\mu\frac{dP_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu},

\displaystyle(\ell+1)P_{\ell+1}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(2\ell+1)\mu P_{\ell}(\mu)-\ell P_{\ell-1}(\mu).

Finally, at the boundary of their domain of definition, Legendre polynomials and their first derivatives take the values

P_{\ell}(1)=1,\quad P_{\ell}(-1)=(-1)^{\ell},\quad\left.\frac{dP_{\ell}}{d\mu}\right|_{\mu=1}=\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2},\quad\left.\frac{dP_{\ell}}{d\mu}\right|_{\mu=-1}=(-1)^{\ell+1}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2},

(A.18)

which can be easily derived using their symmetry property
P_{\ell}(-y)=(-1)^{\ell}P_{\ell}(y)
.

For the purpose of this paper, it is useful to introduce two classes of functions,
J_{n}
and
K_{n}

\displaystyle J_{n}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu\ \mu^{n}\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}}{d\mu^{2}}=\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}-n\right]\left[1+(-1)^{\ell+n}\right]+n(n-1)\int d\mu\ \mu^{n-2}P_{\ell}(\mu),

(A.19)

\displaystyle K_{n}(w)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu\ \mu^{n}e^{iw\mu}\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}}{d\mu^{2}}=(-i)^{n}\frac{d^{n}K_{0}}{dw^{n}},

where all the terms proportional to
\delta^{K}_{\ell 0}
and
\delta^{K}_{\ell 1}
are not reported, and
K_{0}

\displaystyle K_{0}(w)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu\ e^{iw\mu}\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}}{d\mu^{2}}

(A.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\left[e^{iw}+(-1)^{\ell}e^{-iw}\right]-iw\left[e^{iw}+(-1)^{\ell+1}e^{-iw}\right]-2i^{\ell}w^{2}j_{\ell}(w),

obtained by implementing the Legendre decomposition of the complex exponential

\int d\mu\ e^{iw\mu}P_{\ell}(\mu)=\int d\mu\left[\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}i^{\ell^{\prime}}(2\ell^{\prime}+1)j_{\ell^{\prime}}(w)P_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)\right]P_{\ell}(\mu)=2i^{\ell}j_{\ell}(w),\\

Appendix B
Derivation of the angular power spectra

B.1
Selection of a reference frame

Although equations (
2.6
) and (
2.15
) are valid for any polarization degree of freedom, here we concentrate only on the General Relativity case, where
\lambda=\{+,\times\}
.
Therefore, since the GW propagation vector is orthogonal to the polarization tensor, and the latter is traceless, we have that

p^{j}e^{+,\times}_{ij}=0,\qquad\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{+,\times}=0.

(B.1)

First, we conveniently choose a reference frame where the GW propagation happens along one of the axis and the line-of-sight is generic, so that

\hat{\mathbf{p}}=(0,0,1),\qquad\hat{\mathbf{n}}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta).

(B.2)

In this frame, the polarization tensors read as

e^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad e^{\times}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(B.3)

therefore, the explicit form of the “antenna patterns” typical of the observables analyzed in this work are

\displaystyle\frac{n^{j}n^{k}e^{+}_{jk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}

\displaystyle=(1-\cos\theta)\cos(2\phi),\qquad\frac{n^{j}n^{k}e^{\times}_{jk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}=(1-\cos\theta)\sin(2\phi),

(B.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon_{ijk}p^{i}n^{j}n^{q}e^{+}_{qk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}

\displaystyle=(1-\cos\theta)\sin(-2\phi),\qquad\frac{\varepsilon_{ijk}p^{i}n^{j}n^{q}e^{\times}_{qk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}=(1-\cos\theta)\cos(-2\phi).

The choice made in equation (
B.2
) appears to be very specific, and certainly influences the value of the inferred harmonic coefficients.
Therefore, one might wonder whether the value of the two-point function depends on the choice of reference frame.
In the case, the answer is negative, their value does not depend on any explicit choice of reference frame, as long as the observable
X(\hat{\mathbf{n}})
is invariant under rotations, i.e.,
X^{\prime}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})=X(\hat{\mathbf{n}})
.
To prove this fact, let us consider how the harmonic coefficients transform under rotations

X^{\prime}_{\ell m}=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}X^{\prime}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})=\sum_{n=-\ell}^{\ell}\left(D^{\ell}_{mn}\right)^{*}\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}X(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y^{*}_{\ell n}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{n=-\ell}^{\ell}\left(D^{\ell}_{mn}\right)^{*}X_{\ell n},

(B.5)

where
D^{\ell}_{mn}
are the Wigner D-functions.
Therefore, in terms of the angular power spectrum, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle X^{\prime}_{\ell m}\left(X^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\right)^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}C^{\prime}_{\ell}

(B.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\left(D^{\ell}_{mn}\right)^{*}D^{\ell^{\prime}}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\left\langle X_{\ell n}\left(X_{\ell^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\right)^{*}\right\rangle=\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\left(D^{\ell}_{mn}\right)^{*}D^{\ell^{\prime}}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{nn^{\prime}}C_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left(D^{\ell}_{mn}\right)^{*}D^{\ell}_{m^{\prime}n}\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}C_{\ell}=\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}C_{\ell},

using the properties of the Wigner D-functions, i.e., the angular power spectra are invariant under rotations.
In practice, in this paper all the observable
X(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=z,r\nabla\cdot\delta\mathbf{n},(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times r\nabla)\cdot\delta\mathbf{n}
are scalar.
Aside from the redshift, which is evidently a scalar, for the angular displacement we observe that under a rotation represented by an orthogonal matrix
R
we have

\displaystyle r\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\delta\mathbf{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(r\nabla^{\prime})^{T}\delta\mathbf{n}^{\prime}=[R(r\nabla)]^{T}(R\delta\mathbf{n})=(r\nabla)^{T}(R^{T}R)\delta\mathbf{n}=r\nabla\cdot\delta\mathbf{n},

(B.7)

\displaystyle(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}\times r\nabla^{\prime})\cdot\delta\mathbf{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left[R\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times R(r\nabla)\right]^{T}(R\delta\mathbf{n})=\left[R\left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times r\nabla\right)\right]^{T}(R\delta\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times r\nabla\right]^{T}(R^{T}R)\delta\mathbf{n}=(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times r\nabla)\cdot\delta\mathbf{n}.

Alternatively, we can directly demonstrate the invariance of the redshift and angular displacement functions: for instance, in the case of the redshift, we see that the antenna patterns are such that

F^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})=\frac{(\hat{n}^{\prime})^{T}(e^{\lambda})^{\prime}\hat{n}^{\prime}}{1+(\hat{p}^{\prime})^{T}\hat{n}^{\prime}}=\frac{(R\hat{n})^{T}(Re^{\lambda}R^{T})(R\hat{n})}{1+(R\hat{p})^{T}(R\hat{n})}=\frac{\hat{n}^{T}e^{\lambda}\hat{n}}{1+\hat{p}^{T}\hat{n}}=F_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}).

(B.8)

Similar relations can also be explicitly checked for the angular displacement, proving that our choice of reference frame is not affecting the estimate of the angular power spectra.

B.2
Angular displacement angular power spectrum

Applying the general definitions of appendix
A
, we have that

\displaystyle E^{f,r}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})r\nabla_{i}\delta n^{i},

(B.9)

\displaystyle B^{f,r}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\ \varepsilon_{ijk}n^{j}r\nabla_{k}\delta n^{i}.

where, after some lengthy calculation reported in sections
B.2.1
and
B.2.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle r\nabla_{i}\delta n^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\left[1-\frac{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}{2}e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}\right.

(B.10)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{i}{2\pi fr}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right)\left(e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}-1\right)\right],

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ijk}n^{j}r\nabla_{k}\delta n^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{ijk}p^{i}n^{j}n^{q}e^{\lambda}_{qk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\left[1-e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}\right].

The first term in square brackets corresponds to the infinite distance limit, whereas the other terms represent the finite distance correction.
In the following, we present the calculation of the harmonic coefficients in two fashions, one explicit, the other that exploits the recursive relations introduced in appendix
A
.
Both procedures converge to the same results, which can be expressed in a general form as

\displaystyle E^{f,r}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}

(B.11)

\displaystyle\times\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\left[h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}+\delta^{K}_{m,-2})-ih_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}-\delta^{K}_{m,-2})\right],

\displaystyle B^{f,r}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi iF^{B}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}

\displaystyle\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\left[h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}+\delta^{K}_{m,-2})+ih_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}-\delta^{K}_{m,-2})\right],

where
\tilde{x}=2\pi fr
, and the limit
\tilde{x}\to\infty
for the two distance functions is
F^{E}_{\ell},F^{B}_{\ell}\to(-1)^{\ell}
.
The angular power spectrum estimators are built as

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{EE}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m}E^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(E^{f^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}}{2\ell+1}

(B.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x}^{\prime})\right]^{*}}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}d\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\left[h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{+}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})+h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{\times}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{BB}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m}B^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(B^{f^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}}{2\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi F^{B}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(\tilde{x}^{\prime})\right]^{*}}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}d\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\left[h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{+}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})+h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{\times}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\right],

so that their expectation value reads as

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{C}^{EE}_{\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})C^{EE}_{\ell}(f,r,r^{\prime})

(B.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}S_{h}(f),

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{C}^{BB}_{\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})C^{BB}_{\ell}(f,r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}S_{h}(f).

At this stage, the expectation value can, in principle, take complex values when
r\neq r^{\prime}
.
However, if we consider angular power spectrum integrated over the frequency range, we find that

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{EE}

\displaystyle=\int dfdf^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{C}^{EE}_{\ell}\right\rangle

(B.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}df\frac{1}{2}F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}S_{h}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\frac{F^{E}_{\ell}(-2\pi fr)\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(-2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}+F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}}{2}S_{h}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\ \mathrm{Re}\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}\right]S_{h}(f),

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{BB}

\displaystyle=\int dfdf^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{C}^{BB}_{\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}df\frac{1}{2}F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}S_{h}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\frac{F^{B}_{\ell}(-2\pi fr)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(-2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}+F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}}{2}S_{h}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\ \mathrm{Re}\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(2\pi fr^{\prime})\right]^{*}\right]S_{h}(f),

since, as we demonstrate in the next section, we have that
\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\right]^{*}=F^{E}_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})
and
\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\right]^{*}=F^{B}_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})
.
Therefore, as expected, the frequency-integrated angular power spectrum is always a real quantity.

We also report for completeness the real part of the distance functions

\displaystyle{\rm Re}{\left[{F^{E}_{\ell}}^{*}[2\pi fr]F^{E}_{\ell}[2\pi fr^{\prime}]\right]}=\frac{1}{16{\tilde{x}}^{2}(\tilde{x}^{\prime})^{2}}\bigg(2{\tilde{x}}\left(\ell^{2}+\ell+2{\tilde{x}}^{2}\right)j_{\ell-1}({\tilde{x}})

(B.15)

and

\displaystyle{\rm Re}{\left[{F^{B}_{\ell}}^{*}[2\pi fr]F^{B}_{\ell}[2\pi fr^{\prime}]\right]}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4{\tilde{x}}{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}}j_{\ell}({\tilde{x}})\left[j_{\ell}({\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\left(\left(2(\tilde{x}^{\prime})^{2}\left(\ell^{3}-\ell+2{\tilde{x}}^{2}\right)+(\ell-1)^{2}\ell^{2}{\tilde{x}}{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}+\left(\ell^{2}-1\right)\ell\left(\ell^{3}-\ell+2{\tilde{x}}^{2}\right)\right)\cos({\tilde{x}}-{\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\right.\right.

\displaystyle+{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}j_{\ell-1}({\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\left(\left(2{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}\left(\ell^{3}-\ell+2{\tilde{x}}^{2}\right)+\left(\ell^{2}-1\right)\ell^{2}{\tilde{x}}\right)\sin({\tilde{x}}-{\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.-\ell\left((\ell+1)\left(\ell^{3}-\ell+2{\tilde{x}}^{2}\right)-2(\ell-1){\tilde{x}}{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}\right)\cos({\tilde{x}}-{\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}}\left[j_{\ell-1}({\tilde{x}})\left({\tilde{x}^{\prime}}j_{\ell-1}({\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\left(\left(\ell^{2}(\ell+1)^{2}+4{\tilde{x}}{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}\right)\cos({\tilde{x}}-{\tilde{x}^{\prime}})+2\ell(\ell+1)({\tilde{x}}-{\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\sin({\tilde{x}}-{\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.+\ell\left(-2(\ell-1){\tilde{x}}{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}+2(\ell+1)(\tilde{x}^{\prime})^{2}+(\ell-1)\ell(\ell+1)^{2}\right)\cos({\tilde{x}}-{\tilde{x}^{\prime}})\right)\right)\right]\,.

B.2.1
Explicit calculation

Here we want to explicitly compute the E- and B-mode harmonic coefficient, which have the generic structure given by

X_{\ell m}=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}X(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}\int d\mu d\phi X(\mu,\phi)P_{\ell}^{m}(\mu)e^{-im\phi}.

(B.17)

Starting from the integrand of equation (
2.8
), we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{i}=\frac{n^{i}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}}{2(1+\gamma)}\,,\quad\mathcal{B}^{i}=\frac{p^{i}n^{j}n^{k}e^{\lambda}_{jk}}{2(1+\gamma)}\,,\quad\mathcal{C}^{i}=n_{j}e^{\lambda}_{ji}\,,

(B.18)

\displaystyle a=1-\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{(2+\gamma)}{(1+\gamma)}[1-e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}]\,,\quad b=1-\frac{i}{\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}[1-e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}]\,,\quad c=b-\frac{1}{2}\,,

where
\gamma=\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}
.
The angular gradient of these quantities is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Omega,j}\mathcal{A}^{i}=

\displaystyle\frac{[n_{m}n_{k}\delta_{ij}+\delta_{mj}n_{i}n_{k}+\delta_{kj}n_{i}n_{m}-3n_{k}n_{j}n_{i}n_{m}](1+\gamma)}{2(1+\gamma)^{2}}e^{\lambda}_{mk}

(B.19)

\displaystyle-\frac{n^{i}n^{m}n^{k}p_{j}+\gamma n^{i}n^{m}n^{k}n_{j}}{2(1+\gamma)^{2}}e^{\lambda}_{mk},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Omega,j}\mathcal{B}^{i}=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{p_{i}\delta_{mj}n_{k}}{(1+\gamma)}-\frac{n_{m}n_{k}}{2(1+\gamma)^{2}}(2n_{j}p_{i}+n_{j}p_{i}\gamma+p_{i}p_{j})\right],

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Omega,j}\mathcal{C}^{i}=

\displaystyle e^{\lambda}_{ij}-n_{j}n_{k}e^{\lambda}_{ik},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Omega,j}a=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{[1-e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}]}{(1+\gamma)^{2}}-\frac{(2+\gamma)}{(1+\gamma)}e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}\right]\nabla_{\Omega,j}\gamma,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Omega,j}b=\nabla_{\Omega,j}c=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{[1-e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}]}{(1+\gamma)^{2}}-\frac{e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}}{(1+\gamma)}\right]\nabla_{\Omega,j}\gamma.

In the case of the E-mode harmonic coefficient, we have to consider the divergence of the angular deflection, thus

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Omega,j}\delta n^{j}=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Omega,j}[a\mathcal{A}^{j}+b\mathcal{B}^{j}-c\mathcal{C}^{j}]

(B.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1-\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{(2+\gamma)}{(1+\gamma)}+\left[\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{(2+\gamma)}{(1+\gamma)}-\frac{1}{2}(1+\gamma)\right]e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}\right]\frac{n_{m}n_{k}e^{\lambda}_{mk}}{1+\gamma}.

Since it is always possible to choose a reference frame where
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
is oriented along the z-axis, we have that
\gamma=\mu
.
Therefore, we obtain the results showed in equation (
B.4
), and the integrals over the angle
\phi
are those reported in equation (
A.26
).
Thus, the only remaining integral to be solved is

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu P_{\ell}^{m=2}(\mu)(1-\mu)\left[1-\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{(2+\mu)}{(1+\mu)}+\left[\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{(2+\mu)}{(1+\mu)}-\frac{1}{2}(1+\mu)\right]e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\mu)}\right]\,.

(B.21)

Using the properties of Legendre polynomials, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu P_{\ell}^{m=2}(\mu)(1-\mu)=

\displaystyle 4(-1)^{\ell},

(B.22)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu P_{\ell}^{m=2}(\mu)(1-\mu)\frac{(2+\mu)}{(1+\mu)}=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu(1-\mu)^{2}(2+\mu)\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[(1-\mu)^{2}(2+\mu)\frac{dP_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu}\right]_{-1}^{1}-\int_{-1}^{1}3[\mu^{2}-1]\frac{dP_{\ell}(\mu)}{d\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\ell(\ell+1)(-1)^{\ell},

(B.23)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu P_{\ell}^{m=2}(\mu)(1-\mu^{2})e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\mu)}=

\displaystyle 2j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})i^{\ell}e^{i\tilde{x}}\left[\frac{(1+\ell)(2+\ell)(2\ell^{2}+6\ell-3)}{(2\ell+3)(2\ell+1)-(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}\right]

\displaystyle-2\frac{\ell(\ell-1)(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}{(2\ell+1)(2\ell+3)}j_{\ell+2}(\tilde{x})i^{\ell}e^{i\tilde{x}}

\displaystyle-2\frac{\ell(\ell-1)(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}{(2\ell-1)(2\ell+1)}j_{\ell-2}(\tilde{x})i^{\ell}e^{i\tilde{x}},

(B.24)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell}^{m=2}(1-\mu)\frac{(2+\mu)}{(1+\mu)}e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\mu)}=

\displaystyle e^{i\tilde{x}}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu P_{\ell}^{m=2}\left(\frac{2}{1+\mu}-\mu\right)e^{i\tilde{x}\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4(-1)^{\ell}i\tilde{x}+2(-1)^{\ell}\ell(\ell+1)-4e^{i\tilde{x}}j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\tilde{x}i^{\ell}[\tilde{x}+i(\ell+1)]

\displaystyle+4\tilde{x}^{2}ii^{\ell}j_{\ell+1}(\tilde{x})e^{i\tilde{x}}+\frac{2\ell(\ell-1)(\ell+1)}{(2\ell+1)}e^{i\tilde{x}}i^{\ell+1}j_{\ell+1}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle+\frac{2\ell(1+\ell)(2+\ell)}{2\ell+1}e^{i\tilde{x}}i^{\ell-1}j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x}).

(B.25)

Moreover, thanks to the properties of the associated Legendre polynomials described in appendix
A
, the calculation for
P_{\ell}^{m=-2}
returns the same results of that of
P_{\ell}^{m=2}
.
In the end, using the recursive relations of the Bessel functions,
j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})=\frac{(2\ell-1)}{\tilde{x}}j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})-j_{\ell-2}(\tilde{x})
, we find

\displaystyle E^{f,r}_{\ell m}=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\left[h^{+}_{f,\hat{\mathbf{p}}}(\delta_{m,2}+\delta_{m,-2})-ih^{\times}_{f,\hat{\mathbf{p}}}(\delta_{m,2}-\delta_{m,-2})\right]

(B.26)

\displaystyle\times\left\{e^{i\tilde{x}}i^{\ell}\left[j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2\tilde{x}}+\tilde{x}\right)-j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left(i\tilde{x}-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell^{2}-1)}{4\tilde{x}^{2}}+\ell\right)\right]\right\}\,.

On the other hand, in the case of B-modes, we have

(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\nabla^{(2)})_{i}\delta n^{i}=\varepsilon_{kij}n^{i}\nabla_{\Omega,j}\delta n^{k},

(B.27)

which implies

B(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\varepsilon_{ikj}n^{k}\nabla_{\Omega,j}\delta n^{i}=\varepsilon_{ikj}n^{k}\nabla_{\Omega,j}[a\mathcal{A}^{i}+b\mathcal{B}^{i}-c\mathcal{C}^{i}]\,.

(B.28)

Using the results obtained above, and observing that the non-symmetric parts of
n^{k}\nabla_{\Omega,j}\mathcal{A}^{i}
and
n^{k}\nabla_{\Omega,j}\mathcal{C}^{i}
vanish, we get

\displaystyle B(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ikj}\left[\frac{n^{k}}{(1+\gamma)}\left(p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}+n_{m}e^{\lambda}_{mi}p_{j}e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}\right)-\frac{i}{\tilde{x}}\frac{[1-e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}]}{(1+\gamma)^{2}}n^{k}(p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}+n_{m}e^{\lambda}_{mi}p_{j})\right],

(B.29)

which can be further simplified by observing that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ikj}\left(p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}+n_{m}e^{\lambda}_{mi}p_{j}e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}\right)=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ikj}p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}+\varepsilon_{jki}n_{m}e^{\lambda}_{mj}p_{i}e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ikj}p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}\left[1-e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}\right],

(B.30)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ikj}(p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}+n_{m}e^{\lambda}_{mi}p_{j})=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ikj}(p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}-n_{m}e^{\lambda}_{mj}p_{i})=0\,,

(B.31)

yielding

\displaystyle B(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\varepsilon_{ikj}\frac{p_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j\ell}n_{\ell}n_{k}}{(1+\gamma)}\left[1-e^{i\tilde{x}(1+\gamma)}\right].

(B.32)

Therefore, in this case, we need to solve the two additional integrals

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu P_{\ell}^{m=2}(\mu)e^{i\tilde{x}\mu}=

\displaystyle 2[e^{i\tilde{x}}+(-1)^{\ell}e^{-i\tilde{x}}]+4\tilde{x}i^{\ell}j_{\ell+1}(\tilde{x})-2(\ell+1)(\ell+2)i^{\ell}j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})

(B.33)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu P_{\ell}^{m=2}(\mu)\mu e^{i\tilde{x}\mu}=

\displaystyle 2[e^{i\tilde{x}}-(-1)^{\ell}e^{-i\tilde{x}}]-(i\tilde{x})4i^{\ell}j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})-\frac{2\ell(\ell-1)(\ell+1)}{(2\ell+1)}i^{\ell+1}j_{\ell+1}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle-\frac{2\ell(1+\ell)(2+\ell)}{1+2\ell}i^{\ell-1}j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})\,.

(B.34)

In the end, the B-mode harmonic coefficients read as

\displaystyle B^{f,r}_{\ell m}=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\left[h^{\times}_{f,\hat{\mathbf{p}}}(\delta_{m,2}+\delta_{m,-2})+ih^{+}_{f,\hat{\mathbf{p}}}(\delta_{m,2}-\delta_{m,-2})\right]

(B.35)

\displaystyle\times(-i)i^{\ell}e^{i\tilde{x}}\left[j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left(\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}i+\frac{\ell(\ell^{2}-1)}{2\tilde{x}}+\tilde{x}\right)+j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})\left(i\tilde{x}-\frac{\ell(1+\ell)}{2}\right)\right]\,.

Parity of the form functions.

Finally, in this small section, we show how these new form factors change under a frequency reversal transformation
f\to-f
, or, more generally,
x\to-x
.
Under such a coordinate change, we have that

\displaystyle F^{E}_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=i^{\ell}e^{i(-\tilde{x})}\left[j_{\ell-1}(-\tilde{x})\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2(-\tilde{x})}+(-\tilde{x})\right)-j_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})\left(i(-\tilde{x})-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell^{2}-1)}{4(-\tilde{x})^{2}}+\ell\right)\right]

(B.36)

\displaystyle=(-i)^{\ell}e^{-i\tilde{x}}\left[j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2\tilde{x}}+\tilde{x}\right)-j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left(-i\tilde{x}-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell^{2}-1)}{4\tilde{x}^{2}}+\ell\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\right]^{*},

\displaystyle F^{B}_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=(-i)i^{\ell}e^{i(-\tilde{x})}\left[j_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})\left(i\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}+\frac{\ell(\ell^{2}-1)}{2(-\tilde{x})}+(-\tilde{x})\right)+j_{\ell-1}(-\tilde{x})\left(i(-\tilde{x})-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=i(-i)^{\ell}e^{-i\tilde{x}}\left[j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\left(-i\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}+\frac{\ell(\ell^{2}-1)}{2\tilde{x}}+\tilde{x}\right)+j_{\ell-1}(\tilde{x})\left(-i\tilde{x}-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\right]^{*},

where we used the
j_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})=(-1)^{\ell}j_{\ell}(\tilde{x})
property of the spherical Bessel functions.

B.2.2
Calculation via recursive relations

The results of the previous sections can also be derived via the recursive relations reported in appendix
A
.
The E-mode harmonic coefficients for the two polarization degrees of freedom read as

\displaystyle E^{f,r,+}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\frac{n^{j}n^{k}e^{+}_{jk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}

(B.37)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}{2}e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}+\frac{i}{2\pi fr}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right)\left(e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}+\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\int dy(1-y)(1-y^{2})\left[1-\frac{1+y}{2}e^{ix(1+y)}+\frac{i}{x}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+y}\right)\left(e^{ix(1+y)}-1\right)\right]\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}}{dy^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}+\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\left[(J_{0}-J_{1}-J_{2}+J_{3})-\frac{1}{2}e^{ix}(K_{0}-2K_{2}+K_{4})\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{i}{x}(2J_{0}-3J_{1}+J_{3})+\frac{i}{x}e^{ix}(2K_{0}-3K_{1}+K_{3})\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}+\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{4}i^{\ell}e^{ix}\left[(16-4ix+x^{2})j_{\ell}+\frac{12+14x^{2}}{x}\partial_{x}j_{\ell}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+(24+2x^{2})\partial^{2}_{x}j_{\ell}+10x\partial^{3}_{x}j_{\ell}+x^{2}\partial^{4}_{x}j_{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle E^{f,r,\times}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ (-i)h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}-\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{4}i^{\ell}e^{ix}\left[(16-4ix+x^{2})j_{\ell}+\frac{12+14x^{2}}{x}\partial_{x}j_{\ell}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+(24+2x^{2})\partial^{2}_{x}j_{\ell}+10x\partial^{3}_{x}j_{\ell}+x^{2}\partial^{4}_{x}j_{\ell}\right].

On the other hand, the B-mode harmonic coefficients are given by

\displaystyle B^{f,r,+}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\frac{\varepsilon_{ijk}p^{i}n^{j}n^{q}e^{+}_{qk}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\left[1-e^{2\pi ifr(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}\right]

(B.38)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ ih_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}-\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\int dy(1-y)(1-y^{2})\left[1-e^{ix(1+y)}\right]\frac{d^{2}P_{\ell}}{dy^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ ih_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}-\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\left[\left(J_{0}-J_{1}-J_{2}+J_{3}\right)-e^{ix}\left(K_{0}-K_{1}-K_{2}+K_{3}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ ih_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}-\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{2}i^{\ell}e^{ix}\left[(2+2ix+x^{2})j_{\ell}+(6i+4x+ix^{2})\partial_{x}j_{\ell}+x(6i+x)\partial^{2}_{x}j_{\ell}+ix^{2}\partial^{3}_{x}j_{\ell}\right],

\displaystyle B^{f,r,\times}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi i}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}+\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{2}i^{\ell}e^{ix}\left[(2+2ix+x^{2})j_{\ell}+(6i+4x+ix^{2})\partial_{x}j_{\ell}+x(6i+x)\partial^{2}_{x}j_{\ell}+ix^{2}\partial^{3}_{x}j_{\ell}\right].

The form factors found in this approach exactly match those of the previous section.
The interested reader can easily prove it by using the spherical Bessel function recursive relations and the fact that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}j_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{x}j_{\ell}-j_{\ell+1},

(B.39)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x}j_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\ell-1)-x^{2}}{x^{2}}j_{\ell}+\frac{2}{x}j_{\ell+1},

\displaystyle\partial^{3}_{x}j_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell-2)\left[\ell(\ell-1)-x^{2}\right]}{x^{3}}j_{\ell}+\frac{x^{2}-\ell(\ell+1)-6}{x^{2}}j_{\ell+1},

\displaystyle\partial^{4}_{x}j_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{4}+\ell(\ell-1)(\ell-2)(\ell-3)-2x^{2}\left[\ell(\ell-1)+4\right]}{x^{4}}j_{\ell}+\frac{4\left[2\ell(\ell+1)-x^{2}+6\right]}{x^{3}}j_{\ell+1}.

B.3
Redshift angular power spectrum

The redshift harmonic coefficients for the two GR polarization degrees of freedom are given by

\displaystyle z^{f,+}_{\mathrm{\ell m}}

\displaystyle=\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\ z^{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})=\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}\frac{n^{j}n^{k}e^{+}_{jk}}{2(1+\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})

(B.40)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})\int d\cos\theta(1-\cos\theta)P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)\int d\phi\cos(2\phi)e^{-im\phi}

\displaystyle=2\pi(-1)^{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}+\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

\displaystyle z^{f,\times}_{\mathrm{\ell m}}

\displaystyle=2\pi(-1)^{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ (-i)h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})(\delta^{K}_{m,2}-\delta^{K}_{m,-2})

using the results of the appendix
A
.
The estimator of the angular power spectrum of
z^{f}_{\mathrm{\ell m}}=z^{f,+}_{\mathrm{\ell m}}+z^{f,\times}_{\mathrm{\ell m}}
is then built as

\hat{C}^{zz}_{\ell}=\frac{\sum_{m}z^{f}_{\mathrm{\ell m}}\left(z^{f^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{\ell m}}\right)^{*}}{2\ell+1}=2\pi\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}d\hat{\mathbf{p}}\left[h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{+}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})+h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{\times}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\right],

(B.41)

and its expectation value is given by

\left\langle\hat{C}^{zz}_{\ell}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})C^{zz}_{\ell}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})4\pi\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}S_{h}(f).

(B.42)

Alternatively, the two-point function in harmonic space can also be directly computed from the angle-dependent two-point function as

\displaystyle\left\langle z^{f}_{\ell m}z^{f^{\prime}}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})S_{h}(f)\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}d\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})\mathrm{HD}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})

(B.43)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})S_{h}(f)\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}d\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}\gamma_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}P_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})S_{h}(f)\int d\hat{\mathbf{n}}d\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}m^{\prime\prime}}\gamma_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}\frac{4\pi}{2\ell^{\prime\prime}+1}Y_{\ell^{\prime\prime}m^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime\prime}m^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\gamma_{\ell}S_{h}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}C^{zz}_{\ell}(f),

where the Legendre coefficients
\gamma_{\ell}
of the Hellings-Downs curve, given by
[
55
]

\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{1}=0,\quad\gamma_{\ell}=(2\ell+1)\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!},

(B.44)

ensure that we recover the result presented above.

B.4
Cross-correlation of observables

So far we provided a form only for the auto-angular power spectrum; however redshift and angular displacement are in principle correlated, since both effects are generated by the same GWB.
Therefore, it is possible to built the estimator for the cross-angular power spectra as

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{Ez}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m}E^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(z^{f^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}}{2\ell+1}

(B.45)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi F^{E}_{\ell}(x)}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}d\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\left[h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{+}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})+h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{\times}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{Bz}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m}B^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(z^{f^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}}{2\ell+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi iF^{B}_{\ell}(x)}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}d\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\left[h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{\times}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})+h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{+}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{EB}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m}E^{f,r}_{\ell m}\left(B^{f^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{\ell m}\right)^{*}}{2\ell+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi iF^{E}_{\ell}(x)\left[F^{B}_{\ell}(x^{\prime})\right]^{*}}{\ell(\ell+1)}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int d\hat{\mathbf{p}}d\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\left[h_{+}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{\times}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})+h_{\times}(f,\hat{\mathbf{p}})h^{*}_{+}(f^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\right],

therefore, their expectation value is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{C}^{Ez}_{\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})C^{Ez}_{\ell}(f,r)=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{D}(f-f^{\prime})\times\left[-\frac{4\pi F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}S_{h}(f)\right],

(B.46)

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{C}^{Bz}_{\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{C}^{EB}_{\ell}\right\rangle=0,

where the absence of correlation between the components
(E,z)
and
B
is due to the statistical independence of the polarization degrees of freedom.
As previously commented, although at this stage
C^{Ez}_{\ell}
is still a complex quantity, once we integrate over frequencies we obtain that

\displaystyle\int dfdf^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{C}^{Ez}_{\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}df\frac{1}{2}F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)S_{h}(f)

(B.47)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\frac{F^{E}_{\ell}(-2\pi fr)+F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)}{2}S_{h}(f)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}\frac{(\ell-2)!}{(\ell+2)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}df\ \mathrm{Re}\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(2\pi fr)\right]S_{h}(f),

using the fact that
\left[F^{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{x})\right]^{*}=F^{E}_{\ell}(-\tilde{x})
, finding in this way a real angular power spectrum.
An alternative fashion to reach the same conclusion is based on the observation that, in the long-distance limit,

z_{\ell m}^{f}=-\frac{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}{2}E_{\ell m}^{f,r\to\infty}.

(B.48)

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{zE}(r)=\langle z_{\ell m}{E_{\ell m}^{f,r}}^{*}\rangle=-\frac{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}{2}\langle{E_{\ell m}^{f,r^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}}{E_{\ell m}^{f,r}}^{*}\rangle=-\frac{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}{2}C_{\ell}^{EE}(r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty).

(B.49)

Since
C_{\ell}^{EE}(r,r^{\prime})
are real angular power spectra, as already argued above, it descends that the same holds for
C_{\ell}^{zE}(r)
.
