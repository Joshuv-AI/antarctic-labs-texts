On C ∗ C^{*} -algebras with Local Lifting Property and Weak Expectation Property ORCID 0000-0002-3091-2049
by

Abstract
We give a new, somewhat simpler, presentation of the author’s recent construction of a non-nuclear C ∗ C^{*} -algebra which has both the local lifting property (LLP) and the weak expectation property (WEP).

Introduction

The main goal of this note is to give a hopefully simpler
proof that there exist non-nuclear
C^{*}
-algebras with
both the local lifting property (LLP) and the weak expectation property (WEP).
(See
[
9
]
for operator spaces with analogous properties).
We will obtain this goal in Theorem
4.5
. While this paper is mostly expository, the new approach produces some “new” results, e.g. in Remarks

3.12
and
3.13
.

We start by an outline of
our main results.

We first introduce some basic terminology and notation.

Let
A,B
be
C^{*}
-algebras and let
E\subset A
be an operator subspace.
Given a
C^{*}
-algebra
D
, a linear map

u:E\to B
will be called
D
-nuclear with constant
c
if

\|Id_{D}\otimes u:D\otimes_{\min}E\to D\otimes_{\max}B\|\leq c.

If this holds with
c=1
we say that
u
is
D
-nuclear.

This terminology is inspired by the well known definition of
nuclear
C^{*}
-algebras:
A
is nuclear if the identity
on
A
is
D
-nuclear for all
D
.

We first need some basic notation.

For any
C^{*}
-algebra
C
we denote

\mathcal{L}(C)=\ell_{\infty}(C)/c_{0}(C).

For short we set
L=\ell_{\infty}(C)
and
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(C)
. Let
Q:L\to\mathcal{L}
denote the quotient map.
For any
x=(x_{n})\in L
we have

\|Q(x)\|=\limsup\nolimits_{n\to\infty}\|x_{n}\|.

Our basic starting point is an isometric
*
-homomorphism

F:C\to\mathcal{L}

together with a completely contractive self-adjoint lifting

f=(f_{n}):C\to L
. Lifting means here as usual
that
F=Qf
and self-adjoint means
f(x^{*})=f(x)^{*}
for all
x\in C
.
The existence of such an
f
is of course a nontrivial assumption,
but it is guaranteed to hold if
C
has the LP.

Incidentally, when
C
is separable, it is open whether
F
always admits
a contractive lifting.

Unfortunately we need to impose a more technical condition,
as follows:

(1.1)

\text{For any f.d.}\ E\subset C\ \ \ \limsup\|{f^{-1}_{n}}_{|f_{n}(E)}\|_{cb}\leq 1.

This means that the coordinates of the lifting
f=(f_{n})
are asymptotically
locally almost completely isometric.

We will associate to
F
two
C^{*}
-algebras
Z\subset L

and
A\subset\mathcal{L}
.
Let
E_{n}\subset C
be f.d.s.a. subspaces
and let
(m(n))
be a sequence of integers.
We set

T_{n}={f_{m(n)}}_{|E_{n-1}}:E_{n-1}\to E_{n},

assuming
\{E_{n}\}
adjusted so that

\forall n\geq 0\qquad T_{n}(E_{n-1})+T_{n}(E_{n-1})T_{n}(E_{n-1})\subset E_{n}.

For any
k>n
let
T_{[k,n]}=T_{k}T_{k-1}\cdots T_{n}:E_{n-1}\to E_{k}
and hence also
T_{[n,n]}=T_{n}
.

Let
Z_{n}\subset L
be the set formed by
all

such that
x_{n}\in E_{n}

and
x_{k}=T_{[k,n+1]}(x_{n})
for all
k>n
. We set

Z=\overline{\cup_{n}Z_{n}}\subset L.

Clearly
x_{k}=0
for all
k\geq N
implies

and hence
c_{0}(C)\subset Z
. Moreover,
it is easy to see that

Z_{n}\subset Z_{n+1}
. If each map

T_{[k,n]}
was the restriction of a
*
-homomorphism it would follow easily that

Z_{n}Z_{n}\subset Z_{n+1}
.
But in our setting the
T_{n}
’s will only be approximately multiplicative
and if they are so at a sufficiently fast rate we still find
that
Z=\overline{\cup Z_{n}}
is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
L
.
We then define the
C^{*}
-algebra
A=Q(Z)
or equivalently

A=Z/c_{0}(C).

Theorem 1.1
.

If
C
has the lifting property (LP in short),
and if (
1.1
) holds, the spaces
(E_{n})
and the sequence
(m(n))
can be selected so that
Z
has the LP and
A
the LLP.

Moreover, if
D
is a separable
C^{*}
-algebra for which
F:C\to\mathcal{L}
is
D
-nuclear, they can be selected so that
in addition
A
is
D
-nuclear.

The proof of this theorem appears as a combination of statements in §
3
.
See Propositions
3.6
(construction of the system),
3.8
(

has the LP),
3.9
(

has the LLP) and Theorem

-nuclearity).

Thus if we can produce an

that is

-nuclear (here
\mathscr{C}=C^{*}(\mathbb{F}_{\infty})
) on a

C
with the lifting property (LP in short) then we will obtain a
C^{*}
-algebra

with both WEP and LLP. Indeed, in Kirchberg’s
viewpoint

has WEP (resp. the LLP) iff

is
\mathscr{C}
-nuclear (resp.
\mathscr{B}
-nuclear, where

). In addition if

is non-exact and we can arrange

)) for the finite dimensional obstructions to the exactness of

The obvious question is then: for

or

-nuclear

This is provided by the setting of cone algebras, see §

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
C
be a
C^{*}
-algebra with LP.
For any f.d. subspace
E\subset C
there is
t^{E}
in the unit ball
of
\mathscr{C}\otimes_{\max}E
such that
for any other
t
in the same unit ball there is a unital
*
-homomorphism

\pi_{t}:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}
such that
[\pi_{t}\otimes Id_{E}](t^{E})=t
.

Let
C,B
be
C^{*}
-algebras and let
E\subset C
and
F\subset B
be operator subspaces.
We denote by
E\otimes F
the algebraic tensor product.
We denote (somewhat abusively) by
E\otimes_{\max}F

the closure of
E\otimes F
in
C\otimes_{\max}B
.
We should emphasize that
E\otimes_{\max}F
depends strongly
on the ambient
C^{*}
-algebras
C,B
.
We
denote by
MB(E,F)
the set of (linear ) maps
u:E\to F

such that
Id_{\mathscr{C}}\otimes u
is bounded from
\mathscr{C}\otimes E
equipped
with the norm induced by
\mathscr{C}\otimes_{\max}C
to
\mathscr{C}\otimes F

equipped
with the norm induced by
\mathscr{C}\otimes_{\max}B
.
When this holds, we
define

\|u\|_{mb}=\|Id_{\mathscr{C}}\otimes u:\mathscr{C}\otimes_{\max}E\to\mathscr{C}\otimes_{\max}F\|,

and we equip the space
MB(E,F)
with this norm.

If
C
has the LP, it is easy to check with Proposition
1.2
that

(1.2)

\|u\|_{mb}=\|[Id_{\mathscr{C}}\otimes u](t^{E})\|_{\max}.

See Lemma
3.2
for full details.

Since any
C^{*}
-algebra
D
is a quotient of the
C^{*}
-algebra of some free group
we have for any such

Some abbreviations:
We use c.b. (resp. c.c.) for completely bounded
(resp. completely contractive), c.p. (resp. u.c.p.) for completely positive (resp. unital completely positive), and c.i. for
completely isometric. Note that a “contractive” operator or a “contraction”
is an operator of norm less or equal to one. Similarly “completely contractive” or “complete contraction” means the
cb-norm is less or equal to one. We also abbreviate self-adjoint by s.a.

Some background:

A
C^{*}
-algebra
C
has the lifting property (LP) if any c.p.c.c. map into a quotient
C^{*}
-algebra admits a c.p.c.c. lifting. It is known that this holds for
A
if and only if it does for the unitization of
A
.
A unital
C^{*}
-algebra
C
has the lifting property (LP) if and only if any u.c.p. map into a quotient
C^{*}
-algebra admits a u.c.p. lifting.
Nuclear
C^{*}
-algebras have the LP (due to Choi-Effros)
but also
\mathscr{C}=C^{*}(\mathbb{F}_{\infty})
has the LP (due to Kirchberg). See
[
10
]
and
[
3
]

for more background, precise references and more recent results on the LP.

2
Inductive systems

For lack of valuable examples, we cannot
use for our lifting
f
a bona fide
*
-homomorphism,
but using instead an approximate
*
-homomorphism
(or what we call an
\varepsilon
-morphism) in §
4

we will find examples in the framework of cone algebras
to which Theorem
1.1
can be applied
to produce
non-nuclear
C^{*}
-algebras with the WEP
and the LLP. In this section we show by rather elementary arguments how
our inductive limits of linear spaces can lead to
C^{*}
-algebras.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
C,C_{1}
be
C^{*}
-algebras with s.a. subspaces
E\subset C
and
E_{1}\subset C_{1}
. Let
\varepsilon>0

A linear map
{\psi}:E\to E_{1}

(we will restrict in (iii) to the s.a. case for simplicity)
will be called an
\varepsilon
-morphism
if

(i)

\|{\psi}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
,

(ii)

for any
x,y\in\mathcal{E}
with
xy\in E
we have

\|{\psi}(xy)-{\psi}(x){\psi}(y)\|\leq\varepsilon\|x\|\|y\|,

(iii)

{\psi}
is self-adjoint i.e. for any
x\in E
we have
{\psi}(x^{*})={\psi}(x)^{*}
.

Obviously the restriction to
E
of any
*
-homomorphism from
C
to
C_{1}

is an
\varepsilon
-morphism for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
E_{n}\subset C_{n}
be s.a. subspaces of
C^{*}
-algebras
(n\geq 0)
.
Let
{\psi}_{n}:E_{n-1}\to E_{n}
be an
\varepsilon_{n}
-morphism.
Assuming

{\psi}_{1}(E_{0}){\psi}_{1}(E_{0})\subset E_{1}
,
the composition

{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}:E_{0}\to E_{2}

has norm
\leq(1+\varepsilon_{1})(1+\varepsilon_{2})

and hence satisfies (i) and (ii) with
\varepsilon=\delta_{2}
for

(2.1)

\delta_{2}=(1+\varepsilon_{2})\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}(1+\varepsilon_{1})^{2}.

This is immediate by the triangle inequality
since

{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}(xy)-{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}(x){\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}(y)=[{\psi}_{2}({\psi}_{1}(xy)-{\psi}_{1}(x){\psi}_{1}(y))]+[{\psi}_{2}({\psi}_{1}(x){\psi}_{1}(y))-{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}(x){\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}(y)].

More generally, assuming
{\psi}_{n}(E_{n-1}){\psi}_{n}(E_{n-1})\subset E_{n}
for all
n\geq 1

then
{\psi}_{n}\cdots{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}:E_{0}\to E_{n}
has norm

\leq(1+\varepsilon_{n})\cdots(1+\varepsilon_{2})(1+\varepsilon_{1})
.
Let
\pi_{n}=(1+\varepsilon_{n})\cdots(1+\varepsilon_{1})
.
Repeating the argument for (
2.1
) shows that
{\psi}_{n}\cdots{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}

satisfies (ii) with
\varepsilon=\delta_{n}
for

\delta_{n}
satisfying for all
n\geq 2
:

\delta_{n}=(1+\varepsilon_{n})\delta_{n-1}+\varepsilon_{n}\pi_{n-1}^{2}.

Thus the number
\delta_{n}^{\prime}=\pi_{n}^{-1}\delta_{n}

satisfies

\delta^{\prime}_{n}=\delta^{\prime}_{n-1}+\varepsilon_{n}\pi_{n-1}^{2}/\pi_{n}\leq\delta^{\prime}_{n-1}+\varepsilon_{n}\pi_{n-1}.

Thus if we assume that
\sum\varepsilon_{n}<\infty
so that
\sup_{n}\pi_{n}=c<\infty
we find for all
n\geq 2
:

\delta_{n}^{\prime}\leq\delta_{1}^{\prime}+c\sum\nolimits_{2}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}

and hence for any
n\geq 1
(note
\delta_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}\geq\delta_{1}^{\prime}
)

(2.2)

\delta_{n}\leq\pi_{n}(\delta_{1}^{\prime}+c\sum\nolimits_{2}^{n}\varepsilon_{k})\leq c\varepsilon_{1}+c^{2}\sum\nolimits_{2}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}.

In conclusion, we have

(2.3)

\delta_{n}\leq e^{2}\sum\nolimits_{1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k},

and hence
{\psi}_{n}\cdots{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}
satisfies (ii) with
\varepsilon=e^{2}\sum\nolimits_{1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}
.
In addition, we observe that if
\sum\varepsilon_{k}\leq 1
then

\pi_{n}\leq\exp{\sum\varepsilon_{k}}\leq 1+(e-1)\sum\varepsilon_{k}
, and hence
{\psi}_{n}\cdots{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}
satisfies (i) with
\varepsilon=e^{2}\sum\nolimits_{1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}
.

Thus we have proved the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\psi_{k}:E_{k-1}\to E_{k}
be
\varepsilon_{k}
-morphisms with
\sum\varepsilon_{k}\leq 1
.
The composition
{\psi}_{n}\cdots{\psi}_{2}{\psi}_{1}:E_{0}\to E_{n}
is an
\varepsilon
-morphism
with
\varepsilon=e^{2}\sum\nolimits_{1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
C_{n}
be
C^{*}
-algebras.
By an admissible inductive system of o.s.
we mean a sequence
(E_{n},T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
where, for each
n
,
E_{n}\subset C_{n}

is a f.d.s.a. operator subspace,
the map

T_{n}:E_{n-1}\to E_{n}
is a
\varepsilon_{n}
-morphism such that
T_{n}(E_{n-1})T_{n}(E_{n-1})\subset E_{n}

and
(\varepsilon_{n})
is such that

\sum\varepsilon_{n}\leq 1.

Let
L=\ell_{\infty}(\{C_{n}\})
,
\mathcal{I}_{0}=c_{0}(\{C_{n}\})

and
\mathcal{L}=L/\mathcal{I}_{0}
. Let
Q:L\to\mathcal{L}
be the quotient map.

Recall that for any
x\in\mathcal{L}

admitting
y=(y_{n})\in L
as a lift (i.e.
Q(y)=x
)
we have

(2.4)

\|x\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\limsup\|y_{n}\|.

Let
\theta_{n}:E_{n}\to L
be
the mapping defined for
n\geq 0
by

(2.5)

\forall x\in E_{n}\quad\theta_{n}(x)=(0,\cdots,0,x,T_{n+1}(x),T_{n+2}T_{n+1}(x),T_{n+3}T_{n+2}T_{n+1}(x),\cdots)

where

stands at the place of index
n
. The summability of
(\varepsilon_{n})
ensures that

is a bounded sequence.

Note that

(2.6)

\forall x\in E_{n}\qquad\theta_{n}(x)-\theta_{n+1}T_{n+1}(x)\in c_{0}(C),

and hence
Q\theta_{n}=Q\theta_{n+1}T_{n+1}
.
Let

Z_{n}=C_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n-1}\oplus\{(x,T_{n+1}(x),T_{n+2}T_{n+1}(x),T_{n+3}T_{n+2}T_{n+1}(x),\cdots)\mid x\in E_{n}\}\subset L

or equivalently

(2.7)

Z_{n}=[C_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n-1}\oplus 0\oplus 0\oplus\cdots]+\theta_{n}(E_{n})\subset L

and

A_{n}=Q(Z_{n}).

Note that
(Z_{n})
and
(A_{n})
are non decreasing sequences.
We set

Z=\overline{\cup Z_{n}}\subset L\text{ and }A=\overline{\cup A_{n}}\subset\mathcal{L}.

Proposition 2.5
.

For any admissible inductive system as above,
A
and
Z
are
C^{*}
-subalgebras.

Proof.

Note that the infinite product

\prod_{j\geq 1}(1+\varepsilon_{j})
converges.
We define
\eta_{n}>0
by the equality

1+\eta_{n}=\prod\nolimits_{j\geq n}(1+\varepsilon_{j})

so that
\eta_{n}\to 0
.

A priori

Z=\overline{\cup Z_{n}}\subset{L}.

is a s.a. subspace.
To check that
Z
is actually a subalgebra of
{L}

we will show that for all
n
and all
a,b
in
Z_{n}
the product
ab
lies in
Z
.

For
x\in E_{n}
let

(2.8)

w_{n}(x)=(x,T_{n+1}(x),T_{n+2}T_{n+1}(x),\cdots)

so that

(2.9)

\theta_{n}(x)=(0,\cdots,0,w_{n}(x))

By Lemma
2.3
, the maps
w_{n}
and
\theta_{n}
are
\varepsilon
-morphisms
for
\varepsilon=e^{2}\sum_{k>n}\varepsilon_{k}
.

Note that a typical element

(resp.

) of

is of the form
a=(a_{0},\cdots,a_{n-1},w_{n}(a_{n}))

(resp.
b=(b_{0},\cdots,b_{n-1},w_{n}(b_{n}))
.
Then we also have

(resp.

Note

.
Thus

d(ab,Z_{n+1})\leq\|w_{n+1}(T_{n+1}(a_{n}))w_{n+1}(T_{n+1}(b_{n}))-w_{n+1}(T_{n+1}(a_{n})T_{n+1}(b_{n}))\|

and hence since

and

d(ab,Z_{n+1})\leq e^{2}(\sum\nolimits_{k>n+1}\varepsilon_{k})\|T_{n+1}(a_{n})\|\|T_{n+1}(b_{n})\|\leq e^{2}(\sum\nolimits_{k>n+1}\varepsilon_{k})(1+\varepsilon_{n+1})^{2}\|a\|\|b\|.

But now since

for any

, this also implies

\forall a,b\in Z_{n}\quad d(ab,Z_{n+m+1})\leq e^{2}(\sum\nolimits_{k>n+m}\varepsilon_{k})(1+\varepsilon_{n+m+1})^{2}\|a\|\|b\|\to 0,

when

and hence

.

Clearly the same conclusion holds for any

, so that

(which, as we already noticed, is s.a.) is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra
of
{L}
. Since

is closed, it follows
that

is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The algebra
Z
consists of all sequences
(z_{n})
such that
for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
N
and
x\in E_{N}

such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|z_{n}-\theta_{N}(x)_{n}\|\leq\varepsilon
.

We recall that we say that an o.s.
X
locally embeds in another one

if for any
\varepsilon>0
and any f.d. subspace
E\subset X

there is a subspace
F\subset C
and an isomorphism

such that
\|w\|_{cb}\|w^{-1}\|_{cb}\leq 1+\varepsilon
. Moreover
we set

d_{cb}(E,F)=\inf\{\|w\|_{cb}\|w^{-1}\|_{cb}\}

where the infimum runs over all possible such

.

More generally, we say that
X
locally embeds in a sequence
\{C_{n}\}
of
C^{*}
-algebras
if for any
\varepsilon>0
and for any

there is for some
n
a subspace
F\subset C_{n}

such that
d_{cb}(E,F)\leq 1+\varepsilon
.

We will need to discuss some additional properties:

(2.10)

\|{T_{n}}\|_{cb}\leq 1+\varepsilon_{n}.

and hence

(2.11)

\|{T_{[k,n]}}\|_{cb}\leq\prod\nolimits_{n\leq j\leq k}(1+\varepsilon_{j})\leq 1+\eta_{n}.

We will also need

(2.12)

\|{T_{n}^{-1}}_{|{T_{n}(E_{n-1})}}\|_{cb}\leq 1+\varepsilon_{n}.

and hence

(2.13)

\|{T_{[k,n]}^{-1}}_{|{T_{[k,n]}(E_{n-1})}}\|_{cb}\leq\prod\nolimits_{n\leq j\leq k}(1+\varepsilon_{j})\leq 1+\eta_{n}.

Proposition 2.7
.

Assume
that the inductive system satisfies (

) and (

),
then
A
locally embeds in
\{C_{n}\}
. In fact for all

(2.14)

d_{cb}(E_{n},A_{n})\leq(1+\eta_{n+1})^{2}.

Proof.

By perturbation it clearly suffices to show that

locally embeds in
C
for all

. Fix

.
Recall that

denotes the quotient map.
Consider the map

and let

.
We have

\|\alpha_{n}\|_{cb}\leq\|\theta_{n}\|_{cb}\leq\max\{1,\sup\nolimits_{k>n}\|T_{[k,n+1]}\|_{cb}\}
and hence by (

)

\|\alpha_{n}\|_{cb}\leq 1+\eta_{n+1}
.
To estimate

we will use (

Let

The latter remains true for any

and any fixed

This implies

when

\{C_{n}\}

This clearly implies that the pair

has the LLP.

As in the proof of Proposition

let

On the one hand by

4
Cone algebras

Proposition
2.5

tells us how to associate a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
to a
*
-embedding
F:C\to\mathcal{L}(C)
, equipped with
a completely contractive lifting
f:C\to L(C)
.
By Theorem
3.11
if
f

satisfies (
1.1
), if

C
has the LP and if
F
is
\mathscr{C}
-nuclear, the algebra
A
has WEP and LLP. To valuably apply this result, we need to exhibit
F
’s with the required properties. Their existence is not so immediate but cone algebras
crucially provide us with the missing ingredient.

Let
C_{0}=C((0,1])
and
C_{1}=C([0,1])
. For any
C^{*}
-algebra
A
,
we denote by
C_{0}(A)=C_{0}\otimes_{\min}A
the so-called cone algebra
of
A
and by
C(A)
its unitization. (Warning:
C(A)\not=C_{1}\otimes_{\min}A
!)

When dealing with a bounded mapping
u:A\to B
between
C^{*}
-algebras
(or operator spaces) we will denote by

u_{0}:C_{0}(A)\to C_{0}(B)

the bounded map extending
Id_{C_{0}}\otimes u
.

The algebra
C
considered throughout §
3

will now be replaced by the cone algebra
C_{0}({\bf C})
or
C({\bf C})
.
We use the letter
{\bf C}
for a
C^{*}
-algebra to avoid a notational confusion.

Let
q:{\bf C}\to B
be a surjective
*
-homomorphism and let
\mathcal{I}=\ker(q)
.
We will use freely the natural identifications

B\simeq{\bf C}/\mathcal{I}\quad\text{ and }\quad C_{0}(B)\simeq C_{0}({\bf C}/\mathcal{I})\simeq C_{0}({\bf C})/C_{0}(\mathcal{I}),\quad C(B)\simeq C({\bf C}/\mathcal{I})\simeq C({\bf C})/C_{0}(\mathcal{I}).

Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a quasicentral approximate unit in
\mathcal{I}
.
Our construction is simpler if there is such a

(\sigma_{n})
(and hence
(1-\sigma_{n})
) formed of projections.
Then the mappings
x\mapsto(1-\sigma_{n})x
are approximatively multiplicative. In general this does not exist. However,
if we replace
{\bf C},B,q
by
C_{0}({\bf C}),C_{0}(B),q_{0}
then it does exist
and the construction of the preceding section can be applied.

This trick of passing to cone algebras is
closely related to Kirchberg’s
[
5
, §5]
,
but we learnt it from
[
1
, Lemma 13.4.4]
. A similar idea already
appears in
[
2
, Lemma 10]
for the suspension algebra in the context of
approximatively multiplicative families indexed by a continuous parameter in
(0,\infty)
, but our goals seem unrelated.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
q:{\bf C}\to B
be a surjective
*
-homomorphism between
C^{*}
-algebras, with separable kernel
\mathcal{I}
.
There is a pointwise bounded sequence of maps

\rho_{n}:C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}\to C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
that induces in the limit a
*
-homomorphism

\rho_{\infty}:C_{0}({\bf C})\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}({\bf C}))

that vanishes on
C_{0}(\mathcal{I})
and for which the
associated map

\rho_{[\infty]}:C_{0}({\bf C})/C_{0}(\mathcal{I})\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}({\bf C}))

(which satisfies
\rho_{[\infty]}q_{0}=\rho_{\infty}
) is an isometric
*
-homomorphism.

Moreover, we have
q_{0}\rho_{n}(x)=q_{0}(x)
for all
x\in C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
and all
n
.

We could refer to the proof of Lemma 6.1 in
[
8
]
(itself based on
[
1
, Lemma 13.4.4]
),
but we give the details for the reader’s convenience.

Proof.

Recall
\mathcal{I}=\ker(q)
.
Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a net forming a quasicentral approximate unit of
\mathcal{I}
.
This means
\sigma_{n}\geq 0
,
\|\sigma_{n}\|\leq 1
,

\|\sigma_{n}x-x\|\to 0
for any
x\in\mathcal{I}
and

\|\sigma_{n}c-c\sigma_{n}\|\to 0
for any
c\in{\bf C}
.
We identify
C_{0}({\bf C})=C_{0}\otimes_{\min}{\bf C}
with the set of
{\bf C}
valued functions

f:[0,1]\to{\bf C}
such that
f(0)=0
.
The set of polynomials

\mathcal{P}_{0}={\rm span}[t^{m}\mid m>0]
is dense in
C_{0}
.
Let
\rho_{n}:C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}\to C_{0}({\bf C})
be the map taking

t\mapsto f(t)c
(
f\in C_{0},c\in{\bf C}
) to

t\mapsto f(t(1-\sigma_{n}))c
. For instance (monomials)

\rho_{n}
takes
t\mapsto t^{m}c
to

t\mapsto t^{m}(1-\sigma_{n})^{m}c
.
Note
\|1-\sigma_{n}\|\leq 1
therefore for any
f\in C_{0}
the function

t\mapsto f(t(1-\sigma_{n}))
is in
C_{0}({\bf C})

with norm
\leq\|f\|_{C_{0}}
. This shows that

\sup_{n}\|\rho_{n}(y)\|<\infty
for any
y\in C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
, so that
(\rho_{n})
defines
a map
\rho:C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}\to\ell_{\infty}(C_{0}({\bf C}))
.

We should warn the reader that a priori we do not know
that
\rho
is bounded on
C_{0}\otimes_{\min}{\bf C}=C_{0}({\bf C})
, but we will show that it becomes contractive after composition with the quotient map

Q:\ell_{\infty}(C_{0}({\bf C}))\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}({\bf C}))
.

Since
\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{I}

we have for all
x\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
(and hence all
x\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
)

(4.1)

q_{0}\rho_{n}(x)=q_{0}(x).

Indeed,
(
4.1
) clearly holds
in the case
x=t^{m}\otimes c
and hence it holds by density
for any
x\in C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
.

Since
\sigma_{n}
is quasicentral we have

\forall x,y\in C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}\quad\limsup\nolimits_{n}\|\rho_{n}(xy)-\rho_{n}(x)\rho_{n}(y)\|=0.

Indeed this reduces to the case of monomials which is obvious,
and also

\limsup\nolimits_{n}\|\rho_{n}(x)^{*}-\rho_{n}(x^{*})\|=0.

It follows that after composing
\rho
by the quotient map

Q:\ell_{\infty}(C_{0}({\bf C}))\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}({\bf C}))
we obtain
a map

\rho_{\infty}:C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}({\bf C}))
which
is a
*
-homomorphism, such that
\rho_{\infty}=Q\rho
on
C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
.
Since
C_{0}
is nuclear, this extends to a
*
-homomorphism

\rho_{\infty}:C_{0}({\bf C})\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}({\bf C}))
defined on the whole of

C_{0}({\bf C})
with
\|\rho_{\infty}\|\leq 1
.

We have

(4.2)

\forall f\in C_{0}\otimes\mathcal{I}\quad\limsup\nolimits_{n}\|\rho_{n}(f)\|=0,

and hence
\rho_{\infty}(C_{0}\otimes\mathcal{I})=Q\rho(C_{0}\otimes\mathcal{I})=\{0\}
.
Therefore, after passing to the quotient by
C_{0}(\mathcal{I})\subset\ker(\rho_{\infty})
, we derive from
\rho_{\infty}

a (contractive)
*
-homomorphism

\rho_{[\infty]}:C_{0}({\bf C})/C_{0}(\mathcal{I})\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}({\bf C})).

We have clearly
\rho_{[\infty]}q_{0}=Q\rho
on
C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
.
To check that
\rho_{[\infty]}
is isometric it suffices to show that
for any
x\in C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
we have

\|\rho_{[\infty]}q_{0}(x)\|\geq\|q_{0}(x)\|.

By (
4.1
)
we have

(4.3)

\forall x\in C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}\ \forall n\qquad\|q_{0}(x)\|\leq\|\rho_{n}(x)\|.

This implies

\|q_{0}(x)\|\leq\limsup\|\rho_{n}(x)\|=\|Q\rho(x)\|=\|\rho_{[\infty]}q_{0}(x)\|.

Thus
\rho_{[\infty]}
is isometric. ∎

1.1
) holds.

Proof.

Let
V\subset\mathscr{C}
be a f.d. subspace and let

\Delta=\mathcal{P}_{N}\otimes V\subset C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
where

\mathcal{P}_{N}={\rm span}[t,\cdots,t^{N}]
. Recall

jV\subset B={\bf C}/\mathcal{I}
.
Let
\psi:jV\to{\bf C}
be any linear lifting, i.e. we have

q\psi j(x)=j(x)
for any
x\in V
, and hence

q_{0}\psi_{0}j_{0}(x)=j_{0}(x)
for any
x\in\Delta
. Let

g_{n}=\rho_{n}\psi_{0}j_{0}:\Delta\to C_{0}({\bf C})
(actually
g_{n}
takes values
in
\mathcal{P}_{N}\otimes{\bf C}
). We first claim that
g_{n}
is asymptotic
to
{f_{n}}_{|\Delta}
by which we mean

\forall x\in\Delta\qquad\|g_{n}(x)-f_{n}(x)\|\to 0.

Indeed, we have

Q(f(x))=F(x)=\rho_{[\infty]}j_{0}(x)

and also
Q(g(x))=Q\rho\psi_{0}j_{0}(x)=\rho_{\infty}\psi_{0}j_{0}(x)=\rho_{[\infty]}q_{0}\psi_{0}j_{0}(x)=\rho_{[\infty]}j_{0}(x)
. Thus

Q(f(x))=Q(g(x))
which is equivalent to our first claim.

Our second claim is that

\forall n\qquad\|{g^{-1}_{n}}_{|g_{n}(\Delta)}\|_{cb}\leq 1.

Indeed, since (see Lemma
4.1
)
q_{0}\rho_{n}=q_{0}
on
C_{0}\otimes{\bf C}
we have
q_{0}g_{n}=q_{0}\psi_{0}j_{0}=j_{0}
, from which we derive

\|x\|=\|j_{0}(x)\|\leq\|g_{n}(x)\|
.
Equivalently
\|{g^{-1}_{n}}_{|g_{n}(\Delta)}\|\leq 1.

Similarly since
q_{0}
is c.c. and
j_{0}
completely isometric
we obtain our second claim.

To conclude, an elementary perturbation argument
shows that the two claims together with the complete contractivity of the
f_{n}
’s imply

(4.4)

\limsup\|{f^{-1}_{n}}_{|f_{n}(E)}\|_{cb}\leq 1.

Indeed, by
[
6
, Lemma 2.13.2 p. 69]
for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
E\subset C_{0}({\bf C})
there are

{\Delta}\subset{\mathcal{P}}\otimes{\bf C}
and a complete isomorphism
h:C_{0}({\bf C})\to C_{0}({\bf C})
such that

h({\Delta})=E
and
\|h-Id\|_{cb}<\varepsilon
and also for convenience
\|h^{-1}-Id\|_{cb}<\varepsilon
.
Since
{\Delta}
is f.d.
\limsup\|(f_{n}-g_{n})_{|{\Delta}}\|_{cb}=0
by the first claim. By the two claims for any
0<\varepsilon<1
there is

N=N_{\varepsilon}
such that
\sup\nolimits_{n\geq N}\|(f_{n}-g_{n})_{|{\Delta}}\|_{cb}\leq\varepsilon
and also

\|{g^{-1}_{n}}_{|g_{n}({\Delta})}\|_{cb}\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}
.
Fix
n\geq N
.
Let
x\in E
with
\|x\|=1
.
Let
y=h^{-1}(x)\in{\Delta}
.
Then
1-\varepsilon\leq\|y\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
.
We have
\|g_{n}(y)\|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|y\|
and
\|f_{n}(y)-f_{n}(x)\|\leq\|y-x\|\leq\varepsilon
.
It follows

\|f_{n}(x)\|\geq\|f_{n}(y)\|-\varepsilon\geq\|g_{n}(y)\|-\varepsilon\|y\|-\varepsilon\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|y\|-\varepsilon\|y\|-\varepsilon\geq(1-2\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon.

This shows that
\|{f^{-1}_{n}}_{|f_{n}(E)}\|\leq[(1-2\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon]^{-1}
.

An entirely similar reasoning with

x
in the unit sphere of (say)
\mathscr{B}\otimes_{\min}E
shows the same upper bound
for
\|{f^{-1}_{n}}_{|f_{n}(E)}\|_{cb}
. Since this holds for any
n\geq N
and any
\varepsilon>0
(
4.4
) alias (
1.1
) follows.
∎

We can now apply the results of the preceding section to the case when
C=C_{0}(\mathscr{C})
or
C=C(\mathscr{C})
:
there is clearly an embedding
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathscr{B}
, and it is easy to show that there is a separable
C^{*}
-subalgebra
B\subset\mathscr{B}

with WEP that contains
\mathscr{C}
. Let
j:\mathscr{C}\to B
be the inclusion map.
Since
B
is separable we also have a surjective
*
-homomorphism
q:\mathscr{C}\to B
, whence
F=\rho_{[\infty]}j_{0}:C_{0}(\mathscr{C})\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}(\mathscr{C}))
.
The passage to the cone algebras is harmless:

C_{0}(\mathscr{C})
has the LP and
C_{0}(B)
the WEP, i.e. is
\mathscr{C}
-nuclear.
A fortiori the map
F
is
\mathscr{C}
-nuclear.
We are thus in a position to apply Theorem
3.11
.

Theorem 4.5
.

In the situation just described
there is an inductive system associated to the map
F:C_{0}(\mathscr{C})\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}(\mathscr{C}))

for which the associated
C^{*}
-algebra
A
has the WEP and the LLP
but is not nuclear. Moreover,
A
locally embeds in
\mathscr{C}
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.11

with
C=C_{0}(\mathscr{C})
to the map
F=\rho_{[\infty]}j_{0}:C_{0}(\mathscr{C})\to\mathcal{L}(C_{0}(\mathscr{C}))

derived above from the coexistence of
j
and
q
(or of
j_{0}
and
q_{0}
) on
\mathscr{C}
.
By Lemma
4.4
, the condition (
1.1
) holds. By Proposition
2.7
the algebra

locally embeds in
C_{0}(\mathscr{C})
which itself
locally embeds in
\mathscr{C}
(see Remark
2.8
). By Proposition
3.9

has the LLP.
We use
D=\mathscr{C}
in Theorem
3.11
. Then
A

\mathscr{C}
-nuclear which means it has the WEP.

Let us now show how to make sure that
A
is
not

For that purpose we choose for

a suitable subspace of

for instance we can select

-tuple

(How to ensure that

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A
be a
C^{*}
-algebra that locally embeds in
\mathscr{C}
.
Then
A
has the LLP if and only
A
is
j
-nuclear,
where
j:\mathscr{C}\to B
is any given
*
-homomorphism from
\mathscr{C}
to a

C^{*}
-algebra
B
with WEP.

Proof.

The only if part is obvious. To show the if part, assume that

A
is
j
-nuclear.
Let
u:A\to C/\mathcal{I}
be a c.p.c.c. map.
Since
Id_{A}
is
j
-nuclear, the map
j\otimes u

is a contraction from
\mathscr{C}\otimes_{\min}A\to B\otimes_{\max}C/\mathcal{I}
.
Let
E\subset A
be a f.d. subspace.
Let
t\in E^{*}\otimes A

be the tensor associated to the inclusion map
E\to A
.
We have
\|t\|_{\min}=1
. Recall that
E^{*}
always locally embeds in
\mathscr{C}
when
E
does (see
[
6
, Lem. 21.10 p. 339]
).
Therefore there is a c.i. embedding

k:E^{*}\to\mathscr{C}
. We have
[k\otimes Id_{A}](t)\in\mathscr{C}\otimes A
with
\|[k\otimes Id_{A}](t)\|_{\min}=1
.
After composing with
j
, we find

\|[jk\otimes Id_{A}](t)\|_{\max}\leq 1
, and since c.p.c.c. maps between
C^{*}
-algebras preserve the max norm,
we have
\|[jk\otimes u](t)\|_{\max}\leq 1
.
Here
t^{\prime}=[jk\otimes u](t)\in jk(E^{*})\otimes_{\max}C/\mathcal{I}\subset B\otimes_{\max}C/\mathcal{I}
.
By the projectivity of the max-tensor product,
there is an element
s
in the unit ball of
jk(E^{*})\otimes_{\max}C\subset B\otimes_{\max}C
lifting
t^{\prime}
, meaning,
denoting by
q:C\to C/\mathcal{I}
the quotient map,
that
t^{\prime}=[Id\otimes q](s)
. A fortiori
\|s\|_{\min}\leq 1
.
Since
jk
is c.i. we recover from
s
an element
\hat{t}
in the unit ball of
E^{*}\otimes_{\min}C
, such that
[Id\otimes q](\hat{t})=t
. Let
v:E\to C
be the linear map associated to
\hat{t}
.
Then
v
lifts the inclusion
u_{|E}
and
\|v\|_{cb}=1
.
This yields the LLP for
A
.
∎
