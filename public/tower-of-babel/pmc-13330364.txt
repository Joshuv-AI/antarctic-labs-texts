Scaffold-based evaluation metrics for fair comparison of molecular generators
Authors: Valeriia Fil, Remco L Van Den Broek, Martin Šícho, Ivan Čmelo, M Isabel Agea, Willem Jespers, Gerard J P Van Westen, Daniel Svozil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular generators enable the exploration of chemical space to identify novel compounds with desirable properties. However, assessing their performance remains challenging due to the structural diversity and volume of the generated molecules. Commonly used evaluation metrics, focusing on chemical validity and novelty, do not fully align with the primary goal of molecular generation: the discovery of new biologically active compounds. To address this limitation, we introduce scaffold-based metrics that enable a fair comparison by evaluating a generator’s ability to recover biologically relevant scaffolds absent from the input set. We applied the scaffold Recovery Score (RS), SEt scaffold Diversity (SED), and Absolute SEt scaffold Recall (ASER) metrics to compare several molecular generators, including Molpher, DrugEx, REINVENT, and Graph-based genetic algorithm. The proposed scaffold-based metrics provide a realistic framework for evaluating and optimizing molecular generators for their practical use in drug discovery scenarios, particularly in the design of focused virtual chemical libraries. The metrics are available as open-source in a GitHub repository at https://github.com/filvaleriia/scaffold-based-metrics. Introduction

Organic chemical space is defined as the virtual space that includes all possible organic compounds that could potentially exist [1]. The number of small molecules satisfying Lipinski’s rule-of-five criteria [2] varies between 1023 and 1033, while estimates for the broader chemical space can reach as high as 10180 [3]. Given this enormous size, only a minute fraction of organic chemical space is currently accessible in practice: existing chemical libraries and modern make-on-demand catalogs together comprise tens of billions (≈1010–1011) of known or readily synthesizable molecules [3–5]. Therefore, sufficiently sampling this accessible region of chemical space remains a challenge for de novo drug design [6].

Chemical space exploration in the context of de novo drug design generally involves two main tasks: hit identification, which focuses on finding promising initial molecules, and lead optimization, which aims to improve their properties. In both cases, the goal is to find molecules that meet multiple important criteria at the same time-such as binding affinity to the biological target, synthetic accessibility, and low toxicity [7]. To support this process, de novo molecular generators have been developed, some of which have already contributed to important advances in drug discovery by yielding viable clinical candidate compounds [8–10].

A wide range of such tools is available [11], including Molpher [12], DrugEx [13–16], REINVENT [17], and Graph-based genetic algorithm (GB_GA) [18]. Molecular generators can be broadly classified into two main categories: deep generative models [11] and search algorithms [19] (e.g. genetic algorithms (GAs), swarm optimization, Monte Carlo tree search or hill climb) [20].

In deep generative modeling [11], various types of neural networks are employed to generate novel molecular structures with desired properties. These include recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [14, 16], autoencoders (AEs) [21, 22], graph transformers (GTs) [15], generative adversarial networks (GANs) [23–25], and graph neural networks (GNNs) [26]. The generative model is trained to learn the underlying distribution of the training set, enabling it to generate new molecules that are close neighbors of the structural features in training data in chemical space. When combined with reinforcement learning (RL), the generation process can be further directed toward molecules exhibiting desirable properties such as affinity towards a specific pharmacological target. Such properties are typically predicted or calculated using methods such as QSAR models for binding affinity and toxicity [27], synthetic accessibility scores (SAScore) [28], or lipophilicity (calculated log P) [11]. RL facilitates the exploration of novel chemical space regions more distant from the training data, but it also increases the risk of reflecting the bias of the scoring functions and generating irrelevant data points as a result.

Molecular generators based on genetic algorithms (GAs) [20, 29] are inspired by the Darwinian evolution [30] and the concept of natural selection. In this approach, a set of proposed solutions (“a population”) is generated and evaluated based on a defined objective (“a fitness pressure”). The best-performing solutions are then selected and recombined to produce a new generation of candidates through genetic operations such as mutation and crossover. Over multiple iterations, this evolutionary process enables the discovery of molecules with improved or desired properties [31].

Due to the diversity of molecular generation methodologies [11], and the inherently difficult task of chemical space exploration [3], fair comparison between generators remains a considerable challenge [32]. It is also difficult to assess which generators are most suitable for a specific use case, as their performance can vary significantly depending on the task [33]. Therefore, simple benchmarks and open-source evaluation pipelines are needed to verify that generators produce reasonable and diverse molecules and to enable fair and transparent comparison.

In the context of this work, a benchmark refers to a standardized set of tasks and evaluation metrics used to compare the performance of different generative models in a consistent and reproducible way. In recent years, several benchmarking frameworks have been introduced for de novo drug design to facilitate standardized evaluation and enable objective comparison of generative models [34–37]. Among these, the most widely used are Molecular Sets (MOSES) [38] and GuacaMol [39]. MOSES provides a set of metrics that focus on benchmarks associated with distribution learning-an approach where the model is trained to reproduce the distribution of molecular properties found in known chemical datasets. Distribution-based benchmarks aim to evaluate the structural diversity and relevance of the generated molecules by comparing their chemical space to that of known compounds from the training data [32].

The GuacaMol benchmark uses similar distribution-based metrics as the MOSES and extends them by introducing goal-directed benchmarks, thus evaluating the ability of a molecular generator to produce compounds that satisfy predefined property objectives, such as structural aspects (e.g., molecular weight, number of aromatic rings, or number of rotatable bonds) or physicochemical properties (e.g., topological polar surface area (TPSA) or log P). Unlike distribution-based benchmarks that assess how well the generator reproduces the overall training data distribution, goal-directed benchmarks focus on evaluating individual molecules based on how well they meet the specified properties [39].

Existing evaluation methods primarily focus on technical aspects, such as confirming molecular validity (i.e., if the generated molecule is chemically suitable), uniqueness (i.e., to what extent are the generated molecules not repeating), novelty (i.e., whether the generated molecules are absent from the training set), and property distribution (i.e., whether the generated molecules fall within predefined ranges of selected properties). Overall, these metrics often overlook the primary goal of molecular generators: to produce biologically meaningful and synthetically accessible compounds [40]. This limitation can lead to flawed conclusions when simplistic models achieve high benchmark scores without delivering practically useful results. For example, many of the benchmarks mentioned above focus on evaluating how well the generator tackles the multi-objective optimization problem. However, they rarely look at whether the underlying structures would actually be useful in a practical drug discovery project.

Furthermore, in the case of GuacaMol, Renz et al [33] showed that on AddCarbon model-in this simple model, a random “C” character is inserted into the SMILES strings-was able to achieve perfect scores in validity, novelty, and uniqueness. The high performance of their model was driven mainly by how well it matched the distribution of the training set rather than its ability to generate practically useful molecules. We refer to this example to highlight the limitations of using distribution-based metrics alone.

Another important limitation of the GuacaMol benchmark is that the goal-directed tasks use a single scoring function that attempts to capture all desirable molecular qualities. However, condensing such complex requirements into one numerical objective is inherently difficult. As a result, optimizing this score may lead to the generation of molecules that are unstable, synthetically unrealistic, or contain highly uncommon substructures [33].

Given these limitations we introduce three metrics based on the scaffold-recall capacity of molecular generators: scaffold Recovery Score (RS), SEt scaffold Diversity (SED), and Absolute SEt scaffold Recall (ASER). These measures capture different aspects of a generator’s performance-RS evaluates how accurately pharmaceutically relevant compounds are reproduced, SED quantifies the balance between exploration and exploitation of chemical space by the given generator, and ASER captures the biological relevance of the generated molecules. Since these metrics are based on comparing molecular scaffolds directly rather than opaque combinations of numerical objective values, we argue that they simulate real drug discovery pipelines much more closely while being simple to calculate and interpret.

An open-source, python-based implementation of the scaffold-based metrics is available from a public GitHub repository: https://github.com/filvaleriia/scaffold-based-metrics.

Data and methods

Generators

In this project, molecular generators from both major categories, deep generative models (DGM-based) and genetic algorithm-based models (GA-based) were used. From the DGM-based category, DrugEx RNN v2 [14], DrugEx GT v3 [15], and REINVENT 4 [16] were selected. From the GA-based group, Molpher [12] and GB_GA [18] were employed. Additionally, AddCarbon [33] was used as a baseline generator.

Molpher is an iterative atom-based molecular morphing algorithm that explores chemical space by generating intermediate structures between a start and a target molecule through mutation-like structural modifications. DrugEx RNN v2 is a SMILES-based recurrent neural network generator optimized via multi-objective reinforcement learning to balance predicted activity and synthetic accessibility. DrugEx GT v3 is a Transformer-based extension of DrugEx that enables fragment-guided graph generation with reinforcement learning-driven multi-objective optimization. REINVENT 4 is an RNN-based SMILES generator that uses transfer and reinforcement learning to bias molecule generation toward desired property profiles. GB_GA is a graph-based genetic algorithm that applies crossover and mutation operations on molecular graphs to optimize predefined objective functions. AddCarbon is a simple baseline generator that creates new molecules by randomly inserting a carbon atom into the SMILES representation of training set compounds. Detailed descriptions of the individual generator architectures and training procedures can be found in their respective original publications. The generator settings are provided in the Supplementary Information (Sect. 8.2).

For DrugEx and REINVENT, the optimization criteria included pChEMBL thresholds ≥ 6 or ≥ 7 (depending on the receptor). In addition, a SAScore constraint < = 6 was applied to all generators except AddCarbon, to prioritize compounds with predicted biological activity and acceptable synthetic accessibility.

Scaffold representation

The term scaffold refers to the core structure of a molecule, commonly used in drug design, and as a fundamental building block for chemical synthesis [41]. In this work, we used “Bemis and Murcko scaffold” (BMS) [42] and “cyclic skeleton” scaffolds (CSK) [43] (Fig. 1). BMS are defined as the combination of molecule’s ring systems and linkers, preserving both heteroatoms and bond types but excluding side chains [42].

Types of scaffolds used in this study: an original molecule and its corresponding Murcko and cyclic skeleton (CSK) scaffolds

BMS in this work were generated using the RDKit library [44], that introduces a small modification of the original BMS definition: terminal atoms connected to the scaffold via double bonds are retained, as they are considered to have a notable effect on the overall shape and electronic properties represented by the scaffold [45]. To distinguish RDKit murcko scaffolds from the original BMS definition, we refer to these as Murcko scaffolds (MURCKO).

CSK scaffolds are a generalized version of Murcko scaffolds. In this representation, all heteroatoms are converted to carbon atoms and all higher-order bonds are replaced with single bonds [41].

Scaffold-based metrics

In this work, a set of novel metrics is proposed to evaluate the quality of virtual libraries generated by molecular generators. The metrics are based on the scaffold analysis of the dataset of known active ligands. This set is divided into two subsets: the Input Set (IS) and the Recall Set (RS), using a scaffold-based splitting strategy, that is described in detail in Sect. “Data preprocessing and split generation”. The molecular generator is trained on the IS, and the scaffolds present in the generated virtual library (Output Set, OS) are compared to those in the RS. The ability of the generator to reproduce biologically active scaffolds from the RS is quantified by the proposed metrics, with higher values indicating more successful generation.

In this context, an active scaffold is defined as a scaffold present in the RS. Since the same scaffold may be associated with multiple compounds, each distinct scaffold is counted only once (Unique scaffolds), regardless of how many compounds contain it. A unique active scaffold refers to the scaffold that is both unique and present in biologically active compounds in the RS (Fig. 2).

Set composition-overview of each set, including the Output Set (OS), Recall Set (RS), and unique active scaffolds in OS or RS (Unique Active scaffolds(OS, RS)), active scaffolds in OS or RS (Active scaffolds(OS, RS)), and other scaffolds whose activity is unknown

scaffold recovery score (RS) quantifies the ability of the generator to produce molecules containing scaffolds that occur in known biologically active compounds. It is calculated as the ratio of the number of Unique Active scaffolds in the Output Set to the number of Unique Active scaffolds in the Recall Set. RS value ranges between 0 (no unique active scaffolds from RS were found in the OS) and 1 (all unique active scaffolds from RS were found in the OS). RS is analogous to the recall metric used to evaluate classification tasks in machine learning [46].

SEt scaffold diversity (SED) reflects the scaffold diversity of the generated set. It is calculated as the number of unique scaffolds in the Output Set relative to the total number of compounds in the Output Set (cos). A value of 1 indicates that every scaffold in the OS is unique, while 0 means no valid scaffolds were generated.

SED enables striking a balance between exploration and exploitation. A high SED value indicates that the generator explores diverse regions of chemical space by discovering novel scaffolds, which is essential for expanding the chemical space. On the other hand, a low SED value suggests a focus on exploiting similar scaffolds, refining specific chemical structures. Adjusting the generator parameters based on the SED value enables fine-tuning the balance between exploring diverse molecular structures and exploiting specific scaffolds.

Absolute SEt scaffold recall (ASER) indicates how effectively the generator explores the target region of chemical space. It is calculated as the ratio of the number of active scaffolds in the OS to the total number of compounds in the Output Set (cos). The ASER metric ranges from 0 to 1, where a value of 0 indicates that none of the generated compounds contain an active scaffold, and a value of 1 indicates that every generated compound contains an active scaffold.

A higher ASER value means the generator is better at producing molecules resembling known biologically active compounds. This suggests that the generator is exploring the right chemical space for drug discovery.

SED and ASER represent competing objectives. Under realistic conditions, they cannot simultaneously reach their maximum value of 1. When the number of unique active scaffolds in the Recall Set is much smaller than the number of generated compounds, achieving a high ASER requires repeated generation of the same active scaffolds, which necessarily reduces SED. Conversely, when SED is maximized and scaffold repetition is avoided, the maximum achievable ASER is limited by the ratio between the number of unique active scaffolds in the Recall Set and the size of the Output Set. This trade-off implies that generative models can be tuned toward higher diversity (higher SED) or stronger enrichment of active scaffolds (higher ASER) depending on the intended application. The proposed metrics explicitly capture this balance and allow different generator configurations to be compared along these two complementary dimensions.

Both SED and ASER depend on the size of the Output Set. Therefore, to ensure a fair comparison between different generators or model configurations, the generated Output Sets should have the same or at least comparable sizes. Differences in Output Set size may otherwise bias the interpretation of SED and ASER values.

A hypothetical ideal generator would achieve an RS value of 1 by recovering all known active scaffolds, while maintaining an appropriate balance between SED and ASER, depending on the intended trade-off between structural diversity and biological relevance. In contrast, a random generator would generally produce low RS and ASER values, as it does not preferentially generate biologically meaningful scaffolds, although it may display relatively high SED due to the lack of structural bias.

Data preprocessing and split generation

In the data preprocessing step, multiple Input Sets and Recall Sets were prepared to enable consistent evaluation of the molecular generators. The splits were defined using the Cyclic Skeleton Scaffolds (CSK) because of its increased ability to separate compounds of different chemical series between testing and training. In total, ten sets were generated: five Input Sets and five Recall Sets for each of the two splits (dissimilarity and similarity). Both CSK and Murcko scaffolds (MURCKO) were used for the calculation of scaffold-based metrics during evaluation.

Active ligands for the Glucocorticoid Receptor (GR, CHEMBL2034) and Leukocyte Elastase (LE, CHEMBL248) were retrieved from the ChEMBL31 database (released in August 2022)[47], yielding 962 GR ligands (183 unique CSK scaffolds) and 1,060 LE ligands (216 unique CSK scaffolds). Compounds were selected using IC₅₀ thresholds of 100 nM (pIC50 = 7) for GR and 1000 nM (pIC50 = 6) for LE [48].

Different molecular generators (Molpher, DrugEx, REINVENT, GB-GA, and AddCarbon) were used in this project, each requiring a specific type of input data. DrugEx, REINVENT, GB-GA, and AddCarbon, accept active compounds as the input. For these generators, the compound Input Set (cIS) was created by using active ligands with their corresponding scaffolds from the scaffold Input Set (sIS). The same procedure was applied to construct the compound Recall Set (cRS), using scaffolds from the scaffold Recall Set (sRS). Molpher requires on its input a pair of active compounds between which a path is generated. Due to the time complexity of molecular morphing, only unique active scaffolds were considered. For each scaffold, the most active compound with the highest pChEMBL value was selected. These compounds were then paired with each other to form all possible pairs, which together constituted the compounds Input Set for Molpher.

Virtual library generation

The generators were initialized to build virtual libraries using optimized settings (Supplementary Information 8.2 for details). For each generator every Input Set (cIS) resulted in five distinct compound Output Sets (cOSs). For most generators, the Output Set size was fixed at approximately 1,000,000 compounds. The only exception was Molpher, as it does not allow explicit control over the number of generated molecules. As a result, for the Glucocorticoid receptor, Molpher produced fewer than 1,000,000 compounds, while for the Leukocyte elastase it generated a larger library of approximately 1,500,000 compounds. In the case of DrugEx, which was constrained to generate 200,000 compounds at a time due to hardware limitations, each cOS was created by running generation five times, after which the results were merged and deduplicated to form the final Output Set.

Data analysis

For each compound output set (cOS), scaffold-based metrics were calculated and averaged across all five cOSs. The analysis was performed separately for dissimilarity and similarity splits. The influence of different scaffold types (CSKs and Murcko scaffolds) on the metrics was also assessed. Finally, the metric target-dependence was assessed for the molecular targets: Glucocorticoid receptor and Leukocyte elastase.

The results are visualized using threshold-categorized heatmaps, which group metric values into comparison categories based on the interpretation thresholds described in Sect. “Metrics interpretation thresholds”. These heatmaps were generated using either the original metric values or their min–max normalized values.

Subset analysis

Each Output Set contains approximately 1,000,000 generated compounds. However, generating and processing such large sets is both time- and memory-intensive. Previous work has indicated that metric values often stabilize once the number of generated molecules reaches at least 100,000 [51], which motivated a systematic evaluation of how Output Set size influences the stability of the scaffold-based metrics used in this study. To assess whether substantially smaller subsets can be employed in future analyses without compromising metric reliability or interpretability, we compared multiple subset sizes. In this study, the full Output Sets were generated for each generator, and all subsets were obtained from them via random sampling. Specifically, five subset sizes (10,000; 62,500; 125,000; 250,000; 500,000) with the full OS (~ 1,000,000 compounds) were compared.

Metrics interpretation thresholds

This section outlines the derivation of thresholds used to categorize differences in scaffold-based metrics.

Given the large size of the Output Sets, uncertainty was estimated using bootstrap resampling [52] applied to fixed-size subsets selected from each generator output. For each generator, receptor, split type, and scaffold representation, the scaffold-based metrics (RS, SED, and ASER) were repeatedly recomputed on resampled subsets of equal size. This procedure yielded bootstrap distributions for each metric, from which mean values and confidence intervals were estimated.

To assess the practical relevance of differences between generators, we further analyzed pairwise metric differences. For each metric, absolute differences

were computed for all pairs of generators under identical experimental conditions. The resulting distributions of Δ values were used to derive comparison thresholds based on quartiles. Differences were categorized according to their position within the distribution: values below the 25th percentile (trivial), between the 25th and 50th percentiles (small), between the 50th and 75th percentiles (moderate), and above the 75th percentile (large). Because CSK and Murcko scaffold representations differ in granularity, interpretation thresholds were derived separately for each scaffold type.

Results and discussion

Data preprocessing

The Input and Recall Sets were based on five clusters of active scaffolds for the Glucocorticoid receptor and Leukocyte elastase. For these clusters, inter- and intra-cluster similarities were calculated using the average Tanimoto coefficient across molecular pairs. The inter-cluster similarities were generally low to moderate, ranging from 0.196 to 0.277 for the Glucocorticoid receptor and from 0.152 to 0.304 for the Leukocyte elastase (Supplementary Information: Table 2 and Table 3). For Leukocyte elastase, the maximum similarity was slightly higher but still indicated well-separated clusters. Within clusters, similarity values were moderate (0.314–0.381 for the Glucocorticoid receptor and 0.341–0.390 for Leukocyte elastase; Supplementary Information: Table 4 and Table 5), indicating that clustering grouped structurally related compounds. At the same time, the observed variability suggests sufficient diversity within clusters while maintaining clear distinctions between different clusters. While average pairwise Tanimoto similarity provides a general overview of inter- and intra-cluster relationships, it does not capture how individual molecules relate to the closest structures in another set. To better quantify the separation between the Input Set and the Recall Set, we therefore performed a nearest-neighbor similarity analysis. For each molecule in the Recall Set, we calculated its Tanimoto similarity to the closest molecule in the Input Set (Recall → Input) and to the closest molecule within the Recall Set itself (Recall → Recall).

The results are summarized as cluster-wise median values and visualized as heatmaps for both targets (Fig. 7). In the dissimilarity split, Recall → Input similarities are consistently lower than Recall → Recall similarities across all clusters, confirming that the Recall Set is well separated from the Input Set. In contrast, the similarity split shows higher Recall → Input similarities, indicating that recall molecules are generated closer to the input chemical space, while still forming structured and distinct sets. Data analysis

Generator performance was evaluated using scaffold-based metrics (RS, SED, and ASER) across receptors, data splits, and scaffold representations. Results are interpreted using interpretation thresholds derived from pairwise metric differences.

Metrics interpretation thresholds

Thresholds used to interpret differences in scaffold-based metrics were derived to identify practically meaningful performance differences between molecular generators. Uncertainty estimates obtained from bootstrap resampling were combined with an analysis of pairwise metric differences.

Based on the subset analysis (Sect. “Subset analysis”), bootstrap resampling was performed using fixed-size subsets of 250,000 compounds sampled from each Output Set. This subset size represents a practical compromise between computational efficiency and metric stability and was applied consistently across all generators, receptors, data split strategies, and scaffold representations.

For each metric (RS, SED, and ASER), bootstrap confidence intervals were computed to account for sampling variability. Absolute pairwise differences between generators were analyzed to derive interpretation thresholds from their empirical distributions. Thresholds were computed separately for CSK and Murcko scaffold representations to account for differences in scaffold granularity. Comparison of generator performance

Generator performance was compared across multiple dimensions: scaffold representations (CSK and Murcko), data split strategies (dissimilarity and similarity), and generators and their parameter settings. Results are presented using threshold-categorized heatmaps based on original metric values (Fig. 8, Table 9–Table 12) and their min–max normalized version (Fig. 10).

The comparative analysis reveals systematic differences in generator performance across scaffold representations, evaluation metrics, and model configurations. Across all evaluated conditions, DrugEx GT (ε = 0.6) and GB_GA (mutation rate = 0.5) fall into the highest performance categories for at least one metric, although their strengths differ. DrugEx GT (ε = 0.6) consistently achieves high scaffold diversity (SED) and strong scaffold recovery (RS), whereas GB_GA (mutation rate = 0.5) is characterized by exceptionally high enrichment of active scaffolds, reflected in the highest ASER values across both receptors. DrugEx RNN (ε = 0.6) and GB_GA (mutation rate = 0.01) also demonstrate competitive performance in selected settings. In contrast, AddCarbon consistently yielded the lowest metric values across all experimental settings, and GB_GA_log_p variants performed poorly. Molpher and REINVENT generally achieved intermediate performance.

Differences between receptors were observed across all metrics and experimental conditions. The Leukocyte elastase task consistently yielded higher metric values than the Glucocorticoid receptor task. One possible explanation is the broader chemical diversity of known Glucocorticoid receptor ligands, which include both steroidal and nonsteroidal compounds as well as their hybrids, thereby expanding the active scaffold space [53]. For example, in the dissimilarity split with CSK scaffolds, the highest RS value for Leukocyte elastase reaches 0.81, compared to 0.76 for the Glucocorticoid receptor, while the corresponding ASER values are 6.29 · 10−2 and 1.75 · 10−2, respectively. A similar pattern is observed in the similarity split, where Leukocyte elastase achieves RS = 0.90 and ASER = 6.43 · 10−2, compared to RS = 0.88 and ASER = 2.55 · 10−2 for the Glucocorticoid receptor. These results indicate that generators are generally more successful in recalling active scaffolds and generating associated compounds for the Leukocyte elastase target.

The choice of scaffold representation had a pronounced effect on metric values across all experiments. The more generic Cyclic Skeleton (CSK) scaffolds generally resulted in higher RS and ASER values than Murcko scaffolds, while SED values were typically lower for CSK scaffolds. This trend was consistent across receptors, data splits, and generators and reflects the reduced granularity of CSK scaffolds.

Across metrics and evaluated conditions, several recurring generator-specific patterns were observed. For the RS metric in the similarity split, DrugEx GT (ε = 0.6) and GB_GA (mutation rate = 0.5) consistently ranked among the top-performing methods for both receptors, with trivial difference for CSKs and small difference for Murcko scaffold. DrugEx RNN (ε = 0.6) achieves particularly strong RS performance in the dissimilarity split with CSK scaffolds for Leukocyte elastase. For the ASER metric, particularly in the Leukocyte elastase task, GB_GA (mutation rate = 0.5) repeatedly achieved the highest values, indicating a strong tendency to generate compounds containing known active scaffolds. In contrast, DrugEx GT (ε = 0.6) consistently achieved the highest SED values and ranked among the top methods in RS across most experimental conditions, while maintaining moderately lower ASER values. Lower ASER values should not be interpreted as inherently unfavorable, as the metric does not account for potential false negatives, i.e., compounds containing active scaffolds that are not represented in the Recall Set.

In addition, we compared generator performance without fine-tuning, where possible (REINVENT and DrugEx). Interestingly, pretrained models achieved comparable or, in many cases, better results than their fine-tuned counterparts. For REINVENT and DrugEx with ε = 0.1, pretrained models often outperformed fine-tuned variants across most metrics. For DrugEx with ε = 0.6, fine-tuned models achieved higher values for RS and ASER, while pretrained models retained higher SED values. These results suggest that fine-tuning does not consistently lead to substantial improvements in scaffold-based metrics and that general chemical knowledge captured during pretraining can already enable recovery of relevant scaffolds.

Taken together, these results provide a structured comparative overview of scaffold-based generator performance across receptors, scaffold representations, and evaluation metrics, serving as a basis for the subsequent analysis of metric comparison thresholds.

Subset analysis

In this section, the stability of evaluation metrics was assessed using subsets of varying sizes (10,000; 62,500; 125,000; 250,000; 500,000; full OS (~ 1,000,000 compounds)) sampled from the original Output Set, to determine the minimum number of generated compounds required to obtain reliable and unbiased results. The smallest subset of 10,000 compounds was included because this size is commonly used in de novo molecular design studies.

To enable a consistent comparison across metrics with different numerical ranges (RS, SED, and ASER), min–max normalization was applied to the metric values. Metric stability was subsequently evaluated by analyzing differences in the normalized values relative to the full OS. A difference threshold of 0.1 was used as a practical criterion to identify noticeable deviations in metric behavior across subset sizes. This threshold was not intended to define an absolute cutoff for any individual metric, but rather to highlight meaningful relative changes after normalization.

To visualize the impact of decreasing subset size on the evaluation metrics, normalized heatmaps (min–max scaled) were created (Fig. 11 a1–a4; Fig. 12 a1–a4), and additional heatmaps were generated that display only the differences in the normalized metric values greater than 0.1 compared to the full OS (Fig. 9; Fig. 11 b1–b4; Fig. 12 b1–b4). Figure 9 displays the results for the Glucocorticoid receptor (dissimilarity split and CSKs), while the remaining results are provided in the Supplementary Information (Fig. 11 and Fig. 12).

Analysis of how different subset sizes (10,000; 62,500; 125,000; 250,000; 500,000; and Full OS-original Output Set of approximately 1,000,000 compounds) affect the metric values for the Glucocorticoid receptor for dissimilarity split with CSK scaffolds. Heatmaps show differences in normalized metric values relative to the full Output Set used as baseline. Only absolute differences exceeding 0.1 are visualized

The results indicate that for the Glucocorticoid receptor (Fig. 9), no meaningful differences were observed between the full OS and the 500,000 subsets, as all metric deviations remained below the 0.1 threshold. For the Leukocyte elastase (Fig. 12), the CSK scaffolds likewise showed no notable differences in the 500,000 subset, whereas for the Murcko scaffolds a deviation in the RS metric for the Molpher generator was observed (− 0.15 and − 0.11). However, this slightly exceeds the defined threshold and can still be considered negligible in the context of the overall evaluation.

For the 250,000 subset, only minor variations in normalized metric values were detected when compared to the full OS, indicating relatively stable and consistent behavior. In contrast, the subsets of 125,000 and 62,500 compounds exhibited more frequent and larger deviations, particularly in some metrics and splits, indicating a loss of representativeness at smaller data sizes.

These trends can be interpreted in the context of the nature of the metrics. For RS, larger numbers of generated molecules provide more opportunities to recover active scaffolds explaining the systematic decrease in performance at smaller subsets. For SED, smaller subsets tend to show higher values because scaffold repetitions are less frequent, whereas in larger sets the number of repeated scaffolds often increases faster than the number of newly discovered ones, which can lead to lower overall diversity. For ASER, the observed deviations remained below the 0.1 threshold for almost all tested subsets. Although the number of compounds in the OS explicitly appears in the denominator of the ASER definition, the numerator representing the number of recovered active scaffolds tends to increase proportionally with the number of generated compounds. As a result, the ASER ratio stabilizes once a sufficiently large and representative subset is reached, explaining the observed robustness of this metric with respect to variations in OS size.

The smallest subset of 10,000 compounds exhibited frequent and inconsistent deviations across multiple metrics, generators, and data splits. Compared to larger subsets, the metric behavior for this size often differed from the trends observed for the full OS, indicating insufficient representativeness for reliable evaluation. Overall, these findings suggest that while reducing the subset size below 250,000 molecules increases metric instability, a subset of 250,000 provides a reasonable balance between computational efficiency and reliable, unbiased results.

Conclusions

This work presents a set of novel scaffold-based metrics designed to evaluate the performance of molecular generators. We introduced three complementary evaluation criteria: RS, which quantifies the generator’s ability to recover active scaffolds (analogous to recall in classification tasks); SED, which measures scaffold diversity; and ASER, which evaluates the generator’s ability to explore biologically relevant chemical space by producing compounds containing active scaffolds. Across all metrics, RS and SED typically reach relatively high values (approximately 0.30–0.90 and 0.26–0.61, respectively), whereas ASER generally remains much lower (typically below 6.43 · 10−2), highlighting that effective exploration of new active regions is still a major challenge and a key area for improvement in molecular generator development.

The proposed scaffold-based metrics were successfully applied to evaluate the performance of five molecular generators: Molpher, REINVENT, DrugEx, GB_GA, and AddCarbon used as the baseline. The evaluation considered two scaffold definitions (Cyclic Skeleton Scaffold and Murcko Scaffold), two biologically distinct targets (Glucocorticoid receptor and Leukocyte elastase), and two different split strategies (dissimilarity and similarity). As expected, the more general CSK scaffolds led to higher RS and ASER values, while SED values were generally lower compared to the more specific Murcko scaffolds. These patterns were consistent across splits, output set sizes, molecular targets, and generators, and the baseline AddCarbon demonstrated that the metrics appropriately capture low-performing generators. Overall, the scores behaved as expected, reflecting their ability to distinguish scaffold recovery, diversity, and exploration potential in different chemical contexts.

Based on this analysis, DrugEx GT (ε = 0.6), GB_GA (mutation rate = 0.5), and DrugEx RNN (ε = 0.6) consistently achieved the best performance in at least one of the evaluated metrics. DrugEx GT consistently showed high scaffold diversity (SED) together with strong scaffold recovery (RS). GB_GA achieved the highest ASER values across conditions, reflecting strong enrichment for compounds containing known active scaffolds. It also reached high RS values for the Glucocorticoid receptor with CSK scaffolds, while in most other settings its RS performance differed from the best method only by trivial or small margins. DrugEx RNN achieved strong RS performance in the dissimilarity split with CSK scaffolds for Leukocyte elastase and high ASER values for the Glucocorticoid receptor with CSK scaffolds. These findings highlight that no single generator optimizes all aspects simultaneously, and performance depends strongly on the desired balance between scaffold recovery, diversity, and enrichment. In addition, the comparison of pretrained and fine-tuned models indicates that pretraining alone can already capture sufficient chemical knowledge for scaffold recovery, and that fine-tuning does not consistently lead to further improvements in scaffold-based performance.

When using these scaffold-based metrics, two important factors must be considered to ensure fair and meaningful comparisons of molecular generators. First, the choice of scaffold type (Cyclic Skeleton vs. Murcko) can strongly influence metric values, and second, the size of the generated virtual library must be comparable across generators, as larger libraries inherently increase the chances of recovering active scaffolds and exploring chemical space. For generators in which the number of produced molecules cannot be explicitly controlled, the library size of the remaining methods should be adjusted to the number of compounds they yield.

Beyond metric definition and benchmarking, this work explicitly addresses how to interpret scaffold-based evaluation results. Uncertainty was quantified using bootstrap resampling, and metrics interpretation thresholds were derived from empirical distributions of pairwise differences between generators. This analysis demonstrates that not all numerical differences in RS, SED, or ASER values correspond to meaningful performance changes. Small differences, such as RS values of 0.76 versus 0.72, typically fall within ranges of trivial or minor variation and are often associated with overlapping confidence intervals, whereas larger differences, such as 0.76 versus 0.54, exceed moderate thresholds and reflect systematic and practically relevant improvements. By combining uncertainty estimation with scaffold-dependent, metric-specific thresholds and a subset size selected based on prior stability analysis, the proposed framework provides concrete guidance for distinguishing between negligible numerical fluctuations and substantive differences in generator performance.

Still, our new metrics have also intrinsic limitations. As a recall-based metric, RS only assesses the recovery of known active scaffolds and cannot account for scaffolds whose activity is unknown. SED, on the other hand, can be exploited by generators that focus solely on scaffold hopping while disregarding the chemical relevance of molecular decorations. ASER, which measures exploration of biologically relevant chemical space, can be influenced by the chemical diversity of the generated library and may remain low even when RS and SED are high, highlighting the difficulty of generating truly novel active compounds outside the known chemical space. Importantly, these limitations stem from the simplified nature of scaffolds, which inherently lose structural and chemical detail.

Despite these limitations, this work provides new means and a blueprint to compare molecular generators in practical applications. The proposed framework and metrics allow to obtain insights to guide the choice of generator algorithms and their hyper-parameters for specific goals (i.e., early-stage hit discovery vs. late-stage optimization). They also facilitate more comprehensive understanding of constantly appearing novel generative models by assessing different aspects of molecular design-such as how accurately a generator can reproduce pharmaceutically relevant scaffolds, how structurally diverse its outputs are, and how effectively it explores novel chemical regions with biological relevance. Overall, the proposed metrics provide a more comprehensive and informative assessment of generator performance, particularly when used alongside commonly applied benchmark metrics, which can in some cases yield overly optimistic evaluations.
