Correlation of Mitochondrial Flavoprotein Fluorescence and Sectoral OCT in Patients with Glaucoma, Suspects, and Ocular Hypertension
Authors: Tiffany Heaster-Ford, Rafaella Nascimento e Silva, Justin Elstrott, Dolly S Chang, Julia A Kim, Joyce Kang, Yan Zhao, Yihao Li, Chen Chen, Sofia De Arrigunaga, Sandra E Freeman, Kanza Aziz, Collin A Rich, Michael M Lin, David S Friedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFlavoprotein fluorescence (FPF) can be measured using a noninvasive investigational device, the OcuMet Beacon (OcuSciences). The Beacon uses blue light to stimulate oxidized mitochondrial flavoproteins, which subsequently emit green autofluorescence. As mitochondrial metabolic stress may reflect axonal damage in glaucomatous eyes, this cross-sectional, exploratory study investigated relationships between optical coherence tomography (OCT) structural parameters and FPF values in patients with glaucoma, ocular hypertension, and glaucoma suspects.Patients and MethodsIndividuals with mild/moderate glaucoma, suspected glaucoma, or ocular hypertension receiving treatment. FPF images were obtained at the optic nerve head (ONH), peripapillary, and macula regions. FPF intensities in the peripapillary and macula regions were divided into 4 and 6 sectors, respectively, corresponding to Cirrus OCT retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer thickness (mGCIPL). FPF values adjusted for lens autofluorescence and signal attenuation were averaged within each sector and applied for regression modeling with OCT measures.ResultsA small cohort of 73 eyes from 54 patients was assessed from the initial study population (141 eyes, 83 early/mild/moderate glaucoma patients undergoing treatment) following image quality exclusion. Older age, thinner RNFL, and phakic status were significantly associated with higher FPF intensity at the ONH (age, P=0.009; RNFL, P<0.001; lens status, P=0.04) and peripapillary region (age, P<0.001; RNFL, P=0.01). Inferior peripapillary correlation between RNFL thickness and FPF was significant (P=0.003), while nasal peripapillary correlation was significant only after adjusting for age and lens status (adjusted P=0.04; unadjusted P=0.08). Global macular FPF intensity was not associated with age (P=0.25), lens status (P=0.14) or mGCIPL thickness (P=0.79), nor were there any sectoral mGCIPL–FPF associations potentially given limited disease severity.ConclusionAge, lens status and RNFL thinning were the main correlates of ONH and peripapillary FPF values. After accounting for age and lens status covariates, higher FPF intensity values mainly correlated with inferior peripapillary RNFL thinning.

Introduction

Evaluating glaucoma severity and progression is clinically challenging due to its slow, often asymptomatic progression, which frequently results in substantial vision loss before diagnosis.1,2 Standard functional endpoints (ie, visual field) are highly variable and introduce significant patient burden, while conventional structural biomarkers show modest change preceding functional decline.1,2 This presents a clinical need for identifying more sensitive functional biomarkers to promote early detection of glaucoma onset and progression. Mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction have been suggested to precede axon loss and visual impairment, positioning them as potential early indicators of retinal neurodegeneration.3–6 Thus, exploring the utility of functional biomarkers reflecting mitochondrial health and metabolic status could promote greater detection sensitivity for diagnosing glaucoma and enable future improvement in progressive disease monitoring.

Mitochondria support numerous essential cellular functions, including adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation, regulation of calcium and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and lipid synthesis across retinal cell populations.3,7 Specifically, retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), in addition to other retinal neurons, require substantial energy production to maintain normal membrane potential gradients for transmitting electrical signals.4,8 As a result, mitochondria play very specialized roles in supporting vital RGC activity in the healthy retina. For example, healthy RGCs exhibit highly dynamic mitochondrial trafficking, as mitochondria will redistribute to regions along axons that require greater ATP supply for action potential generation and propagation for visual processing, specifically in unmyelinated prelaminar and laminar regions.5,9–11 This results in an abundance of mitochondria distributed between the ganglion cell and nerve fiber layers of the inner retina and the lamina cribrosa, observed from histological studies of mitochondria and mitochondrial enzyme localization.5,9,10,12,13 Furthermore, RGC mitochondria sustain a high level of functionality largely through somal mitochondrial biogenesis and repair, as well as fusion and fission events to clear damaged mitochondria along the axon.3,6 However, these mitochondrial functions become dysregulated in response to damage along the optic nerve, often observed as mitochondrial stalling and more fragmented mitochondria due to the prevalence of fission events.3,6,14 The unique behavior of mitochondria in response to RGC health presents an opportunity for them to serve as a functional biomarker of retinal disease state.

Mitochondrial dysfunction has been widely identified as a major contributing factor for glaucoma pathogenesis and progression.3–6 Optic nerve damage and RGC degeneration associated with glaucoma onset have been commonly associated with impaired axonal and somal mitochondria.3,15 The susceptibility to injury of the optic nerve head despite the local abundance of mitochondria for energy supply and repair further suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction is detrimental to RGC preservation. Preclinical and clinical studies have linked genetic and metabolic signatures of mitochondrial dysfunction to glaucoma-related neurodegeneration and demonstrated that therapeutic restoration of mitochondrial health protects RGCs, further supporting mitochondrial function as a driver of glaucoma susceptibility.16–18 Notably, elevated intraocular pressure, a common indicator of glaucoma, has been shown to alter mitochondrial structure and energy production and ultimately induce increased oxidative stress.19 Mitochondrial instability and elevated oxidative stress have also been characterized in response to other glaucoma risk factors, including limited retinal blood flow and aging.4,8,19

Although several trigger mechanisms have been reported, generation of oxidative stress ultimately develops due to an imbalance of protective antioxidant defenses and the overproduction of ROS within the cell. The cells under this condition will suffer mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to damage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and further production of defective encoded proteins, generating a cycle of damaged electron transport chain (ETC) protein subunits, more ROS production, less energy in the cell, and cell apoptosis.20 These oxidative stress-mediated mitochondrial defects (eg, redox dysregulation, mtDNA damage, increased apoptotic signaling) are a common feature across multiple retinal diseases, including glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and age-related macular degeneration.21 The wide-ranging impacts of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction that precede retinal degeneration may be a promising area of investigation to enable earlier assessment of dysregulation across several retinal diseases.

Central to mitochondrial maintenance, flavoproteins are identified as enzymatic proteins composed of a flavin group (eg, flavin adenine dinucleotide/flavin mononucleotide), primarily responsible for regulating cellular oxidation-reduction reactions required for limiting oxidative stress.22,23 As several classified flavoproteins have been associated with mitochondria-specific protein complexes, these have been largely implicated in maintenance of normal mitochondrial function by supporting electron transfer for ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation.22–24 The flavin structure is directly involved in this electron flux supporting energy production, cycling between oxidized or reduced states.22–24 When oxidized, this flavin group demonstrates unique fluorescence properties, with blue light excitation leading to the emission of green autofluorescence.25,26 Mitochondrial dysfunction and associated ROS imbalance increase flavoprotein oxidation, suggesting flavoprotein fluorescence (FPF) may reflect cellular oxidative stress in retinal disease.26–28

Retinal FPF can be measured using a noninvasive investigational device known as the OcuMet Beacon (OcuSciences, Ann Arbor, MI). The Beacon uses full-field illumination of blue light to stimulate oxidized mitochondrial flavoproteins and captures the resulting autofluorescence signal by narrowly filtering the emitted green fluorescence. While contribution of off-target tissue fluorescence or optical clarity changes complicates this and related metabolic retinal imaging techniques (eg retinal oximetry, hyperspectral imaging), filtering and correction of other age-dependent native fluorophores (eg, lipofuscin, glycated proteins) has been optimized for isolation of retinal FPF signal.29–32 To date, this technology has demonstrated acceptable intra-visit reproducibility within healthy and diseased patients across several retinal pathologies, including AMD, glaucoma, DR, and has shown sensitivity for both disease detection and severity in select conditions.29,33–39 However, the utility and application of FPF imaging for assessing retinal metabolic stress in glaucoma patients is under investigation.33–35,39 These studies have focused primarily on either global FPF associations at the macula or ONH or sectoral associations solely in the peripapillary region, reported only for POAG and OHT patients.33–35,39 Thus, additional characterization of this readout of mitochondrial function is needed to determine if it can be used as an early biomarker of retinal disease progression.

In this manuscript, we aim to evaluate our hypothesis that mitochondrial metabolic stress correlates with or precedes axonal tissue loss in glaucomatous eyes by characterizing region-specific differences in FPF response across diseased eyes. Despite prior characterization of high mitochondrial density at the optic nerve head and axonal regions traversing the retina to the lamina cribrosa, additional investigation is needed to establish whether mitochondrial flavoproteins become more concentrated in a particular region of RGCs (eg, somas or axons) under metabolic stress. Thus, the purpose of this study is to examine correlations of tissue dysfunction (FPF intensity) with tissue degeneration and thinning across mitochondria-dominant retinal axonal (ONH and RNFL) and somal/dendritic (GCIPL) regions. Principally, we investigated the relationship between optical coherence tomography (OCT) structural parameters and FPF values in patients being monitored for glaucoma, suspicion of glaucoma or ocular hypertension, expanding existing understanding of FPF relationships to broader patient populations and across ONH, peripapillary and macular sectors.

Materials and Methods

This was an observational, cross-sectional exploratory study of an ongoing cohort being conducted at Massachusetts Eye and Ear (MEE) to investigate the relationship between optical coherence tomography (OCT) structural parameters assessed by Cirrus HD-OCT (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc.) and FPF values. The observational, cross-sectional design limits causal conclusions but allowed for examination of associations between these variables. The study protocol was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board prior to patient recruitment and conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization E6 Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. Each subject provided written informed consent for participation in the study before enrollment.

Study Participants

Participants were patients 18–80 years of age with the diagnosis of known glaucoma, suspected glaucoma, or ocular hypertension (OHTN). Inclusion criteria were a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/40 or better; Humphrey Visual Field (VF) mean deviation (MD) of −15 decibels (dB) or better in at least one eye. Exclusion criteria were systemic or ocular disease other than glaucoma resulting in VF loss; inability to understand English; intraocular surgery within 90 days of the study; and Six-Item Cognitive Impairment Test score of 8 or greater.40 If both eyes met the inclusion criteria, the patient could decide to enroll both eyes throughout the study. The diagnostic definitions for all recorded glaucoma subtypes based on the International Classification of Disease (ICD) code, 10th edition and defined as follows: OHTN- H40.05; Primary open angle glaucoma (POAG)- H40.11; Normal/low tension glaucoma- H40.12; Pigment dispersion glaucoma- H40.13; Primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG)- H40.20, H40.22; Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma- H40.14; Glaucoma suspect- H40.00, H40.01, H40.02, H40.03.

Imaging Protocol

Patients underwent FPF imaging at the ONH and macula using the OcuMet Beacon with or without pupil dilation. Ocumet Beacon is an ophthalmic imaging device that captures a selected green fluorescence emission band through a barrier filter (520 and 540 nm) from the retina following illumination through a band-pass filter for peak, full-field excitation at 458 nm. Infrared (IR) images are also acquired to provide a reference image for mapping FPF regions of interest (ROIs) within a broader retinal area. The machine requires a minimum pupil size of 2.5 mm to acquire the 60° IR and 17° fluorescence images. The auto-focus mode was applied.

The raw images were extracted and analyzed by OcuSciences in the requested region of interest using proprietary software (version V1.5). At the ONH, the boundary was automatically detected by the software in reference to simultaneously acquired IR images. Then, four symmetric sectors were obtained over the entire ONH and at the rim of a fixed radiometric ellipse with a distance of 0.5 and 1.0 times the ONH perimeter (Figure 1A). The peripapillary region was defined from the boundary of the ONH to the 3.46 mm diameter circle from the ONH center (corresponding to the Cirrus peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer OCT) and was also divided into 4 symmetric sectors (Figure 1A). ONH and peripapillary FPF measurements were segmented and quantified separately to ensure accurate regional concordance with sectoral OCT measures. Corresponding to the Cirrus OCT Ganglion Cell Analysis, the macula FPF intensity was calculated within an elliptical annulus center in the fovea with the inner radius of 0.5 mm vertical and 0.6 mm horizontal and outer radius of 2.0 mm vertical and 2.4 mm horizontal and then divided into 6 sectors (Figure 1B). OCT imaging was performed using the Cirrus for both the macula and ONH region (standard parameters for 200 × 200 macular or optic disc cube scans) at the same visit, except in cases where a reliable test was available within the previous 12 weeks. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Only eyes with available FPF images and OCT data on the ONH and macula at the baseline visit were included. This exploratory analysis derives from a larger study whose power calculations were based on the primary study outcomes. Correlation between OCT and FPF measurements was obtained through univariate and multivariate regression adjusted for age and lens status. Adjustment for age was supported by previous studies demonstrating a significant positive correlation of FPF values with age in primary open angle glaucoma eyes.34 Although the Ocumet Beacon algorithm minimizes lens fluorescence, due to the inclusion of pseudophakic and phakic eyes, the analysis included lens status to improve the accuracy of correlative results.29,36 Given the exploratory nature of these comparisons and increased Type I error risk, effect sizes are reported as confidence intervals along with p-values. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) with exchangeable correlation structure were used due to inclusion of both eyes in the analysis, compensating for intereye correlation.

Results

Out of the initial 141 eyes from 83 patients with OCT and FPF data, 68 eyes (48.2%) were excluded due to the following: 1) OCT ONH or macular with signal strength <6 or 2) due to the following FPF quality metrics evaluated by OcuSciences staff (C.R.): A) qualitative exclusion in macula or ONH images with shadowing, poor target/fixation, insufficient dilation, segmentation error, other fluorescence contamination present; B) MATLAB-calculated FPF image quality score for macula or ONH image less than 30; C) MATLAB-calculated IR image quality score for macula or ONH images less than 30. All qualitative exclusion flags and image quality scores were cross-referenced with the corresponding IR and/or FPF images and reviewed by OcuSciences staff during post-processing. The final analysis included 73 eyes of 54 patients.

The study population was 46.3% male, 70.4% white, with a mean age 59.7 years. The main diagnosis was POAG (38.4%) and suspects (31.5%), with the majority under phakic lens status (89.0%, Table 1). Discussion

Our study explored governing factors in retinal FPF intensity changes and key regional associations between FPF and OCT structural measures in patients with glaucoma or OHTN diagnosis and glaucoma suspects. Specifically, in the ONH and peripapillary regions, age and RNFL thinning were the main determinants of FPF values. Len status also governs FPF values solely in the ONH region. Upon extension of this analysis to examine sectoral correlations, increased FPF intensity values were found to be mainly correlated with corresponding RNFL thinning in the inferior peripapillary sector. However, no correlation was found in the macula region.

Exploratory regression analyses suggest that FPF values may not be solely associated with retinal structural changes as measured by OCT but demonstrate dependence on age and lens status. This finding is in concordance with previous FPF studies which have similarly reported correlation between FPF measurements and patient age and lens status, independent of disease state.29,36 It has been suggested that key differences in levels of biological fluorophores arise within the aging retina and lens.41 Additionally, natural lenses and lens implants affect the FPF signal differently.29,41,42 This clearly highlights a need for additional processing to provide reliable quantification of FPF measures and accurate interpretation of FPF trends across varied patient populations for any given disease.

Significant correlations observed between FPF and RNFL thinning at the ONH and peripapillary regions may reflect FPF sensitivity to pathological features associated with glaucoma progression and severity. Axonal regions surrounding the lamina cribrosa, notably abundant in mitochondria, have been suggested to be most susceptible to damage resulting from glaucoma-associated IOP elevation.5,9 This may explain the FPF elevation and RNFL loss observed at the peripapillary and ONH regions given this vulnerability to damage and its potential to trigger increased ROS and accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria preceding gradual axonal degeneration. Furthermore, glaucomatous damage has been proposed to elicit unique pathways of degeneration, which may additionally support these regional FPF changes. Wallerian degeneration is commonly identified as the primary route of axonal loss affecting the distal axon regions and is characterized by metabolite and ATP depletion and elevated calcium and ROS levels.43,44 Alternatively, distal axons can experience the dragging process of dying back, where similar intracellular dysregulation develops but propagates backwards from the axon terminals.44 These degeneration pathways could concurrently impact both the axon integrity and mitochondrial health within prelaminar and laminar regions, potentially reflected in measured FPF and OCT parameters but adding complexity to this structure–function relationship. As the observed FPF and structural trends complement the pattern of RGC and, ultimately, vision loss typically characteristic of glaucomatous damage, it is possible that these endpoints jointly capture notable contributions of RGC structure and function in early glaucoma presentation.45 However, only a limited view of early disease changes can be presently inferred as much uncertainty remains about the precise causal events of early RGC degeneration and the study findings at present are cross-sectional.5 Greater investigation is needed to fully understand the temporal relationship and predictive utility of FPF changes with intracellular dysfunction following glaucoma-associated RGC damage.

Notably, FPF and OCT structural changes observed in this exploratory study suggested regional specificity for correlation significance between these two endpoints. The negative correlation observed between FPF intensity and retinal layer thickness was specific to the ONH and peripapillary regions, with no significant relationships resulting from macular FPF and GCIPL thickness changes. This was also true for correlations derived from sectoral analysis, where the primary association between FPF and OCT metrics was observed for the inferior peripapillary region and which may be related to the understanding that inferior degeneration and vision loss can be an early indicator of glaucoma onset/progression.45 The heightened response at and immediately surrounding the ONH may be explained by its common characterization as the primary region of glaucomatous axon damage, and potentially varied vulnerability of the macula to damage exhibited from this patient population with relatively mild glaucoma, if present.45–47 Due to the nature of the cross-sectional study design and limited sample size, only a snapshot of the severity of pathological features can be discerned within this patient population. Additionally, the patient population largely presented with mild, relatively stable disease at the time of visit, with most actively receiving IOP-lowering treatment (eg, eye drops). These factors likely contribute to mild differences in both FPF and OCT measures and limited, region-specific correlations between FPF and OCT metrics observed here. Follow-up studies enriching for patients with greater disease severity and diverse subtypes are warranted to more robustly establish the strength and consistency of relationships between regional FPF and OCT measures and better reflect any underlying macular FPF associations. However, additional FPF distribution analysis was explored for all patient data to establish a valid range of population averages per sectorized region (Supplemental Table 2) which were used to identify patient outliers with distinctly elevated FPF values representative of potential progressing patients enrolled in the study. Extreme outlier scores were identified from boxplot visualizations of FPF distribution across all regions and sectors. Six eyes of 5 patients had upper extreme outlier FPF scores in at least 1 sector across these 3 regions. All outlier eyes had visual field mean deviations characteristic of mild stage glaucoma (MD ≥ −6 dB), but 4 out of the 6 outlier eyes demonstrated mean RNFL or GCIPL thicknesses lower than normal limits (data not shown). This could be suggestive of increased likelihood of potential disease progression, but longitudinal follow-up is needed to establish any predictive value of extreme FPF values and progressive glaucoma.

There are some caveats for the results of this study. Firstly, disease severity was relatively homogeneous, with the majority of patients classified with mild glaucoma based on visual field indices. Additionally, patients included in the analysis were predominantly suspected of glaucoma onset or diagnosed with POAG, of which all were being treated. As evidenced by the modest effect sizes reported in our analyses, the pathological features measured in this study may not deviate considerably across mild disease and non-glaucomatous patients, especially under conditions intended to stabilize progression. The mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction that typically present with axonal degeneration may also vary with glaucoma diagnosis, given the source of elevated intraocular pressure and injury development is unique for each form of glaucoma. A more stratified analysis based on diagnosis or functional endpoints may better elucidate FPF differences but was not performed here due to limited patient number for particular disease subtypes and severities. Also, this cross-sectional study only provides a static view of endpoints and disease status for each patient at a single visit and does not reflect measurement changes associated with disease progression. It does not independently confirm that metabolic dysfunction occurs before tissue loss. However, additional studies are being explored to further investigate associations of FPF with patient disease status and associated retinal thinning longitudinally. Because each glaucoma type has different modes of injury and progression, expanding the analysis to include follow-up data, along with additional disease factors (eg, IOP elevation, ischemia) representing the various glaucoma types, would be beneficial to better assess the sensitivity of FPF across heterogeneous glaucoma populations. Furthermore, despite having sizable study enrollment, a high proportion of the imaging data from both FPF and OCT collection did not pass quality standards for inclusion in the final analysis. This may have limited the effect size of the observed measurement changes and correlations, overall yielding a lower powered study from the initial design. While previous studies have reported high intra-visit repeatability of FPF measures in both healthy and diseased eyes, enhancing real-time quality metrics provided from both imaging modalities and controls to ensure high standard and consistency in image collection during both training and implementation would be beneficial for improved adoption and evaluation of these measurements.34,37

Overall, this study identified key regional associations in FPF metrics with clinical and structural features in a subset of glaucoma patients suggesting a potential concordance in ONH FPF and structural changes related to glaucoma status. Further characterization specifically focused on longitudinal assessments is needed to fully understand the capacity of FPF to reflect early disease features and changes in glaucoma patients and opportunities to improve the sensitivity and reliability of this metric for glaucoma monitoring.
