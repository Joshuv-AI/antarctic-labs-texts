Urinary Proteomics Enables Noninvasive and Longitudinal Monitoring of Tumor Burden Dynamics and Early Recurrence in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Xiaohua Xing, En Hu, Lei Song, Zhao Zheng, Linsheng Cai, Jiahe Ouyang, Jie Li, Xinting Pan, Mingxin Liu, Yingchao Wang, Xiaolong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To develop a noninvasive, urine-based approach for dynamic monitoring of tumor burden and early detection of recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), addressing the limited sensitivity of conventional serum biomarkers such as AFP and DCP, particularly for minimal residual disease (MRD) assessment. We established a prospective, multi-cohort urinary proteomics framework encompassing four longitudinal clinical cohorts (378 patients, 972 urine samples). In the discovery cohort, 26 patients contributed 130 longitudinal urine samples from those undergoing primary and secondary resections, which were analyzed by mass spectrometry at five standardized follow-up time points to identify proteins associated with tumor burden dynamics. The validation cohort (n = 46) used parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) to confirm candidate biomarkers and construct a composite urine-based tumor burden monitoring model integrating HPGD, AFP, DCP, and GGT. The model was then applied to an early recurrence cohort (306 patients, 612 urine samples) to detect MRD and predict recurrence prior to radiological confirmation. Among 8563 quantified urinary proteins, 217 significantly correlated with tumor burden, with HPGD closely mirroring dynamic changes. The integrated model achieved a pre-recurrence AUC of 0.86, sensitivity of 73%, and specificity of 87%, outperforming AFP (0.73, 39%, 96%) and DCP (0.64, 59%, 88%). It predicted recurrence a median 4.1 months earlier than imaging and served as an independent prognostic factor for recurrence-free (RFS) and overall survival (OS, p < 0.001). This urine-based model enables dynamic assessment of tumor burden and early recurrence detection, surpassing conventional serum biomarkers and providing a clinically actionable tool for personalized surveillance and therapeutic decision-making in HCC.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with its prognosis hindered by high recurrence rates and limited options for effective tumor burden monitoring (1, 2). Accurate evaluation of tumor burden, particularly minimal residual disease (MRD), is critical for improving outcomes through early intervention and precisely determined therapeutic strategies.

Current methods for tumor burden assessment, including imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (3), as well as serum biomarkers like alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP) (4), face significant limitations. Imaging techniques often fail to detect lesions smaller than 1 to 2 mm, with reported false-negative rates ranging from 15% to 30% (5). These methods are not only cost-intensive but also operator-dependent, making them impractical for frequent monitoring. Similarly, serum biomarkers lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity, particularly in cases of low tumor burden or early recurrence (6). Despite extensive efforts to identify novel biomarkers, their clinical application remains unvalidated in large cohorts or real-world settings (7). Even with efforts to integrate multiple indicators through machine learning-based modeling or deep learning-based radiomics analysis, the predictive performance of the models remains suboptimal, with limited sensitivity for detecting minimal residual disease (8, 9). This underscores the urgent need for novel, non-invasive, and reliable biomarkers to dynamically monitor tumor burden, detect MRD, and enable earlier prediction of recurrence with higher accuracy.

Urine has emerged as a promising biofluid for biomarker discovery due to its non-invasive nature, easy of collection, and ability to reflect systemic physiological and pathological changes (10, 11). Compared with plasma, urine generally exhibits a less complex proteomic background and reduced interference from highly abundant circulating proteins, facilitating the detection of lower-abundance proteins associated with disease-related biological processes (12). Advances in mass spectrometry (MS), particularly data-independent acquisition (DIA) techniques, have enabled high-throughput and reproducible analysis of complex biofluids (13, 14). Urinary proteomics have demonstrated diagnostic and prognostic potential in several malignancies, including gastric, prostate and colorectal cancers (15, 16, 17). More recently, dynamic urinary proteomic profiling has been extended to integrate single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data, providing unprecedented insights into the tumor microenvironment and enabling the prediction of immunotherapy response in biliary tract cancer (18). These advances highlight the potential of urine-based proteomics not only as a diagnostic tool but also as a dynamic, systems-level window into tumor biology and disease evolution. However, despite these breakthroughs, the application of urinary proteomics in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains in its infancy. Existing studies have primarily focused on cross-sectional comparisons between HCC patients and controls, rather than longitudinal tracking of proteomic changes reflecting tumor burden dynamics or treatment outcomes (19, 20, 21). To date, no study has systematically characterized urinary proteome alterations associated with HCC progression and recurrence across the disease continuum.

In this study, we address this critical gap by utilizing a unique longitudinal cohort of urine samples collected at five key clinical time points across the HCC disease course. Through comprehensive proteomic profiling and advanced machine learning approaches, we aim to identify tumor burden-associated urinary biomarkers and develop monitoring models for tumor burden dynamic changes, focusing on early recurrence prediction and longitudinal surveillance of HCC progression. This study underscores the potential of urinary proteomics for transforming HCC management by offering non-invasive, dynamic monitoring tools that enhance clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes.

Experimental Procedures

Clinical Cohorts

This study included three longitudinal clinical cohorts: the discovery cohort, the validation cohort, and the exploration cohorts (a postoperative follow-up cohort). In total, 378 HCC patients were enrolled, with a combined total of 972 samples.

The discovery cohort consisted of 26 patients who underwent radical resection for primary HCC between August 2015 and May 2022, experienced recurrence, and subsequently underwent a second resection. For each patient, urine samples were collected at five key time points: within 1-week Pre-primary HCC surgery (Pre1), approximately 2 months Post-primary HCC surgery (Post1), the last follow-up before recurrence confirmed by imaging (FU), within 1-week Pre-secondary HCC surgery (Pre2), and approximately 2 months Post-secondary HCC surgery (Post2). A total of 130 urine samples were collected. Inclusion criteria for HCC patients were (1): histopathologically confirmed HCC (2); receipt of radical hepatic resection (3); postoperative recurrence with subsequent secondary surgery (4); complete follow-up data, including comprehensive physiological, biochemical, clinicopathological, and imaging results; and (5) availability of urine specimens at key time points. The median follow-up time for the discovery cohort was 38.5 months. Comprehensive clinicopathological characteristics of all patients are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The validation cohort was recruited using the same criteria as the discovery cohort and included 46 patients with HCC. Urine samples were collected at five key time points for each patient, resulting in a total of 230 samples. The median follow-up time for the discovery cohort was 39 months. Additionally, for both the discovery and validation cohorts (41 HCC patients in total), serum samples were collected at the same five follow-up time points, totaling 205 serum samples. For all patients, routine clinical physiological and biochemical tests were performed, including AFP, DCP, ALB, TBIL, ALT, AST, GGT, triglycerides (TG), glucose (GLU), white blood cell count (WBC), and platelet count (PLT). Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted at all five follow-up time points. Comprehensive clinicopathological characteristics of all patients are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

The exploration cohort was a postoperative follow-up cohort of 306 patients who underwent radical resection for HCC. Urine samples were prospectively collected at two different follow-up time points for each patient, yielding 612 samples. For recurrence patients (n = 168), samples were collected at the last follow-up before recurrence and at the time of recurrence confirmed by imaging. For non-recurrence patients (n = 138), samples were collected at two follow-up time points corresponding to those of the recurrence group. The median follow-up time for this cohort was 52.9 months. Comprehensive clinicopathological characteristics of all patients are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Sample Collection

Urine samples were collected as first-morning fasting midstream urine specimens from all participants. Immediately post-collection, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature to separate the supernatant from the sediment. The supernatant was then aliquoted into five sterile 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tubes (approximately 1.7 mL per tube) using autoclaved pipette tips, and each tube was assigned a unique identifier. Sample metadata, including specimen type, aliquot number, and collection and processing timestamps, were recorded in a Sample Information Management System, with additional clinical data automatically linked from the hospital information system. All aliquots were promptly organized into sample boxes and stored at −80 °C until further analysis to ensure sample integrity. All samples were subjected to no more than one freeze–thaw cycle prior to proteomic analysis.

All samples were obtained from the clinical biobank of Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital of Fujian Medical University. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. Keshen 2022_046_01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at enrollment. The collection and use of clinical specimens strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample Preparation

The n-LAPE/MS TM Kit (ProteinT Biotechnology Co., Ltd) was utilized for protein isolation. For each patient, 800 μL of urine was diluted with 10 μL of magnetic beads and 200 μL of Binding Buffer. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 1000 rpm for 10 min. Following incubation, unbound proteins were removed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 5 min, and the enriched beads were collected. These beads were washed three times with 500 μL of Washing Buffer, with centrifugation after each wash.

Subsequently, the beads were resuspended in 50 μL of Lysis Buffer containing TCEP and CAA and heated at 95 °C for 10 min under agitation. After cooling to room temperature, 2 μL of trypsin solution was added to the mixture, followed by incubation at 37 °C with agitation for 4 h to facilitate protein digestion. Peptides were precipitated by adding 950 μL of acetonitrile (ACN) and then desalted using the SP3 method on magnetic beads. Finally, peptides were eluted with 20 μL of Elution Buffer, and their concentrations were quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

For DIA analysis, equal amounts of indexed retention time (iRT) peptides (Biognosys) were spiked into each run. Peptide samples generated from a fixed starting volume of 800 μL urine per sample were processed using the SP3 workflow, and the entire peptide yield from each sample was injected into the LC–MS/MS system to ensure consistent sample input across all analyses. DIA acquisition was performed on a Thermo Scientific U3000 nanoflow LC system coupled with a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer. Peptide samples were dissolved in a loading buffer containing 2% ACN and separated on a 150 μm ID × 25 cm C18 column (1.9 μm, 120 Å, Dr Maisch GmbH) using a 60-min effective gradient. The mobile phases included solvent A (2% ACN, 0.1% FA) and solvent B (80% ACN, 0.1% FA), with the following gradient: 0 to 5 min, 3 to 6% B; 6 to 44 min, 6 to 90% B, 45 to 54 min, 90% B; 55 to 60 min, re-equilibration. The flow rate was maintained at 600 nl/min. A spray voltage of 2000 V was applied in positive ion mode, and the ion transfer tube temperature was set to 270 °C.

During the DIA acquisition, a full MS scan at a resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 200) was performed. The AGC target value was set at 1e6 or 20 ms for the maximum injection time by the Orbitrap mass analyzer (350–1500 m/z). For MS/MS scans, the AGC target value was also set at 1e6 with an auto setting of max injection time generated during HCD fragmentation at a resolution of 30,000 at m/z 200. Normalized Collision Energy (NCE) was set at 28%. For proteome DIA MS runs, fragment analysis was divided into 40 DIA isolation windows, with varying widths determined based on the results of the DDA searches (Supplementary Table S4). MS scans were performed before each DIA cycle to ensure comprehensive analysis.

Database Searching

The DIA data were processed by searching against the human UniProt database containing 20,413 sequences (accessed in Novermber 2022). Analysis was conducted using DIA-NN (version 1.8.1) under default parameters, incorporating a trypsin/P digest rule allowing up to 1 missed cleavage, with peptide length restricted to 7 to 30 amino acids. Up to three dynamic modifications were considered, including methionine oxidation and methionine loss at the protein N-terminus, while carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification. Mass tolerances for precursor and fragment ions were automatically optimized within the DIA-NN search, and median mass accuracy of MS1 and MS2 was under 3 ppm. A false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01 was applied to ensure high confidence and reliable data interpretation. To ensure high-confidence protein identification, proteins identified based on a single unique peptide were excluded from the final protein list, and only protein supported by at least two unique peptides were retained for downstream analysis.

Quantification and Normalization

To ensure data quality, samples were screened using two selection criteria: identifying a minimum of 3000 proteins per sample and detecting each protein in at least 30% of the samples. For normalization, protein data were normalized using the eliminated TOP20-FOT (Fraction of Total) method, where protein intensities were adjusted as follows:IpSall−Stop20×1000000

Here, Ip represents the intensity of each protein; Sall denotes the total intensity of all proteins in a sample; and Stop20 indicates the total intensity of the top 20 most abundant proteins in a sample. This approach excludes the top 20 most abundant proteins to reduce the influence of highly abundant urinary proteins, such as uromodulin, which can dominate total signal intensity and introduce inter-sample variability. Missing values were imputed with 0. This stringent normalization approach ensured the robustness and reliability of downstream analyses.

Quality Control of the MS Platform

To ensure the reliability and robustness of the MS system, pooled peptide samples derived from urine samples of multiple healthy individuals were used as quality control (QC) standards throughout the study. A total of 15 QC samples were systematically distributed across experimental runs to monitor system performance. DIA-MS analyses of QC samples were performed under identical conditions as the urinary sample analyses. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the consistency of QC runs, using the R packages reshape2 (v1.4.4) and corrplot (v0.92) within the R environment (v4.0.2).

Identification and Functional Analysis of Differentially Abundant Proteins

Differentially abundant proteins between two follow-up time points within the same patient were identified using a two-sided paired Wilcoxon test implemented via the "Wu Kong" analysis platform (https://www.omicsolution.com/wkomics/main/). Proteins with a p-value <0.05 and a fold change (FC) > 1.2 were designated as differentially abundant. To investigate their biological significance, functional enrichment analyses including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were conducted. Statistical enrichment significance was determined using the hypergeometric test, with multiple testing corrections applied via the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Relationships between the quantified differentially abundant urinary proteins and their enriched functions were visualized using network charts generated by the R packages clusterProfiler (v4.12.0) and ggplot2 (v3.5.1).

Screening of Tumor Burden Dynamic Change Associated Urinary Biomarkers

To identify urinary proteins associated with tumor burden dynamics, differential abundance analysis was first performed across surgical time points. Proteins exhibiting significant changes in both Post1 versus Pre1 and Post2 versus Pre2 comparisons were selected to capture tumor burden–related alterations following two independent surgical events. Differentially abundant proteins were defined using the thresholds of fold change (FC) > 1.2 or < 0.83 with p < 0.05. This step identified 217 proteins that showed consistent abundance changes associated with tumor removal.

To further characterize temporal fluctuation patterns, the 217 proteins were subjected to soft clustering analysis using the Mfuzz R package. The fuzziness coefficient (m) was set to 2.016, and the number of clusters was set to 6. Among these clusters, proteins in Clusters 1 to 5 (n = 179) exhibited abundant changing trends consistent with tumor burden changes, including reduction after tumor removal and recovery during recurrence-associated stages. These proteins were retained as tumor burden–related candidates.

To further identify proteins with potential predictive value before recurrence, differential analysis was performed between follow-up (FU) and Pre2 time points. Proteins that showed significant differences in the FU vs. Pre2 comparison using the same thresholds (FC > 1.2 or < 0.83, p < 0.05) were selected as recurrence-associated candidates. This step yielded 11 candidate proteins, which were subsequently subjected to targeted validation.

Targeted Quantitative Validation of Candidate Protein Biomarkers

For PRM assay development, proteotypic peptides corresponding to candidate proteins were evaluated using stringent selection criteria. Peptides were required to be unique, unmodified, and within an optimal length range (8–12 amino acids). Each peptide was required to exhibit at least five fragment ions with intensities exceeding 104. One representative peptide per protein meeting these criteria was selected for subsequent parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) validation. Stable isotope-labeled peptides (13C615N4-arginine or 13C615N2-lysine labeled) corresponding to target proteotypic peptides were used as internal standards. The performance of internal standards was evaluated by confirming stable retention time alignment and consistent peak shapes across repeated analyses.

Urine samples were thawed at 4 °C, centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min to remove debris, and mixed with acetone (1:5 ratio). Following overnight incubation at 4 °C, protein precipitates were collected by centrifugation at 6000×g for 10 min, with residual acetone evaporating. The precipitates were dissolved in 100 μL lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Subsequently, 20 μg of protein per sample underwent alkylation, digestion, and desalting using the EasyPept Auto100 workstation (Omics). Peptides were dried for further PRM analysis.

The targeted PRM analysis performed in this study corresponds to Tier 2 according to the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) Tier classification guidelines for targeted proteomics. Synthesized isotope-labeled peptides (1 pg) corresponding to candidate unique peptides were spiked into peptide samples (1 μg) for absolute quantification. Peptide mixtures were analyzed on a Q Exactive Plus hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an EASY-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were loaded onto a trap column (Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap C18, 100 μm × 2 cm) at 10 μL/min, followed by separation on an analytical column (nanoEase M/Z C18 T3 100 Å, 75 μm × 25 cm) at 300 nL/min. The gradient of Buffer D (0.1% formic acid in water) was as follows: 92% (0–5 min), 87% (5–17 min), 68% (17–20 min), and 20% (20–25 min). MS was performed with a resolution of 70,000 (at 200 m/z) and an m/z range of 300 to 1800, using an electrospray voltage of 1.9 kV. Target ions underwent MS/MS using HCD, with a resolution of 17,500 (at 200 m/z) and a 1 m/z isolation window.

PRM transitions were processed and evaluated using Skyline (Version 3.6.0.1). To identify and minimize potential interferences, chromatographic peak quality and fragment ion consistency were carefully evaluated in Skyline. Transitions were required to exhibit consistent co-elution profiles and matching retention times between endogenous (light) and isotope-labeled (heavy) peptides. Fragment ions showing abnormal peak shapes, inconsistent retention times, or deviation from expected relative ion intensity patterns were excluded from quantification. Only high-quality transitions with clear peak boundaries and consistent ion ratios were retained for final peptide quantification. Each patient sample was treated as an independent biological replicate. To assess process reproducibility, one representative urine sample was selected as a process replicate and repeatedly processed and analyzed at regular intervals throughout the PRM acquisition workflow. Technical reproducibility was further evaluated by repeated injections of selected samples. Measurement precision was evaluated using the coefficient of variation (CV) of repeated measurements from process and technical replicates. The observed CV values demonstrated acceptable reproducibility of PRM-based quantification. All samples were processed and analyzed in a blinded manner with respect to clinical outcomes. The peak areas were quantified by summing the three most abundant transitions for each peptide. A comprehensive list of peptides analyzed in the PRM experiments is detailed in Supplementary Table S5. The abundance of endogenous (light) peptides was calculated based on the peak area ratio of light to heavy peptides.

Construction of Tumor Burden Monitoring Model

Candidate variables included the urinary biomarker identified from longitudinal proteomic analyses together with prespecified clinical and biochemical variables, including AFP, DCP, ALB, TBIL, ALT, AST, GGT, PLT, age, and gender. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed using the survival and survminer packages in R to evaluate the association between each candidate variable and recurrence-free survival (RFS). Variables with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and retained for model construction. Significant variables were subsequently integrated using a gradient boosting machine (GBM) algorithm implemented in the gbm R package. Hyperparameters were optimized using 10-fold cross-validation, and the final model parameters were: n.trees = 150, interaction.depth = 1, shrinkage = 0.1, n.minobsinnode = 10. The GBM model generated a predicted probability score representing recurrence risk based on the combined contribution of selected variables. For risk stratification, the optimal cutoff value was determined using the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity - 1) derived from receiver–operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in the training dataset. This approach identifies the threshold that maximizes overall diagnostic performance.

Prediction of Early Recurrence in HCC Using the Tumor Burden Monitoring Model

Model scores before and after recurrence were calculated for 168 patients with HCC having postoperative recurrence, and these scores were utilized to assess the performance of the tumor burden monitoring model in predicting early recurrence. Additionally, samples from 138 postoperative non-recurrent patients at corresponding time points were collected as controls for comparison. Differences in model scores between the two groups at different time points, along with their correlation with patient prognosis, were analyzed. The positive predictive value of the model for early recurrence was also evaluated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the reliability of the tumor burden detection model in predicting early recurrence.

Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale

This study employed a longitudinal, multi-cohort urinary proteomics design to characterize tumor burden–associated proteomic dynamics in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and to develop a non-invasive recurrence monitoring model. Three clinical cohorts were included (discovery, validation, and exploration cohorts), comprising 378 patients with HCC and 972 biospecimens. In the discovery cohort (n = 26), urine samples were collected at five clinically defined time points spanning primary resection, recurrence, and secondary resection, enabling paired within-patient comparisons that minimized inter-individual variability. An independent validation cohort (n = 46) with identical sampling was used to confirm reproducibility. Matched serum samples were additionally analyzed in a subset of patients to assess biomarker consistency across biofluids.

DIA-based quantitative proteomics was performed for discovery, and differentially abundant proteins between paired time points were identified using two-sided paired Wilcoxon tests, selected due to the longitudinal design and non-normal distribution of proteomic data. Proteins with fold change >1.2 or <0.83 and p < 0.05 were considered significant. Time-series clustering was applied to identify proteins showing consistent tumor burden–associated dynamics across both primary and secondary resections. Selected candidates were validated by targeted PRM-MS using stable isotope–labeled internal standards.

For clinical evaluation, a tumor burden monitoring model was constructed using Cox proportional hazards regression in a large postoperative follow-up cohort (n = 306), with RFS as the primary endpoint. Model performance and early recurrence prediction were assessed using ROC analysis and Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis with log-rank testing. This integrated design ensured statistical robustness, biological relevance, and clinical translatability.

Results

Study Design and Clinical Characteristics of Urinary Samples

To identify urinary protein biomarkers that dynamically reflect tumor burden changes in HCC, we established a prospective, longitudinal, and multicohort study integrating discovery, validation, and translational application phases (Fig. 1). Across three independent clinical cohorts, 378 patients with HCC and 972 urine samples were analyzed, with all samples collected at standardized clinical milestones and paired with synchronous clinical and imaging assessments. The discovery cohort consisted of 26 patients with HCC who underwent curative resection, tumor recurrence, and subsequent secondary resection, representing a unique and rare longitudinal resource for serial tumor burden tracking. Urine samples were prospectively collected at five standardized time points: Pre-primary surgery (Pre1), Post-primary surgery (Post1), Routine follow-up (FU), Pre-secondary surgery (Pre2), and Post-secondary surgery (Post2), yielding 130 samples in total. Urinary proteomic profiling was performed to screen dynamic biomarkers associated with tumor burden changes. Each collection was accompanied by corresponding clinical parameters and radiologic evaluation (CT/MRI), enabling precise mapping of urinary proteomic changes to actual tumor burden dynamics (Supplementary Table S1). Notably, conventional serum biomarkers such as AFP and DCP showed significant postoperative decreases but did not exhibit consistent longitudinal patterns associated with tumor burden dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S1), underscoring the limitation of current biomarkers and the needs for more sensitive, real-time indicators.

The validation cohort included 46 patients with HCC (21 with recurrence and 25 without recurrence), with urine collected at the same standardized intervals, further supporting the robustness of this prospective design (Supplementary Table S2). Candidate urinary biomarkers identified in the discovery phase were quantified using targeted parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) proteomics, confirming the reproducibility and specificity of the selected proteins across independent samples. These validated biomarkers were then integrated with serum biomarkers (AFP, DCP, GGT, etc.) to construct a composite urine-based tumor burden monitoring model.

To evaluate the clinical applicability and translational performance of the model, we further analyzed an early recurrence cohort comprising 612 urine samples, with paired pre- and post-recurrence samples. The model successfully identified MRD and detected recurrence with a median of 4.1 months earlier than imaging-based confirmation (Supplementary Table S3). Compared with conventional biomarkers (AFP and DCP), the model demonstrated markedly higher early detection accuracy and sensitivity, supporting its potential for real-time surveillance, which would benefit earlier therapeutic intervention.

Collectively, this study represents one of the largest and most comprehensively profiled longitudinal urinary proteomic investigations in HCC, integrating discovery (proteomic screening), validation (PRM-based quantification), and clinical application (early recurrence monitoring). The secondary resection cohort and paired imaging-proteomics design provide a unique and unprecedented opportunity to define urinary molecular signatures that accurately reflect tumor burden dynamics and translate them into clinically actionable biomarkers.

Proteomic Landscape of Urine in HCC

To ensure the stability and reproducibility of urinary proteomics data, 15 quality control (QC) samples were interspersed throughout the MS acquisition workflow. Across all QC runs, a total of 7728 proteins were identified, and proteins consistently detected in all QC samples accounted for approximately 70% of the proteins identified in each run, indicating high identification consistency (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Quantitative reproducibility was further confirmed by strong pairwise correlations of protein intensities among QC samples (r = 0.90–0.99; median r = 0.99), demonstrating excellent analytical stability (Supplementary Fig. S2B).

Building on this robust foundation, we comprehensively profiled the urinary proteome from the longitudinal HCC cohort and compared it with the serum proteome derived from 205 serum samples collected at the same five follow-up time points from 41 patients. In total, 8563 urinary proteins were identified, with an average of 4143 proteins per sample, substantially exceeding the approximately 300 proteins that were quantified per serum sample (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S5). Notably, the urinary proteome encompassed nearly 80% of proteins detected in serum, highlighting its broad coverage of circulating proteins and strong potential for biomarker discovery (Fig. 2B). Regarding protein abundance distribution, the top 10 most abundant urinary proteins accounted for only 27.6% of the total protein abundance, markedly lower than the 73.1% observed in serum (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. S2D). This indicates that urine captures a wider dynamic range, particularly for medium- and low-abundance proteins, which is advantageous for detecting subtle molecular alterations associated with tumor burden changes. However, due to higher biological variability and environmental influences, the coefficient of variation (CV) for urinary proteins (median range: 0.63–0.74) was greater than that for serum proteins (median range: 0.44–0.47) (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. S2E, underscoring the need for rigorous standardization in sample collection and data normalization.

Despite these challenges, urinary proteomics demonstrated clear advantages in both protein identification depth and abundance coverage compared with serum. The molecular weight distribution of urinary proteins closely resembled that of serum, with a median around 64 kDa and an approximately normal distribution (Fig. 2F). Subcellular localization analysis revealed overlapping enrichment in extracellular space, exosome, and plasma membrane proteins, indicating shared biological origins between urinary and serum proteomes (Fig. 2G).

Collectively, these results establish the urinary proteome as a comprehensive and dynamic molecular reservoir, supporting its utility as a powerful, non-invasive platform for biomarker discovery and real-time monitoring of tumor burden dynamics in hepatocellular carcinoma.

Functional enrichment analysis showed that these proteins were predominantly enriched in blood coagulation and platelet activation pathways, reflecting the hypercoagulable state commonly observed during tumor progression and its potential contribution to vascular remodeling and metastasis. Additional enrichment in pathways related to cytoskeletal organization, calcium signaling, and immune cell adhesion suggested involvement in tumor cell motility, invasiveness, and immune evasion (Fig. 3C). Network analysis further delineated the molecular interactions underlying tumor burden dynamics. The ribosome pathway (e.g., RPL7, RPL19, RPS15A) indicated enhanced translational activity in proliferating tumor cells. Enrichment of the COVID-19 signaling pathway implied a link between systemic inflammation and tumor progression. The ECM–receptor interaction pathway (e.g., LAMC1, SDC1, TNC) highlighted the role of extracellular matrix remodeling and cell adhesion in invasion and metastasis, while the N-glycan biosynthesis pathway (e.g., MGAT2, MAN2A1, MGAT5) underscored aberrant glycosylation as a driver of tumor–microenvironment communication and immune escape (Fig. 3D).

Together, these findings identify a group of urinary proteins that mirror biological processes associated with tumor burden dynamics in HCC, offering potential noninvasive biomarkers for real-time tumor monitoring and therapeutic targeting.

Dynamic Protein Abundance Patterns and Biomarker Identification

To characterize the temporal patterns of these tumor burden–related urinary proteins, we performed Mfuzz clustering on the 217 shared proteins identified in both Post1/Pre1 and Post2/Pre2 comparisons. This analysis classified the proteins into six distinct clusters, among which Clusters 1 to 5 showed consistent abundance changes across both surgical events, indicating strong correlation with tumor burden fluctuations, whereas Cluster 6 did not exhibit a consistent trend (Fig. 4A). These proteins were detectable in urine and showed significantly elevated levels in the preoperative HCC state compared with the postoperative tumor-free state. However, although significant perioperative differences were observed, these biomarkers did not exhibit consistent longitudinal dynamic changes across subsequent follow-up time points, indicating limited utility for continuous tumor burden monitoring compared with the six dynamically responsive proteins identified above (Supplementary Fig. S2F).

These results define a subset of urinary proteins with strong and reproducible associations with tumor burden dynamics, supporting their potential as non-invasive biomarkers for real-time monitoring and recurrence risk assessment in HCC.

Urinary HPGD and the Integrated Tumor Burden Monitoring Model

To validate candidate urinary biomarkers reflecting tumor burden dynamics, the six proteins identified in the discovery cohort were quantified by PRM in an independent longitudinal validation cohort (n = 46 HCC patients, 230 urine samples), including 21 patients with recurrence and 25 without recurrence. Three of the six proteins, HPGD, QSOX1 and ENPP2, were successfully quantified by PRM (Supplementary Fig. S3A, Supplementary Table S6). Among these, HPGD exhibited the most robust and consistent changes, closely tracking tumor burden variations. Urinary HPGD levels decreased sharply after both primary and secondary surgeries (Post1 vs Pre1, Post2 vs Pre2, p < 0.001), followed by a significant rise during follow-up, peaking before recurrence (Pre2) when radiologic relapse was confirmed (Fig. 5A). These temporal trends were highly consistent with DIA-MS findings from the discovery phase. The other two proteins showed weaker or inconsistent patterns, leading to the prioritization of HPGD for model development (Supplementary Fig. S3B). In contrast, AFP and DCP declined only after the first surgery and remained largely unchanged during recurrence, indicating limited sensitivity to MRD (Fig. 5, B and C). Importantly, urinary HPGD levels began to rise before radiologic recurrence, suggesting its potential as an early warning biomarker. In univariate Cox regression, urinary HPGD, serum AFP, DCP, and GGT were significantly associated with recurrence risk (p < 0.05, Supplementary Table S7). Based on these variables, we constructed an integrated tumor burden monitoring model combining urinary HPGD with serum AFP, DCP, and GGT. The model scores decreased significantly after both primary (Post1/Pre1) and secondary (Post2/Pre2) resections, followed by a progressive increase during follow-up (FU) and prior to recurrence (FU/Pre2) (Fig. 5D). Individual imaging confirmed that model dynamics closely paralleled tumor burden changes and outperformed AFP, DCP, and imaging in reflecting disease progression (Fig. 5E). Variable importance analysis demonstrated that all four predictors contributed independently to model performance, with GGT, AFP, HPGD, and DCP showing comparable contributions (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Stepwise model construction further showed progressive improvement in predictive performance as AFP, DCP, and GGT were sequentially added to HPGD, indicating complementary predictive value among these biomarkers (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Furthermore, comparison with a baseline clinical model based only on AFP, DCP, and GGT demonstrated that incorporation of urinary HPGD improved overall discrimination, increasing the AUC from 0.82 to 0.86 and specificity from 0.60 to 0.73, while sensitivity remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S4F). In addition, patients with high model scores had markedly shorter recurrence-free survival (median 18.43 months vs not reached, p < 0.001) with 76.2% positive detection rate. A similar trend was observed for overall survival (OS), with patients in the high-score group exhibiting significantly poorer outcomes (Figs. 5F, Supplementary Fig. S4G). To further investigate prediction discrepancies, all misclassified cases were systematically reviewed, and their clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S8. False-negative cases were predominantly observed in patients with chronic hepatitis or cirrhosis backgrounds and were frequently associated with relatively early recurrence, suggesting that tumor burden at the time of sampling may have been minimal and tumor-derived signals remained weak. In contrast, false-positive cases were more frequently observed in patients with impaired liver function backgrounds, indicating that postoperative liver injury or inflammation may contribute to elevated risk scores in the absence of recurrence.

Together, these findings demonstrate that urinary HPGD serves as a reliable marker of tumor burden dynamics, and the integrated scoring model provides a quantitative, noninvasive, real-time tool for early recurrence detection and risk stratification, significantly surpassing conventional serum biomarkers and imaging modalities.

Tumor Burden Monitoring Model for Early Warning of HCC Recurrence

To evaluate the clinical efficacy of the tumor burden monitoring model in the early warning of HCC recurrence, we prospectively analyzed urinary HPGD and serum biomarkers (AFP, DCP, GGT) in a cohort of 306 postoperative patients with HCC, including 168 with recurrence and 138 without recurrence. For each patient, urine samples were collected at two follow-up time points: one before recurrence (pre-recurrence) and another at the time of radiologic recurrence (recurrence) or at matched time points in the non-recurrence group. Before recurrence, urinary HPGD levels were significantly higher in the recurrence group than in the non-recurrence group (p < 0.001), indicating elevated tumor burden prior to imaging confirmation (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Together, these findings demonstrate that the tumor burden monitoring model can well detect early signals of recurrence before imaging, accurately reflect tumor burden dynamics, and provide a powerful non-invasive tool for longitudinal surveillance which would benefit to early clinical intervention in patients with HCC.

Discussion

Hepatocellular carcinoma remains a major global health challenge, with high rates of recurrence and limited tools for non-invasive, dynamic monitoring of tumor burden (22). Our study demonstrates that urinary proteomics provides a highly sensitive and reproducible platform to capture molecular changes associated with tumor burden dynamics, offering both mechanistic insights and clinical utility in HCC management.

Compared with serum, urinary proteomics enabled identification of a broader range of proteins, particularly medium- and low-abundance species often masked by high-abundance serum proteins (12). Notably, urine captured nearly 80% of serum proteins, highlighting its capacity to reflect systemic proteomic changes while offering a non-invasive and easily accessible biofluid for longitudinal monitoring. The high reproducibility of DIA-based acquisition further supports the robustness of urine as a substrate for biomarker discovery (23).

Through longitudinal profiling across five clinically relevant time points, we identified HPGD as a urinary biomarker closely reflecting tumor burden dynamics. Its fluctuations mirrored MRD and preceded radiologic recurrence, suggesting a potential role as an early indicator of subclinical disease. Mechanistically, HPGD functions as a key enzyme in prostaglandin metabolism, regulating inflammation and tissue remodeling (24, 25, 26). Dynamic changes in urinary HPGD may therefore reflect shifts in local tumor microenvironment, inflammatory signaling, or metabolic activity, processes intimately linked to HCC progression and recurrence. These observations indicate that urinary HPGD not only serves as a biomarker but also provides a functional readout of tumor biology, capturing early metabolic and immune evasion events. The robustness of HPGD as a biomarker is further supported by accumulating evidence across multiple cancer types (27). HPGD functions as a tumor suppressor through regulation of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) metabolism, and its downregulation has been widely reported in diverse malignancies (28, 29). Importantly, HPGD-related molecular alterations have been incorporated into non-invasive cancer detection strategies, such as fecal methylation-based screening approaches for colorectal cancer, supporting its feasibility for clinical translation (30). Although several previously reported HCC-associated proteins, including GPC3, HSP70, GP73, and GGT1, were detectable in urine and showed significant perioperative differences, their longitudinal profiles were less stable than that of HPGD. This observation suggests that not all tumor-associated proteins are suitable for dynamic tumor burden monitoring, highlighting the unique advantage of HPGD as a robust longitudinal biomarker.

Integrating HPGD with serum biomarkers (AFP, DCP, and GGT) into a tumor burden monitoring model enhanced predictive performance, enabling early recurrence detection with superior sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional serum markers. Importantly, the model predicted recurrence a median of 4.1 months earlier than radiologic diagnosis, enabling timely clinical intervention and potentially improving patient outcomes through earlier therapeutic management. In addition, the model also provided independent prognostic value, with elevated model scores strongly associated with shorter RFS and OS, further underscore the model’s ability to capture residual disease and biological aggressiveness after relapse.

Several aspects of our study represent notable innovations. First, the longitudinal multi-cohort design enabled direct mapping of urinary proteomic alterations to tumor burden dynamics across both primary and secondary surgeries, providing unprecedented temporal resolution. Second, the integration of urinary proteomics with targeted PRM validation and machine learning-based modeling facilitated translation into a clinically actionable framework for non-invasive surveillance and risk stratification. Third, the model’s ability to detect early recurrence and stratify patients by prognosis underscores its potential utility for precision monitoring and individualized management in HCC.

Despite these strengths, some limitations should be acknowledged. Although our study included a substantial number of patients and samples, further validation in multi-center, ethnically diverse cohorts will be essential to confirm generalizability and optimize threshold settings for clinical implementation. In addition, although urine collection and storage procedures were standardized across all cohorts to minimize pre-analytical variability, renal function was not specifically evaluated in the present study. Because urinary protein composition may be influenced by renal function, future studies incorporate renal function assessments are warranted. Nevertheless, the longitudinal design with repeated sampling from the same individuals may have partially mitigated the impact of inter-individual variability. Additionally, while HPGD proved a robust biomarker, mechanistic studies are needed to clarify its functional role in HCC progression and recurrence. Future integration of multi-omics data, including transcriptomics and metabolomics, may further improve predictive accuracy and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the biological pathways driving tumor burden dynamics.

In summary, our study establishes urinary proteomics as a powerful, non-invasive platform for monitoring tumor burden in HCC. The integrated tumor burden monitoring model accurately detects early recurrence, reflects dynamic changes in tumor burden, and serves as an independent prognostic indicator. This integrated tumor burden monitoring model offers a practical and clinically applicable tool for real-time surveillance and guiding personalized treatment in HCC.
