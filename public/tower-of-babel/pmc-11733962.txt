Isolated Cardiac Echinococcosis of Interventricular Septum
Authors: Roopesh R. Singhal, Zeeshan H. Mansuri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cardiac cystic echinococcosis is a rare form of cystic hydatid disease. Hydatid cysts are multilocular and are picked up incidentally. We here describe a case of an isolated unilocular cardiac hydatid cyst of the interventricular septum with multimodality imaging, treatment approach, and operative findings.

A 32-year-old woman, having nonvegetarian diet from a lower socioeconomic class presented with palpitation on exertion associated with atypical chest pain for 6 months. The results of her physical examination, electrocardiogram, and chest X-ray were within normal limits. Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography (Figures 1A to 1D, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3) revealed a large anechoic unilocular cyst measuring 4.5 × 3.7 cm with a thin hyperechoic capsule in the interventricular septum. Because of to its proximity to the left ventricular free wall, the midcavitary gradient of the cyst was 35 mm Hg. Cardiac multislice computed tomography (Figure 1E) revealed a 4.4 × 3.6 × 4.8 cm rounded well-defined, thin-walled minimally enhancing cystic lesion bulging into both ventricular cavities, the left more so than the right. Screening computed tomography of the brain, thorax, and abdomen did not reveal cysts elsewhere. A diagnosis of isolated hydatid cyst of the interventricular septum was made. The patient underwent surgical evacuation of the cyst. Operative findings (Video 4) revealed an outer fibrous pericyst and an inner slimy endocyst containing straw-colored serous fluid. Microscopic examination of the fluid and capsule revealed the scolex of the parasite, confirming the diagnosis (Supplemental Figure 1). The post-operative period was free of any complications, and the patient was discharged with prescribed albendazole 400 mg/day for 6 months. Cardiac hydatid disease is usually a part of systemic dissemination; isolated cardiac involvement is rare.1 Cardiac hydatid cysts are most commonly in the left ventricle (55-60%), followed by the right ventricle (15%), pericardium (8%), left atrium (8%), pulmonary artery (6%), and interventricular septum (5-9%).2

Symptoms are observed in about 60% of patients and include atypical chest pain, dyspnea, and palpitations. The result of physical examination is usually noncontributory, although cardiac murmurs from intracavitary cysts encroaching on the ventricular cavity may be heard.

Investigations used for diagnosis are enzyme-linked immunoadsorbent assay, Western blot, immunofluorescence, immunodiffusion, indirect hemagglutination, specific immunoglobulin E, specific immunoglobulin G, and complement fixation.1,3 It is also possible to detect the protoscolices or hydatid membranes after aspiration of hydatid cyst fluid. A histopathologic examination of the capsule may show the thick fibrous capsule with attached daughter cyst.

A typical echocardiogram reveals a cystic echolucent mass with internal trabeculations. A detached membrane is the only diagnostic sign.3 Whenever the result of transthoracic echocardiography is suboptimal, the use of transesophageal echocardiography is encouraged. Three-dimensional echocardiography may further enhance the available information and the anatomical details. Cardiac CT usually reveals a watery image of the tumor with a hyperechoic capsule.2,3

The differential diagnosis of cardiac hydatid cysts includes dysontogenetic cyst, congenital pericardial cyst, cardiac myxoma, and a variety of tumors in the heart. However, the multivesicular nature of the cystic mass and membrane detachment indicate the true diagnosis.1,2

With cardiac hydatid cysts, the frequency of intracardiac perforation is very high (25-40%). After cyst perforation, most deaths occur from septic shock or embolic complications. Thus, cardiac hydatid cysts should always be surgically removed in symptomatic and asymptomatic patients.1 Operative precautions include keeping the operative field protected by sheets soaked with a hypertonic saline solution to prevent any pericardial contamination. The cyst is then punctured and emptied with a syringe; it is sterilized by injecting a hypertonic saline solution followed by its excision.
