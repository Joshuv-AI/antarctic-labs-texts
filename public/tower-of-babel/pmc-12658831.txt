Nickel(II) Porphyrin Complex-Derived Electrocatalysts for Oxygen Evolution
Authors: Suzhen Bai, Yabo Wang, Zhengshan Tian, Qiaoru Liu, Kesheng Cao, Haoqi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is a critical bottleneck in electrocatalytic water splitting due to its inherently sluggish kinetics and the high overpotential required to drive the reaction, which significantly limits overall energy efficiency. Although nickel tetraphenylporphyrin (NiTPP) shows potential as an OER catalyst, its performance in pristine form is often insufficient for practical applications. To overcome these limitations, this work introduces a strategic approach involving the controlled carbonization of the NiTPP precursor to synthesize a highly active and durable catalyst. By pyrolyzing NiTPP under a controlled atmosphere and at various temperatures (400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C), we embedded catalytically active nickel species within a conductive carbon matrix derived from the porphyrin ligand. Utilizing the synergistic effects between the active metal sites and the graphitic support, the resulting material exhibits improved charge transport and exposes a greater number of active sites. Electrochemical evaluations demonstrate that the NiTPP-800 sample, obtained by carbonization at 800 °C, exhibits superior performance. It delivers a low overpotential of only 347 mV at a current density of 20 mA cm–2 and a low Tafel slope of 86 mV dec–1 in 1 M KOH, surpassing that of the uncarbonized and lower-temperature carbonized samples. The catalyst also exhibits outstanding long-term stability, maintaining its performance for over 1000 CV cycles.

Introduction

The global transition toward a sustainable energy economy hinges on the development of efficient energy storage and conversion technologies, with electrocatalytic water splitting (2H2O → 2H2 + O2) representing a cornerstone for producing green hydrogen. − However, the overall efficiency of this process is severely constrained by the sluggish kinetics of the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), a complex four-electron transfer process that requires a substantial overpotential, thereby leading to significant energy losses. − While state-of-the-art catalysts based on precious metal oxides like IrO2 and RuO2 exhibit high activity, extensive studies have focused on developing earth-abundant alternatives, including transition metal compounds, perovskites, and layered double hydroxides. − Still, the design of low-cost, highly active, and durable OER catalysts remains a paramount challenge.

Among various molecular precursors, metalloporphyrins have attracted considerable attention. Specifically, nickel tetraphenylporphyrin (NiTPP), which features a central Ni­(II) ion coordinated within a large, planar, and aromatic porphyrin macrocycle, stands out as a particularly promising candidate. − The inherent physicochemical properties of NiTPP including its robust thermal stability, well-defined molecular structure, and a conjugated π-electron system that facilitates charge delocalization, make it an ideal platform for designing advanced catalysts. The ability to tune its electronic structure via the central metal ion or peripheral substituents allows for systematic investigation of structure–activity relationships. , However, these complexes often exhibit insufficient catalytic activity and poor stability under the harsh oxidative conditions of OER in their pristine molecular form.

To overcome these limitations, various strategies have been investigated. For instance, chemical functionalization of the porphyrin ligand can tune the electronic properties of the catalyst but often involves complex synthetic routes and may result in catalysts that lack long-term stability. Another approach, immobilizing NiTPP onto conductive supports like graphene, improves charge transport but frequently suffers from catalyst leaching due to weak physical attachment, compromising durability. In contrast, the thermal transformation of these molecular precursors into robust, heterogeneous catalysts offer a more comprehensive solution. This method simultaneously generates highly active catalytic sites and embeds them within an intrinsically conductive carbon framework, thereby addressing the critical challenges of activity, stability, and conductivity in a single, scalable step. This strategy has been successfully validated across numerous studies. For example, pyrolyzing cobalt porphyrins on carbon supports has been shown to generate highly dispersed Co–N x active sites within the carbon matrix, which are instrumental for efficient OER. Similarly, iron porphyrins have been thermally converted into effective catalysts where the pyrolysis conditions, such as the protective atmosphere, critically influence the nature of the resulting Fe–N–C sites and, consequently, their catalytic activity and stability. Furthermore, extending this method to porphyrin-based molecular frameworks has yielded catalysts with exceptional durability, as the interconnected precursor structure leads to a more robust and integrated final composite material after carbonization.

Building on this strategy, we herein report the synthesis of a highly active and durable OER catalyst derived from the controlled pyrolysis of NiTPP at varying temperatures (400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C). This facile thermal treatment effectively converts the molecular precursor into a synergistic composite, where catalytically active nickel species are securely embedded within a conductive, graphitic carbon matrix. These integrated composites are shown to dramatically enhance charge transport and expose a high density of active sites. Notably, the catalyst prepared at 800 °C (NiTPP-800) demonstrates exceptional performance, outperforming the other synthesized materials in 1 M KOH. Moreover, the catalyst demonstrates excellent long-term stability over 1000 CV cycles. These results significantly surpass the performance of uncarbonized or lower-temperature analogues and indicate the immense potential of this pyrolysis approach for engineering advanced, low-cost catalysts for energy conversion applications.

Experimental Sections

Chemicals and Reagents

Benzaldehyde (C7H6O) was purchased from Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Nickelous acetate (C4H6NiO4) was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co.,Ltd. (Shanghai, China). N,N-Dimethylformamide (C3H7NO) and pyrrole (C4H5N) were purchased from Shanghai Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Propionic acid (C3H6O2) and acetone (C3H6O) were sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All chemical reagents were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of TPP and NiTpp

Benzaldehyde was added to propionic acid, followed by the addition of pyrrole. The mixture was refluxed at 140 °C for 3 h. After cooling to room temperature, the product was filtered and sequentially washed with propionic acid and acetone until the filtrate became colorless. The resulting purple powder was dried in an oven at 80 °C. TPP (1 equiv) was dissolved in DMF, and nickel­(II) acetate (5 equiv) was added. The solution was heated to 150 °C under reflux for 8 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, excess deionized water was added to induce precipitation. The precipitate was collected via vacuum filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water, and dried at 80 °C.

Carbonization Process

A 100 mg sample of NiTPP was placed in a crucible and loaded into a tubular furnace. The system was purged with Ar gas for 30 min to remove residual air. Subsequently, the temperature was raised to target values (200, 400, 600, or 800 °C) at a heating rate of 5 °C min–1 under continuous Ar flow (50 mL min–1) and maintained for 2 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, the resulting catalysts (denoted as NiTPP-T, where T represents the pyrolysis temperature) were collected. The preparation process is shown in Figure .

Physical Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) characterization was carried out on a Bruker D8-Focus Bragg–Brentano X-ray Powder Diffractometer equipped with a Cu sealed tube (λ = 1.54178 Å) at 40 kV and 20 mA. UV–visible absorption spectrum were measured using a QE65Pro high-sensitivity spectrometer. Nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen (1H NMR) tests were performed using a Varian 400 MHz nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer. Molecular weight analysis was performed using a TOF/TOF time-of-flight mass spectrometer. The field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was conducted on a JEOL JSM-6700F device. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha surface analysis system to examine the surface chemical composition. The surface morphology, particle size distribution, elemental composition, and elemental mapping were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Sigma360) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) system and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Thermo Fisher iCAP PRO, USA).

Electrochemical Measurements

The catalyst (5 mg) was dispersed in 1 mL of an ethanol solution containing Nafion, and 200 μL of the resulting suspension was drop-cast onto a 1 × 1 cm carbon paper substrate to serve as a working electrode. Electrochemical performance evaluation was conducted with a three-electrode configuration on a CHI 660 potentiostat (Shanghai Chenhua Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). A platinum electrode functioned as the counter electrode, whereas a silver/silver chloride electrode was employed as the reference electrode. All experiments were performed in 1 M KOH. The calibration equation employed for potential measurements was E RHE = E Ag/AgCl + 0.0591 × pH + 0.197. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves of the catalysts were recorded at a scan rate of 50 mV s–1 in the voltage range of 1.0–2.5 V. The double layer capacitance was estimated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) at scan rates of 40, 80, 120, 60, 160, and 200 mV s–1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data were measured in the frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz. To evaluate durability, LSV curves of the catalyst were recorded after completing 1000 CV cycles.

Results and Discussion

Physical Characterizations

The microstructures of these samples were analyzed using XRD, UV–vis, and XPS. XRD patterns in Figure a reveal the evolution of the crystal structures of TPP, NiTPP, and their carbonized derivatives. The diffraction pattern of TPP exhibits broad and low-intensity features, with a weak hump appearing in the 2θ range of 10–30°, indicating a predominantly short-range ordered amorphous structure. This characteristic is attributed to the planar π-π stacking interactions of the porphyrin macrocycles. Upon coordination with Ni2+ to form NiTPP, the diffraction pattern changes significantly. Multiple sharp characteristic peaks appear in the 2θ range of 5–30°, with peak positions and relative intensities consistent with previously reported data for crystalline metalloporphyrins, indicating the highly crystalline nature of the metal–organic complex. Notably, the NiTPP sample carbonized at 400 °C still retains the original characteristic peaks, suggesting that no significant structural transformation occurs at this temperature. However, as the temperature has increased to 600 and 800 °C, the diffraction peaks associated with NiTPP almost completely disappear. Instead, only a broad (002) diffraction signal appears around 25°, which is characteristic of disordered graphitic structures commonly found in carbon materials. This observation suggests that high-temperature treatment induces the decomposition of the molecular framework and facilitates its transformation into an amorphous carbon-based material with a less ordered structure. ,

(a) XRD patterns of TPP, NiTPP, NiTPP-400, NiTPP-600, NiTPP-800; UV vis spectra of porphyrin compounds in DMF: (b) TPP, (c) NiTPP.

Figure b,c shows the UV–vis absorption spectra of the porphyrin compounds in DMF. A typical porphyrin compound displays distinct UV–vis absorption features arising from electronic transitions between the ground state (S0) and the two lowest excited singlet states, namely S1 (associated with Q bands) and S2 (corresponding to the Soret or B band). Transitions from S0 to S1 produce four relatively weak Q bands within the visible light range (500–700 nm), whereas transitions from S0 to S2 generate a pronounced Soret absorption band in the near-ultraviolet region (380–450 nm), which is attributed to π-π* transitions occurring in the porphyrin macrocycle (Figure S1). , In Figure b, the Soret band of TPP appears at 413 nm, while four distinct Q bands are observed at 513, 548, 592, and 648 nm. In free-base porphyrins, which possess D2h symmetry, these four Q bands arise from vibronic coupling to two allowed electronic transitions (Figure S2). Upon coordination with a metal ion, the symmetry of the complex increases to D4h, which simplifies the spectrum. When the nickel ion coordinates within the porphyrin core, the molecular symmetry changes, reducing the number of allowed transitions. Consequently, the Q bands coalesce into a single primary peak at 530 nm with no observable vibronic sidebands. The redshift of the B band may be attributed to enhanced π-conjugation induced by metal–ligand interactions or changes in molecular symmetry. These pronounced changes in the UV–vis absorption profile provide clear evidence of successful coordination between the metal ion and the porphyrin ligand, confirming the formation of a metalloporphyrin complex.

The XPS survey spectrum of NiTPP (Figure a) confirms the presence of C, N, and Ni elements in the synthesized composite. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum (Figure b) reveals three distinct peaks located at approximately 284.8, 285.4, and 289.1 eV. The peak at 284.8 eV is attributed to C–C/CC bonds originating from the phenyl groups, while the peak at 285.4 eV is ascribed to the aromatic rings and pyrrole subunits in the structure. The C–O peak observed at 289.2 eV is likely due to surface oxidation from ambient air exposure. In the N 1s spectrum (Figure c), the peak at 399.5 eV is attributed to Ni–N coordination, indicating the interaction between the central nickel ion and pyrrole nitrogen atoms. The peak at 403.2 eV corresponds to pyrrolic nitrogen. Collectively, these results confirm the successful synthesis of NiTPP. The Ni 2p spectrum (Figure d) displays four peaks at approximately 855.2, 860.8, 873.3, and 880.0 eV. The peaks at 855.2 and 873.3 eV correspond to Ni 2p3/2 and Ni 2p1/2, respectively, reflecting the binding energy of the Ni 2p3/2 and Ni 2p1/2 states. Additionally, satellite peaks are observed at 860.8 and 880.0 eV, further supporting the presence of nickel species in the prepared composites. Moreover, the data obtained from ICP-OES for all samples confirmed their detailed compositions, especially the loading of nickel content in the prepared samples.

(a) XPS full spectra of NiTPP; (b–d) high-resolution XPS spectra of C 1s, N 1s and Ni 2p, respectively.

Figure a–d presents SEM images illustrating the morphological evolution of NiTPP under different carbonization temperatures. In Figure a, the uncarbonized NiTPP sample exhibits a densely aggregated granular structure with indistinct particle boundaries and a broad size distribution, indicating low structural order. Upon carbonization at 400 °C, the material undergoes initial pyrolysis, forming block-like fragments with locally fractured edges and significantly increased surface roughness. At 600 °C, further structural reconstruction occurs, exposing irregular sheet-like structures with small pores on the surface, forming a three-dimensional network. When the temperature reaches 800 °C, the sample exhibits pronounced structural transformation, characterized by a loose and porous morphology. The image reveals highly dispersed ultrafine particles and nanosheets with abundant mesopores on the surface, while maintaining a continuous and coherent skeletal framework. In the TEM image (Figure e), the uniformly distributed nanoparticles and the lattice fringes with contrasting dark and light regions indicate the formation of a composite structure consisting of metallic nickel nanocrystals (dark regions) and graphitized carbon (light regions). The disordered amorphous background contrast likely corresponds to a residual amorphous carbon network formed during carbonization, which encapsulates and stabilizes the nanocrystals, providing a robust supporting framework that enhances structural integrity and electron transport. Meanwhile, elemental mapping using energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy (Figure g–I, Figure S3) confirms the uniform distribution of Ni, C, and N elements across the entire NiTPP-800 material, further supporting the formation of a homogeneously structured catalyst.

(a–d) SEM images of NiTPP, NiTPP-400, NiTPP-600 and NiTPP-800; (e) TEM images of NiTPP-800; (f) EDX spectrum of (g) nickel, (h) carbon, and (i) nitrogen of NiTPP-800.

To investigate the changes in the surface area and porous structure of the catalysts upon thermal treatment, nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were conducted. The pristine NiTPP precursor exhibits a Type II isotherm, which is characteristic of nonporous or macroporous materials (Figure a). The adsorption and desorption curves are nearly perfectly reversible with no hysteresis loop, indicating a very low specific surface area and a lack of significant porous structure. The samples pyrolyzed at 400 and 600 °C show isotherms that are very similar to the pristine precursor. They both display Type II behavior with minimal N2 uptake, suggesting that these lower pyrolysis temperatures are insufficient to generate a significant porous network within the material (Figure b,c). The specific surface areas for these samples remain low, consistent with the observed isotherm shapes. In stark contrast, the NiTPP-800 sample exhibits a composite isotherm with features of both Type I and Type IV. There is a sharp uptake of nitrogen at very low relative pressures (P/Po < 0.1), which is a hallmark of microporous materials (Type I isotherm). This indicates the formation of a significant volume of micropores during pyrolysis at 800 °C. Furthermore, a distinct H4-type hysteresis loop is observed in the higher relative pressure range (P/Po from ∼ 0.45 to 1.0). This Type IV behavior is characteristic of mesoporous materials, and the H4 loop is often associated with slit-like pores or narrow voids between plate-like particles. The BET analysis clearly demonstrates the profound effect of the pyrolysis temperature on the material’s morphology. The treatment at 800 °C is highly effective in transforming the nonporous NiTPP precursor into a hierarchical porous structure rich in both micropores and mesopores. This dramatic increase in specific surface area and the development of a porous network is crucial for exposing a greater number of active catalytic sites and facilitating efficient mass transport of reactants and products, which directly contributes to the superior electrocatalytic performance of the NiTPP-800 sample.

Electrochemical Analysis

The OER performances of TPP, NiTPP, NiTPP-400, NiTPP-600, and NiTPP-800 were evaluated in 1 M KOH electrolyte using a conventional three-electrode setup. Based on the LSV profiles presented in Figure a, NiTPP-800 exhibits the best electrocatalytic performance. At a current density of 20 mA cm–2, the overpotentials for NiTPP, NiTPP-400, NiTPP-600, and NiTPP-800 are 609 mV, 485 mV, 422 mV, and 347 mV, respectively. This observation clearly indicates that the catalytic activity progressively improves with increasing carbonization temperature, with NiTPP-800 showing the lowest overpotential and thus the highest OER catalytic capability. This is attributed to the high-temperature carbonization at 800 °C, which more effectively activates the catalytic nickel centers in NiTPP, enhances electronic conductivity, and exposes more electrochemically active sites. Additionally, the LSV curve of NiTPP-800 exhibits a distinct oxidation peak around 1.43 V, which likely results from the transformation of Ni2+ to Ni3+ species in the alkaline electrolyte, further supporting its excellent electrochemical behavior. , To evaluate the charge transfer kinetics at the electrode–electrolyte interface, Tafel slope analysis was performed, as illustrated in Figure b. Among the samples, NiTPP-800 exhibits the lowest Tafel slope of 86 mV dec–1, suggesting more efficient electron transfer at the interface. In contrast, NiTPP, NiTPP-400, and NiTPP-600 display significantly higher Tafel slopes of 205, 167, and 140 mV dec–1, respectively, indicating slower kinetics compared to NiTPP-800 (Table S2).

(a) OER polarization curves; (b) Tafel slope plots; (c) The double-layer capacitance; (d) Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, of the prepared samples.

The double-layer capacitance (C dl) was determined through cyclic voltammetry (CV) performed at different scan rates, as illustrated in Figure S4. Among the samples, NiTPP-800 exhibited the highest C dl value of 12.8 mF cm–2, considerably exceeding the values measured for NiTPP (0.68 mF cm–2), NiTPP-400 (0.94 mF cm–2) and NiTPP-600 (1.13 mF cm–2). This substantial increase indicates that NiTPP-800 possesses a much larger electrochemically active surface area, contributing to its superior catalytic performance. In addition, as the temperature increases, the area of the graph gradually increases, confirming that high-temperature carbonization significantly improves the electrochemical performance. In Figure c, the increasing slope with higher pyrolysis temperature confirms that high-temperature carbonization significantly improves electrochemical performance, which is consistent with its superior OER activity. This result is further supported by the charge transfer resistance (R ct) results obtained from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements. The R ct of each catalyst was determined using EIS, with the corresponding data presented in Figure d. The highest active catalyst, NiTPP-800, shows a reduced R ct value of 8.94 Ω, whereas NiTPP, NiTPP-400, and NiTPP-600 exhibit R ct values of 61.83, 40.06, and 18.45 Ω, respectively, suggesting that NiTPP-800 offers lower resistance to electron transfer across the interface.

Importantly, NiTPP-800 retained approximately 99% of its initial current density after 1000 CV cycles, demonstrating outstanding electrochemical stability, as shown by the negligible shift in the LSV curve (Figure ). To further confirm the structural integrity of our catalyst, we conducted SEM and EDX analysis on the NiTPP-800 electrode after the stability test. The postmortem SEM analysis reveals that the catalyst’s morphology was remarkably well-preserved (inset of Figure ). When compared to the images of the fresh catalyst, the posttest sample shows no signs of significant structural degradation, such as particle agglomeration, cracking, or delamination from the electrode substrate. The porous and interconnected architecture of the catalyst remained intact, even after prolonged exposure to the harsh oxidative conditions and vigorous gas evolution of the OER. This minimal decay in performance under continuous operation highlights the outstanding stability of the catalyst, which we attribute to the robust structure where active nickel species are securely embedded within the protective graphitic carbon matrix (Figure S5).

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated a controlled carbonization strategy for nickel tetraphenylporphyrin (NiTPP) to enhance OER activity. The carbonization process improves charge transport and promotes the formation of highly dispersed nickel active sites. The optimized catalyst, NiTPP-800, showed excellent OER activity (347 mV at 20 mA cm–2) and maintained 99% of its initial activity after 1000 CV cycles. This work not only provides an effective carbonization-based approach for modulating porphyrin-derived catalysts but also offers new experimental guidance for designing efficient and economically viable OER electrocatalysts for future clean energy technologies.
