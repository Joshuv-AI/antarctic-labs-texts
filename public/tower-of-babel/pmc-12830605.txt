CCL8 suppresses ovarian cancer progression via M1 macrophage polarization and NF-κB-mediated apoptosis
Authors: Jie Fang, Xinyue Zhao, Jin Wang, Yaping Yang, Yujia Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCCL8, a chemokine overexpressed in ovarian cancer (OC), has drawn attention for its role in tumor progression. This study aimed to explore the function of CCL8 in OC and its effects on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and related mechanisms. Bioinformatics analysis revealed a correlation between high CCL8 expression and M1 macrophage infiltration, as well as a favourable prognosis in OC patients. In vitro, CCL8 polarised THP1-derived macrophages towards an M1 phenotype, and the conditioned medium from these macrophages suppressed ES2 cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Mechanistically, CCL8-induced macrophages promoted apoptosis in OC cells via activation of the NF-κB p65 pathway, as evidenced by increased Bax and Caspase3 expression, and these effects were reversed by p65 inhibition. The findings demonstrate that CCL8 exerts a tumor-uppressive effect by inducing M1 macrophage polarisation and activating the NF-κB pathway, positioning it as a potential immunotherapeutic target in OC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32883-1.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Macrophages, CCL8, NF-κB, P65Subject terms: Cancer, Immunology

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is a common malignant tumors of female reproductive system. In 2020, there were about 314,000 new OC cases and more than 207,000 deaths worldwide, with a case-fatality rate of 60%1. Due to its insidious onset and lack of obvious early symptoms, OC often progresses silently, leading to widespread organ involvement and lymph node metastasis by the time of diagnosis. As a result, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, contributing to the persistently high mortality rate. The 5-year survival rate is only about 30%2. Therefore, it is important to understand the mechanisms underlying the development and progression of OC and to develop targeted therapies.

CC motif chemokine ligand 8 (CCL8), also known as monocyte chemoattractant protein-2 (MCP-2), is a key chemokine involved in inflammatory and immune responses. In the tumor microenvironment, CCL8 plays diverse and context-dependent roles. It can be secreted by tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune cells in response to pro-inflammatory stimuli such as IL-1β and IFN-γ3. CCL8 recruits various immune cells, including monocytes, T cells, eosinophils, and natural killer (NK) cells, many of which participate in anti-tumor immunity4,5. CCL8 plays a complex and highly context-specific role in tumor biology, acting either as a tumor suppressor or promoter depending on the tumor type and immune landscape. In melanoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, CCL8 has been reported to exert anti-tumor effects by enhancing immune cell infiltration and promoting cytotoxic responses6,7. Conversely, in acute myelogenous leukaemia, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and some other malignancies, CCL8 has been implicated in promoting tumor progression, metastasis, and immune evasion. It can facilitate epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), enhance tumor cell migration, and shape an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by attracting tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) or regulatory T cells8–12. Although CCL8 has been implicated in various aspects of tumor biology, the precise mechanisms and clinical implications of CCL8 in OC remain to be fully elucidated.

This study utilized bioinformatics approaches to investigate the expression of CCL8 mRNA in OC tissues and to analyze the correlation between CCL8 expression and macrophage infiltration and activation within the ovarian tumor microenvironment. In addition, we performed in vitro experiments in which ES2 cells were co-cultured with supernatants from CCL8-induced polarized macrophages to examine the effects of CCL8-treated macrophages on tumor cell behavior and to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms. These findings provide novel insights into the immunomodulatory role of CCL8 in OC and offer potential directions for targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

TCGA-GTEx data mining

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in OC were identified using transcriptome data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx, https://www.genome.gov/Funded-Programs-Projects/Genotype-Tissue-Expression-Project) databases. RNA-seq data were processed in Transcripts Per Million (TPM) format. A total of 36,724 unpaired samples were included in the analysis, comprising 15,654 normal ovarian tissue samples from GTEx and 21,070 tumor samples from TCGA.

Differential expression analysis between OC and normal tissue groups was performed in R. Significantly differentially expressed genes were identified using the criteria |log2FoldChange| ≥ 4 and P <.001. A volcano plot was generated to visualize these DEGs, and fold change (FC) values represented the magnitude of gene expression differences.

Functional enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes was conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway via R and the result was visualized using dot plot.

The GSE18520 dataset from the NCBI GEO database was used to visualize the expression levels of selected genes in OC and normal ovarian tissues through heatmap analysis using R. Perform gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on genes from normal ovarian tissue and ovarian cancer tissue. ES: Enrichment Score; FDR: False Discovery Rate.

Additionally, Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA1.0, http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) was used to compare CCL8 expression between normal ovarian and OC tissues, and to analyze its correlation with cancer stage and patient prognosis.

To further explore immune correlations, we used the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER2.0, https://compbio.cn/timer2/) to assess the relationship between CCL8 expression and the infiltration levels of total macrophages, M1 macrophages, as well as the mRNA expression of inflammatory cytokines. TIMER also provided a prognostic analysis based on CCL8 expression and the abundance of M1 macrophages.

Human study

A total of ten paired epithelial OC tissue samples and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing ovarian surgery in the Department of Gynecology at the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University. All samples were pathologically verified. Patients who received preoperative treatment, had a history of cancer or other complications were excluded from the study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University (approval number: KY2023K0406), and all participants signed a written informed consent. Information about the OC samples used was summarised in Table S1. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture

Human myeloid leukaemia mononuclear cells (THP1) and normal ovarian epithelial cell line IOSE80 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)−1640 medium (MA0215-2, Mellen Biologics, China), while the human OC cell line ES2 was cultured in high-sucrose Dulcimer’s modified eagle medium (DMEM, MA0212-2, Meron Bio, China). All medium were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, CA0075, Regan, China) and cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 concentration. The cell lines were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. All cultured cells were free of mycoplasma contamination. The identification of THP1, IOSE80 and ES2 cell lines was confirmed to be in accordance with the standard by short tandem repeat sequence (STR) analysis.

Polarization and stimulation of THP1-derived macrophages

THP1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and exposed to 100 ng/mL PMA (2-Mercaptoethanol, phorbol 12-myristate 12-acetate16561-29-8, Sigma, USA) for 24 h to induce differentiation into M0 macrophages13. M0 macrophages were then exposed to 50 ng/mL of Recombinant Human CCL8/MCP-2 Protein (RP01655,ABclonal, China) for 0 h、24 h、48 h to induce M0 macrophage polarisation, and an equal volume of PBS was added to the control group. Supernatants of CCL8-macrophage conditioned medium (CCL8-CM) and PBS-macrophage conditioned medium (NC-CM) were collected by centrifugation after 48 h of induction.

ES2 cells were inoculated into 6-well cell culture plates (3 × 10⁵/well), and the original medium was replaced with medium containing CCL8-CM or NC-CM (1:1 ratio with DMEM medium) after wall attachment. After 48 h, ES2 cells were collected to detect the expression of NFκB/p65 and apoptosis-related proteins by protein blotting assay, and apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry for membrane-associated protein V/7-amino actinomycin (Annexin V/7-AAD) and TUNEL staining. For inhibitor studies, 10 mM NFκB/p65 inhibitor (S3633, Selleck, USA) was added to ES2 cells along with CCL8-CM.

Flow cytometry

THP1 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in a 6-well plate and treated with 100 ng/mL PMA for 24 h to induce differentiation into M0 macrophages. Subsequently, M0 macrophages were treated with 50 ng/mL recombinant human CCL8/MCP-2 protein for 24–48 h to induce polarization, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. Cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min per wash before discarding the supernatant. Cells were resuspended at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/100 µL in centrifuge tubes using 1 × Flow Cytometry Staining Buffer (PF00018, Proteintech, China). 1 × 10⁶ THP1 cells were surface co-stained with 5 µL FITC Anti-Human CD206 (Anti-Human CD206 Rabbit Recombinant Antibody, 98458-3-RR, Proteintech, China) and 5 µL PE Anti-Human CD86 (BU63) (PE-65165, Proteintech, China) or left unstained, then incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 40 min. Wash cells with 1 × PBS, centrifuge at 500 × g for 5 min, and discard the supernatant. Resuspend cells in 400 µL 1 × flow cytometry staining buffer. Cells were not fixed. Monocytes were gated.

ES2 cells were inoculated into 6-well plates. The Annexin V-PE/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The brief steps were as follows: cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS, then 100 µL 1 × Binding Buffer was added and gently blown to a single-cell suspension, while 5 µL Annexin V-PE and 5 µL 7-AAD Staining Solution were added and gently blown; the cells were incubated for 10 min, protected from light and at room temperature (20 ~ 25 °C); and 400 µL 1 × Binding Buffer and mix gently. The apoptosis rate was determined by flow cytometry and the data were analysed using FlowJo software.

ELISA

Measure CCL8 using the Human CCL8 ELISA Kit instructions. Collect the supernatant from CCL8-treated THP1 cells at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h, dilute it 100-fold. Mix samples with the CCL8 reagent at a 5:1 volume ratio, centrifuge at 3000 × g for 20 min at 2–8 °C, incubate at 37 °C in a water bath or incubator for 60 min. Transfer the supernatant to a 96-well plate and measure absorbance at 450 nm. CCL8 content was calculated based on a standard curve generated using CCL8 standards (0–800 pg/mL).

Plasmids transfection

The CCL8 overexpression plasmid was introduced into PMA-activated THP1 cells using Lipofectamine 6000 transfection reagent produced by Beyotime. The experiment commenced 48 h post-transfection.

Cell colony formation experiments

Groups of cells in logarithmic growth phase were collected, washed with PBS, digested with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco), dispersed into single cells, and counted. 1000 cells were placed in each well of a 6-well cell culture plate and incubated for 14 days (37 °C, 5% CO2). The culture was terminated when clonal cell clusters were visible to the naked eye. The culture medium was aspirated and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Next, add 4% paraformaldehyde and fix for 20 min. The fixative was discarded and the cells were stained with crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min, then photographed and the clonal area was calculated14.

CCK-8 assay

1000 cells per well were seeder in a 96-well cell culture plate, and incubated for 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h. Subsequently, the complete medium was replaced with CCK-8 solution (composed of 90 µL of DMEM complete medium and 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent), and light avoidance was maintained during the operation15. After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C in an incubator, the OD450 value was measured using an microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Transwell migration and invasion test

The cells of each group were seeded into the upper chamber of a 24-well transwell plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well, with 150 µL of FBS-free medium added to the upper chamber and 600 µL of complete medium added to the lower chamber. After culturing for 24 h at 37 °C, the medium from the upper chamber are removed and the cells on the lower surface of the membrane are the migrated cells. Fix the cells beneath the upper chamber surface with 4% paraformaldehyde and stain them with 0.1% crystal violet solution. Then, three fields were randomly selected under a microscope for counting. The Transwell invasion assay was performed according to the aforementioned protocol, with modifications wherein 50 µL of Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) diluted 1:4 in DMEM was added to each chamber and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Subsequently, cells were seeded onto the membrane surface and maintained in culture for an additional 24 h16,17.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to the experimental procedure of Mouse/Rabbit IgG - Immunohistochemistry Kit (SABC Ready-to-Use) (SA1020, Boster, China). Sections were incubated with CCL8 (MCP-2 Rabbite pAb, bs-1984R, Bioss, China) or CXCL9 (22355-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) primary antibody at a dilution of 1:500 at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, 100 µL of secondary antibody was added to each slice in an incubator at 37℃ for 30 min. Following this, tissue sections were subjected to 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining for 5 min, after which hematoxylin counterstaining was performed. Ultimately, all specimens were permanently preserved using neutral balsam following dehydration through a graded ethanol series. Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated using a semi-quantitative H-score system that integrated staining intensity (scored 0–3: negative, weak, moderate, strong) and the percentage of positive cells (0–100%). The final H-score (range 0–300) was calculated as: Σ(intensity × percentage). The average optical density (AOD) was quantified using ImageJ-win64 software by two independent pathologists blinded to sample identity. For each specimen, at least 5 random high-power fields (×400 magnification) were analyzed, with background subtraction performed using adjacent non-staining tissue areas18.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total cellular RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (R401, Vazyme, China) and the purity and concentration of total RNA was assessed using a Nano Drop One spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). RNA was passed through the HiScript 1 st Strand cDNA synthesis kit (MR101-02, Thermo, USA)Reverse transcription to cDNA was performed and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was carried out in a real-time PCR detection system (CFX96, Bio-Rad, USA) using ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q312-02, Vazyme, China). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal reference gene. The primer sequences were shown in Table S2. The expression values were normalized to the average CT value of GAPDH.

Protein extraction and Western blotting

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (89901, Thermo Scientific, USA) containing protease inhibitors. The protein concentration of total extracts was determined by BCA protein quantification kit (Beo Tianmei, China). Approximately 40 µg of protein per sample was separated by 12% One-Step PAGE Gel Rapid Preparation Kit (E304-01, Vazyme, China) and transferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). After blocked with 5% non-fat milk, the membrane was separated from CD80 (66406-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), CD206 (60143-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), MMP2 (66366-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), MMP9 (60600-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), Ecad (BS1098, Bioworld, China), Ncad (13116 S, CST, USA), Pcna (13110 S, CST, USA), Oct4 (60242-1-Ig, proteintech, China), Bax (50599-2-Ig, proteintech, China), Bcl2 (68103-1-Ig, proteintech, China), Caspase3 (9664 S, CST, USA), NFκB/p65 (80979-1-RR, Proteintech, China) primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-labelled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein expression levels were detected by chemiluminescence (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and normalized to GAPDH (AC027, ABclonal, China). The nuclear protein was normalized using H3 (Histone H3 Polyclonal antibody, 17168-1-AP, Proteintech, China) as the internal reference.

Apoptosis assay

To determine the rate of apoptosis in ES2 cells, we performed a terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL) assay using the TUNEL BrightRed Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, A113) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Red fluorescent (TUNEL positive cells) were counted as apoptotic cells to calculate the apoptosis rate19.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed and plotted using GraphPad software (GraphPad 9.5.1, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical differences between the two groups were analysed by parametric or non-parametric Student’s t-test, and multiple comparisons were analysed by one-way or two-way ANOVA. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*P <.05, **P <.01, ***P <.001, ****P <.0001). All experiments were repeated independently at least three times.

Results

CCL8 is highly expressed in OC and associated with good prognosis

We analyzed transcriptome data from the TCGA-GTEx database and identified 2,489 up-regulated and 932 down-regulated genes in OC tissues compared to normal ovarian tissues (Fig. 1A). KEGG pathway20 analysis of up-regulated DEGs were mainly involved in cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, chemokine signaling pathways, and viral protein-cytokine and cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 1B). A Venn diagram revealed 15 overlapping genes among these three pathways, all of which were chemokines (Fig. 1C). Chemokines are involved in the recruitment and activation of various immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, directly influencing tumor development and progression21. We used a heatmap to visualize the expression levels of these 15 chemokines in OC and normal ovarian tissues using the GEO database (GSE18520). The results showed that several chemokines were upregulated in OC, with CCL8 showing the most significant increase (FC = 1.48, P <.0001) (Fig. 1D). GSEA also revealed that genes associated with NF-κB-activated kinase activity exhibited enriched expression in OC tissue compared to normal ovarian tissue (Fig. 1E).

Consistent with this, analysis via the GEPIA platform confirmed that CCL8 mRNA expression was elevated in OC tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 1F). We found that CCL8 was highly expressed in the OC cell line (ES2) compared to normal ovarian epithelial cells (IOSE80) (Fig. 1G). Immunohistochemistry showed increased CCL8 protein expression in OC tissues compared to normal ovarian tissues(Fig. 1H). Surprisingly, analysis of TCGA data revealed that higher CCL8 expression in OC patients was associated with longer overall survival (Fig. 1I) and earlier tumor stage progression (Fig. 1J), suggesting a potential protective role of CCL8 in OC.

Together, these findings suggest that CCL8 is highly expressed in OC and may be associated with a favorable prognosis.

CCL8 is positively correlated with M1 macrophage infiltration and activation in OC tissues

TAMs in the tumor microenvironment (TME) are highly heterogeneous and easily influenced by various cytokines. They can polarize into M1-like (anti-tumor) or M2-like (pro-tumor) phenotypes22,23, plays a vital role in the progression of OC. Although CCL8 is a member of the chemokine family, it has been demonstrated to not only mediate macrophage recruitment but also participate in the regulation of macrophage polarization within the tumor microenvironment24. To explore the association between CCL8 expression and macrophage infiltration in OC tissues, we utilized the TIMER database and found that CCL8 expression was positively correlated with macrophage infiltration, particularly with M1 macrophages (Fig. 2A). Previous studies have shown that M1-type macrophages are characterized by markers such as CD80 and CD8625,26, and are capable of secreting cytokines including IL-1β, IFN-γ, CCL2, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 to activate T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity27–31,. Consistently, we found that CCL8 expression was positively correlated with the expression of these M1 macrophage-related markers in OC tissues (Fig. 2B). Immunohistochemical analysis of OC tissues revealed a positive correlation between CCL8 and CXCL9 protein expression, as regions with high CCL8 expression consistently showed elevated CXCL9 levels, while regions with low CCL8 expression exhibited reduced CXCL9 expression (Fig. 2C). Notably, OC patients with high CCL8 expression and abundant M1 macrophage infiltration in tumor tissues exhibited the longest overall survival (Fig. 2D).

These findings suggest that CCL8 may promote anti-tumor immunity in OC by enhancing M1 macrophage infiltration and activation, thereby contributing to improved patient survival.

CCL8-induced M1 macrophages inhibits proliferation, invasion and migration of ES2 cells

To further investigate the role of CCL8 in macrophage polarization, we treated THP1-derived macrophages with exogenous recombinant CCL8 protein at time points of 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h. The results of this study demonstrate that following a 48 h exposure to CCL8, mRNA expression levels of genes associated with proinflammatory responses and M1 macrophage polarization significantly increased, including IL-1β, IL-632, and CXCL10. Conversely, genes associated with anti-inflammatory responses and M2 polarization, including CD206, CD163, and IL-10, remained largely stable, while ARG expression was significantly downregulated (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, Western blot analysis revealed that treatment of THP1 derived macrophages with CCL8 for 48 h upregulated the protein expression of the M1 marker CD80, while downregulating the expression of the M2 marker CD206 (Fig. 3B). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that the proportion of CD80+ cells significantly increased, whereas the proportion of CD206+ cells remained unchanged (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that CCL8 promotes the polarization of THP1 macrophages toward the M1 phenotype. ELISA results showed that following stimulation with exogenous recombinant CCL8 protein, THP1 cells exhibited a time-dependent decrease in CCL8 levels in the conditioned medium. Compared to the 0 h baseline, CCL8 content in the 24 h group was significantly reduced, while the 48 h group further decreased to levels similar to the NC group (Fig. S1). This result suggests that exogenous CCL8 protein may be continuously consumed or degraded by THP1 cells during stimulation.

To further investigate the effects of CCL8 on THP1 cells, a stable CCL8-overexpressing THP1 cell line was successfully established via plasmid transfection, and the overexpression efficiency was verified (Fig. S2A). The results of the PCR analysis revealed that, following the plasmid-mediated expression of CCL8, there was an increased expression of M1-type markers (IL-6, CXCL9, IL-10) and a decreased expression of M2-type markers (CD206, CD163) in the THP1 cells (Fig. S2B). The analysis of the samples via flow cytometry revealed that the overexpression of CCL8 led to a significant increase in the surface expression of the M1 marker CD86 (Fig. S2C). In contrast, the expression level of the M2 marker CD206 remained unchanged. These results further confirm that CCL8 induces THP1 cells to polarize toward M1-type macrophages.

To mimic the crosstalk between TAMs and ES2 cells within theTME, and to investigate the effects of cytokines secreted by CCL8-induced polarized macrophages, we treated THP1-derived macrophages with recombinant CCL8 protein, then collected the conditioned media (CM). ES2 cells were subsequently cultured with either control CM (NC-CM) or CCL8-treated CM (CCL8-CM), followed by collection of ES2 cells for phenotypic and functional assays (Fig. 3D). The colony formation assay revealed reduced clonogenic ability in the CCL8-CM group compared to the NC-CM (Fig. 3E), while the CCK-8 assay showed a time-dependent decrease in ES2 cell viability following treatment with the CCL8-CM (Fig. 3F). Moreover, transwell assays demonstrated that both migration and invasion abilities of ES2 cells were significantly impaired in the CCL8-CM group compared to NC-CM (Fig. 3G and H).

Further Western blot analysis revealed altered expression of metastasis-associated proteins and EMT-related proteins33. E-cadherin (Ecad) expression was upregulated, while MMP9, MMP2 and N-cadherin (Ncad) was downregulated, in ES2 cells exposed to the CCL8-CM. Additionally, the expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (Pcna)34,35 as well as the backbone transcription factor, octamer-binding protein 4 (Oct4)36,37 was significantly reduced (Fig. 3I).

These findings suggest that CCL8 promotes M1-like polarization of THP1 macrophages, which in turn secrete factors that suppress ES2 cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and EMT.

CCL8-induced M1 macrophages promotes apoptosis in ES2 cells via the NF-κB p65 pathway

GSEA analysis revealed significantly elevated gene expression associated with NF-κB kinase activity in OC tissues (Fig. 1E). To validate this finding, we performed Western blot analysis on CCL8-CM-treated ES2 cells. Results showed a marked increase in p65 protein levels in nuclear extracts from CCL8-CM-treated ES2 cells compared to the NC-CM group, while cytoplasmic p65 expression remained stable and relatively low (Fig. 4A). This indicates that CCL8-CM activates the NF-κB signaling pathway by promoting the translocation of p65 protein from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. Related studies suggest that NF-κB signaling activation may be associated with cancer cell apoptosis38.

To investigate this, we examined two key apoptosis-related proteins in CCL8-CM-treated ES2 cells: the proapoptotic protein Bax, involved in mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis39,40, and the critical executioner of apoptosis, Caspase341 Western blot analysis revealed significantly elevated expression levels of both Bax and Caspase3 proteins (Fig. 4B). TUNEL staining revealed markedly increased tumor cell apoptosis in the CCL8-CM group (Fig. 4C). Similarly, Annexin V-PE and 7-AAD flow cytometric analysis demonstrated enhanced apoptotic activity in CCL8-treated ES2 cells compared to the NC-CM group (Fig. 4D).

To further validate the role of NF-κB p65 pathway activation in apoptosis, we co-administered a p65-specific inhibitor (p65i) with CCL8-CM treatment. Western blot analysis demonstrated that the p65 inhibitor effectively blocked the translocation of p65 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus in ES2 cells (Fig. 4E). Following p65 inhibition, the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-240 was upregulated, while Bax and Caspase3 expression was downregulated (Fig. 4F). TUNEL staining and flow cytometry analysis both confirmed the reversal of apoptosis (Fig. 4G and H).

Collectively, these results indicate that CCL8 promotes ES2 cell apoptosis by inducing M1 macrophage polarization and activating the NF-κB p65 signaling pathway.

Discussion

OC remains one of the most lethal gynecological malignancies, largely due to its asymptomatic early stages, frequent late diagnosis, high recurrence rate, and development of chemoresistance42–46. This study identified CCL8 as a chemokine highly expressed in OC tissues and associated with favorable prognosis. CCL8 expression correlated with M1 macrophage infiltration and activation, and CCL8-induced macrophages triggered p65 nuclear translocation and apoptosis in ES2 cells. These results suggest that CCL8 acts as a beneficial immune modulator and a potential therapeutic target in OC.

Chemokines and their receptors are expressed by nearly all cell types within the tumor microenvironment, including epithelial, immune, mesenchymal, endothelial, and other stromal cells47. A large-scale analysis of 14,492 solid tumors revealed that a 12-chemokine signature—comprising CCL2/3/4/5/8/18/19/21 and CXCL9/10/11/13—was associated with the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures and improved overall survival in a subset of melanoma patients48. Recent studies indicate that CXCL9 is highly expressed in OC and positively correlates with patient survival49. Similar to our study, we found that CCL8 is highly expressed in OC tissues and cell lines, suggesting that tumor cells may be a major source of CCL8 within the tumor immune microenvironment. Its elevated expression was associated with earlier tumor stage and improved patient survival, implying a potential tumor-suppressive function in OC.

CCL8, a member of the CC chemokine family, plays roles in both inflammatory responses and immune regulation50,51. The role of CCL8 in cancer appears to be closely linked to the types of immune cells it regulates. In head and neck cancers, CCL8 functions as a pro-inflammatory cytokine that recruits dendritic cells, thereby enhancing responses to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy52. In non-small cell lung cancer, the CCL8-CCR2 axis can recruit CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T cells to infiltrate and kill tumor cells53. However, its impact on macrophage polarization remains controversial, with studies reporting both pro-tumor and anti-tumor effects depending on the tumor context. For example, in cervical cancer, hypoxia-induced ZEB1 drives the CCL8–CCR2 axis, promoting M2-like TAM infiltration and facilitating a metastasis-supportive microenvironment54. In contrast, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, CCL8 correlates with M1 macrophage infiltration and enhances anti-tumor immunity, especially when combined with photodynamic therapy7,55. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that CCL8 expression in the OC microenvironment positively correlated with macrophage infiltration, particularly the M1 subtype, which is known for its anti-tumorigenic function56. Additionally, CCL8 levels were strongly associated with M1-related markers, including CD80, CD86, IL-1β, IFN-γ, CCL2, and CXCL9/10/11, as well as increased CXCL9 protein expression. Importantly, OC patients with high CCL8 expression and abundant M1 macrophage infiltration had the most favorable prognoses, supporting a potential immune-activating and tumor-suppressive role of CCL8 in OC.

To validate these observations, we conducted in vitro experiments using THP1-derived macrophages. Upon treatment with recombinant CCL8, THP1 cells exhibited increased expression of M1 markers such as IL-1β, IL-6, CXCL9, CXCL10, and iNOS, confirming M1-like polarization. When ES2 cells were cultured with conditioned media from these CCL8-polarized macrophages, their proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT progression were significantly inhibited. These results suggest that CCL8 exerts anti-tumor effects by reprogramming TAMs toward a tumor-suppressive M1 phenotype and altering the tumor-supportive microenvironment.

Additionally, we identified the NF-κB p65 signaling pathway as a key downstream mechanism through GSEA analysis and investigated the pro-apoptotic effects of CCL8-induced macrophages. While the NF-κB pathway is often associated with tumor promotion, particularly through its role in immune response and cell proliferation57–59, evidence also suggests that NF-κB can promote apoptosis and act as a tumor suppressor in certain contexts. For instance, NF-κB activation enhances p53-mediated apoptosis60, predicts better prognosis in gastric cancer61, and promotes expression of pro-apoptotic genes such as PDCD5 and Bax in breast cancer62. Supporting the tumor-suppressive aspect of NF-κB signaling, our study found that CCL8-polarized M1 like macrophages increased p65 nuclear translocation and activated downstream apoptosis in ES2 cells, as demonstrated by upregulation of Bax and Caspase3, increased TUNEL-positive cells, and higher apoptotic rates by flow cytometry. Importantly, inhibition of p65 reversed these effects, restoring Bcl-2 expression and reducing apoptosis, indicating that CCL8 promotes ES2 cell apoptosis via activation of the p65/NF-κB signaling pathway.

The results of this study indicate that CCL8 plays a previously underexplored anti-tumor role in OC, mediated through M1 macrophage polarization and activation of the NF-κB signaling cascade in tumor cells. These findings not only provide mechanistic insights into the immunomodulatory function of CCL8 in the OC microenvironment but also suggest that targeting the CCL8–NF-κB axis may offer promising therapeutic potential in OC treatment.
