Disseminated Cryptococcal Gattii infection in a patient with anti-granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating-factor autoantibody: a case report
Authors: Chi-Tung Chen, Mao-Wang Ho, Wei-Hsin Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCryptococcosis is an opportunistic fungal infection in immunocompromised patients. The major species include Cryptococcus grubii, Cryptococcus neoformans, and rarely, Cryptococcus gattii. Here we present a disseminated Cryptococcus gattii infection in a patient with elevated granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating-factor autoantibody which was successfully treated with antifungal therapy.Case presentationA 61-year-old healthy man presented with a 3-week history of blurred vision, low-grade fever, headache, and a one-year history of low back pain following a fall on his farm. Physical examination revealed lower back tenderness and diplopia. He was tested negative for hepatitis B, C, and human immunodeficiency virus. Chest X-ray revealed a focal opacity in the right retrocardiac paraspinal region and pleural effusion. Magnetic resonance imaging showed a mass located at the L1 prevertebral region and multiple rim-enhancing lesions in bilateral cerebral hemispheres. Thoracoscopy demonstrated cystic lesions at the right costopleural angle. Pathology and microbiology studies confirmed the diagnosis of disseminated Cryptococcus gatti infection. Autoantibodies to granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor were detected and were considered to cause disseminated cryptococcosis. The patient was started on amphotericin B followed by fluconazole treatment. One month later, the symptoms ameliorated and repeated image studies after 1 year of follow-up showed the resolution of lesions.ConclusionThis report describes the first case of disseminated Cryptococcus gattii infection involving the musculoskeletal system, respiratory system, and central nervous system with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating-factor autoantibody by evidence of histology and microbiology.

Introduction

Cryptococcosis is an opportunistic and the most life-threatening mycoses worldwide which have wide-ranging presentations from asymptomatic to disseminated infection [1]. Epidemiologically, cryptococcosis infections are classified into three categories, including patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection [2], solid organ transplantation [3], and lastly non-HIV/nontransplant patients (NHNT). Recently, NHNT patients were reported to account for 20–50% of cryptococcosis cases [4]. Importantly, the unawareness of cryptococcosis in NHNT, in fact, resulted in worse outcomes than those in people with HIV [5] with a 50–80% mortality in 90-day mortality of cirrhotic patients, and 18% mortality in the 30-day among patients with lymphoproliferative diseases [6].

Cryptococcus gattii, in contrast with Cryptococcus neoformans, is endemic in Africa and Australia and can cause disseminated infections, defined by positive blood culture or positive culture from at least 2 different sites, in immunocompetent hosts. Among patients without known risk factors, elevated anti-granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating-factor (anti-GM-CSF) autoantibody was found to be associated with Cryptococcus gattii central meningitis [7]. Here we present the first case of anti-GM-CSF autoantibody-associated disseminated Cryptococcus gattii infection.

Case presentation

The 61-year-old Taiwanese male plumber presented to the emergency department with 3 weeks of blurred vision, low-grade fever, and headache. The medical history was unremarkable except for intermittent low-back pain after a falling accident while working about 1 year ago for which he did not seek medical attention. Six months before the presentation, he developed a chronic cough and rhinorrhea. The physical examination revealed a temperature of 37.5℃ without hemodynamic compromise. Neurological examination showed bilateral diplopia with left lower limb weakness with a muscle power 4 out of 5. The personal history was unremarkable apart from being a smoker. He denies alcohol abuse, illicit drug use, and high-risk sexual activities. Laboratory studies only revealed mild leucocytosis.

Serial image studies were performed to identify the infection focus. Chest X-ray revealed a focal opacity in the right retrocardiac paraspinal region in chest x-ray (Fig. 1). The opacity was confirmed by the spinal magnetic resonance image (MRI) showing a well-defined mass with a size of 9 × 10 × 11 cm, located over the prevertebral region at T10 to L1 levels and encasing the abdominal aorta (Fig. 2). Brain MRI revealed a high signal, numeral small rim-enhancing lesions involving bilateral cerebral hemispheres, together with slight leptomeningeal enhancement (Fig. 3). A decision was made to surgically remove the mass to alleviate the severe back pain and focal neurologic sign and obtain tissue proof of this mass. Histological staining methods with Grocott methenamine silver and Periodic acid-Schiff were both positive confirming the cryptococcus infection. (Fig. 4).

After the surgery, progressive dyspnea and fever developed. Chest computed tomography revealed an empyema formation, therefore thoracoscopic decortication of the pleura was performed. A cystic lesion with mucus content was noted at the right side costopleural angle (Fig. 5). To elucidate the underlying cause of heightened susceptibility to occult infection, sequential immunologic assessments and screening for anti-cytokine autoantibodies were performed and confirmed the elevated anti-GM-CSF autoantibody. Eventually, Cryptococcus gattii was isolated from his pus culture and blood culture.

The patient was started on a 4-week course of IV amphotericin B (0.7- 1.0 mg/kg/day) followed by oral flucytosine (100 mg/kg/day). Fluconazole (400 mg/day) was given for consolidation therapy for 8 weeks and reduced to 200 mg per day for maintenance. One year after discharge, he remains pain-free and there was no re-accumulation of the abscess.

Discussion

Cryptococcosis is a fungal infection resulting in significant mortality. Two major species Cryptococcus neoformans, and Cryptococcus gattii have numerous different geographical distributions and host predilections [8]. Cryptococcus neoformans is universally distributed and mostly isolated from avian feces. In contrast, Cryptococcus gattii is more prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions and mostly found in Eucalyptus trees [9]. Cryptococcus neoformans accounts for the majority of cryptococcal infection in immunocompromised patients, and Cryptococcus gattii, in contrast, affects mainly NHNT individuals [10]. The symptoms may range from asymptomatic pulmonary infections to potentially life-threatening CNS infections. Other sites of dissemination include skin, bones, joints, kidneys, adrenal glands, and spleen. Being influenced by early diagnosis and aggressive antiviral therapy in HIV, the incidence of NHNT cryptococcosis has doubled in the past 10 years [11]. To further pursue the route of exposure to Cryptococcus gattii, details of personal history were obtained, which revealed that the patient has been living in a specific area only where Eucalyptus trees can be found in Taichung, the city in the middle of Taiwan. This finding emphasizes the importance of inquiring about the meticulous details of personal history in managing infectious diseases.

Anti-GM-CSF antibodies have been reported to influence immunological effects on monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, which play an important role in host control of Cryptococcus [12]. These antibodies have been found in normal individuals which could lead to cryptococcal infection following exposure. After inhalation, the yeast cells are lodged in the alveoli of the lungs, where they can replicate, and form localized infections. The fungus avoids detection and destruction by the thick polysaccharide capsule, inhibiting phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages. GM-CSF is crucial for activating alveolar macrophages and enhancing their ability to kill fungi. In the presence of anti-GM-CSF autoantibodies, the ability was impaired and allows the proliferation in the lungs. After proliferating in the lungs, the fungus spreads through the bloodstream by hijacking macrophages to other organs, including the CNS [13–15]. This highlights the direct relationship between cryptococcosis and anti-GM-CSF autoantibodies in these NHNT patients.

It was shown in the case that the optimal treatment for disseminated Cryptococcus gattii infection is based on the severity of the disease, the organ systems involved (especially the CNS), and the patient’s immune status. Therapy generally follows the approach used for disseminated cryptococcal infections, with adjustments for C. gattii, as this species may require prolonged therapy due to its virulence and tendency for delayed resolution. Broad-spectrum fungicidal action of Amphotericin B binds to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane, causing pore formation and cell death. This makes it a fungicidal agent, particularly effective against Cryptococcus species, including Cryptococcus gattii. On the other hand, flucytosine inhibits fungal deoxyribonucleic acid and ribonucleic acid synthesis after being converted to 5-fluorouracil within the fungal cells. When flucytosine is combined with Amphotericin B, more flucytosine enters the fungal cell, resulting in effective eradication of the infection, and reducing the risk of relapse. After initial induction therapy with amphotericin B and flucytosine for 2–6 weeks (depending on severity), patients are usually transitioned to fluconazole for consolidation and maintenance therapy, as fluconazole is effective for long-term suppression and has better oral availability. Lifelong therapy may be required for patients with persistent immune defects [16].

There are 14 reported cases demonstrating the association between anti-GM-CSF and Cryptococcus gattii meningitis without the coexistence of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis but a case with systemic infection with comprehensive studies has not been reported [17]. Our case report is the first, to the best of our knowledge, disseminated Cryptococcus gatti infection involving the musculoskeletal system, central nervous system, and respiratory system presenting with low back pain. We demonstrated comprehensive image studies and biochemical studies and confirmed the elevated anti-GM-CSF antibody. These findings underscore the importance of recognizing the association between anti-GM-CSF antibodies in immunocompetent patients and disseminated cryptococcal infection, facilitating early diagnosis, prompt initiation of treatment, and reduction of mortality.
