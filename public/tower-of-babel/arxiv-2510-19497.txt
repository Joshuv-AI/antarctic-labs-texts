Modeling realistic human behavior using generative agents in a multimodal transport system: Software architecture and Application to Toulouse
Trung-Dung Vu, Benoit Gaudou, Kamaldeep Singh Oberoi

Abstract
Modeling realistic human behaviour to understand people’s mode choices in order to propose personalised mobility solutions remains challenging. This paper presents an architecture for modeling realistic human mobility behavior in complex multimodal transport systems, demonstrated through a case study in Toulouse, France. We apply Large Language Models (LLMs) within an agent-based simulation to capture decision-making in a real urban setting. The framework integrates the GAMA simulation platform with an LLM-based generative agent, along with General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) data for public transport, and OpenTripPlanner for multimodal routing. GAMA platform models the interactive transport environment, providing visualization and dynamic agent interactions while eliminating the need to construct the simulation environment from scratch. This design enables a stronger focus on developing generative agents and evaluating their performance in transport decision-making processes. Over a simulated month, results show that agents not only make context-aware transport decisions but also form habits over time. We conclude that combining LLMs with agent-based simulation offers a promising direction for advancing intelligent transportation systems and personalised multimodal mobility solutions. We also discuss some limitations of this approach and outline future work on scaling to larger regions, integrating real-time data, and refining memory models.

Introduction

Understanding human mobility behavior in multimodal transport systems is essential for advancing traffic planning and the design of efficient transport networks. The challenge lies not only in representing travel patterns but also in building a simulation framework that supports the study of individual behavior, the testing of behavioral hypotheses, and the development of improved multimodal transport and urban traffic planning strategies.

The emergence of personalized multimodal transport systems reflects a broader shift toward user-centric mobility, where public transit, walking, cycling, and shared services are integrated to provide journeys tailored to individual preferences
(
Smith et al. 1995
;
W Axhausen et al. 2016
)
. Capturing such systems in simulation, however, is particularly challenging because mobility choices are shaped by diverse socioeconomic profiles, accessibility constraints, and personal experiences
(
Grignard et al. 2018
)
. As highlighted by
(
Oberoi 2024
)
, personalization in Mobility-as-a-Service requires addressing the heterogeneity of users—including their preferences, contexts, and accessibility needs—which remains a significant challenge for developing realistic transport models. Modeling, therefore, requires both flexibility and realism to capture heterogeneous and adaptive travel decisions across populations.

Existing transport simulations often struggle with this complexity and with the availability of data needed to calibrate models. In particular, detailed trajectory and itinerary datasets—which are critical for capturing the nuances of multimodal travel behavior—are extremely scarce
(
Fourez et al. 2025
)
. Such datasets exist for a limited number of major metropolitan areas, for example, Tokyo, Japan, but are largely unavailable elsewhere
(
Feng et al. 2024
)
. This dependence on scarce calibration data restricts the applicability of existing models in smaller or less data-rich regions. Moreover, integrating multiple external data sources requires extensive rule engineering, producing brittle models that are difficult to scale and prone to failure in unforeseen conditions.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and generative AI offer a promising alternative
(
Park et al. 2023
)
. Generative agents can perceive their environment, process contextual information, learn from experience, and adapt their behavior over time. Beyond transport applications, LLM-based agents have already demonstrated their potential in diverse domains. For example, they have been applied to tasks such as software testing, advertising analysis, and brainstorming in the Tiny Troupe framework
(
Salem et al. 2025
)
, as well as automating research through Agent Laboratory
(
Schmidgall et al. 2025
)
. These approaches create ensembles of agents with different roles, capabilities, and personas that collaborate to achieve common goals. Additionally, other studies have evaluated the ability of LLM agents to proxy human emotions by testing their performance on psychological tasks grounded in the OCEAN Big Five personality traits
(
Serapio-García et al. 2025
)
. Results show that these agents can simulate emotional intelligence and empathy, which highlights their potential to proxy human traffic decisions by considering context, preferences, and even inheriting personal traits. At the same time, the GAMA platform provides a powerful environment for spatially explicit agent-based modeling, with strong support for GIS integration and real-world datasets such as GTFS

Related Work

2.1
The Conceptual Framework

The notion of generative agents has gained traction from the influential work of
(
Park et al. 2023
)
, which describes a core architecture for artificial agents endowed with memory, planning, observation, and reflection modules. In this architecture, agents record experiences in a memory stream, manage and summarize memories through reflection, and reference these memories to guide future behavior via planning and reasoning. These core modules—perception, memory (both short-term and long-term), planning, and reflection—form the backbone of autonomous, context-aware agent behavior. In this work, generative agents leverage LLMs to generate behavior and interact with one another in a human-like manner. Building on this foundation, subsequent studies have extended the generative agent paradigm to various domains. For instance,
Voyager
is an agent that continuously explores the world, learns, and makes novel discoveries without human intervention, demonstrated within the Minecraft environment
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. Similarly,
TinyTroupe
and
XAgent
enable the simulation of groups of people collaborating to achieve shared goals
(
Salem et al. 2025
;
Team 2023
)
.

Extending this architecture into the domain of human mobility,
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
propose a conceptual framework tailored to transportation systems, grounded in the generative agent paradigm. Their framework augments traditional modules with transport-specific components, including general planning, travel planning, and dual memory layers (long-term and short-term memory). It also incorporates personal identity and traits as inputs, allowing agents’ decisions to be influenced by their unique socio-demographic and behavioral profiles. In this setup, LLMs are prompted with identity- and trait-based inputs, enabling them to act as proxies for human decision-makers. The models generate textual outputs that represent decisions, which are then executed by agents in the simulation environment. In their demonstration on a simplified transport network divided into four zones, agents use multi-layer travel planning and experiential learning to iteratively optimize travel routes, which helps alleviate traffic congestion in that small network.

This framework effectively adapts the generative agent architecture to mobility contexts, enabling agents to reason over high-level intentions (general plan) and concrete route choices (travel plan), while learning from experience and adjusting behavior over time. Provides a conceptual baseline for integrating generative agents into transport modeling by embedding agent cognition (memory, planning, trait-driven decisions) within a networked mobility environment.

2.2
City-scale Mobility Simulation

One promising direction in mobility modeling is using generative or agentic frameworks to predict or simulate city-scale movement. A recent effort, called AgentMove
(
Feng et al. 2024
)
combines LLM reasoning with spatial modeling to forecast agents’ next destinations. This work casts the city as a multi-layer graph to encode spatial context and decomposes the prediction task into modules for spatio-temporal memory, world knowledge generation, and collective pattern extraction. While this approach achieves strong predictive accuracy on benchmark datasets, it relies on rich trajectory and survey data that are rarely available in most cities, which limits its general applicability.

A more traditional but practical approach is demonstrated by Ile-de-France’s synthetic itinerary generation (via the EQASIM framework)
(
Hörl and Balac 2021
)
. This method uses population data, origin-destination matrices, and statistical matching techniques to generate daily itineraries for synthetic agents. Although less expressive in modeling individual cognition, it is grounded in widely available demographic and commuter data and can be applied even in regions lacking fine-grained trajectory logs.

At a larger scale, the
AgentSociety
framework is designed to simulate 10,000 agents in an urban environment
(
Piao et al. 2025
)
. This work primarily focuses on optimizing the underlying architecture to support large-scale populations, enabling agents to form daily routines, interact, and influence one another in socially meaningful ways. However, it does not go into the complex of transport systems.

Another recent contribution is GATSim
(
Liu et al. 2025b
)
which introduces a generative-agent architecture over a simplified transport network consisting of 16 fully connected nodes in a single-modal setting. Agents in GATSim are equipped with memory hierarchies, planning and reflection mechanisms, and learning capabilities, which allow them to generate realistic travel behaviors. The framework demonstrates that emergent traffic patterns align with expected macroscopic dynamics (such as peak spreading), and that agent trajectories reproduce aspects of human-like decision-making. However, its reliance on a small, simplified network and single-mode transport limits its representativeness for complex urban environments.

Taken together, these works map out a design space between pure prediction (AgentMove), synthetic generation (Ile-de-France), and full simulation (GATSim, AgentSociety). They illustrate both the potential and the barriers of applying the human traveling behavior simulation at city scale. In the next section, we will position our approach in this space, aiming to bridge toward multimodal, large-scale transport simulation with generative agents.

The public transport system of Toulouse, France, combining metro, tram, bus, and cable car, visualized in the GAMA platform with GTFS data.

2.3
Research Gap and Opportunities

Despite recent advances, current generative-agent approaches remain limited to simplified or small-scale transport networks, restricting their relevance for real multimodal systems. To be practically useful, agents must operate in complex environments where multiple modes and dense urban structures interact. For example, the public transport system of Toulouse— combining metro, tram, bus, and cable car — illustrates such a setting, where realistic decision-making requires agents that adapt travel choices to context and experience (Figure
1
); the transport network is far more complex than the 16 fully connected nodes used in GATSim, as it features a dense structure with numerous stops and routes, integrates multiple modes, and can be represented as a large multi-layer graph, where each stop and route corresponds to a node and an edge, respectively.

Another gap lies in the construction of simulation environments. Many studies develop platforms from scratch, which demands significant coding effort and hinders scalability. Integrating generative agents with the established GAMA platform provides a more efficient path, allowing researchers to focus on agent performance while leveraging GAMA’s built-in spatial modeling, visualization, and data integration capabilities. The GAMA platform is particularly well-suited for multi-agent, spatially explicit transport simulations, as it supports seamless import of GIS datasets such as shapefiles and OpenStreetMap (OSM) data, and can simply agentify spatial features (e.g., roads, buildings) to represent transport networks and urban environments in detail. Other than that, no prior work has explored the integration of LLM-based generative agents with the GAMA platform at the time of this study.

Addressing these gaps creates opportunities to advance city-scale simulations by embedding generative agents in multimodal transport networks, enabling richer behavioral insights and supporting sustainable urban mobility planning.

Methodology

3.1
Overall Architecture

The proposed architecture builds upon the conceptual framework introduced by
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
. Its core components are: (i) a multimodal transport system modeled in the GAMA Platform, (ii) Generative Agents, and (iii) a Data Exchange Integration layer that connects these two modules. Through this integration layer, the Generative Agents control and coordinate the behavior of agents within the GAMA simulation, while continuously adapting their future decisions according to evolving contexts and feedback. An overview of the architecture is illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
The architecture of the proposed framework, integrating the GAMA platform with Generative Agents and the OpenTripPlanner module.

The architecture relies on a diverse set of input data. At the individual level, each agent is initialized with a
personal identity and traits
as well as a set of
daily activities
represented as a sequence of actions that specify the time, location, and purpose (e.g., attending school, commuting to work, engaging in leisure, or returning home). At the system level, the architecture integrates
geographic data
and
GTFS data
, the latter providing the schedules of the city’s public transport system.

Decision-making is supported by the
Generative Agents
, which query LLM models to generate context-aware plans and adapt them to real-time conditions. A dedicated
Daily Activity Planning
module (i) translates each agent’s activity sequence into a structured schedule, (ii) triggers activities at their designated times, and (iii) adjusts departure times to ensure timely arrivals. For example, if an agent departs for work at 8:00 AM on the first day but arrives 20 minutes late, it is likely to adjust its departure time to 7:40 AM on the following day. The
Daily Activity Planning
module then invokes the
Travel Planning
module, which decomposes each activity transition into a detailed journey, specifying travel legs, selecting mobility modes, and determining the routes to be followed. Within this setup, perception data—including travel outcomes such as delays, waiting times, in-vehicle travel times, and arrival deviations—are transmitted to the Generative Agent memory in real time. This enables agents to update their short-term observations and long-term reflections as the simulation progresses.

In this architecture, each agent in the GAMA platform simulation corresponds to a generative agent. We extend the original framework of
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
by incorporating an additional OpenTripPlanner module. The module provides access to detailed transit data and multimodal routing information, enabling Generative Agents to evaluate alternative journeys and adapt their travel plans dynamically. More importantly, it allows agents to operate within the complex topology of transport networks, represented as large multi-layer graphs with numerous stops, routes, and modes of transport. By embedding this capability into the architecture, agents are able to replicate human decision-making more realistically: consulting route information, responding to disruptions, and selecting journeys that best align with their preferences and context.

To summarize,
Generative Agents
, supported by various modules and data sources, leverage LLMs to emulate human behavior based on personal identity and traits. With the integration of
OpenTripPlanner
as a travel assistant, agents can navigate the simulation environment and complete their daily activities without being constrained by the inherent complexity of transport networks. Finally, the GAMA platform provides the simulation environment in which agents learn, interact, and act across diverse experimental scenarios.

3.4
Generative Agent Memory

The memory system is central to the agent’s ability to learn from experience and adapt behavior over time. This module takes a crucial role in storing, retrieving, and managing knowledge at both the naive and the reflective levels, joining both reflection and decision-making phases. How the agent performs depends heavily on the design of this memory system. Like other related works, we implement a dual-layer memory architecture consisting of short-term and long-term memory components.

3.4.1
Short-term Memory

The short-term memory is designed to store recent experiences and observations in a relatively simple form. It captures the agent’s immediate context and the events occurring in its surroundings. For example, in the current implementation, the short-term memory includes details of recent trips, such as the
delays, waiting times, travel durations
, and
arrival late time
. In future work, this memory could be extended to include additional contextual factors—such as weather conditions, route disruptions, or traffic incidents—to support experimentation under varied scenarios. Short-term memory is volatile and primarily serves as input to the reflection process at the end of each simulated day. Structurally, it is organized as a chronological stream of events, allowing agents to review and reason over daily experiences in sequence.

3.4.2
Long-term Memory

The long-term memory is responsible for storing knowledge consolidated from the short-term memory over time. This includes successful strategies, learned preferences, and other insights that inform future decision-making. Unlike short-term memory, it is less volatile and designed to retain information across multiple simulation runs, enabling the agent to develop a richer understanding of its environment and progressively improve performance.

To maximize the utility of information derived from short-term memory, we define two distinct types of long-term memory: (i)
concepts
, which represent specific events or facts independent of personal traits or identity, and (ii)
reflections
, which summarize broader insights and lessons learned, often incorporating the agent’s traits and identity. Concepts serve as concise representations of short-term memory, optimizing token usage in large language model prompts, while reflections capture higher-level abstractions and patterns that can guide future behavior in alignment with the agent’s characteristics.

For example, a concept might be
“Bus 67 tends to be slow during the evening commute”
. A reflection, however, could vary depending on the agent’s traits: one individual might prefer this bus because of its predictability, while another with high conscientiousness might avoid it due to its slowness, even though both agents have learned the same underlying concept.

Another type of long-term memory involves abstracting the
reflections
into higher-level generalizations, which are discussed further in Section
4
.

3.4.3
Memory Retrieval

The memory retrieval process (Figure
4
) is crucial for the agent’s decision-making capabilities. To make it realistic, we aim to mimic the human cognitive process of recalling relevant information from memory when faced with a decision. Our approach integrates:

•

Time decay
: Recent memories are more accessible than older ones, reflecting the human tendency to prioritize recent experiences.

•

Relevance
: Memories that are contextually relevant to the current situation are prioritized, ensuring that the agent retrieves information that is most likely to inform its decisions effectively. We consider two kinds of
Relavance
:

–

Contextual relevance
: Memories related to the current context (e.g., location, time of day) are prioritized.

–

Seasonal relevance
: Because the transport system can be affected by seasonal changes (e.g., holidays, rush hours, weekdays and weekends), memories from similar periods in the past are given higher priority.

For
contextual relevance
retrieval, we use Chroma
5
5

5

Chroma
is the open-source search and retrieval database for AI applications,
https://www.trychroma.com/
- a vector database to store long-term memories as high-dimensional vectors. Each memory entry is encoded using a pretrained language model (the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
model from Sentence Transformers) to capture semantic meaning. When the agent needs to retrieve memories, it generates a query vector based on the current context and searches the vector database for the most similar entries. After that, the retrieved memories are ranked based on
time decay
and
seasonal relevance
criteria to ensure that the most pertinent information is prioritized.

Figure 4:
The memory stream consists of historical experiences that may be relevant to the current transport context. A retrieval step identifies a subset of these experiences to be provided to the language model, enabling more informed selection among the available travel options.

To be more specific, the ranking score for each memory entry is calculated as follows. First, we calculate the
semantic\_score
by measuring the cosine similarity between the query vector and each memory vector. The query represents a short sequence describing the context of the upcoming activity. For example, this context may include the activity’s time, location, and purpose, as well as the sequence of stops and routes suggested by OpenTripPlanner.

semantic\_score(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{r})=\frac{\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{r}}{\|\mathbf{c}\|\,\|\mathbf{r}\|}

(1)

where
\mathbf{c}
and
\mathbf{r}
are the embedding vectors of the memory entry and the query, respectively.

Next, the
similarity\_score
is calculated based on the
BLEU-2
score as

similarity\_score=\textbf{BLEU-2}(C,R)

(2)

where
C
is the content of the memory entry and
R
is the query. We use this score to heuristically deal with the
seasonal relevance
criterion, while also boosting the
contextual relevance
criterion. For instance, the seasonal relevance can be captured by keywords such as
“weekend”
,
“holiday”
, or
“rush hour”
, the time range like
“10 AM - 2 PM”
, or specific day of week, and the keywords like
“weekdays”
or
“weekends”
.

We calculate also the
recency\_score
based on the time stamp of the memory, using an exponential decay function as

recency\_score(t)=\lambda^{(T-t)}

(3)

where
T-t
is the number of days since the memory was created and
\lambda
is a decay rate parameter that controls how quickly the score decreases over time.

Finally, the
overall\_score
is calculated as a weighted sum of
semantic\_score
,
similarity\_score
, and
recency\_score
:

overall\_score=\alpha\cdot norm(semantic\_score)+\beta\cdot norm(similarity\_score)+\gamma\cdot norm(recency\_score)

(4)

where
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
are the weights assigned to each score component respectively, and
norm()
is a normalization function that ensures the scores are on a comparable scale. This
overall\_score
is used to rank the memories and select the top-
k
most relevant ones for the current query.

Figure
4
illustrates the memory retrieval process. First, the next activity is triggered by the
Daily Activity Planning
module, which specifies the travel purpose, planned departure time, and source and destination locations. Next, travel options are added to the context by querying OpenTripPlanner. The generative agent then performs a semantic search to retrieve the most similar memory entries from the Memory Stream database (Chroma in this implementation). These entries are ranked using the scoring system described above, and the top-k entries are selected and incorporated into the prompt that is sent to the LLM to support decision-making.

Looking more closely at the memory entries in Figure

, the 1st and 4th entries achieve the highest
semantic\_score
since they relate to experiences with
Tram T1
and the
Arènes
bus stop. However, the 1st entry does not yield a high
similarity\_score
because it occurred in the
evening
, whereas the current context is in the
afternoon
. The 2nd entry, by contrast, has a lower
semantic\_score
but a higher
similarity\_score
, as it corresponds to an
afternoon
route for
education
, which aligns more closely with the context. This demonstrates how
semantic\_score
and
similarity\_score
complement each other in identifying the most relevant experiences, while the
recency\_score
ensures that more recent experiences are weighted more heavily than older ones.

3.5
GAMA Platform Integration

The integration of LLM–based Generative Agents with the GAMA simulation environment is achieved through a combination of synchronous and asynchronous communication mechanisms, specifically HTTP and WebSocket protocols. The details of this integration are shown in Figure
5
. Since GAMA platform has built-in support for these protocols, data can be exchanged between the two systems in real time. Within GAMA, a dedicated model, referred to as the
LLM Agent Interface
, serves as the communication bridge to the Generative Agent Server, which is implemented as a Python-based HTTP and WebSocket server.

Building on this integration layer, the Generative Agent (GA) itself is implemented outside of the GAMA simulation platform in order to take advantage of the rich Python ecosystem. While GAMA provides strong capabilities for modeling and simulating spatial environments, its native language (GAML) does not offer support for essential libraries commonly used in large language model development, such as retrieval-augmented generation systems, vector databases, and advanced machine learning frameworks.

By hosting the Generative Agent as a separate Python-based server application, we encapsulate all its functionalities and expose them through a set of application programming interfaces for communication with the GAMA environment. This design ensures that the simulation benefits from GAMA’s strengths in spatial modeling while still enabling advanced reasoning, memory management, and decision-making capabilities powered by the Python ecosystem. The integration process involves several key steps:

•

Action Sending:
The first step is to send the actions - the sequence of
transfer
and
transit
actions (see Section
3.2
) defined in the LLM Generative Agents to the GAMA simulation. Whenever an inhabitant agent in GAMA receives a new action command from its corresponding LLM GA, it executes the action within the simulation environment.

•

State Sharing:
Because the agents in GAMA have a one-to-one mapping with their corresponding agents in the LLM GAs, the state of each agent in GAMA is shared with its counterpart in the GAs. This allows the GAs to have a consistent view of the simulation state and make informed decisions based on the current context. The shared state includes (i) the
simulation timestamp
and (ii) the
spatial location
of each agent. A critical aspect of this process is timestamp synchronization: because it takes time for the GAs to process the information and respond to the simulation, so we need to delay GAMA to force it to wait.

•

Feedback Loop:
A feedback loop is established between the GAs and the GAMA simulation, allowing the GAs to continuously receive updates on the outcomes of their actions. This feedback is crucial for the GAs to learn and improve their decision-making over time. Specifically, the feedback corresponds to the observations described in Section
3.4.1
.

State Sharing
step is performed in a synchronous manner, because the GAs need to have the most up-to-date state of the agents in GAMA before making any decisions. On the other hand, the
Action Sending
and
Feedback Loop
steps are handled asynchronously, as the GAs can process actions and feedback independently of the simulation’s main loop. Therefore,
State Sharing
step is implemented using HTTP protocol, while
Action Sending
and
Feedback Loop
are implemented using WebSocket protocol to improve the speed of the simulation.

3.6
Evolving Learning and Reflection

Figure 6:
The reasoning flow of the Generative Agent.

The reasoning flow of the Generative Agent, illustrated in Figure
6
, operates through two interconnected phases that enable agents to adapt their travel behavior over time:

•

Reflection phase
— At the end of each simulated day, the agent reviews perception data (such as delays, waiting times, and travel outcomes) stored in short-term memory and integrates this information into long-term memory. This process consolidates daily experiences into structured knowledge that can be reused in subsequent decisions.

•

Decision-making phase
— At the beginning of the next simulated day, the agent retrieves relevant knowledge from long-term memory to inform travel planning. The plan is shaped by daily activities, personal identity, individual traits, and accumulated experience. To select among alternatives, the agent queries the OpenTripPlanner module to obtain multimodal route options across the transport network; these candidate itineraries are then evaluated against the agent’s preferences and constraints. The finalized itinerary is then transmitted to the corresponding inhabitant agent in the GAMA simulation environment for execution.

This iterative cycle allows agents to refine their choices across simulation days. For example, as depicted in Figure
6
, an agent may learn on day
D_{k-1}
that waiting times at the Jean Jaurès stop average 18 minutes for Bus 16. On day
D_{k}
, the agent applies this knowledge by avoiding Bus 16 and selecting an alternative route. The mechanism is conceptually similar to a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) in natural language processing, where outputs from the step
D_{k-1}
serve as inputs to the next step
D_{k}
. Here, however, continuity is maintained by the explicit memory management system, and the objective is to generate adaptive travel plans rather than text sequences.

Figure 7:
The data flow for constructing a prompt to be used with the LLM.

Figure
7
illustrates the data flow for constructing a prompt used with the LLM—the core engine of the Generative Agent. The process begins with an
activity
that is triggered at a specific time. Next,
OpenTripPlanner
is queried to provide several candidate
travel options
. The
activity
and
travel options
are then combined and sent to the
memory stream
(long-term memory) to retrieve
related memory entries
, as discussed in Section
3.4.3
. Finally, four elements are integrated to build the prompt: (i) the
activity
, (ii) the agent’s
personal identity
, (iii) the
related memory entries
, and (iv) the candidate
travel options
. This prompt is then passed to the LLM, which selects the most suitable travel route for the agent in the GAMA simulation.

Experimental Setup

The experiments were conducted using the public transport system of Toulouse, France. The simulation environment was constructed from General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) data published in March 2025 and complemented with geographic layers derived from OpenStreetMap (OSM). 5
Discussion

The proposed framework provides a foundational step toward integrating large language model-based generative agents with agent-based transportation simulations in complex multimodal systems. Unlike previous studies that focus on simplified or small-scale networks, this work demonstrates integration with the complex multimodal transport network. Future research should extend the framework to incorporate additional complexities such as traffic incidents, social interactions, and inter-agent influences, which are critical in realistic multimodal environments.

In theory, the integration of generative agents addresses two longstanding challenges in transportation modeling: reliance on extensive calibration data and the limited adaptability of rule-based agents. However, this flexibility comes with trade-offs. Unlike rule-based systems, generative agents function as a “black box,” making their decision-making difficult to interpret or control. The hallucination problem further complicates reliability, requiring careful validation and mitigation.

Prior research has employed human-in-the-loop methods, where experts evaluate generative agent decisions at a microscopic level. While this improves reliability, such methods are resource-intensive and difficult to scale when applied to large populations in city-scale simulations.

Finally, computational cost and runtime present practical limitations. Running simulations with large language model-based agents is both expensive and time-consuming, constraining scalability.

Looking ahead, several directions may help address these challenges.

•

Validation
: The validation issue can be partially mitigated by exploiting existing transportation surveys. In Toulouse, survey data could be used to validate the behavior of agents at the city scale. Although these datasets lack detailed information on personal traits, they nonetheless provide a feasible basis for testing whether simulated mobility patterns align with observed behaviors.

•

Cost effective approaches
: To address the cost of large language models, future research should investigate resource-efficient techniques such as agent clustering, batch prompting, or hybrid methods that combine generative reasoning with lightweight rule-based components. These approaches may allow large-scale simulations while keeping costs manageable.

•

Hybrid models
: Incorporating soft rule-based constraints into generative agent behavior could reduce unpredictability without undermining flexibility. By bounding the outputs of large language models within reasonable ranges of expected behaviors, such hybrid methods may preserve adaptability while avoiding unrealistic decisions.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduced a simulation framework integrating large language model-based generative agents with GAMA to model adaptive decision-making in multimodal transportation. By separating spatial simulation from intelligent reasoning, agents leverage perception, memory, and reflection to plan routes using real-world GTFS and OSM data, producing behavior patterns resembling human decision-making. Compared to rule-based approaches, the framework reduces reliance on calibration and enables more human-like responses.

Results show that the agent is capable of converging to a habit over the course of the simulation, while simultaneously optimizing its travel decisions to arrive on time for daily activities. However, this convergence occurs slowly due to weekly variations in the GTFS data. In addition, we need to iteratively evaluate the process to identify the most effective setup and gain a deeper understanding of the behavior of the LLM models.

Future work will focus on scaling simulations to larger populations through clustering or batch prompting, incorporating social interactions to capture collective behavior; and validating agent behavior against real-world survey data to ensure realism; and combination with rule-based methods to enhance interpretability and control. Overall, this work lays the groundwork for more flexible and adaptive transportation modeling using generative agents.
