Availability and utility of tools to evaluate resilience of public health emergency preparedness and response in LMICs: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Michael Kiremeji, Hendry Sawe, Eliudi Eliakimu, Stellah Mpagama, Rehema Chande Mallya, Leonard Katalambula, Stephen M Kibusi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) experience recurrent public health emergencies, including infectious disease outbreaks, climate-related shocks and humanitarian crises, which expose persistent weaknesses in health system preparedness and response. Although resilience—defined as the capacity of health systems to anticipate, absorb, adapt to and recover from shocks—has gained increasing attention, there is no comprehensive synthesis of tools specifically designed to evaluate the resilience of public health emergency preparedness and response (PHEPR), particularly in LMICs contexts.ObjectiveTo identify, describe and critically appraise existing tools and frameworks used to evaluate PHEPR resilience, and to assess their methodological robustness, availability and applicability to LMICs health systems.MethodsThis systematic review protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 statement and will follow the PRISMA 2020 reporting guideline. A comprehensive search will be conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS), Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention repositories and relevant governmental and organisational platforms. Eligible sources will include peer-reviewed and grey literature published in English between 2005 and 2025 that describe, develop, validate or apply PHEPR resilience assessment tools. Two reviewers will independently undertake screening, full-text review and data extraction using a piloted 24-item extraction form. Methodological quality of empirical studies will be appraised using appropriate Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools, while resilience assessment instruments will be evaluated using structured qualitative criteria examining their conceptual foundations, development methods, validation evidence and implementation characteristicsEthics and disseminationEthical approval was not required because this review will use publicly available literature. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication, conference presentations and policy briefs to inform future development and implementation of resilience assessment tools for public health emergency preparedness and response in LMICs.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251243633.

Introduction

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to experience a high frequency of public health emergencies, including Ebola and Marburg virus disease outbreaks, cholera, COVID-19 and a wide range of vector-borne diseases.1 2 These events occur against a backdrop of constrained financing, shortages of skilled personnel, weak surveillance systems and chronic underinvestment in emergency preparedness capacities.3 Climate variability, rapid urbanisation, cross-border mobility and fragile governance structures further complicate emergency risk landscapes. These challenges underscore the need for resilient public health systems capable of preparing for, responding to, adapting to and recovering from public health emergencies.4 5

Public health emergency resilience refers to the capacity of health systems and communities to prepare for, withstand, respond to, adapt to and recover from emergencies while maintaining essential public health functions.6 7 Unlike preparedness alone, resilience emphasises dynamic system performance, including the ability to absorb shocks, reorganise resources, sustain service delivery and incorporate learning following emergencies. Core dimensions of resilience include governance and leadership, sustainable and flexible financing, workforce surge capacity, functional surveillance and information systems, continuity of essential services, multisectoral coordination including One Health and community engagement.6 8 In this review, preparedness and response are treated as core components of resilience, consistent with contemporary health system resilience frameworks that emphasise dynamic performance across the emergency cycle.

Several tools are currently used to assess public health emergency preparedness and response; however, they capture only selected components of resilience. The Joint External Evaluation and States Parties Annual Reporting, developed to monitor implementation of the International Health Regulations (IHR) (2005), primarily assess the existence of technical capacities, legal frameworks and coordination structures.9–11 While valuable for benchmarking preparedness, these tools do not systematically assess system adaptability, operational performance under stress, financing flexibility or post-event learning. The WHO health system resilience framework provides a conceptual understanding of resilience across health system building blocks but is not operationalised as a standardised or validated assessment tool for public health emergency preparedness and response.12 13 Consequently, although existing approaches provide valuable information on preparedness capacities, they offer only partial assessments of health system resilience and have not been systematically evaluated with respect to their conceptual foundations, methodological quality, validation and applicability within LMIC health systems.

Recent global policy developments have further reinforced the need for robust resilience assessment. The WHO Health Emergency Preparedness, Response and Resilience (HEPR) framework, the 2024 amendments to the IHR (2005), and the adoption of the WHO Pandemic Agreement in 2025 all emphasise strengthening resilient health systems, improving accountability, promoting continuous monitoring of preparedness capacities and enhancing countries’ ability to prevent, detect and respond to public health emergencies.14–17 These developments highlight the growing importance of reliable, context-appropriate assessment tools that measure resilience beyond traditional preparedness capacities and support continuous health system improvement.15–17

Despite these advances, there remains no comprehensive synthesis of the tools and frameworks available to evaluate public health emergency preparedness and response (PHEPR) resilience. Existing reviews have primarily adopted scoping review methodologies to map the available literature but have not critically examined the conceptual foundations, methodological quality, validation processes, implementation characteristics or measurement properties of resilience assessment tools.18 19 Furthermore, the applicability of these approaches within LMIC health systems has not been comprehensively evaluated. In this review, feasibility and adaptability refer not to lowering internationally accepted preparedness standards, but rather to the extent to which assessment tools can be effectively implemented across diverse health-system contexts while maintaining those standards. This includes consideration of implementation requirements such as governance arrangements, workforce capacity, data availability, digital infrastructure, financial resources and contextual factors that may influence successful application in LMIC settings.12 20

A systematic review was selected because the objective extends beyond mapping the available evidence to critically evaluating existing resilience assessment approaches. Unlike a scoping review, which primarily characterises the evidence base, a systematic review provides a rigorous and transparent methodology for identifying, comparing and critically appraising assessment tools, their conceptual frameworks, methodological development, validation evidence, implementation experience and reported strengths and limitations.14 19 This approach will enable identification of methodological gaps, inform interpretation of the available evidence and support the development and selection of robust, evidence-based resilience assessment tools for LMIC health systems.

The aim of this systematic review is to identify, critically appraise and synthesise existing tools and frameworks used to assess the resilience of public health emergency preparedness and response systems. Specifically, the review will examine their conceptual foundations, methodological development, validation approaches, implementation characteristics and measurement domains, and map these against internationally recognised frameworks, including the IHR (2005), the WHO Health System Building Blocks, the WHO HEPR framework, and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction. By providing a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence, this review will support policymakers, researchers, Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and other global partners in selecting, adapting and developing context-appropriate resilience assessment tools for LMIC health systems. To our knowledge, this will be the first systematic review to comprehensively evaluate the methodological quality, conceptual underpinnings, validation evidence and implementation characteristics of PHEPR resilience assessment tools.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review will be conducted in accordance with internationally recognised methodological and reporting standards for evidence synthesis. The review will be guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis, with methodological principles informed, where relevant, by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (V.6.4).14 21 The review process will comprise the following core stages: (1) formulation of the review questions, (2) development of a comprehensive and reproducible search strategy, (3) study screening and selection, (4) critical appraisal of methodological quality, (5) data extraction and (6) evidence synthesis. The systematic review will commence in January 2026. Literature searching, screening, data extraction, critical appraisal and synthesis are expected to be completed by May 2026.

To ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility, this protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines,22 23 and a completed PRISMA-P checklist is provided as online supplemental file 1. The final review will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement.

In line with JBI best-practice recommendations, a dual-reviewer approach will be applied at all key stages of the review. Two reviewers (MK and RCM) will independently undertake study selection, methodological appraisal and data extraction. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer (EE) when consensus cannot be reached. This approach is intended to enhance methodological rigour, minimise bias and strengthen the reliability of the review findings.

This review is explicitly designed as a systematic review of measurement instruments and frameworks, conducted in accordance with JBI methodology for reviews of instruments and tools. The primary objective is to identify, describe and critically appraise tools and frameworks used to evaluate the resilience of PHEPR systems, with particular emphasis on their relevance, feasibility and applicability to LMICs health-system contexts.

Given the anticipated conceptual, methodological and contextual heterogeneity of PHEPR resilience assessment tools, findings will be synthesised using a narrative and tabular approach, consistent with JBI guidance. Methodological quality of the included evidence will be assessed using the appropriate JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists, selected according to the design and purpose of each source.24 25 Where qualitative synthesis of tool development or implementation experiences is feasible, confidence in findings will be assessed using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)-Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative research (CERQual).26 27 All methodological decisions will be fully documented to ensure transparency, auditability and reproducibility.

Exclusion criteria

Tools or frameworks not related to PHEPR or health system resilience; tools developed exclusively for high-income country contexts with no relevance to LMICs settings; commentaries, editorials, perspective pieces and expert opinions without methodological description; and clinical decision-making tools or bedside clinical algorithms not linked to system-level preparedness or resilience assessment.

Search strategy

We will conduct a comprehensive and reproducible search across multiple electronic databases and organisational repositories to identify all relevant studies. The following databases will be systematically searched: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS), Africa CDC repositories and key governmental and organisational platforms (eg, Ministries of Health, US CDC, UNICEF). The reference lists of included studies and major policy documents will be hand-searched to identify additional eligible publications. The search will include literature published from January 2005 to the date of search execution. The year 2005 was selected as the starting point because it marks the beginning of the modern era of global health security, emergency preparedness and resilience-focused health system assessment. During this period, major global initiatives—such as the revised IHR 2005, the global health security agenda, the rise of all-hazards emergency management frameworks and structured approaches to PHEPR capacity measurement began to emerge. This timeframe also corresponds to the development and wider adoption of standardised preparedness and resilience assessment tools, enabling a more consistent evaluation of system-level capabilities across countries, including LMICs settings. Selecting 2005 as the cut-off, therefore, ensures that the review captures tools developed within a period of substantial conceptual advancement, methodological standardisation and formalisation of preparedness and resilience assessments in global health. This ensures the review captures tools developed during a period of methodological standardisation, conceptual evolution, and increasing relevance to preparedness and resilience assessment.

The search strategy will be developed and refined in consultation with an experienced research librarian. For each database, search strings will incorporate both controlled vocabulary terms (eg, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) for PubMed) and free-text keywords, structured around the PCC framework. Boolean operators, truncation symbols and proximity operators (where supported) will be applied to optimise sensitivity and precision. A set of search terms is provided in online supplemental file 2a for peer-reviewed databases and online supplemental file 2b for grey literature search strategy.

All search results will be exported into Zotero reference management software. Duplicate records will be removed prior to screening. The search will be updated immediately before final synthesis to ensure inclusion of the most recent evidence.

Study selection

Following removal of duplicates, titles and abstracts will be screened independently by two reviewers against the inclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible sources will then be retrieved and assessed independently. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process will be reported using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

A standardised and piloted 24-item data extraction tool will be used to chart relevant information from all systematically included documents. The extraction form will be piloted on a small subset of studies to assess its clarity, completeness and relevance to the review questions. Following the pilot, the form will be refined through team discussion to ensure consistency and alignment with the review objectives. Data extraction will be conducted by one reviewer and independently verified by a second reviewer. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion, and when necessary, adjudicated by a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and reduce bias.

The charting process will capture both descriptive characteristics of the included documents and methodological features of the PHEPR resilience assessment tools they describe. Specifically, the 24-item extraction tool will document: study identification, tool background, tool characteristic, methodological rigour, feasibility and usability and LMICs applicability. Critical appraisal

Where empirical evaluation studies are included, methodological quality will be appraised using the appropriate JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist according to study design. Because no validated appraisal instrument exists specifically for resilience assessment tools, the tools themselves will be evaluated using a structured qualitative assessment of conceptual foundations, development methods, validation evidence, implementation experience, usability and reported strengths and limitations

Data synthesis

Data will be synthesised narratively following PRISMA 2020 and JBI guidance. Given the fact that the included tools are conceptually and methodologically heterogeneous, no meta-analysis is planned.

A descriptive summary will first present key characteristics of the included tools, including publication year, geographical distribution, application level, domains assessed, scoring approaches and available validation evidence. These findings will be organised into summary tables and simple visualisations.

A qualitative thematic synthesis will then be conducted to examine the conceptual foundations, methodological development processes, indicator structures, feasibility requirements, digital integration and LMICs adaptability of each tool. Recurring themes will be identified inductively and organised around core PHEPR resilience domains.

To support comparability, tools will also be mapped against major global frameworks including the IHR 2005, the WHO Health System Building Blocks and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction—in order to identify areas of alignment and conceptual gaps.

Finally, methodological and contextual gaps will be summarised, with particular emphasis on validation gaps, under-represented resilience domains, and challenges related to LMICs implementation.

Ethics and dissemination

The review uses publicly available documents and therefore does not require ethical approval. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication, policy briefs, and presentations to national and regional public health stakeholders.
