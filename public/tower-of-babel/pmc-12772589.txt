Prevalence of invasive bacterial infection among febrile infants with positive urinalysis results: a planned secondary analysis of the Febrile Infants Diagnostic assessment and Outcome (FIDO) prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Holly Drummond, Etimbuk Umana, Clare Mills, Thomas Waterfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of invasive bacterial infection (IBI) in a UK cohort of febrile infants aged 90 days and younger with positive urinalysis (PU) results.DesignA planned secondary analysis of data from the Febrile Infant Diagnostic Assessment and Outcome study, a prospective multicentre observational cohort study.Setting35 paediatric emergency departments and assessment units across the UK and Ireland, between 6 July 2022 and 31 August 2023.PatientsFebrile infants aged 90 days and younger presenting to emergency care.Main outcome measuresIBI rates, namely bacterial meningitis or bacteraemia, among febrile infants with PU results were compared with those with negative urinalysis (NU) results.Results1480 of 1821 infants underwent urinalysis testing. 549 infants had PU results and 931 had NU results. 42/549 (7.7%) and 20/931 (2.2%) infants had IBI in the PU and NU groups, respectively. Of the IBI cases within the PU group, 5/549 (0.9%) were bacterial meningitis and 39/549 (7.1%) were bacteraemia, with two concomitant cases. Of the IBI cases in the NU group, there were 4/931 (0.4%) cases of bacterial meningitis and 18/931 (1.9%) cases of bacteraemia, with two concomitant cases. There were no bacterial meningitis cases in infants over 60 days of age or those with confirmed urinary tract infection (UTI).ConclusionsThe prevalence of bacteraemia was high (7.1%) among PU infants, while the prevalence of bacterial meningitis was low (0.9%), with PU or NU. These findings support existing data that older infants with suspected UTI are at low risk of bacterial meningitis.

Introduction

Fever remains one of the most common presenting complaints in paediatric healthcare.1 Febrile infants aged 90 days and younger are at high risk of invasive and serious bacterial infections (SBIs). Invasive bacterial infections (IBIs), namely bacteraemia and bacterial meningitis, are reported in 1%–4% of febrile infants attending emergency care and SBIs, the most common being urinary tract infection (UTI), are reported in 105–15% of febrile infants.25 Although the prevalence of IBI is low, infants with UTI are theoretically considered at risk for bacterial meningitis due to the risk of haematogenous spread to the meninges. This is clinically important as the treating physician must decide whether febrile infants with positive urinalysis (PU) results should receive invasive lumbar punctures as part of their evaluation.6 These invasive procedures can be distressing for both the child and their family, with an average of three attempts required before a sample of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) can be collected.2 7 Additionally, although serious complications are rare events with a lumbar puncture, some complications include postlumbar puncture headache, bleeding, cerebral herniation and infection.8

Guidance on the necessity of CSF testing among febrile infants varies depending on age. For infants aged 28 days and younger, lumbar punctures are routinely performed.911 Contrastingly, for infants aged over 28 days, risk stratification based on clinical presentation, blood tests and urinalysis testing occurs.911 Depending on the risk of IBI, the decision to perform a lumbar puncture is decided by the treating physician. According to commonly used risk-stratification criteria and recently derived clinical decision aids, febrile infants with PU are placed in the high-risk category, where invasive lumbar punctures, hospitalisations and broad-spectrum antibiotics are recommended.31214

However, several studies have reported that an abnormal urinalysis is not associated with an increased risk of bacterial meningitis in infants aged over 28 days.1517 These studies suggest that the decision to perform lumbar puncture should not be guided by urinalysis test results alone, and it may not be necessary for febrile infants aged over 28 days to undergo the invasive procedures. Although these results are clinically significant, the studies are often limited by small sample sizes, and most of the available data come from European and American cohort studies, with a lack of data among cohorts within the United Kingdom (UK). The present study aims to determine the prevalence of IBI, namely bacterial meningitis and bacteraemia, in a UK cohort of febrile infants aged 90 days and younger with PU results.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This was a planned secondary analysis of data from the Febrile Infant Diagnostic assessment and Outcome (FIDO) study. The FIDO study protocol has been published previously and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05259683).18 19 The FIDO study was a prospective observational cohort study conducted across sites within the Paediatric Emergency Research in the UK and Ireland (PERUKI) network. This study has been reported in line with STROBE guidance.

Study population

The inclusion criteria for the FIDO study were infants aged 90 days and younger presenting with fever ≥38°C in the emergency department or assessment unit, or with a history of fever ≥38°C recorded by anyone using any thermometer within 24 hours of presentation. There were 35 participating sites within the PERUKI network, with 30 participating sites in England, 2 in Scotland, 1 in Wales, 1 in the Ireland and 1 in Northern Ireland. Infants were recruited from 6 July 2022 to 31 August 2023.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was to report the prevalence of IBI among febrile infants with PU results, further divided by the prevalence among different age categories (≤28 days, 29–60 days and 61–90 days). The secondary outcome measures were to compare demographics, investigations performed, laboratory values and IBI rates in infants with PU results to those with negative urinalysis (NU) results.

Reference standards

Urine samples were collected via invasive methods (catheter and suprapubic aspiration) and non-invasive methods (clean catch, urine bag and urine pad).18 19 A PU was classified based on previously published optimum cut-offs in the UK20 by any of the following:

Abnormal urinary dipstick test (leucocyte esterase ≥1+, or nitrite ≥Trace).

Abnormal urinary dipstick test (leucocyte esterase ≥1+, or nitrite ≥Trace).

Abnormal urine microscopy (≥10 white cells per high-power field in centrifuged urine).

Abnormal urine microscopy (≥10 white cells per high-power field in centrifuged urine).

The presence of a UTI was confirmed by any of the following:

Pure growth greater than 100 000 CFU/mL of a single organism from a single clean catch urine sample.

Pure growth greater than 100 000 CFU/mL of a single organism from a single clean catch urine sample.

Pure growth greater than 10 000 CFU/mL of single organism from either a trans-urethral bladder catheter or suprapubic aspiration urine sample.

Pure growth greater than 10 000 CFU/mL of single organism from either a trans-urethral bladder catheter or suprapubic aspiration urine sample.

Pure growth, from two urine samples, greater than 100 000 CFU/mL of the same organism from urine pad/bag samples and pyuria (>10 white blood cells per high-power field) on laboratory microscopy.

Pure growth, from two urine samples, greater than 100 000 CFU/mL of the same organism from urine pad/bag samples and pyuria (>10 white blood cells per high-power field) on laboratory microscopy.

Bacterial meningitis was confirmed by culture or molecular testing of CSF using United Kingdom Accreditation Service accredited National Health Service laboratories. IBI was defined as bacterial meningitis or bacteraemia (non-contaminant) confirmed by culture or quantitative PCR for a bacterial pathogen from blood or CSF. The detection of coagulase-negative staphylococcus, Streptococcus viridans, Cutibacterium acnes or Diphtheroids was considered as contaminants.18 19

Study procedures and data management

On presentation, infants were screened for eligibility by clinical staff and clinical findings were prospectively recorded using a case report form (CRF). These data were recorded prior to consent discussions and all infants received clinical care as per local guidance without delay. Research without prior consent methodology was used to consent patients, which has been described in full in the FIDO study protocol.18 Research Electronic Data Capture tools were used for data collection and management.21 A second CRF was completed 7 days following discharge and recorded investigation results, length of stay and unplanned return attendances. Laboratory investigations and treatment were at the discretion of the treating clinician in all instances.

Statistical analysis

The study population was described using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables. Mean and SD or medians and IQRs were used for continuous variables depending on the distribution of the data (based on Shapiro-Wilk normality test). To compare the PU and NU groups, t-tests or Mann-Whitney U test were used for numerical data depending on distribution of data (based on Shapiro-Wilk normality test) and Fisher’s exact test or χ2 analysis were used for categorical data. Statistical significance was defined as p≤0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts).

Results

Of the 1821 infants included in the parent FIDO study, a total of 1480 infants underwent urinalysis testing, with 549 infants receiving PU results and 931 infants receiving NU results (figure 1). The characteristics of the two groups, PU and NU, are summarised in table 1. The two groups were not significantly different in terms of age and fever height at presentation to hospital (p=0.781 and p=0.077, respectively). There were significant differences between the groups, with infants in the PU group more likely to undergo lumbar punctures (p=0.004), receive parenteral antibiotics (p<0.0001), be admitted to hospital (p<0.0001) and have longer hospital stays (p=0.003) compared with those in the NU group. There were 43/549 (7.8%) unplanned return attendances within 7 days of discharge in the PU group compared with 49/931 (5.3%) unplanned return attendances in the NU group (p=0.058). No infant with an unplanned reattendance received a subsequent diagnosis of bacterial meningitis or bacteraemia. A total of 527/549 (96.0%) infants in the PU group and 862/931 (92.6%) in the NU group had blood tests performed (p=0.010), and the median values of blood markers are presented in table 2. Of the 341 infants without urinalysis performed, there were no cases of bacterial meningitis and five cases of bacteraemia (one Escherichia coli, one Streptococcus agalactiae (GBS), one Streptococcus gallolyticus, one Enterococcus spp).

Discussion

Among this large UK and Irish cohort of febrile infants aged 90 days and younger, the prevalence of bacterial meningitis was low, less than 1%, regardless of urinalysis results. In contrast, the rates of bacteraemia were high, with 7.1% of infants with PU results diagnosed with bacteraemia.

There were no significant differences in the prevalence of bacterial meningitis among infants with PU results compared with those with NU results, including when divided by age categories (≤28 days, 29–60 days and 61–90 days). Additionally, this study found no cases of bacterial meningitis in infants aged 60 days and older. In contrast, however, the prevalence of bacteraemia was significantly higher (7.1%) among infants with PU than among those with NU (1.9%) (p<0.0001).

As anticipated, infants in the PU group had higher hospital admission rates, increased length of stays, received more lumbar punctures and were administered more parenteral antibiotics than those in the NU group. These results are remarkably similar to several previous studies reporting that there were no differences in the prevalence of bacterial meningitis among infants with PU results compared with those with NU results.61517 This finding was further demonstrated in a large systematic review and meta-analysis of 48 studies with over 25 000 febrile infants aged 29–60 days.17 These results suggest that while infants with PU are at significantly higher risk of bacteraemia, the same is not true for bacterial meningitis. Many clinical decision aids, however, do not explicitly highlight this, with many clinicians performing both blood cultures and lumbar puncture on infants with PU.31214 17 Of the five cases of bacterial meningitis among infants with PU, two were infants under 29 days of age, one of whom was unwell appearing, and three were infants aged 29–60 days, of whom two were unwell, including one infant with clear signs of meningism.

Additionally, the present study demonstrated that none of the infants with proven UTI received a concomitant diagnosis of bacterial meningitis. Several studies have highlighted a low prevalence of concomitant UTI and bacterial meningitis, particularly in infants aged over 28 days, and suggested that a selective approach to perform lumbar puncture is warranted.22 23 Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 20 studies demonstrated that among infants aged 29–90 days with UTI, lumbar puncture would need to be performed in 400 infants (95% CI 143 to 1111) to diagnose one case of bacterial meningitis.14 Although universally performing lumbar punctures ensures no missed cases of bacterial meningitis, these findings suggest that this cautious approach is exposing many infants to unnecessary pain and harm. Patient discomfort, distress, costs and complications may be mitigated by reducing the need to perform lumbar puncture on all febrile infants with PU results.

These combined data provided compelling evidence that while all febrile infants with PU should undergo blood culture testing, invasive lumbar punctures are not routinely required. This is especially true where there is a high clinical suspicion of UTI and where the child is over 60 days of age. Instead, clinicians should adopt a tailored approach and evaluate infants on a case-by-case basis.

The present study has many strengths including the prospective recruitment of a large number of febrile infants across many geographical locations in the UK and Ireland. However, the study is not without limitations. Despite the large sample size, the prevalence of bacterial meningitis and bacteraemia was low. However, this is reflective of the low prevalence of bacterial meningitis and IBI in the target population, taken in the context of the results from several other studies and a large meta-analysis, they should be considered reliable.17 Finally, the absence of a universally recognised definition of UTI introduces variability and therefore should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

The prevalence of bacteraemia is high among febrile infants aged 90 days and younger with PU. In contrast, the rates of bacterial meningitis are low, especially among older infants and those with proven UTI. These results suggest that universal lumbar punctures may not be appropriate, especially for those older infants where there is a high clinical suspicion of UTI.
