Stochastic Kinematic Optimal Control on SO(3)
Xi Wang, Xiaoyi Wang, Victor Solo

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a novel method for deriving a global optimal control strategy for stochastic attitude kinematics on the special orthogonal group SO ​ ( 3 ) \text{SO}(3) . We first introduce a stochastic Lie-Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (SL-HJB) equation on SO ​ ( 3 ) \text{SO}(3) , which theoretically provides an optimality condition for the global optimal control strategy of the stochastic attitude kinematics. Then we propose a novel numerical method, the Successive Wigner-Galerkin Approximation (SWGA) method, to solve the SL-HJB equation on SO ​ ( 3 ) \text{SO}(3) . The SWGA method leverages the Wigner-D functions to represent the Galerkin solution of the SL-HJB equation in a policy iteration framework, providing a computationally efficient approach to derive a global optimal control strategy for systems on SO ​ ( 3 ) \text{SO}(3) . We demonstrate the effectiveness of the SWGA method through numerical simulation on stochastic attitude stabilization.

INTRODUCTION

Stochastic optimal control aims to find an optimal policy for systems governed by stochastic differential equations, minimizing a given cost functional, which is a fundamental problem in control theory. Due to its effectiveness in decision-making under uncertainty, stochastic optimal control has found wide applications in fields such as finance, biology, and robotics
[
10
,
17
,
21
]
.

Over the past few decades, control systems on Lie groups have attracted significant research interest
[
5
,
6
,
15
]
. Among these, attitude control on the special orthogonal group
\text{SO}(3)
has been a focus of study, as it serves as a foundational model for describing the rotation of a rigid body in a three-dimensional space. The importance of attitude control is underscored by extensive applications in robotics and aerospace engineering
[
8
,
9
,
1
]
, making stochastic optimal attitude control on
\text{SO}(3)
a critical research topic.

Despite the importance of stochastic optimal attitude control on
\text{SO}(3)
, deriving a global stochastic optimal control strategy, i.e., a optimal feedback control policy
u^{*}(R)
that is well defined throughout
R\in\text{SO}(3)
, remains a challenge. Traditional Euclidean stochastic optimal control methods, when applied to systems on
\text{SO}(3)
, often produce only locally optimal policies, leading to singularities or ambiguities when extending the control policy across the entire
\text{SO}(3)
. For instance, deterministic attitude control strategies on
\text{SO}(3)
using Rodrigues parameters or unit quaternions have been developed by
[
24
,
12
,
14
]
. However, as discussed in
[
3
]
, Rodrigues parameters fail to represent rotations exceeding
\pi
, while unit quaternions, which provide a double covering of
\text{SO}(3)
, introduce non-uniqueness in attitude representation. The ambiguity can lead to the unwinding phenomenon
[
3
]
, causing unnecessary large-angle rotations even when the initial orientation error is small.

More recently, some research has focused on developing deterministic global optimal attitude control strategies by leveraging the geometry of
\text{SO}(3)
. For example,
[
7
]
proposed almost global controllers for various control designs in deterministic attitude control problems. Furthermore,
[
2
]
investigated global optimal control for deterministic attitude kinematics on
\text{SO}(3)
and
[
13
]
developed a global optimal control approach for deterministic attitude dynamics. More recently,
[
4
]
has proposed a global optimal LQR control strategy for deterministic systems on general Riemannian manifolds. However, these deterministic methods are not directly applicable to stochastic systems on Lie groups, because noise in stochastic systems interacts with the curvature of the Lie group, leading to a pinning drift term orthogonal to the tangent space
[
22
]
. Stochastic control on Lie groups is required to account for the effect of the pinning drift term, which fundamentally differentiates it from deterministic control methods. In addition, these methods are restricted to systems with quadratic cost functions.

Stochastic optimal attitude control has received less attention. Using a global parametrization for stochastic dynamics,
[
20
]
developed a stochastic control method for attitude dynamics based on the recursive Lyapunov design. In the work,
[
20
]
did not deal with optimal control.

Motivated by the existing research gap in global stochastic optimal control on
\text{SO}(3)
, this paper develops a novel approach for deriving a global optimal control strategy for stochastic attitude kinematics with general cost functions. Our main contributions are as follows.

•

We derive a stochastic Lie-Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (SL-HJB) equation on
\text{SO}(3)
for stochastic attitude kinematics, which theoretically provides an optimal condition for the global optimal control strategy. The SL-HJB equation is a second-order linear partial differential equation on
\text{SO}(3)
with reduced dimensionality, which makes it more computationally efficient to solve.

•

We propose a novel numerical method, the Successive Wigner-Galerkin Approximation (SWGA) method, to solve the SL-HJB equation on
\text{SO}(3)
. The SWGA method is a policy iteration algorithm inspired by the Galerkin approximation pioneered by
[
16
]
and based on the Fourier series expansion in
\text{SO}(3)
denoted by Wigner-D functions, offering a computationally efficient approach to derive the global stochastic optimal control strategy for systems on
\text{SO}(3)
.

•

We illustrates our theoretical findings through numerical simulation on stochastic attitude stabilization. The results of the simulation demonstrate the efficiency of our proposed method in deriving a global optimal control for stochastic systems on
\text{SO}(3)
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the geometry and calculus of
\text{SO}(3)
. In Section 3, we formulate the optimal control for stochastic attitude kinematics on
\text{SO}(3)
. In Section 4, we introduce the SL-HJB equation on
\text{SO}(3)
for stochastic attitude kinematics. Then we propose the SWGA method to solve the SL-HJB and derive the global optimal control strategy for stochastic attitude kinematics. In Section 5, we present numerical simulation on stochastic attitude stabilization, which illustrates the effectiveness of our theoretical findings and the SWGA method. Finally, we conclude the paper in Section 6.

PRELIMINARIES

In this section, we review the geometry and the calculus of
\text{SO}(3)
, which give the foundation for solving the stochastic optimal control problem on
\text{SO}(3)
.

The special orthogonal group
\text{SO}(3)=\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\mid R^{T}R=I,\det(R)=1\}
is formed by all
3\times 3
orthogonal matrices with determinant
1
. The associated Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(3)=\{S\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\mid S^{T}=-S\}
is the set of all
3\times 3
skew-symmetric matrices. The skew matrix operator

\displaystyle S(\omega)=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega_{3}&\omega_{2}\\
\omega_{3}&0&-\omega_{1}\\
-\omega_{2}&\omega_{1}&0\end{bmatrix},

provides the isomorphism between
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
{\mathfrak{so}}(3)
. Note that for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have
S^{2}(a)=aa^{T}-\|a\|^{2}I
and
S(a)b=-S(b)a
.

When the Lie group
\rm{SO}(3)
is equipped with a Riemannian metric that defines an inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle_{R}=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(X^{T}Y)
for all
X,Y
in the tangent space
T_{R}\rm{SO}(3):=R\mathfrak{so}(3)
, the Lie group
\text{SO}(3)
becomes a Riemannian manifold. The tangent vectors
Z_{i}(R)=RS(e_{i})
for
i=1,2,3
, where
e_{i}
denotes the
i
-th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, form an orthonormal basis for
T_{R}\rm{SO}(3)
. The geodesic on
\text{SO}(3)
at
R
with initial tangent vector
Z=RS(a)
is given by
\gamma(t)=R\exp(tS(a))
, where
\exp(A)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{n}}{n!}
is the matrix exponential.

Given a smooth function
f:\text{SO}(3)\to\mathbb{R}
, the Lie derivative of
f
at
R
in the direction of
Z=RS(a)
is given by differentiating
f
along the geodesic
\gamma(t)=R\exp(tS(a))
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}f=\frac{d}{dt}f(R\exp(tS(a)))\Big|_{t=0}.

The Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{a}f
can be expressed in terms of the Lie derivatives along the orthonormal basis
Z_{i}=RS(e_{i})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}f=\sum_{i=1}^{3}a_{i}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{e_{i}}f=\nabla^{T}f\cdot a

where we refer
\nabla f=[\mathcal{L}_{e_{1}}f,\mathcal{L}_{e_{2}}f,\mathcal{L}_{e_{3}}f]^{T}
to the Lie gradient of
f
.

For the reminder of this paper, with a slight abuse of notation, when
X
is a matrix, we let
\mathcal{L}_{a}X
denote the Lie derivative applied element-wise to
X
, i.e.,
(\mathcal{L}_{a}X)_{ij}=\mathcal{L}_{a}X_{ij}
.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

In this section, we formulate the optimal control for the stochastic attitude kinematics on
\text{SO}(3)
and present the classical stochastic Euclidean Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (SE-HJB) equation of the optimal control strategy. We then discuss the challenges in solving the SE-HJB equation.

3.1
Stochastic Attitude Kinematics

The deterministic attitude kinematics on
\text{SO}(3)
is well-known as the following matrix ordinary differential equation:

\displaystyle dR(t)=R(t)S(u(t)),

(1)

where
R(t)\in\text{SO}(3)
is the state variable representing the attitude of a rigid body in three-dimensional space, and
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the control input corresponding to the body’s angular velocity.

In the presence of stochastic noise, the stochastic attitude kinematics on
\text{SO}(3)
is governed by the matrix-Stratonovich stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\displaystyle dR(t)=RS\left(udt+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sigma_{k}\circ dW_{k}(t)\right),

(2)

where
\sigma_{k}:\text{SO}(3)\to\mathbb{R}
are independent vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
dW_{k}(t)
are the independent standard Brownian motion with variance
dt
. The symbol
\circ
indicates that the equation is written in the Stratonovich form.

Using the Stratonovich-Ito conversion formula
[
23
]
, we can rewrite (
2
) in the matrix-Itô SDE form as follows

\displaystyle dR=RS(u)dt-\frac{1}{2}R\Sigma dt+R\sum_{k=1}^{3}S(\sigma_{k})dW_{k},

(3)

\displaystyle\Sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{3}S^{2}(\sigma_{k})=\mathrm{tr}(\sigma\sigma^{T})I-\sigma\sigma^{T},

where
\sigma=[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}]^{T}
. Note that
\Sigma
is symmetric.

Compared (
3
) with (
1
), it is important to note that, unlike deterministic cases, where the infinitesimal increment
dR
always lies in the tangent space
T_{R(t)}\text{SO}(3)
, the stochastic attitude kinematics introduces a term
-\frac{1}{2}R\Sigma dt
that is orthogonal to the tangent space
T_{R(t)}\text{SO}(3)
. This term, known as the pinning drift term, ensures that the system remains on
\text{SO}(3)

[
22
]
. As a result, control on the stochastic system (
3
) needs to deal with the pinning drift term, and is fundamentally different from the deterministic counterpart.

3.2
Stochastic Optimal Control for Stochastic Attitude Kinematics

In this paper, we aim to develop a global optimal control strategy for stochastic attitude kinematics system (
3
) on
\text{SO}(3)
. Our objective is to determine an optimal control policy
u^{*}(R(t))
that is globally defined on
\text{SO}(3)
and minimizes the expected cost functional:

\displaystyle J(u)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\left(l(R(t))+\|u\|^{2}_{W}\right)dt\right].

Here,
t_{0}\in R
is a fixed time horizon,
\|u\|^{2}_{W}=u^{T}Wu
represents the control cost, and
l(R)
is the running cost, which is a positive but not necessarily quadratic function on
\text{SO}(3)
.

To derive the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation for the system (
3
), we first transform the system (
3
) into a vector form by defining
\mathbf{r}=\textbf{vec}(R)=[r_{11},r_{21},r_{31},r_{12},r_{22},r_{32},r_{13},r_{23},r_{33}]^{T}.

Using the identity
\textbf{vec}(ABC)=(C^{T}\otimes A)\textbf{vec}(B)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\textbf{vec}(RS(a))=-S(a)\otimes I\mathbf{r},\quad\forall a\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

\displaystyle\textbf{vec}(R\Sigma)=\Sigma\otimes I\mathbf{r}.

Thus, the system (
3
) can be rewritten in vector form as

\displaystyle d\mathbf{r}=f(u,\mathbf{r})dt+g(\mathbf{r})dt+\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}(\mathbf{r})dW_{k},

(4)

\displaystyle f(u,\mathbf{r})=-S(u)\otimes I\mathbf{r},\quad g(\mathbf{r})=-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma\otimes I\mathbf{r},

\displaystyle h_{k}(\mathbf{r})=S(\sigma_{k})\otimes I\mathbf{r}.

We can now apply some classical results from stochastic optimal control theory to derive the stochastic Euclidean HJB equation of the optimal control strategy.

Theorem 1

For the system (
4
), the stochastic optimal control
u^{*}(\mathbf{r})
and the corresponding optimal value function
V^{*}(\mathbf{r})
should satisfy the following stochastic Euclidean HJB (SE-HJB) equation:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0=\min_{u}\left\{l(\mathbf{r})+\|u\|^{2}_{W}+\mathcal{D}_{u}^{E}V^{*}\right\},\\
u^{*}(r)=\arg\min_{u}\left\{l(\mathbf{r})+\|u\|^{2}_{W}+\mathcal{D}_{u}^{E}V^{*}\right\},\end{cases}

(5)

where the operator
\mathcal{D}_{u}^{E}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{u}^{E}V=V_{\mathbf{r}}^{T}f(u,\mathbf{r})+V_{\mathbf{r}}^{T}g(\mathbf{r})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}^{T}(\mathbf{r})V_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}}h_{k}(\mathbf{r}),

and
V_{\mathbf{r}}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial\mathbf{r}}
,
V_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}}=\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\mathbf{r}^{T}\partial\mathbf{r}}
.

Proof.
Follows from
[
10
]
[Theorem 6.3].
\square

Unfortunately, solving the SE-HJB equation (
5
) presents two significant challenges. First, the operator
\mathcal{D}_{u}^{E}
is a second-order nonlinear differential operator, making it difficult to solve. Second, although the system (
3
) evolves on
\text{SO}(3)
, a 3-dimensional manifold, the SE-HJB equation is formulated in
\mathbb{R}^{9}
, creating a redundancy in dimensionality that significantly increases the computational complexity of solving (
5
). These challenges represent the primary technical obstacles in deriving a global optimal control strategy for the system (
3
).

4
Global Stochastic Optimal Control for Stochastic Attitude Kinematics

In this section, we address the challenges in solving the SE-HJB equation (
5
) by transforming it into a stochastic Lie-HJB (SL-HJB) equation on
\text{SO}(3)
. We then propose a novel numerical method, the Successive Wigner-Galerkin Approximation (SWGA) method, to solve the SL-HJB equation on
\text{SO}(3)
and derive the global optimal control strategy for the stochastic attitude kinematics (
3
).

4.1
Optimality Condition: SL-HJB Equation

We now convert the SE-HJB equation (
5
) into a stochastic Lie-HJB (SL-HJB) equation on
\text{SO}(3)
using the Lie derivative. Note that the Lie derivative of a smooth function
V:\text{SO}(3)\to\mathbb{R}
at
R
in the direction of
Z=RS(a)
can be expressed by the Euclidean derivative as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}V

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}V(R\exp(tS(a)))\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\frac{\partial V}{\partial r_{ij}}(R)\left\{\frac{dR(\exp(tS(a)))}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\right\}_{ij}

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}(R)\frac{dR(\exp(tS(a)))}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\right),

(6)

where
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
denotes the scalar-by-matrix derivative of
V
with respect to
R
, i.e.,
\textbf{vec}(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R})=\frac{\partial V}{\partial\mathbf{r}}
.
Note that
\frac{dR(\exp(tS(a)))}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}=RS(a)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}V={\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}RS(a)\right).

Now we address the components in the SE-HJB equation (
5
). First, we have

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{r}}^{T}f(u,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\textbf{vec}^{T}(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R})\textbf{vec}(RS(u))

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}RS(u)\right)=\mathcal{L}_{u}V=\nabla^{T}V\cdot u.

Next, we compute

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{r}}^{T}g(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\textbf{vec}^{T}(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R})\textbf{vec}(R\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}R\Sigma\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}{\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}RS^{2}(\sigma_{k})\right).

Using
\frac{dR(\exp(tS(a)))}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}=RS(a)
again, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}(RS(\sigma_{k}))=(RS^{2}(\sigma_{k})).

Therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{r}}^{T}g(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}{\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}(RS(\sigma_{k}))\right).

(7)

Finally, for
V^{\prime}_{ij}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial r_{ij}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(V_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}}h_{k}(\mathbf{r})\right)_{3i+j}=\frac{\partial V^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{r}^{T}}\textbf{vec}(RS(\sigma_{k})).

Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{r}^{T}}\textbf{vec}(RS(\sigma_{k}))=\textbf{vec}^{T}(\frac{\partial V^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial R})\textbf{vec}(RS(\sigma_{k}))

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V^{\prime}_{ij}}{\partial R}RS(\sigma_{k})\right)=\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}V^{\prime}_{ij}=\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}\frac{\partial V}{\partial r_{ij}},

we have

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}}h_{k}(\mathbf{r})=\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}(V_{\mathbf{r}})=\textbf{vec}(\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}V_{R}).

Thus,

\displaystyle h_{k}^{T}(\mathbf{r})V_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}}h_{k}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\textbf{vec}^{T}(RS(\sigma_{k}))\textbf{vec}(\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}\frac{\partial V}{\partial R})

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R})RS(\sigma_{k})\right).

(8)

Combining (
7
) and (
8
), we obtain

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{r}}^{T}g(\mathbf{r})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}^{T}(\mathbf{r})V_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}}h_{k}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}{\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}RS(\sigma_{k})+\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R})RS(\sigma_{k})\right),

which implies

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{r}}^{T}g(\mathbf{r})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}^{T}(\mathbf{r})V_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}}h_{k}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}{\rm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}RS(\sigma_{k})\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}(\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{k}}(V)).

Finally, by defining
\Delta_{\sigma}V=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\sigma_{k}}(V)
, the operator
\mathcal{D}_{u}^{E}
in the SE-HJB equation (
5
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{u}^{E}V=\nabla^{T}V\cdot u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\sigma}V

Therefore, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2

The global stochastic optimal control
u^{*}(R)
and the corresponding value function
V^{*}(R)
for the system (
3
) satisfy the following SL-HJB equation on
\text{SO}(3)
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0=\min_{u}\left\{\nabla^{T}V^{*}\cdot u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\sigma}V^{*}+l(R)+\|u\|^{2}_{W}\right\},\\
u^{*}(R)=-\frac{1}{2}W^{-1}\nabla V^{*}.\end{cases}

(9)

Proof.
Follows from the above discussion.
\square

Theorem
2
shows that the SE-HJB equation (
5
) can be transformed into the SL-HJB equation on
\text{SO}(3)
. The SL-HJB equation (
9
) is a second-order linear PDE on
\text{SO}(3)
, reducing the dimensionality of the problem from 9 to 3. This transformation lays the foundation for developing a numerical method to solve the SL-HJB equation (
9
) on
\text{SO}(3)
and to derive the global optimal control strategy for the system (
3
).

\theta^{(0)}

\{\phi_{\alpha}(R)\}_{p=1}^{2N}
as per in (

).

: Pre-compute matrix
A_{1}
,
b_{1}
as per in (
13
);
M^{r}
as per in (

).

i=0,1,2,\cdots

Compute
A_{2}^{(i)},b_{2}^{(i)}
based on
\theta_{p}^{(i)}

).

To solve the SL-HJB equation (
9
), we introduce the Successive Wigner-Galerkin Approximation (SWGA) method, as in Algorithm
1
. Since for any compact Lie group
\mathcal{G}
, the space of square-integrable complex-valued functions on
\mathcal{G}
forms a Hilbert space, denoted as
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, we can write the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
as

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle f,g\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(R)\overline{g(R)}dR,

where
\overline{g(R)}
is the complex conjugate of
g(R)
and
dR
is the Haar measure on
\mathcal{G}

[
8
]
.

The key idea behind the Successive Wigner-Galerkin Approximation (SWGA) method is as follows. First, we extend the successive iteration framework from the Euclidean space
[
16
,
25
]
to the SL-HJB equation (
9
) on
\text{SO}(3)
. In each iteration, given the control input
u^{(i)}
, we solve the generalized SL-HJB equation for the value function
V^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{D}_{u^{(i)}}V^{(i+1)}+l(R)+\frac{1}{2}\|u^{(i)}\|_{W},

(10)

where
\mathcal{D}_{u^{(i)}}V:=\nabla^{T}V\cdot u^{(i)}+\Delta_{\sigma}V
. After obtaining the value function
V^{(i+1)}
, we can derive the updated control strategy using
V^{(i+1)}
as

\displaystyle u^{(i+1)}=-\frac{1}{2}W^{-1}\nabla V^{(i+1)}.

Then, we seek a Galerkin solution to the generalized SL-HJB equation (
10
). Specifically, we represent
V^{(i+1)}
as a linear combination of a finite set of basis functions

\displaystyle V_{N}^{(i+1)}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2N}\theta_{\alpha}^{(i+1)}\phi_{\alpha}(R),

(11)

where the coefficients
\theta^{(i+1)}
are to be determined. By substituting the finite element representation of
V_{N}^{(i+1)}
into (
10
), we can determine the coefficients
\theta^{(i+1)}
by ensuring that the residual
e^{(i+1)}
is orthogonal to all basis functions,i.e.,

\displaystyle e^{(i+1)}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2N}\theta^{(i+1)}_{\alpha}\mathcal{D}_{u^{(i)}}\phi_{\alpha}(R)+l(R)+\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{W}

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle e^{(i+1)}(R),\phi_{\beta}(R)\rangle\!\rangle=0,\quad\forall\beta=1,2,\cdots,2N.

(12)

The compact Lie group structure of
\text{SO}(3)
significantly facilitates the selection of basis functions for the SWGA method. A finite-dimensional unitary representation of a Lie group
\mathcal{G}
is a matrix-valued map
\rho:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
that satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\rho(g_{1})\rho(g_{2})=\rho(g_{1}g_{2}),&\quad\forall g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{G},\\
\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{-1},&\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G},\\
\rho(g)\overline{\rho(g)}^{T}=I_{n},&\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G}.\end{cases}

When
\mathcal{G}
is compact, all finite-dimensional unitary irreducible representations of
\mathcal{G}
are countable and denoted by
\rho^{l},\,l\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, the Peter-Weyl theorem
[
8
]
states that the matrix elements of the representation
\rho^{l}_{m,n}
form a complete orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, allowing us to extend the concept of Fourier series to the compact Lie group
\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f(g)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m,n}F_{m,n}^{l}\rho_{m,n}^{l}(g),\quad g\in\mathcal{G},\\
F_{m,n}^{l}=\langle\!\langle f(g),\rho_{m,n}^{l}(g)\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathbb{C}.\end{cases}

Specializing in the compact Lie group
\text{SO}(3)
, the matrix elements of its unitary irreducible representations are known as the Wigner-D functions
[
8
]
, denoted by
D_{m,n}^{l}(R)
, where
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
m,n\in\{-l,-l+1,\ldots,l\}
. We use the Wigner-D functions as basis functions for implementing the SWGA method. Since each
D_{p}
is complex-valued, we approximate
V^{(i+1)}(R)
using a linear combination of the real and imaginary components of the Wigner-D functions. Specifically, we set

\text{Re}D_{p}(R)=\phi_{2\alpha_{p}-1}(R),-\text{Im}D_{p}(R)=\phi_{2\alpha_{p}}(R),

where
\alpha_{p}
is the lexicographic order number of the tuple
p=(l,m,n)
. By substituting (
11
) into (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2N}\theta_{\alpha}^{(i+1)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle\!\langle\Delta_{\sigma}\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle\nabla^{T}\phi_{\alpha}\cdot u^{(i)}(R),\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle\right)

\displaystyle+\langle\!\langle l(R)+\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{W},\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle=0,\quad\forall q=1,2,\cdots,2N.

By introducing the undetermined coefficients
\theta^{(i+1)}
, we obtain the following equation:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\theta^{(i+1)}=(A_{1}+A_{2}^{(i)})^{-1}(b_{1}+b_{2}^{(i)}),\\
A_{1}=\big[A_{1}\big]_{\beta\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\!\langle\Delta_{\sigma}\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle,\\
b_{1}=\big[b_{1}\big]_{\beta}=-\langle\!\langle l(R),\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle,\\
A_{2}^{(i)}=\big[A_{2}^{(i)}\big]_{\beta\alpha}=\langle\!\langle\nabla\phi_{\alpha}^{T}u^{(i)},\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle,\\
b_{2}^{(i)}=\big[b_{2}^{(i)}\big]_{\beta}=-\langle\!\langle\|u^{(i)}(R)\|_{W}^{2},\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle.\end{cases}

(13)

Morever, by pre-computing the matrix

\displaystyle M^{\gamma}=\big[M^{\gamma}\big]_{\beta\alpha}=\langle\!\langle\nabla\phi_{\alpha}^{T}W^{-1}\nabla\phi_{\gamma},\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle,

(14)

the matrix
[A_{2}^{(i)}]_{\beta\alpha}
and
b_{2}^{(i)}
can be implemented a linear combination of
M_{\beta\alpha}^{\gamma}
and
\theta^{(i)}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}[A_{2}^{(i)}]_{\beta\alpha}&=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma}{\theta_{\gamma}^{(i)}}[\langle\!\langle\nabla\phi_{\alpha}^{T}W^{-1}\nabla\phi_{\gamma},\nabla\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle]\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma}{\theta_{\gamma}^{(i)}}M^{\gamma},\\
\big[b_{\beta}^{(i)}\big]&=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha,\gamma}\theta_{\alpha}^{(i)}\theta_{\gamma}^{(i)}\langle\!\langle\nabla\phi_{\alpha}^{T}W^{-1}\nabla\phi_{\gamma},\phi_{\beta}\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha,\gamma}\theta_{\alpha}^{(i)}\theta_{\gamma}^{(i)}M_{qp}^{\gamma}.\end{cases}

(15)

In the cases, we finally obtain the SWGA method as in Algorithm
1
. We also note that all the matrix elements in (
13
)-(
15
) can be computed analytically. We put the detailed computation in the appendix due to space limit.

The following result shows that the global stochastic optimal control strategy can be obtained when the SWGA method converges.

Result 3

Assume that for each fixed
i
, the SWGA method converges to a value function
V_{N}
, and the sequence
\{V_{N}\}
converges to
V^{*}
. Then, the control strategy
u_{N}^{(i)}
converges to the global stochastic optimal control strategy
u^{*}
with the value function
V^{*}
.

Proof.
For a fixed
N
, as the SWGA method converges, the value function
V_{N}
and the control strategy
u_{N}
become the fixed point of the iteration. Hence, for all
p=1,2,\ldots,2N
, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{D}_{u_{N}}V_{N}+l(R)+\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{T}Wu_{N},\phi_{\alpha}\rangle\!\rangle,\\
u_{N}=-\frac{1}{2}W^{-1}\nabla V_{N},\end{cases}

which implies that,
V_{N}
is a weak solution of the following equation in the subspace
\text{span}\{\phi_{\alpha}\}_{p=1}^{2N}
:

\displaystyle 0=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\sigma}V_{N}+l(R)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{T}V_{N}W^{-1}\nabla V_{N}.

(16)

Since the sequence
\{V_{N}\}
converges to
V^{*}
, and
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}
forms a complete orthonormal basis, it follows that
V^{*}
is a solution of (
16
). In addition, the control strategy
u^{*}
can be derived as:

\displaystyle u^{*}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}=\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{2}W^{-1}\nabla V_{N}

(17)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}W^{-1}\nabla V^{*}=\arg\min_{u}\{D_{u}V^{*}+l(R)+\|u\|_{W}\}.

Putting (
16
) and (
17
) together, we see
V^{*}
and
u^{*}
satisfy the SL-HJB equation (
9
), which completes the proof.
\square

6
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have developed, for the first time, a solution to optimal control for stochastic attitude kinematics on SO(3). We begin by introducing the stochastic Lie-HJB (SL-HJB) equation on
\text{SO}(3)
, leveraging the Lie group structure to establish theoretical optimality conditions for the global control strategy. Next, we employ the unitary irreducible representations of
\text{SO}(3)
, known as the Wigner-D functions, to develop a novel numerical method, the Successive Wigner-Galerkin Approximation (SWGA) method, for solving the SL-HJB equation on
\text{SO}(3)
. Simulation results demonstrate both the convergence and effectiveness of the derived global optimal control strategy. This work provides a comprehensive theoretical framework and a practical numerical solution for stochastic optimal control on
\text{SO}(3)
.

In the future work, we plan to extend the method to the stochastic optimal control on more general Lie groups and also to deal with dynamics. Potential applications include the stochastic optimal control of the quantum systems on the unitary group
{\rm{U}}(n)
and the stochastic optimal control of the rigid body poses on
{\rm{SE}}(3)
.

Appendix B
Intergal of Wigner-D functions

We begin by presenting the representation of the Wigner-D functions. Using the Z-Y-Z Euler angle parameterization
[
11
]
of
R\in\text{SO}(3)
, we have

R=R_{ZYZ}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\exp(\alpha S(e_{3}))\exp(\beta S(e_{2}))\exp(\gamma S(e_{3})).

The Wigner-D functions can then be expressed as
[
8
]

D_{m,n}^{l}(R_{ZYZ}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma))=e^{-im\alpha}d_{m,n}^{l}(\cos\beta)e^{-in\gamma}.

Here, the Wigner small-d functions
d_{m,n}^{l}(\cos\beta)
are defined as

d_{m,n}^{l}(\cos\beta)=(-1)^{m-n}P_{m-n}^{l}(\cos\beta),

and the associated functions
P_{m,n}^{l}(z)
are given by

P_{m,n}^{l}(z)=\left[\frac{(l-n)!(l+m)!}{(l-m)!(l+n)!}\right]^{1/2}\frac{(1-z)^{(m-n)/2}(1+z)^{(m+n)/2}}{2^{m}(m-n)!}F_{1}\left(l+m+1,-l+m;m-n+1;\frac{1-z}{2}\right),

where
F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the hypergeometric function
[
11
]
.

It is important to note that the Wigner-small-d functions satisfy the following symmetry property

d_{p_{\ominus}}^{l}(z)=(-1)^{m-n}d_{p}(z).

where=
p=(l,m,n)
and
p_{\ominus}=(l,-m,-n)
.

The following lemma establishes the orthogonality of the Wigner-D functions.

Lemma 1
(
[
8
]
, Sec. 9.2)

For
p=(l,m,n)
and
q=(l^{\prime},m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
, the Wigner-D functions satisfy

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle D_{p}(R),D_{q}(R)\rangle\!\rangle:=\int_{\text{SO}(3)}D_{p}(R)\overline{D_{q}(R)}dR=\frac{1}{2l+1}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2l+1}\delta_{p,q}.

Now, we separate the real and imaginary parts of the Wigner-D functions. For
p=(l,m,n)
, we denote
D_{p}=D_{p}^{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{j}D_{p}^{\mathbf{I}}
, where
\mathbf{j}
is the imaginary unit. Specifically, we have

\begin{cases}D_{p}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)=\text{Re}(D_{p}(R))=\phi_{2p-1}(R),\\
D_{p}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)=-\text{Im}(D_{p}(R))=\phi_{2p}(R).\end{cases}

The following lemma analytically computes integrals of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\square_{1},\square_{2}}:=\langle\!\langle D_{p}^{\square_{1}}(R),D_{q}^{\square_{2}}(R)\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{\text{SO}(3)}D_{p}^{\square_{1}}(R)D_{q}^{\square_{2}}(R)dR,\quad\square_{1},\square_{2}\in\{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I}\}.

Lemma 2

For
p=(l,m,n)
and
q=(l^{\prime},m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
, the Wigner-D functions satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}}=\frac{1}{2(2l+1)}(\delta_{p,q}+(-1)^{m-n}\delta_{p,q_{\ominus}}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{I}}=\frac{1}{2(2l+1)}(\delta_{p,q}-(-1)^{m-n}\delta_{p,q_{\ominus}}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I}}=\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{R}}=0.

Proof.
From the orthogonality of the Wigner-D functions, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2l+1}\delta_{p,q}=

\displaystyle\int_{\text{SO}(3)}D_{p}(R)\overline{D_{q}(R)}dR

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\text{SO}(3)}\left(D_{p}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)-\mathbf{j}D_{p}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)\right)\left(D_{q}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)+\mathbf{j}D_{q}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)\right)dR

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}}+\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{I}}+\mathbf{j}(\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I}}-\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{R}}).

Furthermore, since
D_{q}(R)=(-1)^{m-n}\overline{D_{q_{\ominus}}(R)}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2l+1}\delta_{p,q_{\ominus}}(-1)^{m-n}=

\displaystyle\int_{\text{SO}(3)}D_{p}(R)(-1)^{m-n}\overline{D_{q_{\ominus}}(R)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\text{SO}(3)}D_{p}(R)D_{q}(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\text{SO}(3)}\left(D_{p}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)-\mathbf{j}D_{p}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)\right)\left(D_{q}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)-\mathbf{j}D_{q}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)\right)dR

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}}-\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{I}}-\mathbf{j}(\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I}}+\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{R}}).

By comparing the real and imaginary parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}}+\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{I}}=\frac{1}{2l+1}\delta_{p,q},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}}-\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{I}}=\frac{1}{2l+1}\delta_{p,q_{\ominus}}(-1)^{m-n},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I}}-\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{R}}=0,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I}}+\mathcal{N}_{p,q}^{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{R}}=0

Solving the above equations, we obtain the desired result.
\square

Appendix C
Lie Derivative of Wigner-D functions

For the index
p=(l,m,n)
, we denote
p^{\#}=(l,m,n+1)
,
p^{\flat}=(l,m,n-1)
,

, and
c^{\flat}_{p}=\sqrt{(l+n)(l-n+1)}
. The following lemma describes the Lie derivative of the Wigner-

Lemma 6
(
[
8
]
, Sec. 9.11)

For
p=(l,m,n)
, the Lie derivative of the Wigner-D functions satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{1}}D_{p}(R)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{j}\left(c^{\#}_{p}D_{p^{\#}}(R)+c^{\flat}_{p}D_{p^{\flat}}(R)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{2}}D_{p}(R)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(-c^{\#}_{p}D_{p^{\#}}(R)+c^{\flat}_{p}D_{p^{\flat}}(R)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{3}}D_{p}(R)=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{j}nD_{p}(R).

Separating the real and imaginary parts, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{1}}D_{p}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)=\frac{1}{2}\left(c^{\#}_{p}D_{p^{\#}}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)+c^{\flat}_{p}D_{p^{\flat}}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{1}}D_{p}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(c^{\#}_{p}D_{p^{\#}}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)+c^{\flat}_{p}D_{p^{\flat}}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{2}}D_{p}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)=\frac{1}{2}\left(-c^{\#}_{p}D_{p^{\#}}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)+c^{\flat}_{p}D_{p^{\flat}}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{2}}D_{p}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)=\frac{1}{2}\left(-c^{\#}_{p}D_{p^{\#}}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)+c^{\flat}_{p}D_{p^{\flat}}^{\mathbf{I}}(R)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e_{3}}D_{p}^{\mathbf{R}}(R)=-nD_{p}^{\mathbf{I}}(R),

Corollary 2

For any
f\in L^{2}(\text{SO}(3))
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\text{SO}(3)}\mathcal{L}_{a}f(R)=0.

Proof.
By the completeness of the Wigner-D functions, it suffices to show that

\int_{\text{SO}(3)}\mathcal{L}_{e_{i}}\left(D_{p}(R)\right)dR=0,\quad\forall p=(l,m,n)\text{ and }i=1,2,3.

For
l=0
, we know
D_{0,0}^{0}(R)=1
, so the result is trivial.

For
l\neq 0
, by Lemma
6
, the Lie derivative of the Wigner-D functions does not change the degree
l
. Therefore, using the orthogonality of the Wigner-D functions, we have

\int_{\text{SO}(3)}\mathcal{L}_{e_{i}}\left(D_{p}(R)\right)dR=\int_{\text{SO}(3)}\mathcal{L}_{e_{i}}\left(D_{p}(R)\right)D^{0}_{0,0}(R)dR=0,\quad\forall l\neq 0\text{ and }i=1,2,3.

This completes the proof.
\square
