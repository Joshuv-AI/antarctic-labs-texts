Energy Conservation in Blast Furnace: Analysis on the Replaceability of Coke with Varying Reactivity and Postreaction Strength
Authors: Zhexi Li, Sunny Song, Junchen Huang, Qi Wang, Tingle Li, Songtao Yang, Song Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Currently, coke with significant differences in CRI/CSR (coke reactivity index and coke strength after reaction) can already be effectively utilized in blast furnaces (BFs). However, there remains a considerable controversy on the replaceability of high and low CRI/CSR coke. Therefore, an analysis was conducted on the metallurgical performance of C1, C2, and C3(CRI:C3 > C2 > C1&CSR:C3 < C2 < C1) through simulated BF under the ore-coke coexistence (OCC) experiment. The behavior of solution loss and degradation, reduction, and thermal function of coke was investigated, and the difference of C1 ∼ C3 was quantified through deviation analysis. The findings are as follows: opposite to the result of CRI/CSR testing, the solution loss and degradation behavior of the cokes is similar under OCC. This behavior results in roughly equivalent impacts of coke on the gasification point, indirect and direct reduction. Thermodynamic analysis reveals that there is minimal difference in heat flux trend, particularly within key temperature zones. The deviation analysis result of C2 and C3 comparing to C1 is less than 5%. Therefore, it can be inferred that coke exhibiting significant variations in CRI/CSR may demonstrate same metallurgical performance. Based on this inference, the replacement of C2 and C3 to C1 in BF production could be considered as a potential strategy to optimize resource utilization and cost saving.

Introduction

As an integral component of blast furnace (BF) iron-making, the quality of coke directly determines the production efficiency.1−3 Coke thermal properties, as a crucial index,4−7 significantly impact the smooth and energy consumption of BF,8−12 making it a focal point for research.13 The NSC method proposed by Nippon Steel in the 1970s,14,15 which is widely adopted for testing the thermal properties of coke, has been utilized ever since with appropriate modifications made by China.16 Undoubtedly, CRI/CSR still serve as vital criteria for iron-worker to determine coke type and make suitable choice.

The significance of CRI/CSR in the efficient operation of BF has been extensively demonstrated by numerous studies, exerting a profound impact on the stability of coke ratio, utilization coefficient, and heat exchange. Ansteel17 has conducted an analysis on the correlation between CRI and BF fuel ratio trends, revealing that an increase in CRI results in a corresponding rise in the fuel ratio. This finding is also supported by Naito M’s study.18 Zhijie Cheng19 further elucidated the beneficial impact of low CRI and high CSR on the BF utilization coefficient, coke ratio, and other indices through an analysis of BF data. Liquan Lin20 investigated the relationship between CRI and BF index, concluding that an increase in CRI leads to reduced production efficiency and increased coke ratio. Therefore, the utilization of low CRI and high CSR coke in BF has consistently been regarded as the prevailing viewpoint of researchers, contributing significantly to the production efficiency.

However, as BF scale continue to expand, the availability of high-quality coal resources is becoming increasingly scarce,21−23 the costs associated with coking are progressively escalating which makes the price of low CRI and high CSR coke remains significantly elevated. Consequently, some enterprises are compelled to utilize coke with high CRI and low CSR in order to sustain their production operations.24,25 Surprisingly though, this alteration has a minimal impact on the BF itself, resulting in cost savings without compromising the production process. Similarly, coke with high CRI and low CSR continues to be utilized successfully in BF while maintaining fine indices.25 Nevertheless, there remains some debate on whether coke with a substantial difference in CRI/CSR can yield equivalent smelting effects.

There is still no reasonable explanation for the aforementioned situation, and the fundamental issue lies in whether the metallurgical performances of different CRI/CSR coke are equivalent, a key aspect that is often overlooked. Several scholars have presented their perspectives on this matter. Wang26 conducted thermal property measurements of coke with varying CRI/CSR using the equal solubility method and observed consistent coke strength after solution loss, despite significant differences in CRI/CSR. Similarly, Zhang27 examined the thermal properties of coke under simulated BF conditions and obtained same results. Ujisawa28 approached this problem from a perspective of coke matching and demonstrated that different ore-blending can significantly influence the reducing effect of coke, suggesting that even cokes with distinct CRI/CSR may exhibit comparable reducing function when influenced by ore-blending. By establishing a thermodynamic model, Meng29 proposed a possibility that different CRI/CSR coke may exert similar thermal effect on BF. While these findings provide partial explanations, they only address specific aspects, further research is necessary to elucidate why cokes with vastly different CRI/CSR can still be utilized.

Therefore, in this paper, the metallurgical performance of C1 ∼ C3 with varying CRI/CSR was investigated by comparing the results of ore-coke coexistence (OCC) experiment and thermodynamic analysis, focusing on solution loss and degradation, reducing function and thermal function. Next, the difference in above trend was quantified through deviation analysis, elucidating the potential for substitution. The aim of this study is to gain a clearer understanding of the metallurgical performance of coke and provide theoretical references for cost reduction through rational substitution of coke, thereby promoting more efficient resource utilization.

The experimental device for coke CRI/CSR testing, as depicted in Figure 1, was self-constructed. Following the experiment, the coke samples were processed by using an I-type drum device. Experimental Procedure of CRI/CSR Testing

The experimental procedure was conducted in accordance with national standard GB/T4000—2017. Following drying, the coke sample (200 g) is placed inside the furnace and heated at a rate of 5 °C/min, while injecting 5 L/min of protective gas N2 (99.9%). Once the temperature reaches 1100 °C, we recalibrated the balance and waited for temperature stabilization before transitioning from N2 to CO2 (99.9%). After a duration of 2 h, it switched back from CO2 to N2, when the sample cooled to room temperature, then the sample was removed and weighed for CRI calculations. Subsequently, drum the coke at a speed of 20 rpm for 30 min. CSR is determined by comparing particles larger than 10 mm with their mass after reaction.

Experimental Procedure of the OCC Experiment

After being dried, the test samples were sequentially loaded into the crucible, as shown in Figure 4. Bottom layer consists of coke, which is soluted loss of 25% at 1100 °C, with a mass of 75 g and particle size ranging from 13–15 mm. Middle layer has a mineral sample weighing 500 g with particle size ranging from 10–12.5 mm. Top layer comprises fresh coke weighing 100 g and the size between 18 and 20 mm. A load of 1 kg/cm2 was applied using a cylinder throughout the experiment. The initial heating rate was set at 10 °C/min until it reached 600 °C during which a protective gas N2 (99.9%) at a flow rate of 5 L/min was injected. Then, thermal balance was maintained for 40 min at 500 °C. Once the central temperature reached 600, N2 (99.9%) was replaced by a reaction gas 30%CO(99.9%)+70%N2(99.9%) which is to simulate BF gas composition32−36 at a flow rate of 15 L/min until the end of the experiment. The central temperature then increased to 900 °C at the rate of 10 °C/min and then followed by a gradual increase from 900 to 1100 °C at the rate of 2.5 °C/min, which is to simulate the heat exchange in the thermal reserve zone of BF.35−38 At 1100 °C, the heating rate was adjusted to 5 °C/min, which is to simulate the heat exchange in the softening and melting zone of BF.35−38 When the central temperature reached 1600 °C, the inlet gas was switched to N2 (99.9%) at a flow rate of 5 L/min. As can be obtained from Figure 7a, which is calculated from Figure 2, the reaction rate of C1 ∼ C3 exhibits obvious differences, with almost no overlapping points observed among the three curves. Moreover, the reactivity decreases in accordance with the order of C3, C2, and C1, aligning with their respective CRI. These findings unequivocally demonstrate a complete differentiation in reactivity among C1 ∼ C3. Similarly, significant differences in particle size distributions are observed among C1 ∼ C3, as illustrated in Figure 7b. The respective values exceeding 18 mm are 55, 46, and 38%, exhibiting substantial variations which is same as the range of 15 mm ∼18 mm. These findings unequivocally demonstrate the incongruity in degradation behavior across C1 ∼ C3. Based on the aforementioned results, it is evident that C1 ∼ C3 has enormous disparity of solution loss and degradation behavior when there are subjected to CRI/CSR testing, indicating significant variations in the thermal properties.

Analysis on the Trend of Coke Reducing Function

This section investigates the reduction function of C1 ∼ C3. In the BF, CO2 is generated through coke gasification, which influences the ore reduction process. The ore reduction in this reaction can be simplified into indirect reduction (IR) and direct reduction (DR), which are divided by the coke gasification point (Tc). The extent of IR development relates to the environmental impact of coke gasification, while DR is closely associated with coke’s ability to reduce the ore. These factors represent the reducibility of coke. Therefore, in this section, Tc, IR degree, and DR degree are employed as criteria for evaluating the reducibility of coke while analyzing the influence of C1 ∼ C3 on this process.

In general, coke with different CRI/CSR should have distinct reducibility. Tc is closely associated with the CO2 concentration provided by ore reduction and coke itself reactivity. Based on the result of CRI/CSR testing, the reaction trends of C1 ∼ C3 differ significantly, which means the discrepancy in their CO2 consumption capabilities, inevitably leading to variations in environmental CO2 concentration. Additionally, considering their inherent reactivity differences, it can be inferred that the Tc value for C1 ∼ C3 will vary greatly. This principle also applies to IR degree and DR degree trends, IR is directly influenced by CO concentration levels, from the result of CRI/CSR testing, there is significant variation in solution loss from C1 ∼ C3. Consequently, this inevitably results in different environmental CO concentrations which further implies a substantial difference in IR degree trends. As DR represents a combination of IR and coke gasification reaction, under different IR degree and gasification behavior trends, DR degree of C1 ∼ C3 should follow the completely unlike trend.

However, this trend was not observed in the OCC experiment. The trends of Tc during the experiment are illustrated in Figure 9, which is determined by identifying the inflection point on the curve depicting the rate of coke weight loss under the Qisunny method.30,31Tc of C1 ∼ C3, as depicted in Figure 9, has minimal variation around 1000 °C. The maximum difference observed is merely 3 °C, which can be considered negligible. Such a case is highly unlikely to occur in CRI/CSR testing, implying that a significant disparity in CRI/CSR does not necessarily correspond to a substantial difference in Tc.

The trends of IR degree and DR degree during the experiment are illustrated in Figure 10, which is calculated from the weight loss data in Figure 6 by applying the Qisunny method through the carbon oxygen balance.30,31 The figure clearly indicates that there are rarely significant changes observed in the IR degree and DR degree of C1 ∼ C3. As depicted in Figure 10a, the process of the IR can be divided into two stages. In the initial reduction stage (600–1000 °C) without coke gasification, the curves exhibit a consistent fit, which aligns with the characteristics of the same ore-blending. During the coke gasification interval (1000–1400 °C), C1 ∼ C3 demonstrate similar behavior. Despite minor fluctuations in values, these differences remain negligible with a maximum error less than 5%. Throughout the reaction process, there are a lot of instances where the curves fit. Considering the overall situation, it is suggested that their influence on IR is roughly comparable. As the same, DR degree of C1 ∼ C3 exhibits similar behavior. Although there is minor fluctuation observed in the initial stage of the reaction (1000–1250 °C), it is evident that the fluctuation is negligible and has almost no significant impact on the overall trend. As the reaction progressed into the intense zone (1250–1400 °C), a remarkable consistency is observed in the DR degree. The three curves display an almost perfect fit, making it challenging to distinguish between them. Overall, it can be concluded that the DR degree trends from C1 ∼ C3 are nearly identical. Base on the aforementioned findings, it appears that the reduction function of C1 ∼ C3 is approximately equivalent.

Conclusions

By conducting OCC experiment and performing thermodynamic analysis, the solution loss and degradation, reduction function, and thermal function of C1 ∼ C3 with different CRI/CSR were analyzed in this study, and the disparity in metallurgical performance of C1 ∼ C3 was quantified through deviation analysis, elucidating the potential for mutual substitution. The key conclusions are as follows:(1).According to the results of the OCC experiment, the solution loss and degradation behavior of C1 ∼ C3 exhibit remarkable similarity, with consistent skeleton action. The same coke gasification point, as well as the trends in indirect and DR, indicate nearly identical reduction function. Thermodynamic analysis reveals minimal disparity in heat flow trend among C1 ∼ C3, resulting in similar effect on ambient temperature, thus demonstrating comparable thermal function.(2).The results of all deviation analyses indicate that the deviation of C2 and C3 to C1 is below 5%. Consequently, it can be inferred that the metallurgical performance of C1 ∼ C3 is comparable. Hence, in BF smelting, when coke with significant differences in CRI and CSR exhibits similar metallurgical performance, a replaceability relationship can be established.
