Coarse-to-Fine Hierarchical Alignment for UAV-based Human Detection using Diffusion Models
Wenda Li, Meng Wu, Sungmin Eum, Heesung Kwon, Qing Qu

Abstract
Training object detectors demands extensive, task-specific annotations, yet this requirement becomes impractical in UAV-based human detection due to constantly shifting target distributions and the scarcity of labeled images. As a remedy, synthetic simulators are adopted to generate annotated data, with a low annotation cost. However, the domain gap between synthetic and real images hinders the model from being effectively applied to the target domain. Accordingly, we introduce Coarse-to-Fine Hierarchical Alignment (CFHA), a three-stage diffusion-based framework designed to transform synthetic data for UAV-based human detection, narrowing the domain gap while preserving the original synthetic labels. CFHA explicitly decouples global style and local content domain discrepancies and bridges those gaps using three modules: (1) Global Style Transfer—a diffusion model aligns color, illumination, and texture statistics of synthetic images to the realistic style, using only a small real reference set; (2) Local Refinement—a super-resolution diffusion model is used to facilitate fine-grained and photorealistic details for the small objects, such as human instances, preserving shape and boundary integrity; (3) Hallucination Removal—a module that filters out human instances whose visual attributes do not align with real-world data to make the human appearance closer to the target distribution. Extensive experiments on public UAV Sim2Real detection benchmarks demonstrate that our methods significantly improve the detection accuracy compared to the non-transformed baselines. Specifically, our method achieves up to + 14.1 +14.1 improvement of mAP50 on Semantic-Drone benchmark. Ablation studies confirm the complementary roles of the global and local stages and highlight the importance of hierarchical alignment. The code is released at this url .

Introduction

While deep learning-based detectors have achieved remarkable performance on benchmark suites such as COCO
21
and Open-Images
20
, their success is fundamentally dependent on
large-scale, labor-intensive human annotations
. The creation of such datasets is a monumental effort—Microsoft COCO alone required over 25,000 worker-hours. This reliance on manual annotation creates a significant bottleneck, rendering these powerful models costly or even infeasible to deploy in many long-tail or safety-critical domains, such as drone-view imagery, rare-defect inspection, and robotics.

Synthetic data emerges as an attractive alternative

The ability of modern game engines and generative models to render massive volumes of labeled data at negligible marginal cost offers a compelling alternative to manual annotation
35
;
12
. However, the promise of synthetic data is consistently undermined by the notorious
sim-to-real
(Sim2Real) domain gap. This discrepancy between simulated and real-world images is the primary cause of poor generalization, as detectors trained on synthetic data often fail when deployed on real photographs due to subtle yet critical differences in low-level appearance, lighting, and textures
25
. Bridging this gap remains a significant and open problem, particularly for the unique and highly variable conditions of UAV-captured imagery.

Which factors
actually
matter for detection?

While the Sim2Real domain gap is a known obstacle for detectors, a critical question remains: which specific factors of this gap most significantly impact performance? To answer this, we propose a factor-driven approach to dissect the problem, identifying two distinct components:

•

The global style gap:
including differences in lighting, texture statistics, and camera pose.

•

The local content gap:

discrepancies in the characteristics of the objects themselves, such as their density and arrangement. Notably, such local inconsistencies may also emerge during global style transformations, where hallucinations can introduce unrealistic object patterns.

Existing Sim2Real methods have focused almost exclusively on closing the global style gap. However, our key insight is that the local content gap is an equally critical, yet overlooked, source of performance degradation. For instance, synthetic images may contain hundreds of people, while real-world drone images typically feature fewer than ten. This vast difference in human instances density is a content gap that style transfer fails to address. Therefore, this paper introduces a novel hierarchical framework that, for the first time, systematically bridges both the style and content gaps—the latter using a diffusion model to seamlessly edit instances—leading to superior performance.

Diffusion models for UAV imagery translation

State-of-the-art diffusion models, with their ability to generate photorealistic images while preserving content
28
;
26
, are powerful tools for Sim2Real style transfer. However, their application to critical domains like UAV-based object detection is hindered by two significant challenges. First, existing diffusion-based methods are data-hungry, requiring abundant real images for fine-tuning, making them impractical in common scenarios where only a handful of real images are available. Second, standard style transfer techniques often fail on UAV imagery due to the presence of very small objects, for example humans, frequently corrupting their semantic information and introducing severe artifacts.

To overcome these limitations, we propose Coarse-to-Fine Hierarchical Alignment (CFHA), a unified framework that perform style transfer with a novel super-resolution component and perform hallucination removal. Our approach is specifically designed to operate in a few-shot setting while simultaneously preserving the fidelity of small, distant objects. In summary, our contributions are threefold:

•

We propose
CFHA
, a novel three-stage framework that systematically bridges the Sim2Real domain gap by addressing
both
global style disparities and local content inconsistencies.

•

To the best of our knowledge, CFHA is the first framework to leverage diffusion models for the complex domain adaptation problem in UAV-based person detection. It also utilizes advanced generative and vision-language models to ensure high-fidelity, semantically-aware image translation.

•

Through extensive experiments on public benchmarks, we demonstrate that our method establishes a
new state-of-the-art
, with detectors trained on CFHA-generated data significantly outperforming existing approaches.

Related Work

Sim2Real Adaptation for UAV-based human Detection

Human detection in unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) imagery is a typical long-tailed, low-data vision task, due to large variations in scale, viewpoint, and background, as well as the high cost of collecting and annotating real-world UAV data.
To mitigate data scarcity, many works leverage either advanced algorithms
37
;
22
or large-scale synthetic datasets
31
;
5
with free annotations to enhance UAV perception tasks, including object detection and semantic segmentation.
However, the significant appearance gap between synthetic and real UAV imagery makes effective simulation-to-real (Sim2Real) domain adaptation essential. Additionally, synthetic data may introduce performance degradation
32
, requiring more careful treatment and utilization in model training.

To bridge the Sim2Real gap, several works explore image-level or feature-level adaptation techniques. For instance,
4
introduces Sim2Air, a domain adaptation framework for cross-weather UAV tracking, which incorporates spatiotemporal attention to mitigate style and temporal drift. Their work highlights the challenge of temporal consistency in real-world UAV deployment.
36
proposed a bi-directional simulation-to-reality approach for object detection, leveraging dual generative models to jointly enhance synthetic realism and improve detector adaptation. This method emphasizes the hierarchical feedback between detection quality and image fidelity.
29
develops a multi-scale adversarial adaptation framework for UAV pedestrian detection, using feature-level alignment across scales to improve robustness under varying UAV altitudes and viewpoints, underscoring the importance of spatial resolution modeling in aerial detection.

Despite the growing interest in Sim2Real adaptation, relatively few results have specifically addressed the Sim2Real gap for UAV-based human object detection. One notable exception is PTL
31
, which progressively transforms the local humans in synthetic images into realistic ones using a conditional GAN framework. In contrast, our framework transforms the images from both global and local perspectives and utilizes diffusion models as the generative method.

2.1
Diffusion Models for Image-to-Image Translation

Diffusion models
42
;
15
;
26
;
28
have recently emerged as a powerful class of generative models, demonstrating superior performance in diverse image synthesis tasks, including unconditional
15
;
33
, inpainting
39
;
23
, and conditional translation
24
;
6
. Their iterative denoising formulation avoids common issues in GAN-based models such as mode collapse and training instability, making them particularly appealing for high-fidelity image translation. Chitwan et al.
30
proposed Palette, a conditional diffusion model tailored for image-to-image tasks such as colorization and inpainting. Jooyoung et al.
9
introduced ILVR, which guides the diffusion process using low-resolution conditioning, allowing for semantically aligned outputs while preserving local structures. Their work showed that diffusion models can effectively preserve the content structure when guided by weak conditions. Chenlin et al.
24
developed Contextual Diffusion Models (CDM) for controllable image synthesis by injecting semantic maps or sketches as spatial conditions.
Omri et al.
2
proposed Blended Diffusion, a technique for localized image editing via spatially modulated noise, enabling region-specific transformations while keeping the rest of the image unchanged. This aligns with our objective of performing localized refinement in Sim2Real transfer while retaining structural consistency.

Despite these advances, existing diffusion-based translation methods primarily focus on artistic editing or general image synthesis tasks, and do not explicitly address domain adaptation or the hierarchical decomposition of distribution shifts. In contrast, our proposed HSRA framework leverages two dedicated diffusion modules for global style alignment and local structure refinement, enabling effective Sim2Real transfer in detection-centric tasks under a unified and interpretable architecture.

Figure 1
:

Overview of our proposed three-stage pipeline for synthetic-to-real image translation.

Stage I: Global Style Transfer (GST)
performs style alignment in the latent space by applying DDIM inversion to both content and style images. A structure-aware cross-attention mechanism computes attention weights, which are used to extract aligned style features and fuse them with the content features via AdaIN, resulting in a globally styled image that preserves the structure of the content.

Stage II: Local Refinement (LR)
enhances local fidelity by cropping object-centric patches and generating corresponding text prompts via image captioning. A pretrained diffusion model fine-tuned by low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
17
;
40
, conditioned on these prompts, refines the visual realism of each patch. The diffusion backbone is frozen while the LoRA adapters are trainable.

Stage III: Hallucination Removal (HR)
addresses unrealistic or unwanted artifacts by applying CLIP-based semantic filtering and mask-guided deletion, ensuring a cleaner and more realistic final output.

2.2
Hierarchical Disentanglement for Domain Transfer

Visual domain adaptation methods in often treat the domain gap between source and target domains as a single transformation, applying global alignment techniques such as adversarial training or feature matching. However, recent research has recognized that domain shift may occur at multiple semantic levels—e.g., in style (color, illumination) and structure (object geometry, fine textures)—and that explicitly modeling such hierarchy can lead to more robust and interpretable adaptation. Yuhua et al.
7
proposed a hierarchical feature alignment (CFHA) method, aligning features at both global and class-specific levels across domain layers. By modeling feature shift hierarchically, HFA improves generalization in semantic segmentation and object detection tasks. Judy et al.
16
pioneered class-wise feature alignment for visual recognition, proposing that inter-class and intra-class discrepancies should be addressed separately. Their framework serves as an early instance of disentangled domain alignment. Chenfan et al.
44
proposed iFAN, a hierarchical alignment framework for unsupervised domain adaptive object detection that integrates both image-level and instance-level alignment. By designing category-aware and category-correlation instance alignment modules, iFAN effectively models multi-level domain shifts and achieves remarkable results on domain adaptation datasets: SIM10K to Cityscapes and Cityscapes to Foggy Cityscapes.

While these methods explore hierarchical alignment at different hierarchical structures, they do not incorporate image-level generative modeling to handle domain shifts in pixel space. Our proposed CFHA framework advances this line of work by employing two diffusion-based model to collaborately transfer global style and refine local details, which is more suitable for UAV-based images, resulting in a more fine-grained and controllable Sim2Real adaptation pipeline for UAV detection.

Preliminary

3.1
Human Detection Tasks

Human detection aims to locate human instances in an image or video frame.
Formally, given an image
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
, a human detection model predicts a set of bounding boxes

\mathcal{B}=\{(a_{k},b_{k},w_{k},h_{k})\}_{k=1}^{M},

where
(a_{k},b_{k})
represents the center of the
i
-th detected human, and
(w_{k},h_{k})
denotes the width and height of the bounding box.
Optionally, the detector outputs confidence scores
\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{M}
indicating the likelihood that each bounding box corresponds to a human.

3.2
Sim2Real Adaptation

In practical scenarios, synthetic images are commonly employed to facilitate low-cost model training.
Nevertheless, the domain shift between synthetic and real data poses a significant challenge, limiting the detector’s generalization to the target domain.
This domain shift can arise from variations in illumination, weather, background, camera viewpoints, or image resolution.

Formally, let
\mathcal{D}_{s}=\{(\bm{x}_{i}^{s},\mathcal{B}_{i}^{s})\}_{i=1}^{N_{s}}
denote the labeled source domain dataset, and
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{(\bm{x}_{j}^{t},\mathcal{B}_{j}^{t})\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
denote the limited target domain dataset.
Here,
\bm{x}_{i}^{s}
and
\bm{x}_{j}^{t}
represent the source and target images, respectively.

The goal of Sim2Real adaptation for human detection is to learn a detector
f_{\theta}
that performs well on
\mathcal{D}_{t}
despite being primarily trained on
\mathcal{D}_{s}
. In our human detection setting, the source domain consists of synthetic datasets, while the target domain corresponds to in-the-wild scenarios such as UAV surveillance or crowded scenes.
Effective domain adaptation is crucial to bridging this gap and achieving robust cross-domain detection performance.

3.3
Diffusion Models

Diffusion models generate data by gradually transforming Gaussian noise into structured samples via a
stochastic denoising process.
Instead of directly estimating the probability density function (PDF) of the image distribution
P_{0}
,
they define a
forward diffusion
that corrupts data with noise and a corresponding

reverse process
that recovers the data
34
;
15
.

The forward process
14
incrementally adds Gaussian noise to a clean sample

\bm{x}_{0}\sim P_{0}
, producing a noisy sample
\bm{x}_{t}
at time
t
with distribution

p(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}\Big(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\bm{x}_{0},\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\bm{I}\Big),

where
\bar{\alpha}_{t}
is a predefined noise schedule.
This process can be equivalently described as a stochastic differential equation (SDE)
34
:

d\bm{x}=f(\bm{x},t)\,dt+g(t)\,d\bm{w},

where
f(\cdot,t)
is the drift term,
g(t)
is the diffusion coefficient,
and
\bm{w}
is standard Brownian motion. The reverse-time SDE drives Gaussian noise back to the data distribution:

d\bm{x}=\Big[f(\bm{x},t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x})\Big]\,dt+g(t)\,d\bm{w},

where
dt<0
evolves the sample from
t=T
back to
t=0
.
The key unknown in this process is the
score function

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x})
, which is learned by a neural network

\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x},t)
via score matching:

\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim p_{t}}\Big[\lambda(t)\big\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x})-\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x},t)\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

4
Method

4.1
Hierarchical Style-Content Alignment

UAV images pose a unique challenge in domain adaptation due to their high-altitude views, small object sizes, and rich background context.
We observe that the domain gap between synthetic and real UAV images arises at multiple levels:

(a)

Global style shifts
— e.g., illumination, color temperature, and environmental rendering artifacts;

(b)

Local content shifts
— e.g., human number and poses.

Addressing both types of shifts simultaneously often leads to either over-smoothed global styles or locally inconsistent artifacts (as shown in Stage 2 of
Figure

1
).
To mitigate this, we introduce a Coarse-to-Fine Hierarchical Alignment (
CFHA
) to bridge the gap, including the following three stages:

•

Global style transfer, where we transfer the overall conditions, such as the color temperature and the illumination.

•

Local refinement, which uses a super-resolution diffusion to enhance local fidelity and mitigate distortion.

•

Hallucination removal, where we use diffusion models to edit the images and remove some persons with hallucinations.

This hierarchical framework allows us to disentangle the global style transfer and local adaptation. Then we design different targeted loss functions for each component, resulting in adapted images that are (i) photorealistic, (ii) spatially consistent, and (iii) task-friendly for detection.

The final detector of our method is trained on the dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\cup\mathcal{D}_{t}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{s}
is the transformed synthetic data and
\mathcal{D}_{t}
is the real data. We use FID
13
to measure the image-level realism of the translated samples and evaluate detection performance on downstream UAV benchmarks.

4.2
Stage I: Global Style Transfer (GST)

The first stage of CFHA is a training-free method that performs global appearance alignment—colour palette, illumination, atmospheric haze—between a synthetic content image
\bm{x}_{i}^{s}
and a small set of real reference images, while strictly preserving spatial labels. We follow the diffusion–based style-transfer formulation in recent work
8
and adopt a streamlined
image-wise
AdaIN mechanism in latent space.

Let
(E,D)
be the VAE encoder/decoder of the Stable-Diffusion
backbone. Encoding a content image
\bm{x}_{i}^{s}
and a randomly
sampled style example
\bm{x}_{j}^{t}
yields latents

\bm{z}_{i,0}^{s}=E(\bm{x}_{i}^{s})
and
\bm{z}_{j,0}^{t}=E(\bm{x}_{j}^{t})
.
After DDIM inversion to timestep
T
, we obtain noisy latents

\bm{z}_{i,T}^{s},\bm{z}_{j,T}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{C^{\prime}\times H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}}
.
For each reverse step
t=T,\dots,1
we perform

\bm{z}_{i,t-1}\;=\;\mathrm{UNet}_{\phi}\Bigl(\,\underbrace{\text{AdaIN}(\hat{\bm{z}}^{s}_{i,t},\bm{z}^{t}_{i,t})}_{\text{global statistics}},\,t\Bigr)

(1)

where

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=W_{q}^{s}\bm{z}^{s}_{i,t},\quad K=W_{k}^{t}\bm{z}^{t}_{j,t},\quad V=W_{v}^{t}\bm{z}^{t}_{j,t},

(2)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),\hat{\bm{z}}^{s}_{i,t}=AV,

(3)

AdaIN

\displaystyle(\hat{\bm{z}}^{s}_{i,t},\bm{z}^{t}_{i,t})=\sigma(\bm{z}^{t}_{i,t})\cdot\frac{\hat{\bm{z}}^{s}_{i,t}-\mu(\hat{\bm{z}}^{s}_{i,t})}{\sigma(\hat{\bm{z}}^{s}_{i,t})}+\mu(\bm{z}^{t}_{i,t}).

(4)

GST aligns global appearance statistics—colour temperature, illumination, atmospheric haze—while preserving the spatial structure of the synthetic image.

After
T
reverse steps the decoder produces

\tilde{\bm{x}}=D(\bm{z}_{0})
, a photorealistic image whose global
appearance closely matches the target distribution while the
object geometry—and hence all bounding-box annotations—are
unchanged.
These GST outputs serve as inputs to the Local Refinement
stage (Stage II
4.3
CR), which restores semantic, style information and removes residual artifacts.

4.3
Stage II: Local Refinement (LR)

The second stage targets the
local shifts
left unresolved by GST: blurred object contours, over-smoothed textures, and human
artifacts (see
Figure

1
).
We employ a
super-resolution diffusion model
that (i) up-scales GST outputs by a factor
s{=}2

and (ii) removes the artifacts created in the vanilla style transfer method.

Inspired by OSEDiff
38
we regard the GST output

\tilde{\bm{x}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
as a
latent starting point

rather than random noise.
Let
E_{\bm{\theta}}
and
D_{\bm{\theta}}
denote the VAE encoder/decoder of a
pre-trained Stable-Diffusion backbone, equipped with rank-4 LoRA layers.
The latent diffusion network
\,\epsilon_{\theta}
is fine-tuned in the same
low-rank manner.
For an up–sampling factor
s{=}2
we form the bicubic

\bm{x}_{\!\uparrow}
, encode it to
\bm{z}_{L}\!=\!E_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{\!\uparrow})
and apply
one reverse step at timestep
T
:

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{H}=F_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{L};\,\bm{c}_{y})=\frac{\bm{z}_{L}-\beta_{T}\,\epsilon_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{L};T,\bm{c}_{y})}{\alpha_{T}}

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}=D_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{H}),

(6)

where
(\bm{\alpha}_{T},\bm{\beta}_{T})
are DDPM coefficients and
\bm{c}_{y}
is a tag-style prompt extracted from
\tilde{\bm{x}}
via DAPE
8
, and
\bm{z}_{L}
is only modified within the predefined mask corresponding to each instance. Training minimizes a weighted data term and a
latent variational score distillation
(VSD) loss that aligns the score field of
\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}
with that of the frozen teacher
\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\phi}}
.

This one-step mechanism restores fine textures while
avoiding the heavy cost of multi-step sampling. We visualize examples of style transformation in

4.4
Stage III: Hallucination Removal (HR)

GST may still hallucinate synthetic-looking humans. To further suppress unrealistic identities, we introduce a
CLIP-guided similarity filter
that preferentially retains human instances whose visual style aligns with real-world data. We leverage the pre-trained CLIP model
27
due to its robust generalization capabilities, learned from web-scale data. Its joint image-text embedding space is particularly advantageous, as it allows for textual guidance to anchor the visual concepts we wish to model.

Instead of performing a costly nearest-neighbor search against a large memory bank of real human embeddings
\{\bm{u}_{m}\}
, we distill their central tendency into a single, prototypical reference vector
\bm{t}_{*}
. This vector is optimized to be maximally similar to the mean embedding of real examples,
\bar{\bm{u}}=\text{normalize}(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\bm{u}_{m})
, while also staying close to a text anchor embedding,
\bm{t}_{\text{anchor}}
, generated from a general description like “a photo of a person taken from a drone." This text anchor acts as a regularizer, grounding the visual prototype. The optimization objective is to find a vector
\bm{v}
on the unit hypersphere that maximizes:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{v})=\bm{v}^{\top}\bar{\bm{u}}+\lambda(\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{t}_{\text{anchor}})

(7)

where
\lambda
is a hyperparameter controlling the influence of the text anchor. While this can be solved with iterative gradient descent, this objective has a more efficient closed-form solution. The optimal reference vector
\bm{t}_{*}
is found by simply normalizing the weighted sum:

\bm{t}_{*}=\frac{\bar{\bm{u}}+\lambda\,\bm{t}_{\text{anchor}}}{\|\bar{\bm{u}}+\lambda\,\bm{t}_{\text{anchor}}\|_{2}}

(8)

With this learned reference, we embed each synthetic person crop
\bm{c}_{k}
into
\bm{v}_{k}=\mathrm{CLIP}(\bm{c}_{k})
and define its similarity score
s_{k}
as the cosine similarity to
\bm{t}_{*}
. The scores are converted into a sampling distribution using a softmax function:

s_{k}=\frac{\bm{v}_{k}^{\!\top}\bm{t}_{*}}{\|\bm{v}_{k}\|_{2}\|\bm{t}_{*}\|_{2}},\qquad p_{k}=\frac{\exp(\alpha s_{k})}{\sum_{j}\exp(\alpha s_{j})},

(9)

where
\alpha
adjusts the sharpness of the distribution. During mini-batch construction, we
stochastically retain
crop
k
with probability
p_{k}
; all other human instances are disregarded from the images and annotations, yielding a cleaned training set. We visualize the examples of HR in
Figure

3
.

5
Experiments

In this section, we present a comprehensive set of experiments to demonstrate how our proposed framework can mitigate the domain gap both in style and in content across various datasets. We begin by highlighting its performance in detection accuracy in a cross-domain setting.

5.1
Experimental Settings

Datasets

Our adaptation is performed on the synthetic UAV datasets to real UAV datasets. Since the annotated target images are very sparse in the real application, the training dataset contains only
20
real images from the target domain and
500
synthetic images from the source domain.

We use the Synplay dataset
41
as the synthetic dataset.
Synplay
is a synthetic human dataset featuring over 73k images and 6.5M human instances, designed to capture diverse human appearances with realistic motions and multiple camera viewpoints. It enables improved human detection and segmentation, particularly for data-scarce and cross-domain learning scenarios.

For the real datasets, we adopt three benchmarks, Okutama-Action
3
, Semantic drone
19
, VisDrone2018
43
and Manipal-UAV
1
.

Okutama-Action

3
is a video dataset for aerial-view concurrent human action detection, containing 43 fully-annotated minute-long sequences with 12 action classes. It introduces challenges such as dynamic action transitions, large-scale and aspect ratio changes, abrupt camera movements, and multi-labeled actors, making it more realistic and difficult than existing datasets.
Semantic Drone

19
provides high-resolution urban aerial imagery for advancing autonomous drone perception. It includes 600 images with pixel-accurate annotations for 20 semantic classes and bounding boxes for person detection, supporting both object detection and semantic segmentation tasks.
VisDrone2018

43
which offers 263 videos and 10,209 images with over 2.5 million annotated instances for object detection and tracking in diverse drone scenes. We also conduct experiments on
Manipal-UAV-Person

1
comprises 33 UAV videos and 13,462 sampled images with 153,112 annotated person instances, captured using multiple drone platforms under diverse altitudes, lighting, and weather conditions, specifically designed to benchmark small-scale and densely distributed human detection in challenging UAV scenarios.

Metrics

In our evaluation for the detector, we report the mAP@50 and mAP@50-95.
mAP@50
(mean Average Precision at IoU threshold 0.5) evaluates the average detection performance under a relatively loose matching criterion.
mAP@50-95
, following the COCO evaluation protocol, averages mAP across IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 with a step size of 0.05, providing a more comprehensive and stringent assessment of detection accuracy under varying overlap conditions.

5.2
Implementation Details

Our framework is implemented using PyTorch. For the core translation stages, we integrate several pre-existing models. The Local Refinement (LR) stage utilizes the super-resolution checkpoint from OSEDiff
38
. For the content removal stage, we employ AttentiveEraser
10
to ensure high-fidelity instances removal. The semantic aligner within our CLIP-guided filter uses the pre-trained ViT-L-14
11
from the OpenCLIP library
18
. In our experiments, we randomly select a training set of 500 images and a validation set of 50 images from the SynPlay dataset
41
. For the CLIP-guided filtering stage, we set the hyperparameter
\lambda=0.2
. This value controls the influence of the textual anchor when generating the visual prototype, ensuring that the filtering process is grounded in a general semantic context.

Detector Training

Once the image translation framework CFHA has generated the adapted dataset

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{s}=\{(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}^{s},\mathcal{B}_{i}^{s})\}
,
we train the detector
f_{\theta}
using both the
original real images
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and the translated images
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{s}
.
This dual training scheme stabilizes detector learning:
the original data maintain clean labels and geometric fidelity, while the translated data bring appearance realism.

The total detection loss can be defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{det-total}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\;\lambda_{\text{orig}}\,\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x},\mathcal{B})\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}}(f_{\theta}(\bm{x}),\mathcal{B})\bigr]

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{tran}}\,\mathbb{E}_{(\tilde{\bm{x}},\mathcal{B})\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}}(f_{\theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}),\mathcal{B})\bigr],

(10)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}}
is the standard detection loss
(e.g., classification + box regression as in Faster R-CNN or YOLO),
and
\lambda_{\text{orig}},\lambda_{\text{tran}}>0

are scalar weights. We set
\lambda_{\text{orig}}=\lambda_{\text{trans}}=1
in the experiments but they could also be tuned due to the imbalance between synthetic and real images. We use YOLO 11x as the detection model architecture.

5.4
Ablation Study

In this section, we conduct some ablation experiments to compare the different effects and roles of each stage for the final detection performance. As introduced in the Method Section, the proposed CFHA framework consists of three main components: Global Style Transfer (GST), Local Refinement (LR), and Hallucination Removal (HR). These modules work collaboratively to achieve alignment in both style and content between domains. From
Table

2
, we can observe that

•

The GST method by itself can even degrade the performance due to the introduction of artifacts on the small human objects.

•

Local refinement improves the detection performance by eliminating the artifacts.

•

Object(Human) removal further enhances the detector by aligning the content distributions.

We further investigate how patch-wise FID and image-wise FID correlate with detection performance. As shown in Table
2
, simply reducing the overall background appearance discrepancy (i.e., lower image-wise FID from 320 to 235 in the “GST" row) brings performance degradation in mAP@50. GST slightly worsens the patch-wise FID (107 vs. 104), indicating that global style alignment may introduce artifacts near human regions.

However, once local refinement is introduced (“GST + LR"), the patch-wise FID drops significantly from 107 to 69, accompanied with a increase in image-wise FID (from 235 to 323). This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that LR specifically enhances local patches around human instances using caption-guided diffusion, while leaving the background untouched. As a result, the enhanced human regions may exhibit improved realism but introduce stylistic inconsistency with the surrounding background, thus increasing the overall FID when computed across the full image. Even if the image-wise FID increases, there is a substantial gain in detection performance, from 64.6 to 68.2 in mAP@50 and from 22.4 to 25.3 in mAP@50–95. These results highlight that patch-level realism—specifically around object instances—is much more predictive and essential for downstream detection performance than global visual quality. It supports our motivation to include the local refinement stage with object-centric generation and prompt guidance.

Conclusion

We introduced
CFHA
, a hierarchical diffusion-based framework that bridges both the global style gap and the local content gap for UAV human detection. By combining global style transfer with super-resolution refinement and local hallucination removal, CFHA effectively narrows the Sim2Real domain gap while preserving annotation fidelity. Experiments on public benchmarks show that CFHA significantly improves detection performance, achieving up to
+14.1
mAP@50 over baselines. Our results highlight the importance of jointly aligning style and content for UAV human detection and provide an effective method to improve the performance on human detection.
