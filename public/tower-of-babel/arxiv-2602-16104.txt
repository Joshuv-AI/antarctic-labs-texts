On the discrete Heine-Shephard problem for four lattice polygons
Darren Gerrity, Ivan Soprunov

Abstract
. We study the set of square-free parts of volume polynomials associated with four planar lattice polytopes. This is motivated by the problem of describing possible pairwise intersection numbers of four curves in ( ℂ ∗ ) 2 (\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2} with prescribed Newton polytopes and generic coefficients. It is known that for arbitrary convex bodies in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , the corresponding square-free polynomials are characterized by the Plücker-type inequalities. We show that this characterization fails in the lattice setting: the interior of the space defined by the Plücker-type inequalities contains integer polynomials that are and are not realizable by lattice polytopes. This phenomenon arises from additional arithmetic constraints on the mixed areas of lattice polytopes. These constraints become apparent when we study a “discrete diagram”, which maps a pair of planar lattice polytopes to their mixed area together with their lattice widths in a given direction.

1.
Introduction

In
[
14
]
Minkowski introduced the notion of mixed volume
\vv(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
of any collection of
d
convex bodies
K_{1},\dots,K_{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is the unique symmetric and multiliniear (with respect to Minkowski addition) function which coincides with the volume when all the bodies are the same,
K_{1}=\dots=K_{d}
. Many geometric invariants of a convex body, such as surface area, mean width, or volume of a projection can be expressed using mixed volumes. In 1970’s, thanks to the results of Bernstein, Kushnirenko, and Khovanskii
[
4
,
13
,
10
]
, mixed volumes made their appearance in algebraic geometry as intersection numbers of hypersurfaces defined by generic polynomials with prescribed configuration of the exponent vectors (Newton polytopes). To make things more precise, consider a bivariate Laurent polynomial

f(x,y)=\sum_{(a,b)\in A}\lambda_{(a,b)}x^{a}y^{b},

where
A
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\lambda_{(a,b)}
are non-zero complex numbers. The set
A
is called the support of
f
and its convex hull is called the Newton polytope
P_{f}
of
f
. According to the BKK Theorem
[
6
, Sec 7.5]
, given two Laurent polynomials
f,g
with Newton polytopes
P_{f}
and
P_{g}
and generic coefficients, the number of common zeros of
f
and
g
in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
equals twice the
mixed area

\vv(P_{f},P_{g})
. The latter can be computed as

(1.1)

\vv(P_{f},P_{g})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\vol_{2}(P_{f}+P_{g})-\vol_{2}(P_{f})-\vol_{2}(P_{g})\right),

where
P_{f}+P_{g}
is the Minkowski sum of the polytopes and
\vol_{2}
denotes the area, i.e. the 2-dimensional Euclidean volume.
In other words,
2\vv(P_{f},P_{g})
is the (global) intersection number
(\Gamma_{f}\cdot\Gamma_{g})
of two curves
\Gamma_{f}
and
\Gamma_{g}
in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
defined by
f
and
g
.

This paper is motivated by the following question. Let
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{n}
be
n
curves in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}

defined by generic Laurent polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
with Newton polytopes
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
.
They define
{n\choose 2}
pairwise intersection numbers
(\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Gamma_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
. What are possible relations between these numbers? Equivalently, what are possible relations between

{n\choose 2}
mixed areas
\vv(P_{i},P_{j})
defined by arbitrary collections
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}

of
n
lattice polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
?

In
[
2
]
it was shown that starting with
n=4
there are algebraic relations between the
{n\choose 2}
mixed areas of
n
lattice polytopes (in fact, arbitrary convex bodies) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In particular, for
n=4
, the six mixed areas
v_{ij}=\vv(P_{i},P_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq 4
satisfy three quadratic inequalities, called the Plücker-type inequalities:

v_{12}v_{34}+v_{13}v_{24}\geq v_{14}v_{23},\quad v_{13}v_{24}+v_{14}v_{23}\geq v_{12}v_{34},\quad v_{14}v_{23}+v_{12}v_{34}\geq v_{13}v_{24}.

Moreover, the Plücker-type inequalities, together with the non-negativity of the
v_{ij}
, completely describe the set of all possible 6-tuples of mixed areas of 4 convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
[
2
, Th 3.2]
.
However, if we restrict ourselves to the case of lattice polytopes, will there be additional relations besides the Plücker-type inequalities and the necessary condition
2v_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
? In this paper we show that, although there are no additional algebraic relations between the
v_{ij}
, there are some arithmetic conditions the six integers
2v_{ij}
must satisfy.

The question of describing all possible relations between mixed volumes of a collection of convex bodies goes back to the results of Heine
[
9
]
and Shephard
[
17
]
. Let
K_{1},\dots,K_{n}
be convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

and consider their Minkowski linear combination
x_{1}K_{1}+\dots+x_{n}K_{n}
for some non-negative scaling factors
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Then, as shown by Minkowski
[
14
]
, the volume of
x_{1}K_{1}+\dots+x_{n}K_{n}
is a homogeneous degree
d
polynomial in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with mixed volumes as coefficients:

(1.2)

\vol_{d}(x_{1}K_{1}+\dots+x_{n}K_{n})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\leq n}\!\vv(K_{i_{1}},\dots,K_{i_{d}})x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}}.

(Note that we allow repetitions of the indices in the above sum.)
For example, for
n=d=2
, using the symmetry of the mixed volume and the fact that
\vv(K,K)=\vol_{2}(K)
, we obtain the following quadratic form

(1.3)

\vol_{2}(x_{1}K_{1}+x_{2}K_{2})=\vol_{2}(K_{1})x_{1}^{2}+2\vv(K_{1},K_{2})x_{1}x_{2}+\vol_{2}(K_{2})x_{2}^{2}.

The distinctive feature of this form is that it has non-negative coefficients and is
indefinite
. The latter is provided by the Minkowski inequality
[
16
, Th 7.2.1]

(1.4)

\vol_{2}(K_{1})\vol_{2}(K_{2})\leq\vv(K_{1},K_{2})^{2}.

The Heine-Shephard problem asks for a description of the space of all volume polynomials (
1.2
), which we denote by
\cal V_{n,d}
, in terms of polynomial inequalities on their coefficients. For example, the space
\cal V_{2,2}
of volume polynomials of pairs of planar bodies (
1.3
) is precisely the space of indefinite quadratic forms with non-negative coefficients. Apart from the case of
n=d=2
above, the only known descriptions of
\cal V_{n,d}
are by Heine for
n=3
and
d=2
and by Shephard for
n=2
and arbitrary
d\geq 3
, see
[
9
,
17
]
.

The question we consider in this paper is related to the
discrete
version of the Heine-Shephard problem, in which the class of all convex bodies is replaced with the class of lattice polytopes. The smallest nontrivial case of the discrete Heine-Shephard problem for
n=d=2
was recently solved in
[
18
]
. Namely, it was shown that the space

\cal V_{2,2}(\mathbb{Z})
of volume polynomials of pairs of planar lattice polytopes equals the space of indefinite quadratic forms with non-negative integer coefficients. In this paper, we consider
n=3,4
and
d=2
, but we focus on the
square-free
part of the volume polynomial
\vol_{2}(x_{1}K_{1}+\dots+x_{n}K_{n})
, i.e.

v(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\!2\vv(K_{i},K_{j})x_{i}x_{j}.

Let
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{n,2}
denote the space of all such polynomials and
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{n,2}(\mathbb{Z})
the subset of those corresponding to lattice polytopes. For
n=3
the situation is simple:
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{3,2}(\mathbb{Z})
consists of all trivariate square-free quadratic forms with non-negative integer coefficients, see Proposition
3.1
. For
n=4
, as we pointed out above,
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
is a subset of the
discrete Plücker set

{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
of square-free quadratic forms whose coefficients are non-negative integers and satisfy Plücker-type inequalities

{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})=\Big\{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\!v_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\,:\,v_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\,v_{ij}v_{kl}+v_{ik}v_{jl}\geq v_{il}v_{jk},\text{ for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}=[4]\Big\}.

In Section
6
we show that every boundary point of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
(i.e. where at least one of the Plücker-type inequalities becomes equality) belongs to
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
. In Sections
7
and
8
we describe families of polynomials in the interior of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
that lie inside and outside of
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Our analysis is based on understanding a “discrete diagram” which is the image of the map that sends a pair of planar lattice polytopes to the triple consisting of their lattice widths (in some direction) and their mixed area. We do this in Section
4
.

Another version of the Heine-Shephard problem, also motivated by a question in intersection theory, was recently considered in
[
11
]
. There, the authors study the problem of describing all relations among the areas of the projections of a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
onto the six two-dimensional coordinate subspaces. These projections can be expressed as mixed volumes
\vv(K,K,I_{i},I_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq 4
, where
I_{1},\dots,I_{4}
are the coordinate unit segments. Thus, the problem in
[
11
]
amounts to describing part of the volume polynomial of
(K,I_{1},\dots,I_{4})
, with the segments fixed and
K
varying. As in the present paper, the authors show a discrepancy between the continuous and discrete cases.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a real
d
-dimensional vector space and
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the integer lattice.
We use
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
to denote the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\langle u,v\rangle
the standard Euclidean inner product.
For any
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, their
Minkowski sum

A+B
is the pointwise sum
A+B=\{a+b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:a\in A,b\in B\}
.

Recall that a
lattice polytope
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the convex hull of a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
denote the set of all lattice polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The set
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is invariant under the group
\AGL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
of
affine unimodular transformations
consisting of maps
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the form
F(x)=Ax+b
for some unimodular
matrix
A\in\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
and a lattice vector
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

As common in lattice geometry, we normalize the Euclidean volume of lattice polytopes by a factor of
d!
and denote it by
\Vol_{d}(P)
.
This way for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
, the normalized volume
\Vol_{d}(P)
is a non-negative integer. For example, for the standard
d
-simplex
\Delta_{d}=\conv\{0,e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
we have
\Vol_{d}(\Delta_{d})=1
.
Consequently, the
normalized mixed volume
, defined by

(2.1)

\V(P_{1},\dots,P_{d})=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}(-1)^{d+\ell}\!\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}}\!\Vol_{d}(P_{i_{1}}+\dots+P_{i_{\ell}}),

is an integer for any
P_{1},\dots,P_{d}\in\cal P(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
.

We list some fundamental properties of the mixed volumes that we will use throughout the paper:
\V(P_{1},\dots,P_{d})
is (a) non-negative; (b) monotone in each entry with respect to inclusion; (c) invariant under independent lattice translations of the
P_{i}
; (d) invariant under simultaneous affine unimodular transformations of the
P_{i}
; and (e) Minkowski-linear in each entry, i.e.

\V(P_{1},\dots,\lambda P_{i}+\mu P_{i}^{\prime},\dots,P_{d})=\lambda\V(P_{1},\dots,P_{i},\dots,P_{d})+\mu\V(P_{1},\dots,P_{i}^{\prime},\dots,P_{d})

for non-negative
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.

In the 2-dimensional case, which is the focus of this paper, (
2.1
) simplifies to

(2.2)

\V(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Vol_{2}(P+Q)-\Vol_{2}(P)-\Vol_{2}(Q)\right).

Note that
\V(P_{1},P_{2})=2\vv(P_{1},P_{2})
relates the normalized mixed area and the (usual) mixed area introduced in (
1.1
). An immediate consequence of (
2.2
) is the formula for the mixed area of segments. Given a pair of lattice segments
I=\conv\{(0,0),(a,b)\}
and
J=\conv\{(0,0),(c,d)\}
their normalized mixed area equals the absolute value of the corresponding determinant
\V(I,J)=|ad-bc|
. In particular,
\V(I,J)=0
when
I
and
J
are parallel. It is not hard to see from the monotonicity property that
\V(P,Q)=0
if and only if either one of
P,Q
is a point or
P
and
Q
are parallel segments.

We will need another formula for the normalized mixed area in terms of the support function of
P
and the boundary of
Q
, see
[
1
, Sec. 2.5]
. Recall that a vector
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is called
primitive
if its coordinates are relatively prime. Furthermore, for a lattice segment
I\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
define its
lattice length

\Vol_{1}(I)
to be
|I\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|-1
. In particular,
if
I=\conv\{(0,0),(a,b)\}
then
\Vol_{1}(I)=\gcd(a,b)
.
Lattice segments of lattice length one are called primitive. Finally, let
h_{P}(u)=\max\{\langle x,u\rangle:x\in P\}
be the support function of
P
. We have

(2.3)

\V(P,Q)=\sum_{u\in U_{Q}}h_{P}(u)\Vol_{1}(Q^{u}),

where
U_{Q}
is the set of primitive outer normals to the sides of
Q
and
Q^{u}
is the side of
Q
with the outer normal
u
. In practice, it is more convenient to express (
2.3
) using the
normalized outer normals

\Vol_{1}(Q^{u})u
:

(2.4)

\V(P,Q)=\sum_{u\in U_{Q}}h_{P}(\Vol_{1}(Q^{u})u).

Example 2.1
.

Let
P
be the unit square and
Q=\conv\left\{(0,0),(0,2),(3,3)\right\}
a triangle as in Figure
2.1
. Then
Q
has three normalized outer normals

u_{1}=\left(\begin{matrix}-2\\
\ \ 0\end{matrix}\right),\ \ u_{2}=\left(\begin{matrix}-1\\
\ \ 3\end{matrix}\right),\ \ u_{3}=\left(\begin{matrix}\ \ 3\\
-3\end{matrix}\right).

Figure 2.1.
The vertices of
P
and the normalized outer normals of
Q

We have

\V(P,Q)=h_{P}(u_{1})+h_{P}(u_{2})+h_{P}(u_{3})=0+3+3=6.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
a primitive vector. Define the
lattice width
of
P
in the direction of
u
by

\w_{u}(P)=\max\{\langle x,u\rangle:x\in P\}-\min\{\langle x,u\rangle:x\in P\}.

For example, when
u=e_{2}
the lattice width
\w_{e_{2}}(P)
is the height of
P
, that is, the difference between the largest and the smallest values of the second coordinate of points of
P
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Then

(1)

for a lattice segment
I\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
we have
\V(P,I)=\w_{u}(P)\Vol_{1}(I)
,
where
u
is a primitive vector orthogonal to
I
;

(2)

\V(P,\Delta_{2})
equals the smallest non-negative integer
\delta
such that a lattice translate of
P
is contained in
\delta\Delta_{2}
.

Proof.

Part (1) follows right away from (
2.3
) and the observation
\w_{u}(P)=h_{P}(u)+h_{P}(-u)
.
For part (2), the formula (
2.3
) provides

\V(P,Q)=h_{P}(e_{1}+e_{2})+h_{P}(-e_{1})+h_{P}(-e_{2}).

After a lattice translation we may assume
h_{P}(-e_{1})=h_{P}(-e_{2})=0
. Then
h_{P}(e_{1}+e_{2})=\max_{x\in P}(x_{1}+x_{2})
is precisely the smallest factor
\delta
such that the dilate
\delta\Delta_{2}
contains
P
.
∎

It is not hard to see that lattice polytopes
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
of normalized volume one are
\AGL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalent to the standard
d
-simplex
\Delta_{d}
. In this case,
P
is called a
unimodular
d
-simplex
. In
[
7
]
Esterov and Gusev classified all collections
P_{1},\dots,P_{d}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
whose normalized mixed volume equals one. In the following proposition we present the 2-dimensional case of their result. For the sake of completeness, we provide a short proof.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
P,Q
be lattice polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\V(P,Q)=1
. Then either one of them is a primitive segment
or both are lattice translates of the same unimodular triangle.

Proof.

Suppose neither
P
nor
Q
is a lattice segment. Then
\Vol_{2}(P)
and
\Vol_{2}(Q)
are positive integers.
By the Minkowski inequality (
1.4
),

1\leq\Vol_{2}(P)\Vol_{2}(Q)\leq\V(P,Q)^{2}=1.

This shows that
\Vol_{2}(P)=\Vol_{2}(Q)=1
and, hence,
P
and
Q
are unimodular triangles. It remains to show that
P=Q
. After a unimodular transformation, we may assume that
Q=\Delta_{2}
. Then, by part (2) of Proposition
2.2
,
\V(P,\Delta_{2})=1
implies
that
P\subseteq\Delta_{2}
(after a lattice translation) and, hence,
P=\Delta_{2}
, as
P
is 2-dimensional.

Now assume that
P
is a lattice segment. Then, by the linearity of the mixed area,
P
has to be of lattice length one, i.e. a primitive segment.
∎

3.
The case of three planar lattice polytopes

In this short section we show that, in general, there are no relations between the mixed areas of
\V(P_{1},P_{2})
,
\V(P_{1},P_{3})
, and
\V(P_{2},P_{3})
defined by
three lattice polytopes
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(v_{12},v_{13},v_{23})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}
. Then there exist lattice polytopes
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

v_{ij}=\V(P_{i},P_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq 3
. Equivalently,

\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{3,2}(\mathbb{Z})=\{v_{12}x_{1}x_{2}+v_{13}x_{1}x_{3}+v_{23}x_{2}x_{3}:v_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}.

Proof.

Assume
v_{12}
and
v_{13}
are not both zero and
let
d=\gcd(v_{12},v_{13})
. Since
v_{12}/d
and
v_{13}/d
are relatively prime,
there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
av_{13}/d-bv_{12}/d=v_{23}
. Now the lattice segments

P_{1}=\conv\{(0,0),(d,0)\}
,
P_{2}=\conv\{(0,0),(a,v_{12}/d)\}
, and
P_{3}=\conv\{(0,0),(b,v_{13}/d)\}
satisfy
the above requirement.

If
v_{12}=v_{13}=0
we put
P_{1}=\{(0,0)\}
,
P_{2}=\conv\{(0,0),(1,0)\}
, and
P_{3}=\conv\{(0,0),(0,v_{23})\}
.

∎

4.
The discrete diagram
\left(\w_{u}(P),\V(P,Q),\w_{u}(Q)\right)

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
be a lattice polytope and
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
a primitive vector. Recall that the lattice width of
P
in the direction of
u
is defined as
\w_{u}(P)=\max_{x\in P}\langle x,u\rangle-\min_{x\in P}\langle x,u\rangle
. We are interested in describing the image
\Phi:=\operatorname{Im}\varphi
of the map

\varphi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{Z}^{3},\quad(P,Q)\mapsto\left(\w_{u}(P),\V(P,Q),\w_{u}(Q)\right),

which we call a
discrete diagram
. The terminology comes from the work of Blaschke
[
5
]
and Santaló
[
15
]
who proposed the study of diagrams, i.e. images of maps that send a convex body (or a tuple of convex bodies) to a tuple of its geometric invariants, such as volume, surface area, and mean curvature.

Note that the discrete diagram
\Phi
does not depend on the choice of the primitive vector
u
. Indeed, for any primitive vectors
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
there exists a unimodular matrix
A\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
u^{\prime}=Au
. Consider the lattice polytopes
P^{\prime}=PA^{-1}
and
Q^{\prime}=QA^{-1}
, where by definition

PA^{-1}=\{xA^{-1}:x\in P\}
. Then, by the invariance of the mixed area,

\left(\w_{u}(P^{\prime}),\V(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}),\w_{u}(Q^{\prime})\right)=\left(\w_{u^{\prime}}(P),\V(P,Q),\w_{u^{\prime}}(Q)\right).

Remark 4.1
.

It is easy to see that in the case of general convex bodies
K,L
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
the “continuous” diagram

\left(\w_{u}(K),\V(K,L),\w_{u}(L)\right)
coincides with
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}\setminus\left(0\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times 0\right)
. First, note that if
\w_{u}(K)=\w_{u}(L)=0
then
K
and
L
must be line segments (possibly degenerated to a point) orthogonal to
u
which implies
\V(K,L)=0
.
Now, every triple
(w_{1},v,w_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
with
w_{1}+w_{2}>0
can be realized as
\left(\w_{u}(K),\V(K,L),\w_{u}(L)\right)
for some segments

K,L
. Indeed, without loss of generality, we may assume
u=e_{2}
and
w_{2}>0
. Then the segments

K=\conv\{(0,0),(v/w_{2},w_{1})\}
and
L=\conv\{(0,0),(0,w_{2})\}

satisfy the required.

Now consider
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Clearly, there are no restrictions on
the lattice widths
\w_{u}(P)
and
\w_{u}(Q)
besides being non-negative integers. Therefore, to understand the discrete diagram
\Phi
we fix the values of
\w_{u}(P)
and
\w_{u}(Q)
and look for possible values of
\V(P,Q)
. As above, if both widths are zero then so is the mixed area, so for the rest of the section we will assume that the widths are not both zero.
The next proposition shows that in general, given
\w_{u}(P)
and
\w_{u}(Q)
, there is a gap in the possible values of
\V(P,Q)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
let
\w_{u}(P)
and
\w_{u}(Q)
be the lattice widths of
P
and
Q
in the direction of a primitive vector
u
.
Then either
\V(P,Q)=0
or
\V(P,Q)\geq\min\left\{d,\frac{\w_{u}(P)}{d},\frac{\w_{u}(Q)}{d}\right\}
, where

d=\gcd(\w_{u}(P),\w_{u}(Q))
.

Proof.

Applying an appropriate unimodular transformation, we may assume that
u=e_{2}

and
y_{P}=\w_{u}(P)
,
y_{Q}=\w_{u}(Q)
are the heights of
P
and
Q

By the monotonicity of the mixed volume,

\V(P,Q)\geq\V(I_{P},I_{Q})=|x_{P}y_{Q}-x_{Q}y_{P}|\in d\mathbb{Z}.

Assume

. Then
x_{P}y_{Q}=x_{Q}y_{P}
, which implies that
\frac{y_{P}}{d}\mathrel{|}x_{P}
and

. Since

), either

orthogonal to

orthogonal to

\V(P,Q)\geq\V(P,I_{Q})=\w_{u_{Q}}(P)\Vol_{1}(I_{Q})\geq\gcd(x_{Q},y_{Q})\geq\frac{y_{Q}}{d}.

The statement now follows.
∎

The pair of relatively prime integers
\left\{\frac{\w_{u}(P)}{d},\frac{\w_{u}(Q)}{d}\right\}
from Proposition

generates a semigroup which turns out to be a part of the discrete diagram.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
not both zero and let

\w_{u}(Q)=w_{2}

Proof.

By the discussion above, we may assume that
u=e_{2}
. Let

denote the segment
\conv\{(0,0),(1,0)\}
. By the division algorithm, there exist

such that
m_{1}=q_{1}d+r_{1}
and
m_{2}=q_{2}d+r_{2}
. Define

and
Q_{0}=\text{conv}\{(0,0),(0,r_{1}),(\frac{w_{2}}{d},w_{2})\}
, see Figure

and

.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(w_{1},v,w_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}

,

,

Proof.

As in the previous proof, it is enough to consider

. As before, let

.
Without loss of generality, we may assume
w_{1}\leq w_{2}
. By the division algorithm, there exist

Take

. We have two cases depending on the order of

w_{1}
.

Case 1.
Suppose

. Define

We summarize our results about the discrete diagram

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
not both zero and let

(1)

(Gap) Let

(2)

(Semigroup) Let
S_{w_{1},w_{2}}=\left\{m_{1}\frac{w_{1}}{d}+m_{2}\frac{w_{2}}{d}\,:\,m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\}
. Then

S_{w_{1},w_{2}}\subseteq\Phi_{w_{1},w_{2}}.

(3)

(Saturation) Let
\beta=\min\left\{w_{1}\bmod w_{2},\ w_{2}\bmod w_{1}\right\}
.
Then
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq\beta}\subseteq\Phi_{w_{1},w_{2}}.

5.
The sets
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})

Recall from the introduction that the space of square-free parts of volume polynomials of four planar convex bodies

\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}:=\Big\{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\!2\vv(K_{i},K_{j})x_{i}x_{j}\,:\,K_{1},\dots,K_{4}\text{ convex bodies in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\Big\}

has a semialgebraic description in terms of the Plücker-type inequalities,
[
2
, Th 3.2]
. Namely,
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}
coincides with the
Plücker space

{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}:=\Big\{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\!v_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\,:\,v_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\,v_{ij}v_{kl}+v_{ik}v_{jl}\geq v_{il}v_{jk},\text{ for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}=[4]\Big\}.

We are interested in the discrete counterpart of
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}
when the convex bodies are lattice polytopes

\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z}):=\Big\{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\V(P_{i},P_{j})x_{i}x_{j}\,:\,P_{1},\dots,P_{4}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\Big\}.

Its elements are square-free quadratic forms with nonnegative integer coefficients satisfying the Plücker-type inequalities. In other words,
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
is a subset of the set

{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z}):=\Big\{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\!v_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\,:\,v_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\,v_{ij}v_{kl}+v_{ik}v_{jl}\geq v_{il}v_{jk},\text{ for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}=[4]\Big\},

which we call the
discrete Plücker set
. As we show in Section
8
the inclusion
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})\subset{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
is, in fact, proper.

It will be convenient for us to identify a square-free quadratic form
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}v_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}
with its coefficient vector
v=(v_{ij}\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq 4)
which we represent as a complete weighted graph
\mathbf{K}_{4}
on
\{1,\dots,4\}
with the weights
v_{ij}
assigned to its edges, see Figure
5.1
.

Figure 5.1.
A complete weighted graph representing a point in
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})

Furthermore, we will use graph-theoretic terminology when we speak about elements of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}
. For example, we say “adjacent coordinates” of
v\in{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}
meaning that the corresponding edges of the complete weighted graph associated with
v
are adjacent.

There is a natural action of
\Sym_{4}
on square-free quadratic forms by permuting the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{4}
. This corresponds to the
\Sym_{4}
-action on the coefficient vector
\sigma\cdot v=(v_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq 4)
. It can be easily seen that
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}
,
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}

and their discrete counterparts are invariant under this action. There is also the action of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{4}
on
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}
given by rescaling the bodies
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
by positive real numbers. Analogously, rescaling lattice polytopes
P_{1},\dots,P_{4}
by positive integers defines a map from
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
to itself. In coordinates, this map is given by

v\mapsto\lambda\cdot v,\ \ \text{ where }\ \lambda\cdot v=(\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}v_{ij}\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq 4).

for
v\in\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that
v\in{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
is
\mathbf{K}_{4}
-primitive
if at each node the three adjacent coordinates of
v
are relatively prime, i.e., for every
i\in[4]
we have
\gcd(v_{ij}:j\in[4],j\neq i)=1
.

The action of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{4}
on
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}
allows to reduce the proof of
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}={\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}
to the case when all but two adjacent coordinates of
v\in\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}
are equal to 1, see
[
2
, Sec 5]
. The following proposition gives a discrete analog of this statement.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
w\in{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that no three adjacent coordinates of
w
are zero. Then
w=\lambda\cdot v
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
and
\mathbf{K}_{4}
-primitive
v\in{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Moreover, if
v
lies in
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
then so does

.

Proof.

Let the coordinates of
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
be
\lambda_{1}=\gcd(w_{12},w_{13},w_{14})
,

\lambda_{2}=\gcd(w_{12}/\lambda_{1},w_{23},w_{24})
,

\lambda_{3}=\gcd(w_{13}/\lambda_{1},w_{23}/\lambda_{2},w_{34})
, and

\lambda_{4}=\gcd(w_{14}/\lambda_{1},w_{24}/\lambda_{2},w_{34}/\lambda_{3})
. Define
v_{ij}=w_{ij}/\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq 4
. Then
v
is an integer vector satisfying
\lambda\cdot v=w
. One can readily see that
v
is
\mathbf{K}_{4}
-primitive.
Finally, if there exist
P_{1},\dots,P_{4}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
such that

v_{ij}=\V(P_{i},P_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq 4
then

and, hence,

.
∎

6.
The boundary of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})

In this section we show that every boundary point of the discrete Plücker set
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
is realizable as a point in
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
. By definition,
we say that
v\in{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
is a
boundary point

if
v
lies in the boundary of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}
, i.e., if at least one of the Plücker-type inequality becomes equality at
v
; otherwise we say
v
is an
interior point
. In fact, we will see that every boundary point of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
can be realized using four (possibly degenerate to a point) lattice segments.

We will need the following elementary property of the gcd.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},b\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
\gcd(a_{1},a_{2},b)=1
then

\gcd\left(\frac{b}{\gcd(a_{1},b)},\frac{b}{\gcd(a_{2},b)}\right)=\frac{b}{\gcd(a_{1},b)\gcd(a_{2},b)}.

Proof.

Denote
d_{1}=\gcd(a_{1},b)
and
d_{2}=\gcd(a_{2},b)
. Since
\gcd(a_{1},a_{2},b)=1
we have
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=1
.
This implies that
(d_{1}d_{2})|b
. Now

\gcd\left(\frac{b}{d_{1}},\frac{b}{d_{2}}\right)=\gcd\left(d_{2}\frac{b}{d_{1}d_{2}},d_{1}\frac{b}{d_{1}d_{2}}\right)=\frac{b}{d_{1}d_{2}}\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\frac{b}{d_{1}d_{2}}.

∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
v=(v_{12},\dots,v_{34})
be a boundary point of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then there exist
lattice segments
P_{1},\dots,P_{4}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
such that
v_{ij}=\V(P_{i},P_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq 4
.

Proof.

Since
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
is invariant under the
\Sym_{4}
-action described in the previous section, we may assume
without loss of generality that
v
lies on the part of the boundary given by

(6.1)

v_{13}v_{24}=v_{14}v_{23}+v_{12}v_{34}.

Suppose
v
has three adjacent zero coordinates, say,
v_{12}=v_{13}=v_{14}=0
. By Proposition
3.1
there exist
P_{2},P_{3},P_{4}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
such that
v_{ij}=\V(P_{i},P_{j})
for
2\leq i<j\leq 4
. Then, letting
P_{1}=\{(0,0)\}
we see that

0=v_{1j}=\V(P_{1},P_{j})
for
j=2,3,4
, as well.

Now suppose that
v
does not have three adjacent zero coordinates. By Proposition
5.2
we may assume that
v
is
\mathbf{K}_{4}
-primitive. In particular,
\gcd(v_{12},v_{13},v_{14})=1
.

First, assume
v_{14}=0
. Then (

) becomes
v_{13}v_{24}=v_{12}v_{34}
and, since

and
v_{34}=cv_{13}
for some

.
Also, there exist

such that

.
We put

P_{1}=\conv\{0,e_{1}\},\quad P_{2}=\conv\{0,ae_{1}+v_{12}e_{2}\},\quad P_{3}=\conv\{0,be_{1}+v_{13}e_{2}\},\quad P_{4}=\conv\{0,ce_{1}\}.

One can readily see that
v_{ij}=\V(P_{i},P_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq 4
.

Now, assume
v_{14}>0
. We claim that there exist
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the polytopes

P_{1}=\conv\{0,e_{1}\},\ P_{2}=\conv\{0,ae_{1}+v_{12}e_{2}\},\ P_{3}=\conv\{0,be_{1}+v_{13}e_{2}\},\ P_{4}=\conv\{0,ce_{1}+v_{14}e_{2}\}

satisfy
v_{ij}=\V(P_{i},P_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq 4
, i.e., satisfy the linear system

\begin{cases}av_{13}-bv_{12}=v_{23}&\\
av_{14}-cv_{12}=v_{24}&\\
bv_{14}-cv_{13}=v_{34}.&\\
\end{cases}

Over
\mathbb{Q}
this system has a 1-parameter family of solutions

(6.2)

a=\frac{cv_{12}+v_{24}}{v_{14}},\quad b=\frac{cv_{13}+v_{34}}{v_{14}},\quad c\in\mathbb{Q}.

Thus, we need to show that there exists
c\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
v_{14}
divides both
cv_{12}+v_{24}
and
cv_{13}+v_{34}
.

First, we convert the fractions in (
6.2
) to lowest terms. For this we introduce

m_{i}=\frac{v_{14}}{\gcd(v_{1i},v_{14})},\quad\ u_{1i}=\frac{v_{1i}}{\gcd(v_{1i},v_{14})},\quad\text{and}\quad\ u_{i4}=\frac{v_{i4}}{\gcd(v_{1i},v_{14})},\ \text{ for}\ \ i=2,3.

The fact that
u_{i4}
are integers follows from (
6.1
) and the assumption
\gcd(v_{12},v_{13},v_{14})=1
.
Then our goal is to show the existence of
c\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying the system of congruences

\begin{cases}cu_{12}+u_{24}\equiv 0\;\bmod\;m_{2}&\\
cu_{13}+u_{34}\equiv 0\;\bmod\;m_{3}.\end{cases}

Let
u_{1i}^{-1}
denote the inverse of
u_{1i}
modulo
m_{i}
, which exists since

\gcd(u_{1i},m_{i})=1
, by construction. Then the above system can be written as

(6.3)

\begin{cases}c\equiv-u_{12}^{-1}u_{24}\;\bmod\;m_{2}&\\
c\equiv-u_{13}^{-1}u_{34}\;\bmod\;m_{3}.\end{cases}

The existence of a solution to (
6.3
) now follows from the Generalized Chinese Remainder Theorem (see, for example,
[
12
, Th 3.12]
). Indeed, let
g=\gcd(m_{2},m_{3})
. By Lemma
6.1
,

g=\frac{v_{14}}{\gcd(v_{12},v_{14})\gcd(v_{13},v_{14})}.

Thus (
6.1
) is equivalent to

u_{13}u_{24}=gv_{23}+u_{12}u_{34}
. This implies

u_{12}^{-1}u_{24}\equiv u_{13}^{-1}u_{34}\bmod g
, as required.

∎

7.
Realizable family in the interior of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})

In this section we describe an infinite family of interior points in the discrete Plücker set
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
which can be realized as points in
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
v
be an interior point of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
with two adjacent coordinates being 1. Then
v\in\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

As before, by the
\Sym_{4}
-invariance of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
we may assume that
v_{12}=v_{13}=1
. Then in the coordinates

x=v_{24}
and
y=v_{34}
the intersection
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}\cap\{v_{12}=v_{13}=1\}
is a closed half-strip given by the inequalities
|x-y|\leq v_{14}v_{23}\leq x+y
(see
Figure
7.1
for the case of
v_{14}v_{23}=3
).
Note that
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\{v_{12}=v_{13}=1\}
is the union of lattice points on two families of rays. The first family is given by

L_{k}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y=x+v_{14}v_{23}-2k,\ x\geq k\},\quad\text{for }k=0,1,\dots,v_{14}v_{23}.

The corresponding lattice points are colored blue.
The second family is

M_{k}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y=x+v_{14}v_{23}-2k+1,\ x\geq k\},\quad\text{for }k=1,\dots,v_{14}v_{23},

and its lattice points are colored green.

y
v_{14}v_{23}

x=v_{24},y=v_{34}
coordinates

Our goal is to construct
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})
which realize the points depicted in Figure
7.2
, where
(x,y)
is a lattice point in
L_{k}
or
M_{k}
.

{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})

Let the first three polygons be lattice segments

K_{1}=\conv\{0,e_{1}\},\quad K_{2}=\conv\{0,e_{2}\},\quad K_{3}=\conv\{0,v_{23}e_{1}+e_{2}\}.

Clearly,
\V(K_{1},K_{2})=\V(K_{1},K_{3})=1
, and
\V(K_{2},K_{3})=v_{23}
.
Now, to realize the lattice points in
L_{k}
we choose
K_{4}
to be the parallelogram

K_{4}=(x-k)K_{1}+\conv\{0,ke_{1}+v_{14}e_{2}\}.

Then
\V(K_{1},K_{4})=v_{14}

Again,
\V(K_{1},K_{4})=\w_{e_{2}}(K_{4})=v_{14}

\V(K_{2},K_{4})=\w_{e_{1}}(K_{4})=x

8.
Non-realizable family in the interior of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})

In this final section we present an infinite family of interior points in
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
which do not belong to
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
with
d=\gcd(a,b)>1
. Then for any integers
c>1
and
0<r<\min\left\{\frac{a}{d},\frac{b}{d},d\right\}
the interior point
v
of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
represented in Figure
8.1

does not belong to
\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Figure 8.1.
A family of non-realizable interior points of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})

Proof.

First, it is easy to verify that
v
is an interior point of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Suppose
v\in\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then there exist lattice polytopes
P_{1},\dots,P_{4}
such that

v_{ij}=\V(P_{i},P_{j})
and
v_{12}=1
,
v_{13}=a
,
v_{14}=b
,
v_{23}=ac
,
v_{24}=bc
, and
v_{34}=r
.
Since
v_{12}=1
, by Proposition
2.3
, either one of the
P_{1},P_{2}
is a primitive segment, or
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are equal to the same unimodular triangle. The latter is impossible since
P_{1}=P_{2}
would imply
v_{13}=v_{23}
, but
v_{13}<v_{23}
by construction. If
P_{1}
is a primitive segment, then
a=v_{13}=\w_{u}(P_{3})
and
b=v_{14}=\w_{u}(P_{4})
, where
u
is the primitive vector orthogonal to
P_{1}
. This contradicts part (1) of Theorem
4.5
, as by construction,
r=v_{34}
lies strictly between 0 and
\min\left\{d,\frac{\w_{u}(P_{3})}{d},\frac{\w_{u}(P_{4})}{d}\right\}
. Similarly,
P_{2}
cannot be a primitive segment.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

It follows from the above proof that the infinite family presented in Theorem
8.1
is part of a larger subset of non-realizable interior points of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Loosely speaking, as long as one coordinate of
v
, say
v_{12}
, equals 1 and the corresponding non-adjacent coordinate
v_{34}
is small relative to
d=\gcd(v_{i2},v_{i3})
and the quotients
v_{i2}/d
,
v_{i3}/d
(for
i=1,2
)
then
v\not\in\cal V^{\,\rm{sf}}_{4,2}
. However, we do not have a complete description of the set of non-realizable interior points of
{\large\text{Pl}}\,_{4,2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
