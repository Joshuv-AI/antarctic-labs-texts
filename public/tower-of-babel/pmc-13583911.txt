The Impact of Environmental, Nutritional, and Animal Welfare Labeling of Plant‐Based Cream Cheese on Consumer Perception
Authors: Cayla E J Weiler, Chase D Morhart, Brianna Power, Zach Adams, Matthew B McSweeney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant‐based alternatives (PBAs) to dairy products are increasingly positioned as nutritionally beneficial, sustainable, and ethical food choices; however, consumer acceptance of PBAs remains limited, partly due to sensory challenges and uncertainty surrounding effective product communication strategies. This study investigated the influence of environmental, nutritional, and animal welfare labeling on consumer perception of plant‐based cream cheese (PBCC). A total of 114 consumers evaluated the same commercially available PBCC under four labeling conditions: blinded (control) and with statements about nutritional benefits, environmental benefits, and animal welfare. Participants completed hedonic evaluations of appearance, flavor, texture, and overall liking; check‐all‐that‐apply (CATA) questions for sensory and emotional attributes; and a willingness‐to‐purchase question. Prior to sensory testing, participants also completed word association tasks and comment questions to assess baseline perceptions of PBCC. Sensory properties, particularly creaminess, smoothness, and spreadability characteristics, emerged as central drivers of consumer expectations and acceptance. Labeling significantly influenced consumer responses despite the product remaining unchanged. Nutritional and animal welfare claims significantly increased overall liking compared to the control (p < 0.05), while animal welfare labeling also significantly improved the texture liking and willingness to purchase scores. Environmental labeling produced comparatively weaker effects. CATA results demonstrated that nutritional and animal welfare labels were associated with more favorable sensory and emotional responses, supporting the presence of a label‐induced halo effect. These findings suggest that strategic nutritional and ethical messaging may enhance consumer acceptance of PBCCs. The results provide valuable insight for the development and marketing of plant‐based dairy alternatives.Practical ApplicationsThe findings of this study provide insight for manufacturers and marketers of plant‐based alternatives seeking to improve consumer acceptance of plant‐based cream cheese. Results demonstrate that nutritional and animal welfare labeling can positively influence consumer perception, overall liking, and willingness to purchase, even when product formulation remains unchanged. Animal welfare claims showed the strongest positive influence, indicating that ethical messaging may resonate more strongly than environmental sustainability claims for this product. However, sensory properties remain important, as smoothness, spreadability, dairy‐like characteristics, and the absence of aftertaste were identified as critical drivers of acceptance.Keywords: dairy alternatives, food labeling, sensory perception, sustainable food, willingness to pay

Introduction

Global food systems are increasingly recognized as a major driver of both environmental degradation and diet‐related disease, raising questions about the long‐term sustainability of current consumption patterns. Agriculture and food production account for approximately one‐third of global greenhouse gas emissions (Bhatti et al. 2024), while agriculture contributes approximately 8%–10% of Canada's total greenhouse gas emissions, with livestock production accounting for nearly half of agricultural emissions (Environment and Climate Change Canada 2026). Food production also occupies nearly 50% of the world's habitable land and accounts for approximately 70% of global freshwater withdrawals (Poore and Nemecek 2018). In Canada, agriculture accounted for approximately 8%–10% of national greenhouse gas emissions in recent national inventories, with livestock production (primarily enteric fermentation and manure management) contributing nearly half of agricultural emissions (Environment and Climate Change Canada 2026). At the same time, modern dietary patterns are characterized by high intakes of saturated fats and certain animal‐derived foods, particularly red meats, which have been associated with rising rates of obesity, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and other non‐communicable diseases worldwide (Marrón‐Ponce et al. 2019; Matos et al. 2021). These concerns have led to scientists, policymakers, and international health organizations asking for a shift toward more sustainable dietary patterns, ones that are lower in animal products, richer in plant‐based foods, and capable of feeding a projected global population of nearly 10 billion by 2050 (Falcon et al. 2022; Röös et al. 2017).

In response to these sustainability challenges, plant‐based alternatives (PBAs) to conventional animal‐based products have emerged as a rapidly growing segment of the global food market. The global market for PBAs in dairy products has experienced substantial growth in recent years. According to Fortune Business Insights, the market was valued at USD 28.55 billion in 2023 and is projected to reach USD 32.38 billion by 2025, reflecting the increasing consumer demand for plant‐based dairy products (Dairy Alternatives Market Size, Share, Growth Report 2032 2025). The production of dairy products has received considerable attention because of its environmental footprint, including greenhouse gas emissions, land use, and water consumption, although substantial improvements in production efficiency have reduced the environmental impact of milk production over recent decades. For example, Canadian dairy farming has improved milk yield per cow while reducing greenhouse gas emissions per kilogram of milk through advances in genetics, feed efficiency, herd management, and production practices (Dahal et al. 2026). Nevertheless, PBAs to dairy products generally exhibit lower life‐cycle greenhouse gas emissions and land requirements than conventional dairy products, contributing to increasing consumer interest in these products (Ammann et al. 2023; Kanyama et al. 2021; Poore and Nemecek 2018). Consequently, an increase in consumer interest in PBAs in dairy products, including milk, yogurt, butter, and cheese, has emerged (Ammann et al. 2023). This increase in consumption has been driven by a combination of environmental concerns, health consciousness, lactose intolerance, and ethical considerations surrounding animal welfare (Ammann et al. 2023; Schiano et al. 2020). Despite the commercial success of products such as plant‐based beverages, the development and consumer acceptance of plant‐based cheese (PBC) and plant‐based cream cheese (PBCC) remain comparatively limited. This is largely due to challenges in replicating certain desirable sensory properties of animal‐based food products (Giacalone et al. 2022; McClements and Grossmann 2022). PBCC represents a particularly suitable model system for this study, as it is one of the more acceptable categories of PBCs, while still exhibiting opportunities for improving consumer acceptance and market growth (Jaeger et al. 2024). Furthermore, the spreadable nature of cream cheese minimizes some of the structural challenges associated with producing hard or semi‐hard PBCs, allowing the present study to focus on the influence of labeling.

Consumer acceptance of PBAs in dairy products is multifaceted, as it is influenced by the interplay of sensory, psychological, socio‐demographic, and contextual factors. Sensory attributes, including taste, texture, appearance, and aroma, consistently emerge as the primary determinants of consumer acceptance and purchase intention for PBAs in dairy products, with many consumers reporting that unfamiliar or undesirable sensory profiles represent a key barrier to adoption (Appleton 2023; Short et al. 2021). Beyond sensory perception, attitudes toward health and nutrition play a significant role, as consumers frequently evaluate PBAs through the lens of perceived healthfulness, nutritional adequacy, and the presence of processed ingredients or additives (de Castro Carvalho et al. 2025; Sharma et al. 2024). Environmental concern and ethical values, including attitudes toward animal welfare and sustainability, have also been identified as important drivers of acceptance, particularly among younger consumers (e.g., 18–35) and those already predisposed to plant‐forward dietary patterns (Hartmann et al. 2022; Hartmann and Siegrist 2017; Viroli et al. 2023). Socio‐demographic variables such as age, gender, dietary identity, and prior experience with PBAs further moderate consumer responses, with habitual consumers demonstrating considerably higher acceptance than the general population (Cardello et al. 2022; Clark and Bogdan 2019). Acceptance is not solely determined by intrinsic product characteristics but is also shaped by extrinsic cues such as packaging, branding, price, and the information and labeling context in which a product is presented. These characteristics can significantly alter consumer expectations, perceptions, and willingness to purchase (Amayo et al. 2026; Passos et al. 2025).

Product labeling and on‐package claims represent extrinsic cues that can influence consumer perception, expectation, and behavioral intention toward food products, including PBAs. Health‐related claims, such as those emphasizing protein content, vitamin fortification, or overall nutritional benefits, have been shown to positively influence perceived healthfulness and willingness to purchase. However, their effectiveness can vary considerably depending on consumer health consciousness, nutritional literacy, and prior attitudes toward PBAs (Chopra et al. 2025; Isham et al. 2022). Environmental claims highlighting a product's minimal carbon footprint, lower water usage, or broader sustainability credentials have similarly been found to enhance purchase intention, particularly among environmentally conscious consumers. Research suggests that the influence of such claims is often moderated by the degree of consumer trust in green messaging and skepticism toward perceived greenwashing (Ang et al. 2024; Thøgersen 2017). Animal welfare claims, which communicate that a product is free from animal exploitation or suffering, represent an increasingly prominent labeling strategy in the PBA sector and have been found to resonate strongly with ethically motivated consumers, influencing both emotional response and purchase behavior (Clark et al. 2017; Lagerkvist and Hess 2011). Crucially, the framing and context in which such claims are presented can influence not only cognitive evaluations but also hedonic responses, with research demonstrating that labeling information can alter the sensory perception and overall liking of plant‐based foods (Amayo et al. 2026; Schuldt et al. 2012). Nevertheless, investigations of how different labeling contexts, including nutritional, environmental, and animal welfare, influence consumer sensory perception, emotional response, and willingness to purchase PBAs remain scarce, therefore highlighting the need for the present study.

This study aims to identify how consumers perceive PBCC when labeled using four different contexts: nutritional benefits (emphasizing protein and vitamins), environmental (highlighting sustainability and environmental impact), animal welfare (highlighting the product is not animal‐based), or a control (with no label). PBCC was chosen for this study, as past studies have identified it as an acceptable PBA product to consumers (Jaeger et al. 2024). Consumers will evaluate PBCCs with different labels using hedonic scales, check‐all‐that‐apply (CATA), and emotional response measures, which have been successfully used to assess consumer perception of plant‐based foods in previous studies (Cardello et al. 2022; Falkeisen et al. 2022; Lee and Ju 2024). It was hypothesized that nutritional, environmental, and animal welfare labeling would significantly influence consumer perception of PBCC, resulting in differences in hedonic scores, sensory and emotional responses, and willingness to purchase compared with an unlabeled control.

Materials and Methods

The study was approved by the Acadia University Research Ethics Board (#13–72) and followed the guidelines established in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

The study was advertised through the Centre for the Sensory Research of Food (Acadia University, Wolfville, Nova Scotia, Canada) participant database and was open to potential participants aged 18–65 who were not allergic to any ingredients in the samples and were interested in PBAs. Before arriving at the sensory trial, the participants were asked to complete a word association (WA) task (Amyoony et al. 2023) and were asked to identify the first four words that come to mind when they think about PBCC. Furthermore, they were asked an open‐ended comment question about what sensory properties they associate with PBCC. Lastly, they were asked when they would eat PBCC. In total, 119 participants were included in the study, and 114 participated in both days of sensory testing. Therefore, the 114 participants will be discussed in the study (69 women, 43 men, and two people who chose not to identify; 42.3 ± 10.3 years of age). All participants received a $10 gift card to a local establishment (restaurant or grocery store) for participating in the study.

Samples

The PBCC (ingredients = water, butter beans, oats, coconut oil, natural flavor, agave syrup, gum Arabic, sea salt, konjac root powder, calcium citrate, lactic acid, and citric acid) was purchased from local grocery stores and was stored in the refrigerator (4°C) until being evaluated by the participants. The PBCC used in the trial was included based on its consistent availability to consumers across four visits to local grocery stores over a 2‐month period. Samples (∼10 g) were served in ceramic bowls and labeled with randomized numerical codes (Jaeger et al. 2024). There was only one PBCC included in the study, but it was presented to the participants as four different samples with different conditions (will be referred to as different samples). The four conditions were no label (control); a nutritional benefits label (“Plant‐based cream cheese provides protein and contributes to daily vitamin intake, such as vitamin B12 and vitamin D. These products can support overall nutrient intake as part of a balanced diet” (Craig et al. 2022; Musiienko et al. 2026)); an environmental benefits label (“Plant‐based cream cheese generally associated with lower environmental impacts than dairy‐based counterparts, particularly in terms of greenhouse gas emissions, land use, and water use” (Kanyama et al. 2021; Poore and Nemecek 2018)); and an animal welfare label (“Plant‐based cream cheese is produced without the use of animal‐derived ingredients and are not associated with animal farming practices. This product aligns with dietary choices that aim to reduce reliance on animal‐based food systems” (Poore and Nemecek 2018)). The statements were presented as informational messages rather than commercial package claims to ensure that participants received comparable amounts of information under each labeling condition. The statements were printed on a laminated piece of paper and were placed with the sample on the serving tray. Additionally, the serving tray contained an ∼5 g breadstick (Bioitalia Grissini, Sarno, Salerno, Italy) (Jaeger et al. 2024), and participants were instructed to dip it into the PBCC and consume a regular mouthful of the product.

Sensory Procedure

The study employed a within‐subjects (repeated‐measures) design in which each participant evaluated the same PBCC under all four labeling conditions (control, nutritional benefits, environmental benefits, and animal welfare). To minimize the likelihood that participants would recognize they were evaluating the same product repeatedly, testing was conducted over two sessions separated by 1 week. During each session, participants evaluated two of the four labeling conditions. The order of presentation of the four conditions was randomized and balanced across participants so that each label appeared equally often in each presentation position and on each testing day. Upon arrival at the sensory lab, they were greeted by a researcher and were asked to complete a written informed consent form. After this, the participants were seated in the sensory booth and were presented with the samples one at a time. They evaluated the samples on a nine‐point hedonic scale (1 = Dislike Extremely, 5 = Neither Like nor Dislike, 9 = Like Extremely) for their liking of appearance, flavor, texture, and overall liking. They also answered a CATA question about the sensory properties of the sample. The CATA question included the following descriptors: dairy‐like, buttery, salty, sour, sweet, nutty, bitter, beany, earthy, coconut‐like, fermented, aftertaste, no aftertaste, smooth, creamy (mouthfeel), thick, firm, spreadable, dense, grainy, gritty, chalky, oily, white, and off‐white. Sensory properties were selected based on a literature review (Cardello et al. 2022; Falkeisen et al. 2022; Jaeger et al. 2024, 2025; McClements and Grossmann 2022; Michel et al. 2022; Oupathumpanont et al. 2025; Rune et al. 2026; Short et al. 2021; Silva et al. 2024), a preliminary evaluation conducted by research assistants (had participated in trained panels previously and had conducted sensory evaluation research for the past 2 years) to confirm the applicability of the sensory properties to the samples, and properties identified through participants’ responses to an open‐ended comment question (administered prior to the sensory trial). The participants completed a CATA question comprising the emotional responses included in the EsSense25 profile (Nestrud et al. 2016). They were then asked if they would be willing to purchase the product (1 = Extremely Unlikely and 7 = Extremely Likely). Throughout, the participants were asked to evaluate only the PBCC and not the breadstick. There was a two‐minute break in between samples, where the participants were asked to drink water to cleanse their palate. At the end of the evaluation, they completed an exit questionnaire asking about their demographics. The questionnaire was presented on Compusense software (Guelph, Ontario, Canada) using iPads.

Statistical Analysis

The WA data were analyzed according to the methodology described by Ares et al. (2008). Recurrent terms were independently identified by three researchers (all experienced in sensory evaluation studies conducted on food products), and semantically similar words were grouped into categories for each sample. The researchers subsequently convened to reach consensus on category definitions, and frequencies were then calculated based on the number of participants who used terms corresponding to each category. The frequencies were used descriptively to summarize the prevalence of each category. Responses to the open‐ended comment question were analyzed using qualitative content analysis to identify recurring themes (Ares and Deliza 2010). Comments were independently reviewed and coded by the same three researchers who analyzed the results of the WA, with semantically similar responses grouped into thematic categories through consensus to enhance analytical reliability, as recommended for qualitative sensory data analysis (Ares and Deliza 2010; Braun and Clarke, 2006). The frequency of each category was subsequently quantified to assess the prevalence of specific sensory attributes and participant perceptions across samples. For the WA task (Table 1), category frequencies were calculated as the percentage of all words provided by participants. For the open‐ended sensory property and expected consumption comment questions (Table 2), frequencies were calculated as the percentage of participants (n = 114) whose responses were coded within each category. As individual responses could be coded into more than one category, percentages in Table 2 were not expected to sum to 100%. Results from both days of sensory testing were combined, and a two‐way analysis of variance with label condition (control, nutritional benefits, environmental benefits, and animal welfare) included as the fixed effect and participant included as a random effect to account for the repeated‐measures design. When significant effects of label condition were observed, Tukey's honest significant difference test was used for pairwise comparisons among the four labeling conditions. For the CATA data, the frequency with which each attribute was selected for each sample was calculated by summing participant responses. These data were organized into contingency tables summarizing the selection frequency of each descriptor across samples. Differences in attribute selection frequencies among samples were evaluated using Cochran's Q test. Correspondence analysis (CA) was performed on the CATA data using chi‐square distances, as recommended by Vidal et al. (2015), to explore associations between samples and attributes. Penalty lift analysis was conducted to identify sensory attributes associated with changes in overall liking by calculating the difference between mean overall liking scores for observations in which a given attribute was selected and those in which it was not selected, following the approach of Meyners et al. (2013). The CATA data for the emotional responses were analyzed using the same procedures, including Cochran's Q test and CA. Exploratory linear mixed‐effects models were conducted to examine whether participant age or gender influenced hedonic ratings and willingness to purchase. Label condition, age, gender, and the interactions between label condition and age and gender were included as fixed effects, with participants included as a random effect to account for the repeated‐measures design. No significant effects of age or gender, or interactions between these demographic variables and labeling condition, were observed for the primary consumer outcomes (p > 0.05). Analyses were completed using XLStat software (Version 2026.1.0, Lumivero, Denver, Colorado, USA).

Results and Discussion

Word Association and Comment Questions

A WA task was administered to 114 participants, who were each asked to provide the first four words that came to mind when thinking about PBCCs. Responses were coded into 11 categories (Table 1), and these frequencies provide an overview of what emerged from the participants’ responses. Flavor was the most frequently cited category (18%), followed closely by texture (15%) and nutritional benefits (15%), suggesting that sensory expectations and health‐related attributes are the primary drivers of consumer perception for this product category. This result agrees with past studies that have identified sensory properties (specifically flavor and texture) as an important factor, if not the most important factor, when considering PBAs (Giacalone et al. 2022; Pointke et al. 2022). Furthermore, past studies have identified that consumers consider PBAs to have nutritional benefits (Aschemann‐Witzel and Peschel 2019; Van Loo et al. 2017), agreeing with the participants’ opinions in this study as well. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that sensory and health attributes are central to consumer evaluation of PBAs (Baş et al. 2024). Dietary considerations, including veganism, lactose intolerance, and allergen‐friendliness, accounted for 13% of responses, indicating that a notable proportion of participants associated PBCC with specific dietary preferences or health‐related motivations rather than viewing it as a conventional cream cheese alternative for general consumption. Environmental friendliness (10%) and animal welfare (9%) were also prominent themes, collectively representing nearly one‐fifth of all responses. This is noteworthy, as it suggests that a meaningful proportion of the participants connect PBCC with broader ethical and sustainability motivations, values that have been increasingly identified as purchase drivers for PBAs (Graça et al. 2019; Michel et al. 2021). Ingredient composition (5%), usage (5%), processed/additives (2%), and negative associations such as unfamiliarity were less frequently mentioned. Negative associations accounted for 4% of responses and included descriptors such as “weird” and “artificial.” Although these responses represented a relatively small proportion of the total, they may indicate potential barriers to trial or adoption among consumers who perceive PBCC as unfamiliar or inferior to conventional products. Price (3%) and availability (2%) were the least cited categories, suggesting that consumers may be less focused on the functional context of the product and more on its intrinsic attributes. Overall, these results highlight the multi‐dimensional nature of consumer perception of PBCC and underscore the importance of addressing both sensory properties and communicating nutritional and ethical value in product development and marketing strategies.

Consumers were also asked to identify the sensory properties associated with PBCC (Table 2). Texture was the most frequently cited sensory expectation (44%), followed by spreadability (38%) and a sour taste (25%). It should be noted that some descriptors, particularly creamy, may reflect more than one sensory modality and cannot be unequivocally classified as texture or flavor based on the open‐ended responses. Nevertheless, the frequent use of terms such as smooth, velvety, silky, and spreadable suggests that textural characteristics were prominent in consumers’ expectations of PBCC. These results suggest that consumers hold well‐defined textural benchmarks for PBCC and that achieving a smooth, spreadable consistency is likely a prerequisite for product acceptance. These attributes are well‐established in conventional cream cheese (Brighenti et al. 2008; Foguel et al. 2021; Jervis et al. 2014), indicating consumers expect similar sensory properties from PBCCs. The prominence of spreadability implies that consumers evaluate mouthfeel and ease of use as independent quality dimensions. Spreadability is widely recognized as a critical textural attribute in cream cheese (Foguel et al. 2021), directly influencing consumer acceptance and overall product quality. Research has identified creamy and spreadable texture as key attributes for the characterization and acceptance of cream cheese, with firmness and spreadability found to be inversely related; specifically, the greater the firmness, the lower the spreadability (Silva et al. 2024). This is further supported by consumer preference studies in which spreadability has been identified as a critical factor, with products perceived as too firm receiving lower acceptance ratings (Rodrigues et al. 2024). In the context of PBCC, a spreadable texture has been shown to play a key role in differentiating dairy products from PBAs, contributing to comparatively lower acceptance of PBCCs (Silva et al. 2024).

In addition to texture and spreadability, consumers also emphasized flavor‐related attributes. Neutral flavor (18%) and mild aroma (14%) were also commonly cited, indicating that many consumers prefer a minimal flavor profile and do not want to perceive beany, nutty, or otherwise off‐flavors that can arise from plant‐based ingredients (cashew, oat, coconut, or soy) to be acceptable (Leonard et al. 2023). Only 8% of respondents indicated that PBCC should be dairy‐like, suggesting that exact sensory replication of conventional cream cheese may not be a primary consumer expectation. Instead, participants more frequently emphasized desirable sensory characteristics such as textural attributes (e.g., creaminess, smoothness), spreadability, and the absence of undesirable flavors. This finding suggests that consumers may prioritize achieving an acceptable eating experience over creating an exact dairy analogue. Similar observations have been reported for PBC, where consumers did not necessarily expect products to taste identical to conventional cheese but instead valued products with acceptable sensory properties (Warner et al. 2025). Positive associations (6%) and negative associations (5%) were the least frequent, with negative descriptors such as “too processed” or “gross” potentially reflecting prior negative experiences with PBAs. These findings emphasize how texture‐related attributes, particularly creaminess and spreadability, are the dominant sensory properties of consumer expectations and should be prioritized in product formulation efforts.

With respect to expected consumption occasions (Table 2), breakfast was by far the most frequently cited context (50%), with consumers most commonly associating PBCC with bagels, toast, and breakfast sandwiches. This is consistent with the established role of conventional cream cheese as a breakfast staple in North American food culture and suggests that plant‐based versions are largely perceived as direct substitutes within the same usage occasion (Jaeger et al. 2024). Snacking (33%) and lunch or small meals (27%) were the next most common contexts, indicating that consumers also envision broader everyday applications beyond breakfast. Dietary‐driven consumption was cited by 25% of respondents, reinforcing the pattern observed in the WA task whereby a substantial proportion of consumers frame PBCCs within the context of vegan, lactose‐free, or allergen‐sensitive diets rather than as a mainstream choice. Social and celebratory occasions such as gatherings and parties accounted for 18% of responses, suggesting some consumer openness to serving PBCCs in shared food settings. Baking and cooking applications (10%) and healthy eating contexts (8%) were cited less frequently, though their presence suggests that a smaller but distinct consumer segment envisions using PBCCs beyond their conventional use as a spread. Taken together, the expected consumption data indicate that PBCC occupies a versatile but breakfast‐centric role in consumers’ conceptualization.

Sensory Trial

To further explore consumer perception of PBCC, the same sample was evaluated under four labeling conditions: a blinded control (no information), a nutritional benefits label, an environmental label, and an animal welfare label. Mean liking scores across all conditions ranged from like slightly to like moderately on the nine‐point hedonic scale (Table 3), suggesting that the products were generally acceptable, consistent with past research on PBCC (Jaeger et al. 2024). Appearance scores did not differ significantly across any of the four conditions (p > 0.05), ranging from 6.3 (control) to 7.0 (animal welfare), indicating that label information did not meaningfully alter consumers’ visual evaluation of the product. This result may be due to evaluations of appearance being less susceptible to information‐driven bias than other attributes. A past study with a blinded and informed evaluation of caviar did not find any differences in the liking of the appearance (da Silva et al. 2026), supporting this conclusion in our trial. Furthermore, previous research has shown that extrinsic information, such as product labels, has a greater influence on sensory attributes evaluated during consumption than on visual appearance, particularly when the product's physical appearance remains unchanged (Piqueras‐Fiszman and Spence 2015; Deliza and MacFie 1996).

Texture received the lowest mean liking score among the four hedonic attributes evaluated. This descriptive finding is consistent with the results of the WA and comment questions, in which participants frequently emphasized creaminess and spreadability as important characteristics of PBCC. Notably, a significant difference was observed across labeling conditions for the texture liking scores, with the animal welfare label yielding a higher texture liking score (6.0) compared to the control (5.2; p < 0.05). This suggests that positive ethical framing may have carried over into sensory perception, a phenomenon consistent with the “halo effect” documented in food labeling research, whereby favorable non‐sensory information positively biases the evaluation of sensory attributes (Gross et al. 2021; Napolitano et al. 2010). Flavor liking was significantly impacted by the labeling, as significantly higher scores were found for the samples labeled with nutritional benefits (7.3) and animal welfare (7.2) conditions compared to the control (6.4) and environmental (6.4) conditions (p < 0.05). Research has demonstrated that beneficial nutritional labeling can significantly alter consumer acceptability (Pinto et al. 2017), agreeing with this study. This is further supported by evidence that label information can alter the perceived intensity of sensory attributes during tasting, confirming that what consumers read about a product influences flavor perception (Schouteten et al. 2015).

Overall liking scores were significantly higher when labeled with the nutritional benefits (6.7) and animal welfare (7.1) conditions compared to the blinded control (6.0; p < 0.05), while the environmental label condition (6.4) did not differ significantly from any other group. These findings suggest that nutritional and animal welfare messaging are more effective at enhancing overall product acceptance than environmental messaging among this consumer group, which may reflect the relatively greater personal relevance of health and ethical animal treatment compared to environmental sustainability in purchase decision‐making (Hughner et al. 2007). The weaker effect of the environmental label is notable given that environmentally beneficial was cited by 10% of respondents in the WA task and may indicate a gap between stated environmental concern and its influence on affective product evaluation, a disconnect that has been documented in a past study (Hoek et al. 2017). The animal welfare condition yielded the highest willingness to purchase score (6.0), which was significantly greater than the control (5.0; p < 0.05), while the nutritional benefits (5.6) and environmental (5.2) conditions did not differ significantly from either the control or the animal welfare group. The consistently strong performance of the animal welfare label across texture and flavor liking, overall liking, and willingness to purchase suggests that ethical messaging centered on animal welfare may be a particularly compelling value proposition for consumers and warrants further investigation.

To continue to investigate the impact of labeling on consumer perception, a CATA question including sensory attributes was used. The resulting data were analyzed using CA (Figure 1), with the bi‐plot accounting for 77.31% and 16.09% of the total variance along F1 and F2, respectively, for a cumulative explained variance of 93.40%. The first dimension (F1) broadly separated the control and environmental label from the animal welfare and nutritional benefits labels. The control sample, positioned in the right‐hand quadrants, was primarily associated with negative or undesirable textural attributes, including grainy, off‐white, chalky, and aftertaste that have been associated with PBAs in dairy products in past studies (Magwere et al. 2025; McCarron et al. 2024). It was also associated with the descriptors of dairy‐like and smooth, both identified by consumers in the comment question about sensory properties, and smooth is a positive descriptor of conventional cream cheese (Foguel et al. 2021) and agrees with the results of the comment question. The co‐occurrence of the descriptors smooth and grainy may reflect different stages of the consumption experience rather than disagreement among participants. Consumers may have initially perceived the product as smooth during spreading and the first bite, while a grainy sensation may have emerged during mastication or in the later stages of oral processing, as texture perception can evolve dynamically throughout consumption rather than remaining static (Upadhyay et al. 2020). Alternatively, these descriptors may also reflect individual differences in texture perception under blind conditions in the absence of contextual label information. The environmental label condition, also positioned in the upper right quadrant, was characterized by attributes such as firm, white, salty, and beany. Beany is a commonly noted off‐flavor in PBAs (Leonard et al. 2023). This result may suggest that the environmental label did not alter consumers’ perception of less desirable sensory characteristics relative to the control.

A graphical representation of the PBCC evaluated with different labels and the sensory properties based on the correspondence analysis based on the consumers’ responses (n = 114).

The animal welfare label, positioned in the upper left quadrant, was associated with more neutral or positive descriptors including buttery, fermented, sweet, dense, coconut‐like, and nutty. Fermented, coconut‐like, and nutty are commonly used to describe plant‐based foods (Amyoony et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2024) and buttery, sweet, and dense are used to describe conventional cream cheese (Brighenti et al. 2008; Foguel et al. 2021). The nutritional benefits label, positioned in the lower left quadrant, was similarly associated with favorable attributes such as creamy mouthfeel, oily, no aftertaste, thick, bitter, and earthy. The importance of aftertaste in the acceptance of PBAs compared to dairy products has been noted in past studies (Amyoony et al. 2023; Falkeisen et al. 2022). In addition, consumers identified creamy in the comment question, while attributes such as thick have been found to be important to conventional cream cheese (Silva et al. 2024). However, bitter, earthy, and oily have been found to be negative sensory attributes in PBAs (Baxter et al. 2024; Pagliarini et al. 2021; Sharma et al. 2025). The clustering of both the animal welfare and nutritional benefits conditions in the left‐hand quadrants, away from the control and environmental conditions, is consistent with the hedonic liking results discussed previously (Table 3), whereby these two labeling conditions also yielded the highest overall liking and flavor scores. This pattern suggests that the animal welfare and nutritional benefits labels may have influenced not only hedonic ratings but also the specific sensory attributes consumers used to describe the product, further supporting the presence of a label‐induced halo effect on sensory perception (Pinto et al. 2017; Schouteten et al. 2015). These findings were supported by the post hoc McNemar analyses (Supplementary Table S1), which confirmed significantly greater selection of creamy mouthfeel and nutty under the nutritional benefits and animal welfare conditions, while undesirable attributes such as grainy were more frequently associated with the environmental condition. Taken together, the CATA bi‐plot reveals that label information meaningfully shaped the sensory characterization of an otherwise identical product, with consumers evaluating the same PBCC quite differently depending on the information provided.

Penalty‐lift analysis (Figure 2) was conducted to identify which sensory attributes had the greatest positive or negative impact on overall liking of the PBCC. The two attributes with the greatest positive impact on overall liking were no aftertaste and dairy‐like, suggesting that consumers who perceived the product as free from lingering aftertaste and reminiscent of conventional dairy cream cheese experienced a significantly increased liking. This is consistent with previous research: the presence of desirable sensory characteristics typically associated with cream cheese, such as creaminess, smoothness, and spreadability, is a stronger driver of consumer acceptance (Warner et al. 2025). Smooth, nutty, spreadable, and creamy mouthfeel also exerted positive impacts on overall liking, further reinforcing the central role of texture‐related attributes in consumer acceptance of PBCC as discussed previously. Conversely, the attributes thick, grainy, aftertaste, and earthy were associated with penalties on overall liking. The penalizing effect of grainy and thick textures is particularly noteworthy given that these attributes were associated with the control and environmental label conditions in the CATA bi‐plot and suggests that failure to achieve a smooth, spreadable texture represents a tangible barrier to consumer acceptance. The penalty associated with aftertaste is also consistent with the positive lift observed for no aftertaste, collectively underscoring that lingering aftertaste is not well‐liked.

Penalty lift analysis based on the consumers’ (n = 114) overall liking and the sensory properties for the PBCCs evaluated with different labels.

Emotional responses to the PBCC across the four labeling conditions were assessed using the EsSense25 profile and analyzed via CA (Figure 3), with F1 and F2 accounting for 65.52% and 17.89% of the total variance, respectively, yielding a cumulative explained variance of 83.41%. The emotional profiles of the four labeling conditions were clearly differentiated in the bi‐plot, suggesting that label information meaningfully shaped the emotional experience of consuming the same product. The control condition, positioned in the lower left quadrant, was located near emotions including secure, mild, tame, guilty, and calm. The nutritional benefits condition, positioned in the upper left quadrant, was located near interested, adventurous, pleasant, satisfied, and wild. This is consistent with findings from past studies indicating that unlabeled or unbranded products tend to elicit less emotionally engaged responses than their labeled counterparts (Piqueras‐Fiszman and Spence 2015; Schouteten et al. 2017). The nutritional benefits condition, positioned in the upper left quadrant, was associated with a notably more positive and engaged emotional profile, including interested, adventurous, pleasant, satisfied, and wild, suggesting that health‐related label information activated feelings of personal relevance and positive engagement with the product. The environmental label condition, situated in the upper right quadrant, was associated with enthusiastic, understanding, and nostalgic, but also with bored and disgusted, a mixed emotional profile that may reflect ambivalence among consumers toward environmental messaging and is consistent with the comparatively weaker performance of the environmental label on hedonic liking and willingness to purchase scores discussed previously. The animal welfare condition, positioned in the lower right quadrant, elicited the most overtly positive emotional responses, as it was positioned near happy, loving, free, active, good‐natured, and good, suggesting that ethical messaging around animal welfare resonated strongly with this consumer group on an emotional level. The supplementary pairwise analyses (Supplementary Table S2) further demonstrated that nutritional benefits and animal welfare labeling increased positive emotional responses, particularly satisfaction and love, while reducing the frequency of worry, reinforcing the emotional associations generated by these labels.

Limitations and Future Studies

Despite providing insight into the influence of labeling on consumer perception of PBCC, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted using a single commercially available PBCC formulation, and consumer responses may differ for products formulated with alternative ingredients or sensory properties. Second, participants were recruited from Atlantic Canada and were generally interested in PBAs, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader consumer populations with different levels of familiarity or skepticism toward PBAs. These recruitment criteria may have selected participants with greater familiarity with, or more favorable attitudes toward, plant‐based foods and potentially a greater prevalence of plant‐forward dietary patterns than would be expected in the general population. Future studies should recruit more representative consumer samples and include dietary identity and previous PBA consumption as participant characteristics. While the 2‐day evaluation design reduced the likelihood of participants identifying the repeated sample, some degree of recognition or expectation carryover cannot be entirely excluded. Although testing sessions were separated by 1 week and the presentation order was randomized and balanced across participants to minimize potential carryover, first‐order carryover effects were not formally evaluated and therefore may have influenced participants’ responses. In addition, the use of stated willingness‐to‐purchase measures may not fully reflect real‐world purchasing behavior. Future research should examine the influence of labeling across a wider range of PBAs, incorporate more geographically and demographically diverse consumer samples, and evaluate whether these effects persist under real retail conditions. Future studies could examine the sensory and emotional drivers of willingness to purchase, as attributes influencing purchase intention may differ from those driving overall liking. Also, although no significant effects of age or gender were identified, the present study was not designed or powered specifically to investigate demographic or consumer segments. Future studies with larger consumer samples could investigate heterogeneity in responses to PBCC labeling using demographic stratification or consumer segmentation approaches. Furthermore, the labels were presented as informational statements rather than commercial package claims. Consequently, consumer responses may differ under real purchasing conditions, where information is typically communicated using shorter claims integrated within broader package design. Further work should also explore how combinations of labeling claims and product formulation strategies interact to influence long‐term consumer acceptance. The exclusion of adults over 65 years of age limits the generalizability of the findings to older consumers, whose sensory perception, nutritional priorities, and responses to food labeling may differ from those of younger adults. Future research should investigate the influence of labeling on consumer acceptance of PBCC among older adults.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that labeling context significantly influences consumer perception of PBCC, even when the underlying product remains unchanged. Nutritional benefits and animal welfare claims significantly increased flavor liking and overall acceptance, while animal welfare messaging also significantly increased texture liking and willingness to purchase, suggesting that ethically framed information may exert a particularly strong influence on consumer evaluation of PBAs to dairy products. In contrast, the environmental label was not significantly different from the unlabeled control, highlighting a potential disconnect between stated environmental concern and its impact on product acceptance. The findings further reinforce the importance of sensory quality, particularly smoothness, spreadability, dairy‐like characteristics, and the absence of aftertaste, as key drivers of consumer liking. Emotional response data additionally suggest that labeling can shape not only hedonic evaluation but also the affective experience associated with consumption. Collectively, these results indicate that strategic communication of nutritional and ethical attributes, combined with continued optimization of sensory properties, may enhance consumer acceptance and market success of PBCCs and other PBAs.
