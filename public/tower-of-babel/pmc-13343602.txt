Toxic epidermal necrolysis following a furosemide injection: a case report
Authors: Clare Nakubulwa, Deogratius Galagala, Ivan Gidudu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundToxic epidermal necrolysis is a life threatening adverse cutaneous reaction mostly following drug administration. It is characterized by extensive detachment of skin and mucous membranes with a more than 30% total body surface area involvement. We document a rare occurrence of Toxic epidermal necrolysis following a furosemide injection.Case presentationA one-year-old female from Eastern Uganda was referred to our facility with history of a generalized skin eruption that started 2 days after a furosemide injection. On admission, she had extensive skin sloughing involving the face, trunk and limbs with 60% total body surface area involvement. A diagnosis of Toxic epidermal necrolysis was made, and she was started on supportive care. She showed initial improvement but later the parents left the hospital against medical advice and she died from home.ConclusionFurosemide a drug with structural similarities with Sulphonamide antibiotics, has been associated with the rare but life-threatening adverse reaction, Toxic epidermal necrolysis. This case report documents a possible case of Toxic epidermal necrolysis in a child following a furosemide injection to increase awareness amongst clinicians.

Introduction

Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) is a severe adverse cutaneous reaction that predominantly involves the skin and mucous membranes, characterized by epidermal detachment > 30% of the patient’s total body surface area (TBSA) [1]. It’s a rare and life-threatening reaction that is commonly drug induced though other causes like infections or autoimmunity have also been documented [1, 2]. Mycoplasma pneumonia and Herpes simplex infections are some of the infectious causes while antibiotic Sulphonamides, anticonvulsants, Allopurinol and oxicam NSAIDs are drugs commonly associated with TEN cases. It is characterized by generalized erythema, bulla formation, and exfoliation of the epidermis that resembles scalding.

The Incidence of TEN in children is lower than in adults, 0.4% versus 1.9% in a study in USA [1] and has better outcomes [3].The female to male ratio is 1.5:1 and average mortality rate of TEN ranges from 14.8 to 48% with higher rates in elderly patients and those with a larger surface area of epidermal detachment [1, 4]. More than 50% of patients with TEN suffer from long term sequelae of the disease; including ocular lesions, cutaneous scaring, dyspigmentation, dental complications, genitourinary problems, and pulmonary problems [4, 5].

Management generally requires a multidisciplinary team with emphasis on withdrawal of the offending drug, supportive care with respiratory support, nutrition and wound care [3, 6] as well as prevention and treatment of superimposed infection.

This case report documents a rare occurrence of TEN following a furosemide injection in a child.

Case summary

A 1-year-old female from Eastern Uganda who was referred from a peripheral unit with a 4 day’s history of peeling skin. She had been hospitalized 8 days prior with an acute febrile illness managed as malaria on artemether/lumefantrine and paracetamol with initial improvement but later developed failure to pass urine with facial swelling. She was the later given “a drug to help her pass urine” (Furosemide) and 2 days later developed worsening fevers with generalized blisters all over her skin. She was started on steroids and IV antibiotics for 2 days at the peripheral unit without improvement and was then referred to our facility for further management (Fig. 2). This was her index admission and had no known chronic illnesses, drug or food allergies.

On admission, she was sick looking, afebrile with a temperature of 36.8 degrees Celsius, mild pallor and jaundice with no peripheral edema or signs of dehydration. She had periorbital swelling with corneal clouding and sloughing of the oral mucous membranes. She also had burn-like lesions involving the face, trunk and limbs with a positive Nikolsky sign but no discharge (Fig. 1). She had a total body surface area involvement of approximately 60%. By the fourth day after the above management was started, seven days after admission the patient was clinically better, stable off oxygen, able to feed orally (nasogastric tube was removed) and the skin lesions had started to dry and heal (Fig. 1B).

Despite this improvement, two days later the patient was taken from the hospital by her caregivers against medical advice (Fig. 2). Subsequent information obtained from the family indicated that the child died at home. Discussions with other caretakers identified several factors influencing their decision to leave the hospital prematurely. These included a prevailing belief that the child’s survival was unlikely despite treatment, concerns that survival might result in permanent disabilities such as blindness, and apprehensions regarding the long-term caregiving burden. Additionally, significant financial constraints related to extended hospitalization, including costs for food and loss of income, further contributed to their decision.Fig. 2Timeline showing the chronology of events during patient’s illness

Discussion

Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) is almost exclusively attributed to drugs and to a lesser extent infections or autoimmune disease [2, 7]. The drugs commonly implicated are Sulphonamide antibiotics [3], anticonvulsant agents, Allopurinol and rarely Oxicam non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [5, 6, 8]. The aromatic amine moiety in their structure is implicated in the pathogenesis of TEN. Other reports have however, also implicated sulfonamide diuretics like furosemide as a rare cause of TEN [9, 10].These diuretics have structural similarities to Sulphonamide antibiotics with a non-aromatic amines have been documented to cause serious cutaneous adverse reactions such as TEN [11]. A systematic review done between 2004 and 2013 reported that 0.8% of the case reports showed TEN following use of furosemide [12].

For this patient who had just completed her three days course of artemether/lumefantrine and paracetamol, what raised our suspicion on the causal relationship was the onset of a blistering rash two days following the first-time administration of furosemide. The use of the Naranjo ADR probability scale gave us a score of five [5] which also indicated a probable relationship between TENS and furosemide in this patient [13]. The clinical signs, timing of the reaction, improvement following drug cessation, and prior case reports involving furosemide and related sulfonamide-loop diuretics were the basis for this. Due to limitation in resources, we were unable to do drug testing or histology to further confirm the diagnosis.

The differential diagnosis for this case included Stevens-Johnson-Syndrome (SJS), however the more than 30% epidermal detachment as opposed to 10% in SJS made it less likely. Bullous impetigo which causes skin blistering in children was another differential. However, its lesions are usually localized not widespread, with a purulent discharge (absent in this case). In addition, bullous impetigo usually has no mucosal involvement like what was seen in our patient (Fig. 1). A third differential in this case was staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (SSSS) in view of generalized skin peeling with positive Nikolsky sign and its occurrence in infants and young children. The extensive oral mucosa involvement with corneal clouding and drug history made TEN the more likely diagnosis.

Other differential diagnoses included pemphigus vulgaris in view of the positive Nikolsky sign and mucosal involvement, however, it has a chronic course rather than acute and is not typically drug induced. Acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis would be another differential because of it being a drug related skin reaction however, it presents with minimal mucosal involvement and pustular eruptions not epidermal necrolysis.

The documented causal mechanism is of the immune adaptive type due to a class IV delayed hypersensitivity response and keratocyte apoptosis is caused by cytotoxic T cells (CD8) and natural killer (NK) cells (CD56) after being activated by the drug [14]. Drug metabolites like hydroxylamines from the Sulphonamides bind to keratocytes in the skin and render them antigenic with a resultant inappropriate immune activation. Severe cytotoxic proteins and cytokines including tumor necrotic factor (TNF) alpha are mediators of the extensive keratocyte apoptosis with resultant skin loss, systemic inflammation and mucosal involvement.

Our patient had extensive skin loss with a 60% total body surface area involvement which put her in the high-risk group for mortality. There has been reported direct correlation between mortality and percentage of body surface area involved that is, a higher percentage of total body surface area involvement has a higher risk of mortality [5]. This could have contributed to the fluid loss, electrolytes derangements and possible sepsis in our patient.

Conclusion

Furosemide is a drug commonly used in patients with renal or cardiac disease. This case report documents the probable occurrence of a rare but life-threatening drug reaction following its use in the hope of creating awareness amongst clinicians. A high index of suspicion and quick withdrawal of the offending drug with institution of the appropriate supportive care could possibly improve patient outcomes.
