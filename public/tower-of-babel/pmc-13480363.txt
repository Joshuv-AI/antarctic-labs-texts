From Gut to Brain: Probiotics as an Emerging Adjunctive Strategy for Insomnia and Sleep Disturbances
Authors: Yutao Yang, Haoran Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveToo little sleep and incidental insomnia are two common conditions in which sleep is disrupted. Insomnia is often accompanied by emotional, metabolic, and immunological deterioration. The purpose of this narrative review is to better understand the gut-brain axis and evaluate the potential usefulness of probiotics in the treatment of insomnia and related sleep problems.MethodsThis study was designed as a narrative review of the literature on probiotics, gut microbiota, and sleep. A hierarchical method was used to synthesize mechanistic and clinical evidence, differentiating between upstream immune-inflammatory and HPA-axis control and downstream neuroactive metabolites and vagal signaling. Differences in probiotic strains, doses, intervention durations, and outcome measures were also investigated. As this study was designed as a narrative review rather than a systematic review, a formal risk-of-bias or quality assessment of the included randomized controlled trials was not performed.FindingsCurrent research indicates that probiotics may regulate sleep via multiple gut-brain axis routes. Upstream processes in the proposed framework are immune-inflammatory modulation and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis regulation, whereas downstream mediators are neuroactive metabolites and vagal signaling. Clinical research show that probiotics have small and diverse impacts on sleep outcomes, with improvements more frequently reported in subjective sleep assessments than objective tests.ConclusionBy altering the functioning of the brain–gut axis, probiotics may provide a emerging therapeutic option for insomnia. On the other hand, it is uncertain which probiotic strains and dosages will be most effective, as well as the relevant mechanisms; thus, additional well-controlled human intervention studies are warranted.

Introduction

Insomnia is one of the most prevalent sleep disorders, typically characterized by difficulties in sleep initiation, sleep maintenance, or non-restorative sleep.1 Its clinical impact extends beyond impaired sleep itself, as chronic insomnia is closely associated with emotional dysregulation, metabolic abnormalities, immune activation, and increased risk of cardiovascular and psychiatric disorders. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately one-third of adults experience insomnia symptoms at some point, while chronic insomnia affects about 5–10% of the general population, with considerable regional variation influenced by lifestyle, psychosocial stress, and environmental factors.2

Current insomnia treatments are largely pharmacological, physical therapy, and cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which is the first-line treatment and the most effective. Each strategy has benefits as well as limitations. While CBT-I as a non-pharmacological therapy has limitations like access, cost, and maintenance of long-term adherence, drug treatments are usually plagued with tolerance, residual effects in the daytime, or dependence.3 Furthermore, there is still much to learn about the mechanisms behind many physical or complementary therapies, and individual responses to treatment differ greatly. These drawbacks highlight the need for safer, more long-lasting alternative or supplemental approaches that can address the complex pathophysiology of insomnia.

In recent years, the gut–brain axis has emerged as an important conceptual framework linking the central nervous system with intestinal microbiota, immune signaling, and neuroendocrine regulation.4,5 Growing evidence suggests that alterations in gut microbiota composition and function are associated with sleep disturbances, indicating that insomnia may involve not only central nervous system dysfunction but also systemic regulatory imbalance mediated by the gut–brain axis.6–9 This perspective provides a emerging theoretical basis for exploring microbiota-targeted interventions as potential modulators of sleep regulation.

Although increasing data suggests a link between the gut-brain axis and sleep regulation, there are major gaps in the present literature. Existing reviews have mostly addressed connections between gut microbiota, probiotics, and sleep disorders, with no focus on the interactions between the molecular processes that are involved. Although insomnia is the primary focus of this review due to its high prevalence and clinical burden, information from research investigating broader sleep-related outcomes is also addressed to provide a more complete knowledge of sleep regulation. As a result, this review integrates current mechanistic and clinical evidence using a hierarchical framework that connects upstream immune system and neuroendocrine regulation to downstream neural signaling pathways, all while critically evaluating probiotics’ translation potential for insomnia and related sleep disturbances.

Text Retrieval Strategy

PubMed, Web of Science, and other databases were searched by computer, and the search time was set from the establishment of the database to September 2025. The English search terms included “microbiota”, “probiotics*”, “Insomnia”, “mechanism”, “sleep”, “5-HT”, “GABA”, and “short-chain fatty acid”. Inclusion criteria included (1) review articles, experimental research, and clinical investigations on the connection between gut microbiota, probiotics, and sleep or insomnia; (2) studies on gut-brain axis mechanisms related to sleep regulation; and (3) articles published in English. Exclusion criteria included: (1) studies that were not directly related to probiotics, sleep regulation, or the gut-brain axis; (2) duplicate publications; (3) conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, and letters that lacked appropriate scientific data; and (4) articles whose full text was unavailable. For clinical evidence synthesis, randomized controlled trials evaluating probiotic methods and sleep-related outcomes were selected and compared by probiotic strain, intervention duration, study population, and assessment techniques.

Bidirectional Regulation of Intestinal Flora and Sleep

Intestinal microbiota and sleep are closely related in both directions. The ecological balance of the intestinal microbiota can be affected by short sleep duration, sleep deprivation, rhythm abnormalities, and prolonged alertness, which can alter the microbiota’s structure and metabolic activity. The host’s energy metabolism, immunological response, and neurotransmitter production are all impacted by the creation of important metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids. For instance, the number of bacteria like Paraprevobacteria, Clostridium, and Bacteroides increased significantly in the shift population with an irregular circadian rhythm;10 acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid significantly decreased in healthy participants after 40 hours of sleep deprivation;11 similar trends were seen in animal experiments,12 indicating that sleep status changes have a general microecological impact.

Evidence from clinical populations has shown that alterations in gut flora can also impact sleep quality. There may be a link between changes in microbiota structure and sleep difficulties because insomniacs had higher levels of Prussiella and lower levels of Bacteroides.13 The maintenance of circadian rhythms is critical for good sleep regulation.14 Recent research suggests that gut microbe composition and metabolic function may vary during the day, however the causes and biological significance of these changes are not well understood.15,16 The expression of host circadian genes can be influenced by microbiota cycles. Research has indicated that after antibiotics eradicate the flora, mRNA6A alterations in hosts are reprogrammed,17 and m6A-related enzymes are intimately linked to circadian rhythms, offering a genetic regulatory hint to the impact of flora on sleep. The control of sleep is a function of various bacterial species. By secreting GABA, increasing serotonin production, and lowering inflammation, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are suggested to improve the quality of sleep.18 Sleep disruption may result from intestinal barrier impairment, metabolic disorders, and active inflammation linked to Bacteroides and Desulfovibrio.19 Many potential microecological effect routes are revealed by current research, but most of the information is still at the correlation level, and their causal links and important regulatory nodes need to be further confirmed.

Potential Mechanisms of Probiotics Improving Sleep

Current evidence indicates that the effects of probiotics on sleep arise from coordinated, multi-level mechanisms rather than isolated pathways. Modulation of the gut microbiota represents the initiating event, which subsequently influences upstream systemic regulators, including immune–inflammatory signaling and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity, and converges on downstream neural pathways such as neuroactive substance production and vagal signaling. Consistent with this framework, meta-analyses up to 2023 have shown that probiotics exert modest but significant improvements in subjective sleep quality, with these effects likely propagated through cascading gut–brain interactions.20 By reshaping microbial composition and enhancing intestinal ecological stability, probiotics trigger a sequence of immune, neuroendocrine, and neural responses that collectively contribute to sleep regulation.21

Immune–Inflammatory and HPA Axis Regulation as Upstream Mechanisms

Chronic low-grade inflammation is becoming more widely acknowledged as a key component of insomnia, connecting dysregulation of the central nervous system with peripheral immune activation. Immune modulation is positioned as an upstream mechanism in sleep regulation because immunoinflammatory disturbances frequently precede and intensify neuroendocrine and neurotransmitter abnormalities.

Several pathways, such as vagal afferent signaling, cytokine release from the choroid plexus and circumventricular organs, and increased blood–brain barrier permeability, allow peripheral inflammatory signals to affect the central nervous system.22–24 Because they activate microglia and change the synthesis of neurotransmitters, elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines may interfere with the initiation and maintenance of sleep. This idea is supported by reports that serum C-reactive protein levels have an inverse relationship with measures of sleep quality, such as SAS scores and wake time after sleep onset.25 A strong correlation between inflammation and sleep disruption is also supported by infection-related sleep disturbances, such as those reported during viral illnesses26,27 Probiotic supplementation has been shown to attenuate systemic inflammation by reducing high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and oxidative stress markers, while enhancing antioxidant capacity.28 Probiotics may also shift cytokine profiles toward an anti-inflammatory state, partially mitigating inflammation-related sleep impairment.29

Meanwhile, immune activation is closely coupled with HPA axis hyperactivity. Elicited inflammatory signals will cause the secretion of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which in turn will cause more cortisol to be released and the sleep pattern to be disrupted. Immune activation is very much a part of the HPA axis hyperactivity. Pro-inflammatory molecules via CRH secretion increase the duration of cortisol exposure and also cause more sleep fragmentation. Stress-induced HPA axis activation engages corticothalamic and brainstem arousal networks, including the amygdala, locus coeruleus, and prefrontal cortex, leading to reduced slow-wave sleep and REM sleep and impaired sleep efficiency.30–32 Both animal and human studies indicate that probiotics can attenuate stress responses by lowering circulating corticosterone, noradrenaline, and adrenaline levels.33–35 In addition, short-chain fatty acids produced during probiotic metabolism may suppress stress-related receptor expression and dampen HPA axis reactivity.36

Collectively, these findings suggest that immune–inflammatory modulation and HPA axis regulation represent interconnected upstream hubs through which probiotics may exert sleep-promoting effects, with relatively robust translational support from both preclinical and clinical studies.

Neuroactive Metabolites as Downstream Mediators

Probiotics can influence the production and availability of neuroactive metabolites through direct microbial synthesis and indirect modulation of host metabolic pathways; however, these effects are increasingly regarded as downstream consequences of broader microbiota-driven systemic changes rather than independent mechanisms of sleep regulation. Neurotransmitter systems such as cholinergic and catecholaminergic pathways are well known to participate in sleep–wake control, with acetylcholine involved in REM sleep initiation and maintenance and catecholamines promoting arousal.37,38 Certain gut-associated microorganisms have been shown to produce or modulate neuroactive compounds. For example, lactic acid bacteria–based fermentations are rich in γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and specific taxa such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species are capable of synthesizing GABA, while Escherichia coli and Bacillus species can produce catecholamines.39,40 In addition, probiotics may indirectly influence serotonergic signaling by altering tryptophan metabolism, promoting serotonin synthesis, and reducing flux through the kynurenine pathway.41 These microbial and metabolic effects are closely shaped by the upstream intestinal environment. By reshaping gut microbial composition, probiotics can enhance short-chain fatty acid production,42 which in turn may modulate neuroactive signaling via G protein–coupled receptors such as GPR41 and influence sympathetic or vagal pathways. However, despite accumulating experimental evidence, direct links between probiotic-induced peripheral neurotransmitter changes and objective sleep architecture in humans remain limited, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation of these pathways as downstream mediators rather than primary drivers of sleep improvement.

Vagal Signaling and Neural Integration

The vagus nerve serves as an important neural interface within the gut–brain axis, relaying gut-derived immune and neuroactive signals to central sleep–wake regulatory circuits. Clinical Trials of Probiotics Affecting Sleep

Clinical Evidence of Probiotics Improving Sleep

Comparative Patterns Across Clinical Studies

Over the past five years, 17 randomized controlled trials have evaluated the effects of probiotics on sleep outcomes, including 10 studies in healthy populations and 7 in clinical populations (see Table 1). Although this review focuses primarily on insomnia, studies exploring broader sleep disturbances, sleep quality, and circadian outcomes were included because they provide significant insights into the biological pathways that support sleep regulation and probiotics’ potential therapeutic impacts. While healthy individuals more consistently showed improvements in subjective sleep quality, clinical populations often exhibited broader benefits. For example, probiotic supplementation for 12 weeks improved sleep quality in patients with depression receiving an Allergosan® complex formulation,46 and significantly ameliorated rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder in patients with Parkinson’s disease treated with Bacillus licheniformis.49

Subjective versus Objective Sleep Outcomes

Across studies, subjective sleep assessments—most commonly the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)—were more responsive to probiotic interventions than objective sleep measures. Improvements in PSQI total scores and specific domains, such as sleep duration and sleep disturbance, were frequently reported following probiotic supplementation lasting 4 to 12 weeks.55,60 In contrast, objective indicators derived from actigraphy or polysomnography, including total sleep time and sleep efficiency, showed less consistent changes. This discrepancy suggests that probiotics may preferentially influence perceived sleep quality, affective state, or sleep-related symptoms, while their effects on sleep architecture remain less robustly indicated in humans.

Short-Term versus Medium- to Long-Term Interventions

Intervention duration emerged as an important modifier of observed outcomes. Short-term probiotic supplementation (≤4 weeks) was more often associated with improvements in subjective sleep quality and mood-related measures, such as PSQI, visual analog scale scores, and Karolinska Sleepiness Scale ratings.56,59 In contrast, medium- to long-term interventions (≥8 weeks) were more likely to indicate improvements in objective sleep parameters, including sleep duration and sleep efficiency, although such findings were reported in fewer studies and with greater heterogeneity.51,59 These observations suggest that subjective benefits may precede measurable changes in sleep physiology, potentially reflecting a gradual modulation of upstream regulatory systems.

Strain-Specific and Formulation-Related Differences

Considerable heterogeneity was observed across probiotic strains and formulations, limiting direct comparisons. Certain strains, such as Bifidobacterium breve CCFM1025, have been reported to improve sleep quality through modulation of the HPA axis,50 whereas Lactobacillus species appear more closely linked to neurotransmitter-related pathways. Multi-strain formulations and synbiotic approaches have also shown variable effects, with some studies suggesting enhanced benefits compared with single-strain supplementation, while others report no clear advantage.61 Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of strain specificity and formulation design, and caution against generalizing sleep-related effects across probiotic products.

Despite these positive results, the current research is varied and should be considered with carefully. Many clinical studies used small sample sizes and varied greatly in probiotic strains, doses, treatment periods, and outcome measures. In addition, subjective sleep assessments shown more consistent improvements than objective measurements such as actigraphy or polysomnography. Several trials found modest or non-significant effects, which made direct comparisons between probiotic products difficult. As a result, existing information could not presently support firm conclusions about the efficacy of probiotics for insomnia.

Summary and Prospect

This narrative review presents a hierarchical framework for summarizing current biological and clinical evidence relating probiotics to sleep regulation through the gut-brain axis. According to available data, immuno-inflammatory activity and HPA-axis activation are regarded as upstream regulatory centers in this framework because they have a broad impact on several downstream sleep regulation pathways. In contrast, neuroactive metabolites and vagal signaling are considered as downstream mediators, conveying microbiota-derived signals to central sleep-wake networks. As summarized in Figure 1, probiotics may slightly enhance sleep-related outcomes, especially subjective sleep quality, though the degree of benefit appears to differ based on probiotic strain, intervention length, study population, and assessment method. In summary, our data suggest probiotics’ potential as an effective supplementary treatment for insomnia management and give a framework for interpreting the growing amount of gut-brain axis research. In addition to combining existing data, the suggested hierarchical structure can help explain variability in therapeutic results. Probiotic strains that target upstream regulatory mechanisms, such as immune-inflammatory signaling or HPA-axis modulation, may have a larger and longer-lasting impact on sleep regulation than strains that work exclusively on downstream brain mediators. This possibility may explain for some of the varied findings found in current clinical trials and should be directly evaluated in future mechanistic research.

Clinical evidence from randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses indicates that probiotics can modestly improve sleep-related outcomes, particularly subjective sleep quality, although effects vary by population, strain, outcome measure, and intervention duration. Importantly, probiotics are generally well tolerated and exhibit favorable safety profiles, supporting their potential role as adjunctive or long-term strategies for sleep management rather than stand-alone therapies.

Despite these encouraging findings, substantial challenges remain. First, strain-specific effects, optimal dosing, and treatment duration require validation in adequately powered and standardized clinical trials. Second, although mechanistic pathways are well supported in preclinical models, direct human evidence linking specific gut-derived signals to objective sleep architecture remains limited. Third, future research should prioritize integrative study designs that combine microbiome profiling, mechanistic biomarkers, and objective sleep measurements to enable personalized, mechanism-guided probiotic interventions.

In conclusion, probiotics represent a promising but context-dependent approach to sleep modulation via the gut–brain axis. Continued advances in microbiome science and clinical trial methodology are likely to refine their translational potential and clarify their role in precision sleep medicine. The suggested framework could also help in the discovery of mechanism-based biomarkers and the creation of the creation of probiotic interventions that are led by specific biological pathways that are involved in insomnia and sleep problems.
