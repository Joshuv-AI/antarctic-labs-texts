Etiologies of patients with adult-onset epilepsy over the past 25 years: a retrospective study in China
Authors: Xu Zhang, Ziyu Wang, Feng Xiang, Yang Li, Xiaobing Shi, Chenjing Shao, Senyang Lang, Xiangqing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to describe the changes in the etiology of adult epilepsy in different age groups over the past 25 years, with consideration of different time periods.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted using the clinical data of patients with adult-onset epilepsy at The First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital between February 1999 and March 2024.ResultsThe study included 5,712 patients from 25 provinces and 4 municipalities in China. The study found that the prevalence of unknown etiology has decreased overall, particularly after 2015, and that the proportion of epilepsy cases caused by metabolic etiologies has declined since 2016. However, since 2021, there has been an increase in immune-related epilepsy. The proportion of patients with structural epilepsy ranged from 29.7% to 90% across all age groups. Among epilepsy patients aged ≥ 51 years, structural epilepsy was predominant, accounting for the majority of cases. In particular, the proportion of epilepsy attributable to cerebrovascular disease increased with age at onset. With regard to gender, structural epilepsy was more common in males than in females.ConclusionsThis study highlights observed trends and changes in clinical diagnosis in epilepsy etiologies, revealing an obvious decrease in unknown etiology. The recent rise in immune-related epilepsy underscores the need for increased clinical vigilance. The observed gender and age disparities in structural epilepsy, particularly cerebrovascular-related cases in elderly males, provide critical insights for targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04511-y.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder characterized by a chronic inclination to experience seizures [1]. It affects more than 70 million people worldwide [2, 3], particularly in developing countries [4]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the prevalence of epilepsy in China is approximately 10 million, with an annual incidence of 400,000 to 600,000 new cases [5, 6].

The classification of epilepsy etiology is crucial for diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis evaluation. The pathogenesis of epilepsy is complex and may be caused by various factors, including genetic, structural, metabolic, and infectious factors [7]. Compared with childhood epilepsy, adult-onset epilepsy has more diverse etiologies and is more closely associated with age [8].

The acceleration of economic growth has resulted in substantial advancements in the field of epilepsy treatment and research over the past two decades [5]. Consequently, the etiology of epilepsy may have evolved during this period. Although previous studies have explored the causes of epilepsy, there are few long-term big data analyses on incidence trends, particularly regarding etiological changes in adult-onset epilepsy [8, 9]. Additionally, while epilepsy incidence and etiology are closely associated with age, the trends and variations in different etiologies across age groups remain unclear. Lastly, whether there are significant gender differences in epilepsy incidence, as well as the interaction between etiology, age, and gender, requires further investigation.

Methods

Participants

We conducted a systematic review of clinical data from 5712 patients with adult-onset epilepsy who attended the epilepsy clinic at the Department of Neurology, The First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital, between February 1999 and March 2024. All patients were diagnosed based on the diagnostic criteria set by the ILAE in 2017. The diagnoses were jointly confirmed by two senior physicians and further analyzed and treated by at least two epilepsy specialists. In this study, the category of febrile convulsion–related epilepsy refers to patients with a clear history of febrile seizures in childhood, followed by the later onset of unprovoked seizures fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for epilepsy. It does not include acute symptomatic seizures occurring during febrile illness or prolonged febrile status epilepticus. Use of the data was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital(S2021-417-01).

Clinical data

On the basis of the age of onset, we further divided patients aged ≥ 20 years into groups with intervals of 5 or 10 years. Patients were categorized into five periods on the basis of the time of initial consultation: before 2005, 2006–2010, 2011–2015, 2016–2020, and after 2021. This study aimed to examine the current status and temporal trends in the potential etiology of epilepsy across different time periods and age groups. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 9 software. Descriptive statistical methods were used to summarize the patient demographics and baseline characteristics. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at a P value < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The database includes 11,156 patients with epilepsy. Of these, 5,712 adult-onset epilepsy patients aged 18 years and older had complete records. The cohort comprised 3,439 males (60.21%) and 2,273 females (39.79%). Patients originate from 25 provinces and 4 directly administered cities, predominantly located within the northern regions of China (Fig. 1). Epilepsy patients with an onset age of 18–30 years accounted for 53.6% of the adult epilepsy patients in this database. The largest number of epilepsy patients in our database sought consultation between 2011 and 2015. Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical diagnostic information of the patients with adult-onset epilepsy. Notably, the median durations were 36 months for female patients and 24 months for males. Table 2 summarizes the diagnostic and demographic information of patients with adult-onset epilepsy of various ages. The age of onset peaks between 21 and 25 years, after which the incidence decreases with increasing age. The proportion of cases was calculated by dividing the number of patients with a specific etiology within different age groups during a defined period by the total number of epilepsy patients in the corresponding age group during the same period (depicted in the line graph in Figs. 2 and 3). Next, we analyzed the distribution of several primary potential etiologies contributing to epilepsy among patients with different ages of onset.

Evolution of the etiologies

Patients with unknown etiology were the predominant group among those with epilepsy onset between the ages of 18 and 25 years, as well as between 36 and 40 years (Table 2). In our database, structural etiologies constituted the most common classification of epilepsy etiology (Table 1), especially for patients with onset after the age of 26 years (Table 2). The incidence of genetic epilepsy among patients aged 31–60 years has shown an increasing trend since 2016, whereas the incidence among patients aged 18–30 years has decreased (Fig. 2A). With increasing age of onset, there was a significant upward trend in the proportion of epilepsy cases attributed to structural etiologies within each age group (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 2.B). Among patients with onset ages between 18 and 50 years, structural etiologies accounted for 39.91%–61.22% of epilepsy cases across different periods, which was significantly lower than the proportion seen in patients aged 51 years and older (Supplementary Table S1, Table 2; Fig. 2B). For patients with onset after the age of 50 years, structural etiologies accounted for 64.63%–95.35% of cases. The distribution of epilepsy patients with structural and infectious etiologies across different ages and time periods roughly followed a normal distribution. Over the past 25 years, the proportion of infectious etiologies decreased with increasing age of onset (Supplementary Table 2. Figure 2C). The proportion of epilepsy cases caused by metabolic etiologies showed no significant differences across different age groups but exhibited a declining trend since 2016 (Fig. 2D, bar chart). Prior to 2021, epilepsy cases due to immune etiologies accounted for approximately 0% of all age groups. However, since 2021, there has been an increasing trend in immune-related epilepsy, particularly among patients with onset between the ages of 18 and 20 years (Fig. 2E). The prevalence of unknown etiology has decreased overall, particularly after 2015(Fig. 2F).

Gender distribution with different etiologies

Females outnumbered males in the genetic epilepsy group with onset ages between 18 and 40 years, while the patients with onset ages between 41 and 60 years, only males were present. None of the elderly patients with genetic epilepsy were included in our database (Fig. 3A). Structural epilepsy was more common in males than in females (Table 1). Patients with disease onset after the age of 70 years typically have a clear etiology. Males outnumbered females across all age groups for patients with structural etiologies (Fig. 3B). A similar trend was observed among patients with infectious etiologies aged 31 years and older (Fig. 3C). However, the gender distribution for patients with metabolic and immune etiologies varied across different age groups, showing no consistent pattern (Fig. 3D and E). Potential etiologies across different ages of onset

We analyzed several major possible causes (Table 3) and presented them as stacked bar charts (Fig. 4). The incidence of epilepsy potentially caused by febrile convulsions, brain injury, perinatal injury, intracranial infection, brain malformations, and hippocampal sclerosis decreases with increasing age of onset. The proportion of cases linked to cerebrovascular malformations remained relatively consistent across the age groups. Traumatic brain injury appears to be the leading cause of epilepsy among patients with onset between the ages of 18 and 40 years. However, the proportion of patients with epilepsy possibly resulting from cerebrovascular disease increased markedly with increasing age of onset (P < 0.0001). Additionally, the incidence of epilepsy associated with craniotomy peaks in young and middle-aged adults.

Discussion

Our findings revealed notable shifts in the etiological patterns of adult epilepsy, with a pronounced predominance of structural etiologies among patients aged ≥ 26 years, especially in patients aged ≥ 51 years. Notably, the proportion of patients with cerebrovascular epilepsy increased with increasing age of onset. This work enhances the understanding of the evolving epidemiology of epilepsy and provides valuable insights for improving the diagnosis and tailoring of clinical management strategies for adult patients with epilepsy.

This study revealed that the incidence of epilepsy with unknow etiology exhibited an overall declining trend among patients in different age groups, especially after 2015. Rapid advancements in neuroimaging, genetic testing, and metabolic analysis have significantly improved the accuracy of diagnosing the etiology of epilepsy [10–13]. In particular, the progressive increase in MRI magnet strength—from 0.5 to 1.0 T systems used in the early 2000 s to the widespread adoption of 3.0 T scanners after 2010—has markedly enhanced the detection of subtle epileptogenic lesions [14–16]. Earlier imaging modalities, such as CT or low-field MRI, lacked the sensitivity to detect subtle lesions including focal cortical dysplasia, hippocampal sclerosis, small vascular abnormalities, and neuronal migration disorders. Similarly, the wider availability of genetic testing and autoimmune antibody panels in recent years has greatly improved etiological identification. Many cases that were previously classified as epilepsy with unknow etiology can now be reclassified as having structural, genetic, or metabolic causes owing to these advanced technologies. Reduction in unknow etiology facilitates the development of more precise treatment plans and improves patient outcomes. The proportion of cryptogenic cases is expected to decrease further as imaging resolution and molecular diagnostics continue to advance.

Since 2016, genetic epilepsy has been increasingly recognized among patients aged 31–60 years, whereas its proportion has declined among those aged 18–30 years. These patterns may partly reflect improved diagnostic awareness and broader availability of genetic testing rather than a true change in disease prevalence. In recent years, with advances in genomics, an increasing number of pathogenic genes have been identified, and the widespread availability of genetic testing has significantly improved the diagnostic rate of genetic epilepsy [17]. This has not only clarified the underlying causes for many cases previously classified as epilepsy with unknow etiology, but has also accelerated the development of personalized medicine. Additionally, the observed decline in genetic epilepsy among younger patients may be closely related to changes in environmental factors, lifestyle modifications, and implementation of early intervention measures [18]. Similar age-related trends have been reported in studies conducted abroad; however, due to differences in race, cultural background, and geography, specific patterns may vary [4]. Thus, when comparing and analyzing these trends on a global scale, it is essential to account for these factors to ensure the accuracy and applicability of the research findings.

Following the classification framework suggested by the ILAE in 2017, structural causes dominate among adult patients with epilepsy, particularly in those aged 26 years and older, with the proportion increasing significantly with age. Cerebrovascular diseases, brain tumors, and brain atrophy are more prevalent among older adults, contributing to the increased incidence of structural epilepsy in this population [19–21]. Stroke and other vascular events are significant triggers for seizures in these individuals, underscoring the importance of controlling cardiovascular risk factors and preventing cerebrovascular incidents [22]. Multiple domestic and international studies, have supported the high prevalence of structural epilepsy among elderly individuals, indicating that with the aging global population, structural epilepsy is becoming an increasingly important public health issue [19, 23, 24].

The advancements in diagnostic technologies, particularly in the identification of autoimmune diseases, such as autoimmune encephalitis, have led to a notable increase in the diagnosis of immune-related epilepsy. These advancements have refined our understanding of immune-mediated epilepsy, driving progress in the development of targeted and individualized treatment approaches. In light of these findings, enhancing infection prevention strategies in older adults and improving immune surveillance in younger populations has become essential for reducing the incidence of both infectious and immune-related epilepsy. Moreover, international studies have highlighted an increase in immune-related epilepsy, likely attributable to improved diagnostic techniques and heightened recognition of immune-mediated neurological conditions [25, 26].

In the 18–40 years age group, idiopathic epilepsy is more common in females than in males; however, in structural and infectious epilepsy, male patients outnumber female patients across all age groups. Estrogen and progesterone may influence the susceptibility to epilepsy by modulating neuronal excitability and seizure thresholds [27, 28]. In women with epilepsy, anovulatory cycles with higher estradiol to progesterone ratios during the luteal phase have been linked to increased seizure activity [29, 30]. Specifically, estrogen is generally considered a proconvulsant, whereas progesterone tends to have an anticonvulsant effect [31, 32]. Nevertheless, it has been proposed that the impact of estrogen and progesterone on seizures is contingent upon a number of variables, including treatment duration, dosage, hormonal status, and the specific type and model of seizure under investigation [33]. Furthermore, interactions between hormonal contraceptives and antiseizure medications (ASMs) are highly important in the management of epilepsy in women [32, 34]. The complex interplay between hormones, ASMs and seizure activity highlights the necessity for individualized patient management and the importance of raising awareness of reproductive health issues in women with epilepsy.

Men are relatively more likely to engage in high-risk occupations, increasing their exposure to traumatic brain injuries (TBI), which is a significant risk factor for the development of epilepsy. Studies have consistently shown sex differences in both the incidence of epilepsy and the underlying mechanisms, highlighting the need for further research to understand how gender affects the development of epilepsy and related conditions [35, 36].

TBI is the primary underlying cause of epilepsy in patients aged 18–40 years, while the proportion of epilepsy caused by cerebrovascular disease increases significantly with age. Strengthening traffic safety education and workplace injury prevention programs is crucial for reducing the incidence of TBI in younger populations. Implementing measures such as enforcing helmet use, ensuring seatbelt compliance, and strengthening road safety regulations can significantly reduce the risk of traumatic brain injuries, especially in regions where traffic accidents are a leading cause of such injuries. Furthermore, controlling risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes is essential to prevent cerebrovascular diseases in middle-aged and elderly populations, as these conditions significantly contribute to the onset of epilepsy in these groups [37, 38].

The present study demonstrated the etiological classification of epilepsy exhibited marked temporal trends, accompanied by notable associations and interactions among age, gender and etiology. The prevalence of epilepsy with structural epilepsy, particularly that associated with cerebrovascular disease, increased significantly with age at onset.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it may be subject to selection and information biases. However, our institution is one of the largest tertiary hospitals in China and provides care to patients from 25 provinces and 4 directly administered municipalities, which helps to mitigate potential sampling bias to some extent. Second, this retrospective study is inherently prone to selection and information biases. Patients with incomplete MRI or EEG data were excluded, which might have led to an underestimation or overestimation of certain etiological categories. In addition, data accuracy depended on the quality of medical documentation, which may have introduced information bias. Third, etiological analysis of acute symptomatic seizures was not incorporated into the epilepsy outpatient database, limiting the scope of the study’s conclusions. Fourth, the reluctance to pursue genetic screening among adult patients with epilepsy and their families may have led to an underestimation of the genetic causes. Fifth, because socioeconomic and comorbidity data were incomplete, full multivariable adjustment was not feasible; therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, the potential underdiagnosis and undertreatment of dementia in epilepsy clinics may have resulted in the underestimation of dementia-related epilepsy in elderly individuals. Moving forward, multicenter studies and prospective research are necessary to validate our findings, improve data quality, and enhance the broader applicability of our results.

Conclusions

A temporal trend was identified in the etiological classification of epilepsy, with clear associations between age, gender and etiology. Of particular concern is the observation that, since 2021, there has been an increase in immune-related epilepsy. Our findings also revealed a significant increase in the proportion of epilepsy associated with cerebrovascular disease with advancing age of onset. This study has enhanced our understanding of the evolving epidemiology of epilepsy and has provided valuable insights for improving the diagnosis and tailoring clinical management strategies for adult patients with epilepsy.
