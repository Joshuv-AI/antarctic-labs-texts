Comparison of the modified flexible lightwand versus flexible bronchoscope for nasotracheal intubation: a protocol for a randomised controlled trial

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Nasotracheal intubation serves as a prevalent airway management approach for patients undergoing general anaesthesia in oromaxillofacial surgery. Compared with orotracheal intubation, it offers distinct advantages including unobstructed surgical field visualisation, reduced pharyngeal reflex stimulation and enhanced patient tolerance.1 2 Currently employed adjuncts for nasotracheal intubation—conventional laryngoscopes, video rigid stylets and flexible bronchoscopes—each present inherent limitations. Laryngoscope insertion often triggers significant haemodynamic fluctuations, carries risks of dental and oropharyngeal soft-tissue injury and correlates with higher postoperative sore throat incidence.3 Its use is contraindicated in patients with limited mouth opening, cervical spine injuries, restricted cervical mobility or those requiring awake intubation. Video rigid stylets, due to their rigid construction, may increase airway trauma and epistaxis risk; additionally, blood obscuring the optical lens can severely compromise visualisation.4 5 Although flexible bronchoscopy remains the gold standard for difficult airway intubation,6 it involves complex and time-consuming manoeuvres, high equipment costs, potential cross-infection risks from reprocessing7 and susceptibility to interference by oropharyngeal blood and secretions.8 Moreover, the gap between the bronchoscope and the endotracheal tube may lead to tube-tip impingement on the vocal cords or arytenoid cartilages, resulting in failed tube advancement.9 10

Some investigators have employed cervical transillumination techniques to facilitate nasotracheal intubation. For example, a lighted stylet can be inserted into the trachea via an esophageal-tracheal guiding catheter to guide nasal intubation; however, this approach necessitates preserved spontaneous breathing and prolongs intubation duration.11 Other studies have used modified flexible lightwands for nasotracheal intubation guidance but have not conducted direct comparisons with flexible bronchoscopy,12–15 thereby failing to demonstrate the lightwand’s distinct advantages of procedural simplicity and shorter intubation time. Longer intubation times increase the risk of hypoxaemia during apneic periods, particularly in patients with compromised oxygen reserve capacity.16 Currently, no comparative study exists between modified flexible lightwands and flexible bronchoscopes for nasotracheal intubation. Therefore, our study aimed to compare the clinical performance of these two airway devices. We hypothesised that the modified flexible lightwand enables faster tracheal intubation and is easier to manipulate.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a prospective, single-centre, single-blind, parallel, randomised controlled trial. The study was registered at www.chictr.org.cn (Registration No. ChiCTR2500112183) prior to patient enrolment and will be conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University in China. The study is scheduled to commence on 13 November 2025, with anticipated completion within 1 year. The study protocol is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines, and the reporting of trial results will follow the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement.

Participants and recruitment

From 13 November 2025 to 10 November 2026, clinical research coordinators will inform patients scheduled for elective oromaxillofacial surgery requiring nasotracheal intubation about this study 1 day prior to surgery. Willing participants will be considered potential subjects, and their basic information will be recorded. Clinical research coordinators will assess potential subjects to determine eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before randomisation. A model informed consent form is provided as online supplemental material 1. After enrolment, patients will be randomly allocated to either the modified flexible lightwand (MFL) group or the flexible bronchoscope (FB) group in a 1:1 ratio (figure 1).

Intervention

As shown in figure 2a, the modified flexible lightwand (Jiaren Medical Equipment Trading Co, Ltd, Henan, China) consists of three components: optical fibres, a positioning plug and a light-source handle. The flexible optical fibre design helps minimise airway trauma. A flexible bronchoscope from Insides Medical Technology Co, Ltd. (Shenzhen, China) will be used. All patients will be intubated with a size 5.5 endotracheal tube (Rrenmed Medical Technology Co, Ltd, Xiamen, China). Prior to intubation, the endotracheal tube cuff will be completely deflated, and the distal portion of the tube will be coated with lidocaine ointment.

The modified flexible lightwand and operational schematic diagram. (a) The modified flexible lightwand. (b) Insertion and fixation of the modified flexible lightwand into the endotracheal tube (size 5.5). ET, endotracheal tube; LH, light-source handle; OF, optical fibres; PP, positioning plug.

All patients will fast for 8 hours and abstain from liquids for at least 2 hours preoperatively. On entering the operating room, they will be placed in a supine position with the occiput elevated by 7 cm. Standard continuous monitoring will be applied, including electrocardiography, heart rate (HR), non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, bispectral index (BIS) and train-of-four (TOF) neuromuscular monitoring. Unless otherwise requested by the surgeon, the right nostril will be preferentially selected for intubation.17 If significant resistance is encountered, the contralateral nostril will be used instead. Before induction, 5 mL of 2% lidocaine combined with 6 mg (1 mL) of ephedrine will be sprayed into the patient’s nasal cavity and nasopharynx. After adequate preoxygenation, anaesthesia will be induced with intravenous midazolam (0.03 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.4 µg/kg), propofol (1.5 mg/kg) and cis-atracurium (0.2 mg/kg). A 20-gauge arterial catheter will be inserted into the radial artery for continuous blood pressure monitoring. When the TOF count reaches 0 and the BIS value falls below 60, nasotracheal intubation will be performed by an attending anaesthesiologist with over 5 years of clinical experience (all operators will have completed standardised training in both flexible bronchoscope and flexible lightwand intubation prior to the study).

In the FB group, the flexible bronchoscope will be inserted into the endotracheal tube, and its proximal end will be secured to the tube connector with adhesive tape to ensure the bronchoscope tip did not extend beyond the distal end of the tube. The endotracheal tube will then be advanced through the selected nostril; on passing the posterior nasal aperture (indicated by a loss of resistance), it will enter the oropharynx. An assistant will perform the jaw-lift manoeuvre.18 The bronchoscope tip will be manoeuvred to identify the epiglottis and glottis, then will be advanced into the subglottic region. Finally, the endotracheal tube will be guided over the bronchoscope into the trachea.

In the MFL group, the sterilised (via autoclaving) optical fibre will be connected to the power handle and inserted into the endotracheal tube. The positioner will be adjusted to ensure the lightwand tip remains within the endotracheal tube (approximately 0.5 cm from the distal end of the tube) (figure 2b). After activating the light source, the lightwand-tube assembly will be introduced through the selected nostril. When the endotracheal tube reaches a depth of 14 cm, 15 mL of air will be injected into the cuff.13 14 19 The tube-tip position will be adjusted by advancing, withdrawing or rotating the assembly left/right based on transillumination guidance from the anterior neck. If a unilateral glow appeared at the laryngeal level, the tube-tip position will be corrected by either moving the patient’s head toward the ipsilateral side or displacing the larynx laterally in the same direction.20 A distinct bright spot over the cricothyroid membrane or the midline of the anterior neck will indicate proper alignment or entry of the tube tip into the glottis (figure 3). At this point, the assembly will be gently advanced until resistance is felt. The cuff will then be deflated, the tube will be further inserted to a depth of 24 cm and the lightwand will be removed.

Schematic diagram of nasotracheal intubation using the modified flexible lightwand.

After intubation, the endotracheal tube will be connected to the breathing circuit. Correct tracheal placement will be confirmed by an end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO₂) reading exceeding 20 mm Hg. The intubation time will be segmented into two periods: period 1 (from insertion of the tube into the nostril to its passage through the posterior nasal aperture into the oropharynx) and period 2 (from the start of fibreoptic- or lightwand-guided manoeuvres to connection of the tube to the breathing circuit). The total intubation time will be defined as the sum of period 1 and period 2. A single intubation attempt will be considered failed if the duration exceeds 180 seconds, the airway device (lightwand, bronchoscope or tube) is withdrawn from the nostril or oesophageal intubation occurs. If oxygen saturation falls below 95%, the endotracheal tube will be withdrawn and mask ventilation will be resumed until SpO₂ reaches 100%, after which another attempt will be made. Intubation will be deemed a failure after three unsuccessful attempts, at which point video-laryngoscope-assisted nasotracheal intubation will be performed. The attending anaesthesiologist will perform nasotracheal intubation, with assistance from an anaesthesia assistant. The intraoperative recorder will document intubation time, haemodynamic parameters from the monitor and other relevant intubation data exclusively at the direction of the anaesthesia assistant. The entire intubation procedure will be video-recorded. Postoperatively, an independent follow-up assessor will conduct patient follow-up and will record intubation-related complications.

Anaesthesia maintenance will be achieved using propofol, remifentanil and sevoflurane, with the BIS targeted within a range of 40–60. Sufentanil may be administered as needed, and cis-atracurium (0.05 mg/kg) will be supplemented when the TOF count recovers to 1. At the conclusion of surgery, the patient will be transferred to the post-anaesthesia care unit, where the endotracheal tube will be removed after return of spontaneous breathing and emergence from anaesthesia.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was based on the primary outcome of intubation time. With an assumed SD of 60 s and aiming to detect a between-group difference of at least 50 s at a two-sided significance level of 0.05 with 90% statistical power, a minimum of 64 participants was required. To account for potential attrition (estimated at 10%), the planned sample size was set at 70 patients.

Randomisation and blinding

Randomisation numbers will be generated by an independent statistician not involved in participant recruitment or data collection, using Python programming language (Python Software Foundation V.3.9) in a 1:1 allocation ratio with a block size of 10. For the total sample of 70 patients, seven blocks will be created, with five patients allocated to group MFL and five to group FB within each block. The resulting random sequence will be sealed in sequentially numbered envelopes (01–70). On the day of surgery, the corresponding envelope will be opened in sequential order by a study coordinator who will not be involved in anaesthesia or intubation.

This study is a single-blind trial, in which patients and postoperative follow-up assessors are blinded to group allocation. Interventions are administered after anaesthesia induction to ensure successful patient blinding. The attending anaesthesiologist performs nasotracheal intubation, with assistance from an anaesthesia assistant. The intraoperative recorder documents intubation time and other relevant intubation data exclusively at the instruction of the anaesthesia assistant and remains unaware of the intubation technique used. The entire intubation procedure is video-recorded. Postoperatively, an independent follow-up assessor conducts patient follow-up and records intubation-related complications. Although the attending anaesthetist and anaesthesia assistant performing intubation cannot be blinded, intraoperative data collection and postoperative follow-up are conducted outside the view of the operators. Furthermore, the study operators and follow-up personnel are mutually unaware of each other’s collected data, with distinct and independent roles.

Allocation concealment will be strengthened by using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared and signed across the seal by the independent researcher who generates the random sequence. The envelopes will be stored by a study coordinator who is not involved in recruitment, intubation, outcome assessment or data entry. Any damaged envelope, out-of-sequence opening or allocation error will be recorded as a protocol deviation and reported in the trial file before data analysis.

To minimise performance and detection bias, all intubations will be performed by anaesthesiologists who have completed protocol-specific training and have experience with both devices before trial initiation. Postoperative assessors who evaluate sore throat, hoarseness and nasal pain using VAS scores will be fully blinded to group allocation, intubation device and intraoperative events. All postoperative assessments will be performed using a standardised script to reduce inter-assessor variability.

All intubation procedures will be video-recorded for source verification of procedural outcomes. Each recording will be labelled only with the participant study identification code and reviewed independently by two assessors who are not involved in recruitment, intubation, intraoperative recording or postoperative follow-up. The assessors will verify period 1, period 2, total intubation time, number of attempts, oesophageal intubation and ease of glottic passage according to the prespecified definitions in the Outcomes section. Any disagreements between the two reviewers will be resolved by consensus or by adjudication from a third senior independent assessor blinded to group assignment.

Data collection and management

Research data will be documented using designated case report forms. Prior to anaesthesia induction, baseline data for each patient will be collected by the research personnel. All outcome measures will be recorded during nasotracheal intubation and in the postoperative period. Each enrolled participant will be assigned a unique patient identification code. On completion of data collection, all study data will be entered into a dedicated Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.

Data entry will be performed using the participant identification code only. A second research coordinator will verify key outcome fields against the original case report forms and video-review records. Corrections after initial entry will be dated, justified and retained in an audit trail; the final anonymised dataset will be password-protected and accessible only to authorised members of the research team.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables will be assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD. Non-normally distributed variables are reported as median and IQR (25th–75th percentiles). Categorical variables are summarised as frequencies and percentages (%). Continuous variables will be compared using the independent-samples t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables will be analysed with the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS Statistics V.26.0. The analyses will be conducted by a statistical professional who is independent of the data collection process. All analyses will use a two-sided significance level of 0.05. The primary analysis set will follow the intention-to-treat principle and will include all randomised participants according to their assigned group. A per-protocol sensitivity analysis will additionally exclude participants with major protocol deviations, such as use of the wrong intubation device, withdrawal before intubation or absence of evaluable intubation outcome data. Effect estimates will be reported with 95% CIs: mean difference or median difference for continuous outcomes and risk difference or relative risk for categorical outcomes. Baseline characteristics will be summarised descriptively without formal between-group hypothesis testing, and no interim efficacy analysis is planned.

Missing data will be minimised through real-time case report form checks, cross-verification with intraoperative video records and postoperative follow-up within the predefined 24-hour window. Participants who require rescue airway management will remain in the intention-to-treat analysis, and the event will be recorded as a protocol-relevant outcome rather than being excluded.

Data monitoring

Prior to patient enrolment, all study physicians and clinical research assistants will receive dedicated training on the study protocol and completion of case report forms. Given the limited sample size in each group, an independent data monitoring committee will not be established for this study. The data management process will be as follows: All raw data will be collected through case report forms under strict confidentiality. Completed forms will undergo verification by a qualified research coordinator designated by the principal investigator to ensure data accuracy.

Safety assessments

All adverse events potentially related to airway manipulation will be actively assessed from anaesthesia induction until 24 hours postoperatively. Prespecified adverse events include epistaxis, visible blood staining of the airway device or tube, nasal or oropharyngeal mucosal injury, dental or lip injury, sore throat, hoarseness, nasal pain, laryngospasm, bronchospasm, oxygen desaturation (SpO2<90%), oesophageal intubation, aspiration, haemodynamic instability requiring treatment, vocal cord or arytenoid injury and any unplanned escalation of airway management. Each event will be recorded with onset time, severity, relationship to the study procedure, treatment provided, outcome and whether it meets serious adverse event (SAE) criteria.

If a participant experiences an SAE suspected to be related to the nasotracheal intubation procedure, the treating physician has the authority to suspend that participant’s involvement in the study. For this study, an SAE is defined as any event assessed by the investigator or sponsor that results in any of the following outcomes: death, life-threatening condition, requirement for hospitalisation or prolongation of existing hospitalisation, persistent or significant disability/incapacity or substantial disruption of normal life functions.

For severe adverse events directly caused by airway manipulation—such as cardiac arrest, acute circulatory failure, death or injury to the vocal cords or arytenoid cartilages—reporting to the Ethics Committee is mandatory. Although no specific reporting pathway for unexpected SAEs has been established in this study, all SAEs must be documented and reported in accordance with standard protocols. In the event of any adverse event, appropriate measures, including medical intervention, shall be taken immediately as necessary. Unexpected SAEs and any SAE considered possibly, probably or definitely related to study intubation will be reported to the principal investigator immediately and to the Ethics Committee within 24 hours of the research team becoming aware of the event. Non-SAEs will be summarised by group in the final trial report, and all SAEs will be described individually regardless of group assignment.

Discussion

Flexible bronchoscope-guided nasotracheal intubation is a technique that uses a flexible bronchoscope as a guide to advance an endotracheal tube through the nasal cavity into the trachea. It serves as an effective approach for managing both anticipated and unanticipated difficult airways, particularly in patients with limited mouth opening, restricted neck mobility or abnormal pharyngeal anatomy. While the flexible bronchoscope offers the advantages of direct visualisation and precision, clinical experience indicates that difficulties may still arise even with substantial operator experience and training. This technique demands not only exceptional hand-eye coordination for rapid execution but also requires systematic training and ongoing practice to maintain proficiency.9 22–24

The use of the modified flexible lightwand for intubation guidance is not affected by blood or secretions in the oropharynx. Furthermore, unlike flexible bronchoscope-guided intubation, it does not require pre-placement of the wand into the trachea, thereby eliminating the risk of tube advancement being hindered by the gap between the tube and the wand—a common cause of obstruction at the vocal cords or arytenoid cartilages. Additionally, the disposable nature of the flexible optical fibre effectively prevents cross-infection.

The selection of female participants is based on documented anatomical differences in airway structures between males and females, including variations in laryngeal prominence height, glottic angle and laryngopharyngeal cavity dimensions, all of which may influence intubation difficulty. The 5.5-mm endotracheal tube is selected because clinical experience consistently demonstrates that this size facilitates easier nasal passage and is associated with a lower incidence of epistaxis.

This study carries several methodological limitations. First, the single-centre design of this study may restrict the external validity of the research findings. Second, solely female patients are enrolled in this study; thus, the generalisability of the results to the male patient population remains limited. Thirdly, patients with anticipated difficult airways are excluded from the study cohort, which renders the applicability of the conclusions to this clinically critical patient subgroup uncertain. Fourthly, due to the inherent nature of the interventional procedures, blinding of the attending anaesthesiologist performing intubation and the anaesthesia assistant is unfeasible, which may potentially introduce detection bias, especially for subjective outcome indicators including the ease of glottic passage and intubation difficulty score. To minimise this risk of bias, the intraoperative recorder documents intubation time and other relevant data solely at the instruction of the anaesthesia assistant and remains unaware of the intubation technique employed. All intubation procedures are video-recorded and postoperatively verified and reviewed by independent investigators. Finally, given that this is a protocol-based study with a relatively small sample size, the clinical trial may be underpowered to detect rare complications and perform valid exploratory subgroup analyses.

By comparing multiple clinical indicators including intubation time between modified flexible lightwand-guided and flexible bronchoscope-guided nasotracheal intubation, this study seeks to identify the optimal intubation approach and evaluate whether the modified lightwand can serve as a viable alternative to the bronchoscope. By refining intubation techniques to reduce procedural duration, this research aims to provide valuable insights for nasotracheal intubation methods under general anaesthesia. We believe that modified lightwand-guided nasotracheal intubation will play an increasingly significant role in future clinical practice.
