SPINDER: an open-source 18-DoF hexapod robot with hierarchical central pattern generator control and analytic inverse kinematics
Authors: Abderraouf Maoudj, Azhar Aulia Saputra, Brahim Brahmi, Mourad Nouioua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents SPINDER, a compact 18-DoF hexapod robot designed for reproducible bio-inspired locomotion research. The platform integrates a time-of-flight depth camera, six-axis inertial measurement unit, and per-foot contact switches, with computation split between an ARM-based single-board computer and a real-time microcontroller driving Dynamixel actuators. We derive a closed-form inverse kinematics solver for the yaw-pitch-pitch leg configuration that enforces workspace feasibility and handles kinematic singularities, achieving real time computations per leg. The locomotion controller combines a coupled-oscillator central pattern generator for inter-leg coordination with local motion controllers that regulate torso motion and generate cycloid-based swing trajectories with stance compensation, before mapping foot targets to joint commands via analytic IK. Hardware experiments demonstrate stable tripod, ripple, and wave locomotion, including omnidirectional commands, smooth online gait transitions that settle within approximately two gait cycles, and successful traversal of structured uneven terrain (height variations up to 18 mm) with 100% completion rate and mean body-pitch deviation below 5. All mechanical designs, kinematic models, and control software will be released to support replication and extension.Keywords: Hexapod robot, Central pattern generator, Inverse kinematics, Legged locomotion, Bio-inspired robotics, Sim-to-real transferSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Hexapod robots maintain tripod support configurations that provide inherent static stability on irregular terrain and reduces the need for high-bandwidth whole-body balancing compared with bipeds and quadrupeds. This advantage makes hexapods attractive for search-and-rescue1, field inspection, and planetary exploration2, where loss of stability can lead to irreversible failure. Insects exploit this morphology through surprisingly simple neural circuits: decentralized central pattern generators (CPGs) produce inter-leg coordination without centralized planning, and local reflexes adapt the gait to terrain on a step-by-step basis3.

Despite this biological precedent, most robotic hexapods still rely on explicitly scheduled coordination. Common implementations prescribe swing and stance sequences using gait tables or finite-state machines and execute them through inverse kinematics4. While model predictive control (MPC) and trajectory optimization offer flexibility, as demonstrated on platforms such as ANYmal5 and wheeled-legged hybrids6,7, they typically bind coordination to specific operating points and require explicit replanning for gait transitions. As a result, transitions between canonical hexapod patterns (e.g., wave, ripple, alternating tripod) are often treated as discrete mode switches rather than as a continuous reorganization of inter-leg phase relationships3.

CPG-based controllers can overcome this limitation. Coupled oscillator networks generate gait patterns that shift smoothly with frequency or coupling strength, reproducing the speed-dependent transitions observed in insects8. Owaki and Ishiguro showed that purely local foot-contact feedback (the Tegotae principle) produces adaptive hexapod coordination without any inter-leg coupling9, and Bellegarda and Ijspeert combined deep reinforcement learning with CPG parameter modulation for robust quadruped sim-to-real transfer10. More recently, hierarchical perception-action decompositions have been shown to improve locomotion robustness in unstructured environments by separating rhythmic coordination, local body stabilization, and perception-driven adaptation into modular control layers11.

Nevertheless, most CPG implementations for hexapods either use predefined coupling matrices that lock the gait pattern12 or operate at the joint-torque level without a geometric layer for precise foot placement. In contrast, our controller explicitly separates rhythmic gait generation (CPG layer) from Cartesian trajectory generation and analytic inverse kinematics. This hierarchical decomposition simplifies gait transitions while preserving consistent geometric execution.

Furthermore, accessible hardware platforms combining onboard depth sensing, inertial sensing, and real-time CPG-based control remain unavailable to most research laboratories. Several existing hexapod platforms rely on custom actuators and specialized manufacturing, which limits reproducibility and large-scale experimental use13. In contrast, open and affordable quadrupeds such as Stanford Pupper14, ODRI15, and Wheely16 have accelerated reproducible research, yet comparable sub-kilogram hexapods with onboard depth sensing and real-time locomotion control remain rare. MiniRHex17 demonstrates an inexpensive hexapod design, but does not integrate vision and primarily uses pre-programmed gaits.

This paper presents SPINDER, a sub-kilogram hexapod designed to be reproduced in a typical university lab using 3D printing and off-the-shelf components. Beyond describing the platform, We also report the practical decisions, trade-offs, and lessons learned during its development, information that is often omitted from research publications but is valuable to practitioners building similar systems.

Related work

IK-based and optimization-based hexapod control

The standard recipe for hexapod locomotion pairs an analytical inverse kinematics solver with a finite-state gait machine that dictates which legs swing and when. Silva and Machado survey the long history of this approach, classifying gaits by duty factor and stability margin18. For 3-DOF legs the IK reduces to a planar two-link cosine problem and runs in O(1) per leg, which is attractive for embedded controllers but says nothing about when each leg should step. That timing is usually hard-coded per gait, so switching between, say, a wave and a tripod pattern requires a separate table and an explicit transition rule.

MPC and trajectory optimization remove the need for fixed tables by solving online for whole-body motions under kinematic and friction-cone constraints. Hutter et al. demonstrated this on ANYmal5; Bjelonic et al. and de Viragh et al. extended the idea to wheeled-legged hybrids with ZMP and terrain-contact constraints6,7. These methods produce dynamically feasible motions, but their solve times make high-frequency control difficult, and gait transitions still require explicit replanning. Our controller avoids both issues: the CPG generates gait timing directly from its dynamics, and the IK layer runs in closed form at every control tick.

CPG-based locomotion

Central Pattern Generators (CPGs) provide low-dimensional rhythmic structure mimicking insect neural circuits. Ijspeert’s review3 established CPGs as a practical alternative to centralized planning: a network of coupled oscillators produces coordinated rhythmic signals whose phase relationships encode a gait. The Matsuoka half-center model19 remains the most common building block, and recent theoretical work by Namura and Nakao reduces six coupled oscillators to two phase-difference equations via phase reduction theory, yielding analytic conditions for wave-to-tripod transitions20.

On the experimental side, two lines of work are particularly relevant. Owaki and Ishiguro showed that hexapod coordination can emerge from purely local foot-contact feedback, the Tegotae principle, without any inter-leg coupling at all9. Bellegarda and Ijspeert took a different route, learning CPG parameters via PPO and demonstrating robust sim-to-real transfer on a quadruped10. Fujiki et al. observed that biological gait transitions exhibit hysteresis: the speed at which an animal switches from wave to tripod differs from the reverse direction8, a feature that most robotic CPGs do not reproduce.

A gap remains in combining these ideas. Existing hexapod CPG controllers either fix the coupling matrix at design time, locking the gait pattern12, or generate joint-level torques without a geometric trajectory layer for precise foot placement. Our proposed controller addresses this by separating rhythmic gait generation (CPG, Level 1) from Cartesian trajectory execution (cycloid + IK, Level 2), so that changing the oscillator coupling produces a new gait without touching the trajectory logic.

Accessible legged platforms

The cost of legged-robot hardware has historically limited who can do locomotion research. HECTOR, for example, uses custom actuators and costs well above USD 50 00013. Several recent projects have lowered the barrier for quadrupeds, Stanford Pupper14, ODRI15, Wheely16, all under USD 1 000 using off-the-shelf servos. On the hexapod side, MiniRHex17 is cheap and compact, but it has no onboard vision and ships with fixed open-loop gaits. To our knowledge, no sub-kilogram hexapod combines a depth camera, onboard 6-axis IMU, contact sensing, and a real-time CPG–IK controller at a price point reachable by a typical university lab. SPINDER is designed to fill exactly that niche.

Although several prior works address individual aspects of legged locomotion, important gaps remain in their integration within accessible hexapod platforms. Existing open-source systems typically focus either on low-cost mechanical reproducibility or on locomotion control, but rarely combine onboard sensing, closed-form Cartesian inverse kinematics, and dynamically coordinated CPG-based locomotion within a compact research platform.

In particular, many existing hexapod controllers rely on predefined gait tables or fixed coupling structures, which tightly couple gait generation and trajectory execution. In contrast, the proposed architecture explicitly separates rhythmic coordination from geometric motion generation through a hierarchical CPG–IK decomposition. This separation enables smooth gait transitions through coupling modulation while preserving consistent Cartesian foot placement.

Furthermore, unlike larger research platforms that depend on custom actuators and specialized hardware, SPINDER integrates onboard depth sensing, inertial feedback, contact sensing, analytic inverse kinematics, and real-time locomotion control using commercially available components within a sub-kilogram open-source hexapod platform intended for reproducible robotics research.

Robot design and control architecture

Morphology and actuator layout

The hexapod follows an insect-inspired body plan with six legs arranged in three bilateral pairs: front-right/left (FR/FL), mid-right/left (MR/ML), and rear-right/left (RR/RL). Each leg comprises three revolute joints: a hip yaw joint (, rotating about the local z-axis), a femur pitch joint (, about the local x-axis), and a tibia pitch joint (, about the local x-axis), yielding 18 degrees of freedom for locomotion. All joints are actuated by Dynamixel XC330-M288-T servos (0.93 N m stall torque, 80 rpm no-load speed at 5 V).

The structural links (coxa bracket, femur strut, and tibia strut) are fabricated from PLA via fused-deposition modeling (FDM) at 0.2 mm layer height with 40% infill, balancing stiffness against mass. The design permits rapid replacement of individual leg modules without disassembling the body frame. Table 1 lists the kinematic parameters extracted from the URDF model.

Compute and sensor architecture

The control architecture employs a master–slave topology (Fig. 1). A Khadas VIM4 single-board computer (quad-core ARM Cortex-A73 at 2.2 GHz, 8 GB RAM, Ubuntu 22.04) serves as the master, executing the CPG network, perception processing, and high-level control. An OPENRB-150 board (Microchip SAMD21, ARM Cortex-M0+ at 48 MHz) serves as the real-time slave, managing communication with the Dynamixel actuators over a half-duplex TTL bus at 1 Mbps and receiving target joint positions from the master via UART at 200 Hz. This separation ensures deterministic servo timing while supporting computationally intensive perception tasks

Sensory subsystem

Sensory feedback is organized into four categories.

Proprioception: Binary foot-contact switches , , detect ground contact at each foot tip. Per-leg condition flags indicate whether a leg is currently operational.

Body state: A torso-mounted 6-axis IMU (3-axis gyroscope + 3-axis accelerometer) is sampled at 200 Hz. Roll and pitch are estimated by fusing gyroscope integration with an accelerometer gravity reference (complementary filtering), yielding and for feedback stabilization.

Task commands: Three supraspinal signals , , encode the desired body velocity along the sagittal, coronal, and yaw axes, set via remote control (SSH) or an autonomous planner.

Exteroception: The Pico Flexx ToF camera provides depth observations intended for future terrain-aware locomotion and autonomous navigation experiments. In the current work, the depth stream is logged and synchronized with the locomotion pipeline but is not yet integrated into the online foothold-planning or gait-adaptation controller. The present study focuses on validating the hierarchical CPG–IK locomotion architecture and the reproducible robotic platform itself.

Hierarchical control architecture

The locomotion controller is organized as a two-level hierarchical architecture (Fig.??). Level 1 generates rhythmic inter-leg coordination using a coupled-oscillator CPG network, while Level 2 converts the resulting phase signals into Cartesian foot trajectories and joint commands.

More specifically, the CPG layer produces continuous phase trajectories and swing trigger events that define the temporal structure of locomotion. These signals are passed to the local motion controller, which computes swing trajectories, stance compensation, and torso-motion regulation in Cartesian space. The resulting foot targets are then mapped to joint-space commands through the closed-form inverse kinematics solver.

This hierarchical decomposition separates rhythmic coordination from geometric execution, improving modularity and simplifying gait transitions. The organization is conceptually consistent with recent hierarchical perception-action frameworks proposed for adaptive legged locomotion in unstructured environments11 (Fig. 4).

Sim-to-real transfer

The controller optimized in MuJoCo is deployed to the physical prototype without fine-tuning. To assess robustness beyond flat terrain, the robot traverses a 2 m course of structured uneven ground (Fig. 14), consisting of foam tiles with height variations up to 18 mm. The robot completes the course with a 100% success rate while maintaining a mean body-pitch deviation below . These results indicate that the swing clearance and stance timing produced by the CPG-triggered local controllers tolerate moderate terrain irregularities without requiring terrain-specific tuning.

Hardware experiment on structured uneven terrain (foam tiles, up to 18 mm height variation). The robot maintains forward locomotion and a stable body attitude despite contact uncertainty.

To further evaluate sim-to-real consistency, we compared the body pose evolution between simulation and hardware during representative forward-locomotion runs. The dominant discrepancies were observed near stance-exchange events, where servo backlash and compliance of the 3D-printed links introduced small transient body oscillations that are not fully captured by the rigid-body simulation. Nevertheless, the overall gait timing, body attitude trends, and locomotion stability remained qualitatively consistent between simulation and hardware without controller retuning.

Practical lessons learned

This section collects observations from the design, assembly, and testing of SPINDER that may be useful to other groups building small hexapod platforms. These findings emerged through trial and error over several hardware iterations and are not always obvious from theoretical analysis alone.

Servo bus timing and communication

The Dynamixel half-duplex TTL bus operates at 1 Mbps and uses a request–response protocol: the master sends a position command to one servo and waits for an acknowledgment before addressing the next. With 18 servos, a full update cycle takes approximately 3.6 ms when every servo replies, which limits the practical servo-update rate to about 250 Hz. We found two points worth noting. First, enabling the status return packet on every servo is useful during development for detecting wiring faults and stalled motors, but disabling it for non-critical reads (e.g., temperature polling) frees bus time for position commands. Second, the OPENRB-150 half-duplex direction switching has a brief dead time (≈5 μs); sending a new packet too soon after receiving a reply occasionally corrupted the next transaction. Adding a 10 μs guard delay between transactions eliminated this issue.

3D-printed structural compliance

The PLA structural links (40% infill, 0.2 mm layers) are adequate for a sub-kilogram platform but introduce noticeable compliance. Under walking loads, the femur struts deflect by roughly 0.3–0.5 mm at the tip, which is small relative to the 106 mm link length but accumulates across the kinematic chain. In practice, this compliance acts as passive shock absorption during foot strikes and did not degrade gait stability on flat or mildly uneven surfaces. However, it does mean that the actual foot position differs from the kinematic model by 1–2 mm, which matters if one attempts precision foot placement for tasks like stair climbing. We found that increasing infill to 60% on the femur strut reduced deflection by about 40% with only 3 g of additional mass per leg, a worthwhile trade-off for applications requiring stiffer links.

Sim-to-real calibration

We tuned the CPG parameters in MuJoCo and deployed them to hardware without fine-tuning. Two calibration steps were important for this direct transfer to work. First, the ground contact friction in MuJoCo was set to μ = 0.8 to match the slip behavior on our laboratory floor (vinyl tile). Using the default MuJoCo friction (μ = 1.0) produced gaits that slipped noticeably on the real floor because the controller assumed more traction than was available. Second, the servo position–torque response in MuJoCo was modeled as an ideal PD controller, but the real XC330 servos exhibit a dead band of approximately near the target position. We compensated for this by slightly increasing the swing height (H) by 2 mm relative to the simulation value, which ensured adequate ground clearance despite the servo dead band.

Foot contact switches

The binary contact switches mounted at each foot tip proved reliable on hard surfaces but occasionally gave false negatives on soft or compliant terrain (e.g., thick carpet), where the foot sinks gradually rather than making a distinct contact. For the experiments reported here (rigid and semi-rigid surfaces), the switches provided consistent ground-truth contact timing for gait analysis. Groups working on soft terrain may want to consider force-sensitive resistors or current-based contact detection as alternatives.

Power and thermal considerations

The 18 XC330 servos draw approximately 1.5 A total during walking at 5 V supply, with brief peaks up to 2.5 A during simultaneous swing-to-stance transitions. A 2S Li-Po battery (7.4 V nominal) through a 5 V BEC provides roughly 30 minutes of continuous walking. We observed that after 15–20 minutes of sustained tripod gait, the hip yaw servos (which bear the largest static loads) warmed to approximately 55C, at which point the built-in thermal protection begins to reduce torque output. Inserting 10-second rest intervals every 10 minutes of walking kept servo temperatures below 50C.

Conclusion

This paper describes the design, integration, and experimental evaluation of SPINDER, a sub-kilogram 18-DoF hexapod intended as a reproducible research platform. The robot is built from 3D-printed PLA parts and off-the-shelf Dynamixel servos, and integrates a depth camera, a 6-axis IMU, and per-foot contact switches within a split compute architecture.

The closed-form inverse kinematics solver handles mirrored joint conventions and runs in constant time per leg, supporting real-time Cartesian foot placement on an ARM processor. The hierarchical CPG–IK controller produces stable tripod, ripple, and wave gaits with omnidirectional commands, and achieves smooth gait transitions by modulating oscillator coupling weights without changes to the trajectory layer.

Across experiments, the controller produces repeatable gait cycles, bounded oscillator trajectories, and consistent stance–swing timing. The platform supports several canonical gaits and transitions between coordination patterns through velocity-conditioned coupling modulation, without modifying the local trajectory generator. On hardware, the robot maintains locomotion on flat ground and on structured uneven terrain, suggesting that the control decomposition is robust to moderate contact variability.

We also reported practical lessons on servo bus timing, structural compliance, sim-to-real calibration, and thermal management that are relevant to anyone building a small legged robot from commodity components. CAD files, URDF models, and control code will be released to support replication and extension by other groups.

Future work will close the loop from the depth camera to the foot-placement controller for terrain-aware stepping, and will extend the platform toward contact-loss detection and fault-tolerant gait reorganization.
