On the Green’s functions and Martin boundary structure of a planar diffusion in a discontinuous layered medium Thanks: This project has received funding from Agence Nationale de la Recherche, ANR JCJC programme under the Grant Agreement ANR-22-CE40-0002 (ANR RESYST).
Sandro Franceschi, Irina Kourkova, Maxence Petit

Abstract
. We consider a two-dimensional diffusion process in a two-layered plane, governed by distinct covariance matrices in the upper and lower half-planes and by two drift vectors pointed away from the x x -axis. We first analyze the case where the generator of the process is in divergence form, that is, when the flux is continuous across the interface. Then we extend the study to a broader class of processes whose behavior at the interface forms an oblique two-dimensional analogue of the skew Brownian motion. We provide a detailed theoretical analysis of this transient process. Our main results are as follows: (i) we derive explicit Laplace transforms of the Green’s functions; (ii) we compute exact asymptotics of the Green’s functions along all possible trajectories in the plane; (iii) We determine all positive harmonic functions, identifying the full and minimal Martin boundaries, which turn out to be distinct. The nonminimality of the Martin boundary is a noteworthy phenomenon for diffusions with regular coefficients. To obtain an analytical description of the process, we fully develop a three-variable version of the so-called kernel method by deriving and exploiting a functional equation involving unknown Laplace transforms of Green’s functions and two known kernels γ + ​ ( x , y ) \gamma_{+}(x,y) and γ − ​ ( x , z ) \gamma_{-}(x,z) . The introduction of independent auxiliary variables y y and z z , associated with each half-plane, is a key idea.



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Context, goals and strategy

The study of diffusion processes in multi-dimensional discontinuous or layered media is a challenging problem.
Diffusion processes with discontinuous coefficients arise naturally in various applied contexts, such as geophysics, ecology, and astrophysics, where the medium may be composed of layers with different diffusion and advection properties (see
[
22
,
23
]
and references therein).
In such models, discontinuities of the diffusion matrix act as permeable interfaces, leading to complex boundary behaviors.

In
[
23
]
, Lejay proposed a simulation algorithm for 2-dimensional diffusion processes in layered media. More precisely, the process is governed by different diagonal covariance matrices in the upper and lower half-plane of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the value of the singular drift on the axis is chosen to have a generator in the divergence form.
In the present paper, we consider the generalisation of this process with arbitrary covariance matrices
\Sigma^{+}
and
\Sigma^{-}
in the upper and lower half-planse and drifts
\mu^{+}
and
\mu^{-}
directed away from the
x
-axis. This model can be interpreted as a “bang-bang” type process, switching between two regimes, a structure reminiscent of stochastic control models
[
34
]
.
Our main focus is on the case where the generator of the process is in divergence form, that is, when the flux is continuous across the interface. In the last section of this paper, we also consider a broader class of processes whose behavior can be viewed as a natural two-dimensional generalization of a Skew Bang-Bang Brownian Motion (SBBBM), with oblique interaction at the interface.

A rich literature exists for skew Brownian motion in dimension one, see the foundational paper by Harrison and Shepp
[
12
]
and the survey by Lejay
[
22
]
. See also Fernholz et al. for a detailed introduction and study of one-dimensional SBBBM

[
10
]
and Lenotre who studies and simulates this type of process
[
24
]
.
Some results also exist in higher dimensions, a recent proof of weak existence and uniqueness was provided by Atar and Budhiraja
[
2
]
.
The first study of multidimensional skew Brownian motion was conducted by Portenko who constructed a diffusion process whose infinitesimal generator involves a singular drift concentrated on a smooth hypersurface
S

[
31
,
32
]
.
Zaitseva studied a stochastic differential equation describing a multidimensional Brownian motion
with an oblique skew penetration through a hyperplane, under the assumption that both the skewness coefficient and the direction are constant
[
38
]
. The extension to the general case of an oblique skew diffusion with non-constant coefficients remains a challenging open question
[
1
,
33
]
.

The skew Brownian motion is deeply linked to diffusion processes generated by

(1.1)

\mathcal{L}=\frac{\rho(x)}{2}\,\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(a(x)\,\frac{d}{dx}\right)+\mu(x)\,\frac{d}{dx},

where
a
and
\rho
have discontinuities
[
7
]
.
Indeed, the diffusion process
X
generated by
\mathcal{L}
can be described as the solution of an SDE with local time:

(1.2)

dX_{t}=\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}+\mu(X_{t})\,dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu(dx)\,dL_{t}^{x}(X),

where
\sigma^{2}=\rho a
and
\nu
is a finite signed measure whose mass is concentrated at the points where
a
or
\rho
are discontinuous. See Legall’s work
[
21
]
in dimension one and Stroock’s work
[
35
]
in higher dimension. The process studied in this paper satisfies an equation of type (
1.2
).

From a structural viewpoint, our model also connects with discrete analogues such as random walks or queueing processes evolving under different dynamics in distinct regions. The semi-martingale reflecting Brownian motion (SRBM) in a cone and its discrete counterparts are now well understood in dimension two and beyond (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
36
]
), but models involving different generators in several cones sharing a common boundary have been much less studied. Among discrete examples, the joining-the-shortest-queue (JSQ) model (studied by Kurkova and Suhov
[
19
]
, Kobayashi et al.
[
15
]
) and the doubly two-sided quasi-birth-and-death (DQBD) process (studied by Miyazawa
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
) display related features, although symmetries sometimes reduce them to single-kernel functional equations. In contrast, our diffusion model genuinely involves two distinct kernels corresponding to the two half-planes.
Some time-space continuous models for the multi-level single server queue are also studied in
[
16
,
29
]
.

Our aim is to provide a detailed theoretical analysis of the transient process presented above.
The main results of the present paper are as follows:

(i)

We make explicit the Laplace transforms of the Green’s functions.

(ii)

We derive the exact asymptotics of the Green’s functions along all possible trajectories in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(iii)

As a consequence, we obtain explicit expressions for all positive harmonic functions and determine the full Martin boundary. We also identify the minimal Martin boundary, which turns out to be strictly smaller than the full one.

The nonminimalty of the full Martin boundary reveals a genuinely new phenomenon, in contrast with known results for diffusions with regular coefficients, whether free or confined in cones, see
[
6
,
11
]
. This phenomenon can be also expected for diffusions in two-dimensional cones with special assumptions on drifts making the process escape to infinity along the boundaries, see
[
18
,
13
]
for the proof in the case of the analogous discrete random walks. However, the minimal Martin boundary in the last example is reduced to only two points, while for our process it is homeomorphic to a union of arcs of a circle.

The main tool of our analysis is a version of the so-called kernel method usually used to study random processes in cones; see Zhao
[
37
]
for a survey. The kernel
\gamma(x,y)
of the SRBM is usually given by the sum of one half of the quadratic form associated with the covariance matrix and the linear form corresponding to the drift. In our model, we consider two kernels,
\gamma^{+}(x,y)
and
\gamma^{-}(x,z)
, which share the same first variable and have distinct second variables. The crucial idea of our method is to introduce the independent variables
y
and
z
, which correspond to the two half-planes.

This approach was first fruitfully applied in the large deviation analysis of random walks in
\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
with a discontinuity on a hyperplane; as discussed by Ignatyuk et al.
[
14
]
. It was also employed in
[
27
]
, where the exponential decay rate of the stationary distribution for a DQBD process along the axes is determined by certain extremal points of the domains bounded by two kernels.

In this paper, we extend the kernel method to three variables by fully developing it through the derivation and exploitation of a functional equation involving the Laplace transforms of Green’s functions, see (
1.13
) below. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example of a three-variable implementation of the method, leading to a complete analytical characterization of the model.

1.2.
Planar skew bang-bang diffusion with piecewise constant coefficients

We now define a two-dimensional generalization of the SBBBM, which acts obliquely at the interface between two half-planes. The diffusion coefficients and the drift vector are piecewise constant, taking different values in the upper and lower half-planes.
Let
\Sigma^{+},\Sigma^{-}
be covariance matrices and
\mu^{+},\mu^{-}
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We define the matrices
\Sigma(y)
and
\sigma(y)
and the vector
\mu(y)
such that

\Sigma(y)=\sigma(y)\sigma^{\top}(y)=\Sigma^{-}\mathds{1}_{y<0}+\Sigma^{+}\mathds{1}_{y\geq 0}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(y)=\mu^{-}\mathds{1}_{y<0}+\mu^{+}\mathds{1}_{y\geq 0}.

Let
q=(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times(-1,1)
. This vector represents a singular drift on the axis
\{y=0\}
. The parameter
q_{2}=2\beta-1
corresponds to the classical skewness intensity at the boundary, when the process hits the axis
y=0
, it restarts on the
y>0
side with probability
\beta=(1+q_{2})/2
, and on the
y<0
side with probability
1-\beta=(1-q_{2})/2
. The parameter
q_{1}
fixes the singular drift component along the axis and thus introduces the oblique direction of the skew behaviour at the interface.

Definition 1.1

(Planar skew bang-bang diffusion with piecewise constant coefficients)

We define a planar skew bang-bang diffusion associated with coefficients
(\Sigma^{+},\Sigma^{-},\mu^{+},\mu^{-})

where:

(i)

z_{0}=(a_{0},b_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the starting point;

(ii)

(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an adapted two-dimensional standard Brownian motion;

(iii)

(L^{0}_{t}(B))_{t\geq 0}
is the symmetric local time at
0

defined by

(1.4)

L^{0}_{t}(B)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}(B_{s})\,ds.

1.3.
Green’s functions and functional equation

We have just seen that, when
q=q_{0}
, the generator can be expressed in divergence form. From Strook
[
35

, we deduce that for all

, the law of

has a density

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

(t,z_{0},z)\longmapsto p_{t}^{z_{0}}(z)

(0,+\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

Definition 1.3

(Green’s measures and Laplace transforms)
.

For
z_{0}=(a_{0},b_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we define the Green’s measure on the plane
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by

(1.8)

G(z_{0},S)={\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathds{1}_{S}(Z_{t})\,dt\right]=\iint_{S}g^{z_{0}}(a,b)\,da\,db,\quad\forall S\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})

where its density
g^{z_{0}}
is called the Green’s function and is equal to

(1.9)

g^{z_{0}}(a,b)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{t}^{z_{0}}(a,b)\,dt,\quad\forall(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

For
x,y,z\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we also define the Laplace transforms of
G(z_{0},\cdot)
restricted to the half-planes
{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,+\infty)
and
{\mathbb{R}}\times(-\infty,0)
by

\varphi_{+}^{z_{0}}(x,y)={\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{xA_{t}+yB_{t}}\mathds{1}_{B_{t}>0}\,dt\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{z_{0}}(a,b)e^{xa+yb}\,da\,db,

\varphi_{-}^{z_{0}}(x,z)={\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{xA_{t}+zB_{t}}\mathds{1}_{B_{t}<0}\,dt\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}g^{z_{0}}(a,b)e^{xa+zb}\,da\,db.

Finally, we define the Green’s measure on the axis
{\mathbb{R}}\times\{0\}
by

(1.10)

H(z_{0},S)={\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathds{1}_{S}(A_{t})\,dL^{0}_{t}(B)\right],\quad\forall S\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}})

and its Laplace transform
\varphi^{z_{0}}
as

(1.11)

\varphi^{z_{0}}(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{xA_{t}}\,dL^{0}_{t}(B)\right].

For
x,y,z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we set:

(1.12)

\begin{cases}\gamma_{+}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(x,y)\cdot\Sigma^{+}(x,y)+(x,y)\cdot\mu^{+}=\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma^{+}_{11}x^{2}+2\Sigma^{+}_{12}xy+\Sigma_{22}^{+}y^{2})+\mu_{1}^{+}x+\mu^{+}_{2}y\\
\gamma_{-}(x,z)=\frac{1}{2}(x,z)\cdot\Sigma^{-}(x,z)+(x,z)\cdot\mu^{-}=\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma^{-}_{11}x^{2}+2\Sigma^{-}_{12}xz+\Sigma_{22}^{-}z^{2})+\mu_{1}^{-}x+\mu^{-}_{2}z\\
\gamma(x,y,z)=q_{1}x+\frac{1}{2}(y(1+q_{2})+z(q_{2}-1)).\end{cases}

2.1.
Construction of the process

In this section, we prove Proposition
1.2
. The proof is adapted from
[

, Proposition 1]
, where the covariance matrix is assumed to be diagonal. We first establish the existence and pathwise uniqueness of the solution to equation (
1.3

Existence and uniqueness of (
1.3
)

First, note that (
1.3
) can be written as the following SDE:

(2.1)

\begin{cases}dA_{t}&=\sqrt{\Sigma_{11}(B_{t})-\frac{\Sigma_{12}^{2}(B_{t})}{\Sigma_{22}(B_{t})}}\,dW^{1}_{t}+\frac{\Sigma_{12}(B_{t})}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}(B_{t})}}\,dW^{2}_{t}+\mu_{1}(B_{t})\,dt+\frac{\Sigma_{12}^{+}-\Sigma_{12}^{-}}{\Sigma_{22}^{+}+\Sigma_{22}^{-}}\,dL^{0}_{t}(B),\quad(1)\\[4.30554pt]
dB_{t}&=\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}(B_{t})}\,dW^{2}_{t}+\mu_{2}(B_{t})\,dt+\frac{\Sigma_{22}^{+}-\Sigma_{22}^{-}}{\Sigma_{22}^{+}+\Sigma_{22}^{-}}\,dL^{0}_{t}(B),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad(2)\end{cases}

Here,
\big((W^{1}_{t},W^{2}_{t})\big)_{t\geq 0}
is a standard two-dimensional Brownian motion, and
L^{0}(B)
denotes the symmetric local time of
B
at
0

Indeed, the matrix

\sigma(B_{t})=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\Sigma_{11}(B_{t})-\frac{\Sigma_{12}^{2}(B_{t})}{\Sigma_{22}(B_{t})}}&\frac{\Sigma_{12}(B_{t})}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}(B_{t})}}\\

satisfies
\sigma(B_{t})\,\sigma(B_{t})^{T}=\Sigma(B_{t})
.

Let
(W^{1},W^{2})
be a standard two-dimensional Brownian motion. By
[
21
]
, there exists a pathwise unique solution
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of equation
(2)
starting from
b_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, equation
(1)
admits a pathwise unique solution starting from
a_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
given by:

A_{t}=a_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\Sigma_{11}(B_{s})-\frac{\Sigma_{12}^{2}(B_{s})}{\Sigma_{22}(B_{s})}}\,dW^{1}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\Sigma_{12}(B_{s})}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}(B_{s})}}\,dW^{2}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{1}(B_{s})\,ds+\frac{\Sigma_{12}^{+}-\Sigma_{12}^{-}}{\Sigma_{22}^{+}+\Sigma_{22}^{-}}L^{0}_{t}(B).

Using a generalisation of the Yamada Watanabe Engelbert theorem (see
[
20
, Proposition 2.10]
), uniqueness in law also holds for the process starting from
(a_{0},b_{0})
. By standard arguments, we deduce that the family of probability laws indexed by
(a_{0},b_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defines a Markov process.

Shape of the generator

Let us prove that the generator can be written in divergence form. To this end, consider the continuous piecewise affine regularisations of
\Sigma
and
\mu
defined by:
\Sigma^{n}(b)=\Sigma^{+}
if

,
\Sigma^{n}(b)=\Sigma^{-}
if

, and
\Sigma^{n}

(similarly for

Let
(W^{1},W^{2})
be a two-dimensional Brownian motion. By standard arguments, the SDE

\begin{cases}dA^{n}_{t}&=\sqrt{\Sigma^{n}_{11}(B^{n}_{t})-\frac{{\Sigma_{12}^{n}}^{2}(B^{n}_{t})}{\Sigma^{n}_{22}(B^{n}_{t})}}\,dW^{1}_{t}+\frac{\Sigma^{n}_{12}(B^{n}_{t})}{\sqrt{\Sigma^{n}_{22}(B^{n}_{t})}}\,dW^{2}_{t}+\left(\mu^{n}_{1}(B^{n}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dy}\Sigma^{n}_{12}(B^{n}_{t})\right)dt,\\[4.30554pt]

By
[

[

converges in the

for all

.
By identifying the limits, we conclude that (

Inequalities (

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-2uq_{1}L_{\infty}^{0}(B)}\right]\cdot e^{-2ut\left(\min(\mu_{1}^{+},\mu_{1}^{-})-u\max(\Sigma_{11}^{+},\Sigma_{11}^{-})\right)}.

If we choose
u>0
small enough so that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-2uq_{1}L_{\infty}^{0}(B)}\right]<+\infty
by Lemma
2.4
and such that
\min(\mu_{1}^{+},\mu_{1}^{-})-u\max(\Sigma_{11}^{+},\Sigma_{11}^{-})>0
, then the right-hand term tends to zero as
t\to+\infty
, which completes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Short version of the proof of the functional equation

We now present a brief argument to establish Proposition
1.4
. The idea of the proof is to apply Itô’s formula to

f(a,b)=e^{ax+by}\mathds{1}_{b\geq 0}+e^{ax+bz}\mathds{1}_{b<0}.

Note that
f\notin\mathcal{C}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
. In this section, we assume that all local martingales are true martingales, ignore convergence issues, and apply the standard Itô formula despite the lack of smoothness of
f
.
We compute:

(2.11)

\nabla f(a,b):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}xf(a,b)\\
ye^{ax+by}\mathds{1}_{b\geq 0}+ze^{ax+bz}\mathds{1}_{b<0}\end{array}\right)

and

\nabla^{2}f(a,b)=\begin{pmatrix}x^{2}f(a,b)&xye^{ax+by}\mathds{1}_{b\geq 0}+xze^{ax+bz}\mathds{1}_{b<0}\\
xye^{ax+by}\mathds{1}_{b\geq 0}+xze^{ax+bz}\mathds{1}_{b<0}&y^{2}e^{ax+by}\mathds{1}_{b\geq 0}+z^{2}e^{ax+bz}\mathds{1}_{b<0}+(y-z)e^{ax}\delta_{b=0}\end{pmatrix}.

Formally, Itô’s formula yields

(2.12)

f(Z_{t})=f(Z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla f(Z_{s})\,dZ_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f(Z_{s})\,d\langle Z^{i},Z^{j}\rangle_{s}

(with notation
Z^{1}=A
and
Z^{2}=B
).
Since
dZ_{t}=\sigma(B_{t})dB_{t}+\mu(B_{t})dt+q\,dL_{t}^{0}(B)
, we get

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\nabla f(Z_{s})\,dZ_{s}\right]=(x\mu_{1}^{+}+y\mu_{2}^{+}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}\,ds\right]+(x\mu_{1}^{-}+z\mu_{2}^{-}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}\,ds\right]

+\left(q_{1}x+\frac{q_{2}(y+z)}{2}\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{s}}\,dL_{s}^{0}(B)\right].

Now, let us consider the second derivative terms. Similarly, we have:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f(Z_{s})\,d\langle Z^{i},Z^{j}\rangle_{s}\right]=\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}\Sigma_{11}^{+}+2xy\Sigma_{12}^{+}+y^{2}\Sigma_{22}^{+}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}\,ds\right]

+\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}\Sigma_{11}^{-}+2xz\Sigma_{12}^{-}+z^{2}\Sigma_{22}^{-}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}\,ds\right]

+\left(\frac{y-z}{2}\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{s}}\delta_{B_{s}=0}\,ds\right].

The last term can be interpreted as
\left(\frac{y-z}{2}\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{s}}\,dL^{0}_{t}(B)\right]
. Then, by rearranging the terms in the expected value of (
2.12
), we obtain

(2.13)

\gamma_{-}(x,z){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}\,ds\right]+\gamma_{+}(x,y){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}>0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}\,ds\right]+\gamma(x,y,z){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{s}}\,dL_{s}^{0}(B)\right]

={\mathbb{E}}[f(Z_{t})]-\left(e^{xa_{0}+yb_{0}}\mathds{1}_{b_{0}\geq 0}+e^{xa_{0}+zb_{0}}\mathds{1}_{b_{0}<0}\right).

Assuming that
x
,
y
, and
z
are chosen such that
{\mathbb{E}}[f(Z_{t})]\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
and that the Laplace transforms converge, we obtain the desired equation.

2.4.
Detailed version of the proof of the functional equation

In this section, we provide a detailed justification for the reasoning presented in Section
2.3
when
b_{0}\neq 0
(see Remark
2.6
for the case
b_{0}=0
). Note that an Itô–Tanaka formula exists in dimension
2
for non-smooth functions
[
9
]
. However, it is not straightforward to use, so instead we opted for an approximation using smooth functions.
We then approximate the preceding function
f
by smooth functions
f_{\epsilon}
defined by

(2.14)

f_{\epsilon}(a,b)=e^{ax+bz}+\psi(b/\epsilon)(e^{ax+by}-e^{ax+bz}),

where
\psi
is an increasing
C^{\infty}
function satisfying

\psi(u):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\textnormal{if}\quad u\leq-1,\\
1&\textnormal{if}\quad u\geq 1,\\
\frac{1}{2}&\textnormal{if}\quad u=0.\end{array}\right.

Note that
f_{\epsilon}(a,b)=e^{ax+by}
if
b\geq\epsilon
and
f_{\epsilon}(a,b)=e^{ax+bz}
if
b\leq-\epsilon
. The Itô formula can now be applied to the
C^{\infty}
function
f_{\epsilon}
: for all
t\geq 0
, we have

(2.15)

f_{\epsilon}(Z_{t})=f_{\epsilon}(Z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\,dZ_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\,d\langle Z^{i},Z^{j}\rangle_{s}.

Step 1 : Study of integrals against
dZ_{s}
.
Note that

(2.16)

\nabla f_{\epsilon}(a,b):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}xf_{\epsilon}(a,b)\\
ze^{ax+bz}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(b/\epsilon)(e^{ax+by}-e^{ax+bz})+\psi(b/\epsilon)(ye^{ax+by}-ze^{ax+bz})\end{array}\right).

Then,

(2.17)

\int_{0}^{t}\nabla f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}\,dZ_{s}=x\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\,dA_{s}

+\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}\left(ze^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(B_{s}/\epsilon)(e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})+\psi(B_{s}/\epsilon)(ye^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-ze^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})\right)dB_{s},

where
\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}\,dA_{s}=(\Sigma_{11}^{+}dB^{1}_{s}+\Sigma_{12}^{+}dB^{2}_{s}+\mu_{1}^{+}ds)+q_{1}\,dL_{t}^{0}(B)
and
\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}dB_{s}=(\Sigma_{12}^{+}dB^{1}_{s}+\Sigma_{22}^{+}dB^{2}_{s}+\mu_{2}^{+}ds)+q_{2}dL_{t}^{0}(B)
. Then, by Lemma
2.4
, the Brownian integrals are martingales (not just local martingales), so their expectations vanish. Therefore,

(2.18)

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\nabla f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}\,dZ_{s}\right]=x\mu_{1}^{+}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\,ds\right]+y\mu_{2}^{+}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq\epsilon}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}\,ds\right]

+\mu_{2}^{+}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{0\leq B_{s}<\epsilon}\left(ze^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(B_{s}/\epsilon)(e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})+\psi(B_{s}/\epsilon)(ye^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-ze^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})\right)ds\right]

+\left(q_{1}x+\frac{q_{2}(y+z)}{2}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{s}}\,dL_{t}^{0}(B)\right].

By the dominated convergence theorem and inequality (
2.4
),

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{0\leq B_{s}<\epsilon}\left(ze^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}+\psi(B_{s}/\epsilon)(ye^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-ze^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})\right)ds\right]\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0,

and

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq\epsilon}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}\,ds\right]\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}\,ds\right].

Moreover, by the definition of
g_{t}
, we have:

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{0\leq B_{s}<\epsilon}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(B_{s}/\epsilon)(e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})\,ds\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(b/\epsilon)(e^{ax+by}-e^{ax+bz})g_{t}(a,b)\,dbda.

Since
b_{0}\neq 0
, estimates (
2.3
) yield the following limit for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
as
\epsilon\to 0
:

(2.19)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(b/\epsilon)(e^{ax+by}-e^{ax+bz})g_{t}(a,b)\,db

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(u)(e^{ax+\epsilon uy}-e^{ax+\epsilon uz})g_{t}(a,\epsilon u)\,du

(2.20)

\displaystyle\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}(e^{ax}-e^{ax})g_{t}(a,0)=0.

(Note that
\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(\cdot/\epsilon)\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\delta_{0}
in the sense of distributions.) Integrating in
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
and using (
2.3
) again, we get

(2.21)

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{0\leq B_{s}<\epsilon}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(B_{s}/\epsilon)(e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})\,ds\right]\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

By a similar analysis on
\int_{0}^{t}\nabla f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}\,dZ_{s}
, the following limit holds:

(2.22)

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\nabla f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})\,dZ_{s}\right]\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}(x\mu_{1}^{+}+y\mu_{2}^{+}){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}\,ds\right]+(x\mu_{1}^{-}+z\mu_{2}^{-}){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}\,ds\right]

+\left(q_{1}x+\frac{q_{2}(y+z)}{2}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{s}}\,dL_{t}^{0}(B)\right].

Step 2: Study of integrals against
d\langle Z^{i},Z^{j}\rangle_{s}
.

Since
\partial_{1}f_{\epsilon}(a,b)=xf_{\epsilon}(a,b)
, we can similarly show that

(2.23)

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{1}\partial_{1}f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})d\langle A,A\rangle_{s}\right]\underset{{\epsilon}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\Sigma_{11}^{+}x^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}ds\right]+\Sigma_{11}^{-}x^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}ds\right]

and

(2.24)

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{1}\partial_{2}f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})d\langle A,B\rangle_{s}\right]\underset{{\epsilon}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\Sigma_{12}^{+}xy{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}ds\right]+\Sigma_{12}^{-}xz{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}ds\right].

Now, let us study the last term
i=j=2
. We have
d\langle B,B\rangle_{t}=\Sigma_{22}^{+}\mathds{1}_{B_{t}\geq 0}dt+\Sigma_{22}^{-}\mathds{1}_{B_{t}<0}dt
, and

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\partial_{2}f_{\epsilon}(a,b)

\displaystyle=z^{2}e^{ax+bz}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\psi^{\prime\prime}(b/\epsilon)(e^{ax+by}-e^{ax+bz})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(b/\epsilon)(ye^{ax+by}-ze^{ax+bz})+\psi(b/\epsilon)(y^{2}e^{ax+by}-z^{2}e^{ax+bz}).

By the dominated convergence theorem and inequality (
2.4
), we obtain similarly:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left(z^{2}e^{A_{t}x+B_{t}z}+\psi(b/\epsilon)(y^{2}e^{A_{t}x+bB_{t}}-z^{2}e^{A_{t}x+B_{t}z})\right)d\langle B,B\rangle_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{{\epsilon}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\Sigma_{22}^{+}y^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}ds\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\Sigma_{22}^{-}z^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}ds\right].

By the same arguments as for (
2.21
), we have

(2.25)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{-{\epsilon}\leq B_{s}<{\epsilon}}\frac{2}{\epsilon}\psi^{\prime}(B_{s}/\epsilon)(ye^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-ze^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})d\langle B,B\rangle_{s}\right]\underset{{\epsilon}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}(\Sigma_{22}^{-}+\Sigma_{22}^{+})(y-z)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{ax}g_{t}(a,0)da.

Additionally,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{0\leq B_{s}<{\epsilon}}(1/\epsilon^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(B_{s}/\epsilon)(e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}-e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}})ds\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(b/\epsilon)(e^{xa+yb}-e^{xa+zb})g_{t}(a,b)dbda

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime\prime}(b)\frac{e^{ax+b\epsilon y}-e^{ax+b\epsilon z}}{\epsilon}g_{t}(a,\epsilon b)dbda

\displaystyle\underset{{\epsilon}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}(y-z)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{ax}g_{t}(a,0)da\int_{0}^{1}b\psi^{\prime\prime}(b)db

\displaystyle=(y-z)(\psi(0)-\psi(1))\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{ax}g_{t}(a,0)da=\frac{(z-y)}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{ax}g_{t}(a,0)da

Here, the limit is once again justified by the estimates in (
2.3
). Combining this with Lemma
2.2
, we have shown that

(2.26)

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f_{\epsilon}(Z_{s})d\langle Z^{i},Z^{j}\rangle_{s}\right]\underset{{\epsilon}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma_{11}^{+}x^{2}+2\Sigma_{12}^{+}xy+\Sigma_{22}^{+}y^{2}){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}\geq 0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}ds\right]

+\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma_{11}^{-}x^{2}+2\Sigma_{12}^{-}xy+\Sigma_{22}^{-}y^{2}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}ds\right]+(y-z){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{s}}dL^{0}_{s}(B)\right].

Step 3: Sum up and take the limit as
t\to+\infty
.

Combining (
2.15
), (
2.22
), and (
2.26
), we obtain:

(2.27)

{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}[f(Z_{t})]=\gamma_{-}(x,z){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}ds\right]+\gamma_{+}(x,y){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}>0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}ds\right]

+\left(q_{1}x+\frac{y(1+q_{2})+z(q_{2}-1)}{2}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{t}}dL^{0}_{s}(B)\right]+e^{xa_{0}+yb_{0}\mathds{1}_{b_{0}>0}+zb_{0}\mathds{1}_{b_{0}<0}}.

Moreover, note that
f(Z_{t})\leq e^{xA_{t}}
since
y<0
and
z>0
. By Lemma
2.5
,
{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}[f(Z_{t})]\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
if
-\eta<x<0
.

Note that
x<0
,
y<0
,
z>0
can be taken arbitrarily close to
0
such that the conditions
\gamma_{+}(x,y)<0
,
\gamma_{-}(x,z)<0
,
y-z<0
, and
q_{1}x+\frac{y(1+q_{2})+z(q_{2}-1)}{2}<0
are satisfied. For such
x,y,z
, the quantity

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}(x,z){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}<0}e^{xA_{s}+zB_{s}}ds\right]+\gamma_{+}(x,y){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{B_{s}>0}e^{xA_{s}+yB_{s}}ds\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(q_{1}x+\frac{y(1+q_{2})+z(q_{2}-1)}{2}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{z_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{xA_{t}}dL^{0}_{s}(B)\right]

converges to

\gamma_{-}(x,z)\varphi^{z_{0}}_{-}(x,z)+\gamma_{+}(x,y)\varphi^{z_{0}}_{+}(x,y)+\left(q_{1}x+\frac{y(1+q_{2})+z(q_{2}-1)}{2}\right)\varphi^{z_{0}}(x)\in[-\infty,0)

as
t\to+\infty
(if some terms diverge, they are equal to
-\infty
so the sum remains well defined). By (
2.27
), the previous sum is finite when
x\in(-\eta,0)
, so
\varphi^{z_{0}}_{-}(x,z)
,
\varphi^{z_{0}}_{+}(x,y)
, and
\varphi^{z_{0}}(x)
are finite for
x\in(-\eta,0)
,
y<0
and
z>0
arbitrarily close to
0
.
Now that we know the Laplace transforms converge for such
x,y,z
, the asymptotics of (
2.27
) as
t\to+\infty
yield the desired functional equation.

Remark 2.6

(Choice of
b_{0}\neq 0
)
.

The choice
b_{0}\neq 0
for the initial point
(a_{0},b_{0})
is made for essentially technical reasons. Indeed, it ensures that
g_{t}
remains finite on the strip
{\mathbb{R}}\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small; this avoids unnecessary difficulties when taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in (
2.20
) and (
2.25
). The functional equation also holds for
b_{0}=0
, which can be proved by taking the limit of (
1.13
) as
z_{0}\to(a_{0},0)
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1
and
2
: Laplace transforms and asymptotics on the axis

Since the proof of Theorem
2
is quite independent of the proofs of the other theorems, it has been placed in a separate section.
Let us recall the expressions for
Y^{\pm}(x)
and
Z^{\pm}(x)
given in (
1.14
) and (
1.15
).

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists
{\epsilon}>0
such that for all
u\in[-{\epsilon},0]
and
v\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\Re\bigl(Y^{-}(u+iv)\bigr)<0\quad\text{(resp.}\quad\Re\bigl(Z^{+}(u+iv)\bigr)>0\text{).}

Proof.

By elementary considerations (see
[
11
]
, correcting an error without any consequence), we have

(3.1)

\Re(Y^{\pm}(u+iv))=\frac{1}{\Sigma^{+}_{22}}\Big(-\Sigma^{+}_{12}u-\mu^{+}_{2}\pm\frac{\sqrt{\det(\Sigma^{+})}}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(u-x^{+}_{min})(x^{+}_{max}-u)+v^{2}+|(u+iv-x^{+}_{min})(x^{+}_{max}-u-iv)|}\Big).

Then,
\Re(Y^{-}(u+iv))\leq\Re(Y^{-}(u))
for all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The inequality
Y^{-}(0)=-\frac{2\mu^{+}_{2}}{\Sigma^{+}_{22}}<0
yields the first inequality. The second one is obtained symmetrically since
\mu_{2}^{-}<0
.
∎

We are now able to prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

For
x\in(-{\epsilon},0)
, we have
Y^{-}(x)<0
and
Z^{+}(x)>0
. We can then substitute
(x,Y^{-}(x),Z^{+}(x))
into the functional equation (
1.13
) to derive the equality (
1.17
) for
x\in(-{\epsilon},0)
. Since
\varphi
is a Laplace transform, equation (
1.17
) holds in the entire domain of convergence of
\varphi
by holomorphicity. Since the expression is meromorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigl((-\infty,\widetilde{x}_{b}]\cup[{x_{b}},+\infty)\bigr)
, it remains only to show that the equation
\gamma(x,Y^{-}(x),Z^{+}(x))=0
has no solution on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigl((-\infty,\widetilde{x}_{b})\cup({x_{b}},+\infty)\bigr)
. Using expressions (
1.5
), (
1.14
) and (
1.15
), we can write
\gamma(x,Y^{-}(x),Z^{+}(x))
as:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,Y^{-}(x),Z^{+}(x))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Sigma_{22}^{+}+\Sigma_{22}^{-}}\big((\Sigma_{12}^{+}-\Sigma_{12}^{-})x+\Sigma_{22}^{+}Y^{-}(x)-\Sigma_{22}^{-}Z^{+}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Sigma_{22}^{+}+\Sigma_{22}^{-}}\Big(-\mu_{2}^{+}+\mu_{2}^{-}-\sqrt{(\Sigma^{+}_{12}{}^{2}-\Sigma^{+}_{11}\Sigma^{+}_{22})x^{2}+2(\mu^{+}_{2}\Sigma^{+}_{12}-\mu^{+}_{1}\Sigma^{+}_{22})x+(\mu^{+}_{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\sqrt{(\Sigma^{-}_{12}{}^{2}-\Sigma^{-}_{11}\Sigma^{-}_{22})x^{2}+2(\mu^{-}_{2}\Sigma^{-}_{12}-\mu^{-}_{1}\Sigma^{-}_{22})x+(\mu^{-}_{2})^{2}}\Big).

Furthermore, by Assumption (
1.7
),
-\mu_{2}^{+}+\mu_{2}^{-}<0
and by the same arguments as for (
3.1
),

\Re\left(\sqrt{(\Sigma^{\pm}_{12}{}^{2}-\Sigma^{\pm}_{11}\Sigma^{\pm}_{22})x^{2}+2(\mu^{\pm}_{2}\Sigma^{\pm}_{12}-\mu^{\pm}_{1}\Sigma^{\pm}_{22})x+(\mu^{\pm}_{2})^{2}}\right)\geq 0.

Considering the real part, we see that
\gamma(x,Y^{-}(x),Z^{+}(x))
does not vanish on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigl((-\infty,\widetilde{x}_{b}]\cup[{x_{b}},+\infty)\bigr)
, and the first result follows.

Equation (
1.13
), combined with the explicit expression of
\varphi^{z_{0}}
(see Theorem
1
), provides an explicit formula for
\varphi^{z_{0}}_{+}(x,y)
. Indeed, by substituting
z=Z^{+}(x)>0
(with
x<0
small enough) and
y<0
into (
1.13
), we obtain

(3.3)

\varphi^{z_{0}}_{+}(x,y)=\frac{-\gamma(x,y,Z^{+}(x))\varphi^{z_{0}}(x)-e^{xa_{0}+yb_{0}\mathds{1}_{b_{0}\geq 0}+zb_{0}\mathds{1}_{b_{0}<0}}}{\gamma_{+}(x,y)}.

Using the holomorphic properties of the Laplace transforms, (
3.3
) holds for all
(x,y)
in the domain of convergence of
\varphi^{z_{0}}_{+}
.
∎

We now state a final preparatory lemma prior to the proof of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 3.2

(Behaviour of
\varphi^{z_{0}}
at
{x_{b}}
)
.

Let
C_{0}
and
f_{0}(z_{0})
be defined as in Theorem

Lemma 4.1

(Inverse Laplace theorem)

Proof.

Lemma 4.2

(Reduction to simple integrals)

(4.3)

(4.4)

Proof.

Note that

Appendix A
Two-sided Tauberian theorem

Tauberian theorems are known for Laplace transforms on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, giving a link between the asymptotics of a function and its Laplace transform (cf
[
5
, Theorem 37.1]
or
[
4
, Lemma C.2]

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be a continuous function and
\varphi(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{xz}f(x)dx
. Suppose that there are
a<0<b
such that
\varphi
converges absolutely in
a<\Re(z)<b
. For
\delta>0
and
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
, let
G^{+}_{\delta}(s)=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}},\Re(z)>0,z\neq s,|\arg(z-s)|>\delta\}
(where the complex argument is taken in
(-\pi,\pi]
). Suppose that there exists
0\leq\delta<\pi/2
such that

—

Function
\varphi
has a holomorphic continuation on
G^{+}_{\delta}(b)
.

—

The following asymptotics hold:

\varphi(z)\underset{|z|\to+\infty\atop z\in G^{+}_{\delta}(b)}{\longrightarrow}0.

—

There exists
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and constants
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

such that

(A.1)

(\varphi(z)-c)(b-z)^{\lambda}\underset{z\to b\atop z\in G^{+}_{\delta}(b)}{\longrightarrow}A.

Then:

f(x)\underset{x\to+\infty}{\sim}\frac{A}{\Gamma(\lambda)}e^{-bx}x^{\lambda-1}

Obviously, the symmetric lemma for the asymptotics of

in
-\infty
holds.

Appendix B
An integral asymptotic lemma

Lemma B.1
.

The following asymptotics hold as
q\to+\infty

\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1-s}e^{qs^{2}}ds\underset{q\to+\infty}{\sim}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\sqrt{2}}\frac{e^{q}}{q^{3/2}}.

Proof.

It suffices to establish that, for some
\epsilon>0
, the integral from
over
[1-\epsilon,1]
has the same asymptotic. Since the asymptotic of the integral over
[0,1-\epsilon]
is bounded by
O(\exp(q(1-\epsilon)^{2})))
, as
q\to\infty.
Let us look at

J(q,\epsilon)=\exp(-q)\int_{1-\epsilon}^{1}\sqrt{1-s}\exp(qs^{2})ds

for some
\epsilon>0
small enough. After the change of variables
t=q-qs^{2}
, it becomes

J(q,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2q}\int_{0}^{q(1-(1-\epsilon)^{2})}\frac{\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1-t/q}}}{\sqrt{1-t/q}}\exp(-t)dt.

For any
\epsilon>0
small enough there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Big|\frac{\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1-s}}}{\sqrt{1-s}}-\sqrt{\frac{s}{2}}\Big|\leq Cs\sqrt{\frac{s}{2}},\ \ \forall s\in[0,1-(1-\epsilon)^{2}].

Then

J(q,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2q}\int\limits_{0}^{q(1-(1-\epsilon)^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{t}{2q}}\exp(-t)dt+R(q,\epsilon)

where

|R(q,\epsilon)|\leq\frac{1}{2q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{t}{2q}}\frac{t}{q}\exp(-t)dt=O(q^{-5/2}),\ \ \ q\to\infty

is of smaller order than the first term. It remains to compute

\int_{0}^{q(1-(1-\epsilon)^{2})}\sqrt{t}\exp(-t)dt\to\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2},\ \ q\to\infty.

∎
