Non-immune hydrops fetalis (NIHF): diagnostic evaluation and lessons learned from a case series
Authors: Jihad Al Hassan, Georges Yared, Charlotte El Hajjar, Manal Dahrouj, Christopher Massaad, Ahmad El Moghrabi, Kariman Ghazal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFetal hydrops, characterized by abnormal fluid accumulation in two or more fetal compartments, is a critical condition with a poor prognosis, often resulting in significant fetal mortality. The condition itself is not a diagnosis but a clinical sign with many underlying causes, ranging from infections and chromosomal abnormalities to rare maternal conditions such as mirror syndrome.Case PresentationThis review examines three cases of fetal hydrops, highlighting common etiologies, such as infections and chromosomal disorders, and more unusual causes like maternal complications.Diagnostic ApproachThe current approach to diagnosing hydrops fetalis relies primarily on ultrasound imaging and Doppler studies to identify abnormal fluid accumulation and pathophysiology.Future PerspectiveHowever, advances in genomic testing are rapidly transforming the diagnostic pathway. With increasing accuracy, speed and accessibility of genetic testing, it is likely that these tools will be integrated earlier in the diagnostic process, potentially improving prognostic assessments and treatment outcomes.ConclusionThe importance of early and accurate identification of the cause is essential to inform clinical management, improve fetal survival rates and support parental decision-making.Keywords: Non-immune hydrops fetalis, fetal hydrops, chromosomal abnormalities, mirror syndrome, ultrasound imaging, genomic testing, perinatal mortality, prognosis

Introduction

Fetal hydrops is defined as abnormal fluid accumulation in two or more fetal compartments, occurring in approximately 1 in 1,700 pregnancies [1]. While it can manifest at any stage of pregnancy, early detection is typically noted by an increase in nuchal translucency during first-trimester ultrasound. Nuchal translucency, which refers to the region behind the fetal neck, enlarges due to subcutaneous edema or jugular lymphatic sac dilation. Hydrops fetalis is diagnosed when two or more of the following are present: pericardial effusion, pleural effusion, ascites, or generalized skin edema. Additional features, such as placental thickening and polyhydramnios, though commonly associated with fetal hydrops, are not necessary for diagnosis.

Once diagnosed, fetal hydrops necessitates immediate referral to a center specializing in maternal-fetal medicine. Ultrasound imaging, along with Doppler studies and a detailed obstetric and family history, are key tools for diagnosing the condition, planning treatment, and estimating the prognosis. A study of 167 fetuses with hydrops revealed a mortality rate of 66%, with many dying before or shortly after birth [2]. Similarly, a Japanese study of 214 cases of non-immune fetal hydrops found a perinatal survival rate of 43%, with only 37% of infants surviving beyond one year, and only 52% of those children demonstrating appropriate neurodevelopment [3].

Hydrops fetalis is broadly classified into two categories and can stem from immune and non-immune etiologies. Immune hydrops fetalis primarily results from red cell alloimmunization, whereby maternal antibodies target fetal red blood cells which subsequently leads to hemolysis and anemia. However, with current advancements when it comes to Rh immunoprophylactic, the incidence of IHF has significantly declined, which makes therefore non-immune hydrops fetalis (NIHF) the predominant form today and accounts for approximately 90% of hydrops fetalis cases.

In turn, NIHF is a heterogeneous condition with encompasses more than 80 potential etiologies, and includes genetic, infectious, cardiovascular, lymphatic, hematologic, and metabolic causes. Unlike immune hydrops fetalis, the pathophysiology of NIHF is often multifactorial and more complex. As depicted in Figure 1, NIHF can result from decreased oncotic pressure, high central venous pressure, or lymphatic obstruction (Figure 1).

NIHF is a critical indicator of mortality and morbidity, making it imperative to identify the underlying etiology. In some cases, timely diagnosis of the cause can allow for the initiation of treatment, thereby improving fetal survival or neonatal care outcomes. In others, identifying a genetic abnormality provides essential information to parents about the prognosis and recurrence risk in future pregnancies. Numerous studies have classified the various etiologies of fetal hydrops, though this categorization often proves challenging due to overlapping clinical presentations. For instance, fetuses with monosomy X (Down, Turner Syndrome) can be categorized into multiple systems, including chromosomal, lymphatic, cardiac, and syndromic classifications [4,5] (Tables 1 and 2).

This paper presents a clinical analysis of three cases of fetal hydrops with different etiologies, and emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in diagnosis and management. By shedding light on the different etiologies of hydrops fetalis, this paper provides valuable insights into the diagnostic and management, especially in the Lebanese healthcare system where these cases were reported. Indeed, resource limitations and multiple etiologies of hydrops fetalis can pose peculiar challenges, thus this case series proposes actionable recommendations for clinical practice.

Case 1:

A 30-year-old gravida 1, para 1 woman was seen at 24 weeks’ gestation following a confirmed maternal parvovirus infection, indicated by positive IgG and IgM serology. Initial sonographic evaluation revealed minimal fetal ascites accompanied by pleural effusion. By 28 weeks’ gestation, the condition had progressed, with an increase in ascites and the development of pericardial effusion. Figure 2(A, B, C, D). The final diagnosis was congenital parvovirus B19 infection complicated by non-immune hydrops fetalis (NIHF) and fetal anemia.

Ultrasound evaluation revealing fetal hydrops, presenting with pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ascites, and hepatomegaly.

Subsequent ultrasounds confirmed fetal hydrops, presenting with pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ascites, and hepatomegaly. Figure 2(C, D, E, F, G).

Doppler velocimetry of the umbilical artery (UA) and middle cerebral artery (MCA) revealed an abnormal pulsatility index, and the peak systolic velocity (PSV) of the MCA was indicative of fetal anemia.

Despite extensive counseling, the family opted for expectant management and declined any fetal intervention, including intrauterine transfusion. At 33 weeks’ gestation, intrauterine fetal demise was diagnosed, and labor was induced. Due to failed induction, a cesarean section was ultimately performed.

Case 2:

A 29-year-old gravida 2, para 1 woman, first-degree consanguineous, presented at 28 weeks of gestation for a routine third-trimester ultrasound at a private clinic. Severe fetal hydrops was identified for the first time during this examination. The pregnancy had been uneventful up to this point. Ultrasound revealed a female fetus with minimal fetal movement but a regular heartbeat. Polyhydramnios was noted, with the deepest pocket of amniotic fluid measuring 100 mm (Figure 3).

Ultrasounds revealing (A) fetal edema and pleural effusion. (B) Fetal hydrothorax. (C) Fetal hydrops with ascites and hydrothorax.

The ultrasound also revealed fetal hydrops with ascites, severe bilateral hydrothorax, generalized subcutaneous edema, and mild abdominal ascites (Figure 3).

Amniocentesis was proposed in to rule out chromosomal abnormalities such as trisomy 21. Nonetheless, after thorough counseling of the parents regarding the risks and benefits of the procedure, the parents declined invasive prenatal testing. The karyotype obtained postnatally confirmed the diagnosis of trisomy 21.

In addition, serological tests for parvovirus and cytomegalovirus (CMV) returned negative results. Due to the presence of generalized edema, the fetus was measured at the 95th percentile for growth (Figures 3 and 4). Morphological examination of the fetal anatomy revealed no structural malformations. Echocardiographic evaluation showed mild pericardial effusion, normal cardiac anatomy with situs solitus, but with mesocardia.

Neonate with endotracheal intubation and intratracheal instillation of surfactant.

This case demonstrates the challenges of managing fetal hydrops, especially when associated with polyhydramnios and hydrothorax, without definitive anemia or structural abnormalities. Despite the absence of an identifiable cause, the presence of severe hydrops significantly complicates the prenatal management and prognosis of the case.

Due to the presence of hydrothorax, further evaluation of the fetal cardiovascular system was performed. A flow-rate study revealed incontinence of the atrioventricular valves and pulmonary valves (Figure 2A). Doppler velocimetry of the umbilical artery (UA) and middle cerebral artery (MCA) was conducted, showing normal pulsatility index values (UA: PI 1.07; MCA: PI 2.27). The peak systolic velocity (PSV) of the MCA was measured at 45.51 cm/sec, slightly higher than the expected value of 41.93 cm/sec (multiples of the median [MoM] 1.09), which suggested the fetus was non-anemic.

Given the severity of the clinical picture, the respiratory distress prophylaxis with betamethasone was administered, and a cesarean section was planned. At birth, the newborn presented with severe hydrops and she was cyanotic, bradycardic (the heart rate was < 100 bpm), hypotonic and she has features of down syndrome.

In accordance with neonatal resuscitation protocols, the neonate underwent immediate endotracheal intubation and intratracheal instillation of surfactant, and was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) (Figure 4).

Bilateral thoracocentesis was performed and, after inserting chest tubes into the 4th intercostal space on both sides, 200 cc of citrine yellow fluid were drained from the pleural cavities and sent for analysis and culture. In addition, O RhD-negative red blood cells were transfused due to mild anemia (hemoglobin 10.3 g/dL).

The Apgar scores at 1, 5, and 10 minutes were 1, 5, and 8, respectively. Despite resuscitative efforts, the infant’s clinical condition remained extremely critical. Blood gas analysis at birth revealed severe metabolic and respiratory acidosis. Mechanical ventilation was initiated, first using the Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV) mode then switching to High-Frequency Oscillation Ventilation (HFOV) mode to achieve respiratory control.

A chest X-ray showed an enlarged cardio-mediastinal shadow, elevated diaphragm, severe soft tissue edema, hydrothorax and right lung pneumothorax (Figure 5). These findings, coupled with the infant’s critical metabolic state, further highlight the severity of the condition despite aggressive postnatal interventions.

Chest X-Ray after few hours from birth.

Due to suspected pulmonary hypertension caused by severe pulmonary hypoplasia, nitric oxide therapy was administered, with some initial success. A cardiologist was consulted for echocardiography. Neurological evaluation, including brain ultrasound, revealed significant ventriculomegaly brain development. Despite these efforts, the infant died. The karyotype confirmed the diagnosis of Down’s syndrome.

Case 3:

A 26-year-old gravida 4, para 1 woman with a history of one live birth, one abortion second month and one intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD) 6 months, was referred to the hospital at 39 weeks’ gestation from a peripheral outpatient clinic. The patient had not been following up regularly at the clinic. At the time of referral, her medical attendant noticed excessive weight gain and hypertension. Upon admission, her only complaint was swelling in her feet, which had developed insidiously over the past two weeks. She denied experiencing any premonitory symptoms such as shortness of breath or chest discomfort.

Her past medical and surgical history was unremarkable, aside from one previous normal delivery and a history of IUFD with edema. On examination, she weighed 87 kg (compared to a pre-pregnancy weight of 63 kg), with a blood pressure of 170/110 mm Hg. Her blood pressure had been recorded as normal at her last visit, which was 4 weeks earlier. She also had mild pallor, and significant pedal edema extending to the mid-thighs and abdominal wall edema were noted. Despite the edema, fetal heart sounds were detected, though palpation of fetal parts was difficult due to excessive abdominal wall swelling. The patient’s blood type was A Rh positive.

She was admitted for detailed investigations and close monitoring and was started on Labetalol for hypertension. Initial laboratory results showed hemoglobin (Hb) of 8.3 g/dL, a normal leukocyte count, but thrombocytopenia (platelets 77,000/mL). Prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), and blood sugar were within normal ranges. Kidney and liver function tests, as well as viral markers (HIV and others), were normal.

An ultrasound performed on the day of admission revealed fetal ascites, bilateral pleural effusion, and skin edema (Supplementary Figure S1). The fetal head circumference and femur length corresponded to 39 weeks of gestation, but the abdominal circumference measured 32 weeks, indicating fetal growth restriction. The heart, spine, kidneys, and amniotic fluid levels were all normal. The placenta was located fundo-posteriorly. The etiology of fetal hydrops was unclear, and Rh isoimmunization and ABO incompatibility were ruled out. Additionally, the TORCH test and maternal hemoglobin electrophoresis were normal.

Of note, chromosomal microarray analysis and whole exome sequencing (WES) were not performed in this case. This was due to limitation in resources in our setting and their associated cost.

Given the poor fetal prognosis and maternal risk, the parents were counseled, and they agreed to proceed with termination. Cervical ripening was initiated due to a poor Bishop’s score. After receiving priming doses for induction of labor, the patient spontaneously went into labor. However, during the late first stage of labor, she developed signs of revealed abruptio placenta, losing approximately 300 mL of fresh blood. Amniotomy and augmentation were performed, after which the patient progressed normally and delivered a male stillborn infant weighing 1.5 kg. The baby exhibited massive abdominal distention due to ascites (Supplementary Figure S2). The placenta was markedly enlarged and weighed more than expected (Supplementary Figure S3).

Discussion

Non-immune hydrops fetalis is estimated to affect 1 in 1700 to 3000 pregnancies but only 1 in 4000 live births due to the high incidence of intrauterine fetal demise and pregnancy termination [4]. However, such estimates may be inaccurately high due to selection bias toward high-risk pregnancies in the literature. Larger population birth registry studies from the United States (California) and Sweden estimate a much lower rate of hydrops fetalis of 1.6 to 2.5 per 10,000 live births [5,6].

Multiple factors can increase the risk of interstitial fluid accumulation relative to lymphatic drainage which characterizes hydrops fetalis [7,8]. Fetal total body water and extracellular fluid content are higher than in the neonate and infant. This additional free fluid may contribute to the risk of developing hydrops fetalis [9]. Additionally, the endothelial glycocalyx layer that lines blood and lymphatic vessels is more permeable when newly formed in the fetus, especially following inflammation or ischemia [7,9]. The immature fetal lymphatic system, which drains excess fluid from interstitial to intravascular compartments, may not keep pace with fluid accumulation or may be damaged or obstructed [10,11].

Etiologies of hydrops fetalis encompass failures in the above fluid regulation mechanisms. The four primary mechanisms that contribute to the development of hydrops fetalis are as follows: (1) increased capillary hydrostatic pressure, (2) reduced plasma oncotic pressure, (3) obstructed lymphatic flow, and (4) damaged peripheral capillaries [3,11,12]. (Figure 1).

Hydrops fetalis can result from various etiologies, including cardiovascular conditions (hypoplastic left heart, Ebstein anomaly, endocardial cushion defect, bradyarrhythmias, tachyarrhythmias) accounting for 21.4% of cases, idiopathic causes (18.2%), chromosomal abnormalities (12.5%), hematologic causes (10.1%), lymphatic dysplasia (7.5%), infections (6.8%), congenital thoracic abnormalities (5.3%), and twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (5.3%) [5,13–16].

Additionally, multiple disease processes may coexist within the same fetus including Trisomy 21, 13 and 18 along with congenital heart disease [17–20].

In our first case, the cause was traced to parvovirus B19 infection. The rate of transmission of maternal parvovirus B19 infection to the fetus ranges from 17% to 33%, with an increased risk of transmission between 9 and 20 weeks of gestation [19–21]. Nonethelss, most fetuses experience spontaneous resolution of the infection without adverse perinatal outcomes [22,23]. Only acute parvovirus B19 infection poses a risk for complications such as anemia and hydrops [24]. Unlike other viruses, such as rubella, parvovirus B19 is not teratogenic. However, fetal tissues, including hematopoietic cells in the liver, myocardium, endothelial cells, platelets, megakaryocytes, and fibroblasts, express the viral P antigen receptor, explaining the variety of fetal manifestations, particularly anemia and hydrops [25–27].

Although pregnancy does not seem to alter the course of parvovirus B19 infection in the mother, it can significantly affect the fetus. Furthermore, the likelihood of spontaneous abortion decreases with gestational age, with a rate of 13% before 20 weeks’ gestation and 0.5% after 20 weeks [27]. The risk of developing hydrops correlates with gestational age at the time of maternal infection. If infection occurs in the first trimester, the risk of hydrops ranges from less than 5% to 10%. Between 13 and 20 weeks, the risk decreases to 5% or less, and drops further to 1% or less after 20 weeks [27–30].

Hydrops is typically characterized by ultrasound findings of ascites, skin edema, pleural and pericardial effusions, and placental edema. Fetal anemia due to viral infection of erythrocyte precursors in the fetal bone marrow, coupled with the shorter lifespan of fetal erythrocytes, leads to severe anemia, hypoxia, and high-output heart failure. Severe neonatal anemia, particularly when hemoglobin levels drop below 6 g/dL, correlates with increased fluid accumulation at multiple sites, a hallmark of hydrops fetalis. Additionally, fetal viral myocarditis can cause heart failure and impair liver function due to hepatocyte damage and hemosiderin deposition [21,23,31]. Spontaneous resolution of fetal hydrops occurs in approximately 34% of cases by the time of delivery [28].

In our first case, Doppler ultrasound diagnosed severe anemia (< 6 g/dL), accompanied by accumulation of fluid in multiple body cavities.

The etiologies of anemia leading to hydrops fetalis include both inherited conditions, such as hemoglobinopathies, and acquired conditions, such as hemolysis, fetomaternal hemorrhage, or red cell aplasia. Among hemoglobinopathies, alpha thalassemia is the most common cause of NIHF. This autosomal recessive disorder is particularly prevalent in Southeast Asian populations, where it accounts for 28–55% of NIHF cases [11].

Chromosomal abnormalities, such as Turner Syndrome (45 X), Down syndrome (Trisomy 21), and Edward syndrome (Trisomy 18), account for 13% of cases of NIHF in a large systematic review [5]. These abnormalities are responsible for more than 50% of cases before 20 weeks of gestation, while congenital heart disease becomes the most common etiology after 24 weeks. According to Ota et al., chromosomal abnormalities are the most common cause of hydrops fetalis before 22 weeks of gestation, while cardiac structural anomalies become the leading cause after 22 weeks [4].

In our case series, we also encountered a case of Down syndrome (Case 2) with no associated congenital heart disease. Down syndrome is the most common autosomal chromosomal abnormality [9,32]. Nuchal translucency thickness, bowel and cardiac echogenic focuses, shortness of the extremities, increment in the iliac angle, and pyelectasis are the ultrasonographic signs of Down syndrome. Also, fetal hydrops, hepatomegaly, and cardiac arrhythmias may be seen [10].

One of the reasons for the development of fetal hydrops in Down syndrome is transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM). This condition presents in 10% of the babies born with Down syndrome [11]. In this case, pancytopenia, which is rarely seen, can be observed on transient abnormal myelopoiesis [12]. The etiological mechanisms for hydrops in cases of Down syndrome with TAM include heart failure due to anemia, hepatomegaly, and portal hypertension due to extramedullary hematopoesis and hypoalbuminemia [4,12]. In TAM, fetal hydrops has a prognostic value [12]. Another mechanism for the development of hydrops in babies with Down syndrome is disseminated lymphatic dysplasia [32]. If the etiology of hydrops is due to chromosomal abnormalities, the first sign is increased thickness of the nuchal translucency. Subsequently, depending on the severity of the dermal edema, pleural effusion and ascites can be seen [7].

NIHF can result from cardiac etiologies such as structural abnormalities, arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, cardiac tumors, or vascular abnormalities [3,14,15]. The prognosis for NIHF due to cardiac anomalies is poor, with a fetal and infant mortality rate of 92%, primarily due to structural heart defects and congestive heart failure [16].

Other significant thoracic causes of NIHF include congenital cystic adenomatoid malformations (CCAM), diaphragmatic hernia, extra-pulmonary sequestration, and chylothorax [13].

Mirror syndrome, first introduced by O’Driscoll in cases of fetal hydrops caused by Rh isoimmunization [1], involves maternal edema, excessive weight gain, and clinical signs resembling preeclampsia. However, unlike preeclampsia, Mirror syndrome is characterized by maternal hemodilution, rather than hemoconcentration [5]. John W. Ballantyne first described the association between maternal edema and hydrops fetalis in 1892, making this one of the oldest known maternal-fetal conditions [2].

In one case, a 26-year-old woman presented with severe maternal edema, hypertension, and an excessive weight gain of 24 kg during pregnancy. Ultrasound revealed fetal ascites, bilateral pleural effusion, and skin edema, consistent with fetal hydrops. Despite extensive investigations, including ruling out Rh isoimmunization, ABO incompatibility, and TORCH infections, the cause of the hydrops remained unclear. Mirror syndrome does not typically present with eclamptic seizures, which differentiates it from preeclampsia [5]. However, maternal complications can include worsening hypertension, pulmonary edema, and organ dysfunction if left untreated.

In our case, labor was induced, and despite the occurrence of placental abruption, the mother had a normal delivery of a stillborn male infant with significant ascites. Maternal prognosis is generally favorable following delivery, but fetal mortality remains high in cases of Mirror syndrome associated with severe hydrops [10]. The fetus in this case exhibited significant growth restriction, with an abdominal circumference corresponding to 32 weeks of gestation despite a head and femur length consistent with 39 weeks. The stillborn infant weighed 1.5 kg and had massive abdominal distention due to ascites, further emphasizing the severity of the hydrops. The rapid resolution of maternal symptoms after delivery or fetal death is well-documented, with symptoms typically improving within days of delivery [10,11]. The three reported cases illustrate distinct pathophysiological pathways leading to NIHF.

Conclusion

Non-immune hydrops fetalis (NIHF) is a significant contributor to abortions, stillbirths, and neonatal mortality. Comprehensive evaluation, including antenatal ultrasonography, fetal 2D echocardiography, and indirect Coombs testing, as well as a detailed postnatal clinical examination and investigations, should be conducted in all cases.

Postnatal management of NIHF involves several critical steps, including initial resuscitation to stabilize the neonate and identifying and treating the underlying cause. Most infants with hydrops fetalis require endotracheal intubation due to respiratory depression. Procedures such as thoracentesis, paracentesis, and occasionally cardiocentesis may be necessary to manage pleural effusion, ascites, and pericardial effusion. A blood transfusion may be required in cases of severe anemia. Despite aggressive treatment, survival rates can be as low as 10%, with survivors at risk for neurodevelopmental and cognitive deficits. The prognosis of hydrops fetalis primarily depends on the underlying cause, gestational age at diagnosis, timing of delivery, extent of fetal edema, and intrauterine interventions [22]. Early diagnosis and timely intervention can significantly improve perinatal outcomes, highlighting the importance of vigilant prenatal monitoring and targeted treatment strategies Neonates with thoracic causes and bronchopulmonary malformations generally have a better prognosis, whereas those with chromosomal abnormalities, structural defects, or genetic metabolic disorders often face a poorer prognosis [23].

Close collaboration among healthcare providers such as obstetricians, maternal-fetal medicine specialists, radiologists, neonatologists, nurses, and pharmacists, as well as social workers, is crucial in managing NIHF. The interprofessional healthcare team focuses on the well-being of both the mother and fetus, ensuring early detection of fetal hydrops symptoms through careful evaluation and monitoring. Pharmacists contribute by managing medications and educating patients, while nurses provide essential support and education to the woman and her family.
