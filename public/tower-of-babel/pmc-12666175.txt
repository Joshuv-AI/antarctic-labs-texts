Barriers and facilitators to the initiation and maintenance of exercise training for women with clinically severe obesity before, during and after participation in a weight loss study: a qualitative investigation
Authors: Daniel van den Hoek, Clint Miller, Steve Fraser, Bronwyn Sudholz, Steve Selig, John Dixon, Megan Teychenne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen with clinically severe obesity are at risk of poor physical and mental health. Lifestyle interventions must be developed to support exercise participation for this group. The factors influencing the involvement of women with clinically severe obesity in such programmes require investigation. This study aimed to identify barriers and facilitators to the initiation and maintenance of exercise training for women with severe obesity before, during and after participation in a weight loss study.Women with obesity were recruited for participation in a randomised controlled trial (n=60) investigating the effects of energy restriction (ER) with (energy restriction plus exercise (EXER): n=30) and without (ER: n=30) the addition of exercise. Following the 12-month intervention, n=11 (all from the EXER group) women agreed to participate and were interviewed to retrospectively explore their experiences of exercise engagement before, during and after the programme. Participants also provided their own suggestions for improving future exercise and weight loss programmes. Data were then transcribed verbatim, and reflective thematic analysis was used.Five themes related to intrapersonal and social components of the social ecological model were constructed and included: ‘weight’, ‘concerns about physical limitations’ and ‘exercise confidence and enjoyment’. Social themes were ‘work and family commitments’ and ‘accountability and support’.Women with clinically severe obesity most commonly reported intrapersonal and social influences of exercise participation. This study’s results may help inform the exercise programme development of practitioners and further intervention development at a population level.

Introduction

Adults with obesity are reported to have the lowest exercise participation rates of all body mass index (BMI) categories.1 Insufficient exercise reduces cardiometabolic, musculoskeletal and functional health and increases the risk of developing poorer psychological and social outcomes such as depression and stigmatisation.2 Australian guidelines indicate that exercise should be combined with energy restriction for lifestyle interventions as a frontline therapy for obesity.3 Dietary change is often seen as a more achievable outcome, as adults with obesity perceive a greater number of barriers to exercise than changing diet.4 5

Globally, 27% of adult females do not participate in sufficient volumes of physical activity for health benefits.6 In Australia, the USA and the UK, the prevalence of insufficient physical activity among women is even greater, with a peak of 42% in the USA.6 Further, the prevalence of overweight (BMI ≥25 kg/m2) among females in these countries is approximately 60%.7 Of greater concern is the proportion of women with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2), ranging from 30% in Australia and the UK to 42% in the USA.7

Although many factors influence the decision to exercise, most existing data are derived from adults with overweight (BMI ≥25 kg/m²) or obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²), rather than those with clinically severe obesity—defined as a BMI≥40 kg/m² or ≥35 kg/m² with obesity-related comorbidities.8 Adults, particularly women with clinically severe obesity, may experience unique challenges in initiating and maintaining regular exercise training routines.4

Of the small body of existing research investigating the influences on exercise in populations with clinically severe obesity, it has been suggested that adults with clinically severe obesity place little emphasis on exercise as a means of weight loss or addendum to dietary intervention.4 5 Women with obesity view that obtaining a healthy weight is likely to be unattainable through exercise alone or in addition to energy restriction. So consequently, surgical intervention is perceived to be more efficacious,9 which acts as a barrier to exercise participation. Participants may perceive surgical intervention as the only effective method for achieving substantial weight loss, leading to diminished confidence in the utility of lifestyle approaches such as exercise. This belief may reduce the motivation to initiate or maintain physical activity, particularly among individuals with clinically severe obesity.9 Adults with clinically severe obesity, in particular, women, report themselves to be ‘too fat (sic)’ to exercise,10 lack motivation and enjoyment11 or lack the skills for participation in exercise training.12 These factors also contribute to feelings of embarrassment and intimidation, especially when exercising with individuals of healthy weight.13

The social ecological model posits that behaviour change is influenced by multiple levels, including personal (eg, beliefs, motivation), social (eg, support, accountability) and physical environmental (eg, access to facilities) factors that influence exercise participation.14 This study applies the model to analyse how these multilevel influences shape the exercise behaviours of women with clinically severe obesity.15 This model has been widely used in studies among the general population; however, little research has investigated the various ecological influences on exercise among women with severe obesity using the social ecological framework. Given that the influences on exercise initiation are likely to differ from those of exercise maintenance,16 it is vital to investigate the influences on initiation and maintenance of exercise for women with clinically severe obesity to design targeted exercise interventions that address the specific barriers of this population. Therefore, this study aimed to identify barriers and facilitators to the initiation and maintenance of exercise training for women with clinically severe obesity before, during and after participation in a weight loss study. The study also explored participants’ generated suggestions for improving future exercise interventions for women with severe obesity.

Methods

Study design

We employed a qualitative descriptive approach,17 underpinned by a postpositivist paradigm, acknowledging the limitations of achieving complete objectivity in research and recognising that knowledge is shaped by individual perspectives and experiences.18 Reflexive thematic analysis was adopted to capture participants’ experiences in depth. Although reflexive thematic analysis is more commonly situated within the interpretivist or constructivist paradigms, our choice to frame it within a postpositivist context reflected the broader context of the randomised controlled trial (RCT). This stance allowed us to value systematic analytic procedures while acknowledging that participants’ accounts could not be interpreted in a purely objective manner, thereby balancing rigour with sensitivity to meaning-making. consistent with, and guided by, Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis.19 The barriers and facilitators to exercise participation and suggested strategies for future weight loss programmes for women with clinically severe obesity were explored through one-on-one, semistructured telephone interviews with former participants of the EMPOWER weight loss RCT.20 All participants in the RCT were invited to participate in the qualitative interviews. However, only participants from the exercise intervention (energy restriction plus exercise group, EXER) group volunteered.

Recruitment

This study used a sample of physically inactive women with clinically severe obesity (mean BMI 40.0 kg/m2) who had been recruited from an RCT examining the effects of energy restriction (ER) with (EXER; n=30) and without (ER; n=30) the addition of exercise, the complete protocol of which has been published previously.20 In brief, the inclusion criteria for the RCT were women aged 18–50 with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 and completing less than 150 min/week of moderate-intensity physical activity. Exclusion criteria were conditions that limited ability to participate in exercise, Type 1 diabetes mellitus, previous weight loss surgery, use of weight loss medications and weight loss greater than 5 kg in the preceding 3 months or use of medicines that significantly influenced weight gain.

Women enrolled in the RCT had consented to be contacted regarding further research. After completing the weight loss programme, all (60) participants were invited to participate. A researcher contacted all participants from the RCT via a mailed letter describing the research project. They were given a reply-paid slip, which they were asked to send back, indicating whether they would be interested in participating in the interviews. Interested participants were contacted and provided a plain language statement before giving informed written consent.

Interventions

Energy restriction with and without exercise trial

All participants followed a calorie-controlled diet with the close support of an accredited practising dietitian. The dietitian-supervised care involved fortnightly consultations and education regarding using the very-low-energy diet and any necessary modifications to enhance compliance and behaviour change. A significant reduction in daily calorie intake was achieved by using commercially available calorie-controlled meal replacements (shakes/bars/soups; Optifast, Nestlé Health Science, Australia) instead of regular whole food. Each participant commenced the dietary intervention following the protocol below (table 1). However, the diet followed by each participant was tailored depending on the participant’s needs using standard clinical decision-making approaches to achieve an energy intake similar to the different phases of the original protocol.

Exercise training

The EXER group followed the same energy-restricted diet as the energy restriction alone group (table 1)with the support of an accredited practising dietitian. The exercise intervention consisted of a 12-month, supervised exercise programme of individual exercise training incorporating aerobic and resistance exercise. The supervised exercise training sessions were supplemented with a home-based exercise training programme. Each session consisted of approximately 20–30 min of aerobic (treadmill walking/running, stationary cycling or rowing ergometer) and 30–45 min of resistance training. The resistance exercise involved 6–10 different, predominantly multijoint exercises for the upper and lower extremities. Participants progressively increased the volume and frequency of home-based exercise during the period of the study so that all participants were to achieve a total weekly duration of 200–300 min per week at a moderate to vigorous intensity (12–15 on the Borg ratings of perceived exertion scale).

Procedures

We conducted semistructured interviews to explore the barriers and facilitators to initiating and sustaining exercise engagement informed by the social ecological model.14 The interviews lasted between 13 and 25 min. The full interview schedule is included in online supplemental appendix A. Example questions included: “Thinking back to before you entered the EMPOWER study, how much exercise were you doing? (And if something, who was it with?)”, “During the study, what kinds of things (or experiences) influenced your physical activity? These may be related to the study or not” and “What is the thing (s) that would most help you to be more active/exercise more now?”. CM and BS conducted the interviews. CM is an accredited exercise physiologist and was involved in testing and training of some participants. CM did not conduct testing or training in participants he interviewed for this study. BS was an independent psychologist with experience in qualitative interviewing. All interviews were conducted after participants had completed the 12-month intervention. Participants were asked to retrospectively reflect on their experiences of exercise engagement during the periods before, during and after the intervention. In brief, the following areas were discussed with participants during interviews: (1) Barriers and facilitators for physical activity participation relating to intrapersonal, social and environmental factors relating to periods preintervention, intraintervention and postintervention; (2) Key influences on continued or altered exercise participation following engagement in the study and (3) Suggestions for improvement of the intervention study to improve ongoing engagement in exercise participation. Open-ended questions were developed by the research team based on the ecological framework; participants were also provided with some prompt examples to help them expand on their responses. Interviews were conducted via telephone and audio recorded (with participant permission). Data were then transcribed verbatim. Following transcription and deidentification, audio recordings were deleted to protect participant anonymity. Member checking was not conducted.

Analysis

Reflexive thematic analyses were performed according to the methods reported by Braun and Clarke.19 The lead author (DJvdH) first familiarised themself with the data by listening to recorded interviews and reading and re-reading transcripts to understand the breadth and depth of response to questions (phase 1; familiarisation). All data were read at least twice before coding, allowing an uninterrupted means of searching for patterns and meanings of participant responses. During this phase, patterns and areas of interest for further exploration were noted by the researchers (DJvdH and MT). Data were then uploaded to the qualitative data analysis programme Nvivo (NVivo qualitative data analysis Software; QSR International, V.10, 2012.) Following this phase, coding was conducted inductively, allowing themes to be constructed from the data and to identify key features of participant response relative to the research question (phase 2; coding). The codes generated in this analysis phase formed a subset of the final (broad) themes. The NVivo programme was used to organise data and simplify the creation of categories and subcategories and their associated quotes. Codes were then reorganised and combined to focus on broader themes (phase 3: constructing themes). To aid this process, researchers formatted codes into tables to visually represent the links between codes and themes. Once the main themes were identified, a process of cross-coding was undertaken as a form of reflexive dialogue between researchers, intended to enhance reflexivity and enrich theme development rather than to establish coding reliability.19 All transcripts were coded by one author (DJvdH), and a random subset of four transcripts was independently cross-coded by another author (MT) (phase 4: developing and reviewing themes and phase 5: refining, defining and naming themes). The subsequent discussions focused on deepening interpretation and ensuring transparency of analytic decisions. This approach is consistent with Braun and Clarke’s19 guidance that reflexive thematic analysis does not prescribe intercoder reliability checks, as might be used in codebook approaches. Any differences in interpretation were explored collaboratively, with final decisions informed by reflexive discussion. Results are reported using deidentified participant quotes (phase 6: writing up).

The authors acknowledge that researcher reflexivity can influence results due to the researcher’s beliefs and experience. Researcher reflexivity involved ongoing consideration of how the researchers’ values, assumptions and positionality shaped data collection, analysis and interpretation. Coauthors, BS and CM, conducted the interviews. BS is a female psychologist and researcher with previous experience in qualitative research, but no prior relationship with the participants. CM is a male accredited exercise physiologist who was involved in delivering the exercise intervention for participants of the related trial. CM was not involved in the intervention delivery for participants he interviewed for this study. Their familiarity may have encouraged participants to share their experiences more openly. At the same time, this familiarity may have influenced what participants chose to disclose. This relational dynamic was considered throughout the reflexive process. Researchers who analysed and coded data (MT; behavioural epidemiologist, female and DV; accredited exercise physiologist, male) brought additional knowledge and interpretation to the data. DV was also involved in the recruitment and training of the intervention study participants. This provided him with greater insight into the programme and participants, which further assisted in interpreting responses. No other authors were involved in data collection (interviews) or analysis but were allowed to review the themes identified.

Results

11 women from the original RCT were interviewed for this study (mean (range), age: 34 years (22–42), body weight at RCT commencement 115.8 kg (92.4–148.4), representing approximately one-fifth of all participants and one-quarter of the women who completed the 12-month trial. Participants were interviewed from 3 to 30 months following their participation in the trial. All participants were from the EXER group. There were no differences in age (p=0.068) or bodyweight (p=0.89) at baseline between participants and non-participants of this study from the original RCT cohort. The following findings are based on retrospective accounts provided by participants after completing the 12-month intervention. Five key themes were identified, and results are presented consistent with the social ecological framework (figure 1). However, only social and intra-personal factors were constructed as key themes. There were no common themes for physical environmental influences of exercise participation.

Themes related to the social ecological model constructed from qualitative interviews.

Intrapersonal influences of exercise

The following themes reflect retrospective accounts provided by participants at the conclusion of the 12-month programme.

Weight

The most commonly reported theme related to exercise participation for women in the RCT was weight. Almost all women reported weight, weight loss or weight gain as having an influence on their exercise participation at some point during the study.

Prior to intervention participation, about one-third of women from the EMPOWER study reported that their current weight was a barrier to exercise.

During and following intervention participation, weight loss was reported as a positive influence on exercise participation by more than half of participants.

Yeah, once you start seeing that, it shifted, and you go “Oh, I should do more” and so on. (Participant 59)

So I lost lots of weight and I started running… (Participant 15)

Definitely weight loss, because I increased my stamina (Participant 22)

The numbers of it and the, sort of, the speed of the change made it really noticeable and made it a lot more motivating to get going. (Participant 57)

the weight loss part of it, we found that we were able to be more creative than we previously were as well, we were better in the gym but we were also better on the home side of it as well. (Participant 38)

Weight regain following the completion of the intervention study was reported to have hindered participation in exercise. This was the case for almost half of the women interviewed.

Immediately after the study, it [my weight] stayed the same for probably 6 months and then I started to regain weight… which sort of prevented me then from going back to the gym. (Participant 5)

I do feel like a terrible letdown because I have put on weight since the study. (Participant 57)

Concerns about physical limitations

While participants with conditions that would prevent safe participation in exercise were excluded from participation in the RCT, three-quarters of interview respondents still described experiencing day-to-day physical limitations (eg, joint pain, low fitness, or difficulty with mobility) as barriers to engaging in the programme. These limitations did not meet the threshold for exclusion but influenced how participants perceived and approached exercise.

Before the study

I think… I’ve had a back injury through pregnancy…through delivery that I think made me really cautious of taking on anything, even walking. (Participant 57)

You just didn’t have the energy to do it. (Participant 59)

I was aware that my knees weren’t great… I didn’t want to do anything that would aggravate it. (Participant 57)

During the study

I am asthmatic so with this sickness, it was generally cold but we would just kind of factor in. Then when I get a cold I generally find my asthma plays up a lot more. (Participant 38)

I had an accident last year, broke the ankle so I haven’t been able to really do anything. (Participant 59)

…it was difficult also painful. So I pretty much stopped. (Participant 15)

After the study

I have actually put a little weight back on… so exercising is difficult again. (Participant 14)

After the program ended, I started to feel the pain more again… I couldn’t get up from the floor easily. (Participant 57)

Exercise confidence and enjoyment

Confidence to exercise was frequently reported as an influence of women’s exercise participation. Prior to intervention, a few women reported a lack of confidence as having a negative effect on exercise participation.

…I should start doing some light exercises like… But I knew that I wouldn't do that, can't do it by myself. (Participant 14)

During and following intervention, almost half of the women interviewed reported that greater confidence acted as a positive influence on exercise participation. This improved confidence related to body image and exercise capability.

I certainly hadn’t done any of that beforehand, and, let alone have the confidence to be able to walk into the gyms or certainly walk into some of the shops to buy some of the clothing. Even the styles of clothing I'm wearing now, I definitely wouldn’t have worn beforehand. (Participant 60)

Self-efficacy appeared to be enhanced by the increased knowledge gained from the exercise intervention. As such, about half of women suggested this as a facilitator for exercise maintenance during and following the weight loss intervention.

What I realised was that when it was time to lose weight again, I felt much better equipped to do that properly. (Participant 57)

Exercise self-efficacy and, in turn, enjoyment of exercise appeared to be enhanced by both confidence in exercise and knowledge of exercise. Enjoyment of exercise was reported by almost all women as having a favourable influence on exercise at some point during the intervention. About half described enjoyment as a facilitator, an equal number described lack of enjoyment as a barrier to being active.

Yes, I enjoyed the exercise so when you discover something you loved to do. You know you want to keep doing it…I enjoyed the gym sessions and I discovered that I really enjoyed running… I loved the challenge of pushing myself. (Participant 15)

I found when I was there, I started doing rowing so that sort of opened up an interest for me which I do as often as I can now. (Participant 5) … It wasn't something that I enjoyed. I knew I should do more but I just didn’t feel motivated for it and it wasn’t a priority. (Participant 26)

I know how good it makes me feel after I have exercised especially if I have a really bad night, the night before at work or something like that. It’s just about the stress relief. (Participant 58)

Social influences of exercise

Accountability and support

Most women reported that having appropriate support and being accountable to someone or something was substantial, positive influences on exercise engagement. Responsibilities related to pet ownership, being accountable to others (exercise physiologist, dietitian or family members) and provision of social support from friends, family and research staff were all highlighted as key factors.

If you’ve committed to meeting somebody at a certain time, then you’re more likely to go do it than if you're just relying on your own self-motivation. (Participant 22)

… once I came into the study… my husband and my mother particularly were a hundred per cent on board with helping me be involved as I possibly could be. So with child-minding and, you know, all sorts of things to make it easier for me to get to the sessions and everything. (Participant 57)

A few participants also reported that the knowledge of research staff and the expert advice provided throughout their time in the study acted as a positive influence to exercise maintenance during and following their enrolment.

What I realised was that when it was time to lose weight again, I felt much better equipped to do that properly. So since then, since recognising that I've been, […], going to the gym again, doing all those sorts of programs that Trainer X and Y set up for me. […] I feel like I'm in actually a really good position to take it on. That feeling is successful. (Participant 57)

Work and family commitments

Work and family commitments were reported as a barrier to exercise participation for most women. At times, the need to care for others, work schedules and other commitments were reported to be priorities over women’s exercise participation. More than half of the women acknowledged that work was an important factor due to the financial support that is provided to their families. A few reported commitments related to their children played a role in reducing their ability to participate in regular exercise.

I’m actually working almost full time. And my husband also works full time. So it’s kind of challenging to get out, so I probably haven’t. Not probably, I haven’t maintained anywhere near, what I wanted to when I was at the end of the study and that’s been challenging. (Participant 15)

Recommendations for future programmes

A handful of women reported that greater freedom of choice in exercise activities would enhance their engagement in exercise for future interventions.

Probably giving them different styles of exercises, and the reasoning behind some of the exercises rather than, “just do this”. (Participant 60)

Beyond providing a greater freedom in the choice of exercise, there were numerous suggestions for future intervention modifications. These were not consistent among participants. A sample of statements from participants that the authors considered of interest is included below.

One participant suggested that a greater amount of feedback on progress should be included.

I would say just pretty much everything like whether they are doing a good job yet, commentary and be specific in sort of what they’re doing and everything, and if it’s something bad even, comment on it to just sort of encourage them to keep pushing at it. (Participant 59)

Another suggested modifying the timing for transition phases in exercise and dietary interventions would be useful.

I think that probably… at this time when you transition off the intensive program… of the Optifast, is also the time when you probably transition to having less contact with the trainers…. But I think… I wonder if there’s an argument in there somewhere to have like those things not coincide so directly… (Participant 57)

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the specific barriers and facilitators to the initiation and maintenance of exercise training for women with clinically severe obesity before, during and after participation in a weight loss study. Five themes related to intrapersonal and social components of the social ecological model were identified. Intrapersonal themes included: ‘weight’, ‘concerns about physical limitations’ and ‘exercise confidence and enjoyment’. Social themes were ‘accountability and support’ and ‘work and family commitments’. Social themes and influences did not differ significantly for the initiation or maintenance of exercise.

Intrapersonal influences on exercise

Weight

Participants described weight as a barrier to exercise both before and after the intervention, though the nature of this barrier varied across time points. Prior to the programme, current weight was commonly cited as limiting physical capacity and confidence to engage in exercise, consistent with previous literature on severe obesity, which reported that excess weight has a strong influence on physical capacity and willingness to exercise.21 During the intervention, weight loss was experienced as a turning point, shifting perceptions from limitation to empowerment and enabling greater physical activity participation. Towards the end of the programme, some participants experienced weight regain, which was associated with reduced motivation and diminished engagement in physical activity. These findings illustrate the evolving influence of weight across the intervention timeline and highlight the need for exercise-based interventions to address both physiological changes and the psychological responses they may elicit.

During intervention participation, women reported weight loss as facilitating exercise participation, which is consistent with previous literature showing that bariatric surgery candidates perceive weight loss as the main factor in exercise engagement.22 Weight loss may be a facilitator of exercise for a number of reasons, including improvements in self-esteem and body confidence associated with weight loss.23 In the current study, confidence (related to body image and exercise skill) was reported as a positive influence of exercise during periods of weight loss, which is consistent with previous studies among populations with obesity, including bariatric surgical patients,24 young adults and11 mothers.25

The experience of weight regain, not current weight at study end, was reported to contribute to reduced exercise participation, while participants who maintained or continued to lose weight reported ongoing exercise participation. Weight regain has previously been identified as a barrier to exercise among young adults and middle-aged women with obesity, resulting in a reduction in weekly exercise time.26 Weight gain is a normal, unavoidable, physiological response to weight loss,27 yet its occurrence can negatively influence motivation, self-efficacy and adherence to physical activity.28 In this study, several participants described weight regain as a barrier to continued exercise, often linked to perceived loss of progress or diminished confidence. These findings suggest that long-term interventions may require targeted supports to help individuals navigate weight fluctuations and sustain behavioural commitment to physical activity. Drawing on recent qualitative research,29 strategies such as participant-led planning, motivational boosters and development of self-regulation skills may help individuals manage momentary lapses and maintain engagement. Additionally, reinforcement of non-weight-related benefits of exercise (eg, improved mood, function and quality of life) may help to mitigate against the demotivating effects of weight regain and promote ongoing physical activity participation.

Concerns about physical limitations to exercise participation

Concerns about physical limitations were identified as a key theme for exercise participation for women with clinically severe obesity. Participants described concerns about pain and injury as barriers to exercise. These limitations were experienced and influenced participation. It is well accepted that adults with obesity are more likely to report symptoms of pain compared with those with a healthy weight.30 Low back, knee and hip pain are more common in adults with obesity and are exacerbated by increasing degrees of obesity.30 Further, difficulty with performing daily activities and the probability of falls, trips and stumbles increase with obesity, as does the risk of resultant injuries, and this may be related to altered gait patterns seen in adults with obesity.31 The increased risk of injury, compounded with increased pain from osteoarthritis and poor gait mechanics, furthers the reluctance of adults with obesity to participate in exercise.32

Participants’ reflections on the exercises included in the intervention highlighted the importance of both structure and variety. Several participants found previously unfamiliar forms of activity, such as resistance training or water-based sessions, to be unexpectedly enjoyable and empowering. In contrast, others described discovering a preference for more challenging or structured exercise routines. These findings suggest that the RCT’s diverse and progressive programming facilitated not only physical engagement but also shifts in exercise identity.

Importantly, participants’ experiences varied across timepoints. Before the study, many were inactive due to concerns about physical limitations, low confidence or lack of structure. During the intervention, increased support, accountability and exposure to new activities helped shift these perceptions. However, postintervention, some participants reported difficulty maintaining motivation without external support or due to injury and life demands. This highlights the importance of sustained support and flexible programming to promote long-term engagement, particularly in populations managing clinically severe obesity. Therefore, intervention design should recognise these barriers and prescribe exercise accordingly. The use of low-impact exercises is likely to reduce pain and ambivalence in exercise.32 This should also enhance exercise enjoyment and increase willingness for exercise participation.

Women reported feeling more energetic and experiencing less pain following weight loss. This is supported by previous literature, which reported reductions in pain, increased energy and motivation following weight loss for populations with obesity.22 32 These findings highlight a need for practitioners to address concerns about physical limitations as well as the psychological factors related to these concerns to engage women with clinically severe obesity in exercise effectively.

Exercise self-efficacy

In this study, weight loss was perceived to enhance energy and confidence to exercise and was reported to facilitate exercise participation, which is consistent with previous research among bariatric surgery candidates and general populations with obesity.22 32 Contrary to these findings, previous research has suggested that the perception that reaching a healthy-weight BMI is unlikely, which can, in turn, reduce self-efficacy to exercise, is a barrier to exercise participation in populations with severe obesity.24 Although participants in that study had undergone bariatric surgery rather than lifestyle intervention with exercise, this may have influenced participant outcome expectations with surgical patients, perhaps anticipating greater weight loss than is realistic or achievable. In that study, confidence in exercise and participation increased following weight loss,24 which is similar to women from the current study, who, following weight loss, reported increased confidence to exercise, greater perceived benefits of exercise and reduced fear of injury as facilitators to exercise participation.

Self-efficacy is an important determinant of exercise participation in the general population.33 However, a recent review with meta-analysis explored the behaviour change techniques that increase self-efficacy and physical activity among adults with obesity34 and determined that an increase in physical activity self-efficacy was not necessarily required to increase physical activity participation for adults with obesity. Thus, other mechanisms may be more important to increase exercise participation of adults with clinically severe obesity compared with non-obese populations, and further research is required to determine such factors.

Social influences on exercise participation

Commitments, accountability and support

Family and work commitments were frequently reported by women from the EMPOWER study to be barriers to exercise participation, which is consistent with previous literature among normal weight, overweight and obese populations.35 36 In our study, the provision of social support from friends, family and domestic partners was reported to facilitate engagement in exercise and could enhance feelings of accountability. This is congruent with previously reported social influences of exercise in populations with obesity.

Supervision of programmes and accountability to professionals have been identified as a positive influence in exercise programmes among adults with T2DM37 and for women with overweight and obesity.38 Other research35 identified the need to use familial relationships to support weight control. Medalie and Cole-Kelly39 described the family as a group of complex configurations, including census, biological, household and functional family connections. This is important as family, and the broader interpretations thereof, act as a major influence for change in both the participant and other members of the family unit.40 Such inclusion has previously been reported to enhance participants’ feelings of acceptance and support.5 Given these findings, the ability of exercise professionals to instil feelings of commitment, support and accountability is critical for the ongoing exercise participation of women with clinically severe obesity. Practitioners must also ensure that participants are encouraged to seek support and encouragement from their family units.

Recommendations for future programmes

There was only one consistent theme (choice of exercise) reported by participants in relation to recommendations for future programmes. This does not suggest that the programme has limited ability to be improved. Rather, the diversity of response may point to the breadth of need in relation to delivering an exercise and dietary intervention for women with clinically severe obesity.

The findings of this study align closely with several established behaviour change theories. For example, the importance of enjoyment and social connection reflects intrinsic motivation and relatedness as central tenets of self-determination theory.41 Similarly, the themes related to weight loss and health benefits as motivators parallel perceived benefits in the Health Belief Model.42 Barriers such as pain, embarrassment and competing demands reflect perceived barriers and self-efficacy constructs within both the health belief model and social cognitive theory.43 Notably, participants’ ability to adapt and maintain exercise postintervention was often linked to feelings of competence and autonomy—both core components of sustained behaviour change in self-determination theory. Motivation and intention to exercise are improved through autonomy and represent a recognition of the importance of a behaviour.44 Participants may not have the expertise to select and prescribe exercise programmes appropriately. As such, practitioners must work collaboratively with their participants to design programmes that aid in achieving desired outcomes (weight loss or otherwise), goals and behaviours. For example, a recent study identified that both practitioner and individual weight loss goals are inappropriately high compared with what is required for improved health.45 If weight loss goals, exercise selection or other factors are misaligned, it is likely that weight regain and cessation or reduction in exercise will occur. This is congruent with weight gain reported as a barrier to continued exercise participation within this study. A qualitative study by Byrne et al46 reported that those who do not achieve the weight loss and other desired outcomes they had hoped for may consider any weight loss to be inadequate or unsatisfactory. Reinforcement of beliefs such as this is likely to result in weight regain and abandonment of positive behaviour change.47 In our study, participants who experienced weight regain described reduced motivation and a sense of lost progress, despite having previously engaged successfully in structured exercise. Unlike weight change, improvements in aerobic fitness, strength or metabolic health (such as walking faster, climbing stairs with less breathlessness, lifting heavier loads or experiencing less fatigue) are often harder for patients to recognise and monitor, even though these outcomes represent meaningful health gains. Body weight is a highly salient and frequently measured indicator, both on the scales and in the mirror. This visibility makes it a powerful psychological cue, often reinforcing feelings of success or failure on a daily basis.28 These findings underscore the importance of shifting the focus of interventions from weight-centric outcomes to broader, health-focused goals. Educating participants about the chronic, relapsing nature of obesity and the multifaceted benefits of physical activity, including improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness, insulin sensitivity, functional capacity and mental well-being may help buffer against the demotivating effects of weight fluctuations and support more sustainable engagement with exercise. Given that many of these benefits of exercise are not immediately perceptible to patients, clinicians should consider strategies to make these outcomes more visible. Functional assessments, biomarker tracking and patient-reported outcomes may help to translate invisible progress into tangible feedback over time but need to be collected in routine practice.

It is worth noting that the intervention, including exercise sessions and dietary support, was provided free of charge, and participants received discounted very low-calorie diet products for the duration of their participation. These provisions may have reduced financial barriers commonly associated with exercise participation and influenced the nature of reported facilitators. As cost is a well-documented barrier to physical activity in adults with obesity,48 future interventions should consider how funding models may affect accessibility to services.

Physical environmental influences of exercise

Although the themes in this study related to intrapersonal and social constructs of the social-ecological model, no themes were constructed based on the physical-environmental constructs. This is in contrast to that of previous research on obesity, which reported that women with severe obesity prefer to exercise indoors due to feelings of safety and comfort.49 The lack of themes related to physical environments could, in part, be explained by using indoor training facilities, supervised exercise sessions and providing comfortable exercise facilities for the intervention study.

Strengths and limitations

As this was an extension of a partially completed study, it was impossible to incorporate interviews for all participants during their enrolment. Future studies may benefit from longitudinal qualitative designs that capture evolving perspectives in real time.

Further, despite interviews conducted by researchers not involved in treatment delivery or data collection, there remains a potential for participants to give socially desirable responses during the interview. A key limitation of this study is that all interview participants were from the exercise intervention (EXER) group. As such, the findings primarily reflect the perspectives of individuals who engaged in the intervention and may have had more positive experiences with exercise. This may have introduced a self-selection bias, potentially skewing interpretations towards those who found the programme acceptable or beneficial. As no participants from the control group contributed, we are unable to compare perspectives across trial arms. Therefore, the insights presented here should be interpreted as reflective of individuals who chose to participate in structured exercise and not necessarily generalisable to those who declined or disengaged.

This study provides valuable insights into the experiences of women with clinically severe obesity who participated in a structured exercise intervention. A strength of the study is the depth of participant accounts, supported by the use of reflexive thematic analysis. While steps were taken to enhance trustworthiness—such as researcher reflexivity, careful documentation of the analytic process and inclusion of illustrative quotes—these strategies were applied pragmatically to enhance trustworthiness within the scope of available resources, but not in an exhaustive or formalised manner.

Conclusions

The results demonstrated that intrapersonal (‘weight’, ‘concerns about physical limitations’ and ‘exercise self-efficacy’) and social (‘social commitments’ and ‘accountability and support’) influences of physical activity and exercise participation are most commonly reported for women with clinically severe obesity. Although confirmation of these findings is required, the results of this study may help inform the development of exercise programmes by practitioners and further the development of interventions at a population level. Future programmes to promote engagement and maintenance of exercise in severely obese women should target the intrapersonal and social constructs of the social ecological model to improve initiation and maintenance of exercise behaviours for women with clinically severe obesity. Health-focused programmes, enhancing self-efficacy and providing substantial social support and accountability are most likely to deliver successful outcomes.
