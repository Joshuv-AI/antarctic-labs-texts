Psychological intervention for disputing irrational beliefs associated with work stress amongst tourism educators and mathematicians with hypertension
Authors: Joy Nneka Uchenye Ejikeme, John Nwabueze Igabari, Kelechi Ruth Ede, Uzoamaka Patricia Agu, Ngozi Petronilla Omeje, Lovelyn Nwakaego Obayi, Nkechi Theresa Egenti, Ngozi Ngwanma Awoke, Nneka Anthonia Obumse, Ndubunma Jane Chinweike, John Chinedu Nnaemeka, Moses Onyemaechi Ede, Eucharia Ngozi Ezeh, Joy Obiageli Oneli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Employees with hypertension face challenging job pressures that have a negative impact on their health, leading to ineffective and unsatisfactory work. Additionally, this can affect an organization’s overall level of productivity. This study aimed to examine the effects of rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT) on stress among lecturers in tourism and hospitality management, educational scientists, and home economics and hospitality from state and federal universities who have hypertension, with a focus on the health and well-being of individual workers in specific university faculties.Methods:A randomized controlled study was conducted, involving 93 staff members from private, state, and federal universities. Two dependent measures were assessed at Times 1, 2, and 3. A repeated measures statistical analysis was used to evaluate the participants’ responses.Results:Participants’ stress levels and illogical beliefs significantly decreased as a result of the REBT intervention. Before and after the REBT session, the participants’ reactions to stress and irrational beliefs differed significantly. The follow-up study’s findings confirm the long-lasting benefits of REBT and show the intervention’s long-term influence.Conclusion:The REBT intervention helps educators cultivate a positive mindset, decrease stress, and eliminate irrational beliefs.

1. Introduction

When blood vessel pressure reaches 140/90 mm Hg or above, it is classified as hypertension (high blood pressure).[1] If left untreated, it can become a serious issue despite its common occurrence. The majority (two-thirds) of the 1.28 billion individuals aged 30 to 79 with hypertension worldwide are located in low- and middle-income countries. Hypertension is most prevalent in the World Health Organization (WHO) African Region (27%) and least prevalent in the WHO Region of the Americas (18%). According to WHO, the number of adults with hypertension increased from 594 million in 1975 to 1.13 billion in 2015.[1] This increase was mainly observed in low- and middle-income countries, where the condition is often poorly managed.[2] The primary reason for this rise is the increase in risk factors for hypertension in these populations. Hypertension is one of the leading causes of premature death globally. A 33% reduction in hypertension between 2010 and 2030 is one of the global goals for noncommunicable diseases. Despite the growing amount of research on hypertension in Nigeria, most studies only focus on descriptive data, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of how to support stressed lecturers.[3]

The body releases a multitude of hormones when under stress.[3] These hormones cause blood vessels to constrict and the heart to beat faster, temporarily raising blood pressure. According to Anyaegbunam et al, inappropriate stress responses can raise blood pressure and the risk of a heart attack in 2 ways: by releasing stress hormones into the bloodstream and by accelerating the heartbeat.[3] Both recurrent increases in blood pressure and the nervous system’s increased production of vasoconstricting hormones are ways in which stress can lead to hypertension.[4] Factors such as white coat hypertension, occupational stress, race, social environment, and emotional distress can influence blood pressure through stress. Additionally, the impact on blood pressure is heightened when one risk factor is combined with other stressors. Overall, research suggests that stress can influence the development of hypertension but does not directly cause it.[4] According to Balachandran et al, 83.7% of Indian university instructors reported moderate to high levels of stress.[5] A Canadian study found that 22% of participants experienced more physical health symptoms and 13% had psychological distress.[6] A study conducted in the United Kingdom revealed equally high levels of stress.[7] According to Anyaegbunam et al, education scientists in Nigeria are among those who experience significant levels of stress,[3] with a reported prevalence of 62%.[8] Nwimo and Onwunaka[9] also noted higher levels of stress among teaching staff.

Several factors have been identified as causing uncontrollable stress for biological science lecturers at tertiary institutions, including heavy workloads, research pressures, administrative tasks, exam preparation and grading, tight deadlines, funding challenges, adapting to evolving educational technology, the struggle to balance teaching and conducting research, and other responsibilities outside of teaching.[10–12] For instance, Mathematics teachers might thus not have enough time to plan, create, or even just evaluate math curricula at school.[13] Many of the challenges faced by mathematics teachers have only gotten worse in recent years.[14] University lecturers face numerous risk factors for stress and hypertension.[5] According to Balachandran et al, university instructors may be at a significant risk of developing hypertension due to a combination of age and long-term work-related pressures.[5] Workplace health initiatives and stress management interventions are crucial to preventing stress and hypertension in this occupational group. Stress increases the risk of lifestyle choices that contribute to hypertension, such as smoking, excessive drinking, prolonged sitting, and poor diet.[15–17] Research indicates that combining any of these risk factors with other stressors can exacerbate hypertension.[4]

High standards, goals, and demanding performance evaluations have been identified as primary stressors among service industry workers.[18] Similarly, universities and other higher education institutions face a similar situation where instructors are expected to fulfill specific duties related to teaching, research, coursework, and administration. It is worrisome that the health conditions of tourism lecturers and educational scientists are experiencing such pathetic health problems. Currently, researchers have observed that a significant number of individuals complain when interviewed. Stress is often connected to emotions like fear and anxiety. The emotional aspect of stress pertains to psychological strains, which can also manifest behaviorally. Stress can lead to social withdrawal and other behavioral symptoms. Therefore, the researchers are testing the impact of rational emotive behavior intervention on reducing stress.

Rational emotive behavior intervention, created by Albert Ellis in the 1950s, explains how irrational and rational beliefs are developed, nurtured, and displayed in individuals. Irrational and rational beliefs are influenced by social factors, emotional reactions, and cognitive processes. Irrational beliefs stem from cognitive distortions, emotional biases, and harmful thought patterns, while rational beliefs are based on logical reasoning, evidence, and a realistic assessment of situations. Irrational beliefs are rigid, extreme, and illogical, while rational beliefs are flexible, non-extreme, and logical according to rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT). Rational and irrational beliefs can be categorized into 4 groups, with 3 secondary beliefs combining to form rational beliefs with preferences as the core belief.

If irrational and rational beliefs are schemas, they are complex systems that reflect an individual’s behavioral responses to reality and their perceptions of it. Emotions stem from rational and irrational beliefs, determining if a situation is motivationally relevant and incongruent. The ABC philosophy, which is the foundation of REBT’s theoretical and therapeutic approach, helps understand the mechanisms behind emotional responses and guides treatment.

Irrational beliefs lead to higher levels of stress.[19] According to Amutio and Smith,[20] irrational beliefs are a major long-term source and factor that sustains the stress response. Therefore, a key element of stress management and other psychological therapies intended to reduce stress-related symptoms is the early assessment and diagnosis of stress-related beliefs. It is possible to prevent irrational beliefs through the training provided by the instructors (Tunç & Günay 2020). Empirical-based findings have indicated that the proper application of REBT techniques and its underlying principles is effective in reducing the causes and effects of stress symptoms.[21–23] Similar studies suggest that the objective of the REBT program is to teach clients to be more optimistic and rational in order to overcome any irrational and erroneous emotions that could trigger stress-related symptoms.[22–24] Therefore, successful REBT practice helps individuals exhibit a positive change in attitude, thinking, and improvement in self-limiting beliefs.

1.1. Research problems

Academics worldwide face significant ongoing occupational stress,[25] impacting the well-being of tourism and hospitality management lecturers, educational scientists, and home economics and hospitality lecturers. Without effective coping strategies, their lives are at risk. Previous research has shown that lecturers’ psychological well-being is relatively poor.[25] In a study by Gillespie et al, over 60% of participants reported negative psychological impacts of stress, such as anxiety, depression, burnout, anger, irritability, and helplessness.[26] Currently, Nigerian university lecturers are experiencing similar challenges.[27] Unfortunately, certain aspects of stress have been overlooked, including the effects of organizational well-being, employee interactions and health, and administrative behavior.[28] To ensure the educational system operates effectively and meets its established goals and objectives, these gaps must be addressed.[26] Improving working conditions for educators and school officials is essential, as educators’ high levels of stress have been linked to a lower quality of life. Identifying appropriate therapies is crucial in mitigating the harmful effects of stress.[26] Since previous research has demonstrated that summated scales can provide reliable standards for interpreting treatment outcomes and for identifying and drawing consistent conclusions about changes in irrational beliefs as a psychological construct, we thought about combining 2 instruments to evaluate the participants.[29] The same is true for some studies on stress management among teaching staff that did not include integrity check records, the interaction effect of stress on gender, the degree of commitment of the participants and the therapist during intervention sessions, or the correct identification of the therapist.[30] to date, there are limited REBT studies in Nigeria compared to the United States of America.[31] The current study addressed all of the previously listed problems, which is why it contributed to the knowledge gap. This study aims to investigate how cognitive-behavioral therapy can impact recurrent negative thinking in secondary school teachers and administrators experiencing job-related stress. Given these challenges, there is a pressing need for consistent research on stress coping strategies that consider human psychological, health, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral well-being.

It is crucial to understand how well REBT works in treating stress-related symptoms in hypertensive educators and scientists.[3] Therefore, we hypothesized that rational emotive behavior intervention would not be cost-effective in reducing stress levels among Tourism Educators and Scientists with hypertension. The findings of this study will contribute to understanding and identifying principles for improving people’s ability to perform well and lead fulfilling lives at work and in their personal lives.

2. Methods

2.1. Ethical approval

The Strategic Contacts, Ethics, and Publication Committee of the University of Nigeria granted permission to perform this study in accordance with the American Psychological Association’s ethical standards, with UNN/EC/STA/010/FE = RC102. This was registered retrospectively with the UMIN Clinical Registry and with a unique number: UMIN000061715.

2.2. Participants, recruitment, and procedure

A total of 99 participants were enrolled and recruited for the study. They included tourism and hospitality management lecturers, educational scientists, and home economics and hospitality lecturers from state and federal universities. These participants were recruited through advertising and broadcasting in their respective workplaces. The research team invited 140 prospects, but only 99 participants responded to the invitations. Using this number, the team distributed a baseline test to ascertain the control condition level. Following this, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were considered to identify other indicators that could negatively influence the main effect of the intervention. To participate in the study, each participant had to be affiliated with one of the mentioned departments, have a blood pressure reading, be diagnosed with stress, sign a commitment letter stating their readiness to participate in the program, and have a score of at least 50 on the dependent measures. Those who did not meet these requirements were eliminated from the list of potential participants. Names of individuals receiving psychotherapies and medical treatments at the time of the investigation were also excluded.

Based on the specified conditions, 93 participants were randomly assigned to 2 groups (intervention and control). See Figure 1 for more information. The researchers presented a black basket containing red and green ribbons and instructed each person to choose either a red or green ribbon without replacement. This process facilitated using Saghaei random allocation software[32]; the study used a straightforward random allocation sequence. Those who chose red were assigned to the intervention group, while those who chose green were assigned to the control group. The subjects were split up into control and treatment groups. In order to prevent selection bias throughout the recruiting and randomization process, the researchers made sure to keep the allocation sequence a secret from both research assistants and study participants. We further hide some information in the questionnaires that could reveal which group that received the intervention, blinding the data analyzer until the analysis was finished. All members of the control group and REBT group were given a posttest (Time 2) following the course of therapy.

Participants’ eligibility flowchart.

The intervention group participants received REBT. At the end of every therapeutic session, the participants were given practice exercises beginning from session 2 which were often reviewed before the commencement of the next session program.

The participants’ flowchart diagram in Figure 1 illustrates the enrollment, allocation, follow-up, and analysis stages.

2.3. Follow-up

After 3 months, a 2-week follow-up was also carried out, which resulted in the third evaluation (Time 3). Only to ascertain the level of retention, a follow-up test was administered to the therapy group. To make sure none were missing, the research assistants on the scene helped distribute and collect the questionnaires from the participants at each evaluation. Throughout this exercise, there were no reports of dropouts, and double blocking was implemented to ensure that the process remained confidential.

2.4. The therapist

The treatment package created for this study was managed and administered by 2 female and a male counseling professional who have practiced over 8 years of experience in psychological therapy. A month before the start of the treatment program, they were given the REBT handbook and asked to review it, as well as offer any modifications or comments. The manual gained value from their additions and corrections.

2.5. Treatment

A treatment manual created by Muñoz et al was modified for the current investigation Muñoz.[33] The handbook was altered to conform to the current nomenclature in the fields chosen for study. In order to help the university teaching staff change illogical thinking and false ideas that could make them more prone to stress, the treatment in this study sought to instill REBT principles, skills, techniques, and methodologies in their thoughts. Assist them in cultivating a positive outlook to overcome any ideas and opinions that are not grounded in reality and that are inflexible, rigid, and situationally extreme. Assist the clients in becoming more self-aware, motivated, and focused; identifying and practicing new cognitive, behavioral, and emotional abilities to attain balance; taking action to address their problems; The handbook was intended to be used for 12 weeks and 12 sessions. Weekly sessions of the treatment lasted 1 hour each, for a total of 12 hours. But after 3 months, the researchers also carried out a 2-week follow-up evaluation. In accordance with a similar process used by earlier researchers, the treatment techniques used include, but are not limited to, group talks, mood relaxation, feedback, role acting, case studies, mindfulness practice, cognitive restructuring, and disputation exercises.[34]

2.6. Integrity check

Six research assistants were hired by the researchers. Their responsibilities included overseeing and keeping an eye on the therapeutic program as it progressed. This includes the participation and replies of the participants, their answers to homework, and the monitoring of the therapists’ adherence to the protocols outlined in the intervention handbook created specifically for this study. Additionally, they were asked to write down their observations. The goal is to guarantee sufficiency in all aspects of treatment delivery.

2.7. Measures

2.7.1. Educationists’ Irrational Belief Scale (EIBS)

The Teacher Irrational Belief Scale is a 30-item scale developed by Bernard[35] and was modified to align with education scientists. The objective of the instrument is to measure teachers’ irrational beliefs in relation to their responsibilities and tasks as teachers. Thus, the focus of EIBS is demandingness, low frustration tolerance, awfulizing, and global evaluation. The instrument is rated on a 5-point Likert scale of Strongly Disagree, Disagree, Not sure, Agree, and Strongly Agree. In this study, the rating options were rearranged as follows (Strongly Disagree = 5 points). Hence, the high scores indicate the severity of irrational beliefs. The reliability test of this study showed an internal consistency of 0.82 alpha, which indicates that the instrument is valid and reliable. The validity of Teacher Irrational Belief Scale has been justified across the globe, including Nigeria. The reliability is α 0.76. of EIBS was reported in a study conducted among school employees.[36]. Bora et al[37] reported an overall reliability index of 0.74. In Spain, Bermejo-Toro and Prieto-Ursua[38] reported the validity of EIBS; Ugwoke et al[39] also validated the reliability of EIBS among technical college teachers in Southeast Nigeria.

2.7.2. Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)

Cohen et al[40] developed a PSS. PSS aimed at measuring the extent to which individuals respond to stressful situations based on thoughts and feelings in the home and working environment. PSS is a 10-item, scale rated on a 5 points Likert scale of Never = O, Almost Never = 1, Sometimes = 2, Fairly Often = 3, and Very Often = 4. Thus, the high score is rated as the high stress level of the individual.[41] The reliability test of this study showed an internal consistency of 0.86 alpha, indicating that the instrument is both valid and reliable. Related studies have validated the consistency and reliability of PSS items.

2.8. Data analysis

Using repeated measures of the analysis of variance statistical approach, data gathered from the pretest, posttest, and follow-up test were statistically examined. Using partial eta square, the impact of the intervention on the dependent variables was documented. Using Mauchly tests as assessed by irrational beliefs and stress scales, it was determined that the sphericity assumption was violated. As a result, we utilized the Greenhouse–Geisser method to evaluate the 2 measures, which were more than 0.75. The univariate analysis was used to examine the follow-up data.

4. Discussion

The increasing rate of stress in educational settings, especially in universities in Nigeria, is becoming alarming and therefore requires academic research to seek how such situation could be managed. This is because stress is a life-threatening health condition, and the lives of lecturers who are directly involved are important. This motivated this research, which the results showed a significant reduction in stress perception of participants exposed to REBT intervention. This suggests that participants’ responses to the stress scale vary considerably when compared with before and after the REBT intervention. The follow-up result confirmed the positive impact of REBT intervention was maintained. According to a similar stress study, the REBT intervention improved teachers’ ability to reduce stress.[42] The finding agreed with a previous study[3] that REBT was effective in decreasing the stress of academic staff exposed to treatment and the effect was maintained at the follow-up test. Our research supports a previous study that found that the REBT therapy approach effectively reduced students’ academic stress.[43] Another study’s findings similarly showed that REBT had a retention effect on the reduction of academic stress at follow-up.[44] The results of this study are consistent with those of another study that found that participants in the intervention group had substantially lower levels of academic stress than those in the control group. The therapeutic advantages persisted at the follow-up appointment.[45] According to Uba et al, Cognitive-behavioral counseling was effective in reducing the stress of teachers in special schools in Enugu state.[46]

Stress alters a person’s behavior, emotions, physical condition, and cognitive capacities, claims Taylor[47] Senior high school students might think about using group therapy because research indicates that it greatly lowers academic stress.[48] According to another study, teens who are struggling academically may benefit from therapy.[49] According to research findings, counseling and stress management are strongly and favorably correlated.[50] The study also showed that counseling can significantly improve the stress management skills of student moms attending public secondary schools in Kenya’s Kimilili sub-county.[50]

The results in Table 2 also show a significant reduction in irrational beliefs among participants exposed to REBT intervention. The results also suggest a statistically significant effect of time on irrational belief among participants as measured by EIBS. This indicates that participants’ responses to the Irrational Belief Scale at different times of measurement are significantly different. The results also indicate that irrational belief among participants’ scores was not influenced significantly by group and time interaction effect. The follow-up result reveals that the positive impact of REBT intervention was maintained overtime as measured by EIBS. Like the present study, Anyaabulam[3] remarks that stress was identified as the aftermath of irrational thoughts and erroneous beliefs, but through the application of REBT principles and strategies, their pessimistic patterns of thought which was anchored on inflexibility, rigidness, and extremism were dealt with. The goal of therapy is to help clients change their thought patterns, particularly those that are not grounded in reality. Therefore, any change to irrational thought and erroneous belief will undoubtedly result in a decrease in any psycho-emotional disturbance caused by stress symptoms.[51]

In terms of the findings regarding gender, the findings are consistent with a study by Nwimo and Onwunaka[9] that discovered that male special education teachers are more stressed than their female counterparts. The results also corroborate those of Wang et al,[52] who discovered that men were more likely than women to experience stress reactions to psychological stress. This might be because stress has an impact on both men and women. However, men might be under more stress than women to a certain degree. In keeping with this, the findings of several studies may indicate that women feel higher levels of stress than men. By indication, school counselors should oversee and organize a regular stress management plan in special education settings. Because of its proven effectiveness, the results show that counseling interventions utilizing cognitive therapies are among the best ways to address stress among nonspecialist teachers and administrators in special schools.

5. Conclusion

The result of an excessive workload is typically high levels of stress and a negative impact on well-being. The goal of this study is to identify the factors that affect lecturers’ stress levels and overall well-being, as their well-being has not received enough attention in previous research. Existing studies have not addressed the unique stress factors specific to certain universities, academic disciplines, and gender, either in a comprehensive or specific manner. This study has addressed these factors and found a significant decrease in stress levels and irrational beliefs among participants who underwent REBT intervention. This suggests that participants’ responses to stress and irrational beliefs varied significantly before and after the REBT intervention. The follow-up results confirmed that the positive impact of the REBT intervention was sustained. Therefore, it is recommended to implement REBT in various departments and faculties by establishing REBT institutes. Given the psychological benefits of REBT, it is necessary for Nigerian REBT professionals to consider the cultural factors that influence people’s perceptions when they have hypertension. In Nigeria, influences from family and society may contribute to “irrational beliefs” more than personal convictions. For instance, educators, mathematicians, and scientists whom people accept with esteem and respect, may feel what people are saying about their health. The worry could be linked to the expectations and general well-being of their family, not only their personal convictions. Therefore, accepting these social variables would make a REBT approach insufficient and less successful.

REBT professionals in Nigeria should recognize the client’s cultural background and worldview. REBT principles can be made more accessible and intelligible by including Nigerian cultural anecdotes, proverbs, and analogies. When feasible, therapy in the client’s mother tongue can improve comprehension and foster a stronger emotional bond with the therapeutic ideas.

6. Practice implications

The outcome of this study is positive; hence, practitioners in schools and outside educational institutions are called to adopt or adapt the principles of REBT in cushioning the effect of work stress in their respective workplaces. Drawing reference from Tourism and Hospitality management lecturers who are poised with excess workload, can embrace and practice the skills and techniques of REBT. This could be done daily after exhausting the daily activities. They should liaise with REBT experts, especially when they observe poor coordination and consistent misinterpretation of events, who will counsel them to become more adaptable. It also implies that tourism and hospitality management lecturers’ abilities to understand how their interpretations contribute to emotions and behavior will significantly help to cope with work stress.

For those in the sciences, the outcome of this research has shown the good benefit of REBT principles in managing and cushioning the effects of stress. Thus, scientists, irrespective of fields and not limited to mathematicians, should seek ways of incorporating principles of REBT in their conferences, workshops, and seminars. They should also collaborate with REBT professionals who will help them actualize this. Just like those tourism and hospitality management lecturers experience excess workloads, they share similar experiences; therefore, REBT principles are recommended.

The findings of this study also imply that gender, university, and core field/areas/discipline do not matter when it comes to stress. Meaning that every teaching staff member experiences stress. By implication, lecturers should accept, accommodate, and seek how to cope with stressful circumstances surrounding teaching and learning in universities.

7. Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its recruitment of participants from various disciplines and universities, ensuring adequate representation of both males and females to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, assessing the level of irrational beliefs among participants helps determine if work stress in private, state, and federal universities is influenced by their belief systems, further strengthening the methodology.

This study is unique in its focus on stress among tourism and hospitality management lecturers, educational scientists, mathematicians and home economics and hospitality lecturers across private, state, and federal universities. The differences in work conditions and environments among these institutions, including infrastructure and staff numbers, make this research particularly valuable.

Only one previous study has examined the impact of REBT on stress among participants with high blood pressure. Fortunately, this study is the second to investigate the health status and stress levels of participants with hypertension in Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa.

Despite the overwhelming uniqueness of this study, there are limitations, one of which is that an additional stress measure should have been used. Such a measure should have been a qualitative method. A second follow-up test would add value to this study, but it was not conducted.
