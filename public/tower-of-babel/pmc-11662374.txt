Rates and Factors Related to COVID‐19 Vaccine Uptake in Racialized and Indigenous Individuals in Canada: The Deleterious Effect of Experience of Racial Discrimination
Authors: Rose Darly Dalexis, Seyed Mohammad Mahdi Moshirian Farahi, Junio Dort, Idrissa Beogo, Lewis A Clorméus, Lisa Caulley, Yan Xu, Jude Mary Cénat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRacialized and Indigenous communities have been disproportionately affected by COVID‐19 infections and mortality, driven by systemic socioeconomic inequalities. However, how these factors specifically influence COVID‐19 vaccine uptake is not documented among racialized individuals in Canada. The present study aims to examine COVID‐19 vaccine uptake rates and related factors among racialized and Indigenous communities compared to White people in Canada. In total, 41,931 individuals aged 16 and older, consisting mainly of Arab, Asian, Black, Indigenous, and White individuals, were randomly invited to participate from a panel of 420,000 Canadian households. A total of 4220 participants completed this cross‐sectional survey study in October 2023. In total, 89.60% of participants were vaccinated: 2.38% received one dose, 31.45% received two doses, 33.46% received three doses, 18.86% received four doses, and 13.86% received five doses and more. There were significant differences according to racial and ethnic background in vaccine uptake (χ 2 = 57.45, p < 0.001), with Indigenous and Black individuals having lower vaccination rates (83.54%, OR = 1.98% and 84.76%, OR = 2.16, p < 0.01) compared to White individuals (91.66%). Confidence in health authorities was positively associated with vaccine uptake (B = 0.62, p < 0.001). Conspiracy beliefs partially mediated this association (B = −0.13, p < 0.001). Racial discrimination attenuated vaccine uptake, even if respondents had confidence in health authorities (B = −0.08, p < 0.001). Vaccine uptake was lowest amongst Indigenous and Black communities, placing them at higher risk of severe COVID‐19 infection. In addition to mistrust of public health authorities due to racism, racialized communities are affected by conspiracy beliefs that hinder COVID‐19 vaccine uptake. A whole‐of‐society approach to address barriers to vaccine uptake among racialized populations, including wage disparities, racial discrimination, is needed to eliminate racial health disparities and increase vaccination rates in racialized communities.Keywords: Arab, Asian, Black, confidence in health authorities, conspiracy theories, Indigenous, racial discrimination, vaccine uptake

Introduction

Racialized and Indigenous people had paid the highest toll of COVID‐19‐related infections, especially in major Canadian cities, including Toronto, Ottawa, Montreal, Edmonton, Calgary, Vancouver, and Winnipeg [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. In addition, these populations have been hardest hit by the social and economic consequences of COVID‐19 [9, 10, 11]. Data published by Statistics Canada also showed that racialized and Indigenous people were more likely to die from COVID‐19 than White people (31 deaths/100 000 population in racialized communities vs. 22 deaths/100 000 population for their White counterpart) [12]. The results showed that Black people were particularly likely to die from COVID‐19 (49 deaths/100 000 population), with an even higher risk among Black men (49 deaths/100 000 population) and Black people earning low incomes (103 deaths/100 000 population) [12]. Black people were 2.1 times more likely to die from COVID‐19 than White people [12]. A systematic review showed that Indigenous people living in Canada and the United States were 2.45 times more likely to die from COVID‐19, while they were also more likely to be infected and to suffer from a severe form of COVID‐19 [13]. Studies have shown that historical social and systemic inequalities have amplified the impact of COVID‐19 on racialized and Indigenous communities [11, 14, 15, 16].

Despite the disproportionately higher infection rate in racialized and Indigenous communities, data revealed by Statistics Canada showed that they were also more reluctant to be vaccinated and were among the least vaccinated groups in the general population [17]. Similarly, studies of various racialized and Indigenous communities (excluding Asians) have shown them to be more wary of the vaccine and less trusting of public health instructions [18, 19, 20, 21]. Although many studies have made these observations, very few independent studies to date have compared the uptake of the COVID‐19 vaccine between different communities. Studies to date have focused on investigating racialized communities as a block (visible minorities or racialized communities). Moreover, no study has investigated the factors associated with vaccine uptake in a diverse Canadian population, including racialized and Indigenous communities. Studies, so far, failed to capture significant samples or often overlooked them or merely categorized them as a “visible minority” group or non‐Indigenous group [17].

Conspiracy theories in racialized and Indigenous communities in Canada often arise from historical and ongoing experiences of systemic oppression, mistrust of governmental institutions and a lack of representation in decision‐making processes [8, 22, 23, 24, 25]. As these studies demonstrated, historical injustices, such as residential schools, medical experimentation, racial profiling, and racial disparities in child protection services, education and health foster skepticism toward official narratives, including during the COVID‐19 pandemic [26, 27, 28, 29]. In health precisely, inequalities and racial discrimination experienced in the healthcare settings by racialized and Indigenous individuals enhanced conspiracy beliefs and lower their trust in medical authorities and public health agencies [30, 31, 32]. Conspiracy beliefs may have a strong association with vaccine uptake which is less documented in the racialized and Indigenous communities in Canada. In addition, the association between racial discrimination and vaccine uptake is empirically undocumented among different racialized groups in Canada, while studies showed its impact on fueling vaccine mistrust and hesitancy in different countries [27, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. It is critical to empirically document among racially diverse individuals the association between conspiracy beliefs, confidence in COVID‐19 vaccine, as well as the role of racial discrimination.

Using data from the “Vaccine Effectiveness Perception Project”, this study aims to: (1) examine vaccination rates by racial and ethnic background and other sociodemographic characteristics; (2) analyze factors associated with vaccination in Black communities, including health literacy, conspiracy beliefs, and racial discrimination. We hypothesize that (1) conspiracy beliefs being strongly linked to vaccine refusal will completely mediate the link between COVID‐19 vaccine confidence and COVID‐19 vaccine uptake; (2) the experience of racial discrimination influencing health seeking behaviors among individuals [34] will contribute to lower COVID‐19 vaccine uptake among racialized and Indigenous individuals.

Methods

Design and Data Collection

Data were collected as partof the “Perception of COVID‐19 Vaccine Effectiveness in Racialized and Indigenous Canadian Communities” project. A representative sample of data was obtained through an online survey using Computer Assisted Web Interviewing (CAWI) conducted by Léger, Canada's leading research and marketing firm. Respondents were chosen at random from the Leo panel (operated by Léger Marketing), which encompasses more than 420,000 Canadian households. Within this panel, 65% of profiles were refreshed within the 6 months before the survey, and 50% of profiles align with the Statistics Canada census data. In total, 41,931 people were randomly invited to participate in the current study. Of those, 10.06% completed the survey. In order to achieve a sample that reflects racial and ethnic diversity, individuals of Arab, Asian, Indigenous, and Black backgrounds were specifically sought after using ethnic profiling data accessible within the Leo panel. This approach yielded a varied sample comprising these racial groups, which are frequently overlooked in research or categorized simply as a “visible minority” group. Inclusion criteria consisted of: (1) living in Canada; (2) aged at least 16 years; and (3) being able to express themselves in English or French. This study was conducted in October 2023 [27]. Both English and French version of questionnaires were made available. The median survey duration was 14 min and 29 s. The University of Ottawa Research Ethics Board (#H‐04‐23‐9199) granted approval for this study. All participants provided an electronic informed consent. The present research followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross‐sectional studies. To facilitate understanding of the results, researchers from all communities were involved from the beginning of the project. Advisors from different communiities were also involved in recruiting and analyzing the data and interpreting the results.

Measures

Sociodemographic

Participants were asked to indicate their gender, age, education, religion, province, race, language, job status, annual household income, and marital status.

COVID‐19 Vaccine Status

Participants were asked about COVID‐19 vaccine status (unvaccinated vs. vaccinated) and the number of doses. A binary outcome variable on the vaccination status (code 0 = unvaccinated, code 1 = vaccinated) for the prevalence rates and logistic regression was used. For mediational analyses, the following coding was used: 0 = unvaccinated, 1 = received one dose, 2 = received two doses, 3 = received three doses, 4 = received four doses, and 5 = received more than four doses.

COVID‐19 Vaccine Confidence

The COVID‐19 vaccine confidence subscale from COVID‐19 vaccine confidence and mistrust beliefs scale [38] was used. This subscale includes nine items and participants were asked to respond to each item on a 5‐point Likert scale (from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). It was used among racialized groups in Canada with excellent internal consistency. It was used among racialized groups in Canada with excellent internal consistency. [39] The Cronbach's alpha in this study was 0.96.

Everyday Racial Discrimination

The Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS) [40] was used to assess everyday discriminatory experience (i.e., “people act as if they are afraid of you”, “you receive worse service than others at restaurants or stores”). Participants were asked to respond to each item on a 6‐point Likert scale (from “never” to “almost everyday”). The EDS was recently commonly used in racialized communities in Canada [41, 42, 43]. The Cronbach's alpha in the current study was 0.91.

Conspiracy Beliefs

The COVID‐19 Conspiracy Belief scale [44] was used to measure conspiracy beliefs. This scale contains 10 items (i.e., “COVID‐19 was deliberately created by scientists in a laboratory.”, “nobody really died from COVID‐19”) on a 5‐point Likert scale (from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). Higher scores on this scale indicate higher levels of conspiracy beliefs. The Cronbach's alpha in our sample was 0.92.

Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were performed in SPSS 29. The prevalence of COVID‐19 vaccination status (vaccinated vs. unvaccinated) was cross‐tabulated by sociodemographic factors. Chi‐square tests were used to test the associations of vaccination status and sociodemographic factors. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratio (OR and AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported by using simple and multiple logistic regressions, respectively. In adjusted model, all sociodemographic factors were included as covariates to test changes on specific factor after adjusting for other factors in comparison to unadjusted model. Therefore, due to an interest in exploring potential related factors to vaccination status, we included all sociodemographic factors in the model. The macro‐PROCESS for SPSS 29 was used to conduct the moderated mediation analysis. The model included COVID‐19 vaccine confidence as independent variable, conspiracy beliefs as mediator, everyday discrimination as moderator, and COVID‐19 vaccination status as outcome variable. Continuous scores of COVID‐19 vaccination status were included in the model (from 0 = unvaccinated to 5 = received more than four doses). Bootstrapping procedures with 95% CIs were used to infer the statistical significance of indirect effects. A significance level of ≤ 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Table 3 presents sociodemographic factors associated with vaccination status using unadjusted and adjusted ORs. Results showed that participants aged 65–74 years old (OR = 0.49, p = 0.003) and aged over 75 years (OR = 0.21, p = 0.001) were less likely to be unvaccinated. As for education, participants who held a university certificate or bachelor's degree (OR = 0.37, p < 0.001) and graduate degree (OR = 0.29, p < 0.001) were less likely to be unvaccinated. Similar results were found for bilingual (English and French) participants, for those participants whose income was over $40 000, for those who had no religion, and for married/common‐law partner or divorced/widowed participants. Results also showed that retired participants were less likely to be unvaccinated, while unemployed and homemaker participants were more likely to be unvaccinated. Regarding racial identity, Black participants (OR = 1.98, p < 0.003) and Indigenous participants (OR = 2.16, p < 0.001) were more likely to be unvaccinated.

In the multivariate model that accounted for all sociodemographic variables, participants aged 65–74 years old (adjusted OR [AOR] = 0.26, p = 0.001) and aged 75 years old and over (AOR = 0.16, p = 0.003) were less likely to be unvaccinated. Moreover, Black participants were 1.94 times more likely to be unvaccinated (AOR = 1.94, p = 0.003) and homemaker participants were 1.75 times more likely to be unvaccinated (p = 0.023). Finally, participants who had a university certificate/bachelor's degree and graduate degree, who spoke a language other than English or French, who had an income of $100 000–$124 999 and $125 000–$149 999, and who were women were less likely to be unvaccinated (adjusting for other sociodemographic factors).

A moderated mediation model for the relationship between COVID‐19 vaccine confidence and COVID‐19 vaccination status through conspiracy beliefs was conducted (Table 4; Figure 1). Results showed that COVID‐19 vaccine confidence was positively associated with COVID‐19 vaccination uptake (B = 0.62, p < 0.001), and conspiracy beliefs were negatively associated with COVID‐19 vaccination uptake (B = −0.13, p < 0.001), indicating partial mediation. The indirect effect of COVID‐19 vaccine confidence on COVID‐19 vaccination through conspiracy beliefs was significant (B = 0.08; 95% CI, [0.05, 0.10]). Moderation role of everyday discrimination on the relation between COVID‐19 vaccine confidence and COVID‐19 vaccination uptake was also tested. A significant interaction effect between everyday discrimination and COVID‐19 vaccine confidence on COVID‐19 vaccination uptake was observed (B = −0.08, p < 0.001; Figure 2). This finding indicated that the relation between COVID‐19 vaccine confidence and COVID‐19 vaccination uptake was stronger among those who had lower levels of everyday discrimination.

Discussion

The first aim of this article was to examine vaccination rates according to racial and ethnic origin and other sociodemographic characteristics. The results showed that Indigenous and Black people were less likely to have received COVID‐19 vaccinations. Asians, followed by Arabs and Whites, had highest vaccination uptake levels. These results particularly highlighted the vulnerability of Indigenous and Black communities, who are the most victims of racial discrimination in Canada, especially in health services [45, 46, 47]. Although the link between racial discrimination and vaccination has only been empirically established in Black communities in Canada [9, 45], this link has been widely established in the United States for other communities [48, 49]. For example, in the USA, one study showed that the index of racist experiences in different states was associated with vaccination rates in Black and Hispanic communities [21, 49, 50]. There are also other structural disparities that may also explain these results. For example, while Black people in the sample have higher graduation and employment rates compared to White people, they have lower wage levels (see Table 1). While 46.73% of Black people and 55.3% of Indigenous people earned < $60 000 annually, this rate was only 34.97% in White communities, 33.96% in Asian communities, and 41.15% in Arab communities. These systemic disparities, deeply linked to skin color, are elements that should be studied with interdisciplinary teams in order to better understand them and develop sustainable solutions to resolve them.

While we expected that Arab communities, which experience significant religious discrimination [51], would have similar vaccination rates to Indigenous and Black communities, the opposite was observed. However, there is heterogeneity within this general observation: for example, Arab respondents who speak only English had lower vaccination rates compared to the overall Arab population, with vaccine uptake levels that are similar to those observed among Indigenous and Black communities. Although the current perception in Canada is that Bill 21 has disproportionately targeted Arab people in Quebec (a French‐speaking province), which bans religious symbols for public service workers, a measure that particularly affects Arab women. However, Quebec is the second province where Arabic people were more vaccinated. Even though the fear of being discriminated against as communities can encourage vaccination, these are issues that need to be analyzed in depth, considering spoken language, religious affiliation, gender, and the province of residence, in order to better understand the needs of different communities. Although Asians in Canada have always had a high vaccination rate in general, including for influenza [52], the racial discrimination to which they were exposed during COVID‐19 did not prevent them from being more vaccinated than the rest of the population. We can even hypothesize that this led them to vaccinate even more, as if to participate in the effort to stop the pandemic and show that they are part of the national community. Similarly, while there were no significant differences between participants born in Canada and those born elsewhere, among Arab people there was a clear difference, with those born in Canada vaccinating significantly less (83.8%) than those born elsewhere (95.82%). Taken together, the intersections of culture, language, immigration, and community on racial and ethnic disparities in COVID‐19 vaccine uptake and hesitancy requires further exploration.

The regression analyses presenting the odd ratios (Table 3) confirmed the greater vulnerability of Black people to COVID‐19 vaccine access. While in the unadjusted model, Indigenous respondents were 2.16 times less likely to receive the COVID19 vaccine and Black respondents 1.98 times less likely, in the adjusted model the results were significant only for the latter. In fact, they were 94% less likely to be vaccinated than White people. These results also showed that the most educated people and people aged 65–74 and those aged 75 and over were more likely to be vaccinated against COVID‐19. These latest results confirm those of previous studies in different communities [17, 18, 19, 20, 48, 49, 50, 53, 54, 55, 56]. They also indicate groups to which prevention programmes should pay particular attention. Regarding Indigenous communities, similar to our findings, a study found that vaccination rate among First Nations, Inuit and Métis in Toronto (58.2%, 95% CI 51.4%–64.9%) and London (61.5%, 95% CI 52.9%–70.0%) were lower than the rates for the two cities and Ontario [56].

The second objective of this study was to examine factors associated with COVID‐19 vaccination among an ethnically diverse sample in Canada. First, we hypothesized that conspiracy beliefs would completely mediate the relationship between trust in vaccines and public health instructions and COVID‐19 vaccine uptake. The results of the moderated mediation model show that despite the weight of conspiracy beliefs, they only partially mediated the relationship between trust in vaccines and public health instructions and COVID‐19 vaccine uptake. This partly confirms our first hypothesis. It is widely recognized that exposure to and adoption of conspiracy beliefs increases vaccine hesitancy and has contributed to decreased uptake of the COVID‐19 vaccine in disadvantage communities [57] as well as in the general population [58]. This link is particularly relevant to racialized communities, which are exposed to both general conspiracy beliefs and racially‐specific conspiracy beliefs, such as the belief that there were vaccines dedicated solely to Black people, Indigenous people and immigrants during the COVID‐19 pandemic, aimed at reducing their life expectancy or sterilizing them [59] However, despite the addition of conspiracy beliefs to the equation, trust in public health authorities continues to have a positive association with COVID‐19 vaccine uptake. This is an issue little explored in the scientific literature, and one that needs to be considered when examining strategies to increase the trust of the population, especially those from racialized and Indigenous communities.

The second hypothesis explored in this study was that the experience of racial discrimination would reduce COVID‐19 vaccine uptake. The results confirmed the important role of the experience of racial discrimination. Although this is the first study to confirm the role of racial discrimination in an ethnically diverse sample in Canada, this hypothesis has been widely tested in similar samples in the United States and in Black communities in Canada [60]. As the complex trauma theory explains, racial discrimination has an impact on the relationship that racialized people have with themselves and with others [61]. It helps to increase racialized people's distrust of political decision‐makers and their messages and instructions in various fields, including public health. As other studies have shown, racial discrimination also increases belief in conspiracy theories [60, 62, 63]. All this may explain the strong negative link between racial discrimination and vaccine uptake in racialized and Indigenous communities in Canada.

Limitations

Although this study is the first to have analyzed in depth factors associated with uptake of the COVID‐19 vaccine in different racial groups in Canada, it has three main limitations. First, although it would be difficult to do otherwise, the self‐reported data should be taken with caution and constitutes a limitation of this study. Second, as the research design is cross‐sectional, it is not possible to establish causal links between the different variables. The moderation and mediation analysis used should therefore be considered with this limitation in mind. It will be important to carry out longitudinal studies in order to observe how the different models explored hold up over time. Third, even though it was expected, our study has a relatively low response rate (10.06%), and panels in general tend to attract White well‐educated individuals living in urban areas, with access to technology. However, we made a significant effort to oversample the racialized and Indigenous groups in order to achieve better representation and be able to compare different groups. Finally, the lack of such similar studies on different ethnic groups limited the discussion of the results. In particular, we did not have a referent for the results within the groups, but this opens avenues of research to be addressed through qualitative micro‐studies in order to better understand the results of the present study.

Implications and Conclusions

Despite these limitations, this study has important implications for research and the development and implementation of public health programs. First, from a research point of view, we demonstrate the need for micro‐studies to explore findings that cannot be fully explained due to lack of information captured in the current study. For example, the fact that English‐speaking Arabs and those born in Canada were among the least vaccinated groups would benefit from qualitative micro‐studies to better explain the results of this study.

Second, in terms of public health interventions, the results show the need for public health authorities to work to increase public confidence, particularly in racialized and Indigenous communities. This requires engaging with communities and their representatives, including community and religious leaders and elders who play an important role in the daily lives of racialized and Indigenous peoples. The findings on the role of racial discrimination in reducing vaccine uptake among racialized people confirm a major challenge for public health agencies, health services and Canadian society as a whole. Indeed, this is a major public health concern, since racialized and Indigenous people represent about a quarter of the general population, and their non‐vaccination would put the health of the entire Canadian population at risk. To tackle racial discrimination, we need to stop pretending that Canada is a multicultural society and end the color‐blind approach while taking strong measures to reduce racial inequalities. Moreover, it must be recognized that this is a major avenue for systemic intervention which should be of interest not only to public health authorities, but also to political decision‐makers in general.

It is by engaging the whole of society in a process of eliminating racism, reducing salary disparities (even among the best‐educated racialized people) and considering other intersectional aspects such as age, ethnicity/race, religion, education, among others, that we will succeed in increasing vaccination uptake among different racial groups. This will take time, but it is an obligation, because each community made more at‐risk also puts the public health of the entire population at risk.
