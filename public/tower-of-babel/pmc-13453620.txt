Adapting international clinical practice guidelines for rehabilitation management of acute spinal cord injury in Iran
Authors: Seyed Behnam Jazayeri, Seyed Farzad Maroufi, Judith Aarabi, Ahmad Pour-Rashidi, Maryam Shabani, Heshamtollah Ghawami, Sobhan Pourmasjedi, Mohammad Sadeghian, Maryam Kheyri, Roya Habibi Arejan, Farzin Farahbakhsh, Amir Hassan Kohan, Mohsen Sadeghi-Naini, Khatereh Naghdi, Sina Azadnajafabad, Samuel Berchi Kankam, Zahra Ghodsi, Vafa Rahimi-Movaghar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to adapt international clinical practice guidelines for the rehabilitation of acute spinal cord injury (SCI) in Iran.MethodsThis study was conducted based on guideline adaptation methods (the ADAPTE (ADaptation and eVALuation of a prE-existing guideline) framework). After an organizing committee was created and health topics selected, we searched PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library from January 2011 to April 2021 to find guidelines regarding acute/subacute rehabilitation of SCI patients. The quality of the clinical practice guidelines was examined using the Appraisal of Clinical Guidelines for Research & Evaluation II. Recommendations were made by the committee and categorized according to population, intervention, professions, outcomes, and health care setting. The decision-making process was based on a systematic evaluation of each recommendation, utilizing a combination of quality for supporting evidence or against each recommendation, as well as consideration of feasibility, acceptance, and adoptability for the healthcare context of Iran.ResultsOur selection yielded 1 high-quality guideline containing 4 recommendations that underwent an adaptation process. Rehabilitation should be implemented as soon as patients are hemodynamically and neurologically stable and can tolerate the intensity. We suggest establishing specialized rehabilitation centers with trained personnel and proper equipment, providing tele-rehabilitation, education, and encouraging caregivers to help with early rehabilitation. Negotiating with insurance companies is imperative to cover the cost of long-term occupational therapy and rehabilitation. We suggest conventional over-ground training rather than treadmill training due to the lack of facilities. Individuals with acute/subacute cervical SCI should be offered functional electrical therapy. We find that additional training in unsupported sitting has no clear benefit associated with it when compared to the current standard rehabilitation.ConclusionsImplementation of international guidelines for SCIs and rehabilitation is a process that involves feasibility, acceptability, relevance, and adaptability when considering developing countries. The recommendations presented in this study have the potential to significantly improve the care provided to patients with SCI, not only in Iran but also could help guide developing countries in the healthcare arena.Keywords: Practice guideline, Rehabilitation, Spinal cord injuries, Developing countries

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) leads to sensory, motor, and autonomic disorders that affect a person's physical, mental, and social functions.1 The incidence of traumatic SCI ranges from 5.1 to 150 cases per million people based on national studies2 and 22.5 cases per million people in developing countries.3 Males account for a majority of cases, and traffic accidents, falls, and violence are the main etiologies.4

The financial implications of caring for individuals with SCI are undeniably significant, often surpassing those of other chronic disorders.5 Cervical level of injury, American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) impairment scale (AIS) groups A and B, concomitant injuries, and in-hospital complications have been identified as significant contributors to the increased cost burden associated with SCI. For instance, the average direct costs of acute SCI care in the United States range from $73,850 for AIS D, E to $612,590 for high cervical (C1-C4) AIS A, B, C cases, and inpatient rehabilitation costs range from $19,540 for people with AIS D, E injuries to $433,044 for those with quadriplegia.6 Beyond the direct medical costs, individuals with SCI often face various indirect and long-term expenses. The lifetime financial burden incurred by individuals who sustained injuries at the age of 25 is diverse and correlates with the seriousness of the injury, varying from $0.5 million to $2.3 million.7

The main goals of acute SCI rehabilitation are to minimize functional impairment after injury, promote neuro-recovery, and prevent secondary complications.8 Rehabilitation is a continuous and time-consuming process, which demands equipment, skilled human resources, and specific strategies to improve SCI patient outcomes. Rehabilitation strategies are primarily focused on motor control, activity-dependent neuroplasticity, and regaining function by treating underlying impairments. The best strategies must be determined for SCI patients to receive the greatest physical and psychological benefits from rehabilitation.8,9

The process of developing new clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) from scratch can be resource-intensive and time-consuming.10 It involves conducting systematic reviews, evaluating the evidence, convening expert panels, and synthesizing recommendations. In Iran and other developing countries where resources may be limited, the cost of de novo guideline development can pose a significant challenge.11 However, a cost-effective alternative is to consider guideline adaptation. This involves the customization of existing international guidelines to align with local context capabilities. By using this approach, developing countries can save resources, reduce development time, utilize established evidence, ensure contextual relevance, and promote capacity building.

Recently, we adapted CPGs regarding pharmacologic management of acute SCI12 and timing of decompressive surgery.13 In this study, we aimed to investigate the adoptability of rehabilitation guidelines for SCI with a systematic and evidence-based approach.

Methods

Study design

This guideline was conducted according to the ADAPTE (ADaptation and eVALuation of a prE-existing guideline) framework.14,15 The ADAPTE offers a methodical approach for adapting guidelines created in one specific setting, making them suitable for implementation in diverse cultural and organizational contexts.14,16 The ADAPTE framework has been widely utilized across various countries and healthcare settings, contributing to the efficient and effective adaptation of guidelines. The ADAPTE framework includes 3 phases: set-up, adaptation, and finalization. The summary of our methods is illustrated in Supplementary Table 1.

Set up phase

This step involved the formation of the organizing committee. This group contained an executive committee and an expert panel. The executive committee consisted of 7 medical students with the responsibility of translating and managing the adaptation process of the guidelines. This team was supervised by a mentor, who was responsible for monitoring the team's performance and coordinating meetings for the expert team. The project coordinator of the team was responsible for communication between the expert team and scheduling meetings. The expert team consisted of stakeholders who were facilitators of the guidelines. They had clinical knowledge, personal experience, administrative policy, and expertise in adapting SCI guidelines. This team consisted of 17 experts, including 8 neurosurgeons, 2 general physicians, 2 health science specialists, 1 spine orthopedist, 1 rehabilitation specialist, 1 neurologist, 1 sport physical medicine specialist, and 1 occupational therapist. The team was responsible for adapting recommendations and suggestions from the reference guidelines based on the support needs of the communities. The selection criteria for the expert panel were as follows: (1) Experience in guideline adaptation with a history of published SCI care guidelines; (2) Robust research background in the relevant field; (3) Possession of the knowledge, expertise, and experience to address the specific needs of the Iranian population, based on actively working in rehabilitation and managing SCI patients daily; (4) Being well-respected and recognized in their professional communities, lending credibility to the guidelines; (5) Having experience in implementing guidelines in real-world settings to provide practical insights. This team was carefully chosen to ensure a comprehensive and authoritative approach to the guideline adaptation process.

The topic of interest was rehabilitation management of SCI. We aimed to offer evidence-based recommendations for the rehabilitation of Iranian adults (over 14 years old) with acute or subacute blunt SCIs, divided into grades A to D according to the ASIA impairment scale after stabilization. The ASIA impairment scale is a standardized neurological assessment to evaluate the sensory and motor levels affected by SCI.17 An outline of this project was established by the executive committee and approved during an online meeting of the committee. All members signed the conflict of interest form.

Searching and retrieving guidelines

A detailed search for relevant published guidelines was conducted in April 2021. Five websites of guideline clearinghouses and relevant organizations (list of the guideline portals in Supplementary Table 1), and bibliographic databases including PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library were searched for records (from January 2011 to April 2021). The search terms were (a) “Spinal Cord Injuries”; (b) “Guidelines”, or “recommendations”; (c) “rehabilitation”, “intervention”, “practice”, or “management”. The full search strategy is provided in the supplemental materials.

Titles, abstracts, and full texts of identified publications were evaluated by 2 independent members of the executive committee using the following inclusion criteria:

Patient group: Only guidelines for adults (>14 years) with acute or subacute SCI were included.

Study type: CPGs are defined as “systematically developed statements to assist practitioners' and patients’ decisions about appropriate healthcare for specific clinical circumstances”.18 In this study, guidelines were included if they met 2 specific criteria: (1) evidence-based recommendations that were derived from a systematic review of the available evidence, and (2) a clear and explicit connection between the recommendations provided and the supporting evidence. These guidelines could be in the form of comprehensive documents or journal reports.

The scope of the guideline was centered on rehabilitation after SCI or included a dedicated section specifically addressing this aspect. In the event of any discrepancy between members of the executive committee, the senior author was consulted to resolve the discrepancy.

Guideline selection

The quality of each guideline was independently assessed by 4 reviewers using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation (AGREE II).19 It consisted of a comprehensive set of criteria and domains that were used to evaluate various aspects of guideline development, including scope and purpose, stakeholder involvement, rigor of development, clarity of presentation, applicability, and editorial independence. A cut-off score of 60% in the key domain "rigor of development" was set as the minimum requirement for final guideline selection. Guidelines that achieved an average score of at least 60% in this domain, based on the combined scores of the 4 reviewers, were selected for the next steps.

Developing clinical scenarios

After selecting the final guidelines, recommendations were extracted and tabulated as possible scenarios answering the PICO (Patient and/or problem; Intervention, or diagnostic test, etc.; Comparison intervention, if relevant; and Outcome) clinical questions.

Decision-making

To further evaluate each recommendation, a matrix was developed that contained several components. First, the guideline scores as assessed by AGREE II were included. Second, the body of the recommendations and the associated health questions were incorporated. Third, the quality of the evidence and the rationale of each suggestion/recommendation were included. Finally, the robustness of each suggestion, evaluated through the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation framework in accordance with the reference guideline, was included. This chart was exhibited to all members of the expert panel, who were then prompted to assign scores to each recommendation utilizing a 3-point Likert scale (disagree = 0 points, neutral = 1 point, agree = 2 points) across 4 categories: feasibility, relevance, acceptability, and adoptability. Feasibility involved assessing the existence of appropriate infrastructure for implementing the recommendations, including tools, technology, and other facilities. Relevance focused on aligning the characteristics described in the clinical population with those of the Iranian population. Acceptability considered the preferences of patients in the country, considering cultural customs, societal norms, and cost-effectiveness of the interventions. Overall adoptability evaluated the overall appropriateness and adoptability of each recommendation.

This evaluation process took place in 2 rounds. In the first round, the expert panel members were provided with a form, both online (using Google Form) and offline (using MS Word software), containing the initial list of recommendations. The members received the forms via email and were able to provide multiple-choice and descriptive answers in both formats. Each recommendation, along with its supporting evidence level, was presented on separate pages of the forms. Questions were also included regarding the adoptability of the recommendations and descriptive suggestions for establishing infrastructure, where deemed necessary. The forms, including details of experts’ scoring, are available in the supplementary materials.

With a panel consisting of 17 experts, each recommendation was scored in each of the 4 domains, resulting in a range of scores from 0 to 34. These scores were then categorized into 3 levels: low (scores 0 – 20), medium (scores 21 – 27), and high (scores 28 – 34). The decision-making process for each recommendation was based on the scores assigned by the expert team in the domain of overall adoptability. Recommendations that received a low score were rejected, as they were deemed not adoptable. Recommendations with a medium score underwent further discussion in the second round of expert panel opinions. During this round, the expert panel considered measures and strategies to adapt these recommendations to better suit the specific context and needs of the target population. In contrast, recommendations that received a high score were adopted, indicating a high level of agreement among the expert panel members regarding their adoptability. These recommendations were considered suitable for implementation without significant modifications.

Results

A total of 1315 records from bibliographic databases and 421 records from guideline database websites were identified (Fig. 1).20 After excluding 74 duplicates, titles and abstracts of 1662 records were screened by 2 members of the executive committee independently. After exclusion of 1624 irrelevant records, the full texts of 38 guidelines were reviewed and assessed using the inclusion criteria. This process limited the number to 5 guidelines, of which 4 were excluded after AGREE II evaluation. In the final stage of the selection process, the following guideline was selected as the source guidelines for data extraction, based on the final inclusion criteria: A clinical practice guideline for the management of patients with acute spinal cord injury: recommendations on the type and timing of rehabilitation. The overall score for the rigor of the development domain from the AGREE II assessment for the selected guideline was 94%. This guideline contained 4 recommendations regarding the type and timing of rehabilitation in acute SCI. The information regarding the results of the adaptation process has been provided (Table 2).

Recommendation 1: We suggest rehabilitation be offered to patients with acute SCI when they are medically stable and can tolerate the required rehabilitation intensity

Considering the low score of the feasibility of this recommendation (20 out of 34) and the high importance of the issue for patients, the experts made suggestions to provide the necessary infrastructure for the implementation of this recommendation. The experts suggested the following:

(1)Establishing specialized inpatient rehabilitation centers: It is necessary to establish specialized inpatient rehabilitation facilities in Iran, initially focusing on a few provincial centers. These facilities are advised to offer an extensive array of rehabilitation services, encompassing occupational therapy, physiotherapy, speech therapy, audiology, and specialized orthotic support.

Experts also considered the earliest possible time for initiation of rehabilitation in Iran. They agreed that there is no definite time for starting rehabilitation because it depends on a variety of factors such as the patient's health condition before the injury, age, type of trauma, comorbidities, etc. However, it should begin as soon as the patient is hemodynamically and neurologically stable and can tolerate rehabilitation. The ultimate timing would be within 1 week after injury.

Recommendation 2: We suggest offering body weight-supported treadmill training as an option for ambulation training in addition to conventional over-ground walking, dependent on resource availability, context, and local expertise

The experts agreed that treadmill training is not applicable (low score for feasibility and overall adoptability) in the current health care situation of Iran because the equipment does not exist or is limited in most centers. Also, the high cost of purchasing equipment and the fact that rehabilitation services are not covered by insurance in Iran hinder the implementation of this recommendation. Considering the limited evidence supporting body weight-supported treadmill training and the economic challenges associated with its implementation, it was suggested that conventional over-ground walking be the primary approach for ambulation training in SCI patients within Iran's healthcare system. This approach emphasizes the use of available resources, cost-effectiveness, and practicality. It was also agreed that, if patients have the necessary financial means and sufficient compliance to carry out these interventions at home, it could be considered as a therapeutic recommendation.

Recommendation 3: We suggest that individuals with acute and subacute cervical SCI be offered functional electrical therapy as an option to improve hand and upper extremity function

The consensus of expert opinion was that hand and upper extremity function is of utmost importance in individuals with SCI. Several randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the efficacy of functional electrical therapy in improving motor function in the hand and upper extremity. Additionally, the affordability and low cost of equipment required for electrical therapy make it a viable option within the Iranian healthcare system. Therefore, experts strongly advocated for the implementation of this type of rehabilitation. However, they also offered suggestions to ensure the feasibility of this recommendation:

(1)Personnel training: Physiotherapists or specialty nurses should receive continuing education and training in the proper use of specific devices and techniques for electrical stimulation.

(2)Addressing patient perceptions: Some patients may have concerns or perceive electrical therapy as ineffective. Providing adequate education about the benefits, rationale, and potential outcomes of electrical therapy can help address these concerns and improve patient acceptance and compliance.

Recommendation 4: We suggest that additional training in unsupported sitting is not necessary beyond what is currently incorporated in standard rehabilitation

The experts agreed that this recommendation is adoptable in the Iranian healthcare setting. The decision was made because the available evidence indicates that unsupported sitting training does not offer any significant additional benefits to the current standard rehabilitation.8,21

Discussion

In this study, we adapted international clinical practice guidelines and aligned them with the healthcare context in Iran using a systematic approach. We searched different databases looking for guidelines outlining the rehabilitation of SCI patients. Our guideline selection process yielded a high-quality guideline that was developed under the auspices of AO Spine North America, AO Spine International, and the American Association and Congress of Neurological Surgeons. This guideline was the result of a systematic review of the literature, and 4 recommendations arose for rehabilitation management for acute/subacute SCI.8 Although most of the suggestions had a low level of evidence, the expert panel agreed that implementing the recommendations in clinical practice is an effective approach with the potential to enhance the care and well-being of individuals with SCI.

In total, this study identified 4 recommendations pertaining to the type and timing of rehabilitation after SCI. Among these recommendations, 2 were identified as requiring infrastructural advancements to be effectively implemented. One was rejected due to low adoptability, and 1 was adopted. While Fehlings and colleagues8 suggested body weight-supported treadmill training as an option for ambulation training in addition to conventional overground walking, our expert panel only recommended conventional overground walking because it is more affordable for individuals with SCI using the developing countries current healthcare system and because the results are similar to those of treadmill training.22,23 In contrast, we adopted “not offering additional training in unsupported sitting beyond what is currently incorporated in standard rehabilitation” due to limited empirical evidence supporting the efficiency of this recommendation.21 In relation to the remaining 2 recommendations, the expert panel identified specific infrastructural needs that would be required for their successful implementation. These proposed infrastructural needs serve as suggestions for addressing any gaps or deficiencies in the existing healthcare system, facilities, or resources.

The establishment of a comprehensive rehabilitation infrastructure in Iran requires careful consideration of several key factors. These requirements encompass the necessity for dedicated inpatient rehabilitation facilities, educational initiatives for healthcare practitioners, caregiver training, tailored rehabilitation protocols, tele-rehabilitation options, and extended insurance coverage for ongoing rehabilitation. To rectify these deficiencies in infrastructure, the government must allocate both resources and funding to facilitate the establishment of specialized rehabilitation centers, educational programs, and tele-rehabilitation services. Collaboration with international organizations and countries that have well-established rehabilitation systems can provide valuable guidance, knowledge exchange, and potential funding opportunities. Raising public awareness campaigns can also serve as a mechanism to emphasize the significance of rehabilitation and gather support from stakeholders and patrons.

Health policymakers should extend assistance to individuals with SCI, particularly by aiding them in securing insurance coverage for rehabilitation services. Given the considerable costs associated with these services and the financial constraints faced by most SCI patients, this backing is crucial. The pivotal factor influencing health policymakers to allocate resources to this realm is the evident effectiveness of such support. While no existing study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of distinct rehabilitation approaches for SCI,24 the recommendation directed at healthcare policymakers and governmental health system overseers is to allocate funds for early rehabilitation in cases of SCIs. This proactive measure aims to preclude numerous complications stemming from SCI and potentially alleviate the long-term financial strain on the healthcare system. Although the initial costs of early rehabilitation are higher, they ultimately avert substantially elevated expenses and an onerous financial encumbrance on the healthcare infrastructure.25,26

Based on the consensus among experts, it is suggested that individuals with acute and subacute cervical SCIs be offered functional electrical therapy as an option to improve hand and upper extremity function. This recommendation is based on the recognition of the critical importance of hand and upper extremity function in SCI patients, supported by several randomized controlled trials demonstrating the efficacy of functional electrical therapy in motor function improvement.27, 28, 29 Considering the Iranian healthcare system, the affordability and low cost of equipment required for electrical therapy make it a feasible option.

However, the experts also provided suggestions to ensure the successful implementation of this recommendation. First, it is important to provide specialized training for physiotherapists or specialty nurses in the proper use of devices and techniques for electrical stimulation. This will ensure that healthcare professionals have the necessary skills and knowledge to deliver effective electrical therapy. Addressing patient perceptions is another crucial aspect. Some patients may have concerns or doubts about the effectiveness of electrical therapy. It is recommended to provide comprehensive education to patients, explaining the benefits, rationale, and potential outcomes of electrical therapy. This will help address any misconceptions and improve patient acceptance and compliance with the recommended treatment.

Additionally, the lack of equipment in government hospitals for functional electrical stimulation poses a challenge to the implementation of this recommendation. Establishing occupational therapy and rehabilitation units equipped with appropriate devices in trauma hospitals would address this issue. This would improve infrastructure development, thereby enabling the provision of functional electrical therapy to SCI patients effectively.

This guideline marks the 4th SCI care guideline adapted by our team.12,13,30 We are currently working on an ongoing project to implement these guidelines and incorporate them into the care delivery system of the National SCI registry of Iran to increase the quality of care in SCI patients.31 This initiative aims to ensure systematic data collection and analysis, enabling us to effectively monitor the long-term guidelines' impact and make timely adjustments as necessary.

The main finding of our study was the feasibility and relevance of adapting international CPGs for the rehabilitation of acute SCI in Iran. Future recommendations are directed toward healthcare policies and rehabilitation management, focusing on addressing infrastructure issues and improving optimum functioning while reducing disability. In addition, 3 major obstacles in implementing clinical practice guidelines for rehabilitation management of individuals with SCI in Iran are a lack of specialized rehabilitation centers, economic hardship, and inadequate education among patients as well as personnel. Another recommendation would be to promote tele-rehabilitation for remote areas, as this would eventually reduce healthcare costs and deliver care more efficiently. Although the adaptation of CPGs from international countries to developing economies such as Iran is challenging, this article offered a framework to apply valuable guidelines for the rehabilitation management of acute SCI, and to expand our knowledge on the optimal timing, type, and challenges in providing rehabilitation following SCI in Iran.
