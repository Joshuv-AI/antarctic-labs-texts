Betacyanin biofortification in wheat via synthetic biology for nutritional enhancement
Authors: Hao Peng, Weihong Huang, Surong Wang, Zhiyang Han, Xi Li, Huali Tang, Xingguo Ye, Yuqing Lu, Le Xu, Ke Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEngineered wheat produces natural antioxidant pigments in whole plants and flour, enabling healthier, visually distinctive foods and offering a tool to study gene activity in specific tissues.

Dear Editor,

Betalains are natural, water-soluble nitrogen-containing pigments, mainly consisting of red-violet betacyanins and yellow betaxanthins (Carreón-Hidalgo et al. 2022). Betacyanins possess strong antioxidant activity and are associated with diverse health benefits, including anti-cancer, cardioprotective, anti-diabetic, hepatoprotective, and neuroprotective effects (Sadowska-Bartosz et al. 2021). The RUBY system was developed to enable heterologous betacyanin biosynthesis in plants (He et al. 2020). Because RUBY utilizes tyrosine, which is theoretically ubiquitously present in all plant species as its enzymatic substrate, it has been reported that RUBY accumulation can be successfully achieved in many plant species, including rice, maize, tomato, soybean, and others. RUBY is a powerful tool that has been widely applied in plant bioengineering (Wang et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025; Tan et al. 2025). In fact, nearly 200 publications document RUBY accumulation in about 70 plant species (https://zhaolab.biosci.ucsd.edu/ruby/).

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a primary staple food for over 3.5 billion people, supplying approximately 20% of global dietary calories and protein (Hawkesford et al. 2013). With economic and social development, people increasingly demand not only sufficient food but also improved nutrition and health, highlighting the growing importance of dietary quality. Here, we applied the RUBY system in wheat to generate transgenic germplasm with stable betacyanin accumulation, offering a feasible biofortification strategy to enhance the nutritional value of this important crop.

To enhance betacyanin accumulation in wheat, the vector pWMB110-RUBY (Shi et al. 2025)—in which the ZmUbi promoter drives RUBY expression—was introduced into the Fielder and Yangmai 16 wheat cultivars via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Supplementary Materials and Methods). Red pigmentation appeared in immature embryos after 3 days of co-cultivation and later in calli during the resistant callus induction stage (Fig. 1-A1–A3). The roots, stems, and leaves of regenerated transgenic plantlets exhibited pronounced red–purple coloration (Fig. 1-A6). Eight and 37 transgenic plants were identified in Yangmai 16 and Fielder, respectively, by PCR analysis in the T0 generation (Figure S1). Among the Fielder transgenic plants, substantial variation in betacyanin accumulation was observed, manifested as differences in color intensity (Fig. 1-b). Based on pigmentation patterns, the positive plants could be categorized into two groups. The first group showed relatively low betacyanin accumulation with uneven pigmentation among tissues: some plants displayed green or red stems and leaves (Fig. 1-B2, B3), green and light red (Fig. 1-B6) or red-green variegation on spikes (Fig. 1-B7). The second group accumulated higher levels of betacyanins, with roots, stems, leaves, and seeds uniformly displaying deep red–purple coloration (Fig. 1-B4). Spikes of transgenic Yangmai 16 plants also exhibited variable red coloration (Figure S2); however, the endosperm of all the transgenic plants remained white. The RT-qPCR analysis (Supplementary Materials and Methods) revealed a positive correlation between RUBY transcript levels and leaf pigmentation in transgenic plants, with higher expression producing deeper coloration (Fig. 1-B13). In addition, different tillers from the same T0 plant displayed distinct coloration patterns (Fig. 1-B2, B3), indicating that individual tillers represent independent transgenic events, consistent with previous reports (Zang et al. 2022). The seeds harvested from the darkest red–purple T0 plant, R14, produced T1 progeny that retained the same pigmentation phenotype, demonstrating stable inheritance of betacyanin biosynthesis (Figure S3).

Because wheat flour is primarily derived from the endosperm and pWMB110-RUBY showed little betacyanin accumulation there, we replaced the ZmUbi promoter with the endosperm-specific 1Dx5 promoter of HMW-GS, generating pWMB110-PDx5-RUBY (Fig. 1-c). After introducing this construct into Fielder, 36 transgenic plants were confirmed by PCR (Figure S4). Phenotypic observation revealed that at 20 days post anthesis (DPA), the spikes of transgenic plants remained green, whereas the seeds exhibited red–purple coloration (Fig. 1-d), while the seed coat remained green. In all positive plants, the aleurone layer was red; however, most of these plants exhibited white or light red endosperm, and only a few showed pronounced red pigmentation in the endosperm. Among these, transgenic line D32 displayed the most intense endosperm coloration, which was likely attributable to higher RUBY expression levels.

To further characterize betacyanin accumulation patterns among different transgenic plants, pollen and grain traits of R14, D32, and their wild-type Fielder were systematically analyzed. Pollen and embryos of Fielder and D32 were white, whereas those of R14 were red (Fig. 1-E1, E3). At 20 DPA, wild-type grains were green, R14 grains were uniformly red, and D32 grains were predominantly red with a green region surrounding the embryo (Fig. 1-E2). Cross-sectional analysis of grains (Fig. 1-E4), as well as grains with embryos and aleurone layers removed (Fig. 1-E5, E6), revealed that wild-type grains had light-green seed coats, colorless aleurone layers, and white endosperm; R14 grains exhibited red seed coats and aleurone layers but retained white endosperm; whereas D32 grains showed light-green seed coats with both aleurone layer and endosperm displaying red pigmentation. After milling mature grains (Supplementary Materials and Methods), flour from wild-type plants was white, flour from R14 was red, and flour from D32 exhibited a deeper red coloration (Fig. 1-E7). Notably, after removal of the embryo and aleurone layer, only flour derived from D32 remained red (Fig. 1-E8), further confirming that betacyanins accumulated in the endosperm of pWMB110-PDx5-RUBY transgenic plants. To assess the impact of the exogenous RUBY gene, three transgenic lines driven by the ZmUbi and 1DX5 promoters were evaluated, and no substantial differences in major agronomic traits were observed compared with the wild type (Table S1).

The RUBY gene has been widely developed as a visual reporter across diverse plant species. Compared with the conventional GUS and GFP reporter systems, the RUBY system enables direct, nondestructive, and real-time monitoring of transgenic events, thereby substantially simplifying the screening process. At present, the RUBY reporter has been mainly applied in three major areas. First, it has been used for real-time monitoring of plant genetic transformation. In the wheat transformation system, red–purple pigmentation was visible in embryos, calli, and regenerated shoots (Fig. 1-a), demonstrating RUBY as an effective visual marker. Second, RUBY has been applied in doubled haploid breeding technologies. In maize and tomato (Wang et al. 2023), RUBY was combined with haploid induction genes to produce visible haploid inducer lines, enabling rapid haploid identification and streamlined breeding. Third, RUBY has been employed to facilitate the selection of CRISPR/Cas9-edited plants. In soybean (Chen et al. 2024), co-transformation of RUBY with the CRISPR/Cas9 system allows transgene-free edited plants to be rapidly identified in segregating progeny based on color separation, substantially reducing the workload associated with molecular screening.

In addition to these established applications, our study reveals several previously unrecognized utilities of the RUBY gene. First, we observed that grains of pWMB110-PDx5-RUBY transgenic plants accumulated betacyanins not only in the endosperm but also in the aleurone layer. This finding suggests that the 1Dx5 promoter may not be strictly endosperm-specific but also active in the aleurone layer, highlighting the potential of RUBY as a convenient tool for validating tissue-specific promoter activity patterns. Second, with the growing interest in agro-landscape tourism, farmlands with artistic or ornamental patterns are attracting increasing attention. The vivid red–purple coloration of the transgenic wheat developed in this study markedly enhances the visual appeal of wheat fields, providing valuable germplasm resources for the development of “color agriculture” systems that integrate crop production with landscape tourism, in line with the diversification trends of modern agriculture. Third, as a natural antioxidant, betacyanins confer potential health benefits to red endosperm wheat. Flour derived from betacyanin-enriched grains not only improves the visual attractiveness of wheat-based foods but also holds promise for the development of nutritionally fortified functional foods. Future comprehensive analyses of the nutritional and functional components of these materials will facilitate the development of wheat products that combine esthetic value with health-promoting properties.
