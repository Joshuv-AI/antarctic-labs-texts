Camel calf morbidity and mortality in low-income settings: Critical risk factors revealed by a cohort investigation in eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Ambachew Motbaynor Wubaye, Abdusabur Mustefa, Welelew Edmew Worku, Alubel Alemu, Yihenew Getahun Ambaw, Shihun Shimelis, Mina Ali, Simegnew Adugna Kallu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The morbidity and mortality of camel calves remain persistent problems in Ethiopia. This study aims to assess the incidence of camel calf morbidity and mortality and identify its predictors in the Dire Dawa administrative council, eastern Ethiopia. A questionnaire-based cross-sectional survey, along with a longitudinal follow-up study, was conducted on 385 purposively selected households to assess camel calf morbidity and mortality. Descriptive analysis was employed to examine the occurrence of calf morbidity and mortality. Survival analysis using the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curve and parametric proportional hazard regression was used to compute the time-to-event data on morbidity and mortality. The overall calf morbidity and mortality incidence rates (IR) was 75 cases per 100-calf six-months at risk (risk rate of 52.8%) and 16.8 cases per 100-calf six-months at risk (risk rate of 15.5%), respectively. Age, parity, delivery status, and access of colostrum were found to be significant (p < 0.05) predictors of calf morbidity and mortality. The risk of morbidity and mortality was lower in calves that ingest free access colostrum (HR = 0.47), aged over three months (HR = 0.036). The risk of mortality was decreased in normally delivered calves (HR = 0.35), older than three months (HR = 0.21), and free access colostrum ingestion (HR = 0.51). The morbidity and mortality rates reported in this study area are high, posing a substantial threat to herd replacement and overall production. Even though this research was carried out under local production circumstances, the recognized risk factors especially insufficient access to colostrum, age, delivery status, and factors related to the dam reflects the difficulties frequently noted in camel production systems in comparable pastoral environments. Therefore, an urgent improvement in veterinary service delivery, along with targeted awareness programs for camel herders regarding colostrum management and good husbandry practices is required so as to reduce calf losses and boost productivity in similar production systems.

Introduction

Camel production is vital in the arid and semi-arid regions of Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia, especially for pastoral and agro-pastoral communities where their food security and household income is linked to camel production (Asiimwe et al., 2020). Ethiopia is one of the countries with largest camel populations in the world. In Africa, it ranks third, next to Somalia and Sudan. Camels are normally distributed throughout subtropical dry areas in Africa and Asian countries (Chalchisa & Bersissa, 2023). The agro-pastoral and pastoral production systems are where camels primarily kept. According to the Central Statistical Agency (CSA, 2020), it is estimated that there are 8.1 million camels in Ethiopia.

Camelid husbandry is most commonly found in the Eastern and Southern regions of the country like Afar, Somalia, and Oromia (Babege et al., 2021). The morbidity and mortality rate of camel calf in Ethiopia is 32.5–35.2% and 8.9% respectively (Zeru, 2016). These, high incidence of calf morbidity and mortality incurs great economic loss to camel producers associated with death loss, treatment cost and decreased lifetime productivity (Muluneh et al., 2022a). Camel diseases are the main constraints to camel production as well as calf survival to reproductive ages (Muluneh et al., 2022b). Although camel production plays a crucial role in supporting pastoral livelihoods, there is still limited location-specific data on calf health and survival in many regions where camels are raised. The Dire Dawa Administrative Council is one of the regions with a significant population of camels. However, to the researchers' knowledge, there is limited information available regarding the morbidity and mortality rates of camel calves in this area. Producing this type of evidence is crucial not only for enhancing herd health management locally but also for guiding disease control and calf management strategies that can be applied to comparable pastoral production systems in other low-income areas. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the incidence rate of camel calf morbidity and mortality in the Dire Dawa Administrative Council.

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional herd-level questionnaire survey followed by a prospective follow-up study was used to assess the incidence of morbidity and mortality in camel calves and identify its determinants during the period from January to July 2024.

Study area description

Dire Dawa administration (DDA) is located at 515 km to east of Addis Ababa (Fig. 1). It is situated between 9°28′ and 9°48′ N latitude and 41°50′ and 42°20′ E longitude. The altitude of DDA is from 960 m above sea level in the northeast to 2450 m above sea level in the southwest. It divided into just two main agro-ecological zones: Woina Dega (above 1500 m) and Kola (below 1500 m). The region gets two distinct seasons of rainfall: the little rainy season, which extends from February to May, and the high rainy season, which is from July to September. October to January is when the dry season occurs. The region experiences an average annual rainfall ranging from 550 to 850 mm, while the monthly mean maximum temperature ranges from 28.1 °C to 34.6 °C (DDPAC-AB, 2008). Sample size determination and sampling procedures

The sample size (n) was determined based on the formula provided by Lwanga et al. (1991).n=(Z1−α/2ε)2Where, Z1−α/2 is the functions of confidence level, ε is the relative precision and n is sample size. Thus at 95% confidence level and 10% relative precision gives a total sample size of 385 calves. The lists of peasant associations and households (herds) were obtained from district agricultural offices and the respective selected peasant association. Two districts from the administrative council and four kebeles from each selected districts were selected randomly. Rapid household survey was conducted from each selected kebele to determine household that have at least one pregnant camel, and then a random sample of these pregnant camel owning households was drawn to take the study. For owners having more than one pregnant camel, we were selecting one using lottery method. The calves were observed in a two-week interval throughout the study period (for six months). For the questionnaire survey, all the selected households were interviewed.

Data collection

For the questionnaire survey, semi-structured questionnaire (Supplementary material) was designed and implemented through face-to-face interviews with 385 camel producers to gather herd-level data. The survey aimed to collect information on various aspects, including herd management, feeding practices, healthcare, perceptions and restrictions related to colostrum intake, timing and methods of colostrum feeding, frequency of colostrum administration, as well as the history of calf morbidity and mortality within the herd. Additional data points included sex, and delivery status (normal or assisted). The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and subsequently translated into the local language, Afan Oromo, to ensure clarity and ease of communication. All interviews were conducted in Afan Oromo by fluent speakers of the language.

For the calf follow up study, a total of 385 camel calves were enrolled and followed regularly in a two-week interval until the six-month cohort period was completed or lost to follow-up. When calf loss occurs during the study period, date and reason of loss was documented. The calves recruited for the study was identified individually by giving their own identification number used by the investigator.

Data analysis

The collected data were encoded using Microsoft Excel 2016 spreadsheet and exported to STATA Version 16 software for statistical analysis. The data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential analysis techniques. Descriptive statistics such as percentage, and Kaplan Meier (KM) curve were computed. The incidence rate of camel calf morbidity and mortality were calculated. Incidence rates (IR) was estimated according to Martin et al. (1987).

Number of calf-month at risk was calculated by adding the number of days at risk of obtaining a new case in each calf from birth to end of the study (Martin et al., 1987) . The time period between the birth date of camel calf to date of failure or censored was used to calculate animal-month of follow-up. For this analysis, the total calf days at risk were converted into calf months at risk, as the calves' age was documented up to six months. Both time scales will be referenced as needed. Additionally, to facilitate easier comparison of results with other studies, the incidence rate (true rate) was transformed into the incidence risk ratio (IR) or cumulative incidence rate (CIR). The log-rank test was employed to evaluate whether a predictor should be included in the final model, using a p-value threshold of 0.25 as the criterion for variable selection in the multi-variable model.

Cox proportional hazard assumptions were checked graphically using log (-log) plot whether the curves are approximately parallel or not and statistically using Schoenfeld’s residual (global test) for morbidity (p = 0.0002) and mortality (p = 0.000) used to determine whether the hazard assumptions were met. The cut-off point of greater than 0.05 was used to declare the proportional hazard assumption achieved (Abeysekera and Sooriyarachchi, 2009). In parametric survival model, the survival time was expected to follow a known distribution.

The Log-Likelihood Ratio (LR), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were used to select the best-fitted model, and a model with minimum AIC and BIC value was considered (Weibull and Gompertz pH) the best-fitted model for camel calf morbidity and mortality. The model’s fitness was tested using Cox-Snell residual test (Fig. 5). Variables with a p- value < 0.25 in the univariable analysis were become candidates for multivariable analysis, and adjusted hazard ratio (AHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were added to evaluate the strength of associations and variables with a p-value <0.05 were considered statistically significant predictors.

Discussion

Camel production in Ethiopia is extensive management and mainly for subsistence, which is characterized by low production and productivity. The major challenges to the pastoralist production system are camel calf morbidity and mortality, which results in decreased replacement stock and production (Allan et al., 2023, Muluneh et al., 2022a).

As indicated by demographic data most of the respondents (88.6%) had no formal education. This may be related to factors like, cultural traditions, economic conditions, and the nomadic lifestyle of camel herders. In many pastoral communities, traditional knowledge and abilities pertaining to herding and livestock management are highly valued, which frequently supersedes formal schooling Fernández-Giménez (2000). Additionally, many camel herders are from low-income families with little access to educational possibilities. Families in rural places may find it difficult to send their children to school due to financial constraints. Moreover, camel herding requires a nomadic lifestyle that requires constant mobility in pursuit of water sources and grazing areas. Due to this mobility, kids may face difficult to attend class regularly, which can interfere with formal education.

Camel calve husbandry practices survey highlighted several areas of concern. The habit of "residual suckling" for all camel calves (100%) was raised by all respondents. Such type of suckling practice (not let the calf to suckle adequate milk) was reported by Isako (Isako et al., 2020) and this is due to milk competition between the producer and calf (Awoke & Ali, 2015). The camel calve should suckle the whole teat for the first thirty days to be healthy andsurvival (Isako et al., 2020). Restricting of calve from suckling of adequate colostrum and then milk in their neonate time negatively influence growth and survival of newborn calves (Rirash et al., 2017). Furthermore, 78.7% of calves did not get supplementary feed before weaning and this nutritional deficiency in early life could be a contributing factor for calf morbidity and mortality (Abdella & Bakheit, 2016).

Based on our survey results, <20% of camel owners separate and supply supplementary feed to pregnant camels, whereas over 80% of respondents neither separate nor provide supplementary feed to pregnant camels. The finding is in agreement with a study conducted in Pakistan by Padalino et al. (2024) which states that, only 18.5% camels receive feed supplement. A number of interrelated factors like, cultural customs and habits, financial limitations, and a lack of knowledge or education, may be responsible for this phenomenon. Many camel herders tend to rely on traditional practices that emphasize natural grazing over supplementary feeding (Askar et al., 2024). Additionally, the supplementary feed cost can be a major barrier, since many camel herders may not have the financial resources required to invest in additional nutrition for their camels. Furthermore, most camel owners might not be aware of the advantages of providing additional nutrition to pregnant camels.

According to the questionnaire survey, 7.3% and 8.6% of the pregnant camels faced still birth and abortion, respectively. The occurrence of abortion and stillbirth in camels may be linked to the practice of not isolating pregnant females from the main herd, particularly during the critical time of giving birth. When pregnant camels are not separated, they face increased risks of stress, disease transmission, and potential injuries, all of which can elevate the likelihood of stillbirths and abortions. Additionally, poor hygienic practices and the sharing of resources with other animals further exacerbate these detrimental outcomes. Ensuring proper care and separating during pregnancy is essential for improving the health and survival of both the mother and her offspring (Hammadi et al., 2001; Tilahun et al., 2013).

Based on the questionnaire survey, more than half of sick animals never get medical treatment. This finding is higher than 11.1% and 12.5% medical treatment from Pakistan and Kenya respectively (Lamuka et al., 2017; Padalino et al., 2024). This issue may be related to access to veterinary services and cultural beliefs and practices. There may be scarce or no veterinary service available in many pastoral areas (Jilo et al., 2016). As a result, some pastoralist tribes frequently use local knowledge or traditional medicine to treat their animals. These practices may increase the risk of disease transmission and hinder timely intervention for sick calves. Untreated calves are more susceptible to diseases, malnutrition, and environmental challenges which directly impact morbidity and mortality rates.

The prospective cohort study showed that there was a significant amount of camel calf morbidity and mortality. During the follow up period, the overall incidence rate (IR) of calf morbidity was 75 cases per 100 calf-six months at risk, with a risk rate of 52.8%. This finding is lower than the 74.3% morbidity in Borana (Muluneh et al., 2022). As expressed by the authors higher morbidity rate in the previous study area could be recurrent drought and shortage of feed with other concurrent constraints. This is obvious animals are more susceptible to diseases at the time of drought and feed scarcity. The risk rate of calf mortality was 15.5%, which is in agreement with the 12.43% in Afar (Keskes et al., 2013b) and the 10.5% in Jigjiga zone of Somali Region (Keskes et al., 2013a), but lower than the 63% of calf mortality reported in northern Kenya ((Isako et al., 2020). This high calf mortality may be attributed to malnutrition, inadequate veterinary care, and management practices. Camel calves may experience malnutrition, particularly if their moms are not getting enough feed or if pasture is in short supply. Their immune systems may be weakened by malnutrition, leaving them more vulnerable to illness. In addition, in rural pastoralist settings, limited access to veterinary care (Jilo et al., 2016) may cause delays in illness diagnosis and treatment. In the absence of routine health examinations and immunizations, illnesses can spread quickly among herds of calves.

Diarrhea and respiratory disease were identified as the leading cause of camel calf morbidity and mortality. The finding is in line with other reports (Muluneh et al., 2022; Tamire Ane & Muktar, 2019), they stated that diarrhea and respiratory disease are recorded as the major cause of calf morbidity and mortality. The morbidity and mortality rates in calves are influenced by different factors, such as the absence of separation pens, inadequate colostrum feeding, inadequate treatment for sick calves, and inadequate feed supplementation, all of which are common in the study area.

Age of camel calf, colostrum feeding, parity and delivery status during parturition were the significant predictors that predict cause of camel calf morbidity and mortality. Calf age was one of the major predicting factors for morbidity and mortality rate in our longitudinal cohort study. A large amount of calve of camel died particularly under three months of their life. The correlation between younger age with high mortality and morbidity has been documented in previous studies (Megersa, 2014; Wudu et al., 2008; Zeleke & Bekele, 2000). Younger animals are more susceptible for disease and death due to immature immune system, dependence for passive immunity on colostrum, naïve immune system, underdeveloped digestive and respiratory system (Chase et al., 2008; Goshu et al., 2023). Based on our study, inadequate colostrum provision was a significant risk for morbidity and mortality, where calf that had been restricted to suckle adequate colostrum could be at higher risk for morbidity and mortality. This is because colostrum is rich in immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM), which are vital to the health of new born calf (Bishr et al., 2013). Because of their underdeveloped immune systems, newborn calves depend on the transfer of these antibodies from colostrum to keep them safe from infections.

Parity of the mother was also an important determinant factor for a calf to develop disease. Camel calves from multiparous camels experienced lower morbidity risk compared to calves from primiparous (Nagy & Juhasz, 2019; Hammedi et al., 2021). This may be explained by several factors related to maternal experience, behavioral factors, and reduced stress. Due to their past birthing experiences, multiparous camels may be better equipped to control the calf birth process. In addition, the nursing and feeding experience that multiparous camels have contributed to their better established nutritional status. This may result in higher-quality milk and colostrum production, both of which are essential for the calf's general health and immune system. Moreover, Multiparous mothers may show improved maternal behaviors, like protecting and grooming their calves, which can lessen stress and improve the health of the young animals (Hammadi et al., 2021).

According to our finding, assisted delivery increases the chance of mortality in calves than that of normal parturition and this is in consistent with other reports (Ferede, 2015; Tora et al., 2021). This can be justified by a number of factors related to the stress, complications, and possible trauma of assisted deliveries, since forceful manipulation during assisted deliveries is common and can result in stress and suffering for both the mother and the calf (Kovács et al., 2016). This stress reaction might worsen the health of the calf and raise the possibility of complications. Maternal activities including nursing, bonding, and licking happen spontaneously after birth in a normal parturition, but these behaviors, which are essential for the survival and development of calves, may be postponed during assisted births (Kovács et al., 2016). Absence of maternal care can lead to poor thermoregulation, inefficient colostrum consumption, and increased susceptibility to infections. An aided delivery might cause stress, which can impair the calf's immune system. The calf may be more vulnerable to illnesses if it is born in stressful circumstances because it may not be able to establish a strong immune response.

The main limitation of this study was that, disease conditions were identified by clinical findings and filled observations without confirming by laboratory diagnostic methods, due to financial constraints. This may led to misclassification bias even though the diagnoses were made by qualified animal health professionals using widely accepted clinical indicators. Geographic scope of the study was also another limitation, since it conducts with in a specific pastoral production system. Future studies conducted across multiple regions and incorporating laboratory-based diagnostics would enhance the generalizability and diagnostic accuracy of findings.

Conclusion

Improvement in camel calf management and veterinary service delivery is crucial and urgent. The findings highlighted that important risk factors that need to be addressed to increase calf survival include, insufficient colostrum provision, young calf age (especially under three months), assisted delivery, and absence of supplemental feeding. Therefore, early-life calf management practices, particularly provisions of adequate colostrum especially within the first six hours of birth, good feeding practices for pregnant camels and young calves and aware of camel herders about the significance of calf nutrition and neonatal care should be improved. Additionally, expanding and improving veterinary services for timely diagnosis and treatment is important to reduce the disease burden and death of calves due to preventable disease.
