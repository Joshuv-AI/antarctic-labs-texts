A self-powered chloroplast-driven nanophotosystem for treating skin photoaging through rejuvenating mitochondria and revitalizing senescent fibroblasts
Authors: Xin Shou, Changjiang Chen, Lingyao Zeng, Wucan Liao, Zhiyun Liu, Dan Wu, Chengjie He, Miaomiao Tan, Lanjie Lei, Liyun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin aging is an inevitable biological process caused by cellular senescence and overexposure to harmful environmental factors such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Senescent fibroblasts with proliferation arrest, mitochondrial dysfunction and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) depletion have been proposed as a major mechanism driving skin photoaging, but the specific therapies are currently lacking. Inspired by the self-powering potential of plant-derived photosynthetic system, we herein fabricated a novel nanophotosynthetic platform that integrated Chlorella-derived nanothylakoid units (NTUs) with hyaluronic acid (HA)-based microneedles (MNs) to specifically target senescent fibroblasts for treating skin photoaging. By equipped with photosynthesis (PS)-I/II and quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase (QPRT), the NTU-MN photosystem remarkably increased mitochondrial biogenesis and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation, resumed NAD(P)H pool and increased cellular anabolism, addressing the heighted bioenergetic and biosynthetic requirement for highly turnover of fibroblasts during photoaging. Furthermore, with skin penetrating ability of MNs and camouflaging of fibroblast membranes, topical application of the nanophotosystem facilitated the intradermal release of NTUs, leading to regeneration of damaged tissues, increased collagen synthesis, decreased senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), and hence alleviated photoaging of skin. Thus, we developed a “green nanoplatform” with significantly anti-aging efficacy, biocompatibility, and biosafety, opening new avenues for light-driven therapies for degenerative diseases.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04561-5.Keywords: Chloroplast, Nanophotosystem, Mitochondria, Microneedle, Skin photoaging

Introduction

Skin aging is a complicated process impinged by a combination of intrinsic and extrinsic factors, such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation, exposure of smoking, heat and environmental pollutants [1]. While mild or short-term UV exposure caused the pathological alterations that are readily reversible, whereas excessive or prolonged UV exposure may overwhelm these repair mechanisms and lead to tissue senescent phenotypes [2–4]. As individuals age, skin undergoes significant morphological and functional changes, characterized by reduced dermal thickness, fragmentation of collagen fibrils, imbalanced extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis, and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)-mediated chronic inflammation [5]. Fibroblasts are primary cells within the dermal layer responsible for synthesizing the extracellular matrix and collagen, which is essential for maintaining the strength and resilience of the skin [6, 7].

Upon UV radiation or during natural aging, dermal fibroblasts shift to the senescent phenotype with arrested cell growth, reduced collagen synthesis, and elevated matrix-degrading metalloproteinases (MMP) production. Accumulation of senescent fibroblast has been proved to cause impaired tissue homeostasis and skin damaging, pressing the need for developing targeted therapies based on the advanced knowledge. Although the precise mechanisms driving senescence fibroblasts are not completely elucidated, cumulative evidences support the critical involvement of dysfunctional mitochondria, insufficient bioenergetics supply, and defective anabolism [8, 9]. Mitochondria is known as a cellular power factory responsible for generating energy to support high-speed turnover of the skin. It operates through tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to generate adenosine triphosphate (ATP) via the electron transport chain (ETC) [10]. Also, mitochondria mediate the production of metabolic intermediates to support biosynthesis of macromolecules, such as nucleotides, lipids, and proteins, all of which are essential for skin repair and regeneration [11]. Nevertheless, the damaging factors like UV irradiation would substantially affect the biogenesis and function of mitochondria, leading to defective ATP generation, imbalanced metabolism and oxidative stress. Hence, the strategies to correct mitochondrial distress, presumably through transplanting healthy mitochondria or its core components, directly transporting ATP, or transgenic editing the regulatory molecules, have recently been developed [12, 13, 14] but plagued by the key issues, such as limited sources, low efficacy, and ethnic concerns. Successful regeneration of the damaged skin cells needs the approaches with increased specificity and efficacy.

In addition to mitochondrial dysfunction, senescent dermal fibroblasts are also featured with defective NAD(P)H generation as a consequence of compromised TCA cycle, impaired pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and overwhelming glycolysis [15]. It is currently known that the reductant equivalents, typically NAD(P), is essential for generating the electrons and hence forming proton motive force (PMF) to drive ATP production. Besides, NAD(P)H plays a pivotal role in defensing against oxidant stress and biosynthesis of fatty acids, pyrimidine, proline and collagens, etc [16–18]. All of these bioactive precursors are in especially great demand for self-renewing and repairing of skin cells but frequently destroyed during aging or damaging. Accordingly, supplementation of NAD(P)H or its oxidative precursor NAD, as well as the intermediates like nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), has been developed for skin revitalization. However, the disadvantages such as limited bioavailability, inadequate skin penetration, transient therapeutic effects and potentially side effects limit current NAD/NADH therapy [19, 20] necessitating the discovery of innovative approaches for revitalizing the aged skin.

Thylakoids, the membrane-bound organelles derived from chloroplasts, has been recognized as a cross-species machinery to regenerate cellular energy and resuming tissue homeostasis. Armored with the photosystem (PS)-I and PS-II systems, thylakoids can efficiently convert light energy into ATP and NADPH, the latter further bolstering the PMF reconstitution and reinvigorating mitochondrial fitness in senescent cells [21, 22]. Of particular interest, thylakoid membranes possess a great deal of quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase (QPRT), an enzyme responsible for the de novo synthesis of NAD [23]. The elevated NAD level would increase NAD(P)H pools and promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative homeostasis, counteracting the deleterious effects caused by damaging factors like UV irradiation. Due to their multifaceted function and “green prosperity”, the chloroplasts or thylakoids have recently been exploited to tentatively treat the degenerative diseases such as myocardial infarction, [24] osteoarthritis [25] diabetic wounds [26], and aging pathologies [27]. Despite these advancements, the limited specificity, cell-targeting and potentially immunological rejection associated with the chloroplasts, and particularly, the lack of safe, effective and stable delivering methods significantly hamper the clinic translation of this optogenetic therapy. To address these restraints, microneedles (MNs), the micron-sized needles fabricated by biomaterials, provide a promising option to deliver the therapeutic agents into deeper tissues such as hypodermal in a painlessly and efficient way [28, 29]. Furthermore, microneedles, with specific modification, can achieve targeted, controlled, and in situ release of therapeutic materials, raising the therapeutic efficacy and minimizing the side effects [30, 31].

In this study, to address the heightened energetic and biosynthetic requirements associated with enhanced anabolism, cellular proliferation and ECM maintenance for regeneration of photoaged skin, we develop a light-driven therapeutic nanosystem (NTU@FM-MNs) that integrated self-powered nanothylakoid and transdermal microneedles to specifically target senescent fibroblasts (Fig. 1). Particularly, nanothylakoids units (NTUs) were isolated from chloroplasts of Chlorella vulgaris, a genus of autotrophic organisms efficiently synthesizing biomass and bioenergetics under light illumination. Cell membranes of fibroblasts (FM) subsequently camouflaged NTU to increase their targeted efficacy and biocompatibility. In order to overcome the limited skin-penetrating ability of the photosynthetic units, hyaluronic acid (HA)-based MNs were fabricated to deliver the NTU@FM system across the stratum corneum. HA is known as the principal component of ECM of skin and plays an essential role in maintaining skin hydration, resiliency, and overall health [32, 33]. Studies have shown that HA production declines with age, leading to skin dryness, wrinkles, and loss of elasticity, which are hallmarks of skin aging. Importantly, simple HA-based hydrogels or HA-containing regenerative moisturizers have been reported in human studies to rapidly improve, and in some cases reverse, visible signs of skin aging, including reduced wrinkles, improved hydration, enhanced elasticity, and better overall skin quality. Chiou et al. reported that regenerative moisturizers such as hyaluronic acid can enhance collagen synthesis [4]. Therefore, the incorporation of HA into the MN formulation is expected to provide additional benefits for revitalizing aged skin. Strikingly, our data showed that topical supplementation of HA microneedles enabled the painless intradermal penetration and sustained release of the NTUs. The in vitro experiments demonstrated that NTU@FM-MNs significantly resumed mitochondrial membrane potential and NAD(P)H pool, increased cellular anabolism, enhanced type I collagen synthesis, and reversed the senescent phenotypes of UVB-irradiated fibroblasts. Accordingly, administration of the light-driven nanosystem led to resumption of tissue structure and function, relief of oxidative and SASP stress, regeneration of ECM and collagens, and ultimate alleviation of photoaging of skin. Importantly, the devised green machinery demonstrated highly biocompatibility, cost-effectiveness, and excellent biosafety, indicating its translational potential in treating photoaging skin or other degenerative diseases.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of FM-NTUs

Chlorella vulgaris is one of widely cultivated eukaryotic microalga, whose extracts have been extensively utilized in the pharmaceutical products as health supplements. Previous studies have reported that C. vulgaris can be cultured in growth media under continuous illumination provided by light-emitting diode (LED). As illustrated in Fig. 2a, C. vulgaris is a unicellular green microalga with a cell diameter ranging from 2 to 10 μm. The chloroplast, which exhibits bright green autofluorescence, occupies a large portion of the cell (Fig. 2b).

In order to extract chloroplasts, C. vulgaris was subjected to sonication to breakage the cell wall, followed by centrifugation to remove cell debris. The extracted chloroplasts were further processed using an extruder to produce nanosized thylakoid units (NTUs). For the preparation of membrane-coated NTUs, cell membranes from L929 fibroblasts were mixed with NTUs at a 1:1 ratio and co-extruded to ensure a uniform coating of the cell membrane on the surface of NTUs (FM-NTUs), thereby endowing FM-NTUs with the ability to target skin fibroblasts.

Morphological characterization was conducted using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine the structure features of chloroplasts, NTUs, and FM-NTUs. As shown in Fig. 2c, TEM analysis confirmed the presence of a continuous L929 cell membrane layer on the surface of FM-NTUs. In addition, dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the average hydrodynamic diameters of NTUs, L929 cell membranes, and FM-NTUs were 50, 170, and 200 nm, respectively, with corresponding polydispersity index (PDI) values of 0.172 ± 0.009, 0.127 ± 0.012, and 0.214 ± 0.038 (Fig. 2d and e). The zeta potentials of NTUs, L929 cell membranes, and FM-NTUs were − 6 mV, -22 mV, and − 19 mV, respectively (Fig. 2f). To assess the stability of NTU particles in a physiological environment, NTUs were stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and their particle sizes were measured on days 0, 3, and 7. The results showed that the particle sizes of NTUs remained relatively stable over the storage period, with no significant differences observed among day 0, day 3, and day 7, indicating good storage stability of NTUs in PBS (Figure S1).

Thylakoids are well-known for their capacity to synthesize adenosine triphosphate (ATP) under light illumination. Given that ATP synthesis is influenced by light intensity, the influence of varying light intensities on ATP production was also investigated. ATP generation increased with increasing light intensity from 0 to 72 µmol photons·m⁻²·s⁻¹, whereas at higher light intensity (108 µmol photons·m⁻²·s⁻¹), the ATP production rate began to decline (Fig. 2g). Therefore, a light intensity of 72 µmol photons·m⁻²·s⁻¹ was selected for subsequent experiments. To evaluate the ATP synthesis capacity of FM-NTUs, ATP production was monitored over time under red-light illumination (72 µmol photons·m⁻²·s⁻¹). The results showed that ATP production remained stable for the first two hours but gradually declined after four hours, ultimately decreasing to approximately 20% of the initial level by 32 h (Fig. 2h).

Under dark conditions, thylakoids primarily undergo dark reactions, whereas light illumination rapidly activates light-dependent reactions, leading to substantial production of ATP and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). The synthesis capacities of ATP and NADPH in FM-NTUs were evaluated under both dark and light conditions. In the absence of light, ATP and NADPH production occurred at a slow rate. By contrast, light exposure triggered a rapid increase in the synthesis of both ATP and NADPH, demonstrating that illumination significantly enhanced the photosynthetic activity of FM-NTUs (Fig. 2i and j). As QPRT is a key enzymatic component linking the nanophotosystem to NAD⁺ biosynthesis and subsequent mitochondrial recovery, phthalic acid (PA) was used to inhibit QPRT activity. Upon treatment with PA, the light-driven production of ATP and NADPH by FM-NTU was significantly reduced (Fig. 2k and l). Collectively, these results confirmed the successful preparation of L929 cell membrane-coated NTUs with robust light-driven ATP and NADPH generation capabilities.

Cellular uptake and biocompatibility of FM-NTUs

Before evaluate the cellular ATP production capability of FM-NTUs, the uptake process and biocompatibility of FM-NTUs were investigated. First, the cytotoxicity of FM-NTUs on L929 cells was assessed, and the results exhibited favorable biocompatibility (Figure S2). Subsequently, the internalization process was explored using DiD-labeled FM-NTUs, which were hypothesized to exhibit homologous targeting capabilities due to the coating with L929 cell membranes. FM-NTUs were co-cultured with L929 cells for varying durations to observe their cellular uptake behavior. The results indicated that the internalization of NTUs increased in a time-dependent manner from 2 to 12 h and reached a plateau after 12 h, as no significant difference was observed between 12 and 16 h (Fig. 3a and b). When compared with bare NTUs, the cell membrane coating significantly enhanced the internalization of NTUs (Fig. 3c and e).

To elucidate the internalization mechanism of FM-NTUs into L929 cells, the cell membranes and NTUs were separately labeled by DiO and DiD dyes before preparing FM-NTUs. After incubating L929 cells with dye-labeled FM-NTUs, the uptake behavior was observed. The results showed that the membrane coating of FM-NTUs fused with the plasma membrane of L929 cells, facilitating the entry of NTUs into the cells (Fig. 3d). After 6 h of incubation, dissociation of the DIO-labeled FM from DID-labeled NTU was observed. To investigate the final intracellular location of NTUs, lysosomes were labeled with LysoTracker, and the co-localization of internalized NTUs with lysosomes was analyzed. The absence of co-localization after 2-hour incubation indicated that NTUs evaded lysosomal degradation after entering the cells, thereby preserving their functionality (Fig. 3f and g).

To delve deeper into the internalization mechanism of FM-NTUs, L929 cells were pre-treated with specific inhibitors targeting macropinocytosis (amiloride), clathrin (chlorpromazine, CPZ), and caveolae-dependent endocytosis (Filipin). Subsequently, the inhibitor-treated cells were co-cultured with DiD-labeled FM-NTUs, and the cellular uptake was quantified analyzed using flow cytometry. The results indicated that none of the aforementioned inhibitors significantly suppressed the internalization of FM-NTUs. In contrast, a low-temperature treatment (4℃) led to a substantial reduction in FM-NTUs uptake (Fig. 3h). This observation could be attributed to the decreased membrane fluidity at low temperatures, which hindered the internalization process. Therefore, these finding suggested that FM-NTUs may enter cells through a membrane fusion mechanism. Finally, the targeting ability of FM-NTUs toward different cell types was evaluated. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that L929 cell membrane-coated NTUs exhibited remarkable targeting specificity toward L929 cells, underscoring their potential for targeted cellular delivery applications (Fig. 3i and j).

FM-NTUs reverse the senescent phenotypes of fibroblasts

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is a primary contributor to skin photoaging, and ultraviolet B (UVB)-induced cellular senescence serves as a pivotal model for anti-aging research. To evaluate the efficacy of FM-NTUs in mitigating UVB-induced cellular senescence, L929 cells were first exposed to UVB radiation and subsequently treated with FM-NTUs under both light and dark conditions. The expression of β-galactosidase (β-gal), a well-recognized biomarker of cellular senescence, was significantly upregulated in UVB-exposed L929 cells, validating the successful establishment of an in vitro cellular senescence model (Fig. 4a). Notably, compared to dark condition, FM-NTU treatment followed by light exposure led to a substantial reduction in β-gal expression (Fig. 4a and b). This finding strongly indicated that FM-NTUs could effectively alleviate UVB-induced cellular senescence in a light-dependent manner.

Further mechanistic exploration was conducted by quantifying the expression levels of senescence-associated proteins, including p53, p21, and p16. UVB irradiation markedly upregulated the expression of these proteins, while co-incubation with FM-NTUs under light conditions resulted in their downregulation, further confirming the anti-senescence effects of FM-NTUs (Fig. 4c and Figure S3). Additionally, γ-H2AX, a sensitive marker of DNA damage and cellular senescence, was significantly elevated in UVB-treated L929 cells. However, treatment with FM-NTUs under light conditions led to a notable decrease in γ-H2AX expression, suggesting a reduction in DNA damage and subsequent senescence (Fig. 4d and f). Consistently, the expression pattern of p21 followed the same trend of γ-H2AX further corroborated the anti-senescence function of FM-NTUs (Fig. 4e and g).

Skin inflammation ensues when senescent cells accumulate within the cutaneous skin. As illustrated in Fig. 4h and m, UVB exposure led to a significant upregulation of inflammation-related genes (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and collagen degradation-associated genes (MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-9). Conversely, treatment with FM-NTUs under light illumination resulted in the downregulation of these genes, indicating a substantial reduction in inflammatory responses and extracellular matrix degradation (Fig. 4h and m). Notably, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), a pivotal regulator of tissue repair and anti-inflammatory processes, exhibited a contrasting expression pattern. UVB irradiation downregulated TGF-β expression whereas FM-NTUs treatment under light conditions effectively upregulated TGF-β levels (Fig. 4n). In summary, these results demonstrate that FM-NTUs, when activated by light, can effectively alleviate UVB-induced cellular senescence through multiple mechanisms. Specially, FM-NTUs modulate senescence-associated biomarkers, reduce DNA damage, suppress inflammation cascades, and promoting tissue repair pathways, thereby offering a novel approach for combating skin photoaging.

Rectification of mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic deficits by FM- NTUs in senescent fibroblasts

The process of cellular senescence is intricately associated with mitochondrial function. To further investigate whether the alleviation of cellular senescence by FM-NTUs upon light illumination is related to the enhancement of mitochondrial function in cells, we initially assessed the level of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS). The results demonstrated that FM-NTUs, following light exposure, could significantly suppress the UVB-induced mitochondrial ROS production in cells (Fig. 5a and b). Simultaneously, mitochondria were labeled with JC-1 dye to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential. The results revealed that FM-NTUs could markedly ameliorate the mitochondrial dysfunction induced by UVB under light irradiation conditions (Fig. 5c). Moreover, the production levels of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) were also elevated (Fig. 5d and e). Specifically, inhibition of QPRT activity by phthalic acid significantly attenuated the FM-NTU-induced increases in ATP and NAD⁺/NADPH levels (Fig. 5d and e). In parallel, the beneficial effects of FM-NTUs on mitochondrial function were markedly reduced, accompanied by an increase in mitochondrial ROS production in cells (Fig. 5a and e).

Additionally, we examined the expression of mitochondrial-related proteins. The expression of genes associated with mitochondrial complexes I, II, III, IV, and V was downregulated in UVB-treated L929 cells. However, after cultivation with FM-NTUs and subsequent light irradiation, the expression of genes related to mitochondrial complexes was upregulated (Fig. 5f). In addition, following treatment of L929 cells with FM-NTUs and light exposure for a certain period, the transcription level of mitochondrial-related proteins such as NDUFV2, COX15, and ATP5H was also upregulated (Fig. 5g and Figure S3). Based on the aforementioned results, it is postulated that FM-NTUs, when co-cultured with senescent L929 cells, may alleviate the cellular senescence process by increasing the intracellular synthesis of ATP and NADPH, enhancing the expression of genes related to mitochondrial complexes, thereby improving mitochondrial function and reducing the generation of mitochondrial ROS. Nevertheless, further experiments are needed to validate intracellular de novo ATP/NADPH synthesis by internalized NTUs, particularly to distinguish this mechanism from indirect effects on endogenous mitochondrial pathways.

Fabrication of FM-NTU-loaded microneedle patch for transdermal delivery

Given the excellent in vitro anti-photodamage efficacy of FM-NTUs, we wondered whether they could exert robust anti-aging effect in vivo. Upon exposure to UV radiation (especially the UVB), the epidermis undergoes thickening, dermal collagen fibers diminish in quantity and exhibit disordered architecture, collectively contributing to skin aging. Microneedle (MN) patches, a cutting-edge transdermal drug-delivery system, consist of an array comprising hundreds of micrometer-scale needle tips (typical 150–400 μm in length). These tips are orderly arranged on a base substrate enabling a uniform transdermal release of bioactive agents.

Herein, a core-shell structured hydrogel hybrid microneedle patch was fabricated. Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) was dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM) to form a homogeneous solution, which was subsequently cast into a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold, and a solid PLGA shell was formed via solvent volatilization. Subsequently, methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HA-MA) was synthesized as the core filling material for microneedles. FM-NTUs (1 × 10¹¹ particles/mL) were incorporated into a 10% (w/v) HA-MA solution and crosslinked under UV irradiation in the presence of 0.5% (w/v) HMPP as the photoinitiator. As illustrated in Fig. 6a and b, and Figure S4a, a representative microneedle patch was successfully fabricated, featuring a 20 × 20 needle array with a tip height of approximately 600 μm. In addition, the results showed that the microneedles exhibited sufficient mechanical strength for skin insertion, with a measured force of 45.2 N, which exceeds the force generally required to penetrate the skin (Figure S4b).

To further characterize the core-shell structure of the microneedles, the PLGA solution were premixed with FITC and the FM-NTUs were labeled with DiD. A green fluorescence clearly appeared in the shell of microneedles, while the core content displayed red fluorescence, with FM-NTUs homogeneously dispersed within the hydrogel core (Fig. 6c and d). This distinct dual-fluorescence labeling confirmed the successful fabrication of core-shell structured microneedles and the uniform encapsulation of FM-NTUs. To evaluate the transdermal puncture capability, the microneedle patch was applied to the dorsal skin of mice. After different puncture times (0, 5, 10 min), the patches were gently removed to assess needle tip integrity and investigate the dissolution of patch in the skin (Fig. 6e). It was found that most microneedles completely dissolved within 10 min, with no apparent skin irritation detected at the application site throughout the observation period (Fig. 6e and f).

To compare the distribution of FM-NTUs via different delivery approach, FM-NTUs were labeled with DiO following transdermal delivery via microneedles or injected subcutaneously into the dorsal skin of mice. The tissue distribution showed that injection with a syringe needle led to uneven delivery of FM-NTUs, with aggregation in specific areas of the dermis and subcutis (Fig. 6g, left). In contrast, FM-NTUs delivered by microneedles was better dispersed in the dermis and subcutis (Fig. 6g, right). To evaluate the in vivo homologous targeting capability conferred by the fibroblast membrane, the fibroblast-specific accumulation of FM-NTUs was investigated. FM-NTUs were pre-labeled with DiO and delivered into the skin via microneedles. The results showed a higher correlation coefficient (0.91) between FM-NTUs and the fibroblast marker vimentin than with other dermal cell markers, including F4/80 for macrophages (0.07) and CD31 for endothelial cells (0.27). These findings provide direct evidence that FM-NTUs preferentially accumulate in fibroblasts in vivo (Fig. 6h and i). In summary, we successfully prepared a microneedle patch loaded with FM-NTUs, which exhibits excellent delivery efficiency.

FM-NTUs@MNs ameliorate skin aging in UVB-irradiated mice

In order to evaluate the anti-aging efficacy of FM-NTUs@MNs on photoaged skin, a nude mouse model of skin photoaging was established via continuous UVB irradiation for 8 weeks. During this period, the UV intensity was gradually increased from 60 mJ/cm2 minimal erythema dose (MED) to 240 mJ/cm2 until the photoaging model was successfully established (Fig. 7a). The mice were randomly assigned to five groups: Control group, UVB-only, UVB + blank microneedle patch, UVB + FM-NTUs@MNs (dark conditions), and UVB + FM-NTUs@MNs (light conditions). After treatment for 2 weeks, mice were sacrificed, and their skin tissues were harvested for analysis. Compared with the UVB-only group, FM-NTUs@MNs significantly reduced both the area and number of wrinkles under light conditions (Fig. 7b and f). Histopathological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated that FM-NTUs@MNs markedly increased dermal thickness while reducing epidermal thickness, indicating improved skin architecture (Fig. 7c and g, and 7h). In addition, the elevated expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP-9, was significantly reversed by FM-NTUs@MNs treatment (Fig. 7d and i). To further assess collagen fiber regeneration, Picro-Sirius Red staining was performed to evaluate collagen deposition. As shown in Figs. 7e, UVB irradiation disrupted collagen fiber organization and caused voids between collagen fibers. However, Sirius Red staining of the dorsal skin showed a significant increase in collagen fiber deposition after treatment, with densely organized collagen fibers observed in the FM-NTU@MNs/L group. Given the inflammatory nature of skin aging, mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β were quantified. UVB exposure induced a significant upregulation of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which was reversed by FM-NTU@MNs treatment (Fig. 8c, h and e). In contrast, the mRNA expression level of TGF-β was significantly increased after treatment with FM-NTU@MNs, consistent with the in vitro findings (Fig. 8f). Additionally, UVB irradiation upregulated the transcription of matrix metalloproteinases, including MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-9, whereas treatment with FM-NTUs@MNs suppressed their expression, suggesting a role in extracellular matrix remodeling (Fig. 8g, h and i). Collectively, these results demonstrated that UVB-induced photoaging promotes inflammatory responses and MMP-mediated collagen degradation, leading to dermal atrophy and wrinkle formation. By contrast, FM-NTU@MNs treatment, especially under light irradiation, mitigates skin aging by suppressing the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and enhancing collagen biosynthesis. Because the effective light intensity reaching the dermis in vivo may be attenuated by the stratum corneum and epidermis, future studies are needed to quantify light transmission through skin and optimize irradiation parameters under clinically relevant conditions.

The biocompatibility and safety evaluation of FM-NTU@MNs

The in vivo safety of transdermal delivery system is essential for their applications in clinical treatment. Because DCM was used to solubilize PLGA during preparation and was evaporated before microneedle insertion, its residual content was evaluated as part of the safety assessment. Therefore, residual DCM was analyzed by gas chromatography (HS-GC), and the measured level was 5.57 ppm, which is approximately 100-fold lower than the permitted limit for pharmaceutical products and medical devices (600 ppm) (Figure S5).

Because hyaluronidase is an endogenous enzyme widely distributed in vivo, HA-MA is expected to undergo gradual enzymatic degradation after administration. To further examine the degradation behavior of crosslinked HA-MA, an in vitro degradation study was performed by incubating HA-MA hydrogel with hyaluronidase at concentrations ranging from 5 to 20 U/mL. The results demonstrated that HA-MA hydrogel was readily degraded by 20 U/mL hyaluronidase within 24 h, confirming its favorable biodegradability (Figure S6a). In addition, degradation of HA resulted primarily in the generation of uronic acid and other HA-derived fragments. To evaluate the degradation behavior of the HA-based material, the amount of released uronic acid, a representative degradation product of HA, was measured using a carbazole reaction technique [34]. It was found that the level of released uronic acid gradually increased during the incubation period, demonstrating the progressive breakdown of the HA network (Figure S6b). The degradability of the HA-MA hydrogel reduces the risk of long-term accumulation, and its degradation products can be naturally cleared through physiological pathways, demonstrating the biosafety of HA-MA and its suitability for in vivo biomedical applications.

To evaluate the biological safety of FM-NTU-loaded microneedle patches during the treatment of photodamaged skin of nude mouse, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested from the mice. Histopathological analysis via H&E staining revealed that no significant structural abnormalities or pathological lesions occurred in the microneedle-treated group when compared with the healthy control group (Figure S7). Additionally, hematological and biochemical evaluations were conducted to characterize systemic safety. Blood cell counts, including red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), hemoglobin (HGB), and platelets (PLT), showed no statistically significant differences among all experimental groups (Figure S8). Similarly, serum levels of key renal and hepatic markers, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), remained within normal physiological ranges, with no notable discrepancies among groups (Figure S8).

A key translational limitation of chloroplast-derived optogenetic systems lies in the potential immunological risks of plant-derived biomaterials and the absence of a clinically practical delivery method. As reported previously, repeated administration of chloroplast-derived nanothylakoids over a 28-day period demonstrated good immune stability, with no significant alterations in serum IgG/IgE levels, peripheral blood cell counts, or inflammatory cytokine levels [35]. In addition, other studies on chloroplast-derived systems have indicated that chlorophyll and its degradation products are non-genotoxic and exhibit minimal immunogenicity [36–38]. In the present work, our platform enables minimally invasive, spatially confined delivery to target skin tissue, potentially reducing systemic dissemination and unwanted immune activation. In addition, the use of isolated thylakoids rather than intact chloroplasts preserves light responsiveness while avoiding the greater complexity of whole organelles. Together, these features suggest improved safety and translational potential for in vivo application. Nevertheless, further studies are required to comprehensively evaluate long-term immune compatibility and biosafety.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study developed an innovative photosynthetic system that demonstrated the remarkable potential in revitalizing senescent fibroblasts and reversing the aging manifestation in photoaged skin. By leveraging chloroplast-derived PSI/II, QPRT and other bioactive machineries, FM-NTU@MNs significantly improved mitochondrial biogenesis and bioenergetics supply, increased the NAD(P)H pool and redox balance, enhanced anabolism and collagen synthesis, thereby creating an optimized microenvironment for repairing and regeneration of damaged skin. With the camouflage of fibroblast membranes, we endowed the nanosystem with cell targeting ability and resistance to immunological rejection, facilitating its cross-species application. Additional benefits were from HA-based microneedles, which enabled the therapeutic agent to across the stratum corneum for in situ release and action, addressing the limitation of poor cell penetrating of current therapies. Thus, the present study introduces a concept of building an organelle-like photosynthetic system, which may provide a bioinspired strategy to support the energetic and biosynthetic demands required for skin regeneration. Although FM-NTU@MNs exhibited promising anti-photoaging activity in cellular and animal models, the potential clinical advantage of this treatment strategy remains to be verified in future human studies. Given its encouraging efficacy, feasibility, biocompatibility and biosafety in the present preclinical models, the engineering technology developed herein might ultimately be translated into a clinically relevant approach for treating aged skin or other degenerative diseases. To achieve this goal, extensive studies on both the mechanism of action and methodological optimization under physiologically relevant chronic exposure conditions are still needed.

Experimental methods

Materials: JC-1 staining kit, DID, DIO, and LysoTracker were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Mito SOX was purchased from Invitrogen (Waltham, USA). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-p53 (mouse monoclonal, 1:1000, Cat# sc-71820, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-p21 (mouse monoclonal, 1:500, Cat# sc-471, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-p16 (rabbit monoclonal, 1:500, Cat# 10883-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-COX15 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, Cat# 11441-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-NDUFV2 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, Cat# 15301-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-ATP5H (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, Cat# 17589-1-AP, Proteintech, China), and anti-RbcL (Mouse monoclonal, 1:1000, Cat# PHY1927, PhytoAB, USA). HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, Cat# SA00001-2, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:2000, Cat# SA00001-1, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were used as secondary antibodies.

Chloroplast isolation

Chloroplasts were extracted from Chlorella vulgaris using a chloroplast isolation Kit with previously reported methods [39, 40]. Briefly, Chlorella vulgaris in logarithmic growth phase was centrifuged at 2,500 ×g for 10 min. The resulting pellet was then resuspended in 5 mL of wash buffer and incubated at room temperature for 3 min, followed by a repeated centrifugation at 2,000 ×g for 10 min. Subsequently, the pellet was treated with 10 mL of extraction buffer, subjected to homogenization via sonication for 15 min, and filtered through a 40-µm cell strainer. The filtrate was centrifuged at 800 ×g for 5 min, after which the supernatant was further centrifuged at 2,000 ×g for 15 min. The chloroplast pellet was washed twice, resuspended in storage buffer and stored at 4 °C.

L929 cell membrane extraction

L929 cell line was purchased from BOSTER Biological Technology co.ltd, and maintained in our laboratory. L929 cells were suspended in an ice-cold membrane protein extraction buffer, followed by sonication for 5 min on the ice. The resulting homogenate was then centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The process was repeated three times to remove intact cells and nuclei. Subsequently, the supernatant underwent ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C to isolate the cell membranes, which were stored at -80 °C.

Cell membrane coating of thylakoids (FM-NTUs)

Thylakoids and L929 cell membranes were mixed and sonicated for 2 min. The resulting mixture was extruded through a 100-nm polycarbonate membrane using an Avanti mini extruder. The extrudate was centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 60 min at 4 °C, after which the pellet was resuspended in a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and supplemented with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for long-term storage.

ATP/NADPH synthesis assay

FM-NTUs photosynthetic activity was assessed in a 1 mL reaction mixture comprising 50 mM HEPES-KOH buffer (pH 7.8), 3 mM ADP, 5 mM K₂HPO₄, 3 mM NADP⁺, 10 mM sodium ascorbate, 5 mM MgCl₂, and 1.5 µM catalase. The samples were exposed to red light at a wavelength of 630 nm, and aliquots were collected at intervals of 5 min for the quantification of ATP utilizing an ATP Assay Kit. Similarly, NADPH production was monitored over 30 min at 5-min intervals using a NADPH Assay Kit. Control experiments were conducted under light and dark conditions.

Cellular uptake of FM-NTUs

L929 cells were incubated with DiD-labeled FM-NTUs for 2, 4, 8, 12, or 16 h. After that, L929 cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), stained with DAPI, and imaged using confocal microscopy (Zeiss, Germany).

Membrane fusion assay

L929 cell membranes were prelabeled with DiO dyes. After that, FM-NTUs were co-cultured with L929 cell for 8 h. Subsequently, L929 cells were fixed with 4% PFA and imaged using confocal microscopy.

JC-1 Staining

L929 cells were stained with mitochondrial membrane potential probe JC-1 (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescent images of mitochondria, which labeled with the JC-1 probe, were obtained utilizing a confocal microscope.

β-Galactosidase staining

L929 cells were fixed using 4% PFA and stained with a Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Skin tissues were frozen in OCT on dry ice and sectioned into a thickness of 10 μm. The frozen sections were immersed in staining solution overnight in the dark and imaged using confocal microscopy.

Western blotting

L929 cells were lysed by using RIPA buffer and the whole cell extracts were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Target proteins were probed with primary and secondary antibodies and the signal detection was accomplished using ECL chemiluminescence kit (Bio-Rad).

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix. Amplification and quantification of cDNA were performed using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit with the specific primers listed in Table 1. Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin and analyzed employing the 2 − ΔΔCt method.

Immunofluorescence

L929 cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. The cells were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for 12 h, followed by incubation with FITC- or Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies, and imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Mitochondrial ROS detection

L929 cells were incubated with 5 µM MitoSOX Red for 20 min in the dark, washed, and subsequently imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Uronic acid assay

Uronic acid released into the degradation medium was quantified using a carbazole reaction assay according to an established method [34]. Briefly, a 100 µL aliquot of the degradation solution was mixed with a sulfuric acid/sodium tetraborate decahydrate solution and incubated at 100 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of 0.125% carbazole in absolute ethanol was added, and the mixture was further heated at 100 °C for 15 min. After the reaction was complete, the absorbance was measured at 530 nm. The uronic acid concentration in each sample was quantified by comparison with a calibration curve generated from standard solutions of known concentrations.

Microneedle fabrication: PLGA (50:50, Mw = 15,000) was obtained from Jinan Daigang Biomaterial Co., Ltd. Hyaluronic acid (10 kDa) was obtained from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd and chemically modified via methacrylation to produce methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HA-MA). The fabrication process was performed according to previously reported methods [41, 42]. PLGA was solubilized in dichloromethane (DCM) with a ratio of 1:3 (w/w) and cast into molds made of PDMS. Following the evaporation of DCM, a solid PLGA shell was formed. The casting process was repeated iteratively to achieve the desired thickness and structural uniformity of the PLGA shell. Further, a HA-MA solution, which containing FM-NTUs and 0.5% 2-Hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP) as a photoinitiator, was prepared in a PBS solution and introduced into the PLGA-shelled molds. UV light (254 nm) was employed to initiate the crosslinking of HA-MA. The molds were further filled with a 15% hyaluronic acid as substrate. Microneedles were demolded and utilized for anti-aging therapy.

Skin penetration assay

Mouse were shaved and removed the hair from dorsal skin using depilatory cream. Dio-labeled FM-NTU@MN patch was slowly pressed on the skin for 5, 10 min and gently pulled out. After that, skin was observed under a microscope to examine the recovery of the pierced site. Additionally, skin tissue was embedded in OCT and frozen in liquid nitrogen, sectioned to a thickness of 12 μm. The skin sections were stained with DAPI and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Establishment of photoaging model: All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Shuren University. UV irradiation induces the photoaging was performed according to previously reported methods [35]. Briefly, nude mice (3-month-old) were subjected to UVB irradiation on their dorsal skin at a height of 30 cm every other day for 8 weeks. The irradiation dosages were as follows: 1 MED (60 mJ/cm²) during weeks 1–2, 2 MED (120 mJ/cm²) in week 3, 3 MED (180 mJ/cm²) in week 4, 4 MED (240 mJ/cm²) during weeks 5–8, culminating in a total cumulative UVB dose of approximately 80 MED.

H&E Staining and Picro-Sirius Red staining: Paraffin-embedded tissue samples were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol solutions, followed by hematoxylin staining for 5 min and eosin staining for 30 s. Finally, the stained sections were mounted with neutral resin and examined under a light microscope. To assess changes in collagen fibers, skin sections were subjected to Sirius Red staining using commercial kits. Stained sections were then imaged, and the resulting histological images were quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, and differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
