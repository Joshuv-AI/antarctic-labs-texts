Risk assessment of mountain tunnel collapse under complex geological conditions
Authors: Shantong Lu, Zhiyang Lu, Shuo Xu, Renyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMountain tunnels have the characteristics of long construction period, multiple uncontrollable factors, and high construction difficulty. To investigate collapse risks associated with complex geological conditions, a deep-buried mountain tunnel in Baise, Guangxi, China, was selected as a case study. The risk of tunnel collapse during construction was analyzed using theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and field monitoring. The research results indicate that: (1) using intuitionistic fuzzy theory and skewed normal weighing function can better evaluate the risk of mountain tunnel collapse. The analysis shows that the consequence level of Baise Tunnel is III (severe); (2) The construction of three-bench method with temporary cross braces, combined with long anchor rod support and deep grouting, can effectively control the deformation of surrounding rock; (3) Based on the risk assessment and numerical simulation results, several on-site construction measures were proposed to mitigate the risk of mountain tunnel collapse. These include segmented excavation at tunnel entrances, increased monitoring frequency, and timely closure of the surrounding rock. The research results can effectively improve the safety of tunnel construction, save energy and costs, and promote the transformation of engineering construction towards green and low-carbon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48658-1.Keywords: Mountain tunnel, Intuitionistic fuzzy theory, Theoretical analysis, Construction scheme, Green buildingSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Despite significant advances in tunnel construction, safety issues remain prominent, particularly in mountainous regions where complex geological conditions and construction uncertainties frequently lead to accidents such as collapses, sudden water inrush, rock bursts, and severe deformation. Additionally, as tunnel construction extends to the mountainous regions in western China, accidents are becoming more prevalent because of high altitudes, high ground temperatures, and high ground stress1–5. In response, the Ministry of Railways of China and the State Administration of Work Safety have emphasized the importance of risk awareness and emergency management measures in tunnel engineering projects.

Compared to other engineering projects, tunnels in mountainous regions suffer from complex geological environments and various uncontrollable factors. Accidents occurring in these tunnels can cause irreversible losses, adversely affecting the whole society. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms of tunnel accidents and conducting risk assessments to keep direct and indirect losses within acceptable limits are crucial for preventing accidents in mountain tunnel projects6–8. The complex and changeable geological environments in these channels make it challenging to capture the geological and hydrological characteristics in the early stages of tunnel construction, resulting in information delay and thus tunnel lining design that fails to fully meet the stability requirements9,10. Consequently, tunnels built in mountainous regions face many risks from the design stage to construction and then operation. Research on the mechanisms of accidents and risk assessment during construction can provide a risk factor list for designers, builders, and operators, helping them implement targeted measures against major risks and weak links, thereby improving the overall decision-making quality for tunnel projects.

Great achievements have been made in research on the collapse risk in mountain tunnels under complex geological conditions. Senent et al.11, considering the possibility of partial collapses in layered soils, conducted a parametric study on the influence of various geometric and mechanical parameters on tunnel face instability using a rotation collapse mechanism. The results showed that weak material in the upper part of the tunnel face could significantly increase collapse pressure, making a partial collapse possible. Yang et al.12 proposed a new collapse mechanism of the tunnel roof based on a nonlinear failure criterion. They used limit analysis and variational principles to obtain results related to the potential roof stability. A subsequent parametric study helped derive the changing patterns of the impacts on collapse shapes by the respective parameters. Liu et al.13 developed a three-dimensional collapse mechanism that considers the collapse of both the tunnel roof and sidewalls. By employing the power balance principle in the upper bound theorem, they established a 3D upper bound support pressure formula. The results indicated that tunnel stability increased with higher nonlinear failure coefficients, tensile strength, initial cohesion, and tangential internal friction angle, but was threatened by greater burial depth, unit weight, tunnel width, and height. Chen et al.14 investigated the impact of cavities in phyllite strata on railway tunnel collapse through field surveys, on-site monitoring, and numerical simulations to analyze the characteristics and failure mechanisms and tunnel collapse. They found that tunnel excavation caused groundwater seepage towards the cavity and the water invading resulted in phyllite dissolution and weakened mechanical properties of surrounding rock, which were the main causes of the collapse. Lyu et al.15 employed the nonlinear Hoek-Brown yield criterion and limit analysis theorem to establish a new two-dimensional progressive unsymmetrical collapse mechanism for tunnels in inclined rock strata. They analyzed the collapse characteristics and derived formulas for calculating the range and total weight of unsymmetrical collapsing blocks in rectangular and circular tunnels. The validity of the method proposed was confirmed through comparisons with existing studies.

Tunnel collapse risk assessment involves significant uncertainty and often relies on expert judgment due to the complexity and variability of geological conditions. Traditional probabilistic approaches generally require sufficient statistical data, which are often unavailable in tunnel engineering projects. Although classical fuzzy set theory has been widely applied in engineering risk assessment, it only considers membership degree and cannot adequately capture the hesitation and uncertainty inherent in expert evaluations. Intuitionistic fuzzy sets (IFS), which incorporate membership, non-membership, and hesitation degrees, provide a more comprehensive framework for representing uncertain information. Therefore, this study adopts intuitionistic fuzzy theory combined with a dynamic weighting method to establish a collapse risk assessment model for mountain tunnels under complex geological conditions. A deep-buried tunnel in Baise, Guangxi, China, is selected as a case study to validate the proposed approach through theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and field monitoring.

The Baise region in Guangxi Province is characterized by complex geological and hydrological conditions. The mountainous terrain is composed mainly of highly weathered sedimentary rocks such as siltstone and limestone with well-developed joints and fissures. In addition, the region experiences intense seasonal rainfall and significant groundwater activity, which may weaken the mechanical properties of surrounding rock and increase the risk of tunnel collapse during excavation. These unfavorable geological conditions make tunnel construction in this region particularly challenging and provide an ideal case for investigating collapse risk assessment methods.

Establishment of risk indexes for mountain tunnels

Bourne et al.16 argued that the factors influencing system evolution are mainly related to four aspects: internal, external, process, and transformation. The tunnel construction process faces a wide range of complex and hazardous environmental factors, which constitute both internal and external factors for tunnel collapse. Meanwhile, the construction conditions of tunnel projects in mountainous regions and the techniques required are far more challenging than those of conventional tunnel projects. The strata and construction methods change as excavation progresses, providing process and transformation factors for tunnel collapse. According to the Technical Code for Risk Management of Railway Tunnel Engineering (Q/CR 9247 − 2016), the risk factors for tunnel collapse are categorized into geological factors, support design factors, construction factors, and management factors.

Geological factors

Geological conditions for engineering are complex and unpredictable. A tunnel passing through an area with unfavorable geological conditions disrupts the stability of the surrounding rock structure and then leads to a redistribution of stress within the rock. Minor disruptions may cause rock blocks to fall, while severe ones can result in tunnel collapse.

Support design factors

Inaccurate advanced geological prediction or investigation reports during the design phase may prevent tunnel designers from precisely obtaining the properties of the surrounding rock. The resulting inaccurate design may result in inadequate support strength that cannot withstand tunnel deformation, ultimately causing collapse.

Construction factors

Insufficient understanding of geological conditions may lead to inappropriate selection of construction techniques, such as excessive use of explosives, delayed or inadequate support, or long exposure of the surrounding rock. Inappropriate methods without corrective measures may cause surrounding rock collapse. Additionally, improper handling of collapse may lead to more severe collapse.

Management factors

Monitoring, measurement, and safety management during tunnel construction are crucial. Many accidents occur in the construction phase due to weak safety awareness, inadequate safety inspections, and insufficient control over site operations.

Based on the above analysis and the SMART principle, four first-level indexes and fifteen second-level indexes were established to form a comprehensive risk assessment system for tunnels built in mountainous regions, as shown in Fig. 1. The system includes four first-level risk categories: geological factors, support design factors, construction factors, and management factors. These categories are further divided into fifteen second-level indexes representing specific risk sources that may influence tunnel collapse during construction. Then, the risk indexes were quantified through expert scoring based on the consequence levels defined in the Technical Code for Risk Management of Railway Tunnel Engineering (Q/CR 9247 − 2016).

Risk assessment of mountain tunnel collapse based on intuitionistic fuzzy sets and dynamic weighting

Considering the uncertainty and ambiguity in expert evaluations of tunnel construction risks, the intuitionistic fuzzy set theory was adopted in this study. Compared with traditional fuzzy methods, intuitionistic fuzzy sets can simultaneously describe the membership degree, non-membership degree, and hesitation degree of an evaluation, thus providing a more accurate representation of uncertain decision information. This method has been widely applied in multi-attribute decision-making and engineering risk assessment.

Intuitionistic fuzzy number and hesitancy degree

Let A={A1,A2…Am} be an index set consisting of m indexes Aj(j = 1,2,…,m) and D={D1,D2…Dn} be a decision set consisting of n experts Di. The decision evaluation by expert Di on the index Aj is denoted as an intuitionistic fuzzy number xij=(uij,vij) where 0 ≤ uij≤l, 0 ≤ vij≤l, and 0 ≤ uij(x)+vij(x) ≤ l. uij(x) and vij(x) represent a membership function and a non-membership function, respectively, which express the uncertainty of the fuzzy object from two perspectives. The hesitancy degree πij = 1-uij-vij expresses to what extent the fuzzy object is unknown.

Project background

A mountain tunnel located in Baise, Guangxi Province, China was selected for the case study. The total length of the tunnel is 2,050 m, and its location is highly undulating, with the presence of unsymmetrical pressure distribution. Additionally, the region experiences heavy rainfall during the rainy season. The detailed geological conditions are as follows: the soil is predominantly silty clay derived from residual slope deposits, with poor erosion resistance; the surrounding rock is classified as grade V, comprising fragmented, heavily weathered siltstone, moderately weathered siltstone, tectonic cataclasite, and limestone, all characterized by highly developed joints and fissures. The bench method was adopted for tunnel excavation, supplemented by advanced pipe-roof support. The shallow-buried sections required reinforcement, along with corresponding engineering waterproofing and drainage measures. Due to the fractured surrounding rock and poor geological conditions at the tunnel entrance, management emphasizes the principle of “early entry, late exit”. This principle refers to advancing the support installation close to the excavation face while delaying the removal of temporary supports. It helps reduce the exposure time of unsupported surrounding rock, thereby improving stability. Figure 3 illustrates the surrounding rock conditions of the tunnel.

Comparative analysis of different anchor rod lengths

Anchor rods are a support measure used to stabilize rock masses or soil, and their length directly impacts the effectiveness of the support system. Longer rods can better secure the surrounding rock or soil, enhancing the stability and reliability of the support system, but they also require more construction time, which may extend the project duration. This section discusses the influence of different anchor rod lengths on the stability of mountain tunnels under complex geological conditions. The results showed that as the anchor rod length increased, the maximum horizontal displacement on both sides of the tunnel showed an overall decreasing trend. Specifically, when the rod length rose from 4 to 5 m, the decline was not significant; when the rod length rose from 5 to 6 m, the horizontal displacement greatly declined. Therefore, it can be inferred that under complex geological conditions such as weak rock layers and soft soil layers, longer anchor rods can fix the anchor point in deeper, stable strata, thereby improving the support structure’s ability to resist deformation and slippage. In terms of vertical displacements, longer anchor rods alleviated both crown settlement and arch bottom heaving. Specifically, when the rod length rose from 4 to 5 m, the maximum crown settlement and arch bottom heaving decreased by 6.22% and 3.68%, respectively; when the rod length rose from 5 to 6 m, they decreased by 6.47% and 7.98%, respectively. These findings indicated the positive effect of increasing the anchor rod length on mitigating crown settlement and arch bottom heaving.

Comparative analysis of different grouting thicknesses

The support structure of a tunnel is prone to tensile cracking and failure due to the great burial depth and significant ground stress. Grouting the surrounding rock can fill the pores and cracks, reduce the permeability of the rock mass, prevent the infiltration of water, mud, and other materials, and maintain surrounding rock stability. Grouting also strengthens the cohesion between the support system and the surrounding rock to enhance system stability and load-bearing capacity, thus extending its service life. This section investigates the effect of grouting thickness on tunnel stability under complex geological conditions. Based on the use of the three-bench method with temporary cross braces and anchor rods installed at certain intervals, the original thickness of 3 m was compared with 4 and 5 m. The results showed that as the grouting thickness increased, the maximum horizontal displacement on both sides of the tunnel greatly dropped. Specifically, when the grouting thickness rose from 3 to 4 m, the maximum horizontal displacement on both sides decreased by 15.83% and 14.88%, respectively; when the thickness grew to 5 m, the displacements further declined by 34.34% and 33.43%, respectively. These findings indicated that grouting provides support and constraint around the tunnel, thereby controlling displacement to some extent. In terms of vertical displacements, greater grouting thicknesses significantly alleviated both crown settlement and arch bottom heaving. Specifically, when the grouting thickness rose from 3 to 4 m, the maximum crown settlement and arch bottom heaving decreased by 10.65% and 11.31%, respectively; when the thickness grew to 5 m, they further declined by 19.37% and 24.25%, respectively. These observations suggested that grouting could enhance the load-bearing capacity of the surrounding rock, reduce deformation and displacement, and thus limit vertical displacement in the tunnel.

Prevention and treatment of tunnel collapse under complex geological conditions

Based on the theoretical analysis and numerical simulations in previous sections, the following measures are recommended for the prevention and treatment of tunnel collapse under complex geological conditions during construction:

For tunnel entrance sections with grade V surrounding rock, the bench method is recommended for excavation, supplemented by advanced pipe-roof support. For the shallow-buried sections, the support system should be reinforced. Advanced pipe-roof support forms a pre-reinforcement arch above the tunnel crown before excavation. This reinforcement structure improves the stability of the surrounding rock and prevents collapse by transferring loads to the stable strata ahead of the excavation face. For the main part of the tunnel with grade V surrounding rock, the bench method is recommended for excavation, supplemented by advanced support using small duct grouting and corresponding waterproofing and drainage measures. For grade IV surrounding rock, the bench method is recommended for excavation, along with the New Austrian Tunneling Method in construction.

For the excavation of the entrance section, a slope ratio of 1:1.0 to 1:1.25 is recommended, and excavation and support should proceed simultaneously. Either an open-cut method or one that follows the natural slope can be adopted. An intercepting ditch should be placed on the upper part, and the slope at the entrance should be reinforced using a combination of anchor rods and a concrete frame, along with vegetation inside.

The basic principles for tunnel collapse prevention are: “control water first, minimize disturbance, adopt weak blasting, and use shotcrete-bolt support early.” Monitoring of the surrounding rock should be enhanced to detect and address potential collapse hazards in time. For water control, measures include prevention (installing waterproof layers between the lining and support to prevent groundwater infiltration), blockage (injecting cement grout behind the support to fill gaps between the support and surrounding rock), interception (cutting off surface water and groundwater pathways into the tunnel), and drainage (building drainage ditches or blind drains). By adopting the above construction methods, tunnel collapse can be effectively avoided, unnecessary energy consumption and economic losses can be reduced, and it is in line with the trend of green and low-carbon development22–24.

In the event of a collapse, the following measures are recommended:

For minor collapses, it is advisable to cautiously remove loose rocks in the collapsed cavity using long rods in sections with temporarily stable surrounding rock and then use shotcrete-bolt support to reinforce the surrounding rock. During initial support, the spacing between steel arches should be reduced, and more feet-lock bolts should be installed. For major collapses, trolleys can be used to transport debris into the tunnel for backfilling to stabilize the tunnel face and prevent further collapse. Grout cement should be injected for overall reinforcement. If the collapse has reached the surface (commonly known as a roof collapse), it is necessary to cover the exposed collapse area first and seal it with shotcrete to prevent further water infiltration. Meanwhile, grouting the surface helps prevent the collapse from worsening. Similarly, for a collapse occurring in the tunnel cavity, the next step is to backfill the surface.

Conclusion

In this paper, a mountain tunnel in western China under complex geological conditions was selected for a case study. Geological survey data and tunnel collapse risk assessment theory in mountainous regions were used to analyze collapse risks. The risk assessment model obtained was applied to an actual tunnel project. Numerical simulations were conducted to compare surrounding rock displacement, plastic zone development, and stresses on the support structure under various construction conditions and support forms. Based on on-site monitoring data, suggestions were made for improving construction and design. The main research findings are as follows:

An intuitionistic fuzzy theory combined with a skewed normal weighting function was employed to establish a collapse risk assessment model for mountain tunnels. A risk index system consisting of four first-level indicators and fifteen second-level indicators was developed. The comprehensive risk assessment result for the Baise tunnel was 0.6854, corresponding to risk level III (severe), indicating that effective monitoring and preventive measures are required during construction.

The observations showed that the three-bench method with temporary cross braces effectively controlled surrounding rock deformation, which is therefore recommended for use. Long anchor rods distributed loads over a larger area, reducing the impact of local loads on the tunnel structure and better controlling surrounding rock deformation. Thicker grouting corresponded to smaller deformation. Based on the risk assessment and numerical simulation results, along with the conditions of the on-site tunnel construction, measures and recommendations are provided for the prevention and treatment of tunnel collapse under complex geological conditions. Future research could extend the proposed risk assessment model to other tunnel projects with similar geological conditions, such as tunnels located in highly fractured rock masses or regions with strong groundwater influence. From a practical perspective, future tunnel construction should also incorporate green and low-carbon technologies, such as optimized support systems, energy-efficient construction equipment, and sustainable materials, to reduce environmental impacts while maintaining construction safety.
