Active surveillance of drug safety in healthcare data: Sequential monitoring of bacterial and serious urinary tract infection risk in sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor users
Authors: Haoxin Le, Dilsad Simay Peker, Lukas Frederic Westphal, Nikolai Brun, Christine Erikstrup Hallgreen, Morten Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionActive surveillance of adverse events using healthcare data is emerging as complementary to the monitoring of spontaneous reports and stand‐alone pharmacoepidemiologic studies. The risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) was listed as a special warning for sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) when marketed in Europe in 2012. This study tested the feasibility of active surveillance, continuously re‐evaluating the UTI risk following approval.MethodThis sequential cumulative cohort study emulated surveillance of UTI among type 2 diabetes patients initiating SGLT2is using Danish healthcare data (2007–2022). Outcomes were bacterial UTI‐related prescriptions and UTI‐related hospital contacts. Dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4is) and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) were active comparators. Annual incidence rates (IRs) and propensity score weighted hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated.ResultThe 198 613 eligible initiation episodes were included. Among SGLT2is, the UTI‐related prescription IRs were in the range 140.9–200.9 per 1000 person years, declining steadily after 2014. IRs of hospital contacts ranged between 11.8 and 21.7 per 1000 person years, initially decreasing but rising again after 2018. The earliest conclusive risk estimate was observed in 2016, indicating no increased UTI risks of interest associated with SGLT2i use. HRs for bacterial UTI‐related prescriptions remained under 1 over time in both comparisons, while the HR for serious UTI events varied with follow‐up time.ConclusionThis is the first study demonstrating the feasibility of continuous surveillance for evaluating real‐world UTI risk among SGLT2i users without aiming to establish causality. The IR of serious events was low, and stable HRs indicate no increased risk from 2016.Keywords: active safety surveillance, adverse event, patient monitoring, SGLT2 inhibitors, type 2 diabetes, urinary tract infection

INTRODUCTION

While randomised clinical trials (RCTs) provide essential evidence regarding a drug's efficacy and safety, they have inherent limitations in fully characterizing the safety profile due to limited sample sizes, relatively short follow‐up periods and restricted generalisability. 1 , 2 , 3 Consequently, post‐authorization surveillance is essential for identifying potential adverse events that may not be apparent in pre‐market trials, with particular emphasis on the timeliness of signal detection, monitoring and re‐assessment throughout the drug's lifecycle.

Spontaneous reporting remains the primary data source for signal detection following marketing authorization, although it is limited by delays in reporting and the potential for under‐recognition of emerging safety information. 4 , 5 Complementing this, active near‐real‐time monitoring systems have been developed over the past decades for the targeted surveillance of medicinal products using real‐world data—exemplified by the US Food and Drug Administration's Sentinel Program. 6 Although knowledge of active surveillance systems for safety event detection is developing both in terms of application and methodology, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 their broader application remains underexplored. This is largely due to the absence of standardized methodologies and limited experience across therapeutic areas and drug classes in drug safety evaluation. 1 , 15 , 16

In Denmark, Aakjær et al. demonstrated the feasibility of implementing an active surveillance system using Danish national healthcare registries, concluding that such a system represents valuable complements to existing pharmacovigilance practices. 17 When surveillance is based on epidemiological methods, employing a new user active comparator cohort design, it can provide both measures of absolute risk and adjusted estimates of relative risks with relevant comparators comparable to those of a conventional post‐authorization safety study. Thus, a surveillance system may offer continuous follow‐up of already established signals and observation of potential changes in risk over time, which could contribute to signal management.

Diabetes represents a therapeutic area with a large patient population, widespread use of co‐medications and frequent changes in treatment regimens. 18 Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are common in these patients, especially in women. 19 Depending on severity, its treatment spans from those that do not require pharmacological treatment to complicated conditions that require hospital admission. 20 At the time of approval of the first sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) in the EU (November 2012), UTIs were included in the Risk Management Plan as an Important Identified Risk, 21 potentially influencing clinical decision‐making. However, although early systematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs found higher UTI risks among type 2 diabetes (T2D) patients, 22 , 23 more recent evidence reported that no increased UTI risk was observed when comparing SGLT2is to placebo. 24 , 25 , 26 Bapir et al. 27 conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs, reporting an increased UTI risk associated with SGLT2i use. Notably, they observed that UTI risk was higher among patients with T2D receiving a placebo compared to non‐diabetic individuals, 27 suggesting that underlying diabetes status may contribute to baseline susceptibility.

Real‐world studies have further explored the causal relationship between SGLT2i exposure and UTI risk among T2D patients, but the evidence remains inconsistent. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 These inconsistencies likely reflect variations in study populations, data sources and particularly the definitions used for UTI outcomes. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Dave et al. 31 conducted a comprehensive investigation analysing prescriptions and diagnostic codes separately, whereas other studies either combined these measures 28 or only used healthcare contacts and diagnostic codes. 29 , 30 , 32 , 33 , 34 However, the generalisability of Dave et al.'s finding is limited by their commercially insured population with minimal representation of older adults over 65, 31 an important population given evidence that advanced age substantially increases UTI risk. 35 , 36 , 37

This study aims to evaluate the feasibility of applying an active surveillance system for follow‐up of Important Identified Risks in the post‐marketing phase using routinely collected healthcare data. Using the association between SGLT2i use and UTIs among T2D patients as a case example, the study implements a cumulative sequential analysis framework enabling us to investigate the performance of continuous re‐evaluation of the risk as data accrue over time. Furthermore, we aim to evaluate the influence of methodological strategy, including different comparator choices, outcome definitions and methods for confounder adjustment.

METHODS

Study design

To emulate active surveillance, we conducted sequential cohort studies comparing the incidence of UTIs during SGLT2i use with the incidence during use of dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4is) and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs). Two outcomes were investigated: UTI‐related prescriptions and UTI‐related hospital contacts. Cumulative analyses were performed, representing the evidence that would have been obtained at different time points.

Data sources

The study used nationwide Danish healthcare and administrative registry data. The Danish Prescription Registry 38 was used to identify dispensing of antidiabetic medications, UTI‐indicated medications and relevant co‐medications based on Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes. 28 , 31 The Danish National Patient Registry 39 provided data on UTI diagnoses, exclusion criteria and comorbidities using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes. 28 , 31 , 40 The Danish Health Service Classification System (SKS) codes were employed for procedures associated with UTI risk. Mortality data were obtained from the Danish Cause of Death Registry, 41 while migration status was identified through the Danish Civil Registration System. 42 The National Cancer Register 43 was used to identify previous cancer diagnoses. All datasets were linked at the individual level using the unique civil registration number assigned to all residents of Denmark.

Study population

We included adults (aged ≥18 years) initiating treatment with SGLT2is, GLP‐1 RAs, or DPP‐4is, between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2022, who had either prior use of other antidiabetics or a T2D diagnosis (ICD‐10) at any time before. Patients could contribute with multiple treatment episodes if they met the eligibility criteria following a one‐year washout period for re‐initiation. Use of other antidiabetic agents (excluding the study drugs) within 1 year before the index date was permitted (Figure 1). The index date of each episode was defined as the dispensing date of the first prescription meeting the eligibility criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria based on prior antidiabetic drug exposure with a one‐year washout period. Initiation episodes were eligible if individuals had no exposure to any sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i)/dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor (DPP‐4i)/glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1 RA), either as monotherapy or in fixed‐dose combinations, within the 12 months preceding the index date. Prior use of other antidiabetic active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) not under investigation was permitted during this period.

Treatment episodes were excluded if patients received dual therapy involving SGLT2is, DPP‐4is or GLP‐1 RAs on the index date or during the one‐year washout period. Dual therapy was defined as (1) concurrent use of two active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) from different drug classes, evidenced by prescriptions for two distinct APIs from the aforementioned classes, or (2) use of a fixed‐dose combination containing two APIs of interest. Additionally, episodes in which multiple APIs from the same drug class were initiated on the index date were excluded. Further exclusion criteria were a history of renal insufficiency, type 1 diabetes, or a human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) diagnosis before the index date (Figure 2).

Outcome

Two UTI outcome definitions were applied. Mild to moderate UTIs requiring treatment were identified through dispensed prescriptions for antibiotics exclusively indicated for bacterial UTIs in Denmark. 31 , 44 , 45 Serious UTIs were identified using ICD‐10 diagnostic codes recorded in the context of a hospital contact (Table S1). We focused on bacterial UTIs, acknowledging limitations in capturing complete information on prescriptions for fungal UTIs in the Danish Prescription Registry, as topical fungal treatments are often over‐the‐counter. Episodes with both a UTI diagnosis and prescription on the same date were included in both outcome definitions.

Covariates

To reflect real‐world clinical scenarios, we included all eligible episodes of monotherapy with SGLT2is, DPP‐4is and GLP‐1 RAs, as well as their previous and concurrent use with other antidiabetic APIs not under investigation as exposures or comparators. These antidiabetic APIs include metformin, sulfonylureas (SUs), insulin, thiazolidinediones (TZDs) and other antidiabetics. We adjusted for the use of these antidiabetic APIs, applying a one‐year lookback period including the index date.

Because patients with prior UTI were eligible, UTI history was also adjusted for. We further controlled for comorbid conditions and clinical factors associated with increased UTI risk. Other covariates were selected based on prior studies, 24 , 28 , 31 , 33 including chronic comorbidities listed in the Charlson Comorbidity Index and related confounding factors. 40 , 46 Covariates were defined using a combination of diagnostic codes and prescription records. Figure 2 summarizes covariates and their assessment windows, with definitions provided in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All analyses followed an intention‐to‐treat approach, with a primary 6‐month follow‐up and sensitivity analyses to 12 months. Censoring criteria included: (1) occurrence of the outcome of interest, (2) death, (3) migration or (4) completion of the follow‐up period, whichever occurred first. Sequential analyses were performed by one‐year calendar intervals from 2013, coinciding with SGLT2i market entry. Cohorts were constructed cumulatively (e.g., 2013; 2013–2014; 2013–2015), with follow‐up censored at each cohort's calendar year end. Comparator cohorts included all initiation episodes since 2007 to incorporate evidence available at SGLT2i introduction.

Crude incidence rates (IRs) were calculated for each cumulative cohort. Using Cox regression, hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated for two comparisons: (1) SGLT2is vs. DPP‐4is and (2) SGLT2is vs. GLP‐1 RAs. HRs were estimated using four models: M0 (crude HR), M1 (adjusted for age and sex), M2 (multivariable model) and M3 (primary analysis: propensity score [PS] weighted model). In M2, adjustments were made for age, sex and all covariates with a frequency greater than 10 (N > 10) in each cumulative cohort, ensuring adequate variability and minimizing the risk of biased estimates.

The same approach was employed for the selection of covariates for PS estimation in M3. PS were estimated using logistic regression, with drug exposure as the dependent variable (SGLT2i vs. comparator) and all eligible covariates within each cumulative cohort as independent variables. We used PS weighting to provide the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) to derive estimates, 47 with trimming at 5th and 95th percentiles of the PS based on the Stürmer method. 48 , 49 We present 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with robust (sandwich) variance estimation to account for repeated patient inclusion. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

The study was approved by the University of Copenhagen under the Danish Data Protection Agency (reference number: 514‐0943/24‐3000). Under Danish law, register‐based studies do not require ethical approval. Analyses used SAS (Version 9.4). Due to confidentiality requirements, results are not presented for groups or strata with an event count <5.

RESULTS

The 238 665 treatment initiation episodes were identified from 2007 to 2022. After applying exclusion criteria, 198 613 eligible treatment episodes were identified. Of these, 53 591 were SGLT2i episodes, 89 549 were DPP‐4i episodes,and 55 473 were GLP‐1 RA episodes (Figure 3).

Cumulative HRs of two comparisons

The sequential analysis starts from 2016, because the 2013–2015 cohorts included too few SGLT2i initiation episodes and events to control reliably for confounding. The cumulative HRs in both comparisons (SGTL2is vs. DPP‐4is and GLP‐1 RAs, respectively) were relatively stable over time after some initial fluctuations, for both the hospital contact and the prescription‐based outcome (Figure 5). From 2016 to the end of the study period, no significant increase in UTI risk associated with SGLT2i use was observed in either comparison group, neither in prescription‐based nor hospitalization‐based analyses.

For the UTI‐related hospital contacts, there was a clear difference in HRs, depending on the choice of comparator (Table 3). No increased risk of UTI‐related hospitalization was observed in the SGLT2i vs. GLP‐1 RA comparison (HR in 2022: 1.080, 95% CI: 0.903, 1.291, p > 0.05), and the SGLT2i vs. DPP‐4i comparison demonstrated that the SGLT2i users had a lower risk (HR in 2022: 0.868, 95% CI: 0.762, 0.989, p < 0.05). After adjustment using PS weighting, the prescription‐based HR (in 2022) was 0.930 (95% CI: 0.878, 0.986; p < 0.05) for SGLT2i vs. GLP‐1 RA comparison and 0.936 (95% CI: 0.893, 0.982; p < 0.05) for SGLT2i vs. DPP‐4i comparison (Table 4). DISCUSSION

This study evaluates the utility of the active surveillance system for monitoring the risk of bacterial and serious UTIs associated with SGLT2i use among T2D patients, using administrative and healthcare register data. Motivated by the inconsistent findings across randomized controlled trials and real‐world studies, we observed that cumulative IRs and HRs for the UTI outcomes of interest have remained consistent with published evidence since 2016, shortly after SGLT2i marketing. These results highlight the potential of active surveillance in continuous safety monitoring and re‐evaluation, without implying a causal relationship. Below, we discuss the timeliness of surveillance, comparator selection, outcome definition and follow‐up strategy.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate an active surveillance system for monitoring UTI risk associated with SGLT2i use across multiple outcome definitions and comparator groups. Although cumulative IRs among SGLT2i users varied over time, cumulative HRs remained relatively stable and consistently indicated no increased UTI outcomes of interest in either comparison group since 2016, the first year with available cumulative analyses. These findings suggest that conclusions regarding the UTI safety profile of SGLT2i could have been reached earlier than the publication of most prior UTI‐focused studies. 24 , 25 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 34 For example, the study by Dave et al. covered the period from 2013 to 2015, 31 yet was not published until 2019. Implementing active surveillance could substantially reduce the time lag between data availability and risk identification. Nonetheless, cumulative estimates can be influenced by changes in clinical practice or regulatory interventions, and our observations depend on sustained clinical awareness and consistent routine pharmacovigilance activities from 2012 to 2022, during which no safety‐label revisions were recommended. 57

Our evaluation aligns with previous pharmacoepidemiologic studies reporting no increased risk of UTIs among users of SGLT2is compared to those using DPP‐4is or GLP‐1 RAs, despite heterogeneity in UTI outcome definitions. 28 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Yang et al. report a higher risk comparing SGLT2i to DPP‐4i (HR: 1.57, 95% CI: 1.39–1.77). 29 This discrepancy may reflect their focus on metformin add‐on users, exclusion of non‐metformin switchers and potential misclassification between genital infections and UTIs due to data source limitations, which may have influenced risk estimates.

Emulating active surveillance enabled assessment of different comparator groups and outcome definitions within the same population, offering a flexible framework for evaluating safety signals. Our findings underscore that even well‐designed observational studies may yield different conclusions depending on comparator choice and outcome operationalisation, consistent with prior evidence. 31 , 33 Dave et al. 31 concluded that SGLT2i use was not associated with increased serious or non‐serious UTI risks compared with GLP‐1 RAs or DPP‐4is. In our study, hospital‐based HRs in GLP‐1 RA comparison fluctuated around 1. Dave et al. used US commercial insurance data, whereas our analysis captured the full Danish population. Notably, our hospital‐based findings in GLP‐1 RA comparison align with Ueda et al., 33 who used Scandinavian registry data, supporting the broader generalisability.

A follow‐up strategy is important in active surveillance. In our analysis, HRs based on 12‐month follow‐up tended to be closer to 1, particularly for hospital contact‐based cumulative HRs. While longer follow‐up may be necessary to capture rare and more serious outcomes, it may induce non‐differential misclassification due to treatment discontinuation, biasing HRs towards the null.

Confounding by indication remains a key concern in pharmacoepidemiology, 58 making confounder control critical in the surveillance of healthcare data using pharmacoepidemiologic methods. We applied PS weighting using a dynamic covariate selection process based on event counts to further address confounding. Temporal dynamic of covariate inclusion (Figure S3) was consistent with the initiation episode characteristics (Table 1), indicating that the selection process primarily involves covariates obtaining fewer events. Improvements in standardized mean differences after PS weighting (Figures S1 and S2) demonstrate enhanced covariate balance between SGLT2i users and those receiving DPP‐4is or GLP‐1 RAs.

The inclusion of multiple potential confounders carries a risk of multicollinearity. While estimated PS should not be biased, sparse data may lead to extreme weights and biased effect estimates. There is a trade‐off between bias and precision in this context. 59 Our dynamic covariate selection procedure using a frequency threshold and PS trimming should have contributed to mitigating the problem, and comparisons of estimates and CIs across models M0‐M3 do not indicate major issues with bias or variance inflation. That we did not use more sophisticated variable selection procedures, penalized regression methods robust to collinearity or overlap weighting can, however, be considered a limitation. 60 , 61

Residual confounding may persist. We observed an increasing trend in hospital contact‐based UTI HRs in the SGLT2i vs. GLP‐1 RA comparison, potentially reflecting confounding by indication as SGLT2is are also prescribed for patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), a condition associated with elevated UTI risk. Due to the lack of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) data, CKD status was approximated using baseline hospital diagnosis codes, which may not capture mild to moderate CKD. Residual confounding may arise because CKD status can change during follow‐up and is associated with both T2D and genitourinary infection risk. 62 , 63 The PS density curves (Figures S1 and S2) also suggest residual confounding related to obesity. Although we adjusted for obesity using baseline diagnosis codes and obesity‐indicated drugs, the absence of body mass index data limited the detection of mild and moderate obesity, particularly relevant given the increasing off‐label use of GLP‐1 RAs for obesity. 64

A key strength of our study is the application of sequential surveillance in a real‐world setting, enabling observation of drug performance over time concerning specific safety outcomes. However, the study does not aim to establish causality. Using ICD‐10 codes or prescription records separately to approximate UTI severity may reduce accuracy compared with approaches integrating diagnoses, symptoms and prescriptions. 65 However, a combined definition is not feasible in the Danish setting due to limited access to primary care diagnoses and treatment information during hospital admission. Nonetheless, active surveillance supports continuous monitoring of cumulative incidence rates, reflecting changes in clinical practices, patient characteristics and awareness of adverse events.

Among SGLT2i users, cumulative IRs of UTI‐related prescriptions increased until 2014 and subsequently declined, suggesting adaptation in prescribing towards lower‐risk patients. In contrast, UTI‐related hospital contacts IRs initially decreased but rose after 2018, possibly reflecting expanded SGLT2i use following emerging evidence of renal and their indication for CKD in 2021. Narasaki et al. reported increased genitourinary infection risks among SGLT2i users with stage 4–5 CKD. 62 These findings illustrate how evolving clinical evidence may influence prescribing behaviour, which in turn may influence the risk profile of the treated population.

Our study has several limitations. Instead of glycated haemoglobin A1c, we used a proxy definition based on prior prescriptions and diagnoses. This approach may have excluded new users of SGLT2is, DPP‐4is and GLP‐1 RAs without previous treatment or those who received a diagnosis in primary care. About 12% to 14% of new users in Denmark initiate these therapies without prior antidiabetic treatment, 66 which may limit external validity when evaluating UTI risk among newly diagnosed patients. On the other hand, the restriction to patients with prior diagnoses or treatment for T2D and the exclusion of patients with renal insufficiency diagnoses may have improved internal validity by targeting a more accurately defined T2D population. The absence of eGFR data may have allowed including mild to moderate CKD SGLT2i users without CKD diagnosis, while the expanded indications for SGLT2is appeared later in the study period. The consistency of our results with previous studies suggests that our definitions are reasonably robust.

We focused on bacterial UTIs and excluded fungal infections, which could lead to an underestimation of overall UTI risk. Similarly, the incidence of prescription‐based bacterial UTIs across three drug classes may be underestimated, as antibiotics indicated for broader infectious diseases were excluded to prioritize internal validity. 45 Overall, our risk estimates reflect bacterial UTIs requiring pharmacological treatment or hospital contact, which are expected to result in lower observed risk compared with the broader UTI definitions documented in SmPC. These definitions encompass mild symptoms not requiring treatment, use of antibiotics not included in this study and fungal infections. This study included antidiabetic users with recurrent UTIs but adjusted for previous UTIs and other risk factors at baseline, which may have explained the higher incidence compared with Dave et al. 31 In general, our objective was not to assess causality but to evaluate the capability of the active surveillance system across multiple therapeutic areas beyond its prior application in antidepressants. 17 Therefore, including patients with previous UTI outcomes and focusing on populations reflecting real‐world clinical scenarios aligns with our study purpose.

Ideally, safety concerns should be evaluated at the API level. However, due to the limited population size, this study compared outcomes at the drug‐class level. For the same reason, calendar year was excluded from the PS weighting process, nor was calendar year stratification applied in sequential analysis. Omitting calendar time may have introduced residual confounding, given evolving prescribing patterns and clinical practices over the 15‐year study period. Instead, we used all available data from 2007 for two comparators and 2012 for SGLT2i, estimating cumulative IRs and HRs once the thresholds for minimizing bias were reached. Our results align with findings by Dave et al. 31 and Ueda et al., 33 both of whom applied PS matching. Future research could explore annual or even more frequent surveillance using larger datasets to better reflect temporal trends.

Crude UTI IRs among SGLT2i users were lower than those among DPP‐4i and GLP‐1 RA users, an unexpected observation given that UTIs are not documented in the SmPCs for the latter classes. This may reflect confounding by indication, channelling in clinical practice and residual confounding, as only a 1‐year washout for prior exposure was applied. Further study is warranted to investigate this phenomenon. Changes in risk estimates suggest that such systems may partially capture evolving clinical practice, patient characteristics and adverse events awareness. However, these changes cannot be conclusively attributed to risk minimization interventions, as they may also reflect unmeasured confounders. Future research should assess the role of active surveillance in evaluating the effectiveness of risk minimization measures and explore advanced analytical approaches such as machine learning, with careful consideration of study design and research objectives.

CONCLUSIONS

This study emulated targeted active surveillance of bacterial and serious UTI risk among SGLT2i users without aiming to establish causality. We found low IRs of serious UTI events and stable HRs indicating no increased risks for the UTI outcomes of interest as early as 2016. Our findings demonstrated the feasibility of applying active surveillance for timely signal assessment and continuous monitoring in a sufficiently large population, although methodological limitations remain in the analysis.
