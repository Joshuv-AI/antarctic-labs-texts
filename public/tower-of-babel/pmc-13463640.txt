Knowledge level of ECG interpretation on STEMI, its equivalents and mimics among emergency department medical officers: a single-centre study
Authors: Wan Syazmin Wan Mokhtar, Julina Md Noor, Muhammad Nur Azmi Baharuddin, Nurul Huda Ahmad, Mohd Shahril Ahmad Saman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation is essential in emergency care, particularly for recognising ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), STEMI equivalents, and STEMI mimics. These patterns guide reperfusion decisions and early management, yet the evolving myocardial infarction paradigm has increased interpretive complexity. This study aimed to assess the ECG interpretation knowledge of STEMI, STEMI equivalents, and STEMI mimics among medical officers in the Emergency Department (ED) and to identify factors associated with their performance.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 62 medical officers in a high-volume tertiary ED in Malaysia (approximately 110,000 annual attendances). Participants completed a validated questionnaire comprising sociodemographic data, training background, and a 20-item ECG assessment focused on STEMI, STEMI equivalents, and mimics. Each ECG was scored dichotomously, and total scores were categorised using Bloom’s cut-off values: poor (< 60%), moderate (60–79%), and good (≥ 80%). Simple logistic regression was used to screen associated variables (p < 0.25), followed by multiple logistic regression to identify independent predictors (p < 0.05).ResultsMost respondents demonstrated poor ECG interpretation knowledge (71%), while 29% achieved moderate scores; none attained a good level. At item level, participants performed better in identifying classical STEMI patterns but showed low accuracy for STEMI equivalents (e.g., posterior myocardial infarction, Smith-Modified Sgarbossa criteria) and mimics (e.g., pericarditis, benign early repolarisation, left ventricular aneurysm). In an exploratory multiple logistic regression analysis, two possible associations were observed: a 6–12-month interval between graduation and employment showed a possible association with higher odds of moderate knowledge (AOR 7.57, 95% CI 1.57–36.57, p = 0.012), and working experience of 2–5 years showed a possible association with lower odds of moderate knowledge compared with > 5 years of experience (AOR 0.19, 95% CI 0.04–0.96, p = 0.045). ECG course attendance, frequency, timing, confidence level, and number of ECGs interpreted per week were not significant predictors.ConclusionsThis single-centre exploratory study identified important gaps in the recognition of STEMI equivalents and mimics among ED medical officers. A 6–12-month interval between graduation and employment and more than 5 years of working experience showed possible associations with moderate knowledge; however, given the cross-sectional design, modest sample size, and wide confidence intervals, these findings are hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as evidence of causation. The results highlight a critical need for targeted training interventions focusing on high-risk, under-recognised ECG patterns to strengthen diagnostic accuracy and improve patient outcomes in the emergency care setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09632-7.

Background

In Malaysia, acute coronary syndrome (ACS) places a substantial burden on emergency care services and affects a relatively young population. Data from the National Cardiovascular Disease Database - Acute Coronary Syndrome Registry (NCVD-ACS) recorded 15,162 ACS admissions between 2020 and 2021. Of these, ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) accounted for 50.3% of cases (n = 7,627), non-STEMI (NSTEMI) for 30.4% (n = 4,603), and unstable angina for the remaining 19.3%, with a mean age at STEMI presentation of 56 years [1].

This high prevalence of STEMI at a younger age underscores the need for timely diagnosis and intervention in the emergency department (ED). Importantly, evidence from the broader literature suggests that approximately 25–34% of patients classified as NSTEMI may harbour an occluded culprit artery despite not meeting classical STEMI criteria, representing a cohort at risk of delayed reperfusion if high-risk ECG patterns are not recognised [2]. While local hospital-level data on rates of false positive STEMI activations or NSTEMI with confirmed coronary occlusion were not available for the present study, this represents an important gap that future institutional audits could address.

Electrocardiography (ECG) is a cornerstone diagnostic tool in emergency medicine, guiding rapid clinical decision-making and time-critical interventions for patients presenting with cardiovascular emergencies [3]. Prompt and accurate ECG interpretation is essential for the early identification of acute myocardial infarction and initiation of appropriate management. However, considerable variability in interpretation accuracy persists among clinicians and studies have consistently reported differences in ECG interpretation knowledge among medical students and healthcare professionals, particularly doctors. This raises concerns about diagnostic errors, potential impacts on patient outcomes, and the need for ongoing improvement of ECG interpretation skills in the fast-paced and high-stakes ED environment [4, 5].

The STEMI and non-STEMI diagnosis paradigm was first officially introduced into ACS guidelines in the year 2000 [6]. The 2013 American Heart Association (AHA) Guideline for the Management of STEMI defines it as ST-segment elevation at the J-point, ≥ 1 mm in ≥ 2 adjacent leads (other than leads V2 and V3, where elevation of ≥ 2 mm in men or ≥ 1.5 mm in women is considered significant) [7]. The Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, developed jointly by the ESC, ACC, AHA, and World Heart Federation, further refines these criteria and specifies slightly different thresholds for leads V2-V3 in young men (≥ 2.5 mm), harmonising international diagnostic standards [8].

Since then, other perilous ECG patterns have emerged such as STEMI equivalents, defined as high-risk ECG patterns that do not fulfil the traditional STEMI criteria but carry equal weight in detecting critical coronary artery stenosis or occlusion. The 2022 American College of Cardiology (ACC) Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on the Evaluation and Disposition of Acute Chest Pain in the Emergency Department includes patterns such as posterior wall STEMI, LBBB with Sgarbossa Criteria, LBBB with Smith-Modified Sgarbossa Criteria, De Winter sign and hyperacute T waves as STEMI equivalents [9]. Wellen’s syndrome occupies a distinct position within this group, rather than indicating active coronary occlusion, it represents spontaneous reperfusion of an occlusive myocardial infarction (OMI) episode that may have gone unrecorded during the chest pain episode [2]. Nevertheless, it carries a high risk of re-occlusion and its recognition warrants urgent angiographic evaluation, justifying its continued inclusion as a high-risk ECG pattern.

Beyond STEMI equivalents, another diagnostic challenge lies in the recognition of STEMI mimics. Pollak et al. (2012) described STEMI mimics as ST-segment elevation but are not caused by an occlusive coronary artery disease [10]. These mimics include myocarditis/myopericarditis, benign early repolarisation (BER), previous infarction with left ventricular (LV) aneurysm, coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal Angina), Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, Brugada syndrome, hyperkalaemia and hypothermia. The discovery of these imitators makes detecting coronary artery occlusion through ECG even more challenging.

The purpose of this study was to assess the degree of knowledge of ECG interpretation in cases of STEMI, STEMI equivalents and STEMI mimics, among doctors working in the ED. By exploring factors such as work experience level, previous ECG training, and number of ECGs interpreted per week, this research aims to provide insights into the current state of ECG interpretation proficiency among emergency medicine practitioners [4].

Methods

Study aim, design & setting

This study aimed to assess the level of ECG interpretation knowledge among medical officers working in the ED and to identify training and professional factors associated with performance. A cross-sectional study design was employed and conducted in the ED of a public tertiary hospital in Selangor, Malaysia. The department manages approximately 110,000 patient attendances annually, reflecting a high-volume emergency care setting. Data collection was carried out between October 2024 and April 2025.

Study participants

All medical officers working in the ED during the study period were eligible to participate. Given the fluctuating nature of ED staffing, the total number of medical officers varied throughout the data collection period as staff joined and left the department. A total of 62 medical officers were present and working in the ED across the full data collection period, all of whom were invited and completed the study, representing a 100% response rate.

In Malaysia, medical officers are fully qualified medical doctors and fully registered medical practitioners as defined under the Medical Act 1971 (Act 50), having completed a medical degree and provisional registration including the requisite two-year housemanship (internship) training, and holding a valid Annual Practising Certificate issued by the Malaysian Medical Council. The designation ‘medical officer’ reflects an employment grade within the Ministry of Health public service, distinct from specialist or consultant grades [11].

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and is provided in full as Supplementary File 1. The ECG knowledge assessment component comprised 20 tracings purposively selected to represent commonly encountered and clinically significant patterns relevant to ED practice, guided by contemporary emergency cardiology literature and with emphasis on patterns that directly influence acute reperfusion decision-making. All tracings were sourced from reputable online educational resources to ensure high-quality, clearly legible recordings and to allow standardisation across participants [12–14]. Some diagnoses were represented more than once to assess consistency.

For each tracing, participants selected the most appropriate diagnosis from a standardised answer pool of 30 options listed by number, a format designed to reflect the breadth of conditions encountered in clinical practice while allowing systematic scoring. Each response was scored dichotomously (one point for a correct answer, zero for incorrect), and total scores were converted to percentage values for analysis.

Content validity was established through independent expert review by an emergency physician with a special interest in cardiology and a cardiologist. Each reviewer independently verified the correct diagnosis for every tracing; any item on which the reviewers disagreed was excluded from the final questionnaire, ensuring that only ECGs with unambiguous, consensus-confirmed diagnoses were included. Prior to the main study, a pilot study among 30 medical officers confirmed the feasibility and reliability of the instrument, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79, which exceeds the accepted threshold of 0.70 and indicates good internal consistency. The pilot study was conducted among medical officers from a separate emergency department and none of the pilot participants were subsequently included in the main study cohort, ensuring independence between the validation and study samples.

Self-reported confidence in ECG interpretation was assessed using a five-point Likert scale. For analytical purposes, this was collapsed into three categories: poorly confident (combining “not confident at all,” “slightly confident,” and “somewhat confident”), fairly confident, and very confident, to improve statistical stability given the sample size.

Procedures and data collection

Participants completed the questionnaire anonymously during allocated departmental sessions. No patient-related data or clinical interventions were provided. Data collection was conducted across multiple sessions to accommodate varying work schedules. As this approach carried a potential risk of information sharing between participants, measures were taken to minimise this, including administering the questionnaire under supervised conditions, restricting access to the ECG images to the session itself, and advising participants not to discuss the questionnaire content with colleagues.

ECG images were standardised across all participants to ensure consistent assessment. Knowledge scores were classified using Bloom’s cut-off criteria (poor < 60%, moderate 60–79%, good ≥ 80%), selected a priori based on their established use in comparable knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) studies in medical education [15].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) Statistics version 29.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and ECG knowledge scores. Simple logistic regression was performed to screen variables associated with ECG knowledge level, followed by multiple logistic regression to identify independent predictors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For variable selection into the multiple logistic regression model, a p-value threshold of < 0.25 was applied at the simple logistic regression stage, in accordance with the approach described by Mickey and Greenland (1989), which demonstrates that more restrictive thresholds (e.g. p < 0.05) may exclude potentially important variables [16]. Given the modest sample size anticipated, the regression analysis was pre-specified as exploratory.

Factors associated with knowledge level

Based on Table 5, four variables with p-value of < 0.25 were identified: Last ECG Course Attended (≤ 6 months), Duration Between Graduation and Employment (6–12 months), Working Experience (2–5 years) and Confidence Level (Fairly Confident). The effective events-per-variable ratio of approximately 4.5 fell below the commonly recommended threshold of 10, and several regression categories yielded non-estimable odds ratios due to small cell counts, limiting the completeness of the model. These variables were subsequently included in the Multiple Logistic Regression analysis. The final model identified only two significant factors associated with moderate knowledge of ECG interpretation: Duration Between Graduation and Employment (6–12 months) and Working Experience (2–5 years).

Multiple logistic regression analysis revealed a possible association between a 6–12-month interval between graduation and employment and higher odds of moderate knowledge (AOR 7.57, 95% CI 1.57–36.57). Another possible association was observed between 2 and 5 years of working experience and lower odds of moderate knowledge compared with more than 5 years of experience (AOR 0.19, 95% CI 0.04–0.96, p = 0.045). In contrast, other factors, such as ECG course attendance, the number and timing of such courses, frequency of ECG interpretation, and self-reported confidence levels, were not significantly associated with knowledge outcomes.

The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated adequate model fit (χ² = 4.16, df = 8, p = 0.842). The model demonstrated sensitivity of 90.9%, specificity of 44.4%, and an area under the ROC curve of 0.807. No evidence of multicollinearity was observed. The disparity between sensitivity and specificity reflects the imbalanced outcome distribution in this sample, with the model performing better at identifying poor knowledge than moderate knowledge, consistent with the limited events-per-variable ratio noted above.

Discussion

“Time is myocardium” in the management of ACS underpins modern emergency cardiac care, yet inappropriate activation of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) pathways also carries important clinical, logistical, and resource implications. While beyond the scope of the present study, the broader literature supports a paradigm shift from the binary STEMI/NSTEMI framework toward OMI and non-occlusive myocardial infarction (NOMI) classification. Approximately 25–34% of patients labelled NSTEMI may harbour an occluded culprit artery, underscoring that reliance on classical ST-elevation criteria alone risks missing acute coronary occlusion [2].

ECG Knowledge among medical officers

This study found that the majority of medical officers working in the ED of a tertiary public hospital in Malaysia demonstrated poor knowledge of ECG interpretation of STEMI, its equivalents and mimics, with only a minority achieving moderate knowledge and none reaching a good level.

This pattern is consistent with prior literature: de Jager et al. (2010) reported an average accuracy of only 46.4% among Emergency Medicine residents in South Africa, and Abdalla et al. (2022) found junior doctors in the United Kingdom scored a mean of 45% on an ECG assessment [4, 18]. In Turkey, Cetin et al. (2021) also demonstrated low overall knowledge among emergency physicians and general practitioners, particularly in the areas of arrhythmias and conduction disorders [19]. The present study extends these findings by demonstrating that the knowledge gap is most pronounced for the high-risk patterns most directly relevant to reperfusion decision-making.

Several factors likely explain the particularly poor recognition of STEMI equivalents and mimics. The concept of STEMI equivalents emerged primarily from emergency cardiology literature and guideline updates rather than traditional undergraduate curricula, and most ECG courses available to medical officers focus on basic rhythm recognition and classical infarction patterns with limited coverage of advanced ischaemia patterns. Standard clinical textbooks further reinforce this gap by emphasising classical STEMI patterns without formally categorising STEMI equivalents as a distinct group [20].

These findings align with established educational theory — ECG interpretation is fundamentally a pattern recognition skill built through repeated case exposure, and rare patterns such as de Winter sign and posterior MI are less likely to be consolidated through routine practice where classical STEMI predominates [21]. Cognitive load theory further contextualises these deficits — interpreting subtle or atypical patterns under time pressure imposes high intrinsic cognitive load, particularly for less experienced clinicians who have not yet automated recognition through sufficient prior exposure [22].

The knowledge gaps identified here may reflect both individual training deficiencies and a teaching culture still anchored in the classical STEMI/NSTEMI dichotomy. Poor recognition of OMI patterns risks delayed reperfusion, while poor recognition of mimics risks unnecessary catheterisation lab activation, precisely the dual limitations the OMI/NOMI framework was developed to address. Reviewing local hospital cases to identify patients with preventable false positive STEMI activations or delayed reperfusion for OMI could provide valuable institutional data to complement the educational findings of this study, and is recommended as a direction for future audit work.

Factors associated with ECG knowledge

A possible association was observed between a 6–12-month interval between graduation and employment and higher odds of moderate knowledge (AOR 7.57, 95% CI 1.57–36.57), though the mechanism underlying this association is unclear and speculative as activities during this interval were not captured. Structured programmes delivered in the transition between graduation and internship have been shown to improve new doctors’ skills and confidence, supporting the notion that this period can be used productively for targeted preparation [23]. This differs with Abdalla et al. (2022), who found no significant effect of time since graduation on interpretation scores in UK junior doctors, suggesting that theoretical reinforcement may offer short-term benefit but long-term interpretive performance remains dependent on sustained clinical exposure [4].

A possible association was also observed between 2 and 5 years of working experience and lower odds of moderate knowledge compared with more than 5 years (AOR 0.19, 95% CI 0.04–0.96), consistent with the hypothesis that sustained cumulative clinical exposure may be associated with higher interpretive performance, though this cannot be confirmed from this study design. This finding aligns with the trend observed in de Jager et al. (2010), where senior residents outperformed juniors, but both groups still demonstrated overall low knowledge [18]. Similarly, Cetin et al. (2021) noted that cardiologists, with longer cumulative exposure, achieved higher success rates compared to emergency physicians and general practitioners [19]. Given the modest sample size, wide confidence intervals, and several non-estimable categories, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and warrant replication in larger, multi-centre studies.

ECG course attendance, course frequency and timing, interpretation frequency, and self-reported confidence were all non-significant predictors of knowledge outcomes — findings that should be interpreted cautiously given the study’s limitations, but which suggest that attendance per se matters less than training depth and content.

Implications and future directions

The study findings have direct implications for training policy in Malaysian emergency departments. Deliberate practice theory offers a useful framework for the null finding on course attendance — meaningful skill acquisition requires focused, effortful engagement with challenging cases and structured feedback, rather than passive exposure [24]. This supports moving away from attendance-based training models toward competency-driven, case-based programmes explicitly targeting the high-risk patterns most frequently missed in this study. Established frameworks such as the Smith-Modified Sgarbossa criteria and Barcelona algorithm provide structured decision support for identifying coronary occlusion, though their coverage of STEMI equivalents and mimics is limited, underscoring the continued primacy of targeted ECG education.

Emerging AI-assisted tools, including PMcardio, utilise machine learning to detect OMI patterns not meeting classical STEMI criteria and have demonstrated sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 92% in independent validation [25, 26]. In high-volume departments where turnover and experience levels vary, such tools may function as a high-sensitivity safety net — an adjunct to, rather than replacement for, clinical judgement. Broader implementation, however, would require coordinated planning across clinical, administrative, and regulatory stakeholders and warrants investigation beyond the scope of this study.

Strength of study

A key strength of this study is its targeted ECG assessment tool, which focused specifically on STEMI, STEMI equivalents, and STEMI mimics — patterns that directly influence reperfusion decisions in the ED. This narrow focus enabled precise identification of clinically significant knowledge gaps, particularly for high-risk but underemphasised patterns such as posterior MI and Wellen’s syndrome. The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79), and a 100% response rate from all medical officers present during the data collection period strengthens the representativeness of findings within the study setting.

Limitations of study

Several limitations should be considered. First, the single-centre design limits generalisability, as case mix, training culture, and staffing composition may differ across institutions. Second, the modest sample size constrained statistical power, resulting in wide confidence intervals, several non-estimable regression categories, and a low events-per-variable ratio; findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Third, the multiple-choice format with a 30-option answer pool may have introduced a cueing effect, whereby recognition of a named diagnosis on a list does not fully replicate the open-ended clinical decision-making context.

Fourth, the questionnaire assessed diagnostic labelling rather than the binary clinical decision of whether to activate the catheterisation lab, and the absence of an ischaemic versus non-ischaemic classification option may have further disadvantaged participants. Relatedly, performance under test conditions may differ from real-time practice where time pressure and clinical context influence decision-making. Fifth, ECG 16 in the assessment depicted a right ventricular paced rhythm with an LBBB morphology rather than a true LBBB. While functionally similar in terms of the application of the Smith-Modified Sgarbossa criteria, this distinction was not explicitly communicated to participants, which may have introduced ambiguity in responses to this item.

Finally, several training-related variables were incompletely captured. Preferred learning methods were not assessed, limiting insight into how future interventions could be optimally structured. Activities undertaken during the graduation-to-employment interval — such as self-directed study, postgraduate preparation, or locum work — were not recorded, limiting the interpretability of that association. Similarly, the content and duration of ECG courses attended were not captured; as courses likely varied considerably in scope, it is unclear whether the null association between course attendance and ECG knowledge reflects a genuine lack of educational effect or simply the limited coverage of STEMI equivalents and mimics in most available programmes.

Conclusions

This single-centre exploratory study identified important gaps in the recognition of STEMI equivalents and mimics among ED medical officers, supporting the need for targeted ECG education focused on high-risk occlusion patterns. Two possible associations were observed in the exploratory regression analysis: a 6–12-month interval between graduation and employment showed a possible association with higher odds of moderate knowledge, whereas 2–5 years of working experience showed a possible association with lower odds compared with more than 5 years of experience. Given the cross-sectional design, modest sample size, wide confidence intervals, and small cell counts in several regression categories, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and interpreted with caution, and causal conclusions cannot be drawn. They raise the hypothesis that sustained clinical exposure and experience may be associated with ECG interpretation knowledge, but this requires confirmation in larger prospective studies.

The results underscore the need for structured, clinically integrated ECG training and ongoing professional development tailored to the demands of emergency practice. Strengthening frontline ECG interpretation knowledge is essential to optimise timely reperfusion decisions, reduce inappropriate activation of intervention pathways, and ultimately improve patient outcomes in the high-stakes emergency care setting.
