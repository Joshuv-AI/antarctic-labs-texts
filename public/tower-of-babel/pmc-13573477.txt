Dynamic pupillometry in patients with visible optic disc drusen: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Işık Yıldırım, Caner Karakaya, Adem Öztekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough often considered asymptomatic, optic disc drusen (ODD) may be associated with subtle changes in visual pathway function. In this study, the ability of dynamic pupillometry to reveal impairment in patients with visible ODD was examined.MethodsIn this cross-sectional case‒control study, 67 eyes from 39 patients with visible ODD and 60 eyes from 30 controls comparable in age and sex were examined. All participants underwent fundus imaging, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and dynamic pupillometry. The parameters included the maximum and minimum pupil diameter (PD), mean constriction velocity (MCV), mean dilation velocity (MDV), constriction amplitude (CA), and mean mesopic and photopic PD. Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) intensity on the optic disc was graded 1 to 4 according to quadrant involvement. Group comparisons and FAF-grade associations were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for inter-eye correlation.ResultsThe MCV was significantly lower in ODD eyes compared with controls (1.50 ± 0.41 vs. 1.96 ± 0.74 mm/s; β = −0.41, 95% CI: −0.67 to −0.15; Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.014). Similar findings were obtained in the right-eye sensitivity analysis (p = 0.009). No significant associations were observed between FAF grade and the pupillometric parameters. Average retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness was also lower in the ODD group (90.0 ± 11.4 vs. 96.9 ± 10.6 µm; β = −6.33, 95% CI: −11.84 to −0.81; p = 0.024). FAF grade was significantly associated with lower average RNFL thickness (β = −5.49, 95% CI: −7.28 to −3.71; p < 0.001).ConclusionsDynamic pupillometry demonstrated reduced MCV in eyes with ODD, a finding supported by single-eye sensitivity analysis. Although pupillometric parameters were not significantly associated with FAF grade after adjustment for multiple comparisons, higher FAF grades were significantly associated with lower RNFL thickness. These findings suggest altered pupillary dynamics in ODD and a structural association between FAF grade and RNFL thinning.

Introduction

Optic disc drusen (ODD) are acellular deposits composed of substances such as calcium, mucopolysaccharides, and nucleic and amino acids, and are located within the optic nerve head [1]. Histological and imaging studies have shown that these deposits are located anterior to the lamina cribrosa and posterior to Bruch’s membrane [1–4]. ODD are estimated to affect between 0.3 and 2% of the general population [2, 3]. In early life, ODD are usually buried within the optic nerve head and are not calcified. As individuals reach puberty, ODD tend to migrate toward the surface, become calcified, and can be detected during an ophthalmoscopic exam [5, 6].

Pupillometry is often used to study conditions that involve the autonomic nervous system, emotional changes, memory and attention, anaesthesia, critical care, neurological dysfunction associated with vitamin deficiencies, and drug effects [7, 8]. Some of these studies have focused on diseases affecting the retina and optic nerve, which are components of the afferent pathway of the pupillary light reflex, such as glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, optic neuritis (ON), nonarteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION), and outer retinal dystrophies [7, 9–14].

Although ODD are often considered asymptomatic, studies employing visual field testing and retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) analysis have demonstrated functional involvement of the visual system. However, to date, no studies have investigated pupillary dynamics using pupillometry in this population. We hypothesized that patients with visible ODD would exhibit impaired pupillary light reflex parameters, and that this impairment would correlate with the severity of fundus autofluorescence (FAF) intensity.

Materials and methods

The study was reviewed and approved by the Istanbul Medipol University Ethics Committee (date: March 6, 2023, decision no: E-10840098-772.02–1695). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and the parents of participants under the age of 18. The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were either under regular follow-up at our clinic or were newly diagnosed with ODD after the date of ethical approval. All measurements and data collection were performed between March 2023 and May 2025, following ethics approval and the provision of informed consent.

This cross-sectional study included 67 eyes of 39 patients with visible ODD and 60 eyes of 30 healthy individuals with no additional ocular disorders. Control participants were selected to have a similar age and sex distribution to the ODD group. Among patients with ODD, 28 had bilateral involvement, and 11 had unilateral involvement; accordingly, each eye was analysed as a separate observational unit, whereas demographic variables were recorded at the patient level. Patients with a typical appearance of ODD—characterized by elevated, lumpy, and irregular structures on the optic nerve head on fundus examination, as described by Auw-Haedrich et al. [4]—and a positive FAF were included in the study. These patients were defined as having visible ODD (Fig. 1). Patients with ODD accompanied by papilledema were excluded, as this condition may affect pupillometry responses through different mechanisms. Cases with blurred optic disc margins or increased peripapillary RNFL thickness on optical coherence tomography (OCT) were excluded to minimize potential misclassification with papilledema, particularly in cases of buried drusen.Fig. 1Colour fundus photograph (top left) and FAF image (top right) of a 74-year-old female patient showing elevated, lumpy, and irregular structures on the optic nerve head, extending beyond the disc margins. All patients underwent routine ophthalmic examinations, including detailed anamnesis, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure measurement in adults and older children, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and fundoscopy. Fundus photography and FAF imaging (Visucam 500, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) were performed in patients with ODD, whereas OCT measurements (Cirrus HD-OCT, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) were obtained in both the ODD and control groups using the same imaging protocol.

BCVA was recorded in logMAR units, and only participants with normal visual acuity were included. Patients with any condition that could affect pupillometry—including ocular pathologies, a history of ocular surgery, use of systemic or topical medications, or systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or thyroid disorders—were excluded. One patient was excluded because of the presence of angioid streaks, and another was excluded because pupillometry could not be performed at the age of 8 years.

Pupillometry was performed using the Aladdin HW2.0 device (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan), a multifunction optical biometry system with integrated pupillometry. Mesopic measurements were obtained using infrared LED illumination, and photopic measurements were performed using white LED light. The device allows both static and dynamic pupillometry and records monocular pupil video sequences of approximately 20 seconds for analysis of pupil size and response dynamics. Pupillary measurements were obtained using a monocular light stimulus, and the direct pupillary response of the stimulated eye was recorded. A green central fixation target prevented accommodation. Measurements were obtained separately for each eye. The device automatically detected the pupil borders and generated a pupil diameter–time (Fig. 2). The temporal characteristics of the light stimulus and pupil response were derived from this graph, in which mesopic, photopic, and scotopic phases are visually represented. The measured parameters included the maximum and minimum pupil diameter (PD, mm), mean constriction velocity (MCV, mm/s), mean dilation velocity (MDV, mm/s), constriction amplitude (CA, mm), and mean mesopic and photopic PD (mm) values. Participants rested in a dark, quiet room (<0.5 lux) for 5 minutes prior to measurement, after which pupillary measurements were obtained sequentially for each eye under identical testing conditions. For each participant, three consecutive measurements were obtained for each eye. Among these, the recording with the most physiologically appropriate PD–time curve and accurate pupil-border detection was selected for analysis. All assessments were conducted by the same ophthalmologist. FAF intensity was graded according to how many quadrants of the optic nerve head were covered. Based on the FAF images, the involvement of one quadrant was classified as Grade 1, that of two quadrants was Grade 2, that of three quadrants was Grade 3, and that of all four quadrants was Grade 4 (Fig. 3). All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical analyses were performed on an eye-specific basis for pupillometric measurements. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Age and refractive error were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Comparisons of pupillometric parameters and average RNFL thickness between the ODD and control groups, as well as their associations with FAF grade within the ODD group, were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for inter-eye correlation. FAF grade was modeled as an ordinal predictor (grades 1–4). GEE models used a normal distribution with an identity link and an exchangeable working correlation structure. Eyes were clustered within participants to account for inter-eye correlation, and robust standard errors were used. Regression coefficients (β), standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were reported. Bonferroni-adjusted p-values were calculated for the seven pupillometric parameters. For between-group comparisons, Cohen’s d was calculated from the unadjusted eye-level means using the pooled standard deviation and is reported as an absolute value. In contrast, p-values for the primary between-group comparisons were derived from GEE models accounting for within-participant inter-eye correlation. As a sensitivity analysis, between-group comparisons of pupillometric parameters were repeated using only right eyes with the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlation analyses were performed using right-eye data only to ensure independence of observations. For the right-eye sensitivity analysis, 35 affected right eyes from the ODD group and 30 right eyes from the control group were included; patients with unilateral ODD confined to the left eye were excluded. Correlation analyses were based on the 35 affected right eyes in the ODD group. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the associations of FAF grade with age, refractive error, and axial length. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationships of pupillometric parameters with age, refractive error, and axial length. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The mean ages of the participants in the ODD and control groups were 24.87 ± 14.71 years (range, 9–74 years) and 25.57 ± 13.78 years (range, 10–60 years), respectively, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.334). Sex distribution was also similar between the groups (ODD: 20 females [51.3%] and 19 males [48.7%]; control: 15 females [50.0%] and 15 males [50.0%]; p = 0.916). All participants had a BCVA of 0.0 logMAR. The spherical equivalent refractive error was −0.62 ± 2.91 D (range, −10.30 to +6.50 D) in the ODD group and −0.89 ± 1.25 D (range, −4.50 to +1.25 D) in the control group, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.198).

Comparison of pupillometric parameters between the ODD and control groups revealed that only MCV was significantly lower in the ODD group (1.50 ± 0.41 vs. 1.96 ± 0.74 mm/s; β = −0.41, SE = 0.13, 95% CI: −0.67 to −0.15; Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.014), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.78). No significant differences were observed in the other pupillometric parameters (all p > 0.05). As a sensitivity analysis, group comparisons were repeated using only right eye per subject, with MCV remaining significantly lower in the ODD group (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.009), whereas no significant differences were observed for the other pupillometric parameters (all p > 0.05). These findings supported the results of the primary GEE analysis.

Average RNFL thickness was also significantly lower in the ODD group (90.0 ± 11.4 vs. 96.9 ± 10.6 µm; β = −6.33, SE = 2.81, 95% CI: −11.84 to −0.81; p = 0.024). Right-eye Spearman correlation analyses showed no significant associations of FAF grade with age, refractive error, or axial length (all p > 0.05). Discussion

ODD is typically seen as a harmless structural condition; however, some studies suggest that it may actually be a neurodegenerative process that causes gradual damage to the nerve fibres in retinal ganglion cells and the afferent visual pathway. Many individuals with ODD exhibit visual field defects and may be at increased risk of developing AION [15].

Studies have shown that 11% to 87% of patients with ODD have visual field defects [6, 16, 17]. A 36-month follow-up study reported a 1.6% reduction in the visual field among patients with ODD [18]. In individuals with a mean age of 43.5 years, visual field defects were observed in 80.5% of cases, whereas a younger cohort with a mean age of 10.5 years had a 33% incidence. These findings indicate that ODD is more progressive during adolescence [6, 19].

Although studies have reported different results, drusen are usually located in the nasal region of the optic disc. RNFL thinning and related visual field defects are consistent with these findings [15, 16, 20]. In our study, average RNFL thickness was lower in the ODD group than in the control group (p = 0.024), in agreement with previous reports. Because eyes with increased peripapillary RNFL thickness were excluded to avoid misclassification with papilledema, this between-group comparison is conditioned on that exclusion and may have been biased toward thinner RNFL values in the ODD group. Moreover, increasing FAF grade was strongly associated with lower average RNFL thickness (β = −5.49 per one-grade increase, p < 0.001). Although detailed OCT-based structural characterization was not the primary aim of the study, this finding provides structural context for the FAF grading results. In one of the largest series reported in the literature, an analysis of 149 eyes revealed peripapillary RNFL thinning in 83.6% of cases. It was also suggested that the increased visibility of ODD may be related to progressive RNFL thinning [21]. In eyes with buried ODD, RNFL thinning was reported in only 4%, whereas a 30% reduction in minimum ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer thickness was noted. These findings indicate that the ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer may be affected earlier than the RNFL [22]. In a swept-source OCT study, optic disc drusen with an anterior margin located ≥ 100 µm from Bruch’s membrane opening were more clearly visualized on FAF imaging [23]. One possibility is that as the buried ODD migrates towards a more superficial location, increasing axonal damage leads to RNFL thinning and visual field defects. Another explanation is that the increased visibility of ODD may be a result of age-related progressive RNFL thinning.

Lower systolic blood flow velocity in the central retinal artery has been reported in both children and adults with ODD and visual field defects. Their findings suggest that drusen may restrict retinal perfusion through mechanical compression of the vessels. In addition, the more crowded the optic disc is, the greater the risk of vascular complications and/or further impairment of neuronal conductivity [4, 24].

In patients with ODD, electrophysiological evaluations revealed either a marked 79% reduction or complete loss of the N95 component in pattern ERG assessments [16]. A significant association has been reported between the visible height of drusen and visual field defects, reduced pattern ERG P50–N95 amplitude, RNFL thinning, and delayed VEP P100 implicit time in eyes with superficial ODD [20]. A significant reduction in the mean amplitude of multifocal VEP has also been observed [16, 25]. These findings suggest that ODD may impair both retrograde and anterograde axoplasmic transport [20, 25].

In eyes with diabetic retinopathy, the scotopic PD is smaller than that in normal eyes [10–12]. Significant miosis has been reported in diabetic patients who underwent panretinal photocoagulation [12]. The occurrence of miosis in early-stage diabetes suggests that sympathetic nerve damage occurs before parasympathetic nerve damage [12]. These studies indicate that pupillometry is helpful in the diagnosis of diabetic autonomic neuropathy.

In patients with Graves’ disease, the mesopic PD is significantly greater, especially during the hyperthyroid phase, and this change can be explained by sympathetic overactivity [26, 27]. Similar abnormalities have been reported to persist even after thyroid function returns to normal [27].

A pupillometry study on sellar region tumours revealed a decreased Neurological Pupil index (NPi) in patients with pituitary adenomas compressing the optic chiasm, as well as in those with tuberculum sellae meningiomas involving oculomotor nerve compression [28].

An ultrasound pupillometry study in patients with acute and past ON demonstrated that ultrasound pupillometry (UP) could distinguish patients with acute ON from healthy controls with 100% accuracy. In addition, relative afferent pupillary defects (RAPDs) were detected using UP in 16% of multiple sclerosis patients who had no previous history of ON. These findings suggest that UP may detect subclinical optic nerve damage in multiple sclerosis patients [29]. A significant reduction in both the velocity and the ratio of pupillary constriction has been reported during the acute stages of ON and NAION [13].

Chromatic pupillometry has revealed diminished pupillary light reflexes in patients with retinal and optic nerve pathologies, including retinitis pigmentosa, Leber congenital amaurosis, and glaucoma [14].

In this study, pupillary dynamics in eyes with visible ODD were evaluated using a model that accounts for inter-eye correlation. MCV was significantly lower in eyes with ODD than in controls (1.50 ± 0.41 vs. 1.96 ± 0.74 mm/s; Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.014), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.78). The right-eye sensitivity analysis yielded a similar result, with MCV remaining lower in the ODD group (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.009).

The optic nerve, as a key component of the afferent pathway of the pupillary light reflex, may be influenced by the presence of drusen. A possible explanation for this finding is that axonal compression or chronic structural changes associated with optic disc drusen may impair afferent transmission. Accordingly, the observed reduction in MCV may reflect subtle alterations in pupillary light reflex function; however, pupillometry alone cannot determine whether these changes are related to afferent or efferent pathway involvement or identify the underlying anatomical or physiological mechanism.

The absence of significant between-group differences in the other pupillometric parameters suggests that the observed alteration was not generalized across all components of the pupillary response. This may indicate that these parameters are less sensitive to early or mild functional changes. In contrast, the reduction in MCV indicates a slowing of the constriction phase and may be consistent with subtle dysfunction involving the pupillary light reflex pathway. Larger-scale and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the clinical relevance of these findings in terms of prognosis and disease monitoring.

Within the ODD group, unadjusted GEE analyses showed negative associations of FAF grade with maximum PD and MCV (p = 0.010 and p = 0.032, respectively). However, neither association was statistically significant after Bonferroni adjustment (adjusted p = 0.070 and p = 0.224, respectively). The seven pupillometric parameters are strongly interdependent, and constriction amplitude is derived from maximum and minimum pupil diameter. Therefore, the Bonferroni correction across seven tests was conservative, and the borderline findings should be interpreted alongside the reported confidence intervals. In contrast, FAF grade showed a strong negative association with average RNFL thickness, with an estimated 5.49-µm reduction in RNFL thickness for each one-grade increase in FAF grade (β = −5.49, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that FAF grade may reflect structural involvement, although its relationship with pupillometric alterations was not significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

These findings suggest that drusen deposition may affect some pupillometric parameters, such as MCV; however, this should be interpreted with caution. These findings are considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Once drusen become visible, the degree of pupillary dysfunction may not increase linearly with FAF intensity grade. In our study, the box plots suggested an overall decrease in MCV across FAF grades; however, the pattern was not monotonic, and the association did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni adjustment. Therefore, the presence of optic disc drusen, rather than increased FAF grade, may be more relevant for functional changes.

Given that drusen volume, depth, and RNFL changes have been associated with functional involvement in ODD, FAF-based grading according to the number of affected quadrants offers an objective approach to stratification but may not fully capture overall disease severity. Nevertheless, the significant association between higher FAF grade and lower RNFL thickness suggests that this approach may reflect certain aspects of structural involvement.

The timing of afferent pathway dysfunction in patients with ODD appears to be closely related to structural changes in the drusen. In early life, ODD are typically buried and non-calcified; however, with puberty, progressive migration toward the surface and increasing calcification render this period a critical transitional phase for functional involvement [5, 6, 16, 21]. The higher prevalence of visual field defects and RNFL thinning in eyes with superficial ODD suggests that functional loss becomes more apparent at this stage [5, 6, 19, 21]. In contrast, while RNFL thickness appears relatively preserved in buried ODD, reports of early involvement at the level of the ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer indicate that subclinical changes may begin in early childhood, even before drusen become superficial, and may progress over time [22]. Accordingly, afferent pathway dysfunction in ODD may not occur at a single time point but instead develop gradually, depending on age and drusen stage. In the present study, increasing FAF grade was strongly associated with lower RNFL thickness (β = −5.49, p < 0.001), which may reflect greater cumulative structural involvement at higher FAF grades. However, no pupillometric parameter was significantly associated with FAF grade after Bonferroni adjustment. Thus, although the lower MCV observed in ODD eyes supports the presence of functional alteration, the present cross-sectional findings do not demonstrate a progressive worsening of pupillary function across FAF grades.

The wide age range of the study population may have introduced heterogeneity. Pupillary dynamics vary with age, and ODD may differ between pediatric and adult patients. In the right-eye analysis, MDV showed a moderate negative correlation with age (r = −0.41, p = 0.016), indicating slower pupillary redilation with increasing age. No significant association was found between age and MCV. MDV also did not differ between the ODD and control groups, and the groups had similar age distributions. However, the effect of age cannot be completely ruled out because age was not included in the main model and different age groups were not analyzed separately. Future studies with larger samples should evaluate pediatric and adult patients separately.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate dynamic pupillometric parameters in individuals with ODD. The lower MCV observed in ODD eyes compared with controls may be consistent with subtle functional alterations in the pupillary light reflex pathway. The strong association between higher FAF grade and lower RNFL thickness further indicates that increasing FAF involvement is accompanied by greater structural alteration, although no corresponding graded change in pupillometric parameters across FAF grades was demonstrated after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

There are several limitations to our study. First, a formal sample size calculation was not performed because of the exploratory nature of the study. However, the sample was deemed sufficient for a preliminary assessment of pupillometric changes in ODD. Second, this was a single-centre study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the inclusion of only eyes with normal best-corrected visual acuity may have favored milder ODD cases and further limited the generalizability of the results. Third, comparing the pupillometric findings with visual field results from this cohort would have greatly enhanced the clinical relevance of the study, as the presence of visual field defects could reasonably be expected to be associated with corresponding abnormalities in pupillary responses. Fourth, dynamic pupillometry was performed using a monocular stimulus that recorded only the direct response of the stimulated eye. Therefore, the present study could not distinguish whether the observed pupillary alteration reflected afferent or efferent involvement in the pupillary light reflex pathway. Fifth, although dynamic pupillometry provides an objective functional measure, various pupillometry devices differ in methodology, and a standardized measurement protocol across devices has not yet been established. Sixth, in unilateral ODD cases, comparison between the affected and fellow eyes could have provided a valuable within-subject control; however, this was not performed given the limited number of unilateral cases, to avoid underpowered analyses. Finally, although grading based on the number of involved quadrants on FAF is a practical approach, it may not reflect drusen depth, size, calcification level, or overall disease severity, which may limit the interpretability of subgroup analyses. Advanced imaging modalities such as enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography (OCT-EDI) may provide a more detailed structural assessment and potentially refine correlations with pupillometric parameters.

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that ODD may represent not only a structural anomaly but also a condition with potential functional consequences for the optic nerve. The observed reduction in MCV in ODD eyes may be consistent with subtle involvement of the pupillary light reflex pathway. Within the ODD group, higher FAF grade was associated with lower maximum PD and MCV in the unadjusted GEE analyses; however, neither association was statistically significant after Bonferroni adjustment. Additionally, FAF grade showed a strong negative association with RNFL thickness, suggesting that FAF grading may reflect aspects of structural involvement. Dynamic pupillometry may contribute to clinical evaluation as an objective and non-invasive complementary tool that can be used alongside other clinical tests in the assessment of ODD. Further studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs are needed to confirm these findings and to better define their clinical relevance.
