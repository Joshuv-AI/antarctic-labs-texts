The Word Problem for Products of Symmetric Groups
Hans U. Simon

Abstract
The word problem for products of symmetric groups (WPPSG) is a well-known NP-complete problem. An input instance of this problem consists of “specification sets” X 1 , … , X m ⊆ { 1 , … , n } X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\} and a permutation τ \tau on { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} . The sets X 1 , … , X m X_{1},\ldots,X_{m} specify a subset of the symmetric group 𝒮 n {\mathcal{S}}_{n} and the question is whether the given permutation τ \tau is a member of this subset. We discuss three subproblems of WPPSG and show that they can be solved efficiently. The subproblem WPPSG 0 is the restriction of WPPSG to specification sets all of which are sets of consecutive integers. The subproblem WPPSG 1 is the restriction of WPPSG to specification sets which have the Consecutive Ones Property. The subproblem WPPSG 2 is the restriction of WPPSG to specification sets which have what we call the Weak Consecutive Ones Property. WPPSG 1 is more general than WPPSG 0 and WPPSG 2 is more general than WPPSG 1 . But the efficient algorithms that we use for solving WPPSG 1 and WPPSG 2 have, as a sub-routine, the efficient algorithm for solving WPPSG 0 .

The Word Problem for Products of Symmetric Groups

The word problem for products of symmetric groups (WPPSG) is a well-known
NP-complete problem
[
2
]
. In Section
3.1
,
we formally state this problem and also remind the reader to some
(mostly known) polynomial reductions among some of its subproblems.
In Section
3.2
, the problem WPPSG is reformulated
in a way that allows for a natural definition of what we call the

sorting strategy
. This strategy is then intensively discussed
in Section
3.3
. The main result,
Theorem
3.8
, states that this strategy efficiently
solves WPPSG restricted to specification sets all of which are sets of
consecutive integers, respectively.

3.1
The Classical Formulation of the Word Problem

The
word problem for products of symmetric groups (WPPSG)

is the following decision problem:

Input Instance:

Integers
n,m\geq 1
, specification sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\subseteq[n]
and
a permutation
\tau_{0}
on
[n]
.

Question:

Is
\tau_{0}
a member of the product of the symmetric
groups
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{1}},\ldots,{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{m}}
, i.e., do there exist
permutations
\sigma_{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{m}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{m}}

such that
\tau_{0}=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{m}
?

Since
{\mathcal{S}}_{X}=\{\mathrm{id}\}
if
|X|\leq 1
, we may always assume without loss
of generality that
n\geq|X_{j}|\geq 2
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

We will also consider some subproblems of WPPSG which are obtained
by imposing additional constraints on the sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
:

•

The subproblem WPPSG
[2]
results
from the constraints
|X_{j}|=2
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

•

The subproblem WPPSG
0
results from the constraint that,
for each
j\in[m]
, the set
X_{j}\subseteq[n]
consists of
consecutive integers.

•

The subproblem WPPSG
{}_{0}[2]
is then obtained by combining
the constraints from the previous two subproblems.

•

The subproblem WPPSG
1
results from the constraint
that the sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\subseteq[n]
must have the
C1-property, i.e., there exists a permutation
\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}

such that, for every
j\in[m]
, the elements of
X_{j}
occur
consecutively in
\pi
(or, equivalently, the set
X_{j}\pi^{-1}

consists of consecutive integers).

Clearly, as witnessed by the identity permutation, WPPSG
0
is a
subproblem of WPPSG
1
. The following hardness results are known:

Theorem 3.1

(
[
2
]
)
.

1.

WPPSG is NP-complete.

2.

WPPSG
\leq_{pol}
WPPSG
[2]
.

3.

WPPSG
[2]
is NP-complete.

The proof of the first assertion employs a polynomial reduction from
“Directed Disjoint Connecting Paths” to WPPSG. The polynomial reduction
which establishes the second assertion makes use of universal sequences
of transpositions (without explicitly calling them such).
See
[
2
]
for details. The third assertion is immediate from the
first two.

The following result makes use of universal sequences of adjacent
transpositions in a straightforward manner. We present a short proof
for sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.2

(Common Knowledge)
.

\mbox{WPPSG}_{0}\leq_{pol}\mbox{WPPSG}_{0}[2]
.

Proof.

The following proof is illustrated in Example
3.3
below.
Let
I=(n,m,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m},\tau_{0})
be an instance of WPPSG
0
.
Then the sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are integer intervals. Let
S_{j}
be a
universal sequence of adjacent transpositions for the interval
X_{j}

and let
\tilde{S}_{j}
be the corresponding sequence of
2
-sets.
Consider the instance
I^{\prime}
of WPPSG
{}_{0}[2]
which is obtained
from
I
by substituting
\tilde{S}_{j}

for
X_{j}
.
I^{\prime}
can clearly
be constructed from
I
in poly-time. It suffices to show that
I

is a YES-instance iff
I^{\prime}
is a YES-instance.

Suppose first that
I^{\prime}
is a YES-instance. Then
\tau_{0}
can be
written in the form
\tau_{0}=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{m}
where
\sigma_{j}

is a product of transpositions of a suitably chosen subsequence of
S_{j}
.
Obviously
\sigma_{j}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{j}}
, which shows that
I
is a YES-instance.

Suppose now that
I
is a YES-instance. Then
\tau_{0}
can be written
in the form
\tau_{0}=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{m}
where
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{j}}
.
By the universality of the sequence
S_{j}
, each
\sigma_{j}
can be written
as a product of transpositions of a suitably chosen subsequence of
S_{j}
.
This shows that
I^{\prime}
is a YES-instance.
∎

3.2
A Reformulation of the Word Problem

Consider two permutations
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
and
a set
X\subseteq[n]
. We write
\tau^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau

if
i\tau=i\tau^{\prime}
holds for each
i\in[n]\setminus X
.
Note that and
\tau^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau

implies that
\{i\tau:i\in X\}=\{i\tau^{\prime}:i\in X\}
.
Moreover
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X}}{{\longrightarrow}}
is an equivalence relation.
We say that
\tau
is
X
-sorted
if, for any two
indices
i<j
from
X
we have that
i\tau<j\tau
.
Clearly every equivalence class with respect to
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X}}{{\longrightarrow}}

contains exactly one representative that is
X
-sorted.
The following observation is rather obvious:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
be two permutations on
[n]
,
let
\sigma=\tau^{\prime}\tau^{-1}
and let
X\subseteq[n]
.
Then
\tau^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau
iff
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\tau^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau

so that
\tau
coincides with
\tau^{\prime}
outside
X
.
Pick an arbitrary but fixed
i\in[n]\setminus X
.
Then
i\sigma=i\tau^{\prime}\tau^{-1}=i\tau\tau^{-1}=i
.
It follows that
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X}
.

Suppose now that
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X}
.
Pick an arbitrary but fixed
i\in[n]\setminus X
.
Then
i=i\sigma=i\tau^{\prime}\tau^{-1}
, which implies
that
i\tau=i\tau^{\prime}
.
It follows that
\tau^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau
.
∎

We will see shortly that WPPSG can be reformulated in the following way:

Input Instance:

Integers
n,m\geq 1
, subsets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\subseteq[n]
and
a permutation
\tau_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
.

Question:

Do there exist permutations
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}

such that
\tau_{m}=(1,\ldots,n)
and, for
j=1,\ldots,m
,
we have that
\tau_{j-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X_{j}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau_{j}
?

We will refer to this reformulated version of WPPSG as V2-WPPSG
where “V2” means “Version 2”. The following result and its proof
show that the problems WPPSG and V2-WPPSG are equivalent in a strong
sense:

Lemma 3.5
.

WPPSG and V2-WPPSG have the same set of YES-instances.

Proof.

Suppose first that
I=(n,m,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m},\tau_{0})
is a YES-instance
of V2-WPPSG. Choose
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m-1},\tau_{m}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
such
that
\tau_{m}=(1,\ldots,n)
and
\tau_{j-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X_{j}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau_{j}

for
j=1,\ldots,m
. For
j=1,\ldots,m
,
set
\sigma_{j}:=\tau_{j-1}\tau_{j}^{-1}
.
Then
\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{m}=\tau_{0}\tau_{m}^{-1}=\tau_{0}
. Moreover,
according to Lemma
3.4
, we have
that
\sigma_{j}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. As witnessed
by
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}
,
I
is also a YES-instance of WPPSG.

Suppose now that
I=(n,m,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m},\tau_{0})
is a YES-instance
of WPPSG. For
j=1,\ldots,m
, choose
\sigma_{j}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{j}}
such
that
\tau_{0}=\sigma_{1}\ldots,\sigma_{m}
and choose
\tau_{j}
such
that
\sigma_{j}=\tau_{j-1}\tau_{j}^{-1}
.
Then
\tau_{0}=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{m}=\tau_{0}\tau_{m}^{-1}
,
which implies that
\tau_{m}=\mathrm{id}=(1,\ldots,n)
. Moreover,
according to Lemma
3.4
, we have
that
\tau_{j-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X_{j}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.
As witnessed by
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m-1},\tau_{m}
,
I
is also
a YES-instance of V2-WPPSG.
∎

.

We have seen in Section
3.3
that the problem
WPPSG
0

In this section, we show that even the more general problem WPPSG
1

can be solved efficiently. Recall that WPPSG
1

restriction of WPPSG to specification sets which have the C1-property.
This will enable us to obtain an algorithm that solves WPPSG
1
efficiently
by combining a well known algorithm of Booth and Lueker
[

]

with the sorting strategy.

The proofs of Lemmas

\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
,
the mapping
\sigma\mapsto\pi^{-1}\sigma\pi

of the group

the sake of completeness.

Lemma 4.1

(Common Knowledge)
.

Let
X
be a subset of
[n]
and let
\sigma,\pi
be permutations
on
[n]
. Then
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X}

iff
\pi^{-1}\sigma\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X\pi}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X}
.
Choose an arbitrary

.
Then
i\pi^{-1}\notin X
so that
i\pi^{-1}\sigma=i\pi^{-1}
.
Hence
i\pi^{-1}\sigma\pi=i\pi^{-1}\pi=i
.
This shows that
\pi^{-1}\sigma\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X\pi}
.

Suppose now that
\sigma^{\prime}:=\pi^{-1}\sigma\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X^{\prime}}

for
X^{\prime}:=X\pi
. We conclude from the first part of the proof
that
\pi\sigma^{\prime}\pi^{-1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X^{\prime}\pi^{-1}}

\pi\sigma^{\prime}\pi^{-1}=\pi(\pi^{-1}\sigma\pi)\pi^{-1}=\sigma

and

that
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X}
.

In Section

5.3
The Main Building Blocks of a Test for Niceness

A central tool in the sequel is the procedure REDUCE-EXPAND which,
given sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\subseteq[n]
, works as follows:

Initialization.

X_{0}:=[n]
and
\Pi_{0}:={\mathcal{S}}_{n}
.

Loop.

For
j=1,\ldots,m
:

Expansion Step:

\Pi^{\prime}_{j}:=\{\pi\sigma:\pi\in\Pi_{j-1}\mbox{ and }\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{j-1}\pi}\}
.

Reduction Step:

\Pi_{j}:=\{\pi\in\Pi^{\prime}_{j}:\mbox{ $X_{j}\pi$ is a set of consecutive integers}\}
.

By the first execution of the for-loop, we get

\Pi^{\prime}_{1}:={\mathcal{S}}_{n}\ \mbox{ and }\ \Pi_{1}:=\{\pi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}:\mbox{$X_{1}\pi$ is a set of consecutive integers}\}\kern 5.0pt.

(8)

We say that the sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\subseteq[n]
have the

Weak Consecutive Ones Property (WC1P)
if REDUCE-EXPAND
with input
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
computes
sets
\Pi_{0},\Pi^{\prime}_{1},\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi^{\prime}_{m},\Pi_{m}
such that
\Pi_{m}\neq\emptyset

(which implies that all these sets are non-empty).
We briefly note that the removal of all expansion steps would have
the consequence that
\Pi_{m}\neq\emptyset
iff
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}

have the C1-property. Since the removal of expansion steps increases
the chances to arrive at some empty set
\Pi_{j}
, it follows that
the C1-property implies the weak C1-property. It does not come as
a surprise that there exist collections
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
having
the
weak
C1-property but not the C1-property itself.
We will illustrate this by an example after we have proven the
following result:

6.1
PQ-Trees

PQ-trees over the universe
[n]
are rooted
ordered trees whose leaves are elements of
[n]
and whose internal
nodes are distinguished as either P- or Q-nodes.
6
6

6

Booth
and Lueker used the universe
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}

instead of
[n]
.

In figures, a P-node is drawn as a circle with its children below it
while a Q-node is drawn as a rectangle with its children below it.
A PQ-tree
T
is
proper
if the following hold:

1.

Every element in
[n]
appears precisely once as a leaf in
T
.

2.

Each P-node has at least
2
and each Q-node has at least
3
children.

In the sequel, we only talk about proper PQ-trees without explicitly
mentioning this again. The symbol
T
is reserved to denote such trees.
Reading the leaves in
T
from left to right yields the
frontier

of
T
, which is denoted by
\mathrm{FRONTIER}(T)
.
We identify
\mathrm{FRONTIER}(T)
with a permutation on
[n]
in the usual way:
if
\mathrm{FRONTIER}(T)=(u_{1}\ldots u_{n})\in[n]^{n}
, then it equals the permutation
which maps
i
to
u_{i}
. Hence the inverse of
\mathrm{FRONTIER}(T)
,
denoted by
\mathrm{FRONTIER}(T)^{-1}
, maps
u_{i}
to
i
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

A PQ-tree
T^{\prime}
is said to be
equivalent to PQ-tree
T
if it
can be obtained by the following
permitted modifications
of
T
,
which may take place at every internal node:

Modification at a P-node:

The children of a P-node can be arbitrarily permuted (including,
as a special case, the option to leave the order of the children
unchanged).

Modification at a Q-node:

The children of a Q-node are either left in their original order
or they are put in reverse order.

If
T
is a PQ-tree and
x
is a node in
T
, then
T_{x}

denotes the subtree of
T
that is rooted
at
x
.
A permutation

is
consistent with

if
\pi
equals
the frontier of one of the trees which are equivalent to
T
. The
set of permutations that are consistent with
T
is denoted by
\mathrm{CONSISTENT}(T)

Definition 6.1

The
consistency problem for PQ-trees
is the following problem:
given a PQ-tree
T
with
n

\pi\in\mathrm{CONSISTENT}(T)
and, if applicable, construct a tree
that is equivalent to
T
and has frontier

.

(Common Knowledge)

The consistency problem for PQ-trees can be solved in time

We suspect that this lemma is known but, for sake of completeness,
