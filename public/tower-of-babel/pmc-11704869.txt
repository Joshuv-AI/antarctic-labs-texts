Response to Nationwide Analysis of Antimicrobial Prescription in Korean Hospitals between 2018 and 2021: The 2023 KONAS Report
Authors: Himel Mondal
Published: 2024

Dear Editor:

The analysis of 2023 KONAS Report provides a comprehensive analysis of antimicrobial prescriptions in Korean hospitals from 2018 to 2021, highlighting significant trends and challenges in antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) across different hospital types. According to the study, there are significant differences in the ways that primary care hospitals (PCHs), secondary care hospitals (SCHs), and tertiary care hospitals (TCHs) use antibiotics. The total and reserve antimicrobial usage in PCHs has increased, which is alarming, despite the fact that overall antimicrobial use has reduced in TCHs and SCHs [1].

The complexity and severity of cases treated in SCHs and TCHs is reflected in the preponderance of broad-spectrum antibacterial drugs used for community-acquired illnesses. More patients with severe infections are usually managed by SCHs and TCHs, who frequently need to start empirical treatment before certain bacteria are found. This supports the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, which are efficient against a variety of bacteria and guarantee prompt treatment for illnesses that might be fatal. Conversely, PCHs treat milder cases, which often include simple, well-defined infections that are successfully treated with narrow-spectrum medications [2]. These medicines lower the risk of antibiotic resistance and other consequences by targeting certain infections with little interference to the patient's natural flora. The differences in antibiotic use between these types of hospitals highlight the necessity for an individualized strategy to infection control based on the complexity and severity of patient demands in various healthcare environments [3].

The emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a worldwide health emergency that requires a concerted, multinational response (Table 1). In the context of AMR, the idea of One Health—which acknowledges the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health—is especially pertinent. Because pathogens know no geographical boundaries, mishandling antibiotics in one nation can have a significant impact on world health [4]. In AMS, international cooperation is crucial for a number of reasons. It makes it easier for nations to exchange information and best practices, allowing them to benefit from each other's achievements and difficulties. This is crucial in low- and middle-income nations since there might not be as much funding available for AMS there. A coordinated efforts can help standardize antimicrobial usage guidelines and reduce the inappropriate use of antibiotics, which is a key driver of AMR [5,6].

The necessity for coordinated AMS policies including the human healthcare, veterinary medicine, agricultural, and environmental sectors is emphasized by the One Health concept. Reducing the use of antibiotics in cattle, for instance, can aid in halting the transfer of resistant germs from animals to people via the food chain. Analogously, tracking environmental reservoirs of resistance—like soil and water—is essential to comprehending and preventing AMR from spreading [7].

The study by Yun et al. concluded by emphasizing the necessity of focused AMS interventions in Korea, especially in smaller institutions. However, AMS initiatives that safeguard public health globally require global collaboration and the adoption of One Health concepts.
