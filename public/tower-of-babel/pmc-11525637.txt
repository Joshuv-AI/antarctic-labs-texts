Decoding marker genes and immune landscape of unstable carotid plaques from cellular senescence
Authors: Gang-Feng Cai, Shao-Wei Chen, Jin-Kai Huang, Shi-Rong Lin, Guo-He Huang, Cai-Hou Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecently, cellular senescence-induced unstable carotid plaques have gained increasing attention. In this study, we utilized bioinformatics and machine learning methods to investigate the correlation between cellular senescence and the pathological mechanisms of unstable carotid plaques. Our aim was to elucidate the causes of unstable carotid plaque progression and identify new therapeutic strategies. First, differential expression analysis was performed on the test set GSE43292 to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the unstable plaque group and the control group. These DEGs were intersected with cellular senescence-associated genes to obtain 40 cellular senescence-associated DEGs. Subsequently, key genes were then identified through weighted gene co-expression network analysis, random forest, Recursive Feature Elimination for Support Vector Machines algorithm and cytoHubba plugin. The intersection yielded 3 CSA-signature genes, which were validated in the external validation set GSE163154. Additionally, we assessed the relationship between these CSA-signature genes and the immune landscape of the unstable plaque group. This study suggests that cellular senescence may play an important role in the progression mechanism of unstable plaques and is closely related to the influence of the immune microenvironment. Our research lays the foundation for studying the progression mechanism of unstable carotid plaques and provides some reference for targeted therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-78251-3.Keywords: Unstable plaques, Cellular aging, Genetics, Immune infiltration, Machine learningSubject terms: Gene expression, Atherosclerosis, Carotid artery disease, Diagnostic markers

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory, progressive, and age-related disease characterized by plaque formation in the walls of large and medium-sized arteries1. The carotid artery is a common site for AS, typically located after the carotid bifurcation, with a prevalence of approximately 18-26.6%2,3. It is a high-risk factor for stroke, which is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide4. With the aging population structure, the prevalence of carotid plaques continues to rise, seriously affecting population health.

Traditionally, the size of carotid plaques and associated luminal stenosis have been the focus of research5. Clinically, surgical indications are usually based on the degree of carotid stenosis or neurological complications. However, recent studies have shown that plaque stability is closely related to the risk of ischemic stroke and depends more on their composition2,3. As a result, unstable carotid plaques have gradually become a research hotspot.Unstable plaques typically have characteristics such as thinner fibrous caps, enlarged lipid cores, intraplaque hemorrhage, and increased inflammation6. These features make plaques prone to rupture or ulceration, potentially leading to intracerebral thrombosis or embolism. In contrast, stable plaques usually have a simple structure, consisting of lipid accumulation and foam cells7. Currently, the early clinical identification of unstable plaques relies on imaging techniques: for example, carotid ultrasound to detect plaque size and morphology and observe fibrous cap thickness; CTA to assess calcification and intraplaque hemorrhage; and high-resolution MRI to evaluate plaque composition, such as lipid core, fibrous cap thickness, and intraplaque hemorrhage7,8. However, pathological staging of arterial plaque specimens is the gold standard for diagnosis. Typically, plaques of stages 1–2 are considered stable, while those of stage 4 and above are regarded as unstable. Stage 3 plaques are usually considered a transitional phase, with stability between stable and unstable9. Therefore, treating unstable carotid plaques is of significant importance for the prevention of ischemic stroke.

Although many preclinical studies have shown therapeutic characteristics of compounds, including herbal medicines and targeted molecular drugs, only a few compounds have been proven to have clinical relevance10. However, even after optimal drug therapy, patients with unstable carotid plaques still have a persistent risk of embolism, mainly because the pathophysiological mechanism of carotid atherosclerotic plaques is still not fully understood, especially the impact of cellular senescence mechanisms11,12.

One of the main features of cellular senescence is the production of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors, including pro-inflammatory cytokines, MMPs, and other factors13. Vascular aging is a special form of aging process affecting vascular structure and function, involving a series of behavioral disorders including infiltrating immune cells, endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells12. Multiple studies have shown that cellular senescence plays a pro-atherosclerotic role, and eliminating these senescent cells can stabilize the fibrous cap14,15. Therefore, senescent cells are closely involved in the pathophysiological process of early and mid-stage AS13. However, the potential mechanism between cellular senescence and plaque stability remains unclear12,13. To this end, we will combine bioinformatics analysis with machine learning to further explore the pathological mechanism between cellular senescence and unstable carotid plaques. Currently, research in this field is still insufficient, and much potential valuable information remains to be discovered.

Materials and methods

Data collection

First, we present the flowchart of this study (Fig. 1). The Bulk-RNA sequencing transcriptome data of unstable carotid plaques were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GEO). The search strategy for this study included: (1) searching for “carotid atherosclerosis”; (2) selecting “Expression profiling by array” for the Study type option; (3) samples from Homo sapiens; (4) datasets including control group samples, arterial plaque stages I and II; (5) carotid plaque stage IV and above in the experimental group. The mRNA sequencing of dataset GSE43292 samples was based on GPL6244. It includes 32 carotid plaque samples of stage ≥ IV and 32 arterial plaque samples of low stage (stages I and II). The mRNA sequencing of dataset GSE163154 samples was based on GPL6104. It contains 27 unstable carotid plaque samples and 16 stable carotid plaque samples. In this study, dataset GSE43292 served as the test set, and dataset GSE163154 as the validation set. The Cell Senescence Database (http://csgene.bioinfo-minzhao.org) provided a list of 503 genes associated with cellular senescence.

Technical roadmap of this study.

Differential expression analysis

Our preliminary analysis of Bulk-RNA sequencing datasets was based on R software (version 4.3.2; https://cran.r-project.org). Before formal data analysis, we calculated distance matrices and performed sample clustering to identify outlier samples, and used the “NormalizeBetweenArrays” function for normalization. DEGs were identified using the “Limma” package16. Genes with |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 were identified as DEGs. Boxplots, volcano plots, and heatmaps were used for visualization.

Identification of cellular senescence-associated genes

Venn diagrams were used to screen the intersection of DEGs and cellular senescence-related genes to obtain cellular senescence-associated DEGs (CSA-DEGs).

Construction of protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

We used the STRING database V12 (http://string-db.org) to construct a PPI network with CSA-DEGs, setting the “minimum required interaction score” to 0.4 and hiding unconnected nodes. We obtained interaction scores for CSA-DEGs, then imported them into Cytoscape17 software V3.9.1for visualization, using the “cytoHubba” plugin18 to score all genes and obtain the top 20 genes scored under the MCC and Degree algorithms.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA is a systems biology method used to identify gene modules related to phenotypes and explore relationships between genes. This analysis was based on the R package “WGCNA”19. First, we cleaned the expression data, processed missing values, and selected the top 10,000 genes with the highest Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) as the data source. Second, hierarchical clustering was performed on the samples to check the similarity between samples and remove outliers. Subsequently, the best soft threshold was selected to construct a weighted network to ensure the network satisfies scale-free characteristics. The parameter networkType was set to “unsigned”, and RsquaredCut was set to 0.85. Then, a one-step method was used to construct a co-expression network (corType set to “bicor”, minModuleSize set to 200, mergeCutHeight set to 0.3), identify gene modules using the dynamic tree cut method, and calculate the correlation and significance between module characteristic genes and phenotypes. Finally, genes within the module most significantly correlated with traits were obtained.

Random forest (RF) and recursive feature elimination for support vector machines (RFE-SVM)

RF is an ensemble learning method that makes final predictions by constructing multiple decision trees and voting or averaging20. This method is very effective in both classification and regression problems, especially when dealing with high-dimensional datasets. By selecting the value with the smallest error as the mtry node value and the image value that tends to be stable as the n tree. According to the feature weight average decrease in accuracy (MDA) and average decrease in Gini coefficient (MDG), the top 20 key genes were selected.

RFE-SVM is a feature selection method that reduces the dimensionality of the feature space by recursively removing features that contribute least to the model21. Key genes were intercepted based on the accuracy curve and the point with the lowest error rate. We used R packages “e1071” and “caret” to implement RFE-SVM analysis, and R package “randomForest” to implement random forest analysis.

Validation and evaluation of CSA-signature genes

Venn diagrams were used to intersect the key module genes from WGCNA, RFE-SVM algorithm, RF algorithm, MCC and Degree algorithm results to obtain CSA-signature genes. The expression differences of CSA-signature genes were compared in the test set GSE43292 and validation set GSE163154 respectively, and the “pROC” package was used to implement ROC curves to evaluate their diagnostic performance.

Functional enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

First, we performed GO functional enrichment and KEGG enrichment analysis on CSA-DEGs. Subsequently, we divided the samples into two groups based on the median value of CSA-signature genes expression, then identified genes differentially expressed between the two groups of samples, and finally performed GSEA with these differentially expressed genes. GO functional enrichment, KEGG enrichment analysis and GSEA analysis were performed using the R package “ClusterProfiler”22. To ensure the reliability of the enrichment results, we used Benjamini-Hochberg FDR to correct the p-values for multiple hypothesis testing, with FDR < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Based on gene expression data, immune cell infiltration analysis was performed using the “CIBERSORT” algorithm23. 22 immune cell genes (LM22) were used as the reference set, with 1000 iterations. Spearman correlation analysis was used to calculate the correlation between CSA-signature genes and immune cells. The results of immune cell infiltration analysis were visualized using “ggplot”24.

Identification of candidate drugs

CSA-signature genes were input into the Enrichr website (https://www.amp.pharm.mssm.edu/Enrichr/) to download target drug information. Candidate drugs were ranked from high to low according to comprehensive scores. Drugs with P < 0.05 and relatively large comprehensive scores were considered significant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.2). Wilcoxon test was used to estimate expression differences between two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CSA-DEGs

The mRNA sequencing data quality of stable plaque group and unstable plaque group samples in dataset GSE43292 was qualified, with no obvious abnormal samples, and there were significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 2A–D). Therefore, all samples were included in the study. After differential expression analysis, 1336 differential genes were obtained based on the threshold (Fig. 2E), of which 596 were down-regulated and 740 were up-regulated. By intersecting with 503 genes in the cellular senescence gene set, we obtained 40 CSA-DEGs (Fig. 2F), of which 25 were up-regulated and 15 were down-regulated.

Construction of PPI network and functional enrichment analysis

First, we constructed a PPI network of CSA-DEGs through the STRING database, which contained 39 nodes and 134 edges, with a PPI enrichment p-value of 1.11e-16 (Fig. 3A). Using the “cytoHubba” plugin, all genes were scored to obtain genes and scores under the most commonly used algorithms (MCC and Degree algorithms) (Supplementary 1). Subsequently, this study used the KEGG pathway database for GO functional enrichment and KEGG enrichment analysis to explore biological pathways related to the occurrence and development of unstable carotid plaques. GO-BP functional enrichment included biological processes such as epithelial cell proliferation, ERK1 and ERK2 cascade, muscle cell proliferation, smooth muscle cell proliferation, mononuclear cell proliferation, T cell proliferation, etc. (Fig. 3B). KEGG enrichment analysis included signaling pathways such as MAPK signaling pathway, Estrogen signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, etc. (Fig. 3C). Finally, this study further conducted GSEA analysis (Fig. 3D), and we found immune-related pathways such as HALLMARK_INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE, HALLMARK_ALLOGRAFT_REJECTION, HALLMARK_INFLAMMATORY_RESPONSE, HALLMARK_IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING. The results of GSEA strongly suggest that immune processes are important factors driving the progression of unstable carotid plaques.

RFE-SVM and RF

First, the results of the RFE-SVM algorithm showed that the top 22 genes were the best combination of the highest accuracy point (0.767) and the lowest error rate point (0.233) (Fig. 4A,B), see Supplementary 1 for details. Second, the model established by the RF algorithm was evaluated, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.74 (Fig. 4C), indicating good model performance. Subsequently, we obtained gene importance rankings based on the RF algorithm (Fig. 4D), taking the top 20 genes as key genes, see Supplementary 1 for details.

WGCNA

First, the data quality control was good, with no obvious outlier samples (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, the best soft threshold was set to 22, and a one-step method was used to construct a co-expression network, obtaining 4 modules (Fig. 5B-C). Through correlation analysis, it was found that MEblue was most correlated with traits (r = 0.59, 2e −07, Fig. 5D). Finally, we obtained 1147 genes in the MEblue module (Table S1). Lastly, we intersected the key module (MEblue) genes from WGCNA, RFE-SVM algorithm results, RF algorithm, MCC and Degree algorithm results to obtain 3 CSA-signature genes, namely PPARG, PLAUR and RPS6KA1 (Fig. 5E).

Validation and evaluation of CSA-signature genes

First, we compared in dataset GSE43292 and found that the mRNA expression levels of the 3 CSA-signature genes were all higher in the unstable carotid plaque group than in the stable carotid plaque group (Fig. 6A–C). Meanwhile, ROC curves suggested good diagnostic performance for PPARG (AUC 0.765), PLAUR (AUC 0.809) and RPS6KA1 (AUC 0.846) (Fig. 6D). Subsequently, we performed external validation in the external dataset GSE163154. This dataset was processed with pre-analysis data processing and the data quality was good (Fig. S1). Our external dataset GSE163154 strongly validated the results of the internal validation (Fig. 6E–H). In view of this, we grouped the expression levels of the above 3 genes and performed GSEA analysis to further clarify their gene functions. Although the GSEA results of the 3 genes had different emphases, immune-related pathways were very significant, including positive upregulation of immune response, immune system processes, immune system processes, etc (Fig. 6I-K). This again suggests that immune processes are important factors driving the progression of unstable carotid plaques.

Immune infiltration analysis

Based on the enrichment analysis results, we evaluated the immune infiltration in unstable carotid plaques using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Overall, macrophages were the most prevalent (especially M2-like macrophages), followed by T cells (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, the immune cells with the most notable differences in proportion between the two groups were CD8 + T cells, monocytes, naive B cells, memory B cells, and neutrophils, with CD8 + T cells and monocytes showing the most significant decrease (Fig. 7B). Subsequently, we assessed the correlation between immune cells and the 3 CSA-signature genes. Pearson correlation analysis showed that PLAUR (r = -0.671), RPS6KA1 (r = -0.644), and PPARG (r = -0.537) were significantly negatively correlated with the proportion of CD8 + T cells (Fig. 7C-E). This suggests that there are significant differences in the immune infiltration pattern in unstable carotid plaques, which are closely related to the 3 CSA-signature genes.

They are CI-1040 TTD 00007168, CI-1040 CTD 00003697, MK 886 CTD 00002517, valsartan CTD 00002971, biochanin A CTD 00000149, Bexarotene CTD 00003225, testosterone CTD 00006844, Arsenous acid CTD 00000922, TERT-BUTYL HYDROPEROXIDE CTD 00007349, and Capsaicin CTD 00005570. Among these, testosterone CTD 00006844, Arsenous acid CTD 00000922, and TERT-BUTYL HYDROPEROXIDE CTD 00007349 may potentially target all 3 CSA-signature genes simultaneously.

Discussion

The progression mechanism of unstable carotid plaques has significant implications for preventing cerebral infarction, which severely impacts patients’ quality of life and disability levels. Research has found that cellular senescence is closely associated with unstable carotid plaques and linked to immune inflammation12. These findings provide new insights and approaches for treating unstable carotid plaques. Therefore, controlling cellular senescence and immunoregulatory strategies may become the most attractive methods for treating unstable carotid plaques11. In summary, studying the occurrence and development mechanisms of unstable carotid plaques from the perspective of cellular senescence and immune regulation is of great significance for in-depth understanding of their pathological mechanisms and identifying new therapeutic targets. To our knowledge, this may be the first study to comprehensively explore the role of cellular senescence-associated gene sets in the pathological mechanism of unstable carotid plaques through integrated bioinformatics analysis.

First, we investigated the expression and function of CSA-DEGs in unstable carotid plaques. The expression of CSA-DEGs in cells from unstable carotid plaques differed significantly from the control group. These genes are mainly related to cellular senescence, apoptosis, cell proliferation regulation, and inflammatory response. Typically, the characteristics of cellular senescence are described as irreversible proliferation arrest and SASP25. Additionally, these genes are involved in pathways such as the MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, and HIF-1 signaling pathway. These pathways play crucial roles in the aging process26,27, and their dysregulation is closely associated with the progression of unstable carotid plaques. Interestingly, we also observed that CSA-DEGs are closely related to T lymphocyte proliferation and cellular immunity, which is consistent with previous studies28.

Subsequently, we identified three robust potential biomarkers as CSA-signature genes (including PPARG, PLAUR, and RPS6KA1) from 40 CSA-DEGs using RFE-SVM, RF algorithms, and WGCNA methods. ROC curves showed that these 3 CSA-signature genes demonstrated good discriminative ability for unstable carotid plaques in both training and validation datasets. Meanwhile, previous studies have provided evidence supporting the critical roles of these 3 CSA-signature genes in the progression of unstable carotid plaques.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARG) is a transcription factor that encodes a member of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor subfamily of nuclear receptors. Recent studies have shown that the upregulation of PPARG in unstable atherosclerotic plaques is a phenomenon worth noting, consistent with previous findings29. The mechanism by which PPARG affects plaque instability is complex.Firstly, PPARG is typically expressed in differentiated macrophages derived from human monocytes and is responsible for regulating several key cholesterol synthesis genes and cholesterol receptors such as CD36. This process leads to lipid accumulation within macrophages and contributes to the formation of foam cells, which is an important indicator of plaque instability30–32. Secondly, PPARG regulates the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells. These cells play a crucial role in plaque progression and rupture mechanisms, so the regulation by PPARG may directly affect plaque stability and vulnerability33. Notably, PPARG can also promote the polarization and infiltration of macrophages to the M1 phenotype. This transformation exacerbates the inflammatory response and may further reduce plaque stability34. In summary, PPARG promotes the development of unstable arterial plaques through multiple mechanisms, including the regulation of lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses in macrophages as well as affecting the behavior of vascular smooth muscle cells. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for developing new therapies for atherosclerosis.

Urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (PLAUR) is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored cell surface receptor that primarily binds to urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA)35. During atherosclerosis, the expression of PLAUR significantly increases, particularly in vulnerable plaque regions, consistent with previous studies36. PLAUR plays multiple important roles in the instability of atherosclerotic plaques. Firstly, during the degradation of the fibrous cap, PLAUR promotes the activation of plasminogen by binding to uPA. This process leads to the degradation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as collagen and elastin, potentially thinning the fibrous cap and increasing the risk of plaque rupture37. Secondly, PLAUR aids in the recruitment and activation of monocytes and macrophages. It can increase the release of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, which accelerate plaque instability and enhance local inflammatory responses38, potentially speeding up plaque instability.The activation of PLAUR also promotes the migration and proliferation of endothelial cells and drives vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype. These changes stimulate neovascularization, further exacerbating plaque instability39,40. In summary, PLAUR accelerates the instability of atherosclerotic plaques through multiple pathways, including fibrous cap degradation, enhanced inflammatory responses, and neovascularization.

Ribosomal Protein S6 Kinase Alpha-1(RPS6KA1 ) is a serine/threonine kinase.

that plays multiple complex roles in the instability of atherosclerotic plaques. Firstly, RPS6KA1 is involved in regulating the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), which is closely related to plaque instability. Abnormal activity of VSMCs may promote plaque progression and rupture41. Then, RPS6KA1 increases the expression of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1 by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. This pro-inflammatory effect may accelerate plaque instability and enhance local inflammatory responses42. Furthermore, RPS6KA1 is closely related to oxidative stress. Studies show that it can affect the redox balance of cells by regulating the expression of antioxidant genes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT)43. This regulation may directly impact plaque stability.Additionally, RPS6KA1 affects the process of apoptosis by regulating Bcl-2 family proteins, potentially leading to the expansion of the necrotic core of plaques and increasing the risk of plaque rupture44,45. Finally, RPS6KA1 also plays an important role in promoting angiogenesis and abnormal lipid metabolism, processes that may further exacerbate plaque instability46,47. In summary, RPS6KA1 influences the stability of atherosclerotic plaques through multiple pathways, including VSMC proliferation and migration, enhanced inflammatory responses, oxidative stress regulation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis. These findings are significant for understanding the role of RPS6KA1 in atherosclerosis and may provide new insights for therapeutic strategies.

Compared to the indirect effects of CSA-signature genes, immune infiltration plays a crucial role in the development of unstable carotid plaques. Our study shows that macrophages (especially M2-like macrophages) are the most abundant in arterial plaques, followed by T cells, but the proportion of CD8+ T cells decreased most significantly in the unstable plaque group. This is consistent with previous studies48,49. Research has indicated that CD8+T cells are mainly present in the plaque shoulder and fibrous cap regions50. In early to mid-stage atherosclerosis, CD8+T cells trigger CCL2 expression in macrophages in an IFN-γ-dependent manner, inducing macrophage M2 polarization and immune suppression51. However, in unstable plaques (late-stage atherosclerosis), CD8+T cells are exhausted, not only decreasing in proportion but also becoming functionally limited, leading to increased lesion size and decreased plaque stability52. In our study, the expression of PLAUR, RPS6KA1, and PPARG in unstable plaques was significantly negatively correlated with CD8+ T cells, consistent with literature reports53,54.

Finally, we predicted 10 candidate drugs based on the 3 CSA-signature genes. Previous studies support the plaque-stabilizing effects of some candidate drugs. For example, studies have shown that valsartan can promote atherosclerotic plaque stability by upregulating Renalase55, and biochanin A has inhibitory effects on lipid metabolism disorders, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and matrix metalloproteinase expression56. Additionally, a study of 776 hypogonadal men showed that long-term testosterone treatment might help reduce cardiovascular risk57. However, other candidate drugs have no significant research records.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations that should be considered.Firstly, our findings are derived from publicly available gene expression datasets. While external datasets have validated the three potential biomarkers for unstable carotid plaques, it is crucial to verify these results using plaque tissue samples from patients with confirmed unstable carotid plaques. This direct validation would strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings.Secondly, additional in vitro and in vivo experiments, such as gene knockout or inhibition studies, are warranted to further validate the functional roles of the identified CSA-signature genes. These experiments would provide more robust evidence for the mechanistic involvement of these genes in plaque instability.

Conclusion

Through integrated bioinformatics and machine learning methods, we not only revealed the CSA-signature genes for unstable carotid plaque progression but also elucidated their relationship with the immune infiltration landscape of unstable carotid plaques. Our study lays the foundation for research into the mechanisms of unstable carotid plaque progression and provides some reference for targeted therapy.
